## Problem

Kinematic metrics like MPJPE average per-frame pose differences and systematically miss the physical artifacts—foot skating, mistimed touchdowns, unstable support switching—that human observers reliably detect in tracking videos. Meanwhile, the standard AMASS test set (140 sequences) is too small and homogeneous to stress contact-rich, long-horizon behaviors, making it difficult to diagnose where and why a tracker fails.

## Input / Output

The HumanScore model consumes 5-second windows of per-frame tokens, where each token concatenates rollout privileged signals (full-body state, contact-related quantities) with time-aligned reference features from the qpos trajectory; it deliberately operates on the underlying time series rather than rendered video to avoid visual confounds. The output is a scalar reward r_θ(τ) that is averaged over non-overlapping segments and then mapped to a 0–100 scale via a setting-level sigmoid calibration (median/IQR normalization). The benchmark additionally reports per-family Succ (%) and MPJPE (rad) alongside HumanScore for each of the four motion categories.

## Method

HumanScore is a lightweight five-layer bidirectional Transformer encoder (2 heads, FFN dim 1024) that encodes per-frame tokens, mean-pools the temporal sequence, and maps the pooled representation through an MLP head to a scalar score. It is trained on 12K human-annotated motion pairs using a Bradley-Terry likelihood for strict preferences and a score-consistency penalty for ties (both-good vs. both-bad), with the tie type calibrating absolute score scale. The preference data is collected by rendering synchronized rollout videos from multiple trackers trained with varied hyperparameters, then having annotators judge 5-second windows on fidelity and physical credibility. Ablations confirm the model relies heavily on contact-related features (removing them drops alignment from 0.93 to 0.74) and benefits from full 5-second temporal context rather than isolated frames, validating that it captures temporally structured physical artifacts rather than a kinematic pose-matching proxy.

## Results

In the zero-shot setting (Table 5), Humanoid-GPT leads on Daily (97.2% Succ, 0.060 rad MPJPE, 72.0 HumanScore) and Interaction (97.6% Succ, 0.051 rad MPJPE, 70.9 HumanScore), while SONIC achieves the highest HumanScore on Ground (35.3) despite 0% success. Ground remains largely unsolved: even the best method (Humanoid-GPT) completes only 19.2% of rollouts. On the held-out preference test split, HumanScore achieves 92.9% pairwise alignment with human judgments, substantially outperforming MPJPE (82.2%), foot-contact accuracy (53.1%), and all other handcrafted diagnostics (Table 3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Humanoid-GPT dominates the zero-shot benchmark on Daily and Interaction, achieving 97.2%/97.6% success and HumanScores of 72.0/70.9, well ahead of SONIC (52.5/65.9) and TWIST2 (24.3/41.2). On High Dynamic, SONIC edges out Humanoid-GPT in success rate (92.3% vs. 89.9%) but trails in HumanScore (43.1 vs. 64.0), suggesting SONIC's rollouts, while more stable, exhibit perceptual artifacts that HumanScore penalizes. Ground is the hardest family for all methods: SONIC and TWIST2 achieve 0% success, GMT only 3.0%, and even Humanoid-GPT reaches just 19.2%; yet SONIC paradoxically earns the highest Ground HumanScore (35.3) with the lowest MPJPE (0.260 rad), indicating its few completed rollouts are perceptually cleaner. GMT is the weakest baseline overall, with 21.1% success on Daily and 30.7% on High Dynamic, roughly 4× lower than Humanoid-GPT. The preference-alignment experiment (Table 3) confirms that HumanScore (0.9291 align rate) outperforms every conventional metric, with MPJPE the closest at 0.8218 and foot-contact accuracy far behind at 0.5307.

## Relation to Prior Work

The paper applies the preference-based reward modeling paradigm from RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017) and the Bradley-Terry pairwise comparison framework to the domain of humanoid motion tracking evaluation, where prior work has relied almost exclusively on frame-wise kinematic errors (MPJPE, joint angle error). It departs from the standard AMASS-based 140-sequence test suite by introducing a 150-hour, four-family optical mocap dataset with explicit text labels, and from handcrafted diagnostics (foot slip, root drift, contact accuracy) by learning a trajectory-level score from human judgments. The trajectory reward model itself is a lightweight bidirectional Transformer, conceptually similar to sequence-scoring architectures in NLP, repurposed here to aggregate temporally structured physical artifacts over 5-second windows.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HumanTracker addresses a concrete and previously under-quantified gap: kinematic metrics systematically miss the contact-timing, support-switching, and drift failures that human observers find most salient, and the standard evaluation suite is too small and homogeneous to expose these failures. The four-family taxonomy (Daily, High Dynamic, Interaction, Ground) enables diagnostic per-category reporting that a single aggregate score cannot provide, and the 150-hour scale with text labels makes the benchmark queryable for targeted stress-testing. Open questions include whether HumanScore generalizes to unseen tracker architectures or simulators, how to mitigate the reward-hacking behavior (stiff postures) observed when using it as an RL reward, and whether the Ground family's near-total failure across all methods reflects a fundamental limitation of current tracking formulations or simply insufficient training data for low-center-of-mass multi-contact transitions.
