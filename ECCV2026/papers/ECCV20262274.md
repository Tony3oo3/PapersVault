## Problem

Contrastive vision-language models like CLIP map semantically opposite phrases (e.g., 'a dog' vs. 'not a dog') to nearly identical embeddings, making them effectively blind to negation. The paper identifies the root cause as Representational Collapse: intermediate text-encoder layers build compositional syntax that separates negated from affirmative captions, but final layers overwrite this structure as they specialize for visual alignment. The central question is whether this lost negation signal can be recovered post-hoc without modifying any pretrained weights.

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard RGB image and a text caption as input, processing them through a fully frozen CLIP encoder. It produces two outputs: (1) an ℓ2-normalized corrected text embedding that is a drop-in replacement for CLIP's standard embedding (preserving the cosine similarity interface for retrieval and generation), and (2) a bounded scalar offset Δs ∈ [−α_max, α_max] added to the cosine similarity for discriminative tasks like MCQ. At inference, both corrections are applied uniformly to every caption without any polarity classification, gating, or external parsing.

## Method

PeakPatch intercepts the frozen CLIP text encoder at two layers identified by a Layer-wise Compositional Divergence (LCD) analysis: a compositional peak layer l_p (where negation separability is maximal) and an anchor layer l_a (a stable pre-alignment baseline). The Embedding Correction Network (ECN) uses a learned query to perform multi-head cross-attention over the full token sequence at l_p, concatenates the result with peak/anchor [EOS] tokens and a mean-pooled peak representation, and passes this through a bottleneck MLP to predict a deviation vector δ that shifts the collapsed final-layer embedding. A complementary Score Correction Network (SCN) contrasts the peak-layer representation, the ECN-corrected embedding, and the image embedding to predict a tanh-bounded scalar score offset. Both modules are trained jointly end-to-end (InfoNCE for ECN, cross-entropy for SCN) with all CLIP parameters frozen, adding only 5.2M parameters (3.5% of the backbone), and the coupled gradient flow between ECN and SCN is essential for optimal performance.

## Results

On NegBench COCO MCQ, PeakPatch achieves 74.3% average accuracy (+35.1 over CLIP's 39.2%, +17.8 over the best encoder fine-tuning method NegCLIP+NF at 56.5%), with negation accuracy jumping from 6.6% to 63.2%. On VOC MCQ, PeakPatch reaches 65.5%, surpassing all fine-tuning baselines. In the fully out-of-distribution retrieval setting (trained without COCO), PeakPatch achieves 31.4% R@1 and 56.9% R@5 on COCO, outperforming all fine-tuning baselines while modifying only 5.2M parameters (3.5% of the backbone). ECN-corrected embeddings also improve text-to-image generation negation score from 29.2% to 47.6% (+18.4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PeakPatch decisively outperforms the CLIP baseline [35] on every metric: +35.1 on COCO MCQ (74.3% vs 39.2%), +27.6 on VOC MCQ (65.5% vs 37.9%), and +6.4 R@1 on fully OOD COCO retrieval (31.4% vs 25.0%). Against encoder fine-tuning methods, PeakPatch beats NegCLIP [52] (26.8% COCO MCQ) and CoN-CLIP [40] (24.4%) by wide margins, and surpasses the strongest fine-tuning baselines CLIP+NF [3] (54.2%) and NegCLIP+NF [3] (56.5%) by 20.1 and 17.8 points respectively on COCO MCQ. On fully OOD retrieval, PeakPatch (31.4% R@1) edges out CLIP+NF (30.4%) and CoN-CLIP (25.7%) despite training only 5.2M vs 151M parameters. The post-hoc DCSM baseline [20] is beaten on both MCQ (74.3% vs 48.6% COCO) and OOD retrieval (31.4% vs 10.6% R@1 in-domain), though DCSM uses a different backbone (ViT-B/16). On text-to-image generation, PeakPatch's ECN-only variant (45.8% combined) surpasses NegationCLIP [33] (44.5%) and CLIP (28.3%) without modifying any encoder weights.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the interpretability line of work by Gandelsman et al. [8] (projecting intermediate ViT layers through the final head) to the CLIP text encoder, and builds on Quantmeyer et al. [34]'s causal-tracing evidence that negation awareness resides in a small fraction of intermediate attention heads. It departs from encoder fine-tuning methods (NegCLIP, CoN-CLIP, NegationCLIP) that are subject to Kang et al.'s [20] geometric impossibility theorem, and from inference-time methods (DCSM, SpaceVLM) that either break the standard cosine-similarity interface or require external parsers. The key conceptual departure is the Layer-wise Compositional Divergence (LCD) diagnostic, which formalizes the 'Representational Collapse' trajectory and provides a deterministic, data-driven rule for selecting which intermediate layer to read from—turning a mechanistic observation into a practical extraction strategy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For the broader CLIP ecosystem (LLaVA, BLIP-2, text-to-image generators, open-vocabulary detection), a frozen-backbone correction that preserves the standard cosine-similarity interface means downstream systems can gain negation awareness without re-validation or retraining. The 5.2M-parameter overhead (3.5% of the backbone) and single-A100 training cost make this practically deployable. Open questions remain: the geometric impossibility theorem [20] implies a fundamental ceiling on what any single embedding space can encode, so whether PeakPatch's corrections compose well with other compositional failures (attribute binding, spatial relations) is untested; the method also operates only on the text side, leaving the image encoder's own compositional limitations unaddressed. Generalization beyond negation to other syntactic operators (conjunction, conditionals) would be a natural next step.
