## Problem

Skeleton-based action recognition methods overwhelmingly assume complete, clean skeleton inputs, yet real-world deployments in egocentric vision, crowded surveillance, and edge robotics frequently suffer from limited field-of-view that causes substantial joint visibility dropout. This paper introduces the constrained FoV setting as a new task formulation and shows that existing models collapse under even moderate visibility restrictions, with accuracy dropping to near-chance levels when only 25% of joints are visible.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262750/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a 3D skeleton sequence of shape T×V×C_r (frames × joints × input channels) together with a joint visibility map of shape T×V whose entries are 1 for visible joints and 0 for invisible ones; after temporal downsampling by the MS-TCN, visibility values may become fractional. The FoV is simulated by selecting a centre joint (head, spine, or base-of-spine) and a window that leaves approximately 75%, 50%, or 25% of joints visible. The output is a single action class label over 60 or 120 categories, produced by temporal pooling followed by a linear projection.

## Method

PartialVisGraph processes each frame through a Hybrid Graph-Hypergraph block that splits features into a GCN branch (1/4 channels, providing anatomical inductive bias) and a hypergraph branch (3/4 channels). The hypergraph branch constructs a soft incidence matrix by computing cosine similarities between temporally-pooled joint summaries and a bank of 10 learnable virtual hyperedge tokens, then applying row-wise sparsemax to yield continuous [0,1] membership weights. A Single-Head Sample-Adaptive Transformer (SHSAT) aggregates joint features onto hyperedge tokens using an attention mask derived from the soft incidence matrix, with a visibility prior bias log(vis+ε) added to attention logits to suppress information flow from invisible joints. Temporal dynamics are captured by a Multi-Scale Temporal Convolutional Network. Training employs curriculum learning that progressively reduces the visible joint ratio from 95–100% to 20–80% over 200 epochs, temporal CutMix augmentation, and three auxiliary losses (hyperedge orthogonality, assignment balance/hinge, and class-centre clustering on the incidence matrix) to prevent hyperedge collapse and encourage semantically meaningful joint groupings.

## Results

Under the novel constrained FoV setting on NTU RGB+D (Head centre, hard difficulty, ~25% joints visible), PartialVisGraph achieves 79.2% X-Sub and 84.5% X-View accuracy, outperforming the strongest baseline Hyper-GCN (31.6% / 34.4%) by over 47 percentage points. On the full-visibility NTU RGB+D benchmark, PartialVisGraph reaches 93.8% X-Sub and 97.6% X-View, and on NTU RGB+D 120 it attains 91.0% X-Sub and 92.3% X-Set, both setting new state-of-the-art with a 4-stream ensemble. The model uses 4.0M parameters and 2.90 GFLOPs, comparable to or lower than most competing methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Under constrained FoV, PartialVisGraph dominates all four baselines tested in this setting (FR-Head, HD-GCN, Hyper-GCN, Hyperformer) by large margins at every difficulty level and FoV centre; the gap is most extreme at hard difficulty, where baselines drop to 20–35% while PartialVisGraph retains 71–85%. Among the full-FoV baselines in Table 3, PartialVisGraph (93.8% X-Sub) narrowly edges out Hyper-GCN (93.7%) and ProtoGCN (93.5%) on NTU RGB+D, and clearly surpasses CTR-GCN (92.4%), HD-GCN (93.0%), BlockGCN (93.1%), SkateFormer (93.5%), and FR-Head (92.8%). On NTU RGB+D 120, PartialVisGraph (91.0% X-Sub, 92.3% X-Set) beats all eight baselines, with the closest competitor being Hyper-GCN at 90.9% / 92.0%. The hypergraph-based baselines (Hyper-GCN, Hyperformer) are the strongest under constrained FoV, confirming that higher-order joint modelling helps, but they still lack the visibility-aware gating and flexible soft-incidence construction that give PartialVisGraph its decisive advantage. Under full FoV, the performance gap narrows to <1%, indicating that the hypergraph architecture is not overfit to the partial-visibility regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of hypergraph-based skeleton action recognition and robustness to incomplete observations. It departs from prior hypergraph methods (HyperSA, Hyper-GCN) by replacing fixed or binary incidence structures with a learned soft incidence matrix built from virtual hyperedge tokens and sparsemax, and from occlusion-focused work by reframing the problem as a sensor-level field-of-view constraint rather than object-level occlusion. The Single-Head Sample-Adaptive Transformer with a log-visibility attention bias is a novel aggregation mechanism not present in either the GCN or hypergraph literature, and the curriculum-learning-plus-temporal-CutMix training strategy is tailored specifically to the progressive visibility degradation this task introduces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The constrained FoV setting is practically motivated by embodied AI, wearable sensors, and edge robotics where cameras or pose estimators cannot observe the full body, yet no prior benchmark or method explicitly targets this regime. The dramatic performance gap (up to ~68% over baselines on the hardest split) highlights how unprepared current skeleton-AR models are for even moderate visibility loss, making this a useful wake-up call for the field. However, the evaluation relies on simulated FoV windows applied to complete NTU RGB+D skeletons with a known visibility map at test time, leaving open whether the approach transfers to genuinely partial observations from real sensors, noisy visibility estimates, or dynamic FoV changes. The modest 0.4% ablation contribution of the visibility prior itself suggests the hypergraph architecture's inherent flexibility does most of the heavy lifting, raising the question of whether simpler masking strategies could achieve similar robustness.
