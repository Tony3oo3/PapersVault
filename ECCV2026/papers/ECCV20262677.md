## Problem

Existing masked text-to-motion generators rely on whole-body, part-agnostic tokenization, making it impossible to edit a single limb without corrupting unrelated body parts. Part-aware alternatives either add per-limb generator branches (ParCo) or fragment capacity to joint-level tokens (MoGenTS), increasing architectural complexity without a clean mechanism to prevent inter-group information leakage during encoding.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262677/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For generation, the model takes a free-form text prompt (encoded via CLIP ViT-B/32) and produces a 22-joint 3D motion sequence at 20 FPS (HumanML3D) or 12.5 FPS (KIT-ML), internally represented as a T′×G×Q discrete token tensor with G=6 kinematic groups and Q=6 residual quantization levels. For localized editing, the input additionally includes a source motion, an edit prompt, and a temporal edit window (~35–60% of sequence); only the target kinematic group's tokens within that window are resampled while all other groups remain frozen, yielding a limb-level edited motion with preserved global trajectory.

## Method

K-Mask introduces a kinematic-group residual VQ-VAE (KG-RVQ) that partitions the encoder latent into G=6 anatomically grounded sub-streams (root, spine, left/right legs, left/right arms) with proportional capacity allocation, each quantized by an independent stack of Q=6 codebooks (K=512). A novel latent-aware kinematic dropout (LAKD) loss enforces disentanglement via three terms: positive within-group reconstruction, in-group suppression (penalizing other groups' latents from contributing to group g's motion), and out-group suppression (penalizing group g's latents from leaking into other groups). Two 8-layer bidirectional transformers—Base-T for coarse tokens (q=0) and Res-T for residual refinement (q>0)—use axis-factorized temporal-then-spatial attention to model the T′×G lattice efficiently. Training employs a hybrid masking strategy that combines random token occlusion with structured kinematic group masking (p_kin=0.25), where entire body subtrees are intermittently hidden to explicitly teach inter-group coordination. This design enables a single unified generator to support both text-to-motion synthesis and strict group-local editing without per-limb encoder/decoder stacks.

## Results

On HumanML3D, K-Mask achieves the best FID of 0.041, R-Precision Top-1 of 0.531, Top-3 of 0.824, and MultiModal Distance of 2.917, outperforming all 13 baselines. On KIT-ML, K-Mask similarly leads with FID 0.154, R-Precision Top-1 0.455, Top-3 0.793, and MultiModal Distance 2.671. In the matched 200-case localized editing evaluation, K-Mask attains the highest Target Success Rate (22.5%) and R@3 (28.4%) while achieving the lowest Spillover (0.108), Drift (0.41), and Skate (0.002), demonstrating superior editability-preservation trade-off.

## Baselines & Comparisons

K-Mask achieves the best FID on both benchmarks (0.041 on HumanML3D, 0.154 on KIT-ML), beating the closest discrete masked baselines MoMask (0.045 / 0.204) and BAMM (0.055 / 0.183) by 9% and 24% respectively on HumanML3D/KIT-ML FID. It also surpasses all diffusion-based methods (MDM 0.544, MLD 0.473, MotionDiffuse 0.630, ReMoDiffuse 0.103) and autoregressive methods (T2M-GPT 0.141, MotionGPT 0.232) on HumanML3D FID. In the matched localized editing evaluation (Table 2), K-Mask leads all five baselines on every metric: TSR 22.5% vs. MoMask 20.0%, BAMM 7.0%, ParCo 11.5%, MoGenTS 19.0%; Spillover 0.108 vs. 0.136–0.190; and Drift 0.41 vs. 0.74–1.33, confirming that kinematic-group tokenization enables targeted edits with minimal collateral change. MoGenTS achieves a slightly lower standard-generation FID (0.028) but trails K-Mask on all editing-specific preservation metrics, indicating a weaker editability-preservation trade-off.

## Relation to Prior Work

K-Mask extends the MoMask two-stage masked-transformer pipeline (base + residual RVQ) and the MaskGIT iterative decoding objective, but departs from the whole-body, part-agnostic tokenization that underlies MoMask, MMM, and BAMM by factorizing the latent space into six anatomically grounded kinematic groups, each with its own residual codebook stack. The LAKD loss is a novel anti-leakage regularizer not present in prior VQ-VAE or RVQ formulations, and the kinematic group masking strategy (occluding entire limb subtrees during training) goes beyond the random token masking used in MaskGIT and MoMask. The paper explicitly positions itself against per-part generator architectures (ParCo) and joint-level granularity (MoGenTS), arguing that group-level factorization within a single tokenizer offers a better complexity-control trade-off.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The kinematic-group token interface provides a practical, low-overhead mechanism for limb-level editing that does not require separate per-part generators or joint-level attention, making it attractive for interactive animation and VR applications where users specify part-specific edits. The 200-case localized editing evaluation protocol (TSR, Spillover, Drift, Skate) is a useful community resource that isolates the edit-vs-preservation trade-off, which standard FID/R-Precision metrics cannot capture. However, absolute TSR values remain modest (22.5%), indicating that semantically meaningful localized motion editing is still far from solved, and the framework's reliance on a fixed six-group anatomical partition limits its generality to other skeletal structures or non-humanoid agents.
