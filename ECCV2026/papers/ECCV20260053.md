## Problem

Existing instruction-guided image editing methods rely on text-based CoT reasoning for semantic understanding but lack explicit visual interaction during the reasoning process, leading to structural fidelity loss and vision-agnostic hallucinations in fine-grained synthesis. Additionally, no dedicated benchmark exists to evaluate whether editing directions conform to progressively finer attribute specifications from a single source image.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260053/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a source RGB image, a natural-language editing instruction, and three spatial conditions (SAM2 segmentation masks, Depth Pro depth maps, PiDiNet edge maps) at original image resolution, plus a 512×512 reference image generated by SD3.5 from a distilled attribute description. The model produces a structured five-stage textual CoT (Description → Grounding → Imagination → Control → Generation) with bounding boxes scaled to [0,1000] and per-condition weights in (0,1], an intermediate visual imagination image decoded from 64 [ref] tokens, and the final edited image synthesized by a DiT conditioned on 128 total tokens (64 ref + 64 context).

## Method

CORE-V integrates a Qwen3-VL-4B MLLM for sequential visual-semantic reasoning with a FLUX-based DiT for generation, connected via a linear connector that applies average pooling, a double-layer MLP, and an affine transformation to align visual token distributions with semantic features. The key architectural insight is the causal token ordering: 64 [ref] tokens first derive a visual imagination of target attributes (trained with L_ref that omits ControlNet to isolate attribute reasoning), then 64 [context] tokens supply supplementary context for final synthesis. Spatial control is injected through three independent ControlNet backbones whose outputs are aggregated via a weighted summation governed by per-condition scalars predicted by the CoT reasoning, enabling adaptive prioritization of mask, depth, or edge cues. Training follows a two-stage schedule: Stage 1 pre-trains the MLLM decoder on L_CE alone (50K steps, lr 1e-4), then Stage 2 jointly optimizes the full pipeline with L_total = L_CE + 1.0·L_ref + 2.5·L_edit (20K steps, lr 2e-5), using LoRA (r=64, α=128) on MLLM and DiT while fully fine-tuning the connector and ControlNet. The approach is trained on the newly curated CORE-Edit-800K dataset (800K samples across six editing categories, each annotated with both textual CoT steps and visual conditions) and evaluated on the introduced CORE-Bench (1,090 coarse-to-fine instruction triples from MSCOCO) using a CLIP_Delta metric that measures directional alignment between instruction and image feature increments.

## Results

On the Emu-Edit benchmark, CORE-V achieves CLIPout 0.313 and DINO 0.885, surpassing the strongest baseline IC-Edit (0.305, 0.866) in instruction alignment and structural consistency, while IC-Edit retains a marginal edge in CLIPim (0.907 vs. 0.902). On Reason-Edit, CORE-V leads all baselines in SSIM (0.893 understanding, 0.849 reasoning) and GPT-Eval (0.586), outperforming BAGEL (0.826, 0.787, 0.553). On the proposed CORE-Bench, CORE-V attains the highest GPT-Eval score (3.24/5) and best DINO similarity at both semantic (0.876) and context (0.881) levels, though BAGEL edges ahead on CLIP_Delta at the semantic level (8.131 vs. 7.998).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CORE-V outperforms all 11 baselines on the majority of metrics across three benchmarks. Against the strongest public-benchmark competitor IC-Edit [45], CORE-V wins on CLIPout (0.313 vs. 0.305) and DINO (0.885 vs. 0.866) but trails by 0.005 on CLIPim (0.902 vs. 0.907). On Reason-Edit, CORE-V dominates in SSIM (0.893/0.849) and GPT-Eval (0.586), beating the next-best BAGEL [4] (0.826/0.787, 0.553) by a clear margin, while BAGEL retains a tiny CLIP edge in the understanding scenario (23.593 vs. 23.575). On the proposed CORE-Bench, CORE-V achieves the top GPT-Eval (3.24) and best DINO at both semantic (0.876) and context (0.881) levels, surpassing Qwen-Image-Edit [39] (2.72, 0.814/0.824) and BAGEL (3.00, 0.756/0.833); however, BAGEL slightly leads on CLIP_Delta at the semantic level (8.131 vs. 7.998). Older U-Net-based methods (InstructPix2Pix, MagicBrush, UltraEdit, GoT) and the LLM-only BAGEL are consistently outperformed by 5–15 points on DINO and by 0.3–0.6 on GPT-Eval, confirming that the visual CoT design yields substantial gains over purely textual reasoning pipelines.

## Relation to Prior Work

CORE-V extends the visual CoT line initiated by GoT (Fang et al., NeurIPS 2025), which first incorporated editing bounding boxes into textual chain-of-thought, by replacing a single bounding-box condition with a richer set of intermediate visual signals—segmentation masks, depth maps, edge maps, and a generated reference image—that are explicitly scheduled by the reasoning pipeline. The generation backbone follows the Flux.1 Kontext DiT architecture, while the reasoning module is built on Qwen3-VL, positioning the work at the intersection of MLLM-based semantic encoders (Step1X-Edit, UniWorld-V1) and ControlNet-style spatial conditioning. Unlike prior visual CoT methods (T2I-R1, Image-CoT, Bagel) that remain text-centric in their intermediate reasoning, CORE-V introduces a dedicated 'visual imagination' stage where a reference image is synthesized from attribute descriptions and fed back into the diffusion process, closing the loop between semantic reasoning and pixel-level synthesis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The introduction of CORE-Bench, the first one-to-many coarse-to-fine editing benchmark with the CLIP_Delta directional metric, addresses a genuine gap: existing one-to-one benchmarks cannot measure whether a model progressively refines edits as instructions become more specific. The 800K-sample dataset with paired textual and visual CoT annotations is a substantial resource for training visual-reasoning editing models. Open questions include whether the visual imagination stage (which relies on SD3.5 for reference generation) introduces compounding errors for highly abstract or novel attributes, whether the six-category taxonomy generalizes to compositional multi-object edits, and whether the CLIP_Delta metric remains discriminative when instruction differences are very subtle.
