## Problem

Existing adversarial robustness studies for LiDAR-based 3D object detection are limited to legacy models, rely almost exclusively on mAP as an evaluation metric, and use classical 2D-style attacks (FGSM, PGD) rather than LiDAR-specific ones. This leaves it unclear whether recent SoTA architectures (e.g., transformer heads, scaled pillar encoders) have actually improved robustness or merely inherit the same structural vulnerabilities as their predecessors.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes raw LiDAR point clouds from nuScenes (32-beam) and Waymo (64-beam) and produces 3D bounding boxes with class labels and confidence scores. Beyond standard detection outputs, the evaluation decomposes localization error into translation, scale, and yaw components, partitions point clouds into inner/outer regions relative to a 0.8-scaled ground-truth box to assess structural sensitivity, and bins objects by ego-vehicle distance (near <20 m, mid 20–35 m, far 35–50 m). The attack success rate is redefined to exclude objects already missed on clean data and to count a correct-class prediction with confidence below 0.15 as a successful attack.

## Method

The paper proposes a holistic adversarial robustness benchmark that decouples structural robustness (point-cloud density and point-cloud localization) from predictive robustness (misclassification, bounding-box localization error, and ego-distance sensitivity). Four baseline detectors spanning pillar-based (PointPillars, PillarNeSt) and voxel-based (CenterPoint, FocalFormer3D) encoders with both anchor-based (RPN) and anchor-free (CenterHead, Transformer) heads are subjected to five LiDAR-specific attacks: three IoU-S variants (attachment, detachment, perturbation), a feature-space Non End-to-End attack, and a black-box LiDAttack using genetic simulated annealing. The key empirical finding is that high-capacity voxel-based detectors are significantly more susceptible to structured coordinate perturbations (PB, NE) than pillar-based detectors, whose vertical aggregation provides structural invariance, while non-anchor-based heads exhibit poor adversarial robustness. The authors attribute this fragility to representation fragility: models memorize canonical point-cloud densities and geometric templates from the training distribution rather than learning robust volumetric invariants.

## Results

On nuScenes, the perturbation attack (PB) causes the largest AP drops for voxel-based detectors: 65.5% for CenterPoint and 38.9% for FocalFormer3D on Cars, and 85.8% and 81.5% respectively on Safety-Critical classes. Pillar-based models show the opposite vulnerability pattern, with the attachment attack (PA) causing 85.4% AP drop for PointPillars and 32.2% for PillarNeSt on Cars. The black-box LiDAttack remains the least effective across all models, with performance drops below 2.6%. On Waymo, the PB attack is even more devastating for CenterPoint (91.0% AP drop on Cars), while PillarNeSt retains the best overall robustness profile with maximum drops of 24.5% (PA, Safety-Critical).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks four detectors (two legacy: PointPillars, CenterPoint; two modern: PillarNeSt, FocalFormer3D) under five LiDAR-specific adversarial attacks. Despite FocalFormer3D achieving the highest clean mAP (70.5 on nuScenes, 71.9 on Waymo), it is not more robust than legacy models: PB causes 38.9% AP drop on Cars and 81.5% on Safety-Critical classes, and its transformer attention mechanism propagates adversarial noise from distant points, generating high false negatives. PillarNeSt, the strongest modern pillar-based model, shows the best overall robustness profile (max 32.2% AP drop on Cars via PA) but remains highly vulnerable to point-injection attacks due to the coarse spatial quantization of pillar encoding. CenterPoint, despite being a legacy model, suffers the worst PB vulnerability (65.5% on nuScenes, 91.0% on Waymo for Cars), confirming that high-capacity voxel-based detectors over-rely on canonical spatial distributions. PointPillars, the simplest baseline, is the most vulnerable to PA (85.4% AP drop) but surprisingly competitive against PB (10.0%), suggesting that architectural complexity does not uniformly improve or worsen robustness. The key takeaway is that no recent model demonstrates meaningful adversarial robustness gains over its predecessors, and the vulnerability profile is architecture-dependent rather than capacity-dependent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial robustness analysis line initiated by Zhang et al. [48] and Dong et al. [11] by shifting the evaluation target from legacy detectors (PointPillars, SECOND) to current SoTA architectures (PillarNeSt, FocalFormer3D) and replacing classical FGSM/PGD-style attacks with LiDAR-specific methods (IoU-S, NE, LiDAttack). It departs from prior work primarily in evaluation scope: where earlier studies reported only mAP or ASR degradation, this paper introduces a five-criterion framework spanning structural factors (point cloud density, point cloud localization) and predictive factors (misclassification, bounding-box localization error, ego-distance), arguing that mAP-centric benchmarks mask critical vulnerabilities. The attack selection is also motivated by architectural bias—each attack is matched to the design choices it most stresses—rather than applying a single generic perturbation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical implication is that the architectural sophistication driving recent mAP gains (transformer heads, sparse convolutions, hard-instance probing) does not translate into adversarial robustness, and in some cases actively worsens it (e.g., FocalFormer3D's global attention propagates adversarial noise across the scene). This challenges the implicit assumption in the 3D-OD community that higher clean-data accuracy implies safer deployment, and motivates incorporating robustness-aware training (distance-aware learning, adversarial augmentation) into the standard pipeline. Open questions include whether the identified 'Representation Fragility' can be mitigated without sacrificing clean-data performance, and whether the proposed multi-dimensional evaluation can be standardized as a community benchmark. A stated limitation is that some baselines were trained from scratch under default configurations, so absolute robustness numbers may shift with better tuning.
