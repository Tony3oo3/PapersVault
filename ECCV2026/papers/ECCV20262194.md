## Problem

Despite 3D Gaussian Splatting being widely used as a geometry representation for mesh extraction and editing, no theoretical guarantee exists that the standard 3DGS objective actually recovers the true scene surface rather than overfitting view-dependent appearance. This paper identifies and formally characterizes a specific failure mode—the 'opaque billboard'—where sufficiently high angular capacity allows a geometrically incorrect surface to perfectly mimic correct appearance by encoding spatial texture as view-dependent color, leaving downstream geometry silently corrupted.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view RGB images with known camera poses and produces a set of 3D Gaussian primitives (3D centers, covariance matrices, opacities, and spherical-harmonic color coefficients) that render novel views. The critical experimental variable is the SH degree L (ranging from 0 to 24), which bounds the angular capacity of each Gaussian's view-dependent color. Geometry quality is measured by normalized symmetric Chamfer distance (nCD) between Gaussian centers and ground-truth surfaces, while appearance quality is measured by PSNR; the 'opaque billboard' label is assigned when PSNR stays within 2 dB of the best while nCD exceeds 5× the best nCD.

## Method

The paper develops a mathematical framework built on a first-hit rendering abstraction that reduces 3DGS to an opaque-surface model where each ray's color is determined solely by its first intersection point, cleanly separating geometry (which point is hit) from appearance (what color is emitted). For any fixed geometry, the optimal appearance function is shown to be a pointwise orthogonal projection of the through-seen target onto the finite-dimensional SH subspace V_L, reducing the global optimization to independent local least-squares problems. The key mechanism is a parallax frequency-transport law: a depth misalignment Δz multiplies spatial texture frequency k to produce an induced angular frequency ω_fake ≈ k·Δz, which a misaligned surface must encode in its SH coefficients. This yields a strict identifiability window: for L < ω_fake, the misaligned surface suffers an irreducible constant error (Proposition 3) while the true surface's error decays as (1+L(L+1))^{-s_true} (Proposition 2), guaranteeing A(L) < B_U(L); for L ≥ ω_fake, the SH basis becomes expressive enough to memorize the parallax-induced texture, permitting zero-loss billboard solutions. Experiments on synthetic stress-test shapes confirm billboard failures emerge precisely at high SH degrees, while real-scene benchmarks with rich spatial texture keep ω_fake beyond tested capacities, preserving surface consistency.

## Results

On ten real-scene datasets, 3DGS reconstructions remain surface-consistent across SH degrees: average nCD increases only from 0.0220 (SH=3) to 0.0254 (SH=24), a factor of ~1.2×, while average PSNR improves from 29.6 dB to 30.2 dB. No run satisfies the opaque-billboard criterion (PSNR within 2 dB of best AND nCD ≥ 5× nCD_best). On the synthetic stress-test (100 shapes), ~89% of shapes show nCD growth with increasing SH degree, with two dominant failure patterns: a 'burst' mode (53% of drifting cases) where nCD spikes sharply at SH 6–12, and a 'linear' mode (42%) where nCD grows gradually, both while PSNR stays within 2 dB of the best.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new reconstruction method and therefore does not compare against external baselines (e.g., NeRF, other Gaussian variants, or mesh-based methods). The experimental comparison is internal: the same 3DGS pipeline is trained at SH degree 3 versus SH degree 24 on identical datasets, isolating the effect of angular capacity on geometric fidelity. The key finding is that on all ten real-scene benchmarks, increasing SH from 3 to 24 degrades nCD by at most ~1.2× while improving PSNR by 0.5–0.7 dB, confirming that standard capture protocols keep reconstructions within the theory's 'safe' identifiability window. The synthetic stress-test serves as the controlled counterfactual: by reducing spatial texture frequency (smaller k), the induced parallax frequency ω_fake drops below tested SH capacities, and billboard failures emerge deterministically—validating the theoretical prediction that the failure mode is capacity-gated rather than inherent to 3DGS.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) and its SH-based view-dependent radiance head, but its conceptual lineage extends to the classical plenoptic bandwidth literature (Buehler, Chai, Do, Gortler, Levoy, Lin, Nguyen), which established that imperfect proxy geometry converts spatial texture into angular frequency. The paper reinterprets this frequency-transport phenomenon not as a sampling-rate constraint but as a geometric-identifiability condition specific to 3DGS's finite-degree SH head. It also positions itself relative to shape-radiance ambiguity analyses in NeRF (Mildenhall, Verbin, Fang et al.) by providing a complementary parallax-frequency explanation rather than a color-estimation one, and it contextualizes its findings against the large body of surface-extraction methods (SuGaR, 2DGS, 3DGSR, PGSR) that implicitly assume 3DGS geometry is trustworthy without theoretical justification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical characterization of when 3DGS geometry is actually surface-consistent versus when it can silently collapse into an off-surface billboard while maintaining near-optimal PSNR. The identifiability window (bounded SH degree below ω_fake ≈ kΔz) gives practitioners a principled criterion for capacity scheduling and explains why geometry-aware variants (2D Gaussians, SDF coupling, surface alignment) are valuable: they shrink or eliminate the billboard-favorable regime. Key limitations include the first-hit abstraction (not full volumetric compositing), the exclusion of mirror-like/transparent materials, and the fact that real-world rich textures naturally push ω_fake beyond tested SH degrees, so the failure mode is primarily a synthetic-stress-test phenomenon. Open questions include whether the identifiability window can be enforced adaptively during training, how it interacts with dynamic scenes, and whether the first-hit assumption holds under sparse-view or heavily transparent settings.
