## Problem

Existing vision-language benchmarks treat object orientation as a secondary subset of spatial reasoning, conflating it with positional relationships and general scene understanding, while suffering from narrow directional focus, synthetic-only data, ambiguous prompts, and egocentric bias. This paper asks whether MLLMs can genuinely reason about object orientation across the full spectrum of perceptual and cognitive demands—from basic frontal perception to compound rotations and canonical pose inference—when orientation is isolated as the primary evaluation target.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262395/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question presents an RGB image (or image pair for rotation tasks) with a bounding box isolating the target object, paired with a structured five-component prompt containing task description, contextual definition, step-by-step instructions, multiple-choice options, and reasoning examples. All questions include a 'Cannot be determined' option to handle symmetric objects or insufficient visual evidence, and responses require both an explicit answer and a chain-of-thought explanation. Coarse questions ask categorical judgments (e.g., 'facing toward or away?'), while granular questions demand precise angular measurements (e.g., 'at what angle relative to the camera?').

## Method

DORI decomposes object orientation into four cognitively motivated dimensions—frontal alignment, rotational transformation, relative orientation, and canonical orientation—each assessed at both coarse (categorical) and granular (metric) levels, yielding 33,656 multiple-choice questions over 13,652 images from 14 sources (37% real, 63% synthetic). Questions are generated by converting structured 3D annotations (rotation matrices, pose parameters) from datasets like KITTI, Cityscapes, 3D-FUTURE, and ShapeNet into orientation queries, supplemented by manual annotation of COCO images with 96.7% inter-annotator agreement. The benchmark isolates orientation from confounds through bounding-box isolation, standardized spatial reference frames (egocentric, allocentric, object-centric), and structured prompts iteratively refined via human feedback. Evaluation of 26 MLLMs reveals that even the best models achieve only 64.2% coarse and 42.9% granular accuracy, with the largest drops on compound rotations and inter-object reference frame shifts, exposing reliance on categorical heuristics rather than continuous geometric reasoning.

## Results

Across 26 MLLMs, the best closed-source model (Gemini 3 Flash pre-release) achieves 64.2% coarse and 42.0% granular accuracy on DORI, while GPT-5-mini leads on granular at 42.9%. Most open-source models perform near or below random chance (25.7% coarse / 16.5% granular baseline), with the best open-source model (Qwen3-32B-Inst) reaching only 46.3% coarse and 36.9% granular. Expert humans achieve 88.0% coarse and 84.8% granular, leaving a ~17–18 percentage-point gap to the best MLLM. Performance drops sharply on compound rotations and allocentric reference-frame shifts, where even top models fall below 25% granular accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-5-mini achieves the highest granular accuracy (42.9%) among all evaluated models, edging out GPT-5.5-Pro (41.9%) and Gemini 3 Flash pre-release (42.0%), while Gemini 3 Flash leads on coarse accuracy (64.2%) over GPT-5.5-Pro (58.9%) and GPT-5-mini (56.1%). Among open-source models, Qwen3-8B-Inst. (51.5% C / 29.9% G) and IntVL-30B-A3B-Inst. (50.7% C / 24.5% G) lead, but both trail the best closed-source models by 12–13 points on coarse and 12–18 points on granular. Notably, GPT-4o ranks 2nd on OmniSpatial and 3rd on MMSI-Bench yet drops to 4th on DORI coarse, confirming that general spatial competence does not transfer to object-centric orientation. Robotics-specialized models (Magma-8B, RP-v1-13B) fail to exceed general-purpose architectures, scoring 30.8% and 35.2% coarse respectively. LoRA fine-tuning on DORI boosts Qwen2.5-VL-3B by up to 27 percentage points on external benchmarks (BLINK, 3DSRBench, SAT), demonstrating that orientation-specific training yields transferable geometric gains rather than prompt-specific memorization.

## Relation to Prior Work

DORI carves new ground by elevating object orientation from a secondary subset of spatial reasoning (as in BLINK, Spatial-MM, EmbSpatial-Bench) to the primary evaluation target, decomposed along four cognitively motivated dimensions (frontal alignment, rotational transformation, relative orientation, canonical orientation) each assessed at both coarse and granular levels. The benchmark repurposes existing 3D annotation pipelines from KITTI, Cityscapes, ShapeNet, 3D-FUTURE, Objectron, and NOCS to generate orientation ground truth, while drawing on developmental neuroscience literature to justify the hierarchical task structure. Unlike prior benchmarks that conflate orientation with positional relationships or restrict to egocentric frames, DORI explicitly separates egocentric and allocentric reference frames and introduces a 'Cannot be determined' option to handle symmetric-object ambiguity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The consistent finding across 26 models—that general spatial competence does not transfer to object-centric orientation reasoning—establishes orientation as a distinct, unsolved challenge with direct implications for robotic manipulation, 3D scene reconstruction, and AR. The large coarse-to-granular gap (64.2% vs. 42.9% for the best model) reveals that current MLLMs rely on categorical heuristics rather than continuous geometric reasoning, a limitation invisible to single-score benchmarks. Open questions include whether stronger architectural geometric priors (beyond CLIP-style contrastive pretraining) are necessary, whether the fusion-strategy findings generalize beyond the confounded Qwen experiment, and whether the ~18-point human-model gap can be closed through targeted orientation training at scale.
