## Problem

Existing surgical vision datasets capture single-trajectory sequences from a single endoscope, making it impossible to distinguish genuine geometric generalization from photometric interpolation along a narrow forward-facing path. No prior dataset provides independent held-out viewpoints for robot-assisted minimally invasive surgery, leaving a critical gap in how 3D perception methods are evaluated under the extreme viewpoint constraints of endoscopic imaging.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262738/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset provides 340 synchronized sequences (~170K timepoints) from two independent stereo endoscopes (4 camera streams at 60 FPS) separated by 2.6±1.2 cm with 18.7°±10.0° orientation difference, captured across ex vivo, postmortem, and live surgical settings. Each timepoint includes calibrated intrinsics, ArUco-based frame-wise 6-DOF camera poses with translational and rotational uncertainty estimates, SAM-3-derived instrument segmentation masks, and clinical metadata (anatomical region, instrument type, motion category). The evaluation protocol trains on Endoscope 2 and tests on Endoscope 1, eliminating trajectory overlap and measuring cross-camera geometric fidelity rather than same-path interpolation.

## Method

The core contribution is a synchronized dual-endoscope acquisition pipeline combined with a train-on-one-endoscope, test-on-another evaluation protocol. Camera poses are estimated via a five-stage ArUco-based pipeline: RANSAC initialization with Levenberg-Marquardt reprojection refinement, stereo fusion via quaternion interpolation, velocity-based outlier rejection (thresholded at 0.7 cm/frame translation and 3.0°/frame rotation), Savitzky-Golay temporal smoothing, and left-right consistency-based uncertainty quantification, achieving sub-2 mm physical accuracy at 5 cm working distance. The benchmark evaluates 23 SOTA methods across rigid NVS (NeRF, 3DGS), deformable NVS (4DGS variants, Endo-4DGS, DynOmo), pose estimation/feature matching (RoMa, MASt3R, GIM+LightGlue, SIFT), and monocular depth (UniDepth-V2, MoGe-V2, Endo3R, EndoDAC), revealing that photometric-only methods collapse under held-out viewpoints while geometrically regularized methods and large-scale foundation models transfer more robustly to surgical imagery.

## Results

Under the train-on-endoscope-2, test-on-endoscope-1 protocol, DynOmo with FoundationStereo depth achieves the best NVS quality (25.29 PSNR, 0.90 SSIM), substantially outperforming photometric-only methods like D-3DGS (14.50 PSNR, 0.51 SSIM) and 4DGS-Wu (15.65 PSNR, 0.33 SSIM). For feature matching, RoMa achieves the best absolute accuracy (1.79° rotation error, 78.1% inlier rate, 3905 inliers), while GIM+LightGlue achieves the best temporal consistency (0.41° drift vs. 1.38–2.00° for dense methods). For monocular depth, general-purpose UniDepth-V2 (0.081 Abs Rel) and MoGe-V2 (0.109) outperform surgical-specific EndoDAC (0.119) and Endo3R (0.187) with median scaling, though without scaling their δ-accuracies drop to zero, revealing a critical global-scale mismatch.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In rigid NVS, NeRF without depth (17.24 PSNR) outperforms 3DGS without depth (14.82 PSNR), but depth supervision has opposite effects: it degrades NeRF (to 15.93–16.29) while improving 3DGS (to 15.33), suggesting NeRF exploits semi-transparent volumes that depth constraints disrupt. In deformable NVS, DynOmo with FoundationStereo (25.29 PSNR, 0.90 SSIM) dominates all other methods by a wide margin, beating Endo-4DGS (16.26 PSNR) by ~9 dB and D-3DGS (14.50 PSNR) by ~11 dB, confirming that dense 3D geometric constraints (local rigidity, isometry, instance-aware grouping) are critical under held-out viewpoints. For feature matching, RoMa (1.79° error, 78.1% inliers) and MASt3R (1.81°, 70.3%) achieve the best absolute accuracy with 2–50× more correspondences than sparse methods, while GIM+LightGlue (1.87°) trades slight accuracy for superior temporal stability (0.41° drift vs. 1.38–2.00° for dense methods). SIFT (4.04° error, 5.44° drift) confirms handcrafted features are inadequate for surgical scenes. In monocular depth, UniDepth-V2 (0.081 Abs Rel) and MoGe-V2 (0.109) outperform the surgical-specific EndoDAC (0.119) and Endo3R (0.187) with median scaling, but without scaling their δ₁ accuracies collapse to 0.000, exposing a fundamental scale-calibration gap that surgical-domain methods avoid by construction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first synchronized multi-endoscope dataset for robot-assisted minimally invasive surgery, departing from all prior surgical datasets (STIR, StereoMIS, MITI, SCARED, SERV-CT) that provide only single-endoscope, single-trajectory captures. The 'train-on-one-endoscope, test-on-another' evaluation protocol is borrowed conceptually from multi-view human capture benchmarks (PanopticStudio, Immersive Video, Plenoptic Video) but repurposed for the extreme constraints of endoscopic imaging, where cameras follow narrow forward-facing paths through deformable tissue. Rather than proposing a new method, the paper repurposes 23 existing SOTA methods (NeRF, 3DGS, 4DGS variants, DynOmo, RoMa, MASt3R, UniDepth-V2, etc.) as benchmarks to expose previously hidden failure modes under physically independent viewpoints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

iMED exposes a fundamental blind spot in surgical vision benchmarking: standard same-trajectory evaluation cannot distinguish genuine geometric generalization from photometric interpolation, and this paper provides the infrastructure to separate the two. The finding that large multi-domain foundation models outperform surgical-specific models (e.g., UniDepth-V2 beats Endo3R and EndoDAC on depth) suggests current fine-tuning protocols may discard invariances needed for cross-device generalization, pointing toward multi-domain training as the path forward. Key open questions include the 16× computational cost of dense 3D regularization (DynOmo vs. Endo-4DGS), which remains prohibitive for real-time clinical deployment, and the need for greater subject and anatomical diversity to validate findings beyond 14 specimens.
