## Problem

Standard 3D Gaussian Splatting relies on positional gradients and covariance scales for density control, which fails to trigger densification in under-densified detailed regions because their gradients are near-saturated. Simply lowering the gradient threshold to force more primitives yields excessive counts with only marginal quality gains, leaving textured patterns and object boundaries poorly reconstructed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261206/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard multi-view RGB images under the 3DGS training protocol (every 8th image held out for testing) and produces a set of 3D Gaussian primitives (position, scale, rotation, color, opacity) plus per-primitive blur levels σ_i. During training, the differentiable blur pipeline additionally consumes the GT images to compute a per-pixel blur map σ_pix, which is backpropagated only to update σ_i without affecting other 3DGS parameters. The CUDA implementation achieves 2.52 ms per blur step at 1256×828 resolution.

## Method

SharpGS introduces a differentiable blur-driven density control that uses per-primitive blur levels σ_i as a perceptual cue for selective densification. A CUDA-based differentiable 2D Gaussian blur is applied to GT images with per-pixel σ_pix values, and the discrepancy between the rendering and the blurred GT is minimized via a combined L1+SSIM loss, with gradients backpropagated exclusively to σ_i. Two regularization schemes prevent redundant growth: a detail-aware loss term L_reg penalizes σ_pix in inherently smooth regions (e.g., sky) using a spatial prior derived from the difference between GT and its heavily blurred version, and a blur-adaptive soft opacity attenuation (applied every 500 iterations) progressively weakens high-blur primitives that fail to contribute to rendering quality. The densification criterion is modified so that a primitive is split or cloned if its positional gradient exceeds τ_p, or if its blur σ ≥ τ_σ while its gradient exceeds a relaxed threshold τ_p·k_p (k_p=0.05), allowing blur to independently trigger densification in regions where gradients are saturated.

## Results

On Mip-NeRF360, SharpGS+3DGS achieves 27.58 PSNR and 0.190 LPIPS with 3,083K primitives, outperforming vanilla 3DGS (27.41 PSNR, 0.218 LPIPS, 3,350K primitives) in both quality and compactness. The best integration, SharpGS+FDS-GS, reaches 27.70 PSNR / 0.203 LPIPS on Mip-NeRF360, 24.23 PSNR / 0.139 LPIPS on Tanks&Temples, and 29.84 PSNR / 0.231 LPIPS on Deep Blending, while using only ~1,500K primitives. Across all three datasets, SharpGS consistently improves LPIPS (perceptual quality) by 0.01–0.04 over the strongest baselines while reducing primitive counts by 10–35%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SharpGS+3DGS outperforms vanilla 3DGS on all three datasets in PSNR (+0.16 to +0.29) and LPIPS (−0.011 to −0.011) while using fewer primitives (e.g., 3,083K vs. 3,350K on Mip-NeRF360). Against Mip-Splatting, SharpGS+3DGS achieves comparable PSNR but substantially better LPIPS (0.190 vs. 0.217 on Mip-NeRF360) with fewer primitives. Compared to Pixel-GS, SharpGS+3DGS matches LPIPS (0.190) on Mip-NeRF360 but uses 45% fewer primitives (3,083K vs. 5,622K); on Tanks&Temples, SharpGS achieves 0.152 LPIPS vs. Pixel-GS's 0.151 with 64% fewer primitives. The strongest baseline, FDS-GS, achieves slightly better LPIPS on Tanks&Temples (0.145 vs. 0.152) and Deep Blending (0.232 vs. 0.237) with similar primitive counts, but SharpGS+FDS-GS integration surpasses it on all metrics (e.g., 24.23 PSNR / 0.139 LPIPS on T&T). Against compactness-focused methods (Steep-GS, Compact-GS), SharpGS+3DGS achieves substantially higher PSNR (+0.58 to +0.69) and lower LPIPS (−0.03 to −0.06) at the cost of modestly more primitives, confirming that the blur-driven densification targets quality-relevant regions rather than indiscriminately reducing count.

## Relation to Prior Work

SharpGS builds directly on 3DGS [16] by replacing its gradient-based density control with a differentiable blur-driven criterion, positioning itself as a modular plug-in that can be integrated with existing 3DGS variants (Mip-Splatting, Pixel-GS, FDS-GS, SteepGS, Compact-GS). It departs from the dominant lineage of improving positional-gradient thresholds or frequency-domain regularization (FreGS, Wavelet-GS) by instead using per-primitive blur as a direct perceptual proxy for under-densification, drawing on image-space optimization techniques [18] for the differentiable blur pipeline. The approach also contrasts with pixel-error-based densification (Revising-3DGS, ConeGS) by learning a spatially-varying blur map rather than relying on raw reconstruction error, and it introduces two regularization mechanisms (detail-aware loss and blur-adaptive opacity attenuation) to counteract the natural tendency of blur-driven densification to over-produce primitives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in SharpGS's modularity: it consistently improves quality across six different 3DGS baselines while keeping primitive counts comparable or lower, making it an attractive drop-in enhancement for the 3DGS ecosystem. The conceptual framing—that under-densification locally mimics a blurred GT image—provides an intuitive and computationally lightweight alternative to Fourier or wavelet-based frequency analysis. Limitations include the resolution dependency of the learned blur (mitigated but not eliminated by normalization), the added per-step CUDA overhead (~2.5 ms), and evaluation restricted to three standard datasets. Open questions include whether the blur cue generalizes to dynamic scenes, sparse-view settings, or non-Gaussian primitive representations, and whether the opacity attenuation strategy interacts well with other pruning or compression schemes at scale.
