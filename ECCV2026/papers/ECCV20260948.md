## Problem

Multimodal Large Language Models exhibit severe deficiencies in fine-grained spatial understanding for Remote Sensing, where sub-meter precision is operationally critical. Existing RS datasets are bottlenecked by reliance on legacy closed-set benchmarks (DOTA, DIOR) with limited semantic scope or crowd-sourced OSM data with geometric inconsistency, failing to provide the dense, verified annotations needed for precise spatial grounding at scale.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260948/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes 672×672 pixel aerial orthophotos at 20 cm spatial resolution paired with text instruction prompts, and produces task-specific outputs: scene captions, class labels from a 135-category hierarchical taxonomy, horizontal bounding boxes [x_min, y_min, x_max, y_max], oriented bounding boxes [x_center, y_center, h, w, θ], polygon segmentation masks as coordinate sequences, or binary presence/absence answers. All spatial coordinates are normalized to [0,1] and discretized to [0–1000] for autoregressive generation, with a median annotation density of 8 objects per image.

## Method

The core contribution is a data curation pipeline that transforms legally verified cadastral vector records (France's IGN, 1 m precision) into 3.8M annotated objects across 510k images and 135 fine-grained semantic categories, bypassing the quality-scalability trade-off of human labeling or OSM. The pipeline filters non-visible entities, resamples scene compositions to reduce 85% redundancy, merges legally partitioned but visually contiguous polygons, and generates instruction templates for seven spatial grounding tasks. For the baseline, LLaVA-1.6 7B is fine-tuned with LoRA (frozen vision encoder, single epoch on 8×A100 GPUs) on 1.85M instructions augmented with text-only polygon reasoning and exhaustive segmentation queries. The key finding is that this high-fidelity data alone—without architectural modifications—enables a standard VLM to outperform both RS-specialized models and Gemini-2.5 Flash by large margins, isolating the data as the decisive variable.

## Results

The fine-tuned GroundSet baseline (LLaVA-1.6 + LoRA) achieves 94.18% classification accuracy (acc@0.8), 49.47 F1@0.5 on object detection, 72.14 F1@0.5 on multi-class detection, 39.45 F1@0.5 on REC, and 44.65 F1@0.5 on segmentation, alongside 104.48 CIDEr for captioning and 97.09 F1 for VQA. These scores substantially exceed the strongest zero-shot baselines: Ferret (best general spatial model) reaches only 18.08 detection F1 and 19.77 segmentation F1, while Gemini-2.5 Flash (best commercial model) achieves 3.79 detection F1 and 2.79 segmentation F1. On classification, the fine-tuned model surpasses Gemini by a 44-point margin (94.18 vs. 49.84), highlighting domain-specific knowledge absent from web-scale pre-training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The GroundSet fine-tuned model decisively outperforms all nine baselines across every task. Against the strongest general spatial grounding models, Ferret leads zero-shot detection (18.08 F1) and multi-class detection (32.83 F1), but GroundSet nearly triples detection (49.47) and more than doubles multi-class detection (72.14). PaliGemma-2 is competitive on segmentation (15.12 F1) yet still trails GroundSet by nearly 30 points (44.65). Among RS-specialized models, SkySenseGPT achieves the best zero-shot captioning (24.03 CIDEr) and VQA (89.69 F1) but collapses on grounding tasks (0.14 multi-class F1), while GeoChat underperforms even its LLaVA-1.5 base on classification (7.59 vs. 28.62), suggesting catastrophic forgetting from domain fine-tuning. Gemini-2.5 Flash is the strongest commercial baseline on VQA (83.56) and classification (49.84) but remains far behind GroundSet on all spatial grounding tasks (e.g., 3.79 vs. 49.47 detection F1). MiniGPT-v2 shows moderate grounding ability (11.69 detection F1) but fails on captioning (0.24 CIDEr) and classification (6.97). The cross-dataset evaluation on VRSBench (Table 4) further confirms that GroundSet transfers well to out-of-distribution grounding tasks (15.47 detection F1, 21.49 REC F1), outperforming in-domain RS-specialists like SkySenseGPT (12.81 detection) and GeoChat (9.20 detection), despite lacking vehicle/airplane categories in training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LLaVA-1.6 (LLaVA-NeXT) modular VLM architecture and standard LoRA instruction-tuning, deliberately introducing no architectural modifications. It departs sharply from the dominant RS dataset lineage (DOTA, DIOR, VRSBench, GeoPixel, GeoChat, SkySenseGPT), which repurposes legacy closed-set detection annotations or OpenStreetMap data, by instead sourcing ground truth from legally verified cadastral vector records at 20 cm resolution. Conceptually, it aligns with the spatial-grounding VLM tradition (Shikra, Kosmos-2, Ferret, PaliGemma) that treats coordinates as textual tokens, but reframes the bottleneck as data fidelity rather than model design, positioning cadastral data as the decisive variable over architectural priors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset addresses the core scalability-quality trade-off in RS annotation by leveraging state-maintained cadastral records, yielding 135 fine-grained semantic classes and 3.8M verified objects at a density far exceeding prior benchmarks. The finding that a vanilla LLaVA fine-tuned on this data outperforms both RS-specialized models and Gemini-2.5 Flash by large margins (e.g., 44 points in classification) suggests that current web-scale pre-training lacks specific geospatial semantic knowledge, and that data provenance may matter more than architectural sophistication for fine-grained grounding. Limitations include single-country (France) coverage, exclusion of mobile objects, and reliance on a single base architecture; open questions include whether the pipeline generalizes to other national cadastral standards, whether non-autoregressive or GNN-based models can better exploit the native vector primitives, and how the model handles the ~15% visual-semantic discrepancy rate in operational settings.
