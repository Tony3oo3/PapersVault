## Problem

Generative video models for adverse-weather synthesis in autonomous driving require massive datasets to learn rare weather patterns, while existing 3D-aware editing methods are bottlenecked by costly per-scene optimization (up to an hour per clip) and suffer from inherent geometric and illumination entanglement that prevents independent control of local light sources and global atmospheric conditions in dynamic driving scenes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260963/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a monocular driving video clip (512×512, 30–57 frames), optionally sparse LiDAR point clouds for metric depth alignment (or a camera-height prior for strictly monocular setups), and an HDR environment map for global ambient illumination. It produces a weather-converted (rain, snow, fog) or time-of-day-converted (dawn, noon, night) driving video at the same resolution and frame count, with all physical effects (snow accumulation, rain ripples, volumetric fog halos, vehicle headlight cones) anchored to the reconstructed 3D geometry. The entire pipeline is feed-forward with no per-scene optimization, running in 2–170 seconds per video on a V100 GPU depending on the target condition.

## Method

AutoWeather4D formulates weather editing as a feed-forward analysis-and-synthesis pipeline built around a G-Buffer Dual-Pass Editing mechanism. The analysis stage extracts a pixel-aligned metric G-buffer (depth, normal, albedo, roughness, metallic, sky mask) by combining Pi3 feed-forward 4D reconstruction for continuous depth with a zero-shot diffusion-based inverse renderer for intrinsic materials, then aligning to metric scale via LiDAR or road-plane fitting. The Geometry Pass physically modifies surface descriptors using explicit models: SPH Poly6 kernels for snow buildup on upward-facing normals, Gunn-Kinzer terminal-velocity particles with SDF depth-testing for rain streaks, and FBM-driven puddle masks that perturb albedo, roughness, and normals. The Light Pass then analytically resolves radiance over the edited G-buffers using Cook-Torrance BRDF for local spotlight relighting (vehicle headlights, streetlights), a single-scattering RTE with Henyey-Greenstein phase function for volumetric fog, and a neural forward renderer conditioned on the HDR map for global ambient, all linearly blended in a deferred shading cycle. A terminal VidRefiner (SDEdit-style, adapted from WAN-FUN 2.2) adds sensor-level photorealism by perturbing VAE latents to a pivot timestep while enforcing spatiotemporally coherent boundary conditioning from the deterministic render, restricting the generative process to high-frequency textural refinement without altering resolved physical structure.

## Results

On 120 Waymo scenes (27,360 frames across rain, snow, fog, and night), AutoWeather4D achieves the best CLIP Score (0.2586), Vehicle 3D Detection IoU (0.915), Vehicle CLIP cosine similarity (0.871), and Human Evaluation win rate (0.826) among all baselines. The method matches or slightly exceeds the strongest generative baseline (Cosmos-Transfer2.5: CLIP 0.2558, IoU 0.913) while providing explicit parametric control over local lights, fog density, and time-of-day that generative models lack. For downstream data augmentation, AutoWeather4D yields +1.24% mIoU on ACDC and +0.71% on Dark Zurich, outperforming Cosmos-Transfer2.5 augmentation (+0.93% and +0.04% respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AutoWeather4D outperforms all four generative baselines on every reported metric. Against the closest competitor WAN-FUN 2.2, it gains +0.0009 CLIP Score, +0.027 IoU, +0.077 CLIP cosine similarity, and a substantial +0.158 in human evaluation win rate (0.826 vs. 0.668). Versus Cosmos-Transfer2.5, the gains are smaller on CLIP Score (+0.0028) and IoU (+0.002) but large on identity stability (+0.034) and human preference (+0.246), indicating that the G-buffer grounding preserves foreground geometry more faithfully. Ditto and Video-P2P trail significantly, with Ditto introducing structural hallucinations and Video-P2P failing to follow editing instructions. Qualitatively, against the domain-specific WeatherEdit [44], AutoWeather4D correctly softens hard shadows under scattering conditions and enables localized snow/ripple interactions, while against DiffusionRenderer [28] it avoids unintended sky relighting through depth-based sky isolation and supports ego-vehicle headlight-driven illumination.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of classical physics-based rendering (Cook-Torrance BRDF, Henyey-Greenstein scattering, particle systems) and modern feed-forward neural extraction, departing from both per-scene optimization pipelines (NeRF/3DGS-based weather editors like RainyGS, WeatherEdit, ClimateNeRF) and purely data-driven generative video models (Cosmos-Transfer2.5, Ditto, WeatherWeaver). It repurposes Pi3's feed-forward 4D reconstruction and DiffusionRenderer's zero-shot inverse rendering as extraction backbones, then applies explicit G-buffer manipulation and deferred shading in place of learned weather synthesis. The VidRefiner stage borrows the SDEdit paradigm from WAN-FUN 2.2, positioning the generative model as a terminal sensor-realism refiner rather than the primary editing engine.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By replacing per-scene optimization (up to an hour per clip) with a feed-forward pipeline running in seconds, AutoWeather4D makes 3D-aware weather editing practically viable for large-scale autonomous driving data generation, while the decoupled geometry/illumination representation enables parametric control (fog density, light toggling, time-of-day) that purely generative baselines cannot offer. The data augmentation experiments suggest geometric fidelity from explicit rendering translates to more reliable downstream perception gains than photorealism alone, though absolute improvements remain marginal. Open questions include handling extreme long-tail interactions (vehicle splash, complex fluid dynamics) and balancing severe atmospheric occlusion with background structural retention, both of which the authors acknowledge as limitations of the decoupled pipeline.
