## Problem

Most existing rPPG datasets rely exclusively on RGB imaging, leaving the spectral effects of melanin absorption, illumination variability, and skin-tone-dependent sensing bias insufficiently studied. No publicly available dataset simultaneously captures synchronized RGB and NIR video under controlled experimental conditions for systematic rPPG evaluation. Furthermore, prior work shows that performance degradation for darker skin tones persists even after balancing training data, indicating the bias is rooted in the sensing and image-formation pipeline rather than mere representation imbalance.

## Input / Output

The dataset provides three co-located camera streams at 2046×2464 resolution and 60 fps (RGB, NIR 850 nm, NIR 940 nm) synchronized with fingertip PPG at 125 Hz from a BIOPAC MP46 system; a subset of 14 participants additionally contributes periocular NIR video at 30 Hz and IMU data from Meta Aria glasses. Each recording segment lasts at least five minutes to support HRV estimation, and all streams are temporally aligned via UTC timestamps. Outputs include heart rate in BPM, continuous BVP waveforms, and HRV metrics, with evaluation stratified by self-reported Fitzpatrick skin-tone groups (I–II, III–IV, V–VI).

## Method

The primary contribution is the Tricam-rPPG dataset itself, collected from 31 subjects across 8 tasks under two controlled illumination settings (8000 K cool, 2700 K warm), with rich metadata including 468 facial landmarks, skin-region masks, and 760-patch intensity signals per frame. For baseline benchmarking, the authors evaluate five standard rPPG algorithms (DeepPhys, TSCAN, EfficientPhys, PhysNet, PhysFormer) via the rPPG Toolbox under a unified pipeline, reporting MAE, MAPE, correlation, and SNR across multiple training sets and skin-tone strata. The key empirical experiment trains a temporal encoder-decoder model (adapted from Li et al.) on different combinations of the five available spectral channels (R, G, B, NIR-850, NIR-940), demonstrating that adding NIR channels increases SNR by up to 100% and reduces MAE by up to 45% for medium-dark skin tones while leaving lighter-skin performance largely unchanged, thereby isolating the benefit of longer wavelengths from algorithmic improvements.

## Results

On the Tricam-rPPG test set, DeepPhys trained on UBFC-rPPG achieves the best overall heart-rate estimation with MAE of 3.85±0.56 BPM, MAPE of 4.85±0.71%, ρ of 0.77±0.06, and SNR of −1.28±0.39 dB. TS-CAN trained on UBFC-rPPG follows with MAE 6.16±1.00 BPM, while most other methods (PhysNet, PhysFormer, EfficientPhys-C) degrade substantially, with MAE ranging from 12.81 to 69.13 BPM depending on training set. The NIR ablation shows that adding 850/940 nm channels to RGB increases SNR by up to 100% and reduces MAE by up to 45% for medium-dark skin tones, while leaving lighter-skin performance largely unchanged.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DeepPhys [7] is the clear winner across all training sets, achieving MAE of 3.85 BPM (UBFC-rPPG), 5.21 BPM (PURE), and 6.90 BPM (SCAMPS), outperforming TS-CAN [20] by 2.31 BPM on UBFC-rPPG and by 30.41 BPM on SCAMPS. TS-CAN [20] is the second-best method but degrades sharply when trained on SCAMPS (MAE 37.31 BPM vs. 6.90 for DeepPhys). PhysNet [48] and PhysFormer [49] consistently underperform, with PhysFormer trained on UBFC-rPPG reaching only ρ = −0.29 and MAE of 31.23 BPM, indicating poor cross-dataset generalization to the Tricam-rPPG test set. EfficientPhys-C [21] shows the most extreme failure mode, with MAE of 69.13 BPM when trained on SCAMPS, effectively producing no useful signal. TSCAN [15] (the same architecture as TS-CAN) is additionally shown in Figure 1 to exhibit monotonic MAE degradation from light to dark skin tones (1.72 → 6.60 BPM for DeepPhys; similar trend for TSCAN and EfficientPhys), confirming that the bias is systematic rather than purely a data-representation issue.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the rPPG dataset lineage (UBFC-rPPG, PURE, MMPD, TokyoTech, MR-NIRP) by introducing the first dataset to simultaneously capture synchronized RGB and two NIR wavelengths (850 nm, 940 nm) under controlled illumination with a structured multi-task protocol. The NIR effectiveness experiments build on the temporal encoder-decoder architecture of Li et al. [19], repurposing it to compare channel combinations rather than to recover full BVP morphology. The benchmarking framework relies on the rPPG-Toolbox [22], positioning the contribution as a data resource and evaluation protocol rather than a new algorithmic method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset directly addresses a critical equity gap: existing rPPG methods degrade monotonically with skin tone, and this paper provides the first controlled multispectral resource to disentangle algorithmic bias from sensing-physics bias (melanin absorption). The finding that NIR channels yield up to 100% SNR gains for medium-dark skin while leaving lighter-skin performance stable suggests a concrete path toward fairer remote monitoring. However, the NIR experiments are limited to 8 subjects, the dataset is access-restricted (DUA required), and the male-dominant demographic (23/31) limits generalizability. Open questions include whether NIR diffusion effects alter waveform morphology used by shape-based methods, and whether gains persist under uncontrolled ambient lighting and motion.
