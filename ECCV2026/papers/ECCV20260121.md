## Problem

Diffusion-based image editing models can manipulate lighting via text prompts but lack explicit spatial control over 3D light placement and struggle to maintain consistency across viewpoints. Forcing these models to condition on spatial lighting maps from scratch—typically via synthetic data—disrupts their well-tuned generative priors, producing synthetic artifacts that sacrifice photorealism for spatial alignment. The paper tackles how to achieve precise, user-specified 3D light placement with multi-view consistency while preserving the native material-light interaction prior of a pretrained diffusion model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260121/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes N multi-view source images of the same indoor scene, a coarse 3D mesh reconstructed via Depth Anything 3, and user-placed light sources (point, spot, or area) within that mesh. The spatial lighting condition is not the 3D light parameters themselves but a receiver-centric rendering: the untextured white mesh rendered from each of the N viewpoints under the user-defined lights, producing per-view 2D shading maps. The output is N multi-view consistent relit RGB images at the same resolution as the inputs, synthesized jointly so that shading, shadows, and specular highlights remain geometrically coherent across all viewpoints.

## Method

Lume-Palette decouples relighting into two sequential stages built on Flux.2 Klein 9B with a flow-matching objective. In illumination distillation, four LoRA modules (rank 32) are trained on the MIT Multi-Illumination dataset to map any input image to a fixed canonical lighting direction (left, right, front, top), extracting a view-specific illumination palette that encodes authentic material-light responses without invasive spatial conditioning. In illumination casting, a higher-rank LoRA (rank 128) synthesizes the target relit image by conditioning on the source image, the rendered spatial lighting map, and the four palette images. To handle dense multi-view inputs efficiently, an asymmetric conditioning scheme is used: within a local window of V views, only the active view receives all dense conditions while inactive views are represented solely as their concurrent noisy latents (gradient-free spatial anchors), reducing the DiT condition sequence from 6V to V+5 tokens. This design preserves the generative prior by never forcing the model to learn a geometry-to-appearance mapping from scratch, while the shared 3D-derived lighting conditions and cross-view latent anchors enforce spatial and multi-view consistency.

## Results

On synthetic multi-view scenes, Lume-Palette achieves PSNR 24.453, SSIM 0.872, and LPIPS 0.126, substantially outperforming all four baselines on image fidelity. For geometry-aware multi-view consistency (warping to adjacent views), it attains MV MSE 0.0090 and MV LPIPS 0.115, again the best among all methods. On real ScanNet++ scenes, a 25-participant user study (150 pairwise judgments, Bradley-Terry aggregation) shows Lume-Palette is consistently preferred across realism (2.051), lighting adherence (2.801), and multi-view consistency (1.514).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Lume-Palette decisively outperforms all four baselines on every reported metric. Against RGB↔X [67] (PSNR 10.497, LPIPS 0.394), the gap is the largest—over 14 dB in PSNR—because RGB↔X relies on explicit intrinsic decomposition that is sensitive to albedo prediction errors and introduces color shifts. IC-Light [70] (PSNR 13.982, LPIPS 0.281) struggles to disentangle and remove baked-in source illumination, yielding a ~10.5 dB PSNR gap. LumiNet [63] (PSNR 16.593, LPIPS 0.399) shows limited ability to impose complex target illumination and has the worst multi-view LPIPS (0.195) among baselines. ScribbleLight [10] (PSNR 12.591, LPIPS 0.431) is the weakest baseline overall, also suffering from albedo-related color shifts. In the real-scene user study, Lume-Palette leads all baselines on all three Bradley-Terry dimensions, with the largest margin on lighting adherence (+2.801 vs. the next-best IC-Light at −1.031), confirming that the receiver-centric spatial lighting condition provides substantially better control than text or scribble-based alternatives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing the photometric stereo principle—capturing a scene under canonical lighting directions to reveal material properties—into a two-stage diffusion pipeline. It departs from prior diffusion-based relighting methods (RGB↔X, IC-Light, ScribbleLight) that directly fine-tune models on spatial condition maps, arguing that such invasive conditioning disrupts the pretrained generative prior and produces synthetic artifacts. The illumination palette concept draws on Poirier-Ginter et al.'s use of directionally relit images as intermediate priors, but replaces per-scene radiance-field optimization with a feed-forward casting stage conditioned on receiver-centric spatial lighting maps. The asymmetric multi-view conditioning scheme is a novel architectural contribution that avoids the computational intractability of symmetric dense conditioning across all views.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The decoupling of semantic lighting priors (via text-prompt distillation) from spatial lighting control (via rendered receiver-centric maps) offers a general recipe for preserving diffusion model quality while adding explicit geometric control—a tension that plagues many diffusion-based editing tasks beyond relighting. However, the method's reliance on coarse 3D mesh reconstruction (via Depth Anything 3) means quality degrades around thin structures, reflective or transparent materials, and complex indirect illumination, limiting its applicability to challenging real-world interiors. Open questions include whether the canonical four-direction palette is sufficient for scenes with strong anisotropic or subsurface scattering, and whether the approach extends to outdoor or dynamic scenes where the fixed-geometry assumption breaks down.
