## Problem

Cancer segmentation models can fail silently, producing anatomically plausible yet incorrect masks that risk missed findings or unnecessary biopsies. Existing output-level uncertainty cues (confidence, entropy, energy scores) offer no insight into why a failure occurs and suffer from a sensitivity-quality tradeoff where increasing detection sensitivity degrades segmentation accuracy. The paper asks whether ViT-based segmentation models encode an internal 'Feeling of Error'—analogous to the human cognitive signal that flags potential mistakes—that can be extracted to detect and explain failures without sacrificing segmentation quality.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on a frozen MedSAM ViT backbone processing 2D medical images (mp-MRI for prostate, CT for pancreatic). Rather than pooling patch tokens into a single image-level vector, SAEs are trained directly on patch-level embeddings (h×w×d) from six uniformly sampled layers (1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11), preserving spatial granularity essential for dense prediction. The concatenated concept vector across all six layers (dimension |L|·D = 6×1536) feeds an XGBoost classifier that outputs a per-patch failure score, per-concept importance rankings, and a corrected mask where patches exceeding a failure threshold are selectively removed rather than discarding the entire prediction.

## Method

The core mechanism trains independent BatchTopK Sparse Autoencoders (D=1536, S=8) on patch-level activations from six ViT layers of a frozen, fine-tuned MedSAM model, decomposing dense embeddings into sparse, interpretable concept vectors. The key empirical finding is that failure cases activate significantly fewer concepts at lower magnitudes than successes (p<1e-5, Cohen's d>1.8), and that specific concepts align with clinical anatomy (e.g., 'tumor', 'peripheral zone', 'transition zone'), validated by two board-certified radiologists. A holistic failure representation is formed by concatenating concept vectors across all layers, capturing both low-level visual anomalies and high-level semantic confusions. An XGBoost classifier trained on this representation learns non-linear interaction patterns among concepts for failure prediction while yielding interpretable feature-importance scores that trace errors back to specific anatomical concepts. For correction, the classifier operates at patch granularity: a predicted mask is selectively edited by removing only those patches whose local failure score exceeds a threshold, preserving high-confidence true-positive regions that output-level methods would discard entirely.

## Results

On the zero-shot Prostate158 evaluation (detectors trained on PI-CAI), the concept-based detector achieves AUROC 73.6, AUPR 74.1, and FPR95 64.4, substantially outperforming all confidence-based baselines whose AUROC scores cluster near random chance (48.0–53.1). On PI-CAI, the full pipeline (all layers + XGBoost) reaches F1 = 63.5 for failure detection while maintaining DSC = 50.1, breaking the Pareto tradeoff that output-level methods face (a 20-point F1 gain via confidence proxies costs 8–10 points in DSC). The method also generalizes to PanTS (CT modality), confirming cross-modality transfer of the learned failure signatures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two families of output-level uncertainty baselines: softmax-probability/entropy methods (MaxProb, MeanProb, Entropy from Hendrycks & Gimpel [24]) and the Energy score (Liu et al. [44]). On the zero-shot Prostate158 test, the proposed concept-based detector wins decisively on all three metrics: AUROC 73.6 vs. 53.1 (Entropy, the best baseline), AUPR 74.1 vs. 65.6, and FPR95 64.4 vs. 97.8–100.0. The baselines essentially collapse to random-chance discrimination (AUROC ≈ 50) on unseen data, indicating that model confidence is poorly calibrated across domains. On PI-CAI (in-domain), the method simultaneously achieves higher F1 (63.5) and higher DSC (50.1) than any point on the baseline Pareto front shown in Fig. 5, meaning it detects more failures without degrading segmentation quality. The advantage is consistent across both MRI (prostate) and CT (pancreatic) modalities, suggesting the internal concept signal is modality-robust.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) from mechanistic interpretability—originally developed to decompose language model activations into monosemantic features—into the medical image segmentation domain, applying them to ViT-based cancer segmentation models (MedSAM). It departs from the standard output-level uncertainty paradigm (MaxProb, entropy, energy scores) by arguing that failure signals are encoded in internal representations rather than final logits, and distinguishes itself from concept bottleneck models by being fully post-hoc and unsupervised (no architectural modification or manual concept annotation required). The BatchTopK SAE training procedure is adopted directly from recent LLM interpretability work, and the XGBoost classifier on concatenated multi-layer concept vectors is a straightforward but effective aggregation strategy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a clinically critical gap: silent segmentation failures that produce plausible but incorrect masks, risking missed diagnoses or unnecessary biopsies. The zero-shot generalization result—where confidence-based baselines collapse to near-random AUROC (~50) on unseen Prostate158 data while the concept-based detector retains AUROC of 73.6—suggests internal representations are more robust failure signals than calibrated confidence, which has implications for deploying frozen clinical models across institutions. Key limitations include cancer-specific SAEs requiring retraining per anatomy, binary failure classification without distinguishing failure modes, and correlation-based (not causal) concept-failure links. Open directions include universal cross-cancer SAEs, causal mediation analysis for interventional debugging, and extension to missing-modality settings.
