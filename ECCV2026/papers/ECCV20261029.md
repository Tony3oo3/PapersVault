## Problem

Existing vision-language benchmarks evaluate isolated sub-skills (OCR, math reasoning, chart reading) in decoupled settings, creating a gap between benchmark performance and real-world tasks that demand simultaneous perception and cognition. Automated exam grading is a prime example: it requires answer-region localization, handwritten text recognition, and semantic correctness judgment to be executed end-to-end in a single pass, yet no prior benchmark tests this compositional pipeline. GradingBench fills this void by providing a full-page, application-driven evaluation of MLLMs' integrated grading ability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261029/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Input is a raw full-page exam paper image (from scanners or mobile cameras, preserving real-world artifacts like uneven lighting and paper creases), optionally augmented with target-question bounding box coordinates (L2) or official reference answers (answer-based setting). Output is a structured JSON list per question containing answer-region bounding boxes in absolute pixel coordinates, recognized handwritten text (with LaTeX for formulas), a chain-of-thought reasoning trace, the derived correct answer, and a binary correct/incorrect judgment. Evaluation uses a three-stage gating pipeline—center-point spatial matching, CER < 0.7 text recognition check, and semantic answer verification—so a question is only scored correct if all perceptual and reasoning stages pass.

## Method

GradingBench comprises 300 full-page exam images with 3,284 annotated sub-questions spanning five subjects, three educational stages, four question types, and two content formats, annotated with bounding boxes, handwritten transcriptions, reference answers, expert grading labels, and multi-dimensional metadata (Cohen's Kappa 0.87). The evaluation framework defines three progressive task levels (single-question, specified-question, full-page) crossed with two answer settings (answer-free, answer-based), yielding six scenarios assessed via a staged accuracy metric and mAP@[0.2:0.5] for localization. To diagnose failure modes, the authors isolate each sub-capability (localization, recognition, reasoning) under controlled conditions, compare single-round vs. three-round decomposed execution, and conduct a diagnostic SFT experiment fine-tuning Qwen2.5-VL-7B on ~10,000 synthetic examples (with CoT chains generated by Gemini 3.1-Pro) across all three task formats in a unified sequence-to-sequence paradigm. The key finding is that localization failure—particularly complete misses—dominates the error budget, and that multi-task coordination overhead further degrades already-weak grounding, a gap that neither task decomposition nor targeted fine-tuning fully closes.

## Results

Across 18 MLLMs evaluated on 3,284 sub-questions from 300 exam pages, Gemini-3.1-Pro achieves the highest overall accuracy of 69.23%, followed by Doubao-seed-2.0-Pro at 67.04%. Localization (mAP@[0.2:0.5]) is the dominant bottleneck: even Gemini-3.1-Pro scores only 63.03% mAP on L1, while most models fall below 30%. The SFT experiment shows Qwen2.5-VL-7B-SFT jumps from 8.20% to 57.47% average accuracy (a +49.27 pp gain), surpassing Gemini-2.5-Pro (36.09%) and Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B (30.21%) in the answer-free setting, yet still trailing Gemini-3.1-Pro (61.27%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini-3.1-Pro leads on overall accuracy (69.23%) and answer-based accuracy across all levels, but Doubao-seed-2.0-Pro surpasses it on L1 mAP (81.09 vs. 63.03) and L3 mAP (64.15 vs. 35.65), indicating superior spatial grounding despite lower end-to-end accuracy. The SFT model (Qwen2.5-VL-7B-SFT) achieves 84.81% L1 mAP, outperforming even Gemini-3.1-Pro (63.03%), and reaches 57.47% average answer-free accuracy, beating Gemini-2.5-Pro (36.09%) and Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B (30.21%) by wide margins, yet still trails Gemini-3.1-Pro (61.27%) by 3.8 pp due to weaker semantic reasoning. A notable L2 anomaly appears across most models: providing explicit bounding-box coordinates (L2) yields lower answer-free accuracy than autonomous full-page grading (L3), e.g., Doubao-seed-2.0-Pro drops from 68.14% (L3) to 46.30% (L2), revealing fragile spatial-textual instruction integration. Multi-round interaction provides consistent but modest gains (e.g., +7.46 pp for Gemini-2.5-Pro, +11.06 pp for Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B on L3 answer-free), confirming that task decomposition partially alleviates but does not resolve coordination failures. GPT-5.2 and Claude-Opus-4, despite strong general capabilities, score among the lowest (24.95% and 24.80% overall), suggesting that general-purpose MLLM strength does not transfer to fine-grained document localization tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of MLLM benchmarks (TextVQA, MathVista, DocVQA, OCRbench) that evaluate isolated perceptual or cognitive sub-skills in decoupled settings. It also extends prior AI-for-Education benchmarks (Exams-v, EDU-CIRCUIT-HW) that cover limited question types and rely on pre-processed or cropped inputs, by requiring the complete grading pipeline—detection, handwritten recognition, and semantic judgment—to be executed end-to-end on raw full-page exam images. The SFT diagnostic experiment builds on Qwen2.5-VL as a base model, and the multi-round interaction protocol repurposes sequential prompting as a coordination workaround rather than a novel architectural contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GradingBench provides the first systematic, application-driven testbed for measuring whether MLLMs can fuse perception and cognition in a single forward pass, a capability that isolated-task benchmarks cannot capture. The finding that localization failure—not reasoning—dominates the error budget, combined with the counter-intuitive L2 anomaly where explicit coordinate hints degrade performance, points to a specific and actionable weakness in current MLLM training. Open questions include whether more fundamental solutions (beyond SFT or multi-round prompting) can close the perception-cognition gap, and whether the hierarchical framework generalizes to subjective grading, multilingual settings, and interactive grading scenarios.
