## Problem

Supervised fine-tuning of video generation models on demonstration videos produces brittle reasoning that fails to generalize to harder mazes, unseen textures, or different maze types. While reinforcement learning could encourage more robust, transferable reasoning, its effectiveness in video generation hinges on reward design—a problem that has received little systematic study, with multimodal reward models shown to fail catastrophically via reward hacking in this setting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262732/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a text prompt describing the goal (e.g., 'navigate to the exit') and a single initial RGB image showing the maze layout or first-frame egocentric observation, then generates a temporally coherent video of an agent (colored ball or robot) moving from start to goal. For game environments, the generated video is post-processed via object tracking to extract a discrete trajectory for reward computation; for robotic navigation (Target-Bench), the video is compared frame-by-frame against a reference rollout using frozen vision encoder embeddings (DINOv2 or CLIP) after linear interpolation to a common length.

## Method

The paper adapts Flow-GRPO to flow-matching video generation by converting the deterministic ODE sampler to an equivalent SDE for stochastic exploration, applying group-relative advantage estimation over G=8 sampled videos per prompt, and using denoising reduction (30 training steps vs. 50 inference steps) for computational efficiency. The key technical contribution is a verifiable reward design: for structured game environments, a multi-component trajectory reward combining exact match (R_EM), precision (R_PR), and maze fidelity (R_MF) computed from tracked agent positions against ground-truth BFS-optimal paths; for robotic navigation, an embedding-level reference-anchored reward measuring frame cosine similarity, temporal-order consistency via normalized cumulative displacement, and endpoint fidelity. The paper demonstrates that these objective, verifiable rewards are essential—learned VLM reward models are exploited via reward hacking, and sparse binary rewards provide insufficient gradient signal—while the multi-component design with weights (α=0.3, β=0.5, γ=0.2) provides dense, decomposable supervision that enables stable training and significant generalization gains over SFT.

## Results

On VR-Bench, Wan-R1 (RL-tuned) improves exact match accuracy over the SFT baseline (Wan-SFT) by +27.8 pp on Regular Maze (61.1% vs 33.3%), +51.4 pp on Trapfield (90.3% vs 38.9%), +29.1 pp on 3D Maze (94.4% vs 65.3%), and +26.4 pp on Sokoban (30.6% vs 4.2%), while slightly underperforming on Irregular Maze (47.9% vs 56.9%). Step deviation drops from 10.3 to 5.2 on Regular Maze and from 3.9 to 1.9 on 3D Maze, indicating more efficient trajectories. On Target-Bench (mapless robotic path planning), Wan-R1 achieves the best overall weighted score, reducing ADE and FDE by roughly half relative to the base model and substantially lowering miss rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Wan-R1 decisively outperforms all six closed-source video models (Veo-3.1-fast/pro, Sora-2, Kling-v1, Seedance-1.0-pro, MiniMax-Hailuo-2.3) on exact match across every maze type; most closed-source models score near 0% EM, while Wan-R1 reaches 61–94%. Against the open-source Wan2.5-i2v-preview, Wan-R1 leads by 61.1 pp on Base EM and 94.4 pp on 3D EM. The most direct comparison is against Wan-SFT (the SFT baseline): Wan-R1 wins on 4 of 5 maze types for EM (gains of +27.8 to +51.4 pp) but loses on Irregular Maze (47.9% vs 56.9%), which the authors attribute to the curved, coordinate-free geometry where trajectory-based exploration helps less. On Target-Bench, Wan-R1 achieves the best overall weighted navigation score among all three evaluated models (base, SFT, RL), halving displacement errors (ADE/FDE) and reducing miss rate, confirming that the embedding-level reference-anchored reward generalizes to real-world robotic navigation beyond structured mazes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Flow-GRPO [20], which adapted Group Relative Policy Optimization to flow-matching generative models, and on the VR-Bench maze-solving benchmark [43] that established 'reasoning via video' as a task. The key conceptual departure is in reward design: rather than relying on learned VLM judges (the standard RLHF paradigm), the authors ground rewards in objective, verifiable task metrics—trajectory extraction for structured games and embedding-level reference-anchored similarity for robotic rollouts. This positions the work at the intersection of RL for generative models and the emerging video-reasoning literature, while explicitly rejecting the multimodal-reward-model approach that dominates text-based RLHF.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that verifiable reward design is not merely preferable but critical for stable RL training in video generation, with learned VLM judges leading to degenerate solutions where agents move confidently along incorrect paths. Practical guidelines on KL regularization, denoising reduction, and cold-start requirements make the recipe actionable for practitioners applying RL to video models. However, the approach is constrained to tasks with objective success criteria (discrete trajectories or reference rollouts), and the single-reference-path assumption in VR-Bench means the reward can undercount valid alternative solutions. Extending verifiable rewards to open-ended video reasoning tasks without ground-truth paths remains an open problem.
