## Problem

Existing human parsing benchmarks (LIP, CIHP, MHP v2, VIP) implicitly assume intact anatomies and define no labels for residual limbs, prosthetic components, or mobility assistive devices, causing disability-related regions to be absorbed into background during training and evaluation. This leaves people with limb deficiencies—estimated at ~0.5% of the global population—unseen in training and unmeasured in evaluation, creating a systematic fairness and reliability gap in human-centric vision systems.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes RGB images (10,555 images, 14,939 person instances) of individuals with limb deficiencies in everyday, sports, and clinical scenes, and produces pixel-wise parsing masks over 61 classes: 27 common body/clothing parts plus 34 disability-related classes covering 8 residual-limb segments, 3 prosthesis types (cosmetic, functional non-articulated, articulated), and mobility aids. For zero-shot evaluation, methods additionally consume text prompts, ground-truth box prompts, or point prompts derived from mask centroids. Evaluation is reported in three subgroup splits (All, Common, Disability-Related) using parsing-specific metrics (mIoU, PCP, AP₅₀ᵖ, APᵥₒₗᵖ) and boundary-sensitive metrics (BIoU) to expose gaps that aggregate scores would mask.

## Method

IH-10K introduces a category schema that extends standard human parsing taxonomies with explicit residual-limb segments (upper arm, forearm, thigh, calf per side), a three-level prosthesis taxonomy (cosmetic, functional non-articulated, articulated) grounded in clinical guidance, and mobility assistive device labels, all annotated with pixel-accurate polygon masks via a multi-pass review workflow (annotator → full-pass reviewer → lead reviewer). The benchmark establishes a subgroup-resolved evaluation protocol that reports metrics separately over Common (27 classes) and Disability-Related (34 classes) groups, making performance disparities explicit rather than hidden by aggregate scores. The empirical study fine-tunes dedicated parsing models (AIParsing, UniParser) and general segmenters (FCN, SegFormer, Mask2Former, SegNeXt) on IH-10K, and evaluates zero-shot open-vocabulary methods (Grounded-SAM, X-Decoder, SEEM, CLIPSeg, GroupViT) and promptable models (SAM, SAM2) without task-specific training. Results reveal a persistent 20–40 point mIoU gap between Common and Disability-Related classes across all supervised methods, and near-complete failure of text-driven zero-shot methods on disability-related concepts, confirming that these regions are absent from prior pretraining corpora and require dedicated supervision.

## Results

On IH-10K, the best fine-tuned body parsing model (UniParser with ResNet101) achieves 41.43 mIoU overall, but only 28.19 mIoU on Disability-Related classes versus 58.10 on Common classes — a 29.9-point gap. Part-centric metrics show the same pattern: AP50p drops from 67.77 (Common) to 28.11 (Disability-Related), and PCP drops from 66.76 to 31.50. Zero-shot open-vocabulary methods (Grounded-SAM, X-Decoder, SEEM, CLIPSeg) collapse to near-zero scores on Disability-Related classes (e.g., Grounded-SAM Huge achieves only 0.55 mIoU vs 10.87 on Common), while SAM2 with ground-truth box prompts reaches 77.06 mIoU overall, confirming the bottleneck is grounding rather than mask tracing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among fine-tuned body parsing models, UniParser (ResNet-101) outperforms AIParsing (ResNet-101) on mIoU (41.43 vs 31.49), AP50p (45.67 vs 46.77 — actually AIParsing edges ahead here), and PCP (47.11 vs 48.81 — AIParsing leads), while AIParsing achieves substantially higher APvolp (47.21 vs 26.60), indicating UniParser is better at region overlap but AIParsing better at volume-aware part detection. Across all fine-tuned segmentation baselines (FCN, SegFormer, Mask2Former, SegNeXt), Disability-Related mIoU is roughly one-third to two-fifths of Common mIoU, and BIoU drops even further, revealing boundary errors at stump-prosthesis interfaces. Zero-shot open-vocabulary methods (Grounded-SAM, Grounded-SAM2, X-Decoder, SEEM, CLIPSeg, GroupViT) all fail on Disability-Related classes, with the best (Grounded-SAM Huge) reaching only 0.55 mIoU versus 10.87 on Common, confirming these concepts are absent from open-vocabulary pretraining. Promptable models (SAM, SAM2) with ground-truth box prompts achieve 73–77 mIoU overall, but drop to 18–27 mIoU with point prompts, and using zero-shot Grounded-SAM2 boxes instead of ground-truth boxes collapses SAM2 to 4.94 mIoU, isolating the grounding/localization bottleneck. The paper's central finding is not that one method wins, but that all methods exhibit a persistent and large Common-vs-Disability-Related gap, validating the need for the IH-10K benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the established human parsing task (LIP, CIHP, MHP v2, ATR) by carving out a previously invisible subpopulation: individuals with limb deficiencies. Rather than proposing a new architecture, it repurposes standard parsing models (AIParsing, UniParser, SegFormer, Mask2Former, SAM2) as baselines on a new label space that adds residual limbs, prosthetic components, and mobility assistive devices as first-class categories. Conceptually, it sits at the intersection of the inclusive-vision line (LDPose, InclusiveVidPose, ProGait) and the human-parsing benchmark lineage, but is the first to provide pixel-level parsing masks with subgroup-resolved evaluation for this population. The key departure from prior work is not architectural but taxonomic: prior benchmarks implicitly assume intact anatomies, so disability-related regions are absorbed into background during both training and evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

IH-10K makes a previously hidden fairness gap measurable: even after fine-tuning on the new data, dedicated parsing models show a ~30-point mIoU drop on disability-related classes versus common classes, and zero-shot open-vocabulary methods essentially collapse (mIoU < 6), indicating these concepts are absent from large-scale pretraining. The boundary-sensitive evaluation (BIoU) reveals that errors concentrate at stump-prosthesis interfaces, pointing to a specific failure mode that region-overlap metrics understate. Open questions include whether the gap can be closed with more targeted training objectives (boundary-aware losses, long-tail reweighting), whether open-vocabulary grounding can learn fine-grained disability-related concepts without explicit supervision, and how these findings transfer to downstream applications like virtual try-on or avatar-based interaction. The benchmark is likely to become a standard testbed for inclusive human-centric vision, though its scope is limited to limb deficiencies and does not cover other forms of disability.
