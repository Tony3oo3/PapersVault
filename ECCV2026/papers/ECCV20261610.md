## Problem

Existing RGB-LUT denoising methods require three identical lookup tables to process R, G, and B channels in parallel, tripling on-chip SRAM consumption. A naive fix—applying LUT only to the luminance (Y) channel in YUV space—degrades restoration quality because ignoring chrominance (UV) channels introduces color distortion and residual artifacts. The paper addresses how to reduce LUT storage by two-thirds while maintaining or exceeding SOTA denoising fidelity.

## Input / Output

The method accepts a noisy RGB image, converts it to YUV space, and processes the 8-bit Y channel through a multi-band LUT pipeline while applying a lightweight mean filter to the U and V channels before converting back to RGB. During training, YUV patches of 48×48 are randomly cropped and augmented with rotation/flipping; at inference, the LUT operates on 4-bit quantized inputs (16 levels per pixel) with 4D simplex interpolation to minimize indexing artifacts. The output is a denoised RGB image at the same spatial resolution as the input.

## Method

Hybrid-LUT decomposes the 8-bit Y channel into 4-bit MSB and 4-bit LSB branches, each processed by an ensemble of complementary LUT units (HD-LUT, HDBLRC-LUT, HDBL-LUT, L-LUT) with different dilation factors and rotation ensembles to cover receptive fields from 3×3 to 13×13. A variance-guided fusion mechanism computes local texture complexity via a 5×5 sliding window, indexes a lightweight 1-D LUT for per-pixel blending weights, and adaptively balances edge-preserving units (high-variance regions) against smoothing units (flat regions). The MSB branch uses a dilation-heavy ensemble to capture long-range structural dependencies, while the LSB branch substitutes the large-RF HDBL unit with a localized L-LUT to avoid importing uncorrelated noise. During training, each kernel pattern is realized as a six-layer CNN (1×3 conv + four 1×1 conv layers with 64 channels + 1×1 Tanh output), which is then converted to a LUT by enumerating all 16^n input combinations; a two-stage cascaded residual architecture and an inter-channel dense module (derived from DNLUT) further refine the output. The UV channels are processed solely by a grid-searched mean filter, reducing total LUT storage to 421 KB—roughly one-third of equivalent RGB-LUT designs—while matching or exceeding SOTA denoising performance.

## Results

On the four synthetic-noise benchmarks (σ=15 AWGN), Ours(Y) achieves 32.47 dB CPSNR on CBSD68, 32.78 on Kodak24, 31.17 on Urban100, and 31.78 on McMaster, using only 421 KB of LUT storage. On the real-world SIDD dataset, Ours(Y) reaches 36.07 dB CPSNR and 0.934 SSIM, surpassing the previous LUT-based SOTA (DNLUT) by 0.63 dB CPSNR and even exceeding DnCNN in SSIM (0.934 vs. 0.900). The symmetric variant Ours(YUV) further improves to 33.11 dB on Kodak24 and 32.92 dB on McMaster at σ=15, matching or exceeding DNLUT across most settings while using 31% less physical memory.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among LUT-based methods, Ours(Y) outperforms DNLUT [27] (the prior SOTA) by 0.06 dB on CBSD68 and 0.63 dB on SIDD (36.07 vs. 35.44 CPSNR), while trailing DNLUT by 0.24–1.10 dB on Kodak24, Urban100, and McMaster at σ=15; the symmetric Ours(YUV) variant closes this gap, beating DNLUT on Kodak24 (33.11 vs. 33.02) and McMaster (32.92 vs. 32.88). Against earlier LUT baselines (SRLUT, BDLUT, MuLUT, RCLUT, SPFLUT), Ours(Y) leads by 1.5–2.7 dB CPSNR across all four synthetic benchmarks. On real-world SIDD, Ours(Y) surpasses DnCNN [29] in SSIM (0.934 vs. 0.900) but trails it in CPSNR (36.07 vs. 36.45); on DnD, Ours(Y) beats DNLUT (36.82 vs. 36.67 PSNR) but remains below DnCNN (37.11). Classical methods CBM3D [5] and MCWNNM [26] are outperformed by 5.9 dB and 4.1 dB respectively on SIDD, though CBM3D retains a 1.0–1.5 dB advantage on synthetic benchmarks. In terms of hardware efficiency, Ours(Y) uses 421 KB model size (97 KB less than DNLUT) and 1.684 MB physical memory (31% of DNLUT's 5.384 MB), with comparable runtime (~400 ms for 512×512 on mobile) and 196× speedup over DnCNN.

## Relation to Prior Work

Hybrid-LUT extends the LUT-based image restoration lineage initiated by SRLUT and advanced by MuLUT, SPLUT, and DNLUT, adopting their core paradigm of pre-computing CNN mappings into lookup tables for zero-MAC inference. It directly builds on DNLUT's inter-channel module and SPLUT/HKLUT's MSB/LSB bit-plane decomposition, but departs fundamentally from the RGB-LUT paradigm by processing channels asymmetrically in YUV space: a full multi-band LUT ensemble on the luminance channel and a simple mean filter on chrominance. The pixel-level variance-guided fusion mechanism is a new contribution that replaces the static, content-agnostic kernel stacking used in prior LUT methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 66.7% reduction in on-chip SRAM (421 KB vs. ~1.2 MB for RGB-LUT) while matching or exceeding SOTA denoising quality makes LUT-based restoration genuinely deployable on resource-constrained edge hardware (FPGA/ASIC, mobile SoCs), a practical gap that prior LUT methods left open. The PSD-based justification for asymmetric channel treatment provides a principled design rule that could transfer to other LUT-based restoration tasks (super-resolution, deblurring). Open questions include whether the mean-filter approximation on UV becomes a bottleneck at very high noise levels or for tasks where chrominance detail matters more, and whether the approach extends beyond denoising to other restoration problems.
