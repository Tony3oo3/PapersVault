## Problem

Current video-language models exhibit a pervasive 'static bias,' relying on objects and backgrounds rather than motion cues to understand actions. This problem is amplified in instructional videos, which contain sequential multi-step actions described by noisy, loosely aligned ASR transcripts—unlike trimmed clips with single atomic actions and clean captions. Existing action-centric techniques (hard negatives, verb-aware objectives) were designed for trimmed videos and do not transfer to the temporally ordered, compositionally complex setting of instructional procedures.

## Input / Output

The model consumes instructional video clips encoded as dense frame-patch embeddings V ∈ R^{T×N×C} (frames × patches × feature dim) paired with LLM-filtered ASR captions that have been decomposed into structured verb phrases following predefined syntactic patterns (V+N, V-ing, V+Prep+N). Training additionally uses two types of synthetic hard negatives: verb-altered captions (same objects, different action verb) and order-swapped captions (same vocabulary, permuted action sequence). The primary output is a compact set of K latent Action Tokens capturing motion-relevant dynamics, which feed into contrastive video-text scoring, a DTW temporal alignment path against the verb sequence, and an autoregressive decoder that reconstructs masked action tokens.

## Method

InstrAct combines a data-driven curation pipeline with an action-centric architecture. On the data side, an LLM-assisted pipeline filters non-instructional captions, extracts structured verb phrases, and generates verb-altered and order-swapped hard negatives to force disentanglement of actions from static objects and to enforce procedural temporal reasoning. On the model side, an Action Perceiver (a Perceiver-based resampler with learnable latent queries restricted to local temporal windows) compresses redundant video features into K motion-aware Action Tokens; a verb-guided distillation stream uses verb-phrase-conditioned teacher tokens to steer the student latents toward action semantics via soft contrastive distillation. Two auxiliary objectives complement global contrastive learning: DTW-Align applies differentiable Soft-DTW between action tokens and verb embeddings with an order-aware hinge regularization that penalizes failure to distinguish correct from reversed action sequences, and Masked Action Modeling (MAM) uses a CoCa-style multimodal decoder to reconstruct masked action verbs conditioned on visual action tokens, strengthening token-level cross-modal grounding. The framework is trained end-to-end on the HowTo100M cooking subset using ViCLIP-B (InternVideo) as the default backbone and generalizes across four VFM backbones.

## Results

On InstrAct-Semantic, InstrAct (trained with Order-Swapped HNs) achieves R@1=28.1%, R@5=78.0%, and MR=3.0, outperforming the strongest ID baseline VideoPrism (R@1=18.2%) by nearly 10 points. On InstrAct-Logic, InstrAct (trained with Verb-Altered HNs) reaches ACC=40.0%, surpassing the best ID baseline UNiVL (33.6%) and the best OOD baseline CLIP-ViP (24.5%). On the real-world InstrAct-Dynamics retrieval benchmark, InstrAct improves mean R@1 from 30.68 (VideoPrism) to 34.53 and mean R@5 from 53.79 to 57.13 across 120 object-centric pools of 16,326 clips.

## Baselines & Comparisons

InstrAct outperforms all eight baselines on every metric across all three benchmarks. VideoPrism [41] is the strongest baseline overall (R@1=18.2% on Semantic, 30.68 mean R@1 on Dynamics), but InstrAct still leads by ~10 points on Semantic R@1 and ~4 points on Dynamics mean R@1. PerceptionLM [3] is competitive on Semantic (R@1=16.5%) but trails InstrAct by over 11 points. Among OOD models, CLIP-ViP [36] is the best (R@1=18.4% on Semantic, ACC=24.5% on Logic), yet InstrAct exceeds it by 9.7 and 15.5 points respectively. MIL-NCE [21], despite being pretrained on HowTo100M, performs worst on Semantic (R@1=7.2%) and Logic (ACC=5.1%), confirming that simple video-text matching cannot distinguish fine-grained actions. UniVL [19] and CLIP4Clip [20] occupy the middle range, with UniVL slightly stronger on Logic (ACC=33.6%) but weaker on Dynamics retrieval. The cross-evaluation protocol (training with one HN type, testing on the other) ensures fair comparison, and Table 3 further shows that adding InstrAct's full framework on top of any backbone (MIL-NCE, CLIP-ViP, CLIP4Clip, ViCLIP) yields consistent gains, with the largest Logic improvements on CLIP-ViP (24.5→37.1) and ViCLIP (30.0→44.7).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the action-centric video-language pretraining line (VFC, Paxion, Something-Something) from trimmed single-action clips to long, multi-step instructional videos with noisy ASR transcripts. It repurposes the Perceiver architecture for compressing redundant video tokens into action latents, adds a verb-guided distillation stream (inspired by momentum distillation in ALBEF), and introduces Soft-DTW-based temporal alignment and a masked-action-modeling objective adapted from BERT-style pretraining. The data curation pipeline (LLM-filtered captions, verb-altered and order-swapped hard negatives) generalizes the hard-negative strategies of VFC and Paxion to the compositional, sequential setting where prior methods were not designed to operate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and empirically validates a concrete failure mode—static object bias—in instructional video understanding, and provides both a diagnostic benchmark (InstrAct Bench) and a training recipe that mitigates it. A notable practical finding is that the data-driven hard-negative augmentation accounts for most of the performance gain, while the architectural components (Action Perceiver, DTW-Align, MAM) contribute only incremental improvements and can even slightly degrade results on weaker backbones (e.g., CLIP4Clip). Limitations include evaluation restricted to the cooking subset of HowTo100M, a cross-evaluation protocol that trains and tests on different hard-negative types, and no demonstration on truly long or multi-domain instructional content, leaving open whether the action-centric priors transfer beyond procedural cooking tasks.
