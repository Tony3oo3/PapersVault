## Problem

Federated learning inherently exposes intermediate model checkpoints to participants at every training round, yet the adversarial risk of this model leakage has been overlooked. While cross-model adversarial transferability is well-studied, the temporal dimension—whether adversarial examples crafted on an early-leaked checkpoint can still deceive the final deployed model after hundreds of additional training rounds—remains unexplored. This paper asks whether a malicious participant selected early in training can exploit the leaked model to launch effective attacks against the final model.

## Input / Output

The attack takes a single leaked intermediate model (architecture and parameters from round S of T total FL rounds) and a benign RGB image as input, and produces an adversarial example with an L∞ perturbation budget of ε=8/255. The method is entirely data-free: it requires no training data, no queries to the final model, and no access to multiple checkpoints. The generated adversarial example is designed to remain effective against the final deployed model at round T even when S is as early as 30% of total training (e.g., round 44 of 145).

## Method

Proteus is a three-phase iterative attack that suppresses parameter sensitivity to promote temporal transferability. Phase 1 computes ensemble gradients over stochastic input transformations, averaging out directions tied to brittle, parameter-sensitive features and retaining persistent adversarial directions. Phase 2 projects the perturbation forward along the gradient sign with a drift step size ρ to establish a safety margin, then re-estimates gradients at the extrapolated point to verify stability. Phase 3 fuses the primary and margin gradients, normalizes by mean absolute value, applies momentum with decay μ, and performs a projected L∞ ascent step. The theoretical foundation is a linearized feature-drift bound (Eq. 2) showing that the Frobenius norm of the feature extractor's parameter gradient at the leaked checkpoint upper-bounds how much the adversarial example's representation will shift under future training, motivating the search for low-sensitivity perturbation regions.

## Results

Using a leaked intermediate model at 30% training progress (the default setting), Proteus achieves transfer ASR of 85.40% on RAF-DB, 90.00% on CIFAR-10, and 79.61% on TinyImageNet against the final deployed model. This substantially outperforms directly applying PGD on the leaked model (59.75% on RAF-DB) and all six baselines. Across architectures, Proteus reaches 77.38% on MobileNetV3 and 82.99% on EfficientNet, consistently leading the strongest baseline by 15–25 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Proteus outperforms all six baselines (FGSM, PGD, OPS, DeCoWa, FoolMix, MuMoDIG) across every dataset and architecture tested. On the default RAF-DB/ResNet18 setting at 30% leakage, Proteus achieves 85.40% transfer ASR versus 59.75% for both FGSM and PGD, a 25.65-point margin. On MobileNetV3, Proteus (77.38%) beats the strongest baseline OPS (≈57.33%) by 20.05 points; on EfficientNet it surpasses FGSM (≈67.44%) by 15.55 points. On the hardest dataset (TinyImageNet, 200 classes), Proteus still leads the strongest baseline by at least 14.89 points. The paper notes that the advantages of existing cross-model transfer methods (OPS, DeCoWa, FoolMix, MuMoDIG) are inconsistent in this temporal-transfer setting, whereas Proteus's parameter-sensitivity suppression provides a systematic and consistent gain.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of transfer-based adversarial attacks and FL security. Prior transfer-based attacks (FGSM, PGD, MI-FGSM, DIM, OPS, DeCoWA, FoolMix, MuMoDIG) all target cross-model transferability against a single fixed victim, while prior FL security work focuses on gradient inversion, membership inference, or backdoor implantation. Proteus introduces the notion of *temporal* transferability—adversarial examples crafted on an intermediate FL checkpoint must survive the model's own subsequent training dynamics—which the authors argue is absent from both lines of work. Methodologically, it repurposes stochastic input transformations and momentum from the cross-model transfer literature but re-grounds them in a new parameter-sensitivity framework (Eq. 1–2) that formalizes why feature drift causes temporal transfer to fail.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a previously unacknowledged attack surface in FL: the mandatory plaintext model sharing to participants creates a channel through which a single early-selected client can compromise the final deployed model. The practical stakes are high because FL is increasingly deployed for privacy-sensitive vision tasks (medical imaging, robotics) where the threat model of a malicious participant is realistic. A key open question is the absence of dedicated defenses—existing mitigations (differential privacy, randomized smoothing, diffusion-based purification) either degrade clean accuracy severely or leave Proteus effective at ~60% ASR. The authors recommend only temporary measures (early stopping, trusted late-stage participation), leaving principled defenses as an important future direction.
