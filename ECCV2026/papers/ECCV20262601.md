## Problem

Detailed image captioning requires simultaneously high factuality (avoiding hallucinated details) and high coverage (capturing all visible elements), yet existing methods improve one axis at the cost of the other. LVLMs in long-form generation progressively rely on language priors over visual evidence, producing hallucinated content while systematically overlooking subtle visual attributes. Supervised fine-tuning on human-authored detailed captions often exceeds the model's perceptual capacity and worsens hallucinations, while inference-time self-correction fails because LVLMs tend to confirm rather than rectify their own errors.

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a single RGB image and a pre-computed set of Structured Reflection Notes (up to K=5 Avoid Notes and K=5 Include Notes per model, distilled offline from 30 exemplar images with human reference captions from IIW-Eval). The output is a single detailed image caption produced through up to three sequential LVLM calls: a grounded base caption, a detail-focused caption, and a merged final caption. The notes are model-specific, compact text directives (e.g., 'Do not infer object colors when they are ambiguous'), and the entire pipeline is gradient-free with no retraining of the target LVLM.

## Method

ReflectCAP is a two-phase, tuning-free framework. In the offline phase, a three-agent pipeline (Captioning Agent, Feedback Agent, Note Organizer) generates zero-shot captions from the target LVLM, critiques them against both the image and human references to identify hallucinations and missing details, then generalizes these instance-specific errors into compact, reusable Structured Reflection Notes via cross-instance aggregation. In the online phase, the notes steer generation through three steps: (1) Avoid Notes are injected to produce a hallucination-suppressed grounded base caption, (2) Include Notes direct attention to typically neglected details to produce a detail-focused caption, and (3) a conservative merge step combines both captions using the image as a grounding reference, prioritizing the base caption in conflicts. The key insight is that LVLMs exhibit predictable, systematic failure modes that can be captured in lightweight prompt-level directives, enabling independent control of factuality and coverage without retraining or multi-agent inference at test time.

## Results

On IIW-400, ReflectCAP achieves the highest factuality-coverage F1 across all eight LVLMs, with gains of +0.4 to +4.3 points over zero-shot (e.g., GPT-4.1-mini: 77.5, InternVL3.5-38B: 68.6, Qwen3-VL-32B: 76.4). On CapArena-Auto, ReflectCAP yields average win-rate margin improvements of +32.2 points for the GPT family and +21.9 points for open-source models over their zero-shot baselines; notably, GPT-4.1-mini with ReflectCAP (90.0) surpasses GPT-5.2 zero-shot (70.0). In terms of compute efficiency, InternVL3.5-4B with ReflectCAP reaches an F1 of 63.0—approaching InternVL3.5-38B zero-shot (64.3)—while requiring roughly 7.8× fewer inference TFLOPs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is CapMAS (Lee et al., ICML 2025), a multi-agent pipeline that decomposes captions into atomic propositions, verifies each against the image, and rewrites by removing unverified content. ReflectCAP outperforms CapMAS on F1 for all eight models on IIW-400 (e.g., GPT-4.1-mini: 77.5 vs. 75.1; InternVL3.5-38B: 68.6 vs. 66.4; Qwen3-VL-32B: 76.4 vs. 76.2), and on CapArena-Auto win-rate margin for all eight models (e.g., GPT-4.1-mini: 90.0 vs. 53.7; InternVL3.5-38B: 12.0 vs. −15.0; Qwen3-VL-32B: 91.7 vs. 87.0). The key distinction is that CapMAS improves factuality by deleting unreliable content, which inevitably sacrifices coverage, whereas ReflectCAP's separate Avoid/Include notes allow it to boost coverage while preserving precision. ReflectCAP also requires 21–36% fewer inference TFLOPs than CapMAS because its multi-agent pipeline runs only offline, leaving at most three single-model calls at inference time. Against simpler baselines, few-shot prompting often degrades factuality (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-7B drops from 62.7 to 53.1 F1), and self-correction yields only marginal gains (+0.2 to +0.7 F1), confirming that neither imitation nor iterative revision addresses the detail-faithfulness tension as effectively as structured reflection notes.

## Relation to Prior Work

ReflectCAP repurposes the reflective-memory paradigm from LLM agent frameworks (Reflexion, ReasoningBank) to LVLM captioning, but makes a critical architectural shift: instead of online, long-horizon trajectory tracking (which dilutes visual evidence in LVLMs), it performs offline, bottom-up distillation of a target model's recurring hallucination and omission patterns into compact Structured Reflection Notes. This departs from CapMAS [15], which applies a multi-agent verification pipeline at inference time and optimizes factuality at the cost of coverage, and from SFT-based approaches, which the paper shows can degrade factuality when human captions exceed the model's perceptual capacity. The method is positioned as a gradient-free, prompt-level intervention that achieves separate control over hallucination suppression and detail recovery without retraining or test-time multi-agent orchestration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: ReflectCAP achieves comparable factuality-coverage F1 to a 9.5× larger model (InternVL3.5-4B vs. 38B) at roughly 8× lower inference cost, and outperforms CapMAS while using 21–36% fewer TFLOPs, making high-quality detailed captioning viable under real-world latency and budget constraints. The finding that SFT on human-authored detailed captions degrades factuality (while SFT on ReflectCAP-generated captions does not) offers a concrete, scalable alternative to expensive annotation pipelines. Open questions include whether the per-model notes transfer across model families or languages, whether the approach extends to video captioning, and how the method behaves when the target model's error distribution shifts after continued pretraining or fine-tuning.
