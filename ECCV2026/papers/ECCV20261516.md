## Problem

Despite the widespread use of noisy or partially labeled data in practice, it remains fundamentally unexplained why models trained on inaccurate annotations can still extract correct label information and generalize well. This paper tackles that open question by investigating how label inaccuracy affects the internal structure of learned weight matrices, providing both empirical evidence and a formal theoretical guarantee for the observed robustness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261516/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

LIP is a training-free post-processing plug-in that takes as input the trained final FC-layer weight matrix W' (of shape q×l), the training feature matrix X (n×q), and the inaccurate label matrix Y (n×l), and produces a refined weight matrix W* of the same shape. The only hyperparameter is k (number of top singular components to retain, empirically set to ⌈0.8l⌉), and the entire procedure runs in milliseconds (≈10 ms on CIFAR-100) with no gradient updates or additional training epochs required.

## Method

The paper first establishes that label inaccuracy acts as a structured perturbation ΔW = K⁻¹XᵀM on the least-squares solution, and proves via the Davis-Kahan sine theorem that the principal subspace angle remains bounded by O(√p) for moderate noise rate p, explaining why task-relevant information survives. Building on this, LIP applies two sequential modules to the trained weights: (1) Principle Subspace Preservation (PSP) performs SVD on W' and retains only the top-k singular components to isolate the noise-robust principal subspace; (2) Label Ambiguity Purification (LAP) refines the discarded lower singular values by solving a closed-form least-squares problem that retrains them against the training data while keeping the principal subspace fixed. The resulting W* = W_k + U_l Σ_l* V_lᵀ combines preserved core information with denoised residual components, and the entire pipeline is training-free with complexity O(nql + min(q²l, ql²)).

## Results

On six real-world partial label datasets (FG-NET, Lost, MSRCv2, Mirflickr, Soccer Player, Yahoo!News), LIP improves the average accuracy of all seven baselines by 3.3%–7.0%, with the largest gains for ALO (48.3→51.7) and PL-AGGD (49.3→52.4). On CIFAR-100 with p=0.05 label inaccuracy, PRODEN+LIP reaches 77.25% accuracy, nearly matching the clean-label performance of 77.31%, while PICO+LIP achieves 73.83% versus 73.88% clean. On the extreme symmetric 80% noise setting of CIFAR-100, PLS+LIP improves from 45.92% to 47.43%, and on real-world noisy label benchmarks (CIFAR-10N, CIFAR-100N, Clothing1M), PLS+LIP gains 0.7–0.8 points over PLS alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LIP is a plug-in that is added on top of existing classifiers, so every comparison is a baseline vs. baseline+LIP. Across all 11 baselines tested (PL-CL, PL-CGR, DPCLS, PL-AGGD, SURE, ALO, PL-LEAF, PLS, AGCE, PICO, PRODEN), LIP consistently improves accuracy on every dataset and noise level, with no case of degradation. The largest relative gains appear for weaker baselines: ALO improves by 7.0% on average and PL-AGGD by 6.3%, while even the strongest baselines benefit (PL-CL +5.7%, PICO +0.3–0.4 pts on CIFAR-100/CUB-200). On the deep-learning baselines, PRODEN+LIP on CIFAR-100 (p=0.05) reaches 77.25%, essentially matching the clean-label upper bound of 77.31%, and PICO+LIP on CUB-200 (p=0.02) reaches 72.90% vs. 76.00% clean. On the extreme 80% symmetric noise setting, PLS+LIP gains 1.51 points (45.92→47.43) and AGCE+LIP gains 1.38 points (24.05→25.43), confirming that LIP helps even under severe corruption. The ablation (Table 5/6) shows both the Principal Subspace Preservation and Label Ambiguity Purification modules contribute independently, with their combination yielding the best results.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by asking *why* models can learn from inaccurate annotations, rather than the dominant question in noisy label learning (NLL) and partial label learning (PLL) literature of *how* to handle them. Prior work has proposed robust loss functions, noise transition matrices, co-teaching, and disambiguation strategies, but the fundamental mechanism enabling robustness remained unexplained. The authors repurpose matrix perturbation theory (Davis-Kahan sine theorem) and Grassmann-distance-based subspace similarity—tools previously applied to low-rank adaptation (LoRA) and eigenvector stability—to provide the first theoretical account of how label inaccuracy maps to weight-matrix geometry. The LIP plug-in then operationalizes this insight as a training-free post-processing step, distinct from all prior NLL/PLL methods that require modified training procedures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Davis-Kahan-based bound (Eq. 9) gives practitioners a quantitative handle on when label noise becomes destructive, and the finding that principal subspaces remain stable up to moderate inaccuracy (p ≤ 0.1 in experiments) reframes robustness as a subspace-preservation problem rather than a loss-design problem. LIP's training-free, millisecond-scale post-processing is practically attractive as a drop-in enhancement for any existing classifier. However, the analysis is confined to the final FC layer under a least-squares formulation, the gap parameter δ in the bound is not easily estimated from data, and the 'certain range' of tolerable inaccuracy is identified empirically rather than predicted a priori. Open questions include whether the subspace-stability picture holds for intermediate layers in deep networks, for non-linear decision boundaries, and whether the threshold can be estimated from the data distribution alone.
