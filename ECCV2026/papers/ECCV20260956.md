## Problem

Standard Visual Place Recognition methods treat each query image in isolation, discarding the sequential context available in any real navigation trajectory, which makes them brittle in feature-poor environments like rural landscapes or uniform highways. Prior sequence-based VPR methods require the reference database to be organized as ordered sequences covering the same routes as queries, an impractical structural constraint. This paper formalizes a task that exploits query-sequence context while operating against an unordered reference database, targeting localization of the final query image.

## Input / Output

The method takes a sequence of T query images (resized to 224×224 RGB) captured by an agent traversing an environment, with frames filtered to ensure at least 25 m inter-frame displacement, and an unordered reference database of N geo-tagged images. It outputs a 3D location estimate for the final query image and a full probability distribution over all reference images. Operationally, only the top-K=10 candidates per frame from a frozen pre-trained VPR backbone (MegaLoc) are considered at each timestep, keeping the candidate set small while the database itself can be arbitrarily large.

## Method

TRAIL is a CRF-based forward algorithm that maintains and updates a probability distribution over candidate reference images as each query arrives, combining two learned potentials: an emission potential (MLP on the element-wise product of global descriptors, measuring visual similarity) and a transition potential (CNN on pairwise DINOv2 local-feature correlations, measuring whether the spatial displacement between two candidate references is consistent with the observed camera motion between consecutive queries). A dustbin state handles frames where no top-K retrieval is correct, and a scaled-sigmoid aggregation function κ converts 'closest retrieval' probabilities into 'within threshold δ' probabilities. The entire pipeline is differentiable and trained end-to-end with binary cross-entropy at every timestep, preceded by individual pre-training of the emission and transition networks. The key mechanism is that transition potentials progressively suppress candidate references whose locations are geometrically inconsistent with the query trajectory, yielding the largest gains precisely where visual cues are scarce.

## Results

TRAIL (built on MegaLoc) improves Recall@T over the raw MegaLoc backbone by up to 8.4 pp on MSLS (66.7% vs 58.3% at T=10), 9.3 pp on Nordland (78.1% vs 68.8% at T=10), and 10.4 pp on 4Seasons (79.0% vs 68.6% at T=10). At T=5, gains are 7.2 pp (MSLS), 7.5 pp (Nordland), and 9.1 pp (4Seasons). On MSLS at T=10, TRAIL ties SelaVPR (66.7%) while using a far lighter re-ranking step, and on Nordland it surpasses SelaVPR by 17.2 pp at T=5 (81.3% vs 64.1%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against its own backbone MegaLoc, TRAIL gains 8.4 pp on MSLS (T=10), 9.3 pp on Nordland (T=10), and 10.4 pp on 4Seasons (T=10), with the largest relative improvements in feature-poor environments. Against the re-ranker SelaVPR, TRAIL ties on MSLS at T=10 (66.7%) but wins by 17.2 pp on Nordland at T=5 (81.3% vs 64.1%) and by 1.6 pp on 4Seasons at T=5, while being substantially cheaper (small MLPs on K=10 candidates vs. cross-attention over local features). Against PairVPR, TRAIL wins on MSLS (66.7% vs 50.0% at T=10) and Nordland (81.3% vs 69.5% at T=5) but loses on 4Seasons (78.2% vs 91.7% at T=5), which the authors attribute to the small 672-image reference set and PairVPR's robustness to the colour-to-grayscale domain shift. Against FoL (ViT-L backbone), TRAIL ties on MSLS at T=10 (66.7%) but leads by 27.4 pp on Nordland at T=5 (81.3% vs 53.9%), showing that sequential context compensates for the larger backbone. All sequence-retrieval methods (SeqNet, JIST, CaseVPR) perform far worse than TRAIL across every dataset, with CaseVPR the strongest at 25.0% on MSLS T=10 versus TRAIL's 66.7%, confirming that the sequence-to-sequence paradigm is ill-suited to unordered databases. Among hand-crafted heuristics, Location Filtering is the strongest competitor (79.7% on Nordland T=5) but TRAIL still edges it out by 1.6 pp there and by 8.8 pp on 4Seasons T=5 (78.2% vs 69.4%).

## Relation to Prior Work

TRAIL departs from the dominant single-image VPR paradigm (MegaLoc, FoL) and from sequence-to-sequence methods (SeqNet, JIST, CaseVPR) that require the reference database to be organized as ordered trajectories. The closest prior formulation is the seq2im task of Warburg et al., which also pairs query sequences with an unordered database but accepts a retrieval as correct if it matches any image in the sequence; TRAIL tightens this to target the final query image specifically. Architecturally, the paper repurposes the CRF forward-algorithm structure from SLAM loop-closure work (Cadena et al., Xu et al.) into a lightweight post-processing layer over a frozen VPR backbone, rather than learning sequence descriptors end-to-end.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical gap: real-world reference imagery is typically unordered (crowd-sourced, mapping vehicles), yet existing sequential methods demand exhaustive route coverage. TRAIL's design as a drop-in post-processing step with minimal overhead makes it immediately integrable into existing localization pipelines, and its largest gains in feature-poor environments (forests, highways, seasonal shifts) target precisely where current SOTA VPR is most brittle. Open questions include whether the approach extends to aerial or off-road domains, whether directly modeling the query position distribution (rather than classifying the closest reference) yields tighter localization, and whether the DINOv2 local features can be compressed to reduce the ~1.3× memory overhead.
