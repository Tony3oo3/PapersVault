## Problem

Existing motion editing methods require either paired input-output supervision or explicit edit guidance (text prompts, reference clips) at inference, both of which are impractical for skill-driven refinement where the goal is to improve execution of the same action rather than alter it. No large-scale paired novice-expert motion dataset exists, and constructing one would require domain experts to diagnose and describe subtle execution errors. This paper addresses the gap by enabling skill-driven motion editing trained exclusively on unpaired expert demonstrations and operating fully conditioning-free at inference.

## Input / Output

The method takes a novice 3D motion sequence represented as per-frame tuples of root translation (R³), root orientation (axis-angle, R³), and joint rotations (R^{3J}), extracted via WHAM at 30 fps (Ego-Exo4D) or provided at 250 Hz (Karate, J=39 joints). It produces an edited sequence of identical duration where only joint rotations are modified at skill-critical phases, while root translation and orientation are copied directly from the input to preserve the actor's spatial trajectory and body orientation. Edits are temporally localized to spans centered on kinematic peaks (e.g., takeoff, ball contact), leaving all other frames unchanged.

## Method

ExpertEdit casts skill refinement as contextual motion infilling using two components: a causal VQ-VAE Pose Tokenizer that learns a discrete expert-motion vocabulary, and a bidirectional Transformer (MotionInfiller) trained with a masked language modeling objective. During training, spans of expert motion tokens are masked at kinematically identified skill-critical peaks (e.g., vertical velocity maxima for basketball, jerk maxima for soccer) and the model learns to reconstruct them from surrounding context. At inference, the same kinematic criterion selects skill-critical phases in a novice sequence, those spans are replaced with [MASK] tokens, and the MotionInfiller one-shot infills expert-like tokens conditioned on the unmasked novice context. The approach is technique-specific (separate tokenizer and infiller per skill), requires no paired novice-expert data, no text prompts, and no reference clips, and preserves the input's duration, global trajectory, and rhythm by construction.

## Results

On four basketball/soccer techniques (Ego-Exo4D), ExpertEdit achieves Pose Improvement (P) of 5.34–6.18% and FID Improvement (F) of 5.72–12.08%, roughly 2–4× larger gains than the strongest baseline (SimMotionEdit: P 2.26–3.95%, F 1.24–4.88%). On four Kyokushin Karate techniques, ExpertEdit leads on F across all techniques (up to 9.73% for Front Kick) but trails SimMotionEdit on P for two techniques (Rev. Punch: 2.07 vs 2.20; High Roundhouse: 2.96 vs 3.30). In a blinded human preference study (112 judgments), ExpertEdit is preferred 62.8% of the time among non-neutral responses versus SimMotionEdit.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ExpertEdit is trained exclusively on unpaired expert demonstrations, while TMED and SimMotionEdit are fine-tuned on 16k novice-expert pseudo-pairs and FLAME operates in inference-time editing mode—giving all baselines privileged supervision that ExpertEdit never receives. On basketball and soccer (Table 1), ExpertEdit decisively outperforms all three baselines on both P and F across all four techniques, with the largest margin on Reverse Layup (F = 12.08 vs. 6.13 for FLAME) and Penalty Kick (F = 9.14 vs. 2.38 for SimMotionEdit). On Kyokushin Karate (Table 2), ExpertEdit wins on F for all four techniques (e.g., Front Kick F = 9.73 vs. 5.05 for SimMotionEdit) but concedes P to SimMotionEdit on Reverse Punch (2.07 vs. 2.20) and High Roundhouse (2.96 vs. 3.30), which the authors attribute to the narrower skill gap in the karate dataset. In the human preference study, ExpertEdit is judged more useful for form improvement 62.8% of the time among non-neutral responses, compared to SimMotionEdit's 37.2%.

## Relation to Prior Work

ExpertEdit departs from the dominant motion-editing paradigm (TMED, SimMotionEdit, FLAME), which requires either paired input-output demonstrations or explicit text/reference conditioning at inference, by reframing skill refinement as contextual motion infilling on an expert-only manifold. The method borrows the VQ-VAE tokenization architecture from PoseGPT and the masked-language-modeling objective from text generation, but applies them to a novel setting: reconstructing kinematically-selected spans of expert motion so that the same mask-and-infill procedure can project novice motion onto the learned expert distribution at test time. In the AI-coaching literature, prior systems (ExpertAF, Vid2Coach, PoseTutor) produce text feedback or require frame-aligned expert references; ExpertEdit instead generates the corrected motion directly, eliminating the human-in-the-loop correction step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes skill-driven motion editing as a distinct task and releases the first public benchmark of temporally-aligned novice-expert pairs across eight techniques, filling a gap that prior coaching and editing work could not address due to the absence of large-scale paired skill data. A key open question is generalization: each technique requires a separately trained tokenizer and infiller, and the method preserves input duration and global trajectory, so it cannot correct temporal pacing errors or large spatial deviations. Broader impact includes enabling AI coaching without expert annotation, improving amateur footage for robotics training, and potentially extending to domains where expert demonstrations are abundant but paired novice-expert data are infeasible to collect.
