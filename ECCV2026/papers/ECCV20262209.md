## Problem

Existing urban spatial understanding benchmarks for MLLMs focus on whole-image-level perception or isolated object recognition (buildings, vehicles, traffic signs), leaving a gap in evaluating fine-grained spatial elements with strong interdependency, such as road markings that collectively form an integrated traffic network. No prior benchmark systematically tests MLLMs' ability to recognize, jointly understand, and reason about these thin, elongated, semantically coupled structures across both bird's-eye and first-person perspectives in real urban settings.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes two image modalities—BEV satellite imagery (2,137 unique images) and FPV in-vehicle camera photos (721 unique images)—along with directed reference line polylines rendered as red arrows on satellite images to indicate target roads, and structured text prompts. Outputs are task-specific: integer lane counts, per-lane multi-label direction types (U-turn, left, straight, right, and combinations), normalized junction point coordinates and directed road segment polylines for network correction, binary main/service road labels, and cross-view fused answers. All coordinates are normalized to [0,1] relative to image dimensions, and the road network correction task requires the model to emit structured geometric outputs (point sets and polylines) rather than free-text descriptions.

## Method

RoadBench constructs a hierarchical evaluation framework of eight tasks spanning local perception (lane counting, lane designation) to global reasoning (road network correction, road type classification) and cross-view synthesis (paired BEV-FPV lane counting and designation), totaling 3,040 manually verified test cases from five Chinese cities. The curation pipeline extracts junction candidates from OpenStreetMap, retrieves ground-truth labels (lane counts, directions, centerlines) from a tier-1 online map provider's database, and matches FPV images by capture coordinates; five domain experts then audit all samples via the LabelU platform, excluding ambiguous cases rather than re-annotating. Evaluation employs task-appropriate metrics—weighted F1 and RMSE for counting, Hamming Loss and accuracy for multi-label designation, Fréchet Distance with a 50% upper bound for directed polyline similarity, and Euclidean RMSE with a 20% bound for junction points—alongside rule-based and random baselines. The paper evaluates 20 mainstream MLLMs (GPT-5 series, Gemini-2.5 series, Qwen2.5-VL, Qwen3-VL, LLaMA-3.2-Vision, Gemma-3, GLM-4.5V) and additionally fine-tunes Qwen3-VL-2B/4B/8B on the benchmark to assess task-specific adaptability, revealing that even the strongest models underperform simple traffic-common-sense baselines on lane designation and struggle with precise coordinate regression.

## Results

On RoadBench, the best-performing closed-source model GPT-5-Mini achieves an F1 of only 0.355 (RMSE 1.162) on BEV Lane Counting and an accuracy of 0.662 on BEV Lane Designation Recognition, indicating that even frontier MLLMs struggle with fine-grained road-marking understanding. After task-specific fine-tuning, Qwen3-VL-8B(tuned) attains the best overall results among open-source models, reaching F1 0.540 on Cross-View Lane Counting, accuracy 0.728 on Cross-View Lane Designation, and Fréchet Distance 0.154 on BEV Road Network Correction. Notably, most MLLMs fail to surpass a simple rule-based baseline on both lane designation recognition tasks, and several underperform a random-choice baseline on lane counting, underscoring the benchmark's difficulty.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 20 MLLMs against rule-based and random-choice baselines rather than comparing a single proposed method. On BEV and FPV Lane Designation Recognition, the rule-based baseline (a traffic-common-sense mapping table) achieves Hamming Loss 0.149/0.127 and accuracy 0.587/0.605, respectively; most MLLMs fail to beat these numbers—e.g., Qwen3-VL-32B scores HL 0.188/0.144 and GPT-5 scores HL 0.159/0.122, both worse than the rule-based baseline on at least one view. On FPV Road Type Classification, MLLMs clearly outperform the 50% random baseline (best: Qwen3-VL-2B(tuned) at 83.0%), confirming that this task is within current model capabilities. On BEV Lane Counting, the 'always predict 2' baseline yields F1 0.248, which several MLLMs (e.g., Qwen3-VL-2B at 0.186, LLaMA-3.2-11B at 0.238) fail to exceed. Fine-tuned Qwen3-VL-8B surpasses all untuned open-source models and approaches GPT-5-Mini on most tasks, but still trails it on BEV Lane Counting F1 (0.339 vs. 0.355) and BEV Lane Designation accuracy (0.338 vs. 0.662), highlighting a persistent gap between open-source and closed-source frontier models on fine-grained spatial reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by positioning fine-grained spatial element reasoning (specifically road markings with topological and regulatory interdependencies) as an underexplored gap in the MLLM urban spatial understanding literature. Existing benchmarks like CityBench, UrBench, DriveBench, and MapDR focus on whole-image perception, isolated object detection, or cross-view matching, but none target the joint understanding of thin, interdependent spatial structures that collectively encode traffic semantics. The work draws on the broader MLLM spatial reasoning evaluation tradition (SPACE, SpatialEval, VSI-Bench) and the autonomous driving MLLM line (VLDrive, UrbanLLaVA, NuPlanQA), but repurposes these ideas into a domain-specific benchmark where the evaluation unit is a set of interdependent road markings rather than a single object or scene-level attribute.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even frontier MLLMs (GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Pro) fail to robustly understand fine-grained spatial structures in urban road imagery, with some models underperforming random or rule-based baselines—a finding that challenges the assumption that scaling MLLMs automatically yields reliable spatial reasoning in structured real-world scenes. Cross-view spatial alignment between BEV and FPV perspectives remains an open challenge, and the finding that parameter count does not monotonically improve performance (GPT-5-Mini outperforming GPT-5 on most tasks) suggests that modality fusion architecture matters more than raw scale for this task. Limitations include geographic restriction to five Chinese cities with consistent traffic conventions, reliance on Google satellite imagery for BEV inputs, and the absence of a human upper-bound baseline, which constrains generalizability claims to other traffic systems.
