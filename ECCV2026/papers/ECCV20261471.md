## Problem

Existing corruption-then-restoration editing methods construct their intermediate (noisy) state in a target-agnostic manner, uniformly corrupting the source image without regard for which content the target prompt actually modifies. This leads to poor source consistency and limited editability when the desired modification substantially deviates from the source, since the intermediate state neither selectively preserves editing-irrelevant regions nor adequately destroys editing-relevant ones.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261471/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a source image, a source prompt describing it, and a target prompt specifying the desired edit, and produces a single edited image. It operates on 512×512 (PIE-Bench) and 1024×1024 (DIV2K) RGB images using a pre-trained flow-matching backbone (SD-3-medium or SD-1.5). No training is required; all optimization happens at inference time over two learnable noise vectors, with the corruption timestep fixed at t=33 out of 50 total steps.

## Method

FlowCycle parameterizes the corruption step with two learnable Gaussian noises (ε_src for the source and ε_tar for the generated target) and jointly optimizes them via a cycle-consistent loop: the source is corrupted with ε_src and denoised under the target prompt to produce a target image, which is then corrupted with ε_tar and denoised under the source prompt to produce a recovery image. Two losses drive optimization — an alignment loss pulling the two intermediate noisy states together in latent space, and a recovery loss pulling the recovered image back to the original source — so that the learned noises selectively destroy editing-relevant content while preserving editing-irrelevant content. At inference, only the optimized ε_src is used to form the target-aware intermediate state, from which a single denoising pass under the target prompt yields the final edit without any additional source-condition injection. A progressive sampling schedule (e.g., sinusoidal increase in ODE steps across optimization rounds) accelerates the iterative process by roughly 1.5–3× with minimal quality loss.

## Results

On PIE-Bench with SD-3-medium, FlowCycle achieves the best Structure Distance (0.013) and highest CLIP Score (25.48) among all compared methods, indicating superior source consistency and semantic alignment simultaneously. The full optimization takes 24,716 s per image, but the progressive-sampling acceleration reduces this to 9,016 s (strong) with only a small quality drop (Distance 0.017, CLIP 25.31). Even the accelerated-strong variant outperforms the optimization-based iRFDS (Distance 0.069, 13,234 s) and the large-model Qwen-Image-Edit (Distance 0.054, 9,260 s) on both quality and speed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FlowCycle outperforms all optimization-free baselines on both metrics: versus FlowAlign it halves the Structure Distance (0.013 vs. 0.028) and gains +0.20 CLIP, at the cost of ~60× more inference time (24,716 s vs. 416 s); versus Qwen-Image-Edit it improves Distance by 4× (0.013 vs. 0.054) with comparable CLIP. Against the optimization-based iRFDS, FlowCycle achieves a 5× lower Distance (0.013 vs. 0.069) and higher CLIP (25.48 vs. 25.12), though the full version is ~1.9× slower; the accelerated-strong variant (9,016 s) is actually faster than iRFDS (13,234 s) while maintaining better quality. Qualitative comparisons in Figure 5 show FlowCycle also surpasses SDEdit, FlowEdit, P2P, and P2P Zero on preserving editing-irrelevant regions, with the paper noting that FlowCycle on SD-1.5 approaches the performance of FlowEdit on the stronger SD-3 backbone. The key trade-off is computational: FlowCycle's cycle-consistent optimization inherently requires more NFEs, but the progressive sampling strategy narrows the gap to near-real-time methods while retaining a clear quality advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operates within the well-established corruption-then-restoration paradigm for text-based image editing, which encompasses both inversion-based methods (ODE inversion, FireFlow) and inversion-free methods (SDEdit, FlowEdit, FlowAlign). Its key departure is identifying that all existing approaches construct the intermediate (corrupted) state in a target-agnostic manner, and repurposing cycle consistency—previously applied in image-to-image translation (CycleGAN) and limited editing work—to instead learn a target-aware corruption via two jointly optimized learnable noises. The framework is built on flow matching (rectified flow) models like SD-3 but is backbone-agnostic, also demonstrated on SD-1.5.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By reframing the corruption step as target-aware rather than target-agnostic, FlowCycle addresses a structural weakness in the dominant editing paradigm that injection-based restoration strategies cannot fix. The practical impact is notable: the method requires no retraining of the base model and the progressive sampling acceleration reduces the optimization overhead to roughly one-third of the full cost, making it competitive with optimization-free methods in wall-clock time. However, the paper acknowledges that the approach still incurs higher compute than purely optimization-free baselines, and the transfer experiment reveals that intermediate states do not generalize perfectly across very different editing magnitudes, leaving open whether a single learned corruption can handle arbitrarily large semantic shifts without re-optimization.
