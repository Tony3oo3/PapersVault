## Problem

Localising multiple simultaneous sound sources in a visual scene suffers from a circular dependency: decomposing additively mixed audio requires spatial guidance from the visual domain, yet identifying which regions produce sound requires already-separated audio signals. This makes the joint problem severely ill-posed. Additionally, existing benchmarks evaluate continuous localisation heatmaps against bounding-box annotations, creating systematic biases that reward over-activation of background regions rather than precise localisation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB video frame (resized to 224×224 per source, concatenated to 224×448 for dual-source) and a 3-second mixed audio signal sampled at 22.05 kHz, converted to log-spectrograms via STFT with 50 ms windows and 25 ms hop. It produces two continuous-valued spatial localisation heatmaps M₁, M₂ ∈ [0,1]^(H×W), each pinpointing one sound source. At inference the full two-stage architecture executes as a single forward pass at 43.2 FPS on one A100 GPU, with the visual encoder (frozen ResNet-18) and audio encoder (ResNet-18 trained from scratch) shared across both stages.

## Method

SCAV is a two-stage progressive framework that exploits an emergent property of contrastive learning—selective convergence—where models trained on mixed audio naturally gravitate toward the most salient audio-visual correspondence rather than representing all sources equally. Stage 1 uses contrastive learning with differentiable thresholding (inherited from LVS) on the mixed audio to produce a coarse spatial prior M_dom identifying the dominant source. Stage 2 partitions visual features by M_dom, refines them with a 4-layer Transformer visual decoupler, and separates the mixed audio via a cross-attention audio decoupler (mixed audio as query, source-specific visual features as keys/values), then trains each decoupled audio-visual pair with source-specific contrastive loss that omits cross-sample negatives. Progressive training—converging Stage 1 before training Stage 2—is critical, as joint end-to-end training yields unstable spatial priors that mislead the decoupler. The framework is fully self-supervised, requiring no manual annotations, class labels, or external text modalities.

## Results

On MUSIC-Duet (BBox), SCAV achieves 47.1% CIoU@0.3, 28.6% AUC, and 47.6% CAP, the best among all self-supervised methods and surpassing the weakly-supervised OA-SSL (45.9% CIoU@0.3). On the larger VGGSound-Duet (BBox), SCAV reaches 53.3% CIoU@0.3, 32.0% AUC, and 53.0% CAP, approaching the weakly-supervised OA-SSL (55.2% CIoU@0.3) while outperforming all other self-supervised baselines by 15–35 points. Under the newly introduced pixel-precise VGGSound-Duet^Mask evaluation, SCAV achieves 33.59% CIoU@0.3 and 23.52% AUC, again the best self-supervised result and exceeding the weakly-supervised AVGN (18.44% CIoU@0.3) by a wide margin.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SCAV outperforms all 10 self-supervised baselines on both MUSIC-Duet and VGGSound-Duet across every reported metric. The largest gains are over DSOL (30.1% → 47.1% CIoU@0.3 on MUSIC-Duet, +17 pts) and Mix-and-Localize (21.1% → 53.3% on VGGSound-Duet, +32 pts), indicating that prior self-supervised methods struggle substantially with dual-source separation. Against weakly-supervised methods, SCAV surpasses AVGN (32.5% → 47.1% on MUSIC-Duet) and approaches OA-SSL (45.9% vs. 47.1% on MUSIC-Duet; 55.2% vs. 53.3% on VGGSound-Duet), the latter being the only method that edges out SCAV on CIoU@0.3 for VGGSound-Duet. Under the stricter mask-based evaluation (VGGSound-Duet^Mask), SCAV's advantage widens: it achieves 33.59% CIoU@0.3 versus 18.44% for AVGN and 9.58% for EZ-VSL, confirming that the gains are not an artefact of bounding-box evaluation bias. The performance gap over baselines also grows with dataset scale (MUSIC-Duet: 11 classes → VGGSound-Duet: 220 classes), suggesting SCAV benefits from data diversity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the contrastive learning framework of Chen et al. (LVS, CVPR 2021) for audio-visual localisation, reusing its differentiable-thresholding mechanism for positive/negative region identification. It departs from prior multi-source approaches (Mix-and-Localize, DSOL, NoPrior, Slot-Attn) that engineer explicit disentanglement mechanisms or rely on weak supervision to break the circular dependency between audio separation and visual localisation. The key conceptual shift is reframing the problem: rather than fighting the model's tendency to focus on one source, the authors identify this 'selective convergence' as a useful emergent property and build a two-stage progressive architecture around it, using the first stage's dominant-source heatmap as a spatial prior for the second stage's cross-attention decoupling. The paper also introduces a pixel-precise segmentation-mask evaluation protocol, departing from the bounding-box-based metrics used in all prior multi-source benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The selective convergence finding offers a practical, annotation-free strategy for multi-source localisation that avoids the hand-tuned thresholds and complex disentanglement mechanisms of prior work, and the performance gap widens with dataset scale, suggesting the approach benefits from data diversity. The introduction of VGGSound-Duet^Mask (3,951 mask-annotated dual-source pairs across 220 categories) addresses a genuine evaluation gap, as bounding-box metrics can reward over-activation on background pixels within boxes. However, the framework is limited to exactly two sources, and whether selective convergence generalises to three or more simultaneous sources—or to other multimodal correspondence tasks—remains an open question. The dominance assignment is model-dependent rather than input-dependent, which could be a limitation in scenarios where both sources are equally salient.
