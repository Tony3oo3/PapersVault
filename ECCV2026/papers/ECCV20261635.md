## Problem

When a real-world scene is captured by a smartphone camera and viewed on its screen, the displayed image often differs noticeably from the original in color, brightness, and contrast. Standard ICC workflows decompose this high-dimensional capture-to-display process into separately calibrated camera and display stages connected by low-dimensional 3×3 color transforms, creating an inherent information bottleneck and accumulating calibration errors that no amount of post-processing can fully eliminate.

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a captured RGB image (linear RAW from the phone's Pro mode) and a pre-calibrated observer coefficient φ ∈ R^{3×1}, producing a per-pixel color-corrected image ready for display on the paired phone screen. The camera-display projector operates as a pixel-wise R³→R³ mapping, while the camera-null correction adds a per-pixel scalar coefficient a_i (predicted by a CNN) scaled by a fixed spectral basis vector e. Training data for the projector is generated via a re-capture protocol (rendering RGB images on the display and re-imaging with the camera), and the null-coefficient predictor is trained on hyperspectral datasets (ARAD-1K, CAVE, ICVL) with L=31 spectral channels.

## Method

The core contribution is treating the camera and display as a single coupled system modeled by an end-to-end learned projector, rather than calibrating each device to a reference space independently. The projector F_C is a lightweight two-layer MLP (hidden width 128) with positional encoding and an AvgPool on the green channel, trained via L1 loss on re-captured pixel pairs to approximate the pseudo-inverse of the composite CD operator. A key theoretical result shows that the residual color error for a human observer lies entirely in the camera's metameric-black subspace (spectral components invisible to the camera), and that this subspace has low intrinsic dimensionality—the first PCA component explains ~93% of variance. This motivates a rank-1 approximation where the correction reduces to a per-pixel scalar a_i (predicted by a spectral-reconstruction CNN trained with combined L1, coefficient, and total-variation losses) multiplied by a fixed basis vector e, scaled by a single observer-specific calibration coefficient φ ∈ R^{3×1} estimated in one step via grid search on ColorChecker patches.

## Results

On the 24-patch ColorChecker evaluation under 15 diverse illuminants (2500–9000 K CCT + 5 RGB-LED colors), the full Color Pass-Through model achieves PSNR 28.65 (Huawei) / 29.10 (Xiaomi), ΔE_mean 5.18 / 4.79, and STRESS 17.48 / 16.12, more than doubling the best baseline on PSNR (16.36) and reducing ΔE_mean by over 3× (13.49). In the user study, the method earns an average +2.0 points over baselines on a 5-point Likert scale for both brightness and color accuracy. The learned camera-display projector alone (without camera-null correction) already reaches PSNR 27.32/27.84, showing the projector carries most of the gain, while the camera-null term adds a further ~1.3 PSNR and ~1.3 ΔE improvement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the three pixel-wise color-transfer baselines in the projector-fitting evaluation (Table 1a), the learned MLP projector outperforms IA-3DLUT [57] by +5.44 PSNR (32.13 vs 26.69) and −2.32 ΔE_mean (3.34 vs 5.66), and edges out both NiLUT [21] (+1.10 PSNR, −0.25 ΔE) and CSRNet [31] (+1.01 PSNR, −0.25 ΔE) while using fewer parameters (30.8K vs 33.9K/36.5K) and running faster (41 ms vs 254 ms for CSRNet). In the full color pass-through comparison (Table 2), the method dominates all three system-level baselines: it surpasses Color-checker calibration [51] by +13.63 PSNR and −13.31 ΔE_mean (Huawei), and Multi-illuminant Auto-WB [2] by +15.81 PSNR and −12.66 ΔE_mean. The default smartphone ISP pipeline, while not a learned method, is also outperformed by +14.87 PSNR. Notably, the Color-checker calibration baseline actually worsens ΔE_mean relative to the default ISP (18.49 vs 14.62 on Huawei), highlighting that standard per-scene ICC-style correction can be counterproductive for the coupled camera-display task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard ICC paradigm of separately calibrating camera and display stages connected by a low-dimensional color transform, instead treating the camera-display pair as a single coupled system learned end-to-end. It repurposes pixel-wise MLP architectures from learned color-transfer literature (e.g., NiLUT, IA-3DLUT) as a non-linear surrogate for the camera-display projector, and introduces a re-capture protocol (render on display, re-image with camera) to generate training pairs without requiring a spectrometer. The theoretical decomposition into a camera-display projection term and a camera-null correction term connects to the classical Luther-Ives condition but extends it to the coupled display setting, with the practical rank-1 PCA approximation of the metameric-black subspace being a new contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The coupled-system framing offers a principled alternative to the error-accumulating two-stage calibration pipeline that underpins most consumer color management, and the one-step observer-specific calibration (a single 3-vector φ) makes per-user adaptation practical for pass-through scenarios like phone photography and AR/VR see-through displays. Key limitations include the requirement for per camera-display pair calibration (limiting cross-device use), the rank-1 approximation of the camera-null space (which may fail for exotic spectra or non-natural scenes), and reliance on a DSLR as a proxy for human perception in quantitative evaluation. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to multi-device workflows, dynamic or HDR content, and whether the re-capture protocol can be replaced by cheaper characterization methods.
