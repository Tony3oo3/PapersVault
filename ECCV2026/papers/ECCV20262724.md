## Problem

While direct VLM jailbreaks and text-based indirect prompt injection (IPI) are well-studied, the security implications of multimodal IPI—where malicious instructions are embedded within rendered visual artifacts such as terminal screenshots and professional documents—remain insufficiently understood in realistic agentic workflows. The central question is whether processing an artifact visually, rather than as text, degrades an agent's ability to detect and resist embedded attacks, and whether alignment priors (e.g., fairness mandates) constitute a distinct, exploitable attack surface.

## Input / Output

Each benchmark sample is presented in two paired configurations: the VL configuration feeds the visual artifact as a rendered image (terminal screenshot, resume PDF, loan form) alongside a task-specific system prompt, while the LM configuration feeds the identical ground-truth text content of that artifact, bypassing the visual encoder entirely and avoiding OCR artifacts. For detection, the model outputs a binary attack/benign label; for attack success, GPT-4.1 acts as a deployed agent producing recommended troubleshooting steps (technical) or a candidate rating (ethics), with ASR measured by whether malicious commands are reproduced or an unqualified candidate receives a favorable rating.

## Method

The M-IPI Benchmark comprises 2,600 high-fidelity visual artifacts across two attack families: 2,000 technically-framed artifacts (1,000 attack + 1,000 matched benign terminal screenshots spanning 20 domains, with malicious commands positioned as executable commands, inline comments, or both) and 600 ethics-framed artifacts (fabricated fairness/compliance directives embedded in resumes, loan applications, and tender bids varying in length, directness, authority framing, and visual placement). The key methodological innovation is the paired VL/LM experimental design: every sample is evaluated in both image and text form with identical content, enabling direct attribution of any performance gap to visual processing rather than content differences. Five model families (Idefics3-8B, Gemma-3-12B, Qwen3-VL-8B, GPT-4.1, GPT-5.2) are tested for detection, while GPT-4.1 serves as the deployed agent for end-to-end attack success, with GPT-5.2 as an independent LLM judge for technical ASR. The ethics-framed attack vector is novel, exploiting documented overcorrection and sycophancy tendencies by embedding fabricated compliance directives that create a conflict between deployment-time evaluation criteria and the model's deeply encoded value priors.

## Results

Across all five model families, VL (vision) configurations consistently underperform LM (text-only) configurations at detecting embedded attacks, with recall gaps ranging from 6.7 pp (GPT-5.2) to 27.0 pp (GPT-4.1) on technically-framed attacks and from 1.6 pp (Idefics3-8B) to 25.0 pp (Gemma-3-12B) on ethics-framed attacks. For end-to-end attack success, GPT-4.1 shows comparable susceptibility in both modalities: 48.1% ASR (VL) vs. 50.9% ASR (LM) on technical attacks (McNemar p = 0.066), and >80% ASR on ethics-framed attacks across both hiring roles. The failure profiles are modality-specific: 88% of VL-unique technical failures involve executable-command-format attacks (1.47× enrichment, χ² p < 0.001), while LM is significantly more vulnerable to comment-format natural-language directives (p = 0.015).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is not against external baselines but between paired VL (image input) and LM (identical text content) configurations of the same five models, isolating the visual modality effect. In every model pair, the LM configuration outperforms VL on detection recall, confirming a consistent 'visual authority' effect: visual rendering suppresses critical evaluation regardless of model scale or family. On attack success, the two modalities are statistically indistinguishable overall (48.1% vs. 50.9% for technical, p = 0.066), indicating the vulnerability is rooted in the shared language backbone rather than the visual encoder. However, the failure profiles diverge: VL agents are disproportionately vulnerable to executable-command-format attacks (88% of VL-unique failures, 1.47× enrichment), while LM agents are more susceptible to natural-language comment-format directives (p = 0.015). The paper also positions itself against prior benchmarks (VPI-Bench, CyberSecEval 3) methodologically rather than numerically, noting that neither provides matched text-only baselines or format-conditioned analysis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multimodal indirect prompt injection (M-IPI) line of work most directly represented by VPI-Bench (Cao et al., 2026) and CyberSecEval 3's visual prompt injection category, but departs from both through a paired VL/LM experimental design that isolates the visual modality effect—something neither prior benchmark provides—and through the introduction of ethics-framed attacks that exploit alignment priors (fairness mandates, sycophancy) rather than technical command injection. Conceptually, it bridges the direct jailbreak literature (which documents visual-channel vulnerability under adversarial user input) and the text-based IPI literature (which studies environmental contamination) by studying how rendered visual presentation in realistic agentic workflows suppresses critical evaluation. The ethics-framed attack vector is a genuinely new contribution, drawing on documented overcorrection and sycophancy tendencies in aligned models to construct a distinct attack surface not present in prior IPI benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The consistent 'visual authority' effect across all five model families and both attack families, combined with the >80% attack success rate for ethics-framed injections on clearly unqualified candidates, provides concrete evidence that current VLM-based agents are vulnerable in high-stakes deployment settings (code execution, hiring, financial decisions) in ways that prompt-level hardening does not address. The finding that the detection gap (VL < LM) does not translate into a corresponding execution gap suggests that agentic reasoning partially compensates for perception failures, pointing toward execution-time guards as a more promising defense layer. Key limitations include the ethics evaluation's restriction to Global-North cultural contexts and the technical dataset's focus on Linux terminals, leaving open whether these vulnerabilities generalize across cultures, operating systems, and artifact types. A comprehensive cross-cultural, multi-OS study of ethics-framed injection and the development of effective agent-level and system-level defenses remain important open directions.
