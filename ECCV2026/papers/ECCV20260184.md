## Problem

Vision-Language-Action models achieve strong performance on static manipulation but suffer severe degradation (e.g., π₀.₅ drops from 44.8% to 7.5% SR) when targets move, due to their reliance on single-frame observations that preclude continuous spatiotemporal reasoning. This gap is compounded by the near-absence of large-scale dynamic manipulation datasets and the lack of object-centric motion prediction in existing VLA architectures, which either ignore temporal context or model only global scene transitions rather than individual target dynamics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260184/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

PUMA consumes a history of h compressed third-person frames (from which optical flow maps are computed at a fixed stride), current multi-view RGB-D observations, proprioceptive state, and a natural-language instruction. The model outputs a continuous dual-arm action chunk of length K in a single forward pass via dedicated action queries, while N auxiliary world queries simultaneously predict future object-centric DINO features (horizon N, typically 4 steps) that are supervised only during training and discarded at inference, adding no runtime cost.

## Method

PUMA builds on the Qwen3-VL backbone and introduces two complementary mechanisms for dynamic awareness. First, scene-centric historical encoding replaces raw frame stacking with explicit optical flow maps computed across spatially compressed historical frames, providing the visual encoder with dense, interpretable motion cues that ablations show outperform raw-frame inputs (11.71% vs. 8.15% SR). Second, object-centric future prediction uses N learnable world queries that aggregate the spatiotemporal context to forecast the target object's future DINO patch-token features, where ground-truth supervision is obtained by grounding the instruction-named object via GroundingDINO and segmenting it with SAM2 in future frames. Training combines L1 behavioral cloning on action chunks with a cosine-similarity loss on the predicted future features (weighted by λ), forcing the shared representation to internalize object dynamics without any future-frame access at test time. The architecture achieves 17.20% SR and 34.97 MS on DOMINO@0.1, a 6.3-point absolute improvement over the strongest baseline, while maintaining 9.6 Hz inference latency comparable to π₀.₅.

## Results

On the DOMINO@0.1 benchmark (Level 1 dynamics, clean setting, Aloha-AgileX robot), PUMA achieves a Success Rate of 17.20% and Manipulation Score of 34.97, outperforming the strongest baseline OpenVLA-OFT with Qwen3-VL backbone (10.86% SR, 30.49 MS) by 6.34% absolute SR. Across all 35 dynamic tasks, PUMA also leads on the task-type breakdown: 24.2% SR on Dynamic Interception and 8.9% SR on Dynamic Tracking, versus 14.3%/2.9% for OpenVLA-OFT and 11.8%/7.0% for π₀.₅. Under higher complexity (Level 2 and Level 3), PUMA maintains its lead over π₀.₅ (10.5% vs. 7.9% SR at Level 2; 4.6% vs. 1.6% at Level 3). In real-world experiments on the AgileX Piper platform, PUMA reaches 42% average SR versus 24% for π₀.₅ and below 10% for ACT and RDT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PUMA outperforms all ten baselines on both SR and MS. The closest competitor is OpenVLA-OFT with a Qwen3-VL backbone (10.86% SR, 30.49 MS), which PUMA surpasses by 6.34% SR and 4.48 MS; the Prismatic-backbone variant of OpenVLA-OFT trails further at 9.06% SR. Among PaliGemma-based models, π₀.₅ (9.63% SR) and π₀ (8.17% SR) are the strongest, yet both fall well short of PUMA. RDT-1B (5.34% SR) and InternVLA-M1 (5.40% SR) show moderate performance, while OpenVLA (1.54% SR) and VLA-Adapter (4.40% SR) struggle substantially in dynamic settings. The ACT baseline (per-task fine-tuning) achieves only 9.4% average SR in dynamic evaluation (Table 1), confirming that single-frame architectures cannot close the gap even with task-specific training. Notably, PUMA's advantage is most pronounced on high-difficulty tasks (Fig. 5) and persists under increased dynamic complexity (Level 2/3), suggesting the gains stem from architectural dynamic awareness rather than dataset-specific overfitting.

## Relation to Prior Work

PUMA builds on the Qwen3-VL backbone and ACT's action-chunking paradigm, but departs from both the single-frame observation assumption of mainstream VLAs (OpenVLA, π₀, RDT-1B) and the scene-level world-modeling approach of DreamVLA/WorldVLA. Rather than tracking task progression via memory mechanisms or forecasting global scene transitions, PUMA introduces object-centric future prediction through learnable world queries supervised by DINO features, combined with explicit optical flow for historical dynamics. The DOMINO benchmark extends the RoboTwin 2.0 and SAPIEN simulation infrastructure by adding a three-stage spatiotemporal synchronization pipeline and a hierarchical difficulty taxonomy (Levels 1–3) absent from prior manipulation benchmarks, which universally assume static environments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DOMINO provides the first large-scale, systematically tiered benchmark for dynamic manipulation (110K+ trajectories, 35 tasks, 5 embodiments), filling a critical gap that has limited progress in this area. The dramatic performance collapse of SOTA VLAs (e.g., π₀.₅ dropping from 44.8% to 7.5% SR) and the oracle experiment showing that naive future-trajectory injection without historical context produces control jitter together establish that dynamic manipulation requires a fundamentally different architectural treatment, not just more data. However, absolute success rates remain low (17.2% for PUMA), real-world validation is limited to 5 tasks with 50 demos each, and the primary evaluation is confined to Level 1 dynamics, leaving open whether the approach scales to the stochastic, multi-segment dynamics of Level 3 or to more complex real-world settings.
