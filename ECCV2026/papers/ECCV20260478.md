## Problem

Current video large language models and their benchmarks predominantly sample only a handful of frames per second, discarding the dense temporal information needed for frame-by-frame reasoning on high-FPS content such as educational subtitles or rapid egocentric motion. This limitation is compounded by the quadratic self-attention cost of standard patch-based tokenization, which makes full-FPS processing intractable, and by existing benchmarks that pose coarse-grained queries answerable from just a few frames, masking the true cost of sparse sampling.

## Input / Output

The method consumes high-FPS video clips (224×224 frames tokenized into 16×16 patches) alongside a text query prompt, operating in a zero-shot, inference-only setting on 0.5B- and 7B-parameter LLaVA-One Vision / Qwen2 backbones. The educational partition requires the model to reconstruct full subtitle sequences from videos averaging ~72,000 frames at 44 FPS, while the high-motion partition overlays a 3×3 grid on each frame and asks the model to output a discrete grid-cell label (e.g., r1c2) for the palm center, converting dense 2D localization into a per-frame classification task. Evaluation metrics include MOS, CER/WER, Grid ADE/FDE, effective FPS, post-token count, and tokenization wall-clock time.

## Method

Gated Residual Tokenization (GRT) is a codec-style, training-free tokenization framework with two stages. First, Motion-Compensated Gated Inter-Tokenization treats each scene as a key frame plus residual P-frames: a binary SSIM-based mask identifies patches that have changed since the previous frame, and the pretrained ViT's convolutional patch-embedding layer is flattened into an equivalent MLP so that only unmasked patches are embedded while masked positions receive zero-vector placeholders for positional encoding, after which only valid tokens enter the multi-head attention layers. This yields sub-linear growth in both tokenization time and token count as FPS increases. Second, Semantic-Scene Token Merging computes Jensen-Shannon divergence between the frequency distributions of key-frame tokens across adjacent scenes; when the divergence falls below a threshold, the two scenes are merged by averaging their mean key-token embeddings and concatenating their P-token sequences in temporal order, further compressing the sequence while preserving motion-specific tokens. The resulting reduced token sequence is projected through a linear vision-projection layer and concatenated with the text prompt before being fed into the LLM without any additional training.

## Results

On DIVE-Bench (educational partition), the 0.5B GRT model achieves a Mean Opinion Score (MOS) of 2.50, outperforming all open-source baselines including the 7B LLaVA-One Vision (1.70) and 7B LLaVA-Video (1.47). On the high-motion partition, GRT doubles the effective FPS from 4.49 to 8.69 while reducing Grid ADE from 1.3618 to 1.1456 and Grid FDE from 1.6085 to 1.1828. On existing long-video benchmarks under matched low-FPS sampling, GRT improves MLVU from 33.00 to 34.07 and VideoMME from 43.96 to 44.04. Tokenization latency at 1 FPS is reduced by 53.6% (0.0487 s → 0.0226 s) compared to the LLaVA-OV baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRT (0.5B) outperforms all three baseline families on DIVE-Bench MOS: it beats LLaVA-Video (7B, 1.47) by 1.03 points, LLaVA-One Vision (7B, 1.70) by 0.80 points, and LLaVA-Next SI (0.5B, 1.73) by 0.77 points, despite using the smallest model size. Against the same-size LLaVA-One Vision (0.5B, 2.01), GRT gains 0.49 MOS, showing that the codec-style tokenization itself—rather than model scale—drives the improvement. On the high-motion grid-localization task, GRT nearly doubles effective FPS (8.69 vs. 4.49) while simultaneously reducing Grid ADE by 16% and Grid FDE by 26% over the LLaVA-OV baseline. On the standard long-video benchmarks (MLVU, VideoMME) under matched low-FPS sampling, GRT yields a modest +1.06 gain on MLVU and a near-neutral +0.07 on VideoMME, confirming that the method does not degrade conventional video understanding while unlocking dense-temporal performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes classical video compression concepts (key-frame/P-frame coding, motion compensation, residual encoding) into the tokenization pipeline of video LLMs, building directly on the LLaVA-One Vision architecture. It departs from the standard VLLM paradigm of sparse uniform frame sampling followed by full-image patch tokenization (as in CLIP/SigLIP-based pipelines) by introducing a gated, codec-style inter-tokenization that filters static patches before embedding. The benchmark construction draws on existing datasets (LPM for educational videos, EgoDex for high-motion clips) but reframes them around dense frame-aligned QA, contrasting with prior long-video benchmarks (MLVU, VideoMME) that tolerate coarse temporal sampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a blind spot in current VLLM evaluation: existing benchmarks can be solved with as few as 6 frames, masking the inability of models to perform frame-by-frame reasoning. DIVE-Bench provides the first stress test for dense temporal perception, and the codec-style GRT approach offers a practical, training-free path toward sub-linear tokenization cost at high FPS. However, the benchmark currently covers only two dense-signal types (subtitle OCR and grid-based palm tracking), and absolute performance remains poor (MOS ~2.5/5), leaving open whether the approach generalizes to real-time streams, medical/surgical video, or more diverse dense-reasoning scenarios. A key future direction is leveraging actual codec priors (motion vectors, residual streams) rather than re-deriving them via SSIM.
