## Problem

Landslides are geomorphological phenomena whose spatial extent is governed by slope continuity and elevation discontinuities, yet most existing segmentation methods treat them as generic appearance-defined regions. When Digital Elevation Models are incorporated, they are typically fused via simple channel concatenation rather than used as structural constraints that guide spatial reasoning, leading to fragmented predictions and boundary leakage in visually ambiguous terrain.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262572/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a high-resolution RGB image (processed at 64, 128, and 256 spatial scales) and an aligned DEM as input, producing a full-resolution pixel-level landslide mask. When no ground-truth DEM is available (e.g., the Luding dataset), the terrain branch substitutes monocular depth estimates from Depth Anything V2 to derive the slope map without modifying the network architecture. All feature maps are spatially aligned to a common resolution H'×W' before joint processing.

## Method

TerrainGraphNet reformulates landslide segmentation as a terrain-conditioned structured prediction problem through three stages. First, a dual-stream visual encoder (frozen SAM for global context plus a lightweight multi-scale CNN for local texture) is paired with a terrain encoder that computes explicit slope magnitude from the DEM. Second, a Terrain-Modulated Feature Interaction module uses sigmoid gating maps to adaptively regulate cross-modal influence—terrain features amplify visual embeddings in steep regions while visual features reinforce terrain embeddings in visually distinctive areas. Third, a Terrain-Aware Graph Reasoning module constructs a sparse k-NN graph where node affinity is defined jointly by learned feature similarity and slope continuity (Eq. 9), then propagates information via graph attention so that predictions diffuse along terrain-consistent regions while being suppressed across ridges and valleys. The residual graph output is decoded by a hierarchical decoder with skip connections, trained with a weighted Dice+BCE loss plus auxiliary supervision at intermediate stages.

## Results

On the Bijie dataset, TerrainGraphNet achieves an IoU of 79.08, F1 of 88.32, and mIoU of 88.26, outperforming the strongest baseline FFS-Net (IoU 77.19, F1 87.12) by 1.89 and 1.20 points respectively. It also attains the best boundary F1 (57.31 vs. 52.22) and lowest connectivity error (0.0172 vs. 0.0474). On Landslide4Sense, the method reaches IoU 54.20 and mIoU 76.39, and on Luding (using estimated depth from Depth Anything V2), it achieves IoU 78.92 and F1 88.22, consistently leading all 12 baselines across all three datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TerrainGraphNet outperforms all 12 baselines on every dataset and every reported metric. The strongest competitor, FFS-Net [28], is surpassed by +1.89 IoU, +1.20 F1, and +5.09 BF1 on Bijie, and by +2.83 IoU and +1.80 F1 on Luding; however, on Landslide4Sense FFS-Net edges out TerrainGraphNet on BF1 (65.46 vs. 63.49) and CE (0.1772 vs. 0.3169), though TerrainGraphNet still leads on IoU (54.20 vs. 53.84) and mIoU (76.39 vs. 76.22). Transformer-based baselines (TransUNet, SegFormer) and Mamba-based SegMamba trail by 9–19 IoU points on Bijie, reflecting their lack of explicit terrain constraints. Landslide-specific methods (OLSNet, CalandNet, SCGC-Net, Swin-MA, LandSegNet) cluster in the 64–70 IoU range on Bijie, roughly 9–15 points below TerrainGraphNet. The gap widens on boundary-sensitive metrics: TerrainGraphNet's BF1 of 57.31 exceeds the next-best (FFS-Net, 52.22) by over 5 points, and its connectivity error (0.0172) is roughly 2.8× lower than FFS-Net's (0.0474), confirming that terrain-aware graph propagation specifically improves structural coherence rather than merely pixel overlap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two established lines of work: (1) multi-modal landslide segmentation methods that fuse DEM data via simple channel concatenation or uniform attention, and (2) graph-based segmentation approaches that construct spatial connectivity based solely on feature similarity or pixel proximity. By reformulating landslide segmentation as a terrain-conditioned structured prediction problem, the authors repurpose standard GAT propagation and SAM-based encoding under a new constraint: graph topology is defined jointly by learned feature similarity and slope continuity derived from elevation data. The terrain-modulated gating mechanism for cross-modal fusion is a lightweight departure from prior adaptive fusion strategies, but the core novelty lies in making terrain an explicit structural constraint on spatial reasoning rather than an additional appearance channel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The terrain-constrained graph formulation addresses a concrete failure mode in landslide mapping—fragmented predictions and boundary leakage in visually ambiguous regions—by enforcing geomorphological coherence during spatial propagation. The demonstration that estimated depth (Depth Anything V2) can substitute for real DEMs on the Luding dataset broadens practical applicability to settings where elevation data is unavailable. Open questions include whether this terrain-aware graph construction generalizes to other geomorphological segmentation tasks (flood extent, erosion patterns), whether the top-k sparse graph scales efficiently to very large scenes, and whether richer terrain descriptors (aspect, curvature) could further improve performance in specific terrain regimes.
