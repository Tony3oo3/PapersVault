## Problem

Existing low-light image enhancement methods apply a globally uniform enhancement strategy across the entire image, but real-world degradations are spatially heterogeneous—mildly under-exposed regions are easy to recover while severely dark, noisy, or chromatically unstable areas remain challenging. This uniformity leads to compromised trade-offs such as over-smoothing textures in hard regions or over-enhancement in easier areas. The paper addresses this gap by explicitly modeling per-region restoration difficulty and adaptively allocating enhancement capacity accordingly.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single low-light RGB image (trained on 256×256 crops) and outputs an enhanced well-exposed image. Internally, the network operates in the HVI color space, decomposing the problem into an intensity channel and a hue-value (HV) channel processed by separate branches. At inference, no ground truth or residual map is required; the difficulty prior is synthesized entirely from the single low-light input via a latent conditional diffusion model, enabling fully residual-free enhancement.

## Method

DCASR employs a dual-branch backbone in HVI space where a Global Intensity Modulator (GIM) applies channel-wise affine modulation (gain and bias) to the intensity branch for globally coherent brightness correction, while a Structural Refinement Modulator (SRM) uses DCT-based high-frequency analysis of the difficulty prior to sharpen spatial attention in the HV branch for detail-sensitive refinement. An Adaptive Prior Weighting (APW) loss reweights the reconstruction objective toward intrinsically difficult regions via a learnable scalar controlling the transition from uniform to hard-example-minimized supervision. To make the difficulty prior available at test time, a two-stage teacher-student strategy is used: a privileged teacher extracts an oracle prior from residual cues (|I_gt − Φ_llie(I_low)|) during training, and a latent conditional diffusion student is then trained to generate aligned priors from the low-light input alone, with a prior alignment loss (L1 between generated and oracle priors) ensuring fidelity. The full system is trained in two 500-epoch stages—first jointly optimizing the enhancement network and teacher, then fine-tuning with the student diffusion model and APW loss—using a total of 6.70M parameters.

## Results

On the LOL benchmark, DCASR achieves the highest PSNR across all three subsets: 24.04 dB on LOL-v1, 24.05 dB on LOL-v2-Real, and 25.61 dB on LOL-v2-Synthetic, surpassing the primary baseline CIDNet by 0.23, 0.15, and 0.48 dB respectively. DCASR also attains the best LPIPS on LOL-v1 (0.082) and LOL-v2-Real (0.109), while matching CIDNet on LOL-v2-Synthetic LPIPS (0.045). On unpaired real-world datasets, DCASR achieves the lowest mean NIQE of 3.76, outperforming CIDNet (4.06) and URetinex (4.12). In the cross-dataset robustness test (trained on LOL-v1, tested on LOL-v2-Synthetic), DCASR leads all supervised methods with 18.79 dB PSNR, 0.814 SSIM, and 0.217 LPIPS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DCASR consistently outperforms CIDNet [53], its primary architectural baseline, on PSNR across all three LOL subsets (+0.23 dB on LOL-v1, +0.15 dB on LOL-v2-Real, +0.48 dB on LOL-v2-Synthetic) and on LPIPS for LOL-v1 (0.082 vs. 0.086) and LOL-v2-Real (0.109 vs. 0.122), though CIDNet retains a slight SSIM edge on LOL-v1 (0.857 vs. 0.850). Against other strong baselines, DCASR surpasses CWNet [60] by 0.44 dB PSNR on LOL-v1 and 2.41 dB on LOL-v2-Real, and outperforms URetinex++ [48] by 0.21 dB on LOL-v1 and 6.62 dB on LOL-v2-Synthetic. In the cross-dataset robustness test (Table 2), DCASR leads CIDNet by 1.14 dB PSNR and LLFlow [44] by 1.67 dB, while also achieving the best SSIM (0.814) and LPIPS (0.217) among all methods. On unpaired real-world datasets (Table 3), DCASR attains the lowest mean NIQE (3.76), beating CIDNet (4.06) by 0.30 and URetinex [47] (4.12) by 0.36, with the largest margin on MEF (3.37 vs. 3.60 for CIDNet). The gains are most pronounced on LOL-v2-Synthetic and in the cross-dataset setting, suggesting the difficulty-conditioned mechanism is particularly effective under severe or out-of-distribution degradations.

## Relation to Prior Work

DCASR builds directly on CIDNet's HVI color-space decomposition and dual-branch architecture, extending it with difficulty-conditioned modulation in each branch. The generative student prior model repurposes the DDPM forward/reverse diffusion process and DiffIR's latent-space efficiency to synthesize difficulty priors from a single low-light input, a task not previously addressed in LLIE. Conceptually, the paper imports the difficulty-guided learning paradigm from semantic segmentation, medical imaging, and blind super-resolution into low-light enhancement, where spatially heterogeneous degradation has been acknowledged but not explicitly modeled. The adaptive prior-weighted loss and DCT-based structural gating are new mechanisms that go beyond the globally uniform enhancement strategy shared by most prior regression- and generation-based LLIE methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The difficulty-conditioned framing offers a principled way to allocate restoration capacity where it is most needed, addressing a recognized but underexploited limitation of uniform-mapping LLIE. However, the practical gains over the strong CIDNet baseline are modest (≈0.2–0.5 dB PSNR), and the added complexity of a two-stage teacher-student diffusion pipeline (6.7M params, 0.148 s/image) raises questions about whether simpler prior predictors could capture most of the benefit. Open questions include whether difficulty-conditioning transfers to other restoration tasks (dehazing, deblurring), whether the latent diffusion prior is truly necessary versus a well-designed deterministic regressor, and how the framework scales to higher resolutions beyond the 256×256 crops used in evaluation.
