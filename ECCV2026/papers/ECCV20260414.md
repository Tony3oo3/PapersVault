## Problem

Fixed, heuristically designed visual prompts for LVLMs saturate at benchmark-specific ceilings and cannot adapt to the instance-dependent nature of optimal visual cues. The natural remedy—retrieving the best prompt per query-image pair—requires prompt-level supervision, yet prompt quality is inherently ambiguous and difficult to assess even for humans, making reliable annotation infeasible.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260414/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

AutoV takes an input RGB image and a free-form textual query, along with a pool of N (≤8) pre-generated visual prompt candidates (e.g., attention heatmaps from different LLM layers, red circles, blur-reversal masks), each at the same resolution as the input. The ranking network consumes visual tokens (L×D, projected into the LVLM's word-embedding space) and text tokens, outputting a scalar reward per candidate; the highest-scoring prompt is selected and its visual tokens are concatenated with text embeddings for LLM decoding to produce the final answer.

## Method

AutoV is a lightweight ranking network that scores visual prompt candidates via a three-stage pipeline: (1) a frozen CLIP encoder and LVLM projection layer convert each prompt image into visual tokens; (2) the first layer of the LLM decoder performs modality interaction between visual and text tokens, followed by single-FFN vision and text mapping modules that compress features to a low-dimensional space (h≪D); (3) a cross-attention operation between the compressed visual and text features yields a scalar reward score. Training uses a pairwise reward loss (analogous to RLHF preference modeling) where supervision is fully automated: each prompt-query pair is scored by a pretrained LVLM's conditional language-modeling loss, and lower-loss prompts are designated 'chosen' over higher-loss 'rejected' counterparts. A pre-filtering step at inference removes the most cosine-dissimilar candidate to mitigate distributional bias, and the entire ranking network comprises only ~2h(D+h+2) trainable parameters, requiring no fine-tuning of the backbone LVLM.

## Results

AutoV achieves an average improvement of 3.5% over base models across four LVLM families and seven benchmarks. The most striking gain is +10.2% on VizWiz for LLaVA-OneVision (58.2→68.4), and +3.8% on MMMU for Qwen2.5-VL (50.1→53.9). On LLaVA-1.5-7B, AutoV improves over the base by +2.4 on MMMU, +2.1 on VizWiz, +2.3 on MMVet, +2.4 on TextVQA, +3.2 on LLaVA-Wild, +2.0 on MMStar, and +2.7 on MathVista. The method also transfers to closed-source models, boosting Gemini-1.5-Pro by +9.6 on VizWiz and GPT-4o by +9.0 on VizWiz without any retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AutoV consistently outperforms all three visual prompt engineering baselines (FGVP, RedCircle, and API) across every model and benchmark in Table 1. FGVP is the weakest baseline, frequently producing large negative gains (e.g., −11.9 on TextVQA for LLaVA-7B, −13.5 on MMVet for Qwen2.5-VL), indicating that its blur-reversal prompts are harmful for many query types. RedCircle is more stable but still inferior to AutoV on nearly all metrics; for instance, on LLaVA-OneVision VizWiz, Circle reaches 60.2 versus AutoV's 68.4. API (attention prompting) is the strongest single-prompt baseline, achieving +1.4 on MMMU and +8.7 on VizWiz for LLaVA-OneVision, yet AutoV extends these to +4.6 and +10.2 respectively, yielding a 2.1–3.2 point absolute margin. The paper also shows in Table 2 that AutoV's pair-wise ranking surpasses MoE gating (+1.1 vs +2.3 average) and list-wise ranking (+1.6 vs +2.3), confirming that the relative-preference formulation is critical for the observed gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the fixed-prompt engineering paradigm (FGVP, RedCircle, API) by reframing visual prompting as an instance-adaptive retrieval problem: rather than designing a single universally optimal prompt, it selects the best candidate from a diverse pool given a specific image-query pair. Conceptually, it repurposes reward modeling from RLHF (pairwise preference optimization) and learning-to-rank from information retrieval to supervise a lightweight ranking network, while inheriting the visual encoder and LLM decoder layers directly from a pretrained LVLM without fine-tuning. It also contrasts with MoE-style visual gating (e.g., GateNet) by arguing that explicit pairwise preference learning outperforms soft differentiable selection, and with retrieval-augmented LVLMs by operating entirely within a compact in-model candidate pool rather than retrieving external visual data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AutoV offers a practical, plug-and-play improvement for existing LVLMs without backbone retraining, and its demonstrated transferability across open-source architectures and even closed-source models (GPT-4o, Gemini) suggests the learned prompt-selection policy captures a model-agnostic visual cue preference. However, the supervision signal relies on the LVLM's own prediction loss as a proxy for prompt quality, which may conflate true visual grounding with language priors—a concern the authors partially address via variance filtering but do not fully resolve. Open questions include whether the candidate pool can be expanded beyond ~8 prompts without diminishing returns, whether the loss-oriented ranking generalizes to video or multi-turn settings, and whether the approach remains effective when the backbone LVLM is significantly weaker than the one used for supervision.
