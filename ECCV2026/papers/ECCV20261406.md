## Problem

Estimating the 3D translational and angular velocity of fast-moving spherical objects from rolling-shutter imagery remains unsolved: existing rolling-shutter motion methods require explicit 3D-2D correspondences (difficult for textureless or repetitive-pattern spheres), rely on straight-line features absent on spheres, or jointly optimize many coupled variables in a highly non-convex landscape. No dedicated formulation robustly recovers full 6-DoF spherical motion directly from the structured distortions that rolling-shutter readout naturally produces.

## Input / Output

The method consumes one or more rolling-shutter RGB images (2048×2048 in simulation; 6.5 MP in the real prototype), camera intrinsics, the known sphere radius, and the per-row readout time τ (calibrated with a flashing LED). For spin estimation, the sphere must carry a designed icosahedron-based surface pattern of 20 equilateral-triangle dot groups; for translation, only the silhouette contour is needed, making the method applicable to textureless spheres. The output is the object's 3D position t_co, translational velocity v_co, and angular velocity ω_co, all expressed in the camera frame.

## Method

The core idea is to back-project 2D image measurements into 3D rays in the object coordinate frame and temporally align all rays to a common reference timestamp using the rolling-shutter timing model Δt(u) = k_s + τ·u_y, thereby converting the implicit forward-projection problem into an explicit geometric consistency constraint. The estimation is decomposed into two decoupled stages: (1) translational velocity is recovered from silhouette contour points by finding the closest point on each back-projected ray to the sphere center and minimizing the deviation of those points from the known sphere radius, augmented with a spread regularization to prevent degenerate collapse, optimizing 6 parameters (t_co, v_co) via L-BFGS-B; (2) angular velocity is then estimated from the designed surface pattern by back-projecting detected feature points to their first sphere intersection and minimizing the variance of pairwise distances among equidistant point pairs, optimizing only 3 parameters (ω_co). The framework extends to multi-camera settings by summing per-image objectives and to multi-frame settings by adding a truncated Chamfer-distance refinement loss that enforces temporal consistency of back-projected surface points across frames with inter-frame delay.

## Results

On synthetic data (100 trials, 0–50 m/s, 0–5000 rpm), the proposed method achieves translational velocity MAE of 0.23/4.61/2.95 m/s (x/y/z) in the single-image setting, improving to 0.19/0.10/0.26 m/s with stereo + multi-frame. Angular velocity MAE drops from 49.82/17.18/27.89 rad/s (single image) to 10.39/3.93/2.68 rad/s with stereo + multi-frame + spin refinement. In the real-world golf launch-monitor evaluation against SkyTrak, the system reports speed MAE of 2.4 mph, launch-angle MAE of 1.0°, and backspin MAE of 107 rpm (excluding one outlier shot).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Ait-Aider et al. [1] (which requires ground-truth 3D-2D correspondences), the proposed correspondence-free method achieves substantially lower MAE in every scenario: e.g., in the single-image setting, translational velocity error drops from 3.24/10.14/14.33 m/s to 0.23/4.61/2.95 m/s, and angular velocity error drops from 164.16/172.60/138.25 rad/s to 49.82/17.18/27.89 rad/s. The advantage widens further with additional views and frames, where the proposed method reaches 0.19/0.10/0.26 m/s (translation) and 10.39/3.93/2.68 rad/s (spin with refinement) versus 2.76/1.16/9.84 m/s and 153.16/151.77/114.63 rad/s for Ait-Aider. The global-shutter PnP baseline, which ignores rolling-shutter distortion, performs poorly in multi-frame settings (e.g., 131.00/126.50/120.88 rad/s angular error), confirming that RS distortion must be explicitly modeled. In the real-world golf comparison, the system matches SkyTrak within 2.4 mph on speed and 1.0° on launch angle across 10 shots; spin agreement is strong for 9 of 10 shots (backspin MAE 107 rpm, sidespin MAE 247 rpm excluding one outlier), with the outlier attributed to SkyTrak's reliance on consecutive global-shutter frames rather than rolling-shutter temporal cues.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant rolling-shutter literature, which treats sequential-scanline distortion as an artifact to be corrected (Baker et al., Grundmann et al.) or models it within pose/bundle-adjustment pipelines (Albl et al., Hedborg et al.). It also diverges from prior RS motion-estimation work (Ait-Aider et al. 2006, Magerand et al. 2012) that requires explicit 3D-2D correspondences or straight-line features—both impractical for fast, textureless spheres. The key conceptual move is a correspondence-free back-projection formulation that exploits row-dependent capture timing as a temporal cue, combined with a two-stage optimization that decouples translation (via sphere-silhouette geometry) from rotation (via a designed icosahedron-based surface pattern), reducing the jointly optimized parameter count from 12 to 6 per stage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for sports and industrial applications: a pair of commodity 6.5 MP rolling-shutter cameras can approximate a commercial launch monitor (SkyTrak) at a fraction of the cost, with speed MAE of 2.4 mph and launch-angle MAE of 1.0° over 10 golf shots. However, the method is tightly scoped—it requires a known sphere radius, a pre-printed icosahedron pattern, and a constant-velocity assumption, limiting applicability to non-spherical objects or time-varying motion. Open questions include whether the back-projection framework generalizes to ellipsoids or arbitrary rigid bodies, whether the pattern requirement can be relaxed via learned feature detection, and whether the constant-velocity model degrades gracefully under acceleration (e.g., Magnus-effect curves in flight).
