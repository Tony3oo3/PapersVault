## Problem

Appearance-based gaze estimation models suffer severe cross-domain degradation because they overfit to source-domain low-frequency components that encode domain-specific factors such as illumination, background, and skin tone. Existing domain generalization methods operate entirely in the spatial domain and cannot directly target these low-frequency components. The paper further shows that simply removing low-frequency components causes performance collapse, so the correct strategy is perturbation rather than removal.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260060/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB face image resized to 224×224 and normalized to [0,1] as input, and outputs a 2D gaze direction vector (pitch, yaw) in degrees. During training, each source image is additionally augmented via frequency-domain mixing to produce a second input with perturbed low-frequency amplitude statistics, but the original gaze label remains valid for both. At inference, PGNet is discarded entirely and only GazeNet processes the raw face image with no frequency-domain operations, introducing zero additional computational overhead.

## Method

ALFP consists of two adversarially trained networks: GazeNet (ResNet backbone + MLP regression head) for gaze prediction, and PGNet (a four-block convolutional upsampler) that generates instance-specific perturbation images in the bounded spatial domain [0,1], conditioned on GazeNet's intermediate features and random noise. The low-frequency amplitude statistics of each perturbation image are mixed with those of the source image via AdaIN-style statistics transfer within a circular low-frequency region (radius β·min(H,W)/2, β∈(0,0.5]), preserving the original phase spectrum and relative spectral structure. GazeNet is trained with three losses (original gaze, augmented gaze, and clean-augmented consistency), while PGNet is adversarially optimized to maximize GazeNet's error on augmented images, regularized by a batch-level log-space statistical constraint that prevents extreme amplitude shortcuts. This adversarial loop progressively reduces GazeNet's reliance on source-domain low-frequency patterns while retaining the moderate low-frequency response needed for accurate gaze prediction.

## Results

ALFP reduces average cross-domain angular error from 8.54° (ResNet-18 baseline) to 6.41° (ResNet-18+ALFP) and from 8.08° (ResNet-50 baseline) to 6.15° (ResNet-50+ALFP) across four source-to-target settings. The largest single-setting gain is 34.9% on D_E→D_D (9.77°→6.36°), and the best improvement over the strongest prior method is 15.0% on D_G→D_M (6.83°→5.84°). ALFP with ResNet-50 achieves the lowest error on three of four settings and the lowest average, while ResNet-18+ALFP already outperforms all prior methods on D_E→D_D, D_G→D_M, and the overall average.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ALFP (ResNet-18, avg 6.41°) outperforms all seven compared methods on average angular error. Against the strongest spatial-domain baselines FSCI (6.95°) and GFAL (6.81°), ALFP wins on three of four settings but trails on D_E→D_M (5.92° vs. 5.79°/5.72°), where those methods' causal or frontalization strategies appear to handle that particular domain shift better. Against the VLM-based methods CLIP-Gaze (6.97°) and LG-Gaze (6.84°), ALFP wins on D_E→D_M, D_E→D_D, and D_G→D_M but loses on D_G→D_D (7.26° vs. 7.06°/6.86°), suggesting language priors help in that specific setting. Against PureGaze (8.29°), CDG (7.25°), and Xu et al. (7.63°), ALFP achieves consistent wins across all four settings. With the ResNet-50 backbone, ALFP narrows its D_E→D_M gap to 5.68° (still slightly behind GFAL's 5.72°) and achieves the best D_G→D_D result (7.19°), closing the remaining weakness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends cross-domain gaze estimation from the spatial domain into the frequency domain, building on the AdaIN statistics-transfer mechanism (Huang & Belongie, ICCV 2017) originally designed for spatial style transfer and repurposing it to mix low-frequency amplitude statistics in the Fourier domain. Prior domain-generalization methods for gaze (PureGaze, FSCI, GFAL, CLIP-Gaze, LG-Gaze) all operate on spatial features—adversarial alignment, contrastive regression, causal de-confounding, or language priors—without explicitly characterizing domain shift as a low-frequency amplitude distribution shift. The adversarial PGNet–GazeNet loop draws on GAN-style min-max training but is novel in generating instance-specific perturbation images whose sole purpose is to supply challenging low-frequency statistics for AdaIN mixing, rather than to produce realistic images.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, target-data-free recipe (ALFP) that improves cross-domain gaze estimation by up to 34.9% over a ResNet-18 baseline and 15.0% over the best prior method, with zero inference overhead, making it directly deployable in unconstrained AR/VR and HCI settings. The diagnostic finding that low-frequency components must be perturbed rather than removed is a useful design principle for any frequency-aware domain-generalization method. However, the authors acknowledge that ALFP does not address spatial-domain shifts such as head-pose distribution bias, gaze-range mismatch, or occlusion, leaving open the question of how to jointly handle frequency and spatial domain gaps. Whether the frequency-perturbation idea transfers to other appearance-based tasks (e.g., face recognition, expression recognition) remains unexplored.
