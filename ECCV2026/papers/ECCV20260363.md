## Problem

Diffusion-based policies for physics-based character control produce rich, expressive motions but rely on gradient-based test-time guidance to satisfy task objectives, which is computationally expensive, requires differentiable reward functions, and reduces robustness. Because these policies are trained purely offline without environment interaction, they struggle with contact-rich dynamics, uneven terrain, and non-differentiable task constraints. The paper asks how to leverage an expressive diffusion prior for fast, robust, task-specific whole-body control without iterative inference-time optimization.

## Input / Output

The RL navigation policy receives a compact proprioceptive state (canonicalized root position, 6D orientation, linear/angular velocities, and per-joint 6D rotations and angular velocities — excluding redundant local joint positions and linear velocities) plus task-specific environment state (e.g., goal position, target velocity) and, for terrain tasks, a 4×4 m heightmap at 12.5 cm resolution centered on the agent. The policy outputs a single latent noise vector that is duplicated into a k-step chunk (k=4 for terrain/agile tasks, k=8 for flat ground), denoised via 5-step DDIM-ODE through the frozen diffusion prior, and decoded by PULSE's frozen action decoder into joint-level target positions. These positions are converted to torques via a PD controller and executed open-loop at 30 Hz on an SMPL-like 24-joint character in Isaac Gym.

## Method

NaP-Control freezes a pretrained causal-transformer diffusion prior (12 layers, 768-dim hidden, 8 heads) trained on AMASS motion data to model the joint state-latent-action distribution, and trains a lightweight PPO policy to predict the initial diffusion noise that steers the prior toward task-specific behaviors. The key mechanism is treating the diffusion prior's noise space as a controllable search manifold: the RL policy outputs a noise vector, which is repeated into a k-step chunk, denoised via DDIM-ODE into latent action chunks, and decoded to joint positions — all executed open-loop in the physics simulator. The entire diffusion prior and action decoder are encapsulated as part of the MDP environment, so the RL agent learns through closed-loop interaction with the simulator, accumulating chunk-level rewards with a discount factor and updating via PPO with GAE. This eliminates both the need for extensive reward engineering (the diffusion prior regularizes motion naturalness) and iterative gradient-based guidance at inference, yielding 22.5 FPS inference versus 2.9 FPS for UniPhys while achieving higher success rates across far-goal reaching, agile hand reaching, velocity control, object interaction, and unseen uneven terrain.

## Results

On flat-ground far goal reaching, NaP-Control achieves a 98.4% success rate with 1266 m/s³ jerk, outperforming UniPhys (81.9% success, 528 m/s³ jerk) in success while running 7.8× faster at inference (22.5 vs. 2.9 FPS). For agile hand reaching, NaP attains 99.1% success with 1146 m/s³ jerk, substantially smoother than PULSE (3631 m/s³) and MaskedMimic (2750 m/s³). In velocity control, NaP achieves a velocity error of 0.1753 m/s with 752 m/s³ jerk, bridging the gap between UniPhys's smoothness (401 m/s³) but high error (0.7971 m/s) and PULSE's low error (0.0455 m/s) but high jerk (2273 m/s³). On object interaction (sofa sitting), NaP reaches 97.9% success with 491 m/s³ jerk, the lowest jerk among all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against UniPhys (the closest diffusion-based baseline using test-time gradient guidance), NaP wins decisively on success rate (98.4% vs. 81.9% for far goal reaching; 99.0% vs. 92.1% for velocity control) and inference speed (22.5 vs. 2.9 FPS; 24.4 vs. 5.2 FPS), though UniPhys achieves lower jerk on far goal reaching (528 vs. 1266 m/s³) and velocity control (401 vs. 752 m/s³). Against PULSE (VAE-based RL prior), NaP produces substantially smoother motion across all tasks (e.g., 1146 vs. 3631 m/s³ for hand reaching; 491 vs. 1373 m/s³ for object interaction) while maintaining comparable success rates. Against MaskedMimic, NaP wins by a wide margin on success rate in every task (e.g., 98.4% vs. 30.4% for far goal; 97.9% vs. 29.8% for object interaction). Against CLoSD, NaP matches success on far goal reaching (98.4% vs. 99.8%) but outperforms it on object interaction (97.9% vs. 86.2%) and supports tasks CLoSD cannot handle (hand reaching, velocity control). Against task-specific adversarial baselines (AMP, CML, AdaptNet) on far goal reaching, NaP achieves the lowest jerk (1266 vs. 2387/2185/2444 m/s³) and highest success rate (98.4% vs. 96.7%/96.3%/86.8%) with fewer training samples than AMP.

## Relation to Prior Work

NaP-Control sits at the intersection of three research threads: diffusion-based action priors for character control (UniPhys, DiffuseCLoC), latent action representations (PULSE), and RL-based steering of diffusion models (DSRL). The key conceptual departure is from prior diffusion-based control methods that rely on gradient-based test-time guidance to satisfy task objectives; NaP instead trains a compact RL policy to directly predict the initial diffusion noise, eliminating iterative optimization at inference. This extends DSRL's latent noise steering paradigm from low-dimensional robotic manipulation to high-dimensional, contact-rich physics-based whole-body control, where closed-loop environmental interaction is essential for stability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By removing test-time guidance, NaP achieves a ~7.7× inference speedup (22.5 vs. 2.9 FPS) while matching or exceeding success rates, making diffusion-based character control viable for real-time interactive applications. The framework also handles non-differentiable objectives and unseen terrains (via simple heightmap augmentation) in ways that gradient-based guidance cannot, broadening the range of deployable tasks. Open questions include whether the approach scales to more complex loco-manipulation, multi-agent settings, or tasks requiring longer horizons, and whether the frozen diffusion prior's motion manifold becomes a bottleneck for behaviors far from the AMASS distribution.
