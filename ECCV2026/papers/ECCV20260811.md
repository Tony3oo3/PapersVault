## Problem

Existing floor plan generation methods rely on discrete representations (raster masks, ordered vertex sequences, or graph topologies) that are brittle to coordinate perturbations and vertex-order variations, making unified generation and iterative editing difficult. Modifying one room in a layout often propagates structural changes to adjacent spaces, and no prior framework simultaneously supports controllable generation under boundary and room-type constraints with robust, localized editing within a single model.

## Input / Output

The system accepts natural-language room specifications (parsed by an LLM into structured JSON with room types and counts), an apartment boundary contour represented as 64 uniformly sampled 2D points, and optionally an existing polygonal layout with vertex-level precision for editing. It outputs explicit vectorized polygonal room layouts with clockwise-ordered vertices, along with intermediate Gaussian room embeddings (centroid μ_i and isotropic scale σ_i per room). During editing, anchor masks designate fixed regions whose vertices are hard-constrained by direct copy-back after each denoising step, while remaining rooms are resampled under the predicted GRE priors.

## Method

GRE-Diff introduces Gaussian Room Embeddings (GRE), where each room is modeled as a 2D isotropic Gaussian N(μ_i, σ_i²I) capturing its centroid and spatial extent, serving as a continuous room-level latent state that initializes the diffusion process instead of standard isotropic noise. The architecture comprises two networks: GuidanceNet, which fuses semantic tokens (room types/counts), boundary tokens (64-point contour), and polygon tokens (existing layout) through a dual-path encoder (global attention for semantic/polygon branches, local attention for boundary) followed by an autoregressive transformer that sequentially predicts (μ_i, σ_i) pairs; and DenoisingNet, a dual-attention transformer that decodes polygons via conditional diffusion with cross-attention to boundary features and self-attention for inter-room dependencies. A key design choice is the coefficient γ that modulates polygon-token contribution (γ=0 for generation, γ=1 for editing), enabling a single model to handle both tasks. Training uses supervised learning on 72,709 RPLAN samples for 300 epochs with AdamW (lr 2×10⁻⁴, cosine decay) on 8 GPUs, and the model achieves 60M parameters with 0.91s inference per layout.

## Results

On the RPLAN test set (8,079 layouts), GRE-Diff achieves the best distributional similarity across all three metrics: FID 4.36, KID 0.96×10⁻³, and MMD 0.107, outperforming the closest competitor GSDiff (FID 4.55) by 4.2%. It also attains the highest generative diversity (COV 96.09%) and the best controllability (RC 100.00%, F1_BC-RC 99.21%) while using only 60M parameters and generating a layout in 0.91 s. For editing, GRE-Diff achieves 0% Tiny-ROE (zero failed edits) and F1 scores of 86.27% (Add), 98.58% (Delete), and 97.59% (Anchor & Move), surpassing the best baseline (ChatHouseDiffusion) by 17.8, 40.3, and 24.5 points respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRE-Diff outperforms all seven baselines on FID, KID, MMD, COV, RC, and F1_BC-RC simultaneously. Against the closest competitor GSDiff (FID 4.55, KID 1.13), GRE-Diff improves FID by 4.2% and KID by 15%, while also reporting RC (100% vs. not reported) and F1 (99.21% vs. not reported). Compared to Graph2Plan (FID 4.81), GRE-Diff achieves lower FID and substantially higher RC (100% vs. 83.11%) and F1 (99.21% vs. 90.78%), though Graph2Plan retains a slight BC edge (100% vs. 98.44%) and is faster (0.41 s vs. 0.91 s) with fewer parameters (8M vs. 60M). Against ChatHouseDiffusion (FID 5.21), GRE-Diff improves FID by 16.3% and F1 by 29.2 points, and in editing tasks outperforms it by 17.8–40.3 F1 points across Add, Delete, and Anchor & Move operations. MaskPLAN and iPLAN are clearly dominated, with FID values of 23.54 and 12.63 respectively, and F1 scores below 61%. HouseDiffusion is the slowest (8.76 s) and does not support boundary conditioning, limiting direct comparability.

## Relation to Prior Work

GRE-Diff carves new ground by introducing a continuous probabilistic room representation (Gaussian Room Embedding) for floor plan diffusion, drawing conceptual inspiration from word-level Gaussian embeddings [33] and spatially disentangled scene representations (BlobGAN [8], BlobCtrl [18]), but applying them to architectural geometry for the first time. It departs from the dominant discrete representations in prior work—vertex sequences (HouseDiffusion, GSDiff), raster masks (MaskPLAN, WallPlan), and explicit graph topologies (Graph2Plan, HouseGAN++)—by operating in a compact per-room (μ, σ) latent space that initializes and guides the diffusion process. The unified generation-and-editing framework builds on the interactive editing paradigm of ChatHouseDiffusion and the partial-input setting of MaskPLAN, but replaces their mask- or prompt-based conditioning with a dual-path semantic-geometric encoder and autoregressive GRE prediction, enabling a single diffusion model to handle both from-scratch synthesis and localized refinement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a practical, end-to-end system that bridges natural-language/GUI interaction with vectorized floor plan output, addressing a real workflow gap for designers who need iterative, constraint-aware refinement rather than one-shot generation. Its 100% room-controllability rate and 99.21% F1 on RPLAN, combined with sub-second inference at 60M parameters, make it a competitive option for residential layout tools. However, the evaluation is confined to six room types on a single dataset (RPLAN) with predominantly rectilinear boundaries; the authors acknowledge degraded performance on non-orthogonal or highly irregular contours, and the extension to broader architectural typologies, multi-story plans, or furniture-level interior design remains open. Whether the isotropic Gaussian parameterization generalizes to elongated or L-shaped rooms without a full covariance model is an unresolved question.
