## Problem

Existing vision-language-action models implicitly assume gripper invariance, treating all end-effectors as interchangeable, yet different gripper types (parallel-jaw, suction, dexterous hand, soft, three-finger) require fundamentally distinct manipulation strategies for the same task objective. Compounding this, large-scale robotic datasets are overwhelmingly dominated by parallel-jaw grippers, leaving gripper diversity and strategy-level divergence underexplored and preventing VLAs from learning embodiment-conditioned policies.

## Input / Output

The model consumes multi-view RGB-D observations from wrist-mounted and third-view cameras, proprioceptive states, natural language task instructions, and a gripper configuration identifier. The gripper configuration is not fed as raw text or a fixed embedding but is encoded as three-level learnable soft prompt tokens (platform, type, instance) that are prepended to the observation embedding before entering the transformer backbone. The output is a continuous action sequence generated via conditional flow matching, producing executable end-effector trajectories over a fixed action horizon.

## Method

GVLA extends a pretrained VLA backbone (π0 or π0.5) with two gripper-aware components. First, a multi-gripper tokenizer factorises embodiment conditioning into three granularity levels—platform-specific, gripper-type-specific, and instance-specific learnable embeddings—concatenated in a fixed order and prepended to the observation, enabling structured sharing of knowledge across grippers while preserving type-level priors. Second, a dual Mixture-of-Adapters (MoA) is inserted at the final transformer layer (identified via layer-probing analysis showing peak gripper-type sensitivity), where two parallel routers (platform-aware and gripper-aware) each select top-k bottleneck adapters from type-stratified expert pools and apply them as additive residual connections to modulate action generation. Training jointly optimises a conditional flow-matching action loss, an auxiliary gripper-type classification loss to enforce discriminative representations in shared embeddings, and a load-balance regulariser to prevent router collapse across adapter pools.

## Results

On the MiGA benchmark across four task categories (Flat, Stacked, Constrained, Semantic), GVLA with the π0.5 backbone achieves 66.00% average success rate, outperforming the π0.5 baseline (58.38%) by 7.62 percentage points. GVLA with the π0 backbone reaches 48.13%, surpassing the vanilla π0 (38.13%) by 10.00 points. In the tokenizer comparison, GVLA achieves the lowest prediction error (PE = 0.032) and highest Counterfactual Action Prediction Divergence (CAPD = 1.34) and Gripper Contribution Score (GCS = 0.249), indicating both superior action accuracy and stronger gripper-specific behavioral specialization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GVLA (π0.5 backbone) outperforms all seven task-level baselines, with the largest margin over the strongest VLA baseline π0.5 (66.00% vs. 58.38%, +7.62 pp) and a substantial lead over OpenVLA-OFT (66.00% vs. 41.88%, +24.12 pp). Traditional two-stage grasp detectors (AnyGrasp at 11.50%, GraspMAS at 12.00%) and the synthetic-data-pretrained GraspVLA (7.88%) collapse on Flat and Stacked scenes, confirming that gripper-agnostic grasp pose prediction cannot handle morphology-dependent strategies. In the tokenizer ablation (Table 3), GVLA's multi-gripper tokenization dominates all three alternatives: it reduces prediction error from 0.051–0.053 to 0.032, increases CAPD from 0.64–0.82 to 1.34, and raises GCS from 0.002–0.225 to 0.249, showing that MLP, VQ-VAE, and language-prompt encodings fail to produce structured gripper-discriminative representations. The component ablation further confirms that removing the dual MoA drops adaptation success from 0.92 to 0.52, and removing the platform token P^(p) drops it to 0.54, validating that both representation-level prompting and computation-level routing are necessary for cross-gripper transfer.

## Relation to Prior Work

GVLA extends the π0/π0.5 flow-matching VLA backbone by inserting gripper-aware conditioning at two levels: a multi-granularity soft-prompt tokenizer (platform, type, instance) that departs from prior single-level gripper encodings (MLP, VQ-VAE, language prompts) shown to fail at capturing morphological structure, and a dual Mixture-of-Adapters adapted from AdaMIX [60] to route action generation through gripper-specific expert pools. Conceptually, the paper positions itself against two lines of work: (i) gripper representation research (geometry matching, diffusion-based synthesis, contact-centric encodings) that targets grasp pose transfer among morphologically similar end-effectors but overlooks strategy-level divergence, and (ii) VLA datasets (Open X-Embodiment, DROID, Bridge V2) that are overwhelmingly parallel-jaw-dominated and thus cannot teach gripper-conditioned policies. The MiGA dataset fills the data gap by explicitly pairing identical task objectives with qualitatively different strategies across five gripper types.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical and underexplored gap: real robotic deployments use diverse grippers (suction, soft, dexterous) that require fundamentally different manipulation strategies for the same task, yet current VLAs and their training data implicitly assume gripper invariance. The MiGA dataset (103K demos, 5 gripper types, 132 gripper-strategy pairs) is the first to explicitly encode this strategy divergence, making it a valuable resource for the community beyond this specific model. Key open questions remain: the simulation environment (Isaac Lab) has limitations for soft and multi-DoF grippers, the framework lacks explicit kinematic or contact modeling (limiting intra-type precision), and gripper-visual representations remain partially entangled under domain shift. Scaling to more gripper morphologies and incorporating richer geometric priors for instance-level contact planning are natural next steps.
