## Problem

Rubrics-as-Rewards (RaR) requires an ideal reference answer to synthesize evaluation criteria, but many real-world generative tasks—virtual try-on, embodied control, open-ended design—admit multiple valid outputs where quality is defined by the absence of errors rather than resemblance to a single ground truth. This 'reference-free' setting leaves a gap: rubrics become either too generic or too specific when no ideal answer anchors them, and the paper shows RaR can actually underperform naive direct scoring in this regime. The paper proposes Implicit Error Counting (IEC), which inverts evaluation from 'what does the output get right?' to 'what specific errors does it exhibit?', and validates it on virtual try-on.

## Input / Output

The model takes four conditioning inputs: a source person image, a reference garment image (either a flat lay-flat product photo or a non-flat image of the garment worn by a model), an inpainting mask, and a pose image, all at 512×512 resolution. It produces a single virtual try-on image where the reference garment appears on the source person while preserving identity, pose, and background. The reference garment crop is aligned following DiT-VTON conventions, and the non-flat setting additionally requires the model to 'extract' the garment from arbitrary poses and backgrounds, making it substantially harder than flat-reference transfer.

## Method

IEC uses a multimodal judge (GPT-5-mini) that internally enumerates errors across five task-relevant axes—garment transfer, attribute preservation, realism, lighting consistency, and source integrity—and emits a calibrated per-axis score in [0,1] rather than an explicit error list, which the paper shows is too noisy for stable optimization. A group calibration step (MAD-based z-score followed by a logistic squash) reduces prompt-to-prompt reward scale drift while preserving within-group ordering. These per-candidate rewards drive GRPO post-training (K=12 candidates, 60 steps) on a 12B rectified-flow backbone (Kontext-dev) using the DiT-VTON token-concatenation scheme, with no additional paired supervision. The key design insight is that implicit score emission—letting the judge aggregate errors internally rather than parsing surface-form labels—eliminates the high-variance enumeration noise that causes explicit error counting to regress during training, and the approach requires only one judge call per candidate versus two for RaR.

## Results

On MDressBench (flat references), IEC achieves the best CEC score of 5.312 (lower is better), outperforming RaR (5.596), Direct (5.373), and the SFT baseline (6.018). On the harder non-flat subset, IEC reaches CEC 5.203 vs. RaR's 5.533, with relative improvements of 5.96% (CEC), 3.48% (attribute preservation), and 4.33% (realism) over RaR. On standard VITON-HD and DressCode test splits, IEC attains the best LPIPS (0.047), SSIM (0.892), and FID (5.120) on VITON-HD, and best FID (3.610) with tied-best SSIM (0.927) on DressCode, winning 6 of 8 perceptual metrics against six external SFT baselines using only 60 RL steps and no additional paired data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against RaR [12], IEC wins on all eight metrics under both flat and non-flat references; notably, RaR actually underperforms Direct scoring on CEC (5.60 vs. 5.37 flat; 5.53 vs. 5.28 non-flat), confirming that rubric generation without an ideal reference produces misaligned signals. On VITON-HD, IEC beats CatVTON [6] (LPIPS 0.047 vs. 0.057, SSIM 0.892 vs. 0.870, FID 5.120 vs. 5.430) and CatV2TON [7] (FID 5.120 vs. 8.100, KID 0.660 vs. 2.250), though CatVTON retains a KID advantage (0.410 vs. 0.660). IEC also surpasses CAT-DM [47] (LPIPS 0.047 vs. 0.080), IDM-VTON [5] (LPIPS 0.047 vs. 0.102), and OmniVTON [46] (LPIPS 0.047 vs. 0.145) across all VITON-HD metrics. On DressCode, IEC leads CatVTON [6] (FID 3.610 vs. 3.990, SSIM 0.927 vs. 0.892) and OOTDiffusion [44] (FID 3.610 vs. 4.200, LPIPS 0.035 vs. 0.045), but OOTDiffusion retains a KID edge (0.370 vs. 0.790). Overall, IEC achieves the top score on 6 of 8 perceptual metrics across both standard benchmarks despite using only 60 RL steps with no additional paired training data, while all external baselines were trained on substantially larger paired datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against Rubrics as Rewards (RaR) [12], which synthesizes instance-specific evaluation criteria from an ideal reference answer, and identifies a 'reference-free gap' where no such ideal answer exists. It builds on the GRPO post-training paradigm for flow-matching models (Flow-GRPO [22], DiT-VTON [20]) and the rectified-flow backbone of Kontext [18], but departs from the broader RLVR tradition by inverting the reward signal: rather than scoring how well an output satisfies a rubric derived from a reference, IEC enumerates and severity-weights errors across task-relevant axes. The principle of counting structured violations as a quality signal draws on GenEval [10] and TIFA [15], but IEC extends this from evaluation metrics to training-time rewards in a reference-free setting, and introduces group calibration to stabilize the resulting reward for GRPO optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical recipe (implicit error counting + group calibration) for RL post-training in domains where quality is defined by the absence of errors rather than matching a reference, a setting that includes image editing, embodied control, and open-ended design. The finding that RaR underperforms even naive direct scoring in the reference-free setting is a useful negative result that cautions against reflexively applying rubric-based rewards. Key limitations include validation on a single domain (VTO), dependence on proprietary LLM judges (GPT-5-mini/GPT-5) whose API changes could silently alter reward signals, and a CEC human-alignment study of only 25 samples. Open questions include whether IEC generalizes to other reference-free domains, whether open-source judges can replicate the results, and whether the approach scales to tasks with more than five evaluation axes.
