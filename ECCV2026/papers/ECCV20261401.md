## Problem

CLIP-based vision encoders are vulnerable to Typographic Attacks, where irrelevant text superimposed on an image biases the model toward the lexical label rather than the true visual content. Prior interpretability work treated ViT layers as indivisible blocks, and existing defenses were either supervised (requiring annotated data) or relied on expensive greedy circuit searches over noisy attention maps. The paper asks whether the specific attention heads responsible for lexical focus can be identified and intervened upon in a training-free, interpretable manner.

## Input / Output

For circuit mining, the method consumes an unlabeled set of 1,280 text-injected images (0.1% of ImageNet-1K) and produces a fixed set of vulnerable attention head indices within the last 20% of ViT blocks, extracted in under one minute on a single A100. At inference, the intervened model takes a standard RGB image (with or without typographic text) and outputs either a class label (zero-shot object classification) or a multiple-choice VQA answer. The intervention itself is near-zero-cost at test time since it operates on pre-computed head indices, using attention reweighting for CLIP models with a <cls> token or zero ablation for LVLMs that lack one.

## Method

The core contribution is a training-free stochastic concept mining procedure grounded in the Linear Representation Hypothesis: instead of learning a dictionary of concept vectors via Sparse Autoencoders, the method samples K random unit vectors in the lower-dimensional d_head subspace of each Multi-Head Self-Attention head and scores them via a gradient-based attribution formula (∂F_i(u)/∂s_{i,j} = A_{i,j}(V_j(u) − F_i(u))) that jointly captures the routing gate and the marginal concept utility of each patch. A normalized Text Attribution Score (nTAS) then ranks heads by their lexical focus relative to visual focus, and a z-test threshold selects the minimal lexical circuit. The theoretical analysis shows that operating in the d_head bottleneck (rather than the full d_model residual stream) sharply reduces polysemantic interference, so a small number of random samples suffices to recover a concept-faithful attribution map. At test time, the identified heads are intervened upon via attention reweighting (shifting <cls> attention away from text patches) or zero ablation, requiring no gradient updates or additional training.

## Results

On zero-shot object classification under typographic attack, the proposed nTAS circuit mining method achieves average OCA of 71.7% (RTA-100), 78.7% (Disentangling), 74.7% (PAINT), and 77.0% (IN-100-Text) across five CLIP ViT backbones, while incurring less than 1% accuracy loss on clean ImageNet-100 (81.0%). The method reduces Text Confusion Rate by 10–36 percentage points relative to unmodified baselines (e.g., TCR drops from 42.0% to 14.4% on RTA-100 for ViT-H/14). When applied to LVLMs (Qwen3-VL, Gemma3, InternVL3.5), zero-ablation of the mined lexical circuits improves VQA accuracy on RIO-Bench by 0.97–1.73% on average with negligible clean-image degradation (−0.3% to +0.6%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the supervised Defense-Prefix [1], the proposed method wins on every typographic-attack dataset by a wide margin: +8.1 pp on RTA-100 (71.7 vs 63.6), +10.9 pp on Disentangling (78.7 vs 67.8), +6.9 pp on PAINT (74.7 vs 67.8), and +6.9 pp on IN-100-Text (77.0 vs 70.1), while matching clean ImageNet-100 accuracy within 0.4 pp (81.0 vs 81.4). Against the training-free Dyslexify [20], the method achieves an average OCA improvement of 6.1 pp across the four TA datasets (e.g., +4.7 pp on RTA-100, +11.1 pp on Disentangling, +4.6 pp on PAINT, +4.2 pp on IN-100-Text) and slightly trails on clean IN-100 by 0.3 pp (81.0 vs 81.3). Even the stronger Dyslexify* variant (which uses full ImageNet-100 for greedy search) is outperformed on RTA-100 (+3.2 pp) and Disentangling (+8.0 pp). The key advantage is that the proposed method requires only 0.1% of ImageNet-1K (1,280 images) for a one-time <1-minute circuit extraction, whereas Dyslexify needs iterative evaluation over the full training set. On LVLMs, the method provides consistent VQA gains of 0.97–1.73% on RIO-Bench for Qwen3-VL and Gemma3 variants, with no comparable training-free baseline reported for that setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability framework from the Transformer Circuits Thread (residual stream decomposition, attention head analysis) to Vision Transformer encoders in CLIP, specifically targeting typographic-attack vulnerability. It departs from prior CLIP interpretability work (Gandelsman et al., Dreyer et al., Kissane et al.) that relied on Sparse Dictionary Learning or greedy text-description searches by replacing parametric concept discovery with stochastic sampling in the lower-dimensional MHSA head subspace, grounded in the Linear Representation Hypothesis. The attention reweighting intervention is directly adopted from Dyslexify (Hufe et al. 2026), but the circuit identification mechanism—gradient-based attribution with normalized text attribution scores—is novel and eliminates the need for iterative greedy search or labeled data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since CLIP vision encoders underpin most state-of-the-art LVLMs (LLaVA, Qwen-VL, InternVL, Gemma3), typographic-attack vulnerability poses a concrete safety risk in deployment scenarios like autonomous driving. The training-free, constant-time intervention is practically attractive: it requires only 0.1% of an unlabeled ImageNet subset for one-time circuit extraction (under one minute on a single A100) and incurs negligible test-time overhead. Open questions include whether the identified circuits remain stable under distribution shift or more sophisticated multi-image attacks, whether the approach generalizes to non-typographic visual interference (e.g., watermarking, occlusion), and whether the stochastic sampling framework can be extended to deeper causal interventions beyond attention reweighting.
