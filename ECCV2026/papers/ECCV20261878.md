## Problem

Existing video understanding benchmarks primarily evaluate shallow perceptual cues (object recognition, short-term actions) via VideoQA, leaving unexplored whether MLLMs can extract procedural knowledge from video tutorials and transfer it to long-horizon agentic tasks. Additionally, both VideoQA and video-guided agentic tasks share a critical bottleneck: identifying task-relevant temporal content in long videos, where naive frame sampling either misses key moments or introduces excessive redundancy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261878/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a video clip (e.g., 3-minute EgoSchema videos at 1 fps yielding ~180 frames) paired with a text question or GUI task instruction. At any point during search, only the 'visible frames'—the first and last frames of each current video segment—are fed to the MLLM, typically around 15% of total frames. For VideoQA the output is a text answer; for VG-GUI-BENCH the output is a sequence of structured GUI actions from a six-type space (CLICK, SCROLL, TYPE, PRESS, ZOOM, FINISH) with coordinate or text arguments, evaluated over multi-step episodes averaging 10.71 steps.

## Method

TASKER formulates keyframe extraction as a generalized graph-search problem over a binary tree of video segments, where each node is a temporal segment and expansion means a binary split. Four variants map to classical search algorithms: TASKER-BFS (naive wave-like expansion), TASKER-GBFS (task-driven heuristic where the MLLM identifies missing visual information), TASKER-Dijkstra (scene-aware cost where the MLLM prioritizes segments with significant scene transitions), and TASKER-A* (combined task-driven and scene-aware cost). MLLMs evaluate cost functions at each step and determine termination via a voting mechanism that ensembles self-evaluation confidence and temporal summarization confidence. A frame validation step after each expansion discards visually redundant or task-irrelevant frames, and segments yielding only redundant frames are frozen to avoid re-exploration. The entire pipeline is training-free and zero-shot, operating purely through MLLM prompting.

## Results

On VideoQA benchmarks, TASKER-A* with GPT-4 achieves 63.1% accuracy on EgoSchema fullset (surpassing the best baseline VideoTree by 2.0%) and 77.4% average accuracy on NExT-QA (surpassing the best baseline by 1.8%). With GPT-4o, TASKER further improves to 63.6% on EgoSchema fullset and 78.1% on NExT-QA. On the proposed VG-GUI-BENCH, TASKER-A* achieves the highest overall accuracy (40.96%), PIR (0.618), and CLICK score (53.68%) among all keyframe selection methods, while TASKER-Dijkstra attains the best completion rate (74.39%), closely approaching the Oracle Keyframe upper bound (76.32%). In terms of frame efficiency, TASKER requires only ~1/4 of the frames used by VideoTree to reach the same 66% accuracy level on EgoSchema subset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TASKER consistently outperforms all 13 baselines on both EgoSchema and NExT-QA. Against the strongest prior method VideoTree [51] (GPT-4), TASKER gains +2.0% on EgoSchema fullset (63.1 vs. 61.1) and +1.8% on NExT-QA average (77.4 vs. 75.6); with GPT-4o the gap widens to +2.5% on both benchmarks. On the open-source MLLM track (Qwen3-VL), TASKER still leads VideoTree by +0.6% on EgoSchema fullset and +0.8% on NExT-QA. On VG-GUI-BENCH, TASKER-A* (40.96% Acc) edges out VideoTree (40.79%) and VideoAgent (39.86%), while TASKER-Dijkstra achieves the highest completion rate (74.39%) among all dynamic selection methods. Notably, TASKER is training-free and zero-shot, yet surpasses training-based methods like LVNet [33] (61.1% EgoSchema full) and Vamos [46] (48.3%), and uses only ~15% of total frames compared to full-frame methods such as LangRepo [19] and LifelongMemory [49]. The frame-efficiency comparison (Figure 4) shows TASKER reaches 66% EgoSchema-subset accuracy with roughly one-quarter the frames needed by VideoTree, confirming superior information localization.

## Relation to Prior Work

TASKER repurposes classical graph-search algorithms (A*, Dijkstra, GBFS, BFS) as a unified framework for keyframe extraction, departing from VideoTree's static precomputed feature tree by performing question-aware, on-the-fly segment expansion, and from VideoAgent's recursive agent-guided selection by explicitly modeling intra-video scene transitions as a movement cost. The VG-GUI-BENCH benchmark extends the video-guided GUI agent line (Mobile-Agent-V, VideoGUI, Watch-and-Learn, TongUI) by pairing tutorial videos with structured GUI episodes from MONDAY and introducing a standardized six-action space and multi-metric evaluation protocol. Conceptually, the paper carves new ground by framing VideoQA and video-guided agentic tasks as two levels of a single hierarchy sharing the same temporal-selection bottleneck, rather than treating them as unrelated problem families.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The two-level taxonomy and VG-GUI-BENCH provide the community with a concrete testbed for video in-context learning—whether models can extract procedural knowledge from tutorials and transfer it to long-horizon execution—which prior benchmarks did not systematically evaluate. However, the absolute performance on VG-GUI-BENCH remains low (best accuracy ~41%, with 0% on TYPE and PRESS actions across all methods), suggesting that procedural transfer from video to GUI action is still far from solved. Open questions include whether the MLLM-based cost-function evaluation generalizes reliably to much longer videos or more diverse domains, and whether the modest 2% VideoQA gains over VideoTree/VideoAgent justify the additional MLLM calls per search iteration in practice.
