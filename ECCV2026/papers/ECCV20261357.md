## Problem

VLM personalization via test-time fine-tuning (e.g., Yo'LLaVA) requires both positive and negative samples, but users typically provide only 1-3 concept images and retrieved negatives suffer from unpredictable quality. The roles of positive, easy-negative, and hard-negative samples-and how their diversity affects different personalization tasks-had not been systematically characterized, leaving no principled way to supplement scarce or low-quality training data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261357/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes 1-3 user-provided concept images and a concept category label, from which a VLM generates textual descriptions that are aggregated into a three-layer concept tree (category, attribute dimensions, attribute values). A fine-tuned diffusion model then synthesizes image-text pairs for all three sample types; GPT-4o generates the instruction text for training. A CLIP-based PCS score (computed via patch-shuffling perturbation) filters each generated image, retaining only those whose similarity to the reference drops sharply after perturbation, indicating concept-specific rather than background-driven content.

## Method

CaT encodes each concept as a hierarchical tree T_C = (R(C), D_C, A(D_C)) built through VLM image description, LLM batch summarization, and a self-refine voting loop. Positive samples are produced by a diffusion model fine-tuned on the user images and conditioned on the root node; easy negatives are generated by replacing the root with a different category and editing dimensions; hard negatives retain the root but apply one of three tree-editing operations (add, remove, or modify a dimension) to create visually similar but semantically distinct images. Controlling the number and type of editing operations directly tunes the diversity of hard negatives, which the paper shows is critical: moderate diversity improves conversational tasks while excessive diversity introduces noise. A Perturbation-based Concept-Specific (PCS) score-defined as the drop in CLIP cosine similarity between a synthetic image and the reference before versus after patch shuffling-serves as a quality filter that separates concept-specific content from concept-agnostic background, outperforming plain cosine-similarity filtering across all evaluation tasks.

## Results

On the MC-LLaVA benchmark, MC-LLaVA augmented with CaT synthetic data (Real+Syn Plus) achieves 96.3% Recognition, 92.8% Choice-V, 76.0% Choice-T, 72.6 BLEU (VQA), and 80.3% Caption Recall, surpassing GPT-4o+Prompt on Recognition and Choice-V. The same pipeline yields +4.4% Rec and +5.3% Caption for Yo'LLaVA, and +3.2% Rec and +4.5% VQA for MyVLM on the same benchmark. On the Yo'LLaVA dataset, MC-LLaVA(Real+Syn)(P) reaches 97.7% Rec and 97.1% Caption, while on MyVLM it achieves 98.7% Rec and 97.1% Caption. Across all three datasets, the CaT pipeline consistently improves every fine-tuning-based personalization method by 1.2–5.3% depending on task and dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CaT is a data synthesis pipeline rather than a competing model, so the paper evaluates it by augmenting four existing personalization methods. Compared to the original Yo'LLaVA baseline [38], adding CaT synthetic data (Real+Syn Plus) improves Recognition by +4.4%, Choice-V by +4.4%, VQA by +3.9%, and Caption by +5.3% on the MC-LLaVA benchmark. Against MC-LLaVA [3], the same augmentation yields +4.6% Rec, +3.8% Choice-V, +4.2% VQA, and +5.3% Caption, with the augmented MC-LLaVA reaching 96.3% Rec—surpassing GPT-4o+Prompt (74.6%) by a wide margin. For MyVLM [1], improvements are +3.2% Rec, +4.5% VQA, and +2.6% Caption, confirming the pipeline generalizes across architectures. RAP-MLLM [20] serves as a retrieval-based non-fine-tuning baseline and is substantially outperformed on every metric (e.g., 74.7% vs. 96.3% Rec on MC-LLaVA), highlighting the advantage of fine-tuning with high-quality synthetic data over retrieval alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the test-time fine-tuning paradigm for VLM personalization established by Yo'LLaVA, which requires positive, easy-negative, and hard-negative samples, and extends it to multi-concept settings following MC-LLaVA. Rather than proposing a new fine-tuning architecture, the paper addresses the upstream data bottleneck: it introduces a concept-tree representation (CaT) that enables controllable, difficulty-stratified generation of all three sample types via a fine-tuned diffusion model, and a PCS-score-based filter to ensure generated images carry concept-specific rather than concept-agnostic information. The systematic empirical study of how sample count, type, and diversity interact is positioned as a prerequisite that prior personalization work had not conducted, and the tree-editing operations (add/remove/modify dimensions) provide a principled mechanism for dialing diversity that prior retrieval-based negative sampling could not offer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The pipeline is practically significant because it requires only 1–3 user images and produces a full training set, directly addressing the data-scarcity constraint that limits real-world deployment of personalized VLMs. The PCS score is a lightweight, model-agnostic quality metric that could be adopted beyond this specific pipeline for any synthetic-image fine-tuning scenario. Open questions include whether the distribution shift between synthetic and real data (noted by the authors as limiting purely-synthetic training) can be further reduced, whether the tree structure scales gracefully to dozens of concurrent concepts, and whether the approach transfers to video or 3D personalization settings. The moderate but consistent 2–5% gains across three datasets and multiple baselines suggest the technique is robust but not transformative.
