## Problem

Memorization in multi-modal contrastive learning with more than two modalities (e.g., audio, video, image, text) remains unexplored, while existing metrics like CLIPMem and SSLMem are limited to bi-modal or uni-modal settings and fail to capture global cross-modal memorization. The paper asks how to faithfully quantify memorization when all modalities interact jointly, and whether targeted mitigation of the most memorized samples can improve generalization.

## Input / Output

MultiMem takes a multi-modal sample x_i, a pair of contrastively trained models (f trained on the full set S, g trained on S\i), and a held-out set H of unrelated validation samples, then computes a scalar memorization score per sample by comparing cross-modal consistency across all n modalities. The metric averages over random augmentations of each modality for stability, and the held-out set size and composition (random, class-balanced, or out-of-domain) do not significantly affect results. For mitigation, the memorization scores identify the top-k most memorized samples, which are then either noise-augmented during training or removed and fine-tuned post-training to produce a mitigated model.

## Method

MultiMem builds on the leave-one-out framework and introduces a Cross-Modal Consistency (CMC) proxy that constructs an n×d representation matrix Φ for a sample across all n modalities, then scores how strongly the modalities align with each other (via 1_n^T ΦΦ^T 1_n) relative to their alignment with held-out samples. The memorization score is the CMC difference between the model trained with the sample and the model trained without it, capturing global interactions beyond any single modality pair. For mitigation, the paper proposes two strategies: in-training, periodically (every 10 epochs) identifying the top-5% most memorized samples via MultiMem and applying Gaussian noise (σ=0.1) augmentation only to their representations in subsequent steps; and post-training, removing the top-memorized samples and fine-tuning on the remainder. Both strategies reduce memorization by up to 20% while improving retrieval, zero-shot, and linear-probing performance by up to 8%, 10%, and 4% respectively.

## Results

On AudioCLIP (tri-modal, UrbanSound8K), in-training MultiMem-guided mitigation of the top 5% most memorized samples improves retrieval T@5 from 36.9% to 43.3% (+6.4 pp), linear probing from 76.7% to 80.8% (+4.1 pp), and zero-shot classification from 25.4% to 35.1% (+9.7 pp), while reducing global MultiMem from 0.332 to 0.262 (−21%). Post-training removal of the top 500 most memorized samples followed by 25-epoch fine-tuning yields retrieval 39.3%, linear probing 78.6%, and zero-shot 30.5%, with MultiMem dropping to 0.282. The paper reports up to 20% memorization reduction and up to 8%/10%/4% gains on retrieval/zero-shot/classification across models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CLIPMem [27], MultiMem consistently measures higher memorization levels and better separates candidate-set (S_C) from shared-set (S_S) scores on AudioCLIP, AVT-CLIP, and AVIT-CLIP (Figures 1, 7, 8, 10), confirming that pairwise image-text alignment is insufficient for multi-modal settings. In post-training mitigation, MultiMem-guided sample removal outperforms CLIPMem-guided removal on all three tasks (retrieval, linear probing, zero-shot), with CLIPMem ranking second after cosine-similarity-based selection. Against SSLMem [28], MultiMem captures global cross-modal memorization that single-modality SSLMem cannot; Table 2 shows that augmenting only one modality increases SSLMem for that modality while MultiMem (tri-modal) still decreases, and that all-modality augmentation simultaneously lowers both metrics. Compared to ImageBind [8] training, the paper's pairwise InfoNCE alignment across all modalities achieves lower global memorization (0.488 vs. 0.571) and substantially better retrieval (48.2% vs. 36.5%) on the AVIT-CLIP quad-modal model, demonstrating that the training objective itself influences memorization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the leave-one-out memorization framework from Feldman (2020) for supervised learning and Wang et al. (ICLR 2024) for self-supervised learning to the multi-modal contrastive setting, generalizing beyond the bi-modal CLIPMem metric (Wang et al., ICLR 2025). It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) it argues that pairwise or single-modality memorization metrics are insufficient for models with three or more modalities, and (2) it shifts the explanatory focus from text/caption quality (the dominant driver in CLIPMem) to cross-modal semantic misalignment as the primary cause of memorization. The mitigation strategies build on the noise-augmentation approach validated in prior bi-modal and SSL memorization work but apply them selectively to top-memorized samples identified by the new metric.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic framework for quantifying and mitigating memorization in multi-modal contrastive learning, a setting that underpins increasingly important applications (cross-modal retrieval, zero-shot understanding across audio/video/image/text). The finding that multi-modal models' memorization behavior converges toward SSL-like pattern memorization rather than SL-like label memorization reframes how practitioners should think about what these models are actually learning. Open questions include whether the findings generalize to larger-scale pretraining (the experiments use relatively small datasets like UrbanSound8K and MSR-VTT), whether the simple Gaussian-noise augmentation is optimal compared to more sophisticated modality-specific interventions, and how the metric scales as the number of modalities grows beyond four.
