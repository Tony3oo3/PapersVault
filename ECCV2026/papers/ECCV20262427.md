## Problem

Existing safety-alignment benchmarks for text-to-image diffusion models evaluate pre-deployment safety and adversarial jailbreak resistance but do not test whether safety persists under the benign downstream fine-tuning (LoRA personalization, style/domain adapters) routinely applied after deployment. This paper shows that such benign fine-tuning by unintentional actors can silently erode safety alignment while utility metrics remain stable or improve, making the failure mode invisible under conventional quality-centric evaluation.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes a safety-aligned T2I model (SD v1.5, SDXL, or SD v3), applies one of three benign fine-tuning profiles (Lite/LoRA, Moderate/cross-attention-only, Standard/full-parameter) on harmless datasets (COCO, multilingual prompts, or domain-specific style data), then evaluates the adapted model on curated harmful prompt sets (ViSU, I2P, Ring-A-Bell). The output is a single SPQR composite score computed as the harmonic mean of four normalized [0,1] axes: Safety (1 − harmfulness via LLaVA-Guard + NudeNet), Prompt adherence (CLIP score normalized by unaligned SD3 ceiling), Quality (min-max inverted FID with ε-smoothing), and Robustness (sigmoid of the pre/post-BFT safety drift Δh).

## Method

SPQR formalizes an 'unintentional attacker' threat model in which a benign user fine-tunes a safety-aligned model on strictly harmless data (D ∩ H = ∅) using a standard supervised objective, and defines robustness as R = 1/(1+exp(Δh)), where Δh is the increase in harmfulness score on a fixed harmful prompt set after fine-tuning. The four axes are aggregated via a weighted harmonic mean (all weights set to 1 by default), which penalizes imbalance so that excelling on one axis cannot compensate for failure on another. The evaluation protocol fixes compute budgets, uses public benign datasets, and spans three BFT scenarios (general, multilingual across Arabic/Spanish/French/Hindi, and domain-specific artistic/medical) to stress-test cross-lingual and cross-domain stability. The safety pipeline replaces the unreliable Q16 classifier with LLaVA-Guard and is validated against three human annotators on a 250-image subset, achieving macro F1 of 0.87. Across 11 safety-alignment methods on three backbones, the benchmark reveals that RECE and UCE achieve the highest SPQR scores, while parameter-space unlearning methods (FMN, AdvUnlearn) collapse under even mild benign adaptation.

## Results

On the primary SD v1.5 backbone under the General BFT profile, RECE achieves the highest SPQR score of 0.956 (S=0.938, P=0.973, Q=0.934, R=0.980), followed by UCE (0.940) and ESD (0.866). ERASEDIFF, despite near-perfect safety (S=0.988), collapses to a SPQR of 0.166 due to catastrophic quality degradation (Q=0.050). Across multilingual and domain-specific BFT scenarios, RECE maintains SPQR ≥ 0.886, while weaker methods like FMN (0.392) and AdvU (0.411) drop sharply, confirming that robustness to benign fine-tuning is the primary differentiator among safety-alignment methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RECE [Gong et al., ECCV 2024] dominates all 11 baselines on the General SPQR score (0.956 vs. next-best UCE at 0.940) and maintains the top or second-top position across multilingual and domain-specific settings. UCE [Gandikota et al., WACV 2024] is the strongest runner-up, achieving 0.940 general SPQR and 0.930 on both SDXL and SD v3, confirming cross-architecture generalizability. ESD [Gandikota et al., ICCV 2023] and SPM [Lyu et al., CVPR 2024] cluster around 0.865–0.866 general SPQR but suffer notable robustness drops under multilingual BFT (R=0.291 and 0.363 respectively). MACE [Lu et al., CVPR 2024] excels specifically in multilingual robustness (R=0.756, the highest among non-RECE methods) but trails RECE in the general setting (0.842 vs. 0.956). At the bottom, ERASEDIFF [Wu et al., CVPR 2025] achieves near-perfect safety (S=0.988) but its SPQR collapses to 0.166 due to a quality score of 0.050, while FMN [Zhang et al., CVPRW 2024] and AdvUnlearn [Zhang et al., NeurIPS 2024] fail primarily on robustness (R≈0.15), indicating their parameter-space unlearning is easily reversed by benign fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the safety-alignment evaluation landscape for text-to-image diffusion models by introducing robustness to benign fine-tuning (BFT) as a first-class evaluation axis, building on the concept-erasure and unlearning literature (ESD, MACE, UCE, STEREO, RECE, etc.) and the recent finding by George et al. [9] that targeted fine-tuning can revive unlearned concepts. It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) it relaxes the adversarial assumption, defining an 'unintentional attacker' who fine-tunes on strictly benign, unrelated data without knowledge of the suppressed concepts, and (2) it unifies four previously fragmented evaluation dimensions—safety, prompt adherence, quality, and robustness—into a single harmonic-mean score (SPQR), whereas prior benchmarks (UnlearnCanvas, NSFW Benchmark, adversarial prompt suites) each capture only a partial view. The paper does not propose a new alignment method but repurposes the BFT threat model as a quantitative robustness metric.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: model providers who routinely apply LoRA personalization, style adapters, or domain fine-tuning to safety-aligned T2I systems may silently erode safety while seeing utility metrics improve, making the regression invisible under conventional quality-centric evaluation. The SPQR benchmark provides a reproducible, single-score yardstick that could become a standard for comparing alignment methods under realistic post-deployment conditions. Open questions include whether the LoRA-robustness advantage (lighter adaptation preserves safety better) holds at larger scales or with more aggressive rank settings, whether distribution-aware alignment strategies like RECE's can be made more computationally efficient, and how the findings extend to autoregressive or video-generation models where the safety surface is even larger.
