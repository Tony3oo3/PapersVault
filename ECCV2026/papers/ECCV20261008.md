## Problem

Existing privacy protection methods for LVLMs face a fundamental trade-off: they either fail to generalize to unseen sensitive queries or degrade the model's utility on benign tasks. The paper identifies that privacy-related neurons in LVLMs are sparse and highly context-dependent, meaning indiscriminate model editing introduces unnecessary changes that harm both generalization and stability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261008/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on paired samples from PrivacyPair, where each pair shares the same privacy subject (e.g., passport, phone number) in an input image but differs by a single attribute word in the text query (sensitive vs. benign). During the editing phase, the model receives the image and text query, and the output is a modified set of FFN parameters at selected layers, causing the model to produce refusal responses for sensitive queries while preserving normal answers for benign ones. The gate vector M_l is computed over N paired samples per privacy subject and applied as a binary mask during gradient updates.

## Method

Neural Gate introduces a two-stage neuron-level editing procedure. First, for each layer l, a learnable scaling vector m_l is optimized per sample to measure which feature dimensions of the privacy subject require sign reversal to steer the model toward refusal (sensitive) or preserve original output (benign), with L1 regularization encouraging sparsity. Second, these per-sample vectors are aggregated across N samples into a gate vector M_l where each entry is the fraction of samples requiring modification at that dimension; only dimensions with M_l[j] > 0.3 (strongly active neurons, typically <10% of all dimensions) are allowed to contribute gradients during FFN parameter updates. This gradient truncation mechanism ensures editing focuses on neurons that consistently encode privacy concepts across contexts, filtering out context-dependent noise that would otherwise cause overfitting to training samples and degradation of benign task performance.

## Results

Neural Gate achieves the highest Safety Avg score on both MiniGPT (0.8918) and LLaVA (0.8566), outperforming the strongest baseline DINM (0.8417 and 0.8187 respectively). The generalization gap is most pronounced on MLLMGuard, where Neural Gate reaches 0.8440 on MiniGPT and 0.7522 on LLaVA, surpassing DINM by 9.2 and 5.5 points. Utility is largely preserved: Neural Gate scores 0.6330 (MiniGPT) and 0.7230 (LLaVA) on the Utility Avg, within 0.2–0.9 points of DINM and the baseline. On PrivacyPair-test, refusal rates exceed 94% (MiniGPT) and 96% (LLaVA) for sensitive queries while benign response rates drop by only ~3%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Neural Gate outperforms all five baselines on Safety Avg for both MiniGPT and LLaVA. Against the strongest competitor DINM [32], Neural Gate gains +5.0 points on MiniGPT Safety Avg (0.8918 vs 0.8417) and +3.8 points on LLaVA (0.8566 vs 0.8187), with the largest margin on MLLMGuard generalization (+9.2 and +5.5 points respectively). Utility is nearly matched: Neural Gate trails DINM by only 0.2 points on MiniGPT Utility Avg (0.6330 vs 0.6350) and 0.9 points on LLaVA (0.7230 vs 0.7321). The non-gradient editing baselines MEMIT [23] and AlphaEdit [8] achieve moderate safety gains (Safety Avg 0.63–0.69) but show the weakest MLLMGuard generalization (0.45–0.66), confirming their limited transfer to unseen privacy queries. The unlearning baselines SKU* [19] and MemFlex* [29] perform poorly overall; MemFlex* in particular suffers catastrophic utility collapse on MiniGPT (Utility Avg drops to 0.2649 from the baseline 0.5416), indicating that indiscriminate layer-level updates severely damage model capabilities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the MEMIT framework's learnable-vector layer-wise intervention mechanism for controlled feature perturbation and DINM's targeted training paradigm (refusal-style prefixes with FFN editing) for privacy-sensitive queries. It departs from both by introducing a neuron-level gradient gating mechanism: rather than applying full-feature gradients (as in MEMIT/AlphaEdit) or layer-level updates (as in DINM), Neural Gate identifies a sparse subset of neurons that consistently encode privacy signals across paired samples and truncates gradients for all other dimensions. The paper also positions itself against knowledge-unlearning methods (SKU, MemFlex), which use global gradient ascent and tend to degrade benign performance, arguing that neuron-level targeted editing achieves the precision of unlearning without its collateral damage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LVLMs are increasingly deployed in sensitive domains (healthcare, finance), the ability to suppress privacy-leaking behaviors without degrading general utility is practically critical. The PrivacyPair dataset and the mechanistic finding that privacy-relevant neurons are sparse (<10%) and highly context-dependent provide a useful diagnostic framework for future privacy-editing work. However, generalization remains uneven: the method successfully rejects some unseen privacy categories (trade secrets) but fails on others (drones), and multi-layer editing degrades performance on MiniGPT, suggesting the approach has not yet fully solved the out-of-distribution privacy problem. Scaling to larger models and broader privacy taxonomies remains an open question.
