## Problem

Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) are the two dominant post-training stages for large-scale diffusion models, yet existing work treats them as independent, sequential processes. This separation creates a critical gap: RL applied after distillation suffers from reward hacking and mode collapse due to the impoverished anchor of the already-distilled distribution, while pure DMD imposes a 'performance ceiling' by uniformly mimicking the teacher. The paper asks whether these two objectives can be jointly optimized in a unified pipeline where they mutually benefit each other.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261210/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt as input and produces a generated RGB image in as few as 1–4 function evaluations (NFE), compared to 50 steps for the multi-step teacher. It is validated across denoising-based (SDXL-Base, 1024×1024) and flow-based (SD3-Medium, SD3.5-Large, 1024×1024) architectures, as well as class-conditional ImageNet 256×256 generation with the SiT backbone. The few-step student is trained to predict a clean image at each denoising step's state, enabling reward gradients to propagate directly to the initial noise without traversing a long multi-step computation graph.

## Method

DMDR is a two-stage unified framework. Stage 1 (RT-DM) softly integrates preference signals by weighting the DMD gradient with a normalized reward score, so the student distills toward a reward-tilted teacher distribution proportional to r(x₀)·p_real rather than the vanilla p_real; this avoids backpropagating through the full multi-step trajectory. Two dynamic strategies stabilize the cold-start: DynaDG injects trainable LoRA modules into the real score estimator to pull the perceived p_real toward the nascent p_fake (annealed to zero), and DynaRS biases renoise sampling toward high noise levels where score estimates are most reliable. Stage 2 performs joint optimization with L_total = L_dmd + λ_rl·L_rl, where the DMD loss acts as a distributional regularizer that projects reward-ascent gradients back onto the teacher's high-density manifold, preventing reward hacking, while the RL objective (compatible with ReFL, DPO, or GRPO) drives aggressive preference alignment beyond the teacher's ceiling.

## Results

DMDR achieves state-of-the-art few-step generation across all three base models. On SD3-Medium (4 NFE), DMDR reaches HP Score 32.11 and CLIP Score 35.01, surpassing its 50-step teacher (HP 28.40, CLIP 34.90) and the best distillation-only baseline DMD2 (HP 27.37, CLIP 33.94). On SD3.5-Large (4 NFE), DMDR attains HP Score 32.74 and Aesthetic Score 6.15, outperforming the multi-step teacher (HP 28.81, Aesthetic 5.70) by a wide margin. On DPG_Bench and GenEval, the 4-step DMDR model consistently outperforms its multi-step teacher on overall scores (e.g., 85.30 vs. 84.08 on DPG for SD3-Medium; 0.65 vs. 0.62 on GenEval).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DMDR outperforms all distillation-only baselines (DMD2, LCM, Lightning, Flash, TDM) by a large margin on every metric across all three architectures. Against the closest distillation-then-RL baselines, DMDR gains +3.1 HP Score over DMD2-PSO on SDXL-Base (32.98 vs. 29.87), +4.7 HP over Hyper-SD on SD3-Medium (32.11 vs. 22.45), and +4.9 HP over LADD on SD3.5-Large (32.74 vs. 27.85). Notably, DMDR's 4-step model surpasses its own 50-step multi-step teacher on HP Score (e.g., 32.11 vs. 28.40 for SD3-Medium) and on DPG_Bench overall (85.30 vs. 84.08), demonstrating that joint DMD+RL breaks the teacher's performance ceiling. The only baseline that approaches DMDR's HP Score is multi-step ReFL (32.07 on SD3-Medium), but it requires 50 NFE versus DMDR's 4, representing a ~10× inference speedup. On GenEval, DMDR's 4-step model also exceeds its teacher on overall score for all three models (0.57 vs. 0.55, 0.65 vs. 0.62, 0.72 vs. 0.71), though it trails slightly on positional reasoning sub-metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD/DMD2) for step compression and on RL-based preference alignment methods (ReFL, DPO, GRPO) for human-preference optimization, but departs from the dominant sequential paradigm where these are treated as independent, ordered stages. The closest prior works—Flash-DMD and Diff-Instruct++—still follow a 'distill-then-RL' pipeline, whereas DMDR introduces Reward-Tilted Distribution Matching (RT-DM) to inject preference signals from the very first step of distillation, and then transitions to joint DMD+RL optimization. Conceptually, the paper reframes the DMD gradient not merely as a distillation loss but as a distribution-level manifold projection that subsumes the traditional KL regularizer in RL for few-step models, a reinterpretation absent from prior DMD or RL-for-diffusion literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework's model-agnostic and RL-algorithm-agnostic design (validated across UNet/Transformer backbones, flow/denoising paradigms, and ReFL/DPO/GRPO) positions it as a potential standard post-training pipeline for industrial diffusion models, where 4-step inference that matches or exceeds 50-step teacher quality is a significant practical win. The honest acknowledgment of a quality-diversity trade-off (LPIPS drops ~19% from base model) and the tunable λ_rl knob leave open the question of whether adaptive reward weighting or diversity-preserving RL objectives can recover the lost mode coverage without sacrificing preference gains. The finding that sequential RL on a distilled model rapidly collapses semantically (DPG score drops) while joint DMD+RL avoids this is a useful diagnostic for practitioners choosing between pipeline designs.
