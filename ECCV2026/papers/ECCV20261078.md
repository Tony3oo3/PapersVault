## Problem

In video question answering with MLLMs, extended Chain-of-Thought reasoning causes 'visual anchor drifting'—the model's attention to visual inputs decays as the reasoning chain grows, leading to hallucinations and performance degradation. Existing mitigations (external re-attention tools, specialized attention-steering losses) incur prohibitive training costs and fail to generalize across architectures. The paper asks whether a simple, training-free input-level intervention can counteract this visual signal dilution without modifying the backbone model.

## Input / Output

The method consumes N uniformly sampled video frames (typically 128) and a text question. A lightweight scoring module (3.7M parameters, built on frozen CLIP features) assigns an importance score to each frame conditioned on the question, then the top-k frames (k=4 or 8) are duplicated at their original temporal positions, producing an (N+k)-frame sequence fed to the frozen MLLM. Repeated frames receive a 0.01 sampling-interval timestamp offset to preserve temporal coherence. The final output is the MLLM's generated text answer.

## Method

FrameRepeat introduces a cross-attention scoring module that encodes each frame via CLIP with sinusoidal positional encoding, attends to question token embeddings as keys/values, refines through a two-layer FFN, and projects to a scalar score augmented with a zero-centered CLIP cosine-similarity prior. Training uses the Add-One-In (AOI) strategy: for each candidate frame, the frozen MLLM's log-probability of the correct answer is measured with and without that frame duplicated once, yielding a per-frame repeat gain as supervision. The candidate set combines top-K policy-ranked and K random frames to balance exploitation and exploration. The loss combines a standardized regression term aligning scores with repeat gains and a margin-based ranking loss enforcing that positive-gain frames outscore negative-gain and non-candidate frames. Because only the 3.7M-parameter scoring module is trained (backbone and CLIP frozen), the module transfers directly to different MLLM architectures without retraining.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct with 128+8 frames, FrameRepeat achieves 63.4 on VideoMME (+2.5), 55.1 on LongVideoBench (+3.7), 38.2 on LVBench (+2.2), 66.0 on MLVU (+3.0), and 36.0 on Video-Holmes (+1.0) over the base model. On Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking at the same setting, gains are 67.9 (+0.9), 65.4 (+3.3), 44.3 (+3.7), 71.2 (+3.0), and 43.4 (+2.4) respectively. The repeat scoring module is trained only on Qwen2.5-VL-7B yet transfers directly to Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking without retraining, confirming cross-architecture generalizability. The module contains only 3.7M trainable parameters while the backbone VLM remains fully frozen.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary external baseline is TSPO [24], a temporal sampling policy optimization method repurposed for frame repetition. FrameRepeat outperforms TSPO on all three reported benchmarks: VideoMME 63.4 vs. 62.8, LongVideoBench 55.1 vs. 54.3, and LVBench 38.2 vs. 36.9 (Qwen2.5-VL-7B, 128+8 frames). The paper argues that frame selection (TSPO's paradigm) and frame repetition serve fundamentally different objectives: selection overemphasizes already-salient content, while repetition compensates for temporal blind spots. Internally, FrameRepeat consistently beats CLIP-K (cosine-similarity-based selection), Bottom-K, and Random-K across all five benchmarks and both models. Notably, Bottom-K and Random-K sometimes degrade performance below the base model (e.g., MLVU drops to 62.3 and 61.5 vs. 63.0 base on Qwen2.5-VL), confirming that indiscriminate repetition introduces interference. The cross-model transfer is a key differentiator: the scoring module trained on Qwen2.5-VL-7B is applied zero-shot to Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking and still yields consistent gains, whereas architecture-specific methods (e.g., attention-steering losses) would require retraining per model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper addresses the well-documented 'visual anchor drifting' problem in video CoT reasoning, where models progressively attend less to visual tokens as generated text grows. Prior mitigations either introduce inference-time mechanisms (external tools, multi-turn frame spotlighting) or modify the backbone via specialized attention-steering losses, both of which are architecture-specific and computationally expensive. FrameRepeat departs from this lineage by operating entirely at the input stage: a lightweight 3.7M-parameter cross-attention scoring module selects frames for in-place duplication, leaving the VLM backbone completely frozen. The AOI training strategy is conceptually related to marginal-contribution attribution in interpretability, but repurposed here as a self-supervised signal for frame importance ranking rather than for explanation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The architecture-agnostic design (trained on Qwen2.5-VL-7B, transferred to Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking without retraining) makes this a practical drop-in preprocessing step for any Video-LLM pipeline, with negligible inference overhead beyond a few extra frames. However, the gains are modest (1–4 points) and the approach has only been validated on two Qwen-family backbones; whether the scoring module transfers to fundamentally different architectures (e.g., InternVL, LLaVA) remains untested. An open question is whether the benefit saturates or degrades for very long videos (hundreds of frames) where the candidate pool for AOI supervision becomes much larger, and whether the fixed top-K repetition strategy can be made adaptive per-sample.
