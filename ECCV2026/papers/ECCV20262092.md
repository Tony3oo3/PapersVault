## Problem

Cross-site fMRI diagnosis suffers severe domain shift from scanner heterogeneity and demographics, yet conventional domain adaptation requires concurrent source and target data access, violating clinical privacy regulations. Moreover, functional connectivity matrices are Symmetric Positive Definite (SPD) and reside on a Riemannian manifold, but existing source-free methods operate in Euclidean space, causing geometric distortions (the 'swelling effect') that corrupt diagnostic patterns. No prior source-free cross-site fMRI method has jointly addressed transferable SPD-valid prototypes, cross-site generalization, and empirical privacy leakage under explicit attacks.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 116×116 functional connectivity matrices (SPD) derived from rs-fMRI preprocessing, with ROI identity vectors as node features, encoded by a GIN backbone. At the source stage, labeled data from one or multiple sites are used to optimize K=4 prototypes per class (8 total matrices); at the target stage, only these compact prototypes plus unlabeled target FC matrices are available—no raw source data is transmitted. The output is a binary class label (e.g., TD vs. ASD or TD vs. MDD) for each target subject, alongside the learned SPD prototypes themselves, which are parameterized as symmetric tangent-space matrices mapped back to the manifold via the matrix exponential.

## Method

BrainRiem learns compact brain prototypes on the SPD manifold via a bi-level optimization scheme: the inner loop trains a student classifier on the prototypes, while the outer loop evaluates the student on held-out source validation data, with gradients propagated through T unrolled inner steps using a first-order approximation. Prototypes are parameterized in the Log-Euclidean tangent space (symmetric matrices) and mapped to valid SPD matrices via Exp(·), enforcing manifold validity at every optimization step. Three regularization terms shape the prototypes: a Log-Euclidean manifold consistency loss preserving local geodesic distances to m-nearest source samples, a Dirichlet Energy spectral calibration aligning prototype frequency statistics with source-domain averages to suppress scanner-induced high-frequency artifacts, and a teacher-guided cross-entropy loss maintaining class-discriminative semantics. At deployment, only the 8 prototype matrices are transmitted to the target site, where a local model is adapted via prototype supervision combined with entropy minimization on unlabeled target data, requiring no source data access.

## Results

Under the Leave-One-Site-Out (LOSO) protocol, BrainRiem achieves 71.4% average accuracy on ABIDE (15 sites) and 70.2% on REST-meta-MDD (23 sites), outperforming the best source-free baseline NRC by 2.1% and 6.5% respectively, and the best graph DA baseline StruRW by 1.0% and 6.2%. On single-source adaptation (Table 1), BrainRiem gains 5.8% average over StruRW on ABIDE, with the largest margin on the UM→UCLA transfer (71.0% vs. 64.0%). The method shows the largest absolute gains on REST-meta-MDD sites with extreme demographic shifts (e.g., S9: 77.8%, S14: 74.2%), where all baselines drop below 70%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BrainRiem outperforms all 12 baselines on both datasets under both LOSO and single-source protocols. Among source-free methods, NRC is the closest competitor (69.3% on ABIDE, 63.7% on MDD), but BrainRiem leads by 2.1% and 6.5% respectively; SHOT, 3C-GAN, and G-SFDA trail further behind. Among graph DA methods that require source access, StruRW is the strongest baseline (70.4% ABIDE, 64.0% MDD), yet BrainRiem surpasses it by 1.0% and 6.2% while operating in a strictly source-free setting. Traditional DA methods (DANN, CORAL) and source-only SPD methods (SPDNet, SPD-GNN) show the largest gaps, with SPDNet falling 12.0% below BrainRiem on ABIDE and 20.3% below on MDD, confirming that Euclidean or non-adaptive approaches fail to handle cross-site domain shift. The gap widens substantially on REST-meta-MDD (up to 20.3% over SPDNet) compared to ABIDE (12.0%), suggesting the Riemannian prototype mechanism is particularly effective under the more severe demographic and scanner heterogeneity present in the MDD consortium.

## Relation to Prior Work

BrainRiem sits at the intersection of three established lines: Log-Euclidean Riemannian geometry for SPD matrices (Arsigny et al.), prototype-based knowledge transfer (Snell et al.), and bi-level optimization à la MAML (Finn et al.). Its key departure is from prior source-free domain adaptation methods (SHOT, NRC, 3C-GAN), which operate entirely in Euclidean feature space and thus corrupt the SPD structure of functional connectivity matrices, and from prior Riemannian brain-network methods (SPDNet, SPD-GNN), which require concurrent source-data access. The paper is, to its knowledge, the first to jointly enforce SPD-validity, source-free transfer, and empirical privacy guarantees in a single framework for cross-site fMRI diagnosis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practically critical bottleneck in multi-site neuroimaging: raw data cannot be shared under GDPR/HIPAA, yet models must generalize across scanners and demographics. The simultaneous achievement of strong accuracy gains (up to ~6.5% over best baselines on MDD), near-random privacy leakage (AUC 0.53 vs. 0.99 for raw data), and biologically interpretable prototypes (DMN underconnectivity in ASD) makes it a credible template for leakage-aware collaborative diagnosis. Open questions include scalability beyond binary classification and fixed 116-ROI atlases, extension to dynamic or multi-scale connectivity, and whether the bi-level optimization cost is prohibitive for very large prototype sets in clinical deployment.
