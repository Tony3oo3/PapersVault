## Problem

Structured light scanning requires careful geometric and radiometric calibration of the projector-camera pair, and errors in modeling projector imperfections (lens distortion, interpixel gaps, blurring, vignetting) directly degrade reconstruction quality. Existing neural inverse SL methods like TurboSL still explicitly model the projector, limiting them to well-calibrated, high-end hardware. This paper asks whether accurate SL reconstruction is possible without any explicit projector modeling at all.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260311/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For static scenes, the method takes k monochromatic pattern images (as few as 3) plus one white flash image (4 captures total); for dynamic scenes with an analog projector, it takes 2 trichromatic RGB images. The patterns need not be specifically designed for the setup—random, Hilbert, sinusoidal, and triangular patterns all work. Output is a 3D surface extracted from the SDF zero level set with associated surface normals, achieving sub-millimeter accuracy. A one-time calibration sweep of a planar board across the scene volume is required to build the color mapping, but no projector intrinsics, extrinsics, or radiometric parameters are ever estimated.

## Method

The core idea is to replace explicit projector modeling with a frozen color MLP c: R³→[0,1]ᵏ that maps 3D positions to their projected pattern intensities, trained once from a LookUp3D-style calibration sweep using a multiresolution hash encoding and compact MLP (compressing a 50–121 GB lookup table to ~49 MB). During per-scene reconstruction, c is held fixed while an SDF MLP f (scene geometry) and a 2D ambient MLP a (global illumination) are jointly optimized via differentiable volume rendering. The rendered pixel is computed as Ĩ_pattern = Ĩ(I_white − I_ambient) + I_ambient, where I_ambient is constrained ≤ I_white via a sigmoid-gated multiplication to prevent gradient explosion. Training minimizes a weighted combination of pattern loss, normalized LookUp loss, mask loss, eikonal loss, and sparsity loss over 10,000 iterations, with the SDF's inductive bias providing implicit outlier rejection that pixelwise decoders lack.

## Results

NeLU3D achieves mean absolute depth errors of 0.33–3.87 mm and disparity errors of 0.84–8.13 px across six objects, dramatically outperforming all baselines on suboptimal patterns (random, Hilbert) where competitors produce 40–300 mm depth errors. With well-designed patterns (11 Gray codes, a la carte), NeLU3D remains competitive, achieving 0.33 mm / 0.84 px on the keyboard (best overall) and 9.17 mm / 25.43 px on the teapot (where TurboSL leads at 2.90 mm / 6.41 px). In terms of efficiency, NeLU3D requires 3.7–4.4 GB VRAM and 420–562 s per reconstruction, compared to TurboSL's 4.3–5.2 GB and 1020–2468 s.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NeLU3D wins on 5 of 6 evaluated objects against all three baselines, with the most dramatic margins on suboptimal patterns: on the 'first aid' object with 3 random patterns, NeLU3D achieves 0.53 mm depth error versus 122.00 mm (TurboSL), 13.27 mm (LookUp3D), and 298.44 mm (ZNCC). Against ZNCC, NeLU3D is superior on every object by 1–2 orders of magnitude in depth error, as ZNCC's pixelwise correlation decoding is highly sensitive to noise and pattern quality. Against LookUp3D, NeLU3D wins on 5/6 objects; the sole exception is the teapot with a la carte patterns where LookUp3D (5.70 mm) slightly beats NeLU3D (9.17 mm). Against TurboSL, NeLU3D wins on 5/6 objects; the teapot with a la carte patterns is the one case where TurboSL (2.90 mm) outperforms NeLU3D (9.17 mm), suggesting that when patterns are specifically optimized for the setup, the explicit projector modeling in TurboSL provides an advantage. The paper also notes that NeLU3D requires less GPU memory (3.7–4.4 GB vs. 4.3–5.2 GB) and roughly 2–4× less training time than TurboSL per reconstruction.

## Relation to Prior Work

NeLU3D sits at the intersection of two recent structured light advances: TurboSL's neural inverse rendering pipeline (SDF + volumetric rendering to fit scene geometry and appearance) and LookUp3D's data-driven lookup table that maps 3D positions to projected colors, thereby bypassing explicit projector calibration. The key architectural contribution is compressing LookUp3D's memory-intensive, non-differentiable lookup table into a multiresolution hash-encoded MLP, making it differentiable and compact enough to plug directly into TurboSL's optimization loop. This integration eliminates the need for projector pose registration, radiometric calibration, blur-kernel learning, and careful pattern design that both TurboSL and traditional SL pipelines require.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By removing the projector from the explicit imaging model, NeLU3D makes structured light scanning viable with extremely low-cost ($50) and even analog projectors, broadening accessibility beyond well-calibrated lab setups. The method achieves sub-millimeter accuracy with as few as three monochromatic patterns plus a white flash, and handles suboptimal patterns (random, Hilbert) where prior methods produce noisy or distorted surfaces. Open questions include the ~10-minute-per-frame training time (not real-time), restriction to the calibrated volume, inability to handle transparent or mirror surfaces, and the need to recalibrate the color MLP whenever patterns change. Future directions noted by the authors include bypassing camera calibration entirely and incorporating noise modeling for very short exposures.
