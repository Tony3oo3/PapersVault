## Problem

Existing fall detection datasets are small, staged, and annotated under heterogeneous label schemes, making cross-dataset comparison and evaluation of real-world generalization impossible. No standardized benchmark quantifies how models trained on controlled laboratory recordings transfer to genuine accidents, and the clinically critical sustained fallen state (a person unable to get up) is systematically underrepresented because staged actors stand up immediately. Additionally, the potential of large multimodal LLMs for fall detection remains unexplored.

## Input / Output

Input is RGB video at varying resolutions (240p–720p) and frame rates (16–30 fps); the synthetic component is uniformly 1280×720 at 16 fps in 5-second clips. Output is frame-level 16-class labels comprising nine dynamic actions (fall, sit down, lie down, stand up, walk, kneel down, squat down, crawl, jump), six static states (fallen, sitting, lying, standing, kneeling, squatting), and an 'other' catch-all. These dense per-frame annotations support both clip-level classification (with binary fall/fallen subtasks) and timeline segmentation evaluated via F1@IoU and edit distance.

## Method

The core contribution is a unified benchmark built by manually re-annotating eight public staged datasets frame-by-frame under a single clinically motivated 16-class taxonomy, curating 818 genuine accident videos from OOPS as a held-out in-the-wild test set, and generating 12k synthetic videos with Wan 2.2 using structured prompts that vary demographics (six age groups, seven OMB ethnicity categories), 30 fall mechanisms, environments, and camera angles. For evaluation, they fine-tune VideoMAE-K400 (87M parameters) under four training configurations (single dataset, unified staged, synthetic-only, combined) and compare against zero-shot Qwen3-VL-8B and InternVL3.5-8B prompted with the 16-class taxonomy. Domain shift is analyzed via frozen I3D, VideoMAE, and SigLIP2 embeddings with t-SNE/h-NNE projections and pairwise Fréchet Video Distances. The key empirical finding is that synthetic data with explicit extended post-fall ground contact bridges the staged-to-wild gap more effectively than staged data alone, and that fallen-state recognition specifically requires task-specific training that zero-shot MLLMs lack despite comparable fall-event detection performance.

## Results

On the held-out OF-In-the-Wild test set (genuine accident videos), zero-shot MLLMs (Qwen3-VL-8B, InternVL3.5-8B) achieve Fall F1 of 67.8 and 68.0 respectively, comparable to the best fine-tuned VideoMAE-K400 (St+Sy: 65.6). However, on the clinically critical Fallen state, fine-tuned VMAE trained on OF-Synthetic reaches Fallen F1 of 42.3, substantially outperforming both MLLMs (29.2 and 31.0). The upper-bound configuration (St+Sy+ItW training) reaches Fall F1 of 76.0 and Fallen F1 of 53.6, indicating substantial headroom. On timeline segmentation (F1@50), OF-Syn-trained ASQuery achieves 27.60 on OF-ItW versus 20.26 for OF-Staged-only, confirming synthetic data bridges the domain gap more effectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares fine-tuned VideoMAE-K400 (87M params) against two zero-shot 8B-parameter MLLMs (Qwen3-VL-8B and InternVL3.5-8B) on the OF-In-the-Wild test set. On fall-event detection, the MLLMs slightly edge out the best fine-tuned model: Qwen3-VL achieves Fall F1 of 67.8 and InternVL3.5 achieves 68.0, versus 65.6 for VMAE (St+Sy) — a margin of only 2–2.4 F1 points. On the clinically critical fallen state, however, the fine-tuned VMAE trained on OF-Synthetic (Fallen F1: 42.3) outperforms both MLLMs by 11–13 F1 points (Qwen: 29.2, InternVL: 31.0), because zero-shot models systematically confuse fallen with lying (95–97% of fallen instances misclassified as lying). The paper concludes that fallen-state recognition requires task-specific training that large-scale pre-training alone does not provide, and that the 87M-parameter fine-tuned model achieves this at a fraction of the computational cost of 8B-parameter MLLMs, making it more suitable for edge deployment in care environments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by unifying eight previously fragmented staged fall-detection datasets (CMDFall, UP Fall, Le2i, GMDCSA, EDF, OCCU, CAUCA Fall, MCFD) under a single frame-wise 16-class taxonomy, a step no prior work has attempted at scale. It departs from the binary fall/no-fall formulation dominant in the literature by introducing a clinically motivated fall/fallen distinction, and extends the evaluation paradigm from in-domain accuracy to a standardized staged→wild protocol with a held-out in-the-wild test set. The synthetic component repurposes the Wan 2.2 video diffusion model as a controlled-diversity data generator, bridging the domain gap between laboratory recordings and uncontrolled environments without the ethical barriers of recording real elderly falls.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark addresses a critical gap: no prior protocol quantified how fall-detection models transfer from staged to genuine accident settings, and the heterogeneous label schemes across existing datasets made cross-dataset comparison impossible. The finding that zero-shot 8B MLLMs systematically confuse the clinically critical fallen state with lying (95–97% misclassification) while fine-tuned 87M-parameter models trained on synthetic data with explicit post-fall contact do substantially better has direct implications for deployment decisions in care environments. The main open question is the fallen-state bottleneck itself: even with in-domain training, Fallen F1 reaches only 53.6%, far below Fall F1 (76.0%), meaning long-lie alerts will still be missed in practice. Cross-demographic generalization (age, BMI, ethnicity) and real-time edge deployment remain underexplored.
