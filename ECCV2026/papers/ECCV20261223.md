## Problem

MLLMs fail to allocate sufficient cross-modal attention to safety-critical image regions when harmful intent is embedded in images, causing them to generate unsafe responses to multimodal jailbreaks. Existing inference-time defenses either lack generalizability across diverse attack patterns or incur substantial overhead from iterative response refinement and heavy pre-deployment calibration, leaving a gap for a method that is both robust and efficient.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a frozen MLLM at inference time, taking an RGB image and a text query as input and producing a single text response (refusal or helpful answer). Internally, it first generates a short text summary (visual context) of the image via a forward pass, then uses only the first N=3 response tokens of a reformulated query for risk scoring before steering the original query's activations at the last layer. No model weights are modified; all intervention occurs through activation manipulation during decoding.

## Method

MoRAS (Multimodal Risk-Adaptive Steering) is a three-stage inference-time defense. Stage 1 (Vision-Aware Query Reformulation) appends a generated visual context and a safety prompt to the original query, strengthening cross-modal attention to safety-critical image regions and improving representational separability between safe and unsafe inputs (measured via Fisher Discriminant Ratio). Stage 2 (Exponentially Weighted Risk Evaluation) generates N=3 tokens from the reformulated query, computes cosine similarity between the exponentially weighted output distributions and precomputed unsafe prototypes, and maps this similarity to a continuous risk score in (0,1) via a sigmoid centered at a calibrated baseline. Stage 3 (Scaled Activation Steering) computes a per-token refusal vector as the difference between the unsafe prototype activation and the current activation of the original query, then adds the risk-score-scaled vector to the last-layer activations for the first N tokens. This adaptive scaling means benign queries receive near-zero intervention while high-risk queries receive maximal steering toward refusal, eliminating the need for per-model hyperparameter tuning of steering strength and requiring only ~150 calibration samples.

## Results

On LLaVA-1.5-7B, MoRAS achieves the lowest attack success rate (ASR) across all five jailbreak benchmarks: 7.6% (SPA-VL), 2.8% (FigStep), 2.6% (MM-Safety), 6.4% (JOOD), and 14.3% (VAA), while preserving utility scores identical to the vanilla model (GQA 61.9, MM-Vet 30.5, Sci-QA 69.5, MME-P 1505.1, MME-C 355.7). The paper reports an average 19.4% reduction in ASR, 126.6× less pre-deployment overhead, and 1.6× faster inference throughput compared to prior inference-time defenses. These gains generalize across four MLLM backbones (LLaVA-1.5-7B/13B, LLaVA-OneVision-7B, Qwen-VL-Chat, InternLM-XComposer-2.5), where MoRAS consistently achieves the best or near-best ASR while maintaining vanilla-level utility.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoRAS outperforms all six baselines on the majority of safety benchmarks. Against prompt-based methods, CoCA achieves low ASR on SPA-VL (10.9%) and JOOD (13.5%) but remains vulnerable on FigStep (51.6%) and MM-Safety (19.7%), while FigStep-prompting is weak on its own attack type (52.0% ASR on FigStep) and MM-Safety (26.8%). Among response-refinement methods, ETA is competitive on FigStep (7.8%) and VAA (12.1%) but still trails MoRAS on SPA-VL (17.0% vs. 7.6%) and JOOD (17.1% vs. 6.4%), and both ECSO and ETA incur substantial inference latency from iterative regeneration. Steering-based baselines show the most limited generalizability: AutoSteer drops to 55.0% ASR on FigStep and 37.6% on MM-Safety, while ASTRA fails on SPA-VL (41.5%) and JOOD (44.7%), both far above MoRAS. Critically, MoRAS is the only method that simultaneously achieves the lowest ASR on all five benchmarks while preserving utility scores at vanilla levels, whereas every baseline degrades at least one utility metric (e.g., CoCA drops MM-Vet from 30.5 to 28.9, AutoSteer drops GQA from 61.9 to 59.4).

## Relation to Prior Work

MoRAS extends the activation-steering paradigm for LLM safety (Arditi et al., 2024, who showed refusal is mediated by a single direction) to the multimodal setting, but departs from prior multimodal steering methods (AutoSteer, ASTRA) in two key ways: it makes the steering magnitude risk-adaptive per query rather than fixed, and it augments the input with concise visual contexts to strengthen cross-modal attention before risk estimation. It also positions itself against safety prompting (CoCA, FigStep) and response refinement (ECSO, ETA) by showing that these approaches either fail to improve representational separability (FDR) or incur prohibitive inference latency, motivating a single-pass steering solution with minimal calibration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 126.6× reduction in pre-deployment overhead (50+100 calibration samples vs. thousands) and 1.6× inference speedup make risk-adaptive steering practically deployable for production MLLMs, addressing a key barrier to adoption of prior steering methods. The finding that safety prompts alone do not improve FDR—i.e., telling the model to be safe does not make it attend to safety-critical image regions—challenges a common assumption in prompt-based defenses and suggests that visual grounding must be explicitly strengthened. Open questions include whether the visual-context generation step itself can be adversarially manipulated, whether the approach extends to video or multi-image inputs, and whether the residual vulnerability to white-box VAA attacks (14.3% ASR on LLaVA-1.5-7B) can be closed without additional overhead.
