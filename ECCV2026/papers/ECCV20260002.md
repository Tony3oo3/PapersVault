## Problem

While Unified Multimodal Models (UMMs) integrate image understanding and generation in a single architecture, it remains unclear whether specific capabilities learned in one task genuinely transfer to the other, or whether observed benchmark gains merely reflect indirect effects like additional data or regularization. Prior analyses rely on aggregate benchmark improvements, making it difficult to isolate capability-level cross-task interaction. This paper introduces transferability as a direct, actionable probe of cross-task interaction and asks whether it depends on architectural design.

## Input / Output

For understanding tasks, the model receives an RGB image paired with a text question (e.g., 'How many objects appear in this image?') and outputs a text answer; for generation tasks, it receives a text prompt (e.g., 'A photo of 5 cats') and produces a generated image. Evaluation of generated images is performed externally: an object detector (Mask2Former) verifies object counts and spatial positions, while an OCR model (GLM-OCR) extracts rendered text for comparison against the prompt. The synthetic spatial-relation and text datasets each contain 200K samples constructed from ImageNet class images or rendered Markdown documents, respectively.

## Method

The paper conducts controlled LoRA-based fine-tuning experiments across four representative UMM architectures (Lumina-DiMOO, Janus-Pro, BAGEL, BLIP3-o) using counting as a probe capability, training only on understanding or only on generation and evaluating on the other task. The key empirical finding is that cross-task transferability is architecture-dependent: models with a fully shared transformer backbone and a unified visual encoder (Lumina-DiMOO) exhibit the strongest bidirectional transfer, while loosely coupled designs (separate encoders, MoT, or decoupled transformers) show little or none. Leveraging this, the paper proposes a practical training strategy: to improve a target generative capability (counting, spatial relation, or text rendering), one fine-tunes the corresponding understanding task rather than the generation objective directly, allowing the capability to transfer into generation while avoiding the distribution shift that degrades image quality (measured via FID and Inception Score). This approach achieves comparable or better capability-specific generative accuracy than direct generation fine-tuning while preserving overall generative quality and general multimodal understanding performance.

## Results

On counting transferability (Table 1), Lumina-DiMOO (shared transformer + unified encoder) achieves the strongest bidirectional transfer: und→gen improves generation accuracy from 48.0% to 57.0% (+9.0) and reduces MAD from 1.11 to 0.83; gen→und improves understanding accuracy from 22.0% to 30.0% (+8.0). In the practical strategy comparison (Table 2), und→gen matches gen→gen on counting accuracy (57.0%) but substantially preserves image quality (IS 17.55 vs. 15.29; FID 31.47 vs. 52.51). For spatial relation, und→gen gains +7.0% accuracy with lower FID (30.95) compared to gen→gen (+13.0%, FID 32.28). General multimodal benchmarks (POPE, MMBench, MMMU, MME) remain largely unchanged after capability-focused understanding training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four representative UMM architectures on counting transferability. Lumina-DiMOO (shared transformer + unified encoder) dominates with the strongest bidirectional transfer: +9.0% generation accuracy and +8.0% understanding accuracy from cross-task training. BAGEL (Mixture-of-Transformers) shows moderate bidirectional transfer (+5.0% und→gen, +1.0% gen→und). BLIP3-o (separate transformer with DiT) exhibits unidirectional transfer only in the und→gen direction (+6.0%), with no gain in the reverse. Janus-Pro (shared transformer + separate encoders) is the outlier: und→gen training actually degrades generation accuracy by 6.0%, and gen→und increases MAD by 0.64, indicating no meaningful transfer. In the practical strategy comparison on Lumina-DiMOO, und→gen matches gen→gen on counting accuracy (57.0%) while achieving substantially lower FID (31.47 vs. 52.51) and higher Inception Score (17.55 vs. 15.29), confirming that transfer-based training preserves generative quality better than direct fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against prior work on understanding-generation interactions in UMMs, which typically examines synergy through aggregate benchmark shifts (e.g., MMMU or GenEval scores changing after adding a second task). It departs from that approach by introducing 'transferability' as a controlled, capability-level probe: does training counting in understanding improve counting in generation, and vice versa? The architectural taxonomy it draws on (shared transformer, Mixture-of-Transformers, decoupled transformer) follows the survey by Zhao et al. [109], and the distribution-shift concern motivating the practical strategy connects to the fine-tuning literature for diffusion models [12,47,59]. The paper does not propose a new architecture or training objective; rather, it repurposes the existing UMM architectures (Lumina-DiMOO, Janus-Pro, BAGEL, BLIP3-o) as experimental substrates to isolate and quantify cross-task knowledge flow.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that training a capability through the understanding objective can match or approach direct generation fine-tuning on capability-specific accuracy while substantially reducing distribution shift (lower FID, preserved IS)—offers UMM practitioners a low-cost, quality-preserving route to inject targeted skills without re-running expensive post-training alignment. The architecture-dependency finding (strongest transfer in fully shared backbones with unified encoders) provides a design principle for future UMMs. Open questions remain: whether the oscillatory joint-training dynamics observed here persist at pre-training scale, whether the capability-dependent transfer direction (und→gen for structural tasks, gen→und for fine-grained tasks) generalizes beyond the three capabilities tested, and whether the 7B–8B post-training setting captures the full picture of cross-task interaction.
