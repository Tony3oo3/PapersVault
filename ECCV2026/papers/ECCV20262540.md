## Problem

Despite rapid progress in video generation, no existing benchmark rigorously probes whether video models can perform true multimodal reasoning over arbitrary combinations of text, image, audio, and video contexts. Prior benchmarks rely on straightforward task designs and fragmented scoring metrics that overlook complex reasoning scenarios such as physical simulation, logical inference, and multi-turn interaction, leaving the central question—how far are video models from genuine multimodal reasoning—unanswered.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262540/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

CLVG-Bench provides 1,390 manually curated metadata entries where each test case supplies an arbitrary combination of text instructions, reference images, reference audio, and reference video (including multi-shot and multi-subject videos) as conditioning context. The evaluated model produces a generated video, which is then assessed by a VLM-based judge agent that predicts a set of noticeable weaknesses; these are compared against human-annotated ground-truth weaknesses via a semantic matching function, yielding a normalized 4-element confusion matrix (TP, TN, FP, FN) and natural-language feedback per instance. All metadata is human-created to eliminate copyright and data-contamination risks.

## Method

The paper introduces two coupled components: CLVG-Bench, a benchmark organized into 6 categories (Element Editing, Partial Reference, Script Continuation, Physical Simulation, Perception, Logical Reasoning) spanning 47 subcategories, and the Adaptive Video Evaluator (AVE), a prompt-optimization framework for scalable video evaluation. AVE's key innovation is a semantic matching function that reframes open-ended evaluation as a semantic set-matching problem—using a VLM-based 'Match' agent to compute semantic intersections and set differences between predicted and ground-truth weakness lists—rather than relying on deterministic exact matching. This function produces both a normalized instance-level confusion matrix and interpretable textual feedback (listing omissions and hallucinations). The judge's system prompt is iteratively refined via APO techniques (TextGrad or GEPA) under a fixed cost budget, maximizing a global metric (e.g., F1 or Recall-minus-FPR) aggregated over a validation set, after which the optimal prompt is frozen and evaluated on an unseen test set. The optimized prompt generalizes zero-cost across different judge models, and the framework supports a novel multi-turn interactive generation task where the model must iteratively predict video scripts in response to user feedback.

## Results

On CLVG-Bench, the strongest proprietary model (Seedance 2.0) achieves pass rates of 61.25% (Element Editing), 67.65% (Script Continuation), and 63.54% (Physical Simulation), but drops sharply to 21.25% on Logical Reasoning. Open-source models lag dramatically: the best open-source model (UniVideo) reaches only 8.1% on Element Editing and 3.75% on Logical Reasoning. In multi-turn interactive generation, even top models achieve <10% first-turn success and 0% on the second turn, with final pass rates of only 26–34% after VLM-assisted context organization. The Adaptive Video Evaluator (AVE) with SemanticMatch reaches a peak of 70.7% MCC on Perception tasks and 0.707 Kendall's τ correlation with human judgement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary models, Seedance 2.0 leads on Element Editing (61.25%), Script Continuation (67.65%), and Physical Simulation (63.54%), while Sora 2 outperforms it on Logical Reasoning (38.96% vs. 21.25%) and Perception (45.86% vs. 43.37%), suggesting complementary strengths. Seedance 1.5 and Veo 3.1 Fast trail the top two by 10–20 points on most categories, with Veo 3.1 Fast closing the gap on Partial Reference (46.38% vs. 35.47% for Seedance 1.5). Open-source models are substantially weaker: Wan 2.2 14B (46.67% P.R., 39.00% P.S.) is the strongest open-source baseline but still lags Seedance 2.0 by 13–25 points, while UniVideo and LTX-2 achieve single-digit pass rates on most tasks. The most striking gap is on Logical Reasoning, where even the best proprietary model (Sora 2 at 38.96%) falls far short, and all open-source models score below 12%. In multi-turn interaction, all models fail completely on the second turn without VLM assistance, and even with Seed 2.0 Pro augmentation, final pass rates remain modest (26–34%), underscoring that no current baseline handles iterative reasoning-based generation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Automatic Prompt Optimization (APO) lineage—specifically TextGrad and GEPA—for its Adaptive Video Evaluator, repurposing iterative prompt refinement from NLP QA tasks into open-ended video quality assessment via a novel semantic set-matching function. Conceptually, it departs from prior video benchmarks (VBench, UniVBench, VBVR-Bench) that evaluate tasks in isolation or focus on perceptual fidelity, by unifying element editing, physical simulation, logical reasoning, and multi-turn interaction under a single 'Context Learning in Video Generation' formulation that accepts arbitrary combinations of text, image, audio, and video inputs. The multi-turn interactive generation task, where models must iteratively revise scripts based on user feedback, has no direct precedent in existing video generation benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a critical and quantified gap: even SOTA proprietary models (Seedance 2.0) achieve only 21% on logical reasoning and effectively 0% on second-turn interactive generation, revealing that current video models lack intrinsic physical and causal reasoning rather than merely visual fidelity. The finding that external VLM assistance can make a weaker model (Seedance 1.5) surpass a stronger one (Seedance 2.0) on logical reasoning suggests the bottleneck is in understanding, not generation—a diagnostic that should redirect architectural research toward tighter comprehension-generation integration. Open questions include whether these gaps close with scale alone, whether the multi-turn interaction failure reflects a fundamental architectural limitation, and whether AVE's prompt-optimized evaluators remain reliable as video models improve.
