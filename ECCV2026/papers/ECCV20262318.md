## Problem

4D volumetric videos via Gaussian Splatting enable free-viewpoint rendering but lack interactive part-level motion editing. Existing methods are restricted to single objects, assume rigid articulation, or require manual annotations and known meshes, making them inapplicable to complex multi-object real-world scenes with non-rigid parts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262318/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes synchronized multi-view RGB video as input and produces an editable 4D volumetric scene where users manipulate sparse 6-DoF part handles to perform motion edits (looping, ghosting, echo, speed control, isolation, compositing) and spatial edits (scaling, rotation). Part initialization uses temporal difference images fed to SAM2 in segment-everything mode, and the final representation consists of a single canonical 3DGS with per-primitive skinning weights and per-timestep handle transformations, rendered in real time on an RTX 3090.

## Method

MotionSplicer adapts Linear Blend Skinning to 3D Gaussian Splatting, representing each scene as a canonical set of Gaussians with time-invariant skinning weights and sparse 6-DoF handles. For annotation-free part discovery, it applies SAM2 to temporal difference images (rather than raw frames) to isolate moving regions, lifts the 2D masks to 3D via a logit parameter on each Gaussian, then resolves over/under-segmentation and multi-view inconsistencies through an adaptive Unify-and-Split (U&S) process that merges groups with small relative motion and splits under-segmented groups along PCA-estimated axes. For motion tracking, skinning weights are predicted by an NGP-style multiresolution hash grid with a 2-layer MLP (rather than per-Gaussian parameters), enforcing spatial smoothness, and a streaming temporal consistency refinement loss re-evaluates randomly sampled past frames to maintain coherent part decomposition across the full sequence. The entire optimization is self-supervised, driven by multi-view photometric losses (L1 + SSIM) back-propagated into both handle transformations and the shared feature grid.

## Results

On real 360° scenes (Panoptic + own captures), MotionSplicer achieves 27.36 PSNR, 0.892 SSIM, and 0.1683 LPIPS for reconstruction, and a GPT-EE score of 4.7/5 for edited motion, substantially outperforming all baselines (GPT-EE: 1.7–2.0). On synthetic scenes (OmniGibson), it reaches 34.49 PSNR and 0.0428 LPIPS with GPT-EE 4.7, again leading all competitors. On synthetic objects (Sketchfab), SC-GS achieves higher reconstruction PSNR (40.04 vs. 36.52), but MotionSplicer matches or exceeds it on perceptual editing quality (GPT-EE 4.8 vs. 3.7; KVD 0.61 vs. 0.95). The only metric where MotionSplicer trails is Flow-Warp, which the authors attribute to occlusion-masking ambiguity in optical flow estimation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MotionSplicer outperforms all four baselines on reconstruction quality (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) and perceptual editing fidelity (GPT-EE, KVD) across all three scene categories, with the largest margins on real scenes where GPT-EE jumps from 1.7–2.0 (baselines) to 4.7. SC-GS achieves the highest reconstruction PSNR on isolated synthetic objects (40.04 vs. 36.52) due to its dense control-point optimization, but its GPT-EE drops to 3.7 and it fails on multi-object scenes (GPT-EE 1.0). RigGS matches MotionSplicer's GPT-EE on synthetic objects (4.8) but degrades sharply on real scenes (1.7) because its single hierarchical skeleton cannot handle multiple independent moving parts. RSRD produces the lowest Flow-Warp error (0.0000 on synthetic objects) but this is an artifact of its monocular rigid tracking failing to move anything; its GPT-EE on real scenes is only 1.8. The authors note that Flow-Warp is the sole metric where MotionSplicer does not lead, attributing this to occlusion-masking ambiguity in RAFT-based flow estimation rather than actual motion quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

MotionSplicer builds on 3D Gaussian Splatting for scene representation and Linear Blend Skinning for part-level motion decomposition, but departs from prior part-editing methods (SC-GS, RigGS, ArtiGS, RSRD) that are restricted to single objects, rigid articulation, monocular input, or manual annotations. Its key conceptual departure is a motion-aware part discovery pipeline that feeds temporal difference images into SAM2 (rather than raw semantic frames) and resolves multi-view inconsistencies via a 3D unify-and-split process, followed by a time-invariant NGP-style feature grid with temporal consistency refinement to learn spatially and temporally coherent skinning weights. This positions the work as a generalization from single-object, rigid-part editing to annotation-free, non-rigid, multi-object 4D scene editing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By enabling annotation-free part-level motion editing in complex multi-object 4D scenes, MotionSplicer opens a path toward interactive 4D content for world models, XR, robot simulation, and game engines where captured motion must be selectively manipulated. Key limitations remain: the method requires synchronized multi-view video, performs per-scene optimization (computationally expensive for long/dense sequences), and handles only moderate non-rigidity via LBS—highly deformable dynamics (fluids, loose cloth, topology changes) are out of scope. Open questions include whether a feed-forward, editable 4D model can replace per-scene optimization, whether monocular settings are feasible, and how to incorporate richer deformation priors (e.g., spring bones, material-aware dynamics) into the skinning framework.
