## Problem

Modern video diffusion backbones are trained on fixed-length clips (e.g., 57 frames) and deployed on long-horizon videos via chunked sliding-window inference, where each chunk is processed largely independently. This forces the model to re-infer latent scene factors and appearance cues at every window shift, producing visible temporal discontinuities and cross-chunk drift that make relighting unreliable for shots spanning tens of seconds. The paper addresses this gap by reframing long-horizon relighting as a temporally conditioned latent domain translation problem with explicit cross-chunk continuity.

## Input / Output

The method takes a long-horizon RGB video, a target equirectangular HDRI environment map, and an optional warm-start prompt (a single relit image or short video generated by an external controllable model such as Nano Banana Pro or ChatGPT) as input. The video is encoded via a Cosmos 8×8×8 VAE tokenizer into compressed latents (F/8 × H/8 × W/8 × 16 channels) and processed in 57-frame chunks. Output is a temporally consistent relit RGB video plus per-frame intrinsic G-buffers (basecolor, normal, metallic roughness, depth, specular) produced by a two-stage inverse-then-forward pipeline, with the warm-start prompt consumed only to initialize the first chunk's target-domain state.

## Method

HorizonRelight builds on a Cosmos DiT backbone fine-tuned under the EDM denoising objective with a two-stage inverse/forward architecture. The core training mechanism is masked target-domain self-conditioning: during training, an arbitrary temporal mask is applied to the target-domain latent (G-buffer or relit RGB), and the model is trained to reconstruct the full target from the source latent plus the partially observed target, learning to continue from partial context. At inference, the last b latent frames of each chunk's target-domain prediction are propagated as the conditioning anchor for the next chunk, enabling chained generation where the relit state is continued rather than re-inferred at every boundary. A warm-start prompt (externally generated relit image or video) initializes the first chunk's target-domain appearance, providing a general interface for prompt-based relighting and appearance editing. The propagation mechanism is applied symmetrically to both the inverse stage (intrinsic latent domains) and the forward stage (relit RGB latent domain), and the approach is explicitly non-autoregressive: source video and G-buffers control geometry and content, while the propagated relit frames serve only as a boundary color cue.

## Results

On the synthetic dataset, HorizonRelight reduces boundary MSE for the relit output from 0.1048 (DiffusionRenderer) to 0.0729 (30% improvement) and sequence MSE from 0.1063 to 0.0906, with consistent gains across all G-buffer channels (e.g., Metallic: 0.2050→0.0542). On 100 in-the-wild YouTube clips, the method achieves 0.00080 MSE / 0.934 SSIM (continuous) and 0.00048 MSE / 0.954 SSIM (duplicate-reverse) at chunk boundaries, outperforming all five baselines. On held-out Multi-Illumination reconstruction, the warm-start variant reaches 14.39 PSNR / 0.679 SSIM / 0.163 MAE, within 0.33 dB of UniRelight (14.72 PSNR).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DiffusionRenderer (DR), HorizonRelight wins decisively on every metric: boundary MSE drops from 0.1048 to 0.0729 on the synthetic set (30% reduction) and from 0.0327 to 0.00080 on in-the-wild clips (97.6% reduction), confirming that chained latent propagation eliminates the chunk-boundary discontinuities that plague DR's independent-chunk inference. Against UniRelight, the method achieves a 94% reduction in continuous boundary MSE (0.00080 vs. 0.0131) and a 94.7% reduction in duplicate-reverse MSE (0.00048 vs. 0.00912), while on direct relighting fidelity (Multi-Illumination) it trails UniRelight by only 0.33 dB PSNR (14.39 vs. 14.72) and 0.029 SSIM, a small gap attributable to UniRelight's joint albedo modeling. The ablation row (Ours-no-anc.) shows that removing the target-domain anchor degrades boundary MSE to 0.0254, nearly matching DR, confirming that the propagated relit context is the primary source of the consistency gain. Against the shorter-window baselines (TC-Light, Light-A-Video, RelightVid), HorizonRelight matches or exceeds their boundary SSIM (0.934 vs. 0.937/0.853/0.760 continuous) while operating at the same 57-frame window, demonstrating that the consistency advantage is not simply a function of shorter clips.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the two-stage inverse/forward video relighting pipeline established by DiffusionRenderer and the joint decomposition-synthesis formulation of UniRelight, both of which operate on fixed ~57-frame clips. The key conceptual departure is reframing long-horizon relighting as temporally conditioned latent domain translation: rather than treating each sliding-window chunk as an independent short-clip generation, the model is trained with masked target-domain self-conditioning to learn continuation from partial propagated context, and at inference the previous chunk's ending latents are chained forward. The warm-start prompting mechanism draws an explicit analogy to prefix-based adaptation in decoder-only LLMs, where an externally generated relit anchor initializes the target-domain state for the first chunk.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work addresses a concrete deployment gap: film shots routinely span 5–25 seconds, well beyond the 57-frame windows that current diffusion backbones can process, and the resulting chunk-boundary discontinuities make existing relighting pipelines unreliable for production use. The chained-propagation plus warm-start design is a clean, general mechanism that the authors demonstrate also supports style transfer and appearance editing over long horizons, suggesting broader applicability beyond relighting. The main open limitation is fine-detail degradation beyond roughly five chunks (~300 frames), and the paper acknowledges that extending the usable horizon will require better long-horizon training data coverage. Whether the approach generalizes to even longer continuous takes or to more complex multi-shot editing workflows remains to be tested.
