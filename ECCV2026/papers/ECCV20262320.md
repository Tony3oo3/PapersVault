## Problem

LiDAR simulation for autonomous driving relies on raycasting (e.g., Möller-Trumbore) to compute millions of ray-triangle intersections per frame, creating a severe computational bottleneck for real-time or large-scale simulation. While rasterization and generative-model alternatives exist, they either sacrifice geometric accuracy or produce unrealistic outputs on novel scenes, leaving no efficient, physics-based, and scalable method that matches raycasting fidelity without its cost.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a triangular mesh of the simulated scene in world coordinates together with a LiDAR sensor specification (azimuth-elevation grid resolution, vertical FOV, range limit, and channel count, e.g., 16 to 350 channels). It produces a 3D point cloud where each point corresponds to one LiDAR ray direction, with depth computed exactly via gnomonic projection. Crucially, triangle-point associations are preserved at each intersection, enabling downstream physics-based attributes (material reflectance, range drop-off, Gaussian noise) to be applied identically to conventional raycasting pipelines.

## Method

GnoSphere360 replaces ray-triangle intersection with a three-stage geometric pipeline. Stage 1 projects each triangle onto a unit sphere centered at the sensor, preserving collinearity, then applies range, backface, and elevation culling to discard invisible geometry. Stage 2 bins surviving triangles into candidate-ray sets using their azimuth-elevation bounding boxes, verifying membership with a cheap edge-normal sign test (three dot products) instead of barycentric coordinates. Stage 3 resolves the exact intersection depth per ray via gnomonic projection, reducing to t = (n̂·v₀)/(n̂·S) — only two dot products and one division per ray-triangle pair — with the binning guarantee eliminating the need for a barycentric inside-test. A proxy-occlusion strategy (best-first initialization plus depth-based skipping using the plane offset d) further prunes candidates without loading triangle normals. The entire pipeline is implemented in CUDA with triangle-parallel Stages 1–2 and ray-parallel Stage 3, using Structure-of-Arrays layout for coalesced memory access.

## Results

On the baseline HDL-64E configuration (288K rays, Town03), GnoSphere360 achieves a mean frame time of 2.54 ms, compared to 89.62 ms for CARLA (~35× speedup) and 1.23 ms for OptiX. Under stress-test conditions with hypothetical 256-channel (4.6M rays) and 350-channel (6.3M rays) LiDARs, GnoSphere360 outperforms OptiX by 2.42× (5.60 ms vs 13.54 ms) and 3.65× (17.27 ms vs 62.98 ms) respectively. Geometric fidelity is confirmed by nearest-neighbor analysis: 99.97% of GnoSphere360 points fall within 0.20 m of their OptiX counterparts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CARLA's native LiDAR simulator, GnoSphere360 achieves a ~35× speedup (2.54 ms vs 89.62 ms mean frame time) with dramatically lower variance across both frames (0.070 vs 76.634 ms²) and runs (0.0003 vs 1.122 ms²), indicating far more consistent per-frame performance. Against NVIDIA OptiX (RT-core accelerated, with IAS refitting), GnoSphere360 is slightly slower at the 288K-ray baseline (2.54 ms vs 1.23 ms, a ~1.3 ms gap), but this gap reverses at higher ray counts: GnoSphere360 is 2.42× faster at 4.6M rays and 3.65× faster at 6.3M rays, where OptiX's BVH traversal and register-heavy kernels degrade. In terms of output fidelity, 99.97% of GnoSphere360 points are within 0.20 m of OptiX points, confirming geometric equivalence. GnoSphere360 also does not rely on prior-frame caching (unlike OptiX's IAS refit), making it a fairer comparison and leaving room for further optimization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant raycasting paradigm (Möller-Trumbore) and its GPU-accelerated variants (NVIDIA OptiX, CARLA's native simulator) by reframing LiDAR simulation as a geometric projection problem rather than a ray-traversal problem. It repurposes gnomonic projection—a classical map-projection technique—to compute ray-triangle intersection depths with only two dot products and one division, eliminating the need for BVH traversal entirely. The approach also distinguishes itself from rasterization-based methods (AADS, cubemap projection) and learning-based generators (LiDARGAN, LiDARGen) by remaining fully physics-based and deterministic, while being the first to eliminate raycasting, rasterization, and generative models simultaneously for LiDAR point cloud generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 3.65× speedup over OptiX at 6.3M rays demonstrates that geometric reformulation scales more favorably than BVH traversal at extreme ray counts, which is directly relevant as LiDAR sensors trend toward higher channel counts. However, all benchmarks use static scenes on a single GPU (RTX 3070), and the paper does not test dynamic objects, multi-sensor configurations, or integration into a full driving simulation loop—key practical gaps for autonomous driving deployment. The authors identify prior-frame caching for Stages 1–2 and physics-based extensions (intensity, noise, drop-off) as the most direct paths to further gains, but these remain unimplemented. Open questions include whether the spherical-projection approach degrades in scenes with highly occluded or concave geometry, and whether the method extends naturally to multi-return or time-of-flight LiDAR models.
