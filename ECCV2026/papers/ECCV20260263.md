## Problem

Existing video understanding benchmarks evaluate models through single final-answer queries or short-window streaming tasks, providing limited visibility into how models maintain and update internal world state (current counts, cumulative identities, event occurrences) over extended video playback. This gap prevents diagnosis of specific state-maintenance failure modes such as failing to update counts when objects enter/leave, violating monotonicity in identity tracking, or missing periodic event boundaries.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260263/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For offline models, videos are truncated to each of 4,576 query points and sampled at 64 frames (or 1 fps for Gemini-3-Flash) with a text counting question appended; for online models, video streams are processed continuously at 1 fps with questions injected at query moments. The model outputs a single numerical count per query point, and the full prediction trajectory across all query points for a question is scored with three complementary metrics: GPA (Gaussian Precision Accuracy, σ = 5% of ground truth), MoC (Monotonicity Consistency for cumulative tasks), and UDA (Update Detection Accuracy at state-change steps).

## Method

SVCBench decomposes spatial-temporal state maintenance into 8 fine-grained subcategories: object counting splits into current-state tracking (O1-Snap, O1-Delta) and cumulative identity tracking (O2-Unique, O2-Gain), while event counting splits into point events (E1-Action, E1-Transit) and span events (E2-Periodic, E2-Episode). The benchmark contains 406 videos with frame-by-frame annotations of 10,071 event occurrences and object state changes, yielding 1,000 QA pairs with 4,576 streaming query points distributed along video timelines. The key design principle is repositioning counting as a minimal, deterministic probe: numerical answers avoid open-ended scoring ambiguity, and multi-point streaming queries expose trajectory-level failures (e.g., decreasing counts in identity tasks, missed updates at state-change moments) that final-answer evaluation cannot surface. Two controlled experiments isolate specific failure mechanisms: an oracle experiment overlaying explicit cycle-count annotations on E2-Periodic videos (showing a 21× GPA improvement, indicating the bottleneck is visual-temporal grounding rather than numerical reasoning), and a 360° rotating-camera experiment demonstrating progressive identity-persistence degradation over 60 reappearances of the same object.

## Results

On SVCBench, the best proprietary model (Gemini-3-Flash) achieves an overall GPA of 37.0, MoC of 73.7, and UDA of 73.8, while human agents reach 96.1/100.0/99.3, exposing a large human-machine gap. The best open-source offline model (Qwen3-VL-8B) scores GPA 31.0 but leads in MoC (84.3), and the strongest online model (Flash-VStream-7B) reaches GPA 23.4. Periodic event counting (E2-Periodic) is the most severe failure mode: the best model (LiveStar) achieves only GPA 11.1 versus 93.2 for humans. An oracle experiment with explicit cycle-count overlays improves Gemini-3-Flash's E2-Periodic GPA from 3.9 to 81.8 (21×), indicating the bottleneck is visual-temporal grounding rather than numerical reasoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Proprietary models (Gemini-3-Flash, Doubao-Seed-1.8) lead in overall GPA (37.0 and 36.2) but trail open-source models in MoC (73.7/77.2 vs. 84.3 for Qwen3-VL-8B), suggesting proprietary models make bolder predictions that violate monotonicity more often. The blind LLM baseline (GPT-4-Turbo) achieves MoC 95.7 without any visual input, confirming that language priors alone can produce monotonic sequences, yet its GPA (18.7) and UDA (4.3) are near-random, proving visual grounding is essential. Among online models, Flash-VStream-7B (GPA 23.4) outperforms the 7B offline Qwen2.5-VL-7B (GPA 19.1), but all online models degrade faster over long trajectories due to state compression. On E2-Periodic, every model collapses (best: LiveStar at GPA 11.1; most below 4) while humans score 93.2, and the oracle annotation experiment (GPA 3.9 → 81.8, a 21× gain) isolates the failure to visual-temporal cycle-boundary detection rather than arithmetic reasoning. The rotating-camera identity experiment shows GPA degrading from ~70 to ~25–36 over 60 cycles for three models, versus a constant 100 for humans, confirming that cross-temporal identity persistence is a fundamental architectural weakness.

## Relation to Prior Work

SVCBench carves new ground by repurposing counting — a well-studied task in benchmarks like TOMATO, CountLLM, and VSI-Bench — as a minimal diagnostic probe for spatial-temporal state maintenance rather than a standalone evaluation target. It departs from existing streaming benchmarks (OVO-Bench, StreamingBench, RTV-Bench) by explicitly decomposing state maintenance into eight subcategories with distinct tracking semantics (current-state vs. cumulative-identity, point vs. span events) and by using long-horizon multi-point queries to observe full prediction trajectories rather than isolated answers. The three complementary metrics (GPA, MoC, UDA) are novel in jointly measuring numerical precision, trajectory monotonicity, and update timing, addressing a gap the authors identify in prior work where different tracking requirements were conflated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a large and actionable gap: the best models achieve ~37 GPA versus 96+ for humans, with periodic event counting (E2-Periodic) near zero for nearly all models. The oracle experiment (21× GPA improvement from explicit cycle-count overlays) provides a clean mechanistic diagnosis — models can reason about cumulative counts but cannot detect cycle boundaries from visual input — pointing to visual-temporal grounding as the specific architectural bottleneck. The rotating-camera identity-persistence experiment further shows that cross-temporal object identity representations degrade over repeated reappearances. Open questions include whether dedicated state-maintenance modules (e.g., explicit memory buffers, cycle detectors) can close the gap, and whether the streaming evaluation protocol generalizes beyond counting to other world-state quantities.
