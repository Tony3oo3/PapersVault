## Problem

Deep learning models for document unwarping and illumination correction are fundamentally bounded by their training data, yet the field has relied for nearly a decade on Doc3D (100K samples at 448×448), which is limited in scale, resolution, and geometric diversity due to its dependence on manual 3D scanning. No existing dataset provides the combination of large scale, high resolution, physically accurate deformations, and rich pixel-perfect annotations (albedo, shading, normals, UV maps) needed to push performance further.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262138/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The baseline model consumes a 512×720 RGB image of a warped document, tightly cropped to its boundaries, and jointly predicts a coarse 45×31 backward unwarping grid and a full-resolution shading map. The dataset itself is rendered at 1024×1440 with 128 path-tracing samples per pixel, and each sample carries pixel-perfect annotations including albedo, shading, surface normals, 3D world coordinates, and UV maps (from which the dense backward mapping is obtained by inversion). A higher-resolution variant uses 1024×1440 input with a 121×177 output grid.

## Method

The core contribution is a fully scalable, physics-based dataset generation pipeline. Deformed paper meshes are produced by simulating A4 sheets in ArcSim under three parameterized scenarios—Pull (vertex displacement for smooth curves), Fold (vertex pulling plus rigid-cylinder creasing), and Fall (gravity-driven draping onto spheres, cylinders, or gables)—yielding 584,708 distinct geometries that preserve the developable-surface constraint of real paper. Each mesh is rendered in Blender's Cycles path tracer with four randomized lighting setups (3-point studio, softbox, rim, natural daylight), a PBR background from MatSynth, and a procedural paper material combining Perlin noise (micro-fibers and macro-waves), Worley noise (micro-creases), and texture-modulated roughness for glossy ink. The baseline model adapts the UVDoc architecture by replacing its 3D grid head with a U-Net-style shading decoder that concatenates encoder features via skip connections, and is trained end-to-end with AdamW and L1 losses on the coarse mapping, shading, and reconstructed image. Training on 1M SyntheticDoc samples alone outperforms all prior SOTA methods on the DocUNet benchmark across both visual and OCR metrics.

## Results

On the DocUNet benchmark, the lightweight baseline trained solely on SyntheticDoc achieves the best scores across all five metrics: MS-SSIM 0.558, LD 5.98, AD 0.252, CER 0.161, and ED 628, outperforming the previous best (DvD) by 1.8% on MS-SSIM and 9.4% on AD. For the combined unwarping + illumination correction task (Table 4), the same model attains MS-SSIM 0.584 and PSNR 12.68, surpassing DocTr (0.496 / 10.79) and DocRES (0.473 / 11.68) on visual quality, though it trails DocTr on OCR metrics (CER 0.149 vs. 0.126, ED 601 vs. 491). Ablations confirm that scaling from 100K to 1M samples yields consistent gains, and increasing input resolution to 1024×1440 further improves AD by over 20% relative to SOTA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's baseline, trained exclusively on SyntheticDoc, outperforms all 15 compared methods on every metric in the unwarping-only evaluation (Table 3). The closest competitors are DvD (MS-SSIM 0.548, AD 0.280) and AADD (MS-SSIM 0.542, AD 0.277), both of which trail the proposed method by roughly 1–2% on MS-SSIM and 9–11% on AD. Earlier grid-based methods like UVDoc (AD 0.315) and DocScanner (AD 0.333) show a larger gap, while older approaches such as DisplacementFlow (AD 0.395) and DocRES (AD 0.470) are substantially worse. In the combined unwarping + illumination correction setting (Table 4), the method wins decisively on visual quality (MS-SSIM 0.584 vs. 0.496 for DocTr, 0.473 for DocRES; PSNR 12.68 vs. 10.79 and 11.68) but loses to DocTr on OCR accuracy (CER 0.149 vs. 0.126; ED 601 vs. 491), suggesting that the lightweight architecture trades some text fidelity for superior photometric restoration. The ablation with 100K samples (MS-SSIM 0.532, AD 0.323) already matches or exceeds most SOTA methods, underscoring that the dataset quality—not just scale—drives the gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the UVDoc architecture [47], reusing its network design but replacing the 3D grid prediction head with a shading prediction module to jointly handle unwarping and illumination correction. Conceptually, it departs from prior synthetic datasets (Doc3D, DocProj, UVDoc) that relied on manual 3D scanning, geometric mesh deformation, or depth-camera capture by instead using ArcSim physics-based simulation to generate developable paper geometries at scale. The rendering pipeline follows Doc3D's use of Blender but elevates it with Cycles path tracing, procedural PBR paper materials (Perlin/Worley noise), and diverse lighting setups to narrow the sim-to-real gap. The core positioning is that dataset scale and physical fidelity, rather than architectural complexity, are the binding constraints on document unwarping performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SyntheticDoc is 10× larger than the long-standing Doc3D standard (1M vs. 100K samples) at 4× the resolution, and its physics-based generation pipeline is fully reproducible and extensible, making it a likely new default training set for the field. The demonstration that a lightweight baseline trained solely on this data outperforms all prior SOTA methods on DocUNet suggests that the community's progress has been bottlenecked by data quality and quantity rather than model design. Key limitations include the 5+ TB storage requirement, exclusion of extreme viewpoints, and the fact that the baseline architecture is intentionally simple—whether larger models can exploit the dataset's full potential remains open. The pipeline's procedural nature also opens paths to multi-view datasets, additional annotations (OCR, layout), and more complex fold scenarios.
