## Problem

One-class classifier (OCC) anomaly detectors assume purely nominal training data, making them highly sensitive to label noise and requiring manual curation. Existing fully unsupervised anomaly detection methods are limited in architectural diversity (converging on patch-based approaches) and underperform OCC methods when training data is clean. This paper addresses both gaps by proposing a universal wrapper that transforms any OCC method into a fully unsupervised one without modifying the underlying detector.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a training image set of RGB images that may contain anomalies (no manual curation required), tested with corruption rates up to 40%. Predicted masks are scaled to 256×256 and center-cropped to 224×224 for evaluation. At inference, the output is identical to the underlying OCC method—an anomaly score and pixel-level segmentation mask—with no architectural changes, so inference latency matches the base detector (and is ~2% faster for memory-bank methods due to a smaller filtered memory bank).

## Method

BAAF splits the training set into n non-overlapping bags, trains a separate unmodified OCC instance on each bag, and has each model predict on all other bags. Predictions are normalized to [0,1], and a two-component Gaussian Mixture Model (biased toward nominal via inverse-anomaly-score weighting) is fitted to each bag's cross-predictions; the crossover point between the two fitted Gaussians serves as a per-bag anomaly threshold. A sample is flagged as anomalous if a majority of the other bags' models score it above their respective thresholds, and the final training set is the majority-vote union across K independent bagging iterations. The key insight is that anomalies are rare and heterogeneous (Eq. 3), so an anomaly overfit by one bag's OCC will not generalize to distinct anomalies in other bags, enabling cross-bag filtering. A final OCC is then trained on the filtered dataset, preserving all properties of the base method (e.g., logical anomaly detection capability) while gaining full unsupervised robustness.

## Results

BAAF(3/4)+PatchCore achieves state-of-the-art fully unsupervised anomaly detection on MvTec AD (I-AUROC 0.992 at 0% corruption, 0.988 at 10%), ViSA (I-AUROC 0.960 at 0%, 0.910 at 10%), and Real-IAD (I-AUROC 0.929 at 0%, 0.904 at 10%), outperforming all existing unsupervised baselines in every metric. Across 8 diverse OCC methods on MvTec AD with 10% training corruptions, BAAF yields an average I-AUROC improvement of +0.171 over the unmodified OCC baselines, with the largest gains for patch-based methods (AnomalyDino +0.280, PatchCore +0.211). On the logical anomaly detection task (MVTec LOCO AD), BAAF(1/4)+PUAD reaches I-AUROC 0.849 at 10% corruption, surpassing both the PUAD baseline (0.811) and all existing unsupervised methods (SoftPatch 0.674, FUN-AD 0.618).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the three existing fully unsupervised baselines (InReaCh, SoftPatch, FUN-AD), BAAF(3/4)+PatchCore wins on every metric across all three datasets at both 0% and 10% corruption. The most dramatic gaps appear on Real-IAD, where FUN-AD drops to I-AUROC 0.593 (0%) and 0.619 (10%) while BAAF maintains 0.929 and 0.904 respectively; SoftPatch is the closest competitor on MvTec AD at 0% corruption (I-AUROC 0.985 vs. 0.992) but falls behind at 10% (0.984 vs. 0.988). In the universality experiment (Table 2), BAAF improves all 8 tested OCC methods at 10% corruption, with gains ranging from +0.056 (DinoAnomaly) to +0.280 (AnomalyDino), averaging +0.171 I-AUROC; at 0% corruption the cost is minimal (average −0.017). For logical anomaly detection on MVTec LOCO AD, BAAF(1/4)+PUAD (I-AUROC 0.849 at 10%) outperforms the PUAD baseline (0.811), EfficientAD (0.790), SoftPatch (0.674), and FUN-AD (0.618), establishing the first fully unsupervised logical anomaly detection result. The only scenario where BAAF does not clearly dominate is the non-i.i.d. worst case (12% same-category anomalies), where I-AUROC drops to 0.978 versus 0.981 for random 10% corruption, though it still far exceeds all unsupervised baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

BAAF repurposes Bootstrap Aggregation (Breiman, 1996) — originally a general overfitting regularizer — as an anomaly-filtering mechanism, grounded in a theoretical corollary that two independently sampled anomalies are unlikely to be both similar and anomalous simultaneously. It departs from prior unsupervised anomaly detection methods (InReaCh, SoftPatch, FUN-AD), which all independently converged on patch-based memory-bank designs, by instead wrapping arbitrary existing OCC architectures (memory-bank, autoencoder, student-teacher, normalizing-flow, contrastive) with a data-filtering loop that requires no architectural modification. The method thus positions unsupervised anomaly detection not as a separate design problem but as a post-hoc transformation of any OCC detector.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical implication is that the OCC and unsupervised anomaly detection communities are now linked: any future improvement in an OCC method (e.g., a better feature extractor or a new logical-anomaly detector) automatically yields a competitive unsupervised method via BAAF, eliminating the need to redesign architectures for noise tolerance. The main limitation is a 5–13× increase in offline training time, which precludes online or real-time fine-tuning scenarios. Open questions include whether the i.i.d. sampling assumption can be further relaxed for highly correlated industrial defects, and whether the GMM-based thresholding can be replaced with lighter-weight criteria to reduce the training overhead.
