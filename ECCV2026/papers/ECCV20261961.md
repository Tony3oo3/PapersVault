## Problem

Under signal-dependent camera noise, dark regions suffer from inherently low SNR, yet standard MSE-trained denoisers compound this difficulty by reconstructing dark pixels up to 6× worse relative to their per-band noise floor. The paper identifies two compounding causes—signal-dependent noise inflating bright-pixel residuals and brightness-correlated Jacobian norms—that make bright regions chronically dominate gradient updates, leaving dark regions systematically under-optimized across all architectures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261961/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a noisy RGB image patch in sRGB space and produces a denoised RGB image patch of the same size, with no change to the network architecture or inference pipeline. During training, band assignments are computed from the clean target's smoothed brightness map (7×7 AvgPool of sRGB luminance) rather than the noisy input, avoiding noise-induced misassignments. Per-band evaluation uses dataset-specific brightness thresholds: [0,0.2]/[0.8,1.0] for SIDD and [0,0.45]/[0.7,1.0] for PolyU, reflecting PolyU's extreme bright-to-dark energy ratio of 18.5×.

## Method

BBRD is a zero-parameter, drop-in MSE replacement that corrects brightness bias through three sequential stages: (1) data-driven GMM partitioning of pixel intensities into K* brightness bands (selected via BIC: K*=5 for SIDD, K*=3 for PolyU) with Gaussian soft assignments (σ_g=0.05) to avoid boundary discontinuities; (2) per-band MSE normalization by precomputed empirical noise variance σ̂²_k, placing all bands on a noise-corrected scale so the truly worst-performing band—not merely the noisiest—is identified; (3) softmax Group DRO (η=5) that recomputes band weights at every training step, dynamically redirecting gradient flow to whichever band is currently lagging. The critical design insight is that normalization must precede DRO: without it, DRO misidentifies high-noise bright bands as worst-performing and amplifies the very bias it aims to correct. The method adds only +1.7% training overhead and incurs zero inference cost, as all band computations and DRO weights are discarded after training.

## Results

On SIDD (NAFNet), BBRD achieves 40.12 dB aggregate PSNR and 0.9442 SSIM, improving over MSE (39.47 dB, 0.9389) by +0.65 dB and +0.0053 SSIM. Per-band, BBRD reaches 37.96 dB on the darkest band [0,0.2] and 38.69 dB on the brightest band [0.8,1.0], the only method among 13 tested alternatives to improve both bands simultaneously. On PolyU, BBRD gains +0.56 dB aggregate PSNR (39.06 vs. 38.50) with per-band improvements of +0.47 dB (dark) and +0.52 dB (bright). Across all 8 architectures (CNNs, Transformers, SSMs), BBRD improves every metric on every backbone with no per-backbone tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BBRD outperforms all 13 baselines on aggregate PSNR and SSIM on both SIDD and PolyU, and is the only method that simultaneously improves both PSNR-D and PSNR-B over MSE. The closest competitor, ℓ₁+FFT [8], achieves 37.70/38.28 dB (SIDD) but still lags BBRD by 0.26 dB on dark and 0.41 dB on bright bands. Heteroscedastic NLL [16]—the most directly related prior work—actually worsens PSNR-D below MSE (37.29 vs. 37.51 dB) because its learned per-pixel σ(x) assigns low uncertainty to dark bands, reinforcing the bias. Focal Loss [20] and OHEM [25] both underperform MSE on PSNR-D (37.19 and 37.47 dB), confirming that difficulty-based reweighting amplifies rather than corrects brightness bias under signal-dependent noise. Frequency-domain methods (FFT [27], Focal Freq. [14], Guided Freq. [2]) and structural losses (SSIM [28], MS-SSIM [29]) operate uniformly over all pixels and fail to close the dark/bright gap, with Focal Freq. [14] performing worst overall (36.10 dB PSNR-D). Charbonnier [3] and Huber [12] improve aggregate PSNR slightly over MSE but sacrifice dark-band quality (37.48 and 37.44 dB respectively), illustrating the persistent trade-off that BBRD eliminates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant trend in image denoising research—progressively upgrading architectures (CNNs → Transformers → SSMs) while keeping MSE/Charbonnier loss fixed—by taking the orthogonal direction of correcting the loss while leaving the model unchanged. It adapts Group DRO from classification (Sagawa et al., 2020) to a pixel-level, within-patch setting, but the critical conceptual departure is the empirical noise-variance normalization that must precede DRO; without it, DRO misidentifies high-noise bright bands as worst-performing and amplifies the very bias it aims to fix. The paper also positions itself against difficulty-based reweighting (Focal Loss, OHEM) and heteroscedastic NLL, showing that these methods inadvertently reinforce brightness bias under signal-dependent noise rather than correcting it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of brightness bias as an architecture-agnostic, loss-level failure mode (confirmed across 8 architectures and 13 baselines) reframes a previously invisible training pathology into a measurable, correctable one, and the proposed per-band PSNR protocol gives the community a standard way to detect it. BBRD's zero-parameter, zero-inference-cost design makes it immediately adoptable by any restoration pipeline, and the LumaSet-700 benchmark fills a gap in balanced-brightness evaluation. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to other restoration tasks (super-resolution, inpainting, RAW-domain denoising), whether GMM-based band partitioning is optimal versus learned boundaries, and whether the findings hold under more extreme noise regimes or multi-frame settings.
