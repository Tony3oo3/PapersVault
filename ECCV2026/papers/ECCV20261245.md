## Problem

Most infrared and visible fusion methods are designed for single images and ignore temporal dependencies, producing jittery video outputs. Existing video fusion methods address this with global spatio-temporal attention, but this dense interaction is computationally expensive and wastes capacity on static background regions that do not require strong cross-modal exchange. The paper asks whether motion cues (optical flow) can be exploited to allocate expensive interaction only where it matters—on moving objects—while preserving static backgrounds with minimal computation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes temporally adjacent infrared and visible video frames (t−1, t, t+1) at 640×480 or 1280×720 resolution, with a frozen SEA-RAFT optical flow estimator run at reduced resolution to provide coarse flow fields. Training uses random 384×384 crops with batch size 32 and gradient accumulation over 40,000 iterations. The output is a fused video frame per input pair, evaluated with seven image-quality metrics (Q_G, Q_M, Q_P, Q_S, Q_CB, VIF, Q^{AB/F}) and one temporal-smoothness metric (MS2R).

## Method

MAVFusion decouples fusion into a Motion-Aware Feature Alignment Module (MAFM) and a Motion-Guided Dual-Interaction Module (MDIM). MAFM performs coarse-to-fine alignment: a frozen SEA-RAFT provides initial flow, then a lightweight residual-flow predictor (depthwise separable convolutions conditioned on cross-modal anchor features) refines the warp; temporally aggregated features are injected through a motion-mask soft gate so only dynamic regions benefit from multi-frame cues. MDIM splits the feature map into non-overlapping patches, selects the top-k (k = ⌊0.3N⌋) patches by motion-mask saliency, and applies an asymmetric sparse attention where queries come only from active patches while keys/values are spatially-averaged global context tokens, reducing complexity from O(N²) to O(kN). Static regions are processed by a lightweight depthwise-separable convolution branch that preserves local texture without global attention. The two branches are recombined via mask-gated index-copy and a 3×3 smoothing convolution to avoid stitching artifacts. Training uses a combined spatial loss (pixel L1 + SSIM) and a flow-warped temporal consistency loss with occlusion validity masks.

## Results

MAVFusion achieves state-of-the-art performance across all three video fusion benchmarks (M3SVD, HDO, VTMOT), winning on the majority of image quality metrics (Q_G, Q_M, Q_P, Q_CB, VIF, Q^{AB/F}) and the temporal smoothness metric (MS2R). On M3SVD, it attains Q_G=0.6897, Q_M=1.1544, and MS2R=0.1058, surpassing the strongest video baseline UniVF (Q_G=0.6376, Q_M=0.5661, MS2R=0.1064). In terms of efficiency, MAVFusion requires only 123.37G FLOPs at 640×480 (≈6.6% of VideoFusion's 1874G and ≈5.7% of UniVF's 2164G) while delivering the highest throughput of 14.16 FPS among all video fusion methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MAVFusion outperforms all nine baselines (seven image-fusion and two video-fusion methods) on the majority of metrics across all three datasets. The closest competitor is UniVF (NeurIPS 2025), the only other video fusion method: on M3SVD, MAVFusion leads UniVF by +0.052 on Q_G (0.6897 vs 0.6376), +0.588 on Q_M (1.1544 vs 0.5661), and +0.050 on Q^{AB/F} (0.7550 vs 0.7049), while also achieving a slightly better MS2R (0.1058 vs 0.1064). Among image-fusion baselines, TDFusion and UP-Fusion are the strongest on gradient-based metrics (Q_G), yet both lack temporal modeling and score substantially worse on MS2R (e.g., 0.1059 and 0.1110 on M3SVD vs MAVFusion's 0.1058). VideoFusion (CVPR 2026) is the weakest video baseline, trailing MAVFusion by large margins on every metric (e.g., Q_G 0.3881 vs 0.6897 on M3SVD) and running at only 7.83 FPS versus MAVFusion's 14.16 FPS. In the downstream YOLO26 detection experiment, MAVFusion achieves the highest detection confidence in low-illumination scenes, where methods like SAGE and GIFNet fail to preserve target-to-background contrast due to excessive visible-noise integration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the infrared and visible video fusion line initiated by VideoFusion [27] and UniVF [41], which rely on global spatio-temporal attention to enforce temporal consistency. The key departure is a motion-aware decoupling strategy: optical flow (via frozen SEA-RAFT) identifies dynamic regions where strong cross-modal sparse attention is applied, while static backgrounds are handled by lightweight depthwise-separable convolutions. The coarse-to-fine warping pipeline (low-resolution flow + residual refinement in feature space) repurposes ideas from efficient frame interpolation [20, 21], and the Top-K patch selection for sparse attention is a novel application to multi-modal video fusion rather than a direct adaptation from a single prior method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dramatic efficiency gain—roughly 15–17× fewer FLOPs than existing video fusion baselines while matching or exceeding their quality—makes real-time multi-modal video fusion (14 FPS at 640×480) practically viable for downstream tasks like detection, as demonstrated with YOLO26. The ablation confirming that uniform strong interaction (Full-DB) actually degrades quality relative to the sparse variant provides empirical support for the broader principle that region-adaptive computation allocation outperforms uniform processing in video. Open questions include whether the frozen optical-flow front-end can be further integrated or distilled to reduce its residual cost, and whether the approach generalizes to more than three temporal frames or to other multi-modal video tasks (e.g., RGB-D, LiDAR-camera).
