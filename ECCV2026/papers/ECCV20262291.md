## Problem

Post-hoc Concept Bottleneck Models are typically evaluated by downstream classification accuracy, but this metric cannot distinguish semantically meaningful concept projections from meaningless ones—random projections can achieve competitive accuracy. The paper asks whether the learned concept projection π_θ actually recovers the intended concepts on the target distribution, and identifies two concrete mechanisms by which it fails: covariate shift when training on auxiliary concept datasets, and systematic label noise from VLM-generated surrogate labels.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262291/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images processed by a frozen backbone (ResNet-18, CLIP RN50, or ViT-B/16) into high-dimensional activations, which are then projected via a learned linear layer π_θ into a K-dimensional concept space (K ≪ d). The output is a vector of K concept scores followed by a class label from a sparse linear classifier h. Unusually, the concept projection is never trained on ground-truth concept labels for the target task; instead it is trained either on an auxiliary annotated dataset (e.g., Broden) or on noisy surrogate labels produced by a VLM (CLIP, DINOv3, or Grounding DINO), making the faithfulness of π_θ on the target distribution the central evaluation question.

## Method

The paper formalizes concept faithfulness as minimizing expected risk over the true task distribution and shows, via the manifold Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma, that random projections on low-dimensional activation manifolds preserve geometry well enough for a sufficiently expressive classifier to reconstruct original activations—providing a constructive linear-subspace proof of exact recovery. It then derives two faithfulness failure modes: (1) for auxiliary-dataset training, an upper bound on task error in terms of the empirical HΔH-divergence between auxiliary and target distributions, approximated by a domain discriminator; and (2) for VLM surrogate labels, a gradient analysis showing that unfaithfulness requires the surrogate error to be non-orthogonal to backbone activations, quantified via absolute Pearson correlation between per-concept error vectors and per-neuron activation vectors. Empirically, these metrics are validated on the synthetic Elements dataset (with ground-truth concepts) and real benchmarks (CUB-200, CIFAR-10/100), demonstrating that they detect unfaithful projections that downstream accuracy masks.

## Results

On the Elements dataset, all post-hoc CBM variants achieve near-perfect downstream accuracy (Acc h ≈ 1.00) regardless of training distribution or surrogate label quality, yet the oracle classifier (Acc h*) reveals severe unfaithfulness: PCBM trained on OOD data drops to 21% oracle accuracy, and VLG-CBM with Grounding DINO drops to 4% (barely above the 2.8% random baseline). The geometric alignment metric (sim_◁) confirms this, falling from 0.96 (in-domain) to 0.47 (OOD) for PCBM and to 0.02–0.05 for all VLM-surrogate methods. The estimated HΔH-divergence correlates >0.9 with actual generalization errors, validating it as a practical faithfulness proxy that requires no ground-truth concept labels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PCBM [64] (auxiliary-dataset training) achieves 100% downstream accuracy in all four distribution settings, but the oracle classifier exposes a dramatic faithfulness gap: oracle accuracy drops from 1.00 (in-domain) to 0.21 under OOD training, while the learned h remains at 1.00, confirming that the classifier compensates for unfaithful concept representations. LFCBM [44] with CLIP surrogate labels achieves 99% downstream accuracy but only 54% oracle accuracy, while DINOv3 surrogate labels improve oracle accuracy to 78% (still far below the 98% achieved with 25% random noise, confirming that systematic VLM errors are more damaging than random noise). VLG-CBM [57] with Grounding DINO is the most extreme case: 100% downstream accuracy but only 4% oracle accuracy—barely above the 2.8% random-guessing baseline—indicating that the learned concepts are essentially meaningless despite perfect task performance. Across all three methods, the paper's proposed faithfulness metrics (sim_◁, HΔH-divergence, and surrogate-error correlation) successfully identify these failures that standard accuracy-based evaluation completely misses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a critical analysis of the post-hoc CBM framework introduced by Yuksekgonul et al. (ICLR 2023), extending the evaluation beyond the standard downstream-accuracy paradigm. It draws on domain adaptation theory (Ben-David et al., 2010) to formalize covariate-shift-induced unfaithfulness via the HΔH divergence, and on the manifold Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma (Baraniuk & Wakin, 2009) to show why random projections can preserve enough geometry for competitive classification. Unlike prior information-leakage analyses (Havasi et al., 2022; Makonnen et al., 2025), the authors explicitly decouple projection faithfulness from leakage, arguing the two are complementary rather than equivalent, and generalize empirical observations about surrogate-label flaws (Lai et al., 2024) into a formal orthogonality condition for systematic vs. random error.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first practical, label-free metrics (HΔH divergence estimation and surrogate-error/activation correlation) for diagnosing unfaithful concept projections, which is critical as post-hoc CBMs are increasingly deployed in safety-sensitive interpretability settings. The dramatic empirical gap—VLG-CBM achieving 100% learned-classifier accuracy while the oracle classifier scores only 4% (near random)—concretely demonstrates that the field's primary evaluation metric is fundamentally misleading. Open questions include whether domain-adaptation techniques can be integrated into the post-hoc CBM training loop to close the covariate-shift gap, and whether de-biasing mechanisms can disentangle systematic VLM label errors without access to ground-truth concepts. A limitation is that several metrics (surrogate error magnitude, oracle accuracy) require ground-truth concept labels, restricting their applicability to synthetic or heavily annotated settings.
