## Problem

Existing student-teacher regression frameworks for optimizing diffusion samplers exhibit a structural bottleneck: when the NFE gap between a low-step student and a high-step teacher grows large, regression losses (L2, LPIPS) force the student to sacrifice high-frequency texture fidelity in favor of coarse structural alignment, capping perceptual quality regardless of teacher strength. This paper identifies and resolves this limitation by reframing sampler optimization as a preference-based alignment problem rather than a fixed-target regression task.

## Input / Output

The method takes a frozen pretrained diffusion model (e.g., Stable Diffusion v1.5, InstaFlow), a text prompt, and an initial Gaussian noise sample as input, and produces an optimized sampling policy consisting of a continuous timestep schedule S and per-step classifier-free guidance weights ω. All model weights remain strictly frozen; only these low-dimensional sampler parameters are learned, making the approach orthogonal to and composable with any pretrained backbone. The optimized policy is then applied to a standard ODE solver (iPNDM, UniPC, or DPM-Solver++) to generate images at 4–7 NFE.

## Method

D2PO models the deterministic sampling policy as an Energy-Based Model surrogate to make DPO tractable, defining the energy as a score-based distance that measures discrepancies between two samples by comparing the pretrained score network's predictions across multiple noise levels (weighted by σ_t² to balance scales). This multi-scale metric captures both coarse structural and fine-grained textural differences that conventional perceptual losses miss. The key innovation is the dynamic preference mechanism: the 'winning' sample is generated by the same policy under a denser 2N-step schedule (via linear interpolation of timesteps), while the 'losing' sample is a degraded version of the student's N-step output, creating a self-improving loop where the student learns to minimize its own discretization error rather than converging to a fixed teacher's residual. A theoretical analysis shows this dynamic teacher avoids the asymptotic error floor inherent in static regression, since the teacher's own error vanishes as O(2^{-k}) under refinement.

## Results

On text-to-image synthesis with Stable Diffusion v1.5 (iPNDM solver, COCO prompts), D2PO achieves the best FID at 4 steps (15.69 vs. 17.60 for LD3, 18.12 for GITS, 21.39 for DMN) and the highest Aesthetic score (5.2024) and HPS (0.2237) across all baselines. At 5 steps, D2PO leads in HPS (0.2385) and Aesthetic (5.2701) but shows a slight FID regression on UniPC (14.47 vs. 13.89 LD3) and DPM Solver++ (14.16 vs. 13.27 LD3), reflecting the expected quality-diversity trade-off at higher step counts. On ImageNet-256 (latent space, iPNDM), D2PO achieves FID 7.28 at 4 steps, outperforming LD3 (9.19), DMN (10.15), and GITS (56.00). On the flow-matching InstaFlow model, D2PO achieves FID 40.68 at 2 steps and 15.50 at 4 steps, surpassing both uniform scheduling and LD3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LD3 [46] (the primary regression-based baseline), D2PO achieves a clear FID advantage at 4 steps across all three solvers (e.g., 15.69 vs. 17.60 on iPNDM, a ~11% improvement) and leads in HPS and Aesthetic at every step count. At 5 steps, D2PO wins on perceptual metrics (HPS 0.2385 vs. 0.2346, Aesthetic 5.2701 vs. 5.2463 on iPNDM) but trails LD3 on FID for UniPC (14.47 vs. 13.89) and DPM Solver++ (14.16 vs. 13.27), which the authors attribute to the expected quality-diversity trade-off when perceptual alignment diverges from distributional fidelity. Against GITS [5], D2PO dominates at 4 steps on all metrics and maintains a consistent HPS/Aesthetic lead at 5–7 steps, though GITS achieves slightly lower FID at 6–7 steps on iPNDM (12.33/12.16 vs. 13.54/12.71). Against DMN [56], D2PO wins decisively at 4 steps (FID 15.69 vs. 21.39 on iPNDM) and maintains a perceptual quality lead throughout, while DMN occasionally matches or edges out D2PO on FID at 6–7 steps. On ImageNet-256, D2PO outperforms all baselines at 4 steps (FID 7.28 vs. 9.19 LD3, 10.15 DMN, 56.00 GITS) and at 5 steps (5.48 vs. 6.03 LD3), though the gap narrows at 7 steps (4.70 vs. 4.68 LD3). On InstaFlow (flow-matching), D2PO surpasses LD3 at every step count (e.g., FID 40.68 vs. 63.55 at 2 steps; 15.50 vs. 23.21 at 4 steps), confirming generalizability beyond score-based diffusion.

## Relation to Prior Work

D2PO adapts the Direct Preference Optimization framework from LLM alignment (Rafailov et al.) to the problem of optimizing diffusion sampling policies, a task previously dominated by student-teacher regression (LD3, S4S) or distribution-level search (AutoDiffusion, DDSS). The key conceptual departure is twofold: replacing the intractable marginalized log-likelihood with an energy-based surrogate whose energy is derived directly from the pretrained score network, and replacing the static high-NFE teacher with a dynamic 2N-step refinement of the student's own schedule (inspired by SPIN's self-play). This positions D2PO as orthogonal to weight-tuning preference methods (Diffusion-DPO, SPIN for diffusion) since it keeps the generative backbone frozen and optimizes only low-dimensional sampler parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and empirically demonstrates a structural ceiling in regression-based sampler distillation: as the teacher-student NFE gap grows, the student is forced to sacrifice high-frequency texture fidelity, a limitation that no amount of loss-function tuning within the regression paradigm can overcome. The dynamic preference mechanism theoretically eliminates this fixed error floor, and the score-based energy provides a multi-scale signal that captures textural discrepancies invisible to LPIPS. However, the FID degradation at 6–7 steps (where D2PO trades distributional fidelity for perceptual quality) raises open questions about whether preference alignment is always the right objective, and whether the method extends naturally to other sampling parameters (solver coefficients, guidance schedules) or to video generation where the NFE budget is far tighter.
