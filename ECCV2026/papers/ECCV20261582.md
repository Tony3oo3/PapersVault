## Problem

State-of-the-art latent diffusion models rely on low-compression (8×) autoencoders, producing tens of thousands of redundant visual tokens that make 4K image and 2160p video generation prohibitively slow. While deeply compressed latent spaces (32×, 64×) could eliminate this redundancy, directly fine-tuning pre-trained models on them introduces a representation gap that causes severe quality degradation or training instability, and training from scratch remains computationally infeasible.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261582/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For text-to-image and text-to-video, the method takes a text prompt and produces a generated image (up to 4K) or video (720p, 81 frames); for image-to-video and image editing, it additionally takes an RGB image as conditioning. The key operational detail is that all generation occurs in a deeply compressed latent space (DC-AE-f32 or DC-AE-f64 for images; DC-AE-V with 32× spatial and 4× temporal compression for video), reducing token counts by 16×–64× compared to standard 8× autoencoders. For I2V, the input image is replicated four times with blank frames appended to match the chunk-causal temporal design of DC-AE-V before encoding.

## Method

DC-Gen is a three-stage post-training pipeline that adapts a pre-trained diffusion model to a deeply compressed latent space without retraining from scratch. Stage 1 (patch embedder alignment) spatially downsamples the pre-trained model's embedding tokens and trains a randomly initialized patch embedder to match them via MSE loss, bridging the representation gap that otherwise causes exponential feature divergence through the transformer. Stage 2 (output layer alignment) jointly fine-tunes the patch embedder and output layer under a standard flow-matching objective with the backbone frozen, further synchronizing the output representations. Stage 3 applies lightweight LoRA fine-tuning (rank 256) to restore full generation quality. For guidance-distilled models like FLUX and Z-Image, a corrected flow-matching objective is derived to account for the distilled CFG scale, preventing biased velocity estimation during training.

## Results

On MJHQ-30K (1024×1024), DC-Gen-FLUX achieves a CLIP Score of 28.02 and GenEval of 0.71 with 0.88 s latency, matching or slightly exceeding FLUX.1-Krea-12B (CLIP 27.93, GenEval 0.69) while being ~4.6× faster. At 4K resolution, DC-Gen-FLUX reduces latency from 218.81 s to 4.04 s (53.8× speedup) while improving CLIP Score to 35.07 and Aesthetic Score to 6.31 over Diffusion-4K (33.12 / 6.06). For video, DC-Gen-Wan2.1-T2V-14B achieves a VBench Overall score of 83.97 (vs. 83.73 for the pre-trained model) at 3.58 min latency (7.7× faster). For image editing, DC-Gen-Qwen-Image-Edit reaches a GEdit O-Score of 7.20 (English) and 7.32 (Chinese) at 11.96 s, outperforming the pre-trained Qwen-Image-Edit (5.81 / 5.59) while being 4.8× faster.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DC-Gen-FLUX matches or slightly exceeds FLUX.1-Krea-12B on GenEval (0.71 vs. 0.69) and CLIP Score (28.02 vs. 27.93) while reducing 1K latency from 4.06 s to 0.88 s (~4.6×). DC-Gen-Z-Image ties Z-Image-Turbo on GenEval (0.84) and CLIP (27.58 vs. 27.61) at 4× lower latency (0.47 s vs. 1.92 s). At 4K, DC-Gen-FLUX outperforms Diffusion-4K (FLUX.1-WLF) on both CLIP (35.07 vs. 33.12) and Aesthetic Score (6.31 vs. 6.06) while being ~15× faster (4.04 s vs. 59.42 s at 50 steps). For video, DC-Gen-Wan2.1-T2V-14B achieves a VBench Overall of 83.97 versus 83.73 for the pre-trained Wan2.1-T2V-14B at 7.7× lower latency (3.58 min vs. 27.52 min), and DC-Gen-Wan2.1-I2V-14B reaches 87.13 Overall vs. 86.86 for the pre-trained I2V model. For image editing, DC-Gen-Qwen-Image-Edit surpasses the pre-trained Qwen-Image-Edit on GEdit O-Score (7.20 vs. 5.81 English; 7.32 vs. 5.59 Chinese) at 4.8× lower latency, and also outperforms Step1X-Edit and FLUX.1-Kontext on the same metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

DC-Gen builds directly on the DC-AE family of deep compression autoencoders, which provide the 32×/64× spatially compressed latent spaces that the method targets. It positions itself within the post-training diffusion acceleration literature (alongside few-step distillation and guidance distillation) but carves out a distinct niche: rather than reducing inference steps or quantizing weights, it reduces the number of visual tokens by switching to a more compact latent representation. The key conceptual departure from prior adaptation attempts (e.g., OpenSora-2.0's f8→f32 switch) is the introduction of a lightweight embedding alignment stage that explicitly bridges the representation gap between the original and compressed latent spaces, whereas prior work relied on naive end-to-end fine-tuning that the paper shows leads to quality degradation or training collapse.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: a 53.8× latency reduction for 4K image generation on a single H100 GPU makes high-resolution synthesis feasible in settings where it was previously prohibitive (minutes vs. hours). The embedding alignment technique is generalizable across architectures (FLUX, Z-Image, Wan2.1, Qwen-Image-Edit) and tasks (T2I, T2V, I2V, editing), suggesting it could become a standard procedure whenever latent spaces are swapped. Open questions include whether the approach extends to even higher compression ratios (beyond 64×), whether it applies to non-DC-AE compressed spaces, and whether the LoRA fine-tuning stage can be further reduced or eliminated. A limitation is that the method still requires access to the pre-trained model's weights and a moderate amount of post-training compute (40 H100 GPU-days for FLUX), so it is not a zero-cost solution.
