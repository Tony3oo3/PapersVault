## Problem

Current MLLMs struggle with long-horizon temporal compositional reasoning in sports videos, where answering questions requires locating and integrating evidence from multiple temporally separated events. This limitation stems from two coupled gaps: the scarcity of high-quality annotations that explicitly capture temporally dispersed evidence across multiple time spans, and the absence of methods that force models to identify, localize, and justify the specific temporal evidence underlying their answers, leading to hallucinated or weakly grounded responses.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261522/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes long-form sports broadcast videos (highlights ~10 min, full matches ~50 min) sampled at up to 768 frames for inference (128 for training) along with a free-form text question. A key input modification is that explicit mm:ss timestamp overlays are burned into the top-right corner of every frame, converting temporal grounding into a directly readable visual signal. The output is an open-ended natural-language answer accompanied by a structured chain-of-time reasoning trajectory, where each intermediate step is a tuple of a textual claim and a supporting time anchor (either a point timestamp or a temporal span), making the full reasoning path explicitly verifiable.

## Method

CoTR (Chain-of-Time Reasoning) formulates long-video QA as step-wise reasoning over explicit temporal evidence and operates in three stages. First, visual time anchoring burns timestamp overlays into frames so the model can read time directly. Second, during training, a temporal-reward GRPO (tr-GRPO) objective optimizes the policy with a composite reward combining format compliance, answer correctness, and a temporal reward that jointly measures step-wise anchor coverage (fraction of reasoning steps containing explicit time anchors) and anchor correctness (IoU/distance alignment against ground-truth evidence spans), encouraging the model to ground every claim in verifiable temporal evidence rather than relying on language priors. Third, at inference, Anchor-Triggered Interactive Observation (AT-IO) uses each predicted time anchor as a query to retrieve short local clips from the corresponding neighborhood of the video, then iteratively verifies and revises each reasoning step against the retrieved visual evidence before composing the final answer, replacing global scanning with bounded local observation.

## Results

On SportsTime, CoTR (full) achieves 29.24% average accuracy, improving over the Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct baseline (25.15%) by +4.09 percentage points, with the largest gains on Counterfactual (+8.05%) and Causal (+4.29%) reasoning. Even the strongest proprietary model, GPT-5, reaches only 40.72% average, underscoring the difficulty of temporal compositional reasoning in long-form sports videos. On generalization benchmarks, CoTR improves over Qwen3-VL-4B by +3.7% on LVBench (59.9%), +0.9% on MLVU (76.2%), and +2.8% on Video-MME (72.1%). In the SGA evaluation, tr-GRPO achieves 95.76% anchor coverage and 0.58 mIoU, substantially outperforming time-prompted CoT (91.80% coverage but only 0.12 mIoU).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoTR (full) outperforms all open-source baselines on SportsTime: it beats Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct by +4.09% (29.24 vs. 25.15), InternVL2.5-4B by +4.67% (29.24 vs. 24.57), and Qwen2.5-VL-3B by +12.08% (29.24 vs. 17.16), with the largest margins over weaker models like Ovis2-4B (+20.22%) and LLaVA-OneVision1.5-4B (+17.43%). The tr-GRPO stage alone already surpasses the base model on Tactical (+4.62%) and Causal (+2.62%), while the full pipeline with Anchor-Triggered Interactive Observation adds further gains on Perception and Temporal tasks. On generalization benchmarks (Table 4), CoTR beats Qwen3-VL-4B on all three datasets (LVBench +3.7%, MLVU +0.9%, Video-MME +2.8%) and also edges out the larger Qwen3-VL-8B on LVBench (+1.9%) and Video-MME (+0.2%), though it trails by 1.9% on MLVU. Against proprietary models, CoTR (29.24%) approaches Gemini-2.5-Pro (29.37%) but remains well below GPT-5 (40.72%), indicating that even state-of-the-art systems struggle substantially with this task. In the SGA evaluation, tr-GRPO dramatically outperforms time-prompted CoT on temporal alignment (mIoU 0.58 vs. 0.12; Hit@0.5 56.89% vs. 12.12%), showing that reward-based training produces genuinely grounded evidence rather than superficially formatted timestamps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GRPO reinforcement learning framework (originally from DeepSeekMath) by introducing a temporal-reward variant (tr-GRPO) that explicitly rewards step-wise temporal grounding in video reasoning chains. It builds on the line of temporally grounded video understanding work (TOGA, LongVT, VITAL) but departs from it by treating temporal anchors as first-class intermediate states in a chain-of-time reasoning process rather than as post-hoc evidence retrieval. On the benchmark side, it extends sports video QA (Sports-QA, SPORTU, SportR) from short-clip, multiple-choice settings to long-form, open-ended questions with step-wise temporal evidence annotations, and extends general long-video benchmarks (Video-MME, LVBench, MLVU) by adding domain-specific temporal compositional reasoning demands.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a substantial and quantified gap: even GPT-5 achieves only 40.72% on SportsTime, and the step-wise temporal evidence annotations enable fine-grained diagnosis of *where* reasoning fails (temporal drift vs. semantic error), which prior benchmarks cannot support. The finding that simply prompting for timestamps increases anchor frequency to 91.8% but *worsens* temporal alignment (mIoU drops from 0.26 to 0.12) highlights that format compliance is not evidence faithfulness — a distinction that likely generalizes beyond sports. Open questions include whether the tr-GRPO reward design and anchor-triggered observation loop transfer to non-sports long-video domains, and whether the modest absolute gains (~4 points) scale with larger backbones or longer contexts.
