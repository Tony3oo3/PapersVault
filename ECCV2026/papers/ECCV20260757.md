## Problem

Temporal Action Segmentation models for egocentric videos suffer from poor generalization when deployed in unseen environments, yet collecting diverse annotated videos covering varied objects, tools, and procedures is prohibitively expensive. Existing data augmentation approaches either offer limited semantic diversity through low-level image transformations or rely on computationally heavy generative models that may introduce artifacts, leaving a gap for efficient augmentation that produces large, semantically meaningful variations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260757/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

LogFA operates on pre-extracted video clip features (16-frame sliding window, 8-frame overlap at 10 fps) encoded by a contrastive VLM (SigLIP2), along with action labels available only during training. The augmentation stage produces modified feature vectors in the same VLM embedding space with no pixel-level synthesis, and the downstream TAS model (FACT or MSTCN++) consumes these features to output per-frame action labels and segment boundaries. At inference, the model runs on standard visual clip features from test videos with no augmentation applied.

## Method

LogFA performs augmentation at two complementary levels. For local (intra-action) augmentation, a multimodal LLM generates action-centric captions, a text LLM produces M semantically varied caption modifications (e.g., changing object colors or substituting tools), and a Prompt-based Feature Enhancement module appends clip-specific learnable tokens to the caption embeddings, trained with an alignment loss pulling enhanced text features toward the visual feature and a preservation loss keeping each modified feature anchored to its caption semantics and the original visual feature. This bridges the modality gap in the contrastive VLM space, enabling textual features of modified captions to serve as approximations of visual features for the corresponding modified clips. For global (inter-action) augmentation, an LLM constructs a Generalized Directed Acyclic Graph encoding action dependencies, optional steps, repetition counts, and hierarchical routines; valid action sequences are sampled by converting the GDAG to a standard DAG (sampling repetition counts, removing zero-count nodes, duplicating repeated nodes) and performing topological sort, after which video segments are reassembled in the sampled order. The TAS model is trained with a probability of using augmented features and a probability of reordering clips, with linear interpolation between original and augmented features to expose the model to gradual transitions.

## Results

On EgoPER, LogFA achieves F1@50 of 70.7% on in-domain videos and 66.0% on out-of-domain (error) videos, improving over the base model by 6.6% and 9.3% respectively. On EgoProceL, LogFA reaches F1@50 of 35.0% versus 28.8% for the base model. LogFA also reduces data collection cost by 75%: training with only 3 augmented videos outperforms training with 12 unaugmented videos. Computationally, LogFA requires ~10× less time than generative augmentation (PFE takes ~6 s per augmentation on one GPU vs. ~13.3 GPU-hours per video for Qwen-Edit).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LogFA outperforms both image transformation and generative model baselines across all metrics on both EgoPER and EgoProceL. On EgoPER out-of-domain videos, LogFA achieves F1@50 of 66.0%, exceeding the generative model baseline (Qwen-Edit [58]) by 9.2% and image transformations by 10.5%, indicating that feature-space augmentation captures far more semantic diversity than pixel-level edits. On EgoProceL, LogFA reaches F1@50 of 35.0% versus 32.4% for image transformations; the generative baseline is omitted due to prohibitive cost (3480 GPU-days for the full dataset). The generative model (Qwen-Edit) only applies local-level modifications and cannot alter action ordering, whereas LogFA's global augmentation via GDAG adds procedural diversity that no pixel-level method can replicate. LogFA also generalizes across TAS backbones, outperforming both baselines when paired with MSTCN++ (F1@50: 63.3% vs. 61.2% generative, 58.1% image transform on EgoPER all tasks). Computationally, LogFA is ~10× faster than the generative baseline, requiring only ~6 s per augmentation on a single GPU versus ~13.3 GPU-hours per video for Qwen-Edit.

## Relation to Prior Work

LogFA carves new ground by repurposing the aligned visual-textual space of contrastive VLMs (SigLIP2, CLIP) as a feature-space augmentation mechanism, bypassing both conventional pixel-level transformations (color jitter, affine warps) and computationally expensive generative models (diffusion-based editors like Qwen-Edit). The Prompt-based Feature Enhancement module draws on the prompt-learning literature (CoOp, MAPLE) to inject clip-specific context into textual features, while the Generalized DAG extends standard topological-sort-based procedure modeling to handle optional actions, repetitions, and hierarchical routines—patterns that plain DAGs cannot encode. The overall pipeline composes LLM-driven caption generation, contrastive VLM feature manipulation, and graph-based sequence sampling into a unified augmentation framework that has no direct predecessor in the TAS or data-augmentation literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for data-scarce egocentric AI deployment: LogFA reduces annotation cost by ~75% (3 videos vs. 12) and cuts augmentation compute by ~10× relative to generative baselines while matching or exceeding their generalization gains, particularly on out-of-domain videos. The finding that data diversity (multi-subject, multi-procedure) outweighs data quantity is well-supported and should influence how practitioners allocate collection budgets. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to third-person TAS benchmarks (50Salads, Breakfast, GTEA), how sensitive PFE is to the quality of LLM-generated captions, and whether per-clip prompt optimization scales to very long videos with hundreds of segments. The reliance on LLMs for GDAG construction also raises robustness concerns when action dependencies are complex or poorly specified.
