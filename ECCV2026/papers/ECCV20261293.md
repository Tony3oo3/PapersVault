## Problem

Existing optical tactile representations—raw images and cumulative motion fields—suffer from perceptual ambiguity that makes it difficult to distinguish fine-grained contact states (e.g., making vs. releasing contact), which is critical for contact-rich manipulation. Additionally, prevailing visuo-tactile fusion strategies either concatenate modality features (risking one modality dominating) or train independent policies per modality (forfeiting cross-modal interaction), failing to simultaneously capture cross-modal complementarity and preserve modality-specific properties.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes RGB images from wrist and third-view cameras, three tactile sensor frames (initial non-contact, current, and previous), and the robot's proprioceptive state (end-effector pose, gripper width). The three tactile frames are used to compute optical flow, yielding a pixel-wise TMC map that is encoded separately from the raw tactile appearance, effectively treating motion correlation as a distinct fourth modality alongside visual, tactile-appearance, and proprioceptive embeddings. The output is an autoregressively predicted action chunk of length k, trained with a β-VAE objective to model demonstration stochasticity.

## Method

The key insight is that the pixel-wise dot product between transient motion (optical flow between consecutive frames) and cumulative motion (optical flow from the initial non-contact frame) explicitly encodes contact state: positive values indicate making contact, negative values indicate releasing, near-zero indicates stable or no contact, and the magnitude correlates with applied force. This Tactile Motion Correlation (TMC) representation is sensor-agnostic and physically interpretable. For fusion, the paper adopts a Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) architecture where each of the four modalities (visual, tactile appearance, TMC, proprioception) has dedicated Q/K/V projection matrices, output projections, FFNs, and layer normalizations, while all modalities share a single global self-attention layer for cross-modal interaction. The fused embeddings are fed into a Transformer decoder that autoregressively predicts action chunks, trained end-to-end with a β-VAE loss (L1 prediction loss plus KL regularization) on human demonstrations.

## Results

On four real-world contact-rich manipulation tasks, ViTacMotor achieves the highest whole-task success rate on three of four: 73.3% on tube collection (vs. 60.0% for the best baseline TactileACT), 86.7% on whiteboard erasing (vs. 73.3%), and 60.0% on pencil sharpening (vs. 46.7%). On lightbulb insertion, ViTacMotor ties TactileACT at 40.0% whole-task success. Visual-only baselines (ACT, DP) perform substantially worse, with whole-task success rates ranging from 6.7% to 53.3%, confirming the critical role of tactile feedback in contact-rich scenarios.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ViTacMotor outperforms all baselines on three of four tasks. On tube collection, it achieves 73.3% whole-task success, surpassing TactileACT (60.0%), ACT+T (60.0%), and Policy Consensus (53.3%) by 13.3–20.0 percentage points, with the largest gains in the sub-steps of inserting into the second hole (73.3% vs. 60.0%). On whiteboard erasing, ViTacMotor reaches 86.7%, exceeding TactileACT (73.3%) and Policy Consensus (66.7%), primarily due to superior second-erase performance (86.7% vs. 73.3% and 66.7%). On pencil sharpening, it achieves 60.0% versus 46.7% for TactileACT and 33.3% for Policy Consensus. On lightbulb insertion, ViTacMotor ties TactileACT at 40.0% whole-task success, though both substantially outperform visual-only methods (6.7–13.3%) and decision-level fusion (26.7%). The consistent advantage over visual-only baselines (ACT, DP) across all tasks underscores the necessity of explicit tactile contact-state perception for contact-rich manipulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the ACT/β-VAE policy framework for action-chunk prediction and the Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) architecture for multi-modal processing, repurposing the latter for the first time in visuo-tactile robotic manipulation. It departs from prior tactile representation work (raw GelSight images, cumulative optical-flow fields, Helmholtz-Hodge decompositions) by proposing Tactile Motion Correlation—a pixel-wise dot product of transient and cumulative optical flow—as a physically interpretable, sensor-agnostic contact-state signal. On the fusion side, it moves beyond both feature-level concatenation (TactileACT, ACT+T) and decision-level policy averaging (Policy Consensus) by treating tactile appearance, tactile motion correlation, vision, and proprioception as four distinct modalities within a shared-attention MoT block.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The TMC representation offers a simple, interpretable, and sensor-agnostic way to distinguish fine-grained contact states (making vs. releasing vs. stable contact) and to implicitly encode force magnitude, which could reduce the need for task-specific tactile feature engineering across heterogeneous optical sensors. The MoT-based fusion provides a principled template for multi-modal manipulation policies that preserves modality-specific inductive biases while enabling cross-modal interaction. However, the authors acknowledge limited generalization under extreme visual disturbances or large object-position shifts, and the evaluation is confined to four real-world tasks with a single robot platform, leaving open whether the approach scales to more diverse embodiments, longer-horizon tasks, or sim-to-real transfer settings.
