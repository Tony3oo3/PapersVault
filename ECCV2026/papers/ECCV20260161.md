## Problem

Existing human-preference alignment for video generation suffers from three compounding failures: noisy and biased human annotations corrupt the reward signal, scalar reward models collapse multi-dimensional perceptual preferences (motion, semantics, aesthetics) into a single value, and KL-divergence regularization in policy optimization enforces only local per-step constraints, causing the policy to produce motion-conservative videos that diverge from holistic human judgment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260161/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The reward model takes a video–prompt pair and outputs a K-dimensional probability distribution over perceptual and semantic quality scores rather than a single scalar; it is implemented as a Qwen2-VL-2B backbone fine-tuned with LoRA (rank 8). The video generator consumes text prompts and produces videos via diffusion or flow-matching (Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B, CogVideoX-2B, ModelScope T2V). During policy optimization, each prompt yields a group of G=16 candidate videos over T=20 denoising steps, and the reward model scores each candidate to compute group-relative advantages.

## Method

The framework operates in three stages. First, Elite-Guided Preference Calibration (EGPC) partitions annotations by inter-annotator consistency (all identical or at most one outlier within ±1 of the mode), trains a scorer on the high-consistency subset, and uses it to recalibrate low-consistency samples, yielding an augmented high-quality training set. Second, Preference-Aware Reward Modeling predicts a K-dimensional reward distribution per video–prompt pair and aligns it with the empirical human-preference distribution via a Wasserstein distance loss (W_p) added to a standard MSE regression objective. Third, Wasserstein-based GRPO (WGRPO) replaces the KL-divergence penalty in the GRPO surrogate with a Wasserstein distance between the new policy's output distribution and a reference policy, so that each update aligns the global structure of generated videos with human preference rather than matching local per-step densities, reducing the tendency toward low-risk, motion-conservative trajectories.

## Results

On Video-Bench, fine-tuning three open-source video generators (Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B, CogVideoX-2B, ModelScope T2V) with the calibrated distributional reward model improves the average sub-dimension score by 2.1% (68.16→69.58), 2.9% (66.39→68.32), and 3.2% (63.86→65.92) respectively, with the largest gains in Motion Smoothness and Spatial Relation. The reward model trained on calibrated data achieves 83.33% pairwise accuracy (w/o ties) on Action Consistency and 87.88% on Motion Effects, outperforming the raw-data model by 3.03 and 1.52 points respectively. Replacing KL divergence with Wasserstein distance in GRPO yields a further 1.56-point average gain over the KL-regularized variant (69.58 vs. 68.02).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's calibrated reward model is evaluated as a drop-in replacement within two existing GRPO-based policy optimizers: FlowGRPO [12] and DanceGRPO [38], both applied to Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B. With FlowGRPO, swapping the raw reward model for the calibrated one raises the average score from 69.86 to 70.87 (+1.01), with gains across all seven sub-dimensions. With DanceGRPO, the same swap improves the average from 68.99 to 69.97 (+0.98), again with consistent per-dimension improvements. In the policy-optimization ablation (Table 4), the Wasserstein-based GRPO (WGRPO) outperforms both the unregularized baseline (67.97) and the KL-regularized GRPO (68.02) by 1.56 and 1.56 points respectively, with the largest advantage in Spatial Relation (63.23 vs. 59.40) and Background Consistency (92.55 vs. 91.45). The one consistent trade-off is a small drop in Subject Consistency (e.g., 87.35→86.45 for CogVideoX), suggesting the calibrated reward slightly favors dynamic quality over static identity preservation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GRPO framework (originally from DeepSeekMath) and its video-generation adaptations (FlowGRPO, DanceGRPO) by replacing the KL-divergence regularization with a Wasserstein-distance term, shifting alignment from local, per-step density matching to global distributional transport. On the reward-modeling side, it departs from scalar-reward approaches (VideoScore, VisionReward, VideoAlign) by modeling video quality as a K-dimensional distribution and aligning it via Wasserstein distance, while the elite-guided calibration stage draws on data-quality selection ideas from LLM alignment (DEITA, IFD-based filtering) and adapts them to multi-annotator video preference data. The overall three-stage pipeline—calibrate data, model distributional rewards, optimize policy with Wasserstein GRPO—unifies these threads into a single framework rather than treating each as an independent contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a coherent conceptual argument that scalar rewards and local KL constraints are structurally insufficient for video preference alignment, and the Wasserstein-based alternative provides a principled fix. However, empirical gains are modest (2–3% average score improvement across three small open-source models), and a visible trade-off emerges in subject consistency, suggesting the global-alignment objective may over-prioritize dynamic quality at the expense of static fidelity. Open questions include whether the Wasserstein computation remains tractable and effective at larger model scales, how the approach interacts with multi-objective or safety constraints, and whether the elite-guided calibration generalizes beyond the Video-Bench annotation protocol to noisier, less structured preference data.
