## Problem

Existing video reasoning benchmarks predominantly assess visual perception and grounding, where questions can be answered from explicit prompts or isolated visual cues, failing to capture the multi-step, clue-integration reasoning that characterizes real-world inference. This paper asks whether current MLLMs can perform complex video reasoning comparable to human experts, where one must actively locate and connect multiple scattered visual clues before reaching a conclusion.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261069/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 1–5 minute suspense short films (270 test videos, 233 training videos) sourced from YouTube, presented either as native video streams or as uniformly extracted frames with timestamps (32 frames standard, up to 1 fps for longer-sequence experiments). Audio tracks are optionally included as an additional modality. Each of the 1,837 test questions spans seven task types (social reasoning, physical anomaly reasoning, multimodal hint reasoning, intention & motive chaining, temporal causal inference, timeline analysis, core theme inference) and requires the model to produce a step-by-step reasoning trace before committing to a final answer.

## Method

Video-Holmes is constructed through a three-stage pipeline: (1) manual annotation of 270 suspense short films using a structured template covering segmented plot descriptions, key character relationships, reasoning shots with timestamps and visual clues, supernatural elements, and core themes, with a two-stage quality verification process; (2) definition of seven reasoning tasks explicitly designed around an 'active seeking' paradigm where models must locate and chain multiple clues across video segments rather than answer from given cues; (3) automated QA generation via DeepSeek-R1 from the structured annotations, followed by manual audit to eliminate ambiguous or erroneous pairs. For evaluation, a reasoning process analysis module uses DeepSeek to categorize each model response into six error/success types (VPE, VOE, RE, TRAW, TWAR, TRAR) by comparing the model's reasoning trace against human-annotated ground-truth explanations. An auxiliary training set of 233 videos (1,551 questions) is provided to validate that RL post-training on high-reasoning-demand data yields cross-domain generalization gains on standard video benchmarks.

## Results

On Video-Holmes, the best-performing model Gemini-3.0-Pro achieves only 49.6% overall accuracy, while most evaluated MLLMs score below 40% (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-7B: 27.8%, GPT-4o: 42.0%). A human baseline under a single-view protocol reaches 96.3%, highlighting a substantial gap between current models and human-level video reasoning. Thinking-augmented models show the largest gains on Video-Holmes (+12.5% for Gemini-2.0-Flash-Thinking over Gemini-2.0-Flash) compared to only +0.9–3.2% on other benchmarks, confirming the benchmark's sensitivity to genuine reasoning ability. RL post-training on the auxiliary Video-Holmes training set (233 videos) yields a +12.9% gain on Video-Holmes and cross-domain improvements of +1.8% to +4.8% on general video benchmarks (Video-MME, TempCompass, Video-MMMU, MVBench).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among closed-source models, Gemini-3.0-Pro leads at 49.6% overall, outperforming Gemini-2.5-Pro (45.0%), GPT-4o (42.0%), and Claude 3.7 Sonnet (41.0%). Among open-source models, Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B (46.2%) and Video-RTS (40.0%) are the strongest, while the widely-used Qwen2.5-VL-7B baseline scores only 27.8%. Thinking-augmented variants consistently outperform their vanilla counterparts: Gemini-2.0-Flash-Thinking gains +12.5% over Gemini-2.0-Flash, and Video-R1 (36.5%) surpasses Qwen2.5-VL-7B (27.8%) by +8.7%, confirming that Video-Holmes discriminates reasoning ability far more sharply than existing benchmarks (where thinking gains are only +0.9–3.2%). The reasoning-process analysis reveals that ~60% of errors stem from logical reasoning failures (incorrectly connecting clues) and ~35% from visual omission, while visual perception errors account for only ~3–7%, indicating that current MLLMs' bottleneck is multi-clue integration rather than basic perception. RL post-training on the Video-Holmes auxiliary set transfers to general benchmarks, with Qwen2.5-VL-7B* gaining +2.3% on Video-MME, +4.8% on MVBench, and +2.1% on Video-MMMU, demonstrating that the high-reasoning-density data generalizes beyond the suspense-film domain.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in video reasoning benchmarks (Video-MME, MVBench, VCR-Bench, MMVU) where questions are answerable from explicit prompts or isolated visual cues, by introducing an 'active seeking' formulation that requires models to locate and chain multiple scattered visual clues across video segments. Conceptually, it draws on the CoT reasoning and RL post-training literature (GRPO, Video-R1, VideoChat-R1) as the methodological backdrop for evaluation, but its core contribution is a new benchmark design inspired by detective-style reasoning rather than a new model architecture. The use of suspense short films as a video source is a deliberate departure from the open-domain or academic-domain videos used in prior benchmarks, chosen specifically for their dense causal and clue structures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The striking 49.6% best-model accuracy versus 96.3% human accuracy, combined with the finding that ~60% of model errors stem from reasoning failures (incorrectly connecting clues) rather than perception failures, reframes the central bottleneck in video understanding from 'seeing' to 'integrating and reasoning.' The cross-domain transfer results (RL post-training on suspense films yielding +2.3% on Video-MME and +4.8% on MVBench) suggest that high-reasoning-density data can serve as a general-purpose training signal, opening a path toward using domain-specific hard data to improve broad video understanding. Open questions include whether the active-seeking paradigm generalizes beyond 1–5 minute suspense films, how to architecturally encourage models to perform systematic clue search rather than pattern-matching, and whether the observed CoT degradation in weaker models (Qwen2.5-VL-7B scoring lower with reasoning prompts) reflects a fundamental limitation of current RL post-training recipes.
