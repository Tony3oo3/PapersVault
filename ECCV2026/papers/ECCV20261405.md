## Problem

Fine-tuning large multimodal models on a new skill typically degrades their performance on previously learned tasks (catastrophic forgetting), yet the mechanisms behind this degradation and which model components drive it remain poorly understood. This paper asks: which parts of an LMM can be updated to acquire new skills while preserving broad vision-language ability, and why does apparent forgetting sometimes reverse after tuning on a subsequent task?

## Input / Output

The evaluated LMMs (LLaVA-OneVision, LLaVA-NeXT, Qwen2.5-VL) consume RGB images or videos alongside a text instruction, with the vision encoder producing visual tokens that a projector maps to the language-model width before concatenation with text token embeddings. The model outputs free-form text responses generated autoregressively. For the counting-bias probe, the model is additionally run on a fixed held-out captioning batch (LCS-558K) where greedy-decoded captions are scored by the maximum probability assigned to any numeric token at each position, yielding a scalar bias metric per training step.

## Method

The paper identifies two selective tuning recipes that learn new skills with minimal forgetting: (i) updating only the self-attention projection matrices (W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O) across all transformer blocks, and (ii) updating only the MLP gate and up projections while freezing the down projection (W_down). The mechanistic rationale is that self-attention primarily routes and combines contextual information, whereas MLPs act as key-value memories whose down projection writes activated features back into the residual stream, directly shifting the next-token distribution. A counting-bias probe (NTB) measures the expected likelihood of numeric tokens on non-counting captions and shows this shift covaries strongly (Spearman ρ=0.84) with held-out performance drop, confirming that output-distribution bias—not memory erasure—drives most forgetting. Training uses standard next-token cross-entropy on the current target task, optionally augmented with a KL-divergence distillation loss against the previous-stage checkpoint (LwF), and the recipes are validated across five sequential target tasks, eight held-out benchmarks, and three model families without any rehearsal data or auxiliary modules.

## Results

On LLaVA-OneVision, tuning only the self-attention projection layers (SA Proj.) achieves +24.9 target learning with only -0.6 held-out forgetting, while tuning only the MLP Gate&Up layers achieves +30.5 learning with -2.1 forgetting — both dramatically outperforming full-LLM tuning (+31.8 / -23.3) in the learning-forgetting trade-off. The counting-bias probe confirms that output-distribution shift (Spearman ρ=0.84 with forgetting) is the dominant mechanism: MLP tuning surges numeric-token likelihood on non-counting captions, while SA Proj. tuning keeps it near baseline. Results generalize to LLaVA-NeXT (SA Proj.: +28.3 / -7.7; MLP Gate&Up: +28.0 / -8.7) and Qwen2.5-VL (SA Proj.: +16.1 / +0.6; MLP Gate&Up: +16.8 / -4.6), where SA Proj. even slightly improves held-out performance on Qwen2.5-VL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LwF (Learning without Forgetting), the selective tuning recipes (SA Proj., MLP Gate&Up) match or exceed the learning-stability balance: LwF reliably curbs forgetting but can reduce target-task gains, whereas SA Proj. and MLP Gate&Up achieve comparable or better held-out retention while delivering stronger target learning, and require no auxiliary distillation loss or frozen teacher. Against LoRA, the selective methods clearly win on both axes — LoRA often lags behind on target performance and does not match the held-out stability of SA Proj. or MLP Gate&Up; additionally, SA Proj. is faster in practice (1.46 vs. 1.27 samples/sec on 4×H100) because it avoids adapter forward/backward overhead. Against WiSE-FT (weight-space interpolation), the selective methods are more robust: WiSE-FT can preserve held-out accuracy but requires careful, task-dependent selection of blending coefficients per stage, whereas SA Proj. and MLP Gate&Up need no per-stage tuning. MoE (mixture-of-experts expansion) also fails to match the learning-stability balance and is substantially slower (0.44 samples/sec) due to routing overhead. Overall, the selective tuning recipes match or exceed all four baselines while remaining simpler — no replay data, no auxiliary parameters, no per-stage weight blending, and no architectural modifications.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of continual learning for LMMs and mechanistic interpretability of transformers. It departs from the standard continual learning toolkit (replay, regularization, parameter expansion, adapters) by instead asking *which existing components* to update, guided by the interpretability literature that frames self-attention as routing and MLPs as key-value memories that write into the residual stream. While prior LLM fine-tuning studies (Luo et al., Biderman et al., Lin et al.) documented the learning-forgetting trade-off for full-model vs. LoRA tuning, and prior LMM continual learning work (Chen et al., various VQA-CL papers) proposed architectural expansions or distillation, this is the first systematic study to open the LLM into its sub-components (SA projections vs. MLP gate/up/down) and to connect the observed forgetting to a measurable output-token distribution shift via a counting-bias probe.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is immediately actionable: practitioners can teach LMMs new skills by freezing most parameters and updating only SA Proj. or MLP Gate&Up, requiring no extra modules, no replay data, and no per-stage hyperparameter tuning. The conceptual reframing—that apparent forgetting is largely a task-format-induced bias in the output distribution rather than erasure of stored knowledge—opens new avenues for mitigation (e.g., regularizing the output distribution directly) and explains the counterintuitive recovery phenomenon. Key open questions include whether these recipes hold at commercial model scale (the authors note they did not test beyond ~8B parameters), whether they remain effective with billions of training samples, and whether the distribution-shift explanation generalizes to more complex or compositional skills beyond the five tested.
