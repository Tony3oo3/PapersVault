## Problem

Existing illumination-aware rPPG methods suffer from two critical limitations: they rely on the Lambertian assumption, which ignores non-Lambertian specular highlights that frequently corrupt facial skin signals, and they exhibit poor out-of-distribution generalization because their illumination-handling mechanisms are constrained to the lighting conditions observed during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262544/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 320-frame facial RGB video clip (64×64 crops detected via MTCNN) and outputs an rPPG waveform from which heart rate is estimated. During training, the network additionally produces three decomposed video sequences—illumination, reflectance, and specular highlights—used to enforce physics-grounded constraints, but at inference only the reflectance feature extractor and rPPG estimator are active, making the pipeline lightweight.

## Method

SHINE-PPG decomposes each input frame into illumination (L), reflectance (R), and specular highlight (H) components via three parallel encoder-decoder branches following the non-Lambertian model V = R∘L + H. The decomposition is self-supervised, guided by five physics-motivated losses: reflectance invariance under color-jitter augmentation, L1 sparsity and dark-channel-prior KL divergence for specular isolation, illumination-weighted color consistency between highlights and lighting, a multi-scale frequency-domain penalty that pushes high-frequency energy into reflectance and low-frequency energy into illumination, and a log-domain reconstruction loss enforcing the formation model. An adversarial illumination enhancement stage then uses a learnable AdaIN layer to synthesize out-of-distribution lighting conditions that maximize rPPG estimation error, after which the reflectance branch and estimator are fine-tuned on these adversarial samples to learn illumination-invariant physiological features. Training proceeds in four progressive stages (Lambertian initialization → specular isolation → joint refinement → adversarial enhancement) to ensure stable convergence, and the feature extractors and rPPG estimator are inherited from Contrast-Phys.

## Results

On the most challenging Car dataset (dynamic driving illumination), SHINE-PPG achieves MAE 1.68 bpm, RMSE 3.55 bpm, and Pearson R = 0.89, outperforming all 9 baselines. On the stable-illumination datasets (UBFC, PURE, Indoor), SHINE-PPG attains near-perfect correlations (R ≥ 0.99) with MAE as low as 0.13 bpm on PURE. In cross-domain evaluation (Table 2), SHINE-PPG achieves R = 0.74 on PURE→Car and MAE 7.64 bpm on UBFC→Car, nearly doubling the correlation of the next-best methods (R ≈ 0.28–0.30) under severe out-of-distribution lighting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SHINE-PPG achieves the best MAE and RMSE on 4 of 5 intra-domain datasets (UBFC, PURE, Indoor, Car), with the sole exception being COHFACE where RhythmFormer [43] edges ahead (MAE 1.17 vs. 1.47, R 0.97 vs. 0.93). On the most challenging Car dataset with dynamic driving illumination, SHINE-PPG reduces MAE from 2.69 bpm (Shao et al., best background-based) and 2.16 bpm (Chen et al., best Retinex-based) to 1.68 bpm, while improving Pearson R from 0.87 and 0.78 to 0.89. In cross-domain transfer (Table 2), the advantage widens dramatically: on PURE→Car, SHINE-PPG reaches R = 0.74 versus 0.43 (DD-rPPGNet) and 0.29 (Chen et al.), and on UBFC→Car it achieves MAE 7.64 bpm versus 9.80 bpm (RhythmMamba) and 11.08 bpm (RhythmFormer). Methods without explicit illumination modeling (PhysFormer, EfficientPhys) degrade most severely, with Car MAE exceeding 4.7 bpm and cross-domain R dropping below 0.11. The Retinex-based baselines (Chen et al., Yang et al.) improve over unstructured methods but remain vulnerable to specular highlights, confirming the paper's central claim that the Lambertian assumption is insufficient for real-world facial rPPG.

## Relation to Prior Work

SHINE-PPG extends the classical Retinex decomposition (I = R∘L) by incorporating the Dichromatic Reflection Model to add an explicit specular-highlight term (I = R∘L + H), building directly on the non-Lambertian formulation of Baslamisli et al. (CVPR 2018). Within rPPG specifically, it departs from two prior illumination-handling paradigms: background-based methods that use surrounding pixels as lighting priors (ND-DeeprPPG, DD-rPPGNet, Shao et al.) and Retinex-based methods that assume Lambertian skin (Chen et al., Yang et al.), both of which the paper identifies as insufficient for dynamic, real-world lighting. The adversarial illumination enhancement via a learnable AdaIN layer is a novel training strategy not previously applied to rPPG, though AdaIN itself originates from style-transfer literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical gap: rPPG methods that perform well in controlled lab settings (UBFC, PURE) collapse under real-world dynamic lighting (driving scenarios), with correlations dropping to near zero for most baselines. By physically grounding the decomposition and explicitly removing specular highlights, SHINE-PPG provides an interpretable pathway to illumination-robust physiological sensing that could benefit face anti-spoofing and driver-monitoring applications. However, the absolute performance on the Car dataset remains modest (MAE ≈ 1.7 bpm, R ≈ 0.89 intra-domain; R ≈ 0.30–0.74 cross-domain), and the authors acknowledge that spatially non-uniform and dynamically varying illumination beyond what a global AdaIN can model remains an open challenge.
