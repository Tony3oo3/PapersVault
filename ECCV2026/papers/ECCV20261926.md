## Problem

Best-of-N selection in vision-language models is not reproducible: on a fixed candidate pool, small scoring perturbations (temperature ±0.05, probability truncation, dropout) flip the winning answer on 41–47% of items. This instability is specific to likelihood-based scoring, where summed token-level scores conflate answer quality with response length and model state, and prior work on reward hacking with large pools left the more basic question of whether the scoring step can agree with itself on a small, unchanged pool unaddressed.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image paired with a text prompt and a pre-generated pool of N=8 candidate responses that is frozen for the entire evaluation (no new decoding during scoring or replay). Each candidate is scored by computing the per-token log-ratio between the fine-tuned policy and a frozen reference model conditioned on the full input (image, prompt, and all preceding tokens), then averaging across all tokens to yield one scalar per candidate; the output is the argmax candidate. The SRP audit protocol additionally replays only the scoring step under pre-specified perturbations and reports flip-rate and per-replay agreement with the original winner, keeping the pool and compute budget identical across all runs.

## Method

DSPA separates two concerns: a test-time anchored selector that drives selection stability and a training-time preference filter that improves pool factuality. The anchored selector scores each candidate by averaging the per-token log-ratio log[π_θ(y_t | y_<t, x) / π_ref(y_t | y_<t, x)] over all |y| tokens, where π_ref is a pre-committed frozen checkpoint that sees the full image-and-prompt context; averaging (rather than summing) prevents uninformative suffixes from mechanically inflating the score, and the fixed reference prevents score magnitudes from drifting when the policy is updated. At training time, preference pairs are filtered to exclude candidates with verbatim prompt overlap exceeding 12 tokens, preferred/rejected length ratios outside 0.8–1.2, or hallucinated object affirmations on existence questions, before LoRA-DPO fine-tuning. Ablations confirm both constraints are necessary: removing per-token averaging (sequence-level sum) returns flip-rate to 40.8%, and replacing the fixed reference with a self-anchored or co-moving denominator yields 35.4% and 33.6%, versus 24.1% for the full anchored design. On identical N=8 pools, the anchored selector reduces the average SRP flip-rate from 44.3% to 24.9% and POPE hallucination from 9.6% to 6.2%, with roughly 71% of residual flips being semantically equivalent near-ties.

## Results

On identical N=8 candidate pools for LLaVA-1.5 7B, the anchored selector reduces POPE object-hallucination rate from 9.6% (BoN + length-norm) to 6.2%, a 35% relative reduction, while modestly improving VQAv2 (79.4→79.7), OK-VQA (60.8→61.2), TextVQA (59.6→60.1), MME (1532.8→1541.0), and MMMU (36.4→36.8). Under the SRP stability protocol, the average flip-rate drops from 44.3% to 24.9% and agreement with the original winner rises from 57.2% to 77.5% across three perturbation families (temperature jitter, probability truncation, dropout). The stability gain transfers to LLaVA-13B (38.5%→22.3% flip-rate), and the utility gain transfers to Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct (judge-estimated object hallucination 38.4%→8.7%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against RLAIF-V 7B (a separately aligned model using a standard length-normalized selector), the anchored selector achieves a substantially lower flip-rate (24.9% vs. 35.1%) and lower POPE hallucination (6.2% vs. 9.6%), indicating that alignment training alone is insufficient to match the stability and factuality gains of the anchored scoring rule. Compared to the standard BoN + length-norm baseline, the anchored selector cuts the flip-rate by 19.4 percentage points (44.3%→24.9%) and POPE by 3.4 points (9.6%→6.2%) on the same candidate pool. The ε-margin tie-break, which only resolves near-ties without changing the scoring function, yields only a modest flip-rate improvement (37.1%) and no POPE benefit (9.8% vs. 9.6%), confirming that tie-breaking alone is insufficient. The anchored selector also outperforms greedy decoding (N=1) on POPE (6.2% vs. 10.5%) and all standard VQA benchmarks, showing that multi-sample selection with the correct scoring rule dominates single-sample output.

## Relation to Prior Work

The paper repurposes the per-token log-ratio formula from DPO [31] and contrastive decoding [4,22,25] for a different objective: stable selection over a fixed candidate pool rather than preference learning or generation-time steering. It positions itself against the reward-hacking literature [9,18], which studies selector gaming as the pool grows, by asking a more basic question—whether the winner is even reproducible when N and the pool are held constant. The SRP audit protocol draws on replay-style evaluation ideas from verifier-imperfection studies [5,14,38] but enforces a stricter same-pool/same-budget boundary with pre-specified perturbations, and the training-time filtering connects to preference-construction practices in RLAIF-V [40] and length-bias correction in NMT [19,29].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a ±0.05 temperature shift flips the BoN winner on 41–47% of items is practically significant for any deployment relying on likelihood-based selection, and the SRP protocol gives practitioners a concrete audit tool to check their own pipelines. The anchored selector's +5–10% end-to-end overhead for a 19-point flip-rate reduction and 35% relative POPE reduction makes it a low-cost drop-in fix. Open questions include how flip-rate scales with N (outside SRP's fixed-budget scope), whether the perturbation neighborhood (temperature, top-p, dropout) captures real-world framework changes like precision switches, and whether the residual 24.9% flip rate can be further reduced without moving to O(N²) pairwise methods.
