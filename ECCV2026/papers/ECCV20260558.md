## Problem

Attention sinks—tokens that attract disproportionate attention—have been studied in LLMs and ViTs separately, but their cross-modal role in Large Vision-Language Models remains unresolved: prior work offers conflicting interpretations, with some arguing sinks are irrelevant artifacts to suppress and others claiming they encode vital global information. No unified framework exists that distinguishes sinks by computational origin, characterizes what information they carry across LLM layers, or determines when and where modulating them helps versus hurts downstream performance.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a frozen LVLM (LLaVA-1.5-7B with CLIP ViT-L/14 at 336px producing 576 visual patches) that receives an image and a text query, producing a standard autoregressive text response. The gating module's input is the hidden state of the final input token at each LLM layer (a D-dimensional vector that progressively aggregates visual and textual context), and its output is a 2-element softmax vector specifying the relative key-scaling ratio between V-sink and remaining visual tokens for the next layer. No changes are made to the input format or output structure of the base model.

## Method

The paper first establishes a unified taxonomy of visual sinks: ViT-emerged sinks (V-sinks) that carry uniformly elevated norms from the vision encoder through the MLP projector, and LLM-emerged sinks (L-sinks) that develop sharp dimension-specific activations within deeper LLM layers via the same mechanism as text sinks. Linear probing on CLEVR reveals both sink types stably encode multi-granularity scene attributes (count, size, color, shape) across all 32 LLM layers, while oracle key-gating sweeps expose a fundamental trade-off: strengthening sink attention helps global/coarse tasks but hurts fine-grained perception, and the optimal direction reverses across layers. To exploit this, Layer-wise Sink Gating (LSG) inserts a lightweight 2-layer MLP (~262K parameters, 4096→64→2) between each pair of consecutive LLM layers; each gate takes the final input token's hidden state and predicts per-layer key-scaling ratios that modulate the pre-SoftMax attention logits for V-sink versus remaining visual tokens. The entire LVLM backbone is frozen, and gates are trained solely with standard next-token prediction loss on 10K Cambrian-7M samples, requiring no task-specific supervision. Independently trained single-layer gates can be greedily stacked (e.g., 5 layers) to compound gains, achieving +1.55% on MMStar and +3.08% on CVBench over baseline.

## Results

On LLaVA-1.5-7B, the 5-layer LSG stack {L3, L6, L7, L10, L19} achieves +1.55 pp on MMStar (33.27 → 34.82) and +3.08 pp on CVBench (57.29 → 60.37) relative to the unmodified baseline. Even the single best layer (L10) yields +0.72 pp on MMStar and +2.14 pp on CVBench, with 8 out of 10 sub-tasks showing non-negative gains. The gating module adds only ~262K parameters per layer and is trained on 10K general-purpose samples via next-token prediction, keeping the LVLM backbone fully frozen.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LSG outperforms all three prior sink-handling baselines on both MMStar and CVBench. VAR [16] (Kang et al.), which suppresses L-sinks via threshold-based head selection without input conditioning or layer-wise variation, yields only −0.01 on MMStar and +0.75 on CVBench. Sink-to-the-front [23] (Luo et al.), a static reordering of V-sinks to the front of the sequence, actually degrades performance (−0.13 MMStar, −0.39 CVBench). DIYSink (CoT) [23], which conditions on input via chain-of-thought routing but operates at a single input level without layer-wise modulation, achieves +0.25 MMStar and +0.41 CVBench. LSG's advantage stems from combining input-conditioned and layer-wise modulation: the single-layer L10 gate already doubles DIYSink's MMStar gain (+0.72 vs. +0.25) and quintuples its CVBench gain (+2.14 vs. +0.41), while the 5-layer stack further widens the margin to +1.55/+3.08.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of work on attention sinks in LVLMs: Kang et al. [16] identified LLM-emerged sinks (L-sinks) and proposed a suppress-only strategy (VAR), while Luo et al. [23] characterized ViT-emerged sinks (V-sinks) and proposed static reordering or input-conditioned routing. The key conceptual departure is showing that these two categories arise from fundamentally different computational mechanisms (ViT bidirectional attention + projector vs. LLM FFN residual writes) and therefore encode different information, motivating a joint rather than separate treatment. The key-gating intervention (Eq. 3) is adapted from Wang et al. [36]'s continuous rescaling for interpretability, but repurposed here as a learned, token-group-level control signal rather than a per-head diagnostic tool.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a genuine open question in the LVLM interpretability literature—whether attention sinks are harmful artifacts or useful global priors—by showing the answer is task- and layer-dependent, with a concrete trade-off between coarse reasoning and fine-grained perception. LSG's practical appeal lies in its extreme lightness (~262K params per layer, frozen backbone, NTP-only training), making it a drop-in module for existing LVLM pipelines. However, absolute gains are modest (+1.55 MMStar, +3.08 CVBench in the best 5-layer stack), cross-architecture validation is preliminary (two additional variants, single-layer only), and the 2-group formulation leaves open whether finer decompositions or alternative gating signals could yield larger improvements at scale.
