## Problem

Fine-tuning-based backdoor defenses (e.g., NAD, FST, TSBD) assume that clean and backdoor optimization signals can be disentangled using a small clean dataset, but this assumption is never tested against an adaptive adversary who anticipates the defense. The paper shows that an attacker can deliberately entangle backdoor gradients with clean gradients on task-critical neurons, making it impossible to remove the backdoor without destroying clean accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262481/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes clean RGB images (32×32 CIFAR-10 or 64×64 Tiny-ImageNet) along with a surrogate model trained on a clean subset to compute Fisher information, and produces a frozen input-dependent trigger generator T_φ that maps each clean image to a perturbed version x̃ = clip(x + ε·T_φ(x), 0, 1) with a configurable perturbation budget ε (default 0.1). Poisoned samples are relabeled to a single target class (dirty-label), and the final output is a backdoored victim model trained from scratch on the mixed clean/poisoned dataset. The trigger is unique per input rather than a fixed pattern, making reverse-engineering-based detection infeasible.

## Method

The attack proceeds in three stages. First, a diagonal Fisher Information Matrix is computed on a clean subset to identify the top-k% most task-critical neurons, yielding binary importance masks per layer. Second, a neural trigger generator is trained with a multi-objective loss L_T = λ_bd·L_bd + λ_align·L_align + λ_clean·L_clean + L_L2, where the key alignment term L_align = −(1/L)Σ_i (g_i^c·g_i^b)/(‖g_i^c‖₂‖g_i^b‖₂) maximizes cosine similarity between clean and backdoor gradients restricted to Fisher-masked neurons, forcing the backdoor to update the same parameters in the same directions as the clean task. Third, the frozen generator poisons a fraction ρ of the training set (relabeling to the target class) and the victim model is trained from scratch via standard supervised learning. The core insight is that gradient alignment on high-Fisher neurons makes the backdoor and clean objectives share the same gradient subspace, so any fine-tuning or pruning that suppresses the backdoor simultaneously degrades clean accuracy.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, the proposed gradient-aligned backdoor attack achieves 100% Attack Success Rate (ASR) with 93.27% Clean Accuracy (ACC) pre-defense. After applying eight post-training defenses, the attack maintains >99.5% ASR against fine-tuning-based defenses (FT, NAD, FST, TSBD) and an average of 90.4% ASR across all defenses, substantially outperforming the next-best attack (COMBAT at 30.65% under FST). On Tiny-ImageNet, the attack achieves 99.90% ASR pre-defense and retains 91.38% ASR under TSBD, while FST reduces ASR to 21.38% only at the cost of collapsing ACC to 32.74%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against all seven baseline attacks, the proposed method achieves substantially higher post-defense ASR. Under FST, Ours maintains 99.58% ASR versus COMBAT (30.65%), Narcissus (93.91%), and BadNets (2.08%); under TSBD, Ours retains 99.84% versus COMBAT (35.57%) and BadNets (1.78%). The closest competitor is Input-Aware (96.50% ASR under FT), but Ours still leads by 3.5 points and extends the gap to 3.47 points under NAD (99.97% vs. 98.80%). Against the eight defenses, the attack is most vulnerable to ANP (46.50% ASR on CIFAR-10), but this defense fails entirely on Tiny-ImageNet where Ours retains 99.90% ASR. Fine-tuning-based defenses (FT, NAD, FST, TSBD) are the most targeted: Ours sustains >99.5% ASR under all four, whereas traditional attacks like BadNets and WaNet drop below 15% ASR under the same defenses. The DER metric confirms this: Ours scores 48–55 (lower is worse for the defense) across fine-tuning defenses, while BadNets scores 93–96, indicating the defense is far more effective against conventional attacks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the generator-based trigger paradigm of Input-Aware (Nguyen & Tran, NeurIPS 2020), but repurposes the generator's optimization objective: rather than minimizing perceptibility, it maximizes cosine similarity between backdoor and clean gradients on Fisher-identified task-critical neurons (borrowing the diagonal FIM approximation from EWC, Kirkpatrick et al. 2017). Conceptually, it departs from the entire fine-tuning-based defense literature (NAD, TSBD, FST, I-BAU, RNP, ANP, Unit) by identifying a shared structural assumption—that clean-data fine-tuning can disentangle backdoor from benign signals—and constructing an attack that makes that assumption false by design. The work also extends the adaptive-attacker threat model beyond prior stealth-focused attacks (WaNet, COMBAT, Narcissus) by explicitly optimizing for defense resilience rather than trigger invisibility.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical stakes are high for MLaaS and continual-learning pipelines where fine-tuning on a small clean set is the primary (and often only) backdoor mitigation; this paper shows that an adversary who anticipates that step can render it ineffective, sustaining ~99% ASR across eight defenses and five architectures. The key open question is whether any post-training defense can be robust against a fully adaptive adversary who has access to the defense's optimization dynamics, or whether the field must shift toward training-stage guarantees or architectural-level protections. A limitation is that the attack requires the adversary to know the defense family (fine-tuning) and to compute Fisher information on a surrogate, which narrows the threat model somewhat; whether the gradient-alignment principle transfers to defenses that do not rely on clean-data fine-tuning (e.g., certified unlearning or formal verification) remains unexplored.
