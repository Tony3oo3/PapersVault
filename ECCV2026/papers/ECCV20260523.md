## Problem

Current VLM benchmarks evaluate perceptual realism—whether generated outputs look correct—but leave untested whether models can actually construct physically valid artifacts under engineering constraints. The gap between perceiving a structure and building one (physical generative reasoning) is unmeasured by existing leaderboards, making it invisible whether frontier models possess the structural, procedural, and code-compliance knowledge needed for design-to-construction pipelines.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260523/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives five 512×512 orthographic renders (front, front-right, back, left, right) of a target timber-frame structure plus building context and rules, under two conditions: Frame (bare framing visible) and Facade (framing occluded by finished cladding, requiring inference of hidden load-bearing topology). The model outputs executable Blender Python construction code that instantiates a full 3D scene graph with individual foundation sills, floor joists, wall studs, headers, ridge beams, rafters, and collar ties at correct positions, orientations, and IRC cross-sections. The task is iterative: the model receives structured per-test pass/fail feedback with violation counts (e.g., 'BeamPost gap exceeds 3.0 m; detected spacing 7.0 m') and refines its code across multiple turns within a retry budget.

## Method

DreamHouse grounds evaluation in residential timber-frame construction, a domain with fully codified correctness criteria (International Residential Code) and discrete verifiable components, yielding 26,543 structurally verified structures across 13 architectural styles at LOD 350 fabrication-grade detail. A deterministic 10-test validation suite operates directly on the scene graph—covering load-path integrity, span limits, member connectivity, dual-end restraint, deflection, and fabrication geometry—bypassing expensive physics simulation for fast, interpretable scoring. The evaluation is formulated as an agentic generation task with three protocols that ablate scaffold design: Planner-Atomic (single-pass full script), Planner-Reactive (self-determined phase decomposition with full regeneration on failure), and Planner-Managed (externally sequenced phases with per-phase retry and persistent scene state). An additional iterative refinement protocol tests whether models can improve visual fidelity via side-by-side render feedback without breaking structural validity. The key finding is that structural validity and visual fidelity are orthogonal axes, and protocol choice (scaffold design) produces larger performance swings than model selection, revealing physical generative reasoning as a distinct, underdeveloped capability.

## Results

Across 21,600 agentic tasks (~5B tokens), the best joint pass rate (satisfying all 10 structural tests AND visual fidelity ≥ 0.6) is only 7.1% (Claude, Planner-Atomic, Frame). Structural pass rates range from 15.7% to 79.2% depending on model and protocol, while visual fidelity scores remain low (0.175–0.406) across all conditions. Protocol choice dominates model choice: Gemini swings from 45.4% to 78.5% structural pass rate simply by switching from Planner-Atomic to Planner-Managed, a 33-point gain that reverses its ranking relative to GPT-5. Open-source models (Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, LLaVA-OV-72B, InternVL3, etc.) achieve near-zero structural pass rates, with only Qwen3.5 reaching 23.1% under Planner-Atomic with thinking mode enabled.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper, so the 'baselines' are the three frontier VLMs evaluated under identical protocols. GPT-5 achieves the highest structural pass rate under Planner-Atomic (79.2% Frame) but collapses to 33.3% under Planner-Managed, a 46-point drop indicating strong single-pass synthesis but weak incremental commitment. Gemini exhibits the opposite pattern, gaining 33 points (45.4% → 78.5%) when moved to Planner-Managed, ultimately overtaking GPT-5 by 45 points under that protocol. Claude is the most protocol-stable (71.6% vs. 71.3%) and achieves the best joint pass rate (7.1%), reflecting a balanced but moderate performance on both axes. Under the harder Facade condition (occluded framing), Claude and Gemini improve structurally while GPT-5 drops 15.5 points, suggesting GPT-5 relies on direct structural mimicry rather than architectural inference. Open-source models are far behind: Qwen3.5-397B-A17B reaches only 23.1% structural pass (Planner-Atomic, thinking mode), and all other open-source models (LLaVA-OV-72B, InternVL3, Qwen3-VL-30B/8B, Kimi K2.5) score 0% across all protocols.

## Relation to Prior Work

DreamHouse departs from the dominant line of physical-reasoning benchmarks (PhysBench, VSI-Bench, Think-with-3D), which evaluate passive comprehension—observing a scene and answering questions—by targeting the harder generative gap: producing a structure that is itself physically valid without any reference to recall. It also diverges from 3D scene generation work (TRELLIS, SpatialGen) that optimizes photometric or geometric metrics against reference renderings, leaving structural validity unmeasured, by operating at fabrication-grade detail (LOD 350) with deterministic engineering compliance as the evaluation signal. Methodologically, it adopts the iterative code-driven agentic loop popularized by VIGA (WRITE→RUN→RENDER→COMPARE→REVISE) but replaces the photometric oracle with a 10-test structural validation suite grounded in the International Residential Code, a substitution that makes structural failures (missing members, span violations) visible where rendering-based feedback cannot detect them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a capability axis—physical generative reasoning—that is largely invisible on existing leaderboards, with even the strongest model achieving only a 7.1% joint pass rate for structural validity and visual fidelity simultaneously. The finding that protocol design can reverse model rankings (Gemini overtakes GPT-5 under Planner-Managed despite trailing by 34 points under Planner-Atomic) challenges the assumption that model capability is protocol-independent and has direct implications for how agentic systems are deployed. Key limitations include restriction to timber-frame construction, evaluation limited to closed-source frontier models due to computational cost, and the absence of input-side analysis (what visual representations or training objectives would let models internalize structural grammar rather than recover it through iterative feedback). Extending the constraint vocabulary to concrete, steel, or multi-material assemblies, and scaling to open-source models, are natural next steps.
