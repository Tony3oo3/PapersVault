## Problem

Existing runtime controllers for real-time object detection rely on coarse frame-level accuracy metrics (average AP or IoU) to guide detector selection, which obscures large per-object heterogeneity in accuracy degradation caused by temporal mismatch. A frame can maintain high average IoU while individual fast-moving objects drop to zero IoU, leading to suboptimal scheduling that fails to protect safety-critical objects under resource constraints.

## Input / Output

The OLAP model consumes only geometric bounding-box parameters (center, width, height) from two consecutive detection cycles plus the previous inference time in frames, producing a 10-dimensional feature vector per object; no image pixels or learned embeddings are required. The control algorithm additionally takes a user-defined IoU threshold τ, per-object priority weights ω, and a set of candidate detectors with detectability profiles and estimated inference times, and outputs a single selected detector for the next cycle. The per-object deadline is expressed as a closed-form integer in frames, making it directly usable as a scheduling constraint.

## Method

The core model is a Generalized Linear Model that maps 10 hand-crafted geometric features (aspect ratio, area, polar position, GIoU between real-time and offline boxes, normalized velocities, velocity magnitude/direction, and previous inference time) to two parameters via Sigmoid and Softplus link functions: β (IoU intercept) and γ (exponential decay rate). These parameterize an exponential IoU decay curve IoU = β·e^(−γ·a), from which the per-object deadline is obtained in closed form as C = ⌊ln(β/τ)/γ⌋. The OLAP-C controller then computes a Relative Average Precision (RAP) score for each candidate detector by summing a drop-window component (weighted precision while the detector is running) and a future-window component (expected post-inference quality scaled by detector detectability), both evaluated against the predicted per-object deadlines and user-defined importance weights. The detector maximizing RAP is selected for the next cycle. Training uses synthetic inference times paired with ground-truth labels and a visual tracker to simulate real-time detections, optimizing regularized MSE without requiring retraining across different detectors.

## Results

Across 71 video scenarios on Argoverse-HD and MOT17, OLAP-C achieves 0.287 sAP on the Intel Node (39% over ROMA, 86.4% over Best Static), 0.391 sAP on Apple M3 (24.6% over ROMA), and 0.408 sAP on Jetson Orin Nano (1.3% over ROMA). For deadline prediction, the OLAP model reduces RMSE by 56% on average over the frame-level ARISE/FLAP baseline, with gains up to 67% at longer horizons. The control overhead remains below 10% of average detector inference time on all platforms.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ROMA [15] (the state-of-the-art frame-level closed-loop detector-selection controller), OLAP-C wins on all three hardware platforms: +39% sAP on the Intel Node (0.287 vs. ~0.206), +24.6% on Apple M3 (0.391 vs. ~0.314), and +1.3% on Jetson (0.408 vs. ~0.403). The largest gap appears on the most constrained device (Intel Node), where suboptimal high-latency detector choices cause severe temporal mismatch that frame-level metrics fail to capture. Against the ARISE/FLAP frame-level IoU baseline [13] for deadline prediction, OLAP reduces average RMSE by 56% (up to 67% at longer horizons), while the intermediate OLAP-tiny variant (same two features as FLAP but applied per-object) already achieves a 32.3% RMSE reduction, confirming that object-level granularity alone is the primary source of gain. The Best Static baseline (single fixed detector) is outperformed by 86.4% on Intel, 29.8% on M3, and 2.2% on Jetson, demonstrating that adaptive control is most critical under tight compute budgets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the streaming perception framework of Li et al. (ECCV 2020), which introduced temporal mismatch as the key accuracy-degradation mechanism, and the closed-loop detector-selection paradigm of ROMA (WACV 2023), which uses frame-level AP prediction to guide model switching. The critical departure is granularity: where prior runtime controllers (ROMA, ARISE) aggregate accuracy into a single frame-level scalar, this work models IoU decay independently per object using a 10-feature Generalized Linear Model, then inverts the exponential decay to obtain a closed-form deadline. The control algorithm (OLAP-C) reuses ROMA's detectability-profile structure and drop/future-window scoring but replaces the frame-level AP signal with per-object, priority-weighted deadline feedback.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For safety-critical edge perception (autonomous driving, robotics), the ability to guarantee that a specific object (e.g., a crossing pedestrian) is re-detected before its bounding box becomes unreliable is more actionable than maximizing average frame-level AP. The paper demonstrates that a deliberately simple model (two 10-dimensional linear layers with Sigmoid/Softplus links) suffices, keeping overhead below 10% of inference time on constrained hardware. Limitations include the proof-of-concept scope of the controller (only detector switching, no DVFS or task scheduling), reliance on ground-truth labels during training-data synthesis, and evaluation restricted to YOLO11m at varying resolutions. Open questions include whether the exponential-decay assumption holds for highly non-linear motion (e.g., occlusion, turning), how the approach generalizes to 3D detection or multi-sensor fusion, and whether the priority-weighting mechanism can be learned rather than hand-specified.
