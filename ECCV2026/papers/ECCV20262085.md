## Problem

Polysemanticity—where a single neuron encodes multiple, often unrelated visual concepts—fundamentally limits single-neuron interpretability. Existing attribution-clustering methods for disentangling these concepts are confined to CNNs and require manually specifying the number of concept clusters (K) per neuron, making them neither generalizable to modern Transformer architectures nor scalable to large models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262085/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained vision model (CNN or Transformer), a probing image set (ImageNet validation), and a target neuron identifier, where a 'neuron' is defined as a full channel in CNNs or a hidden dimension in Transformers. It produces a set of disjoint concept clusters (each a group of images with similar predecessor-attribution patterns), the per-neuron adaptive cluster count, and optionally VLM-generated textual descriptions. Spatial or sequence-position activations are aggregated into scalar neuron values before attribution is computed, so the footprint matrix has one row per highly-activating sample and one column per predecessor neuron.

## Method

SPICE computes Input × Gradient attribution vectors from all predecessor neurons in layer l−1 to the target neuron for each highly-activating sample, stacks them into an attribution footprint matrix, and normalizes along the neuron dimension to counteract feature-space anisotropy. An iterative clustering algorithm then progressively partitions the footprint rows: starting at k=2, it identifies clusters whose average pairwise cosine similarity (cohesion) exceeds an adaptive threshold τ, accepts them as final concepts, removes them from the working set, and adjusts k until no further cohesive clusters remain. The threshold τ is computed per neuron as the maximum of the 95th percentile of pairwise similarities and the second KDE peak, accommodating both the unimodal similarity distributions typical of early layers and the bimodal distributions that emerge in deeper layers. This design eliminates any preset K, requires no architecture-specific propagation rules, and operates identically on CNNs and Transformers.

## Results

SPICE is evaluated on separability (ratio of intra-cluster to inter-cluster CLIP cosine similarity) across four architectures (ResNet-50, ViT-B, DenseNet, CLIP ViT-B) at multiple depths. SPICE achieves the highest separability in the majority of settings, with the largest gains in deep layers: 1.577 ± 0.089 on ViT-B B11 (vs. 1.035 for PURE, 1.357 for LE, 0.998 for CPE) and 1.410 ± 0.102 on CLIP ViT-B B11. In early layers (e.g., B2, L2.1), LE is competitive or slightly ahead, but SPICE's advantage grows with depth as concepts become more abstract and polysemantic. A simulation-based CoSy evaluation (AUC/MAD with GPT-4.1 + SDXL) further confirms SPICE's semantic faithfulness over all baselines across ViT-B blocks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPICE outperforms all three baselines in the majority of model/layer combinations, with the most pronounced advantage in deep layers where polysemanticity is strongest. Against PURE (CVPR 2024), SPICE gains up to +0.54 separability (ViT-B B11: 1.577 vs. 1.035), and PURE's fixed-K requirement (set via silhouette score) limits its ability to discover the correct number of concepts per neuron. Against LE (ICML 2024), SPICE is slightly behind in early layers (e.g., ViT-B B2: 1.137 vs. 1.199) but clearly surpasses it in deep layers (ViT-B B11: 1.577 vs. 1.357; CLIP ViT-B B11: 1.410 vs. 1.244), where LE's text-prior-based approach degrades. Against CPE (CVPR 2025), SPICE dominates across all settings; CPE's near-constant score (~0.99) reflects an evaluation mismatch—its VLM-generated text concepts are measured in CLIP image-embedding space, and its protocol links each sample to ~3 concepts per neuron, inflating inter-cluster similarity. The CoSy simulation-based evaluation (AUC/MAD) further confirms SPICE's lead over all three baselines across ViT-B blocks, validating that the gains are not an artifact of the CLIP-embedding metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPICE extends the attribution-clustering line of work initiated by PURE and Hesse et al., which grouped a neuron's gradient-based footprints to separate encoded concepts but was restricted to CNNs and required a manually specified number of clusters K. It also departs from VLM-probing approaches (LE, CPE) that rely on external text priors and suffer from representation misalignment, and from Sparse Autoencoders that disentangle features without revealing the model's intrinsic attribution flow. The key methodological departure is an adaptive, per-neuron cohesion threshold (combining a percentile and KDE second-peak criterion) that eliminates the fixed-K heuristic, enabling the first unified analysis of polysemanticity across both CNN and Transformer architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The first systematic cross-architecture comparison reveals distinct inductive biases: CNNs preserve cohesive concepts through local processing with a sharp drop in final blocks, while ViTs exhibit a U-shaped diversity pattern driven by global self-attention, and the similarity distribution shifts from unimodal to bimodal with depth. The finding that polysemantic neurons form through fundamentally different computational pathways (disjoint vs. overlapping upstream circuits) suggests interpretability tools must be mechanism-aware rather than one-size-fits-all. Open questions include extending the framework to circuit-level (multi-neuron) disentanglement, scaling to much larger models, and developing verification protocols that do not depend on CLIP's high-level semantic space, which structurally limits evaluation of early-layer, low-level features.
