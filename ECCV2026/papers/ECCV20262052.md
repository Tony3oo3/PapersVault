## Problem

Existing novel view synthesis methods (NeRF and 3D Gaussian Splatting) have not been adequately tested on the extreme conditions found in underground cave environments: very low signal-to-noise ratios, high-frequency geological texture, self-occlusion, contrast loss from co-located lighting, high dynamic range, and glare. No public multi-view RAW dataset from a UNESCO World Heritage cave system existed to benchmark these methods under such conditions, leaving a gap between the needs of palaeoarchaeological documentation and the capabilities of current NVS research.

## Input / Output

The dataset provides 1,100+ RAW images at 4k×3k resolution (10-bit sensor, Samsung Galaxy S24 ultrawide camera) across 10 scenes with 80–175 views each, plus camera poses estimated via COLMAP. For sRGB-based methods, images are demosaiced, converted to CIE XYZ then sRGB primaries without gamma correction, and normalized to the 99th percentile peak luminance. Evaluation renders a held-out view at full sensor resolution and compares it against an HDR+ reference created by averaging 80–100 burst exposures, so the ground truth is inherently denoised while training inputs remain single noisy frames.

## Method

The paper's key finding is that Raw-Nerfacto—a simple adaptation of Nerfacto (InstantNGP's multi-resolution hash encoding) to accept RAW images with a per-pixel RAW color-filter loss—consistently outperforms purpose-built low-light methods like RawNeRF, HDRSplat, LE3D, Gaussian-DK, and Luminance-GS. Three design choices explain its robustness: (1) RAW supervision preserves a linear Poisson-Gaussian noise model that is independent across views, unlike demosaiced sRGB which introduces spatially correlated non-linear artefacts; (2) the multi-resolution hash table shares weights across all rays, acting as a regularizer that resists fitting per-pixel noise while still resolving multi-scale spatial detail; and (3) replacing RawNeRF's exponential output activation with a sigmoid stabilises training under high noise, while NeRF-W-style per-image appearance embeddings absorb residual lighting inconsistencies from dynamic co-located sources. The paper benchmarks 10 methods (5 RAW-input, 5 sRGB-input) across all 10 scenes at full resolution, finding that 3DGS methods degrade more than NeRF methods under noise, that learned denoisers (PMRID) fail due to domain shift from urban training data, and that standard metrics (PSNR, LPIPS) are unreliable for assessing low-light reconstruction quality.

## Results

On the 10-scene Sterkfontein Caves benchmark, Raw-Nerfacto achieves the highest average PSNR of 18.07 dB, outperforming all purpose-built low-light methods. The best sRGB baseline (Nerfacto) reaches 17.08 dB, while purpose-built low-light 3DGS methods (Gaussian-DK, Luminance-GS) score 15.97 and 10.63 dB respectively. Aleth-NeRF fails to produce coherent reconstructions on any scene, and LE3D suffers OOM crashes on 2 of 10 scenes. The paper also demonstrates that standard metrics (PSNR, LPIPS) are unreliable for assessing low-light reconstruction quality, with SSIM better reflecting visual differences between methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Raw-Nerfacto (18.07 dB avg PSNR) outperforms RawNeRF (16.41 dB) by ~1.7 dB on average, with the largest gap on D3 (19.6 vs 12.1 dB) where RawNeRF catastrophically fails due to its exponential output activation. Against the sRGB equivalent Nerfacto (17.08 dB), Raw-Nerfacto gains ~1 dB, confirming the benefit of RAW supervision. Among 3DGS baselines, Raw-Splatfacto (15.64 dB) and Splatfacto (15.85 dB) both trail Raw-Nerfacto by 2+ dB, with 3DGS methods particularly vulnerable to noise-driven overfitting of Gaussian primitives. Purpose-built low-light methods underperform: Gaussian-DK (15.97 dB) and Luminance-GS (10.63 dB) produce large blurry regions and ill-fitted high-frequency Gaussians, while Aleth-NeRF (12.51 dB) fails to converge on any scene. HDRSplat (16.87 dB) is competitive on well-lit scenes (D3: 20.1 dB) but its PMRID denoiser introduces blobby artefacts and purple colour shifts on low-luminance scenes due to domain shift from its urban training data. LE3D (17.09 dB on 8/10 scenes) is competitive where it runs but crashes with OOM on D7 and D9 due to its per-Gaussian MLP memory overhead.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the low-light novel view synthesis line (RawNeRF, Gaussian-DK, Aleth-NeRF, Luminance-GS, LE3D, HDRSplat) by introducing the first public multi-view RAW dataset captured in a real cave environment, where challenges like glare, contrast loss, self-occlusion, and extreme dynamic range go beyond what prior low-light NVS benchmarks (which typically use sRGB with black-box ISP denoising) expose. Methodologically, it builds on the NeRFStudio framework (Nerfacto/Splatfacto) and InstantNGP's multi-resolution hash encoding, adapting them to RAW supervision in the spirit of RawNeRF, while also drawing on HDR+ exposure stacking for reference generation. The paper departs from prior heritage documentation work (photogrammetry, laser scanning, lidar) by framing the problem as photorealistic appearance modelling rather than geometric accuracy, and from prior NVS benchmarks by emphasising uncontrolled field capture with a smartphone rather than controlled studio or well-lit outdoor settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset fills a clear gap: no prior public benchmark combines RAW multi-view capture, high-frequency geological texture, and the full spectrum of cave-specific illumination challenges (glare, co-located light contrast loss, extreme HDR, self-occlusion) in a scientifically relevant heritage context. The finding that purpose-built low-light methods (Gaussian-DK, Luminance-GS, Aleth-NeRF) do not outperform a simple RAW-adapted baseline, and that learned denoisers (PMRID) actively degrade quality due to domain shift, redirects community attention toward noise-aware representation choices rather than post-hoc enhancement. Open questions remain substantial: no tested method coherently reconstructs occluded or glare-affected regions, bracketed exposures for true HDR are absent, and geometric/material ground truth (laser scanning, gonioreflectometry) is unavailable, leaving the full problem of high-quality digital preservation of dark heritage sites unsolved.
