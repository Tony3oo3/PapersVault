## Problem

Passive depth from focus (DFF) fundamentally requires texture to assess per-pixel focus, making it unreliable for scenes with sparse or no resolvable texture features. The conventional definition of texture—spatial variations in surface BRDF at the camera's working magnification—implies that surfaces with constant BRDF are 'textureless' and thus unrecoverable by passive methods. This paper challenges that assumption by showing that wave-optical scattering from wavelength-scale surface microgeometry produces a previously overlooked form of texture that can be amplified under ordinary ambient illumination.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260316/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a focal stack of images captured by stepping the focus of a standard lens-based camera, with a narrowband spectral filter (10–100 nm FWHM, center ≈530 nm) mounted in front of the lens. The filter reduces incident flux, requiring exposure time increases of 4×–32× to maintain adequate signal-to-noise ratio. The output is a per-pixel depth map obtained by locating the in-focus index via a Laplacian-based focus measure, with unreliable estimates (low robust z-score confidence) masked out as gray pixels in the depth map.

## Method

The paper develops a wave-optical appearance model in which the sample BRDF is a random functional of the surface heightfield, rather than the ensemble-averaged BRDF used in classical computer vision, yielding a decomposition of irradiance into a first-order (ensemble-averaged) component and a second-order component—subjective speckle from microgeometry the camera cannot resolve. Proposition 6 shows that the in-focus coefficient of variation of this speckle scales as 1/(MQ), where M is proportional to spectral bandwidth and Q is the ratio of in-focus PSF area to coherence area, meaning a narrowband filter directly amplifies second-order texture contrast. Proposition 7 then proves that the resulting texture contrast–SNR product improves even with only sublinear exposure time increases, because the contrast gain from reduced bandwidth outweighs the flux loss. The practical system is a standard DFF pipeline with a single narrowband filter, validated through closed-form theory, Monte Carlo simulation, and real-world indoor and outdoor experiments showing dramatic depth recovery improvements on seemingly textureless scenes.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative finding is that adding a narrowband spectral filter (10–100 nm FWHM, center ≈530 nm) dramatically reduces the DFF error probability Pr(c̃²_if < c̃²_oof) for first-order textureless surfaces, with theory and Monte Carlo simulation predictions closely matching (Figure 7). A 10 nm filter requires a 32× exposure increase to maintain proper exposure, yet DFF performance already improves with only a 4× increase (3 stops underexposure), and further improves approaching proper exposure (Figure 10). The method is validated at reproduction ratios 1/50–1/10, f-numbers up to 6, and 3 μm pixel pitch under both indoor (ceiling lights) and outdoor (sunlight) ambient illumination, with coherence areas Δi ≈ 50–100 μm and in-focus PSF sizes Δc,if ≈ 30–150 μm. The pixel-adaptive aggregation experiment (Figure 12) shows that the filter enables depth recovery at significantly smaller patch sizes, yielding higher lateral resolution in the reconstructed depth maps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against other depth estimation methods; its central comparison is between standard DFF (no spectral filter) and DFF augmented with a narrowband spectral filter, under identical ambient illumination and camera settings. Without a filter, the incoherent summation across the full visible spectrum eliminates subjective speckle, leaving first-order textureless surfaces effectively unrecoverable (high η, flat focus curves). With a 10 nm filter, the in-focus speckle contrast κ_I² ≈ 1/(MQ) becomes large enough to overcome sensor noise even at 3–4 stops underexposure, producing sharply peaked focus curves and low η. Theory and simulation agree closely on the error probability surface over bandwidth and exposure time (Figure 7), confirming that the optimal operating point for a given bandwidth is at the saturation limit, and that sublinear exposure increases (less than needed to maintain constant SNR) still improve the texture-contrast-to-SNR product Θ². The paper also notes two failure modes where the filter does not help: translucent surfaces (subsurface scattering diminishes speckle) and illumination with very large directional bandwidth (e.g., overcast sky, large nearby light sources), where the coherence area Δi² becomes much smaller than the PSF area Δc,if².

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends classical wave-optical speckle theory (Goodman) into the computer vision domain by showing that the ensemble-averaged BRDF used in standard appearance models (Stam, Walter et al.) hides a second-order texture component—subjective speckle from sub-resolution microgeometry—that is actually resolvable by a finite-aperture camera. It departs from the geometric-optics assumption underlying all prior DFF and stereo work, which treats constant-BRDF surfaces as fundamentally textureless, by deriving a pointwise irradiance model (Proposition 4) that retains the randomness of the sample BRDF. The practical mechanism—a narrowband spectral filter enhancing speckle contrast—differs sharply from prior passive interferometric approaches (Cossairt, Chen, Kotwal) that require complex optical setups, achieving similar coherence-based textureless depth recovery with a trivial hardware addition to a standard DFF camera.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work challenges a roughly 30-year assumption (dating to Sundaram and Nayar) that passive depth recovery of textureless scenes is fundamentally limited, showing instead that the texture requirement is far less severe than believed and can be actively enhanced with a simple spectral filter. The practical impact is significant for machine vision and robotics applications where controlled illumination is unavailable, though the method requires 3–4 stops of additional exposure and degrades under diffuse or translucent conditions. Open questions include whether second-order speckle statistics can be exploited by learning-based methods at very low SNR, whether multi-view speckle correlations enable passive stereo of textureless scenes, and how this framework interacts with confocal or focal-flow approaches.
