## Problem

State-of-the-art video super-resolution methods rely on explicit optical flow estimation, deformable convolutions, or transformer attention, all of which introduce custom CUDA kernels and heavy memory bandwidth demands that prevent real-time deployment on edge accelerators like TensorRT. No existing compact VSR architecture achieves a competitive quality-throughput balance on resource-constrained hardware such as the NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262823/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The network consumes a temporal window of T=15 low-resolution RGB frames (T×3×H×W), with the temporal dimension folded into the batch axis for parallel per-frame feature extraction. It produces a 4× upscaled high-resolution video sequence, where the final output is the sum of the network's residual prediction and a bilinearly upsampled LR base, preserving absolute spatial scale. All edge evaluations use FP16 TensorRT compilation at 180×320 or 270×480 input resolutions.

## Method

NanoVSR is a purely convolutional, bidirectional recurrent VSR network that replaces explicit motion compensation with an additive propagation scheme: forward and backward hidden states are updated via element-wise addition with input features rather than channel concatenation, halving memory bandwidth during temporal propagation. Each structural block is trained as a multi-branch topology (3×3 conv, 1×1 conv, identity, each with BatchNorm) but collapses via structural reparameterization into a single 3×3 convolution at inference, eliminating kernel-launch overhead and enabling deep TensorRT fusion. A two-stage progressive curriculum first pre-trains on 7-frame Vimeo-90K sequences for spatial feature extraction, then fine-tunes on 30-frame REDS sequences to develop long-range recurrent alignment. The reconstruction head uses cascaded PixelShuffle upsampling with a global bilinear residual connection, and the entire pipeline is trained end-to-end with Charbonnier loss under BFloat16 mixed precision.

## Results

On the REDS4 benchmark, NanoVSR-644k (644k params) achieves 28.64 dB PSNR / 0.8215 SSIM at 2.982 ms/frame on an H100, while the scaled NanoVSR-1.7M variant reaches 29.15 dB / 0.8364 SSIM at 4.268 ms/frame. On the NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX 16 GB (25 W), NanoVSR-226k delivers 43.86 FPS and NanoVSR-644k sustains 27.20 FPS at 180×320 resolution, compared to only 8.04 FPS for BasicVSR under the same conditions. The ultra-lightweight NanoVSR-226k (226k params) attains 28.23 dB on REDS4 at just 1.910 ms/frame, approaching the quality of models 10–50× larger in parameter count.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the heavyweight VSR baselines, NanoVSR-1.7M trades PSNR for speed: it scores 29.15 dB on REDS4 versus 30.53 dB (EDVR-M), 31.42 dB (BasicVSR), 31.67 dB (IconVSR), 32.75 dB (RVRT), and 31.09 dB (EDVR), while running 6.4×, 3.5×, 6.8×, 11.2×, and 17.3× faster respectively on the H100. Against the frame-by-frame SISR baseline SPAN, NanoVSR-1.7M wins on all three benchmarks (26.44 vs 25.43 dB on Vid4, 29.15 vs 28.48 dB on REDS4, 35.49 vs 35.06 dB on Vimeo-90K-T) while also being faster (4.268 vs 5.820 ms), confirming that temporal aggregation yields a better speed–accuracy trade-off than per-frame restoration. Against MobileRNN (the Mobile AI 2022 VSR Challenge baseline), NanoVSR-644k outperforms it on both valid benchmarks (+0.63 dB on Vid4, +0.55 dB on Vimeo-90K-T) at nearly 2× the speed (2.982 vs 5.676 ms). On the Jetson Orin NX 16 GB, NanoVSR-644k sustains 27.20 FPS at 180×320, whereas BasicVSR manages only 8.04 FPS under identical TensorRT compilation, a 3.4× throughput gap that makes NanoVSR viable for real-time streaming where BasicVSR is not.

## Relation to Prior Work

NanoVSR sits at the intersection of two established threads: structural reparameterization (RepVGG) and bidirectional recurrent VSR (BasicVSR and its variants). It departs from the dominant VSR paradigm—explicit optical flow (EDVR, BasicVSR) or deformable-attention transformers (RVRT, VRT)—by eliminating all custom CUDA operations and motion-compensation modules in favor of a purely convolutional, additive-propagation recurrent design. The progressive two-stage curriculum (short-sequence pre-training then long-sequence fine-tuning) is borrowed from general deep-learning practice but applied here specifically to force the recurrent hidden states to learn residual motion implicitly. Among efficient VSR methods (MobileRNN, SWRN, EVSRNet), NanoVSR is distinguished by its full TensorRT/ONNX compatibility via reparameterization, enabling the entire temporal propagation to collapse into a single stream of standard convolutions at inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a practical gap: prior SOTA VSR methods (RVRT, EDVR) are computationally prohibitive on edge hardware, while existing lightweight baselines (MobileRNN, SPAN frame-by-frame) sacrifice either quality or speed. NanoVSR demonstrates that a 644k-parameter model can sustain 27 FPS on a 25W Jetson Orin NX at 28.64 dB PSNR on REDS4, making real-time video upscaling viable on consumer-grade embedded devices. Key limitations remain: the bidirectional window (T=15) introduces buffering delay unsuitable for strict frame-by-frame streaming, and implicit alignment struggles with extreme non-rigid local motion. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to higher input resolutions (>480p) and whether the additive-propagation scheme can be further extended to handle longer temporal contexts without quality degradation.
