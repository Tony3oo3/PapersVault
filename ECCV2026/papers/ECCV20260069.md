## Problem

Existing streaming co-speech gesture generation methods operate in an open-loop fashion: each clip is synthesized conditioned only on past context with no mechanism to verify or correct trajectory plausibility. Small per-clip errors compound silently over minute-scale horizons, causing distributional drift where motion progressively departs from natural pose distributions. The paper identifies that this drift is not a local quality problem but stems from the absence of forward constraints—each clip knows where it starts but has no information about where it should arrive.

## Input / Output

The method consumes streaming speech audio (encoded via WavLM into 1024-dim frame-level embeddings, augmented with regressed facial features), a speaker identity code, and the last 8 frames of the previous clip as a pose prompt. At each 60-frame clip boundary, it additionally retrieves a single tail key pose from a speaker-specific motion database (joint rotations only, excluding translation) to serve as a destination anchor. Output is a 60-frame SMPL-X motion sequence (55 joints × 6D rotations + 3D root translation per frame), produced clip-by-clip in real time at 76 FPS with 8-frame overlap between consecutive clips.

## Method

StreamTalk formulates streaming generation as sequential motion inpainting and introduces a closed-loop generate-retrieve-refine cycle (SPG) at each clip boundary: a coarse clip is produced by a flow-matching Part-aware DiT, its tail pose is matched in joint space (via forward kinematics) against a speaker-specific database to retrieve the nearest plausible key pose, and the clip is then refined by re-integrating from the cached ODE midpoint with this anchor as a destination constraint. To train the model to exploit such sparse anchors, Stochastic Anchor Masking (SAM) independently masks random pose and translation frames during training, teaching the network to inpaint complete motion from partial boundary conditions that mirror the inference-time input pattern. The Part-aware DiT architecture splits motion into three branches (Hands, Body, Translation) with FiLM-style conditioning and cross-attention to separate pose/translation prompts, preventing global displacement from corrupting local articulation and enabling SPG to retrieve pose anchors without borrowing context-dependent trajectories. Training uses a flow-matching regression loss combined with a forward-kinematics structural loss and a prompt consistency term that enforces agreement with visible anchors.

## Results

On the BEAT2 benchmark (1-Speaker setting), StreamTalk achieves the best FGD of 0.383, outperforming the previous best methods GestureLSM (0.409) and SemTalk (0.428), while its Beat Consistency (0.704) is closest to the ground truth (0.703). Under the more challenging All-Speakers setting, StreamTalk reaches FGD 0.293, surpassing the prior best RAG-GESTURE (0.487) by a large margin, with Diversity (7.27) nearly matching the ground truth (7.29). The method runs at 76 FPS on a single V100 GPU, enabling real-time minute-scale streaming generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

StreamTalk achieves the lowest FGD (0.383) among all 19 compared baselines in the 1-Speaker setting, beating the nearest competitors GestureLSM (0.409) and SemTalk (0.428) by 6.4% and 10.5% respectively. Its Beat Consistency (0.704) is the closest to ground truth (0.703) of any method, whereas baselines like AMUSE (0.832) and MambaTalk (0.781) overestimate rhythmic alignment. In the All-Speakers setting, StreamTalk's FGD of 0.293 substantially outperforms the previous best RAG-GESTURE (0.487), a 40% relative improvement, while its Diversity (7.27) nearly matches GT (7.29), confirming that retrieval-based anchoring does not suppress motion variety. On velocity and acceleration distances (Table 2b), StreamTalk (VEL 2.15e2, ACC 2.33e2) also leads all baselines, with ACC roughly 35% lower than the next best (SemTalk, 3.58e2). The user study (27 participants, 400+ ratings) confirms these quantitative gains with statistically significant preference for StreamTalk in realism and rhythm consistency (p < 10⁻³, Wilcoxon signed-rank test).

## Relation to Prior Work

StreamTalk builds on the flow-matching generative framework [20] and the WavLM-based audio encoding pipeline from DiffSHEG [7], but departs fundamentally from the open-loop streaming paradigm shared by all prior co-speech gesture methods (VQ-VAE-based like EMAGE, SemTalk, and diffusion-based like DiffSHEG, GestureLSM). The key conceptual departure is reframing long-horizon drift as a *direction* problem rather than a local-quality problem: instead of improving per-clip generation or extending context windows, the paper introduces a retrieval-based forward constraint (a single tail key pose) that periodically pulls the trajectory back to the plausible motion manifold. This positions the work as a closed-loop extension of streaming generation, analogous to periodic recalibration in navigation, rather than another incremental improvement to the generative backbone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The closed-loop paradigm is backbone-agnostic and could in principle be applied to any streaming motion generation task beyond co-speech gestures, making the conceptual contribution broader than the specific architecture. The finding that a single destination anchor suffices—while multiple anchors actively degrade quality—challenges the implicit assumption that more constraints yield better control, and provides a simple, low-cost correction mechanism (16.6 MB database, 0.039 s overhead) that runs in real time. Limitations include evaluation on a single dataset (BEAT2), reliance on speaker identity being known at inference, and the database being constructed from training data (limiting zero-shot generalization to truly unseen motion styles). Open questions include whether the approach extends to other streaming generation domains (dance, locomotion) and whether learned retrieval could replace nearest-neighbor matching for more robust anchoring.
