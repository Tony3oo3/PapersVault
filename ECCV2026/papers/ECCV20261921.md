## Problem

Most existing shadow detection methods predict a single unified shadow mask or focus exclusively on cast shadows, leaving attached shadows either unannotated or treated identically to cast shadows. Attached shadows arise from a fundamentally different mechanism—surface orientation relative to the light source rather than occlusion—making them fragmented, low-contrast, and resistant to appearance-based detection. This paper addresses the gap by explicitly modeling the physical coupling between light direction and surface geometry to detect both shadow types separately.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261921/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a surface normal map (derived from Depth Anything V2 relative depth) as input, and produces a per-pixel multi-class segmentation over {background, cast, attached} along with a 3D unit light-direction vector in a camera-centric frame (+x right, +y down, +z inward). During iterative refinement, the light estimator also generates a binary partial attached-shadow map (pixels where n·l > 0) that is fed back as an additional channel to the detector. The dataset provides 1,458 images with manually annotated cast, attached, and 'undefined' shadow masks, plus heuristic 3D light directions computed from object-to-shadow displacement and relative depth.

## Method

The core architecture is a dual-module closed loop: a shadow detection network (SILT backbone) performs multi-class segmentation over {bg, cast, attached}, while a ConvNeXt-S light estimation module regresses a 3D light direction from the normal map and predicted shadows. The key mechanism is a geometry-based partial attached-shadow map computed as M = 1[n·l > 0], which is fed back to the detector as an additional input at each of three iterative refinement passes, progressively sharpening both the shadow prediction and the light estimate. Training uses a composite loss combining BCE+Dice for full-shadow segmentation, multi-class cross-entropy with a class-conditional margin (m=0.2) to enforce cast/attached logit separation, weighted BCE aligning the partial map to ground-truth attached masks, L1 regression on the light direction, and a unit-norm constraint. The iterative scheme saturates at three passes, yielding a 33% reduction in attached-shadow BER over retrained baselines while maintaining competitive cast and full-shadow performance.

## Results

On the proposed Cast and Attached Shadow Dataset (1,458 images), the method achieves the best Full F1 (86.61), best Attached BER (12.92), best Attached F1 (86.49), and best Cast F1 (85.46) among all compared methods. Compared to the strongest retrained baseline (SILT†), attached-shadow BER drops from 26.57 to 12.92 (a 51% reduction), while Full F1 improves from 82.98 to 86.61. Against SBU-pretrained baselines, the attached-shadow BER gap is even larger: e.g., SILT (pretrained) reports 32.70 attached BER versus 12.92 for the proposed method. The method's Cast BER (5.04) is marginally higher than SILT† (4.52) and FDRNet† (4.65), but its Cast F1 (85.46) is substantially better than all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all seven SBU-pretrained baselines (BDRAR, DSD, FSDNet, MTMT, FDRNet, SDCM, SILT) on every reported metric, with the largest gaps on attached shadows (e.g., attached BER 12.92 vs. 32.70–42.02 for pretrained baselines). Against the four retrained baselines (BDRAR†, FSDNet†, FDRNet†, SILT†), the method achieves the best Full F1, Cast F1, Attached BER, and Attached F1, reducing attached-shadow BER by 34–51% relative to these baselines. The only metrics where the method trails a retrained baseline are Cast BER (5.04 vs. 4.52 for SILT† and 4.65 for FDRNet†), a small difference that the authors attribute to the baselines' cast-focused training. SILT† is the closest competitor on Full BER (6.51 vs. 6.50), but its Cast F1 (68.31) and Attached BER (26.57) are substantially worse, confirming that uniform shadow supervision cannot match explicit cast/attached separation. The ablation further shows that removing surface normals degrades attached BER to 20.87, approaching retrained-baseline levels, underscoring that the geometry-light coupling is the primary source of the advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the light–shadow reciprocity principle first explored by Panagopoulos et al. [35] in a modern deep-learning setting, coupling a shadow segmentation network (backbone from SILT [61]) with a ConvNeXt-S light-direction estimator in a closed iterative loop. Unlike prior shadow detectors that output a single unified mask or treat attached shadows as an afterthought, the framework explicitly models the physical dependence of attached shadows on surface orientation and illumination direction, using normals from Depth Anything V2 as geometric input. The iterative feedback mechanism—where a geometry-derived partial attached-shadow map refines detection, which in turn sharpens light estimation—has no direct architectural precedent in the shadow detection literature, though the mutual-refinement idea echoes earlier joint shadow/illumination inference work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first dataset with explicit per-pixel cast/attached shadow separation (1,458 images), filling a gap that limits evaluation of geometry-aware shadow methods. The 33% attached-shadow BER reduction over retrained baselines demonstrates that physical cues (normals + light direction) are essential for attached shadows, which appearance-based methods fundamentally cannot capture. However, the single-directional-light assumption and reliance on off-the-shelf normal estimation (which can produce flipped normals) constrain real-world applicability; extending to multi-source or area lighting and improving geometric robustness are clear open directions. The shadow-removal user study (80.7% preference for separate-mask guidance) suggests practical downstream value, but the narrow evaluation scope (one dataset, one removal model) leaves broader impact unquantified.
