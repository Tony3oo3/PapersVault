## Problem

Existing MLLM safety guardrails operate on an input-aware paradigm, making safety decisions based solely on the input prompt without considering whether the model would actually generate a harmful output. This causes severe over-refusal: benign queries or harmful inputs that the model would safely handle through refusal or advisory responses are indiscriminately blocked, degrading utility and user experience. The paper identifies that the root cause is the failure to account for the model's intrinsic alignment capabilities that can neutralize risky inputs into harmless outputs.

## Input / Output

OutGuard consumes hidden state representations (vectors of dimension d) from each token at each layer of the backbone LLM during inference, rather than the raw image-text pair or the final generated text. Each layer's collection of N token-level hidden states forms a 'bag' that inherits the safety label of the corresponding generated output. The final output is a binary safe/unsafe decision obtained by majority voting across all selected layer classifiers, with the entire safety check completing within a single inference pass before the response is delivered to the user.

## Method

OutGuard formulates output-side safety detection as a multiple instance learning problem, where each token's hidden state is an instance and the sequence-level safety label is the bag label. A per-layer MICL classifier aggregates token-level representations via a gated attention mechanism (tanh-gated with element-wise multiplication) into a bag-level vector, then applies a linear head for binary classification. Training combines three losses: bag-level binary cross-entropy, an entropy regularization term that encourages sparse attention on salient tokens, and an intra-bag InfoNCE contrastive loss that pulls top-attention instances together while pushing them apart from low-attention instances. A Safety-Score metric (averaging correct rejection of positives and correct acceptance of negatives) selects layers above a 0.9 threshold, and the final decision is made by majority vote across selected layers. The entire pipeline runs as a lightweight independent module during inference, adding only ~0.2s latency and ~1.4–2.2 GB memory overhead.

## Results

On the in-distribution test set, OutGuard achieves AUPRC of 0.9725 (LLaVA 1.6) and 0.9937 (Qwen 3.5), with AUROC of 0.9733 and 0.9935 respectively, and F1-Score of 0.9335 and 0.9635. Critically, OutGuard keeps both the unsafe-response rate (URUP) and the over-refusal rate (ARSP) below 0.1 (URUP: 0.055/0.012; ARSP: 0.075/0.063), whereas the strongest baseline LoD-PP still incurs ARSP of 0.112/0.130 and all other baselines exceed 0.62. On OOD data, OutGuard maintains AUPRC above 0.93 on both models while the next-best method (LoD-PP) drops to 0.71/0.56. Under jailbreak attacks (Visual-Adversarial-Examples, HADES, JOOD), OutGuard achieves near-perfect detection (AUPRC ≥ 0.92) with ARSP ≤ 0.23, consistently outperforming all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OutGuard outperforms all four baselines on every metric for both models. Against the strongest baseline LoD-PP (retrained with matching labels), OutGuard improves AUPRC by +0.09 (LLaVA) / +0.21 (Qwen) and reduces ARSP from 0.112/0.130 to 0.075/0.063, while also lowering URUP. QGuard achieves zero URUP by refusing every query, but incurs complete over-refusal (ARSP = 1.0), making it practically unusable. HiddenDetector and LoD (original) show moderate detection (AUPRC 0.50–0.53) but suffer severe over-refusal (ARSP 0.62–0.71), confirming the input-aware paradigm's failure to distinguish safe model responses from genuinely harmful ones. ProGuard, a post-output classifier, performs worst overall with high URUP (0.19–0.20) and high ARSP (0.65–0.69), indicating that examining only the final text without in-line hidden-state context is insufficient. The ablation in Table 9 further confirms that applying the same MICL classifier to inputs rather than outputs inflates ARSP from 0.075 to 0.625, isolating the output-aware paradigm as the key differentiator.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant input-aware safety guardrail paradigm (HiddenDetector, QGuard, LoD) by shifting the evaluation point from input risk to output harm, arguing that MLLMs' built-in alignment (refusal, admonishment) already neutralizes many harmful inputs. It repurposes multi-instance learning and InfoNCE-style contrastive learning—techniques well-established in other domains—for the specific problem of distinguishing genuinely harmful outputs from safe-but-triggered-by-harmful-input responses at the hidden-state level. Unlike ProGuard, which is a standalone post-output classifier, OutGuard operates in-line during inference using hidden states, enabling single-pass safety verification without additional model queries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The over-refusal problem is a critical practical barrier to deploying MLLMs in real applications, and this paper provides a principled diagnosis (input-aware evaluation ignores the model's own safety capabilities) with a lightweight solution that reduces ARSP from 0.6–1.0 to ~0.07 while maintaining strong detection. Key limitations include OOD performance degradation (ARSP rises to ~0.21–0.26), false negatives on implicit harm (political opinions, hate speech), and the training-time dependency on five LLMs for labeling. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to larger or differently-aligned MLLMs, whether the LLM-labeling bottleneck can be replaced with cheaper supervision, and how the method performs under evolving jailbreak strategies not seen during training.
