## Problem

Existing learning-based HDR fusion methods achieve high reconstruction quality but incur prohibitive computational costs (thousands of GMACs, millions of parameters), making them impractical for real-time edge deployment in autonomous driving. A key inefficiency is that these methods apply expensive multi-exposure fusion uniformly across the entire image, even though saturation is spatially sparse—only ~16% of pixels in typical driving frames are clipped—while the remaining well-exposed majority only needs lightweight denoising.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261209/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a synchronized three-exposure LDR stack (short, mid, long) at 2880×1856 resolution with 2-stop (4×) exposure intervals, where the mid-exposure is normalized to the short-exposure reference scale and all frames pass through a differentiable μ-tonemap for training stability. It produces a linear-domain HDR radiance image, which is subsequently tone-mapped and fed to a YOLOX-Tiny detector for evaluation. During inference, the image is partitioned into 128×128 tiles, and a luminance-derived saturation mask (computed from the mid-frame's weighted RGB luminance against a 90% threshold of the exposure ratio) determines which tiles execute the expensive fusion path versus the lightweight denoising path.

## Method

SplitHDR extends the LightFuse dual-branch backbone (GlobalNet with depthwise-separable convolutions + DetailNet with point-wise convolutions) from dual to three-exposure input, using the mid-exposure as a residual reference via a global skip connection. The core mechanism is a saturation-aware dual-mode design: the saturated mode activates the full backbone (1.8k params) to fuse all three exposures for clipped highlight recovery, while the unsaturated mode removes the short-exposure input and halves intermediate channel widths (0.67k params) for efficient denoising. A soft saturation mask α∈[0,1] with a linear transition band (90%–100% of the exposure-ratio boundary) routes pixels between modes, and training uses exclusive masked L1 supervision so each mode specializes on its target region. At inference, tile-wise conditional execution classifies each 128×128 tile by its mean mask value and runs only the necessary configuration, reducing total compute to 3.63 GMACs (2.47k params) while matching or exceeding the detection accuracy of models 600× larger.

## Results

On the ROD benchmark, SplitHDR achieves AP 38.38, AP50 58.75, AP75 40.31, and AR 47.87 for object detection, matching or exceeding all heavier multi-exposure baselines (AHDRNet, SCTNet, SAFNet) while using only 2.47k parameters and 3.63 GMACs — roughly 668× fewer parameters and 2,130× fewer MACs than AHDRNet. For HDR reconstruction, it attains PSNR-μ 44.96 and SSIM-μ 0.985, trailing the best multi-exposure methods (SCTNet: 48.28 PSNR-μ) by a few dB but substantially outperforming the lightweight LightFuse (40.67 PSNR-μ). In the efficiency analysis (Table 3), SplitHDR achieves a ΔPSNR-μ/GMACs ratio of 4.008 and ΔAP/GMACs of 1.140, approximately 2.8× and 2.2× higher than LightFuse, with the lowest inference latency (15.0 ms) and peak memory (1233 MB) among all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the heavy multi-exposure baselines (AHDRNet, SCTNet, SAFNet), SplitHDR matches or slightly exceeds detection performance (AP 38.38 vs. 38.38/38.33/38.16; AP75 40.31 vs. 40.19/40.12/39.98) while using 300–600× fewer parameters and 1,000–2,000× fewer MACs, though it trails them by 2–3 dB in PSNR-μ (44.96 vs. 47.37–48.28). Compared to the single-image HDRUNet, SplitHDR improves AP by 2.02 points (38.38 vs. 36.36) and PSNR-μ by 7.77 dB, demonstrating the benefit of multi-exposure input. Versus the closest lightweight competitor LightFuse (1.57k params, 7.13 GMACs), SplitHDR achieves +0.50 AP, +0.51 AP50, +0.56 AP75, +0.67 AR, and +4.29 dB PSNR-μ while halving the computational cost (3.63 vs. 7.13 GMACs). Against the non-learning Exposure Fusion baseline, SplitHDR gains +4.44 AP and +19.95 dB PSNR-μ. In scene-wise analysis (Table 2), SplitHDR achieves the best day-scene AP (38.67) and is within 0.01 AP of the best night-scene result (38.27 vs. 38.28 for AHDRNet), confirming robustness across illumination conditions.

## Relation to Prior Work

SplitHDR extends the LightFuse backbone (dual-exposure, depthwise-separable CNN) to a three-exposure interface with residual learning around the mid-exposure frame, then introduces a saturation-aware dual-mode routing mechanism that partitions the HDR task into a lightweight denoising path for unsaturated pixels and a full fusion path for saturated pixels. Conceptually, it departs from prior multi-exposure HDR networks (AHDRNet, SCTNet, SAFNet) that apply a single heavy fusion architecture uniformly across the entire frame, instead exploiting the observation that saturation is spatially sparse (~16% of pixels on average) and that different exposures are optimal for different regions. The tile-based conditional execution strategy, where the expensive fusion configuration runs only on tiles containing saturation, is a novel engineering contribution for edge deployment that has no direct precedent in the HDR fusion literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 99%+ parameter reduction (2.47k vs. 961k–1651k) while matching or slightly exceeding state-of-the-art detection AP (38.38 vs. 38.38 for AHDRNet) is practically significant for latency-constrained automotive edge platforms where existing HDR networks are infeasible. However, the evaluation is limited to a single synthetic dataset (ROD-derived) with a single detector (YOLOX-Tiny), and the synthetic noise model may not fully capture real sensor degradation. Open questions include generalization to real multi-exposure sensor stacks with residual motion artifacts, performance under scenes with widespread saturation (e.g., direct sun), and whether the tile-based routing overhead remains negligible on actual FPGA/ASIC hardware rather than GPU.
