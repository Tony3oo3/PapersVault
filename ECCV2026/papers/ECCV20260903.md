## Problem

Vision-language alignment—the stage bridging pretrained vision encoders with LLMs—is universally treated as requiring full-parameter LLM updates, yet it is fundamentally supervised fine-tuning atop pretrained knowledge, precisely the regime for which low-rank adaptation was designed. Applying low-rank methods during alignment is largely unexplored, and the implicit biases such constraints introduce into visual token representations remain uncharacterized, leaving it unclear why and how low-rank alignment could outperform full-parameter alignment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260903/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The alignment stage consumes RGB images (encoded by a frozen vision tower such as CLIP-Vision, DINOv2, or SigLIPv2) paired with text captions or perceptual QA prompts, producing text outputs; the trainable parameters are the LLM backbone (or its low-rank branches) plus a lightweight MLP adapter. For mechanistic analysis, the paper additionally extracts per-token C-dimensional visual feature vectors from the LLM's input layer and trains independent linear classifiers on each token position to yield per-token class-label accuracy (linear separability scores) on ImageNet, STF-dogs, and CUB.

## Method

The paper applies low-rank adaptation (LoRA, LoHa, or LoKr) exclusively to all linear modules in the LLM during alignment while freezing the vision encoder, then systematically characterizes the resulting implicit biases at three levels: behavioral (Type I/II error rates on POPE showing a shift from hallucinatory to conservative/decisive decisions), feature-level (per-token linear separability preservation versus the LS-curse where full alignment drops token accuracy to 83.6%), and geometric (compact angular coverage and homogeneous feature manifolds versus the aggressive spread of full alignment). Theoretically, under the Unconstrained Feature Model, Theorem 1 proves that the coupled LoRA gradient flow (Ȧ ∝ B·(grad+noise), Ḃ ∝ (grad+noise)·A) induces a noise-weighted smoothing effect that preferentially reinforces updates along feature directions robust to stochastic perturbations, while Theorem 2 shows the steady-state parameter distribution concentrates on subspaces that are simultaneously flat in the loss landscape and robust to input noise. The net effect is that low-rank alignment preserves modality-specific knowledge structures inherited from the pretrained vision tower and defers entity-level semantic fusion to instruction tuning, in contrast to full-parameter alignment's aggressive restructuring of the visual feature space.

## Results

Low-rank alignment (best of LoRA/LoHa/LoKr) outperforms full-parameter alignment on all four benchmark categories across every model scale from 1.4B to 13B. Headline gains include MME-perception improving from 208.1 to 453.5 (+245.4) at 1.4B and from 236.6 to 1079.7 (+843.1) at 13B; GQA knowledge from 18.9 to 28.0 (+9.1) at 1.4B; MME-reasoning from 22.9 to 766.6 (+743.7) at 1.4B; and POPE hallucination from 2.5 to 72.8 (+70.3) at 1.4B. Training time is roughly halved (e.g., 4.53 h → 2.52 h at 1.4B; 35.32 h → 13.23 h at 13B on 8×A100). On Qwen3-8B/14B, low-rank alignment also wins on GQA (+8.8/+13.6), MMBench (+30.6/+33.5), MME-P (+216.3/+462.6), and TextVQA (+3.8/+6.0), with exceptions on POPE and MMMU-Pro.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is full-parameter alignment (all LLM weights updated), which is the standard practice in the VLM community. Low-rank alignment (best operator/rank) wins on MME-perception, GQA, MME-reasoning, and POPE at every scale from 1.4B to 13B, with the largest relative gains at smaller scales (e.g., +743.7 on MME-R at 1.4B) and consistent absolute gains at 13B (e.g., +843.1 on MME-P, +16.0 on POPE). Among the three low-rank operators, LoHa (Hadamard) generally achieves the highest perception and hallucination scores (e.g., 1079.7 MME-P and 82.9 POPE at 13B), while LoKr (Kronecker) excels on reasoning (e.g., 332.5 MME-R at 7B vs. 251.4 for LoRA). On Qwen3-8B/14B, low-rank alignment loses on POPE (25.1 vs. 92.7 at 8B) and MMMU-Pro (5.7 vs. 12.1 at 8B), which the authors attribute to the low-rank constraint acting as a regularizer that limits deviation from the pre-trained weight manifold at higher ranks. Training time is reduced by approximately 40–60% across all scales, confirming the efficiency advantage alongside the accuracy gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes low-rank adaptation methods originally designed for supervised fine-tuning (LoRA, LoHa, LoKr) to the vision-language alignment stage, which the field conventionally treats as a pretraining-like phase requiring full-parameter updates. It builds on the Unconstrained Feature Model framework for its theoretical analysis, but departs from the classical implicit-bias literature on matrix factorization (which predicts min-norm/low-rank solutions) by deriving a distinct flat-gradient, noise-robust subspace bias arising from the coupled A/B gradient dynamics in a non-linear, pretrained, stochastic setting. VLoRA provided preliminary evidence that cross-modal transfer is intrinsically low-rank, but this paper goes further by systematically characterizing *how* the low-rank constraint reshapes visual token geometry (LS-curse, angular coverage, manifold homogeneity) and grounding the behavior in two optimization theorems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that low-rank alignment outperforms full-parameter alignment across 1.4B–14B scales while cutting training time by roughly half challenges a default assumption in VLM pipelines and offers immediate practical savings for any team training multimodal models. The LS-curse concept and the progressive-fusion interpretation (general knowledge preserved at alignment, entity-specific fusion deferred to instruction tuning) provide a diagnostic lens for understanding *why* certain alignment choices succeed or fail. Open questions include whether the POPE/MMMU-Pro exceptions on Qwen3 indicate a fundamental rank ceiling for hallucination-sensitive tasks, whether the UFM-based theorems transfer to architectures with multi-layer visual injection (deepstack), and whether the conservative-behavior bias becomes a liability in domains requiring affirmative identification.
