## Problem

State-of-the-art rectified flow models like SD3.5 require 25+ inference steps, 16GB+ VRAM, and 30+ seconds per image, confining them to datacenters. The core technical obstacle for few-step distillation is that standard distribution matching (DMD) re-noises generated samples to random timesteps, moving them off the learned ODE trajectory where velocity predictions become unreliable and gradients collapse. Additionally, severe capacity constraints in few-step models force a trade-off between aesthetic quality and prompt-image alignment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260886/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a free-form text prompt and produces a generated RGB image in exactly 4 denoising steps at resolutions from 512×512 to 1024×1024 pixels. The pipeline conditions on CLIP-L/G text embeddings (always present) and optionally T5-XXL embeddings (removable via encoder dropout for memory-constrained devices), feeding an MM-DiT diffusion model whose latent output is decoded by a VAE. Deployed variants range from a 16-bit model with T5 (~18 GiB VRAM) down to a 6-bit CoreML-quantized model without T5 (~8 GB unified memory) for iPhone and iPad.

## Method

SD3.5-Flash distills the 2.5B SD3.5M teacher into a 4-step student through three training stages: (i) trajectory guidance pre-training that aligns the student's few-step path with the teacher's ODE trajectory, (ii) distribution matching distillation (DMD) combined with a multi-head adversarial objective where the proxy student serves as a feature extractor, and (iii) split-timestep fine-tuning. The key algorithmic innovation, timestep sharing, replaces DMD's stochastic re-noising to random timesteps with evaluation of the KL divergence at the next noisy state on the student's own denoising trajectory, keeping samples on-trajectory where velocity estimates remain reliable. Split-timestep fine-tuning temporarily doubles model capacity by branching into two sub-networks trained on disjoint timestep ranges (0–500 and 500–1000), then fuses them via 3:7 weight interpolation to recover prompt alignment lost during compression. Pipeline-level optimizations include 16-bit→6-bit quantization via CoreML on Apple Silicon, T5-XXL removal exploiting encoder dropout pre-training, and custom RMSNorm rewrites for the Apple Neural Engine.

## Results

SD3.5-Flash 16-bit (+T5) achieves an ImageReward of 1.10, GenEval of 0.70, Aesthetic Score of 6.38, and CLIPScore of 31.65 in just 4 steps, surpassing its 50-step teacher SD3.5M on IR (1.10 vs 0.91), GenEval (0.70 vs 0.64), and AeS (6.38 vs 5.99). In large-scale user studies (54K+ A/B comparisons, 178 annotators), SD3.5-Flash is preferred over all 12 competitors and even the 50-step teacher in image quality, with an Elo rating of 1088.7 in the server-compute tier. FID is the weakest metric (29.80), which the authors attribute to FID's sensitivity to low-level image statistics rather than perceptual quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SD3.5-Flash clearly dominates the SDXL-based 4-step distillations (SDXL-DMD2, SDXL-Lightning, SDXL-Turbo, NitroFusion) on IR (1.10 vs 0.74–0.91) and GenEval (0.70 vs 0.54–0.56), reflecting substantially better prompt alignment and composition. It also surpasses its own 50-step teacher SD3.5M on IR, GenEval, and AeS, confirming that the distillation process improves rather than degrades perceptual quality. Against the closest SD3.5M-based competitors, SD3.5-Flash trails SWD-M slightly on CLIP (31.65 vs 32.00), IR (1.10 vs 1.12), and GenEval (0.70 vs 0.72), and loses to SANA-Sprint 1.6B on AeS (6.38 vs 6.61) and GenEval (0.70 vs 0.73), though it wins on IR. FID is the one metric where SD3.5-Flash is worst (29.80 vs 16.64–25.90 for competitors); the authors argue this reflects FID's reliance on Inception low-level features and note that the teacher SD3.5M itself scores worse (20.06) than SDXL (14.72) on FID despite higher perceptual quality. In the Elo-based user study, SD3.5-Flash 16-bit (+T5) achieves the top score (1088.7) in the server-compute tier, ahead of SD3.5M (1026.3) and SWD-M (1009.8), and the 6-bit mobile variant (1090.2) outperforms SDv2.1 (909.8) on phones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DMD2 distribution matching distillation framework (Yin et al., 2024) to the rectified flow setting, where the standard re-noising-to-random-timestep procedure corrupts gradients because samples leave the learned ODE trajectory. The key algorithmic departure is 'timestep sharing,' which evaluates the KL divergence at the student's own intermediate trajectory states rather than stochastically re-noised points, and 'split-timestep fine-tuning,' which temporarily doubles model capacity by branching on disjoint timestep ranges to recover prompt alignment lost during compression. The adversarial training setup (multi-head discriminators with periodic refresh, proxy-as-feature-extractor) follows NitroFusion (Chen et al., 2024), and the base architecture is the SD3.5 MM-DiT rectified flow model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a 4-step, 6-bit model running in under 10 seconds on an iPhone democratizes high-quality text-to-image generation for edge devices, a deployment story that prior few-step methods (SDXL-Lightning, DMD2, SANA-Sprint) have not achieved at comparable quality. The timestep sharing insight is specific to flow-matching distillation and may inform future work on few-step flow models beyond SD3.5. Open questions include whether the approach scales cleanly to much larger models (the paper shows minor artifacts on FLUX.1-dev), whether the FID degradation reflects a genuine diversity loss, and whether split-timestep fine-tuning generalizes beyond the 4-step regime. The paper's focus on consumer-hardware deployment rather than algorithmic generality means the broader methodological contribution is somewhat bounded.
