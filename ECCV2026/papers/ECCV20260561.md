## Problem

Current video diffusion models fail to generate physically plausible rigid body interactions and lack object-level motion control, often producing hallucinated collisions, disappearing objects, or acausal motion. Existing control methods either require pre-defined drag trajectories or explicit 3D scene reconstruction, preventing the inference of causal interaction effects from initial conditions alone. The paper asks whether a VDM can synthesize realistic multi-object interactions given only an initial image and object velocity, and how data and textual conditioning shape the emergence of causal physical effects.

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a single real-world image, a user-drawn arrow specifying object direction and speed (automatically mapped to an RGB velocity mask via SAM2 segmentation), and a GPT-5-generated textual description of expected scene effects. The output is a 49-frame video at the input image resolution showing the controlled object moving and interacting with other scene elements. The velocity mask is non-zero only in the first frame (m_⊙ = {m_0, 0, ..., 0}), meaning the model must infer all subsequent motion and interactions from initial conditions.

## Method

KineMask attaches a ControlNet branch to a frozen pretrained VDM (CogVideoX-5B) and conditions it on per-frame RGB velocity masks encoding instantaneous object velocity vectors along x, y, z axes. The core training strategy is two-stage: the first stage trains the ControlNet with full velocity masks across all frames to learn dense motion-to-video mapping, while the second stage applies mask dropout (randomly zeroing velocity masks in later frames, biased toward early frames) so the model learns to extrapolate future dynamics from initial-frame velocity alone. Training data consists of 10,000 Blender-rendered videos of boxes and cylinders with random initial velocities on textured surfaces, paired with VLM-generated interaction captions. At inference, the user provides only a single-frame velocity mask and a text prompt, and the VDM synthesizes the full interaction sequence including collisions, object displacement, and secondary effects like liquid spilling or glass breaking.

## Results

On the Interactions synthetic test set, KineMask trained on Interactions data achieves MSE 158.7, FVD 250.7, FVMD 143.8, and IoU 0.355, substantially outperforming zero-shot CogVideoX (MSE 344.6, FVD 807.3, FVMD 3514.9, IoU 0.192) and CogVideoX finetuned on the same data (MSE 201.9, FVD 368.2, FVMD 162.9, IoU 0.243). In a 30-participant user study on Real World scenes, KineMask is preferred over all baselines on motion fidelity, interaction quality, and overall physical consistency. The two-stage training strategy (Table 1) reduces FVD from 288.8 (2nd stage only) to 160.3 and IoU from 0.237 to 0.367 on Simple Motion, approaching the upper bound of 89.7 FVD / 0.684 IoU when full per-frame masks are available.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KineMask is compared against six baselines: CogVideoX (backbone, zero-shot and finetuned), Wan2.2-I2V-5B, TORA (drag-based), MotionI2V (mask-constrained drag), Force Prompting (physics-guided), and Cosmos2.5-2B. On the Interactions quantitative benchmark, KineMask (Interactions-trained) reduces FVD by 69% over zero-shot CogVideoX (250.7 vs 807.3) and by 32% over CogVideoX finetuned on the same data (250.7 vs 368.2), while improving IoU from 0.192 to 0.355. In the 30-participant user study on Real World scenes, KineMask is preferred over all five baselines (CogVideoX, Wan, TORA, MotionI2V, Force Prompting) on all three evaluation axes—motion fidelity, interaction realism, and physical consistency—with no ties reported. The paper explicitly notes that Force Prompting, the closest concurrent work, produces unrealistic dynamics and distorted shapes, whereas KineMask generates causally consistent collisions. Generalization is confirmed by applying KineMask to Wan2.2-5B and Cosmos2.5-2B backbones, where both KineMask-Wan and KineMask-Cosmos outperform their respective original models in a second user study, demonstrating that the framework is backbone-agnostic.

## Relation to Prior Work

KineMask builds directly on the ControlNet architecture for dense conditioning, repurposing it to encode per-pixel velocity masks rather than edges or depth. It departs from drag-based methods (TORA, MotionI2V, DragVideo) that require full pre-defined trajectories, and from physics-simulator-based approaches (WonderPlay, PhysGen, PhysGen3D) that demand explicit 3D scene reconstruction. The closest concurrent work, Force Prompting, also uses simulated data to inject physics into VDMs but offers coarser control and does not model object-object interactions. KineMask's key conceptual departure is the two-stage mask-dropout training that forces the model to infer all downstream dynamics from a single-frame velocity signal, combined with high-level VLM-generated captions for interaction semantics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a VDM trained on simple Blender-rendered rigid-body collisions can generalize to real-world scenes and exhibit emergent causal reasoning (velocity-dependent effects, mass awareness), which is a meaningful step toward using video models as world simulators for robotics and embodied planning. However, the conditioning is limited to center-of-mass velocity with no explicit friction, shape, or air-resistance parameters, and the camera is static, leaving substantial gaps for full physical world modeling. The finding that training on non-interacting motion data actively prevents interaction generation—while interacting data enables it—highlights that VDMs do not automatically acquire causal dynamics from visual priors alone. Extending to soft-body dynamics, part-level actuation, and camera motion are natural next steps the authors identify.
