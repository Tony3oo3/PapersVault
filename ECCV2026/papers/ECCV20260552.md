## Problem

Existing change captioning benchmarks describe changes arbitrarily or implicitly, confine evaluation to narrow single-domain datasets, and rely on fixed reference-based metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) that cannot capture the semantic nuance of open-ended descriptions. As a result, the true generalization performance of both conventional models and large multimodal models across diverse real-world change contexts remains largely unexplored, and critical failure modes such as position bias and order-induced artifacts go undetected.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes a pair of 512×512 RGB images (tagged as 'before' and 'after') together with context-specific change criteria text that specifies what changes to detect, what to ignore, and the desired tone. Models produce a single coherent paragraph describing only the detected changes, enclosed in output tags. Evaluation is performed by GPT-5.2 as a judge across five 1–10 scale dimensions (correctness, specificity, fluency, relevance, aggregation) plus a binary reversibility score obtained by swapping the input image order and checking whether the two captions describe the same underlying change with opposite directionality.

## Method

C3-BENCH reformulates change captioning as a context-conditioned task (Eq. 2), where the model prediction is explicitly guided by a criteria vector C_c that delineates the semantic boundaries of each of 51 real-world change contexts organized under four domains (natural scenes, remote sensing, image editing, anomalies). The dataset comprises 4,996 human-annotated image pairs collected from over fifteen public benchmarks and supplemented with imagery from platforms like Google Earth, with annotations guided by auxiliary metadata (change masks, damage polygons, anomaly categories) and validated through a multi-stage quality-control pipeline. For evaluation, the paper introduces the first LLM-as-Judge framework in change captioning, using GPT-5.2 to score fine-grained semantic dimensions and a novel reversibility metric that probes symmetric consistency under input-order reversal. All 32 benchmarked models (6 conventional, 9 proprietary LMMs, 17 open-source LMMs) are evaluated off-the-shelf using a structured system prompt that plugs in the context-specific criteria, sequential image tags, and canonical before-first temporal ordering—ablations confirm that each of these prompt components is necessary for reliable performance.

## Results

On C3-BENCH (4,996 image pairs, 51 contexts), GPT-5.2 achieves the highest Aggregation score of 5.51±0.20 among all 32 benchmarked models, with Correctness 5.47, Specificity 5.16, Fluency 8.65, Relevance 5.98, and Reversibility 0.62. Gemini-3-Pro matches GPT-5.2 on Aggregation (5.50) but leads on Reversibility (0.73 vs. 0.62). All six conventional change captioning models collapse to Aggregation scores of 2.0–2.4 (e.g., DIRL: 2.30, CLIP4IDC: 2.43) with Reversibility below 0.28, while human evaluators reach 7.45 Aggregation and 0.93 Reversibility on C3-BENCH-SMALL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Conventional change captioning models (DUDA, R³Net, CLIP4IDC, SCORER, VARD, DIRL) all collapse to Aggregation scores between 2.09 and 2.43, with Reversibility rates of 0.13–0.28, despite maintaining relatively high Fluency (7.73–8.29)—indicating they produce fluent but semantically irrelevant descriptions outside their training domains. Among proprietary LMMs, GPT-5.2 (5.51) and Gemini-3-Pro (5.50) are nearly tied on Aggregation, but Gemini-3-Pro achieves the highest Reversibility (0.73) among all LMMs, while GPT-5.2 leads on Correctness (5.47) and Relevance (5.98). Among open-source LMMs, Qwen3-VL-32B (5.16) comes closest to proprietary models, trailing GPT-5.2 by only 0.35 Aggregation points and matching Gemini-2.5-Pro (5.23) within margin. Llama-4-Scout-17B-16E (4.71) and Qwen2.5-VL-72B (4.61) represent the mid-tier open-source range, clearly surpassing all conventional models but falling short of top proprietary LMMs by ~0.8–0.9 points. The gap between the best LMM (GPT-5.2, 5.51) and human performance (7.45) remains substantial, with the largest deficit in Correctness (5.47 vs. 6.96) and Reversibility (0.62 vs. 0.93), while Fluency is the only dimension where LMMs (8.65) match or exceed humans (8.32).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the conventional change captioning paradigm (Eq. 1 in the paper), which treats change description as context-agnostic, by introducing an explicit context-criteria conditioning (Eq. 2) that unifies heterogeneous change-centric problems from change detection, image editing, and anomaly detection into a single formulation. It extends prior evaluation benchmarks (CLEVR-Change, LEVIR-CC, SpotTheDiff, IER) that each cover only one narrow domain and one or a few contexts, by spanning 51 contexts across four domains with clearly defined criteria. The LLM-as-Judge evaluation framework is borrowed from NLP (G-Eval, MT-Bench) and applied here for the first time in change captioning, while the reversibility metric draws on position-bias and temporal-consistency literature but operates at the level of open-ended descriptions on single image pairs rather than long sequences.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that conventional change captioning models, despite strong in-domain results, collapse to near-random performance (Aggregation ~2.0–2.4) when the change context departs from their training distribution, exposing brittle, dataset-tied semantics. Even state-of-the-art LMMs like GPT-5.2 (Aggregation 5.51 vs. human 7.45) exhibit systematic domain-dependent errors and a pronounced pairwise position bias that was previously thought to manifest only in long image sequences (5–100 images), not in simple before-after pairs. The finding that multi-dataset training actually degrades performance (Table 7) challenges the intuitive data-scaling assumption and highlights the need for context-aware training formulations. Open questions include how to eliminate position bias at the architecture level, whether explicit criteria conditioning can be internalized through training rather than prompting, and how to build truly order-invariant change representations.
