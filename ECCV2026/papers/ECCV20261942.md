## Problem

Screenplay-to-shot alignment is a foundational task for narrative video understanding, yet prior methods reduce it to subtitle-dialogue text overlap, ignoring the visual direction and narrative cues that screenplays encode. No existing benchmark systematically compares alignment paradigms (sparse retrieval, contrastive encoders, temporal grounding, VTR models) or isolates how much each of the dialogue and visual channels contributes to alignment quality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261942/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each screenplay scene is an ordered sequence of tagged text elements (heading, stage direction, character name, dialogue), and each video shot is represented by three keyframe images plus an optional aggregated subtitle string. The output is a monotone shot-to-scene mapping φ that respects temporal ordering, with the constraint that scene indices are nondecreasing across shots; approximately 20% of scenes have no realized shots due to editorial deletion and are excluded from scene-level IoU metrics. Evaluation uses mean SIoU (averaged per-scene then per-movie), boundary F1 with a shot-tolerance window, and per-shot retrieval ranks (R@1, R@5, MedR).

## Method

The benchmark employs a unified two-stage pipeline: each method computes a similarity matrix S ∈ R^{N×M} between shots and scenes using its own modality-specific scorer (BM25/TF-IDF for dialogue, contrastive encoders like CLIP or Qwen3-VL-Embedding for visual, or temporal grounding/VTR models), and a shared Drop-DTW assignment with a Gaussian length prior converts the matrix into a monotone mapping, ensuring performance differences reflect the similarity signal rather than the decoder. The strongest result comes from adaptive multimodal fusion: (1−α)·S^BM25 + α·S^visual when a shot has subtitles, S^visual otherwise, with α selected by 5-fold movie-level cross-validation (α=0.7 for Qwen3-VL-Embedding, 0.5 for CLIP). The key mechanistic insight is the 'dialogue anchoring effect': BM25 reliably anchors dialogue scenes to correct positions, and Drop-DTW's temporal ordering constraint propagates these anchors to neighboring non-dialogue scenes, narrowing the visual search space and yielding a 50% relative mSIoU gain over BM25 alone. Despite this, a persistent gap remains between dialogue (IoU 0.654) and non-dialogue (IoU 0.496) scenes even under the best fusion, exposing visual encoding of screenplay-specific language as the bottleneck.

## Results

On the From Script to Shot benchmark (50 films, 53,448 shots), adaptive multimodal fusion of Qwen3-VL-Embedding with BM25 (α=0.7) achieves the strongest overall alignment at 0.599 mSIoU and 0.695 BndF1, a 50% relative gain over BM25 alone (0.399 mSIoU). Among single-modality methods, Qwen3-VL-Embedding leads visual encoders (0.500 mSIoU) while BM25 leads dialogue methods (0.399 mSIoU); temporal grounding and VTR models all fall below 0.130 mSIoU. A persistent dialogue/non-dialogue gap remains even under the best fusion (IoU_d 0.654 vs. IoU_nd 0.496), identifying visual encoding of screenplay language as the key bottleneck. In the zero-shot scene segmentation application, alignment-derived boundaries achieve 32.24 mIoU on MovieNet-SSeg, matching the best unsupervised method (DP, 32.00) but trailing supervised methods (48–54 mIoU).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among dialogue methods, BM25 (0.399 mSIoU) and TF-IDF (0.396) perform comparably and both substantially outperform the word-level DTW approach M/S (0.324), yet all three collapse on non-dialogue scenes (IoU_nd ≤ 0.19), confirming that lexical subtitle matching cannot ground visually-driven scenes. Among visual encoders, Qwen3-VL-Embedding (0.500 mSIoU) dominates FG-CLIP2 (0.317), CLIP (0.252), InternVideo2 (0.222), and SigLIP (0.023), but even the best visual encoder underperforms BM25 on dialogue scenes (0.521 vs. 0.505) and shows near-identical dialogue/non-dialogue scores (0.521 vs. 0.456 for Qwen3-VL-Emb.; 0.253 vs. 0.252 for CLIP), revealing that contrastive encoders are insensitive to screenplay-specific language. Temporal grounding models (T-MASS 0.130, MATR 0.016, TAN 0.009) and VTR models (CLIP4Clip 0.012, DiffusionRet 0.011, DiscoVLA 0.013) all fail to transfer from short-clip localization to full-film alignment, with MedR values exceeding 20–70 compared to 2.7–6.4 for top methods. Fusion consistently outperforms either channel alone: Qwen3-VL-Emb.+BM25 (0.599) beats Qwen3-VL-Emb. alone (0.500) and BM25 alone (0.399) by 20% and 50% respectively, and even CLIP+BM25 (0.531) surpasses CLIP alone (0.252) by more than 2×. The dialogue anchoring effect explains the fusion gain on non-dialogue scenes: BM25 anchors dialogue scenes correctly, and Drop-DTW's temporal ordering propagates these anchors to neighboring non-dialogue scenes, raising CLIP's IoU_nd from 0.252 to 0.403 without changing the visual features. In the downstream scene segmentation task, the zero-shot alignment baseline (32.24 mIoU) matches the unsupervised DP method (32.00) and approaches Grouping (37.20) but remains well below supervised methods like CAT (53.67) and TranS4mer (51.91), with boundary agreement F1 of 0.752 at ±3-shot tolerance against segmentation annotations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the screenplay-video alignment line initiated by Cour et al. (ECCV 2008), which treated the task purely as subtitle-to-dialogue text overlap, by introducing a benchmark that explicitly separates dialogue matching from visual grounding and evaluates both channels under a unified protocol. The shared evaluation pipeline builds on Drop-DTW (NeurIPS 2021) for monotone sequence assignment and a Gaussian length prior from TALL (ICCV 2017), ensuring that performance differences reflect the quality of the similarity signal rather than the assignment strategy. The paper positions itself against three families of prior work—video-text retrieval (CLIP, CLIP4Clip, DiscoVLA), temporal grounding (TAN, MATR, T-MASS), and contrastive encoders (CLIP, SigLIP, InternVideo2, Qwen3-VL-Embedding)—and demonstrates that none of these, designed for short clips with homogeneous captions, transfer well to the heterogeneous, long-form structure of screenplays.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that contrastive vision-language encoders trained on object-centric captions are effectively blind to screenplay-specific language (stage directions, scene headings), performing worse than 30-year-old BM25 text retrieval on this task—a finding that has implications beyond screenplay alignment for any application requiring understanding of structured, multi-channel text. The persistent dialogue/non-dialogue gap (0.654 vs. 0.496 IoU even under the best fusion) identifies visual encoding of narrative direction as a concrete bottleneck for future work. Limitations include reliance on a single data source (MovieNet), skew toward English-language Western cinema, and evaluation restricted to matched scenes; jointly detecting unmatched scenes and extending to non-English or non-Western film traditions remain open.
