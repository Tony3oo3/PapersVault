## Problem

Camera-trap species recognition has been predominantly framed as cross-domain generalization, but ecological practitioners' primary need is maintaining reliable recognition at a fixed deployment site over time, where seasonal changes, weather, and animal migration continuously shift both backgrounds and species distributions. No prior benchmark or unified study has evaluated models under this realistic temporal streaming lifecycle, leaving practitioners without actionable guidance on when and how to adapt deployed models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262385/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Input consists of time-stamped camera trap images cropped around detected animal instances (via MegaDetector) with bounding boxes enlarged by 50% to preserve background context, partitioned into 30-day intervals per camera trap. At each interval j, the model receives cumulative labeled training data from intervals 1 through j for parameter updates, and is evaluated on a class-balanced test split from the subsequent interval j+1. Output is a per-image species class label, with performance measured as balanced accuracy averaged across intervals.

## Method

The paper introduces STREAMTRAP, a benchmark of 546 camera traps from 17 LILA BC datasets with a streaming evaluation protocol that mirrors real deployment lifecycles, and conducts a unified empirical study around it. The recommended adaptation recipe combines LoRA (parameter-efficient fine-tuning on attention projection layers) with Balanced Softmax loss to address severe class imbalance and low-shot data, initialized from the BioCLIP 2 vision-language foundation model. Three post-processing techniques—post-hoc logit calibration for absent/minority classes, WiSE weight interpolation between pre-trained and fine-tuned weights, and interval model selection—further mitigate inter-interval distribution shifts. The key diagnostic finding is that naive accumulated fine-tuning degrades below zero-shot performance due to two compounding drivers: extreme class imbalance (top-2 classes account for ~71% of images) and pronounced temporal class distribution shift (quantified via the proposed TCDS metric), making streaming adaptation substantially harder than standard in-domain fine-tuning.

## Results

On the STREAMTRAP benchmark (546 camera traps, streaming evaluation), BioCLIP 2 zero-shot achieves 75.8% average balanced accuracy across 20 representative traps, but naive accumulated fine-tuning degrades to 53.2%—well below the zero-shot baseline. The paper's recommended recipe (LoRA + Balanced Softmax, denoted ★) recovers the accumulated model to 82.2%, surpassing zero-shot by 6.4 points. The oracle upper bound (fine-tuned on all intervals with the ★ recipe) reaches 90.4%, leaving a persistent ~8.2-point gap attributable to inter-interval temporal distribution shift. Across all 546 traps, the ★ recipe enables 474 sites to outperform zero-shot (vs. only 332 for naive fine-tuning).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is BioCLIP 2 zero-shot (75.8% avg balanced accuracy), a biological vision foundation model trained on 800K+ species. The most striking finding is that naive accumulated fine-tuning (Accum) consistently degrades below zero-shot by 22.6 points on average (53.2% vs. 75.8%), with some traps dropping as low as 11.4% (C16). The paper's adaptation recipe (LoRA + Balanced Softmax) reverses this: Accum★ achieves 82.2%, beating zero-shot by 6.4 points on average and outperforming it on all 20 representative traps. However, a substantial gap remains between Accum★ (82.2%) and the Oracle★ upper bound (90.4%), which the authors attribute to inter-interval temporal distribution shifts in both species composition and background context. Post-processing techniques (logit calibration, weight interpolation, interval model selection) can partially close this gap under optimistic hyperparameter selection, but practical deployment without future data access remains an open challenge.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant cross-domain generalization framing of camera-trap research (iWildCam challenges, domain adaptation) by reframing the problem as fixed-site temporal reliability under streaming data arrival. It draws on biological vision foundation models (BioCLIP 2) as the base architecture and repurposes existing adaptation tools—LoRA, Balanced Softmax, WiSE weight interpolation, and logit calibration—into a unified practical recipe, but the core contribution is the STREAMTRAP streaming evaluation protocol rather than a new model. The continual learning literature informs the streaming protocol, though the paper explicitly diverges by assuming full access to all past labeled data (no storage constraints), isolating temporal shift as the sole difficulty.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STREAMTRAP (546 camera traps, 17 datasets, 6+ months each) is the first large-scale benchmark that mirrors the temporal lifecycle of real ecological deployments, and its FAIR-compliant pipeline enables practitioners to convert their own data into comparable benchmarks. The finding that naive fine-tuning on all accumulated data degrades below zero-shot on most sites, and that even an oracle model trained on all data underperforms zero-shot on ~40% of sites, fundamentally challenges the assumption that more data always helps adaptation. Critical open questions remain: predicting zero-shot sufficiency at unseen sites before deployment, and the Adapt-or-Skip problem of determining when an update is actually beneficial—both shown to be far harder than simple confidence-based heuristics can handle.
