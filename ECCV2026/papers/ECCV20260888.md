## Problem

Temperature Scaling and its extensions apply a single, globally smooth rescaling of logits, implicitly assuming miscalibration is uniform across the confidence spectrum. However, quantile-wise analysis reveals that the largest correctness-confidence gaps are highly heterogeneous and regime-dependent—concentrated in low quantiles under standard settings but shifting to mid-quantiles under class imbalance—leaving these dominant error regions largely uncorrected by existing methods.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the raw logits of a pre-trained classifier and a held-out calibration set (no model weights or gradients needed). It first computes the empirical CDF of confidences on the calibration set to map each prediction to its quantile q(x) ∈ [0,1], then applies a per-sample temperature T(x) to rescale all K logits before the final softmax. The output is a calibrated K-class probability vector that preserves the predicted class (argmax is invariant) while adjusting confidence magnitudes.

## Method

QaTS reparameterizes the Expected Calibration Error on a uniform quantile axis (τ = F_conf(p)), exposing where miscalibration is concentrated and preventing dense confidence regions from masking rare-region errors. It then defines a sample-wise temperature T(x) = a·(1 − q(x)) + b, a monotone linear function of the empirical confidence quantile with two learnable positive parameters (a, b) optimized via NLL on the calibration set. Low-quantile (overconfident) predictions receive higher temperatures that soften their probabilities, while high-quantile predictions receive near-unity temperatures that preserve accuracy. Because the temperature depends on the rank of confidence rather than its absolute magnitude, the method inherits rank-based stability under monotone logit transformations, yielding robustness to distributional shifts that rescale logits while preserving relative ordering.

## Results

On standard image classification, QaTS achieves the lowest ECE across nearly all dataset-architecture combinations: 0.29–0.51 on CIFAR-10, 1.32–2.56 on CIFAR-100, and 1.13–2.49 on ImageNet (5 backbones each). Under long-tailed imbalance (R-100), QaTS reduces ECE from 45–52 (uncalibrated) to 2.30–8.37, while TS and AdaTS remain above 33–49. Under distributional drift (CIFAR-100-C, severity 5), QaTS achieves 9.89 ECE versus 29.27 (TS) and 20.55 (IR), and even outperforms HFE (15.62) which requires access to target-domain OOD samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

QaTS outperforms all six baselines (Uncal, TS, IR, AdaTS, GC, FeatClip) in 15 out of 15 standard classification settings, with the sole exception of CIFAR-10/ViT-S where IR (0.45) edges out QaTS (0.51). The strongest competitor is FeatClip, which QaTS beats by margins of 0.05–0.45 ECE on standard benchmarks (e.g., 2.56 vs. 3.01 on CIFAR-100/RN-50; 2.49 vs. 2.93 on ImageNet/ViT-B). TS and IR are substantially weaker, with ECE values typically 2–4× higher than QaTS on CIFAR-100 and ImageNet. AdaTS and GC degrade severely under long-tailed imbalance (R-100 ECE of 33–37 vs. QaTS's 2.30–8.37) and under distributional drift (severity-5 ECE of 29.50 and 22.60 vs. QaTS's 9.89). Critically, QaTS preserves predicted-class accuracy in 94% of 161 evaluated settings compared to FeatClip, which degrades accuracy by up to 4–6% in challenging regimes, making QaTS the only method that simultaneously achieves the best calibration and full accuracy preservation.

## Relation to Prior Work

QaTS extends Temperature Scaling (TS) [10] by replacing the single global temperature with a sample-wise temperature T(x) = a·(1−q(x)) + b that depends on the prediction's empirical confidence quantile rather than its raw logit magnitude. This departs from prior adaptive-temperature approaches (class-wise [15], input-dependent via auxiliary networks [5,40], feature-space modulation [44,45]) by conditioning on rank-based quantile information, which the authors formalize through a quantile reparameterization of ECE. The method also contrasts with non-parametric calibrators (Isotonic Regression, Histogram Binning) by imposing a smooth monotone structure, and with accuracy-degrading methods like FeatClip [38] by preserving predicted classes by design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The quantile-wise analysis revealing that miscalibration is structurally heterogeneous and regime-dependent (shifting from low-quantile dominance in standard settings to mid-quantile dominance under long-tail imbalance) provides a useful diagnostic that explains why uniform rescaling fails. The practical impact is significant: a two-parameter post-hoc method that consistently outperforms stronger baselines across 162 settings while preserving accuracy and remaining robust under distributional shift (2–3× lower ECE than other OOD-free methods) makes it a strong default calibration choice for deployed models. Open questions include whether the linear quantile-to-temperature mapping is truly optimal (ablations suggest more flexible forms overfit), how the method behaves when the calibration set is very small or from a substantially different domain, and whether the quantile-space principle extends naturally to multi-task or generative calibration settings.
