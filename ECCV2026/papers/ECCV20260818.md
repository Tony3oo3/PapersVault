## Problem

Autoregressive HMER models suffer from exposure bias, where early token errors cascade through sequential decoding and are difficult to correct locally, while existing non-autoregressive approaches lack the ability to iteratively refine ambiguous symbols or structural relations. No prior work has applied masked diffusion to HMER, leaving a gap between the need for global structural consistency and robustness to handwriting ambiguity.

## Input / Output

The model takes a rasterized 224×224 pixel image (1-pixel stroke width) of a handwritten expression, originally provided in InkML vector format, and produces a LaTeX expression. The output is structured as a fixed-length sequence (max 150 tokens) of symbol-modifier pairs, where each token corresponds one-to-one with a visible glyph and carries its aligned structural modifiers (carets, underscores, braces) as an additive embedding rather than separate tokens.

## Method

GryphOne formulates HMER as a discrete masked diffusion process: a DINO ViT encoder extracts visual features, and a 5-block Transformer decoder iteratively refines a fully masked symbol sequence over T steps (default 50), where each step predicts all masked tokens in parallel and then stochastically remasks a subset according to a schedule-driven corruption process. Two key components enable effective refinement: Symbol-Aware Tokenization (SAT) decomposes LaTeX into visible symbols and invisible modifiers aligned one-to-one with glyphs, confining structural edits to single tokens and preserving sequence length; and Random-Masking Mutual Learning (RMML) enforces prediction consistency across two independently masked views via symmetric KL divergence, reducing sensitivity to specific noise realizations. Training uses cross-entropy over all positions plus the RMML regularization, while inference alternates between parallel prediction and random remasking to enable repeated global correction without left-to-right causal dependencies.

## Results

On the MathWriting test split, GryphOne-50 achieves 5.51% CER and 59.9% EM (ExpRate), outperforming the best reimplemented AR baseline ICAL (6.03% CER, 57.7% EM) and the commercial OCR system (7.17% CER, 53.0% EM). The ≤1 metric (expressions with at most one token error) reaches 78.1%, a 9.9-point improvement over ICAL. On held-out CROHME 2014–2023, GryphOne-50 attains 65.2% EM on CROHME 2014 and 61.2% on CROHME 2023, surpassing all reimplemented baselines by 2.8–2.9 points in EM. The T=10 configuration runs at 73.7 FPS—faster than all AR baselines—while retaining over 98% of the T=50 recognition performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GryphOne-50 outperforms all six reimplemented AR baselines (BTTR, CoMER, ICAL, TAMER, PosFormer) and the commercial OCR system on every MathWriting test-split metric, with the largest gap over ICAL (best AR baseline): 0.52 points lower CER, 2.2 points higher EM, and 9.9 points higher ≤1. On CROHME, GryphOne-50 leads ICAL by 2.8 EM points on 2014, 2.4 on 2016, 0.8 on 2019, and 2.9 on 2023, and also surpasses the literature-reported NAMER* on 2014 (65.2 vs. 60.5 EM) and 2016 (61.4 vs. 60.2 EM). The mask-predict (MP) variant, which uses the same encoder but decodes in a single pass without iterative refinement, still beats all AR baselines on ≤1 (75.9 vs. 68.2 for ICAL) but lags GryphOne-10 by 2.1 EM points, confirming that the iterative diffusion schedule—not merely parallel decoding—drives the structural gains. In terms of speed, GryphOne-10 at 73.7 FPS is roughly 9–15× faster than the AR baselines (5–8.6 FPS), while GryphOne-50 at 21.2 FPS remains 2.5–4× faster than any AR method.

## Relation to Prior Work

GryphOne departs from the dominant autoregressive encoder-decoder paradigm (BTTR, CoMER, ICAL, TAMER, PosFormer) by reformulating HMER as a discrete masked-diffusion process, and extends the non-autoregressive direction opened by NAMER by adding iterative stochastic refinement rather than one-shot prediction. The first application of masked diffusion to HMER is the paper's core positioning claim; the two supporting mechanisms—Symbol-Aware Tokenization (SAT) for locality-preserving structural edits and Random-Masking Mutual Learning (RMML) for cross-mask consistency—are novel contributions not present in prior diffusion or NAR work. The vision backbone (DINO ViT) and the overall encoder-decoder structure follow standard practice, so the novelty is concentrated in the decoding formulation and tokenization scheme.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that iterative symbolic refinement via masked diffusion can outperform both AR and one-shot NAR decoders on HMER while offering a length-independent accuracy-latency knob (T), which is practically attractive for deployment. Key limitations remain: the method is data-hungry (requires MathWriting-scale training), slower than one-shot NAR at high T, and restricted to fixed-resolution rasterized inputs. Open questions include whether the diffusion formulation transfers to other structured-recognition tasks (e.g., sheet music, chemical structures), whether adaptive or learned masking schedules can further reduce the step count, and whether the approach scales to online (streaming) handwriting.
