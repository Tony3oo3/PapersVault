## Problem

Foundation models such as DINOv2/v3 and CLIP learn representations optimized for semantic invariance, grouping surfaces by object identity rather than physical material properties. Standard self-supervised objectives with photometric augmentations inadvertently destroy or suppress subtle intrinsic cues like reflectance and mesostructure, leaving a gap for tasks that require explicit material reasoning such as intrinsic decomposition, material capture, and appearance-based selection.

## Input / Output

The model consumes RGB images (224×224 global crops and 112×112 local crops during training; 448×448 or 512×512 at evaluation, yielding 1024 patch tokens) and produces two levels of output: per-patch feature vectors (one per 16×16 pixel patch) and a single global [CLS] embedding. Downstream, patch embeddings are used to compute dense cosine-similarity maps for material selection or fed into k-NN classifiers for material classification, while the global embedding supports image-level material identity retrieval.

## Method

Φeat fine-tunes a DINOv3 ViT-B/16 backbone by replacing generic photometric augmentations with physically grounded weak supervision: pairs of renders showing the same material under different geometries and lighting conditions (drawn from ~1M synthetic renders generated via Monte Carlo path tracing over Adobe Substance 3D PBR materials). The training objective combines DINOv3's Sinkhorn-balanced prototype assignment at the image level, iBOT masked patch reconstruction, KoLeo entropy regularization, and Gram anchoring, augmented with a novel in-batch InfoNCE contrastive loss that explicitly pulls together global embeddings of the same material across different extrinsic conditions while pushing apart embeddings of different materials. This contrastive term is the critical addition that resolves the loss of global discriminability introduced by material supervision, yielding features that are simultaneously invariant to lighting and geometry yet tightly clustered by intrinsic material identity.

## Results

On the DuMaS material selection benchmark, Φeat achieves mIoU 72.0 and F1 80.8, outperforming all weakly supervised baselines including DINOv3 (mIoU 59.7, F1 72.2) and PE Spatial (mIoU 61.4, F1 72.3). For k-NN material classification on a 972-material synthetic test set, Φeat reaches 71.8% accuracy and 75.0% precision, substantially exceeding DINOv3 (66.9% acc, 60.0% prec) and PE Spatial (50.3% acc, 44.0% prec). In robustness tests, Φeat shows the lowest pairwise Hamming distance under both illumination (0.221) and geometry (0.305) perturbations. On semantic tasks, Φeat retains 80.1% ImageNet accuracy (vs. 83.5% for DINOv3) and 46.2/66.1 mIoU on ADE20K/Cityscapes (vs. 51.5/71.5), reflecting a moderate specialization tradeoff.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Φeat outperforms all weakly supervised baselines on every reported metric. Against its initialization backbone DINOv3, it improves material-selection mIoU by 12.3 points (59.7→72.0) and k-NN accuracy by 4.9 points (66.9→71.8). The closest competitor, PE Spatial, is marginally better on ℓ₁ (25.2 vs. 25.4) but trails Φeat by 10.6 mIoU points on selection and 21.5 accuracy points on k-NN classification, indicating that dense-prediction-oriented features do not transfer well to global material identity. Vision-language models (CLIP, SigLIP 2) perform poorly across the board (mIoU 21.6–24.4), confirming that image-text alignment conflates material identity with object semantics. Agglomerative RADIOv3 achieves competitive dense features (mIoU 63.5) but collapses in k-NN classification (35.9% acc), further underscoring that multi-purpose distillation does not yield material-specific structure. Against the supervised upper bounds (Materialistic, Guerrero et al.), Φeat remains 13–14 mIoU points behind on selection but does so without any pixel-level supervision or task-specific architecture, positioning it as a drop-in backbone replacement rather than a full prediction pipeline.

## Relation to Prior Work

Φeat extends the DINOv3 self-supervised ViT framework by replacing its generic photometric augmentations with physically grounded variations: the same material rendered under diverse geometries and lighting conditions. This shifts the inductive bias from semantic invariance (same object, different views) to physical invariance (same material, different context), a conceptual departure from both the DINOv2/v3 lineage and from vision-language models like CLIP and SigLIP that conflate material identity with object-level semantics. The patch-level iBOT masked reconstruction and Sinkhorn-Knopp prototype assignment are inherited directly from DINOv3, while the novel cross-material InfoNCE contrastive loss and the semantically aligned synthetic data pipeline (Adobe Substance 3D assets on curated mesh templates) constitute the paper's core methodological contributions. The work also positions itself against task-specific material capture and intrinsic decomposition methods that require dense pixel-level supervision, arguing that a physically grounded backbone can serve as a general-purpose prior for material-centric tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that physically inspired weak supervision—rendering the same material across varied extrinsic conditions—can produce a general-purpose backbone that outperforms strong semantic foundation models on material selection and classification without any dense labels, opening a path toward material-aware perception in downstream pipelines (robotics, intrinsic decomposition, material capture) that currently rely on task-specific architectures. Key limitations include the reliance entirely on synthetic data (domain gap to real-world appearance), the absence of explicit disentanglement into interpretable physical factors (roughness, metalness, albedo), and a moderate but non-trivial drop in general semantic performance (3.4 ImageNet accuracy points). Open questions include whether domain adaptation or self-training on unlabelled real images can close the synthetic-to-real gap, and whether the representation can be extended to handle more complex light-transport phenomena such as subsurface scattering and translucency.
