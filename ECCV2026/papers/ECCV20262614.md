## Problem

Modern modular RL-based ObjectNav systems involve numerous design choices across perception, policy, and inference-time strategies, yet the relative contribution of each component has never been systematically isolated. Without a controlled decomposition, it is unclear whether gains come from better detectors, better policies, or simple test-time heuristics, making fair comparison and targeted improvement difficult.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262614/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each timestep the agent receives an RGB-D observation (4×H×W), a goal category label, and its current pose. The perception module fuses these into a local ego-centric top-down semantic map (240×240 or 480×480, 20 channels by default, or a compressed 3-channel RGB variant), which the policy consumes to predict either a continuous goal coordinate or a discrete corner index. A low-level controller then maps the goal into one of four discrete actions (move forward, turn left, turn right, stop) within the Habitat simulator.

## Method

The paper conducts a large-scale controlled ablation study decomposing modular ObjectNav into three modules—perception (object detector, map size, map augmentation), policy (observation space, action space, network architecture, reward design), and test-time enhancement (untrapping helper, dynamic goal selection, remapping mask)—and isolates each component's contribution using heuristic policies to decouple perception from policy effects. The policy is trained with PPO, and the best configuration combines a Gibson-finetuned Mask R-CNN detector, a 480×480 augmented map, a continuous action space, a ViT-Base or ResNet-18 backbone, a distance-to-target reward, and all three test-time strategies. The key empirical finding is that perception quality and test-time strategies often produce larger performance gains than policy architecture changes alone, and that compressed observations (3-channel vs. 20-channel) retain sufficient information with negligible performance loss. Guided by these results, the authors assemble an enhanced system achieving 85.3% SR and 47.5% SPL on Gibson (SOTA), and introduce a human expert baseline (98% SR, 53.3% SPL) to quantify the remaining gap to human-level navigation.

## Results

On the Gibson benchmark (standard evaluation), the proposed unified modular system achieves 84.4% Success Rate (SR), 48.2% SPL, and 0.74 m DTS, setting a new state of the art among autonomous methods. The closest prior method, NaviFormer, reaches 82.6% SR and 40.9% SPL, while T-Diff and HOZ++ tie at 44.9% SPL. A human expert baseline under identical observation modalities achieves 98.0% SR and 53.3% SPL, revealing a substantial gap between current RL agents and human-level navigation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all nine autonomous baselines on every metric (SR, SPL, DTS). The closest competitor on SR is NaviFormer (82.6% vs. 84.4%, +1.8 pp), while on SPL the gap to T-Diff and HOZ++ (both 44.9%) is +3.3 pp. Against SGM (78.0% SR, 44.0% SPL), the improvement is +6.4 pp SR and +4.2 pp SPL. Older baselines show larger margins: PONI (73.6% SR, 41.0% SPL) is surpassed by +10.8 pp SR and +7.2 pp SPL, and SemExp (65.7% SR, 33.9% SPL) by +18.7 pp SR and +14.3 pp SPL. Notably, NaviFormer leads on DTS among prior methods (0.76 m) but is still slightly worse than the proposed system (0.74 m), while T-Diff and HOZ++ match on SPL (44.9%) yet trail the proposed method by 3.3 pp. The human expert baseline (98.0% SR, 53.3% SPL, 0.26 m DTS) exceeds all autonomous methods by a wide margin, underscoring that even the best RL system remains 13.6 pp below human success rate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the modular RL-based ObjectNav pipeline established by ANS (Chaplot et al., ICLR 2020) and SemExp (Chaplot et al., NeurIPS 2020), which introduced the top-down semantic map + RL long-term goal policy architecture. Rather than proposing a new policy or perception module, the paper departs from the field's typical focus on individual component improvements by systematically isolating and quantifying the contribution of each module (perception, policy, test-time enhancement) under controlled conditions. It also situates its policy experiments within the broader RL literature by comparing CNN (ResNet-18) and Transformer (ViT-Base) backbones and ablation on PPO hyperparameters, while drawing on recent detectors (YOLOv11, RF-DETR, SAM2) that have not previously been evaluated in the ObjectNav context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that perception quality and test-time strategies (untrapping, dynamic goal selection, remapping) often yield larger gains than policy architecture changes reframes where effort should be invested in modular ObjectNav systems, challenging the field's heavy emphasis on novel policy networks. The 98% human success rate versus 85.3% for the best autonomous system underscores a substantial algorithmic gap that current modular approaches have not closed, pointing to the need for more robust perception-reasoning integration. Open questions include whether these component-importance rankings generalize beyond the Gibson/Habitat setting, whether test-time heuristics can be learned end-to-end, and whether the modular decomposition can be extended to multi-floor or outdoor environments where map overlap and scale become more severe.
