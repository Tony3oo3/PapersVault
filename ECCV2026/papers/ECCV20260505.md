## Problem

Underwater degradation (wavelength-dependent absorption and particle scattering) induces highly non-linear geometric distortions in the visual feature space, deviating features from the canonical manifold of clear images and rendering standard Euclidean convolutions suboptimal. Existing countermeasures—image enhancement/restoration and domain adaptation—either over-smooth fine details, hallucinate pseudo-structures, or fail to capture depth-varying physical heterogeneity, leaving detectors to blindly memorize degraded patterns. The paper addresses the gap of explicitly modeling degradation as a physics-conditioned geometric transport problem in latent space rather than treating it as a black-box nuisance.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single underwater RGB image at 640×640 resolution as input, from which it jointly extracts a global spatio-spectral physical prior (frequency-band power spectra, per-channel mean/std, grayscale contrast) and a local structural context from the backbone feature stream. It outputs standard object bounding boxes with class labels (four classes on DUO, variable on other benchmarks) along with a multi-scale evidential vacuity map that quantifies epistemic uncertainty per spatial location, enabling suppression of overconfident false positives in backscatter-dominated regions.

## Method

PhysFlowNet reformulates underwater feature rectification as a Riemannian flow on a learned manifold. A Hierarchical Physical Context Embedding (HPCE) module constructs a unified physical condition z_phy by fusing learnable frequency-band descriptors (via 2D rFFT power spectra with Softmax-normalized band kernels), spatial statistics, and local structural features from the backbone. Conditioned on z_phy via Adaptive Group Normalization, a Parallel Physics-Residual Bottleneck (PPRB) computes a bounded velocity field (tanh-constrained) and a diagonal SPD metric tensor G_diag (zero-initialized to identity), then executes a metric-preconditioned retraction F_rec = GN(F_main + v ⊙ (G_diag + ε)^{-1/2}) to transport distorted features toward canonical semantics, with a learnable spatial gate for progressive activation. A Physics-Guided Manifold Downsampling (PMD) module counteracts cascaded low-pass smoothing by applying physics-guided residual gating before MaxPooling in parallel with a strided convolution. Training uses a Unified Evidential-Contrastive Objective (UECO) combining a supervised contrastive manifold loss (SCML) for intra-class compactness on the curved feature space with an evidential Dirichlet risk (EUR) that calibrates epistemic uncertainty and suppresses backscatter-driven false positives.

## Results

On the DUO benchmark, PhysFlowNet achieves 69.3 AP (AP₅₀=92.7, AP₇₅=80.4), setting a new state-of-the-art that surpasses the best general detector YOLO11 (60.8 AP) by +8.5% and the best underwater-specific method GCC-Net (69.1 AP) by +0.2%. Across the three additional benchmarks, PhysFlowNet reports 49.2 AP on TrashCan, 64.3 AP on RUOD, and 86.1 AP on Brackish, consistently outperforming YOLO11 by 4–6 AP points. The model operates at 14.0 GFLOPs and 39.7 FPS with 8.19M parameters, offering a favorable efficiency–accuracy trade-off compared to heavier UOD methods like GCC-Net (300.8 GFLOPs) and RoIAttn (331.7 GFLOPs).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On DUO, PhysFlowNet (69.3 AP) outperforms every listed baseline: it gains +8.5 AP over YOLO11 (60.8), +3.0 AP over the strongest lightweight UOD method WFDA (66.3), and +0.2 AP over the heaviest UOD method GCC-Net (69.1, 300.8 GFLOPs). On TrashCan, PhysFlowNet leads YOLO11 by +4.1 AP (49.2 vs 45.1) and WFDA by +2.3 AP (49.2 vs 46.9). On Brackish, it edges out SFUDNet (86.1 vs 86.0 AP) and surpasses WFDA by +3.5 AP. On RUOD, PhysFlowNet (64.3 AP) trails AMSP-UOD (65.2 AP) by 0.9 AP but still exceeds WFDA (63.4) and YOLO11 (58.1); it does lead on AP₅₀ (86.4 vs 86.1 for AMSP-UOD). Overall, the Riemannian rectification + evidential calibration pipeline delivers consistent 3–8 AP gains over the YOLO11 backbone across all four datasets while remaining within 2× the FLOPs of the YOLO11 baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on YOLO11 as its detection backbone and on the Dirichlet-evidence framework from evidential deep learning for uncertainty calibration, but departs sharply from the dominant paradigm in underwater object detection, which treats optical degradation as a black-box nuisance addressed via image enhancement, domain adaptation, or ad-hoc attention. The key conceptual departure is reframing degradation-induced feature distortion as a Riemannian transport problem: rather than enhancing pixels or aligning domains statistically, PhysFlowNet conditions a metric-preconditioned retraction on explicit spatio-spectral physical priors (FFT band energies, RGB statistics) derived from the Jaffe-McGlamery imaging model. This positions the work at the intersection of physics-informed learning, manifold geometry, and detection, where prior underwater methods have been largely physics-agnostic and prior Riemannian/flow-based approaches (e.g., rectified flows for generation) have not been applied to feature-space rectification in detection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a principled geometric language for a problem (depth-varying, spatially heterogeneous underwater degradation) that has resisted clean data-driven solutions, and the evidential uncertainty component directly addresses the practical failure mode of high-confidence false positives from backscatter. However, the Riemannian formulation is implemented with a channel-wise diagonal metric and a single retraction step, which limits how much of the full manifold structure is actually exploited; it is closer to a learned, physics-conditioned residual normalization than a true geodesic flow. Open questions include whether the hand-designed spatio-spectral prior generalizes to other degradation regimes (fog, rain, sonar), whether the diagonal metric approximation is sufficient or a full SPD tensor would yield further gains, and whether the approach scales to larger, more diverse underwater datasets beyond the four relatively small benchmarks used here.
