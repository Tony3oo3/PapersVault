## Problem

Existing Action Quality Assessment (AQA) methods follow a 'one-by-one' paradigm, training and deploying a separate model for each action type, which requires prior knowledge of the action type and hinders deployment in open-domain or low-resource settings. Naively training a single model on diverse actions causes severe negative transfer (average SRCC drop of ~10%) due to large semantic discrepancies across action types, as existing architectures lack mechanisms to adaptively select relevant assessment patterns per input.

## Input / Output

The model takes variable-length action video clips (68–124 snippets of 32 frames for long-term actions; 10 snippets of 16 frames for short-term actions), encoded by pre-trained VST or I3D into features of dimension 1024, projected to 256. At inference, no action-type label is required—the soft router computes expert weights purely from visual features. The output is a continuous quality score obtained as the expectation over M=4 predefined grade levels, produced by a Transformer-decoder-based grade-aware head.

## Method

MoAKE employs K=3 action knowledge experts, each containing a shared two-layer MLP adapter (mapping all actions into a common semantic space), a Segment-Aware Temporal Aggregation (SATA) module using N=64 expert-specific learnable prototypes as cross-attention queries to produce fixed-length representations regardless of input duration, and an Adaptive Intra- and Inter-Segment Relationship Modeling (AIISRM) module with three parallel branches (feature-dimension self-attention, multi-scale 1D convolutions with kernel sizes 1/3/5, and sequence-dimension self-attention) fused via residual addition. A lightweight soft router computes expert weights from the input features, and the final mixed feature is decoded by a grade-aware Transformer decoder. Training uses a composite loss combining score MSE (on both the mixed and per-expert auxiliary predictions), a label-smoothed soft cross-entropy routing loss, and a triplet diversity loss on grade representations; the label smoothing (ε=0.3) deliberately prevents rigid expert isolation, enabling positive cross-action transfer.

## Results

In the all-in-one setting on three long-term datasets (RG, Fis-V, LOGO), MoAKE achieves an average SRCC of 0.753 and R-ℓ2 of 2.434, surpassing the best one-by-one baseline CoFInAl (0.744 SRCC, 3.641 R-ℓ2) by 1.2% in SRCC and 28.9% in R-ℓ2. In the all-in-one comparison, MoAKE outperforms the strongest baselines MCMoE (0.726 SRCC) and MLAVL (0.722 SRCC) by 2.7% and 3.1% in average SRCC, respectively. Under zero-shot evaluation on three unseen short-term datasets, MoAKE achieves 0.319/0.210/0.219 SRCC on Diving/Skiing/Surgery, substantially exceeding the best prior method MLAVL (0.211/0.148/0.122). In the few-shot setting, MoAKE reaches 0.664/0.513/0.651 SRCC, outperforming MLAVL (0.618/0.458/0.620) across all three datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoAKE wins across all nine baselines in the all-in-one setting. Against the strongest visual-only MoE baseline MCMoE, MoAKE gains +2.7% SRCC (0.753 vs. 0.726) and reduces R-ℓ2 by 38% (2.434 vs. 3.950). Against the language-guided MLAVL, MoAKE improves SRCC by +3.1% and R-ℓ2 by 31%, despite using only visual features. The Conditional Baseline (shared backbone + per-action heads) achieves 0.718 SRCC, confirming that simple architectural branching is insufficient without adaptive feature-level routing. Notably, MoAKE's all-in-one results even exceed the best one-by-one CoFInAl (0.744 SRCC), demonstrating positive transfer (+4.7% SRCC, +34.4% R-ℓ2) rather than the negative transfer (−10.17% average SRCC) observed when naively applying existing methods to multi-action training. In zero-shot evaluation, MoAKE outperforms all baselines by large margins (e.g., 0.319 vs. 0.211 SRCC on Diving), and in few-shot it leads by 4.6–5.5% SRCC over the best baseline MLAVL across all three unseen datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Mixture-of-Experts paradigm from large language models (Switch Transformers, Sparsely-Gated MoE) to the macro-action level for AQA, departing from token-level sparse routing in favor of soft, action-adaptive blending of complementary experts. It draws on Q-Former-style cross-attention for segment-aware temporal aggregation and on recent grade-aware decoders for score prediction, but the core architectural contribution is the AIISRM module for multi-granularity temporal relationship modeling. The all-in-one framing is borrowed from image restoration and vision-language pre-training, but the paper is the first to systematically apply it to AQA, where the semantic gap between action types creates a distinct negative-transfer challenge not present in those domains. It also extends the MoE-for-AQA line started by MCMoE (which uses experts for missing modalities) to a fundamentally different axis: cross-action knowledge fusion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first comprehensive benchmarks for all-in-one and zero/few-shot AQA, providing a much-needed evaluation protocol for unified action assessment that the field has lacked. The finding that properly designed expert mixing converts the expected negative transfer into consistent positive transfer (4.7% SRCC, 34.4% R-l2 gains over one-by-one) is a useful conceptual result suggesting that action diversity is a resource rather than a liability. However, zero-shot generalization remains weak (SRCC ~0.2–0.3 on unseen actions), and the framework has only been validated on six datasets with three experts; scaling to dozens of action types or truly open-domain video remains an open question. The authors themselves note that self-supervised pre-training and multimodal integration are natural next steps to close the zero-shot gap.
