## Problem

Federated learning is vulnerable to gradient leakage attacks that reconstruct private images from shared gradients, yet existing defenses suffer from a fundamental trade-off: perturbing images or gradients to block reconstruction either produces out-of-distribution samples that degrade model accuracy, or relies on identical-label mixing that remains exploitable by semantic-level attacks. The paper identifies three key gaps—insufficient protection of fine-grained details, the time-limited effectiveness of attacks (early rounds only), and label leakage through classification-layer gradients—that prior methods fail to address simultaneously.

## Input / Output

Each client provides a single private image-label pair (worst-case batch size 1) and a locally trained DDPM (minimum 500 samples) as input. The method outputs a single obfuscated gradient vector of dimension d, constructed by blending the private per-sample gradient with a gradient computed from a DDPM-synthesized image whose class condition is deliberately set to a non-identical label. The obfuscation weights (λ, M, or γ) are sampled per-parameter from a Beta or Bernoulli distribution whose concentration is governed by a cosine-annealing schedule over R federated rounds, making the output stochastic and round-dependent.

## Method

Diff2Obs obfuscates the private gradient by replacing or blending it with the gradient of a synthesized image generated by a client-local DDPM, which preserves the data distribution (aiding convergence) while guaranteeing distinct fine-grained details. Three obfuscation strategies are provided: linear interpolation (Obs-I), binary masking (Obs-M), and statistical matching (Obs-S), each parameterized by a time-adaptive weight sampled from Beta(1−m_r, m_r) where m_r follows a cosine schedule that allocates stronger obfuscation in early rounds (when GLAs are most effective) and weaker obfuscation in late rounds (to recover fine-grained features). A label-agnostic constraint forces the DDPM to condition on the least-likely non-true label, making the synthesized gradient approximately orthogonal to the private gradient and blocking semantic-level label inference. An information-theoretic analysis via the data-processing inequality on the Markov chain (x_l, y_l) → (g_l, θ_DDPM) → ∇̃_θ^r proves the defense does not increase mutual information leakage relative to sharing the raw gradient, since θ_DDPM and the sampled weights remain client-private.

## Results

On ImageNet (the primary evaluation dataset), Diff2Obs (Obs-I) achieves the highest average LPIPS of 0.838 against GI-NAS, 0.361 against HFGradInv, and 0.864 against GGL, indicating strong perceptual and semantic protection. In terms of model utility, Diff2Obs attains 75.48% test accuracy with a PMM of 98.86% and only 1.92× computational overhead, compared to CENSOR's 33.77% PMM and Refiner's 85.54% PMM with 40.76× overhead. Across all three datasets (ImageNet, FFHQ, CIFAR-10) and five attacks, Diff2Obs consistently achieves the lowest SSIM and PSNR (best structural protection) and among the highest LPIPS (best perceptual protection), while maintaining near-98% PMM under both IID and non-IID (α=1) settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Diff2Obs outperforms CENSOR [55] on model utility by a wide margin: PMM of 98.86% vs. 33.77% and test accuracy of 75.48% vs. 25.79%, while achieving comparable or slightly better LPIPS (0.838 vs. 0.835 on GI-NAS/ImageNet) and lower SSIM (0.035 vs. 0.052). Against Refiner [10], Diff2Obs wins on all three dimensions—PMM (98.86% vs. 85.54%), accuracy (75.48% vs. 65.31%), and computational overhead (1.92× vs. 40.76×)—while also achieving higher LPIPS (0.838 vs. 0.785) and lower SSIM (0.035 vs. 0.124). Compared to DCS2 [47], Diff2Obs provides substantially stronger perceptual protection (LPIPS 0.838 vs. 0.642 on GI-NAS) and better PMM (98.86% vs. 95.40%). Against DP-based baselines (Noise [12], FedDPA [50], TA-DPFL [23]), Diff2Obs achieves markedly higher LPIPS (e.g., 0.838 vs. 0.407–0.477) while maintaining 98.86% PMM, whereas DP methods sacrifice 5–6% accuracy for weaker protection. Soteria [41] matches Diff2Obs on accuracy (75.11% vs. 75.48%) but offers weaker privacy (lower LPIPS across all attacks in Table 1). Under the adaptive EoT attack, Diff2Obs remains stable with only marginal metric shifts (e.g., LPIPS 0.844→0.829 on ImageNet), confirming robustness even when the adversary knows the obfuscation mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

Diff2Obs departs from the dominant defense paradigm of directly perturbing private images (DCS2, Refiner) or projecting gradients into orthogonal subspaces (CENSOR) by instead generating synthesized images via a client-local DDPM and obfuscating the private gradient with the synthesized gradient. The time-adaptive scheduling borrows the cosine-annealing idea from time-adaptive DP-FL [23] but repurposes it to modulate the ratio of synthesized-to-private gradient rather than the DP privacy budget. The label-agnostic constraint (using a non-identical class condition for DDPM sampling) addresses a gap left by MixGrad [8], which mixes gradients of same-label images and thus remains vulnerable to semantic-level label inference attacks like GGL [30] and HFGradInv [51].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, well-motivated defense that simultaneously protects fine-grained details (via distribution-matched but detail-distinct synthesized images), semantic labels (via non-identical class conditioning), and temporal vulnerability (via time-adaptive obfuscation aligned with when attacks are most effective). A notable limitation is that the information-theoretic analysis (Eq. 8) only shows Diff2Obs does not *increase* leakage relative to raw gradient sharing, without providing a quantitative privacy guarantee; the actual privacy benefit rests entirely on empirical reconstruction quality. Open questions include how the approach scales to very large diffusion models or multi-modal FL settings, and whether the per-client DDPM training cost (even with warm-start) remains feasible on resource-constrained edge devices in production deployments.
