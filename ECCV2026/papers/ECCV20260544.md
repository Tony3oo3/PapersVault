## Problem

Parameter-level defenses (Params, MergeLock, MergeBarrier) protect fine-tuned models from unauthorized merging by applying secret linear transformations to weights. However, these defenses have never been systematically analyzed for structural vulnerabilities, and it remains unclear whether the linear transformations can be analytically inverted by an adversary who only has access to the public pretrained model. This paper reveals that the protected task vectors are inherently 2–3 orders of magnitude smaller than the pretrained anchor, making all such defenses fundamentally bypassable.

## Input / Output

The attack requires only two inputs: the protected fine-tuned model θ_ft^p and the publicly available pretrained model θ_pre, with no knowledge of which defense mechanism was applied (defense-agnostic setting). It produces a per-module recovery matrix T* (a continuous matrix for attention projections, a permutation matrix for MLP layers) that, when applied to the protected weights, yields the original fine-tuned weights W^a ≈ W_ft. The recovered model can then be merged with other unprotected models via Task Arithmetic, CAT, or LOT merging to restore near-original multi-task performance.

## Method

AGA exploits the magnitude disparity in W^p = W_pre·P + τ·P by approximating W^p ≈ W_pre·P and solving the alignment objective min_T ‖W^p·T − W_pre‖_F² to analytically recover T ≈ P⁻¹. For continuous attention transformations (diagonal, orthogonal, or invertible matrices), AGA decouples each projection (Q, K, V, O) and solves an overdetermined least-squares system yielding a closed-form solution. For discrete MLP permutations, AGA constructs a cost matrix from negative cosine similarity between protected and pretrained row vectors and applies the Hungarian algorithm for globally optimal bipartite matching, with the inverse permutation applied to subsequent layers. The countermeasure ARF introduces a margin-based repulsive loss L_dist = λ_dist · Σ min(0, ρ‖θ_pre‖₂ − ‖θ − θ_pre‖₂) applied exclusively to attention projection matrices during fine-tuning, forcing the task vector magnitude to exceed a safety threshold and thereby invalidating the anchor-dominance assumption that AGA relies on.

## Results

On ViT-B/32 with Task Arithmetic merging, AGA recovers protected-task accuracy to 66.39% (Params), 66.39% (MergeLock), and 64.98% (Params-D), closely matching the unprotected baseline of 67.23%. Against MergeBarrier, which alters MLP topology via Taylor expansion, AGA still recovers 60.80% average accuracy from a suppressed 29.32%. On Qwen2-7B text generation, AGA restores average performance from 3.07% (Params-D) to 47.79%, and from 2.26% (MergeLock) to 49.01%, versus an unprotected baseline of 49.10%. The proposed ARF countermeasure reduces AGA-attacked accuracy to 27.42% on ViT-B/32, well below MergeGuard's 50.56% under the same attack.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AGA decisively defeats all four parameter-level defenses. Against Params and MergeLock (which apply diagonal/permutation and invertible matrix transformations respectively), AGA recovers average accuracy to 66.39%, within 0.84% of the unprotected 67.23% baseline. Params-D, which adds random dropout to the transformed weights, is slightly harder to invert (64.98% recovered) but still yields a 59.48-point gain over the protected state. MergeBarrier is the hardest to fully bypass because its Taylor-expansion-based MLP modification makes exact inversion ill-posed; AGA handles this by reverting to pretrained MLP layers, recovering 60.80% (a 31.48-point gain) versus the 29.32% suppressed baseline. For the ARF countermeasure comparison, ARF + AGA achieves only 27.42% average on ViT-B/32, substantially below MergeGuard + AGA (50.56%) and even below MergeGuard's unattacked state (33.97%), confirming that ARF's localized repulsive fine-tuning effectively neutralizes the anchor-dominance assumption that AGA exploits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Task Arithmetic framework [17], whose task-vector decomposition (θ_i = θ_pre + τ_i) is the mathematical foundation for both the vulnerability identified and the attack proposed. It positions itself as a systematic security analysis of the recently proposed parameter-level defenses—Params [18], MergeLock [36], and MergeBarrier [22]—which all rely on secret linear transformations (permutation, diagonal, or orthogonal matrices) to disrupt unauthorized merging. The key departure from prior work is the observation that these defenses share a common structural weakness: the protected task vector is orders of magnitude smaller than the protected pretrained anchor, making the transformation analytically recoverable via simple alignment. The proposed countermeasure, ARF, extends the tuning-stage defense line initiated by MergeGuard [5] but replaces its global L2 regularization with a localized, margin-aware repulsive loss applied only to attention projections.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all current parameter-level defenses for model merging are fundamentally bypassable by a closed-form least-squares or Hungarian-algorithm recovery has immediate practical consequences: any practitioner releasing a fine-tuned model on a public pretrained backbone should assume their IP is unprotected under these schemes. The paper's ARF countermeasure is effective but only applicable when the defender controls the fine-tuning phase, leaving open the question of how to protect models that have already been released without retraining. More broadly, the result suggests that post-hoc linear parameter transformations are an insufficient security paradigm for model merging, and that robust protection may require fundamentally different mechanisms (e.g., non-linear obfuscation, watermarking, or architectural-level changes) that do not preserve the linear structure exploited by AGA.
