## Problem

Existing LLM-based prompt tuning methods for CLIP generate predefined, class-level descriptions that are not grounded in visual evidence, making them structurally biased toward generic class semantics and unable to capture instance-specific variations. This misalignment between text prompts and actual image content limits generalization in few-shot settings where subtle visual differences (e.g., among aircraft or car models) are essential for accurate recognition.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image (encoded by CLIP ViT-B/16 into a 512-d embedding) and a class name (pre-encoded via OpenAI text-embedding-3-large into a 3072-d LLM embedding, cached before training). It produces a unified context prompt of 4 concept vectors (each 512-d) injected at layer 8 of the CLIP text encoder, which is then used to compute cosine similarities against all class embeddings for classification. The framework is plug-and-play and can be grafted onto existing prompt-tuning backbones (CoOp, CoCoOp, PSRC) without modifying their architecture.

## Method

DICE operates in two stages. First, it pretrains a projection autoencoder that aligns LLM embeddings into the CLIP text embedding space, and a Top-k Sparse Autoencoder (SAE) that decomposes CLIP embeddings into a sparse latent space of 16,384 concept vectors (32× expansion, top-512 active), forming a learnable concept dictionary. Second, during prompt tuning, the Class Context Module projects cached LLM embeddings into CLIP space, while the Instance Context Module passes both the projected class embedding and the image embedding through the shared SAE encoder, computes a co-activation score via element-wise summation, and selects the top-4 most jointly activated concept vectors from the SAE decoder. These are fused with class embeddings through a lightweight meta-network to form the unified context. Training uses a composite loss combining cross-entropy, a knowledge-guided consistency loss (anchoring DICE embeddings to zero-shot CLIP embeddings), and an LLM reconstruction loss, preserving pretrained generalization while adapting to the task.

## Results

On the 11-dataset base-to-novel generalization benchmark (16-shot), DICE achieves the best harmonic mean (H) among all single-textual-prompt methods at 80.43% (Base 84.45%, Novel 76.77%), surpassing TCP (79.51%), PSRC (79.97%), and HPT (80.23%). DICE is within 0.05% of the overall best method CoPrompt (80.48%, multi-prompt) while using only a single textual prompt. In the plug-and-play setting, PSRC w/DICE achieves the highest overall H of 81.31%, outperforming all LLM-based and multi-modal prompt tuning methods. DICE shows the largest gains on fine-grained datasets: FGVC Aircraft (H 41.56% vs. 37.83% for TCP) and Stanford Cars (H 78.04% vs. 77.32% for TCP).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DICE outperforms all single-textual-prompt baselines: CoOp (H 71.66%), CoCoOp (75.83%), KART (74.26%), and TCP (79.51%), with the largest margin over CoOp (+8.77% H). Against multi-modal prompt methods, DICE surpasses MaPLe (76.65%) and PSRC (79.97%), and edges out HPT (80.23%) by 0.20% H. The only method DICE does not beat is CoPrompt (80.48% H), which exceeds DICE by a marginal 0.05% but uses multi-prompt (mp) rather than single-textual-prompt (tp). In the plug-and-play setting, DICE improves CoOp by +5.79% H, CoCoOp by +1.82%, and PSRC by +1.34%, with PSRC w/DICE reaching 81.31% H—the best overall result. Against adapter-based methods (Table 14), PSRC w/DICE achieves 83.21% at 16-shot, outperforming CLIP-Adapter (71.01%) and LDC (82.17%). On fine-grained datasets, DICE's advantage is most pronounced: FGVC Aircraft H improves from 37.83% (TCP) to 41.56%, and Stanford Cars from 77.32% to 78.04%.

## Relation to Prior Work

DICE sits at the intersection of two research threads: LLM-based prompt tuning for CLIP (KART, CoPrompt, HPT) and sparse autoencoders for interpretability (mechanistic interpretability literature). It departs from prior LLM-based methods by replacing free-text class descriptions with projected LLM embeddings decomposed through a Top-k SAE into a sparse concept dictionary, then selecting instance-relevant concepts via a co-activation score between image and class SAE activations. The plug-and-play design explicitly builds on CoOp/CoCoOp/PSRC as host frameworks, while the SAE-as-concept-dictionary repurposing draws from NLP interpretability work (Bricken et al., Cunningham et al.) and recent vision applications (SPlice, PatchSAE) but applies it to prompt construction rather than analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and well-motivated gap: predefined LLM descriptions are structurally biased toward class-level semantics and fail to capture instance-specific visual cues, which is critical in fine-grained few-shot settings. The SAE-based concept dictionary offers an interpretable, learnable alternative to handcrafted descriptions, and the plug-and-play integration (with +5.79% H gain over CoOp) makes it broadly applicable. However, the per-image forward pass through the text encoder limits scalability, and the 0.87% H difference between LLM and CLIP embeddings suggests the structural design matters more than the specific LLM source, raising questions about whether the SAE decomposition is truly leveraging LLM-specific knowledge or simply providing a useful sparse bottleneck. Open questions include whether the concept dictionary generalizes to unseen domains without retraining and whether the approach scales to larger VLMs.
