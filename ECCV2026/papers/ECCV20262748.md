## Problem

On-device fine-tuning and continual learning are severely constrained by the memory cost of storing intermediate activations during backpropagation, which can exceed parameter memory by 5–10×. Existing low-rank activation compression methods (e.g., ASI, iterative HOSVD) reduce this cost but recompute decompositions at every training step, introducing significant computational overhead, and have not been adapted to continual learning where repeated factorization across tasks would require storing large task-specific matrices.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained CNN (MCUNet, MobileNetV2, ResNet18/34, or AlexNet) and a small set of calibration image batches (as few as N=2 mini-batches suffice) to compute fixed low-rank projection matrices offline. During on-device fine-tuning, it consumes standard RGB training images with labels and produces updated model weights plus a compact set of core tensors and orthonormal factor matrices per layer. In the continual learning setting, it additionally maintains a growing memory matrix M^t per layer that encodes the union of task-specific subspaces, requiring no replay buffer or per-task model copies.

## Method

LANCE performs a one-shot higher-order SVD (HOSVD) on calibration activations to extract a reusable low-rank subspace per layer, then projects all subsequent activations into this fixed subspace during the forward pass, storing only the small core tensor G instead of the full activation tensor. During backpropagation, gradients are computed by reversing the projection, and the paper proves these compressed gradients are right-Frobenius orthogonal projections of the full gradients, guaranteeing valid descent directions and convergence to projected stationary points. For continual learning, after each task the last-mode covariance is projected onto the null space of the accumulated memory matrix M^{t-1}, and the new task's mode-d factors are constrained to lie in this orthogonal complement, so tasks learn in non-interfering subspaces without storing large task-specific matrices in SRAM during training. This one-shot design eliminates the per-iteration SVD cost of prior methods (reducing FLOPs by ~1.5× vs. iterative HOSVD) while achieving 25×–380× activation memory reduction and competitive accuracy with full backpropagation.

## Results

On ResNet18 fine-tuning (4 layers), LANCE reduces activation memory from 61.25 MB (full BP) to 1.16 MB (≈53× reduction) while maintaining 75.70% accuracy on CIFAR-100, within 1.4% of BP's 77.07%. LANCE also cuts training FLOPs to 0.05 TFLOPs versus 15.56 TFLOPs for iterative HOSVD (a 310× reduction) by performing the SVD only once. On continual learning (Split CIFAR-100), LANCE achieves 71.52% average accuracy with only 2.32 MB of stored memory, compared to 22–327 MB for gradient-projection baselines like GPM, TRGP, and CUBER.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against iterative HOSVD [30], LANCE wins on both accuracy (75.70% vs 75.35% on CIFAR-100, ResNet18) and FLOPs (0.05 vs 15.56 TFLOPs, a 310× reduction) because it avoids recomputing the SVD at every training step. Against ASI [29], LANCE achieves 3.8% higher accuracy on CIFAR-100 (75.70% vs 71.93%) with 28% less memory (1.16 vs 1.61 MB). Against full backpropagation, LANCE sacrifices only 1.4% accuracy on CIFAR-100 (75.70% vs 77.07%) in exchange for a 53× memory reduction, and the gap narrows to <1% on ResNet34. On continual learning, LANCE (71.52% ACC, 2.32 MB) trails GPM [35] (72.48%, 22.27 MB) and CODE-CL [2] (77.21%, 33.80 MB) by 1–6% in accuracy but uses 10–140× less memory. Compared to TRGP [28] (74.64%, 78.86 MB) and CUBER [27] (75.54%, 326.79 MB), LANCE trades 3–4% accuracy for a 34–141× memory saving, making it the most memory-efficient method in the comparison. On the 5-Datasets benchmark, LANCE reaches 90.76% ACC with 34.96 MB, competitive with CODE-CL (93.51%, 116.72 MB) at roughly one-third the memory.

## Relation to Prior Work

LANCE departs from iterative tensor-decomposition activation compression (Nguyen et al., NeurIPS 2024; ASI, ICML 2025) by performing a single HOSVD at initialization and reusing the resulting subspaces throughout training, eliminating per-step recomputation. It also repurposes the null-space projection principle from gradient-projection continual learning methods (GPM, TRGP, CODE-CL) but applies it to activation subspaces rather than weight gradients, yielding a unified framework that handles both efficient fine-tuning and CL without storing large task-specific matrices. The approach is complementary to system-level memory-reduction strategies (TinyTL, MCUNet, 256KB engine) that freeze layers or restrict updates, since LANCE preserves full backpropagation across all layers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 25×–380× activation memory reduction is practically significant for microcontroller-class devices where SRAM is the binding constraint, and the one-shot calibration (as few as 2 batches) makes the method deployable without expensive offline phases. However, the CL extension, while memory-efficient, trails the strongest gradient-projection baselines by 4–6% in accuracy on Split CIFAR-100, and the 3–13% accuracy gap on ultra-lightweight models (MCUNet, MobileNetV2) limits immediate adoption in the most resource-constrained settings. Open questions include whether the fixed-subspace assumption degrades over very long training horizons, how the method behaves with non-convex or multi-task losses, and whether the convergence guarantees (which assume local layer linearity) hold in practice for deeper, more nonlinear architectures.
