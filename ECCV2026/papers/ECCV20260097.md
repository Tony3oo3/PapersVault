## Problem

Standard noise-to-data diffusion paradigms are ill-suited for conditional generation tasks where the input already closely resembles the target (e.g., image editing, video stylization), as they force the model to reconstruct the entire output from pure noise and incur heavy computational overhead from conditioning tokens. Brownian Bridge models offer a direct data-to-data alternative, but prior work has been limited to small architectures and simple tasks, leaving their potential for complex, large-scale vision translation unexplored.

## Input / Output

All tasks operate in the latent space of a pre-trained VAE encoder, with source and target encoded into paired latent vectors (x₀, x₁) that the Bridge model directly transports between. For image editing, the input is a source image plus a text instruction, producing an edited image; for video stylization, a video clip plus style instruction yields a stylized video; for depth-to-video, a sequence of depth maps produces an RGB video. The 20B image-editing adaptation notably removes the original conditional input channel entirely, relying solely on the data-to-data transport, which eliminates the token-concatenation overhead and yields a 2.3× inference speedup.

## Method

ViBT scales the Brownian Bridge formulation to large DiT architectures (up to 20B parameters) by proposing a variance-stabilized velocity matching objective that introduces a per-sample normalization factor α(x₀,x₁,t) = √(1 + tD/((1−t)‖x₁−x₀‖²)) to rescale the velocity target, ensuring the conditional target magnitude is independent of t and preventing the O(1/√(1−t)) divergence that destabilizes vanilla velocity matching near t=1. At inference, a variance-corrected Euler-Maruyama sampler rescales the stochastic term by √(Δt·(1−t_{k+1})/(1−t_k)) to match the Brownian Bridge's intrinsically decreasing variance as the trajectory approaches the target. The paradigm transformation from a conditional DiT to a Bridge model is lightweight: the conditional input channel is removed and the model is post-trained with a rank-128 LoRA (e.g., 128 GPU-hours for the 20B Qwen-Image-Editing model), with no architectural changes to the Transformer backbone.

## Results

On instruction-based image editing (ImgEdit-Bench), ViBT achieves an average score of 3.76, trailing only the 20B base model Qwen-Image-Edit (3.90) by 0.14 while delivering a 2.3× inference speedup (6.2 s vs. 14.5 s per image). On video stylization, ViBT attains the best scores on TOPIQNR (0.503), MUSIQ (64.045), MANIQA (0.348), and CLIPIQA (0.486), outperforming Ins.V2V, RAVE, and TokenFlow. On depth-to-video synthesis, ViBT achieves the highest VBench Score (0.71), PSNR (11.403), and DISTS (0.230), surpassing Wan Fun Control (same Wan 2.1 base) and all SD-based baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On image editing, ViBT (3.76 avg) surpasses all prior methods—MagicBrush (1.83), InstructPix2Pix (1.88), AnyEdit (2.45), Step1X-Edit (3.06), UniWorld-V1 (3.26), and FLUX Kontext (3.71)—but trails its own base model Qwen-Image-Edit (3.90) by 0.14, losing specifically on Remove (3.03 vs. 3.90) and Action (3.95 vs. 4.51) while winning on Style (4.87 vs. 4.00) and Extract (2.64 vs. 2.44). On video stylization, ViBT outperforms TokenFlow, Ins.V2V, and RAVE on four of six perceptual/aesthetic metrics (TOPIQNR, MUSIQ, MANIQA, CLIPIQA) but is slightly behind Ins.V2V on CLIP Score (0.782 vs. 0.827) and marginally behind on NIQE. On depth-to-video, ViBT beats ControlVideo, Control-A-Video, and VideoComposer on nearly all metrics, and edges out Wan Fun Control (same Wan 2.1 base) on PSNR (11.403 vs. 10.899), DISTS (0.230 vs. 0.281), CLIP Score (0.781 vs. 0.776), and VBench Score (0.71 vs. 0.69), while being comparable on NIQE and TOPIQNR. The apples-to-apples comparison (Table 1) shows the Bridge paradigm outperforms Conditional DiT by 0.75 in average ImgEdit score (3.18 vs. 2.43) with 40% less training time (96 vs. 162 GPU-hrs) and 2.3× faster inference (6.2 s vs. 14.5 s).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Brownian Bridge diffusion formulation (BBDM, Li et al. 2023) from small-scale image translation to billion-parameter Transformer architectures, drawing on the velocity-matching objective from Flow Matching (Lipman et al.) and the variance-corrected Euler-Maruyama discretization from Stochastic Interpolants (Albergo et al.). The key methodological departure is the variance-stabilized velocity matching objective, which resolves the numerical divergence of vanilla velocity targets and the vanishing gradient of displacement targets that plagued prior Bridge training. Conceptually, the paper repositions conditional generation from the dominant noise-to-data paradigm (as in ControlNet, IP-Adapter, and conditional DiTs) to a data-to-data transport paradigm, arguing that the latter is more natural when source and target are already structurally similar.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is the 2.3× inference speedup from eliminating conditional token concatenation, combined with the demonstration that a 20B-parameter model can be converted to the Bridge paradigm with only 128 GPU-hours of LoRA post-training—making the paradigm shift accessible to practitioners with existing large models. Open questions remain: the noise scale parameter s requires task-specific tuning (s=0.5 optimal for editing, s=1–2 for video), and the Bridge adaptation still underperforms on object removal and large action edits, suggesting the data-to-data formulation has inherent limitations for edits requiring substantial structural change. The authors also point toward world and action models as a natural extension, where modeling state-to-state transitions is the native formulation.
