## Problem

Existing Generalized Category Discovery methods based on contrastive learning struggle with fine-grained categories where inter-class visual differences are subtle, as aggressive augmentations tend to overshadow the fine details that distinguish similar species or models. The paper addresses this by asking whether frequency-domain information—specifically the complementary roles of low and high Fourier components—can be exploited to simultaneously support broad generalization to novel categories and precise fine-grained discrimination.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262580/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on RGB images in a semi-supervised GCD setup: a small labeled set of known categories and a larger unlabeled set containing both known and novel categories, with half the total categories designated as known. Images are processed through a ViT-B/16 backbone (pre-trained with DINOv1 or DINOv2), and the Fourier filtering is applied at the pixel level before the backbone, with a Gaussian blur (kernel size 5) applied first to mitigate Gibbs ringing. The output is a class prediction over all categories (known and novel), with the number of novel categories either known a priori or estimated via the Vaze et al. technique.

## Method

The core mechanism is a dual-frequency self-supervision strategy: each image is Fourier-transformed, then split by a low-pass filter (cutoff f_l) and a high-pass filter (cutoff f_h) into two reconstructed views that serve as positive pairs in contrastive learning. The low-frequency view is contrasted using only the leftmost fraction f_l/T_l of the latent dimension, while the high-frequency view uses the rightmost fraction f_h/T_h, creating an implicit hierarchical representation where coarse structure occupies one half and fine detail the other. Cutoff thresholds T_l and T_h are determined dataset-dependently by computing the SNR across frequency bands and selecting the point where SNR reaches 15 dB. A binary cross-entropy classification loss is additionally applied to all three views (original, low-freq, high-freq) for labeled samples, and the total loss aggregates the baseline GCD loss with weighted low-frequency, high-frequency, and classification terms (α_low, α_high, α_cls).

## Results

On the three primary fine-grained datasets with a DINOv1 backbone, FourEx achieves 80.2% (All), 75.1% (Known), and 82.7% (Novel) on CUB-200; 65.9%, 67.9%, and 64.9% on FGVC-Aircraft; and 59.2%, 76.9%, and 50.6% on Stanford-Cars, yielding an average of 68.4% (All), 73.3% (Known), and 66.1% (Novel). With the stronger DINOv2 backbone, FourEx reaches 87.8% (All) on CUB-200, 81.5% (All) on FGVC-Aircraft, and 80.1% (All) on Stanford-Cars, averaging 83.1% (All), 88.4% (Known), and 80.6% (Novel). The method improves over its SelEx baseline by an average of +4.3% (All), +4.0% (Known), and +4.4% (Novel) with DINOv1, and by +0.0%, +1.4%, and −0.7% respectively with DINOv2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FourEx (DINOv1) achieves the best All and Novel accuracy on CUB-200 (80.2% and 82.7%) and FGVC-Aircraft (65.9% and 64.9%), outperforming the strongest prior methods DebGCD (66.3%/63.5% on CUB; 61.7%/60.6% on Aircraft) and MOS (69.6%/68.2% on CUB; 61.1%/58.2% on Aircraft) by 3.9–11.2 points. On Stanford-Cars, however, FourEx (59.2% All) trails DebGCD (65.3%), MOS (64.6%), and FlipClass (63.1%), which the authors attribute to the dataset being comparatively less fine-grained. With the DINOv2 backbone, FourEx leads on CUB-200 (87.8% All vs. SelEx 87.4%) and FGVC-Aircraft (81.5% vs. 79.8%) but falls slightly behind SelEx on Stanford-Cars (80.1% vs. 82.2%). The plug-and-play Fourier augmentation consistently improves both SimGCD (FourSim: +10.0% All on CUB over SimGCD) and SelEx (FourEx: +3.8% All on CUB over SelEx†), confirming the method's generality across different GCD baselines. On the estimated-category-count setting (Table 3), FourEx achieves 77.3% All on CUB-200, surpassing the next-best NC-GCD (70.3%) by 7.0 points, demonstrating robustness when the number of categories is unknown.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GCD framework established by Vaze et al. (CVPR 2022) and builds directly on two recent baselines—SimGCD (ICCV 2023) and SelEx (ECCV 2024)—by adding a Fourier-domain self-supervision module on top of their contrastive learning pipelines. Conceptually, it departs from the standard spatial-domain augmentation paradigm (random crops, color jitter) that dominates contrastive GCD, instead treating frequency-band filtering as a structured augmentation that encodes an implicit category hierarchy: low-pass views enforce coarse-grained generalization while high-pass views enforce fine-grained discrimination. The approach draws on the F-principle (Xu et al., 2019) and prior Fourier-based augmentation work (FourierMix, FDA), but repurposes these ideas for the specific challenge of distinguishing visually similar fine-grained categories in an open-world setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature of Fourier Self-Supervision makes it immediately applicable to existing GCD pipelines, and the consistent gains across five fine-grained datasets (including long-tailed Herbarium) demonstrate that frequency decomposition is a genuinely useful inductive bias for fine-grained recognition. However, the method's reliance on a dataset-specific SNR threshold (15 dB) and the assumption that category hierarchy aligns with frequency bands limits its generality; it remains unclear whether the same frequency split transfers across domains or modalities. Open questions include whether the dual-frequency strategy can be extended to video or 3D data, and whether the implicit hierarchy learned from low frequencies can be made explicit for interpretability.
