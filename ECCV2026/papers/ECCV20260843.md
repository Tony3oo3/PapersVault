## Problem

Existing physics-integrated neural rendering methods model mechanically driven dynamics (elasticity, fluids, weather) but neglect thermomechanical phase-change phenomena such as melting and solidification, where invisible temperature variations govern visually perceivable shape changes. Integrating these effects into Gaussian-based rendering is particularly challenging because thermomechanical coupling introduces extreme deformations and topology changes that cause cracking, floaters, and interior exposure in standard splatting pipelines.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260843/cropped_page0_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes posed RGB image sequences (from Mip-NeRF360, Tanks and Temples, or iPhone captures) to reconstruct a 3DGS scene, then accepts user-specified spatial heat-source regions and thermophysical parameters (thermal diffusivity, melting threshold, material properties) to drive the simulation. Beyond the rendered photorealistic video, the system outputs a temporally evolving per-pixel temperature field (rendered via the same alpha-compositing pipeline as color), deformed 3D geometry, and extractable high-quality meshes from the evolving Gaussian representation via IMLS implicit surfaces.

## Method

MeGAS augments each 3D Gaussian with a per-splat temperature attribute and couples the scene to a hybrid MPM-LBM simulator: a Lattice Boltzmann solver advances the heat advection-diffusion equation (with Boussinesq buoyancy for thermal convection) on a grid, and particle temperatures are transferred via P2G/G2P operations. When a particle's temperature exceeds a melting threshold, its constitutive model switches from StVK hyperelasticity (solid) to Herschel-Bulkley viscoplasticity (liquid), producing spatially localized, temperature-driven melting. To handle the resulting extreme topology changes, a topology-adaptive rendering strategy combines three components: (1) training-time regularization that prunes low-contribution internal Gaussians, enforces anisotropy loss, and clamps scale to produce a uniform, floater-free distribution; (2) ray-tracing-based internal filling that uniformly populates the object interior for volumetric MPM support; and (3) online adaptive surface densification that detects internal particles migrating into cracks, projects them onto an IMLS-fitted implicit surface, and reorients their Gaussian kernels with surface-aligned normals to seal emerging cracks.

## Results

The primary quantitative evaluation is a user study (Table 1) with rankings (best=4, worst=1) across three criteria. MeGAS achieves the highest scores on all three: Physical-Realism 3.731, Photo-Realism 3.654, and Multi-View Consistency 3.577, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline AutoVFX (2.692 / 2.154 / 2.385). The extreme-deformation comparison (Fig. 8) is qualitative, showing that MeGAS avoids the internal floaters, needle-like artifacts, and surface cracking that plague Original 3DGS, PhysGaussian, and VR-DoH under large-scale melting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MeGAS wins decisively against all three editing baselines in the user study. DGE [8], a text-driven diffusion editing method, scores lowest on Physical-Realism (1.538) because it cannot produce coherent dynamic melting behavior. AutoVFX [14], which uses an LLM to script mesh-based physics, achieves the best baseline score on Physical-Realism (2.692) but still trails MeGAS by over 1 point, and its decoupled geometry/shading pipeline causes non-photorealistic compositions. Runway Gen-4.5 [36], a commercial video generator, produces visually vivid but physically implausible motion with identity drift and poor multi-view consistency (1.885). For the extreme-deformation rendering comparison (Fig. 8), MeGAS is qualitatively superior to Original 3DGS [19], PhysGaussian [44], and VR-DoH [30]: all three baselines accumulate internal floaters, needle-like artifacts, and surface cracking under large melting deformations, whereas MeGAS's topology-adaptive strategy (anisotropy regularization, interior-free pruning, and IMLS-guided adaptive densification) maintains crack-free, volume-consistent geometry throughout.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the physics-integrated neural rendering line (PieNeRF, PhysGaussian, Gaussian Splashing) by introducing thermomechanical phase-change dynamics—specifically temperature-driven melting and solidification—into 3D Gaussian Splatting, a dimension prior works have entirely neglected in favor of purely mechanical or environmental effects. The deformation-to-Gaussian mapping follows PhysGaussian's affine push-forward, while the simulation backbone combines MPM (APIC) with a Lattice Boltzmann heat advection-diffusion solver and Boussinesq buoyancy, a coupling not previously explored in the 3DGS context. The topology-adaptive rendering strategy (anisotropy regularization, interior-free pruning, IMLS-guided adaptive densification) departs from standard 3DGS training by building on planar-based Gaussian Splatting and adding online surface-aware refinement specifically to handle the extreme topology changes that phase transitions induce.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MeGAS opens a previously unexplored axis of physics-integrated rendering—thermophysical editing with phase change—and provides a practical, extensible pipeline (MPM + LBM + 3DGS) that naturally generalizes to multi-object collisions, bidirectional phase transitions, and complex colliders. The topology-adaptive rendering strategy is a broadly applicable technique for any physics-integrated splatting method facing large deformations, not limited to melting. Key limitations remain: the framework does not model material-dependent appearance changes (e.g., PBR property shifts during melting), and the authors suggest coupling with video diffusion models as a promising but unrealized direction. Whether the thermomechanical formulation scales to more complex multi-phase materials (e.g., alloys, polymers with glass transitions) or to open-world scenes with heterogeneous thermal properties remains an open question.
