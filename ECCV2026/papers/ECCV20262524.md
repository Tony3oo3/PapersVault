## Problem

Existing transfer-based adversarial attacks optimize initialization, perturbation, and surrogate adaptation as isolated or heuristic factors, failing to capture their interaction dynamics. This fragmented treatment causes adversarial examples to overfit surrogate-specific artifacts rather than promoting cross-architecture transferability, and no unified optimization formulation has been proposed to systematically coordinate these interdependent variables.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262524/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

BMAT takes a clean RGB image, a white-box surrogate model (e.g., ResNet-50 for classification, FCN/DLV3-R50/Segformer for segmentation), a pseudo-surrogate (Inception-v3 or a Bayesian weight-sampled version of the surrogate), and the ground-truth label. It outputs a single adversarial example constrained to an ε-bounded ℓ_q norm ball. Notably, the surrogate's pretrained weights serve as hard anchors that are restored at each outer iteration, so only local soft-weight adaptation occurs within the inner loop without cross-batch drift.

## Method

BMAT casts transfer attacks as a bilevel-minimax problem: the inner minimax jointly adapts the perturbation φ and surrogate soft weights ω to surface robust, transferable gradients, while the outer level learns the initialization perturbation (IP) δ that seeds the attack trajectory. The Soft Weight Modulator (SWM) solves the inner problem with a single backward pass per step, co-updating φ and ω to flatten the surrogate loss landscape and cultivate universal gradient features. The Implicit Gradient Approximator (IGA) computes the outer hypergradient via a Fletcher-Reeves conjugate gradient solver on the Hessian-vector system, avoiding the prohibitive cost of unrolling the full inner trajectory. A damped Hessian (∇²f + ρI) ensures numerical stability, and the learned IP warm-starts a standard sign/projection attack for efficient black-box deployment against unknown victims.

## Results

On ImageNet classification (ResNet-50 surrogate, 10 victims spanning CNNs, ensembles, and Transformers), BMAT enhances 9 base attackers with an average ASR gain of 23.28% across 24 attacker–victim combinations. Under a normalized budget of 40 backward passes, BMAT achieves 15.03% average ASR versus 5.44% for RAP and 12.06% for BETAK, while using only 7.89 GB peak memory (vs. 22.69 GB for BETAK). On Cityscapes segmentation, BMAT reduces mIoU by up to 46.4% (ADE20K) and 43.7% (Cityscapes), with Segformer as surrogate yielding nearly 2× higher transferability than all baselines (e.g., 10.48 mIoU on FCN vs. 31.43 for PGD).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BMAT consistently outperforms all 17 baselines across both tasks. In classification, BMAT-enhanced MI achieves 42.45% avg CNN ASR vs. 31.22% for vanilla MI and 35.78% for GMI (initialization-based), while BMAT-enhanced VMI reaches 51.25% vs. 41.19% for vanilla VMI. Against surrogate-adaptation baselines, BMAT (21.84% on IncRes-V2) beats DRA (20.34%) and FAUG (21.70%) on the hardest CNN victim. Under normalized budgets (BP=40), BMAT (15.03% avg ASR) surpasses RAP (5.44%) by 2.8× and remains superior even when RAP's budget is increased 10× to BP=400 (9.68%); versus BETAK (12.06%), BMAT gains +2.97% ASR while using 65% less peak memory (7.89 vs. 22.69 GB). In segmentation on Cityscapes, BMAT with Segformer surrogate achieves 10.48 mIoU on FCN (vs. 31.43 for PGD, 24.09 for MI, 31.12 for EBAD), and with DLV3-R50 surrogate reaches 2.60 mIoU on FCN (vs. 7.55 for PGD, 5.57 for MI, 7.42 for EBAD). The single-surrogate zero-prior variant of BMAT still improves average ASR by 30.17% over PGD and 58.34% over PGD+MBA, confirming gains stem from internal bilevel coordination rather than ensemble diversity.

## Relation to Prior Work

BMAT builds on the standard PGD/VTA transfer attack paradigm but departs from it by recasting the three key variables—initialization perturbation, adversarial perturbation, and surrogate parameters—into a unified bilevel-minimax optimization rather than treating them as independently tuned or fixed. Conceptually, it extends the bilevel optimization literature from adversarial training (where inner/outer levels handle perturbation vs. model robustness) to the black-box transfer setting, and subsumes prior initialization-learning methods (BETAK, GMI) and surrogate-adaptation methods (DRA, FAUG) as special cases of its hierarchical formulation. The algorithmic design draws on implicit function theorem-based hypergradient computation and Fletcher-Reeves conjugate gradient to avoid the prohibitive cost of unrolling the inner minimax trajectory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, unified optimization framework that replaces the ad-hoc, factor-wise design choices prevalent in transfer attacks, offering a clear mechanistic explanation for why jointly coordinating initialization, perturbation, and surrogate adaptation improves cross-architecture generalization. The finding that merely increasing PGD iterations leads to overfitting while BMAT's trajectory-level optimization yields 40%+ gains under comparable budgets challenges the implicit assumption that stronger surrogate over-optimization translates to better transfer. Limitations include the extra computational overhead of SWM and IGA (roughly 2–3× runtime over plain PGD), and the authors note that lighter first-order variants remain an open direction. The framework's plug-in compatibility with diverse base attackers (MI, VMI, SGM, Ghost, etc.) makes it practically accessible, but whether the bilevel formulation generalizes beyond vision tasks or to query-based black-box settings remains unexplored.
