## Problem

Multimodal large language models achieve over 85% accuracy on standard action recognition benchmarks but collapse below 50% when asked to deny an action whose defining motion is absent, despite strong contextual cues (persons, objects, locations) being present. Existing video benchmarks contain almost exclusively positive examples, so models are never trained or evaluated on hard counterfactual negatives where the scene suggests an action but the motion does not occur, leaving a critical blind spot in causal video reasoning unaddressed.

## Input / Output

The evaluation uses a fixed 11-option multiple-choice VQA format with randomized option order (limiting random guessing to <10%). For Action-Denial clips, the correct answer is a 'None of the choices describes this video' option while the primary distractor is the target action label rewritten as a descriptive sentence; for Action-Presence clips, the correct answer is the target action itself. When CausalAct is applied, the model additionally receives a natural-language causal graph prompt specifying the dependency chain (context → spatial relation → interaction → motion → action) and is expected to produce a verification reasoning trace before selecting the MCQ answer.

## Method

The paper introduces UCF101-AD, a benchmark of 11,283 clips with paired positive and negative classes where negatives preserve the target action's context but remove its defining motion, split into Type 1 (context only, no motion) and Type 2 (same context, different motion). To improve denial, the authors propose CausalAct, a directed acyclic graph with contextual nodes (Persons, Objects, Location), relational nodes (Spatial Relation, Interaction), a dynamic node (Motion), and an action node, enforcing the dependency A ← f(I, M) ← f(I) ← f(P, O) to block shortcut paths like A ← L. CausalAct is operationalized as a structured natural-language prompt that guides the model to verify each causal link before predicting. For smaller models that cannot follow the prompt zero-shot, an auxiliary finetuning stage trains them on automatically generated graph-based VQA tasks (node identity, edge relationships, causal path queries, property consistency) derived from the graph structure without exposing ground-truth action labels, teaching the model to internalize the causal verification procedure and transfer it to action denial.

## Results

On UCF101-AD, the best-performing model (VideoLLaMA3-7B) achieves only 51.5% Overall Action-Denial accuracy, while most of the 20 evaluated MLLMs score between 20–35%. In stark contrast, Action-Presence accuracy is consistently high (85–97.6%), with the best model (Qwen2.5-VL-72B) reaching 97.6%. The Harmonic Mean between denial and presence accuracy peaks at 67.0 (VideoLLaMA3-7B), confirming that strong action recognition does not translate to robust denial. CausalAct-0 (zero-shot causal graph prompting) improves denial accuracy on external datasets by +7.4 to +23.1 points (e.g., HMDB51: 51.2→74.3, SSv2: 16.0→37.4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

VideoLLaMA3-7B is the clear leader on action denial (51.5% Overall-AD, 67.0 HM), outperforming the next-best Qwen2.5-VL-72B (45.7% Overall-AD, 62.3 HM) by ~6 points on denial and ~5 on HM. Among 7B-class models, Ovis2.5-9B (34.1% Overall-AD) and InternVL2.5-8B (31.6%) trail VideoLLaMA3-7B by 17–20 points, while Valley-Eagle-7B is the weakest at 11.1%. Reasoning/thinking variants generally underperform their non-thinking counterparts: Video-R1-7B (14.6% Overall-AD) and Lumian-VLR-7B-Thinking (10.3%) score far below their base models, and even Ovis2.5-9B-thinking (40.4%) only marginally improves over its standard version (34.1%). GPT-4o-mini (21.5% Overall-AD) performs comparably to mid-tier open-source 7B models, indicating that scale alone does not resolve the denial gap. CausalAct-0 applied to Ovis2.5-9B improves denial accuracy on external benchmarks by +7.4 (UCF101), +11.5 (K400), +23.1 (HMDB51), +21.4 (SSv2), +22.7 (Diving48), and +15.8 (FineGym), demonstrating that the causal graph prompting generalizes beyond the UCF101-AD benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of three research threads: (1) action recognition benchmarks that overwhelmingly contain only positive examples, (2) causal/scene-graph representations used for prediction and explanation in video understanding, and (3) the growing literature on LLM sycophancy and agreeableness bias. It departs from prior debiasing work (which weakens background/object correlations in positive-action regimes) by reframing the problem as explicit denial: verifying that a defining motion is absent rather than merely reducing spurious correlations. The CausalAct framework repurposes structured scene-graph prompting—previously used to improve multimodal video QA—into a verification mechanism that checks whether causal preconditions for an action are instantiated, rather than using the graph to predict or explain an action.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a fundamental and previously unmeasured blind spot: models that exceed 85% on standard action recognition collapse below 50% when the action is absent but context is preserved, with implications for safety-critical applications (surveillance, autonomous systems, sports analytics). The finding that explicit reasoning/thinking models perform *worse* than their non-thinking counterparts on denial is a cautionary signal for the broader 'add CoT to improve robustness' narrative. Open questions include whether CausalAct's gains transfer to open-vocabulary or long-tail settings, whether the sycophancy–shortcut-learning correlation is causal or merely correlational, and whether scaling alone (e.g., the 72B Qwen2.5-VL still scores only 45.7% on denial) can close the gap without structured causal guidance.
