## Problem

Existing open-set action recognition methods treat predictive uncertainty purely as an inference-time rejection score, leaving the learned representation unchanged and allowing spurious background or context cues to persist. This paper asks whether ensemble-derived epistemic uncertainty can be repurposed as an explicit training signal to refine representations and improve known-unknown separation, rather than being measured only after representation learning is complete.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262824/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At deployment, the method takes a single RGB video clip and produces two outputs from one forward pass: a class probability vector over the known action set and a scalar epistemic-uncertainty estimate from an auxiliary head, which is thresholded for known/unknown rejection. During training, optical flow (RAFT) and FlowI-SAM segmentations are computed offline as a weak motion prior for Stage 1, but no flow, Grad-CAM, or ensemble inference is required at test time. The attention masks in Stage 3 are recomputed at the start of each epoch from the current ensemble and kept fixed during that epoch's updates.

## Method

The framework trains a deep ensemble (M=10, Kinetics-400 pretrained) through three sequential refinement stages. Stage 1 aligns the classifier's Grad-CAM saliency with a FlowI-SAM motion prior via an MSE loss, biasing initial representations toward action-relevant evidence. Stage 2 regularizes the ensemble's epistemic uncertainty on in-distribution training samples, reducing ensemble disagreement without requiring unknown data. Stage 3 (UAM-IA) backpropagates epistemic uncertainty to the input to obtain attribution maps, converts them into a smooth residual attention mask A(x)=1+α(1−M(x)) that amplifies low-attribution regions, and fine-tunes each ensemble member on the attended input. After refinement, the ensemble is distilled into a single student in two phases: classification distillation matching the ensemble mean distribution, then epistemic-uncertainty regression with log-scale MSE, pairwise ranking, and moment-matching losses, enabling single-pass deployment at ~39 ms versus ~323 ms for the full ensemble.

## Results

On the TPN backbone (the strongest setting), the distilled student achieves 93.10 AUROC and 78.04 open maF1 on UCF101+MiT-v2, and 88.50 AUROC and 88.97 open maF1 on UCF101+HMDB51, with 95.98% closed-set accuracy. Across all four backbones (TPN, TSM, SlowFast, I3D), the method attains the best or near-best open-set metrics, with SlowFast reaching 95.75 AUROC (MiT-v2) and 97.33% accuracy. In the matched deep-ensemble comparison (Table 2), the three-stage refinement improves AUROC by 2.5–4.7 points over a vanilla 10-member ensemble on every backbone, confirming that the gains come from the training strategy rather than ensembling alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares six baselines (SoftMax entropy, OpenMax, MC-Dropout, BNN-SVI, DEAR, and SOAR) across four backbones. On TPN, the proposed method dominates all baselines on every metric for both unknown sets, achieving 93.10 AUROC vs. SOAR's 91.45 (MiT-v2) and 88.50 vs. 86.67 (HMDB51), with a 3.4-point open maF1 gain over SOAR on MiT-v2. On SlowFast, the method matches or exceeds SOAR on MiT-v2 (95.75 vs. 95.72 AUROC) and achieves the best open maF1 (80.82 vs. 76.47), though SOAR retains a slight AUROC edge on HMDB51 (90.72 vs. 88.38). On TSM, SOAR edges out the proposed method on AUROC for HMDB51 (85.96 vs. 84.24), but the proposed method leads on TPR@10 (78.84 vs. 65.98) and closed-set accuracy (95.55% vs. 92.49%). On I3D, SOAR achieves the highest AUROC for both unknown sets (94.60/88.10), but the proposed method still delivers the best FPR@95 on MiT-v2 (24.50 vs. 25.33) and competitive open maF1. Overall, the method is strongest on TPN and SlowFast, with the largest margins over the nearest competitor (SOAR) appearing in open maF1 and FPR@95, while the matched-ensemble ablation (Table 2) confirms that the 2.5–4.7-point AUROC gains over a vanilla ensemble are attributable to the three-stage refinement rather than ensemble size alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al.) for epistemic uncertainty estimation and gradient-based uncertainty attribution (Wang et al., CVPR 2023) for localizing uncertainty to input regions, but departs from prior OSAR work by repurposing uncertainty as a training-time refinement signal rather than an inference-time rejection score. Where evidential methods (DEAR, MULE) and scene-debiasing approaches (SOAR) address open-set recognition through scoring or reconstruction, this paper introduces a staged fine-tuning pipeline—motion-guided initialization, in-distribution epistemic regularization, and UAM-IA—that actively reshapes the representation using ensemble-derived uncertainty. The distillation strategy extends standard knowledge distillation (Hinton et al.) by adding an auxiliary epistemic-uncertainty regression head, going beyond matching only the predictive distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core conceptual contribution—using epistemic uncertainty not just to reject unknowns but to guide where and how the model should attend during fine-tuning—offers a principled path toward reducing spurious context reliance in action recognition without requiring external semantic priors or vision-language supervision. The practical distillation to a single RGB student (~8× speedup over the 10-member ensemble) makes the approach deployable, which is important for real-world OSAR systems. Key limitations include the offline training cost of ensemble refinement and attribution recomputation each epoch, evaluation restricted to UCF101 as the known set with CNN backbones only, and the open question of whether the staged refinement transfers to transformer or foundation-model video backbones at scale.
