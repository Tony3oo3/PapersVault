## Problem

Controllable video generation methods typically rely on task-specific 2D signals (skeletons, optical flow, depth maps) that require bespoke data pipelines and model designs for each control type, limiting scalability. Even unified appearance-motion decomposition frameworks suffer from first-frame dependency, fixed control granularity, geometric aliasing between adjacent elements, and lack of explicit depth information, making them unable to handle diverse tasks like camera control and spatial object editing within a single model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261188/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The appearance condition is a partially masked video where unedited regions retain original pixels and edited regions are filled with gray (127), paired with a binary mask that delineates target areas and enables distinct denoising timesteps for edited versus preserved regions. The motion condition is an 18-channel temporal signal rendered from a 3D point cloud, organized as six RGB-like streams (one identity, four multi-frequency, one depth-aware) that are partitioned and encoded by the same pre-trained VAE as the appearance. Point cloud density is variable, allowing the user to trade off structural precision against generative flexibility depending on the task.

## Method

FlexAM disentangles video into appearance (masked video + binary mask) and motion (3D dynamic point cloud rendered as an 18-channel motion video), injecting both into a pre-trained Wan2.2Fun 5B DiT backbone. The motion signal encodes each point's initial 3D coordinates via an identity stream and four multi-frequency positional encodings (cos(2^l·π·v), l∈{0,1,2,3}) to resolve geometric aliasing between adjacent elements, plus a time-varying depth stream mapped through the Spectral colormap to resolve depth ambiguity. The six RGB-like motion streams are encoded by the shared VAE, fused by a lightweight CNN Adapter, and a density embedding (analogous to the diffusion timestep embedding) is injected to inform the network of the current point density. Training employs stochastic downsampling of the point cloud so the model learns to operate across the full sparse-to-dense spectrum, enabling a single model to handle I2V/V2V editing, camera trajectory control, and spatial object manipulation.

## Results

On the motion transfer task (Table 1), FlexAM achieves the highest text-video alignment (32.55 CLIP score), temporal consistency (0.976), subject/background fidelity (0.99/0.99), and aesthetic quality (0.64) among all methods. On I2V camera control over 100 RealEstate10K sequences, FlexAM attains the lowest rotation error, while remaining competitive on translation error without specialized pose modules. For spatial object editing, FlexAM achieves a CLIP score of 0.9536, surpassing DaS (0.9437) and GeoDiffuser (0.9110). In a 27-participant user study on partial editing, FlexAM is preferred over VACE in lighting consistency (56% vs. 25%) and unedited-region preservation (51% vs. 28%), while reducing inference time from 348 s to 91 s.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DaS [12], FlexAM wins on every metric in Table 1 (e.g., Tex-Ali 32.55 vs. 32.14, aes_ql 0.64 vs. 0.60) and also outperforms it qualitatively on V2V camera re-rendering and spatial object editing (CLIP 0.9536 vs. 0.9437). Against Wan2.2Fun Control [1], FlexAM leads on text alignment, temporal consistency, subject fidelity, and aesthetics, while Wan2.2Fun retains a marginal edge on I2V translation error due to its explicit pose-conditioned supervision. Against VACE [18], FlexAM shows a large gap in subject/background consistency (0.99 vs. 0.86) and aesthetic quality (0.64 vs. 0.57), and is consistently preferred in the user study. Against ReCamMaster [2], FlexAM produces fewer rendering artifacts and better trajectory adherence in V2V camera re-rendering. Against GeoDiffuser [31], FlexAM achieves a higher CLIP score (0.9536 vs. 0.9110) on spatial object manipulation, reflecting better semantic alignment and occlusion reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

FlexAM directly extends DaS (Diffusion as Shader), which first proposed 3D point clouds as a motion control signal for video diffusion, by enriching the point attributes with multi-frequency positional encoding, time-varying depth channels, and stochastic density variation. It departs from the broader controllable-video-generation literature (VACE, MotionCtrl, CameraCtrl, ReCamMaster) that relies on task-specific 2D signals—depth maps, skeletons, optical flow, or camera poses—by arguing that a single 3D-aware motion video can subsume all of these. The appearance side also moves beyond first-frame conditioning (as in Tora, MagicMotion) to accept arbitrarily masked video, unifying I2V and V2V editing under one interface. Architecturally, the method plugs into the Wan2.2Fun 5B DiT backbone via a lightweight CNN adapter and shared VAE encoding, avoiding a bespoke motion encoder.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value lies in replacing a multi-condition pipeline (depth + skeleton + mask + pose) with a single 18-channel motion video, simplifying data preparation and enabling a 5B model to match or beat 14B baselines at roughly 4× lower inference cost. Open questions remain around robustness: the method inherits errors from upstream 3D tracking (DELTA), degrades under severe geometric mismatch between source motion and target appearance, and shows occasional lighting inconsistencies due to limited training-data diversity. Scaling to more diverse lighting conditions and longer sequences, and reducing dependence on accurate per-point 3D trajectories, are natural next steps.
