## Problem

Real-world object removal must simultaneously handle non-local visual effects (shadows, reflections), tolerate imperfect user-provided masks, and run fast enough for interactive or edge deployment. Existing diffusion-based methods achieve strong perceptual quality but require dozens of denoising steps, making them impractical for real-time use, while faster GAN-based approaches lack the representational capacity for effect-aware and mask-robust results. No prior method addresses all three requirements—efficiency, effect-awareness, and mask-robustness—at once.

## Input / Output

OSOR takes an RGB image (up to 1024×1024) and a user-provided binary removal mask as input, along with a fixed text prompt ('Remove the instance of object'). In Phase II, the conditioning mask may be intentionally incomplete (e.g., a tight object-core mask that excludes shadow regions), and the model additionally outputs a soft alpha map at latent resolution that defines the effective editing region for adaptive blending. The final output is a single object-removed image produced in one forward pass, with 1024×1024 processing completing in under one second on a single A100 GPU.

## Method

OSOR formulates object removal as one-step latent restoration: the input image is encoded to a VAE latent, weakly noised to an intermediate timestep (t=400), and a single forward pass through a pretrained diffusion inpainting backbone (SDXL-Inpainting or FLUX Fill) predicts the clean latent, which is decoded to the output image. For boundary-consistent single-step training, an occupancy-guided multi-scale PatchGAN discriminator computes fractional mask occupancy via area pooling at each patch location across four scales, providing exact soft targets that avoid the over-confidence of hard downsampling or the bandwidth sensitivity of Gaussian smoothing. A lightweight alpha head—implemented as an expanded terminal output projection—predicts a soft alpha map in the same forward pass, enabling alpha compositing in latent space instead of hard blending so the model can modify regions beyond the provided mask. Training proceeds in two phases: Phase I adapts the backbone with perfect effect-aware masks and hard blending, then Phase II introduces incomplete-mask conditioning (tight object-core masks plus geometric perturbations) and the alpha head, with adversarial and reconstruction losses always evaluated on the full effect-aware target to prevent degenerate alpha shrinking. The training data (CORNE, 280K pairs) is curated by a semantic-anchored verification pipeline (SAVP) that filters noisy instruction-based triplets using multi-feature difference heatmaps, GroundingDINO semantic matching, and SAM2 segmentation fusion to produce effect-aware masks.

## Results

On CORNE-Val (the paper's own 280K-pair dataset), OSOR(FLUX) achieves the best scores across all six metrics under both object-only and effect-aware masks: FID 12.32, CMMD 0.0073, LPIPS 0.0462, PSNR 32.03 dB, SSIM 0.9373, and CFD 0.1538, while running in 0.80 s per image. On AnimeEraseBench, OSOR(FLUX) leads with FID 26.84, LPIPS 0.1070, PSNR 27.78 dB, and SSIM 0.8859, outperforming the second-best method (ObjectClear) by 2.24 dB in PSNR. Inference latency is 0.42–1.06 s on an A100 GPU, yielding 4×–30× speedup over multi-step diffusion baselines (5–26 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

OSOR(FLUX) decisively outperforms all seven baselines on CORNE-Val and AnimeEraseBench across every reported metric (FID, CMMD, LPIPS, PSNR, SSIM, CFD), with the largest gaps over multi-step diffusion backbones (FLUX-Fill, SDXL-INP) which suffer FID values 4–7× higher. Among the specialized removal methods, ObjectClear is the closest competitor on CORNE-Val (FID 22.05 vs. 12.32) and AnimeEraseBench (FID 36.69 vs. 26.84), but OSOR still leads by 9.7 and 9.9 FID points respectively. OmniPaint achieves the lowest FID among non-OSOR methods on CORNE-Val (18.61) but is 32× slower (26.12 s vs. 0.80 s). On RORD-Val, OSOR(FLUX) remains competitive (FID 27.36) while OmniPaint (27.57) and ObjectClear (25.47) are close, though OSOR is still 14–40× faster. The key differentiator is the combination of sub-second latency with effect-aware quality: baselines that remove shadows well (ObjectClear, OmniPaint) require 6–26 s, while fast methods (CLIPAway, SDXL-INP at ~6 s) sacrifice perceptual quality (LPIPS 0.19–0.24 vs. OSOR's 0.05–0.11).

## Relation to Prior Work

OSOR sits at the intersection of adversarial diffusion distillation (Sauer et al., ECCV 2024; Yin et al., CVPR 2024) and mask-conditioned inpainting, adapting the one-step generation paradigm to the specific constraints of object removal. It departs from prior one-step distillation work by introducing a task-specific occupancy-guided multi-scale discriminator that provides fractional boundary supervision, and by formulating removal as latent restoration from a weakly noised input rather than pure inpainting. The alpha head for mask correction draws on the matting and alpha-compositing literature (Levin et al., Porter & Duff), repurposing soft opacity maps to handle imperfect user masks in a diffusion setting. For data, the SAVP pipeline over noisy instruction-based triplets (NHR-Edit) represents a verification-driven approach to dataset curation that complements, rather than replaces, the paired-video and counterfactual data strategies used by OmniEraser and ObjectDrop.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The sub-second inference on a single A100 for 1024×1024 images makes effect-aware object removal viable for interactive and edge-device applications, a regime previously inaccessible to diffusion-based methods. The release of CORNE (280K verified pairs) and the AnimeEraseBench/TextEraseBench benchmarks addresses a real gap in evaluation for domain-specific removal scenarios. Open questions include whether the two-phase training curriculum is strictly necessary or whether a single unified phase could suffice, whether the occupancy-guided discriminator generalizes to other one-step editing tasks beyond removal, and how the approach scales to video or 3D content where temporal consistency of removed effects becomes critical.
