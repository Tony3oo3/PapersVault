## Problem

Existing forgery localization methods, including recent SAM-based approaches, struggle with cross-domain generalization and robustness to common corruptions. SAM-based pipelines either depend on labor-intensive manual prompts or reduce SAM to a single final embedding, overlooking hierarchical cues and the task-specific manipulation information that SAM's natural-image pretraining does not provide.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262158/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image resized to 256×256 and produces a pixel-level binary forgery localization mask. The mask is generated through six sequential refinement stages, each supervised with BCE loss against the ground-truth mask, so intermediate predictions are also trained. No external prompts (points, boxes, or masks) are required at inference; the initial mask is produced prompt-free from the fine-tuned SAM embeddings alone.

## Method

SARIF pairs a frozen SAM ViT-L encoder with a LoRA-adapted counterpart (rank 32) and computes block-wise residual features at global-attention blocks {5, 11, 17, 23} and the final embedding to distill forgery-specific cues. The Forgery-Specific Information Extractor (FSIE) fuses cosine-similarity maps, channel-attention-weighted concatenated features, and a lightweight convolutional block with skip connections to produce compact task-specific prompts at each stage. The Feedback-Guided Mask Decoder (FGMD) then iteratively refines the mask: the previous prediction is encoded by SAM's prompt encoder, element-wise summed with the FSIE output to form a task-specific prompt, and fed into the shared-weight mask decoder alongside the fine-tuned image embedding. This coarse-to-fine feedback loop progressively sharpens boundaries and suppresses spurious regions while preserving SAM's strong generalization, and the entire pipeline is trained end-to-end with deep supervision at all six refinement stages.

## Results

Trained on CASIAv2 and evaluated on seven unseen datasets, SARIF achieves the best seen-domain DSC of 63.1 and mIoU of 56.7, outperforming the next-best M2SFormer (58.8 DSC / 50.8 mIoU) and SAFIRE (58.0 DSC / 45.5 mIoU). On unseen domains, SARIF leads on CoMoFoD (65.4 DSC, 55.8 mIoU) and Columbia (58.4 DSC, 49.7 mIoU), while SAFIRE remains stronger on DIS25k (50.7 DSC), IMD2020 (53.1 DSC / 41.1 mIoU), and In the Wild (63.3 DSC / 51.0 mIoU), and M2SFormer is best on MISD (69.1 DSC / 56.9 mIoU). On the more recent CocoGlide and TGIF benchmarks, SARIF achieves 34.0 DSC / 27.3 mIoU and 30.8 DSC / 23.7 mIoU respectively, surpassing all SAM-based baselines by a wide margin.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SARIF substantially outperforms all CNN-based baselines (UNet, MantraNet, RRUNet, TransForensic, FBINet, MT-SENet, MVSSNet, PIMNet) across every dataset, with the largest gaps on CoMoFoD (65.4 vs. 24.7 DSC for PIMNet) and In the Wild (55.7 vs. 31.2 DSC for PIMNet). Among Transformer-based methods, SARIF beats EITLNet on all datasets and edges out M2SFormer on six of seven unseen benchmarks, though M2SFormer retains a narrow lead on MISD (69.1 vs. 66.2 DSC). Among SAM-based baselines, SARIF clearly surpasses vanilla SAM, AutoSAM, and IMDPrompter on every metric and dataset. The closest competitor is SAFIRE, which wins on DIS25k (50.7 vs. 47.8 DSC), IMD2020 (53.1 vs. 48.4 DSC), and In the Wild (63.3 vs. 55.7 DSC), as well as on IMD2020 and In the Wild for mIoU; however, SARIF dominates SAFIRE on CoMoFoD (65.4 vs. 39.5 DSC) and MISD (66.2 vs. 48.3 DSC), yielding the strongest overall average across all seven unseen domains.

## Relation to Prior Work

SARIF builds directly on the Segment Anything Model (SAM) as its backbone, extending the line of SAM-based forgery localization methods (IMDPrompter, SAMIF, SAFIRE) that have adapted SAM's promptable architecture to this task. It departs from these prior SAM-based approaches in two key ways: (1) instead of relying on manual prompts or a single final embedding, it introduces a dual-encoder design (frozen SAM + LoRA-adapted SAM) that extracts block-wise residual cues at global-attention layers to capture forgery-specific information, and (2) it replaces single-pass decoding with a feedback-guided iterative mask refinement loop that reuses previous predictions as prompts. The LoRA adaptation strategy is borrowed from parameter-efficient fine-tuning in NLP, and the dual-encoder residual concept draws inspiration from opponent-channel models in biological vision.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SARIF addresses two persistent weaknesses in forgery localization—cross-domain generalization and corruption robustness—by leveraging SAM's broad pretraining while injecting task-specific cues through a lightweight dual-encoder mechanism, all without requiring manual prompts. However, the method's computational cost is substantial (998.43 FLOPs, 47.5 ms inference) compared to efficient transformer baselines like M2SFormer (15.16 FLOPs, 2.5 ms), raising open questions about whether the accuracy gains justify the ~66× FLOPs overhead in deployment settings. The paper also acknowledges that the feedback loop can amplify errors when the initial mask is incorrect, and that SAM's prompt encoder introduces blocky artifacts, both of which limit practical reliability. Future work could explore reducing the number of refinement stages, lighter adapter configurations, or hybrid approaches that combine SAM's generalization with more efficient decoders.
