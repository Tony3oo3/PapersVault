## Problem

Reliable localization of non-line-of-sight (NLOS) pedestrians in outdoor urban driving environments remains highly challenging because ego-vehicle motion makes radar multipath propagation complex and noisy, and existing NLOS perception methods predominantly assume static sensing setups, predefined geometric layouts, or controlled indoor conditions. No prior approach performs learning-based reflection-type point extraction and reflective surface estimation while being validated under ego-dynamic outdoor driving with a moving vehicle-mounted radar-camera platform.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261639/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a synchronized front-view RGB image and a temporal stack of K past 2D radar point clouds (each point carrying BEV coordinates, Doppler velocity, and RCS), where the radar frames are ego-motion compensated and aligned to the current coordinate frame before encoding. It produces two learned intermediate representations—per-point reflection-type labels (first/second/third-order, object-originated vs. surface-originated) and a binary reflective surface probability heatmap in BEV space—which are then fed into a deterministic physics-guided ray tracing module that outputs the final NLOS pedestrian position as a set of spatially merged candidate locations.

## Method

The framework has two stages. In the reflection-aware representation learning stage, an LSS-based encoder projects the camera image into a BEV feature map, a radar encoder encodes the ego-motion-compensated temporal PCD stack into a matching BEV grid, and a transformer cross-attention module fuses them with radar features as queries and camera features as keys/values; two heads then predict point-wise reflection-type logits (trained with cross-entropy) and a reflective surface heatmap (trained with binary cross-entropy against pseudo-labels generated by anisotropic Gaussian splatting of first-order surface reflections from temporally aggregated radar). In the physics-guided ray tracing stage, DBSCAN clusters the predicted first- and third-order points, candidate reflector positions are searched along rays from the radar origin to third-order cluster centers using the surface heatmap for likelihood scoring, and the NLOS pedestrian position is recovered via geometric mirroring of the third-order cluster center across the estimated planar reflector. The key insight is that data-driven decomposition of multipath signals into physically interpretable reflection orders, combined with learned reflective surface geometry, enables structured ray tracing without requiring prior scene structure, achieving sub-meter NLOS localization under ego-dynamic conditions.

## Results

The proposed framework achieves an average localization error of 0.44 m (LOS) and 1.01 m (NLOS) under ego-static conditions, and 0.54 m (LOS) and 1.23 m (NLOS) under ego-dynamic conditions. For reflection-type point segmentation, it reaches 94.6% accuracy and 0.915 macro-F1 (ego-static) and 89.9% accuracy and 0.814 macro-F1 (ego-dynamic). Reflective surface estimation yields sub-meter reflection point errors (0.28–0.42 m) and angle errors below 7.3° across all configurations. The system runs at 13.85 FPS (72 ms end-to-end) and detects NLOS pedestrians 0.77 s earlier than first-order radar observations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Scheiner et al. [27] (radar+LiDAR geometry-based), the proposed method reduces NLOS localization error from 2.36 m to 1.01 m under ego-static conditions (57% reduction) and from 4.74 m to 1.23 m under ego-dynamic conditions (74% reduction). Against Park et al. [24] (camera-assisted radar with heuristic point interpretation), NLOS error drops from 1.69 m to 1.01 m (40% improvement) statically and from 3.23 m to 1.23 m (62% improvement) dynamically. For reflection-type point segmentation, the proposed method outperforms Kraus et al. [14] (0.946 vs. 0.686 accuracy, ego-static) and Wu et al. [34] (0.946 vs. 0.930 accuracy, ego-static), with the gap widening under ego-dynamic conditions where Wu et al. degrades sharply (0.610 accuracy) while the proposed method retains 0.899. The proposed method wins on every reported metric across all four baselines, with the largest gains in NLOS dynamic localization where prior geometry-based and heuristic methods struggle most.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in radar-based NLOS perception, where prior methods (e.g., [10, 37]) rely on predefined wall/corner geometry and rule-based reflection modeling validated in static or indoor settings. It also extends camera-radar fusion work [6, 20, 35], which targets LOS object detection, by explicitly modeling multipath reflection order and reflective surface geometry. The key conceptual move is replacing hand-crafted geometric assumptions with a learned reflection-type segmentation and surface estimation pipeline, then coupling it with a deterministic physics-guided ray tracing step — a hybrid that no prior NLOS radar-camera system has attempted under ego-dynamic outdoor conditions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that NLOS pedestrian localization can remain sub-meter (1.23 m) even when the ego-vehicle is moving, a setting where prior geometry-based methods degrade to 3–5 m errors. This is practically significant for urban autonomous driving, where 84% of pedestrian fatalities occur in structurally occluded areas. However, validation is confined to a single T-junction testbed with at most three pedestrians and a 2D 77 GHz radar, leaving open whether the framework generalizes to denser urban scenes, higher-order reflections beyond third bounce, or 4D radar sensors. The finding that reflective surface estimation accuracy is a strong bottleneck (localization error spikes when surface prediction degrades) suggests future work should focus on more robust surface inference in cluttered, non-linear-wall environments.
