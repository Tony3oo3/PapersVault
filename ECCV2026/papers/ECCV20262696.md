## Problem

Existing reward models for multimodal reasoning RL face a fundamental trade-off: preference-based models cannot reliably judge correctness of long, complex reasoning responses, while rule-based verifiers fail on semantically equivalent answers (e.g., 'one week' vs. '7 days') and enforce rigid formatting that discards large fractions of valid RL data. No prior approach provides a reward signal that is simultaneously semantically flexible, accurate on hard reasoning, and scalable to large RL training pipelines.

## Input / Output

The CRM consumes only text inputs—a question, a candidate response, and a ground-truth answer—deliberately excluding the image, which ablation confirms yields higher accuracy than image-conditioned variants. It outputs a short Chain-of-Thought comparison followed by one of two special tokens (<|+|> for Consistent, <|-|> for Inconsistent), from which two reward forms are extracted: a hard binary reward (±1) and a soft continuous reward equal to P(Consistent) − P(Inconsistent), ranging in [−1, 1]. The soft reward captures partial consistency (e.g., mixed correct/incorrect elements), providing a denser optimization signal than binary labels.

## Method

The key insight is reframing reward as a consistency judgment—comparing whether the candidate answer is semantically and mathematically equivalent to the ground truth—rather than as an absolute correctness assessment, which is far simpler for a small model. The model (Qwen2.5-Instruct, 7B or 1.5B) is trained as a generative verifier that produces a three-step CoT (Analysis → Reflection → Conclusion) before emitting a judgment token, with rewards derived from the token logits. Training data comprises 860K question–response pairs from 13 LLMs/VLMs, annotated by triple-judge agreement (GPT-4o plus two independent Doubao-1.5-vision CoT runs), balanced 1:1 between consistent and inconsistent labels. For RL deployment, the authors use GRPO with a reject-sampling filter that discards samples whose normalized advantage sign contradicts the hard reward, preventing policy updates that oppose the reward model's judgment and stabilizing soft-reward training. This design eliminates the need for strict answer-format verifiability, enabling direct use of 220K raw multimodal data (4× prior rule-based pipelines) and supporting stable RL up to 72B policy models.

## Results

On the primary RL training results (Table 4), CRM-RL-7B with soft rewards achieves 75.2% on MathVista, 57.1% on MathVerse, 30.6% on MathVision, and 70.5% on WeMath (avg 58.4%), outperforming the rule-based baseline (55.2% avg) by 3.2 points. Scaling to 72B, CRM-RL-72B reaches 76.5% MathVista and 41.7% MathVision (avg 63.4%), surpassing the base Qwen-2.5-VL-72B (60.7% avg). As a static judge, CRM-7B achieves 98.0% validation accuracy and 88.40% average on VerifyBench-Hard, and a 77.8% win rate over VLMEvalKit across MathVista, MathVision, and MathVerse.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against rule-based RL (MathRuler), CRM-RL-7B (soft) gains +2.8% on MathVista (75.2% vs 72.4%) and +2.0% on MathVision (30.6% vs 28.6%), and the rule-based baseline collapses after ~200 training steps while CRM sustains improvement to 1200 steps (Table 5). Against xVerify-RL-7B, CRM wins on all four benchmarks (e.g., MathVerse 57.1% vs 53.5%, WeMath 70.5% vs 67.8%). Against CompassVerifier-RL-7B, CRM again leads on every metric (MathVerse 57.1% vs 54.8%, WeMath 70.5% vs 68.6%). As a static judge, CRM-7B outperforms GPT-4o-mini (98.0% vs 96.9% val acc), xVerify (98.0% vs 93.2%), and achieves 88.40% avg on VerifyBench-Hard versus CompassVerifier's 85.90% and xVerify's 83.10%. CRM also achieves a 77.8% average win rate over VLMEvalKit's GPT-4o-mini + string-matching pipeline, with particularly large margins on MathVision (97.0% win rate for Qwen-2.5-VL-7B responses). On zero-shot generalization, CRM-7B still outperforms VLMEvalKit on OCRBench (66.7% win rate) and MMMU (82.2% win rate) despite no OCR/MMMU data in training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two established reward-modeling paradigms for RL: preference-based models (Bradley-Terry / RLHF) that judge absolute response quality, and rule-based verifiers (MathRuler, SymPy, sandbox execution) that enforce strict symbolic matching. Rather than building on either, it reframes the reward signal as a *consistency judgment*—a generative CoT comparison between the candidate answer and the ground truth—drawing on the LLM-as-judge tradition (xVerify, CompassVerifier) but simplifying the task from 'is this correct?' to 'is this equivalent to the reference?'. The continuous soft reward derived from judgment-token logits, combined with a reject-sampling filter for GRPO stability, is a methodological contribution not present in prior verifier work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: by removing the need for strict answer-format verifiability, CRM enables 4× more RL training data and sustains stable optimization where rule-based rewards collapse after ~200 steps, directly addressing a key bottleneck in scaling multimodal RL. The finding that text-only consistency judgment outperforms image-conditioned variants suggests the reward model's role is fundamentally linguistic, which simplifies deployment. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to truly open-ended generation without GPT-4o-derived checklists, whether the 860K annotation pipeline (relying on GPT-4o + Doubao agreement) introduces systematic biases, and whether the stability advantage persists at even larger scales or with noisier reward distributions.
