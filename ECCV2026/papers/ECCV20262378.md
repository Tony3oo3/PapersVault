## Problem

Existing video generation benchmarks evaluate physical commonsense in everyday scenarios (falling objects, mirror reflections) that require only basic or high-school-level knowledge, leaving untested whether video models can reason across multiple interacting scientific concepts at an undergraduate level. The paper asks: can video generation models serve as zero-shot scientific reasoners that correctly portray complex phenomena requiring synthesis of at least two physics or chemistry principles?

## Input / Output

The benchmark provides 160 text-to-video prompts and 40 image-to-video items (paraphrased prompts paired with a first-frame image of the experimental setup), each describing a controlled scientific experiment lasting ≤10 seconds. The evaluation pipeline consumes the generated video, the original prompt, and (for I2V) a reference video, then produces per-dimension ordinal scores on a 4-point scale (1=absent/contradictory to 4=clearly correct) across five criteria, aggregated into a single weighted score where Phenomenon Congruency carries the largest weight (0.3) as the ultimate scientific outcome.

## Method

VideoScience-Judge is a checklist-based VLM-as-a-Judge framework that grounds automated scoring in concrete visual evidence. An LLM agent first generates a prompt-specific checklist of expected phenomena (validated at 95.6% accuracy against ground truth), and the VLM judge (GPT-5 mini) selects key frames where checklist violations occur. Deterministic CV modules—Grounding DINO for entity detection, ByteTrack for cross-frame identity association, RAFT optical flow for motion quantification, CLIP4Clip for text-video alignment, and LPIPS for perceptual similarity—produce compact JSON evidence records that the VLM must cite before assigning deductions. Ratings are derived reductively: the fraction of satisfied checklist items per category maps to a 4-point score, and the five dimension scores are combined with fixed weights (0.3/0.2/0.2/0.15/0.15) into an aggregate. This evidence-grounded design reduces the score inflation and false-positive rates observed with direct VLM prompting, achieving Kendall's τ=0.90 and Spearman's ρ=0.96 correlation with expert human annotations.

## Results

On VideoScience-Bench, the checklist-augmented VSci-Judge (CL and CL+CV variants) achieves the strongest alignment with human expert annotations, reaching Kendall's τ = 0.90 and Spearman's ρ = 0.96, substantially outperforming all baseline evaluation frameworks. The base VSci-Judge (without checklist) still leads with τ = 0.78 and ρ = 0.86. Among the seven video models evaluated, Sora-2 and Veo-3 rank highest on human-annotated Phenomenon Congruency (~64% and ~58.7% on a 4-point Likert scale), while the best open-source model (Wan-2.5) reaches only ~46%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VSci-Judge (CL+CV) decisively outperforms all three baselines on both ranking correlation metrics. Against PhyGenEval (τ = 0.62, ρ = 0.82), VSci-Judge gains +0.28 in τ and +0.14 in ρ; the paper attributes PhyGenEval's weaker performance to its rigid three-tier binary judgment framework that cannot capture multi-concept cascading dynamics. Against VideoScore2 (τ = 0.45, ρ = 0.53), the gap is even larger (+0.45 in τ, +0.43 in ρ), as VideoScore2's physical-consistency dimension was designed for broad commonsense violations rather than deep scientific reasoning. Against T2V-CompBench (τ = 0.62, ρ = 0.71), VSci-Judge improves by +0.28 in τ and +0.25 in ρ, with the paper noting that T2V-CompBench's VLM component struggles to capture nuanced physics violations that VSci-Judge's evidence-grounded checklist approach handles. Notably, even the base VSci-Judge without checklist or CV augmentation (τ = 0.78, ρ = 0.86) already surpasses all three baselines, indicating that the scientific-reasoning-specific prompt design alone provides substantial gains over general-purpose video evaluation frameworks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of physical-commonsense video benchmarks (PhyGenBench, VideoPhy, Physion, IntPhys) by escalating from single-principle, everyday scenarios to multi-concept, undergraduate-level scientific reasoning spanning 103 physics and chemistry concepts. On the evaluation side, it builds on the VLM-as-a-Judge paradigm established by Video-Bench, EvalCrafter, and T2V-CompBench, but departs from them by introducing a prompt-specific checklist grading scheme and deterministic CV evidence modules (Grounding DINO, ByteTrack, RAFT, CLIP4Clip) to ground the VLM's judgments in verifiable frame-level signals rather than holistic visual impressions. The paper explicitly positions itself against VideoScore2 and PhyGenEval, arguing that their rule-based or general-purpose physical-consistency checks cannot capture the compositional, multi-law reasoning that VideoScience-Bench demands.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

VideoScience-Bench is the first benchmark to treat video generation models as zero-shot scientific reasoners rather than mere visual generators, and its findings—that even Sora-2 and Veo-3 achieve only ~64% and ~59% on Phenomenon Congruency, and that models exhibit a systematic 'setup vs. execution' bottleneck—provide a concrete diagnostic for where current world-modeling falls short. The 'illusion of coherence' observation (e.g., Kling-v2.5 scoring 4.00 on Spatio-Temporal Coherence while scoring 1.04 on Phenomenon Congruency) challenges the assumption that visually smooth, artifact-free video implies physical correctness, with implications for how the community should weight evaluation dimensions. Open questions include whether the benchmark's 160-item scale is sufficient for reliable per-topic conclusions, whether the VLM-as-a-Judge framework generalizes to longer or more complex multi-step experiments, and whether the observed chemistry-over-physics advantage reflects a fundamental limitation in spatial-geometric reasoning or simply a data-coverage gap in training corpora.
