## Problem

Existing privacy-preserving visual localization (PPVL) methods each use different evaluation protocols and make independent privacy claims, leaving no unified benchmark to compare their actual vulnerability to inversion attacks. The prior feed-forward inversion approach (FFConv) struggles with sparse, noisy, or information-poor representations, making it inadequate for assessing modern PPVL methods. This paper asks: what sensitive information can an adversary actually recover from each family of PPVL representations, and how do they rank under a shared, more powerful attack?

## Input / Output

The inversion model takes a privacy-preserving representation as conditioning—either a set of sparse keypoints with descriptors (SIFT, SuperPoint, XFeat), a dense segmentation mask (DPT, SAM2, SegLoc, GSFF, PpNeSF), or obfuscated descriptors (AASE, NinjaDesc)—alongside a random noise tensor as the diffusion starting point. For geometric obfuscation methods (RandLine, CoordPerm), a deobfuscation step first recovers approximate keypoint positions (with 5–10 px median error) before feeding them to the model. The output is a full RGB image reconstructed in latent space, enabling direct visual and quantitative comparison against the ground-truth query image.

## Method

The attack fine-tunes a latent diffusion model (Stable Diffusion) via a multi-modal LoRA scheme that uses a single set of low-rank weights per representation group (sparse features vs. dense segmentations), modulated per-representation by learned scale and bias parameters predicted from a small MLP—allowing one model to invert multiple representation types with minimal overhead. Conditioning is applied at two levels: locally, by concatenating the privacy-preserving representation to the noisy latent along the channel dimension, and globally, by encoding keypoint positions or mask centroids into sub-window graphs processed by a GNN, whose embeddings are injected through cross-attention layers. Training minimizes the standard noise-prediction MSE over the diffusion schedule on over 2 million images from MegaDepth and ScanNet-v2. The paper further introduces a Reconstructed Diversity (RD) metric that measures pairwise SSIM/LPIPS across reconstructions from the same conditioning but different noise seeds, capturing probabilistic uncertainty as a privacy proxy, alongside VLM-based semantic metrics (Scene Recognition, Object Recovery, Texture Recovery) that go beyond purely perceptual scores.

## Results

On Cambridge Landmarks, the proposed diffusion-based inversion model consistently outperforms all feed-forward baselines across every conditioning type. For SegLoc conditioning, it achieves SSIM 0.40 / LPIPS 0.39 / FID 91, versus 0.37/0.51/230 (FFConv), 0.35/0.51/245 (FFTrans), and 0.37/0.47/189 (FFUnetSA). On the VLM-based semantic metrics, the diffusion model recovers substantially more scene content (e.g., OR 0.48 vs. 0.35–0.38 and TR 0.47 vs. 0.10–0.21 for SegLoc), confirming it extracts fine-grained private details that feed-forward models miss. The advantage persists under noisy deobfuscated keypoints and at higher sparsity (500 kpts/img), where SSIM drops from 0.49 to 0.41 for the diffusion model but from 0.45 to 0.38 for FFConv.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The diffusion-based inversion model decisively outperforms the FFConv baseline [35] on all three perceptual metrics (SSIM, LPIPS, FID) and all three VLM-based semantic metrics (SR, OR, TR) for every conditioning type tested; for example, on CoordPerm(SIFT) it achieves FID 76 vs. 160 and TR 0.46 vs. 0.10. The two additional feed-forward baselines (FFTrans, FFUnetSA) are also consistently outperformed, with FFUnetSA being the closest competitor on SSIM but still trailing by 0.03–0.05 and yielding FID values roughly 2× higher. Among the PPVL targets evaluated in Table 2, segmentation-based methods (GSFF, PpNeSF) are the most privacy-preserving, showing the highest reconstruction diversity (RD(L) 0.34–0.46) and lowest VLM recovery scores (OR 0.30–0.38, TR 0.23–0.37), while geometric obfuscation methods (RandLine, CoordPerm) and descriptor obfuscation methods (AASE, NinjaDesc) leak substantially more information, with AASE(SP) achieving the worst privacy (LPIPS 0.27, OR 0.44–0.49). The paper also demonstrates that the diffusion model is more robust to noisy keypoint positions (5–10 px median error from deobfuscation) than FFConv, which degrades more sharply under the same noise. Overall, the benchmark reveals a clear privacy hierarchy: segmentations > geometric obfuscation > descriptor obfuscation > raw sparse descriptors, with only a modest localization accuracy cost (MTE 0.14–0.22 m vs. 0.07–0.12 m) for the more private segmentation-based approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the latent diffusion model framework (Stable Diffusion) and extends LoRA with a multi-modal modulation scheme to create a unified inversion attack across heterogeneous representation types (sparse descriptors, segmentations, obfuscated features). It departs from the prior feedforward inversion approach of Pittaluga et al. [35], which struggled with sparse and noisy inputs, by leveraging the generative flexibility of diffusion models with dual-scale conditioning (local channel concatenation + global GNN cross-attention). Conceptually, it also extends the privacy evaluation paradigm from isolated per-method assessments to a shared benchmark protocol that jointly measures perceptual fidelity, semantic recovery via VLMs, and probabilistic reconstruction diversity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first unified benchmark comparing geometric obfuscation, descriptor obfuscation, and segmentation-based PPVL methods under a single attack protocol, providing practitioners with actionable guidance for choosing representations in AR/VR and autonomous deployment. The finding that segmentation-based methods offer substantially stronger privacy than geometric obfuscation with only a modest localization accuracy drop challenges the prevailing assumption that hiding keypoint positions is sufficient protection. Open questions include the vulnerability of 3D scene representations (explicitly deferred), the context-dependence of what constitutes a privacy breach, and whether the diffusion-based attack generalizes to even sparser or more heavily obfuscated representations.
