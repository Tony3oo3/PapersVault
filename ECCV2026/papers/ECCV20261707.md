## Problem

Existing adversarial attack detection methods typically require attack-specific assumptions, white-box access to the target classifier, or training on adversarial examples, limiting their generalizability in real-world settings where both the attack mechanism and the model architecture are unknown. This paper tackles the highly challenging zero-shot, attack-agnostic, and classifier-agnostic detection setting, which had not been addressed in prior work.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261707/cropped_page2_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

At test time, the method takes a single RGB image and produces a scalar detection score (higher values indicate greater likelihood of adversarial perturbation). During a one-time setup phase, it requires approximately 200 clean images from the target dataset solely to estimate the PCA aggregation direction—no adversarial examples, no model weights, and no fine-tuning are needed. The fixed prompt dictionary of 10 hand-crafted text captions is pre-computed and shared across all datasets and classifiers.

## Method

The core insight is that CLIP embeddings shift in a structured, non-arbitrary way under adversarial perturbations, and this shift can be characterized using text prompts in CLIP's shared image-text space. For each input image, the method computes cosine similarities between its CLIP image embedding and 10 fixed text prompts describing noise and perturbation characteristics, yielding an N-dimensional similarity vector. This vector is standardized using statistics estimated from a small clean validation set, then projected onto the first principal component of the clean set's similarity matrix to produce a single detection score. The PCA-based aggregation is chosen over simpler pooling (mean, min, max) because it captures the dominant variance direction that best separates clean from adversarial samples. The entire pipeline requires only a single CLIP forward pass per image plus lightweight linear algebra on a 10-dimensional vector, enabling real-time inference at ~9 ms per image.

## Results

A4D achieves average AU-ROC of 92.53 (Wide-ResNet), 93.32 (ResNet34), 92.47 (DeiT-Small), and 84.97 (ConvNeXt-Tiny) across seven attack types on Tiny-ImageNet, outperforming all attack-agnostic baselines on every architecture. On the three architectures where it is best, A4D exceeds the strongest attack-agnostic baseline (Wavelet) by 14–22 AU-ROC points. Inference requires only 0.0091 s per image on a single RTX 3090, enabling real-time detection. The method also generalizes to the StreetSurfaceVis dataset (published after CLIP's training data), confirming that results are not an artifact of dataset overlap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the Mahalanobis-distance detector of Lee et al. [16], evaluated in both an attack-aware variant (tuned per attack) and an attack-agnostic variant (Mahalanobis*). In the fair attack-agnostic comparison, A4D outperforms Mahalanobis* by 25.4, 54.4, 37.5, and 1.5 AU-ROC points on Wide-ResNet, ResNet34, DeiT-Small, and ConvNeXt-Tiny respectively, with the largest gap on ResNet34 where Mahalanobis* collapses to 38.92 average. A4D also surpasses the attack-aware Mahalanobis on three of four architectures (by 15.6, 14.4, and 28.8 points), losing only on ConvNeXt-Tiny (84.97 vs. 88.85). The two simpler noise-statistics baselines (MAD and Wavelet) are consistently weaker, with Wavelet being the strongest attack-agnostic competitor at 72.86–78.36 average AU-ROC, still trailing A4D by 6–22 points. Notably, A4D's advantage is most pronounced on CW and DeepFool attacks, where all baselines drop to near-chance (~50 AU-ROC) while A4D retains 62–86 AU-ROC, suggesting the CLIP prompt-based signal captures structured, edge-aligned perturbations that pixel-statistics methods miss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes CLIP's image-text alignment mechanism—previously applied to anomaly detection (AnomalyCLIP, AdaCLIP, AA-CLIP)—for the distinct task of adversarial perturbation detection. It departs from the dominant adversarial detection literature, which typically requires attack-specific assumptions, white-box classifier access, or supervised training on adversarial examples, by operating entirely in CLIP's embedding space with only a small set of clean validation images. The key conceptual departure is treating CLIP not merely as a semantic encoder but as a sensitive probe to non-semantic, low-level perturbations, where the directional shift in embedding space is captured by a fixed dictionary of noise-related text prompts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a practical, near-training-free detection pipeline (single forward pass + lightweight PCA projection, ~9 ms/image) that requires no knowledge of the attack or the target classifier, addressing a realistic deployment gap in adversarial defense. However, performance degrades notably for edge-aligned attacks (CW, DeepFool at ~77% AU-ROC vs. ~99% for noise-like attacks), and prompt selection remains manual and dataset-dependent, limiting generalizability. Open questions include developing principled, automated prompt selection, extending the approach to distribution-shifted or out-of-domain test data, and understanding whether the directional-shift property holds for future vision-language models beyond CLIP.
