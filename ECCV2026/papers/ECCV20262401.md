## Problem

Most sign language translation (SLT) methods are confined to a single sign-spoken language pair, and existing multilingual SLT approaches struggle to learn a unified representation that minimizes cross-lingual interference while capturing shared semantics and preserving language-specific signing variations. Forcing multiple sign languages into a single continuous visual embedding space causes negative transfer, where language-specific patterns corrupt the shared manifold and undermine robust multilingual modeling.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262401/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes sign pose sequences of up to 256 frames, each frame containing 79 2D keypoints (21 per hand, 11 body, 26 face), along with a language prompt specifying source sign language and target spoken language (e.g., 'Translate <DGS> to German'). The pose stream is first segmented into variable-length segments (max 32 frames) and quantized into discrete Q-unit tokens; the LLM then autoregressively generates spoken-language text in any of three target languages (German, English, Chinese) from the Q-unit sequence, enabling many-to-many translation from a single model.

## Method

Q-BridgeNet decomposes sign representations via a shared-private residual vector quantization (RQ-VAE) scheme: a shared base codebook (1,024 embeddings) captures language-agnostic signing primitives at the first quantization layer, while language-specific residual codebooks (512 embeddings each for ASL/CSL/DGS) refine heterogeneous signing semantics in subsequent layers. Temporal segmentation is solved jointly with quantization via block-coordinate descent—alternating between codebook updates and dynamic-programming-based boundary refinement—so that variable-length segments align with semantic units rather than fixed temporal windows. A pretrained Qwen3-1.7B LLM is then fine-tuned with LoRA to operate directly in the discrete Q-unit space, trained on a multilingual many-to-many dataset where GPT-5 translates each native caption into the other two spoken languages, yielding a single unified model for all sign-spoken language combinations.

## Results

Q-BridgeNet achieves state-of-the-art BLEU-4 scores on all three native sign-spoken pairs: 16.44 on How2Sign (ASL→EN), 26.33 on CSL-Daily (CSL→ZH), and 33.40 on PHOENIX14T (DGS→DE), also leading on BLEU-1 and ROUGE across all benchmarks. The multilingual model consistently outperforms its monolingual counterpart (e.g., 33.40 vs. 30.16 BLEU-4 on PHOENIX14T), confirming that cross-lingual supervision regularizes the discrete Q-unit space. On non-native pairs, the model approaches native-pair accuracy (e.g., ASL→ZH reaches 16.83 BLEU-4 vs. 16.55 for ASL→EN), demonstrating strong many-to-many generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Q-BridgeNet (multilingual) outperforms all 12 baselines on every reported metric across all three datasets. On PHOENIX14T, it surpasses the strongest prior method SCOPE [21] by 0.56 BLEU-4 (33.40 vs. 32.84) and 0.82 ROUGE (61.39 vs. 60.06), while beating CV-SLT [42] by 4.13 BLEU-4. On CSL-Daily, it leads MSKA [9] by 0.81 BLEU-4 (26.33 vs. 25.52) and Uni-Sign [18] by 0.72 BLEU-4. On How2Sign, it edges out ShuBert [10] by 0.24 BLEU-4 (16.44 vs. 16.20) and Uni-Sign [18] by 1.94 BLEU-4. Notably, the multilingual model also beats its own monolingual ablation on all three native pairs (e.g., +3.24 BLEU-4 on PHOENIX14T), indicating that cross-lingual supervision acts as a regularizer rather than a source of interference. The gains are consistent across BLEU-4, BLEU-1, and ROUGE, suggesting improvements in both long-range n-gram accuracy and token-level precision rather than a single-metric artifact.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of gloss-free SLT methods (SL-Transformers, TwoStream, GloFE, MMSLT) from single sign-spoken pairs to a many-to-many multilingual setting, and builds on recent multilingual SLT efforts (Uni-Sign, MGSLT, IMSLT) that rely on continuous visual embeddings. Its key methodological departure is the introduction of a shared-private residual vector quantization scheme with adaptive variable-length segmentation, where a language-agnostic base codebook captures cross-lingual signing primitives and language-specific residual codebooks refine local variation—addressing the cross-lingual interference problem that prior continuous-representation approaches leave unresolved. The LLM (Qwen3-1.7B) is fine-tuned to operate directly in the discrete Q-unit space rather than on continuous pose embeddings, following the broader trend of LLM-based SLT but with a structured discrete interface.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a practical path toward unified accessibility across diverse sign and spoken language communities, and the finding that non-native spoken targets sometimes outperform native ones (e.g., ASL→zh exceeding ASL→en) suggests that shared discrete sign representations can recover semantics underrepresented in native captions. Open questions include whether the approach scales beyond three sign languages, whether the GPT-5-generated cross-lingual captions introduce systematic biases that limit true cross-lingual evaluation, and whether the Q-unit space can support the reverse direction (sign language production) as the authors suggest. The reliance on a single LLM backbone and the modest dataset sizes (8K–35K clips) leave room for validation at larger scale.
