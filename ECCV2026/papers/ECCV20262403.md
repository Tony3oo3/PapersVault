## Problem

In-context learning (ICL) is widely used to adapt large vision-language models to downstream tasks, yet its effect on gender bias remains unexplored. It is unclear whether the gender composition of ICL demonstrations shifts model bias, whether similarity-based retrieval methods offer any debiasing advantage, or whether standard caption quality metrics can detect such bias shifts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262403/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a query image, a sequence of k in-context image-text demonstration pairs whose gender composition is systematically controlled (male-only, female-only, balanced, random, or similarity-retrieved), and a task-specific instruction prompt. Outputs are free-form generated captions, next-token pronoun probabilities (his vs. her), or VQA answers, from which gender is inferred via keyword matching or token probability comparison. Validation sets are gender-balanced while training pools preserve original gender distributions, and VQA test queries are textually neutralized to isolate ICL context effects from explicit gender cues.

## Method

VL-BICLE is a systematic evaluation pipeline that varies six ICL settings (random, male-only, female-only, balanced, image-image similarity retrieval, image-text similarity retrieval) across three tasks (image captioning, pronoun prediction, VQA) and four datasets, evaluated on six LVLMs at k ∈ {0,2,4,6,8} shots. The key empirical finding is a cross-gender mechanism: gendered ICL demonstrations disproportionately degrade performance on the opposite gender (e.g., male-only context raises female error rates while male error rates stay flat), acting as a directional force that can flip a model's baseline bias entirely. Similarity-based retrieval methods inherit the source pool's gender imbalance and offer no debiasing advantage over random selection, while BLEU-4 and CLIPScore remain stable even as ER_m-f shifts substantially. For mitigation, the paper replaces real in-context demonstration images with synthetic ones generated by FLUX or Stable Diffusion 3.5-Large from the same captions, which reduces |ER_m-f| on revealed samples without degrading caption quality, confirming that visual content of context images—not just text—drives gender bias in ICL.

## Results

Across six LVLMs and four datasets, zero-shot gender bias (ER_m-f) ranges from −45.36 (MiniCPM on VisoGender-OP) to +35.91 (Phi35V on VisoGender-OP), showing large model- and task-dependent variation. Gendered ICL acts as a directional force: male-only (MS) context shifts ER_m-f negative while female-only (FS) shifts it positive, with the cross-gender mechanism producing extreme gaps (e.g., ER_f = 85.5 under MS vs. 9.7 under FS for QwenVL on VisoGender-OP at 8-shot). Standard caption quality metrics (BLEU-4, CLIPScore) remain stable (max cross-setting CLIPScore range: 0.67) even as bias shifts substantially. Replacing real in-context images with FLUX or SD35L synthetic images reduces |ER_m-f| on revealed samples for 3 of 4 models under both MS and FS, without degrading CLIPScore (differences < 0.5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two similarity-based retrieval (SBR) baselines—SIIR (image-image cosine similarity, Li et al., CVPR 2024) and SITR (image-text cosine similarity, Yang et al., NeurIPS 2024)—against gender-controlled ICL settings (RS, MS, FS, BS) to test whether retrieval-based example selection offers a debiasing advantage. Neither SBR method outperforms male-only (MS) ICL on ER_m-f: at 8-shot on COCOBias, Phi35V under SIIR and SITR yields ER_m-f of −2.25 and −2.35 respectively, showing *more* male bias than MS (−1.88 ± 1.03). This occurs because the COCOBias training pool is male-skewed (538 M vs. 267 F), so top-k similarity retrieval over-selects male examples. Even when the retrieval pool is down-sampled to be gender-balanced, SIIR and SITR still underperform the balanced-sample (BS) setting for QwenVL, Idefics3, and Phi35V, confirming that the retrieval mechanism itself can reintroduce bias. Overall, SBR methods are neither more nor less biased than each other, and both fail to provide any consistent debiasing benefit over simple random or gender-controlled sampling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: gender bias evaluation in vision-language models (Hendricks et al. 2018, Zhao et al., Howard et al. 2024) and in-context learning for LVLMs (Flamingo, MiniGPT-4, Lever LM). It departs from prior bias-mitigation efforts that target VLMs like CLIP or rely on post-hoc logit calibration, instead investigating how the *composition* of ICL demonstrations itself acts as a bias-shifting mechanism in LVLMs. The authors explicitly position VL-BICLE as the first systematic study of ICL's effect on gender bias in LVLMs, repurposing similarity-based retrieval methods (Li et al. 2024, Yang et al. 2024) as baselines rather than as the primary contribution. The mitigation strategy—replacing real demonstration images with SDM-generated ones—repurposes diffusion models in a novel role: not as generators for training data, but as a debiasing intervention on the ICL context itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that gendered ICL demonstrations act as a directional force that can flip a model's baseline bias is practically critical for anyone deploying LVLMs with few-shot prompting, as it means the choice of context examples is not neutral with respect to fairness. The observation that standard quality metrics (BLEU-4, CLIPScore) remain blind to these bias shifts warns against relying on caption quality as a proxy for equitable behavior. Open questions include whether the cross-gender mechanism generalizes to other protected attributes (race, age), whether it persists in larger or differently-architected models, and why the effect is entirely absent in VQA—suggesting a deeper coupling between output-space format and bias propagation that remains unexplained. The simple synthetic-image mitigation, while effective, has only been validated on two SDMs and one captioning dataset, leaving its robustness across tasks and model scales untested.
