## Problem

Long-video language models reduce videos to discretized frames or clips and rely on the LLM to implicitly infer temporal structure, which fails on queries requiring computable temporal quantities such as counting repeated events, comparing rates, estimating intervals, or determining order. The paper argues that time should be modeled as a continuous, queryable process rather than treated as a discrete index attached to tokens, so that temporal reasoning becomes explicit computation rather than heuristic retrieval or free-form summarization.

## Input / Output

The method takes a video spanning a continuous time horizon [0,T] and a natural-language query, operating under a strict non-generative setting with a fixed frame budget (F=64 sampled frames) and a separate, decoupled event budget (N=32 proposed events). The video encoder produces dense timestamped features that are compressed into a sparse set of continuous-time events; the output is a textual answer conditioned on a compact structured evidence vector z (a few scalars or small vectors encoding counts, probabilities, or time estimates) serialized into a fixed schema, keeping the context footprint minimal.

## Method

CTEPM represents each video as a marked temporal point process with type-specific conditional intensities that capture both global temporal trends (via Fourier-feature MLPs) and cross-event triggering/inhibition (via a Hawkes-style interaction matrix with a mixture-of-exponentials kernel). An event proposal module converts dense video features into N sparse events using a differentiable Gumbel-Top-N relaxation on learned eventness scores, with semantic marks obtained by local attention pooling. A lightweight query parser maps the question to a temporal operator (count, rate, order, interval, or duration), which is executed on the learned process via Monte Carlo quadrature or Ogata thinning to produce structured evidence. Training combines the marked point-process log-likelihood, QA cross-entropy, and proposal sparsity regularization, with only LoRA adapters trained on a frozen LLaVA-NeXT-Video-7B backbone.

## Results

On LongVideoBench (64-frame budget), CTEPM achieves 49.2% Top-1 accuracy overall, with the largest gain on very long videos (900–3600 s) at 45.6% versus 41.0% for uniform sampling (+4.6 pts). On Video-MME (w/ subtitles), CTEPM reaches 55.0% overall and 49.9% on long videos (30–60 min), outperforming uniform sampling by +2.5 and +3.4 pts respectively. On MLVU, CTEPM attains 37.8% M-Avg with particularly strong gains on temporally compositional sub-tasks: Action Order 21.5% (+5.5 over uniform) and Action Count 27.8% (+6.8 over uniform). On MVBench operator-aligned subsets, CTEPM yields roughly 9–10 point improvements on Count and ~7 points on Interval/Duration queries.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CTEPM consistently outperforms all baselines under matched 64-frame budgets across all three long-video benchmarks. Against uniform sampling, CTEPM gains +3.2 pts on LongVideoBench, +2.5 on Video-MME, and +3.8 on MLVU; against segment retrieval the margins are +2.4, +2.1, and +3.2 respectively; and against FastVID token pruning the margins are +2.7, +2.3, and +3.5. Notably, CTEPM with only 64 frames surpasses the 128-frame uniform sampling baseline by +2.1 pts on LongVideoBench, indicating that process-level temporal computation is more sample-efficient than simply doubling the input. Compared to the MA-LMM-style streaming memory bank (same 64-frame cap), CTEPM improves by +1.7 pts overall and +3.5 pts on the >30 min subset of LongVideoBench, and by +2.9 pts M-Avg on MLVU, with the largest gap on Action Count (+5.8 pts). On MVBench operator-aligned tasks, CTEPM achieves roughly 9–10 point gains on Count and ~7 points on Interval/Duration over uniform sampling, retrieval, and FastVID, confirming that the explicit temporal operators (expected counts, waiting-time estimates) are the primary source of improvement rather than heavier sampling or retrieval.

## Relation to Prior Work

CTEPM sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines: continuous-time temporal point processes (Hawkes processes, RMTPP, Neural Hawkes, Transformer Hawkes) and long-video language models (VideoChat, LLaVA-Video, MA-LMM, LongVA). The key departure is representational: rather than treating time as a discrete index over sampled frames or clips and leaving temporal aggregation to the LLM, CTEPM models the video as a marked point process with learnable type-specific intensities and executes explicit temporal operators (count, rate, order, interval, duration) on that process. The closest prior work—event-centric memories like Video-EM and EventMemAgent—organize events as episodic records for retrieval, whereas CTEPM parameterizes event occurrence and cross-event triggering/inhibition with a probabilistic process, making temporal quantities directly computable rather than inferred by a language model over retrieved context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a compact continuous-time process (N=32 events) can outperform substantially larger frame budgets (e.g., MA-LMM at 1000 frames on MLVU), suggesting that explicit process-level temporal computation is more sample-efficient than scaling input length for compositional temporal queries. Open questions include how the approach scales to truly hour-long or multi-hour videos where event proposal quality may degrade, whether the discrete event-type assumption (K types) is sufficient for open-vocabulary scenarios, and how sensitive the operator outputs are to the quality of the initial event proposal. The framework is also limited to a single backbone family in the main experiments, and the operator library, while clean, covers a relatively narrow set of temporal query types.
