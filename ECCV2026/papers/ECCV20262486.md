## Problem

Learning-based NLOS reconstruction methods rely on dense 3D volumetric backbones with O(N³) computational complexity, making high-resolution reconstruction memory-prohibitive. Physics-based operators like the light-cone transform provide principled inversions but are brittle under real-world noise and geometric complexity. The paper addresses the gap by asking whether a physics-guided coarse initialization can be combined with a scalable learned refinement stage that avoids full 3D processing.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262486/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a time-resolved transient tensor T ∈ R^{τ×H×W} (128×128 spatial pixels, 512 time bins at 32 ps resolution) captured by a SPAD array in a confocal NLOS setup, with Gaussian and Poisson noise injected during training. It produces a 3D volumetric reconstruction V ∈ R^{D×H×W} and, via a lightweight rendering head, 2D intensity and depth maps. The 3D volume is never processed end-to-end by a 3D network; instead it is decomposed into three 2D triplane views, processed independently, and recombined, which is the key structural constraint on how the volume is consumed.

## Method

TriNLOS is a hybrid physics-ML pipeline with three stages. First, a learnable Enhanced Light-Cone Transform (ELCT) — a differentiable refinement of the classical LCT with only three learnable parameters (depth exponent α, gate scale s, gate power p) — produces a physically consistent coarse 3D volume via depth re-parameterization, cone-aligned frequency-domain sharpening, and an inverse Wiener filter. Second, this volume is projected onto three orthogonal 2D planes via axis-wise max-projection (which suppresses diffuse background), and each plane is encoded by a lightweight 2D backbone combining ConvNeXt-style residual convolutions and Restormer channel-attention, reducing volumetric complexity from O(N³) to O(N²). Third, a slice-wise axis cross-attention mechanism exchanges geometric cues between planes along their shared axes, and a voxel-wise confidence-weighted fusion recombines the three lifted volumes with the ELCT initialization through a shallow 3D refinement head. Training uses an L₁ composite loss on rendered intensity and depth, with gradients from the depth branch explicitly blocked at the volumetric argmax/max operators to prevent depth supervision from flattening intensity contrast.

## Results

On synthetic confocal NLOS transients, TriNLOS achieves the best reconstruction quality across all metrics: PSNR of 30.26 dB (Seen) and 26.13 dB (Unseen), SSIM of 0.958/0.911, depth RMSE of 0.054/0.087, and depth MAD of 0.011/0.025. Among learning-based methods, it uses the least memory (8.0 GB vs. 12.2 GB for LFE and NLOST) while running in 103.22 ms—faster than NLOST (165.29 ms) but slower than LFE (48.80 ms). On real-world data, TriNLOS preserves fine peripheral structures and distant objects that degrade in NLOST and LFE, and outperforms all physics-based baselines (ELCT, LCT, FK, Phasor) in both sharpness and noise robustness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TriNLOS outperforms all six baselines on every reconstruction metric. Against the strongest learning-based competitor NLOST [20], it gains +1.49 dB PSNR (Seen) and +0.48 dB (Unseen), improves SSIM by 0.012/0.026, and reduces depth RMSE from 0.063 to 0.054 (Seen) while using 35% less memory (8.0 vs. 12.2 GB) and running ~37% faster (103 vs. 165 ms). Versus LFE [6], the gains are larger: +2.84 dB PSNR (Seen), +0.064 SSIM, and depth RMSE drops from 0.066 to 0.054, though at roughly 2.1× the runtime. Among physics-based methods, FK [23] is the strongest baseline (PSNR 26.24/24.96, SSIM 0.874/0.816), but TriNLOS exceeds it by +4.02 dB PSNR and +0.084 SSIM on Seen data. The ELCT initializer alone (the physics-guided component of TriNLOS) already surpasses LCT [35] in PSNR on both Seen and Unseen splits, and the full TriNLOS pipeline further lifts it to 30.26 dB. On non-confocal data, TriNLOS (with RSD [25] replacing ELCT) achieves 27.92/28.12 dB PSNR versus RSD's 24.97/25.33 dB, a gap of ~3 dB, confirming generalization beyond the confocal setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines two previously separate lines of work: the physics-based Light-Cone Transform (LCT) for confocal NLOS inversion, which the authors extend into a learnable Enhanced Light-Cone Transform (ELCT) with depth re-parameterization and frequency-domain sharpening, and the triplane factorization originally introduced for efficient 3D GANs (Chan et al., CVPR 2022), which is repurposed here to reduce volumetric backbone complexity from O(N³) to O(N²). The hybrid design—physics-guided coarse initialization followed by a triplane refinement network with ConvNeXt-style blocks, Restormer attention, and axis cross-attention—departs from prior learning-based NLOS methods (LFE, NLOST) that rely on dense 3D or transformer backbones without explicit physical priors, and from purely physics-based methods that lack learned refinement. The triplane representation had been applied to 3D generation, medical imaging, and neural fields, but its use for NLOS reconstruction is new to this paper.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in demonstrating that a structured 2D factorization can match or exceed dense 3D volumetric processing for NLOS reconstruction while cutting memory usage by roughly 35% (8.0 GB vs. 12.2 GB), making high-resolution NLOS more accessible on consumer-grade hardware. The no-gradient depth branch is a small but non-obvious training insight: blocking depth gradients from the shared backbone stabilizes intensity reconstruction, suggesting that coupled multi-task supervision in volumetric settings can be counterproductive. Limitations include the confocal-only primary evaluation, a runtime that is still ~2× slower than LFE due to sequential triplane processing, and reliance on synthetic ShapeNet training for the main quantitative results. Open questions include whether the triplane approach scales to truly view-consistent 3D NLOS with partial occlusions, and whether the sequential triplane bottleneck can be parallelized without sacrificing the cross-axis attention quality.
