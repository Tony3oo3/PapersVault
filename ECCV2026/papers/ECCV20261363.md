## Problem

Approximate machine unlearning methods consistently underperform exact retraining, yet the role of the initial learning strategy in determining unlearning difficulty has been largely unexplored. This paper asks whether a theoretically grounded training scheme can produce 'unlearning-compatible' models that are inherently easier to unlearn, by identifying and minimizing the loss-landscape sharpness that governs both the cost of reversing SGD and the amount of subject-specific knowledge retained.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261363/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard supervised training data (RGB images with class labels) and model parameters, but during each training step it additionally computes the full gradient of the empirical loss and its l1 norm, requiring a double backpropagation pass (one forward + two backward passes vs. one forward + one backward for standard training). The output is a trained model (CNN, ViT, or DDPM) that produces class labels or generated images, but whose parameter trajectory is flattened so that any downstream unlearning procedure (fine-tuning, gradient ascent, weight sparsity, etc.) closes the gap to exact retraining more effectively. Peak memory increases by roughly 1.2–2.0× due to retained autograd state for the second backward pass.

## Method

The paper proves that among sharpness definitions over l1, l2, and l∞ perturbation norms, the l∞-perturbation sharpness is the worst-case and its first-order approximation is ρ‖∇ℓ(θ)‖₁, the l1 norm of the gradient (a gradient-sparsity measure). A PAC-Bayes analysis further shows that the additive complexity term is smallest for l∞-sharpness, so minimizing ‖∇ℓ(θ)‖₁ simultaneously yields the flattest learning trajectory, the tightest generalization bound, and the lowest unlearning error (proportional to accumulated Hessian curvature). The proposed unlearning-compatible loss is ℓ_D^unc(θ) = ℓ_D(θ) + ρ‖∇ℓ_D(θ)‖₁, where ρ controls the perturbation radius; in practice this is optimized with a double-backpropagation step (or a finite-difference approximation along the sign(g) direction to avoid graph retention). Beyond unlearning, the l1 gradient-norm regularizer acts as a spike damper: poisonous data points that produce large gradient outliers in backdoor-attack settings are suppressed, reducing the model's learned malicious behavior (poison-test accuracy drops from 86.6% to 24.6% on CIFAR-10) while preserving clean-data performance.

## Results

On ResNet-18/CIFAR-10, gradient sparsity (GS) regularization consistently reduces the average gap (Avg. Gap) to the exact-retraining gold standard across all five MU baselines in both random and class forgetting. The most dramatic gains appear in class forgetting: IU drops from 40.61 to 5.76, FT from 22.61 to 0.81, and GA from 68.17 to 43.15. In random forgetting, WS improves from 7.87 to 2.06. Additionally, in a backdoor-attack setting (MobileNetV2, 3% poisoned CIFAR-10), GS reduces poison-test accuracy from 86.62% to 24.63% while preserving clean-test accuracy (68.19% vs. 68.10%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares GS-regularized training against five MU baselines (WS, GA, IU, FF, FT) under both random and class forgetting. The largest absolute improvements in Avg. Gap come from class forgetting: IU improves by 34.85 points (40.61→5.76), FT by 21.80 (22.61→0.81), and GA by 25.02 (68.17→43.15), indicating that GS-trained models are far easier to unlearn via these methods. In random forgetting, WS benefits most (7.87→2.06, a 74% reduction), while GA, IU, and FF show only marginal gains (≤0.24 points). FF is the least responsive to GS in both scenarios (≤0.24 improvement), suggesting its fine-tuning-based mechanism is less sensitive to training-trajectory flatness. The paper also shows that L1 gradient-norm regularization outperforms L2 gradient-norm regularization (Figure 3), confirming the theoretical prediction that L1 captures worst-case sharpness. In the backdoor-attack setting, GS reduces poison-test accuracy from 86.62% to 24.63% (a 62-point drop) while keeping clean accuracy essentially unchanged, demonstrating a robustness benefit beyond unlearning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate threads: SAM-style sharpness minimization (Foret et al., 2020), which bounds generalization error via the l2 gradient norm, and the unrolling-SGD analysis (Thudi et al., 2021), which shows unlearning error scales with accumulated Hessian curvature. The key conceptual departure is asking *which* perturbation norm yields the worst-case sharpness, and showing via standard norm inequalities that l∞ perturbations (quantified by the l1 gradient norm) dominate, thereby proposing gradient sparsity as a training-time regularizer rather than an unlearning-time intervention. This positions the work as a training-strategy contribution to an existing MU problem, complementing rather than replacing unlearning algorithms like gradient ascent, fine-tuning, or weight sparsity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is that a single training-time modification (adding ρ‖∇ℓ‖₁ to the loss) can improve unlearning performance across multiple MU baselines without altering the unlearning algorithm itself, offering a drop-in compatibility layer. The secondary finding that gradient sparsity damps gradient spikes from poisoned data (reducing backdoor accuracy from 86.6% to 24.6% while preserving clean accuracy) suggests broader robustness benefits beyond unlearning. However, the 1.5–2× per-step compute overhead and the mixed results on VGG16 (where some baselines actually degrade) temper the practical appeal, and the finite-difference approximation, while removing memory overhead, still loses a meaningful fraction of the unlearning benefit. Open questions include whether efficiency tricks (partial regularization, trajectory-based proxies) can close the gap, and whether the benefit persists at ImageNet scale or with larger architectures.
