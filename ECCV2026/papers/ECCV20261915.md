## Problem

Deep learning defocus deblurring methods are constrained by training data that is either limited in lens diversity (real-captured datasets like DPDD use a single camera) or physically inaccurate (synthetic datasets use disk/Gaussian kernels). Existing wave-optics PSF approaches are computationally expensive and lack explicit sampling criteria, preventing scalable generation of depth-varying defocus pairs across diverse compound lenses with proper camera ISP modeling.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261915/cropped_page7_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The synthesis pipeline consumes sharp RAW images (from DPDD), monocular metric depth maps (from Depth Pro), Zemax compound lens prescriptions, and a sampled focusing distance to produce 384×384 blurred-sharp sRGB image pairs. The PSF computation sub-pipeline takes a lens design, focus distance, object depth, and field position, and outputs a sensor-aligned PSF at the target pixel pitch via 5× supersampled Debye CZT followed by downsampling. The final dataset (CLDefocus) contains 40,000/1,000/1,000 train/val/test pairs with disjoint splits on both source images and lenses (700 lenses split 420/140/140).

## Method

The pipeline has three stages. First, wavefront samples are obtained by dense ray tracing through the lens onto the exit pupil sphere, fitted with Zernike polynomials for a smooth analytical representation, then propagated to the sensor plane using the Debye formulation accelerated by the chirp Z-transform (Bluestein's algorithm), which allows flexible ROI and sampling pitch selection; an explicit sampling condition N = 2·N_inf (derived from the phase factor in the Debye integral) ensures aliasing-free PSFs without empirical tuning. Second, the depth map is quantized into K layers by signed circle-of-confusion values, and defocus is rendered via back-to-front layered compositing with occlusion inpainting in the radiometrically linear RGB space. Third, the blurred linear image is converted back to RAW, sensor noise is synthesized, and the forward ISP (demosaicing, white balance, color correction, gamma) is applied to produce the final sRGB pair, ensuring photometric consistency with real camera imaging. This combination of physically grounded PSFs, depth-varying occlusion-aware rendering, and ISP-aware linear-space blur synthesis distinguishes the approach from prior work that uses simplified kernels, ignores occlusions, or blurs directly in sRGB.

## Results

On the CLDefocus test set, the CLDefocus-trained NRKNet achieves the best full-reference scores: PSNR 32.16 dB, SSIM 0.865, and LPIPS 0.201, outperforming models trained on RTF (26.62/0.845/0.242), RealDOF (24.74/0.764/0.303), and DPDD (24.73/0.776/0.265). On the SYNDOF test set, CLDefocus-trained also leads with PSNR 27.58 and SSIM 0.790. On the real DPDD test set, CLDefocus-trained achieves the highest PSNR (28.27 dB) but the worst LPIPS (0.263) among all training sets, while DPDD-trained attains the best LPIPS (0.223), a gap the authors attribute to residual blur and misalignment in real ground-truth images.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares NRKNet trained on four datasets (CLDefocus, RTF, RealDOF, DPDD) across three test sets. On the CLDefocus and SYNDOF test sets, the CLDefocus-trained model dominates all full-reference metrics, achieving 5.5+ dB higher PSNR than the next-best baseline (RTF) on CLDefocus and 3+ dB over DPDD on SYNDOF. On the real DPDD test set, results are mixed: CLDefocus-trained leads in PSNR (28.27 vs. 26.11 for DPDD-trained) but trails in LPIPS (0.263 vs. 0.223) and SSIM (0.825 vs. 0.833 for RTF-trained). The authors argue that pixel-wise full-reference metrics on real benchmarks are biased by ground-truth imperfections (residual defocus, spatial misalignment, brightness shifts), and that the CLDefocus-trained model's higher PSNR on DPDD actually reflects better alignment with the imperfect GT rather than superior perceptual quality. On no-reference metrics (NIQE, MUSIQ, TOPIQ), the CLDefocus-trained model consistently scores worse than RTF- and DPDD-trained models across all test sets, which somewhat complicates the paper's narrative about perceptual superiority. Overall, the strongest evidence for CLDefocus is its clear advantage on its own test set and on SYNDOF, supporting the claim of improved cross-device generalization from lens-diverse synthetic training data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate threads: wave-optics PSF computation (building on the Debye formulation from microscopy [Leutenegger 2006, Hu 2020] and extending it to photographic compound lenses via Zernike-fitted wavefronts and the chirp Z-transform), depth-aware layered defocus rendering (following Hasinoff 2007 and Kraus 2007), and ISP-aware blur synthesis in linear space (following RSBlur [Rim 2022]). The key departure from prior wave-optics PSF work (Chen 2021, Luo 2024, Müller 2023/2026) is the replacement of the Rayleigh-Sommerfeld/Huygens' principle with the Debye CZT, which provides an explicit sampling criterion and orders-of-magnitude speedup, enabling scalable PSF generation across 700 diverse lens designs rather than a handful. Unlike earlier synthetic defocus datasets that used disk or Gaussian approximations, this pipeline produces physically grounded, lens-specific PSFs with occlusion handling and full ISP simulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CLDefocus dataset (40K pairs across 700 lenses) addresses a fundamental bottleneck in defocus deblurring: the limited optical diversity and ground-truth imperfections of real-captured datasets. The paper's analysis showing that full-reference metrics (PSNR, SSIM) can favor slightly blurred predictions over perceptually sharper ones due to residual blur and misalignment in real GT pairs is a useful caution for the field. Limitations include the Debye approximation's reduced accuracy for severe off-axis fields, dependence on monocular depth estimation quality, and a simplified ISP model that may not capture diverse real camera pipelines. The controllable synthesis parameters (blur level, lens, focus) open the door to curriculum learning and task-specific dataset design that is infeasible with captured data.
