## Problem

When multiple editing instructions target different regions of an image simultaneously, their semantic signals interfere, causing attribute leakage and poor edit disentanglement that worsens with the number of concurrent edits. Existing solutions either apply edits sequentially (multi-turn), split generation into parallel branches (multi-branch, with severe memory/time scaling), or enforce hard binary attention masks that produce visible seams and require architecture-specific layer selection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262664/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a source RGB image, N user-provided (or SAM3-generated) binary segmentation masks, and N text editing instructions (e.g., 'Replace the mouse with a black chick'), plus an optional global prompt for style/lighting changes. Masks are downsampled to the VAE latent resolution before Gaussian smoothing. The output is a single edited image with all N modifications applied in one forward pass, evaluated at 1024² resolution with 4 inference steps on FLUX.2[klein] 4B/9B.

## Method

MICE is a training-free, architecture-agnostic strategy that redefines the additive bias matrix B in the joint attention of MMDiT-based flow matching models. Instead of binary 0/−∞ masks, it computes continuous negative biases from instance-aware Gaussian-smoothed segmentation masks: each mask is smoothed independently with kernel size k, then values touching neighboring masks are zeroed to prevent cross-instance interference. The bias for a token at spatial position j relative to instance n is B(j) = (1/τ) log(s_n[j] + ε), where τ controls falloff intensity. The resulting 8-case bias matrix enforces strict prompt-to-instance binding, allows unbiased background and context self-attention for global coherence, applies spatially-decaying negative bias to instance tokens attending to neighboring regions (enabling harmonization), and sets −∞ for unrelated cross-instance interactions. The same bias is applied uniformly across all MMDiT layers, eliminating the need for per-layer hyperparameter tuning that plagues prior attention-biasing methods.

## Results

On LoMOE-Bench, MICE (FLUX.2[klein]9B backbone) achieves the best Localized CLIP Score of 30.30, outperforming Gemini 3 Pro Image (29.67) and the FLUX.2[klein]9B baseline (29.45). On the denser MICE-Bench (avg. 8.5 edits/image), MICE reaches a LocC of 27.79, substantially ahead of IDAttn (25.54), LayerEdit (24.38), and LoMOE (25.46), while maintaining a 98.97% Attempt Rate. In the LLM-as-judge ELO evaluation, MICE ranks first on both benchmarks (1342.53 on LoMOE-Bench, 1568.46 on MICE-Bench), confirming superior overall visual quality and prompt adherence beyond what CLIP scores capture.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MICE achieves the highest Localized CLIP Score on both benchmarks, beating Gemini 3 Pro Image by 0.63 (LoMOE) and 0.70 (MICE-Bench) points on LocC, and surpassing the FLUX.2[klein]9B end-to-end baseline by 0.85 and 1.45 points respectively. Against the multi-branch methods, MICE outperforms LoMOE (29.40/25.46 LocC) and LayerEdit (29.07/24.38 LocC) on localized edit quality, while also avoiding their linear VRAM/time scaling with edit count. Compared to the attention-based IDAttn, MICE gains 1.04 (LoMOE) and 2.25 (MICE-Bench) LocC points and dramatically improves Attempt Rate on MICE-Bench (98.97% vs. 73.01%), indicating IDAttn's hard binary masks cause it to skip edits under high density. MICE trades slightly higher background MAE (8.88 vs. 7.24 for Gemini, 6.41 for IDAttn on LoMOE) for superior edit localization, a trade-off the authors argue is preferable for multi-instance editing. In the LLM-as-judge ELO evaluation, MICE leads all competitors on both benchmarks, with the gap widening on MICE-Bench (1568.46 vs. 1412.73 for IDAttn), confirming that CLIP-based metrics understate the visual quality advantage of smooth attention biasing over hard masking.

## Relation to Prior Work

MICE builds directly on MMDiT-based flow matching editors (FLUX.2, FLUX.1 Kontext) and departs from the closest prior work, IDAttn, which enforces instance isolation via binary (0, −∞) attention masks applied selectively to architecture-dependent layers. Rather than hard masking with per-layer heuristics, MICE replaces the bias with a continuous, spatially-smoothed Gaussian kernel applied uniformly across all layers, making the method architecture-agnostic and eliminating the need for layer-specific tuning. It also introduces an instance-aware smoothing strategy that prevents inter-instance interference during mask dilation, a detail absent from prior attention-biasing approaches. The multi-branch lineage (MultiDiffusion, LoMOE, LayerEdit) is explicitly positioned as a scaling bottleneck that MICE avoids by operating within a single forward pass.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free, architecture-agnostic nature of MICE makes it immediately applicable to any MMDiT-based editor without retraining, and the demonstrated portability across FLUX.1 Kontext, FLUX.2 4B/9B, and FLUX.2-Dev 32B underscores its practical utility. MICE-Bench (260 samples, 5–40 concurrent edits, avg. 8.5) fills a clear gap left by LoMOE-Bench (avg. 3 edits) and provides the field a stress test for dense multi-instance editing. Open questions include whether the Gaussian smoothing parameters (k, τ) can be learned or auto-calibrated per scene, how the approach extends to video or 3D editing, and whether the reliance on segmentation masks (even from SAM3) limits fully end-to-end deployment. The finding that a single uniform continuous bias outperforms layer-specific binary masks challenges the prevailing assumption that different transformer depths require different isolation strengths.
