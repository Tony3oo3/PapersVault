## Problem

Current procedural material generation systems (VLMaterial, BlenderAlchemy, BlenderMCP) operate exclusively in graph space, synthesizing node graphs without capturing the construction process, parameter reasoning, and design intent that expert artists use. This graph-only approach misses the procedural expertise embedded in how materials are actually built, leading to outputs that are structurally inconsistent, harder to edit, and less aligned with user intent. The paper asks: can process knowledge from expert demonstrations be reflected automatically during generation to improve both quality and editability?

## Input / Output

The system accepts a text prompt, an optional reference image (for image-conditioned generation), or an existing Blender material graph (for editing), and produces a textual process trace (~8k tokens) that is compiled into an executable Blender Python graph-construction script targeting Blender 3.0–4.5. For editing, the input graph is first decompiled into a process trace via exemplar graph-trace matching before the EDITOR synthesizes an updated trace. A single run costs ~2 USD and takes ~30 minutes, with the COMPILER generating N=10 candidate programs and selecting the first syntactically valid one.

## Method

MATERIALAPPRENTICE frames generation as retrieval-time process reasoning: a RETRIEVER selects top-K=3 relevant process traces from a corpus of 158 Blender tutorial videos (extracted via automated video analysis including node/wire detection at mAP50=0.98, keyframe selection, and narration transcription), and an EDITOR (GPT-5) performs in-context synthesis of a target process trace conditioned on the query and retrieved traces, with a self-refinement critique step. A separate COMPILER (same GPT-5 backbone, different prompt) grounds the trace into executable Blender shader-node code, explicitly separating reasoning from execution. For editing, a DECOMPILER reconstructs a process trace from an input graph by matching substructures against exemplar graph-trace pairs via a NARRATOR module. The entire pipeline is training-free, relying on in-context learning and retrieval-time conditioning rather than fine-tuning, and the video analysis pipeline is designed so that minor parsing errors are absorbed by the process-level abstraction in the PROCESSSYNTHESIZER.

## Results

On the In-the-Wild image-to-material generation benchmark, MATERIALAPPRENTICE achieves the best CLIP similarity (0.768), lowest style loss (0.065), and lowest sliced Wasserstein distance (5.949) among all methods, outperforming VLMaterial (SWD 8.973) and BlenderMCP (SWD 8.227). On BlenderKit, it is competitive with VLMaterial (CLIP 0.852 vs. 0.856; SWD 2.567 vs. 2.44) while substantially outperforming BlenderMCP (SWD 2.567 vs. 4.428). For text-based editing, MATERIALAPPRENTICE achieves the highest CLIP similarity on both coarse (0.260) and fine-grained (0.253) edits, and users prefer its outputs over all baselines in 80–100% of comparisons. In the expert study, process-conditioned traces require fewer structural edits (7.84 vs. 10.84) and total edits (13.58 vs. 15.67) than graph-conditioned traces, with 100% expert preference for implementation clarity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against VLMaterial (fine-tuned VLM for node-graph generation), MATERIALAPPRENTICE is competitive on BlenderKit (CLIP 0.852 vs. 0.856, SWD 2.567 vs. 2.44) but clearly superior on the harder In-the-Wild set (SWD 5.949 vs. 8.973, CLIP 0.768 vs. 0.764), with users preferring Ours in 80–89% of comparisons. Against BlenderMCP (LLM-based Blender code generation), MATERIALAPPRENTICE wins on every generation metric on both datasets (e.g., SWD 2.567 vs. 4.428 on BlenderKit; 5.949 vs. 8.227 on In-the-Wild) and on editing CLIP (0.260 vs. 0.237 coarse; 0.253 vs. 0.215 fine-grained), with user preference of 85–100%. Against BlenderAlchemy (iterative VLM-based graph refinement), MATERIALAPPRENTICE achieves higher CLIP on both coarse (0.260 vs. 0.244) and fine-grained (0.253 vs. 0.240) editing, with users preferring Ours in 81–85% of cases. The Nearest-Neighbour baseline (direct retrieval without synthesis) is consistently outperformed on CLIP and SWD for generation and on CLIP for editing, confirming that the process-synthesis step adds value beyond simple retrieval. Notably, MATERIALAPPRENTICE is training-free (in-context learning with GPT-5), whereas VLMaterial requires fine-tuning, making the competitive BlenderKit results particularly strong.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior procedural material generation systems (VLMaterial, BlenderAlchemy, BlenderMCP) that operate exclusively in graph space by introducing process traces—textual records of construction steps, parameters, and design intent—as a first-class intermediate representation. It builds on the LLM-as-program-synthesizer paradigm (3DGPT, Holodeck, L3GO) but applies it to a domain-specific intermediate (process traces) rather than raw code or DSL, and on retrieval-augmented generation by treating expert tutorial videos as a lightweight inference-time knowledge base rather than a training corpus. Unlike VLMaterial, which fine-tunes a VLM on material graphs, MATERIALAPPRENTICE is entirely training-free, relying on GPT-5 prompted in different roles (retriever, editor, compiler, decompiler) with in-context conditioning from retrieved demonstrations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that reasoning over *how* experts build materials (process) rather than *what* the final graph looks like (artifact) yields more editable, structurally coherent outputs that require fewer corrective edits from professional artists—a finding with practical implications for any generative system where intermediate reasoning steps matter. Limitations include dependence on a single LLM (GPT-5), restriction to Blender's shader-node ecosystem, and a video-extraction pipeline that, while effective, is acknowledged as primarily engineering. Open questions include whether process-trace reasoning generalizes to other authoring tools (Substance, Houdini), scales to multi-step design workflows beyond single-material creation, and whether the small retrieval set (K=3) remains sufficient as material complexity grows.
