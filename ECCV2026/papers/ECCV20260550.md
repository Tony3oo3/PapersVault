## Problem

Existing video streaming benchmarks evaluate only single-stream understanding, leaving a critical gap for real-world scenarios like live sports broadcasting, autonomous driving, and multi-screen collaboration that inherently require continuous, multi-stream interaction. No prior benchmark or systematic evaluation protocol exists for assessing whether MLLMs can perform online, cross-stream reasoning under concurrent video inputs with synchronized timestamps.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260550/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark feeds 2–5 temporally aligned video streams (resampled to 2 FPS, chunked into <50 MB segments, 5–30 min duration) together with a text query into an MLLM under a fixed token budget of C_max = 250 tokens per video second. The model must produce a text answer (multiple-choice or free-form) at the correct temporal moment, with forward (proactive) questions requiring the model to remain silent until the answerable condition is met. Evaluation scores are decomposed into Instant, Backward, and Forward categories, plus four multi-stream ability axes (single-stream, cross-stream cooperation, cross-stream reference, cross-stream anti-interference).

## Method

The paper introduces X-Stream, a 4,220-QA benchmark built with a dual-verification pipeline: Multi-Stream Sufficiency confirms a strong model can answer given the full multi-stream clip, while Multi-Stream Necessity verifies the model fails when given any single stream in isolation, thereby eliminating single-stream shortcuts. For evaluation, the authors conceptualize MLLMs as naive multiplexers and propose three stream-integration strategies: Spatial Division (pixel-level concatenation of downsampled frames), Temporal Division (alternating frames with explicit stream identifiers and aligned temporal embeddings), and Semantic Division (DPP-based token pruning that selects k most query-relevant and diverse tokens per stream under a shared token quota). Ablations reveal that no strategy is universally optimal: Spatial excels at cross-stream referencing, Temporal performs best with two streams under relaxed budgets, and Semantic dominates when scaling to ≥3 streams under tight token constraints, mirroring classical signal-multiplexing trade-offs.

## Results

On the X-Stream benchmark (4,220 QA pairs, 932 videos, 2–5 concurrent streams), the best proprietary model Gemini 3 Pro achieves 49.60 overall score, while the best open-source model Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B reaches 34.28. Forward (proactive) scores are critically low across all models—Gemini 3 Pro scores only 20.77 and Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B a mere 0.61—compared to 85.10 for humans. Open-source streaming-specific models (Dispider 15.44, VideoLLM-online-8B 8.48, MMDuet2 6.79) perform substantially worse than general-purpose MLLMs, confirming that current architectures lack genuine multi-stream integration capability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini 3 Pro dominates all proprietary baselines, scoring 49.60 overall vs. Doubao-Seed-1.8 (36.79), GPT-5 (27.78), and GPT-4o (22.46), with a particularly large gap on Forward tasks (20.77 vs. 14.52, 6.51, 4.05 respectively). Among open-source models, Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B leads at 34.28 overall, narrowly ahead of Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B (34.19), but its Forward score collapses to 0.61 due to near-total failure in proactive response timing (98.81% Early Response rate). All three dedicated streaming models (Dispider 15.44, VideoLLM-online-8B 8.48, MMDuet2 6.79) trail the best general-purpose MLLMs by 19–28 points, indicating that streaming-specific architectures have not yet closed the multi-stream gap. On cross-stream abilities, Gemini 3 Pro leads in Cross-Reference (74.79) and Single-Stream (72.45), while Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B edges out Qwen3-Omni on Multi-Cooperation (57.90 vs. 55.68) and Cross-Interference (65.22 vs. 56.58). No model exceeds 75% on any multi-stream ability, and causal reasoning (Table 3) remains the weakest subtask across all tiers, with Gemini 3 Pro at only 41.79 and open-source models below 55.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two previously separate research threads: streaming video understanding (VideoLLM-online, Dispider, MMDuet2) which handles a single continuous stream, and multi-video/multi-view understanding (EgoLife, MVU-Bench, EgoExo4D) which processes multiple videos offline. The key conceptual departure is borrowing the multiplexing framework from telecommunications to formalize how MLLMs—constrained to a single token stream—must integrate multiple concurrent video inputs, yielding three concrete strategies (spatial, temporal, semantic division). For the semantic division strategy, the paper builds on training-free token pruning methods (Surge, DPP-based selection) to implement query-aware token allocation across streams, and adopts the online inference and LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation protocol from PhoStream.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

X-Stream fills a clear and previously unaddressed gap: no prior benchmark evaluates models on continuous, timestamp-aligned multi-stream understanding in an online setting. The finding that even the strongest proprietary model (Gemini 3 Pro) achieves only ~50% overall, with forward/proactive scores dropping to ~21%, quantifies a substantial capability gap that will guide future architectural research. The multiplexing trade-off analysis—showing that no single strategy is universally optimal and that the best choice shifts with stream count and token budget—provides actionable design guidance for next-generation multi-stream agents. Open questions include whether natively multi-stream architectures (rather than post-hoc multiplexing) can close the gap, how to handle audio-visual coupling more gracefully under multiplexing, and whether the dual-verification protocol generalizes to open-ended, non-QA evaluation.
