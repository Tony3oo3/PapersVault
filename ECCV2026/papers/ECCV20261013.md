## Problem

Existing deformable Gaussian splatting methods bake illumination into per-Gaussian colors, producing physically inconsistent appearance under deformation and relighting—especially on glossy surfaces—and their snapshot-based reconstruction prevents post-hoc material editing. No prior framework unifies physically-based inverse rendering, relighting, and deformation-driven control within a single Gaussian representation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261013/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multiview calibrated images (pretrained via Ref-Gaussian, then fine-tuned for 20k iterations), a 128×256 environment map, and a deformation signal (either an MPM-simulated particle cloud or an artist-driven mesh). It outputs a decoupled set of 2D Gaussians with per-vertex material parameters (albedo, metallic, roughness) stored on a TSDF-extracted mesh, enabling physically-based rendering under arbitrary lighting and deformation. During dynamic driving, material lookups shift from per-Gaussian to mesh-vertex storage to accelerate ray tracing.

## Method

DR-GS is a three-stage pipeline: (1) static reconstruction initializes 2D Gaussian surfels and a TSDF mesh, with optional mesh-based reinitialization via barycentric-coordinate parameterization; (2) parameter decoupling jointly optimizes Gaussians and material parameters by solving the full rendering equation with multiple importance sampling (cosine-weighted, GGX, and environment-light distributions) and 2D Gaussian ray tracing for visibility and indirect light, regularized by an RGB loss, a PBR L1 loss, and a white-balance term; (3) dynamic driving transfers deformation from a canonical to a deformed state via GMLS interpolation (particle-driven with MPM) or barycentric interpolation of rotation, shear, and displacement (mesh-driven), updating Gaussian centers, rotations, and scales through polar decomposition. For efficient dynamic rendering, 2D Gaussian ray tracing is replaced by mesh-based ray tracing with material attributes stored on mesh vertices, and Monte Carlo noise is suppressed with a cross-bilateral filter guided by spatiotemporal variance (SVGF).

## Results

On the GlossySynthetic dataset (8 scenes), DR-GS (particle-driven) achieves a mean PSNR of 28.97 dB, SSIM of 0.946, and LPIPS of 0.065 for static reconstruction, outperforming the strongest baseline GSP (26.81 dB / 0.924 / 0.091) by ~2.2 dB in PSNR. For relighting, DR-GS reaches a mean PSNR of 24.95 dB and SSIM of 0.912, surpassing GS-ROR² (23.38 dB / 0.910) and Ref-Gaussian (21.62 dB / 0.898), while training in 0.68 h and rendering at 24 FPS. In the user study (30 participants, 0–5 scale), DR-GS scores highest on physical plausibility (4.56), temporal coherence (4.42), and deformation consistency (4.19), with relighting scores of 4.62 / 4.27 / 4.21 respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DR-GS (particle-driven) outperforms all five dynamic GS baselines on static reconstruction: it leads GSP by ~2.2 dB PSNR and 0.022 SSIM, PhysGaussian by ~3.7 dB, Mani-GS by ~3.7 dB, GaussianMesh by ~4.4 dB, and SuGaR by ~15.4 dB (SuGaR is a mesh-only method and performs poorly on glossy objects). For relighting, DR-GS beats GS-ROR² by ~1.6 dB PSNR, Ref-Gaussian by ~3.3 dB, and IRGS by ~3.5 dB, while also achieving the best SSIM (0.912 vs. 0.910, 0.898, 0.872). In the user study, DR-GS surpasses GSP on all three perceptual criteria (PP 4.56 vs. 4.38, TC 4.42 vs. 4.12, DC 4.19 vs. 3.76) and maintains its advantage under relighting (4.62 vs. 3.82 PP), whereas PhysGaussian does not support relighting at all. The mesh-driven variant of DR-GS is competitive with GSP on reconstruction (24.87 vs. 26.81 dB) but trails the particle variant, reflecting the trade-off between editing flexibility and reconstruction fidelity. Notably, DR-GS achieves its best relighting quality with only 0.68 h training and 24 FPS rendering, compared to IRGS's 0.5 FPS, making it the only method that balances physical fidelity with practical interactivity.

## Relation to Prior Work

DR-GS unifies two previously separate research threads: deformable Gaussian splatting (PhysGaussian, GSP, GaussianMesh) and physically-based inverse rendering (IRGS, Ref-Gaussian, GS-IR). It inherits the 2D Gaussian surfel representation from 2DGS, the deferred rendering pipeline and 2D Gaussian ray tracing from IRGS, the initial pretraining from Ref-Gaussian, and the simulation-rendering separation principle with GMLS interpolation from GSP. The key conceptual departure is that prior deformable GS methods bake illumination into per-Gaussian colors, while prior inverse rendering methods are confined to static scenes; DR-GS explicitly decouples geometry, illumination, and material and evaluates the full rendering equation with multiple importance sampling under continuous deformation, a combination absent from both lineages.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By establishing a fully decoupled geometry-illumination-material pipeline that remains valid under deformation, DR-GS opens a path toward physically consistent 3D asset creation where artists can edit materials, change lighting, and apply physical simulation without re-capturing or re-training. The acknowledged trade-off—solving the rendering equation reduces rendering speed to ~24 FPS versus 122–208 FPS for baselines—remains a significant barrier to real-time interactive use, and the mesh-driven variant's notably weaker performance compared to the particle variant suggests that robust mesh-based deformation of Gaussians is still an open problem. Extending the framework to complex multi-bounce inter-reflections, non-PBR materials, and large-scale scenes with many interacting objects are natural next steps.
