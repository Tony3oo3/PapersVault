## Problem

Visually-guided acoustic highlighting—rebalancing audio loudness to match visual saliency—has been addressed only with discriminative models that assume a one-to-one mapping between degraded and well-balanced mixes. However, audio remixing is inherently many-to-many: a single video admits multiple plausible mixes, and multiple degraded inputs can map to the same target. This paper argues that generative modeling is better suited to this ambiguity and identifies a critical practical challenge: in iterative flow-based generation, early-step errors in source selection compound over steps, driving trajectories off-manifold.

## Input / Output

The model takes a poorly-balanced mono audio waveform at 44.1 kHz and video frames as input, producing a well-balanced audio waveform of the same format. During training, poorly-balanced inputs are generated on-the-fly by separating movie audio into speech/music/SFX stems, randomly adjusting their levels, and remixing them, so the model sees multiple degraded versions of the same target across epochs. Inference uses fixed-step Euler integration over 4 timesteps, and the step count can serve as a user-facing knob for highlighting strength since each intermediate output remains realistic audio.

## Method

The core contribution reframes VisAH as conditional flow matching: a dual U-Net (spectrogram + waveform branches, ~60M params) learns a velocity field v_θ(x_t, t, c) transporting poorly-balanced audio toward well-balanced audio, conditioned on visual cues. Two key innovations address the main failure modes. First, a rollout loss backpropagates through all 4 flow steps and adds an MSE term between the final self-generated prediction and the ground-truth target, explicitly training the model to recover from its own intermediate errors rather than only matching the instantaneous velocity. Second, a cross-attention adapter injects CLAP audio features into the 18th layer of a frozen CLIP vision encoder, enabling early audio-visual fusion so the conditioning module identifies which source to enhance before the U-Net performs vector field regression. The model is initialized from pretrained VisAH weights, with the vector field defined as v_θ = x_0 − u_θ to reuse the discriminative architecture, and trained for 50 epochs with sinusoidal timestep embeddings appended to the conditioning tokens.

## Results

On the Muddy Mix Dataset, VisAH-FM (CLIP-CLAP conditioning) achieves the best scores across all metrics: IB Score 29.12, KLD 9.70, LDif 7.77, Mag 8.28, Env 2.74, and Was 0.63. Compared to the strongest discriminative baseline (VisAH with CLIP-CLAP), the flow matching formulation yields roughly 14% lower KLD, 19% lower loudness difference (LDif), and 21% lower Wasserstein distance. In a subjective test with 13 participants over 9 videos, VisAH-FM achieves a 45.55% win rate versus 17.17% for VisAH, with 37.28% judged as draws.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is VisAH [Huang et al., CVPR 2025], the prior discriminative approach for visually-guided acoustic highlighting, evaluated with three conditioning variants (CLIP, T5, CLIP-CLAP). VisAH-FM outperforms all three VisAH variants on every reported metric. The largest relative gains appear in the remixing-specific metric LDif (7.77 vs. 9.60 for the best VisAH, a ~19% reduction) and in temporal alignment (Was 0.63 vs. 0.80, ~21% reduction), indicating that the generative formulation more accurately rebalances source loudness and preserves temporal structure. Semantic alignment (KLD) improves from 11.28 to 9.70 (~14%), and signal-quality metrics (Mag, Env) also improve by 17–19%. The IB Score gain is modest (29.12 vs. 28.92), suggesting that audio-visual binding was already reasonably captured by the discriminative model, while the flow matching formulation primarily benefits the actual remixing and signal-fidelity aspects. Subjective evaluation confirms the quantitative advantage, with VisAH-FM winning 45.55% of pairwise comparisons versus 17.17% for VisAH.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes the visually-guided acoustic highlighting task introduced by VisAH [13] (CVPR 2025) from a discriminative one-to-one mapping problem into a generative distribution-transport problem using conditional flow matching [24]. It departs from the VisAH architecture by replacing the direct regression objective with a velocity-field estimation trained via flow matching, and introduces two key modifications: a rollout loss that backpropagates through the full multi-step trajectory to counteract error accumulation, and a cross-attention adapter that injects CLAP audio features into intermediate CLIP layers for early cross-modal fusion. The rollout loss is conceptually related to consistency models [38,39] and D-Flow [2], but differs in supervising the final output after multiple recurrent steps rather than enforcing local timestep consistency. The conditioning adapter draws on cross-modal adapter designs from multimodal LLMs [6,8] but applies them to the audio-visual conditioning pathway for audio remixing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that generative modeling is fundamentally better suited to audio remixing than discriminative approaches, given the many-to-many nature of the mapping between degraded and well-balanced mixes. The rollout loss addresses a practical and general problem in flow matching—error compounding over iterative steps—and the finding that it alone (without the standard flow matching loss) outperforms vanilla flow matching suggests that iterative self-correction may be more valuable than the linear trajectory prior in tasks with close input-output distributions. Limitations include evaluation on a single dataset (Muddy Mix), only four inference steps, and occasional artifacts in the output. Open questions include whether the rollout loss generalizes to other flow-matching tasks with larger distributional gaps, and whether the cross-modal adapter design transfers to other audio-visual generation settings.
