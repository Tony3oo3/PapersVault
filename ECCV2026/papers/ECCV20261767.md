## Problem

Existing multimodal benchmarks (MathVista, RealWorldQA, MMMU) score only final answers, making lucky guesses and shortcut exploitation indistinguishable from genuine step-by-step reasoning. This answer-centric evaluation structurally incentivizes hallucination by penalizing uncertainty and hides where in the reasoning chain a model actually fails—perception, inference, or ordering.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261767/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each CRYSTAL instance pairs an RGB image with a text question and a correct answer, and the model must produce an ordered sequence of natural-language reasoning steps followed by a final answer. Evaluation embeds both predicted and reference steps with all-distilroberta-v1, performs greedy 1:1 matching at cosine threshold τ=0.35, and reports per-example precision, recall, and F1 (Match F1) plus an LIS-ratio-weighted variant (Ordered Match F1, α=0.3) that penalizes steps appearing out of logical sequence. The 6,372 instances average 11.6 reference steps (range 3–42) and are stratified into easy/medium/hard by step count and linguistic complexity.

## Method

Reference reasoning steps are constructed through a four-phase Delphi-inspired pipeline: four heterogeneous MLLMs (Qwen2.5-VL-72B, InternVL3-76B, Gemma3-27B, Llama-4-Maverick) independently generate candidate steps, which are embedded and clustered by cosine similarity to form semantic groups; a representative per cluster is selected by minimum within-cluster dissimilarity and ordered into a coherent chain. A fifth MLLM (Molmo-72B) validates logical soundness and visual grounding, and a human annotator performs a final quality gate, with failed examples re-entering generation (fewer than 5% require re-iteration). For training, the paper proposes the Causal Process Reward (CPR), a multiplicative reward that grants the full step bonus only when the answer is correct (R = a_w + s_w·F1_step if correct; s_w·F1_step·λ otherwise, λ=0.3), preventing the model from maximizing accuracy through guessing while ignoring reasoning. CPR-Curriculum further stabilizes learning via a two-phase schedule—Phase 1 trains on format and accuracy rewards alone, then Phase 2 introduces the full CPR at a lower learning rate with progressive difficulty (fewer reference steps first)—and uses PCGrad to project conflicting gradients between the accuracy and reasoning objectives. Applied via GRPO, CPR-Curriculum achieves +32% Match F1 on Qwen2.5-VL-3B and +0.401 F1 on InternVL3.5-4B, where additive composite and answer-only rewards collapse to NaN gradients.

## Results

Across 20 MLLMs evaluated on CRYSTAL (6,372 instances), GPT-5-mini achieves the highest Match F1 (0.773) and Ordered F1 (0.670), while GPT-5 leads accuracy (57.99%) but ranks only 8th in Match F1 (0.612). A near-universal cherry-picking pattern emerges: 19 of 20 models show precision substantially exceeding recall (ratios 1.2×–5.3×), with even GPT-5 recovering only 47.9% of reference steps. No model preserves more than 60% of matched steps in correct relative order (best LIS: 0.669 for Qwen3-VL-2B), and scaling within families produces non-monotonic trade-offs (e.g., Qwen3-VL-32B gains F1 but loses accuracy vs. Qwen3-VL-8B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among commercial frontier models, GPT-5-mini (0.773 F1, 0.670 Ord. F1) outperforms GPT-5 (0.612 F1) and GPT-5.2 Instant (0.564 F1) on reasoning quality despite lower accuracy, while Gemini 2.5 Flash is the sole model with recall (0.765) exceeding precision (0.701), generating 17.10 steps per question versus the 11.6 reference average. In the open-source Qwen family, Qwen3-VL-32B achieves the second-best F1 (0.718) but at 49.22% accuracy, below Qwen3-VL-8B (57.66%, 0.659 F1), illustrating non-monotonic scaling. Cross-family, Gemma3-4B (0.618 F1) surpasses InternVL3.5-38B (0.612 F1) despite 9.5× fewer parameters, and LLaVA-v1.6-7B achieves near-perfect precision (0.961) by generating only 3.94 steps, masking reasoning gaps through confident omission. MiniCPM-v2.6-8B is the clear outlier at the bottom (0.215 F1, 0.134 recall, 1.31 steps), while Llama 3.2-11B (0.471 F1) and LLaVA-v1.6-7B (0.512 F1) cluster near the lower end. The key takeaway is that no single model dominates both accuracy and reasoning fidelity, and the cherry-picking pattern (high P, low R) persists across all 7 model families and from 1B to frontier scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant answer-centric evaluation paradigm (MathVista, MMMU, RealWorldQA) by introducing machine-verifiable step-level scoring via semantic similarity matching, extending the process-evaluation line (Multimodal-CoT, MME-CoT, MPBench) with a deterministic, encoder-based grader rather than LLM-as-judge. For training, it builds on GRPO (DeepSeekMath) but replaces the standard additive reward combination with a multiplicative Causal Process Reward that causally couples answer correctness to step alignment, drawing on staged reward shaping and PCGrad for gradient conflict resolution. The reference construction pipeline repurposes self-consistency voting at the step level within a Delphi-inspired multi-agent aggregation, distinguishing it from single-model CoT elicitation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes systematic reasoning failures—universal cherry-picking and disordered step ordering—that are invisible to accuracy metrics, and these patterns persist even in commercial frontier systems (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Flash) not involved in construction, suggesting a fundamental gap in current training objectives. CPR-Curriculum demonstrates a practical path to improving reasoning transparency without manual step annotation, though validation is limited to 3–4B scale and the multiplicative reward's behavior at larger scales remains open. Key limitations include the possibility that references miss valid alternative reasoning paths, the fixed encoder/threshold choice, and Ordered Match F1's inability to model causal dependencies between steps. Whether the cherry-picking and ordering failures can be addressed through architectural changes or require fundamentally different training signals remains an open question for the field.
