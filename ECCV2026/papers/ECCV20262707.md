## Problem

Existing shape matching methods are tightly coupled to specific geometric representations—spectral bases require meshes, vision-based features require rendering pipelines, and learned descriptors require large training datasets—making cross-representation correspondence (e.g., mesh-to-SDF or mesh-to-point-cloud) difficult or impossible. FUSE addresses this by introducing a representation-agnostic, invertible mapping between shapes that requires no large-scale training, no data-driven priors, and no pair-specific optimization beyond per-shape flow fitting.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two shapes in any supported representation (triangle mesh, unstructured point cloud, neural SDF, or tetrahedral volumetric mesh) along with 5–6 manually selected landmarks on one shape, transferred to the other via ground-truth correspondences. It requires only the ability to sample surface points and compute single-to-all geodesic distances (via Dijkstra, heat method, or fast marching depending on representation), with no explicit surface reconstruction needed for point clouds. The output is a discrete point-to-point correspondence obtained by nearest-neighbor search in the target embedding space, though the underlying flow composition is continuous and invertible, enabling extensions to UV parametrization, body-model fitting, and volumetric correspondence.

## Method

FUSE represents each shape's embedding distribution (geodesic distances to landmarks) as the endpoint of a continuous invertible flow from a shared unit Gaussian anchor, trained independently per shape via Conditional Flow Matching with a neural network velocity field. The correspondence map between two shapes is then the composition of the source's inverse flow (embedding → Gaussian) with the target's forward flow (Gaussian → embedding), requiring no additional optimization at test time. This composition acts as a probabilistic alignment mechanism: by routing both embedding distributions through the same Gaussian, semantically similar points are mapped to nearby regions in the anchor space, reducing sensitivity to non-isometric deformations and embedding variations. The final discrete correspondence is retrieved via KNN in the target embedding space, and the approach naturally extends to any pointwise embedding, any 3D representation supporting point sampling and geodesic computation, and downstream tasks such as UV transfer and point-cloud deformation.

## Results

On FAUST (quasi-isometric), FUSE achieves 0.0289 Euclidean and 0.0274 geodesic error, outperforming KNN (0.0454/0.0434), OT (0.0926/0.0856), and FMaps (0.0401/0.0400), while ULRSSM remains stronger (0.0092/0.0109). With ZoomOut/NAM refinement, FUSE_NAM reaches 0.0179/0.0168 on FAUST and 0.0658/0.0603 on SHREC20 (strongly non-isometric), beating all baselines. FUSE also achieves the highest coverage among non-refined methods (0.5320 on FAUST vs. 0.2452 for KNN and 0.0890 for OT), at the cost of higher Dirichlet energy (less smooth maps). On the SDF-FAUST benchmark, FUSE attains 0.0375 Euclidean error, outperforming KNN (0.0562), NDP (0.0525), and OT (0.0921).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FUSE clearly outperforms KNN, OT, and vanilla FMaps on Euclidean and geodesic accuracy across all four datasets, while FMaps-based methods retain an advantage in Dirichlet energy (map smoothness). Against the refined FMaps variants (ZoomOut, NAM), FUSE-initialized versions (FUSE+ZO, FUSE+NAM) consistently improve over the standard FMaps initialization, most notably on SHREC20 where FUSE+NAM achieves 0.0658/0.0603 vs. FMaps+NAM's 0.1041/0.0962. The data-driven ULRSSM [10] dominates on FAUST and SMAL (0.0092/0.0370 Euclidean) but degrades severely on SHREC20 (0.3286) and is inapplicable to point clouds, whereas FUSE generalizes across all representations. On the Kinect point-cloud dataset, the extrinsic NDP [24] method achieves the best accuracy (0.0457/0.0438), but FUSE still surpasses all geodesic-based baselines (KNN, OT, FMaps) and achieves the highest coverage (0.4931). The vision-based Diff3F [14] is consistently outperformed by FUSE on every dataset and metric, confirming that the flow-based alignment is more effective than distilled semantic features for correspondence retrieval.

## Relation to Prior Work

FUSE repurposes Conditional Flow Matching (Lipman et al. 2022) from its generative-modeling context to represent shape correspondences as compositions of invertible flows through a shared Gaussian anchor, departing from the dominant Functional Maps paradigm (linear operators between spectral bases) and Optimal Transport (transport plans). It is conceptually closest to Geometry Distributions (Zhang et al. 2025), which also encodes shapes as probability distributions, but replaces DDIM with flow matching to obtain exact, deterministic anchoring and to model maps directly over intrinsic feature spaces rather than extrinsic coordinates. Unlike data-driven methods (ULRSSM, Diff3F) that require large-scale training or vision-model priors, FUSE needs only per-shape flow training and a pointwise embedding, making it representation-agnostic by construction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The cross-representation matching capability (mesh ↔ point cloud ↔ SDF ↔ volumetric) without retraining or pair-specific optimization addresses a practical gap that spectral and learned-feature methods cannot fill, and the 'curse of frequency' finding—that low-frequency geodesic embeddings outperform high-frequency learned features under flow alignment—offers a useful design principle for future embedding+alignment pipelines. Key limitations remain: the final KNN step prevents strict bijectivity, geodesic computation degrades on sparse point clouds, and partial matching is unsupported. Open directions include integrating smoothly learned features with flow-based alignment, extending to partial shapes, and deriving fully continuous bijective maps from the flow composition.
