## Problem

Conventional DAgger for robot manipulation operates entirely in the real world with a one-to-one ratio between expert effort and training trajectories, making comprehensive out-of-distribution coverage impractical. Existing Digital Twin augmentation techniques, when applied to DAgger data, treat all timesteps and workspace regions uniformly, ignoring the spatial and temporal failure patterns that DAgger rollouts uniquely reveal, leaving policies vulnerable precisely where and when they fail most.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262505/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy consumes dual-camera RGB images at 640×480 (a third-person RealSense D435 and a wrist-mounted D405), 7-DoF proprioceptive joint angles, and the object's 6D pose estimated via FoundationPose, producing delta joint actions and absolute gripper positions over a 50-step horizon at 30 Hz. The augmentation pipeline additionally requires the binary intervention indicator c_t per timestep from each DAgger trajectory, which marks the exact onset and offset of expert corrections and serves as the primary signal for targeted data generation. The DT is constructed in ManiSkill 3 by importing the robot's URDF and scanned 3D meshes of the workspace and objects.

## Method

Twin-DAgger treats each real-world DAgger intervention as simultaneously a spatial probe identifying failure-prone workspace regions and a temporal prior marking critical deviation moments, then exploits both signals through two targeted augmentation mechanisms within a Digital Twin. Temporally, intervention-focused trajectory warping injects a local Gaussian perturbation into the end-effector pose at each intervention onset, applies a two-phase weight schedule (linear ramp-up before onset, linear decay during the expert correction) to smoothly interpolate between original and perturbed poses via Slerp and linear interpolation, and resolves joint-space commands with warm-started sequential IK, generating a distribution of near-failure states while preserving the unperturbed recovery endpoint. Spatially, difficulty-based stratified sampling partitions the workspace into K regions, records each region's empirical success rate S_i during exploration, and allocates the augmentation budget proportionally to (1−S_i)^γ (γ=5), concentrating synthetic trajectory generation in high-failure-rate regions. The overall system operates as a closed-loop train–explore–augment–train cycle: a base policy is deployed with VR-based expert intervention, the resulting sparse corrections are amplified into targeted DT trajectories, and the augmented data post-trains the policy for the next iteration.

## Results

On a real-world pick-and-place task, Twin-DAgger achieves 55.0% success rate (ACT) and 85.0% (π₀) using only 6 real-world expert corrections augmented into 100 synthetic trajectories within a digital twin. This matches conventional DAgger's 56.7% (which requires 50 interventions) at roughly 15% of the human intervention cost, and outperforms the failure-agnostic DT baseline R2R2R by 22 percentage points on ACT and 30 points on π₀ at an equal 100-sample budget. On a long-horizon categorical-sorting task, Twin-DAgger reaches 33.3%/60.0% versus 23.3%/40.0% for R2R2R under the same data budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against R2R2R (the state-of-the-art failure-agnostic DT augmentation), Twin-DAgger wins decisively at equal data budget: 55.0% vs. 33.0% on ACT and 85.0% vs. 55.0% on π₀ with 100 augmented samples. Even when R2R2R is scaled to 500 samples (5× the budget), it still trails Twin-DAgger by 10 points on ACT (45.0% vs. 55.0%) and actually degrades π₀ below its base policy (43.3% vs. 55.0%), suggesting indiscriminate augmentation can erode existing competence. Against conventional real-world DAgger, Twin-DAgger matches the 56.7% success rate that requires 50 expert interventions using only 6 corrections (~15% of the human effort), confirming the core efficiency claim. On the supplementary contact-rich and long-horizon tasks, Twin-DAgger consistently outperforms R2R2R (43.3% vs. 23.3% on ACT for contact-rich; 33.3% vs. 23.3% on ACT for categorical-sorting) while matching or exceeding conventional DAgger at a fraction of the intervention cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

Twin-DAgger sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: DAgger-style interactive imitation learning (Ross et al. 2011, and subsequent improvements like HG-DAgger, ThriftyDAgger, CR-DAgger) and digital-twin-based data augmentation for robot manipulation (Real2Render2Real, MimicGen, RoboTwin). Prior DAgger variants optimize when to intervene or how to make corrections smoother, while prior DT augmentation methods (R2R2R, MimicGen) apply uniform spatial/temporal transformations designed for fully teleoperated data. The key departure is treating each DAgger intervention as a dual signal—a spatial probe identifying failure-prone workspace regions and a temporal prior marking critical deviation timesteps—and using these signals to direct DT augmentation, rather than applying failure-agnostic uniform warping.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial for robot learning practitioners: matching conventional DAgger performance with only 6 real-world interventions versus 50 dramatically reduces the expert monitoring bottleneck that has limited DAgger's adoption. The finding that uniform DT augmentation can actively degrade policy performance (π0 dropping from 55% to 43.3% with 500 R2R2R samples) challenges the assumption that more synthetic data is always beneficial during post-training. Key limitations constrain broader applicability: the method requires rigid-body manipulation (no deformable objects), relies on VR-based teleoperation with IK-solved joint angles, and inherits DAgger's fundamental inability to recover from already-irreversible failures. Open questions include whether the spatial-temporal targeting principle transfers to multi-robot or deformable-object settings, and whether predictive (rather than reactive) intervention signals could further reduce the intervention budget.
