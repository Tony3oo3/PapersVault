## Problem

Existing credential forgery detection benchmarks focus on pixel-level visual artifacts (splicing, inpainting, texture anomalies), but AI-generated forgeries can be visually flawless while harboring semantic, structural, or factual contradictions that require cognitive reasoning to detect. No dataset or benchmark systematically evaluates MLLMs' ability to identify these cognitive-level forgeries in structured, rule-based credentials.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262683/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample is a rendered credential image (ID card, passport, business license, chat transcript, or shopping receipt) paired with a text instruction. Under the closed-set task (T1), the model receives an enumerated list of 14 forgery subtypes and must select one; under the open-ended task (T2), it receives only a concise abstract instruction and must autonomously determine forgery presence, identify the anomaly, and produce a reasoning explanation. Ground-truth annotations include bounding-box masks for the forged region, a forgery-type tag, and a natural-language reasoning statement describing the specific inconsistency.

## Method

CogniCred is constructed via a four-stage pipeline: (1) template construction using HTML for layout-flexible credentials (chat logs, receipts) and editable PSD layers for layout-fixed ones (ID cards, passports, licenses); (2) content injection where GPT-4o generates semantically consistent field values rendered via Pyppeteer or Photoshop JSX scripts; (3) cognitive anomaly injection that samples a counterfactual but syntactically valid value for one field in a semantically dependent pair (A₁, A₂), producing an inconsistent pair (A₃, A₂) while preserving visual fidelity; and (4) structured annotation with bounding boxes, dependency links, and QA items. The resulting 16,000+ samples span 5 reasoning dimensions (Internal Reasoning, World Knowledge, Visual Reasoning, Contextual Reasoning, Format Compliance) with 14 subcategories. CogniCredBench evaluates 21 MLLMs on two complementary tasks—closed-set classification (strict accuracy) and open-ended reasoning (LLM-as-a-Judge with GPT-4o scoring both the verdict and the reasoning trace)—revealing that models achieve only 20–30% on visually grounded tasks and exhibit a systematic bias toward text-based reasoning over visual-structural analysis.

## Results

On CogniCredBench, human annotators achieve near-perfect accuracy (97.08%–99.51%) across all five reasoning dimensions, while the best model, Gemini-3-Pro, reaches at most 98.50% (Contextual T1) and 95.40% (Contextual T2), but drops to 65.09% (Visual T1) and 56.20% (Format T1). Visual Reasoning and Format Compliance are the most challenging dimensions: even the strongest models score below 45% on T1 and below 30% on T2 in Visual Reasoning. Across all 21 evaluated MLLMs, the open-ended reasoning task (T2) consistently yields 15–40 percentage points lower accuracy than the closed-set classification task (T1), with the largest gaps in Visual Reasoning and Format Compliance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the three closed-source models, Gemini-3-Pro dominates across all five dimensions and both tasks, achieving 97.81% (Internal T1), 98.50% (Contextual T1), and 71.98% (Format T2), substantially outperforming Claude-opus-4 (86.93%, 89.00%, 18.49% respectively) and GPT-4o (60.60%, 52.80%, 4.34%). GPT-4o exhibits a striking failure mode: despite strong World Knowledge scores (45.38% T1, 53.69% T2), it collapses to 1.25% on Visual Reasoning T2 and 4.34% on Format Compliance T2, attributed to high refusal rates and an OCR-biased visual processing pipeline. Among open-source models, GLM-4.5V leads on Contextual T1 (99.20%) and Internal T1 (93.60%), while Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct achieves the best open-source Visual T2 (29.46%) and Format T2 (36.53%), though all open-source models trail Gemini-3-Pro by 10–40 points on most dimensions. A consistent finding across all 21 models is that Visual Reasoning and Format Compliance remain the weakest dimensions, with T2 accuracy frequently below 15%, revealing a systemic over-reliance on textual cues at the expense of visual-structural reasoning. The 'Thinking' variants (Qwen3-VL-32B-Thinking, Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Thinking) improve Contextual Reasoning by 10–29 points over their Instruct counterparts but paradoxically degrade Visual Reasoning by 5–13 points, indicating that extended chain-of-thought reasoning does not translate to genuine visual grounding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of credential forgery detection that targets pixel-level visual artifacts (splicing, inpainting, texture anomalies) and instead formulates forgery detection as a cognitive reasoning problem over structured documents. It also distinguishes itself from general multimodal inconsistency benchmarks (MMIR, MFC-Bench, MMDOCBench) by focusing on the strict, rule-based internal consistency constraints specific to official credentials rather than open-world common sense. The construction pipeline repurposes HTML/PSD template rendering and GPT-4o content generation—techniques common in synthetic data creation—but applies them to inject minimal semantic perturbations that preserve visual fidelity while breaking logical coherence, a design choice absent from prior forgery datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CogniCred is the first large-scale (16K+ samples) dataset and benchmark specifically targeting cognitive-level credential forgeries, filling a gap that leaves security-critical applications vulnerable to AI-generated forgeries that are visually flawless but semantically contradictory. The finding that MLLMs exhibit a systematic text-centric bias—scoring 60–90% on textual reasoning dimensions but collapsing to 20–30% (often single digits) on visual-structural reasoning—reveals a fundamental limitation in current multimodal architectures that is unlikely to be resolved by scaling alone. Open questions include whether the five-reasoning-dimension taxonomy generalizes beyond the five credential types covered, whether the LLM-as-a-Judge protocol for open-ended tasks introduces systematic bias, and how to architect MLLMs that perform genuine image-level reasoning rather than text-mediated visual description.
