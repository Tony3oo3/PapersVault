## Problem

Implicit Neural Representations face a spectral dilemma: periodic activations like SIREN capture fine-grained detail but act as all-pass filters that memorise noise, while spatially compact activations like Gaussians regularise effectively but suffer from low-frequency bias. Existing remedies (Gabor wavelets, variable-periodic functions, Fourier reparameterisation) introduce computational overhead, hyperparameter sensitivity, or restricted representational capacity, and none provides a principled mechanism to adapt bandwidth robustly to the signal's spectral content while rejecting noise.

## Input / Output

The method takes coordinate vectors (2D spatial for images, 1D temporal for audio, 1D for signals) as input to a standard MLP and outputs the corresponding signal value (RGB pixel, audio amplitude, or scalar). It is evaluated under four observation models—clean dense supervision, additive noise, spatially incomplete pixels (20% mask for inpainting, 4× downsampling for super-resolution), and indirect Radon projections for CT—using the same architecture and hyperparameters across all settings without task-specific tuning.

## Method

Each neuron's activation is defined as the steady-state response of a sinusoidally-forced damped harmonic oscillator: σ(z;θ) = A(ω,ω_n,ξ)·sin(ωz+φ), where the amplitude A is the transfer function of a second-order band-pass filter whose centre frequency, bandwidth, and roll-off are governed by the natural frequency ω_n and damping factor ξ. Crucially, A is not a free parameter but is physically coupled to the forcing frequency and damping, so the optimisation cost to admit higher frequencies is provably lower for coherent signal than for random noise (Corollary 1). The gradient of the MSE loss with respect to ξ decomposes into a self-closing term that unconditionally narrows the bandwidth and a signal-opening term that widens it only when the target contains coherent energy at the carrier frequency, yielding an implicit spectral gate with no explicit regularisation. Initialising the oscillator in the stopband (ξ₀ = 1/√2, ω₀ < ω_{n,0}) produces a coarse-to-fine curriculum: the network first captures low-frequency structure and progressively expands its passband as the reconstruction loss demands richer content, with the multi-layer Bessel-function composition ensuring geometric decay of harmonic bandwidth at depth.

## Results

FDHO achieves the highest final PSNR on 4 of 5 image fitting tasks (Tiger: 63.79 dB, Bikers: 57.06 dB, Butterfly: 57.85 dB, Knot: 59.21 dB), with margins up to 10 dB over the closest competitor. On image denoising (σ=0.1), FDHO leads all five images (28.57–31.86 dB) over SIREN (~26.2 dB) and MFN (~27–28 dB), and achieves 39.97 dB on CT reconstruction, outperforming the runner-up MFN (37.19 dB) by 2.8 dB. On audio fitting, FDHO reaches 56.23 dB (Bach) and 43.17 dB (Counting), surpassing SIREN by 4.2 dB and 1.5 dB respectively in final PSNR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FDHO outperforms SIREN on final PSNR in nearly all settings (e.g., Bikers: 57.06 vs. 43.11 dB; Bach audio: 56.23 vs. 52.02 dB), while SIREN occasionally reaches higher peak PSNR but suffers a 5–13 dB degradation between peak and final, confirming its all-pass instability. FINER is the closest competitor, winning on the highly periodic Tiles texture (54.09 vs. 52.24 dB) but trailing FDHO by 4–10 dB on the other four images and by 7–13 dB on denoising. Gauss, WIRE, and BACON are decisively outperformed (by 10–30 dB on image fitting), consistent with their documented low-frequency bias. MFN is the runner-up on denoising and CT reconstruction (37.19 dB) due to its conservative spectral ceiling, but collapses on clean fitting (46.97 dB on Tiger vs. FDHO's 63.79 dB). Fourier Features is competitive on audio (52.36 dB peak on Bach) but degrades on speech and high-frequency 1D signals. FR underperforms across all tasks, often below 25 dB, indicating its reparameterisation amplifies rather than suppresses noise-driven content.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends SIREN's sinusoidal activation by coupling its amplitude to frequency through the transfer function of a damped harmonic oscillator, so that the amplitude is not a free parameter but is physically constrained by the relationship between forcing frequency, natural frequency, and damping. It departs from the broader INR activation-design lineage (Gauss, WIRE, FINER, BACON, MFN) by deriving spectral control from a physical system rather than from hand-crafted spatial or frequency localisation, and from training-dynamics approaches (Fourier Features, FR, IGA-INR) by embedding the spectral gate directly in the activation's differentiable structure. The multi-layer frequency composition analysis extends Yüce et al.'s Bessel-function characterisation of SIREN layer stacking by making the modulation index learnable through the oscillator amplitude.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The gradient decomposition (Theorem 2) provides a formal, per-parameter explanation for why the network rejects noise while fitting coherent signal, which no prior INR activation offers; this could inform future work on principled regularisation in coordinate-based networks. The no-tuning-across-tasks property is practically valuable, though the method still trails on highly periodic textures (Tiles) and clean inpainting, confirming the theory's own prediction of where the gate provides least benefit. Open questions include extending to per-neuron oscillator parameters, formalising multi-layer gradient interactions, and whether the mechanism transfers to 3D/4D representations (NeRF, dynamic scenes) where spectral content is far richer.
