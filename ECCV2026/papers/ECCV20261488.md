## Problem

Omni-modal LLMs fundamentally fail to resolve 'Who said what and when' in multi-person conversations because existing benchmarks permit visual shortcut learning (central framing, visible microphones) and models rely on sparse 1–2 fps visual sampling that destroys high-frequency dynamics like lip movements and micro-expressions. This creates an 'illusion of competence' where high scores mask a severe architectural perception gap in genuine cross-modal spatio-temporal identity binding.

## Input / Output

The model consumes raw video (dual-rate: 1–2 fps for spatial context, 25 fps for temporal dynamics), a continuous audio waveform, and natural-language identity registration queries (e.g., 'Alice: a woman with long curly hair')—no acoustic enrollment is required. For the holistic VR-SDR task, it outputs structured per-speaker records pairing identity labels, precise timestamps, and transcribed content (e.g., 'Alice: our first topic is family... [0.00–6.39s]'); atomic subtasks additionally yield face-coordinate localization, multiple-choice identification labels, and binary verification decisions.

## Method

The core architectural contribution is a Visual Delta Encoder that samples raw video at 25 fps and compresses inter-frame motion residuals into only 6 structured tokens per frame via a Structured Visual Tokenizer (7×7 spatial and k=63 temporal convolutions over a ResNet-18 backbone, followed by a Transformer encoder for cross-frame context). This dual-stream design pairs the delta encoder with a low-frequency Visual Base Encoder (shared with Qwen2.5-Omni) so that spatial identity features and high-frequency temporal dynamics (visemes, speaker trajectories) are explicitly separated yet jointly fed into a shared LLM decoder. Training follows a three-stage progressive pipeline: (1) multi-task pre-training of the delta encoder on speaker-centric data (face-center regression + VSR/AVSR objectives from CoGenAV), (2) modality-isolated alignment where only an MLP projector is trained while the LLM sees exclusively delta-encoder features, and (3) end-to-end joint fine-tuning on ~1.5 M speaker-centric instructions with full fine-tuning of early LLM layers and LoRA on deeper layers. This residual-encoding strategy captures fine-grained lip visemes and continuous spatial trajectories without the quadratic token explosion that naive high-fps ViT sampling would incur, enabling end-to-end lip-reading and speaker localization directly from raw video without face-cropping pre-processing.

## Results

On the HumanOmni-Speaker Benchmark, HumanOmni-Speaker (3B) achieves a Holistic AVG of 37.8% (SA-WER 47.1%, IER 28.5% on VR-SDR Hard), competitive with the closed-source Gemini3-Pro (36.5%) while dramatically outperforming all open-source Omni models. The model achieves a near-perfect 0.8% error on Speaker Localization (vs. 12.8% for Gemini3-Pro and 2.8% for Qwen3-Omni-flash) and 21.1% on Speaker Identification Hard. On VSR/AVSR, it reaches 0.76% WER on LRS3 AVSR and 33.4% on LRS3 VSR from raw video without any lip-cropping preprocessing, matching or exceeding dedicated VSR models like Auto-AVSR (33.0% LRS3 VSR).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the closed-source Gemini3-Pro, HumanOmni-Speaker wins decisively on Speaker Localization (0.8% vs. 12.8%) and Speaker Identification Hard (21.1% vs. 30.5%), but loses on VR-SDR What (47.1% vs. 36.6% SA-WER) and overall Holistic AVG (37.8% vs. 36.5%), indicating Gemini3-Pro retains an edge in full cross-modal diarization. Against open-source Omni models, HumanOmni-Speaker dominates across the board: it reduces Speaker Verification error from 37–51% (Qwen2.5-Omni, OLA, VITA1.5) to 13.2%, cuts Speaker Localization from 7–21% to 0.8%, and lowers VR-SDR SA-WER from 83–95% to 47.1%. Compared to its own Qwen2.5-Omni-SFT baseline (without the Visual Delta Encoder), the full model reduces SI-Hard error from 33.2% to 21.1% and VR-SDR IER from 31.5% to 28.5%, confirming the Visual Delta Encoder's contribution. On VSR/AVSR (Table 3), HumanOmni-Speaker matches Auto-AVSR on LRS3 VSR (33.4% vs. 33.0%) without any lip-cropping, and outperforms LLM-based AVSR models like Llama-AVSR (0.77%) and Whisper-Flamingo (0.76%) on LRS3 AVSR with a competitive 0.76%. On LibriSpeech ASR (Table 4), it achieves 1.88% test-clean WER, slightly beating Qwen2.5-Omni-3B (2.2%) and matching Qwen2-Audio (1.6%) with fewer parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Qwen2.5-Omni as its architectural substrate, retaining its Visual Base Encoder, Audio Encoder, and LLM backbone while adding a novel Visual Delta Encoder (ResNet-18 + Structured Visual Tokenizer) that operates at 25 fps to capture inter-frame motion residuals. It departs from the dominant sparse-sampling paradigm (1–2 fps) in existing Omni-models by arguing that high-frequency dynamics like visemes are essential for speaker-centric tasks, and from prior speaker research that decomposes 'Who/When/What' into siloed unimodal tasks by introducing the unified VR-SDR formulation with natural-language visual registration. The pre-training objectives draw on CoGenAV's contrastive-generative synchronization framework, and the end-to-end design explicitly contrasts with cascaded pipelines (detection → cropping → classification) that the paper shows to be inferior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The VR-SDR benchmark and its Easy/Hard partitioning provide the field with a rigorous diagnostic tool that exposes the 'illusion of competence' in current Omni-models—models that score well on Easy sets collapse on Hard sets, confirming heavy reliance on visual shortcuts rather than genuine cross-modal alignment. The Visual Delta Encoder's 6-token-per-frame compression at 25 fps offers a practical, low-overhead recipe (+38% tokens vs. >10× for naive high-fps ViT) that could be adopted by other Omni architectures needing fine-grained temporal perception. However, the 47.1% SA-WER on VR-SDR and the substantial degradation with 3+ speakers indicate that true multi-party spatio-temporal binding remains far from solved, and the paper's 3B scale limits conclusions about whether the approach scales with model size.
