## Problem

Long-term traffic simulation faces a persistent trade-off between modeling agent population dynamics (spawning/exiting) and interaction-aware motion generation, with existing unified approaches falling significantly behind specialized short-term models. Additionally, standard evaluation metrics rely on strict one-to-one agent matching that breaks down over extended rollouts, and prior workarounds using global distribution matching introduce severe statistical bias. The paper asks whether large-scale sequence models (LLMs) can serve as effective structural priors for traffic dynamics without linguistic supervision, and how to evaluate long-horizon simulation fairly.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a variable-length structured token sequence comprising agent tokens (position, heading, velocity encoded via a scene encoder), map tokens (road network geometry from a pretrained map encoder), and layout tokens (discretized grid and yaw indices of the intermediate motion-updated scene). Output is two-part: first, parallel motion logits for all existing agents (discretized motion tokens), then an autoregressive spawn sequence of (type, grid, yaw) triplets terminated by an EOS token, yielding a dynamically sized set of new agents. The token cardinality changes every timestep as agents enter and exit, which is the key structural difference from fixed-agent short-term simulators.

## Method

RosettaSim leverages a small, heavily frozen LLM (Qwen2.5-0.5B) as the autoregressive backbone, justified by the empirical finding that discretized traffic motion tokens follow Zipf's law with near-identical frequency distributions to natural language, and that attention locality patterns transfer across domains. The architecture decomposes scene evolution into two phases: (1) Parallel Motion Update, where learnable motion tokens attend to agent tokens through the LLM to predict all existing agents' next states simultaneously; and (2) Autoregressive Agent Generation, where new agents are spawned sequentially (type → grid → yaw) conditioned on the motion-updated scene, map tokens, and layout tokens, with sinusoidal position embeddings encoding agent identity throughout. Training is single-stage with a simple causal cross-entropy loss over the concatenated token sequence, avoiding the complex weighted losses or multi-stage pipelines of prior work. The paper further introduces RTE, a retrieval-based evaluation framework that encodes rollout segments into a pretrained VAE latent space and retrieves top-K semantically similar real-world scenarios via Wasserstein distance as context-aware reference anchors, yielding a new RMM-F1 metric that correlates more strongly (r=0.83) with standard WOSAC metrics than prior log-based approaches (r=0.74).

## Results

On the standard 8-second WOSAC 2025 benchmark, RosettaSim achieves a composite score of 0.7833, surpassing all non-closed-loop baselines and establishing SOTA among versatile simulators with dynamic agent insertion. On long-term (31.1 s) simulation, RosettaSim achieves a RMM-F1 of 0.7624, outperforming InfGen (0.7290), CAT-K (0.7381), and SMART (0.7383). The proposed RTE evaluation metric shows a Pearson correlation of r=0.83 with standard WOSAC metrics, compared to r=0.74 for prior log-based approaches, confirming its validity as a long-horizon fidelity measure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the short-term WOSAC 2025 benchmark, RosettaSim (0.7833 composite) outperforms all baselines except CAT-K (0.7846, which uses expensive closed-loop RL tuning) and UniMM (0.7829, a narrow 0.0004 margin). Among versatile simulators capable of dynamic agent insertion, RosettaSim leads by a large margin over SceneStreamer (0.7731) and GUMP (0.7431). On long-term simulation, RosettaSim dominates all three baselines on RMM-F1 (0.7624 vs. 0.7290–0.7383), with the largest gap in Traffic Flow Realism (0.7846 vs. 0.7175–0.7637), confirming its unified architecture handles population dynamics better. Notably, RosettaSim achieves the best Kinematic score (0.9176) in long-term evaluation, while CAT-K and SMART score higher on Interactive metrics (0.8256/0.8280 vs. 0.7663) because they do not spawn new agents, reducing collision opportunities and inflating interaction scores. The ablation further shows that even a fully frozen Qwen2.5-0.5B backbone (0.7638) outperforms the SMART baseline (0.7631), and fine-tuning only the last layer (0.7648) nearly matches full fine-tuning (0.7658), demonstrating that the pretrained structural prior—not model scale or linguistic semantics—drives the gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

RosettaSim builds directly on SMART's [40] pretrained transformer as a scene encoder and adopts InfGen's [46] grid/yaw tokenization for spatial discretization, but departs fundamentally by inserting a frozen or minimally-fine-tuned LLM (Qwen2.5-0.5B) as the autoregressive backbone rather than training a purpose-built sequence model from scratch. Unlike SceneStreamer [27] and InfGen [46], which jointly optimize scene generation and motion generation and suffer a persistent performance trade-off, RosettaSim unifies both into a single structured autoregressive stream where parallel motion updates and sequential agent spawning share the same LLM attention layers. The paper also introduces RTE, a retrieval-based evaluation protocol that departs from the global-distribution-matching approach used by prior long-term simulators [34, 46], grounding metric computation in semantically similar real-world segments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work addresses two practical gaps in autonomous-driving simulation: the inability of current methods to maintain both kinematic fidelity and population dynamics over trip-level horizons, and the lack of a context-aware evaluation protocol for long rollouts. The finding that a 0.5B frozen LLM matches or exceeds a fully trained baseline, and that scaling to 3B provides no benefit, suggests the structural 'grammar' of traffic is simple enough to be captured by minimal fine-tuning of general sequence priors—a result that could reduce training costs for related sequential-generation tasks. Open questions include whether the Zipf's-law alignment is truly causal (the corruption experiment is suggestive but not conclusive), whether the LLM prior advantage persists on more diverse or larger-scale datasets, and how the heuristic agent-removal strategy affects long-horizon realism.
