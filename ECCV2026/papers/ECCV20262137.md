## Problem

Attention-based MIL methods often produce attention maps that highlight spuriously correlated regions rather than truly discriminative evidence, and they only reveal evidence supporting the predicted class while ignoring contradicting evidence. This limits both predictive reliability and interpretability, particularly in medical applications where attention maps justify automated decisions. The paper identifies that counterfactual reasoning, which characterizes predictions through minimal input changes that alter the outcome, has not been integrated into MIL training to explicitly guide how attention should be allocated.

## Input / Output

The method consumes bags of pre-extracted patch features (256×256 patches at 20× magnification from whole-slide images, encoded by a shared encoder such as UNI-V1 or ResNet50) and produces a bag-level class prediction alongside two complementary attention maps: a factual map highlighting regions that support the prediction and a counterfactual map revealing regions whose reweighting would challenge it. The counterfactual branch is a lightweight attention module that shares both the encoder and the downstream classifier with the factual branch, so the only learned difference between the two paths is the attention weighting.

## Method

CAR-MIL introduces a two-branch architecture where a factual attention module and a counterfactual attention module operate on the same instance embeddings but with independently optimized weights, while sharing the encoder and classifier. Training is governed by three complementary losses: (i) standard cross-entropy on the factual branch prediction, (ii) an evidence differential loss that applies cross-entropy to the logit difference ΔF = F(u) − F(u_cf), encouraging the two branches to diverge primarily on the ground-truth class, and (iii) a proximity regularization (normalized L1 or cosine distance) that constrains counterfactual attention logits to remain close to factual ones. This forces prediction shifts to arise from minimal, structured redistributions of attention across instances rather than arbitrary reweighting. The counterfactual attention thus learns to identify which instances, if reweighted, would flip the prediction, providing a learned contrastive signal that guides the factual attention toward truly discriminative evidence during training.

## Results

On five histopathology WSI datasets, CAR-MIL (cosine variant) achieves the best or near-best AUC across all tasks: 95.0 on BRCA, 97.5 on NSCLC, 78.1 on LUAD, 43.1 BACC on BRACS, and 99.3 on CAMELYON16. The largest gains over the ABMIL baseline appear on the more challenging LUAD (78.1 vs. 74.0 AUC, +4.1) and BRACS (43.1 vs. 38.2 BACC, +4.9) tasks. On the synthetic MNIST-bags benchmark, CAR-MIL substantially improves instance-level evidence alignment (AUPRC± = 84.6 on Adjacent Pairs vs. 61.5 for ABMIL; 89.7 on Four Bags vs. 53.1), while maintaining competitive bag-level AUC. Paired t-tests confirm that no attention-based MIL baseline significantly outperforms CAR-MIL on any dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CAR-MIL (cosine) outperforms the ABMIL baseline on the two most challenging tasks—LUAD TP53 mutation prediction (78.1 vs. 74.0 AUC, +4.1) and BRACS multiclass subtyping (43.1 vs. 38.2 BACC, +4.9)—while matching or slightly exceeding it on the easier BRCA and NSCLC subtyping tasks. Against the strongest attention-based competitors, CAR-MIL (COS) leads TransMIL by 6.4 AUC on LUAD and 3.1 BACC on BRACS, and surpasses ACMIL by 2.6 AUC on LUAD and 1.9 BACC on BRACS. Compared to the closest counterfactual-based method CIA-MIL [8], CAR-MIL achieves higher AUC on LUAD (78.1 vs. 77.5) and substantially better BACC on BRACS (43.1 vs. 37.6), while also outperforming it on NSCLC F1 (95.0 vs. 93.0). On CAMELYON16, where patch-level annotations are well-defined, CAR-MIL (L1) attains the best AUPRC⁺ (94.8) among all methods, confirming that the counterfactual branch does not divert attention from diagnostically relevant regions. Paired t-tests confirm that no baseline significantly outperforms CAR-MIL on any dataset, and the L1 variant is competitive on binary subtyping while the cosine variant excels on harder multiclass and mutation-prediction tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

CAR-MIL builds directly on the standard ABMIL architecture (Ilse et al., 2018) and introduces a lightweight counterfactual attention branch that shares the encoder and classifier with the factual branch. Conceptually, it departs from post-hoc counterfactual explanations (Wachter et al., 2017) by integrating counterfactual reasoning into the training objective rather than applying it after training, and from prior counterfactual attention learning methods (CIA-MIL, Rao et al.) by learning structured attention perturbations rather than comparing against random attention. The key positioning is that attention in MIL serves a dual role—as both aggregation mechanism and interpretability proxy—and CAR-MIL is the first to explicitly regularize attention learning through a counterfactual objective that enforces prediction divergence under minimal attention redistribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For digital pathology and other weakly-supervised settings where attention maps are used to justify clinical decisions, CAR-MIL provides a principled way to obtain complementary 'supporting' and 'refuting' evidence maps without additional supervision or significant computational overhead. The method is encoder-agnostic (validated with UNI-V1 and ResNet50) and integrates into existing MIL architectures (DSMIL, TransMIL), making it a practical drop-in regularization. Open questions include whether the learned counterfactual attentions can be repurposed beyond training (e.g., as auxiliary supervision for weak-to-strong learning, active learning queries, or model debugging), and whether the approach extends to settings where instance-level interactions are more complex than what attention-based aggregation captures.
