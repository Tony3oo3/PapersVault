## Problem

Inversion-free rectified-flow editing methods such as FlowEdit struggle with global semantic modifications (e.g., uniform color or material shifts) because, in the high-noise regime, the dominant manifold-seeking transport toward the data manifold overwhelms the comparatively weak text-conditioned velocity difference. As a result, the target trajectory remains locked to the source structural prior during the early timesteps when coarse layout and global attributes are established, leaving insufficient directional momentum for large-scale semantic change by the time the prompt signal becomes stronger.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a source RGB image, a source text prompt, a target text prompt, and an optional binary spatial mask, and produces a single edited RGB image. It is fully training-free and operates on pre-trained rectified flow backbones (FLUX, SD3, SD3.5) using 28 Euler sampling steps with a configurable editing window (default n_min=0, n_max=24). The wavelet compensation is applied in the velocity (latent) space rather than the pixel space, and the only additional cost over vanilla FlowEdit is one extra network forward pass per active timestep to evaluate the target-prompt velocity at the source noisy latent.

## Method

The core mechanism is a wavelet-guided semantic signal compensation injected into the FlowEdit Euler update. First, a same-point semantic probing step evaluates both source and target prompt velocities at the identical source noisy latent x_t^src, yielding a co-located prompt-induced direction v_sem that is free from the geometric confound of evaluating at two distinct latent points. Second, an L-level 2D Haar discrete wavelet transform decomposes v_sem; all detail sub-bands (cH, cV, cD) at every level are zeroed while only the approximation coefficients (cA) are retained, and the inverse transform reconstructs a spatially coherent low-frequency component v_low. Third, this v_low is injected into the update rule with a quadratic time schedule λ·t², so the compensation is strongest at high noise (t≈1) where the geometric signal is least informative and vanishes at low noise (t≈0) to preserve fine-detail fidelity. The original geometric signal Δv_t remains fully active alongside the compensation term, ensuring that high-frequency textures and local attributes continue to be handled by the standard editing dynamics rather than being suppressed by the low-pass filter.

## Results

On PIE-Bench (700 images, FLUX backbone), the proposed method achieves the best CLIP similarity on both the full image (25.52) and the edited region (23.05), while also leading all four background-preservation metrics: PSNR 26.17, LPIPS 54.02×10⁻³, MSE 39.24×10⁻⁴, and SSIM 90.60×10⁻². The primary inversion-free baseline FlowEdit scores 25.39 / 22.62 on CLIP and 22.23 / 111.32×10⁻³ on PSNR / LPIPS, showing a substantial gap in both editing strength and background fidelity. The method's structural distance (DINO self-similarity) is 30.95×10⁻³, higher than the most structure-preserving baselines (DNA-Edit 16.83, FreeDiff 17.98), reflecting the intended trade-off of stronger global semantic deviation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary inversion-free baseline FlowEdit, the proposed method improves CLIP-Edited by 0.43 (23.05 vs 22.62) and CLIP-Whole by 0.13 (25.52 vs 25.39), while nearly halving LPIPS (54.02 vs 111.32) and MSE (39.24 vs 90.98) and raising PSNR from 22.23 to 26.17, indicating substantially better background preservation alongside stronger editing. Compared to the strongest structure-preserving baselines DNA-Edit (Struct. 16.83) and FreeDiff (Struct. 17.98), the proposed method trades structural distance (30.95) for markedly higher CLIP-Edited scores (23.05 vs 22.12 and 22.15) and better background metrics, suggesting the wavelet compensation enables global attribute shifts that those methods cannot achieve. Among inversion-based RF methods, RF-Inv performs worst on background preservation (PSNR 17.97, LPIPS 242.00), while RF-Edit and FireFlow show intermediate results; the proposed method outperforms all of them on both CLIP and background fidelity. In the user study (100 pairs, 10 participants), the method is preferred over FlowEdit 66% of the time, over FireFlow 71%, and over RF-Inv 64%, corroborating the quantitative advantage. The one consistent trade-off is the higher DINO structural distance, which the authors attribute to the intentionally stronger global semantic deviation that the wavelet compensation introduces during early timesteps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is a direct augmentation of FlowEdit, the leading inversion-free editing framework for rectified flow models, modifying its Euler update rule to inject a wavelet-filtered semantic signal during early timesteps. Conceptually, it departs from prior frequency-domain editing work (e.g., FreeDiff) by operating in velocity space with an injection objective rather than constraining the latent trajectory, and from other inversion-free methods (FlowAlign, DVRF) by anchoring the auxiliary evaluation at the source latent rather than the target latent. The 'same-point semantic probing' idea—evaluating both prompts at an identical latent state to isolate the prompt-induced direction from geometric confounds—is the key conceptual contribution that distinguishes the method from simply adding another velocity difference term.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and formalizes a concrete failure mode (semantic indistinguishability under manifold-seeking flow dominance) that practitioners of inversion-free editing will recognize, and offers a training-free, single-extra-forward-pass fix that is easy to adopt on top of existing FlowEdit pipelines. The quadratic time schedule and Haar wavelet decomposition are interpretable and ablated, giving practitioners clear knobs (λ, L) for tuning the edit-strength vs. background-fidelity trade-off. Open questions include whether the low-frequency compensation principle generalizes beyond color/attribute shifts to more complex structural edits, whether other wavelet families or learned frequency filters would outperform Haar, and whether the approach transfers to video or 3D generation where the manifold-seeking dynamics may differ.
