## Problem

Token insertion has become the dominant fusion mechanism for vision-language models, but its memory and compute costs grow linearly with the number of image tokens, making it impractical for long multi-image conversations and streaming video. Cross-attention (CA) avoids this by keeping image tokens out of the KV cache, yet prior CA-based VLMs have been reported to underperform token-insertion models, particularly on fine-grained document and chart understanding. The paper asks whether this gap reflects a fundamental limitation of CA or simply arises from differences in training data, scale, and implementation choices.

## Input / Output

The model consumes RGB images at native resolution up to 896² pixels (1024 tokens per image) and video frames sampled at 2 fps at up to 504² pixels (3-minute clips, up to 46,080 image tokens per sequence), interleaved with text prompts or audio transcripts. In streaming inference, each text token attends only to the most recent frame via cross-attention; all earlier frames are compressed into a single delimiter 'gist token' per frame that propagates through the text stream's self-attention, keeping the KV cache near-constant. Outputs are standard autoregressive text: QA answers, captions, or live video descriptions generated token-by-token.

## Method

The paper identifies five design differences (D1–D5) that progressively bridge CA and token insertion—parameter sharing, joint text-image attention, layer replacement, image-token updates through FFNs, and multi-image history—and shows that lightweight modifications (D1–D3) can even reduce compute overhead. Two controlled training settings are used: (i) jointly finetuning a text-only Helium1-2B LLM with added CA layers, and (ii) adapting a pretrained Qwen2.5-VL-3B by replacing its token-insertion mechanism with CA layers while finetuning the backbone at a reduced learning rate. CA layers are placed in parallel with self-attention layers within each transformer block, using FlashAttention-2's block-wise attention to enforce windowed locality during training via multimodal sequence packing. The key finding is that vanilla CA, without gating, register tokens, or dedicated FFN updates, narrows the gap to token insertion to only ~1.5–6.8% on most benchmarks and outperforms larger prior CA-based VLMs, while maintaining near-constant memory and latency for streaming video captioning.

## Results

When trained from the Helium1-2B text-only LLM under identical conditions, the vanilla cross-attention (CA) model (3B params, 0.13T tokens) achieves 77.5 on ChartQA, 86.4 on DocVQA, and 1703 on MME, within ~1.5% average of the token-insertion counterpart (79.2/88.3/1626). The largest remaining gap is on InfographicVQA (55.1 vs. 58.9), which requires understanding complex high-resolution figures. When adapting the pretrained Qwen2.5-VL-3B to CA in just 25k steps, the model retains 81.8 on ChartQA and 87.9 on DocVQA (vs. 84.0/93.0 for the base), a 6.8% average drop, while gaining near-constant memory and 6× higher FPS at streaming inference.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The CA model (3B) substantially outperforms prior cross-attention VLMs: it beats mPLUG-Owl3-7B (8B) by 18.3 points on ChartQA (77.5 vs. 59.2), 30.5 on DocVQA (86.4 vs. 55.9), and 18.3 on InfoVQA (55.1 vs. 36.8), while also surpassing mPLUG-Owl3-2B by 29+ points on document tasks. Against StreamChat (8B), CA wins on TextVQA (75.4 vs. 72.4) and MME (1703 vs. 1520), though StreamChat leads on AI2D (76.6 vs. 66.0) and GQA (62.4 vs. 55.7). Compared to the token-insertion baseline trained under identical conditions (InsertHe-2B), CA trails by only 1.7 on ChartQA, 1.9 on DocVQA, and 3.8 on InfoVQA, with a 1.5% average gap across all nine benchmarks. On the Qwen2.5-VL adaptation (Table 3), the CA variant retains 81.8/87.9 on ChartQA/DocVQA versus 84.0/93.0 for the base model, with the largest drop on InfoVQA (58.2 vs. 77.1). For live video captioning on LiveSports3K, the 3B CA model reaches 39.4% winrate at 17k steps, approaching the 7B LiveCC-7B-Base (43.2%) despite being 2× smaller. On video benchmarks (Table 4), CA_t+v (180 frames) scores 59.0 on VideoMME and 79.8 on NExT-QA, matching or exceeding mPLUG-Owl3-7B (59.8/82.1) while using far less memory.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper revisits cross-attention (CA) as a vision-language fusion mechanism, a design popularized by Flamingo and subsequently explored in StreamChat, mPLUG-Owl3, and EVLM, but largely abandoned in favor of token insertion in recent SOTA VLMs (Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL2.5, VideoLLaMA3). It departs from prior CA work by systematically identifying five design differences (D1–D5) that bridge CA and token insertion, and by training under a modern pipeline (multimodal sequence packing, block-wise FlashAttention-2, recent public datasets) that prior CA models lacked. The paper also directly adapts a pretrained token-insertion VLM (Qwen2.5-VL-3B) to CA, a conversion path not previously explored, and demonstrates that the reported CA–insertion gap was largely an artifact of training methodology rather than a fundamental architectural limitation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical efficiency gains are substantial: CA maintains near-constant memory and ~6× higher frame throughput during streaming video inference compared to token insertion, making real-time long-horizon video captioning feasible where insertion-based models exhaust their KV cache. The finding that vanilla CA (without gating, register tokens, or FFN-based image updates) narrows the gap to insertion to ~1.5% on average challenges the field's working assumption that CA is architecturally inferior, redirecting attention to training pipeline quality as the dominant factor. A notable limitation remains on fine-grained infographic understanding (InfoVQA), where a 15–20 pp gap persists even with image-token updates, suggesting token insertion retains an advantage for tasks requiring iterative refinement of high-resolution visual representations. Open questions include whether this residual gap can be closed with more training or architectural tweaks, and whether the 'gist token' mechanism for compressing visual history generalizes to longer or more complex video streams.
