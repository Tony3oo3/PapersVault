## Problem

Open-set semi-supervised learning (OSSL) faces a recurring trade-off: filtering-based methods risk discarding informative but hard in-distribution samples (feature starvation), while utilization-based methods risk introducing auxiliary gradients that directly oppose the supervised objective when pseudo-labels are wrong. The paper argues this dilemma is fundamentally a gradient-conflict problem rather than a sample-selection problem, and that robustness can be achieved through geometric control of optimization even when OOD/ID decisions are imperfect.

## Input / Output

The method operates as a plug-in on top of any base OSSL algorithm, consuming the same RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 224×224 for ImageNet-30) with a labeled set of K seen classes and an unlabeled pool contaminated by OOD samples. It produces standard class labels for closed-set evaluation and class-label-or-unknown predictions for open-set evaluation (where all unseen classes are collapsed into a single unknown category, scored by Balanced Accuracy). No modification to the forward pass, loss definitions, or pseudo-labeling mechanism is required; GGR only intervenes on the backward-pass gradients before the optimizer step.

## Method

GGR enforces a first-order non-opposition constraint by projecting the raw auxiliary gradient g_u onto the closed half-space H_safe = {d : ⟨d, g_s⟩ ≥ 0} defined by the supervised gradient g_s, yielding a closed-form rectified direction that removes only the component of g_u opposing g_s while preserving orthogonal signals. Three rectifiers share this principle: VLR (vector-level, the default), OSR (orthogonal subspace, which removes the entire component of g_u lying in a low-dimensional supervised subspace U maintained via periodic Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization), and CSR (conic subspace, which clips only negative coordinates in the U basis, retaining positively aligned components). The framework is strictly asymmetric—the supervised gradient is never altered—and is applied post-hoc in gradient space over a configurable surgery scope (backbone, head, or both), adding roughly 1–11% wall-clock overhead. Theoretical analysis proves that the rectified update guarantees one-step descent of the supervised loss on the rectified coordinate block under a suitable step size, and a stylized mixture model shows GGR matches or exceeds filtering in expected progress while preserving positive aligned components.

## Results

GGR is a plug-in gradient rectification module that improves representative OSSL baselines across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet-30. On CIFAR-10 (6/4 split, 5 labels/class), IOMatch+GGR reaches 91.87% closed-set accuracy vs. 89.87% for IOMatch alone (+2.00 points), and DAC+GGR reaches 78.66% open-set BA vs. 77.48% for DAC on the same setting. On ImageNet-30 (20/10 split, 1% labels), DAC+GGR achieves 87.40% closed-set and 78.40% open-set BA, improving over DAC by +1.00 and +0.62 points respectively. Gains are largest in low-label regimes where pseudo-label noise and ID/OOD ambiguity are most severe.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GGR consistently improves all three OSSL baselines it is applied to (OpenMatch, IOMatch, DAC) in most settings. The largest gains appear in low-label regimes: on CIFAR-10 6/4 with 5 labels, IOMatch+GGR gains +2.00 points closed-set (91.87 vs. 89.87) and +1.63 points open-set BA (74.13 vs. 72.50); DAC+GGR gains +1.70 closed-set and +1.70 open-set BA. On the more challenging CIFAR-100 50/50 split with 5 labels, IOMatch+GGR improves closed-set by +1.12 (60.61 vs. 59.49) and open-set BA by +1.19 (49.93 vs. 48.74). On ImageNet-30 at 5% labels, OpenMatch+GGR shows the most dramatic open-set improvement, jumping from 61.51% to 68.15% BA (+6.64), while DAC+GGR gains +0.93 closed-set and +0.93 open-set. GGR does not outperform the strongest standard SSL baselines (e.g., FixMatch at 93.47% on CIFAR-10 6/4 with 25 labels) but is designed as a complementary plug-in rather than a standalone method, and it consistently adds gains on top of the best OSSL-specific baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant OSSL paradigms of sample-level filtering (OpenMatch, DAC, UAGreg) and utilization (IOMatch, MTCF) by reframing the core dilemma as a gradient-conflict problem rather than a detection problem. Conceptually, it adapts the gradient surgery idea from PCGrad (multi-task learning) but makes it strictly asymmetric: the supervised gradient is treated as a fixed anchor and only the auxiliary gradient is projected, whereas PCGrad treats both tasks symmetrically. The subspace-aware variants (OSR, CSR) extend this with temporal smoothing via a rolling orthonormal basis, drawing on subspace projection ideas from ProSub but applying them to the optimization trajectory rather than feature-space OOD scoring.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GGR offers a lightweight, plug-in optimization control that is orthogonal to whatever OOD detection or pseudo-labeling strategy a base OSSL method uses, making it broadly composable. The first-order non-interference guarantee and the stylized mixture-model analysis provide a clean theoretical lens for understanding why naive utilization can be worse than filtering when pseudo-labels are noisy. However, the guarantees are local and single-step; the paper acknowledges that GGR does not ensure global convergence of the composite objective and is complementary to, not a replacement for, better OOD modeling. Open questions include whether the approach extends to non-convex or multi-step settings, whether it interacts synergistically with stronger uncertainty estimation, and whether the modest 1–2 point gains persist under more extreme label scarcity or larger-scale benchmarks beyond ImageNet-30.
