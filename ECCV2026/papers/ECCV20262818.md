## Problem

Trajectory-steering methods for safe text-to-image generation rely on blacklists of unsafe-concept keywords, yet their design has been largely overlooked: current blacklists are hand-curated, their effectiveness is poorly understood, and common assumptions (longer phrases, larger lists, mixing categories) are empirically wrong. The paper identifies that blacklist effectiveness is governed by how well keywords cover unsafe regions in the CLIP embedding space, not by individual keyword quality or list size, and asks how to automate blacklist construction to optimize this coverage.

## Input / Output

At training, the method takes a frozen diffusion model, a trajectory-steering method, and safety benchmarks (I2P, P4D, Ring-A-Bell, MMA-Diffusion, UnlearnDiffAtk) to produce compact category-specific blacklists of M keywords each, selected from a larger LLM-generated pool of N keywords. At inference, a single text prompt is encoded with CLIP and routed via cosine similarity to the most relevant category blacklist, which is then applied as negative guidance within the existing steering procedure to produce a safe image. The RL component operates on a 5-token sequence generated by a small MLP head on frozen DistilGPT-2, while keyword expansion uses Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct; all selection and routing share the same CLIP text encoder used at guidance time.

## Method

MAPS is a three-stage pipeline that optimizes blacklist coverage in CLIP embedding space. First, soft Q-learning (following RLPrompt) trains a policy to discover a 5-token sequence that, inserted into an LLM prompt template, elicits semantically diverse safety keywords; the reward is the safe rate (1 − ASR) of images generated with those keywords as negative guidance. Second, the optimized token drives an LLM to produce a large candidate keyword pool per unsafe category, spanning slang, euphemisms, multilingual variants, and descriptive phrases. Third, a greedy farthest-point (max-min diversity) selection in the CLIP embedding space distills the pool into a compact blacklist that maximizes minimum pairwise distance, explicitly suppressing near-duplicates and maximizing spatial coverage of the unsafe region. At inference, the prompt is routed to the single most relevant category blacklist via cosine similarity, avoiding the interference from unrelated concepts that the ablations show actively degrades safety. The entire framework is a drop-in enhancement: it modifies neither the diffusion model nor the steering procedure, reducing attack success rates by 25–52% relative across five benchmarks while preserving or improving FID and CLIP scores on COCO-30k.

## Results

MAPS reduces attack success rate (ASR) by 25–52% (relative) across five adversarial benchmarks when applied as a drop-in enhancement to trajectory-steering methods. Under SLD-Medium, ASR drops from 2.53% to 1.21% on I2P (52% relative reduction), from 33.0% to 21.5% on P4D, and from 87.71% to 65.7% on Ring-A-Bell (25% relative reduction). On I2P, SLD-Max+Ours achieves 0.15% ASR, outperforming all training-based methods including MACE (2.3%). Generation quality is preserved or improved: SLD-Weak+Ours improves FID from 32.80 to 30.12 and CLIP score from 32.2 to 34.2 on COCO-30k.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MAPS is a drop-in blacklist optimizer that enhances existing trajectory-steering methods (SLD, TraSCE, SAFREE) without modifying the model. It consistently reduces ASR across all five benchmarks for every steering baseline: SLD-Medium improves by 52% on I2P and 25% on Ring-A-Bell; TraSCE improves from 0.39% to 0.21% on I2P and 8.6% to 5.3% on Ring-A-Bell; SAFREE improves from 38.4% to 26.12% on P4D. On I2P, SLD-Max+Ours (0.15%) beats all training-based methods including MACE (2.3%) and RECE (6.4%), but on P4D, Ring-A-Bell, MMA-Diffusion, and UnlearnDiffAtk, MACE still achieves lower ASR (e.g., 14.6% vs. 50.20% on P4D), reflecting the advantage of full model retraining over inference-time steering. The key trade-off is that MAPS requires no retraining, no model modification, and preserves or improves generation quality (e.g., SLD-Weak+Ours: FID 30.12 vs. 32.80, CLIP 34.2 vs. 32.2), whereas training-based methods like MACE degrade FID to 52.24. Among trajectory-steering baselines, TraSCE+Ours achieves the lowest absolute ASR on I2P (0.21%) and P4D (3.24%), making it the strongest combination when paired with MAPS.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a complementary layer atop existing trajectory-steering methods (SLD, TraSCE, SAFREE), which it treats as fixed and enhances via optimized blacklists rather than modifying the model or the steering procedure. It borrows the RL-based discrete prompt optimization paradigm from RLPrompt but repurposes the reward signal from task accuracy to a safety metric (attack success rate), and applies farthest-point selection from diversity heuristics to the blacklist-construction problem. The key conceptual departure from prior work is treating the blacklist not as a fixed, hand-curated input but as an independent optimization variable governed by embedding-space coverage, a design dimension the authors argue has been overlooked despite being critical to the effectiveness of both steering and concept-erasure methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By establishing embedding-space coverage as the governing principle for blacklist effectiveness, the paper provides a geometric rationale for why hand-curated lists fail and offers a principled, automated alternative that practitioners can adopt as a drop-in without retraining. The finding that irrelevant categories and longer phrases actively degrade safety is actionable guidance for teams maintaining production safety filters. However, the evaluation is limited to two unsafe categories (nudity, violence), a single diffusion model (SD v1.4), and CLIP-based embeddings; extending the coverage principle to broader harm taxonomies, other text-image encoders, and newer diffusion architectures remains open, as does understanding how the approach interacts with adaptive or multi-turn jailbreak attacks.
