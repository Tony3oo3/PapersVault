## Problem

Existing biomedical VLM benchmarks cover only a narrow set of modalities and tasks, lack the structured metadata needed to stratify performance across meaningful axes (specimen, institution, stain, acquisition protocol), and suffer from data contamination because their training sets are frequently reused in model pretraining. This makes it difficult to assess whether reported accuracy reflects genuine visual perception or memorization, and to characterize how models generalize across the full breadth of biomedical imaging.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261424/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each MMBU sample pairs a biomedical image (standardized to PNG from source formats including DICOM, NIfTI, SVS, OME-TIFF, etc.) with a text question prompt constructed from up to 13 structured metadata fields (modality, submodality, specimen, stain, anatomical site, institution, etc.). Outputs depend on task and format: closed-ended VQA requires selecting from a candidate pool, open-ended VQA requires free-form text generation, and object detection requires predicting or selecting a bounding box (scored at IoU ≥ 0.5). The benchmark spans 410 datasets across 11 modalities, 35 submodalities, 20 specimens, and 95 regions of interest, yielding 24 evaluation configurations (4 task types × 2 formats × 3 question templates).

## Method

MMBU is built through a four-stage, human-in-the-loop pipeline: (1) a multidisciplinary committee (clinicians, wet-lab specialists, bioinformaticians) designs a multi-granularity taxonomy refined over four iterations; (2) three rounds of data discovery and filtering—expert curation, automated repository search, and expert quality review—yield 410 contamination-free datasets; (3) a standardization pipeline harmonizes heterogeneous file formats and extracts/corrects structured metadata; (4) questions are generated from three template types (No Context, Modality, Full Context) that condition on metadata fields, then validated by a three-expert review team. Evaluation uses micro-averaged F1 with 1,000-iteration bootstrap 95% CIs, a deterministic hierarchical parser for closed VQA scoring, and Qwen3-32B as a strict biomedical semantic-equivalence judge for open VQA. Seventeen VLMs (15 open-weight, 2 frontier) are assessed across all 24 configurations, with each medically adapted model compared head-to-head against its base counterpart.

## Results

Across 17 VLMs evaluated on MMBU's four core perception tasks, absolute F1 scores remain low: the best closed-format result is 0.693 (Qwen2.5-VL-32B on grounded classification from segmentation), while most scores fall below 0.5. The closed-to-open gap averages 0.26 for classification tasks, indicating heavy reliance on answer-choice cues rather than genuine visual understanding. Object detection is a critical failure mode: no VLM surpasses the random baseline (F1 = 0.172) in the closed setting, and open-setting detection performance is near zero for most models. Medically adapted models show only modest gains over their base counterparts, with an average F1 lead of just 0.08 when stratified by submodality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

No single model dominates all four tasks: GPT-4.1-mini leads ungrounded classification (0.539), Qwen2.5-VL-32B leads grounded classification from segmentation (0.693), and InternVL3.5-8B leads grounded classification from detection (0.439). Among medically adapted models, MedGemma-4B and OctoMed-7B consistently outperform their base counterparts (Gemma-3-4B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B respectively), with MedGemma-4B showing 18.7% wins vs. 13.4% losses in head-to-head comparisons. In contrast, LLaVA-Med-7B underperforms its base LLaVA-v1.5-7B across all tasks (e.g., 0.080 vs. 0.299 on grounded classification from segmentation), and Lingshu-32B degrades on MMBU relative to legacy benchmarks despite gains on PathVQA/VQA-RAD/SLAKE. Larger model variants generally outperform smaller ones within the same family (Qwen2.5-VL-32B > 7B > 3B; Lingshu-32B > 7B), but the gains are modest and task-dependent. Object detection remains a universal failure: even Qwen2.5-VL-32B, which explicitly supports detection, reaches only 0.092 F1 in the closed setting, well below the random baseline of 0.172.

## Relation to Prior Work

MMBU extends the line of biomedical VQA benchmarks (PathVQA, VQA-RAD, SLAKE, GMAI-MMBench, OmniMedVQA) by dramatically broadening coverage from 1–12 submodalities to 35, adding object detection and segmentation-derived tasks alongside classification, and introducing 13 structured metadata attributes per sample for stratified analysis. It departs from prior work in two key ways: it explicitly excludes datasets commonly used in medical VLM pretraining to avoid data contamination, and it provides both closed- and open-ended formulations of each task to disentangle genuine visual perception from answer-choice cue exploitation. The metadata-driven question construction pipeline (collecting specimen, modality, stain, anatomical site, etc.) is a methodological departure from prior benchmarks that reuse generic or dataset-native questions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that medically adapted models tie their base counterparts in over 70% of cases and that no VLM surpasses random performance on closed object detection challenges the prevailing assumption that biomedical fine-tuning yields broad perceptual gains. The cross-dataset generalization results—where models improve on legacy benchmarks (PathVQA, VQA-RAD, SLAKE) but not on MMBU—suggest current adaptation strategies overfit to a narrow set of commonly used datasets rather than building transferable visual perception. Open questions include how to develop spatial reasoning capabilities in VLMs for biomedical imaging, whether larger pretraining data (rather than model size) is the key lever for adaptation success, and whether the benchmark's 410-dataset scale is sufficient to capture the full heterogeneity of real clinical and laboratory imaging workflows.
