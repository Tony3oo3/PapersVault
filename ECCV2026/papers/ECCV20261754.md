## Problem

Current 3D scene editing methods condition image generation on RGB or depth renderings, which keeps edits tightly coupled to the original geometry and prevents substantial structural changes (e.g., turning a bicycle into a motorcycle). No existing approach provides a principled mechanism to control the level of semantic abstraction—how closely an edit should adhere to the original scene content versus following the text prompt—making it impossible to dial in the degree of modification.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained 3DGS scene, a text prompt, and optionally user-marked object regions in one or more views for local edits. TASE features are extracted by passing rendered views through a frozen DINOv3 backbone and a lightweight autoencoder, producing 64-channel patch-wise embeddings at the same spatial resolution as the backbone output. The output is a modified 3DGS (updated means, scales, rotations, opacities, and SH coefficients) plus per-Gaussian semantic feature vectors enabling open-vocabulary segmentation. Editing is iterative: each round samples new camera poses, generates novel views via the ControlNet, and re-optimizes the Gaussians, with the number of retained TASE channels scheduled to increase over iterations.

## Method

TASE projects pretrained DINOv3 features into a channel-ordered embedding space via a symmetric autoencoder trained with Matryoshka representation learning, so that masking suffix channels yields progressively more abstract semantics while retaining all channels preserves fine-grained detail. A scale- and translation-consistency loss (inspired by DVT) is added during autoencoder training to strip 2D positional bias, making the embeddings multi-view consistent when fused into 3DGS. For editing, a ControlNet-style flow matching model (FLUX.1-based) is conditioned on the TASE feature maps to generate novel views, and the 3DGS parameters are optimized to match these views using standard photometric losses plus a feature reconstruction loss and a Mahalanobis-distance regularization. A Difix3D+-inspired finetuning stage trains the generation model on corrupted/clean image triplets (created by perturbing AnySplat-generated 3DGS scenes) to reduce artifacts from large geometric changes. The editing loop schedules the number of retained channels from low (abstract, prompt-driven) to high (specific, scene-faithful) across iterations, providing explicit control over edit strength.

## Results

On local 3D scene editing, TASE achieves the highest CLIP Directional Similarity score of 0.1542, outperforming EditSplat (0.1492), DGE (0.1247), GaussianEditor (0.1179), and DreamCatalyst (0.0985). In a user study comparing geometry and appearance quality, TASE is preferred 64.3% of the time for geometry (vs. 19.1% for DGE and 16.7% for GaussianEditor) and 72.5% for appearance (vs. 18.7% and 8.8% respectively). The advantage is most pronounced on edits requiring substantial geometric changes, such as converting a bicycle into a motorcycle, where baselines largely fail to modify geometry and only shift colors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TASE outperforms all four baselines on CLIP Directional Similarity, with the largest margin over DreamCatalyst (+0.0557) and GaussianEditor (+0.0363), and a smaller but consistent lead over EditSplat (+0.0050) and DGE (+0.0295). In the user study (conducted only against DGE and GaussianEditor), TASE is overwhelmingly preferred: 64.3% vs. 19.1% for geometry and 72.5% vs. 18.7% for appearance over DGE, and 64.3% vs. 16.7% for geometry and 72.5% vs. 8.8% for appearance over GaussianEditor. The paper highlights that the gap widens specifically on edits demanding large geometric modifications (e.g., bicycle → motorcycle, bear → different animal), where RGB- or depth-conditioned baselines struggle to alter geometry and instead only shift colors toward the target. EditSplat, which uses multi-view fusion and attention-guided optimization, is the closest competitor on CLIP dir. sim. but was not included in the user study. DreamCatalyst, which relies on score distillation sampling, lags furthest behind on both metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends 3D Gaussian Splatting by appending per-Gaussian semantic feature vectors and uses a ControlNet-style flow-matching model conditioned on those features (rather than RGB or depth renderings) to drive text-guided edits. The truncation-aware embedding space is constructed via a Matryoshka-trained autoencoder over DINOv3 features, adapting a 2D image-generation technique (channel-ordered latents) to the 3D scene representation setting where it had been under-explored. A scale- and translation-consistency loss, inspired by DVT, removes 2D positional bias from the embeddings to improve multi-view consistency during 3D fusion, and a Difix3D+-style finetuning stage addresses artifacts from large geometric modifications.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By conditioning the editing model on semantic features with controllable abstraction rather than pixel-level renderings, the method enables substantially larger geometric changes (e.g., bicycle → motorcycle) that prior RGB/depth-conditioned approaches cannot achieve, as confirmed by a large user-study margin (64.3% vs 16.7% for geometry preference). The ablation revealing that the consistency loss is critical—without it, optimization 'frequently fails entirely'—highlights how 2D positional bias is a more severe obstacle to 3D feature fusion than previously appreciated. Open questions include whether the approach scales to large outdoor scenes, how to disentangle semantics from appearance to avoid color-bleed artifacts, and whether the hyperparameter schedules (truncation level, noise, learning rate) can be automated rather than manually tuned per edit.
