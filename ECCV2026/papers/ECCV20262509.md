## Problem

Existing single-image reflection removal methods operate in pixel space, where the nonlinear sRGB formation model entangles transmission and reflection layers and limits generalization to real-world conditions. This paper identifies that pretrained VAE latent spaces exhibit dramatically lower mutual coherence between image layers (cosine similarity 0.07 vs. 0.74 in pixel space), suggesting a more favorable working space for decomposition that prior methods have not exploited.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262509/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image with reflection (processed as 512×512 crops) and encodes it via the FLUX VAE encoder into a 16-channel latent at 8× spatial downsampling. A single forward pass of the MM-DiT backbone simultaneously produces both the transmission latent (z_I + v_θ) and the reflection latent (−v_θ), which are decoded back to pixel space by the VAE decoder. No dual-branch architecture or iterative denoising is required; both layers emerge from one velocity-field evaluation at t=0.

## Method

PRISM reinterprets SIRR as a latent linear separation problem under the approximate additive formulation z_I ≈ z_T + z_R in FLUX VAE latent space, learning a flow matching velocity field v_θ ≈ −z_R on a fine-tuned FLUX.2 Klein 4B backbone so that both layers are recovered in a single forward pass. To prevent degenerate entanglement, the paper introduces a Latent Composition Consistency (LCC) strategy that cyclically swaps reflection latents across batch samples to form synthetic mixtures, enforcing consistent decomposition via a cycle loss with stop-gradient anchoring. A Layer Contrastive Separation (LCS) loss then applies patch-level InfoNCE contrastive learning to pull transmission features toward positive anchors and push them away from reflection features, enforcing semantic disentanglement without requiring explicit pixel-space reflection targets. Training combines latent velocity regression, pixel-space L1+LPIPS supervision, the cycle loss, and the contrastive loss, all in bfloat16 on a single A6000 GPU.

## Results

PRISM achieves state-of-the-art PSNR on all six standard benchmarks, surpassing the previous best (RDNet) by 1.56 dB on Real (27.14 vs. 25.58) and 1.84 dB on Wild (29.54 vs. 27.70), with consistent gains of 0.3–1.3 dB on the remaining datasets. On the in-the-wild OpenRR 1K test set (inference-only, no fine-tuning), PRISM leads on PSNR (27.23), LPIPS (0.064), DISTS (0.041), and NIQE (3.55) while matching RDNet's inference speed (104 ms vs. 102 ms on an RTX 4090). On SSIM, PRISM is best on Real, Wild, and Nature but trails DSIT and DAI by ~0.005–0.01 on Object and Postcard, consistent with the perception–distortion tradeoff.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PRISM outperforms all ten baselines on PSNR across every benchmark, with the largest margins over RDNet (+1.56 dB on Real, +1.84 dB on Wild) and DAI (+1.90 dB on Real, +2.10 dB on Wild). On SSIM, PRISM leads on Real (0.853), Wild (0.932), and Nature (0.853) but is slightly behind DSIT (0.932 vs. 0.925) on Object and DAI (0.922 vs. 0.906) on Postcard, which the authors attribute to the perception–distortion tradeoff. On the in-the-wild OpenRR 1K set, PRISM achieves the best PSNR (27.23), LPIPS (0.064, −14.0% vs. RDNet), DISTS (0.041, −14.6% vs. RDNet), and NIQE (3.55) while matching RDNet's inference time (104 ms vs. 102 ms) despite having 12.6× more parameters (3.97B vs. 314M). Older single-stream and dual-stream methods (ERRNet, IBCLN, YTMT, DSRNet) trail PRISM by 2–5 dB in PSNR, reflecting the gap between pixel-space architectures and latent-space flow matching. The diffusion-based DAI, the closest competitor, is outperformed by 0.65–2.10 dB in PSNR on the standard benchmarks and by 1.19 dB on OpenRR 1K, confirming that structured latent decomposition provides a stronger inductive bias than repurposing a generative prior as a pixel-space regularizer.

## Relation to Prior Work

PRISM departs from the entire SIRR literature (ERRNet, DSRNet, DSIT, RDNet, DAI, etc.), which operates exclusively in pixel space with dual-branch or single-stream CNN/Transformer architectures, by lifting the additive decomposition into the latent space of a pretrained FLUX VAE. It repurposes the flow matching velocity field (originally for generative modeling) as a single-pass separation operator, where the velocity target v* ≈ −z_R simultaneously yields both transmission and reflection. The Latent Composition Consistency strategy and Layer Contrastive Separation loss are novel training mechanisms that exploit the empirically observed low mutual coherence (cosine 0.07 vs. 0.74 in pixel space) of VAE latents, a property not previously leveraged for layer decomposition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that pretrained VAE latent spaces are a fundamentally more favorable working space for additive image decomposition than pixel space, a finding that could generalize beyond reflection removal to other layer-separation tasks (e.g., shadow removal, matting). The 1.5–2 dB SOTA gap across six benchmarks and strong in-the-wild generalization (OpenRR 1K) demonstrate practical impact. However, the 3.97B-parameter FLUX backbone limits deployment on resource-constrained hardware, and the authors acknowledge the lack of a principled theory for when and why the VAE encoder approximately preserves additive structure. A key open question is whether the latent-space decomposition insight can be captured by smaller, task-specific encoders without sacrificing the decorrelation property.
