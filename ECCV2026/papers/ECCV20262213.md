## Problem

Existing multimodal table benchmarks report only end-to-end answer accuracy, making it impossible to distinguish whether a model fails to perceive a visual markup, mis-binds it to table structure, or simply ignores it while arriving at a correct answer by other means. This blind spot means answer-centric scores systematically misestimate a model's ability to treat embedded visual cues (highlights, underlines, bold text) as executable reasoning constraints over table structure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262213/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark instance is a tuple (I, M, Q, A): a table image containing one or more visual markups, a natural-language question that references those markups, and a target answer. The expected model output is a schema-constrained JSON structure (not a free-form string), which enables exact-match scoring and exposes whether the model correctly identified the marked region, applied the right constraint, and executed the computation. The benchmark comprises 446 table images and 3,283 questions split into 266 real-world scientific tables and 180 synthetic tables, with a controlled counterfactual subset where only markup placement varies under fixed content.

## Method

HighlightBench decomposes markup-conditioned table reasoning via a diagnostic factorization P(A|I,M,Q) = Σ P(A|I,c,Q)·P(c|z,Q)·P(z|I,M), separating cue-to-structure binding, question-aware constraint interpretation, and downstream execution into five automatically scorable task families (Markup Grounding, Constrained Retrieval, Local Relations, Aggregation & Comparison, Consistency & Missingness). A reference pipeline operationalizes this decomposition: it first converts the input image into a docgraph that explicitly encodes table topology and markup attributes as structured cell-level annotations, then applies a two-stage router (coarse route selection followed by constraint completion) to resolve ambiguity, and finally executes a deterministic DSL plan over the docgraph to produce the answer plus intermediate traces. The counterfactual analysis protocol generates seven image variants per source table (no markup, congruent highlight/underline/bold on the true target, incongruent markup on a distractor) to isolate the causal effect of markup placement on model decisions, revealing that marked distractors dominate failures via a 'marker hijacking' mechanism.

## Results

On HighlightBench (446 table images, 3,283 questions), exact-match accuracy varies dramatically across 12 evaluated MLLMs. Gemini 2.5 Flash achieves the strongest end-to-end performance at 73.9% (synthetic) and 75.3% (real-world), while the paper's reference pipeline reaches 72.3% and 77.1% respectively, surpassing all end-to-end models on the real-world subset. Among open-source models, Qwen3-VL-8B leads at 44.2%/48.4%, whereas LLaVA1.5-7B scores near chance (2.7%/1.4%). Task difficulty is highly uneven: T2 (Constrained Retrieval) is comparatively stable (up to 90.3% for Gemini), while T1 (Markup Grounding) and T3 (Local Relations) remain the weakest across all models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The reference pipeline (Ours) outperforms all 12 end-to-end MLLMs on the real-world subset (77.1% vs. Gemini 2.5 Flash's 75.3%) and is competitive on synthetic (72.3% vs. 73.9%), with its largest gains on T4 (Aggregation & Comparison: 81.5% vs. 67.8%) and T3 (Local Relations: 73.3% vs. 89.1% for Gemini, where Gemini still leads). Among closed-source models, Gemini 2.5 Flash dominates every task family, while Claude Sonnet 4 trails by 26.3 points on synthetic and 16.6 on real-world. GPT-4o mini performs surprisingly poorly (21.1%/16.4%), well below its expected capability, suggesting markup-conditioned reasoning is a specific weakness. Among open-source models, Qwen3-VL-8B (44.2%/48.4%) and Qwen3.5-9B (37.7%/48.8%) lead, with a 25–30 point gap to the best closed-source model. The counterfactual analysis on Qwen3-VL-8B further shows that marker placement alone shifts Extremum Reasoning accuracy by over 20 points, while Cell Retrieval remains stable at 94.5–96.5%, confirming that failures stem from markup interpretation rather than basic table reading.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two adjacent lines of work. First, multimodal document understanding systems (TextMonkey, mPLUG-DocOwl, DocPedia) and table QA benchmarks (TAPAS, TableEval) treat tables as content to be read and queried but do not model embedded visual markups as operational reasoning constraints. Second, visual prompting methods (VIP-LLaVA, Draw-and-Understand, VP-Bench) study externally supplied cues at inference time, whereas HighlightBench targets cues already baked into the table image that the model must interpret as part of the content. The diagnostic decomposition (perception → constraint formation → execution) draws on structured-output evaluation ideas from SO-Bench and ExtractBench, but applies them to the specific boundary of markup-to-structure binding in tables.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a concrete blind spot: answer-only scores conflate 'didn't see the markup,' 'saw it but mis-bound it,' and 'ignored it while answering correctly,' making it impossible to diagnose where models actually fail. The counterfactual protocol (fix content, vary only markup) provides a clean methodological tool for isolating visual-salience effects from reasoning errors, and the finding that 'marker hijacked' errors dominate suggests current MLLMs treat markups as attentional attractors rather than logical constraints. Open questions include whether the diagnostic decomposition generalizes beyond the 446-image scale, whether the counterfactual findings hold across architectures (only Qwen3-VL-8B was probed), and whether training on markup-conditioned data can shift models from salience-driven to constraint-driven behavior.
