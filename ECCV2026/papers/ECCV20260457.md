## Problem

As generative models produce outputs indistinguishable from real data, it becomes impossible to tell whether a given sample was a true training member or a generated output of the same model. Existing membership inference methods systematically misclassify generated samples as members, while attribution methods mislabel true members as generated, because both rely on likelihood signals that are similarly elevated for training examples and the model's own outputs. The paper formalizes this as the Member vs Generated Inference (MGI) task and extends it to model-derivative settings where new models are trained on previously generated data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an RGB image, full access to the target generative model (both its autoencoder and latent generator), and a conditioning signal (class label or text prompt, optionally estimated via BLIP2/LLaVA when ground-truth prompts are unavailable). It outputs a three-way classification—natural member, generated, or natural non-member—and in the model-derivative setting additionally attributes generated samples to their specific source generator (M₁ vs M₂). The image is processed through the full encode-decode pipeline rather than only the latent generator, which is what distinguishes DCB's input consumption from prior likelihood-only approaches.

## Method

DCB (Data Circuit Breaker) is a three-stage cascade that exploits complementary signals from the autoencoder and latent generator. Stage 1 computes an autoencoder self-consistency score combining a double-reconstruction ratio (normalizing per-image reconstruction error) and, for VQ-VAE-based IARs, a quantization error term; generated samples exhibit lower scores because they were produced through the same discrete codebook and decode pipeline. Stage 2 applies a standard MIA (ICAS) only to the non-generated subset, restoring the member-vs-non-member assumption that likelihood-based scores require. Stage 3, active in the model-derivative setting, constructs a 2-D feature vector of conditional log-probabilities under two candidate generators and fits class-conditional KDE densities from reference sets to attribute each generated sample to its source model. An optional encoder fine-tuning step (inversion loss on generated latents) improves Stage 1 robustness under JPEG, resize, saturation, and adaptive adversarial perturbations.

## Results

In the model derivative setting (the hardest MGI scenario), DCB achieves 95.8% overall TPR@1%FPR on VAR, 96.7% on RAR, 82.6% on SD1.4, and 83.7% on SD2.1, consistently outperforming the best baseline (ICAS) by 14–19 percentage points. On the critical 'Among Generated' comparisons (distinguishing which model produced a sample), DCB reaches 95.6–100% TPR@1%FPR while all baselines collapse to 0–50%. In the direct training setting, DCB achieves 87.4% overall TPR@1%FPR across IARs (vs. 52.6% for ICAS) and 77.8% across DMs (vs. 39.7% for CLiD). On memorized training samples, DCB separates originals from verbatim regenerations with 97.5% AUC, compared to 61.8% for the best MIA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DCB decisively outperforms all six baselines across every model and comparison in the MGI task. PIAR [11] (IAR MIA) and CLiD [30] (DM MIA) are the weakest: PIAR scores 0.0% TPR@1%FPR on N_M/G for RAR and LlamaGen, while CLiD drops to 0.0% on N_M/G for both SD models, confirming that likelihood-based MIAs cannot separate members from generated outputs. ICAS [27] is the strongest single baseline (81.3% overall on VAR, 82.1% on RAR, 63.5–64.4% on SDs) but still lags DCB by 14–19 points and fails catastrophically on N_M/G_N (10.3% on VAR) and among-generated comparisons (51.6% on G_N/G' for VAR). PRADA [4] (attribution) is inconsistent: it reaches 100% on N_N/G for RAR but collapses to 0.0% on G_M/G' for both VAR and RAR, and to 0.4–0.7% on all SD comparisons, showing that probability-ratio attribution cannot handle the MGI setting. Even the strong shadow-model-based MIAs LiRA [2] and RMIA [29] (Table 5) fail: LiRA achieves only 40.0–40.6% overall, and RMIA reaches 76.4–82.7%, still trailing DCB by 13–20 points, particularly on N_M/G_N (1.0–65.7% vs. 99.3–99.9% for DCB). The consistent pattern is that all baselines rely solely on latent-generator likelihood signals, which are equally elevated for members and generated samples, whereas DCB's autoencoder-based Stage 1 filtering restores the separation that downstream scoring requires.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of membership inference (MIA) and image attribution, showing that both families of methods are fundamentally inadequate when the task requires separating training members from the model's own generated outputs. It builds on AEDR's double-reconstruction ratio for autoencoder self-consistency scoring and on Zhao et al.'s encoder refinement technique for VQ-VAE-based IARs, but departs from both by combining these autoencoder signals with latent-generator conditional probability discrepancies in a three-stage cascade. The key conceptual departure from prior MIA work (PIAR, ICAS, CLiD, LiRA, RMIA) and attribution work (PRADA) is the recognition that likelihood-based signals are inherently ambiguous between members and generated samples, necessitating a holistic pipeline that treats the autoencoder as an informative component rather than a transparent mapping.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As generative models proliferate and data circuits (models trained on previously generated data) become a realistic threat to model collapse, the ability to distinguish genuine training data from model outputs is practically critical for data provenance auditing and copyright enforcement. The finding that even strong shadow-model-based MIAs (LiRA, RMIA) fail as badly as weak MIAs in the MGI setting challenges the assumption that better statistical estimation alone can resolve membership questions when the signal source is fundamentally ambiguous. Open questions include whether DCB generalizes to video, text, or audio generation, how it performs under stronger adversarial attacks targeting the autoencoder specifically, and whether the cross-generator attribution stage (Stage 3) scales to more than two model versions in long data-circuit chains.
