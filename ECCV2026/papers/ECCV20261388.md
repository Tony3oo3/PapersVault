## Problem

Existing longitudinal brain disease progression methods learn a direct image-to-image mapping that implicitly entangles structural deformation with intensity variation, without modeling the physical mechanisms driving lesion growth. This entangled formulation limits physical plausibility, generalization across irregular sampling, and interpretability of predicted trajectories.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261388/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a baseline MRI scan (T1-weighted, contrast-enhanced T1, or FLAIR depending on the dataset), a binary lesion mask at the baseline time point, and the time interval to the target scan. The binary mask is converted to a continuous lesion cell concentration field via a signed distance function followed by a sigmoid mapping, which serves as the PDE modeling substrate. The output is a predicted future MRI scan and a corresponding predicted lesion mask, with the prediction decomposed into a morphologically warped image and an intensity-refined final image.

## Method

PDF decomposes longitudinal progression into two dedicated flow matching networks: a morphology network that predicts a velocity field on the lesion cell concentration (capturing growth and structural deformation), and an intensity network that models signal changes conditioned on the lesion mask. The morphology network is trained with a standard flow matching loss plus a PDE-regularized loss that projects the predicted velocity onto the diffusion-reaction-advection span defined by the Fisher-KPP equation and an advection term, penalizing the residual to enforce biologically plausible evolution. At inference, the morphology velocity is integrated to obtain an evolved lesion mask, a dense displacement field is estimated via TV-L1 optical flow between the predicted and baseline masks, and the baseline image is warped semi-Lagrangian to capture structural deformation. The intensity flow matching module then refines the warped image to produce the final prediction, incorporating high-frequency texture from the baseline to preserve lesion-specific appearance.

## Results

PDF achieves the best lesion-level performance across all three datasets: DSC of 0.752 (UCSF), 0.534 (LUMIERE), and 0.696 (LMSLS), with correspondingly lowest HD values of 25.952, 33.672, and 20.867. On image-level metrics, PDF attains the highest SSIM (0.920, 0.957, 0.900) and lowest L1 error (0.072, 0.071, 0.058) on all three datasets, though its PSNR is slightly below T-UNet on UCSF (31.364 vs. 31.739) and TFM on LMSLS (29.314 vs. 29.441). The authors note that lesion-level metrics (DSC, HD) are more representative of progression quality since lesion changes are small relative to the whole brain volume.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PDF outperforms all four baselines on the primary lesion-level metrics (DSC and HD) across all three datasets. On UCSF, PDF improves DSC by 2.4 points over T-UNet (0.752 vs. 0.728) and by 5.6 points over TFM (0.752 vs. 0.696), while reducing HD by 1.37 and 5.75 respectively. On LUMIERE, the gains are most pronounced: PDF achieves DSC 0.534 vs. 0.492–0.522 for the baselines, and HD 33.672 vs. 36.105–41.994, with ImageFlowNet showing the largest gap (HD 41.994). On LMSLS (subtle MS lesion progression), PDF's HD of 20.867 is substantially lower than T-UNet's 26.354 and I²SB's 23.617, indicating superior sensitivity to small-scale changes. The only metric where PDF does not lead is PSNR: T-UNet edges ahead on UCSF (31.739 vs. 31.364) and TFM on LMSLS (29.441 vs. 29.314), though the authors argue that image-level PSNR is less informative for lesion progression than DSC/HD since lesion regions occupy a small fraction of the full brain volume.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on flow matching (Lipman et al., ICLR 2023) as its core training objective, applying it to longitudinal brain disease forecasting in the same spirit as ImageFlowNet, TFM, and I²SB. However, it departs from these methods in two key ways: (1) it disentangles the progression into separate morphology and intensity flow-matching networks rather than learning a single entangled image-to-image mapping, and (2) it embeds a Fisher-KPP diffusion-reaction-advection PDE as a projection-based regularizer on the morphology velocity field. This contrasts with prior physics-based tumor modeling work (Learn-Morph-Infer, GliODIL, Weidner et al.), which applies PDE constraints during inverse parameter estimation rather than within a forward generative trajectory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The disentangled + physics-grounded formulation offers a principled path toward interpretable and physically plausible longitudinal predictions, and the PDE regularizer is computationally lightweight (a 3-coefficient least-squares projection) compared to full PDE-constrained optimization. However, the performance gains over strong baselines are modest (e.g., DSC 0.745→0.752 on UCSF), the PDE parameters are estimated per time-pair rather than learned end-to-end, and evaluation is limited to three relatively small 2D datasets. Open questions include whether the Fisher-KPP assumption generalizes beyond glioma and MS lesions, whether the approach scales to 3D volumetric data, and whether the physics span can be enriched with additional biophysical terms (e.g., biomechanical deformation, treatment effects).
