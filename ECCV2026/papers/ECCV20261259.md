## Problem

Distance-based OOD detectors such as Mahalanobis Distance Score conflate feature magnitude with directional information, creating a pathological failure mode where low-norm OOD samples with poor semantic alignment can score as more in-distribution than genuine high-norm ID samples. Existing remedies either patch the scoring function at test time or introduce complex training objectives (synthetic outliers, contrastive losses, negative mining) that add substantial computational overhead without reliably improving near-OOD detection. The paper asks whether a minimal change to standard training can produce feature representations that are intrinsically invariant to norm variations and optimized for pure angular separation.

## Input / Output

The method accepts standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR, 224×224 for ImageNet) and produces both a class prediction and an OOD detection score. The OOD score is computed post-hoc from the ASL-trained features using any distance-based detector (MDS, VIM, KNN, fDBD, etc.) or simply cosine similarity to class means, making the training procedure detector-agnostic. The only architectural modification is an ℓ2-normalization step inserted between the feature extractor and the final linear classifier, with the classifier bias removed and a fixed scaling factor (s=10) applied to the normalized features.

## Method

ASL applies ℓ2-normalization to the feature vector before the cross-entropy loss, so that each logit reduces to ‖w_j‖₂·cos(θ_j), forcing the network to discriminate classes purely by angular position on the hypersphere. The paper proves (Proposition 1) that this objective is equivalent to prototype-based contrastive learning in angular space: it simultaneously minimizes the angle to the correct class prototype and maximizes angles to all others, while weight decay λ bounds classifier norms and acts as a dynamic inverse temperature. A second proposition (Proposition 2) establishes a finite-sample lower bound on the feature-space separability between ID and OOD distributions, showing how larger λ tightens the bound by restricting the hypothesis space and encouraging finer-grained features. The method requires no synthetic outliers, no explicit pair mining, and no auxiliary loss terms—only a normalization step and standard weight decay—yielding training time nearly identical to vanilla cross-entropy (277s vs. 252s per epoch on ImageNet-200) while achieving state-of-the-art FPR95 across near-OOD, far-OOD, and covariate-shift benchmarks.

## Results

On ImageNet-200 (ResNet-18 from scratch), ASL achieves the best near-OOD average FPR95 of 52.4% and AUROC of 85.1%, outperforming the next-best training-based method (ReweightOOD, 54.5%/83.7%) and the cross-entropy baseline (59.3%/82.8%). On CIFAR-10, ASL reaches 25.1% near-OOD FPR95 and 93.3% AUROC, and 12.0% far-OOD FPR95 and 97.2% AUROC. On the safety-critical BIMCV medical dataset, ASL reduces average FPR95 from 23.0% (CE) to 12.4% (standard) and 11.9% (covariate shift), with 97.3% average AUROC. Training time per epoch is 277s, only ~10% slower than standard cross-entropy (252s) but 2.4× faster than CIDER and 12× faster than HamOS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ASL outperforms all training-based baselines (CIDER, LogitNorm, PALM, ReweightOOD, T2FNorm) on near-OOD detection across every benchmark, with the largest margin on ImageNet-200 NINCO (39.1% vs. 41.8% FPR95 for ReweightOOD) and CIFAR-10 (25.1% vs. 25.8% for T2FNorm). On far-OOD, ASL is competitive but not uniformly best: ReweightOOD achieves a lower CIFAR-10 far-OOD FPR95 (10.2% vs. 12.0%), and PALM leads on CIFAR-100 far-OOD (37.8% vs. 47.8%), though ASL still wins on the combined average. Under covariate shift (Table 4), ASL is the only training-based method that improves upon the cross-entropy baseline on far-OOD (29.1% vs. 30.1% FPR95), while all other methods degrade. On ImageNet-1K fine-tuning, ASL boosts near-OOD AUROC from 71.4% to 78.8% (ResNet-50) and from 78.5% to 81.9% (ViT-B/16), surpassing all baselines. In the medical BIMCV setting, ASL dramatically outperforms all methods (12.4% avg FPR95 vs. 23.0% for CE and 26.0% for the next-best ReweightOOD), while methods like LogitNorm and PALM collapse to near-random performance (FPR95 > 85%). Training efficiency is a key differentiator: ASL runs at 277s/epoch, only 10% slower than CE (252s), compared to CIDER (657s), PALM (381s), and HamOS (3399s).

## Relation to Prior Work

ASL carves new ground by reframing a simple architectural choice—L2-normalizing features before the linear classifier—as the core of the learning objective, rather than treating normalization as an auxiliary regularizer (LogitNorm, T2FNorm) or a post-hoc scoring fix (MD++). Conceptually, it bridges the neural-collapse and angular-geometry literature (ORA, NCI, NECO) with the contrastive-learning-for-OOD line (CIDER, PALM, ReweightOOD) by showing that cross-entropy on normalized features implicitly performs prototype-based contrastive learning in angular space, eliminating the need for explicit pair mining or synthetic outliers. The paper departs from the prevailing trend toward increasingly complex training objectives by demonstrating that the Mahalanobis distance failure mode (norm sensitivity) can be addressed at the representation level with a single normalization step, effectively subsuming the role of distance-based post-hoc detectors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a one-line modification (L2-normalization + removing bias) that costs virtually no extra compute can match or surpass methods requiring synthetic outlier generation, contrastive pair mining, or mixture-of-prototype training, prompting a re-evaluation of the complexity-to-performance trade-off in OOD detection. The theoretical grounding (bounded weight norms, IPM-based separability lower bound) provides a principled explanation for why the method works and how the weight-decay hyperparameter λ trades off near-OOD vs. far-OOD performance. Open questions include whether the approach extends to non-image modalities, multi-task or open-set settings, and whether the ad-hoc scaling factor s=10 can be made adaptive; additionally, the method requires retraining rather than operating post-hoc on frozen models, which limits its applicability when retraining is infeasible.
