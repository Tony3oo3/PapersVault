## Problem

Existing person search methods assume complete visual queries or exhaustive tracking, while real-world witness accounts are vague, partial, and spread across multiple cameras and time periods. Prior interactive person retrieval operates on static queries with no active planning mechanism and relies solely on visual appearance, neglecting spatial and temporal cues that are central to real-world investigations. ARGOS is the first to place spatio-temporal filtering under interactive agent control for multi-camera person search, requiring the agent to plan questions, invoke physical-feasibility tools, and interpret ambiguous responses under a limited turn budget.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a vague initial witness statement and engages in multi-turn dialogue (up to 20 turns) with a deterministic witness simulator, while accessing a Spatio-Temporal Topology Graph (STTG) encoding camera connectivity and empirically validated transition times, plus a read-only gallery of 1,273 persons annotated with 24 visual attributes. A critical information asymmetry constrains the agent: it cannot observe which of the 24 attributes the witness can actually report (only 3 are observable), forcing strategic adaptation when queries yield no information. The output is a single predicted target person ID, evaluated by Turn-Weighted Success (TWS), which multiplies correctness by the ratio of oracle-optimal turns to actual turns, jointly measuring accuracy and interaction efficiency.

## Method

The ARGOS Agent is a four-module LLM pipeline (Analyst → Planner → Interviewer → Interpreter) operating in an observe-think-act loop, equipped with eight environment tools including check_temporal (which queries the STTG to eliminate candidates with infeasible transition times) and filter_spatial (which maps zone descriptions to specific cameras). The STTG is a directed weighted graph with three edge types (OVERLAP, SOFT_ADJ, TRAVEL) built from multi-camera tracking ground truth, where each edge carries transition-time statistics (min, median, max, count) enabling physical feasibility reasoning. The benchmark comprises 2,691 tasks across three progressive tracks—semantic perception (Who), spatial reasoning (Where), and temporal reasoning (When)—constructed via a two-stage pipeline that first computes deterministic ground truth algorithmically and then wraps it in natural-language dialogues via an LLM, ensuring correctness is independent of language-model behavior. The same tool set serves all three tracks, yet the Planner must make qualitatively different strategic decisions: extracting attributes from a single dialogue (Track 1), balancing attribute questions against a one-time spatial query (Track 2), or integrating STTG-based temporal elimination with attribute narrowing (Track 3). Ablations confirm that removing domain-specific tools drops Top-1 accuracy by up to 49.6 percentage points, and removing strategic reasoning halves TWS while barely affecting Top-1.

## Results

On the ARGOS benchmark, the best agent achieves a Turn-Weighted Success (TWS) of 0.383 on Track 2 (Spatial, Claude Sonnet 4) and 0.590 on Track 3 (Temporal, GPT-5.2), both far below the Oracle upper bound of 1.000. On Track 1 (Who), structured tool-calling (LLM ToolCall) reaches 81.1% SR@1, outperforming direct LLM inference (73.3%) by 7.8 percentage points. No single LLM backbone dominates both multi-turn tracks: Claude Sonnet 4 leads Track 2 (TWS 0.383, Top-1 76.0%) while GPT-5.2 leads Track 3 (TWS 0.590, Top-1 88.2%), revealing that spatial and temporal reasoning stress complementary capabilities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, ARGOS compares four LLM backbones (GPT-5.2, GPT-4o, GPT-5-mini, Claude Sonnet 4) under an identical tool-augmented agent scaffold rather than competing methods. A notable crossover emerges: Claude Sonnet 4 achieves the best Track 2 TWS (0.383) and Top-1 (76.0%) but ranks last on Track 3 (TWS 0.548), while GPT-5.2 reverses this pattern (Track 3 TWS 0.590, Track 2 TWS 0.338), indicating that spatial and temporal reasoning probe different model strengths. Ablation baselines confirm the agent's design is essential: removing the temporal tool collapses Track 3 Top-1 from 80.6% to 31.0% (−49.6 pp) and TWS from 0.567 to 0.054, while removing strategic reasoning (w/o Strategy) halves Track 3 TWS (0.567→0.373) with only a modest Top-1 drop (80.6%→76.9%), showing that planning efficiency—not raw accuracy—is the primary bottleneck. Single-pass baselines (no multi-turn interaction) achieve only 29.6% Top-1 on Track 3, confirming that agentic interaction is the only viable path to meaningful performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

ARGOS sits at the intersection of three previously separate lines: interactive person retrieval (multi-turn dialogue to refine visual descriptions), spatio-temporal person ReID (fixed transition-time distributions applied as post-hoc filters), and agentic tool-augmented reasoning. The key departure is making spatio-temporal filtering an agent-controlled, interactive decision rather than a fixed retrieval post-process, and grounding the agent in a Spatio-Temporal Topology Graph (STTG) built from empirical multi-camera tracking data (MTMMC). The Turn-Weighted Success metric adapts the SPL design principle from embodied navigation to dialogue turns, and the overall framing draws on agentic video understanding work but extends it to multi-camera topology with transition constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ARGOS provides the first benchmark where an agent must jointly perform semantic parsing, spatial disambiguation, and temporal feasibility reasoning under a turn budget, with the STTG as a physically grounded constraint. The finding that the best agent achieves only 0.383 TWS on spatial reasoning and 0.590 on temporal reasoning indicates substantial headroom, and the ablation showing that strategic planning halves TWS without much Top-1 impact reveals that efficiency and accuracy are separable axes. Open questions include scaling to city-scale heterogeneous camera networks, adversarial or inconsistent witnesses, and multi-hop STTG reasoning over indirect paths. The paper also flags privacy and surveillance-misuse risks inherent to the technology.
