## Problem

No existing video benchmark evaluates the full progression from perception through causal reasoning and simulation to strategic planning in real-world multi-agent settings. In-the-wild videos lack verifiable ground truth for causal and strategic questions, while synthetic environments sacrifice the complexity of genuine multi-agent interaction. SVI-BENCH bridges this gap by using team sports as a dynamic microworld that uniquely combines real multi-agent dynamics with explicit rules and definitive outcomes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260582/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Inputs vary dramatically across the four pillars: perception tasks consume 10-second clips with text questions or retrieval queries; reasoning tasks require full-game videos spanning 55–150 minutes; simulation tasks take an initial frame, a player-removed background video (produced via video inpainting), and either time-aligned bounding-box trajectories or a textual goal specification; and the agentic task provides a natural-language query plus tool access to a corpus of ~1.8M video clips and ~33K text documents. Outputs range from dense structured captions scored on six axes, multiple-choice selections, ranked video lists (R@K), free-form strategic explanations, generated videos evaluated via mIoU and feature similarity, to short factual answers verified by an LLM judge.

## Method

The core contribution is a data engine that transforms five raw modalities (broadcast video, play-by-play logs, expert commentary, game reports, and box-score statistics) into a dense, cross-referenced corpus through four stages: temporal alignment via game-clock timestamps, cross-modal entity resolution into identity graphs, LLM-powered instance generation guided by pillar-aware prompt templates, and automated plus human quality control. Evaluation is organized into 9 tasks across a four-pillar hierarchy (Dynamic Scene Understanding, Causal Reasoning, Strategic Simulation, Agentic Synthesis), with 7 tasks providing training splits. For baselines, the authors fine-tune Wan 2.1 for trajectory- and goal-conditioned video generation, fine-tune InternVideo2 with contrastive loss for compositional retrieval, and evaluate proprietary (GPT-5.2, Gemini 3.x) and open-source (Qwen3-VL, Molmo 2, BIMBA) multimodal LLMs across all tasks. Oracle experiments replace video with ground-truth text to isolate perception from reasoning bottlenecks, and human studies with experienced sports fans establish solvability baselines.

## Results

SVI-BENCH reveals a stark performance cliff across the four-pillar hierarchy: the best perception score (73.91% on T2 Fine-Grained Action QA with fine-tuned LLaVA-Video-7B) drops to ~2/5 on strategic reasoning (T4), <45% on outcome forecasting (T5), and a mere 4.6% on cross-corpus agentic reasoning (T9) with GPT-5.2. On video generation tasks, the best fine-tuned Wan 2.1 model achieves only 0.513 Video mIoU (T7) and 50.2% goal accuracy (T8), meaning roughly half of generated players deviate from prescribed trajectories. Oracle experiments (replacing video with ground-truth text) raise T9 from 4.6% to 54.0% and T6 saliency from 7.1% to 20.6%, confirming that reasoning, planning, and evidence integration—not perception alone—are the binding constraints.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary models, Gemini 3 Flash leads on perception (58.75% on T2) while GPT-5.2 is competitive on reasoning (2.06/5 on T4, 38.2% on T5) but trails Gemini 3.1 Pro on T4 (2.17/5). Fine-tuned LLaVA-Video-7B substantially outperforms zero-shot proprietary models on perception tasks (73.91% vs. 58.75% on T2; 2.17 vs. 1.67 on T1), demonstrating the value of domain-specific training. On video generation, the fine-tuned Wan 2.1 model dominates off-the-shelf baselines: MagicMotion (0.466 mIoU, 31.4% goal acc.) and ATI (0.397 mIoU, 40.5% goal acc.) both fall well short on T7 and T8. For agentic reasoning (T9), GPT-5.2 (4.6%) far exceeds Qwen3-Omni-30B (2.1%), but both are near chance, with GPT-5.2 issuing ~21 tool calls per question versus 3–8 for smaller models. Human experts with 5–10+ years of sport experience reach 75.8% on T2, 4.2/5 on T4, and 58.9% on T5, confirming the tasks are solvable and the gap reflects model limitations rather than task intractability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two parallel lines of work: (1) video benchmarks that evaluate perception and temporal reasoning (Video-MME, EgoSchema, ActivityNet-QA) but lack verifiable ground truth for causal and strategic questions, and (2) synthetic causal/counterfactual benchmarks (CLEVRER, PHYRE) that provide verifiable outcomes but sacrifice multi-agent behavioral complexity. SVI-BENCH bridges this gap by using team sports as a 'dynamic microworld' where explicit rules and definitive outcomes make strategic reasoning measurable at real-world scale. The data engine's cross-modal alignment (video, play-by-play logs, commentary, reports, statistics) and LLM-assisted instance generation with human verification extends prior sports video resources (SoccerNet, BASKET, SportsMOT) from single-task action detection to a unified multi-pillar evaluation framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a stark capability cliff: models achieve ~74% on fine-grained perception but only 5% on cross-corpus agentic reasoning, with oracle experiments confirming the bottleneck extends beyond perception to reasoning, planning, and evidence integration. This provides a concrete, measurable target for the field's progression from video description toward strategic intelligence in multi-agent environments. Key limitations include the sports-specific framing (broadcast conventions, fixed rules may not transfer), reliance on LLM judges for several tasks, and the fact that the agentic task's 5% ceiling may partly reflect tool-interface design rather than fundamental reasoning limits. Open questions include whether the perception-to-agency gap narrows with longer context windows, better tool-use training, or world-model-based simulation, and whether the four-pillar hierarchy generalizes beyond sports to other multi-agent domains (surgery, autonomous driving, military operations).
