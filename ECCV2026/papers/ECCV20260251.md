## Problem

State-of-the-art post-hoc OoD detection methods that edit penultimate-layer activations (ASH, SCALE, ReAct) exhibit inconsistent performance across datasets and architectures because they rely on assumptions about rectified Gaussian activation distributions and specific μ/σ ratios that are frequently violated. The paper identifies a concrete failure mode: when penultimate-layer activations are not rectified (as in ConvNeXt, ViT, EfficientNet), the scaling factor used by ASH-S and SCALE loses its monotonic relationship with the pruning threshold, breaking the theoretical guarantee that scaling improves OoD separation.

## Input / Output

The method requires only unlabelled ID activations at setup time to compute the reference profile μ (a single d-dimensional vector), and no OoD data or hyperparameter tuning at any point. At inference, a standard RGB image is fed through a pre-trained classifier; the penultimate-layer activation vector is extracted, rank-sorted, and element-wise replaced by μ before being passed to the final linear layer. The resulting logits are scored by a pluggable OoD function (EBO, ViM, or GEN) to produce a scalar confidence score, with no tunable threshold introduced at inference.

## Method

RAS (Ranked Activation Shift) replaces the sorted activation magnitudes of each test sample with a fixed in-distribution reference profile μ, computed as the element-wise mean of rank-sorted penultimate-layer activations over ID training data. At inference, the sample's activation vector is sorted via a permutation π, and each position π(j) is overwritten with μ_j, effectively performing a histogram-matching step that forces the activation distribution into the ID statistical envelope while preserving the spatial orientation of the original input. Because the operation is defined purely on rank order rather than activation magnitude, it is agnostic to the sign of activations and requires no percentile threshold, scaling factor, or other hyperparameter. The paper further shows that both the inhibitory component (shifting above-mean activations down) and the excitatory component (shifting below-mean activations up) independently improve OoD separation, with the full RAS compounding both effects primarily through variance compression of the energy score distribution.

## Results

On the four primary OpenOOD benchmarks (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet-200, ImageNet with ResNet18/50), RAS achieves AUROC of 90.24, 82.09, 89.51, and 86.55 respectively, with FPR@TPR95 of 40.16, 52.31, 36.70, and 40.92 — consistently outperforming all compared enhancing methods at their optimally tuned hyperparameters. On non-rectified architectures (ConvNeXt, ViT-B/16) where ASH and SCALE fail entirely, RAS still delivers strong gains: e.g., on ImageNet with ViT-B/16, RAS reaches 81.48 AUROC / 55.19 FPR versus EBO's 72.35 / 88.48. RAS preserves in-distribution accuracy within 0.1–0.2% across all tested backbones (Table 5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RAS outperforms the EBO baseline on all eight dataset-architecture combinations, with the largest gains on non-rectified architectures (e.g., +11.13 AUROC on ImageNet/ConvNeXt-B, +9.13 on ImageNet/ViT-B/16). Against ReAct, RAS wins on every scenario, most notably on ImageNet-200 (89.51 vs 88.13 AUROC) and all non-rectified models where ReAct degrades. RAS surpasses DICE by a wide margin across all settings (e.g., 90.24 vs 82.27 AUROC on CIFAR-10). Compared to ASH-S and SCALE, RAS achieves comparable or better AUROC on rectified CNNs (e.g., 89.51 vs 90.51 on IN-200, a 1-point gap) while dramatically outperforming them on non-rectified architectures where ASH-S collapses to ~22 AUROC and SCALE to ~69 AUROC on ViT-B/16. The one scenario where RAS does not achieve the absolute best AUROC is ImageNet with ResNet50 (86.55 vs ASH-S's 89.68 and SCALE's 89.42), though RAS still improves over EBO by 2.52 points and requires no hyperparameter tuning. RAS also pairs consistently with alternative scorers (ViM, GEN), yielding uniform AUROC gains of 0.2–1.3 points over their unmodified versions (Table 6).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a unifying alternative to the family of score-enhancing activation-editing methods (ReAct, ASH, SCALE, DICE), which all rely on threshold-based clipping, pruning, or scaling of penultimate-layer activations. Rather than building on any single predecessor, it identifies a shared vulnerability in the scaling-based subset (ASH-S, SCALE): their theoretical justification depends on a μ/σ ratio condition that is empirically violated in three of four standard OpenOOD benchmarks, and their scaling factor breaks entirely when activations are not rectified (as in ViT or ConvNeXt). RAS departs from this lineage by replacing magnitude-based thresholding with rank-order matching to a fixed in-distribution reference profile, eliminating the need for a percentile hyperparameter and any assumption about activation sign.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The hyperparameter-free, architecture-agnostic nature of RAS removes a practical friction point in deploying OoD detection: no held-out OoD data is needed for tuning, and the method works on both ReLU-based CNNs and GELU/SiLU-based architectures where prior scaling methods fail. The ablation showing that both inhibitory (suppressing high activations) and excitatory (boosting low activations) shifts independently improve OoD separation challenges the prevailing 'OoD = abnormally high activations' narrative inherited from ReAct, suggesting the benefit is fundamentally about variance compression toward the ID profile rather than directional correction. Open questions include whether the rank-matching idea extends to non-image modalities, whether the reference profile degrades under domain shift in the ID data itself, and whether combining RAS with learned (rather than fixed) reference profiles could further improve performance.
