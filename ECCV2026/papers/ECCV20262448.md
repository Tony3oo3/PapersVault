## Problem

Existing egocentric video datasets capture only the visual effects of hand-object interaction (motion, appearance) but lack the causal physical signal—force—that actually drives manipulation. Force is absent from prior datasets because it cannot be reliably obtained through manual annotation: the visual consequences of applied forces are often too subtle for annotators to detect, making contact boundaries especially ambiguous. This paper introduces FEEL, the first large-scale dataset pairing synchronized force measurements from custom piezoresistive gloves with egocentric video, and demonstrates its utility for contact understanding and force-aware action representation learning.

## Input / Output

For contact understanding, the model takes a single RGB image and produces, per hand (left/right), a binary contact logit and a pixel-level segmentation mask of the contacted object at input resolution. For action representation learning, the video backbone takes a clip and predicts a 6-channel force vector (five fingertips + palm, each from a Flexiforce A301 sensor measuring up to 45 N) during pretraining; after discarding the force heads, the same backbone is transferred to action recognition. Contact supervision is derived entirely from the force signal via dual-thresholding on a geometric-mean force reading, and segmentation pseudolabels are generated weakly from SAM3 concept prompts, SEA-RAFT optical flow, and epipolar geometry—no manual object segmentation annotations are used at any point.

## Method

The core contribution is a custom sensing pipeline (six piezoresistive sensors on a glove + Meta Aria glasses) that yields time-synchronized video, audio, and force streams, from which per-frame contact states are extracted via a multi-stage filtering pipeline (Hampel outlier removal, Gaussian smoothing, rolling-percentile baseline subtraction, 99.5th-percentile normalization, and dual-thresholding on the geometric mean across sensors). For spatial segmentation, a weakly supervised pseudolabel generator selects the contacted object by computing Sampson epipolar error within SAM3 concept-prompted masks and choosing the proposal with maximum mean error that is also 3D-proximate to the hand centroid. The contact understanding model is a Dense Prediction Transformer (DPT) on frozen DINOv3 features, producing per-hand contact logits and two-channel segmentation maps. For action representation learning, video backbones (Hiera-B, EgoVideo-B) are initialized from Kinetics-710 or Ego4D weights, fine-tuned on FEEL's force-prediction objective, and then transferred to downstream action recognition—demonstrating that force supervision instills a physically grounded representational prior that improves frozen-transfer performance more than conventional appearance-based pretraining, especially on smaller downstream datasets.

## Results

On temporal contact detection, the force-supervised model achieves 82.2/81.3 (BC/BSC) on HOI4D, 87.3/79.7 on EPIC-VISOR, and 95.6/88.9 on FEEL, surpassing the strongest fully-supervised baseline (100DOH) on all three benchmarks despite using only self-supervised force labels. For spatial contact segmentation, the weakly supervised model reaches 0.751 IoU on HOI4D, 0.399 on VISOR, and 0.625 on FEEL, outperforming 100DOH+SAM on FEEL by 40.7 points. In action recognition, force pretraining yields consistent gains in the frozen setting: Hiera-B improves from 41.65→46.50 (EPIC verb), 13.11→20.83 (Ego-Exo4D), and 26.42→29.57 (Meccano) top-1 accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The strongest competitor is 100DOH [32], a fully-supervised model trained on two large manually-annotated datasets. On temporal contact detection, Ours beats 100DOH on HOI4D (82.2 vs 90.0 BC, but 81.3 vs 80.5 BSC) and VISOR (87.3 vs 81.3 BC, 79.7 vs 71.9 BSC), and dominates on FEEL (95.6 vs 49.0 BC). On spatial segmentation, 100DOH+SAM narrowly edges Ours on HOI4D (0.765 vs 0.751 IoU) but Ours wins decisively on FEEL (0.625 vs 0.218). EPIC-VISOR [10] and HOIRef [2] are clearly outperformed across all metrics in both detection and segmentation. MOVES [19] (weakly supervised, only reported on FEEL at 0.309 IoU) and SAM3 [5] (concept-prompted, 0.030–0.201 IoU) are substantially below Ours on spatial segmentation. Notably, Ours achieves these results using only self-supervised force-derived labels from a single dataset, whereas the fully-supervised baselines require manual contact annotations from one or two large datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in contact understanding, which relies on large manually annotated datasets (100DOH, EPIC-VISOR, HOI4D) where annotators label contact states from RGB frames—a process the authors argue is both labor-intensive and inherently imprecise. Instead, FEEL introduces force measurements from custom piezoresistive gloves as a direct physical signal that yields temporally precise contact labels without manual annotation, positioning force as a 'primitive' rather than a peripheral signal. The closest prior work, OpenTouch [38], is over 4× smaller and does not explore the action representation learning tasks pursued here. For the pretraining side, the paper extends the self-supervised video pretraining paradigm (Kinetics-700, Ego4D) by adding force prediction as a physically grounded objective, repurposing existing backbones (Hiera, EgoVideo) and tools (SAM3, SEA-RAFT, DINOv3+DPT) in a new force-aware configuration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FEEL is the first large-scale egocentric dataset pairing force with video (~2M frames, 22 hours), opening a new modality for physical action understanding that was previously inaccessible at scale. The demonstration that force-derived supervision enables state-of-the-art contact detection without any manual contact annotations addresses a fundamental scalability bottleneck in the field. Key limitations remain: the hardware is uncomfortable for extended wear, sensors suffer mechanical drift, and the motion-based pseudolabeling cannot segment stationary contacted objects (tables, heavy appliances). Open questions include whether force-aware representations transfer to manipulation policy learning and robotics, and whether improved wearable sensing (e.g., wrist-mounted EMG) can scale this paradigm beyond kitchen environments.
