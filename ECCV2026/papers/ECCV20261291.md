## Problem

Underwater turbidity degrades image quality, but it remains unclear which aspects of that degradation actually harm downstream vision models. Existing image-quality metrics (PSNR, SSIM, UCIQE, UIQM, NIQE) show weak or no correlation with instance-segmentation performance on turbid images, and no publicly available dataset provides high-confidence annotations across the full range of real turbidity conditions needed to characterize this relationship.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261291/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

PCD takes a single RGB image, downsamples it (factor 0.5 in the main experiments), and computes multiscale Gabor-wavelet phase-congruency maps over two orthogonal orientations, yielding a single scalar delentropy score in the range of natural-log information units. The companion TUB dataset provides 1,320 RGB images (4 GoPro viewpoints × 10 turbidity stages × 33 scenes) with over 16,000 instance-level segmentation masks, where turbidity is quantified in NTU via nephelometer and grouped into low (0–10), medium (10–20), and high (20+) categories. Synthetic variants are generated per image using two physical-model-based degradation pipelines (contrast-only and contrast-plus-blur/inhomogeneity), tripling the dataset volume for evaluation.

## Method

PCD replaces the fixed-kernel gradients in Larkin's delentropy formulation with Kovesi's multiscale phase-congruency maps, which are invariant to contrast and color shifts and instead encode edge and boundary structure. Phase congruency is computed over seven Gabor-wavelet scales (minimum wavelength 3, multiplication factor 2.1) in two orthogonal orientations, producing a pair of 'phase-congruency gradients' whose joint probability distribution is then fed into the standard delentropy formula. The key insight is that contrast loss and spectral distortion—common in both real and synthetic turbidity—leave phase-congruency structure largely intact, so PCD remains stable under domain shift while dropping sharply when true structural information (sharp boundaries, distinct region edges) is destroyed by blur or heavy backscatter. Validation is performed by training MaskRCNN, YOLOv11, and Mask2Former on the TUB dataset (real and synthetic splits) and showing that PCD correlates most strongly with per-image AP50 across all three architectures, whereas all standard quality and complexity metrics exhibit weak or inconsistent correlations.

## Results

On the TUB dataset, all three instance segmentation models (MaskRCNN, YOLOv11, Mask2Former) show consistent AP50 degradation as turbidity increases from low to high, with Mask2Former (Swin-B) achieving the highest absolute performance. The key quantitative finding is that PCD exhibits the strongest correlation with model AP50 across both real and synthetic turbid images, while all five baseline metrics (NIQE, UIQM, UCIQE, SSIM, PSNR, and standard Delentropy) show weak to no correlation. On synthetic data (Synth₁ and Synth₂), established quality metrics score synthetic images as substantially lower quality than real turbid images despite models maintaining high-precision predictions, whereas PCD scores them closer to clear-image levels, aligning with actual model behavior.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PCD outperforms all five compared metrics in correlating with downstream instance-segmentation AP50. Standard Delentropy [24], which uses kernel-derived gradients, shows weaker correlation because its single-scale gradients are directly affected by contrast loss; PCD's phase-congruency-based multiscale gradients are invariant to contrast and color shifts, capturing structural degradation more faithfully. NIQE [35], UIQM [41], and UCIQE [55] all exhibit weak correlation even on real turbid images, as turbid scenes collapse into narrow metric ranges despite clear variation in object visibility. SSIM and PSNR [14], computed against clear-water references, also fail to track model performance, particularly on synthetic data where they score Synth₁ images as far worse than real turbid images while models still perform well. The hierarchy across models is consistent: MaskRCNN shows the lowest metric correlations, followed by YOLOv11, then Mask2Former, aligning with their relative predictive capabilities. Overall, PCD is the only metric that correctly distinguishes contrast-only degradation (where models still perform well) from structural/gradient degradation (where models fail), making it the most reliable indicator of task-relevant information loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines two established but previously unconnected ideas: phase congruency (Kovesi, 2000), a contrast- and illumination-invariant structural descriptor, and delentropy (Larkin, 2016), an information-theoretic measure of image complexity based on gradient distributions. The resulting PCD metric departs from the dominant paradigm in underwater image quality assessment, where metrics like UCIQE, UIQM, PSNR, and SSIM are tuned to approximate human perceptual judgment. It also positions itself against the growing body of synthetic turbidity generation work (image-formation models, GAN-based pipelines) by arguing that those methods replicate perceptual degradations (color shift, contrast loss) but not the structural information loss characteristic of real high-scattering conditions, and that no existing metric can distinguish between the two.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The TUB dataset fills a concrete gap: no prior publicly available dataset provides high-confidence instance-segmentation annotations under extreme (20+ NTU) turbidity, making it difficult to train or evaluate models in the most challenging real-world conditions. The finding that standard quality metrics show weak or no correlation with model performance while PCD correlates strongly suggests that the field's reliance on perceptual metrics may be masking whether synthetic training data actually reproduces the information loss that matters for detection. Open questions include whether PCD generalizes beyond instance segmentation to other tasks (detection, tracking, reconstruction), whether it holds on uncontrolled field data with varying scene complexity, and whether synthetic pipelines can be redesigned to match real turbidity in structural (rather than perceptual) terms.
