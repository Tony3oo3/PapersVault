## Problem

Existing video benchmarks either test perception that is solvable after a single viewing or rely on logical structure with simplified synthetic visuals, leaving no benchmark that simultaneously demands long-horizon, perception-centric reasoning requiring repeated evidence gathering across temporally separated video segments. This gap prevents faithful measurement of multimodal test-time thinking that depends on composing multiple perceptual skills over time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261854/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question pairs a 2–10 minute in-the-wild video (selected for high scene complexity via SAM2 instance counts and optical-flow magnitude) with a five-choice text query whose distractors are drawn from the same answer category as the correct option to block language-prior shortcuts. The model outputs a single letter choice; evaluation reports accuracy overall, by video category (outdoor, shopping, sport, home, show, movie, game), and by expert-annotated difficulty level (1–3). For models without native video input, frames are uniformly sampled (50 or 64 frames depending on API constraints).

## Method

PerceptionComp is constructed through a fully manual annotation pipeline (10–20 min per question) that composes multiple perceptual subconditions—targeting semantic recognition, spatial reasoning, temporal localization, visual correspondence, commonsense knowledge, and near-future prediction—under two composition logics: conjunctive (all conditions jointly constrain one target, with no proper subset yielding a unique answer) and sequential (later subconditions depend on entities or states established by earlier ones, inducing multi-hop reasoning where errors propagate). Videos are selected from seven diverse domains using automatic complexity proxies (SAM2 instance density and optical-flow magnitude) to ensure clutter, motion, and scene transitions that resist single-caption summarization. Each question is verified by a second annotator to confirm answer uniqueness and subcondition necessity. Evaluation spans 20+ proprietary and open-source MLLMs, with controlled analyses varying input-frame count (perception budget) and thinking-token budget (reasoning budget), plus agentic rewatching protocols (LongVT, custom wrapper) to test active evidence acquisition.

## Results

On PerceptionComp (1,114 five-choice questions over 279 high-complexity videos), the best-performing model Gemini-3-Flash achieves only 45.96% overall accuracy, while all open-source MLLMs remain below 40%. Human experts with unrestricted rewatching reach 100% accuracy, ordinary humans achieve 85.10%, and single-view humans (no rewatch) drop to near-chance 18.97%. Accuracy degrades sharply with compositional difficulty: Level 3 questions see the largest drops (e.g., Gemini-3-Flash falls from 43.75% on Level 1 to 47.85% on Level 2 but only 45.96% overall; GPT-4o drops from 35.04% to 29.67%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary frontier models, Gemini-3-Flash leads at 45.96%, narrowly ahead of Gemini-3-Pro (44.43%), Gemini-2.5-Pro (44.34%), Seed2.0 Pro (44.34%), and GPT-o3 (43.54%), with all clustering in the mid-40s. Test-time reasoning provides a clear but bounded gain: GPT-o3 surpasses GPT-4o by 11.04 percentage points (43.54% vs. 32.50%), and Gemini-2.5-Pro exceeds Gemini-2.5-Flash by 6.19 points (44.34% vs. 38.15%). However, scaling open-source model size does not consistently help—Qwen3-VL-235B (34.02%) performs essentially identically to Qwen3-VL-8B (34.06%), and Qwen2.5-VL-72B (31.33%) trails Qwen2.5-VL-7B (22.73%) by only ~9 points despite a 10× parameter increase. Thinking-style variants do not uniformly outperform their instruct counterparts: Qwen3-VL-Thinking-8B (33.82%) actually underperforms Qwen3-VL-8B (34.06%), while Qwen3-VL-Thinking-235B (38.20%) does exceed Qwen3-VL-235B (34.02%). Agentic rewatching yields modest gains (Gemini-3-Flash: 45.96%→50.60%; Qwen2.5-VL via LongVT: 22.73%→28.80%), but no model approaches the 85.10% human or 100% expert ceiling, confirming a persistent bottleneck in perception-centric long-horizon video reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in video QA benchmarking by targeting a specific gap: no existing benchmark simultaneously demands long-horizon reasoning, perception-centric evidence gathering, and repeated visual revisiting. It departs from perception-centric benchmarks (VideoMME, Perception Test) that are solvable after a single viewing, and from logic-heavy benchmarks (VideoMathQA, VSP) whose difficulty derives from abstract structure rather than real-world perception. The compositional question design (conjunctive and sequential subconditions) repurposes ideas from multi-hop reasoning in NLP, while the video selection pipeline repurposes SAM2 instance counts and optical-flow magnitude as automatic complexity proxies—tools previously used for segmentation and motion estimation rather than benchmark curation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a concrete and large gap: frontier MLLMs cluster in the mid-40s while humans reach 100% with unrestricted rewatching, and single-view human performance collapses to near chance (18.97%), validating that the difficulty is genuinely perception-driven rather than linguistic. The finding that scaling model size does not consistently help (Qwen3-VL-235B ≈ Qwen3-VL-8B) and that thinking variants can underperform their instruction-tuned counterparts suggests the bottleneck is not generic capacity or language-side deliberation but reliable fine-grained perceptual grounding under clutter and temporal discontinuity. Open questions include whether active rewatching (agentic video access) can close the gap, whether spatial reasoning specifically (60% of mid-chain failures) can be targeted with dedicated training, and whether the mid-40s ceiling reflects a fundamental architectural limitation or simply insufficient perceptual training data.
