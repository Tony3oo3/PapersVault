## Problem

Existing multi-view room layout estimation methods are limited by either restrictive geometric assumptions (e.g., cuboid-only shapes in PixCuboid), poor generalization to new datasets (e.g., Plane-DUSt3R), or independent per-room estimation that fails to exploit shared building structure such as dominant orientation, ground plane, and ceiling height. No prior method combines a general Manhattan polygon representation with joint multi-room optimization and strong cross-dataset generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262726/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of posed perspective images with known camera extrinsics (R_i, t_i) and intrinsics K_i, grouped by room for multi-room scenes. It outputs a Manhattan 3D polygon parameterized by a global-to-local rotation R ∈ SO(3) and a plane offset vector d, where the floor and ceiling are z = d_1, z = d_2 and walls alternate between x and y planes (minimum four, no upper bound). In multi-room scenes the orientation and optionally the floor/ceiling heights are shared across all rooms, reducing degrees of freedom and improving convergence.

## Method

PolyLayout separates learned scoring from explicit geometry: a DINOv2 ViT-S/14 encoder with two convolutional decoders predicts dense feature maps, edge maps, and confidence maps per image, while camera projection and polygon updates remain model-based. The layout is refined through coarse-to-fine Levenberg-Marquardt optimization of a composite cost (featuremetric multi-view consistency, edge alignment, vanishing-point/line-segment consistency, and a perimeter regularizer), with gradients backpropagated through the unrolled optimization during end-to-end training on 2D-3D correspondences. During inference, the polygon topology is adaptively updated: a modified Visvalingam-Whyatt simplification removes redundant wall pairs, and long walls are iteratively split to capture non-cuboid geometry. The initial layout is constructed from camera poses via an α-shape concave hull rasterized into a Manhattan polygon, then aligned to detected line segments before the main optimization begins.

## Results

On the ASE test set, PolyLayout achieves 94.3% IoU and 0.12 m Chamfer distance, substantially outperforming PixCuboid (68.1% IoU, 0.93 m) and rivaling SceneScript with semi-dense point clouds (0.12 m Chamfer, 96.1% wall recall). On the real ScanNet++ v2 test set, PolyLayout again reaches 94.3% IoU and 0.12 m Chamfer, while PixCuboid drops to 78.8% IoU and 0.36 m. On 2D-3D-Semantics (cuboid rooms), PolyLayout (90.0% IoU, 0.15 m Chamfer) narrowly edges out PixCuboid (89.0% IoU, 0.18 m), which was specifically designed for cuboid estimation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PolyLayout decisively outperforms PixCuboid on both multi-room datasets: 94.3% vs 68.1% IoU on ASE and 94.3% vs 78.8% IoU on ScanNet++, with the gap driven by PixCuboid's cuboid-only assumption and lack of cross-room parameter sharing. On 2D-3D-Semantics (cuboid rooms only), the two methods are nearly tied (90.0% vs 89.0% IoU), though PixCuboid retains a small edge in wall recall (93.8% vs 92.2%) and room recall (85.0% vs 80.6%). Plane-DUSt3R fails catastrophically on both multi-room benchmarks, achieving near-zero wall and room recall (≤0.2%) and Chamfer distances of 16–23 m, indicating poor generalization beyond its training distribution. SceneScript with only 10 images per room drops to 16.7% wall recall on ASE and 11.9% on ScanNet++, while its semi-dense point-cloud variant remains competitive on Chamfer (0.12 m) but cannot produce closed volumes for IoU. RoomFormer and all panorama/single-image baselines (Deep3DLayout, LED²-Net, PSMNet, Total3D, Implicit3D) are far behind, with IoU values below 70% on 2D-3D-Semantics and near-zero room recall on the multi-room datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

PolyLayout is a direct extension of PixCuboid [12] by the same first author, retaining the Levenberg-Marquardt optimization framework with featuremetric alignment but replacing the cuboid parameterization with general Manhattan polygons and swapping the ResNet-101 encoder for a DINOv2 ViT-S/14 backbone. The featuremetric alignment concept itself traces back to Sarlin et al. [30] and Hanning et al. [12], while the Manhattan world assumption is a long-standing prior in indoor layout estimation. The key conceptual departure is the joint multi-room optimization that shares orientation and floor/ceiling height across rooms, which neither PixCuboid nor Plane-DUSt3R [17] supports, and the adaptive polygon topology updates (wall splitting and Visvalingam-Whyatt simplification) that go beyond the fixed six-plane cuboid model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The two new multi-room benchmarks (ASE and ScanNet++ v2 with manual layout annotations) fill a notable gap in evaluation, as prior multi-view layout methods were only tested on single-room or cuboid-only settings. The method's reliance on known camera poses and the Manhattan assumption remain practical limitations, and the paper acknowledges that non-Manhattan rooms and shared wall locations are unexplored. The finding that DINOv2 features dramatically outperform ResNet-101 in the featuremetric cost (45.1 vs 24.6 IoU with E_feat alone) suggests that the quality of pre-trained visual features is the dominant factor in optimization-based layout estimation, a lesson likely transferable to other featuremetric alignment tasks. Open questions include whether the approach extends to non-Manhattan geometries (e.g., Atlanta world) and whether the multi-room sharing strategy generalizes to buildings with varying orientations or floor heights.
