## Problem

CLIP's few-shot recognition degrades significantly when class names are technical identifiers or semantically ambiguous (e.g., '727-200', 'Audi V8'), because the text encoder cannot form discriminative prompts for such labels. Existing remedies either require dataset-specific training (fine-grained vision-only methods) or attempt to improve textual prompts, which remain bottlenecked by the limited discriminative granularity of the text embedding space. No prior training-free method explicitly mines local visual evidence to compensate for weak textual priors.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a frozen OpenCLIP ViT-B-16 backbone, a small set of labeled images per class (1–16 shots), and class-name text prompts, producing a single class label per query image. Internally, it extracts 196 patch embeddings of 768 dimensions and the class-token attention map from the final ViT layer; the full unmasked image always passes through the encoder so that contextual information informs patch representations. The output logit is a weighted sum of standard CLIP text-image cosine similarity and a set-to-set patch similarity against the per-class CDPS, with the weight λ selected automatically via leave-one-out cross-validation on the few-shot set.

## Method

The core contribution is the Class-Discriminative Patch Set (CDPS), constructed entirely without parameter updates. First, a preliminary filter retains only the top-ρ percentile patches by class-token attention and then the N_cp largest spatially connected regions, removing isolated noise while preserving coherent semantic regions. Each surviving patch is scored by a discriminative criterion that rewards high intra-class consistency (average max-cosine similarity to same-class images) and penalizes inter-class ambiguity (similarity to hard-negative classes identified from zero-shot logits), yielding a per-patch score. The CDPS for a class is the union of the top-K highest-scoring patches across all its few-shot images. At inference, a hybrid classifier combines the standard CLIP text-image logit with a patch-set-to-CDPS logit (computed via set-to-set max-cosine similarity), where the blending weight λ is selected by grid search under a leave-one-out protocol on the few-shot samples, allowing the classifier to shift reliance toward visual patches when textual prompts are weak or toward text when class names are semantically rich.

## Results

On the average of 12 fine-grained datasets, CDPS achieves 69.77% accuracy at 1-shot, 76.21% at 4-shot, and 80.28% at 16-shot, consistently outperforming all training-free baselines by 4.5–11.3 points. Against training-based methods, CDPS surpasses CLAP at every shot setting (e.g., 76.21% vs. 74.73% at 4-shot) and is competitive with LDC, trailing by only 0.58 points at 16-shot. On the out-of-domain ImageNet-Sketch benchmark, CDPS achieves 56.03% accuracy, exceeding all baselines including ECALP (54.66%) and LDC (49.11%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CDPS outperforms all four training-free baselines (TipA, GDA, ECALP, TIMO) across every shot setting, with the largest margins over ECALP (up to 11.27 points at 4-shot) and TipA (up to 11.17 points at 16-shot). Against the training-based CLAP [47], CDPS wins at all five shot settings, e.g., 78.30% vs. 77.29% at 8-shot, despite requiring no parameter optimization. Against LDC [21], CDPS is competitive: it wins at 2-shot (73.90 vs. 72.56), 4-shot (76.21 vs. 75.34), and 8-shot (78.30 vs. 77.97), but trails slightly at 1-shot (69.77 vs. 70.64) and 16-shot (80.28 vs. 80.86). Among training-free methods, TIMO [22] is the strongest baseline, yet CDPS still leads by 4.48–6.70 points. On the OOD ImageNet-Sketch test, CDPS (56.03%) surpasses all baselines including ECALP (54.66%) and LDC (49.11%), while on ImageNet-V2 it is competitive but slightly below ECALP (63.66% vs. 65.72%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of work: fine-grained visual classification (FGVC) methods that identify discriminative object parts (e.g., RACN, Part-based R-CNNs) and training-free CLIP adaptation methods (TIMO, GDA, ECALP, TipA). While FGVC methods capture local discriminative structure, they require full-dataset training; while training-free CLIP methods avoid training, they operate on global image-text embeddings and remain vulnerable to ambiguous class names. CDPS repurposes CLIP's existing ViT patch embeddings and class-token attention maps—components already computed during inference—to mine class-discriminative patches without any parameter updates, effectively transplanting the FGVC intuition into a training-free, few-shot regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical and underappreciated failure mode of VLMs: when class names are technical identifiers or polysemous, textual prompts provide insufficient discriminative signal, and no amount of prompt engineering or adapter training fully resolves the gap. By showing that a simple patch-mining strategy can match or exceed training-based methods (CLAP, LDC) in low-shot settings without any gradient updates, CDPS offers a lightweight, interpretable alternative for deployment scenarios where fine-tuning is infeasible. Key limitations include the inability to model spatial layout among patches, dependence on the quality of the final-layer attention map, and modest OOD generalization on ImageNet-V2. Open questions include whether multi-layer feature fusion or explicit spatial modeling of patch sets could close the remaining gap to training-based methods, and how the approach scales to very large class sets where the inter-class penalty computation becomes costly.
