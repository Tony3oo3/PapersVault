## Problem

Autoregressive video generation requires encoding long video histories as context, but existing approaches face an unresolved trade-off among history coverage, visual fidelity, and memory cost: sliding windows lose long-range history, compressed VAEs sacrifice high-frequency detail, and sparse/linear attention reduces compute without shrinking the memory footprint. The paper identifies three specific demands that prior methods fail to jointly satisfy—content and identity consistency, complete uninterrupted temporal coverage, and content query/interpretation within a constrained feature manifold—particularly for personal users and offline workflows with tight budgets.

## Input / Output

The method takes a video history H (T_h latent frames at 480p, encoded by the base DiT's VAE) and a text prompt c in storyboard format with timestamps, and produces the next video segment X autoregressively. The history encoder φ(·) compresses H into a lightweight embedding at configurable rates (e.g., 4×4×2 yielding 195 tokens/s, or 2×2×2 yielding 780 tokens/s on Wan 2.1), which is concatenated as context to the DiT. Because the encoder is almost fully convolutional, newly appended frames are encoded independently and concatenated to the existing embedding without reprocessing the full history, reducing per-step encoding cost during autoregressive rollout.

## Method

TinyHistory learns a history encoder through two-stage context learning. In Stage I, the encoder is pretrained on ~5M videos with a randomized frame query objective: a random subset of frame indices Ω is kept clean while the rest is masked with noise, and a LoRA-adapted DiT learns to reconstruct the selected frames conditioned on the encoded masked history, forcing dense temporal coverage and preventing positional shortcuts. In Stage II, the pretrained encoder is integrated into an autoregressive video diffusion model and jointly optimized (not frozen) with newly initialized LoRA parameters on natural video data, repurposing features from frame-level query targets toward content-level consistency shaped by the training distribution. The encoder uses a dual-branch architecture—a low-resolution branch reusing the DiT's own VAE, patchifier, and first-layer projection, plus a high-resolution branch of lightweight 3D convolutions and attention—whose outputs are summed and projected directly into the DiT's inner hidden states (3072 or 5120 channels), bypassing the VAE channel bottleneck (4–64 channels) that constrains prior compression methods.

## Results

TinyHistory (2×2×2) at 780 tokens/s leads five of eight evaluation dimensions: Character consistency (85.90), Object consistency (85.45), Semantic alignment (26.84), FaceID (73.05), and user ELO (1332), while ranking second in Scene (87.11) and Cloth (87.74). The more aggressive 4×4×2 variant at just 195 tokens/s (a 32× reduction from the full-length 6240 tokens/s baseline) still leads all external baselines in five dimensions with Char 80.19, Obj 74.38, Semantic 25.74, FaceID 70.71, and ELO 1262. At the same 780 tokens/s budget, TinyHistory outperforms LTX-VAE+Proj by 23.2 points on Char (85.90 vs. 62.70) and 6.0 points on FaceID (73.05 vs. 67.01), demonstrating that learned DiT-manifold encoding substantially outperforms external VAE compression.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TinyHistory (2×2×2) dominates the comparison: it outperforms Sliding Window (6240 tokens/s) by 39.3 points on Char (85.90 vs. 46.65) and 15.2 on FaceID (73.05 vs. 57.87), showing that raw temporal coverage without learned encoding is insufficient for identity preservation. Against the closest same-budget competitor LTX-VAE+Proj (both at 780 tokens/s), TinyHistory leads every dimension, with the largest gap on Char (+23.2) and Cloth (+20.9), attributed to bypassing the VAE channel bottleneck by connecting directly to DiT inner hidden states. FramePack (2340 fixed tokens) trails TinyHistory by 28.0 on Char and 11.0 on FaceID, while QwenEdit+I2V (K=2) is the strongest image-planning baseline but still loses by 11.2 on Char and 4.2 on FaceID. The only dimensions where TinyHistory does not lead are Dynamic (Sliding Window: 90.61 vs. 86.41), Cloth (CLIP Vision: 93.73 vs. 87.73), and Scene (FOV Retrieval: 88.17 vs. 87.11, a marginal 1.06-point gap), suggesting that the learned embedding slightly trades off motion freedom and a few specialized aspects for overall consistency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of autoregressive video generation and history representation, departing from both compression-based approaches (FramePack, LTX-VAE, token merging) and retrieval-based approaches (WorldMem, Context-as-Memory, IP-Adapter). The key conceptual departure is operating in the DiT's inner hidden state space rather than the VAE latent bottleneck, combined with a two-stage learning protocol: a randomized frame-query pretraining objective that forces dense temporal coverage, followed by joint repurposing with the generator via LoRA. Unlike VAE/codec-style encoders that are trained once and frozen, TinyHistory keeps the encoder trainable in Stage II, allowing the feature manifold to be shaped by the downstream generation task and data distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for resource-constrained settings (personal users, offline workflows, individual-scale finetuning): a 32× context reduction (195 vs. 6240 tokens/s) while matching or exceeding heavier baselines on most consistency dimensions makes long-history autoregressive generation feasible on modest hardware. The modular design (sliding window, cross-attention, multiple encoders, KV-cache) composes with existing acceleration methods like CausVid and Self-Forcing, suggesting broad adoptability. Open questions include whether the two-stage protocol generalizes to substantially different base architectures, how the approach scales to minute-level or hour-level histories, and whether the encoding-rate trade-offs (spatial vs. temporal) can be made adaptive per scene content.
