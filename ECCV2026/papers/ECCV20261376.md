## Problem

Existing multimodal content creation methods are fragmented: video generation, audio generation, video editing, and lip-sync are handled by separate task-specific architectures or require complex cross-attention fusion modules. While unified video models have emerged, they cannot output audio, and joint video-audio models rely on newly initialized alignment layers trained on far smaller corpora than their video backbones, limiting extensibility and architectural simplicity. No single framework unifies both generation and editing across video and audio modalities.

## Input / Output

The model accepts text prompts or multi-task editing instructions, optional reference images (for image-to-video generation), reference videos (for editing), and reference audio (for acoustic context). Visual references are consumed via channel-wise concatenation with a binary mask to avoid token explosion, while audio references are appended as tokens (occupying <1% of video sequence length). The output is jointly generated synchronized video and audio, with the model handling both from-scratch generation and instruction-based editing of existing video-audio content in a single forward pass.

## Method

JoVA employs a dual-branch transformer (video + audio) where the audio branch is initialized by duplicating pretrained video backbone parameters, and all modalities interact through a single joint self-attention mechanism that concatenates video, audio, and text tokens—eliminating separate cross-attention fusion layers. For visual conditioning in editing tasks, a channel-wise strategy concatenates the reference video latent and a binary mask along the channel dimension (fused by a projector), while audio references use direct token concatenation. A localized mouth-area loss applies the flow matching objective restricted to the lip region (identified via facial keypoint detection mapped to VAE latent space), weighted by λ and applied only to human-speech data. Training uses flow matching with a two-stage schedule: the audio branch is first trained in isolation on text-to-audio/speech data, then joint video-audio generation and editing training follows. Temporal-aligned RoPE from MMAudio synchronizes positional encodings across modalities, and the entire system is trained on ~3M curated video-audio-text pairs spanning general scenes, human speech, and synthetic editing data.

## Results

On JoVABench-Gen, JoVA achieves the best lip-sync confidence (LSE-C 6.70), lowest Fréchet Distance (FD 0.67), highest Content Enjoyment (CE 5.40), Production Quality (PQ 6.49), Motion Score (MS 0.97), Aesthetic Score (AS 0.48), and Identity Consistency (ID 0.78) among all joint video-audio generation methods. On JoVABench-Edit, JoVA attains the best LSE-C (5.88), LSE-D (1.66), Inception Score (IS 3.21), and Aesthetic Score (AS 0.47) while jointly synthesizing both video and audio, outperforming cascaded baselines that receive ground-truth audio. On Verse-Bench, JoVA achieves the lowest WER (0.11), best DeSync (0.16), FD (0.82), KL (1.39), CE (4.47), MS (0.75), and AS (0.49), confirming strong generalization across diverse audio categories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among joint video-audio generation methods, JoVA outperforms Ovi (LSE-C 6.41), LTX-2 (6.21), and UniAVGen (5.87) on lip-sync confidence, and leads on FD, CE, PQ, MS, AS, and ID, while remaining competitive on WER (0.19 vs. LTX-2's 0.15) and KL. UniVerse-1 and JavisDiT trail substantially (LSE-C 1.62 and 1.04 respectively), indicating their stitching-based architectures struggle with fine-grained audio-visual alignment. The audio-driven Wan-S2V (LSE-C 6.43) is the closest competitor on lip-sync but lacks audio generation capability entirely. On JoVABench-Edit, JoVA (LSE-C 5.88, LSE-D 1.66, IS 3.21) surpasses all cascaded baselines; the nearest is Ditto+Wav2Lip (LSE-C 5.85) which still lags on video fidelity (IS 2.33 vs. 3.21) and aesthetic quality (AS 0.42 vs. 0.47), and Diff2Lip-based pipelines are far weaker (LSE-C 2.34–4.07). On Verse-Bench, JoVA edges out Ovi (LSE-C 6.65 vs. 6.61, WER 0.11 vs. 0.12) and dominates on DeSync (0.16 vs. 0.49), FD (0.82 vs. 0.97), and MS (0.75 vs. 0.55), confirming broader generalization beyond the training distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

JoVA builds on the Waver transformer-based video diffusion backbone and the MMAudio VAE/RoPE for audio, adopting flow matching as its training objective. Its key architectural departure from prior joint video-audio generation methods (UniVerse-1, Ovi, LTX-2, UniAVGen, JavisDiT) is the replacement of explicit cross-attention fusion layers with a single joint self-attention over concatenated video, audio, and text tokens, reusing pretrained transformer parameters rather than introducing newly initialized alignment modules. For editing, it departs from the fragmented pipeline paradigm (separate video editor + lip-sync model) by handling both modalities natively, using channel-wise conditioning for visual references to avoid token explosion and a localized mouth-area loss for lip-speech synchronization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By unifying generation and editing across video and audio in a single 12B-parameter model, JoVA offers a practical, extensible framework that eliminates the need to stitch together multiple specialized models, and the introduced JoVABench-Gen/JoVABench-Edit benchmarks fill a gap in systematic evaluation for joint multimodal editing. However, the paper acknowledges that audio-video editing is currently limited to human-speech scenes due to data scarcity, fast hand motion still produces finger artifacts, and text-rich backgrounds are not reliably preserved during editing. Open questions include whether the joint self-attention design scales to longer sequences or additional modalities, and whether the automated data synthesis pipeline can be extended to general-scene audio-video editing pairs.
