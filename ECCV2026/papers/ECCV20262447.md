## Problem

The widely adopted cue-conflict benchmark for measuring shape-texture bias in neural networks suffers from three compounding reliability issues: stylization-based cue construction fails to produce perceptually valid, separable, and balanced cues; ratio-based bias scores conflate directional preference with absolute cue sensitivity; and restricted-label evaluation distorts model predictions by ignoring the full decision space. Together, these artifacts confound genuine perceptual bias with cue construction problems, leading to conflicting conclusions across studies.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a pretrained vision model (CNN or ViT) and evaluates it on REFINED-BIAS images: pure shape cues (silhouettes with local geometric structure), pure texture cues (scale-consistent repetitive patterns), and mixed cue-conflict images, all drawn from 20 curated ImageNet superclasses with 300 source images per class. Rather than outputting a single bias ratio, the method produces decomposed Shape-Sens and Texture-Sens scores computed as Mean Reciprocal Rank over the model's full 1000-class logit ranking, from which shape and texture preference scores are derived as normalized ratios. This full-label, ranking-based formulation means a model that ranks the correct shape label 2nd (MRR contribution 1/2) is distinguished from one that ranks it 100th (1/100), unlike binary accuracy.

## Method

REFINED-BIAS has two core components. First, a curated dataset of 6,000 images across 20 superclasses (10 shape-dominant, 10 texture-dominant) where shape is explicitly defined as non-repeating geometric structure encompassing both global silhouette and local substructure, and texture as scale-consistent repetitive patterns recognizable across patch sizes. Cues are generated via a controlled pipeline (not neural stylization) with per-image human inspection, achieving near-perfect inter-rater agreement (Fleiss κ = 0.98 for shape, 0.80 for texture) versus 0.73 and 0.29 for cue-conflict. Second, a sensitivity metric that replaces accuracy with Mean Reciprocal Rank computed over the model's full logit ranking in the complete ImageNet-1k label space, applied only in the denominator of the preference ratio. This separates 'how much a model knows' (sensitivity) from 'which cue it prefers' (preference), enabling fair cross-model comparison and revealing models that genuinely utilize both cues simultaneously.

## Results

REFINED-BIAS achieves near-perfect human inter-rater agreement for shape cues (Fleiss' κ = 0.98) and substantial agreement for texture (κ = 0.80), compared to cue-conflict's κ = 0.73 (shape) and κ = 0.29 (texture). Model recognition on REFINED-BIAS reaches 46% top-1 accuracy for shape and 63% for texture, versus only 4% and 21% on cue-conflict. Across 32 ResNet-50 models with different training strategies, REFINED-BIAS consistently and significantly identifies shape preference for all shape-focused strategies (p = 0.002–0.018), while cue-conflict yields non-significant or contradictory results (p = 0.181–0.684). On large-scale models (OpenCLIP, DINOv2), REFINED-BIAS reliably detects shape preference (p ≤ 0.002) where cue-conflict is inconsistent (p = 0.030–0.565).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the cue-conflict benchmark (Geirhos et al., ICLR 2018), the de facto standard for shape-texture bias evaluation. REFINED-BIAS decisively outperforms cue-conflict in diagnostic reliability: for shape-focused training strategies (shape augmentation, contrastive learning, texture distortion, mixed augmentation), REFINED-BIAS yields statistically significant shape preference in all four cases (p = 0.002–0.018), whereas cue-conflict fails to reach significance for shape augmentation (p = 0.181) and contrastive learning (p = 0.684). Critically, for adversarial training—where no bias shift is expected—REFINED-BIAS correctly reports no significant change (p = 0.489), while cue-conflict erroneously reports a large, significant shift toward shape preference (p = 5.19e-05), contradicting the benchmark's own premise that shape bias improves performance. On large-scale ViT models (OpenCLIP, DINOv2), REFINED-BIAS consistently detects shape preference (p ≤ 0.002) across all six variants, while cue-conflict is inconsistent (p ranging from 0.030 to 0.565), failing to reveal a clear pattern. In the quantization analysis, REFINED-BIAS shows both shape and texture sensitivities decreasing monotonically with stronger quantization, consistent with the drop in in-domain accuracy, whereas cue-conflict paradoxically reports increasing shape preference alongside decreasing accuracy, contradicting its own core insight.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the cue-conflict benchmark of Geirhos et al. (2018), which established stylized shape-texture conflict images as the de facto standard for diagnosing cue preference in CNNs and ViTs. It also engages with a growing line of partial critiques (Tartaglini et al., Burgert et al., Wen et al., Doshi et al.) that each identified one or two flaws—cue imbalance, ratio-metric limitations, or shape-sensitivity measurement—but left the others unresolved. REFINED-BIAS departs from the stylization-based construction entirely, replacing model-derived content/style features with human-perceptual definitions of shape (global + local geometry) and texture (scale-consistent repetition), and replaces the accuracy-based ratio metric with a full-label-space MRR-based sensitivity measure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By resolving the construction, metric, and evaluation-space problems simultaneously, REFINED-BIAS offers the field a more reliable instrument for a question that has produced genuinely contradictory conclusions (shape vs. texture driving performance) depending on which architecture or training strategy is varied. The finding that balanced dual-cue utilization—not a single dominant cue—correlates with in-domain accuracy, and that local-to-global attention mechanisms (Swin, CMT) specifically enhance shape sensitivity, provides a clearer mechanistic story than prior work. Open questions remain around 3D geometry and viewpoint dependence, the limited 20-superclass scope, and whether the human-perceptual cue definitions generalize beyond ImageNet-derived categories.
