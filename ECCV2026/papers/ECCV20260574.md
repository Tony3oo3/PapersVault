## Problem

Current image editing benchmarks are largely confined to natural images and shallow commonsense reasoning, providing limited assessment of whether unified multimodal models can coordinate structured, discipline-specific knowledge with precise visual editing. No existing benchmark explicitly tests a model's ability to apply academic disciplinary knowledge (e.g., geometric theorems, chemical reaction rules, economic diagram conventions) while performing targeted modifications to structured visual content.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260574/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample is an image editing triplet consisting of an input image (academic diagrams, molecular structures, data visualizations, geometric figures, etc.), a textual editing instruction deliberately designed to require implicit discipline-informed reasoning (e.g., asking for a reaction product without specifying structural transformations), and a ground truth edited image. The 520 samples span 10 academic domains with hierarchical sub-disciplines, and the edited output must simultaneously satisfy domain-specific correctness, task-dependent visual consistency constraints, and logical readability of academic representations.

## Method

GRADE introduces a multi-dimensional automated evaluation protocol with three complementary axes. Discipline Reasoning uses GPT-5 to generate weighted binary scoring questions aligned with required disciplinary knowledge per sample, then employs Gemini-3-Flash as a judge to assess the edited result against these explicit criteria alongside the GT image, yielding a normalized 0–1 score. Visual Consistency is decomposed into three task-specific types (localized, style, and independence) scored 0/1/2, while Logical Readability assesses clarity, annotation correctness, and representational coherence also on a 0/1/2 scale. Overall accuracy requires the maximum score across all three dimensions simultaneously, and a relaxed score is computed as a weighted average (0.6/0.3/0.1) of the normalized dimensions. The benchmark is validated through human alignment studies (MAE ~10% across dimensions) and ablations on judge model choice and instruction explicitness.

## Results

On GRADE (520 samples, 10 disciplines), Nano Banana Pro achieves the highest overall accuracy of 46.2%, with 77.5% on Discipline Reasoning, 89.5% on Visual Consistency, and 95.8% on Logical Readability. No model exceeds 50% accuracy, and the best open-source model (Qwen-Edit-2511) reaches only 2.7% accuracy with 18.6% reasoning score, revealing a massive closed-source vs. open-source gap. Under the relaxed weighted score (0.6/0.3/0.1 for Reasoning/Consistency/Readability), Nano Banana Pro scores 82.9 while most open-source models remain below 40.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Nano Banana Pro dominates all 20 evaluated models, achieving 46.2% accuracy and 77.5% reasoning score, substantially ahead of Nano Banana 2 (39.6%) and Seedream 5.0 (24.7%). A striking finding is that GPT-Image-1.5, which performs comparably to Nano Banana Pro on existing editing benchmarks, drops to only 16.0% accuracy on GRADE—a 30.2-point gap—exposing its weakness in discipline-informed reasoning. The closed-source vs. open-source divide is extreme: the best open-source model Qwen-Edit-2511 (2.7% accuracy, 18.6% reasoning) trails the worst closed-source model Seedream 4.0 (3.1%) by a narrow margin, while OmniGen and Bagel (think) score near zero. Among open-source models, DreamOmni achieves the highest consistency (83.2%) and readability (89.1%) but only 1.0% accuracy, indicating it preserves the input image rather than performing meaningful edits. Step-1X variants show that adding 'think' or 'think+reflect' reasoning steps yields marginal gains (1.0% → 1.4% → 2.3%), suggesting that chain-of-thought alone is insufficient for discipline-informed editing.

## Relation to Prior Work

GRADE carves new ground at the intersection of two established research threads: image editing benchmarks (ImgEdit, RISEBench, KRISBench) that test reasoning over natural images with commonsense knowledge, and discipline-specific benchmarks (MMMU, MMMG, GenExam) that evaluate academic knowledge in understanding or generation but not editing. The paper repurposes the MLLM-as-a-judge paradigm but replaces single-instruction prompting with a structured, question-guided evaluation protocol inspired by GenExam and WorldGenBench, decomposing discipline reasoning into weighted binary scoring points. Conceptually, it extends the image editing task from implicit commonsense reasoning to structured, domain-constrained reasoning where models must simultaneously preserve visual structure, apply disciplinary priors, and produce logically coherent academic representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GRADE reveals that even the strongest closed-source model (Nano Banana Pro) fails on over half of discipline-informed editing samples, and that models performing comparably on existing editing benchmarks diverge dramatically here (46.2% vs. 16.0%), exposing a previously unmeasured axis of capability. The error taxonomy (recognition, knowledge, reasoning process, generation process) and the instruction-explicitness ablation—showing open-source models benefit disproportionately from explicit guidance—provide actionable diagnostics for model developers. Open questions include whether discipline-specific knowledge can be injected into editing pipelines without sacrificing general editing quality, and whether the large closed-source/open-source gap will narrow as open models incorporate structured reasoning. The benchmark's reliance on a single judge model (Gemini-3-Flash) and its 520-sample scale, while validated against human annotations, leave room for questions about generalizability to unseen sub-disciplines.
