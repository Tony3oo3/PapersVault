## Problem

Existing JEPA-based self-supervised pre-training methods (e.g., I-JEPA) treat target regions uniformly and predict their latent embeddings in parallel and independently, ignoring both which regions carry the most discriminative semantic content and in what order predictions should proceed. This paper asks whether predictive self-supervised learning can benefit from an explicit notion of where to predict and in what order, motivated by the observation that human visual perception attends selectively and progressively from primary to secondary cues.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262338/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard RGB image (224×224 for ViT-B/16, 336×336 for ViT-L/16, 518×518 for ViT-H/16) and produces patch-level visual representations via a Vision Transformer encoder. During pre-training, the image is partitioned into N=5 irregular, non-overlapping regions derived from an attention saliency map (not uniform grid patches), and the model outputs sequential latent embeddings for each region. An auxiliary [CLS] token is added solely to compute the saliency map and does not contribute to the final representation, keeping inference architecture identical to I-JEPA.

## Method

DSeq-JEPA introduces two synergistic inductive biases on top of the I-JEPA framework. First, Discriminative Region Prioritization computes a saliency map from the similarity between the [CLS] token and patch embeddings at a selected transformer layer, applies Otsu's thresholding and connected-component labeling to extract irregular regions, and ranks them by mean saliency; a probabilistic curriculum linearly increases the probability of using discriminative selection from 0 to 1 over pre-training epochs to stabilize early optimization. Second, Sequential Next-Region Embedding Prediction replaces I-JEPA's flat independent predictions with an autoregressive process: at step k, a predictor estimates the latent embedding of region R_{k+1} conditioned on the context-encoder representations of all preceding regions R_1…R_k plus a positional token for R_{k+1}, trained with a smoothed L1 (Huber) loss. The key insight is that discriminative selection supplies a semantically meaningful prediction trajectory (where) and sequential prediction exploits that trajectory to capture inter-region dependencies (in what order); ablations show neither component is effective in isolation, and their combination breaks the permutation symmetry over target regions that flat prediction implicitly assumes.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K linear probing, DSeq-JEPA achieves 73.5% (ViT-B/16), 77.9% (ViT-L/16), and 82.4% (ViT-H/16) top-1 accuracy, improving over I-JEPA by +1.1, +0.8, and +1.3 points respectively. On fine-grained categorization, DSeq-JEPA with ViT-L/16 reaches 39.5% (iNat21), 68.1% (CUB), and 68.9% (Stanford Cars), yielding a 68.2% average vs. I-JEPA's 67.5%. For dense prediction, DSeq-JEPA (ViT-B/16) attains 50.5 AP^box and 45.0 AP^mask on MS-COCO and 48.1 mIoU on ADE20K, outperforming I-JEPA by +0.6, +0.5, and +0.5 respectively. On CLEVR low-level reasoning, DSeq-JEPA (ViT-L/16) scores 86.4 (Count) and 71.5 (Dist), surpassing I-JEPA by +0.8 and +0.3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against I-JEPA (the primary baseline), DSeq-JEPA wins on every reported metric across all three backbone scales: +1.1/+0.8/+1.3 on ImageNet linear probing and +0.5/+0.2/+0.7 on fine-tuning, plus consistent gains on iNat21, CUB, and Stanford Cars. Against C-JEPA, DSeq-JEPA matches ImageNet accuracy at ViT-B/16 but clearly outperforms it on fine-grained tasks at ViT-L/16 (e.g., 68.1 vs. 65.8 on CUB, 68.9 vs. 67.7 on Cars), and DSeq-C-JEPA surpasses C-JEPA across all settings. Against NEPA (a sequential embedding prediction baseline), DSeq-JEPA and DSeq-C-JEPA achieve higher ImageNet fine-tuning accuracy at both ViT-B (84.2/84.3 vs. 83.8) and ViT-L (86.8/87.2 vs. 85.3). Against LeJEPA, DSeq-JEPA with ViT-H/16 outperforms by +3.4 points on ImageNet linear probing (82.4 vs. 79.0). Against multi-view baselines DINO and iBOT, DSeq-JEPA is competitive on ImageNet fine-tuning (84.2 vs. 82.8/84.0) despite using a simpler single-view recipe and 600 epochs vs. 1600, and substantially outperforms DINO on fine-grained tasks (65.0 vs. 50.4 average). Against MAE, DSeq-JEPA achieves +5.5 ImageNet linear probing (73.5 vs. 68.0) in half the training epochs (600 vs. 1600).

## Relation to Prior Work

DSeq-JEPA extends the I-JEPA framework (Assran et al., 2023) by replacing its uniform, parallel target-region prediction with two structured inductive biases: attention-derived discriminative region selection and sequential next-region latent prediction. Conceptually, it bridges JEPA-style latent-space prediction with the autoregressive next-token paradigm of iGPT and RandSAC, but operates entirely in embedding space rather than reconstructing pixels. The contrastive regularization from C-JEPA (Mo & Tong, 2024) is shown to be complementary, yielding DSeq-C-JEPA. The paper also draws on Otsu's thresholding and connected-component analysis for region extraction, and uses a [CLS]-token similarity map as a lightweight saliency proxy, validated against Grad-CAM.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The consistent gains across classification, fine-grained recognition, dense prediction, and low-level reasoning suggest that explicitly modeling where and in what order to predict yields more structured, part-aware representations than treating all target regions uniformly. The ablation revealing that discriminative selection and sequential prediction are individually harmful but jointly beneficial is a useful design principle for future JEPA variants. Limitations include a modest ~10% pre-training compute overhead, reliance on a fixed number of regions (N=5), and the fact that improvements, while consistent, are in the 0.5–1.5 point range. The authors note future extension to vision-language pre-training as a natural next step, and whether the discriminative-ordering principle transfers to other modalities or architectures remains open.
