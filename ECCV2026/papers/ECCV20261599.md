## Problem

Existing weakly supervised video anomaly detection methods rely on holistic video representations or free-form generative explanations, making it impossible to identify which specific contextual factors (actions, objects, environments) actually drive an anomaly decision. This lack of explicit semantic attribution limits both interpretability and the ability to reason about context-sensitive abnormality, where the same action may be benign in one setting but dangerous in another.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261599/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes untrimmed surveillance videos that are first split into 64-frame segments for offline captioning (via InternVideo 2.5 with three clue-specific prompts) and then into 16-frame segments for online detection. Precomputed CLUE captions (Action, Environment, Object) are cached and encoded by a frozen CLIP text encoder alongside frozen CLIP visual embeddings of keyframes, so the online pipeline requires no captioning model. Outputs include per-segment z-score-normalized anomaly scores, a predicted anomaly category, per-clue attention weights providing factor-level attribution, keyword-level evidence within each clue, and optional natural-language explanations generated by a LoRA-tuned InternVL2-2B MLLM.

## Method

CLUE-VAD decomposes each video segment into three structured semantic clues—Action, Environment, and Object—extracted as separate captions and encoded via a frozen CLIP text encoder, then fused with a CLIP visual embedding. The core mechanism is Category-Aware CLUE Learning: learnable per-category weights (softmax-normalized over the three clues) modulate clue embeddings before projection, so the model adaptively emphasizes the most relevant semantic factor for each anomaly type (e.g., Environment for Explosion, Action for Fighting, Object for Robbery). A lightweight category classifier (trained with focal loss) estimates the video category at inference to retrieve the correct weights, while an anomaly-guided top-K snippet selector focuses learning on informative segments. Training uses a top-k MIL ranking loss for anomaly scoring plus attention-entropy and temporal-smoothness regularizers on the clue attention branch. For explanation, a Reporter module computes per-segment clue attention (yielding interpretable attribution scores) and injects the fused clue representation as a prefix token into a LoRA-adapted MLLM to generate grounded natural-language descriptions.

## Results

CLUE-VAD achieves 89.47% AUC on UCF-Crime and 88.24% AP on XD-Violence, establishing the best performance among all explainable VAD methods (beating Holmes-VAU by +0.51 AUC and +0.56 AP, and Ex-VAD by +1.18 AUC and +1.72 AP). It remains competitive with the strongest multi-modality baselines, trailing only LEC-VAD (89.97/88.47) and π-VAD (90.33/85.37) on UCF-Crime AUC while surpassing π-VAD on XD-Violence AP (88.24 vs. 85.37). For explanation generation, CLUE-VAD (2B parameters) outperforms all 7B–8B MLLM baselines on BLEU-1/2/3/4, METEOR, and ROUGE using InternVL2-2B as the backbone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among explainable VAD methods, CLUE-VAD clearly leads: it surpasses Holmes-VAU (88.96/87.68) by +0.51 AUC and +0.56 AP, Ex-VAD (88.29/86.52) by +1.18 AUC and +1.72 AP, VERA (86.60/88.20) by +2.87 AUC, and LAVAD (80.28/62.01) by a wide margin. Against the strongest multi-modality baselines, CLUE-VAD is slightly below LEC-VAD (89.97/88.47) on both datasets (−0.50 AUC, −0.23 AP) and below π-VAD on UCF-Crime AUC (89.47 vs. 90.33), but it reverses the gap on XD-Violence AP where it beats π-VAD by +2.87 (88.24 vs. 85.37). All single-modality baselines (MIL, RTFM, CLAV, UMIL, UR-DMU, TSA) are outperformed by at least 1.9 AUC on UCF-Crime. For MLLM-based explanation generation (Table 3), CLUE-VAD with a 2B InternVL2-2B backbone outperforms 7B–8B models (Video-ChatGPT, Video-LLaMA, Video-LLaVA, Qwen2-VL, InternVL2-8B) on BLEU-1 (0.2982 vs. 0.2687 best), BLEU-4 (0.0739 vs. 0.0339), METEOR (0.1729 vs. 0.1442), and ROUGE (0.2762 vs. 0.2158), indicating that structured CLUE grounding yields more faithful anomaly explanations than purely generative MLLM pipelines.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLUE-VAD carves out a distinct niche among three existing WSVAD paradigms: multi-modal CLIP-based methods (VadCLIP, PEMIL) that rely on holistic scene-level alignment, vision-space Mixture-of-Experts approaches (Skynet-V1, Sherlock) that decompose along visual axes, and MLLM-based reasoning pipelines (Holmes-VAU, Ex-VAD, VERA) that generate free-form explanations. Rather than modifying scoring dynamics or expert routing, CLUE-VAD restructures the semantic input itself by decomposing each segment into three language-grounded textual clues (Action, Environment, Object) and learning category-aware importance weights under MIL supervision. This positions the work as a bridge between structured semantic decomposition (inspired by TDSD's context/object split) and explainable VAD, but with explicit per-category, per-clue attribution that neither vision-space MoE nor holistic MLLM pipelines provide.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The structured three-clue decomposition offers a practical and interpretable mechanism for factor-level attribution in surveillance anomaly detection—answering not just *when* an anomaly occurs but *which semantic factor* (a weapon, a fire, a violent action) drives the decision. However, the approach inherits a critical dependency on upstream VLM captioning quality, and the segment-level formulation cannot disentangle simultaneous multi-activity events or region-specific anomalies within a single frame. Open questions include whether the category-aware weighting generalizes to open-world or unseen anomaly categories, how robust the pipeline is to captioning errors in low-visibility conditions, and whether the structured decomposition can be extended to spatio-temporal (region-wise) reasoning. The modest performance gains over strong explainable baselines (e.g., +0.5 AUC over Holmes-VAU) suggest the primary value is interpretability rather than raw detection accuracy.
