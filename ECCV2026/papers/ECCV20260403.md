## Problem

State-of-the-art text-guided image editing models frequently fail to preserve an object's intrinsic visual attributes—geometry, texture, color, and fine-grained details—throughout the editing process, even for simple transformations like rotation or background removal. Additionally, the conditioning embedding space in rectified flow editing models, which is not directly supervised during training, was found to encode a latent prediction of the final output even at high noise levels, a property that had gone unexploited.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260403/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 1024×1024 RGB source image depicting a product in a complex real-world 'lifestyle' scene and a text prompt specifying the desired transformation (including degree-level rotation angles and object description), and produces a 1024×1024 studio-quality target image of the same product isolated on a white background with realistic shadow at the requested orientation. The conditioning image is encoded through a VAE, packed into 2×2 spatial patches, and concatenated with the noisy target tokens along the sequence dimension before being processed by the MM-DiT transformer blocks.

## Method

FlowMirror is a parameter-free auxiliary loss that supervises the transformer's conditioning output h_L^cond—normally discarded at inference—against the target image's VAE latent z. The supervision operates in latent space using a multi-scale Laplacian-pyramid decomposition over S scales: at each scale, a high-frequency term (L1 loss between original and Gaussian-blurred tensors) preserves fine details, while a low-frequency term (L2 loss on downsampled blurred tensors) enforces global structural alignment. The total loss combines the standard Conditional Flow Matching objective with this auxiliary term weighted by λ_aux. The key insight is that certain model families (Qwen-based) already encode target predictions in the conditioning space, and explicitly supervising this space amplifies that behavior, causing the model to form accurate output representations early in denoising. Fine-tuning is performed via DoRA (rank 64) for 10k steps, with the auxiliary loss requiring no additional parameters or architectural changes.

## Results

On the ABO-Edit validation set (400 samples), fine-tuning Qwen-Image-Edit-2509 with FlowMirror achieves the best overall FID of 25.91, outperforming the zero-shot best (FLUX.2 at 53.67) by more than 2×. The auxiliary loss consistently improves pixel-level and perceptual metrics: for Qwen-2509, FID drops from 26.48 to 25.91, PSNR rises from 19.64 to 19.79, and LPIPS decreases from 0.112 to 0.111; for FLUX.1-Kontext, FID improves from 31.68 to 31.26 and DINOv2 from 90.99 to 91.25. Semantic alignment (CLIP, DINOv2) remains essentially unchanged, confirming that gains are in structural and detail fidelity rather than semantic shift.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against all four zero-shot baselines, the fine-tuned Qwen-2509 + FlowMirror model achieves a dramatic FID reduction (25.91 vs. 53.67 for FLUX.2, 57.66 for Qwen-2509, 59.32 for FLUX.1, and 88.60 for Kandinsky), with corresponding gains in PSNR (+7 to +10 dB) and LPIPS (−0.2 to −0.47). Within the fine-tuned comparison, FlowMirror consistently improves over its no-aux-loss counterpart on both architectures: for Qwen-2509 it lowers FID by 0.57 and raises PSNR by 0.15 dB, while for FLUX.1-Kontext it lowers FID by 0.42 and raises DINOv2 by 0.26 points. The one metric where FlowMirror does not improve is CLIP similarity for Qwen-2509 (97.21→97.17), a negligible 0.04-point decrease indicating no semantic degradation. Notably, the fine-tuned FLUX.1 + FlowMirror (FID 31.26) still trails fine-tuned Qwen-2509 + FlowMirror (FID 25.91), suggesting that the Qwen architecture's natural predictive behavior in the conditioning space (Observation 1) synergizes more strongly with the auxiliary supervision.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the MM-DiT architecture (Esser et al., ICML 2024) and the rectified flow / conditional flow matching framework (Lipman et al., ICLR 2023), operating within the same training objective and transformer-based latent-space paradigm used by FLUX and Qwen-Image-Edit. Its key departure is an empirical observation about the *unused* conditioning output h_L^cond in MM-DiT: rather than being a discarded byproduct, it encodes a latent prediction of the final image in certain model families (Qwen-based) even at high noise levels. The FlowMirror loss repurposes this previously unmonitored signal as a parameter-free auxiliary supervision target, and the ABO-Edit benchmark extends prior image-editing datasets (MagicBrush, InstructPix2Pix, ImgEdit) by introducing 3D-rendered ground-truth targets with degree-level viewpoint annotations specifically designed to probe object attribute preservation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ABO-Edit fills a practical gap for e-commerce and product-visualization pipelines where pixel-level fidelity of geometry, texture, and fine details is required, and its 3D-rendered ground truth enables objective, reproducible evaluation of object consistency that prior editing benchmarks lack. The FlowMirror loss is attractive in its simplicity (no new parameters, negligible overhead) and shows consistent if modest gains across two architectures, suggesting it could be a cheap add-on for production editing pipelines. However, the domain gap between Blender-rendered targets and real photographs, the limited 10k-step DoRA fine-tuning budget, and evaluation restricted to a single dataset temper the generalizability claims; whether the conditioning-space supervision finding transfers to other architectures or to full fine-tuning remains open.
