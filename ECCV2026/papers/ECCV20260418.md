## Problem

Electronic-nose recordings capture odor contributions from the entire scene, yet paired images show only a partial view, leaving many olfactory cues unobservable from pixels alone. Existing benchmarks like New York Smells align e-nose signals directly with images, but this vision-only supervision cannot account for smells originating outside the camera's field of view or for the mixture of multiple odor sources in a single recording. The paper asks whether language, carrying world knowledge about what a scene implies olfactorily, can serve as a semantic bridge to ground olfactory perception beyond what is directly visible.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a 32-channel e-nose signal formed by temporal concatenation of a baseline (ambient air) and sample (near-object) recording, each spanning ~14 timesteps, yielding a C×2T tensor, alongside a paired scene image. A VLM (Qwen3-VL-30B) is queried on the image to produce structured textual descriptors at three levels: primary object, environmental context, and K inferred ambient smell cues. The output is a 512-dimensional olfactory embedding in a shared CLIP-aligned space, plus two disentangled sub-embeddings (object-specific and contextual) that can be recombined through a decoder to synthesize novel smell fingerprints.

## Method

SCENT operates in three stages. First, a VLM generates structured scene descriptors (object, context, inferred smells) that are encoded by a frozen CLIP text encoder to produce language supervision compensating for visually unobservable olfactory factors. Second, a 6-layer, 8-head Transformer smell encoder maps the e-nose signal to a global embedding, which is projected through two modality-specific MLP heads into the CLIP visual and textual spaces; alignment is enforced via symmetric InfoNCE losses against fine-tuned CLIP image embeddings and frozen CLIP text embeddings. Third, a latent disentanglement stage takes the text-projected smell embedding and routes it through two separate decoders to yield object-specific and contextual odor components, each contrastively aligned with its corresponding VLM-generated text descriptor, while a reconstruction loss on the concatenated latents prevents representation collapse. The key architectural insight is that the dual projection heads and the compositionality of the VLM descriptors (separate object vs. context text) jointly enable both multimodal alignment and interpretable decomposition of complex smell mixtures.

## Results

On the New York Smells (NYS) dataset, SCENT with full semantic descriptors (O+Ctx+S) achieves 23.0 R@5 on smell-to-image (S2I) retrieval, 11.9 R@5 on smell-to-text (S2T) retrieval, and 23.3 R@5 on joint smell-to-image-text (S2IT) retrieval. These represent improvements of +3.0, +3.8, and +4.8 R@5 points over the reproduced NYS† baseline (20.0, 8.1, 18.5 respectively). The S2T gain corresponds to a 47% relative improvement, and the S2IT gain exceeds 7% in R@5. SCENT also outperforms the original NYS* reported numbers (16.5 R@5 on S2I) by 6.5 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the NYS model [44], the prior state-of-the-art on the New York Smells dataset, which is a vision-only architecture aligning e-nose signals with CLIP image embeddings. SCENT outperforms the reproduced NYS† on S2I retrieval by 3.0 R@5 points (23.0 vs. 20.0) and by 1.6 R@20 points (43.6 vs. 42.0), confirming that language guidance improves even purely visual retrieval. On S2T retrieval, where NYS†(adapted) relies on an Image Bridge protocol (smell→image→text), SCENT achieves 11.9 R@5 versus 8.1 R@5 for the best adapted variant, a 47% relative gain attributable to the native textual projection head. On joint S2IT retrieval, SCENT leads by 4.8 R@5 points (23.3 vs. 18.5) and by 5.0 R@20 points (42.5 vs. 40.7). SCENT wins on every reported metric across all three retrieval tasks; no comparison is lost or tied.

## Relation to Prior Work

SCENT sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) cross-modal contrastive alignment (CLIP, ImageBind, LanguageBind), which it repurposes to bind a third modality—e-nose sensor signals—into the CLIP visual-textual space; (2) language-as-semantic-bridge methods (ULIP-2, LAVILA, WineSensed/FEAST), which use VLM-generated descriptions to supervise otherwise hard-to-label modalities; and (3) machine olfaction, where the NYS dataset [44] established smell-to-image retrieval as the primary task but relied exclusively on visual supervision. The key departure is that no prior work combines olfactory sensor signals with both vision and text in a unified framework; SCENT introduces VLM-inferred ambient smell descriptors as an explicit semantic bridge to compensate for the partial observability of visual supervision, and adds a language-guided latent decomposition that has no direct analogue in prior olfactory or multimodal alignment literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to jointly align e-nose measurements with visual and textual representations, opening a path toward olfactory-aware multimodal systems that could support environmental monitoring, food safety, or accessibility applications. The latent disentanglement of object-specific vs. contextual odors is practically useful for understanding complex smell mixtures, and the recombination retrieval experiment demonstrates compositional generalization to unseen (object, context) pairings. However, the approach is currently validated on a single dataset (NYS, 7K samples) with a specific e-nose hardware, and the authors acknowledge a substantial scale gap between olfactory benchmarks and the web-scale data used to train VLM backbones. Open questions include whether the language-bridge strategy generalizes to other underrepresented sensory modalities, how performance scales with larger olfactory corpora, and whether the VLM-inferred smell descriptors remain reliable in adversarial or highly atypical environments.
