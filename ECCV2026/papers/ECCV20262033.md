## Problem

While procedural video understanding has advanced toward complex multi-step mistake detection, it remains unclear whether current MLLMs can even recognize and explain mistakes at the individual step level. Existing benchmarks target long multi-step sequences and focus on detection (presence/absence) rather than explanation, and none provide controlled difficulty labels or sufficient coverage of single-element deviations (verb, ingredient, tool, location) to enable fine-grained analysis.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262033/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs a single-step cooking video (≤120 s, 16 uniformly sampled frames from a fixed overhead viewpoint) with a text instruction that may deviate from the video in exactly one operation element. The model produces a free-form response containing a consistency judgment, an observation of what actually occurs, and reasoning; no predefined answer choices are given and the model must consider all four mistake types simultaneously. An LLM-as-judge (GPT-5-mini) scores the response on three binary criteria of increasing granularity—mistake presence, type classification, and correction accuracy—yielding composite metrics including Full Pass Accuracy.

## Method

The core contribution is a low-cost synthesis pipeline that converts normal cooking videos from COM Kitchens into mistake samples by using Qwen2.5-7B to replace exactly one element (verb, ingredient, tool, or location) in the step instruction, with surrounding recipe context provided for realism. Difficulty labels (easy/hard) are assigned based on semantic proximity of the replacement word (e.g., slice vs. dice is hard; slice vs. boil is easy), and a human annotator filters for visual verifiability, non-synonymy, and label consistency, yielding 1,289 samples (989 mistakes, 300 correct). Evaluation is entirely zero-shot across 13 open-source MLLMs, using a multi-criteria LLM-as-judge framework that decomposes explanation quality into presence detection (MPC F1), type identification (Type Accuracy), and accurate observation (Correction Accuracy), with Full Pass Accuracy requiring all criteria to pass. The paper further analyzes scaling effects by model size and family, error taxonomy (Instruction Bias, Misrecognition, Reasoning Failure, Underspecification), and a hint-condition ablation that specifies the mistake type to isolate the type-classification subtask.

## Results

On Cook-MRE (1,289 single-step cooking video samples), the best model InternVL3.5-38B achieves 87.6% MPC F1 for mistake detection but only 50.2% Correction Accuracy and 57.4% Full Pass Accuracy, revealing a substantial gap between recognizing that a mistake exists and correctly explaining what went wrong. Group A general-purpose MLLMs (Qwen, InternVL, Tarsier) achieve MPC F1 in the 71–88% range, while Group B video-specialized models (ST-LLM, LLaVA-Video, VTimeLLM) show strong detection bias (e.g., ST-LLM: 80.3% MPC F1 but only 51.7% Macro F1) and near-zero correction accuracy (7.8%, 2.4%, 1.9% respectively). Scaling from small to large variants yields consistent gains only in the InternVL family, with other families showing negligible or negative deltas on hard problems.

## Baselines & Comparisons

InternVL3.5-38B leads all 13 models on every metric, achieving 57.4% Full Pass Accuracy—nearly 17 points above the second-best (InternVL3-38B at 56.5% Full Pass, 49.7% Corr. Acc.) and over 25 points above the next Group A model (Tarsier-34B at 40.2% Full Pass). Within Group A, the InternVL family is the only one where scaling from 8B to 38B yields uniformly positive gains across all four type×difficulty conditions (e.g., +35.5 points on verb-easy, +17.4 on ingredient-hard), whereas Qwen2.5-VL and Qwen3-VL show near-zero or slightly negative scaling deltas on hard problems. Group B video-specialized models (ST-LLM, LLaVA-Video, VTimeLLM) are dramatically outperformed: despite ST-LLM's 80.3% MPC F1, its Correction Accuracy is only 7.8% and Full Pass Accuracy 2.6%, indicating it flags mistakes without understanding the video content. Error analysis attributes this to Instruction Bias (56.5% of Group B errors vs. 21.6% in Group A), where models generate explanations driven by the text instruction rather than the actual video. Under the hint condition (mistake type provided), InternVL3.5-38B gains +7.2 points on Corr. Acc. (50.2→57.4) and +4.7 on Full Pass (57.4→62.1), while Group B models see Full Pass Accuracy consistently deteriorate, confirming that prompt engineering cannot compensate for weak visual grounding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant multi-step mistake detection paradigm (EgoPER, EgoOops, CaptainCook4D, ProMQA) by isolating single-step execution mistakes and adding a free-form explanation evaluation axis that prior benchmarks largely omit. Rather than collecting new videos for each mistake condition, it repurposes the COM Kitchens dataset and synthesizes mistakes by modifying text instructions with an LLM, enabling controlled type and difficulty labels at scale. The LLM-as-judge evaluation framework for scoring open-ended corrections extends the detection-only scoring used in prior procedural video benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even single-step cooking mistakes remain substantially unsolved (best Full Pass ~57%), establishing a clear lower bound before tackling multi-step temporal localization. The finding that LLM scaling alone yields limited gains—only the InternVL family, which also scales its vision encoder, shows consistent improvement—challenges the assumption that larger language backbones are the primary bottleneck for fine-grained video understanding. Open questions include whether the synthesized (text-modified) mistakes generalize to naturally occurring errors, whether the four-element framework transfers to other procedural domains, and whether the Instruction Bias failure mode can be mitigated through training rather than prompting.
