## Problem

Existing 3D human avatars achieve high visual fidelity but remain kinematic, unable to physically interact with objects or other avatars. Recent physics-based attempts either support only one-way force transfer, model only pose-level responses without surface deformation, or rely on simplified rigid-body representations. A fundamental obstacle in particle-based simulation (MPM) is that user-defined kinematic velocities are absorbed into the deformation gradient, generating spurious stress that resists the intended motion and prevents the avatar from reaching its target pose.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pose transformation sequence (T×J×4×4), a 3D avatar represented as either Animatable Gaussians (~300K particles) or SMPL-X mesh (~10K particles), 3D interaction objects, and per-region material parameters (density, Young's modulus). It outputs a physically simulated non-rigid avatar with bidirectional momentum exchange, tracked skeletal poses via closed-form Kabsch alignment of embedded bone particles, and a per-particle deformation gradient field. The simulation runs 100 MPM substeps per rendered frame on a 200³ background grid, with kinematic velocity derived from incremental (relative) pose transformations applied through LBS rather than absolute pose differences.

## Method

PIAvatar's key insight is to decouple the user-defined kinematic velocity from the deformation gradient update in MPM. A separate P2G/G2P pipeline transfers only the kinematic velocity to compute a kinematic deformation gradient F_p^k, which is then factored out of the total gradient via F_p ← F_p (F_p^k)^{-1}, ensuring that stress (and thus restorative internal forces) arises solely from physical deformations rather than prescribed motion. For pose tracking under non-rigid deformation, a skeletal structure (from OSSO) is embedded as particle groups within the avatar surface; each bone's rigid transformation is recovered in closed form via the Kabsch algorithm aligning canonical to current particle positions. Kinematic velocity for the next frame is computed from incremental pose transformations (T^{t→t+1} = (T^t)^{-1} T^{t+1}) applied to the current tracked pose, then propagated to all particles through LBS, maintaining stability even when external forces have displaced the avatar from its intended configuration. The entire pipeline is learning-free and operates within a conventional MPM framework, with an additional 200³ grid per avatar (122 MB) storing the kinematic momentum field.

## Results

PIAvatar achieves substantially lower pose-tracking error than the basic MPM baseline across both avatar types. At 100 simulation steps, PIAvatar reports MSE of 0.019 m (Animatable Gaussians) and 0.013 m (SMPL-X), compared to 0.079 m and 0.089 m for the baseline, with Acc@0.01 of 0.844 and 0.908 versus 0.097 and 0.358. The advantage grows over time: at 400 steps the baseline MSE nearly triples (0.332 m for AG, 0.248 m for SMPL-X) while PIAvatar's increases only modestly (0.046 m and 0.039 m), confirming that the deformation-gradient decoupling prevents cumulative stress-induced drift. Runtime overhead is modest, adding ~0.32 ms per step for a single SMPL-X avatar (0.492 ms total vs. 0.170 ms for basic MPM).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole quantitative baseline is the unmodified MPM (referred to as 'Baseline MPM', built on the PhysGaussian codebase without the proposed decoupling). PIAvatar wins decisively on every metric and every simulation-step setting for both avatar representations. For Animatable Gaussians at 100 steps, PIAvatar reduces MSE by 4× (0.019 vs. 0.079) and increases Acc@0.01 by nearly 9× (0.844 vs. 0.097); for SMPL-X the MSE reduction is 6.8× (0.013 vs. 0.089) and Acc@0.01 improves from 0.358 to 0.908. Critically, the baseline's error grows super-linearly with simulation steps (MSE nearly quadruples from 100 to 400 steps for AG), whereas PIAvatar's error grows only ~2.4×, demonstrating that the kinematic-velocity decoupling prevents the progressive stress accumulation that causes the baseline to drift away from the target pose. The paper does not compare against other physics-based avatar methods (e.g., Half-Physics, MPMAvatar, C-IPC-based approaches) with quantitative metrics; those are discussed qualitatively in the related work and introduction as lacking bidirectional interaction or non-rigid deformation.

## Relation to Prior Work

PIAvatar extends the Material Point Method (Jiang et al., 2016) and its 3D Gaussian integration via PhysGaussian (Xie et al., 2024) by introducing a deformation gradient decoupling that separates user-defined kinematic velocity from physical elastic deformation, drawing conceptual inspiration from elasto-plastic snow (Stomakhin et al., 2013) and thin-shell MPM (Guo et al., 2018). For pose tracking under non-rigid deformation, it embeds an OSSO-derived skeletal structure (Keller et al., 2022) and recovers joint transforms via the Kabsch algorithm in closed form, avoiding the expensive fitting or learning-based regression used in prior physics-aware avatar work. This positions the paper as a unification of previously fragmented capabilities—bidirectional momentum transfer, non-rigid surface deformation, and continuous pose tracking—within a single MPM pipeline, whereas prior methods like Half-Physics (pose-level only), MPMAvatar (garment-focused), and RL-based approaches (simplified rigid-body physics) each addressed only a subset.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a practical, closed-form (non-learning) path to physically consistent avatar interactions that could be integrated into real-time AR/VR pipelines, with modest overhead (~0.5 ms per step for SMPL-X) and only 122 MB extra memory per avatar. Key limitations remain: no friction model, no torque control for posture maintenance under gravity, and reliance on manually authored pose sequences from AMASS that lack physical responses to external forces. The VLM-driven Bayesian optimization for material parameters is a promising but preliminary direction, and the authors note that rapid collisions can cause object sticking before stress develops, pointing toward the need for learned scene-configuration understanding. Broader impact depends on whether the decoupling strategy generalizes to more complex multi-body scenes and whether the closed-form pose tracking remains stable under extreme deformations.
