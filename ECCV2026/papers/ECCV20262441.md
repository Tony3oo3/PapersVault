## Problem

Most VLN-CE methods are trained and evaluated in Habitat-style simulators that assume idealized odometry, discrete action primitives, and simplified collision handling, leading to severe performance degradation (up to 50%) when deployed on real robots. The lack of a controllable structure that decouples vision-language reasoning from physical execution makes it difficult to handle compounding localization drift and execution errors in long-horizon navigation.

## Input / Output

The system consumes a surround-view set of RGB-D frames acquired via in-place rotation, a natural-language navigation instruction, real-time LiDAR point clouds for occupancy mapping, and historical RGB-D frames for temporal context. Outputs include a discrete waypoint label from 9 slots (0–8 plus a stop label −1) mapped from short-horizon action sequences, continuous velocity commands published via ROS /cmd_vel, a 2D Interest Score Occupancy Map (ISOM) aligned with the log-odds occupancy grid, a localized point-of-interest in world coordinates, and an ordered sequence of semantic sub-tasks from the LLM planner.

## Method

Hi-Nav decomposes VLN-CE into three levels: a Gemini Pro LLM planner that splits long instructions into sub-tasks with closed-loop progress feedback; a middle-level Hi-VLN model that jointly performs environment perception and waypoint reasoning; and a ROS navigation stack (A* global planner + TEB local planner) for obstacle-aware execution. Hi-VLN uses a dual-stream encoder (RGB + depth) with cross-attention fusion, a BLIP-2-style Q-Former to align visual features with instruction text into shared query embeddings, and learnable temporal embeddings to retain long-horizon context. The queries feed a decoupled dual-branch head: an Image-Text Matching (ITM) branch predicts per-frame semantic scores projected onto the ISOM, while an action branch routes queries through a LoRA-finetuned Vicuna-7B that autoregressively predicts the optimal waypoint label. Training is two-stage: domain-adaptive pretraining of the depth encoder, fusion layer, and ITM head on Flickr30K with binary cross-entropy, followed by navigation tuning on the self-constructed VLN-Waypoint dataset using a combined loss of cross-entropy hard-label supervision and a KL-divergence soft-label term that penalizes predictions by topological distance from the optimal waypoint.

## Results

On the standard VLN-CE R2R Val-Unseen benchmark, Hi-VLN achieves 47.2% SR, 4.47 m NE, and 43.1% SPL, attaining the best Navigation Error among all compared methods while matching Uni-NaVid's success rate. In the Gazebo simulation (full Hi-Nav system), the method reaches 80% SR with 0.65 m NE overall, outperforming the next-best baseline (Uni-NaVid at 33.3% SR, 3.72 m NE) by a wide margin across all three difficulty levels. In real-world experiments, Hi-Nav achieves up to 95% SR (0.49 m NE) for visible single-goal tasks and maintains 35–85% SR on non-visible and multi-goal settings where baselines drop to 0%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the VLN-CE R2R Val-Unseen benchmark (Table 1), Hi-VLN achieves the lowest NE (4.47 m) among all methods, outperforming Uni-NaVid (5.58 m) and NaVid (5.47 m), while matching Uni-NaVid's SR (47.2% vs 47.0%) and exceeding it in SPL (43.1 vs 42.7). In the Gazebo simulation, Hi-Nav dominates all baselines by a large margin: it achieves 80% overall SR versus 33.3% for Uni-NaVid, 30% for NaVid and VLFM, and near-zero for CMA and Seq2Seq, with NE reduced from 3.72 m (Uni-NaVid) to 0.65 m. The gap is most pronounced on Hard tasks, where Hi-Nav reaches 70% SR while all baselines score 0–20%. In real-world experiments (Table 4), Hi-Nav consistently outperforms NaVid and Uni-NaVid across all visibility and goal-count settings; for example, on non-visible 1-goal tasks Hi-Nav achieves 85% SR (0.42 m NE) versus 45% (2.85 m) for Uni-NaVid and 25% (3.97 m) for NaVid, and on 3-goal non-visible tasks Hi-Nav still reaches 35% SR while both baselines fail entirely (0% SR). VLFM, which uses LiDAR-based frontier mapping, performs well on Easy Gazebo tasks (75% SR) but collapses on Medium and Hard settings, highlighting its inability to handle long-horizon multi-subtask instructions.

## Relation to Prior Work

Hi-Nav builds on the Q-Former cross-modal fusion mechanism from BLIP-2 and the waypoint-candidate generation pipeline from VLMNav/WMNav, but departs from the dominant Habitat-based VLN-CE paradigm by introducing a three-level hierarchical decomposition (LLM sub-task planning → ISOM-guided waypoint reasoning → ROS velocity execution) and by evaluating in a ROS/Gazebo stack that supports /cmd_vel control and LiDAR-based occupancy mapping. Unlike end-to-end VLN-CE methods (NaVid, Uni-NaVid) that predict low-level actions directly from egocentric video, Hi-Nav explicitly separates vision-language grounding from robot control, and unlike prior hierarchical VLN approaches that rely on topological region abstractions, it uses a continuous interest-score occupancy map for spatial reasoning. The VLN-Waypoint dataset bridges the gap between nav-graph supervision and waypoint-driven continuous navigation, extending the R2R/VLN-ego data with discrete waypoint labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ROS/Gazebo VLN benchmark is a practically important contribution for robotics practitioners who need to evaluate navigation policies under realistic execution constraints (continuous velocity commands, LiDAR odometry, collision handling) rather than Habitat's idealized discrete actions. The ablation showing that ISOM and sub-task decomposition are strongly synergistic (25% → 80% SR) but individually insufficient highlights that neither semantic map guidance nor instruction decomposition alone solves long-horizon VLN. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond Matterport3D-derived scenes, how it handles genuinely ambiguous or contradictory instructions, and whether the 7B-parameter LLM can be further compressed for edge deployment without degrading waypoint reasoning quality.
