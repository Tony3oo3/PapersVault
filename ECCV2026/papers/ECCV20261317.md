## Problem

Light field cameras can resolve the scale ambiguity of monocular depth via micro-baseline disparities, but learning-based LF depth estimation is bottlenecked by scarce training data (LF datasets are orders of magnitude smaller than RGB-D corpora) and tends to overfit idealized Lambertian appearances. Existing methods also aggregate multi-view evidence through implicit weighting without explicit visibility constraints, leading to brittle performance under real-world occlusions, reflections, and defocus.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261317/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a single light field capture from a 9×9 micro-lens array (multi-view sub-aperture images) and produces a center-view metric depth map. The disparity range is compact (d_min = −9 to d_max = 20), reflecting the narrow micro-baseline geometry, and the predicted disparity is converted to metric depth via a camera-specific disparity-to-depth mapping calibrated offline from the real light field system. Training uses 768×768 crops from RGB-D pairs, with light field supervision synthesized on-the-fly rather than pre-rendered.

## Method

SynLF couples two components: (1) PG-LF synthesis, which generates supervisory light fields on-the-fly from RGB-D via depth-aware forward splatting (z-buffer occlusion resolution), disparity-conditioned defocus blur (CoC proportional to |d|), and non-Lambertian perturbation that displaces projected samples in depth-flat textured regions to break strict photometric matching; and (2) VisDepth, a prior-initialized iterative estimator that first obtains an initial disparity by fusing a raw multi-view correlation volume with frozen Depth Anything V2-Small features via 3D cost aggregation, then performs 24 ConvGRU iterations where per-view visibility maps—computed by the same forward projection used in PG-LF synthesis—explicitly mask occluded source views before cost aggregation. The shared depth-aware projection operator between synthesis and estimation enforces geometric consistency, and the iterative visibility recomputation at each step progressively sharpens disparity in occluded and ambiguous regions.

## Results

On the authors' custom real-world indoor light field dataset (173 annotated captures, 0.5–2.5 m range), SynLF achieves 30.05 mm MAE, 144.34 mm RMSE, 1.38% AbsRel, and 98.77% δ₁ in a strict zero-shot setting (no fine-tuning). This represents a 61.26 mm MAE reduction over the best prior light field method (PlaneNet, 91.31 mm) and an 80.81 mm reduction over ESMNet (110.86 mm). On public synthetic benchmarks (HCI 4D and Inria Dense), SynLF maintains consistent MSE across both datasets (2.08 and 2.35 ×10⁻²), whereas HCI-trained methods degrade substantially when transferred (e.g., PlaneNet MSE rises from 0.69 to 1.97 ×10⁻²).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SynLF outperforms all five prior light field depth methods on every metric in the zero-shot real-world evaluation. The largest gains are over optimization-based OAVC (146.99 → 30.05 mm MAE, a 4.9× reduction) and OPAL (141.77 → 30.05 mm), while the best prior learning-based method, PlaneNet (91.31 mm), is still beaten by 61.26 mm in MAE and 3.10 percentage points in AbsRel. Notably, the same VisDepth architecture trained on the rendered HCI dataset achieves only 52.95 mm MAE, indicating that the 22.9 mm gap to SynLF is attributable to PG-LF synthesis data quality rather than model capacity. On public synthetic benchmarks (Table 4), HCI-trained methods (PlaneNet, OACC-Net) achieve lower MSE on their own training domain but degrade sharply on Inria Dense, whereas SynLF maintains consistent performance across both, confirming the cross-dataset generalization advantage of physics-grounded synthesis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard light field depth estimation pipeline, which relies on pre-rendered multi-view datasets (HCI, UrbanLF) and implicit view aggregation, by introducing a physics-grounded synthesis stage that generates supervisory light fields on-the-fly from abundant RGB-D data. The forward splatting and depth-aware projection are repurposed from rendering pipelines but applied selectively to model only the three effects that corrupt disparity correspondence (occlusion, defocus, non-Lambertian appearance), rather than pursuing full photorealistic simulation. The VisDepth estimator borrows the iterative ConvGRU refinement paradigm from stereo matching but couples it with an explicit visibility mask computed via the same projection operator used in synthesis, and initializes from a frozen monocular foundation model (Depth Anything V2). Conceptually, the paper reframes the data bottleneck in light field depth estimation as a synthesis design problem rather than an acquisition problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The reciprocal control experiment (Table 2) showing that PG-LF synthesis improves both PlaneNet and VisDepth by ~42% in MAE suggests that supervision quality, not model capacity, is the primary bottleneck for real-world light field depth estimation—a useful diagnostic for the field. The 30 mm zero-shot MAE on challenging real captures (transparency, specular, textureless) demonstrates that physics-grounded synthesis from RGB-D can replace costly light field data collection, opening a scalable path for LF perception. However, the approach remains limited to static indoor scenes, the synthesis is an approximation that does not model full optical complexity, and quantitative outdoor evaluation is absent. Open questions include whether the task-driven synthesis principle generalizes to dynamic scenes, other LF tasks (e.g., segmentation, tracking), and whether richer optical modeling (cat-eye bokeh, motion blur) would further close the sim-to-real gap.
