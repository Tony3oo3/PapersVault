## Problem

Existing AI video detection methods—post-hoc forensic detectors and digital watermarks—are locked in an arms race with rapidly improving generative models and lack an unforgeable physical link between the recorded scene and the resulting video. This paper asks whether a deterministic optical phenomenon, specifically the Moiré effect produced by a compact two-layer grating, can serve as a passive, physics-based authentication signature that real cameras produce naturally but generative models cannot faithfully reproduce.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260625/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a video clip (1080p, 60 fps in experiments) containing a visible two-layer grating assembly (lenticular sheet over a printed stripe pattern) with four ArUco fiducial markers at its corners, and requires relative motion between the camera and the assembly to generate measurable fringe shifts. It outputs a per-window Pearson correlation coefficient (evaluated over sliding 30-frame windows) between the extracted fringe phase signal and the rotation-compensated translational displacement of the assembly, where values near 1 certify authenticity and substantially lower values flag the video as potentially synthetic.

## Method

The core contribution is the Moiré motion invariant: a theoretical derivation showing that fringe phase displacement Δφ and the grating's translational image displacement Δu_trans are linearly coupled with a proportionality constant that is independent of viewing distance D, camera focal length f, and grating period p_r, because these unknowns cancel in the correlation. The verification pipeline operates in three stages: (1) ArUco marker detection and per-frame homography warp the grating region into a canonical rectangle, from which a 1D intensity profile is extracted and its dominant FFT bin's phase is tracked incrementally with 2π unwrapping; (2) PnP pose estimation from the four ArUco markers decomposes camera motion into translation and rotation, isolating the translational component (since rotation induces no inter-layer parallax and hence no fringe shift); (3) the Pearson correlation between the cumulative phase signal and the projected translational displacement is computed over sliding windows. The approach is entirely passive—requiring no active illumination or dedicated infrastructure—and the verifier needs no calibration or knowledge of scene geometry, only the two pixel-extracted signals.

## Results

Real recorded videos (n=86) achieve a mean Pearson correlation |ρ| = 0.87 (σ = 0.14) between Moiré fringe phase and grating displacement, confirming the Moiré motion invariant holds under diverse real-world conditions. Physics-based Blender/Cycles renderings reach near-perfect correlation (μ = 0.99), validating the pipeline under ideal conditions. AI-generated videos (n=92, from three state-of-the-art generators) concentrate at substantially lower values (μ = 0.57, σ = 0.21). The separation is highly statistically significant: Welch's t-test yields t(160) = 11.6, p < 10⁻²⁰, with Cohen's d = 1.71 (well above the 0.8 large-effect threshold).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper tests the Moiré motion invariant against three state-of-the-art video generators used as attack baselines: Veo 3.1 (μ = 0.58), Grok Imagine (μ = 0.56), and LTX-2 (μ = 0.57). All three fail to reproduce the physics-governed linear coupling between fringe phase and grating displacement, clustering roughly 0.30 below the real-video baseline (μ = 0.87). No generator achieves a meaningful advantage over the others, and even cherry-picked, manually corrected I2V outputs (conditioned on a real first frame) cannot close the gap. The evaluation protocol is deliberately generous to the attacker—using image-conditioned generation, manual Moiré region tracking with human correction, and only the best 92 of 321 generated samples—making the observed separation a conservative lower bound. The authors note that under a stricter protocol (requiring the attacker to also fool ArUco marker detection and automated tracking), the gap would be even larger.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Moiré effect—previously exploited in optical metrology and camera-tracking systems (MoiréBoard, MoiréTracker, MoiréVision)—as a passive authentication signature for video, a fundamentally different application that eliminates the unknown camera-distance and pose terms to yield a correlation invariant rather than a pose estimate. It departs from post-hoc forensic detectors (which are locked in an arms race with generative models) and from active structured-light signatures (VeriLight, Michael et al.) that require dedicated emitter hardware, by grounding authentication in a purely passive, off-the-shelf optical structure. The theoretical derivation builds on classical Moiré theory (Amidror; Kafri & Glatt) but the specific formulation of a distance-independent, calibration-free correlation test for video authentication is new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a deterministic optical phenomenon can serve as a physically grounded, model-agnostic authentication signal that does not degrade as generative models improve—addressing the core limitation of artifact-based detectors. Key limitations include the requirement for a physical grating assembly in the scene and relative camera-assembly motion, and the inability to detect localized edits (e.g., face swaps) outside the Moiré region, necessitating complementary deepfake detectors. Open questions include whether future physics-aware generative models could in principle synthesize correct fringe-displacement coupling, and how the approach extends to 2D Moiré patterns, dynamic lighting, and production-grade robustness against reflections and motion blur.
