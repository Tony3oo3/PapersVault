## Problem

Long-form video understanding in MLLMs is bottlenecked by limited context windows, forcing keyframe sampling to distill thousands of frames into a small budget. Existing sampling methods (BOLT, AKS, Q-Frame) operate at a single frame-level granularity, selecting which frames to retain but not how visual evidence should be allocated across spatial and temporal scales. This creates a fundamental conflict: detail-oriented queries need fine-grained spatial precision while event-level queries need broad temporal coverage, and a fixed-granularity design cannot satisfy both simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The method takes a long-form video (ranging from seconds to hours, with T discrete frames) and a natural language query as input. It produces a compact set of B keyframes (typically 8, 16, or 32) that is a union of two types: spatially zoomed-in frames where query-relevant regions are highlighted via attention-based alpha masks, and temporally zoomed-out hyperframes formed by concatenating τ=4 neighboring frames along the spatial axis and downsampling to trade spatial resolution for temporal coverage. The final frame set is sorted by original temporal index and fed to an MLLM (Qwen2.5-VL, LLaVA-OneVision, or GPT-5-Mini) for auto-regressive answer generation.

## Method

LENS is a training-free, plug-and-play framework that adaptively partitions a fixed frame budget between two complementary branches. The spatial zoom-in branch selects candidate frames via CLIP/BLIP image-text similarity with watershed-based temporal segmentation, then derives patch-level relevance scores by decomposing CLIP's [CLS] representation into per-token attention contributions across the deepest MSA layers, fusing query-conditioned and query-agnostic saliency via soft OR to produce attention-based visual prompts overlaid on frames. The temporal zoom-out branch constructs an undirected video graph using Gaussian-kernel affinities over frame similarity distances, performs iterative message passing (y^(t) = αSy^(t-1) + (1-α)y^(0)) to propagate and refine relevance scores across the graph, then forms hyperframes by concatenating neighboring frames and downsampling. A lightweight MLLM controller predicts an allocation ratio r∈[0,1] from the query text, splitting the budget between spatial and temporal branches, after which the merged frame set is passed to the MLLM for multi-modal reasoning.

## Results

With Qwen2.5-VL under an 8-frame budget, LENS achieves 60.7% overall accuracy on Video-MME (w/o subtitles), 59.3% on LongVideoBench, and 63.7% on MLVU, outperforming the strongest baseline Q-Frame by 2.0%, 2.7%, and 5.5% respectively. Gains scale with budget: at 32 frames, LENS reaches 67.1% on Video-MME, 60.6% on LVB, and 66.7% on MLVU. The method also generalizes to other backbones, improving LLaVA-OneVision from 53.2% to 58.7% and GPT-5-Mini from 64.8% to 69.0% on Video-MME overall (8 frames).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LENS consistently outperforms all three training-free keyframe sampling baselines (BOLT, AKS, Q-Frame) across every frame budget (8, 16, 32) and all three benchmarks. Against the strongest competitor Q-Frame, LENS gains +2.0% on Video-MME overall (60.7 vs 58.7), +2.7% on LVB (59.3 vs 56.6), and +5.5% on MLVU (63.7 vs 58.2) at 8 frames; the gap widens at 32 frames to +4.8% on Video-MME (67.1 vs 62.3). BOLT is the second-strongest baseline on Video-MME (57.0% at 8 frames) but trails LENS by 3.7 points; AKS is the weakest of the three, underperforming even uniform sampling on LVB at 8 frames (52.0 vs 53.3). The advantage is most pronounced on medium-length videos (9 min) in Video-MME, where LENS reaches 59.6% at 8 frames versus 54.6% (BOLT) and 58.7% (Q-Frame), suggesting the adaptive spatio-temporal allocation is particularly effective when long-range temporal reasoning is needed.

## Relation to Prior Work

LENS extends the training-free keyframe sampling line (BOLT, AKS, Q-Frame) by departing from the single-granularity paradigm where frames are treated as independent, fixed-resolution sampling units. Its spatial zoom-in module directly adapts the CLIP attention decomposition technique from Gandelsman et al. (ICLR 2024) to derive patch-level query-relevance scores, while its temporal zoom-out introduces graph-based message passing over a video graph to propagate relevance across long-range temporal neighborhoods. The key conceptual departure is the adaptive budget allocation between these two complementary branches, which prior methods do not attempt—they select frames but do not decide how to distribute visual evidence across spatial detail versus temporal coverage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LENS offers a practical, training-free plug-in that consistently improves long-form video QA across multiple MLLM backbones (Qwen2.5-VL, LLaVA-OneVision, GPT-5-Mini) and frame budgets, making it immediately deployable without fine-tuning. The finding that a lightweight 7B allocator suffices (stronger proprietary allocators yield only +0.4–0.7% gains) suggests the multi-granular formulation itself, rather than controller capacity, drives the improvement. Open questions include whether the CLIP/BLIP-based relevance scoring generalizes to highly abstract or compositional queries, whether the graph-based temporal reasoning captures dependencies beyond the local neighborhood modeled by the sparse connectivity, and how the approach scales to hour-scale videos where the graph construction and message passing may become less tractable.
