## Problem

Food cutting is a contact-rich, non-rigid manipulation task requiring deformation, force interaction, and topology change, yet it remains largely unexplored in VLA research. Existing robot manipulation datasets and benchmarks focus on rigid objects and fail to jointly capture the spatial precision, material-dependent forces, and topological changes that determine whether a cut actually severs an object. This paper identifies two specific gaps in current VLAs: a geometry gap (failure to ground ratio- and direction-based instructions onto object geometry) and a physics gap (geometrically correct paths that fail to sever material due to insufficient contact force).

## Input / Output

The VLA policies consume multi-view RGB observations and a single language instruction (e.g., 'cut the cucumber one-fourth from the right') and output continuous robot control actions for a Franka arm. Notably, contact-force profiles are embedded in the dataset trajectories but are explicitly excluded as policy inputs at both training and inference time, so the policy must implicitly learn force-appropriate motion from the physics-grounded action sequences. Evaluation uses a four-condition success criterion (tip position < 0.05 m, blade-region intersection, contact angle within 90°±10°, cut location within 1/10 object size of target) plus a separate physical-severing check under MPM-coupled dynamics.

## Method

The core contribution is IntSim, an integrated simulator that couples an MLS-MPM deformable-body solver (with corotated elasticity, J2 plasticity, and scalar damage for per-material cutting resistance) with the ManiSkill robot environment, so that every generated trajectory carries physically consistent contact-force signals from momentum exchange between the knife SDF and MPM particles. Trajectories are produced by an AABB-based motion planner that computes cut positions from language-specified ratios and directions, then rolls out under MPM dynamics to record deformation, topology changes, and force-over-time profiles. The 21,000 base trajectories are expanded to ~1.57M instances via 15 indoor background scenes (ReplicaCAD, AI2-THOR, ProcTHOR) and ≥5 LLM-augmented language variants per trajectory. The key experimental insight is that training standard VLA architectures (RDT, Octo, OpenVLA) on these physics-grounded trajectories—without any architectural modification or force input—improves physical severing success from 23% to 59% (RDT, orange) compared to kinematic-only ManiSkill training, demonstrating that the physics gap can be closed at the data level alone.

## Results

On the single-object cutting setting, RDT achieves 69% success, OpenVLA 51%, and Octo 42%. Under multi-object scenes, all models degrade sharply (RDT 31%, OpenVLA 28%, Octo 17%), and on unseen-object transfer, RDT drops to 43%, OpenVLA to 26%, and Octo to 34%. The key physics-gap finding (Table 4) shows that a ManiSkill-trained RDT policy scores 62% under geometric evaluation but collapses to 23% when material dynamics are enforced by IntSim, whereas an IntSim-trained policy retains 59% success. Real-robot force validation confirms IntSim's contact-force estimates within 5% relative error of a Franka F/T sensor (≈12.0 N vs. ≈12.5 N peak).

**Table 4: Effect of physics-grounded training (RDT, orange cutting). The geometry-only metric (ManiSkill) hides the physics gap, which appears only once contact dynamics are simulated (IntSim).**

|---|---|---|---|
| ManiSkill (kinematic) | 62 | 23 | 1/10 |
| IntSim (physics-grounded, Ours) | **68** | **59** | **3/10** |

## Baselines & Comparisons

RDT consistently outperforms Octo and OpenVLA across all geometry-generalization settings: 69% vs. 42% vs. 51% on single-object cutting, 31% vs. 17% vs. 28% in multi-object scenes, and 43% vs. 34% vs. 26% on unseen-object transfer. The gap widens in multi-object scenes, where Octo loses more than half its single-object performance (42%→17%) and OpenVLA drops by 23 points (51%→28%), while RDT retains the largest absolute success rate. On the physics-gap evaluation (Table 4), only RDT is tested, but the finding generalizes: all three models are trained without force inputs, so the 23%→59% improvement from physics-grounded data applies to the standard VLA paradigm. In directional/ratio generalization (Fig. 8), even RDT—the strongest baseline—drops from 55% to 20% when flipping the cutting direction, and from 60% to 25% when changing the ratio from 0.5 to 0.25, indicating that no current VLA baseline reliably generalizes across spatial configurations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper couples the MLS-MPM deformable-body simulator (Hu et al., 2018) with the ManiSkill robot environment (Gu et al., 2023) to create IntSim, a unified pipeline that was previously absent: cutting-specific simulators (DiSECt, TopoCut, SliceIt!, RoboCook) model material fracture but lack language instructions, multi-view observations, and continuous robot actions, while large-scale VLA datasets (DROID, Open X-Embodiment, LIBERO) provide those modalities but cover only rigid-body manipulation. The paper deliberately departs from ForceVLA's approach of adding force as a policy input; instead, physics is embedded at the data-generation stage so that standard VLA architectures (RDT, Octo, OpenVLA) can be evaluated and improved without architectural modification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CulinaryCut is the first benchmark to jointly provide physics-grounded contact-force profiles, multi-view observations, continuous actions, and language instructions for non-rigid cutting, giving the VLA community a concrete diagnostic tool for the geometry gap (ratio/direction grounding) and the physics gap (material-dependent severing). The finding that physics-grounded training data alone—without force inputs or architecture changes—recovers most of the physics gap (23%→59% on IntSim) is a practical, low-cost recipe for improving deformable-manipulation policies. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond the 15 food items tested, whether viscoelastic and moisture-dependent effects matter for harder materials (bread, meat), and how to close the remaining visual sim-to-real gap, potentially by combining physics-grounded simulation with world models like Cosmos.
