## Problem

Recovering high-speed video from single-photon data under extreme photon sparsity is limited by existing methods' reliance on local spatiotemporal grouping, which discards global correlations and fails when photons are too sparse in space and time for reliable local estimation. No prior approach can simultaneously recover fast scene motion and high-frequency illumination modulation (e.g., 120 Hz flicker, 31 kHz LED) in this regime, because motion-compensation methods assume flux constancy along trajectories while per-pixel temporal methods ignore spatial structure.

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw asynchronous photon detection streams (binary 512×512 frames at 100 kHz from a SPAD512, or picosecond-resolution timestamp streams from a single-pixel SPAD), event streams from a DAVIS240C paired with a co-registered intensity frame for Kalman-filtered fusion, and binary spike streams. It outputs a reconstructed spatiotemporal flux volume resampleable at arbitrary temporal resolution (demonstrated at 100 kfps), a set of detected linear object velocities, and velocity-selective video where a chosen velocity appears static while all other motion is blurred. No training or learned parameters are involved; the entire pipeline is closed-form statistical estimation.

## Method

The core contribution is a spatiotemporal flux probing theory that establishes a direct, provable relation between the sum of a probing function evaluated at photon detection locations and the corresponding Fourier coefficient of the underlying intensity, with martingale noise characterized as approximately complex normal. Using 3D Fourier basis functions as probing functions over the full spatiotemporal volume, every photon contributes globally to every frequency estimate, enabling coherent aggregation across space and time rather than within local neighborhoods. A constant-false-alarm-rate (CFAR) detector based on the noncentral chi-squared distribution of normalized probing energy identifies which spatiotemporal frequencies are present, and inverse Fourier synthesis over detected frequencies reconstructs the flux. Velocity detection is formulated as a plane-consistency problem in 3D frequency space using a nonparametric rank-CFAR test, and velocity-selective videography is achieved by shearing the 3D spectrum so that the target velocity plane maps to temporal DC, making that motion appear static in the reconstruction.

## Results

The paper demonstrates 100 kfps video reconstruction from SPAD512 binary frames under extreme photon sparsity (as low as 0.011 photons per pixel), simultaneously recovering 120 Hz AC illumination flicker and 31 kHz modulated LED dynamics. In the ultra-wideband regime (GHz-scale laser pulses), the method achieves high-quality reconstruction with 30× fewer photons than the prior UWB approach, and recovers a rotating fan with 10× fewer photons where UWB completely breaks down. For asynchronous sensors, the method produces 1 kfps video from DAVIS240C event streams (comparable to EvINR) and 10 kfps video from spike streams (comparable to STIR), all without learned components.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against UWB [52], the proposed method wins decisively in the ultra-wideband setting, recovering fan blades and laser wavefronts with 30× fewer photons, and in the 100 kfps fan experiment it recovers blade structure with 10× fewer photons where UWB fails entirely due to insufficient per-pixel SNR. Against QBP [30], the method produces cleaner reconstructions on the guitar dataset at 0.011 ppp (where QBP yields noisy output) and on the toys dataset under severe timestamp thinning, while also enabling automatic velocity detection that QBP cannot provide. Against bit2bit [28], the method resolves finer spatial texture on the Mandrill dataset and faithfully recovers faint CPU fan flicker that bit2bit oversmooths into spatial structure; on a static balloon with 31 kHz modulation, bit2bit suppresses the flicker as noise while the proposed method recovers it. Against EvINR [51] on event-camera data, the method achieves comparable visual quality without any learned parameters. Against STIR [8] on spike-camera data, the method produces reconstructions of similar quality across fast, periodic, and nonperiodic motion scenes, again without training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of single-photon videography: per-pixel ultra-wideband temporal recovery (Wei et al., ICCV 2023), which estimates Fourier coefficients of time-varying flux independently at each pixel, and spatiotemporal pooling methods like Quanta Burst Photography (Ma et al., TOG 2020), which aggregate photons along local motion trajectories. The key departure is replacing local spatiotemporal grouping with global 3D Fourier probing over the entire observation volume, so that every photon contributes jointly to estimating the full spatiotemporal spectrum. The framework is further extended to event and spike cameras, for which no prior unified reconstruction theory existed, by showing that the same probing measurements apply with modality-specific image-formation corrections.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 30× photon-budget reduction over the prior state of the art (UWB) and the demonstration of simultaneous fast-motion and high-frequency-flicker recovery in a regime where all prior methods fail represent a meaningful shift in what is achievable with single-photon sensors. The velocity-selective videography capability—automatically detecting object velocities from the photon stream and reconstructing refocused video—is, to the authors' knowledge, the first such demonstration. Open questions include whether alternative bases (wavelets, chirplets) can mitigate Gibbs ringing and handle nonlinear motion, and whether the spectral-shear structure can support depth estimation or structure-from-motion from sparse photon streams, which would connect this framework to broader 3D scene understanding.
