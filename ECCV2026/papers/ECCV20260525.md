## Problem

Long-form narrative-to-film generation requires shot-level controllability and cross-clip consistency in both visual identity and character behavior, but current prompt-based workflows remain brittle because they lack a structured intermediate layer between scripts and video models. Existing divide-and-conquer approaches that decompose screenplays into per-scene prompts struggle to preserve appearance consistency (stable identity, props, visual attributes) and persona consistency (behavior aligned with character traits) over long narratives, and failures are difficult to diagnose and repair without retraining the base model.

## Input / Output

The system takes a narrative text (movie/TV plot, episode summary, or script-like text) and produces a long-form video sequence composed of variable-duration clips, alongside a persistent FilmDSL JSON specification that encodes all cinematic decisions. Clips are generated via off-the-shelf video generators in two branches: dialogue clips use a joint audio-video model conditioned on a TTS-synthesized voice-over track, while non-dialogue clips use a video-only model. The FilmDSL output stores stable references (IDs, URIs, hashes) to assets and memory entries rather than raw images, enabling reproducible tool calling and continuity enforcement across the entire production.

## Method

CineCrew introduces FilmDSL, a film-oriented domain-specific language that decomposes a screenplay into a three-layer hierarchical clip specification (Narrative Action → Cinematic Staging → Render Specification), making shot boundaries, camera directives, asset references, continuity links, and persona cues explicit and machine-actionable. A multi-agent crew (Showrunner, Art Department, Story Editor, Cinematographer, Technical Director, Production Operator, Dailies Reviewer, VO Director, Post Supervisor) coordinates through this shared DSL, with a centralized Production Rulebook fusing static cinematic priors and dynamic runtime feedback to synchronize constraints across agents. Generation follows a keyframe-first strategy: storyboard keyframes anchor identity, layout, and cinematic look before clip-by-clip video synthesis, and a Dailies Reviewer produces structured QA reports (identity drift, prop teleportation, keyframe mismatch, temporal instability) that trigger targeted, localized refinement of DSL fields without retraining the underlying video model. A DSL-embedded Persona Schema with trait-to-behavior mappings and beat-level performance blocks ensures character actions align with narrative roles rather than merely visual identity.

## Results

On the CineCrewEval benchmark (20 narratives from MovieBench), CineCrew achieves the best film-level VLM scores on Camera Blocking (4.85), Beat Readability (4.90), Persona Alignment (4.75), and Narrative Coherence (4.70), and the best cross-clip consistency scores on Identity Consistency (75.12), Prop Permanence (78.59), Layout Continuity (4.50), and Cross-clip Coherence (4.65). On VBench general quality, it leads in Subject Consistency (86.27), Background Consistency (91.39), Aesthetic Quality (73.15), and Imaging Quality (79.23). The only metrics where CineCrew trails are Physics Compliance (4.20 vs. LTX-Studio's 4.60) and Dynamic Degree (64.42 vs. 65.42), both of which are sensitive to the underlying video generator's motion prior.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MovieAgent (open-source multi-agent CoT planning), CineCrew wins on every film-level and consistency metric, with the largest gaps in Identity Consistency (75.12 vs. 60.09), Prop Permanence (78.59 vs. 58.78), and Beat Readability (4.90 vs. 2.50), indicating that explicit FilmDSL structure and persistent asset memory substantially reduce identity drift and beat ambiguity. Against AniMaker (MCTS-driven multi-shot animation), CineCrew again leads on all consistency scores (I.C. 75.12 vs. 61.38; P.P. 78.59 vs. 65.17) and on Camera Blocking (4.85 vs. 3.50), while AniMaker edges ahead only on Layout Continuity (3.90 vs. 4.50 is actually CineCrew winning; AniMaker's 3.90 is lower). Against the strongest commercial baseline LTX-Studio (LTX-2 Pro), CineCrew surpasses it on all four narrative-centric film-level metrics (C.B. 4.85 vs. 4.50, B.R. 4.90 vs. 4.25, P.A. 4.75 vs. 4.10, N.C. 4.70 vs. 3.80) and on every consistency metric (I.C. 75.12 vs. 67.15, P.P. 78.59 vs. 73.24, L.C. 4.50 vs. 4.10, X.C. 4.65 vs. 4.40), but concedes Physics Compliance (4.20 vs. 4.60), Dynamic Degree (64.42 vs. 65.42), and Motion Smoothness (99.62 vs. 99.70) to LTX-Studio, which the authors attribute to the underlying generator's motion prior rather than the orchestration layer.

## Relation to Prior Work

CineCrew builds on the multi-agent video generation paradigm established by MovieAgent, AniMaker, and LTX-Studio, which decompose screenplays into per-clip prompts and call off-the-shelf video generators. Its key departure is the introduction of FilmDSL, a domain-specific language borrowed conceptually from software engineering, as a persistent, machine-actionable intermediate representation that explicitly encodes shot structure, camera directives, asset references, and continuity links—rather than relying on ad-hoc prompt decomposition. The Production Rulebook and Persona Schema further extend prior agent pipelines by making cross-clip state and character behavior first-class, queryable objects rather than implicit prompt context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By making consistency a structurally enforced objective rather than an emergent prompting artifact, CineCrew offers a reusable orchestration pattern that could be adopted by other narrative-to-video systems, and the CineCrewEval protocol provides a more targeted evaluation than existing suites for cross-clip failures. Open questions include whether the multi-agent refinement loop scales to truly feature-length narratives (hundreds of clips) without compounding latency, whether the DSL abstraction generalizes beyond film to other long-form media (e.g., interactive video, games), and how much of the measured gain is attributable to the underlying video backbone versus the orchestration layer itself. The evaluation on 20 TV-style segments and reliance on a single VLM judge (Gemini-3-Flash) leave room for broader validation.
