## Problem

Existing multimodal RLVR methods either optimize solely for final answer correctness or add auxiliary perception rewards, but neither explicitly constrains how perceived visual evidence is translated into and utilized during the reasoning process. This leaves the perception-to-reasoning transition unconstrained, allowing models to produce correct answers via spurious correlations rather than faithful grounding on visual facts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262436/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives an RGB image paired with a text question and generates a structured output consisting of a perception description, a reasoning chain (delimited by <think>... tags), and a final answer enclosed in \boxed{} for automated verification. During training, the rollout group is augmented with expert trajectories sampled from a stronger model (Qwen2.5-VL-32B), so the policy is optimized over a joint set of on-policy and off-policy reasoning paths rather than purely self-generated rollouts.

## Method

TGRL extends GRPO and DAPO by injecting expert trajectories from a stronger model into the rollout group and applying three key modifications: (1) an off-policy importance ratio correction (π_θ/π_φ) to handle distribution mismatch between expert and current policy; (2) adaptive token-level reweighting that computes the log-probability discrepancy between expert and current policy at each token, normalizes it within the trajectory, and maps it through a sigmoid to emphasize tokens where the policy deviates most from the expert; and (3) positive trajectory filtering that discards expert trajectories with incorrect final answers to avoid contradictory gradient signals. Advantages are normalized across the joint on-policy and off-policy group, so expert trajectories naturally receive higher relative advantages early in training and the balance gradually shifts toward on-policy exploration as the student improves.

## Results

On five multimodal reasoning and perception benchmarks (WeMath, MathVista, MathVerse, MathVision, HallusionBench), TGRL-DAPO trained on 39K samples achieves the best average score of 60.68, outperforming the strongest baseline NoisyRollout (60.12) by 0.56 points. Even with only 2.1K training samples, TGRL-DAPO reaches 59.51 average, surpassing ThinkLite (58.14) and Perception-R1. The base Qwen-2.5-VL-7B model scores 53.28, and vanilla GRPO/DAPO training improves this to 57.16/56.92, while TGRL adds a further 1.3–2.6 point gain over the respective RLVR baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TGRL-DAPO (39K) achieves the highest average score of 60.68, edging out NoisyRollout (60.12, 6.4K data) by 0.56 points while using 6× more training data. Against ThinkLite (58.14, 11K data), TGRL-DAPO leads by 2.54 points on average, with particularly large margins on MathVerse (+3.31) and MathVision (+3.66). Perception-R1 (1.4K data) reports strong scores on WeMath (72.00) and MathVista (74.20) but does not report HallusionBench results, making a direct average comparison impossible; TGRL-DAPO still matches or exceeds Perception-R1 on MathVerse (53.51 vs. 54.30) and MathVision (31.26 vs. 28.60). VLAA-Thinker (56.42) and OpenVLThinker (56.62) trail TGRL-DAPO by over 4 points on average, while R1-Onevision (52.04, 165K data) is the weakest baseline despite using the largest training set. Notably, TGRL uses Qwen-2.5-VL-32B as its expert policy, whereas Perception-R1 relies on proprietary models like Gemini 2.5 Pro for supervision, suggesting TGRL achieves competitive performance with a comparatively weaker expert.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GRPO and DAPO policy optimization frameworks by injecting expert-generated trajectories into the rollout group, adapting the off-policy trajectory guidance idea from text-only LLM reasoning (e.g., ExGRPO, RLEP) to the multimodal setting. It departs from prior multimodal RLVR work (Perception-R1, Noisy-Rollout) that supervises either the final answer or the perception stage in isolation, by explicitly constraining the perception-to-reasoning transition through trajectory-level alignment. The key technical departure is the two-stage token reweighting (importance ratio correction plus discrepancy-based adaptive weighting) that handles the distribution mismatch between a stronger expert policy and the student policy, which prior multimodal RLVR methods do not address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete structural gap in multimodal RLVR—the perception-reasoning transition is left unconstrained by both outcome-level and perception-level rewards—which provides a useful diagnostic lens for practitioners debugging weakly grounded reasoning chains. The finding that naive expert trajectory injection can degrade performance (Table 1, w/o Reweight below baseline) cautions against simply adding more supervision without proper off-policy correction. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond math reasoning to open-ended multimodal tasks, whether self-generated trajectories can replace the need for a 32B expert model, and whether the token-reweighting mechanism transfers to other policy optimization algorithms beyond GRPO/DAPO.
