## Problem

Localizing a stylized font into a new language requires simultaneously transferring glyph shapes, color/texture, and spacing/kerning across scripts with vastly different structural complexity (e.g., Latin to CJK). Existing font-generation models produce only single glyphs without proper multi-character layout, while end-to-end image-editing models lack the controllability and multilingual coverage needed for production-quality font rendering, leaving designers to hand-synthesize missing characters.

## Input / Output

LoGAN takes a small set of individual glyphs cropped from a reference font or logo (few-shot, no full font file required), a target translation string, and an optional style-finetuning trigger. It produces a complete rendered font image at 512×512 per glyph, vectorized into editable SVG, with an optional PBR texture map overlaid. The pipeline supports 27 languages spanning Latin, Greek, Cyrillic, and CJK scripts, and the VLM agent handles line breaks, alignment, and color codes as part of the output specification.

## Method

LoGAN is a VLM-agent-coordinated pipeline (GPT-5.2 or Qwen3-VL-32B) that decomposes font localization into three stages—design extraction, design transfer, and design generation—rather than performing one-shot end-to-end rendering. The core glyph generator is a 2B-parameter MMDiT (SD3.5-medium backbone) trained from scratch on 15K fonts/40M glyphs, modified with a third attention modality that encodes reference glyphs via SigLIP2 + Perceiver Sampler for few-shot style conditioning, and trained with a cross-lingual sampling algorithm that explicitly balances same-language and cross-language pairs to mitigate the long-tail language distribution. A LoRA (rank 64, ~160M params) fine-tuned on the reference glyphs in ~30 minutes sharpens style fidelity, while a separate LoRA on FLUX.1-Fill-dev recovers full texture maps from textured reference text. Spacing and kerning are transferred via a typographic heuristic that maps each character's left/right contour to one of four abstract shapes (straight, circular, ascending diagonal, descending diagonal) and applies a learned ordering to infer gaps for unseen character pairs, with a training-free AutoCrop module (connected components + VLM OCR grouping) handling character-level segmentation for both input construction and spacing measurement.

## Results

On the CJK evaluation set (42 pairs), LoGAN with GPT-5.2 achieves PSNR 9.49, SSIM 0.692, LPIPS 0.446, DreamSim 0.076, and CLIP 0.948, outperforming the strongest baseline Nano-Banana (PSNR 7.66, CLIP 0.920) by a wide margin. On the Non-CJK set (254 pairs), LoGAN reaches PSNR 10.08, SSIM 0.684, LPIPS 0.373, DreamSim 0.072, and CLIP 0.944, again leading all baselines. In expert evaluation, LoGAN scores 2.8/3 for style transfer and 3.0/3 for translation accuracy versus Nano-Banana's 1.0/3 for style and only 19/80 correct translations; 52.9% of LoGAN outputs are classified as Production-Ready compared to 0% for Nano-Banana.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LoGAN (GPT-5.2) outperforms all six baselines on every metric for both CJK and Non-CJK splits. The gap is most pronounced on CJK, where text-specific models like AnyText2 (CLIP 0.811) and TextDiffuser-2 (CLIP 0.666) degrade severely due to limited multilingual training data, while LoGAN maintains CLIP 0.948. Among general image-editing baselines, Nano-Banana is the strongest competitor (CLIP 0.920 CJK / 0.922 Non-CJK), yet LoGAN still leads by 0.028 and 0.022 CLIP respectively, with substantially better PSNR (9.49 vs 7.66 CJK; 10.08 vs 8.99 Non-CJK). Notably, text-specialized models (Flux-Text, AnyText2, TextDiffuser-2) consistently underperform general-purpose image editing models (Nano-Banana, Flux-Kontext Pro), suggesting that end-to-end text rendering in a single diffusion pass is less effective than LoGAN's decomposed glyph-level approach. Even without the optional LoRA style fine-tuning, LoGAN surpasses all baselines, confirming that the core glyph generation and arrangement pipeline is the primary driver of quality. In the expert evaluation, Nano-Banana scored 1.0/3 on both style and layout (vs. LoGAN's 2.8 and 2.2) and produced correct translations in only 19 of 80 cases, while LoGAN achieved 3.0/3 translation accuracy and 52.9% Production-Ready classification.

## Relation to Prior Work

LoGAN builds on the MMDiT architecture from SD3.5-medium, extending its joint attention with a third modality for reference glyph conditioning, and on FLUX.1-Fill-dev as the base for its texture-expansion LoRA. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant paradigm in font generation (FontDiffuser, VecGlypher) that treats the problem as single-glyph synthesis, and from end-to-end image editing models (FLUX-Kontext, Nano-Banana) that couple all design attributes in one pass. The key architectural departure is the VLM-as-coordinator agentic decomposition, where a VLM (GPT-5.2 or Qwen3-VL) orchestrates specialized sub-models for glyph generation, kerning transfer, vectorization, and texture expansion rather than relying on monolithic generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first end-to-end agent-based pipeline for multilingual font localization spanning 27 languages including CJK, addressing a real production bottleneck (44% of movie logos are font-based). The 52.9% production-ready rate and 3% pipeline failure rate on 1,800 runs demonstrate practical viability, though the 15-minute LoRA fine-tuning cost and single-font limitation constrain broader adoption. Open questions include extending to multi-font layouts, connected characters (calligraphy), and reducing fine-tuning overhead; the paper also leaves unresolved whether the VLM coordinator's role could be replaced by learned routing, and whether the heuristic kerning transfer generalizes to scripts with fundamentally different spatial dynamics.
