## Problem

Vision-language-action (VLA) models for robot manipulation rely predominantly on visual perception, which cannot capture contact forces, surface friction, compliance, and shear during contact-rich tasks. Existing approaches to integrate tactile signals—token concatenation or cross-attention—either increase sequence length and computational cost or require training additional attention parameters, making them heavy for the post-training fine-tuning paradigm that large behaviour models demand.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262809/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy consumes an RGB image from an Intel RealSense camera, a tactile image from a DIGIT sensor (resized to 224×224; for Sparsh, two frames separated by 5 time steps are channel-concatenated), a fixed natural-language prompt, and proprioceptive state (joint positions/velocities, end-effector pose, gripper width) sampled at 10 Hz. The model outputs continuous robot action chunks regressed via an L1 objective from an MLP action head, with no additional tokens appended to the language-model input sequence.

## Method

TacFiLM conditions intermediate visual features on pretrained tactile representations using Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) applied inside the ViT blocks of the OpenVLA-OFT visual backbone (fused SigLIP + DINOv2). At each timestep, a pretrained tactile encoder (Sparsh-DINO) produces patch-level features that are averaged into a single pooled embedding z_t; a small MLP then projects z_t to per-channel scale (γ) and shift (β) parameters for each ViT block, and the modulation F_n ⊙ (1+γ_n) + β_n is applied after layer normalization and before multi-head self-attention. γ and β are zero-initialized so conditioning starts near identity, preserving pretrained visual-language priors. Training uses LoRA on the linear layers of both the VLA backbone and the tactile encoder while the FiLM layers are trained from scratch, keeping the majority of parameters frozen. By default FiLM is applied to all ViT blocks, though ablations show that conditioning only a third of blocks (early, middle, or late) yields comparable performance at lower overhead.

## Results

Across 10 contact-rich manipulation tasks (4 in-distribution, 4 out-of-distribution), TacFiLM achieves an average success rate of 86.67% on both ID and OOD tasks, compared to 58.10% (OpenVLA-OFT), 64.76% (TactileConcat), and 48.00% (Cross-Attn) on ID, and 54.67%, 73.33%, and 49.33% respectively on OOD. TacFiLM also achieves the lowest average maximum contact force (8.65 N ID, 8.40 N OOD) and shortest completion time (81.72 s ID, 87.84 s OOD). On the 3 mm clearance circle-peg insertion (ID) and both 3 mm square/pentagon-peg insertions (OOD), TacFiLM reaches 100% success with 33–47% direct insertion rates, while the vision-only baseline achieves 0% direct insertions on OOD tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TacFiLM outperforms all three baselines on average success rate across both ID and OOD settings, achieving 86.67% versus 58.10% (OpenVLA-OFT), 64.76% (TactileConcat), and 48.00% (Cross-Attn) on ID, and 86.67% versus 54.67%, 73.33%, and 49.33% on OOD. Against the vision-only OpenVLA-OFT baseline, TacFiLM improves success by 28.6 percentage points (ID) and 32.0 points (OOD), while also reducing average maximum force from 14.94 N to 8.65 N (ID) and from 22.46 N to 8.40 N (OOD). Compared to TactileConcat, TacFiLM shows a clear advantage on harder tasks: on the 2 mm circle-peg (ID) it achieves 86.67% vs 73.33% success with 23.33% vs 0% direct insertions, and on HDMI cable plugging (OOD) it reaches 66.67% vs 13.33% success. TactileConcat, however, matches TacFiLM on the 3 mm pentagon-peg OOD task (both 100% success) and exerts lower force on the 2 mm square-peg OOD task (27.72 N vs 7.06 N is actually TacFiLM lower, but TactileConcat is competitive on 3 mm tasks). Cross-Attn consistently underperforms on all tasks, with the largest gap on HDMI plugging (33.33% vs 66.67%) and the longest completion times (149.77 s OOD average vs 87.84 s for TacFiLM), which the authors attribute to the additional trainable cross-attention parameters requiring more data to learn effective multimodal correspondences.

## Relation to Prior Work

TacFiLM builds on the OpenVLA-OFT VLA architecture (fused SigLIP/DINOv2 visual encoder + Llama2 7B decoder) and repurposes the FiLM conditioning mechanism from Perez et al. (2018) for cross-modal tactile integration. It departs from the two dominant tactile-fusion strategies in the VLA literature—token concatenation (TactileConcat, VTLA, ForceVLA) and cross-attention (PolyTouch, ViTacFormer)—by instead applying a per-channel affine modulation to intermediate ViT features, avoiding both sequence-length growth and additional attention parameters. The use of pretrained tactile encoders (Sparsh, T3) as frozen feature extractors, combined with LoRA fine-tuning of the VLA, positions the work firmly in the parameter-efficient post-training paradigm rather than large-scale multimodal pretraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TacFiLM offers a practical, low-overhead recipe for injecting tactile feedback into existing VLA models without retraining large components or increasing token sequences, which is significant given the compute constraints of current behaviour models. The consistent outperformance over cross-attention and concatenation across in-distribution and out-of-distribution tasks, along with the ~3× force reduction, suggests that lightweight feature-level conditioning may be sufficient where heavier fusion mechanisms are assumed necessary. Key limitations include evaluation on a single VLA backbone (OpenVLA-OFT), a limited task suite (insertions and one drawer task), and small per-method rollout counts (30 ID, 15 OOD), leaving open whether the FiLM advantage persists at scale, on other architectures (e.g., π0.5), or across more diverse manipulation scenarios.
