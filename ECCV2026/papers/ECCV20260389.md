## Problem

Existing video emotion datasets capture perceived emotions (what characters express or directors intend) in controlled lab settings, but fail to model the induced emotions that diverse, real-world audiences actually experience. No prior benchmark predicts the full distribution of audience reactions from video content alone, leaving a gap between content creation intent and actual viewer response that is critical for recommendation, moderation, and content quality applications.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260389/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each input is a short movie clip (~135 s average, 23–367 s range) with visual frames, audio, and a text description; preprocessed visual and audio embeddings from pretrained models are also provided. The output is a normalized probability distribution over 21 fine-grained reaction categories (derived from the GoEmotions taxonomy), not a single label—reflecting that different viewers react differently to the same clip. The distribution is constructed from ~24 retained viewer comments per clip (out of ~75 raw), aggregated at the clip level rather than per-frame.

## Method

The paper introduces a two-stage multi-agent LLM annotation pipeline: Stage 1 rephrases raw YouTube comments to explicitly state the viewer's reaction to the clip (filtering off-topic remarks), and Stage 2 extracts reaction labels via majority voting across three medium-sized multilingual LLMs, achieving 86% correctness under dual-blind human verification. The prediction task is framed as Label Distribution Learning, and the benchmark evaluates four algorithm families—Problem Transformation, Specialized LDL Algorithms, Algorithm Adaptation, and Foundation VLMs—along two complementary axes: full-distribution distance (Chebyshev, KL, Cosine, Intersection, CAD) and dominant-reaction classification (MRR, Top-k weighted F1). The key empirical finding is that pretrained VLMs (Gemini 2.5, LLaVA-Next, Qwen2.5-VL) fail zero-shot (Top-1 F1 < 0.30), but lightweight LoRA fine-tuning (rank 8, 3 epochs, with taxonomy reordering and synonym replacement) transforms them into state-of-the-art predictors reaching 77% Top-3 F1, substantially outperforming all classical LDL baselines on dominant reaction metrics while remaining competitive on distributional metrics.

## Results

On the Video2Reaction benchmark, LoRA-finetuned LLaVA-Next-Video-7B achieves the best dominant reaction performance with 0.7672 Top-3 weighted F1, 0.7833 MRR, and 0.6521 Top-1 F1, while finetuned Qwen2.5-VL reaches 0.7725 Top-3 F1. Zero-shot foundation VLMs fail substantially: Gemini 2.5 Flash scores only 0.3794 Top-3 F1 and 0.4378 MRR, and temperature-scaled LLaVA-Next drops to 0.1374 Top-3 F1. Among classical LDL baselines, SA-BFGS is the strongest, achieving 0.6265 Top-3 F1 and 0.7163 MRR, but still trails finetuned VLMs by ~14 points on Top-3 F1. On full distribution metrics, finetuned LLaVA-Next reduces Chebyshev distance to 0.1882 and raises cosine similarity to 0.8663, roughly doubling the zero-shot cosine score of 0.4185.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Finetuned VLMs (LLaVA-Next and Qwen2.5-VL with LoRA) decisively outperform all 10 baselines on dominant reaction metrics, achieving 0.77 Top-3 F1 versus 0.63 for the best classical LDL method (SA-BFGS) and 0.57 for LDSVR. The gap is most dramatic against zero-shot VLMs: finetuning LLaVA-Next improves Top-3 F1 from 0.1374 (temperature-scaled) to 0.7672, a 5.6× gain, and MRR from 0.1992 to 0.7833. Among classical LDL methods, SA-BFGS leads on both distribution metrics (Chebyshev 0.2306, KL 0.5976) and dominant reaction (MRR 0.7163), while LDSVR is competitive on Chebyshev (0.2584) but weaker on ranking. Algorithm Adaptation models (CubeMLP, CTEN, MMIM) cluster in the middle, with CTEN achieving the best MRR (0.6939) among them but trailing SA-BFGS on Top-1 F1 (0.4826 vs. 0.5283). A modality ablation shows that adding text (clip description) to visual features yields the largest single gain for both SA-BFGS (Top-1 F1: 0.306→0.515) and LLaVA-Next (0.586→0.652), while audio contributes only marginally on top of visual+text.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Label Distribution Learning (LDL) framework of Geng [10] from text-based sentiment and age estimation to multimodal video, and departs from prior emotion video datasets (IEMOCAP, MELD, LIRIS-ACCEDE, VCE) that either capture perceived emotion, operate in controlled lab settings, or model reactions as single labels. The closest prior work, VCE [21], also targets induced emotion distributions but relies on controlled crowdworker pools and manual annotation, whereas Video2Reaction sources reactions from natural social media (YouTube) and automates labeling via a two-stage multi-agent LLM pipeline. On the method side, the paper organizes baselines along the standard LDL taxonomy (Problem Transformation, Specialized Algorithms, Algorithm Adaptation) and introduces foundation VLMs as a fourth category, bridging the LDL literature with the vision-language model paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Video2Reaction provides the first large-scale, in-the-wild benchmark for predicting induced audience reaction distributions from video alone, filling a gap between controlled lab studies and the ecological diversity of real media consumption. The scalable LLM annotation pipeline addresses the non-stationarity of audience reactions (demonstrated by longitudinal shifts in dominant emotions), enabling iterative dataset updates that static human-annotated corpora cannot support. Key open questions include whether the YouTube-only, movie-clip scope generalizes to other platforms and content types, how to handle the severe long-tail imbalance (factor 28.36) in rare reactions, and whether the observed cross-dataset transfer (Video2Reaction + 1% VCE outperforming 10% VCE) scales to systematic cross-taxonomy generalization.
