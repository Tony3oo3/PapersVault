## Problem

Despite strong performance on static visual tasks, contemporary VLMs fail to recognize human emotions from video, underperforming even specialized vision-only classifiers. The paper identifies two root causes: long-tailed web-scale pretraining induces head-class bias that collapses rare emotions into common categories, and VLMs lack genuine temporal modeling, treating frames as an order-agnostic 'bag-of-frames' where performance degrades as visual token density increases due to attentional dilution.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262185/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The MSCE pipeline first samples sparse keyframes (e.g., 1 FPS) from the input video, then extracts a small set of intermediate frames (m=4) from each temporal gap between consecutive keyframes. Stage 1 converts these intermediate frames into compact natural-language motion descriptions; Stage 2 interleaves the keyframes and text summaries chronologically into a single structured prompt. The final output is a single emotion class label selected from a provided list, with the intermediate textual summaries acting as auxiliary temporal context rather than a standalone prediction.

## Method

The core contribution is Multi-Stage Context Enrichment (MSCE), a two-stage inference-time pipeline that resolves the conflict between the temporal density needed to capture micro-expressions (0.25–0.5 s) and fixed token budgets. In Stage 1 (Motion-to-Text Translation), a VLM processes the intermediate frames between sparse keyframes and generates a compact textual summary of facial motion, converting high-volume redundant visual tokens into low-volume, information-dense text tokens. In Stage 2 (Interleaved Context Classification), these text summaries are interleaved with the original keyframes in strict chronological order (k₁, t₁, k₂, t₂, …, kₙ) and fed to the VLM for final classification, preserving the emotional trajectory across temporal gaps that sparse sampling would otherwise discard while avoiding the attentional dilution caused by dense visual input. For the long-tail bias, the paper additionally demonstrates a decoupled training strategy—pretraining on the naturally imbalanced dataset followed by LoRA-based fine-tuning on a class-balanced subset—that corrects head-class bias without discarding web-scale representations.

## Results

On MAFW (11-class), the best VLM (Gemini 2.5-Flash) achieves a macro F1 of 0.376, trailing the best vision-only classifier HiCMAE (F1 0.399) and MAE-DFER (F1 0.360). On DFEW (7-class), Gemini 2.5-Flash leads with F1 0.601, edging out HiCMAE (0.573) and MAE-DFER (0.565). The proposed MSCE inference strategy yields consistent F1 gains over sparse-sampling baselines: Qwen2.5-VL improves from 0.245→0.273 (MAFW) and 0.455→0.482 (DFEW), EmotionQwen from 0.258→0.268 and 0.501→0.515, and LLaVA-NeXT-V from 0.144→0.172 and 0.297→0.317. Frame-shuffling experiments confirm VLMs are largely order-agnostic (≤1% F1 change), while vision-only classifiers drop 15–16%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vision-only classifiers, VLMs lose on MAFW (Gemini 2.5-Flash F1 0.376 vs. HiCMAE 0.399) but win on DFEW (0.601 vs. 0.573), suggesting the 7-class setting is more tractable for VLMs. Among VLMs, Gemini 2.5-Flash is the strongest on both datasets, outperforming Qwen3-VL (0.274/0.551) and EmotionQwen (0.258/0.501) by 10+ F1 points on MAFW. The task-specific EmotionQwen narrows the gap to generalist VLMs on DFEW (0.501) but still trails Gemini by ~10 points. MSCE consistently improves every tested VLM baseline (Qwen2.5-VL, EmotionQwen, LLaVA-NeXT-V) by 1–3 F1 points, and outperforms the frame-selection method TCoT (0.241/0.450) by a wider margin (0.273/0.482), confirming that modality translation of in-between frames recovers temporal signal that sparse selection discards. Frame-shuffling reveals that all VLMs are essentially order-agnostic (≤1% F1 shift), whereas MAE-DFER and HiCMAE drop 15–16%, confirming that VLMs rely on bag-of-frames aggregation rather than temporal reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a diagnostic study rather than a SOTA-chasing contribution, explicitly departing from the DFER literature (MAE-DFER, HiCMAE, DK-CLIP, PE-CLIP) that focuses on specialized architectures. It builds on the decoupled pretrain-then-balanced-finetune paradigm from Kang et al. (ICLR 2020) to validate the long-tail hypothesis, and contrasts its MSCE inference strategy with TCoT's frame-selection approach by arguing that translating discarded temporal gaps into text preserves information that selection-based methods irreversibly lose. The frame-shuffling probe extends the temporal-reasoning diagnostics of SpookyBench to naturalistic, high-resolution video, and the Google Books Ngram correlation analysis adapts long-tail inheritance findings from general object recognition (Parashar et al., CVPR 2024) to the affective domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The quasi-bell-shaped performance curve—where VLM accuracy degrades as frame density increases beyond ~5 FPS—is a practically important warning for anyone deploying VLMs on video: simply feeding more frames is counterproductive and can be worse than sparse sampling. The finding that VLMs are largely indifferent to frame order across all tested architectures (7B to 72B, open and closed source) suggests that causal attention masking does not effectively enforce temporal reasoning, pointing to a need for video-native positional encodings or hierarchical temporal attention. Open questions include whether MSCE's text-bridge strategy generalizes beyond facial expressions to other fleeting visual phenomena, whether the Ngram-based long-tail proxy can be replaced with direct pretraining-data audits, and whether the modest MSCE gains (~2–3 F1 points) scale to longer videos or more complex affective tasks.
