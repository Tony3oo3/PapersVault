## Problem

Standard MLLM descriptions of personal concepts include state-dependent and context-specific details (e.g., 'hanging on a hanger', background cues) that do not help—and can actively hinder—unique identification of a target concept among visually similar items. Existing personalization methods either require expensive test-time retraining, rely on the model to identify relevant regions in reference images, or generate descriptions that lack comparative focus on what truly distinguishes a concept from semantically similar alternatives. The paper asks whether an MLLM can be taught to produce descriptions that are simultaneously accurate, discriminative, and free of distracting details.

## Input / Output

The speaker receives a single reference image of a personal concept and outputs a free-text discriminative description; the listener receives that description plus a set of candidate images (one hard positive view of the target and U hard-negative images from the same semantic class) and outputs a binary Yes/No match probability per candidate, from which the argmax guess is derived. At inference, the system takes a query image and a text query, retrieves top-K concepts from a personal database (each entry storing a reference image, name, speaker description, and CLIP visual/textual embeddings) via combined visual-textual similarity, and the listener MLLM performs closed-set reasoning over the retrieved concept-description pairs to produce a personalized caption, recognition decision, or VQA answer.

## Method

RRG frames personalization as a multimodal reference game in which a single MLLM backbone (Qwen2VL) plays both speaker and listener roles, with the speaker further adapted via LoRA parameters. A game round pairs a hard positive (a different view of the target concept, varying in illumination, background, or state) with hard negatives (visually similar items from the same semantic class), forcing the speaker to articulate only invariant, instance-specific features. The speaker is optimized with GRPO using a verifiable contrastive reward: if the listener correctly identifies the target, the reward is the normalized probability mass on the correct candidate (accounting for model uncertainty); otherwise it is zero. This eliminates the need for explicit description supervision while the hard contrastive structure discourages state and context leakage. At inference, the trained speaker generates descriptions for all personal concepts, which are stored alongside CLIP embeddings in a retrieval database; a query image triggers top-K retrieval by combined visual-textual similarity, and the listener MLLM reasons over the retrieved concept-description pairs in a closed-set prompt to produce the final personalized output.

## Results

RRG achieves state-of-the-art captioning F1 across all three benchmarks: 95.0 on MyVLM, 88.6 on Yo'LLaVA, and 86.9 on PerVA, outperforming the strongest description-based baseline R2P-Qwen by +1.6, +2.3, and +19.6 F1 respectively. On recognition, RRG attains the best weighted accuracy on Yo'LLaVA (94.7 Wtd) and is competitive on PerVA (91.1 Wtd), though it trails RePIC on MyVLM (91.8 vs. 96.8 Wtd). For personalized VQA on Yo'LLaVA, RRG reaches 95.3% accuracy, surpassing R2P-Qwen (94.1%) and RAP-LLaVA (93.2%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On captioning, RRG decisively outperforms all baselines on every dataset: it beats R2P-Qwen (the strongest description-based method) by +1.6 F1 on MyVLM, +2.3 on Yo'LLaVA, and a large +19.6 on PerVA, while surpassing RL-based RePIC by +18.3 F1 on MyVLM and +26.2 on PerVA. On recognition, RRG achieves the best weighted accuracy on Yo'LLaVA (94.7 vs. 93.1 for RAP-Qwen) but underperforms RePIC on MyVLM (91.8 vs. 96.8 Wtd), which the authors attribute to hallucination when both reference and query images are provided as input. For personalized VQA on Yo'LLaVA, RRG leads with 95.3% accuracy over R2P-Qwen (94.1%) and RAP-LLaVA (93.2%). Test-time training methods (MyVLM, Yo'LLaVA) report only recognition scores and do not evaluate captioning or VQA, limiting direct comparison. Overall, RRG's advantage is most pronounced on captioning and VQA, where discriminative descriptions directly improve the listener's reasoning, while recognition gains are more modest and dataset-dependent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on GRPO (from DeepSeek-R1) as its RL training algorithm and on R2P's retrieval-and-reasoning inference pipeline, but departs fundamentally from prior personalization methods in how it trains the model. Where MyVLM and Yo'LLaVA learn concept-specific latent codes at test time, RAP relies on large-scale pre-training, and RePIC applies RL to optimize recognition directly, RRG reframes personalization as a multimodal reference game inspired by the 'Guess Who?' paradigm, using the MLLM as both speaker and listener with hard positives/negatives to produce a verifiable contrastive reward. The key conceptual departure is that the RL signal shapes *description generation* (the speaker) rather than *recognition* (the listener), and the game structure with same-class distractors forces the model to learn discriminative, state-invariant attributes rather than generic captions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the bottleneck for MLLM personalization is description quality rather than recognition capability, a finding supported by ablations showing speaker training outperforms listener training. The reference game provides a principled, supervision-free way to generate discriminative descriptions that generalize to unseen concepts without retraining, which is practically important for real-world personalization where users continuously add new items. Limitations include a recognition gap on MyVLM attributed to multi-image hallucination, dependence on a dataset with same-class concepts (PerVA) for constructing hard negatives, and the current separation of speaker and listener training. The authors identify joint speaker-listener training as a natural next step, and the approach's reliance on CLIP-based retrieval at inference may limit its applicability to very fine-grained or novel concept types.
