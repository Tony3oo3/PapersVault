## Problem

Prior LiDAR spoofing attacks either target isolated 3D object classifiers on normalized CAD models or focus on physical realizability of frustum-level perturbations in driving scenes, without analyzing whether detectors concentrate discriminative evidence in specific spatial regions. This leaves open whether a small, consistent subset of frustums can be exploited to hide objects far more efficiently than uniform full-object perturbation, and whether such critical regions transfer across datasets and detector architectures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261871/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

SALL takes raw LiDAR point cloud scenes (KITTI 7,481 scenes; nuScenes 34,149 samples) and produces a universal class-level saliency map as a 64×32 pixel matrix (≈0.1 m × 0.1 m average voxel) via adaptive indexing that normalizes objects of varying size and rotation. EFA consumes the raw scene plus this saliency map and outputs an adversarial point cloud in the same format as the input, where only points within selected 1° frustums are removed or shifted along their original LiDAR ray directions, preserving geometric consistency.

## Method

SALL adapts Integrated Gradients (M=25 steps, base point at the LiDAR origin or a corner of the focus region) to full-scene 3D detection, computing point-level contribution maps per target object and aggregating them across thousands of scenes via simple matrix addition to yield a universal saliency map per object class. EFA is a six-phase pipeline (Extraction → 2D Pillar Indexing → Frustum Simulation → Frustum Selection → Frustum Perturbation → Merge) whose key innovation is the 'Critical Frustum First' selection strategy: instead of perturbing all frustums covering an object, it ranks frustums by the summed saliency of their contained points and perturbs only the top-k most critical ones. The attack operates in a black-box setting (only detector confidence scores are queried) and supports both Remove and Shift perturbation strategies. The central finding is that detectors like PointPillars and SECOND concentrate discriminative evidence in a sparse subset of spatial regions (edges, corners), so targeting those regions achieves >95% attack success with 25–50% fewer perturbed frustums than uniform baselines, and the resulting saliency maps transfer across both detector architectures and datasets.

## Results

On nuScenes, EFA (SALL-guided, Critical Frustum First + Shift Perturbation) achieves 96.55% Attack Success Rate (ASR) for Car objects with a 20-frustum budget, outperforming HFR (78.29%) and PRA (73.47%) by over 20 percentage points. On KITTI, the same configuration yields a ~30-point improvement over both baselines. For small objects (Pedestrians on KITTI), EFA reaches 97.00% ASR with only 3 frustums, surpassing HFR (74.25%) and PRA (73.61%) by ~23 points. Crucially, EFA matches baseline ASR while perturbing 25–50% fewer frustums (10 on nuScenes, 15 on KITTI vs. 20 for baselines).

## Baselines & Comparisons

EFA consistently outperforms all four baselines across every evaluated setting. Against PRA [3] and HFR [16] (the two primary frustum-level hiding attacks), EFA gains 20+ points on nuScenes Cars, ~30 points on KITTI Cars, and ~23 points on KITTI Pedestrians, while requiring 25–50% fewer perturbed frustums to reach equivalent ASR. Against A-HFR [17] under speed-dependent constraints, the SALL-guided variant achieves 92.70% ASR at 50 km/h versus 67.38% for the original method (a 25-point gain), with the advantage growing at higher speeds where perturbation budgets shrink. For point-level attacks, EFA surpasses ORA [9] by more than 37 percentage points on KITTI (91.07% vs. ~54% at 1200-point budget) and by ~20 points on nuScenes, while matching ORA's ASR with 40–50% fewer perturbed points. Cross-model transferability further confirms that SALL maps are not model-specific: a PointPillars-derived map attacks SECOND at 94.84% ASR, exceeding the native SECOND map (88.12%), and a joint map reaches 99.44% on PointPillars.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Integrated Gradients explainability framework of Tan et al. [22]—originally applied to isolated 3D object classification on normalized CAD models—to the substantially more complex setting of full-scene LiDAR-based 3D object detection in autonomous driving. It bridges two previously separate research threads: gradient-based attribution for point cloud models and LiDAR spoofing/frustum-level attacks (PRA, HFR, A-HFR), which had focused on physical realizability rather than understanding *where* detectors concentrate their discriminative evidence. The key methodological departure is aggregating instance-level attributions across thousands of real-world scenes into universal, class-level saliency maps that transfer across both detector architectures and datasets, rather than requiring per-instance optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that modern 3D detectors concentrate discriminative evidence in a small, consistent subset of spatial regions exposes a structural robustness-efficiency tradeoff with direct safety implications for deployed autonomous driving systems. The defense analysis is particularly notable: existing shadow-based defenses (Obstacle Detection) produce a 60.96% false alarm rate on benign scenes, and random frustum dropping requires removing over one-third of the perturbed region to meaningfully reduce attack success, suggesting input randomization alone is insufficient. Open questions include whether adversarial training or architectural regularization can encourage spatial redundancy in detector reliance, and whether these findings extend to multi-sensor fusion pipelines where camera or radar signals may compensate for LiDAR-specific vulnerabilities.
