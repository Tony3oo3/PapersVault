## Problem

RLS-based Analytic Continual Learning methods suffer catastrophic performance degradation under long-tailed class distributions because class imbalance induces spectral collapse in the Gram matrix: tail-class eigenvalues approach zero, making the matrix inversion numerically unstable. Standard isotropic Ridge regularization cannot resolve this, as a uniform penalty is simultaneously too weak to stabilize the tail and too strong to preserve the head, creating an unresolvable bias-variance trade-off.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-extracted feature vectors (D=768 or 1024 dimensional) from a frozen pretrained backbone (DINO-v2 or MoCo-v3) along with their class labels, arriving in a sequential stream of T tasks. It maintains and recursively updates two matrices — the Gram matrix G = Z^T Z and the cross-correlation Q = Z^T Y — and outputs a closed-form classification weight matrix W = (G + τI)^{-1}Q. No gradient computation or backpropagation is involved; the entire update is a single matrix inversion per task, making it suitable for edge deployment.

## Method

GSR (Geometry-Spectral Rectification) replaces isotropic Ridge regularization with a structured anisotropic spectral filter. For each tail class, it synthesizes augmented samples via Spherical Mixup — geodesic interpolation on the unit hypersphere that preserves norm and avoids the energy decay of linear chordal mixing — with an adaptive intensity α_c that scales inversely with class sample count. These augmented samples are injected into a dual-stream Gram matrix update (real fidelity term + β-weighted rectification term), effectively adding a data-dependent perturbation Δ that selectively inflates collapsed tail eigenvalues while leaving head eigenvalues nearly untouched. Theorem 1 provides a quantitative guarantee that this perturbation strictly increases the stable rank of (G + τI), ensuring numerical stability of the RLS inversion. The approach is non-parametric, requires no additional learnable parameters, and operates at O(D) computational cost per augmented sample.

## Results

Using DINO-v2 as the pretrained backbone, AnaCP+GSR achieves 82.51% A_last on Split-CIFAR-100, surpassing the strong non-RLS baseline SimpleCIL (80.42%) and establishing a new state-of-the-art for analytic CIL in long-tailed settings. GSR yields substantial gains over its RLS baselines: GACL improves from 48.78% to 65.81% (+17.03%) and RanPAC from 49.30% to 65.51% on CIFAR-100. On MoCo-v3, AnaCP+GSR reaches 66.01% A_last versus 57.38% for the AnaCP baseline. Tail-class accuracy improves 3–4× (e.g., 12.50% → 38.00% on CIFAR-100), while head-class accuracy drops by at most ~1%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GSR consistently outperforms all RLS-based baselines: GACL improves by +17.03% (48.78→65.81) and RanPAC by +16.21% (49.30→65.51) on Split-CIFAR-100, while AnaCP+GSR reaches 82.51%, surpassing the AnaCP baseline by a wide margin. The key non-RLS competitor, SimpleCIL (80.42% on CIFAR-100), is outperformed by AnaCP+GSR (82.51%) but remains competitive on CUB-200 (73.73% vs. 85.85% for AnaCP+GSR). AIR, the only RLS method explicitly designed for long-tailed settings via inverse-frequency re-weighting, achieves 65.13% on CIFAR-100 (MoCo-v3) but is consistently outperformed by GSR-enhanced methods, confirming that scalar re-weighting cannot recover missing spectral directions. Covariance-based baselines (SLDA, FeCAM, KLDA) perform substantially worse, with FeCAM dropping to 27.64% on CIFAR-100, highlighting the fragility of second-order statistics under extreme imbalance. On MoCo-v3, the improvement trend holds: AnaCP+GSR leads at 66.01% on CIFAR-100, outperforming both the AnaCP baseline (57.38%) and SimpleCIL (55.32%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLS-based Analytic Continual Learning (ACL) lineage (GACL, RanPAC, AnaCP) by diagnosing a specific algebraic failure mode—spectral collapse of the Gram matrix under class imbalance—and replacing isotropic Ridge regularization with an anisotropic, geometry-aware spectral filter. It departs from prior long-tailed CIL approaches (re-sampling, inverse-frequency re-weighting like AIR) by arguing they operate at the wrong level: they rescale scalar contributions without recovering missing feature-space directions. The Spherical Mixup mechanism draws on geodesic interpolation and Vicinal Risk Minimization, but explicitly rejects linear Manifold Mixup as geometrically inconsistent for hyperspherical PTM embeddings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear, theoretically grounded explanation for why state-of-the-art analytic CIL methods catastrophically fail under realistic long-tailed distributions, and offers a drop-in O(D) fix that practitioners can apply to existing RLS pipelines without architectural changes. The finding that a trivial baseline (SimpleCIL) outperforms sophisticated RLS methods in long-tailed settings is a useful cautionary result for the field. Open questions include whether the spectral rectification generalizes beyond linear classifiers on frozen backbones, how it interacts with domain- or task-incremental settings, and whether the adaptive mixing schedule (Eq. 8) remains optimal under non-uniform or shifting imbalance ratios across tasks.
