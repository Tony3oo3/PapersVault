## Problem

State-of-the-art text-to-video generation models still produce videos that violate basic physical laws, yet existing evaluation metrics provide only coarse-grained aggregate scores that cannot localize which specific physical interaction is implausible or why a video failed. A robust physics evaluation must respect logical dependencies (e.g., an object must exist and undergo an action before its physics can be judged), but prior methods lump all factors into a single score, making failure diagnosis impossible.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt describing a physical scene and a short generated video (4–6 seconds, 720p–1080p, 16–30 fps) as input. It produces a per-question binary yes/no judgment with a natural-language reasoning string for each node in the scene graph, from which per-category scores (object, action, physics) and an overall score are computed as the ratio of affirmative answers. The QA step is deliberately two-phase: the VLM first generates an open-ended response, then categorizes it as yes/no, which the authors found improves answer quality over direct binary prompting.

## Method

PQSG is a two-stage, training-free pipeline built entirely on VLM inference with in-context examples. In the question-generation (QG) stage, a VLM is prompted with one in-context example to produce a directed acyclic graph of atomic verification questions organized into three hierarchical categories—Object (existence), Action (prompt-specified behavior), and Physics (implicit physical commonsense such as deformation or absorption)—with edges encoding cross-level prerequisites (object→action→physics) and same-category sequential dependencies. In the question-answering (QA) stage, a VLM views the generated video and answers each node's question one at a time; if a parent node is answered 'no,' all descendant nodes are automatically marked 'no' without being queried, preventing hallucinated judgments on impossible preconditions. This dependency-enforced evaluation yields interpretable, fine-grained failure localization that the authors validate against human Likert annotations on FINEPHYEVAL (195 prompt-video pairs from Sora 2, Veo 3, Wan 2.1, and Cosmos-14B), showing higher Pearson correlation with human judgments than prior metrics and enabling iterative prompt refinement that improves generation quality by ~15% in one round.

## Results

On FINEPHYEVAL, PQSG with GPT-5.5 QA achieves the highest correlation with human overall Likert scores: Pearson's r = 0.478, Kendall's τ = 0.336, and Spearman's ρ = 0.456, outperforming all prior metrics. With Gemini-2.5-Pro QA, PQSG still leads with Pearson's r = 0.467. On the VideoPhy-2 generalization test, PQSG improves semantic adherence correlation from 0.450 to 0.550 and physical commonsense correlation from 0.420 to 0.498 over the VideoPhy-2-Autoeval baseline. In the video generation model comparison (Table 4), proprietary models (Sora 2: 0.78, Veo 3: 0.80 overall) substantially outperform open-source models (Wan 2.1: 0.59, Cosmos 2.5: 0.62), with all models struggling most on physics (avg. 0.57) and action (avg. 0.66) versus object (avg. 0.93).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PQSG outperforms all four baselines across all three correlation metrics on FINEPHYEVAL. Against the strongest baseline, DirectVQA (Pearson's r = 0.382), PQSG with GPT-5.5 improves by 0.096 in Pearson's r and 0.096 in Spearman's ρ. VideoPhy-2-Autoeval (r = 0.346) and VideoScore (r = 0.289) are both surpassed by a wide margin, while PhyGenEval (r = 0.272) and DSG (r = 0.302) show the weakest alignment with human judgments. On the external VideoPhy-2 test set, PQSG improves both semantic adherence (0.450 → 0.550) and physical commonsense (0.420 → 0.498) over the VideoPhy-2-Autoeval baseline, confirming generalization beyond FINEPHYEVAL. The ablation (Table 8) further shows that removing the dependency graph drops Pearson's r from 0.48 to 0.44 (GPT-5.5 QA) and from 0.80 to 0.75 (human QA), while removing fine-grained questions drops it to 0.40/0.68 respectively, confirming both design choices contribute to the gains over baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

PQSG directly extends the Davidsonian Scene Graph (DSG) framework from Cho et al. (ICLR 2024), which used directed acyclic graphs of atomic verification questions for text-to-image evaluation, by adding temporal dimensions (actions, physics) and a three-level object→action→physics hierarchy suited to video. It departs from prior video evaluation metrics (VideoScore, VideoPhy-2-Autoeval, PhyGenEval) that produce single aggregate scores, arguing that these fail to capture logical dependencies (e.g., you cannot judge a fall's physics if the object is absent) and cannot localize specific failure modes. The method leverages VLMs in an in-context-learning setup for both question generation and answering, positioning itself as a structured, interpretable alternative to end-to-end scoring approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FINEPHYEVAL provides the first human-annotated dataset specifically targeting fine-grained physical plausibility in modern video generation (Sora 2, Veo 3, Wan 2.1, Cosmos), filling a gap where prior benchmarks used older models or lacked per-category annotations. The finding that VLMs generate near-human-quality questions (>90% precision/recall) yet answer physics questions at only ~65% accuracy highlights a concrete bottleneck: the evaluation ceiling is set by VLM physical reasoning, not by the evaluation structure itself. Open questions include whether prompt-agnostic physics evaluation (assessing physics the prompt doesn't explicitly entail) is feasible, and whether iterative refinement loops guided by PQSG can close the gap between open-source and proprietary video generators without architectural changes.
