## Problem

Real-time visualization of high-resolution medical volumes (e.g., 512³ MRI or Cryosection grids) is bottlenecked by the computational cost of ray marching and the memory footprint of dense voxel storage. Existing compression methods approximate voxel signals without modeling the volumetric structure needed for rendering, while surface-oriented Gaussian splatting fails to capture the internal cross-sectional anatomy that defines medical volumes. The paper addresses how to learn a compact, continuous volumetric representation from sparse supervision that supports efficient slice-based rendering without sacrificing structural or radiometric fidelity.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a dense volumetric grid (grayscale MRI or RGB Cryosection, typically 512³) but trains on a sparse subset of voxels kept within 25% of the original data size, with 60% drawn from high-gradient regions and 40% sampled uniformly. At inference, the learned Gaussian field is evaluated at arbitrary 3D coordinates to reconstruct the full voxel grid or to generate slice textures along a camera viewing direction. The shear-warp renderer requires only a single slice stack (rather than the three axis-aligned stacks of conventional voxel-based shear-warp), which is refreshed only when the camera rotates beyond a specified angular threshold, reducing memory and recomputation cost.

## Method

The volume is represented as a mixture of m anisotropic Gaussian kernels, each parameterized by a 3D mean, covariance matrix, RGB color, and opacity, whose weighted sum defines a continuous volumetric color field. Training uses an importance-weighted Monte Carlo estimator to approximate the dense voxel-wise MSE from a sparse sample set, with the sampling distribution blending uniform coverage and error-proportional importance sampling to remain unbiased while prioritizing high-gradient regions. A curriculum learning schedule progressively introduces slice-based supervision (sparse pixels on randomly oriented planes) alongside voxel supervision via a sigmoid weight, so that early training establishes global volumetric consistency before structured planar constraints refine spatial coherence within slices. For rendering, a GPU-based shear-warp pipeline samples 2D slice textures directly from the Gaussian field along the current viewing direction, applies a shear transform, and composites front-to-back with alpha blending, achieving 43.86 FPS with an 11.31:1 compression ratio on the BraTS dataset.

## Results

On the Cryosection (Visible Korean Human) dataset, the proposed Gaussian volumetric representation achieves a reconstruction PSNR of 34.81 dB and SSIM of 0.956, with rendered-view PSNR of 54.87 dB and SSIM of 0.995, while running at 43.86 FPS with a compression ratio up to 11.31:1 (BraTS). Across all six evaluated datasets (Brain, Liver, Heart, Lungs, BraTS, Mouse Neonatal), the method consistently reports PSNR ≥ 32 dB and SSIM ≥ 0.94 for reconstruction, and PSNR ≥ 39.77 dB for rendering. The method requires only 120 training epochs and 6.0 hours, with 9.17×10⁹ FLOPs, substantially less than every neural baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all six baselines on every reported metric (reconstruction PSNR/SSIM, rendering PSNR/SSIM, training time, and FPS). The closest competitor is the Voxel MLP [24], which reaches a rendering PSNR of 46.61 dB but lags the proposed method by 8.26 dB and runs at only 18.99 FPS versus 43.86 FPS; its reconstruction PSNR (23.63) is 11.18 dB lower. The Slice MLP [24] improves reconstruction to 30.02 dB PSNR but still trails by 4.79 dB and requires 35 hours of training versus 6.0 hours. The Transformer [36] and GNT-MOVE [6] baselines perform poorly, with reconstruction PSNRs of 9.60 and 11.28 dB respectively, and the Transformer runs at a mere 0.11 FPS. FreeSplatter [39] is the fastest baseline at 36.01 FPS but produces the worst reconstruction quality (14.74 dB PSNR, 0.483 SSIM), indicating that its surface-oriented Gaussian design is ill-suited for internal volumetric structure. UNet [4] achieves a competitive rendering SSIM of 0.982 but requires 36 GB of memory and 16.7 hours of training, and its reconstruction PSNR (26.70) remains 8.11 dB below the proposed method.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends 3D Gaussian Splatting from its original surface-oriented formulation (Kerbl et al.) to model the interior volumetric structure of medical datasets, where meaningful signal is distributed across cross-sectional planes rather than visible surfaces. It integrates two algorithmic ideas—importance-weighted Monte Carlo estimation for unbiased sparse optimization and sigmoid-scheduled curriculum learning for progressive slice supervision—into a unified training pipeline that was not previously combined for volumetric reconstruction. The rendering side departs from ray marching by coupling the learned Gaussian field directly with the classic shear-warp compositing algorithm, exploiting the fact that Gaussians need not be axis-aligned to avoid maintaining three slice stacks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is a 2.91× rendering speedup over ray marching (43.86 FPS) at 11.31:1 compression while preserving high structural fidelity (PSNR > 32, SSIM > 0.94) across MRI and Cryosection modalities, making real-time exploration of large medical volumes feasible on standard GPUs. Key limitations include inherent blurring near sharp anatomical boundaries due to the smooth Gaussian basis and occasional bright dot artifacts from sparse supervision, both of which constrain the method's applicability to high-frequency diagnostic features. Open questions include whether the approach scales to volumes substantially larger than 512³, whether adaptive Gaussian density (splitting/merging) could mitigate boundary blurring, and whether the curriculum schedule generalizes to other slice-based rendering pipelines beyond shear-warp.
