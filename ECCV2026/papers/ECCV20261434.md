## Problem

Existing Earth observation benchmarks provide few modalities per task and lack globally distributed data with explicit geographic test splits, making it impossible to systematically evaluate whether multimodal pretrained models truly generalize across regions. Additionally, a practical mismatch exists between the modalities a model was pretrained on and the full set of modalities available at inference time, leaving most sensor data unused. The paper addresses both gaps by introducing a 12-modality global benchmark and a test-time adaptation method that repurposes all available modalities as reconstruction targets.

## Input / Output

Each sample is a 128×128-pixel tile at 10 m resolution (~1.6 km²) carrying 12 aligned modalities: six pixel-level (Sentinel-2 13 bands, Sentinel-1 8 polarizations, ASTER GDEM elevation/slope, ETH canopy height/uncertainty, Dynamic World 9-class landcover, ESA WorldCover 11-class landcover) and six tile-level (ERA5 precipitation/temperature at three temporal scales, geolocation, S2 acquisition date, biome, ecoregion). Outputs are task-specific: pixel-level regression for biomass (Mg/ha), tile-level regression for soil N/OC/pH, or tile-level multi-label classification for 100 mammal species. Critically, a given model only consumes the subset of modalities it was pretrained on as encoder input, while all 12 modalities remain available as reconstruction targets during test-time adaptation.

## Method

TTT-MMR first performs joint training (JT), where a lightweight linear task-modality decoder is trained to reconstruct all 12 task modalities from the encoder's embedding, alongside the main task decoder. At test time, the frozen modality decoder produces per-modality reconstruction losses whose gradients are individually L2-normalized and then averaged, yielding a single adaptation signal that updates only the encoder via SGD (lr=10⁻², 5 iterations per batch of 8 tiles). This makes every modality contribute equally regardless of scale, and the multi-modality averaging acts as an implicit regularizer. A geographic variant (TTT-MMR-GEO) replaces random batching with k-d-tree–based spatial partitioning, so each batch is a contiguous region; this trades some regularization for domain specialization and substantially improves performance on rare/long-tail samples. The entire procedure is model-agnostic—no architectural changes, no batch-norm or FiLM layers required—and the encoder is reset to its post-JT state after each batch to prevent drift.

## Results

TTT-MMR and TTT-MMR-GEO improve average rank over joint training (JT) for all 10 benchmarked models on both random and geographic test splits (Table 5). For example, on the random split, TTT-MMR-GEO reduces the average rank from ~3.0 (JT) to ~1.1–1.5 across models, while on the geographic split it reduces ranks from ~2.8–3.0 to ~1.3–1.7. The RGB-only models (Scale-MAE, DINOv3 Web/Sat) show the largest absolute rank improvements from TTT (e.g., DINOv3 Sat drops from rank 3.0 to 1.3 on random split), since they gain access to 12 additional modalities at test time. All models still exhibit a geographic generalization gap on 4 of 5 tasks, with soil N and soil OC showing the largest performance drops on the Africa test split.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All seven pretrained baselines (Scale-MAE, DINOv3 Web, DINOv3 Sat, SatlasNet, MPMAE, TerraMind, Copernicus-FM, Galileo) are consistently outperformed by both TTT-MMR and TTT-MMR-GEO on average rank across all tasks and splits. The RGB-only models (Scale-MAE, DINOv3 Web/Sat) benefit most from TTT, improving by ~1.5–1.9 rank positions, because TTT grants them access to 12 modalities they cannot natively ingest. Among multimodal baselines, TerraMind and Galileo show the largest TTT gains on the geographic split (rank ~2.9→1.3–1.5), while Copernicus-FM's Siamese averaging architecture yields slightly smaller improvements. The randomly initialized ConvNeXtV2A-MM (not a pretrained baseline but a key comparison) ranks 4th overall with 100% training data on the random split, competitive with pretrained multimodal models, but drops to 5th on the geographic split where pretraining's exposure to African data becomes advantageous. Notably, DINOv3 Sat's domain-specific pretraining over DINOv3 Web provides little benefit on these tasks, and MPMAE (S2-only) is competitive with multimodal models on biomass and soil OC despite lacking SAR or climate inputs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: self-supervised multimodal pretraining for Earth observation (MMEarth, TerraMind, Copernicus-FM, Galileo), test-time training with self-supervised auxiliary tasks (TTT with masked autoencoders, TENT, joint training), and geospatial benchmarking (PANGAEA, GEO-Bench, SustainBench). The key conceptual departure is repurposing multimodal reconstruction—typically a pretraining or joint-training objective—as a test-time adaptation signal, so that modalities unavailable to the pretrained encoder can still inform adaptation at inference. The geographic batching strategy for TTT regularization extends prior work on batch composition in test-time adaptation by exploiting spatial contiguity as a proxy for domain similarity, a design choice not present in prior TTT or UDA methods for EO.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MMEarth-Bench fills a concrete gap: no prior EO benchmark provides 12 shared modalities across all tasks with truly global coverage and an explicit held-out-continent geographic split, making it the first systematic test of whether pretrained models actually generalize geographically. The finding that a randomly initialized multimodal model trained from scratch is competitive with global pretraining at 100% data challenges the assumption that self-supervised pretraining is always necessary, while the persistent geographic gap on 4/5 tasks indicates that current pretraining strategies do not solve the hardest generalization problem. Open questions include whether TTT-MMR extends to time-series settings, whether geographic batching generalizes beyond a single held-out continent, and whether the method's gains hold under more extreme data scarcity or with larger model families.
