## Problem

Existing vision-language models fail at understanding camera movement described in natural language, frequently confusing translation with rotation, left with right, optical zoom with physical dolly, and object motion with global camera movement. While prior works have noted this deficiency, the underlying failure modes remain unexplored and no dedicated benchmark or training pipeline exists to systematically study and improve this capability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262308/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a short video clip (real-world clips are predominantly under 10 seconds; synthetic clips are 4 seconds) and produces a camera movement label from a 17-class cinematographic taxonomy spanning translations (dolly, truck, boom), rotations (pan, tilt, roll), focal-length changes (zoom), static shots, and object-centric movements (tracking, arc). Evaluation is conducted in both binary yes/no and multi-choice question-answering formats, with one atomic camera movement per clip to isolate individual movement recognition.

## Method

The paper introduces ACaM, a benchmark combining 1,423 real-world videos curated from six existing sources with synthetic videos generated via Veo 3.1 conditioned on structured prompts derived from real clips. For training, a 27K instruction-tuning set is assembled from five data sources (CameraBench, ShotBench, SpatialVID, MultiCamVideo, GenDoP) and converted to multi-choice QA format. The key technical contribution is targeted data augmentation to address the severe long-tail class imbalance: temporal reversal converts dolly-in to dolly-out clips, horizontal flipping augments truck movements, progressive cropping and resizing synthesizes zoom samples, and continuous affine rotation generates roll dynamics. Qwen3-VL-4B and 8B are fine-tuned via LoRA on this balanced corpus, with the combination of targeted resampling and augmentation yielding a 16-point accuracy gain over the unbalanced baseline on synthetic videos.

## Results

On the ACaM benchmark, the fine-tuned Qwen3-VL-8B achieves 74.28% average accuracy on real-world videos and 77.44% on synthetic videos, outperforming Gemini-3.1-Pro (67.73% and 69.58%) by approximately 10% and 11% in relative terms, respectively. The 4B variant reaches 72.27% and 74.40%, already surpassing all prior camera-movement-specialized 7B models. Human performance stands at 93.14% (real) and 97.61% (synthetic), revealing a substantial 19–20 point gap that remains unresolved.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The SFT Qwen3-VL-8B (74.28% avg real / 77.44% avg synthetic) outperforms all 15 baselines on both splits. Against the strongest proprietary model Gemini-3.1-Pro (67.73% / 69.58%), it gains roughly 6.5 and 7.9 absolute points (≈10% and 11% relative), with the largest margins on Zoom (+21.8 / +13.5) and Rotation (+6.7 / +10.4). Among camera-movement-specialized VLMs, CameraModel-7B (60.56% / 68.45%) and ShotVL-7B (57.09% / 64.49%) are the closest competitors but are surpassed by 13.7 and 17.2 points respectively on real-world average accuracy; CamReasoner-7B (44.99% / 53.74%) trails by a wide margin. Geometry-based methods (Mega-SaM, ViPE) cover only a subset of categories and cannot predict zoom, tracking, or arc, limiting direct comparison. Spatial VLMs (G2VLM-2B, Spatial-MLLM, VLM-3R-7B) perform surprisingly poorly (5–35% avg), confirming that geometry-aware pretraining for scene understanding does not transfer to camera-movement semantics. Notably, even the best model remains 19 points below human performance (93.14% real), indicating the task is far from solved.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out natural language camera movement understanding as a standalone research task, deliberately separating it from the broader fine-grained motion understanding (MotionBench, FavorBench), cinematography QA (ShotBench, CineTechBench, Cinematic2K), and camera motion primitive estimation (CameraBench) lines of work. It departs from geometry-based camera pose estimation (Mega-SaM, ViPE, SfM) by arguing that semantic, language-level understanding is more accessible and scalable than per-frame coordinate estimation, while also noting that geometry methods cannot handle object-centric movements like tracking or arc. The training pipeline repurposes clips from five existing datasets (CameraBench, ShotBench, SpatialVID, MultiCamVideo, GenDoP) and applies targeted augmentation (temporal reversal, horizontal flipping, affine rotation, progressive cropping) to balance a long-tailed distribution, a strategy not previously applied to camera movement data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The persistent ~20-point gap between the best fine-tuned VLM (74% avg) and human annotators (93% avg) on real videos signals that natural language camera movement understanding remains far from solved, with five identified failure modes (insensitivity to small motion, translation-rotation confusion, left-right inversion, zoom-dolly conflation, object-vs-camera motion confusion) providing a concrete roadmap for future work. The benchmark's inclusion of synthetic videos and human baselines makes it directly applicable to evaluating and curating training data for modern text-to-video generation models, where automatic verification of camera prompt fidelity is increasingly critical. Open questions include extending from atomic to compound camera movements, closing the human-VLM gap, and whether geometry-aware architectures can complement the semantic approach.
