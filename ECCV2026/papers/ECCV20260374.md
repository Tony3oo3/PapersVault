## Problem

Standard aggregate metrics (FID, KID) used to evaluate adapted text-to-image diffusion models are structurally blind to concentrated, concept-specific damage: when damage is severe enough for these metrics to respond, the model is already nearly unusable, while when the model remains functional, FID and KID stay flat and specific classes silently suffer worst-case accuracy drops of up to 18.9 points. No existing tool identifies which specific visual concepts have been eroded by a given adaptation, leaving practitioners without a concept-level audit before deployment.

## Input / Output

DriftScope takes two checkpoints of the same diffusion architecture (a base model and an adapted variant) plus a user-provided text prompt, and returns a ranked blacklist of tokens whose visual concepts have drifted most between the checkpoints, along with per-token drift scores. The method requires no access to real reference data or model internals beyond standard cross-attention outputs, and a token translator bridges vocabulary mismatches between the masked language model and the target diffusion encoder (e.g., CLIP vs. T5). By inverting the objective, it can equally surface tokens that remain stable across checkpoints.

## Method

DriftScope parameterizes a soft prompt as learnable vectors prepended to a fixed template containing mask tokens, using Gumbel-Softmax relaxation to make discrete token selection differentiable through a masked language model. The optimization objective maximizes the L1 divergence between cross-attention maps produced by the two frozen checkpoints under identical noise seeds, averaged over a batch of seeds and a set of architecture-specific layers most sensitive to adaptation drift. Gradients flow only through the soft prompt parameters while both diffusion checkpoints remain frozen, and the optimization is run multiple times with different initializations to aggregate consistent drift signals. Word-level attribution is computed over the full prompt's cross-attention maps (capturing object relations and context) rather than isolating individual token attention slices, yielding a drift score per realized mask token that reflects how differently the two checkpoints process the entire scene.

## Results

Across all evaluated adaptation methods, aggregate metrics (FID, KID) remain low (e.g., SPM: FID 3.643, KID 0.00002; DreamBooth SD2.1: FID 6.68, KID 0.003) while zero-shot classification reveals worst-case per-class accuracy drops of up to 18.9 points (SPM on CIFAR-10) and 98.1 points (EraseDiff on Flowers-102). For DreamBooth, mean accuracy changes are near zero (e.g., −2.88 on Flowers for SD1.5) but standard deviations reach 12.21, indicating concentrated concept-specific damage invisible to aggregate scores. DriftScope's high-drift tokens show a clear separation from low-drift tokens: for SPM, CLIP-i drops from 0.96 to 0.88 and LPIPS rises from 0.14 to 0.33, confirming reliable discrimination between stable and unstable concepts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not position DriftScope as outperforming a competing diagnostic; rather, it demonstrates that all six adaptation methods (AC, EraseDiff, MACE, SPM for unlearning; DreamBooth across SD1.5/2.1/3.5 for customization) induce hidden concept-level drift that FID and KID structurally cannot detect. SPM is the most insidious case: FID 3.643 and KID 0.00002 suggest near-perfect preservation, yet worst-case CIFAR-10 accuracy drops 18.9 points. EraseDiff is the catastrophic case where FID (321.6) and KID (0.377) do respond, but only after the model is nearly unusable (−98.1 on Flowers). DreamBooth across all three architectures shows the subtlest regime: FID stays under 9 and mean accuracy changes are near zero, yet per-concept standard deviations of 10–12 points reveal concentrated damage. DriftScope's token-level attribution (Tables 2–3) confirms it surfaces non-obvious collateral concepts (e.g., 'seal,' 'boyfriend' for nudity unlearning; 'bible,' 'dragon' for dog fine-tuning) that no aggregate metric would flag, with a clear high-drift vs. low-drift separation (SPM: LPIPS 0.33 vs. 0.14; CLIP-i 0.88 vs. 0.96).

## Relation to Prior Work

DriftScope sits at the intersection of three research threads: sparse autoencoder-based concept analysis (Bohacek et al.), model diffing for interpretability (Lindsey et al.'s crosscoders, Bricken et al.'s stage-wise diffing), and differentiable prompt optimization for probing model internals (DEXTER, SAGE). The key departure is repurposing the prompt-optimization paradigm—previously used to probe a single model's failure modes or explain neuron activations—as a self-contained measurement instrument that compares two checkpoints of the same architecture via cross-attention divergence. Unlike prior model-diffing work that operates on language-model residual streams, DriftScope targets diffusion-model cross-attention maps and produces token-level drift scores without requiring access to model internals beyond standard attention outputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that FID and KID are structurally blind to concentrated, concept-specific damage (flat aggregate scores alongside 18.9-point worst-case zero-shot drops) has direct implications for deployment pipelines that rely on these metrics as quality gates. DriftScope provides a practical, interpretable audit tool that practitioners can run before releasing an adapted model, surfacing non-obvious collateral damage (e.g., unlearning nudity degrades 'seal' and 'boyfriend'; fine-tuning on a dog degrades 'bible' and 'dragon'). Open questions include whether the optimization-based approach can be made faster or more scalable, whether the method generalizes to video or audio diffusion models, and whether the identified drift tokens can inform targeted mitigation strategies beyond detection.
