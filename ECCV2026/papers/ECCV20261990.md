## Problem

Existing 3D head avatar methods capture a person's identity only at a momentary level (single photo or short video), assuming shape invariance. This breaks down on a lifelong scale where facial geometry, texture, and appearance shift substantially across ages, and where data for each lifestage is sparse, unstructured, and unevenly distributed in viewpoint and quantity. No prior method can reconstruct and explicitly animate a single person's head across multiple life stages while preserving long-horizon identity consistency.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261990/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes unstructured RGB photo collections (resized to 512×512) spanning 8–13 lifestages per identity, with 500–3000 frames per stage and highly uneven viewpoint/illumination distributions. FLAME fitting on each frame provides per-frame expression and shape coefficients that drive animation. The output is a personalized neural parametric space from which one can render novel-view head images, animate expressions/shapes across any lifestage, and extract dynamic dense head meshes (via Poisson reconstruction followed by deferred vertex-level warping) for any combination of age, expression, and viewpoint.

## Method

TimeWalker builds a personalized neural space via additive combination of a canonical 2D Gaussian Surfel representation (initialized from the FLAME template) and moment-specific deformations produced by a set of Neural Head Bases stored in multi-resolution hashgrids. The Dynamo module dynamically prunes hashgrids whose blending weights consistently fall below a threshold κ across lifestages, yielding a compact, interpretable basis set that captures core identity-defining features rather than memorizing each appearance. For motion, DNA-2DGS applies an inverse-warping deformation gradient F (computed from FLAME's canonical-to-deformed Frenet frames) to the moment-specific Gaussian Surfels, decoupling lifestage deformation from expression/shape animation. For meshing, a Defer-Warping strategy performs Poisson reconstruction on the static moment-specific surface first, then applies expression-driven vertex displacements to generate dynamic mesh sequences without motion-induced artifacts. Training is end-to-end (Gaussian attributes, hashgrids, residual embeddings, and deform/color MLPs) with a warm-up phase that freezes the Neural Head Basis to establish the canonical mean, followed by joint optimization under image, geometry, and regularization losses.

## Results

On the TimeWalker-1.0 dataset (5 identities, 8–13 lifestages each, 8K–20K frames), TimeWalker achieves PSNR 27.28, SSIM 0.949, LPIPS 0.071, and ID Score 93.71 under the 1-vs-1 protocol (single model for all lifestages), outperforming all baselines on every metric. Under the 1-vs-N protocol (one model per lifestage for baselines), TimeWalker still leads on PSNR (27.28 vs. 27.61 for GS++), LPIPS (0.071 vs. 0.134), and ID Score (93.71 vs. 87.96), with only a marginal SSIM gap to Gaussian Surfels (0.949 vs. 0.950). The ID Score advantage is the most pronounced, exceeding the next-best baseline (INSTA++ at 85.37) by over 8 points, confirming strong long-horizon identity preservation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TimeWalker decisively outperforms INSTA [48] (PSNR 27.28 vs. 20.68, ID 93.71 vs. 51.59) and FlashAvatar [36] (PSNR 27.28 vs. 22.14, ID 93.71 vs. 66.97) in Protocol-1, with gaps of 6.6 dB and 10.4 dB in PSNR respectively. Against the closest multi-lifestage baseline INSTA++ (a PAV [3] re-implementation), TimeWalker gains +0.89 dB PSNR and +8.34 ID Score, showing that simply adding lifestage data to a NeRF pipeline is insufficient without the proposed Dynamo and DNA-2DGS designs. In Protocol-2, Gaussian Surfels++ [9] narrowly edges TimeWalker on PSNR (27.61 vs. 27.28) and SSIM (0.948 vs. 0.949), but TimeWalker dominates on LPIPS (0.071 vs. 0.134) and ID Score (93.71 vs. 76.98), indicating superior perceptual quality and identity fidelity. For mesh reconstruction (Fig. 7), TimeWalker's DNA-2DGS pipeline produces a clean, anatomically plausible head mesh, while Colmap [33] and vanilla Gaussian Surfels [9] fail to generate meaningful geometry from single-lifestage monocular data. In the identity-consistency comparison (Fig. 6), TimeWalker maintains higher ArcFace [10] scores across lifestage transitions than GAGAvatar [7], and GANAvatar [17] and GPHM [38] are further outperformed in appendix comparisons, confirming the advantage of the shared canonical representation over per-stage or generative approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

TimeWalker extends the additive-combination principle of classic 3DMMs (Blanz & Vetter) and FLAME into a neural feature space, using Gaussian Surfels (2DGS) as the base representation and adapting INSTA's inverse warping into a deformation-gradient formulation (DNA-2DGS). The closest prior work is PAV, which also models a person's appearance variations from unstructured video, but TimeWalker departs by introducing an interpretable, dynamically-pruned basis (Dynamo) and explicit multi-dimensional control (shape, expression, viewpoint, appearance) across lifestages rather than a single appearance embedding. The method also builds on multi-resolution hash encoding for compact feature storage and Poisson meshing for surface reconstruction, repurposing both for the dynamic, multi-stage setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic formulation of lifelong head avatar modeling, introducing both a new task and a 2.7M-frame dataset (TimeWalker-1.0) that no prior benchmark addresses. The finding that multi-lifestage training substantially improves single-stage rendering (≈3.5 dB PSNR gain) suggests that cross-age shared representations act as a powerful regularizer against sparse per-stage data, a principle that could transfer to other long-horizon personalization tasks. Key open questions remain: whether the FLAME-anchored framework can handle extreme expressions or unseen anatomy, whether reconstruction-based and generative paradigms can be seamlessly unified for full-life-span coverage, and whether the approach generalizes beyond the 40-celebrity dataset to arbitrary individuals with noisier, less curated data.
