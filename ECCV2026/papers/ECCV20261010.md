## Problem

Existing image editing and generation methods struggle with the 'Scene Staging' task: given a reference scene and a text prompt specifying an actor and its spatial relation, they either rigidly replicate the reference scene while ignoring the prompt, or aggressively follow the text at the expense of scene identity. This failure stems from both a data bottleneck—high-quality paired training data with diverse viewpoints, correct entity-scene relationships, and large scene diversity is extremely difficult to obtain—and the absence of explicit spatial-alignment supervision in standard diffusion training objectives.

## Input / Output

The method takes a reference scene image (RGB, arbitrary aspect ratio) and a text prompt that jointly specifies an actor category (e.g., 'a man', 'a book') and its spatial relationship to the scene (e.g., 'sitting on the left bench'). It produces a single generated image that preserves the reference scene's identity while correctly rendering the actor in the described position. Training uses multi-resolution inputs with the longer side constrained to at most 1024 pixels, and inference runs 28 denoising steps on the Qwen-Image-Edit MMDiT backbone.

## Method

The approach has two core components. First, a scene-consistent data construction pipeline converts real-world photographs into training pairs by: (a) removing the target entity via FLUX.1 Kontext or OmniEdit to obtain clean scene-only images, (b) synthesizing spatially diverse views using the Wan 2.2 image-to-video diffusion model, (c) filtering frames with MINIMA feature matching to discard hallucinated or structurally drifted outputs, and (d) extracting sparse cross-view correspondences that are converted into dense soft correspondence masks. Second, a correspondence-guided attention loss regularizes a single mid-level joint-attention block of the MMDiT backbone by computing a symmetrized cross-view attention score for each token and penalizing its deviation from the corresponding soft mask, thereby forcing tokens at the same scene location across views to attend to each other. The model is trained with a combined flow-matching loss plus λ·L_attn (λ=3.0) using parameter-efficient adapters on the frozen Qwen-Image-Edit backbone, with a temporally-weighted sampling strategy that biases toward later video frames exhibiting larger viewpoint changes.

## Results

On the scene-consistent benchmark (2,125 scene-prompt pairs), the proposed method achieves the best scores across all four evaluation metrics: G2.5F-SA 9.811, G2.5F-TIA 9.639, Scene Align 37.13%, and Text Align 28.45%. The closest competitor, Qwen-Image-Edit, scores 9.434 on scene alignment and 9.306 on text-image alignment, while the training-free SceneDecorator drops to 4.104 on scene alignment. In the user study (6,375 votes from 50 annotators), the method is preferred most often for both scene and text alignment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all three baselines on every metric. Against Qwen-Image-Edit (the strongest baseline), it gains +0.377 on G2.5F-SA and +0.333 on G2.5F-TIA, with a modest +2.24 pp in user-study scene alignment; text alignment is essentially tied (28.45% vs 28.43%). Against FLUX.1 Kontext Dev, the advantage is larger: +1.416 on scene alignment and +0.798 on text-image alignment, reflecting FLUX's weaker scene preservation under viewpoint changes. The gap versus SceneDecorator is dramatic—9.811 vs 4.104 on G2.5F-SA and 37.13% vs 1.69% in user-study scene preference—confirming the paper's hypothesis that training-free attention sharing collapses when camera motion alters visual features. The ablation further shows the correspondence-guided attention loss contributes +0.279 on G2.5F-SA and +1.07 on G2.5F-TIA over the same model without it, and the data pipeline outperforms a DL3DV-10K + post-hoc inpainting baseline by +0.763 on scene alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the MMDiT architecture of Qwen-Image-Edit as its diffusion backbone, applying parameter-efficient fine-tuning via lightweight adapters. It positions itself against three lines of work: general-purpose image editing models (FLUX.1 Kontext, Qwen-Image-Edit) that handle local edits but fail under global viewpoint shifts; inpainting methods (BrushNet, SmartBrush) that assume fixed spatial layouts; and consistent generation methods (StoryDiffusion, IP-Adapter) that target character rather than scene identity. The key departure is the explicit 'Scene Staging' formulation—preserving scene identity while simultaneously changing viewpoint and inserting a text-specified actor—addressed through a novel data pipeline (entity removal + image-to-video multi-view synthesis + correspondence extraction) and a correspondence-guided attention loss that directly regularizes cross-view attention maps, in contrast to SceneDecorator's training-free attention sharing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and addresses a concrete bottleneck in visual storytelling: maintaining scene identity under text-driven viewpoint and composition changes, a problem that existing editing and consistency methods handle poorly. The data construction pipeline (leveraging image-to-video models for multi-view synthesis and feature matching for correspondence) is a practical, scalable recipe that could benefit other scene-consistency tasks. A key limitation is that actor identity preservation is not integrated into the core model, requiring a decoupled two-stage pipeline; the authors explicitly flag end-to-end joint scene-and-actor consistency as an important open direction. The correspondence-guided attention loss, while conceptually simple, offers a reusable mechanism for spatially grounding diffusion models in multi-view settings.
