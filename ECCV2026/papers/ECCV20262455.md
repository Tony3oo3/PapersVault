## Problem

While MLLMs have shown progress in single-step spatial understanding, their ability to perform sequential spatial reasoning across multiple steps—critical for applications like robotic assembly and step-by-step guidance—remains largely unexplored. Existing benchmarks rarely evaluate planning beyond one action step, leaving a gap in understanding whether models can maintain consistent spatial reasoning over longer horizons.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262455/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The Elementary set takes rendered LEGO images (fixed camera viewpoint, rendered via Studio software from open-source projects) paired with text questions and candidate answer images, producing multiple-choice selections or ordered step sequences. The Planning VQA task provides the initial state x₀, target state x_{k+1}, and 2k candidate intermediate images (k correct + k erroneous), requiring the model to select and order the k correct steps. The Planning Generation task is multi-turn: at each turn the model receives the initial state, target state, and all previously generated intermediate states, and must produce the next intermediate state as a new image, continuing until k steps are complete.

## Method

LEGO-Puzzles is a benchmark constructed from open-source LEGO projects with step-by-step assembly instructions, organized into two sets with progressively increasing complexity. The Elementary set contains 1,100 VQA pairs across 11 tasks in three levels (spatial understanding, single-step reasoning, multi-step reasoning), each with 100 balanced samples. The Planning set scales sequence length k from 1 to 8 for VQA (with 20 cases per k) and evaluates multi-turn image generation for k=2–5 (50 cases total). Data curation involves rendering projects with fixed viewpoints, applying deliberate perturbations (rotation, color changes, part removal) to generate distractors, and a three-stage human-in-the-loop quality control process. Evaluation uses exact match accuracy for VQA, supplemented by set-match and overlap-ratio metrics for planning; the generation task is scored by matching each generated step against all topology-expanded valid trajectories via MSE and checking whether matched steps form a complete legal assembly path. All 29 evaluated MLLMs are tested zero-shot, with human baselines (30 experts for Elementary, 5 experts for Planning) establishing the performance ceiling.

## Results

On the Elementary Set (1,100 VQA samples across 11 tasks), GPT-5 achieves the highest overall accuracy at 72.0%, followed by Gemini-2.5-Pro at 68.8%, while human experts reach 93.6%—a gap of over 20 percentage points. In the Planning Set (PLAN-k-Step-VQA), GPT-5's exact-match accuracy drops from 90% at k=1 to 0% at k=8, and all models fall below 50% once k>3, whereas human participants achieve 100% at every horizon. For the image-generation planning task, all evaluated models (GPT-5, Nano Banana Pro, Nano Banana 2) produce zero fully correct trajectories even at k=3, despite generating images with reasonable appearance similarity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary models, GPT-5 (72.0%) and Gemini-2.5-Pro (68.8%) lead the Elementary Set, outperforming GPT-o3 (69.1%), GPT-4o (57.7%), and Claude-3.5-Sonnet (53.6%) by 4–14 points overall. The best open-source model, Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct (57.0%), trails GPT-5 by 15 points and is the only open-source model to exceed the ↑Random threshold (35.5%) on the overall score; 12 of 20 open-source models fall below ↑Random on at least one task. In the Planning Set, GPT-5 maintains a 90% exact-match at k=1 but collapses to 0% by k=8, while Qwen3-VL-32B and InternVL3-78B reach 0% already at k>4; proprietary models retain ~65% overlap ratio at k=7–8 whereas open-source models degrade to near-random. Human participants achieve 93.6% on the Elementary Set and a perfect 100% on all Planning Set horizons, establishing a ≥20-point gap over the best MLLM. In the image-generation planning task, GPT-5, Nano Banana Pro, and Nano Banana 2 all score 0% on fully correct trajectories at every k (2–5), and in single-turn generation (Table 4), even the best proprietary model (Nano Banana Pro, App 2.52 / IF 1.99 out of 3) fails to reliably follow spatial instructions, while open-source generators (Emu2, GILL, Anole) score near zero on instruction following.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of spatial reasoning benchmarks for MLLMs (e.g., 3DSRBench, CLEVR, Super-CLEVR) by shifting focus from single-step spatial understanding and camera-perspective changes to multi-step sequential planning. It draws on cognitive science literature establishing LEGO construction as a validated indicator of spatial intelligence (mental rotation, visuospatial working memory) to ground the task design in human developmental stages. The benchmark also extends evaluation beyond VQA to multi-turn image generation, a dimension largely absent from prior spatial reasoning benchmarks, and validates its generalizability through strong Pearson correlations (0.93–0.98) with 3DSRBench on natural images.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a stark and systematic failure mode: even frontier models (GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Pro) drop to 0% accuracy on multi-step planning as horizon grows, while humans achieve 100%, and image-generation-based planning fails completely even at 3 steps. This suggests that current MLLM architectures lack a coherent mechanism for tracking cumulative spatial transformations across sequential actions, a capability essential for robotics, autonomous assembly, and embodied AI. Open questions include whether longer context windows, explicit 3D scene representations, or dedicated planning modules could close this gap, and whether the observed 2D-projection bias (models reasoning over 2D image height rather than true 3D height) is a fundamental architectural limitation or a data/training artifact.
