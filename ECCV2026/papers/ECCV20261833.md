## Problem

Video world models generate worlds as 2D frame sequences, yet it is unknown whether they can continue evolving scene state when observation is interrupted (occlusion, lights off, camera lookaway). Existing benchmarks cover physical plausibility and consistency only under full observation, leaving untested the critical question of whether state evolution is truly decoupled from what is visible — a prerequisite for larger worlds and longer-horizon interactions where most of the scene is unobserved.

## Input / Output

Each of the 225 tasks provides an initial RGB image of a main object in its pre-evolution state, a text prompt specifying both an action (e.g., 'hand tips over the first domino') and an observation interruption (e.g., 'a cardboard covers the camera for 5 seconds' or 'turn off the light'), and, for camera-controlled models, an explicit camera trajectory (e.g., 'right-30 steps, left-30 steps'). The model outputs a generated video; the evaluation pipeline then produces four binary scores — observation control, action control, state progress, and physics plausibility — from which control success and evolution success are derived.

## Method

STEVO-BENCH constructs 225 tasks across six evolution categories (continuous process, kinematics, relational change, causal change, state transformation, expected human/animal behavior) and applies model-specific observation interruption: in-scene occluders or illumination removal via text prompt for video models, and lookaway camera trajectories for camera-controlled models. Evaluation follows a two-stage protocol: a control-success gate (both observation and action controls must be correctly applied) precedes evolution-success scoring (state progress and physical plausibility must both hold). Automatic specialist verifiers, each built on Gemini 3.1 Pro and posing a single narrowly-scoped binary question, replace monolithic VLM judgment; the state-progress verifier first uses VLM reasoning to predict the expected directional change, then confirms it via a unanimous-vote ensemble (n=3), while the physics-plausibility verifier flags violations across a three-type taxonomy (instantaneous, dynamic, continuity) using majority-vote ensembles to suppress hallucinations. The benchmark is validated against three human annotators and a cross-VLM ensemble (Gemini 3.1 Pro, GPT 5.4, Claude Opus 4.7), showing verifier–human agreement at or above inter-human agreement on all criteria.

## Results

On STEVO-BENCH (225 tasks), all evaluated video world models achieve less than 10% task success under interrupted observation. Among video generation models, Veo 3 leads with 9.0% success (17.6% state progress, 52.5% physics plausibility), followed by Sora 2 Pro at 8.1% success (13.1% progress, 63.3% physics). All camera-controlled models (Genie 3, HY-WorldPlay, Lingbot, GEN3C) achieve 0.0% task success, with state progress below 3.4% across the board. When observation control is removed (full visibility), state progress jumps to 84.6% and success to 46.2% (averaged over Veo 3 and Sora 2 Pro), confirming that the failure is specifically caused by interrupted observation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper, so the 'comparison' is across 9 evaluated models rather than a proposed method vs. baselines. Among video generation models, closed-source Veo 3 (9.0% success) and Sora 2 Pro (8.1%) substantially outperform open-source WAN 2.2 (0.9%), HY-Video 1.5 (0.9%), and CogVideoX 1.5 (0.5%), with a ~10x gap in task success. Sora 2 Pro achieves the highest physics plausibility (63.3%) among video models, while Veo 3 leads in state progress (17.6%). All four camera-controlled models (Genie 3, HY-WorldPlay, Lingbot, GEN3C) achieve 0.0% task success, with Genie 3 showing the highest state progress (2.9%) but still failing to complete any task. The paper further shows that memory-based VMem and 4D-reconstruction-based AETHER (evaluated qualitatively) also fail to evolve state, with VMem reinforcing static-scene bias despite accurate frame recall.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the specific evaluation task of state evolution under interrupted observation, which no prior benchmark addresses. Existing physics benchmarks (VideoPhy, PAIBench) test plausibility under full observation, memory/consistency benchmarks (MIND, World Consistency Score) test static-scene coherence, and instruction-following suites (WorldScore, VBench) test action control — but none combine all four criteria (progress, physics, observation control, action control) simultaneously. The paper also departs from the stateful world model literature (WorldMem, VMem, WonderPlay, FloWM), which designs explicit state representations in constrained domains, by instead probing implicit 'statefulness' in general-purpose video generation models through a black-box evaluation protocol.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-universal failure (<10% success across all nine models) exposes a fundamental architectural gap: current video world models cannot maintain state evolution when the observation channel is interrupted, which is a prerequisite for scaling to larger, longer-horizon worlds where most of the scene is unobserved at any moment. The finding that memory-based architectures (VMem) reinforce rather than alleviate the static-scene bias is a counterintuitive negative result that challenges a natural design intuition. Open questions include whether new attention mechanisms (beyond naive bidirectional all-to-all) or different training data strategies (e.g., synthetic occlusion-interrupted dynamics) can break the evolution-observation coupling, and whether the camera-control/dynamics tradeoff identified here is a data artifact or a fundamental representational limitation.
