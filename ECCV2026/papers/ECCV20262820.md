## Problem

Existing VLM benchmarks evaluate end-to-end answer accuracy on well-defined, single-round questions, leaving the intermediate exploratory reasoning process unexamined. In practice, complex open-ended visual tasks require multiple rounds of active exploration and sub-goal proposal, yet it remains unclear how VLMs plan their exploration direction versus how they execute individual reasoning steps, and whether they can recover from intermediate errors.

## Input / Output

Each sample presents an RGB image alongside a text question, with the model interacting over 2–6 sequential steps (3.57 on average). In the Planning task, the model receives the original question, the accumulated CoQ history (prior sub-questions paired with their ground-truth answers), and a set of candidate sub-questions (one correct, several distractors), and must select the next informative question. In the Following task, the model receives the image, the ground-truth question chain up to step t, and multiple-choice answer options, and must select the correct answer. Performance is scored as per-step intermediate accuracy (fraction of correctly selected options across all steps).

## Method

V-REX introduces the Chain-of-Questions (CoQ) formulation, which models multi-step exploratory reasoning as a sequence of interconnected sub-question/answer pairs and reduces the otherwise infinite exploration space to a finite multiple-choice setting at each step. The key design decision is to disentangle reasoning into two independently measurable dimensions: Planning (selecting the next helpful sub-question while being given ground-truth answers to prior questions, isolating strategic direction-setting) and Following (answering ground-truth sub-questions with distractor options, isolating visual grounding and knowledge retrieval). The benchmark comprises 702 samples and 2,504 questions spanning 4 reasoning categories (Deduction, Guessing, Navigation, Retrieval) and 15 real-world scenarios. Planning distractors are generated via a two-stage LLM pipeline combining step-level (locally plausible but misleading) and chain-level (globally self-consistent but deviating) strategies, while Following distractors are manually curated. Ground-truth QA chains are constructed by PhD-level annotators with two rounds of cross-verification to ensure helpfulness and correct ordering of intermediate steps.

## Results

On V-REX (702 samples, 2,504 questions across 4 reasoning categories), GPT-5 achieves the highest average Following accuracy (84.6%) while O3 leads in Planning (86.1%). Among open-source models, InternVL3-38B reaches 81.6% Planning and 73.5% Following, and InternVL2.5-38B reaches 78.8% Planning and 76.5% Following. Performance scales consistently with model size, with the smallest model (LLaVA-OV-1B) scoring only 35.1% Planning and 50.8% Following. Notably, variance in Following accuracy among same-sized models is substantially smaller than in Planning, indicating that strategic question selection remains the harder capability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-5 and O3 dominate both dimensions: O3 achieves the highest Planning average (86.1%) while GPT-5 leads Following (84.6%), with O1 close behind in Planning (85.3%) but trailing in Following (79.5%). Among open-source models, InternVL3-38B (81.6% Planning) and InternVL2.5-38B (76.5% Following) are the strongest, narrowing the gap to proprietary models to roughly 3–5 points in Planning and 8–11 points in Following. Smaller open-source models (≤4B) lag substantially, with Planning scores between 35–63% and Following between 51–69%, confirming a clear scaling trend. Within the 7B–10B range, Qwen3-VL-8B-IT achieves the best Planning (71.6%) but its Following (67.3%) is lower than Qwen2.5-VL-7B (71.1%), illustrating the Planning–Following imbalance in smaller models. Gemini 2.0/2.5 Flash trail GPT-5 and O3 by 4–8 points in Planning and 7–10 points in Following, while GPT-4o sits in the middle, roughly matching the best 38B open-source models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of visual reasoning benchmarks (VisuLogic, ZeroBench, VisualPuzzles, CaughtCheating) that evaluate end-to-end answer accuracy on well-defined, single-round questions. It also moves beyond process-level evaluation approaches that rely on LLM-as-a-Judge or Monte-Carlo estimation (Chen et al., LlamaV-o1) by reformulating intermediate-step assessment as a finite multiple-choice problem. The Chain-of-Questions formulation is explicitly distinguished from Chain-of-Thought prompting: rather than improving model performance, CoQ serves as a structured evaluation scaffold that disentangles Planning (selecting informative sub-questions) from Following (answering ground-truth sub-questions), a decomposition not present in prior visual reasoning benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

V-REX provides the first fine-grained diagnostic for the exploratory reasoning gap in VLMs, revealing that Planning is the primary bottleneck (higher variance, lower relative performance) while Following is more stable across model families. The finding that smaller models are disproportionately weak at Planning relative to Following, and that models recover better from bad plans than from wrong answers, offers actionable direction for future training and architecture work. Open questions include whether the finite MCQ space adequately captures the open-ended nature of real exploratory reasoning, whether the 702-sample scale is sufficient for robust conclusions, and whether the CoQ structure generalizes to domains requiring truly novel sub-goal generation rather than selection from curated options.
