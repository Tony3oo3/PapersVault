## Problem

Existing training-free token pruning methods for MLLMs rely on single-layer signals such as attention scores or feature magnitudes, which exhibit positional bias and fail to capture the structured cross-layer transformation of visual representations from high-frequency spatial details to low-frequency semantic abstractions. This paper asks whether modeling how token representations evolve across Transformer layers in the frequency domain provides a more reliable and bias-free criterion for identifying semantically critical visual tokens.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260473/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes visual tokens (N×d) produced by a vision encoder and projected into LLM token space, alongside text embeddings, and operates at the first LLM decoder layer boundary (between input embeddings and the first layer output). Visual tokens are reshaped into 2D spatial feature maps (H×W×d) for per-frame 2D FFT; for video, this is applied independently per frame. The output is a reduced visual token sequence of K tokens (K≪N) that replaces the full sequence for all subsequent decoder layers, ultimately yielding a text answer with reduced FLOPs, KV cache, and latency.

## Method

CLSE quantifies token importance by measuring how each token's band-limited spectral structure redistributes across adjacent LLM layers. Concretely, visual tokens at layers ℓ and ℓ+1 are reshaped into 2D spatial maps, transformed via 2D FFT, masked with a Gaussian high-pass filter (cutoff ratio r=0.1) to suppress low-frequency global patterns, and inverse-transformed to yield per-token high-frequency residual energy maps. The evolution intensity is computed as a normalized, clipped L1 difference between adjacent-layer residual maps, with a global-mean regularizer to suppress minor fluctuations in inactive regions. Top-K tokens by evolution intensity are retained while the rest are pruned before layer 2, making the method training-free and plug-and-play. A theoretical bound shows that selecting top-K by CLSE minimizes the upper bound on pruning-induced perturbation of the subsequent decoder, and an optional token-merging variant (CLSE-M) recalls pruned tokens via density-peak clustering for video settings.

## Results

On LLaVA-1.5-7B, CLSE retains 99.4%, 98.1%, and 94.8% of vanilla performance at 192, 128, and 64 visual tokens respectively, consistently outperforming all seven baselines across nine image benchmarks. Under the most aggressive 88.9% token reduction (64 tokens), CLSE achieves 94.8% average while the next-best method (FiCoCo-V) reaches only 89.8%, a 5-point gap. On Video-LLaVA-7B with >90% token reduction (2048→194), CLSE-M matches the vanilla model at 38.7% average accuracy (GPT-4o-mini evaluation), surpassing SparseVLM (38.1%) and FastV (26.1%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLSE outperforms all seven baselines at every token budget on LLaVA-1.5-7B. Against the strongest hard-pruning baseline PDrop (CVPR'25), CLSE leads by 2.5 points at 192 tokens (99.4% vs. 96.9%) and by 17.8 points at 64 tokens (94.8% vs. 77.0%), where PDrop's progressive schedule collapses. Against the strongest token-merging baseline FiCoCo-V (AAAI'26), CLSE gains 3.2 points at 192 tokens and 5.0 points at 64 tokens, indicating that spectral evolution is a more reliable importance signal than FiCoCo's filter-correlate-compress pipeline. Attention-based FastV (ECCV'24) trails CLSE by 7.3 points at 192 tokens and 16.8 points at 64 tokens, confirming the positional-bias limitation of single-layer attention scoring. On video (Video-LLaVA-7B, 2048→194 tokens), CLSE-M matches the vanilla model at 38.7% average accuracy, edging out SparseVLM (38.1%) by 0.6 points while both use token recycling, and surpassing FastV (26.1%) by 12.6 points. The advantage of CLSE widens monotonically with compression ratio, suggesting that cross-layer spectral dynamics become increasingly discriminative when the token budget is tight.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLSE departs from the dominant paradigm of single-layer importance signals (attention scores in FastV, feature magnitudes, similarity in DART) by measuring how visual token representations evolve across Transformer depth in the frequency domain. It draws on the frequency-domain Transformer literature (FNet, VTC-LFC, Fourier-VLM) but repurposes FFT not for within-layer compression or token mixing, but as a cross-layer evolution metric. The approach is also orthogonal to token-merging methods (ToMe, PruMerge, SparseVLM) and can be combined with them, as demonstrated in the CLSE-M variant for video. A concurrent work, V²Drop, explores cross-layer representational dynamics but in the spatial domain, whereas CLSE operates on band-limited spectral residuals, capturing the detail-to-semantics transition more selectively.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that attention-based token importance is systematically biased by positional encoding, and that cross-layer spectral evolution provides a more stable and position-agnostic signal, challenges a widely adopted assumption in MLLM acceleration. The O(N log N) FFT-based scoring also avoids the quadratic latency penalty of materializing dense attention matrices, offering a hardware-efficient path for token reduction at high resolutions. Open questions include whether the Gaussian high-pass cutoff and evolution window (currently layer 0→1) require per-model tuning, whether the approach extends to audio or 3D modalities, and whether the theoretical perturbation bound (Eq. 11) can be tightened to provide formal guarantees under non-Lipschitz decoders.
