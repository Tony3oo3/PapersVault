## Problem

Polarization-based reflection removal requires capturing multiple discrete polarization angles to solve a linear system, but conventional frame-based cameras (e.g., PolarCam at 24 FPS) cannot densely sample the continuous polarization states needed for high-speed dynamic scenes. While spike cameras sense at >20,000 Hz, their 1-bit asynchronous output combined with the light attenuation from a polarizer produces extremely low SNR, making direct application of continuous polarization physics numerically unstable for layer separation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261455/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw spike streams from a spike camera (20,000 Hz) aggregated over a 0.65 ms window into pseudo-intensity frames, paired with the instantaneous polarizer angle from a 30 CPS rotating filter; adjacent reconstruction centers are sampled at a 31.5° angular stride, yielding an output cadence of (30×360)/31.5 ≈ 340 FPS. The output is a temporally aligned pair of transmission (reflection-free) and reflection video layers at this high frame rate, rather than a single static image.

## Method

The framework is two-stage. The baseline removal stage exploits a 'relative zero-motion observation' (object motion is negligible within the short window while polarization modulation remains observable) to build a (2K+1)×2 linear system per pixel, solved by a differentiable Reliability-Weighted Physics Solver (RWPS) that applies learned per-frame confidence weights and iterative reweighted least squares to suppress severe shot noise, with a Reflection-Invariant Registration module handling micro-motions. The refinement stage uses a dual-stream CNN with an angle self-attention encoder (encoding angles as [cos 2φ, sin 2φ] for π-periodicity) that predicts high-frequency residuals conditioned on back-projection residuals and a physics-solver confidence map, adding learned details to the analytic base rather than synthesizing images from scratch. Training is supervised with a three-phase curriculum (teacher-forced → transition → autonomous) and a composite loss enforcing layer fidelity, gradient sharpness, physical re-rendering consistency, and inter-layer gradient exclusion.

## Results

On the synthetic dataset, the proposed method achieves PSNR 27.06 dB, SSIM 0.8638, and LPIPS 0.0894, substantially outperforming all three baselines by large margins. The closest baseline, Lyu et al. [20], reaches only 16.53 dB PSNR and 0.2658 LPIPS, a gap of over 10 dB in PSNR. On real-scene data, the method qualitatively removes near-global reflections and restores high-frequency transmission textures that baselines fail to recover under the low-SNR spike conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines: RDNet [38] (single-image, reversible architecture), PolarFree [34] (polarization + diffusion prior), and Lyu et al. [20] (unpolarized-polarized pair with physics refinement). The proposed method wins decisively on all three metrics against every baseline, with PSNR gaps of 9.6–10.7 dB. RDNet, being purely image-based without physical cues, struggles most with strong specular highlights and complex reflections. PolarFree and Lyu et al. both exploit polarization but rely on discrete frame acquisition (4 angles), leaving ghosting artifacts and residual noise under the low-SNR spike conditions. The authors note the comparison is an 'adapted reference' since baselines were designed for standard frame-based images; spike frames are converted to their expected input formats with gamma correction and channel replication. On real-scene data, all baselines fail to effectively remove reflections under the severe light attenuation from the polarizer, while the proposed method recovers clear transmissions even when reflections are nearly global.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the physically-based polarization reflection separation framework of Kong et al. [11], which constructs a linear system from a small set of discrete polarizer angles, to a continuous-angle regime enabled by a rapidly rotating polarizer coupled with a spike camera. The gradient exclusion loss is borrowed from Zhang et al. [37], and the overall two-stage design (analytic physics solver followed by a learned refinement) follows the pattern established by Lyu et al. [20] and PolarFree [34], but the core departure is the spike-camera modality and the Reliability-Weighted Physics Solver (RWPS) that handles the extreme shot noise inherent to sub-millisecond integration windows. The Reflection-Invariant Registration module and angle self-attention mechanism are new architectural components tailored to the asynchronous, binary nature of spike data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration of 340 FPS reflection-free video reconstruction, opening a path toward real-time, physically grounded layer separation in highly dynamic scenes where frame-based cameras are temporally aliased. The RWPS provides a generalizable template for solving ill-conditioned physics inversions under severe noise via learned per-observation confidence weights, which could transfer to other spike-vision or low-SNR imaging tasks. Key limitations remain: the polarizer's intrinsic attenuation restricts use in low-light settings, the relative zero-motion assumption degrades under large accumulated displacement, and the evaluation relies on a small real-scene set (15 sequences) with comparisons that are explicitly adapted rather than same-modality, leaving open whether the large reported margins hold under fair spike-to-spike baselines.
