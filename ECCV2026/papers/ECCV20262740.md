## Problem

Prior work on demographic bias in text-to-image generation has focused almost exclusively on images depicting humans, leaving the subtler form of bias in non-human objects largely unexplored. When prompts include demographic cues (e.g., 'car for women'), models systematically alter object aesthetics in stereotypical ways, yet no systematic framework exists to measure, compare, and audit these demographic-driven object biases across models and categories.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262740/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes two types of text prompts—base prompts specifying only the object (e.g., 'car, one product only, no people') and demographic-conditioned prompts adding age, gender, or ethnicity cues—as input to five T2I models, producing 20 images per condition (8,000 total across 5 models × 8 objects × 10 conditions). Generated images are then fed to a VLM (GPT-4o at temperature 0) which returns structured JSON classifications of 7 visual attributes per image (4 fixed + 3 object-specific). The final output is three scalar bias scores per model-object-demographic combination: BDS, CDS, and VAC.

## Method

SODA is a four-step auditing framework: (1) controlled prompt generation isolating demographic influence via base vs. demographic-conditioned templates across three dimensions; (2) image generation with five state-of-the-art T2I models; (3) a two-phase VLM-based attribute extraction where Phase 1 discovers 4 distinguishable visual attributes per model-object combination (without demographic labels) and Phase 2 assigns attribute values to all images in structured JSON; (4) statistical measurement via three complementary metrics—BDS (mean JS divergence between base and each demographic group's attribute distributions), CDS (mean JS divergence across all unordered demographic group pairs within a dimension), and VAC (1 minus normalized Shannon entropy of attribute value distributions, capturing within-group concentration). The key design choice is the two-phase attribute discovery, which adapts to model-specific visual patterns while maintaining cross-model comparability through fixed attributes, and the tri-metric structure that separately captures baseline drift, inter-group disparity, and intra-group stereotyping—revealing that debiasing prompts can reduce CDS while paradoxically increasing VAC (the homogenization effect).

## Results

Across 8,000 images from five T2I models, the Base vs. Demographic Divergence Score (BDS) reveals that 'neutral' prompts produce outputs most visually similar to Middle-Aged (avg. BDS = 0.222) and White (0.234) demographics, while Elderly (0.302) and Women (0.295) diverge most. Cross-Demographic Disparity (CDS) reaches 1.0 for car body type by gender (sedans for men vs. hatchbacks for women) and 0.730 for color by gender. Visual Attribute Concentration (VAC) ranges from 0.343 (Imagen) to 0.633 (Qwen), with 26.6% of all model-object-demographic combinations (852 cases) producing fully uniform outputs where all 20 images share the identical attribute value. Under the 'avoiding stereotypical styles' mitigation prompt, CDS drops by 30.9% on average, but VAC increases for GPT (+9.9%) and Qwen (+19.3%), exposing a homogenization effect.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new generative model but instead benchmarks five state-of-the-art T2I models (GPT Image-1, Imagen 4, Flux 2 Pro, Qwen-Image, SDXL) using the SODA auditing framework. On VAC (stereotype concentration), Qwen is the most deterministic (avg. 0.633), followed by GPT (0.547) and Flux (0.511), while Imagen (0.343) and SDXL (0.401) appear more diverse—but this is partly an artifact of prompt-constraint violations (SDXL violates 'no people' in 56.4% of images, Imagen in 22.2%). On BDS, all five models consistently show Middle-Aged and White as the lowest-divergence groups, with Flux 2 Pro showing the strongest White default (0.186) and Qwen/SDXL defaulting toward Men (0.189/0.190). Under the 'avoiding stereotypical styles' mitigation prompt, Flux 2 Pro is the only model that improves both CDS (−34.7%) and VAC (−5.5%) simultaneously, while GPT (CDS −32.8%, VAC +9.9%) and Qwen (CDS −24.6%, VAC +19.3%) exhibit the homogenization effect, collapsing within-group diversity into new rigid defaults.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the established line of T2I bias research (T2IAT, TIBET, DALL-Eval) from human depictions to non-human objects, and departs from OpenBias by using explicit demographic cues rather than neutral prompts to probe bias. SODA's key methodological departure is decomposing attribute shifts relative to a controlled demographic-free baseline using VLM-based automated attribute extraction, enabling direct cross-group comparison that prior frameworks do not support. The three proposed metrics (BDS, CDS, VAC) combine Jensen-Shannon divergence and entropy in a way that separates inter-group disparity from within-group concentration, a distinction absent in prior T2I bias evaluations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The homogenization effect—where debiasing prompts reduce inter-group disparity but collapse within-group diversity into new rigid defaults—challenges the assumption that prompt-level mitigation is sufficient and points toward the need for training-time interventions. The finding that 'neutral' prompts systematically default to middle-aged, White, Men aesthetics across all five models undermines the practical assumption of demographic neutrality in production T2I systems. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to intersectional identities, continuous demographic dimensions, and whether the VAC-CDS trade-off persists under fine-tuning or data-rebalancing strategies. Limitations include categorical (non-intersectional) demographics, reliance on a single primary VLM for attribute extraction, and a limited set of eight object categories.
