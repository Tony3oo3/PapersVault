## Problem

High-resolution artwork outpainting via progressive diffusion suffers from three coupled limitations: the absence of a global planning stage causes structural error accumulation across sequentially generated patches, text-only conditioning cannot enforce precise object placement at user-specified locations, and the inherent sequential dependency of patch generation produces high inference latency that blocks parallel hardware utilization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20261952/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a source artwork image (typically 512×512 or larger), a binary mask designating the region to be generated, optional layout conditions as bounding boxes paired with CLIP-encoded object descriptions, and a global text prompt. Both stages operate at 512×512 latent resolution; the canvas is partitioned into eight overlapping patches (four cardinal + four corner) surrounding the source, and the final output is a high-resolution outpainted image composited in pixel space with the original source region pasted back unmodified. The default evaluation uses a masking ratio ρ=0.333, yielding roughly 225% total pixels relative to the source.

## Method

The framework decouples global structural planning from local high-resolution synthesis in a two-stage diffusion pipeline. Stage 1 generates a low-resolution global blueprint by injecting layout conditions (Fourier-mapped bounding boxes + CLIP text embeddings) into a frozen SD Inpainting U-Net via a Gated Fuser adapted from InstanceDiffusion, while caching timestep-wise guidance features into a feature bank. Stage 2 synthesizes all eight high-resolution patches in parallel by (a) initializing each patch's noise from the blueprint latent through forward diffusion, exploiting the low-frequency preservation property so that global layout survives heavy noise corruption, (b) injecting the Stage 1 guidance features and a patch-specific position token (encoding the patch's canvas coordinates) into the Stage 2 U-Net via a second Gated Fuser, and (c) blending overlapping regions via latent-space averaging at every denoising step. Only the Gated Fuser and position network parameters are trained (U-Net frozen), and the blueprint-based noise initialization is applied exclusively at inference, enabling multi-GPU parallel denoising with a 2.4× speedup over sequential baselines.

## Results

On the IconArt evaluation set (ρ=0.333, ~225% total pixels), the proposed method achieves FID 9.31, pFID₅₁₂ 6.75, and CLIP-A 6.79, outperforming the strongest baseline ProOut (FID 10.30, pFID₅₁₂ 7.25, CLIP-A 6.86). For layout accuracy, the method reaches AP 0.434 and IoU 0.638, substantially exceeding all text-only baselines (best baseline: ProOut at AP 0.360, IoU 0.562). With 8-GPU parallel inference, generation time drops to 7.50 s per image, a 2.4× speedup over ProOut's 18.31 s; on a single GPU the time is 15.73 s, still faster than ProOut but slower than SD Inpainting (13.43 s) and PowerPaint (13.99 s) due to the additional global planning stage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method is compared against four baselines: PQDiff, SD Inpainting, PowerPaint, and ProOut. Against ProOut (the strongest progressive outpainting baseline), the proposed method wins on FID (9.31 vs. 10.30), pFID₅₁₂ (6.75 vs. 7.25), layout AP (0.434 vs. 0.360), layout IoU (0.638 vs. 0.562), and inference time (7.50 s vs. 18.31 s with 8 GPUs), while ProOut retains a slight edge on CLIP-S (0.2052 vs. 0.2033) and CLIP-A (6.86 vs. 6.79). Against SD Inpainting and PowerPaint, the method achieves lower FID and pFID scores and substantially better layout accuracy, but is slower on a single GPU (15.73 s vs. 13.43 s / 13.99 s) due to the two-stage pipeline. PQDiff lags far behind on all image-quality metrics (FID 25.3–30.9) and is not competitive. The Ours* variant (guidance features extracted after cross-attention) further narrows the CLIP-S and CLIP-A gap to ProOut while maintaining the FID and layout-accuracy advantages.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper composes several established diffusion-model components—InstanceDiffusion's Layout Adapter and Gated Fuser for bounding-box conditioning, AlignNoise's attention-guided noise optimization, MultiDiffusion's latent-space overlap blending, and FreeInit's low-frequency preservation property—into a novel two-stage blueprint-guided architecture for high-resolution artwork outpainting. Its key conceptual departure is repurposing the reference-guided synthesis mechanism (typically used for style or identity transfer) as a structural blueprint propagation channel from a global planning stage to parallel local patch synthesis, and exploiting the low-frequency preservation property (originally demonstrated in video diffusion) to initialize image patches from a noisy blueprint rather than pure Gaussian noise. Unlike ProOut, which performs sequential progressive expansion, this work decouples global structural planning from high-resolution detail synthesis, eliminating the sequential dependency that causes error accumulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses three practical bottlenecks in production artwork outpainting (error accumulation, lack of spatial control, and sequential latency) in a single system, and the 2.4× multi-GPU speedup makes high-resolution expansion viable for immersive display applications. Layout-controllable outpainting is a genuinely new capability—no prior diffusion-based outpainting method supports explicit bounding-box placement in extended regions—opening the door to user-directed scene composition for cultural heritage and VR/AR content. Limitations include the fixed 512×512 patch resolution per stage (requiring a 3×3 grid for the default canvas), the need for multi-GPU hardware to realize the full speedup, and the fact that the low-resolution blueprint may not capture very fine structural details that Stage 2 must then hallucinate. Open questions include whether the blueprint-guided parallel paradigm extends to video outpainting or to even larger canvas ratios beyond 600%, and whether the two-stage training can be unified into a single end-to-end objective.
