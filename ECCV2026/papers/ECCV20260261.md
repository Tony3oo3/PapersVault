## Problem

Despite advances in dissecting self-attention mechanics in SSL ViTs, insights specific to contrastive learning (CL) vs. masked image modeling (MIM) are still conflated in the broader community, and positional effect—absolute positional information that appears consistently across images—lacks an established intuitive visualization distinct from the more extensively studied locality bias. This hinders straightforward, cross-model comparison of how different pretraining objectives shape ViT representations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20260261/cropped_page3_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The protocol takes a frozen pretrained ViT backbone and a batch of RGB images, extracting patch-level embeddings (keys, queries, values, or tokens) from any selected layer. It produces per-image pixel-wise segmentation masks (bilinearly upsampled from patch resolution), a dataset-wide aggregated spatial map showing average class probabilities at each pixel location, a normalized mutual information (NMI) score quantifying positional effect, and mIoU under Hungarian matching for segmentation quality. All outputs are also computed for ground-truth labels as a spatial-bias reference.

## Method

The core mechanism is across-image k-means clustering applied to frozen ViT embeddings: patch-level features from a batch of images are assigned to a shared codebook of K centroids via online k-means under cosine similarity, with centroids updated iteratively across batches to capture cross-image structure. Each pixel is then labeled by nearest-centroid lookup after bilinear upsampling, and Hungarian matching globally maps predicted clusters to ground-truth classes. The protocol's key design choice is displaying segmentation results from multiple images side by side alongside GT labels, which makes consistent spatial patterns (positional effect) visually apparent as the same image regions cluster identically regardless of semantic content. This is complemented by an aggregated full-dataset map and class-wise mutual information to quantify positional effect, explicitly separating it from locality bias (which manifests as relative, position-independent attention to nearby patches). The protocol is applied independently to each embedding type (K, Q, V, tokens) at each layer, enabling systematic comparison across training paradigms, layers, and model scales.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative findings are correlation-based: positional effect (measured via normalized mutual information) correlates negatively with unsupervised segmentation mIoU (ρ = −0.59, p < .001) and positively with locality bias in attention (ρ = 0.70, p < .001). MIM-based models (MAE, DINOv2, DINOv3, iBOT) exhibit substantially stronger positional effect in key and query embeddings than CL-based models (DINO, Mugs), with MIM keys/queries forming a distinct low-mIoU subset in the cross-model comparison (Fig. 3). DINOv3-Large shows disproportionately elevated class-wise MI for semantically spurious classes (floor, ceiling, solid) compared to DINOv3-Base, linking strong positional effect to degraded scaling behavior on dense prediction tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 10 models (8 SSL + 2 supervised baselines) using a unified k-means clustering probing protocol on COCO-Stuff. CL models (DINO, Mugs, MoCo v3) show substantially less positional effect in keys/queries than MIM models (MAE, DINOv2, DINOv3, iBOT), with DINO exhibiting the cleanest semantic structure. DINOv2+reg largely mitigates the block-structured artifacts seen in DINOv2 tokens, though at the cost of noisier segmentations. DINOv3-Large underperforms DINOv3-Base on segmentation mIoU, with the gap attributed to strong positional effect in upper/lower image regions forming spurious ceiling/floor clusters. iBOT-Large improves fine-grained part-level clustering over iBOT-Base (e.g., consistent head/limb grouping across species) but retains positional effects. Token embeddings consistently outperform key, query, and value embeddings on segmentation mIoU across nearly all models, contrasting with prior reports favoring keys for keypoint matching. The supervised ViT and CLIP baselines show progressive loss of fine-grained semantic detail in deeper layers, with CLIP exhibiting block artifacts attributed to its positional embedding design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the standard k-means clustering-based unsupervised segmentation protocol (used in CutLER, TokenCut, and related work) not for segmentation performance but as a mechanistic lens into ViT representation structure. It builds on the attention-map and attention-matrix visualizations that have conveyed locality bias in MIM vs. CL models (Bolya et al., Park et al.) and on the formal definition of positional effect (Doshi et al.), but departs from both by establishing cross-image correspondences that make positional effect visually distinguishable from locality bias. The protocol also connects to the broader SSL model-understanding literature on scaling behavior and layer-wise representation quality, providing a unified visualization framework where prior analyses were fragmented across different visualization techniques.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The protocol offers the broader community a simple, interpretable tool to audit ViT representations without the confounds of fine-tuning, potentially becoming a standard diagnostic alongside attention visualizations. The finding that DINOv3-Large's inverse scaling is driven by strong positional effects in tokens—despite RoPE being designed to make positional information relative—raises an open question about whether RoPE actually mitigates absolute positional encoding in practice and whether DINOv3's training paradigm (including distillation) needs reassessment. A key limitation is that the positional-effect-to-segmentation-performance link is correlational (ρ = −0.59) with acknowledged confounders, so the causal claim for DINOv3's underperformance remains a hypothesis rather than a proven mechanism.
