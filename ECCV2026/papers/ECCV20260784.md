## Problem

Existing automatic video compilation methods are confined to predefined tasks (B-roll insertion, trailer generation, highlight detection) and rely on a 'retrieve-and-rank' paradigm that, when applied to complex cinematic content, suffers from contextual collapse (failing to grasp narrative context beyond isolated shots) and temporal fragmentation (producing incoherent clip sequences). The community also lacks a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating instruction-driven cinematic compilation under diverse, in-the-wild user instructions.

## Input / Output

The system takes a free-form text instruction (specifying source selection, target content, temporal requirements, and editing operations such as BGM, text overlays, transitions, or cover images) along with one or more source cinematic videos (films or TV series spanning 1930–2020) and produces a single compiled video with shots selected, reordered, and assembled into a coherent narrative. The system is entirely training-free, using Gemini-2.5-pro as the underlying foundation model, and the hierarchical narrative memory built from a source video is reusable across multiple instructions (costing ~$14 to build once, then ~$0.62 per instruction).

## Method

CineAgents reformulates cinematic compilation as a 'design-and-compose' paradigm orchestrated by a manager agent coordinating specialist agents. In the 'design' phase, a script agent performs reverse-engineering: it detects and traces characters via InsightFace/SOLIDER anchor-and-propagate matching, binds dialogues to speakers using WeSpeaker voiceprints and lip-activity detection, and constructs a four-level hierarchical narrative memory (shot → event → story → character) with a 10-shot context buffer for context-aware summarization. A director agent then translates the user instruction into a story blueprint, which an orchestrator agent iteratively validates against the narrative memory in a top-down grounding loop (Eq. 6), refining the blueprint until every narrative beat is grounded in specific shot IDs. In the 'compose' phase, an editor agent executes the final compiled script by assembling shots from the source videos and applying external tools (background music, text overlays, cover generation, transition effects) to produce the output video.

## Results

On CineBench, CineAgents achieves the best score across all 11 evaluation metrics. Headline numbers include an F1-score of 64.13 (vs. 55.73 for the next-best MetaGPT), a Temporal Correctness Score of 52.09% (vs. 46.21% for MetaGPT), an Execution Success Rate of 92.76%, an Adversarial Rejection Rate of 87.23% (vs. 63.31% for VideoAgent), and a VLM-based Compiled Quality score of 9.01/10. In user studies across four criteria (TPA, SAE, NCE, OQE), CineAgents also receives the highest human preference scores on both live-action and animated content.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CineAgents outperforms all five baselines on every reported metric. Against the strongest baseline MetaGPT, CineAgents gains +8.4 F1 (64.13 vs. 55.73), +5.9 pp TCS (52.09% vs. 46.21%), +6.8 pp ESR (92.76% vs. 85.93%), and +34.9 pp ARR (87.23% vs. 52.31%). The single VLLM baselines (Claude and Gemini) are substantially weaker, with Claude achieving only 20.29 F1 and 18.61% TCS, indicating that a retrieve-and-rank prompting strategy cannot handle cinematic narrative complexity. LAVE, designed for casual video editing, shows moderate recall (63.21) but poor precision (38.22) and low TCS (35.37%), confirming its inapplicability to cinematic compilation. VideoAgent, an all-in-one editing framework, has the lowest F1 (18.84) and CQ (7.34) but a relatively high ARR (63.31%), suggesting it can refuse impossible requests but fails at actual compilation quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of automated video compilation and multi-agent cinematic generation, but does not directly extend any single prior method. It departs from the 'retrieve-and-rank' formulation dominant in casual video compilation (CMVE, QuickCut, LAVE) by arguing that this paradigm fundamentally breaks down for cinematic content due to contextual collapse and temporal fragmentation. It also distinguishes itself from multi-agent video *generation* systems (FilmAgent, MovieAgent, Captain Cinema) by operating on existing footage rather than synthesizing new pixels, and from predefined cinematic tasks (B-roll insertion, trailer generation, highlight detection) by supporting open-ended, in-the-wild user instructions. The 'design-and-compose' paradigm and hierarchical narrative memory are novel conceptual contributions that repurpose standard components (InsightFace, WeSpeaker, AutoShot, BaSSL) into a structured reverse-engineering pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CineBench fills a genuine gap as the first benchmark for instruction-driven cinematic compilation, with professional-editor ground truths and an adversarial subset for hallucination evaluation—resources the community previously lacked. The 'design-and-compose' reframing offers a useful conceptual lens for why local-similarity retrieval fails on narrative-rich content, potentially guiding future compilation architectures. However, the system is training-free and tightly coupled to Gemini-2.5-pro, limiting reproducibility and generalizability of the method itself; the authors acknowledge that off-the-shelf character detection cascades errors in complex scenes, and the 25-participant user study on 50 samples, while reasonable, leaves room for broader validation. Open questions include whether the hierarchical memory approach scales to multi-hour series and whether the iterative planning loop can be made more efficient than its ~6-minute per-instruction cost.
