## Problem

Existing agentic reasoning benchmarks largely underevaluate proactive literature search, multi-evidence integration across papers, and sustained evidence reuse over time. They also lack process-level metrics that diagnose whether agents correctly ground, retrieve, and reuse evidence, making it impossible to distinguish lucky guesses from well-supported answers in multi-turn research workflows.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262502/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each episode begins with a research task description that deliberately omits target paper titles or identifiers, optionally accompanied by a seed cue such as an image snippet or short bibliographic hint. The agent produces a structured objective answer at each turn along with a full tool-use interaction trace; critically, the final turn of hard episodes disables all external tools, forcing the agent to answer exclusively from its accumulated memory pool of previously retrieved figures, tables, and text spans.

## Method

EpiBench constructs 102 episodic multi-turn tasks (37 easy, 65 hard) from a 485-paper corpus, where each episode chains questions that require proactive citation-guided search, cross-paper figure/table alignment, and iterative evidence accumulation. The agent framework builds on SmolAgents with a ReAct-like loop and four tools (web search, PDF extractor, PDF text RAG, and content extractor for figures/tables), maintaining a chronological memory store of all retrieved content. The key evaluation innovation is a budgeted, process-level protocol: evidence units are annotated at paper-and-figure/table granularity for provenance-based scoring (Evidence Correctness), a minimal viable tool-call checklist defines the Minimality Gap, and the final turn's tool-disable constraint isolates memory-based reuse from repeated re-browsing. Failures are further attributed to five categories (retrieval, perception, reasoning, memory-reading, other) via trace analysis, enabling fine-grained diagnosis beyond aggregate accuracy.

## Results

On EpiBench, GPT-5.2 achieves the best overall episode success rate (ESR) of 39.60%, with 59.41% accuracy on the final multi-evidence fusion turn and 87.63% on non-final turns. On the Hard split, even the strongest model (GPT-5.2) reaches only 29.23% ESR, while the best open-source model (Kimi-K2.5) achieves 26.15%. A human baseline of two master's-level candidates attains 85.11% ESR, revealing a large gap between current agents and expert research workflows. The persistent gap between Acc_pre (often >75%) and Acc_final (typically 40–60%) across all models indicates that multi-evidence fusion and memory-based reuse, rather than initial retrieval, constitute the primary bottleneck.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-5.2 leads all models on average ESR (39.60%) and both Acc_final (59.41%) and Acc_pre (87.63%), outperforming the second-best closed-source model GPT-5-Mini (24.51% ESR) by 15.1 points and the best open-source model Kimi-K2.5 (35.35% ESR) by 4.3 points. Among open-source models, Kimi-K2.5 dominates with 35.35% ESR and 81.91% Acc_pre, substantially ahead of Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (18.63% ESR) and Qwen3-VL-235B-Thinking (13.73% ESR). On the Hard split specifically, GPT-5.2 (29.23%) edges out Kimi-K2.5 (26.15%) and GPT-5-Mini (21.54%), while Gemini-2.5-Pro (7.69%) and Grok-4.1 (6.35%) trail significantly. A notable pattern across all models is the sharp drop from Acc_pre to Acc_final (e.g., GPT-5.2: 87.63% → 59.41%; Kimi-K2.5: 81.91% → 57.58%), confirming that the final multi-evidence fusion turn under memory-only constraints is the critical failure point. The human baseline (85.11% ESR, 95.74% Acc_final) exceeds the best model by over 45 points in ESR, underscoring that current agents remain far from reliable research-workflow completion.

## Relation to Prior Work

EpiBench builds on the ReAct-style tool-augmented agent paradigm (Yao et al., 2023) and the SmolAgents framework (Roucher et al., 2025) to instantiate episodic research workflows, but its core contribution is a benchmark formulation that unifies five capabilities—cross-paper integration, multimodal evidence fusion, proactive citation-guided search, evidence memory, and multi-turn dialogue—that no prior benchmark has simultaneously assessed. It departs from paper-centric VQA benchmarks (CharXiv, SPIQA, LiveXiv, SIN-Bench, PaperArena) by removing explicit target identifiers and requiring agents to discover papers through indirect cues, and from multi-turn dialogue benchmarks (MMDU, Multiverse, MMCR) by grounding turns in concrete scientific figures and tables rather than general visual content. The process-level evaluation protocol (evidence correctness, minimality gap, error taxonomy) extends beyond the accuracy-only scoring used in virtually all prior agentic benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even the strongest model (GPT-5.2) reaches only 29.23% episode success on the hard split, with a stark gap between pre-turn accuracy (86.5%) and final-turn accuracy (49.2%), pinpoints multi-evidence fusion and memory-based reuse—not retrieval—as the primary bottleneck for research agents. The memory-only ablation and closed-book ablation together confirm that the benchmark cannot be gamed by parametric knowledge or repeated re-browsing, making it a meaningful stress test for future agent architectures. Open questions include whether scaling model size or introducing explicit evidence-indexing mechanisms can close the memory-reuse gap, and whether the 102-episode scale is sufficient to support reliable cross-model comparisons as the field matures.
