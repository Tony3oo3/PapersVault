## Problem

Sparse autoencoders applied to vision models lack a reliable way to verify that learned latents actually encode human-meaningful concepts, as existing evaluation relies on structural proxies (sparsity, reconstruction) or qualitative inspection rather than measuring semantic correspondence. Functional evaluation via controlled interventions is particularly difficult in vision because attribute changes in natural images rarely occur in isolation, leaving a gap between statistical alignment and causal validation of SAE features.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ECCV20262775/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes image embeddings from pretrained vision encoders (CLIP ViT-L-14 or DINOv2 ViT-S-14), binary attribute annotation vectors (45 curated attributes from CUB-200-2011 or object labels from MS-COCO), and paired synthetic images generated by Flux2 that differ in exactly one attribute. It produces a coalition mapping (which subset of SAE latents corresponds to each attribute), a ΔMATCHScore reflecting improvement over a random-weight baseline, and a TAPAScore that quantifies directional selectivity of matched latents under perturbation. Notably, all matching operates on binarized activations (threshold at zero), deliberately discarding magnitude to penalize polysemantic latents that fire indiscriminately.

## Method

The framework has three components. First, Fully-Binary Matching Pursuit (FBMP) extends classical Matching Pursuit to the fully-binary domain: it greedily selects a small coalition of latents per attribute using F_β similarity for atom selection, logical OR for reconstruction, and AND/NOT for residual updates, terminating when no further latent improves F_1. This supports many-to-one mappings that handle feature splitting, where a single concept is fragmented across multiple specialized latents. Second, synCUB and synCOCO are synthetic intervention benchmarks where Flux2 generates paired images differing in exactly one attribute (or one removed object), enabling controlled single-factor perturbations. Third, TAPAScore computes the signed difference between the maximum binarized activation of matched latents before and after perturbation, averaged over all pairs, testing whether matched concepts respond selectively and in the expected direction. The key insight is that statistical matching and causal alignment can diverge—particularly at high overcompleteness—making TAPAScore a necessary complement to matching scores.

## Results

In the sanity check (Fig. 4), only the proposed MATCHScore (FBMP F₁ k=3 and F₁ k=1) and TAPAScore reliably distinguish trained SAEs from untrained and random baselines, dropping sharply under both failure conditions on both CUB and COCO. TAPAScore shows the strongest separation, falling to near zero for untrained and random conditions. Across dictionary sizes (Figs. 5–6), FBMP F₀.₅ (k=3) consistently achieves the highest matching scores, and the best causal alignment (TAPAScore) is found at moderate dictionary sizes: BatchTopK at 512 and TopK at 1024 on COCO, and both at 256 on CUB. Increasing overcompleteness beyond these sizes degrades TAPAScore while matching scores continue to rise, revealing a dissociation between statistical and causal alignment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three existing SAE evaluation metrics—FMS [18], MS [36], and CKNNA [55]—against the proposed MATCHScore (with FBMP) and TAPAScore under a sanity check protocol (trained vs. untrained vs. random SAEs). FMS [18] fails the sanity check on COCO, showing little sensitivity to whether the SAE is trained or random, and only partially distinguishes conditions on CUB. MS [36] shows limited sensitivity across both datasets, failing to reliably separate trained from untrained or random representations. CKNNA [55] behaves inconsistently: it drops for random activations but paradoxically inflates for the untrained SAE, indicating it rewards untrained representations over trained ones. In contrast, both MATCHScore variants and TAPAScore clearly and consistently drop under untrained and random conditions on both CUB and COCO, making them the only evaluated metrics that pass the sanity check. The paper further shows that FBMP matching outperforms one-to-one F₁ matching across all four SAE variants (BatchTopK, Matryoshka, TopK, JumpReLU) on both datasets, and that TAPAScore provides a necessary causal complement that statistical matching alone cannot supply, particularly at large dictionary sizes where matching improves but perturbation alignment degrades.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sparse autoencoder interpretability evaluation literature from NLP to vision by introducing a human-grounded, intervention-based evaluation protocol. Methodologically, FBMP adapts the classical Matching Pursuit greedy reconstruction framework (Mallat 1993) and its binary variants (Wen 2021) to a fully-binary domain where both target annotations and latent activations are binary, replacing inner products with Fβ scores and arithmetic with logical operations. The synthetic dataset construction follows the SUB benchmark's (Bader 2025) approach of generating attribute-substituted CUB images, but repurposes it for paired single-attribute interventions rather than cross-instance substitution. The paper departs from prior vision SAE evaluation, which relies on proxy metrics (FMS, MS, CKNNA) or qualitative inspection, by grounding evaluation in explicit human-annotated concepts and causal perturbation tests.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that widely-used SAE evaluation metrics (FMS, MS, CKNNA) fail basic sanity checks—being unable to distinguish trained from untrained SAEs, with CKNNA even inflating for untrained models—challenges the current evaluation practice in the field and motivates adoption of the proposed FBMP + TAPAScore protocol. The dissociation between statistical matching and causal perturbation alignment at high overcompleteness provides concrete, actionable guidance: moderate dictionary sizes (256–1024) yield the most interpretable SAEs, a finding that directly informs practitioners choosing SAE configurations. A key limitation is the dependence on annotation quality and granularity, since TAPAScore inherits errors from the matching layer; reducing this dependence on manual curation is an open direction. The synthetic benchmarks (synCUB, synCOCO) are the first intervention-style evaluation resources for SAEs on naturalistic images, filling a gap left by prior work restricted to simple collaged scenes.
