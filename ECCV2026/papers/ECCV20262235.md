## Problem

Differentiable vector graphics rasterization suffers from a 'gradient seesaw': making the forward pass more geometrically exact (e.g., exact Bezier closest-point solvers, adaptive subdivision) systematically degrades the quality of induced gradients, while overly coarse approximations become the binding source of error. As scene complexity grows, existing methods require increasingly elaborate scaffolding—opacity heuristics, auxiliary regularization losses, and ad-hoc annealing schedules—to keep optimization from collapsing, suggesting a fundamental tension between forward fidelity and gradient conditioning that has not been explicitly addressed.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB raster image at its native resolution (DIV2K images at ~2040×1344, Kodak at 768×512) and produces a fixed set of N layered vector primitives, each encoded as connected cubic Bezier segments (10 control points per primitive: 2 segments for closed fills, 3 for open strokes) plus learnable color, stroke half-width, and opacity. The front-to-back layer order is fixed throughout optimization. At evaluation, the learned parameters can be re-rendered at arbitrarily higher resolutions (demonstrated up to 24K) or with an exact Bezier renderer, decoupling training-time approximation from output fidelity.

## Method

CubicSplat introduces Error-bounded Forward Relaxation: each Bezier curve is replaced by a uniform S-segment polyline surrogate so that all distance queries reduce to closed-form point-to-segment computations, eliminating iterative root-finding from the backward pass and yielding a static, well-conditioned computation graph. A smooth coverage kernel maps a topology-dependent signed residual (unsigned distance for open strokes, winding-number-signed distance for closed fills) to per-pixel opacity, and standard front-to-back alpha compositing assembles the final image. The compositing pass naturally exposes per-primitive transmittance-weighted contribution scores, which drive Gini-coefficient-based pruning of degenerate or fully occluded primitives without any explicit geometric regularization. The key empirical finding is that S acts as a relaxation knob for oracle conditioning rather than an approximation budget: beyond a modest threshold S₀, increasing S yields negligible quality gains while increasing cost, confirming that the polyline surrogate is a better-conditioned optimization oracle, not a crude approximation. The geometric error of the surrogate is bounded at O(S⁻²), and the tile-parallel architecture with closed-form distance queries achieves 4× faster training than Bezier Splatting in closed-fill mode while delivering over 2 dB PSNR improvement.

## Results

On the 200-image DIV2K benchmark with 1024 curves, CubicSplat achieves 25.78 dB PSNR / 0.705 SSIM in closed-fill mode and 25.93 dB / 0.708 in open-stroke mode, surpassing Bezier Splatting by over 2 dB PSNR in closed mode while training ~4× faster (2.1 min vs 8.6 min per image). On the 24-image Kodak set, the closed-mode gap widens to 2.75 dB PSNR (29.66 vs 26.91) at 1024 curves. A single forward-backward step on a 2040×1344 image with 2048 curves takes 6.77 ms (open) / 19.64 ms (closed), roughly 1.4–2× faster than Bezier Splatting and over 120× faster than DiffVG.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Bezier Splatting (the strongest prior method), CubicSplat wins on PSNR and SSIM in both open and closed modes at every budget; the closed-mode gap at 1024 curves exceeds 2 dB PSNR (25.78 vs 23.45 on DIV2K, 29.66 vs 26.91 on Kodak) while training time drops from 8.6 min to 2.1 min per image (~4×). In open mode the PSNR gain is smaller (25.93 vs 25.45) and Bezier Splatting retains a lower LPIPS (0.448 vs 0.502), which the authors attribute to stochastic high-frequency texture that LPIPS favours over clean geometric boundaries. Against DiffVG, CubicSplat leads by 3–5 dB PSNR at matched budgets and is 10–17× faster; notably, CubicSplat with only 256 curves already matches DiffVG's 1024-curve PSNR in both modes. Against LIVE and LIVSS (closed-mode only), CubicSplat improves PSNR by 2.7–6 dB at 1024 curves while reducing training time from hours to ~2 minutes. Single-step timing confirms the speed advantage: 6.77 ms (open) and 19.64 ms (closed) per step versus 9.2 ms and 38.68 ms for Bezier Splatting, and over 120× faster than DiffVG.

## Relation to Prior Work

CubicSplat extends the differentiable vector graphics rasterization lineage initiated by DiffVG (iterative Bezier closest-point solvers) and accelerated by Bezier Splatting (Gaussian splatting along curves), while drawing conceptual support from 3D Gaussian Splatting's gradient-pathology literature. Its key departure is the 'gradient seesaw' diagnosis: rather than pushing forward geometric exactness (as DiffVG does) or relying on dense stochastic sampling (as Bezier Splatting does), it deliberately relaxes the forward pass to a uniform polyline surrogate with an O(S⁻²) error bound, trading a small, controlled forward bias for a static, well-conditioned computation graph. The compositing-derived Gini pruning replaces the auxiliary regularization and annealing schedules that LIVE, LIVSS, and SGLIVE require to manage inter-curve gradient interference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The gradient-seesaw framing offers a reusable diagnostic for any differentiable renderer where forward fidelity and gradient quality are in tension, potentially guiding design choices beyond vector graphics. Practically, the 4× training speedup with >2 dB PSNR gain, resolution invariance up to 24K, and 32K-primitive scalability on a single consumer GPU make real-time interactive vector design and video vectorization concrete near-term applications. Open questions include whether the S-equivariance regime holds for more complex scene compositions (e.g., many overlapping semi-transparent layers), how the method interacts with learned priors or perceptual losses for stylized generation, and whether the polyline surrogate's O(S⁻²) bound is tight enough for sub-pixel-precision applications like font rendering.
