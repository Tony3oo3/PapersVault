## Problem

Autoregressive long video generation with a fixed-size sliding-window KV cache irrecoverably discards historical frames, severing long-range dependencies needed for scene consistency and narrative coherence over extended horizons. Simultaneously, the uniform multi-step denoising schedule wastes substantial compute on temporally smooth chunks that converge quickly, while providing limited benefit to complex chunks. Prior mitigations lack a principled, content-adaptive mechanism for deciding which evicted frames to preserve and how much denoising effort each chunk actually requires.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single text prompt and produces a streaming autoregressive video at 832×480 resolution, evaluated at 5-second (VBench) and 60-second (VBench-Long) durations. It is entirely training-free: all inference-time mechanisms operate on top of a pre-trained Self Forcing / LongLive causal diffusion model (Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B backbone) without modifying weights. Generation proceeds chunk-by-chunk with a rolling KV cache, and the surprise-driven memory bank and adaptive denoising scheduler are inserted purely at inference time.

## Method

Surprise Forcing introduces two complementary inference-time mechanisms. First, a Surprise-Gated Memory Bank stores a compact set of informative evicted frames (capacity C=6 for long videos) using a dual-component surprise score that combines global prediction error (mean cosine distance to all bank entries) and local novelty (distance to the nearest bank entry), gated by a budget-norm z-score threshold that adaptively converges to a target write rate via closed-loop feedback. Eviction uses a composite priority score over surprise, retrieval usage frequency, and age, while dynamic frame routing retrieves the top-k most semantically relevant bank frames at attention time via cosine similarity to the current query descriptor. Second, Surprise-Aware Denoising computes an intra-chunk surprise signal (maximum cosine distance between adjacent frames in the step-0 denoised output) after a single forward pass, then ranks chunks within a sliding window of 20 recent chunks; chunks below a percentile threshold skip intermediate denoising steps (reducing from 4 to 2 steps), while high-surprise chunks retain the full schedule, yielding up to 21.6% speedup with negligible quality loss.

## Results

On VBench (5s), Surprise Forcing achieves a Total score of 83.40, surpassing the second-best Self Forcing (82.12) by 1.3 points, and leads on Multiple Objects (87.50 vs. 84.89 for LongLive). On VBench-Long (60s), it attains a Quality score of 81.88 (vs. 80.53 for Self Forcing), the highest Subject Consistency (98.51), and the best Aesthetic score (63.98). Throughput is 17.18 FPS on VBench-Long, slightly below LongLive (18.01) but above all other baselines, while the adaptive step scheduling yields up to 21.6% denoising compute reduction at the most aggressive skip ratio.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Surprise Forcing outperforms all four baselines on the primary quality metrics. On VBench (5s), it leads Self Forcing by 1.3 points in Total score (83.40 vs. 82.12) and by 2.6 points in Multiple Objects (87.50 vs. 84.89 for LongLive), indicating stronger prompt adherence and multi-object handling. On VBench-Long (60s), it surpasses Self Forcing by 1.35 points in Quality (81.88 vs. 80.53) and by 2.27 points in Aesthetic score (63.98 vs. 61.71 for LongLive), while also achieving the highest Subject Consistency (98.51). In terms of throughput, Surprise Forcing (17.18 FPS) is slightly slower than LongLive (18.01 FPS) but faster than CausVid, Self Forcing, and Rolling Forcing (all ≤15.84 FPS), with the gap attributable to the memory-bank retrieval overhead partially offset by the adaptive step skipping. Qualitatively, the paper reports that CausVid suffers severe color drift and quality degradation, Self Forcing shows heavy distortions beyond the training horizon, Rolling Forcing exhibits subject drift out of frame, and LongLive has inconsistent subject details—issues that Surprise Forcing mitigates through its surprise-driven memory management.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Self Forcing paradigm (autoregressive video diffusion trained via DMD self-rollout) and the LongLive streaming inference pipeline, positioning its contributions as orthogonal to training strategies: it operates entirely at inference time without modifying weights. It departs from prior KV-cache management approaches (FIFO eviction, attention sinks, Deep Forcing's in-window token compression) by maintaining a small external memory bank of evicted frames selected via a dual-component surprise score, and from prior efficiency methods (AdaCache's step-level residual reuse, distillation-based few-step generation) by making chunk-level denoising decisions based on a self-referential intra-chunk surprise signal computed after a single forward pass.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free design makes Surprise Forcing immediately applicable to existing autoregressive video diffusion systems without retraining, addressing two practical deployment bottlenecks (bounded memory and uniform compute) that limit real-time streaming generation. However, the approach is validated only on a single 1.3B-parameter architecture (Wan2.1 via LongLive) with a very small memory bank (capacity 6), and the 15.8% denoising speedup, while useful, is modest. Open questions include whether the surprise signal generalizes across model scales and architectures, whether the heuristic z-score gating and priority eviction remain effective at much longer horizons (minutes to hours), and whether learned surprise estimators could replace the hand-crafted cosine-distance metrics for more calibrated difficulty prediction.
