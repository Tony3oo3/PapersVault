## Problem

Block-based color constancy methods suffer from aggregation bias, where dominant uniform regions (sky, grass) disproportionately influence the final illuminant estimate, and learning-based methods implicitly encode training-camera spectral characteristics, limiting cross-sensor generalization. The paper addresses how to combine classical white-patch Retinex assumptions with learned features in a way that adaptively identifies reliable pixels, suppresses uninformative blocks, and generalizes to unseen cameras without calibration.

## Input / Output

The model takes a single 384×384 linear RGB image as input and produces a unit-normalized 3D RGB illuminant vector as output. The image is partitioned into a 12×12 grid of 32×32 non-overlapping blocks (144 blocks total), and no camera-specific calibration matrix, metadata, or additional images are required at inference. The full model is approximately 1.1 MB and runs in ~2.2 ms on an RTX 4090.

## Method

HybridCC is a two-branch architecture. The backbone branch uses MobileNetV3-Small (layer 8) to extract features, from which a saliency head predicts per-pixel reliability weights and a spatial mixing module (two depthwise 3×3 convolutions with residual updates) refines block-level representations. The statistics branch computes per-block illumination priors via a temperature-controlled soft maximum (a differentiable generalization of white-patch Retinex) weighted by the learned saliency map, scaled by a saliency-weighted mean intensity to encode block brightness. Fused features from both branches are refined through 2-head multi-head self-attention over the 144 block tokens, enabling any block to attend to any other regardless of spatial distance so that unreliable blocks (e.g., uniform sky) are corrected by informative ones. Per-block illuminant estimates and scalar weights are predicted from the refined features and combined via temperature-scaled softmax. Training is end-to-end with angular error loss plus an entropy-based sparsity regularizer that encourages concentration of combination weight on a small subset of reliable blocks.

## Results

HybridCC achieves the lowest mean angular error on four of five benchmarks: Gehler-Shi (2.11°), Cube+ (1.56°), INTEL-TAU (2.21°), and NUS-8 (2.26°), outperforming the strongest learning-based baselines CCMNet and C5 by 0.12°–0.31° on average. On the cross-sensor NUS-8 protocol, it reaches 1.58° mean angular error, beating CCMNet (1.71°) and C5 (1.77°). On TA-AWB (an unseen smartphone sensor), the model achieves 2.63° without any training data from that dataset, remaining competitive with methods that were trained on the dataset's own split. The model is compact (~1.1 MB) and runs in ~2.2 ms per image.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HybridCC outperforms all 25 compared baselines on Gehler-Shi (2.11° vs. CCMNet 2.23°, Tra-CC 2.32°, GCC 2.35°), Cube+ (1.56° vs. CCMNet 1.68°, C5 1.92°), INTEL-TAU (2.21° vs. C5 2.52°, Rev.CNNs 2.90°), and NUS-8 (2.26° vs. CCMNet 2.32°, C5 2.54°). On the cross-sensor NUS-8 protocol, it beats CCMNet (1.58° vs. 1.71°) and C5 (1.77°) by a wider margin, indicating stronger camera generalization. On TA-AWB, the model (2.63°) is slightly behind FFCC (2.62°) and substantially behind the dataset-specific TA-AWB method (1.84°), but outperforms TLCC (2.71°), PCC (3.03°), and all learning-free methods (≥4.01°). Notably, the worst-25% angular error is consistently the best or near-best across all benchmarks (e.g., 4.65° on Gehler-Shi vs. CCMNet's 5.46°), showing the model is particularly robust in difficult cases. The diffusion-based GCC [18] achieves competitive accuracy on Gehler-Shi but requires ~180 ms per image versus HybridCC's ~2.2 ms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of classical statistical color constancy (white-patch Retinex, block-based methods like BB-CC and MS-CC) and modern learning-based approaches (FFCC, SIIE, C5, CCMNet). It departs from purely learned methods by embedding a differentiable soft-maximum formulation of the white-patch assumption as an explicit statistical prior, and from purely traditional methods by using a MobileNetV3 backbone to learn saliency maps and contextual features. The key architectural departure is the use of multi-head self-attention over block-level representations to address aggregation bias, which the authors claim is the first application of this mechanism for that specific purpose in color constancy, distinguishing it from prior attention-based methods that operate on feature-domain representations or pixel-level multi-illuminant estimation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in achieving competitive SOTA accuracy with a ~1.1 MB model and ~2.2 ms inference that requires no camera-specific calibration, making it deployable across diverse sensors without retraining. The ablation showing a 22% cross-sensor generalization drop when removing the statistics branch highlights that classical priors remain essential even in learned pipelines. Open questions include whether the uniform-illuminant assumption can be relaxed for multi-light-source scenes, whether the learned layers still encode residual sensor-specific biases despite the statistical grounding, and whether the approach extends to substantially different sensor types (e.g., computational photography sensors).
