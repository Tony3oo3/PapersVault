## Problem

Existing OOD detection methods for Vision Transformers treat the [CLS] token as a monolithic, entangled feature vector, relying on aggregate statistics like magnitude or Euclidean distance that conflate geometric proximity with semantic similarity. While Sparse Autoencoders have proven effective at disentangling dense representations in LLMs, their potential for analyzing ViT features and exploiting the resulting structural invariants for OOD detection remains entirely unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262174/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a standard RGB image, extracts the 768-dimensional [CLS] token from a frozen pre-trained ViT-B/16, and passes it through a trained Top-k SAE that expands it to a 7680-dimensional latent space with only 128 active features per sample. The ViT simultaneously produces a class prediction, and the SAE's sparse activation vector is compared against that predicted class's CAP to yield a single scalar EPD score (a KL divergence value) where higher values indicate greater OOD likelihood. No retraining or fine-tuning is needed at inference time—the SAE is frozen after a one-time ~17-minute training run on ImageNet-1k.

## Method

The method trains a Top-k SAE (hard sparsity, k=128, D_latent=7680, 10× overcomplete) on frozen ViT-B/16 [CLS] tokens from ImageNet-1k, using an MSE reconstruction loss plus an auxiliary dead-neuron loss. For each class, the mean sparse activation vector across all ID training samples defines a Class Activation Profile (CAP), and the top 15% of dimensions by mean activation form the class's core feature set. At inference, both the sample's activation and the predicted class's CAP are restricted to these core dimensions, L1-normalized to form probability distributions on a simplex, and the EPD score is computed as the KL divergence between the sample's energy profile and the class's reference profile. The key structural insight is that ID samples maintain a sharp, concentrated activation head matching the CAP shape, while OOD samples—despite activating the correct core features—exhibit a flattened, diffused profile that systematically disrupts this energy allocation hierarchy, providing a robust detection signal that magnitude-based methods miss.

## Results

On ViT-B/16 (ID accuracy 81.14%), the proposed Top-k SAE + EPD method achieves the best average FPR95 of 40.96% (Near-OOD: 65.23%, Far-OOD: 24.77%) and a highly competitive average AUROC of 87.26% (Near-OOD: 78.98%, Far-OOD: 92.78%), ranking second only to RMDS (87.60%) on AUROC. Per-dataset, the method records the best FPR95 on OpenImage-O (26.03%) and Textures (30.44%), while maintaining strong performance on the challenging Near-OOD benchmarks SSB-hard (82.41%) and NINCO (48.06%). On the Swin-T backbone, the method still achieves the best Far-OOD FPR95 (26.97%) and competitive overall FPR95 (43.99%), though slightly behind RMDS (41.37%) on average.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method decisively outperforms all four primary baselines on average FPR95: it beats KNN (47.35%) by 6.4 points, MSP (63.75%) by 22.8 points, ReAct (66.15%) by 25.2 points, and ViM (47.02%) by 6.1 points. On AUROC, the method also surpasses all four baselines—KNN (84.13%), MSP (81.03%), ReAct (79.12%), and ViM (86.51%)—with the largest margin over ReAct (+8.1 points) and the smallest over ViM (+0.75 points). The closest overall competitor is RMDS, which edges out the proposed method on average AUROC (87.60% vs. 87.26%) and average FPR95 (43.40% vs. 40.96%), though the proposed method still leads RMDS on Far-OOD FPR95 (24.77% vs. 28.75%). On the Swin-T backbone, RMDS achieves the best average FPR95 (41.37%) and AUROC (88.36%), while the proposed method secures the best Far-OOD FPR95 (26.97%) and a competitive average FPR95 of 43.99%, confirming that the gap is architecture-dependent rather than method-specific.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the Sparse Autoencoder paradigm—established for LLM interpretability (Cunningham et al., 2023) and rooted in the Top-k SAE architecture (Makhzani & Frey, 2013)—into the vision domain, specifically targeting the ViT [CLS] token for OOD detection. It departs from both traditional OOD methods (MSP, Mahalanobis, KNN, energy scores) that treat dense features as monolithic, and from soft-sparsity SAEs that permit diffuse low-magnitude activations, by enforcing per-sample hard sparsity to expose a stable, class-specific activation structure. The auxiliary dead-neuron loss from Gao et al. (2024) is adopted to stabilize training, but the core conceptual contribution—Class Activation Profiles and the Energy Profile Divergence score—is novel to this work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first interpretable decomposition of the ViT [CLS] token for OOD detection, reframing the problem from magnitude-based distance to structural shape divergence, which offers a more principled and explainable detection signal. The finding that OOD samples activate the correct core features but with a flattened, diffused energy profile gives a clear mechanistic account of why classifiers fail on OOD inputs. Limitations include the absence of feature-level semantic visualization of individual latent neurons, reliance on ImageNet-1k as the sole ID distribution, and a notable performance drop on Swin-T due to its weaker global feature coherence. Extending the CAP/EPD framework to other modalities, multi-class fine-grained settings, and architectures without a single global token remains open.
