## Problem

As latent dimensionality increases in autoencoder-based diffusion models, reconstruction fidelity improves but generation quality eventually declines—a persistent reconstruction-generation trade-off. The paper identifies the root cause as a frequency-domain mismatch: decoders depend heavily on high-frequency latent components to recover image details, while encoders under-represent exactly those bands when extremely high-frequency RGB signals are present, leading to underfitting during diffusion training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261355/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline operates on 512×512 (or 256×256) RGB images from ImageNet. During the warm-up phase, high-frequency components above a spatial frequency threshold r₀=0.2 are filtered from RGB images before they are tokenized by a frozen autoencoder; the diffusion or flow-matching model then trains on these modified latent embeddings. In the final fine-tuning phase, full-band RGB images are used. The generative model takes a class label and a noise tensor as input and outputs a synthetic image, with no architectural changes to the denoiser.

## Method

FreqWarm is a plug-and-play frequency warm-up curriculum that requires no autoencoder retraining. The core mechanism: during early training, RGB images are low-pass filtered in the frequency domain (removing components above threshold r₀=0.2 via a circular mask in the FFT spectrum), then encoded by a frozen pretrained autoencoder to produce latent embeddings with stronger high-frequency energy. The diffusion or flow-matching model is trained from scratch on these warm-up embeddings, followed by fine-tuning steps on full-frequency-band latents. This addresses the identified conflict where extremely high-frequency RGB signals impede encoding of other high-frequency components (causing amplitude drops in latent space), while the decoder critically needs those high-frequency latent components for detail reconstruction. The method is architecture-agnostic and compatible with diverse backbones (DiT, UViT, USiT) and autoencoders (Wan2.2-VAE, LTX-VAE, DC-AE), with gains scaling with latent channel count.

## Results

On ImageNet 512×512, FreqWarm consistently reduces gFID across all tested high-dimensional autoencoders: 14.11 on Wan2.2-AE (43.67→29.56), 6.13 on LTX-AE (24.18→18.05), and 4.42 on DC-AE-f32c128 (13.84→9.42) when paired with USiT-H. Inception Score gains are equally substantial, e.g., +23.40 for DC-AE-f32c128 (85.40→108.80) and +24.24 for DC-AE-f32c128 with DiT-XL (84.41→108.65). Gains scale with latent dimensionality: DC-AE-f32c512 improves by 12.18 gFID (54.84→42.66), while low-dimensional f32c16/f32c32 show negligible change (0.02). With FreqWarm, DC-AE-f32c128 (128 channels, 32× compression) surpasses the 4-channel SD-VAE baseline in both gFID and IS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is FreqWarm vs. no warm-up on the same autoencoder, but it also benchmarks against low-dimensional autoencoders (Flux-VAE-f8c16 and SD-VAE-f8c4) to show that high-dimensional spaces can outperform them once the frequency issue is addressed. With DiT-XL, FreqWarm + DC-AE-f32c128 achieves gFID 11.02 / IS 108.65, beating SD-VAE-f8c4 (gFID 12.03 / IS 105.25) and Flux-VAE-f8c16 (gFID 27.35 / IS 53.09) despite using 32× spatial compression vs. 8×. With USiT-H, FreqWarm + DC-AE-f32c128 reaches gFID 9.42 / IS 108.80, again surpassing the 4-channel SD-VAE baseline. The key takeaway is that the reconstruction-generation trade-off (Fig. 1) is resolved: high-dimensional autoencoders that previously underperformed low-dimensional ones in generation now exceed them, validating that the bottleneck was frequency under-exposure rather than inherent capacity limitations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the frequency-analysis line initiated by Skorokhodov et al. (SEVAE) on autoencoder latent spectra, but shifts the lens from static frequency distributions to a controlled perturbation study that isolates how encoders and decoders *differently* respond to high- versus low-frequency signals. It departs from the dominant prior strategy of improving diffusibility by retraining or redesigning autoencoders (semantic alignment, hierarchical tokenization, 1D tokenizers) by instead proposing a training-side curriculum (FreqWarm) that operates entirely on frozen, off-the-shelf autoencoders. The method is also orthogonal to the diffusion/flow-matching architecture literature (DiT, UViT, USiT), which the paper treats as interchangeable backbones rather than objects of modification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that extremely high-frequency RGB components actively suppress the encoding of *other* high-frequency latent signals (Finding 3) reframes the reconstruction–generation trade-off in high-dimensional latent spaces as an exposure problem solvable by a simple frequency warm-up, without any autoencoder retraining. This opens a practical path toward adopting higher compression ratios (f64, f128) without the generation-quality penalty that has so far limited their use. Open questions include whether the optimal threshold r₀ generalizes across domains (video, text-conditioned generation, non-ImageNet data) and whether the frequency warm-up interacts with other recent diffusibility techniques (e.g., semantic alignment losses) in a complementary or redundant way.
