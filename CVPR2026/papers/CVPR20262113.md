## Problem

Existing video QA benchmarks evaluate only final answer correctness without assessing intermediate reasoning steps, and most are exocentric rather than egocentric. For egocentric video, where close-up object manipulation creates a continuous flow of activities, models must identify and track key objects over time—a capability that current benchmarks do not diagnose. This paper asks whether frontier models' failures on egocentric video reasoning stem from logical reasoning deficits or from perceptual grounding errors (not knowing where and when to look).

## Input / Output

The benchmark provides 1,160 five-option MCQ questions over 156 egocentric videos (10 s to 75 min) sourced from HD-EPIC, each accompanied by a dense human-annotated reasoning trace referencing timestamps and objects with ground-truth segmentation masks. At inference, models receive linearly subsampled video frames (typically 64, up to 1024 in ablations) plus the question text; no audio is used. The spatio-temporal highlighting variant additionally overlays visual annotations (circles, masks, or bounding boxes with class labels) on selected frames and restricts the frame budget to those where relevant objects are present, producing a modified visual input that the model consumes as standard image frames.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-part empirical framework: (1) a diagnostic benchmark with spatiotemporally-grounded reasoning traces enabling error decomposition via the MINERVA rubric (perceptual correctness, temporal grounding, logical reasoning, completeness), and (2) a spatio-temporal highlighting intervention that guides models on where and when to look without any architectural changes or retraining. In the oracle setting, ground-truth object tracks (HD-EPIC masks interpolated with SAM2) are used to draw circles around relevant objects, write class names on frames, and select only frames where question-relevant objects appear; this combination yields a 5.6% accuracy gain over the baseline. In the practical setting, an agentic pipeline first asks Gemini to identify useful object categories, then runs OWLv2 open-vocabulary detection (threshold 0.6) to obtain segments, and applies the same highlighting and temporal selection, achieving a 2.5% gain. The key finding is that models' primary failure mode is perceptual grounding—referencing only 20–50% of relevant objects in their reasoning—rather than logical reasoning, and that explicit pixel-level highlighting circumvents this limitation in fixed frontier models.

## Results

On Minerva-Ego, the best-performing model (Gemini 2.5 Pro with thinking, 256 frames) achieves 40.1% MCQ accuracy, while GPT-5 reaches 37.6% and Gemini 2.5 Flash with thinking reaches 35.6%. Human performance is 91.8%, revealing a gap of over 50 percentage points. Spatiotemporal highlighting (oracle segmentation + temporal frame selection + class labels) improves Gemini 2.5 Pro from 44.5% (256-frame baseline) to 50.3%, a 5.8-point gain, while using predicted OWLv2 segments yields a more modest 47.0%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini 2.5 Pro (40.1%) leads all models by a 2.5-point margin over GPT-5 (37.6%), which in turn outperforms Gemini 2.5 Flash with thinking (35.6%). GPT-4.1 (30.8%) and Claude Sonnet 4 (30.5%) cluster closely together, both slightly below Gemini 2.5 Flash without thinking (31.7%), suggesting that reasoning/thinking capability provides a meaningful boost on this complex multi-step benchmark. The open-source Qwen-3 (29.3%) trails proprietary models by roughly 7–11 points but remains well above the 20% random baseline. Critically, all models fall far short of the 91.8% human accuracy, with the largest single gap (51.7 points) between the best model and humans, indicating that current video reasoning systems struggle substantially with egocentric multi-step reasoning. The paper further shows that the gap is partly attributable to perceptual grounding errors: providing oracle spatiotemporal hints (circles + class labels + temporal frame selection) lifts Gemini 2.5 Pro to 50.3%, while predicted OWLv2-based hints reach 47.0%, confirming that models fail primarily on identifying *where* and *when* to look rather than on logical reasoning per se.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Set-of-Mark (SoM) visual prompting paradigm from static images to the spatiotemporal domain, adding temporal frame selection alongside spatial highlighting. It also extends the MINERVA video reasoning benchmark from exocentric to egocentric video, and builds on HD-EPIC's ground-truth segmentation masks to provide dense spatiotemporally-grounded reasoning traces. Unlike prior egocentric QA benchmarks (EgoSchema, EgoTempo, HourVideo) that evaluate only final answers, Minerva-Ego pairs each question with human-annotated reasoning traces linking timestamps to segmentation masks, enabling diagnosis of *where* and *when* models fail rather than just *whether* they fail.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that frontier models score well on logical reasoning and completeness but poorly on perceptual correctness and temporal grounding shifts the research agenda: the bottleneck for egocentric video understanding is not reasoning ability but the ability to identify and track the right objects at the right times. The 5.6% improvement from simple spatiotemporal highlighting (circles + class names + temporal selection) demonstrates a practical, architecture-free intervention, while the remaining gap between oracle hints (50.3%) and predicted-segmentation hints (47.0%) highlights that open-vocabulary detection remains a key bottleneck. Open questions include whether models can learn to self-identify relevant objects and frames without external hints, and whether the perceptual grounding gap persists as models scale.
