## Problem

Existing visuo-tactile methods rely on global alignment (e.g., CLS-token or pooled-representation similarity) that can determine whether an image and touch signal share a material but cannot identify *where* in the scene that material appears. This prevents tactile localization—segmenting all image regions that would evoke the same tactile sensation as a given touch—and is further hampered by datasets like Touch-and-Go that contain close-up, temporally invariant, single-material images offering too little visual diversity for robust cross-modal learning.

## Input / Output

The model takes a single 224×224 RGB image and a 224×224 tactile image (from a GelSight sensor) as input. It produces a dense H×W tactile saliency map where each pixel's value reflects the similarity between the aggregated tactile feature and the local visual feature at that location; thresholding or argmax over this map yields the material segmentation mask. The tactile signal is spatially collapsed to a single C-dimensional vector before being compared against every spatial position of the visual feature map, so the output is a per-pixel score rather than a single scalar.

## Method

STT uses a frozen DINOv3 backbone for the visual encoder and a trainable DINOv3 backbone for the tactile encoder, each followed by a Channel-wise LayerNorm + 1×1-conv aligner that maps both modalities into a shared C×H×W feature space. The key architectural choice is a local similarity function: the tactile feature map is averaged over spatial dimensions to yield a single C-vector, which is then inner-producted with every spatial location of the visual feature map to produce a dense similarity map; the contrastive (InfoNCE) loss uses the max-pooled value of this map as the pair score, forcing the model to learn where in the image the tactile property resides. To overcome the limited diversity of Touch-and-Go, the paper introduces a material-diversity pairing strategy that links each tactile frame to multiple visually varied images of the same material category—both in-domain (across different TG instances) and out-domain (32K web/MINC images collected via LLM-generated search queries)—so the model learns robust contextual localization and gains resilience to weak start/end tactile signals.

## Results

On the three tactile localization benchmarks, STT achieves 87.56 mAP / 76.82 mIoU on TG-Test, 77.43 mAP / 60.94 mIoU on Web-Material, and 48.06 mAP / 36.73 mIoU on OpenSurfaces. The most substantial gains appear on the more challenging scene-level datasets: STT improves mIoU by +28.78 over TVL on Web-Material and by +17.66 over the DINOv3 attention map on OpenSurfaces. Material diversity-based pairing contributes an additional +5.21 mIoU on Web-Material and +2.63 mIoU on OpenSurfaces over the local-alignment-only variant (STT-Local).

## Baselines & Comparisons

STT substantially outperforms TVL (Fu et al., ICML 2024) on all three benchmarks, with the largest gap on Web-Material where STT reaches 60.94 mIoU versus TVL's 32.16 mIoU (+28.78), confirming that global CLS-token alignment is inadequate for spatial localization. Against the DINOv3 attention map (Siméoni et al., 2025), STT wins on Web-Material (60.94 vs 47.12 mIoU) and OpenSurfaces (36.73 vs 19.04 mIoU), but the two are close on TG-Test (76.82 vs 74.27 mIoU), which the authors attribute to TG's close-up, single-material framing that inflates vision-only baselines. The two upper-bound references—GroundedSAM (Ren et al., 2024) and Materialistic (Sharma et al., SIGGRAPH 2023)—remain ahead of STT on all datasets (e.g., Materialistic reaches 76.14 mIoU on Web-Material vs STT's 60.94), but these rely on explicit visual or text prompts rather than tactile input, so they serve as ceiling references rather than direct competitors. In the interactive localization setting (Table 3), STT achieves 37.0 IIoU while TVL and STT-CLS collapse to 1.0 and 4.0 IIoU respectively, further underscoring the necessity of local alignment for distinguishing multiple tactile regions in a single scene.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in visuo-tactile learning, which relies on global alignment (CLS tokens or pooled embeddings) to match entire samples across modalities, by instead computing dense local similarity maps between tactile and visual feature maps. It repurposes the cross-modal contrastive learning framework from sound-source localization [27,28,29] and adapts it to the tactile domain, while introducing a material-diversity pairing strategy that has no direct analogue in prior visuo-tactile work. The task is positioned as a cross-modal generalization of material segmentation from visual queries (Materialistic [30]) and a 2D in-the-wild extension of contact localization in constrained domains (garments [19], 3D radiance fields [10]).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals that the widely-used Touch-and-Go benchmark is fundamentally biased for localization (trivial full-square masks achieve 67 mIoU), a finding that should prompt the community to re-evaluate prior visuo-tactile results. The new Web-Material and TG-Test benchmarks, combined with the interactive localization protocol, provide much more discriminative evaluation tools. However, the substantial gap to upper-bound baselines (e.g., 77 vs. 91 mAP on Web-Material) indicates that robust tactile-grounded segmentation remains an open challenge, particularly for weak signals and fine-grained multi-material scenes. Future work could explore whether the local-alignment principle transfers to other cross-modal localization tasks or whether 3D/4D tactile data can close the remaining gap.
