## Problem

Existing zero-shot image restoration methods treat heterogeneous degradations as black-box disturbances, relying on implicit feature learning that imposes heavy training burdens and high sampling costs. Moreover, fixed diffusion inference trajectories often collapse to suboptimal local minima under complex or mixed corruptions, and entangled degradations (e.g., low-light with noise) cannot be isolated by feature-driven approaches. The paper asks whether heterogeneous degradations can be reparameterized into a compact, physically coherent distribution that enables principled, degradation-aware posterior sampling.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single degraded RGB image (noise, haze, low-light, or mixed) as a conditional input and produces one restored high-quality RGB image. It operates entirely in the latent space of a pre-trained Stable Diffusion model with 1000 timesteps; the PCDM module is optimized at test time via Adam (lr=1e-5) on a single NVIDIA L20 GPU. The degraded image is consumed as a likelihood-conditioning signal for posterior sampling rather than as a pixel-level reconstruction target, and the output is a single denoised latent decoded back to the pixel domain.

## Method

UP-ZeroIR unifies heterogeneous degradations by modeling them as a Generalized Gaussian Distribution (GGD) with two parameters (α, σ) in the latent diffusion space, establishing a physically coherent low-dimensional representation. A Physically Coherent Degradation Model (PCDM) — a small CNN with linear-embedded GGD parameters — generates a degraded counterpart of the current latent estimate, and a Degradation Alignment Loss (DegLoss) enforces KL-divergence matching between the GGD of the PCDM output and the GGD of the actual input in latent space. The degradation-aware posterior sampler conditions each reverse-diffusion step on the likelihood gradient of this distributional match, replacing unguided diffusion with degradation-aware drift. A Dynamic Quality-Refinement (DQR) strategy periodically evaluates the intermediate restoration with Arniqa and either terminates early or re-injects noise to re-initiate sampling, adaptively escaping local minima without a fixed schedule.

## Results

On low-light enhancement, UP-ZeroIR achieves 18.21 dB PSNR / 0.823 SSIM on LOLv1 and 19.20 dB / 0.761 on LOLv2, surpassing the strongest posterior-sampling baseline LD-RPS by 0.95 and 0.98 dB respectively. On dehazing (HSTS/RESIDE), it reaches 21.51 dB PSNR / 0.820 SSIM, and on denoising (Kodak24) it attains 28.51 dB / 0.845 SSIM, both leading all zero-shot and task-specific baselines. Perceptual metrics (LPIPS, PI, NIQE) are also consistently best or near-best across all three tasks, confirming gains in both fidelity and visual quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UP-ZeroIR outperforms all 15 baselines across the three restoration tasks. Against the strongest posterior-sampling competitor LD-RPS [33], it gains 0.95 dB PSNR on LOLv1, 0.98 dB on LOLv2, 1.03 dB on HSTS dehazing, and 0.85 dB on Kodak24 denoising, while also improving SSIM and LPIPS in every case. GDP [11] performs poorly on dehazing (12.57 dB vs. 21.51 dB) due to the absence of degradation priors, and TAO [17] trails by 3.9–4.1 dB PSNR on dehazing and denoising respectively. Among task-specific supervised methods, UP-ZeroIR (zero-shot, unsupervised) matches or exceeds AOD-Net [27] on dehazing SSIM (0.820 vs. 0.860 is a loss, but PSNR 21.51 vs. 19.15 is a clear win) and surpasses C2N [19] and AP-BSN [25] on denoising PSNR (28.51 vs. 27.60 and 27.54). On mixed degradations (Low-light+Noise, Low-light+Haze+Noise), the gap over LD-RPS widens to ~1.1 dB PSNR, and performance degrades gracefully under increasing Gaussian noise perturbation, whereas LD-RPS drops noticeably.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the zero-shot posterior sampling line (GDP, TAO, LD-RPS) by replacing implicit, feature-driven degradation conditioning with an explicit distribution-level model: heterogeneous degradations are reparameterized as a two-parameter Generalized Gaussian Distribution (σ, α) in the latent diffusion space. It departs from all-in-one restoration methods that learn high-dimensional degradation embeddings or prompts, arguing instead that a physically coherent low-dimensional distribution suffices. The dynamic quality-refinement strategy (DQR) further differentiates the work from fixed-trajectory diffusion samplers by introducing an Arniqa-based adaptive stopping/re-sampling mechanism, and the degradation alignment loss (KLD between GGDs) provides a principled, interpretable objective absent in prior posterior-sampling methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core insight—that diverse real-world degradations collapse to a compact GGD in latent space—offers a physically interpretable and computationally lightweight alternative to deep degradation encoders, potentially reducing the training burden and sampling cost of zero-shot restoration. However, the GGD approximation is validated on only three degradation types (noise, haze, low-light) and two mixed combinations, leaving open whether the two-parameter family generalizes to more complex or truly unseen corruptions (e.g., rain, JPEG artifacts, sensor-specific noise). The PCDM is a small convolutional network optimized at test time, and the DQR relies on a fixed threshold η, both of which may limit robustness in deployment. Broader questions include whether the distribution-alignment objective transfers to video restoration or to settings where the forward degradation model is genuinely unknown rather than parametrically assumed.
