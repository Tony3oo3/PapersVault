## Problem

Existing OOD detection methods rely on entangled representations or logit outputs, which suffer from simplicity bias—models prioritize easily learnable local cues while overlooking complex semantic relationships between objects in a scene. This limitation is especially severe for near-OOD detection, where subtle semantic deviations (e.g., a dog co-occurring with a shark in an ocean) produce overconfident predictions because the model latches onto a single discriminative region rather than recognizing the scene-level contextual incongruity.

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard RGB image, extracts a feature map from a pre-trained ViT-B/16 or DINOv2 ViT-B/14 backbone, and decomposes it into K=6 slots via Slot Attention. The output is a scenario-dependent OOD detection score: a dual-confidence product for single-pattern samples, a Dempster-Shafer belief combination for typical multi-object patterns, or a max slot confidence for atypical patterns. A single global threshold (calibrated at 95% TPR on ID data) converts the score into an inlier/outlier decision.

## Method

OCO uses a pre-trained DINOSAUR Slot Attention module to decompose entangled features into K disentangled slots, each independently producing class logits that are summed for a global prediction. Training combines cross-entropy on the aggregated prediction with an auxiliary reconstruction loss that forces slots to preserve spatial information, ensuring reliable object localization. During training, the method collects co-occurrence frequency sets (which object categories appear together and how many slots predict each) from all ID samples to build a reference set F_train. At test time, each sample's frequency set is matched against F_train to assign it to one of three scenarios—single-pattern, typical multi-object, or atypical multi-object—and a tailored OOD score is computed per scenario: dual-confidence calibration (S1), Dempster-Shafer pairwise belief contradiction (S2), or raw max slot confidence (S3). This divide-and-conquer scoring avoids the one-size-fits-all paradigm and explicitly exploits semantic contextual incompatibility to flag near-OOD samples that entangled-feature methods miss.

## Results

On the standard OOD detection benchmark (ImageNet-1k → 5 OOD datasets), OCO achieves a mean AUROC of 86.04% (ViT-B/16) and 87.75% (DINOv2-B/14), outperforming the strongest baseline OODD by 2.13% and 0.71% respectively. On the more challenging Full-Spectrum OOD (FS-OOD) evaluation, OCO improves AUROC by 3.51% (ViT) and 3.40% (DINOv2) over OODD, reaching 73.21% and 77.40% mean AUROC. OCO achieves the best FPR95 across all five OOD datasets with both backbones, with particularly strong results on far-OOD: 10.38% FPR95 on iNaturalist and 18.36% on Textures (DINOv2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

OCO outperforms all nine baselines on mean AUROC across both backbones, with the largest margins over SHE (74.19% ViT, 81.05% DINOv2) and NNguide (76.30% ViT, 75.36% DINOv2), which rely on entangled feature-space or nearest-neighbor heuristics. Against the strongest competitor OODD, OCO gains 2.13% AUROC (ViT) and 0.71% (DINOv2) on standard OOD, and 3.51%/3.40% on FS-OOD. However, OCO does not dominate every individual dataset: on the near-OOD SSB-hard benchmark with DINOv2, OODD achieves 76.22% AUROC versus OCO's 72.13%, and on NINCO the gap is negligible (81.30% vs 81.27%). OCO's advantage is most pronounced on far-OOD datasets (iNaturalist, Textures, OpenImage-O), where it achieves the best FPR95 and AUROC with DINOv2, suggesting that object co-occurrence patterns are particularly effective at detecting semantically implausible combinations. FDBD and CoRP, which use decision-boundary or kernel-based scores, are competitive on far-OOD but lag significantly on near-OOD (SSB-hard AUROC: 65.45% and 62.44% with DINOv2 vs. OCO's 72.13%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Slot Attention (Locatello et al., NeurIPS 2020) and the pre-trained DINOSAUR architecture (Seitzer et al., ICLR 2023) from the object-centric representation learning literature, redirecting slot-wise predictions toward OOD detection rather than scene decomposition. It departs from the dominant OOD detection paradigm—operating on entangled logits, features, or energy scores—by introducing a divide-and-conquer strategy that categorizes test samples into three co-occurrence scenarios and assigns scenario-specific scores, drawing on Dempster-Shafer theory for the ambiguous-pattern case. The motivation is grounded in the simplicity bias literature (Shah et al., NeurIPS 2020; Tiwari & Shenoy, ICML 2023), which identifies why entangled representations fail on near-OOD, and in cognitive science findings on human scene understanding via object relationships.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The object-centric co-occurrence perspective offers a principled explanation for why near-OOD detection remains hard: models latch onto easily learnable local cues while ignoring semantic contextual incompatibility. However, the method's reliance on discrete argmax slot predictions for co-occurrence discovery, its dependence on a specific pre-trained slot attention model (DINOSAUR), and the somewhat ad hoc three-scenario partitioning limit generality. Open questions include whether the approach extends to open-vocabulary or video settings, how sensitive it is to the number of slots (performance drops sharply beyond K=6), and whether the co-occurrence statistics collected from a fixed training set remain valid under domain shift. The modest AUROC gains over OODD (2.13% on ViT, 0.71% on DINOv2 for standard OOD) suggest the conceptual contribution is more significant than the performance delta at this scale.
