## Problem

Existing training-free visual token pruning methods for VLLMs perform no better than random pruning in deeper decoder layers, yet the underlying reason for this degradation was unexplained. This paper asks: what information do visual tokens actually carry at each layer, and why do principled pruning strategies lose their advantage as depth increases?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263018/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on an RGB image encoded into a fixed or resolution-dependent set of visual tokens (576 for LLaVA-1.5, up to thousands for Qwen2.5-VL) concatenated with a text prompt, and produces a pruned visual token sequence that the language decoder uses to generate a text answer. Pruning is applied at a chosen decoder layer by masking token hidden states, and the information metric requires the ground-truth first-token label to compute probability differences. The analysis focuses on the prefill step (first generated token), not autoregressive decoding.

## Method

The paper defines a visual token's information at layer i as the difference in the ground-truth output probability when that single token is retained versus when all visual tokens are removed (Eq. 6), isolating each token's marginal contribution. Using this metric, the authors discover an 'information horizon': a layer beyond which all visual tokens' information converges uniformly to zero, making any pruning strategy equivalent to random. They show this horizon shifts deeper for visually complex tasks (OCR vs. VQA) and for stronger models (Qwen2.5-VL vs. LLaVA-1.5). The practical recommendation is a hybrid strategy: apply an existing importance- or diversity-based method (e.g., DivPrune, DART) in shallow layers where tokens carry differentiated information, then switch to random pruning in deep layers, which requires no attention computation and is compatible with FlashAttention.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-VL-7B with 50% visual token pruning, DivPrune+Random achieves 80.9% average accuracy across 7 benchmarks (96.9% relative to the unpruned model), outperforming DivPrune alone (80.7%, 96.7%) and DART+Random (78.3%, 93.9%). On LLaVA-1.5-7B at an aggressive 88.9% pruning ratio (retaining only 64 of 576 tokens), DivPrune+Random retains 94.9% relative performance, substantially exceeding VTW (59.3%), FastV (60.9%), and PDrop (72.3%). In efficiency terms, DART+Random on LLaVA-1.5-7B reduces CUDA latency by 73.0% and FLOPs by 74.4% while maintaining 91.6% of original performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DivPrune+Random consistently outperforms its standalone counterpart DivPrune on Qwen2.5-VL-7B (80.9% vs. 80.7% average accuracy; 96.9% vs. 96.7% relative), with the largest gains on OCRBench (83.3% vs. 84.1% for DivPrune alone, but 83.3% vs. 73.3% for DART+VTW). DART+Random improves over DART alone by 1.2% average accuracy (78.3% vs. 77.3%) and 1.2% relative performance (93.9% vs. 92.7%), with a notable 2.4-point gain on OCRBench (77.9% vs. 75.5%). Against the visual-token-withdrawal baseline VTW, the random-pruning variants dominate at high pruning ratios: on LLaVA-1.5-7B at 88.9% pruning, DivPrune+Random achieves 94.9% relative performance versus VTW's 59.3%, and DART+Random reaches 91.5% versus VTW's 59.3%. Compared to multi-layer pruning methods, FastV+Random surpasses PDrop by 5.5% relative performance (77.8% vs. 72.3%) and DART+Random exceeds SparseVLM by 4.2% (91.5% vs. 87.3%) on LLaVA-1.5-7B at 88.9% pruning. On Qwen2.5-VL-7B, DivPrune+Random also beats the VTW-augmented variants (DivPrune+VTW at 94.2%), confirming that retaining a small random subset in deep layers is superior to removing all visual tokens beyond a fixed layer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a diagnostic study of the existing visual token pruning landscape rather than proposing a new pruning criterion. It builds on the two established families of training-free pruning (importance-based: FastV, SparseVLM, SGL, PDrop; diversity-based: DivPrune, DART) but departs from them by introducing an operational definition of token information via output probability perturbation, rather than relying on attention scores or inter-token similarity. Conceptually, it extends the redundancy observations of VTW and PDrop—which proposed fixed-layer token withdrawal—by showing that the depth at which tokens become redundant (the 'information horizon') is dynamic, shifting with task visual complexity and model capability, thereby motivating a hybrid strategy of structured pruning in shallow layers plus random pruning in deep layers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: practitioners can replace computationally expensive attention-based or diversity-based selection in deep layers with zero-cost random pruning, enabling seamless FlashAttention compatibility and reducing CUDA latency by ~73% while retaining >91% performance. The dynamic information horizon concept provides a principled, task-aware guideline for where to prune, addressing a limitation of fixed-layer methods like VTW. Open questions include whether the information metric (which requires ground-truth labels for calibration) can be estimated without supervision, whether the horizon generalizes beyond the two architectures studied (LLaVA-1.5-7B, Qwen-2.5-VL-7B), and whether it holds during autoregressive decoding rather than just the prefill step.
