## Problem

Monotonicity is known to improve explainability in neural networks, but not every function can be well approximated by a monotone network, and existing pretrained architectures cannot be easily converted. Although the DC decomposition of a ReLU network into two monotone-convex streams was known in theory, it had never been applied to explainability because of severe numerical instability (activation and gradient blowup) in deep networks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20264044/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained ReLU network (VGG16 or ResNet18) and an RGB image as input. The image is decomposed into positive and negative parts (x = x⁺ − x⁻), and the network is converted into a split-pair of two monotone subnetworks without altering predictions. The output is a pixel-level saliency or attribution map computed from the split streams, evaluated on ImageNet-S-50 (566 test images with segmentation masks) using Quantus metrics for faithfulness, localization, and robustness.

## Method

The core mechanism splits each ReLU layer using the identity ReLU(a−b) = max{a,b} − b, replacing it with a Maxout unit in the g-stream and a linear unit in the h-stream, while decomposing all weight matrices into non-negative parts. To prevent activation explosion in the forward pass, the paper re-centers split-pair activations via threshold-based scaling and corrective shifts that preserve the invariant a⁺ − a⁻ = a_original. For the backward pass, it subtracts an α-scaled product of subsequent weight magnitudes from the local sensitivities at each layer (0 < α < 0.5), which reduces gradient magnitudes while preserving relative patterns. On top of this stabilized split representation, three new attribution methods are defined: SplitCAM (LayerCAM without the ReLU filter, applied to split-stream activations and shifted sensitivities), SplitLRP (standard ε-LRP propagated through the non-negative split-pair with winner-takes-all maxpooling), and SplitGrad (channel-averaged shifted local sensitivities). The approach requires no retraining and leaves model predictions unchanged.

## Results

On VGG16/ImageNet-S, SplitCAM (α=0.4, wta) achieves the best Pointing Game score of 0.938 and the best Attribution Localization of 0.635, outperforming all baselines on these localization metrics. SplitLRP achieves the lowest Maximum Sensitivity (0.282), indicating the most robust explanations under perturbation. On ResNet18/ImageNet-S, SplitCAM (α=0.3, wta) at layer 4.1.2 reaches a Pointing Game score of 0.972 and Attribution Localization of 0.673, matching or exceeding the strongest baseline LayerCAM (0.971 / 0.664). The ablation study (Table 3) confirms that backward-pass stabilization with α in the range 0.35–0.45 is critical: at α=0 (no stabilization), Pointing Game drops from 0.956 to 0.401 for SplitGrad.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On VGG16, SplitCAM decisively wins on localization: its Pointing Game score of 0.938 exceeds Guided Backprop (0.887), LayerCAM (0.798), and GradCAM++ (0.827) by 5–14 points, and its Attribution Localization of 0.651 surpasses the next-best LRP (0.615) and Guided Backprop (0.614). However, SplitCAM trails LayerCAM on faithfulness metrics—Pixel Flipping AUC@5 (0.213 vs. 0.317) and AUC@20 (1.794 vs. 2.474)—and on Selectivity (4.720 vs. 2.792 for LayerCAM, 3.189 for LRP). SplitLRP compensates with the best robustness score (Max Sensitivity 0.282 vs. 0.375 for Guided Backprop and 0.519 for LRP). On ResNet18, the gap narrows: SplitCAM at layer 4.1.2 (Pointing 0.972, Attr. Loc. 0.673) essentially ties LayerCAM at 4.1.1 (0.971, 0.664), while GradCAM at the same layer achieves a lower Max Sensitivity (0.131 vs. 0.233). Overall, the split methods trade some faithfulness (Pixel Flipping, Selectivity) for substantially better spatial localization and competitive robustness, with the advantage being most pronounced on the deeper VGG16 architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes the DC decomposition of ReLU networks (previously established in optimization contexts by Awasthi et al. and Siahkamari et al.) for the first time in explainability, overcoming the numerical blowup that had prevented its practical use. It builds on the monotone/ICNN literature (Amos et al., Sill) for the theoretical guarantee that non-negative-weight streams represent monotone and convex functions, and adapts two established XAI methods—LayerCAM and LRP—to the split-pair representation. The CDiNN architecture (Sankaranarayanan & Rengaswamy) is the closest prior work in directly training difference-of-convex networks, but that line targets optimal control rather than explainability of pretrained models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The stabilized DC decomposition offers a drop-in way to improve saliency quality on any pretrained ReLU network without retraining, which is practically valuable for debugging and auditing deployed models. The DIC/DM experiments on MNIST reveal a clean factual-vs-counterfactual separation between the two streams, suggesting a principled route to self-explaining architectures, though this remains a proof-of-concept on a toy dataset. Key open questions include scaling the DIC/DM training to ImageNet-scale data, extending the split to transformers (sketched but not evaluated), and developing automatic layer selection, since attribution quality is highly layer-dependent as the ablations show.
