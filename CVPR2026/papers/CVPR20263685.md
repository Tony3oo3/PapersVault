## Problem

VLA pre-training requires large collections of pre-segmented, action-labeled video clips, but in industrial settings such data is prohibitively expensive to obtain through teleoperation. Existing unsupervised action segmentation methods rely on pixel-level or optical flow signals that capture physical motion rather than semantic task-phase transitions, making them ill-suited for extracting the structured action vocabulary needed for VLA latent pre-training from continuous, unstructured industrial video streams.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263685/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes hours-long continuous unlabeled video from industrial workstations (e.g., two synchronized views of a motor assembly line), processing it through a sliding window to extract dense keypoint trajectories via CoTracker. It outputs structured (video clip, discrete FSQ code sequence) pairs directly suitable for VLA pre-training, along with a finite set of semantic action clusters. The discrete code indices from a 2048-entry FSQ codebook serve as the primary latent action tokens, while continuous quantized vectors are retained internally for energy computation and downstream clustering.

## Method

The LAPS pipeline operates in three stages: (1) a point tracker extracts dense keypoint trajectories from raw video; (2) a lightweight temporal quantized autoencoder (derived from AMPLIFY) with FSQ discretization encodes motion dynamics into a continuous quantized vector stream, from which a novel Latent Action Energy metric — the L2 norm of temporal differences in the quantized latent space — drives a hysteresis-based two-state controller to detect action boundaries; (3) segmented latent sequences are embedded via a frozen (randomly initialized, never trained) 4-layer transformer with mean pooling, then clustered with cosine k-means to discover the finite action vocabulary. The key insight is that segmentation in the abstract latent action space captures semantic behavioral intent (e.g., reaching vs. grasping) rather than low-level physical motion, making it robust to appearance changes and lighting variations. The segmentation threshold is calibrated entirely unsupervised via self-supervised pseudo-labeling from a simple velocity-based proxy signal, and semantic coherence of discovered clusters is validated using a VLM-based Intra-Cluster Semantic Similarity metric.

## Results

On the proprietary Industrial Motor Assembly dataset, LAPS achieves F1@2s of 81.27% (top-down) and 81.93% (exocentric), dramatically outperforming all baselines by over 20 points. On public benchmarks, LAPS is competitive: it reaches 73.12% F1@5s on GTEA (vs. ABD's 81.92%) and 58.82% F1@5s on Breakfast (vs. OTAS's 62.13%). Clustering quality is confirmed by an ICSS of 0.926±0.033 for discovered clusters versus 0.804±0.127 for random pairs, and a Silhouette score of 0.588 for the Frozen Transformer embedding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the industrial dataset, LAPS decisively outperforms both baselines: it surpasses ABD [13] by ~48 points in F1@2s (81.27 vs. 34.08 top-down) and OTAS [20] by ~41 points (81.27 vs. 40.69), demonstrating that latent-space energy signals are far more robust to the repetitive, structured motion of manufacturing than pixel-level or object-feature-based detectors. On public benchmarks the picture is more nuanced: ABD [13] still leads on GTEA (81.92 vs. 73.12 F1@5s), while OTAS [20] edges out LAPS on Breakfast F1@5s (62.13 vs. 58.82), though LAPS narrows the gap on the stricter F1@2s metric. The paper attributes the industrial advantage to the Motion Tokenizer's ability to suppress low-level visual noise and capture semantic intent shifts, whereas ABD's visual-similarity minima and OTAS's object-relation features degrade under the consistent, repetitive motion patterns of assembly workstations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the temporal quantized autoencoder architecture from AMPLIFY [10]—originally designed for learning motion priors for robot policy learning—into a segmentation signal generator. It departs from both prior latent-action approaches (LAPO, LAPA) that assume pre-segmented clips and from traditional unsupervised action segmentation methods (ABD, OTAS) that detect boundaries via pixel-level visual similarity or optical flow. The key conceptual shift is defining a 'Latent Action Energy' metric in the quantized latent space to identify semantic action boundaries, reframing segmentation from 'visual change detection' to 'behavioral intent change detection.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This addresses a critical and underexplored data-sourcing bottleneck for VLA pre-training in manufacturing, where hours of continuous industrial video exist but no automated pipeline converts them into structured (clip, latent-token-sequence) pairs. The approach is limited to highly repetitive, finite-vocabulary tasks typical of assembly lines, and the number of clusters k must be set a priori from domain expertise, which constrains generalization to open-ended domains like households or hospitals. A key open question is whether the discovered latent action vocabulary can be directly consumed by a downstream VLA policy without additional alignment, which the authors flag as their immediate next step.
