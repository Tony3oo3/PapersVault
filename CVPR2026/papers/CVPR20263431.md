## Problem

Existing multimodal misinformation detectors are either opaque end-to-end black boxes or rely on fixed, expert-defined concept sets that cannot generalize to emerging manipulation tactics. No prior system mechanistically replicates the structured, concept-level reasoning that human fact-checkers use—decomposing a claim into interpretable intermediate checks whose soft judgments collectively determine veracity—while remaining adaptable to new misinformation patterns without costly retraining.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263431/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text-image pair (e.g., a news caption with an accompanying photo) and produces a binary fake/real verdict, per-concept probabilities at every layer of the graph, fine-grained manipulation-type scores (textual distortion, visual distortion, cross-modal inconsistency, real) when such labels exist as anchors in layer L₀, and a human-readable evidence chain tracing which concepts and attention weights drove the final decision. Concepts themselves are short interrogative strings (e.g., 'Is the image contextually inconsistent with the caption?') generated and validated by MLLMs, so the concept set grows over training rounds rather than being fixed a priori.

## Method

PCGR follows a build-then-infer paradigm over a layered probabilistic concept graph (DAG). In the build phase, an MLLM (e.g., GPT-5) is prompted with high-loss seed pairs to propose new interrogative concepts, which pass a three-stage filter (cosine-similarity uniqueness ≤ 0.8, Pearson-correlation independence ≤ 0.9, activation range [0.05, 0.95]) before being added; edges between adjacent layers are scored by combining semantic cosine similarity, Soft-PMI over batch-level concept probabilities, and NLI entailment/contradiction scores (DeBERTa-v3), with edges retained above a 0.55 threshold. In the inference phase, each text-image pair is encoded by CLIP into separate text and image embeddings, and each concept is represented by learnable positive and negative prototypes that capture active versus inactive evidence states. Concept probabilities are computed via a low-rank multimodal interaction conditioned on Sentence-BERT concept embeddings, then aggregated top-down through a hierarchical attention mechanism whose multiplicative form approximates a logical AND across parent concepts, yielding the final veracity score. Training alternates between the concept-generation module and the detection module, with a combined loss of binary cross-entropy on veracity plus a concept-orthogonality regularizer to keep concept embeddings independent.

## Results

PCGR achieves state-of-the-art coarse-level MMD accuracy of 80.2% (F1 70.9) on MiRAGeNews, 80.6% (F1 73.5) on MMFakeBench, and 84.3% (F1 79.8) on AMG, outperforming the strongest baseline MGCA by 7.9%, 6.5%, and 6.1% accuracy respectively. On fine-grained manipulation-type classification, PCGR reaches 68.6% Micro-F1 on MMFakeBench (4 classes) and 75.6% Micro-F1 on AMG (6 classes), surpassing MGCA by 8.3 and 2.8 points. Notably, PCGR maintains stable performance on the out-of-domain MiRAGeNews test set (unseen generators/publishers), whereas most baselines show significant degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PCGR outperforms all 13 baselines on every dataset and metric. Against the strongest task-specific detector MGCA, PCGR gains 7.9% accuracy on MiRAGeNews (80.2 vs. 72.3), 6.5% on MMFakeBench (80.6 vs. 74.1), and 6.1% on AMG (84.3 vs. 78.2). Among general-purpose MLLMs, GPT-5 is the strongest (56.8–59.9% accuracy) yet still trails PCGR by 21–24 points, underscoring the benefit of explicit cross-modal concept reasoning over monolithic MLLM prompting. On fine-grained manipulation-type classification, PCGR beats MGCA by 8.3 Micro-F1 on MMFakeBench (68.6 vs. 60.3) and 2.8 on AMG (75.6 vs. 72.8), while GPT-5 lags further behind at 55.3 and 60.7. The gap is most pronounced on the OOD MiRAGeNews test set, where PCGR's concept-graph architecture generalizes to unseen generators while most baselines (e.g., C3N drops from 75.3 to 70.4, MGCA from 78.2 to 72.3) degrade substantially.

## Relation to Prior Work

PCGR synthesizes two distinct research threads: Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs), which predict human-interpretable concepts as intermediates before task outputs, and Graph-of-Thought (GoT) reasoning, which structures inference over graph-organized thoughts in LLMs. The key departure is architectural rather than conceptual: rather than using GoT as an external prompting strategy or CBMs with a fixed, flat concept set, PCGR embeds a layered probabilistic concept graph directly in the model and grows it automatically via MLLM-driven concept discovery. This positions the work between mechanism-guided MMD detectors (which rely on fixed manipulation taxonomies) and post-hoc explanation frameworks, by making structured reasoning an integral, evolvable part of the detection pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The automatic concept growth mechanism addresses a practical pain point: as manipulation tactics evolve, fixed-concept detectors become brittle, whereas PCGR can incorporate new diagnostic concepts without full retraining. The OOD robustness on MiRAGeNews (where most baselines degrade) suggests the concept-graph structure captures more transferable reasoning patterns than end-to-end feature fusion. Open questions include the computational cost of repeated MLLM calls during concept growth, whether the graph topology generalizes beyond the three evaluated datasets, and whether the NLI-based edge scoring scales to very large concept sets. The framework's reliance on MLLMs as an oracle for concept proposal also raises questions about circularity and the ceiling of what MLLMs can discover.
