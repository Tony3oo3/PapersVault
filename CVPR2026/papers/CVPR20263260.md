## Problem

Physics-based humanoid control policies are trained on motion datasets with fixed, predominantly low-dynamic difficulty distributions, which caps their performance on expert-level actions such as martial arts, gymnastics, and combat. Acquiring high-quality professional motion data via MoCap is prohibitively expensive, and existing large-scale corpora (AMASS, Humanoid-X, HuBE) still lack reliable difficulty stratification and controller-aware grading, leaving a gap between what controllers can learn and what professional motion demands.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes structured text prompts instantiated from a 5-domain (martial arts, dance, combat, gymnastics, sports) and 4-axis (base action, combo action, detail, speed/rhythm) variable library, encoded by DistilBERT and fed to MDM for 50-step conditional diffusion sampling. The output is twofold: a progressively harder synthetic 3D motion dataset (filtered by root-height physics checks and VLM semantic alignment) and a trained single-primitive physics-based humanoid controller. Only the RL tracker training is compute-intensive; all other components (MDM synthesis, VLM evaluation, LLM prompt scheduling) are training-free and low-cost.

## Method

CLAIMS is a closed-loop competitive curriculum that co-evolves motion data difficulty and controller capability across K iterations. Each round, a Gemini CoT LLM policy fuses objective tracking metrics (mpjpe-g, mpjpe-l, vel-dist, accel-dist) with subjective VLM difficulty scores (GPT-4o, Qwen-VL-MAX) and the previous prompt into a semantic observation, then selects harder prompts from the difficulty-aware variable library. MDM synthesizes the corresponding motions, which are filtered by physics validity and VLM prompt-motion alignment before being appended to the training set. The physics-based tracker (PHC or MaskedMimic) is retrained via RL on the accumulated corpus, and the loop repeats until mastery thresholds are met. The key insight is that templated compositional prompts elicit out-of-distribution professional motions from MDM's latent space, while the fused objective-subjective feedback signal provides a reliable, multi-dimensional difficulty escalation criterion that no single metric can capture.

## Results

On the PHC single-primitive tracker evaluated across 2,201 test clips (Motion-X/Kungfu, EMDB, AIST++, Video-Convert), the best iterative model (L6) achieves a 76.9% average success rate, compared to 58.3% for the AMASS-trained baseline — a 45% reduction in average failure rate (41.7% → 23.1%). Per-set gains are largest on Video-Convert (58.9% vs. 31.2%) and AIST++ (88.1% vs. 67.6%). The framework achieves this with fewer than 400 training sequences, roughly 1/10 the size of AMASS. For the MaskedMimic tracker, loop1 also surpasses the AMASS baseline on all four test sets (e.g., 65.8% vs. 57.2% on Kungfu, 83.9% vs. 68.9% on AIST++).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole explicit baseline is a PHC tracker trained on the full AMASS dataset. The CLAIMS framework at L6 outperforms this baseline on every test set: Kungfu (60.3% vs. 47.1%, +13.2 pp), EMDB (64.4% vs. 53.3%, +11.1 pp), AIST++ (88.1% vs. 67.6%, +20.5 pp), and Video-Convert (58.9% vs. 31.2%, +27.7 pp), yielding a 18.6 pp average improvement. Notably, this is achieved with fewer than 400 synthesized training sequences versus AMASS's full corpus (~10× smaller). The framework also generalizes to a second tracker paradigm: with MaskedMimic, loop1 beats the AMASS-trained baseline on all four sets (e.g., 65.8% vs. 57.2% on Kungfu, 83.9% vs. 68.9% on AIST++), confirming the method is tracker-agnostic. The iterative progression is monotonic in aggregate: L0 (55.9%) already approaches the AMASS baseline, L1 (64.0%) surpasses it, and subsequent loops continue climbing to L6 (76.9%), demonstrating that the closed-loop curriculum, not raw data volume, drives the gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the MDM motion diffusion model for text-conditioned synthesis and the PHC/MaskedMimic RL tracking pipelines, but departs from the standard paradigm of training on a fixed, pre-collected motion corpus. Where prior iterative data-generation work (PARC, RoboTwin 2.0) relies on single success-rate or image-metric feedback, CLAIMS introduces a multi-dimensional observation vector fusing physical tracking metrics with VLM-based subjective difficulty scoring, driven by a Gemini CoT policy that selects progressively harder prompts from a structured five-domain variable library. The competitive co-evolution framing—treating data difficulty and controller competence as opposing players in a game-like loop—distinguishes it from both static-distribution datasets (AMASS, AIST++, Humanoid-X) and prior closed-loop character control that lacks difficulty stratification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a core bottleneck in physics-based humanoid control: the fixed difficulty ceiling imposed by static training distributions, which causes controllers to fail on professional high-dynamic motions. The 45% failure-rate reduction using roughly 1/10 of AMASS's data, confirmed by a size-matched non-iterative ablation, suggests that curriculum structure matters more than raw data volume for pushing capability boundaries. However, synthesis quality is bounded by MDM's capacity on extreme dynamics, the variable library is manually curated without objective calibration, and cross-tracker validation (MaskedMimic) is limited to two loops, leaving open whether the approach scales to more extreme acrobatic skills or fully generalizes across diverse tracker architectures.
