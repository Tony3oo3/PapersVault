## Problem

Audio-Visual LLMs (AVLLMs) have achieved strong multimodal understanding, yet their ability to perform fine-grained temporal reasoning by jointly integrating audio and visual streams remains largely unexplored. The authors observed that adding audio to vision-language models for Moment Retrieval often degrades performance, and that existing AVLLMs also struggle with cross-modal temporal alignment, revealing a critical gap in how current models synchronize and reason over multimodal temporal information.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261912/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

FAVE operates on ~150-second videos from QVHighlights, with speech as the primary audio modality (~80%) supplemented by manually annotated environmental sounds (~20%). The three sub-tasks have distinct I/O formats: FAVE-align takes a single-modality segment (visual or audio description) and requires the model to produce the counterpart caption, scored via GPT 5-point similarity; FAVE-low presents two event captions and asks for a binary temporal label (order, proximity, or position), scored by accuracy; FAVE-high provides a video segment identified by relative time percentages (not absolute timestamps) and requires an open-ended caption, scored on vision, audio, and overall 5-point relevance. Events are short (most under 20 seconds) with captions averaging 78 words, emphasizing fine-grained detail.

## Method

FAVE is constructed via a scalable pipeline: TransNetV2 shot boundary detection segments videos, LongVA and InternVL2.5 generate visual captions, Whisper and 3D-Speaker handle speech recognition and multi-speaker discrimination, and environmental sounds are manually annotated. A GPT-based event synthesis step produces structured [start, end, caption] annotations with modality-specific QA pairs, followed by a quality-control filter that removes QA pairs whose temporal order is guessable from caption text alone, forcing genuine multimodal grounding. All annotations undergo two-round verification by 15 trained annotators (~85% inter-annotator agreement). The benchmark is organized into three levels—FAVE-align (V2A/A2V cross-modal synchronization), FAVE-low (event sequencing, proximity, and positioning), and FAVE-high (Moment-to-Caption)—to separately probe alignment, basic temporal reasoning, and fine-grained multimodal synthesis. Evaluation of thirteen SoTA models (including Gemini 1.5 Pro/Flash, VideoLLaMA2, Qwen2.5-Omni, and vision-only TimeChat/VTimeLLM) reveals that no model achieves effective joint audio-visual temporal understanding, and that increasing frame sampling does not improve temporal awareness, pointing to architectural rather than input-side limitations.

## Results

On the FAVE benchmark, Gemini 1.5 Flash achieves the highest scores across all three levels: 4.04/5 on FAVE-align (cross-modal synchronization), 80.0% on FAVE-low event order, and 2.81/5 on FAVE-high overall captioning. Among open-source models, Qwen2.5-Omni leads on FAVE-low (73.73% order, 68.69% position) and LongVALE leads on FAVE-align A2V (3.53/5). The human baseline scores 4.63/5 (align), 88.98% (low), and 4.23/5 (high), revealing a substantial gap: even the best model (Gemini 1.5 Flash) trails humans by 0.59 points on alignment, 8.98 points on temporal reasoning, and 1.42 points on moment captioning. No evaluated model achieves effective joint audio-visual temporal integration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini 1.5 Flash and Pro dominate all FAVE sub-tasks, with Flash scoring 4.04/5 on FAVE-align (vs. 3.25 for the best open-source LongVALE) and 80.0% on FAVE-low order (vs. 73.73% for Qwen2.5-Omni). Among open-source models, Qwen2.5-Omni (interleaved paradigm) leads on FAVE-low temporal reasoning (73.73% order, 68.69% position), while LongVALE and VideoLLaMA2 lead on FAVE-align A2V (3.53 and 3.41 respectively), likely benefiting from audio-video joint training. On FAVE-high moment captioning, ChatBridge (2.36 vision) approaches VTimeLLM (2.40), a vision-only model with dedicated temporal awareness, yet no open-source AVLLM matches Gemini 1.5 Flash (2.74 vision, 2.66 audio). The interleaved paradigm (Qwen2.5-Omni, video-SALMONN) does not consistently outperform sequential models, suggesting that token interleaving alone does not resolve cross-modal alignment. Critically, all models fall well below the human baseline (4.63 align, 88.98% low, 4.23 high), and the paper's ablation (Figure 5) shows that increasing sampled frames does not improve temporal scores, pointing to architectural rather than input-side limitations.

## Relation to Prior Work

FAVE carves new ground by unifying three previously fragmented evaluation dimensions—cross-modal temporal alignment, event-level temporal relationships, and moment-level captioning—into a single hierarchical benchmark grounded in audio-visual video. Prior benchmarks either target a single modality (QVHighlights, ActivityNet Caps, AudioCap) or incorporate both audio and vision but with limited task diversity and coarse temporal granularity (AVEL, UnAV-100, VALOR, LongVALE). The construction pipeline repurposes existing components (TransNetV2 for shot detection, Whisper for ASR, 3D-Speaker for diarization, LongVA/InternVL2.5 for captioning) but introduces a GPT-assisted multi-level QA generation step and a rigorous modality-parity verification protocol that prior benchmarks lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FAVE provides the first structured, multi-level diagnostic for a critical and underexplored capability in AVLLMs: fine-grained joint audio-visual temporal reasoning. The finding that increasing sampled frames does not improve temporal scores across models suggests the bottleneck is architectural (how temporal features are encoded and fused) rather than informational, redirecting future work toward better temporal encoders and fusion strategies rather than denser sampling. Key open questions remain: how to balance cross-modal temporal alignment with within-modality coherence (especially for interleaved token schemes), and whether the sequential audio-after-visual processing paradigm is fundamentally limiting. The benchmark's reliance on GPT-based scoring for open-ended tasks introduces a potential circularity concern that future work should address with more diverse evaluation protocols.
