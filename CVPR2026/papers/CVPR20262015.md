## Problem

Training generalist embodied agents for video games has been hindered by the absence of large, diverse, action-labeled datasets: prior behavior-cloning work relies on expensive manual demonstrations limited to a few titles, RL agents are narrow and require dedicated simulators, and LLM-based approaches depend on hand-crafted APIs or complex perception modules. No open framework existed to support both the training and evaluation of multi-game vision-action policies at internet scale.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a single 256×256 RGB frame as context (no multi-frame conditioning, as the authors found no benefit from additional past frames) and produces a 16-step action chunk via flow matching. Each timestep in the chunk is a 20-dimensional vector: a 16-dimensional binary component encoding standardized gamepad buttons (4 d-pad, 4 face, 2 shoulders, 2 triggers, 2 joystick thumbs, start, back) and a 4-dimensional continuous component for left/right joystick x/y positions normalized to [-1, 1]. This unified action layout is shared across all 1,000+ games, enabling direct policy transfer without per-game action remapping.

## Method

NITROGEN is a vision-action transformer adapted from GR00T N1 (with language and state encoders removed), pairing a SigLIP 2 vision encoder that produces 256 image tokens per frame with a diffusion transformer (DiT) action head that generates 16-step action chunks via conditional flow matching. The key data contribution is a three-stage pipeline that extracts frame-level gamepad actions from 40,000 hours of publicly available gameplay videos featuring input overlay software: (1) SIFT/XFeat template matching against ~300 controller templates to localize and crop the overlay, (2) a fine-tuned SegFormer segmentation network that predicts joystick positions on an 11×11 grid and binary button states from consecutive frame pairs, and (3) quality filtering that discards low-action-density segments and masks the on-screen controller to prevent shortcut learning. Training uses the standard conditional flow-matching objective with AdamW, a warmup-stable-decay schedule, image augmentations (brightness, contrast, rotation, crops), and EMA weights (decay 0.9999), with inference performed over 16 denoising steps.

## Results

NITROGEN, trained on 40,000 hours of gameplay across 1,000+ games, achieves non-trivial zero-shot task completion rates across diverse 2D and 3D games without any fine-tuning (Figure 5). When fine-tuned on held-out games, pre-training yields up to 52% relative improvement in combat task success rates (3D action-RPG) and ~10% average improvement on an isometric roguelike, compared to training from scratch with identical data and compute. In a self-imitation experiment on a 2D metroidvania boss fight, success rate climbs from 18.7% (base) to 53.9% (one round) and 90.5% (three rounds). The action extraction pipeline achieves R²=0.84 for joystick positions and 0.96 button accuracy across controller families.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare NITROGEN against other published gaming-agent methods; instead, the primary comparison is between fine-tuning from NITROGEN's pre-trained weights versus training an identical architecture from scratch on the same held-out game data and compute budget. On a 3D action-RPG, pre-training provides a 25% average relative improvement, with combat tasks benefiting most (52% relative gain) and game-specific tasks showing only marginal gains (5%). On an isometric roguelike, the average improvement is smaller (~10%), likely because its visual style and mechanics are underrepresented in the training distribution. A secondary comparison within the self-imitation experiment shows that one round of self-imitation (53.9%) substantially outperforms fine-tuning on the original behavior-cloning data alone (30.1%), indicating that the gains come from the model's own improved rollouts rather than merely additional task-specific data exposure.

## Relation to Prior Work

NITROGEN adapts the GR00T N1 architecture (a vision-action flow-matching model for humanoid robots) to the gaming domain, removing language and state encoders and replacing the robot action head with a unified 20-dimensional gamepad action space. It departs from prior behavior-cloning gaming agents (VPT, MineRL, SIMA) by replacing expensive human-collected or RL-generated demonstrations with a novel data source: publicly available gameplay videos featuring input-overlay software, enabling scaling from a single game (Minecraft in VPT) to over 1,000 titles. The universal Gymnasium harness for wrapping commercial games also distinguishes it from prior work that either relies on hand-crafted APIs (Voyager, Cradle) or dedicated simulators (Dreamer, AlphaStar).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By releasing a 40,000-hour multi-game dataset, a 30-task/10-game benchmark with a universal Gymnasium API, and pretrained model weights, NITROGEN lowers the barrier for the community to develop and evaluate generalist gaming agents without collecting proprietary data. Key limitations remain: the model is a fast-reacting system-1 policy with no language conditioning or long-horizon planning, the dataset is biased toward action games played with gamepads, and zero-shot transfer to truly unseen games is not yet demonstrated. Open questions include whether the self-imitation loop can be automated with learned rewards, whether language conditioning can be reintroduced post-hoc, and whether the approach extends to keyboard-centric genres (strategy, simulation).
