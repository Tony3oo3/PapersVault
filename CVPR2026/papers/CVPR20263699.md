## Problem

Existing human motion generation models treat hands as rigid end-effectors, missing fine-grained finger articulation, contact timing, and bimanual coordination. Hand-centric datasets lack high-fidelity bimanual sequences with rich finger dynamics, and no established evaluation protocol exists for hand contact fidelity or inter-hand coordination, making it difficult to measure progress in dexterous motion synthesis.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263699/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes structured text prompts decomposed into three channels—left-hand, right-hand, and inter-hand descriptions—generated at multiple granularity levels (concise, balanced, comprehensive). Optional spatiotemporal conditions (sparse keyframes, wrist trajectories, or one hand's motion) are injected via partial denoising at inference. The output is a per-frame 3D joint coordinate sequence for both hands (2J×3) augmented with 1-DoF rotation scalars, optionally post-optimized to MANO parameters for mesh rendering.

## Method

The paper benchmarks two generative paradigms on the HandX dataset. The diffusion model uses x-prediction with separate T5-encoded text embeddings for left, right, and inter-hand prompts, each augmented with a learnable CLS token to prevent cross-hand confusion; these are cross-attended with motion embeddings and fused via residual connections, while an inference-time partial denoising strategy enables versatile conditioning (in-betweening, keyframe guidance, hand-reaction synthesis). The autoregressive model employs a local motion representation (relative wrist vectors, local joint positions/velocities, rotation scalars) tokenized by FSQ, with a text-prefix architecture where T5 text tokens receive bidirectional attention and motion tokens receive causal attention. A key annotation pipeline decouples kinematic feature extraction (finger flexion, contact events, inter-hand distances segmented into temporal events as structured JSON) from LLM-based semantic reasoning, enabling scalable multi-level captioning without manual annotation. Scaling experiments across data fractions (5%–100%) and model depths reveal a log-linear relationship between R-Precision and FLOPs (r²=0.96), with performance saturating beyond a moderate capacity threshold.

## Results

On the HandX benchmark, the best diffusion model (12 Transformer layers, 100% data) achieves R-Precision Top-1 of 0.427, Top-3 of 0.631, FID of 1.349, and intra-hand contact F1 of 0.641, compared to ground-truth values of 0.854, 0.948, 0.000, and 0.984 respectively. The best autoregressive model (215.31M params, 4096 codebook) reaches R-Precision Top-1 of 0.281, FID of 1.721, and contact F1 of 0.605. A clear log-linear scaling relationship is observed between R-Precision and FLOPs (correlation coefficient 0.96), with the formula R_prec = 0.4391 × log₁₀(FLOPS) − 3.8707. Scaling beyond 12 layers (to 16 layers or 6.7× more parameters) causes performance degradation, indicating a saturation point.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baseline methods; it is a dataset and benchmark contribution that evaluates two internal model paradigms (diffusion vs. autoregressive) on the HandX dataset. Between the two paradigms, the diffusion model (12 layers, full data) outperforms the best AR model (215.31M, 4096 codebook) on R-Precision Top-1 (0.427 vs. 0.281), FID (1.349 vs. 1.721), and contact F1 (0.641 vs. 0.605), though the AR model achieves a slightly lower matching distance in some configurations. Within the diffusion family, scaling from 4 to 12 layers with full data improves R-Precision Top-1 from 0.168 to 0.427 and contact F1 from 0.517 to 0.641, but further scaling to 16 layers degrades performance (R-Precision drops to 0.382), revealing a clear saturation point. For the AR model, jointly scaling both codebook size and model capacity is necessary: the 4.63M model with 1024 codebook achieves the best R-Precision (0.384), while the 215.31M model with 4096 codebook achieves the best FID (1.721), but scaling only one dimension while fixing the other tends to degrade results.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of human motion diffusion models (Tevet et al., ICLR 2023; Xu et al., ICCV 2023) and autoregressive motion generation with FSQ (Lu et al., CVPR 2025; Fan et al., ICCV 2025), but departs from both lines by restricting the generation target to fine-grained bimanual hand motion rather than whole-body or hand-object sequences. The key conceptual departure is the annotation pipeline: rather than relying on template-based labels or monocular reconstruction (as in CLUTCH, BOBSL3DT, or GigaHands), it decouples structured kinematic feature extraction from LLM-based semantic reasoning to produce multi-level, contact-aware descriptions. The benchmark itself is novel in jointly evaluating diffusion and AR paradigms under a unified bimanual protocol with dedicated contact metrics, whereas prior hand-motion work (HandDiffuse, BOTH2Hands, InterHand2.6M) either lacks language supervision or provides only coarse/action-level annotations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HandX fills a concrete data gap: 54.2 hours of contact-rich bimanual motion with fine-grained, multi-level text annotations that no prior dataset provides at this fidelity, making it a likely reference benchmark for future hand-motion research. The demonstrated log-linear scaling relationship (R² = 0.96) between R-precision and FLOPS, together with the observation that over-scaling the diffusion model beyond 12 layers degrades performance, gives practitioners a practical compute budgeting guideline. Open questions include whether the LLM-annotation pipeline generalizes to motion types outside the captured distribution, whether the 2 cm contact threshold is appropriate across all interaction types, and whether the scaling trends persist at substantially larger data and model scales than those tested here. The demonstrated transfer to a humanoid platform with dexterous robot hands hints at broader embodied-AI applications but remains a single demonstration rather than a systematic study.
