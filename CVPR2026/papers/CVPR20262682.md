## Problem

Most data-centric RFT methods rely on static or heuristic sample selection (e.g., reward variance, difficulty scores), implicitly assuming each sample's contribution to policy improvement is fixed throughout training. This overlooks the non-stationary dynamics of policy learning, where a sample's true marginal impact shifts as the policy evolves, leading to suboptimal updates and inefficient credit assignment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262682/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on minibatches of 128 multimodal (image + text) prompt–ground-truth pairs drawn from the MM-Eureka corpus, using a base VLM policy (Qwen2.5-VL-7B/32B) and binary correctness rewards. At each optimization step it consumes the per-sample policy gradients already computed during standard RFT backpropagation and produces a per-sample importance vector and a reweighted aggregate gradient that replaces the vanilla batch gradient for the parameter update. No separate surrogate model or pre-selection pass is required.

## Method

DIEM integrates two lightweight operations into every RFT iteration. First, a gradient-alignment importance estimator scores each sample by the inner product between its individual policy gradient and the aggregate batch gradient (Eq. 4), yielding a theoretically bounded proxy for the sample's true marginal contribution to batch reward (Proposition 2, error O(ηℓ² + 2ηℓA_max)). Second, a constrained reweighting problem maximizes the weighted sum of importance scores subject to preserving the L2 norm of the aggregate gradient (Eq. 6); this is solved in closed form via Lagrange multipliers as W* = (P⁻¹I)/(√C · √(IᵀP⁻¹I)) where P is the N×N Gram matrix of per-sample gradients (Eq. 7), followed by clipping negative weights to zero. Because all required quantities are byproducts of the standard backpropagation step, the total overhead is only ~1.2% of training time. The dynamic reweighting naturally induces an easy-to-hard curriculum: as the policy saturates on simpler examples, their weights decay while harder examples receive increasing emphasis, without any hand-designed scheduling.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-VL-7B, DIEM achieves a 61.8% average across six multimodal reasoning benchmarks (MathVista, MathVerse, MathVision, MMStar, MMMU, AI2D), surpassing Vanilla RFT (59.1%) by 2.7 points and the best dynamic baseline SPEED-RL (60.0%) by 1.8 points. On the larger Qwen2.5-VL-32B, DIEM reaches 67.3% average, a 2.4-point gain over Vanilla RFT (64.9%) and 1.7 points over SPEED-RL (65.6%), while winning all six individual benchmarks. The 7B DIEM model (61.8%) also exceeds the average of the commercial GPT-4o model (60.9%). DIEM introduces only ~1.2% additional training overhead (71.2 h vs. 70.3 h for vanilla GRPO), far less than PCL (79.1 h), SPEED-RL (94.6 h), or LIMR/HVS (122.0 h).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DIEM outperforms all five data-selection baselines (LIMR, HVS, SPEED-RL, PCL, and Vanilla RFT) on the average score for both model scales. Against the strongest dynamic baseline SPEED-RL, DIEM gains +1.8 points on 7B (61.8 vs. 60.0) and +1.7 points on 32B (67.3 vs. 65.6); against the strongest static baseline HVS, the margins are +2.3 (7B) and +3.4 (32B). On the 32B model, DIEM wins every individual benchmark, with the largest single-benchmark gain on MathVerse (+4.1 over SPEED-RL, +5.3 over Vanilla RFT). In the cross-algorithm generalization test (Table 2, MathVerse/32B), DIEM leads all baselines under GRPO, PPO, Reinforce++, and TRPO, with the largest gap under GRPO (58.0 vs. 54.1 for PCL/LIMR, +3.9). In terms of training efficiency, DIEM adds only 0.9 h (~1.3%) overhead, whereas LIMR and HVS each require 122.0 h and SPEED-RL requires 94.6 h, making DIEM roughly 1.7× faster than SPEED-RL and 1.7× faster than PCL in wall-clock time.

## Relation to Prior Work

DIEM extends the data-selection-for-RFT line of work (LIMR, HVS for static selection; PCL, SPEED-RL for dynamic selection) by replacing heuristic importance proxies (reward variance, pass-rate distance, auxiliary difficulty models) with a gradient-alignment estimator grounded in influence-function theory. It departs from prior dynamic methods in two ways: the importance signal is computed internally from the policy's own gradients rather than by an external evaluator, and the reweighting step is formulated as a constrained optimization (preserving gradient magnitude) with a closed-form solution rather than a hand-designed scheduling rule. The theoretical error bound (Proposition 2) draws on Lipschitz-continuity arguments from supervised influence-function literature but explicitly avoids convexity and stationarity assumptions, making it applicable to the non-convex early-training regime of RFT.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is the near-zero overhead (1.2%) combined with consistent 1–6% gains across six multimodal reasoning benchmarks and four RFT algorithms, making DIEM a drop-in module for existing GRPO/PPO pipelines without requiring a separate surrogate model or pre-training pass. The emergent easy-to-hard curriculum (Sec. 5.3) is a useful diagnostic showing that gradient alignment implicitly encodes difficulty progression, but it remains to be verified whether this property holds for text-only LLMs, larger model scales, or non-binary reward structures. Open questions include how the method behaves under very small batch sizes (where the Gram matrix inversion becomes ill-conditioned) and whether the theoretical bound is tight enough to guarantee monotonic improvement in practice.
