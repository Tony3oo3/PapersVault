## Problem

Existing 3D scene reconstruction methods (Gaussian Splatting, NeRF) assume stable, achromatic illumination, yet real-world scenes in events, performances, and decorative settings are captured under artificially varying, multi-colored 'disco' lighting that introduces severe photometric and geometric ambiguities. No prior method simultaneously reconstructs a 3D scene and recovers its canonical appearance from inputs under such dynamic colored light without relying on color priors, spatial masks, or scene-specific assumptions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261331/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a video of up to 250 frames at 800×450 resolution captured by a moving camera under a stationary but temporally varying colored light source, along with COLMAP-derived camera poses and a sparse point cloud for initialization. At inference, it renders canonical novel views from the optimized Gaussians with a user-controllable brightness scalar applied to the per-Gaussian brightness factor B, enabling simulation of low-light to over-bright conditions. Chroma-Net is discarded after training, so test-time rendering requires only the rasterizer and the optimized Gaussian parameters.

## Method

Disco-GS extends each 3D Gaussian with two new attributes: an intrinsic color feature vector i_k (encoded via spherical harmonics for view-dependence) and a scalar brightness control b_k, so that the canonical image is factorized as J_can(v) = I(v) ∘ B. A lightweight 3-layer CNN called Chroma-Net, conditioned on the observed color image and B, estimates the effective per-pixel transient light E(t) such that J(v,t) = J_can(v) ∘ E(t), enabling self-supervised photometric matching without any color or mask priors. Training combines an L1 photometric loss with an SSIM structural loss (using a decaying exponent η to prevent color leakage into the canonical image), a channel-mean color regularizer, a blurred-brightness regularizer anchoring B near 0.5, and a total-variation loss that keeps E(t) spatially smooth. The entire pipeline is single-stage and end-to-end, converging in ~66 minutes and achieving ~70 FPS inference on an RTX 3090.

## Results

On the Disco dataset (6 scenes), Disco-GS achieves PSNR ranging from 19.90 (Mini-library) to 26.11 (3D-Artwork), SSIM from 0.88 to 0.94, and LPIPS from 0.12 to 0.23, consistently outperforming all four baselines on every scene and metric. The largest gains appear on the 2D-Artwork scene, where Disco-GS reaches 22.24 PSNR / 0.93 SSIM / 0.12 LPIPS versus 12.52 / 0.22 / 0.98 for 3DGS and 7.71 / 0.01 / 0.91 for Wild-Gaussians. Training takes only 66 minutes per scene and inference runs at ~70 FPS on an RTX 3090 at 800×450 resolution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Disco-GS outperforms all four baselines on every scene and every metric (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS). Against 3DGS [12], the gains are substantial on color-heavy scenes: +9.72 dB PSNR on 2D-Artwork (22.24 vs 12.52) and +5.46 dB on Mini-library (19.90 vs 14.44), while 3DGS hallucinates colors from training frames. Against Wild-Gaussians [13], Disco-GS leads by +14.53 dB on 2D-Artwork and +16.89 dB on Casual (23.52 vs 6.64), where Wild-Gaussians' per-image embeddings fail to generalize to unseen color conditions. Against Gaussians-Wild [35], the closest competitor, Disco-GS still wins by +3.35 dB on Newspaper-room and +1.34 dB on 2D-Artwork, while Gaussians-Wild introduces a pink tint and fails on the Casual scene (8.08 vs 23.52 PSNR). RNG [7] (relightable neural Gaussians) collapses under varying color inputs, scoring below 8 dB PSNR on all scenes, confirming that relighting-oriented GS methods are ill-suited for disco-style illumination.

## Relation to Prior Work

Disco-GS extends 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) by augmenting each Gaussian primitive with intrinsic color features (encoded via spherical harmonics) and a brightness control factor, while introducing Chroma-Net—a lightweight 3-layer CNN—to estimate per-pixel transient chromatic illumination during training. It departs from prior illumination-robust reconstruction methods (WildGaussians, Gaussian in the Wild, RNG) that target outdoor scenes with natural lighting variations, instead addressing the more complex and localized challenge of artificial disco-style colored lighting. The brightness and color regularizers draw conceptual inspiration from zero-reference low-light enhancement, but are repurposed here to disentangle canonical scene appearance from transient color effects in a self-supervised GS pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Disco-GS is the first method to jointly perform 3D scene reconstruction and canonical appearance recovery under artificially varying colored light, and the introduced Disco dataset (25 real-world videos) fills a gap in publicly available benchmarks for this setting. The practical brightness manipulation capability at inference time (scaling B) offers a simple tool for simulating low-light or overexposed conditions. Key limitations remain: the method is restricted to static scenes with a fixed light source position, and generalization to dynamic scenes, moving light sources, or more complex multi-source lighting is unaddressed. Whether the per-pixel multiplicative light model (Eq. 2) suffices for highly specular or subsurface-scattering materials under extreme color shifts is an open question.
