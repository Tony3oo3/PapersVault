## Problem

Existing hallucination detection methods for MLLMs operate at coarse granularity—classifying entire sentences or broad object-level errors into only a few categories—making it impossible to pinpoint specific erroneous spans, classify their error types, or generate targeted corrections. This paper introduces a fine-grained task that requires span-level detection, six-category error classification (Object, Attribute, Number, Text, Relation, Fact), and span-level editing, and shows that even GPT-4o and Llama-3.2 perform poorly on it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262803/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes three inputs: an MLLM-generated description, the corresponding RGB image, and a human-written reference caption (from the DCI dataset) that serves as a reliable ground-truth anchor. The output is a structured tagging sequence where each hallucinated span is enclosed in type-specific tags (e.g., <object>, <attribute>), accompanied by an error-type label from the six-category taxonomy and, in the editing setting, a suggested replacement span. The deterministic tagging function guarantees that unedited portions of the original text are preserved verbatim, which is critical for practical deployment.

## Method

ZINA is a two-stage pipeline that decouples token copying from hallucination detection and editing. A detector MLLM (Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Instruct) identifies hallucinated spans and assigns error types, while a deterministic function T inserts the appropriate tags—eliminating the exposure-bias vulnerability of autoregressive tag generation. A reviewer MLLM then validates each tagged span in its surrounding context and, for the editing task, generates suitable refinements. For training data, the authors introduce a graph-based synthetic augmentation method: o3-mini injects errors into hallucination-free captions while explicitly capturing inter-error dependencies (e.g., a hallucinated object inducing downstream attribute or relation errors), and a DAG is constructed and pruned to produce diverse training samples with varying error combinations. Both MLLMs are fine-tuned with cross-entropy loss using a 3-shot prompting setup.

## Results

On the VisionHall dataset, ZINA achieves an F1 of 45.15 for fine-grained hallucination detection, outperforming the strongest baseline GPT-4o (29.37) by 15.8 points and Llama-3.2-90B-Vision-Instruct (16.92) by 28.2 points. For the editing task, ZINA scores 44.02 on BERT-F1 and 50.39 on CLIP-F1, surpassing GPT-4o (24.89 / 30.19) by 19.1 and 20.2 points respectively. On the out-of-domain MHaluBench dataset, ZINA achieves 87.15 claim-level F1 and 91.46 segment-level F1, outperforming GPT-based UniHD (83.89 / 88.98) and GPT-based Self-Check (85.83 / 80.98).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ZINA decisively outperforms all nine baseline families on the VisionHall detection task (F1). The largest gaps are against Llama-3.2-90B-Vision-Instruct (+28.2 F1) and Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Instruct (+23.8 F1), while the closest competitor GPT-4o trails by 15.8 F1 points. On editing metrics (BERT-F1, CLIP-F1), ZINA leads GPT-4o by 19.1 and 20.2 points respectively, though CLIP-S and PAC-S gaps are smaller (0.34 and 0.37), suggesting the editing quality in terms of overall caption fidelity is comparable across strong MLLMs. On the out-of-domain MHaluBench benchmark, ZINA beats GPT-based UniHD by 3.26 claim-level F1 and 2.48 segment-level F1, and outperforms GPT-based Self-Check (n=2) by 1.32 and 10.48 points at claim and segment level respectively. Smaller open-source baselines (LLaVA-1.5, Qwen2-VL-7B, LLaVA-OV-Qwen2-7B) score below 5 F1 on detection, indicating that fine-grained hallucination tagging remains far beyond the capability of current open MLLMs without dedicated training.

## Relation to Prior Work

ZINA extends the fine-grained hallucination detection and editing paradigm introduced by FAVA (Mishra et al., COLM 2024) from text-only LLMs to the multimodal setting, where hallucinations are grounded in visual content. It departs from prior multimodal hallucination detection methods (POPE, AMBER, MHalDetect, UniHD, HalLocalizer) that operate at the sentence or object level by introducing span-level detection with a six-category taxonomy and explicit editing targets. The key methodological departure is a two-stage decoupling strategy—separating a detector MLLM from a reviewer MLLM and delegating token copying to a deterministic function—rather than the single-model tag-insertion approach used in FAVA and similar prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The VisionHall dataset (6.9k human-annotated + 20k synthetic samples) is the first to provide span-level hallucination annotations with taxonomy labels and editing targets for MLLM outputs, filling a gap left by coarse-grained benchmarks like AMBER and MHaluBench. The finding that even GPT-4o achieves only ~29 F1 on detection underscores that fine-grained multimodal hallucination detection remains largely unsolved, motivating continued research. Open questions include whether the two-stage architecture's high inference cost can be reduced for real-time deployment, whether the six-category taxonomy is exhaustive, and whether the graph-based synthetic data approach generalizes to other error-dependent generation tasks.
