## Problem

Existing multimodal continual learning methods treat cross-modal alignment as a static constraint enforced at specific layers, ignoring that alignment is inherently dynamic—shifting with task increments and propagating through the network hierarchy. Small misalignments introduced in shallow layers during multi-task training accumulate and amplify in deeper layers, ultimately causing catastrophic forgetting. The paper asks how to explicitly model and regularize this layer-wise, task-wise evolution of modality alignment to stabilize continual learning.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images paired with text prompts through a frozen CLIP-style vision-language backbone, producing a class label as output. Only 1.8M task-specific prompt parameters are learned per task and stored in a prompt pool; at inference, the appropriate prompt is retrieved by matching the input feature against per-task Gaussian distributions via KL divergence. The model operates under the Domain Class Incremental Learning setting on the MTIL benchmark (11 datasets, 1201 total classes), where both label space and data distribution shift across sequential tasks.

## Method

DAGR constructs a Dynamic Alignment Graph per task whose nodes are K centroids obtained by clustering the fused cross-modal features (α·Z_img + (1−α)·Z_txt) from each cross-attention layer. Intra-layer edges are built via head-wise softmax with a temperature parameter to capture local token dependencies, while inter-layer edges combine cosine similarity with attention-rollout transition matrices to model representation propagation across layers. Three KL-divergence-based regularization losses—edge consistency (single-hop), multi-hop edge consistency (2- and 3-step propagation), and path consistency (inter-layer rollout transitions)—are applied only to old-class subgraphs identified by a binary mask, constraining prior knowledge while leaving new-class subgraphs flexible. The backbone remains frozen throughout; all regularization is realized through the task-specific prompts, and the loss coefficients are decayed across layers (stronger in shallow layers) to preferentially prevent early misalignments from propagating downward.

## Results

On the MTIL benchmark (11 datasets, 1201 classes, Domain Class Incremental Learning), DAGR achieves 79.4% Avg. and 87.1% Last accuracy using only 1.8M trainable parameters and no extra data. This outperforms the strongest full-parameter baseline ZSCL (75.4% Avg., 83.6% Last, 211M params, with rehearsal buffer) by +4.0% and +3.5%, respectively. Among parameter-efficient methods, DAGR surpasses C-CLIP (77.8/85.9) by +1.6/+1.2 and DIKI (76.3/85.1) by +3.1/+2.0, while using the same 1.8M parameter budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DAGR outperforms all 11 baselines on both Avg. and Last metrics across the MTIL benchmark. Against the closest parameter-efficient competitors, DAGR beats C-CLIP (77.8/85.9) by +1.6/+1.2 and DIKI (76.3/85.1) by +3.1/+2.0, while using the same 1.8M trainable parameters and no extra data. Against full-parameter fine-tuning methods (LwF, iCaRL, LwF-VR, WiSE-FT, ZSCL), DAGR gains +4.0 to +18.7% on Avg. and +3.5 to +12.5% on Last, despite using 100–120× fewer parameters and no rehearsal buffer. Against prompt-based methods (L2P, DualPrompt, S-Prompts), DAGR achieves +11.1 to +11.5% on Avg. and +3.7 to +5.1% on Last, indicating that simple prompt reuse is insufficient for handling the distribution shifts in MTIL. The adapter-based MoEAdapters (84M params) is outperformed by +4.0/+2.9, confirming that graph-level regularization provides a more effective stability mechanism than task-specific adapter routing.

## Relation to Prior Work

DAGR builds on the prompt-based multimodal continual learning paradigm (L2P, DualPrompt, S-Prompts, DIKI) but departs from the prevailing assumption that modality alignment can be enforced as a static, top-level constraint. It draws on recent findings about layer-dependent representation drift and coherent evolution of adjacent Transformer layers to argue that alignment errors introduced in shallow layers propagate and amplify in deeper ones. The method repurposes graph-regularization ideas (KL-divergence on subgraph distributions, attention rollout matrices) in a novel way: rather than regularizing parameters or final logits, it constrains the evolution of cross-modal alignment structure across layers and tasks, operating entirely through task-specific prompts on a frozen backbone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dynamic-alignment framing offers a principled explanation for why static alignment constraints fail in sequential multimodal settings, and the graph-based regularization provides a concrete, lightweight mechanism (1.8M params, no rehearsal buffer) to address it. However, forward forgetting remains only marginally improved (69.3 vs 68.7 transfer score over DIKI), and the paper acknowledges scalability challenges for large-scale, many-task scenarios. Open questions include whether the layer-wise decay of regularization coefficients generalizes to non-Transformer backbones, and whether the clustering-based node construction remains effective when the number of tasks or classes grows substantially.
