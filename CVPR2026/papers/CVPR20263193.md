## Problem

Existing token compression methods for streaming video understanding apply a single, globally fixed pruning or merging policy across the entire visual stream, ignoring the time-dependent utility of memory: recent frames require dense preservation for grounding while distant history tolerates aggressive compression. This leads to under-pruning of long-term context and over-pruning of short-term details critical for causal reasoning, and fixed thresholds fail to adapt to highly variable scene dynamics.

## Input / Output

FluxMem consumes streaming video frames sequentially at 1 fps (up to 256 visual tokens per frame, 256 frames per video for online; 64 tokens per frame, 1024 frames for offline) and produces a text response when a user query arrives or when a proactive scene-change trigger fires. The module is strictly causal and single-pass: each frame is encoded, buffered, and progressively compressed as it moves through the memory hierarchy, with no access to future frames beyond the immediate neighbor used for TAS scoring. The output is a standard LLM text generation conditioned on the concatenated tokens from all three memory levels.

## Method

FluxMem organizes visual tokens into a three-level cascaded memory (short-term: 8 frames, mid-term: 64 frames, long-term: remainder) and applies two lightweight, training-free compression modules at each boundary. At the short-to-mid boundary, Temporal Adjacency Selection (TAS) computes per-token cosine distances to both adjacent frames within a 3×3 spatial window and retains only tokens exceeding an Otsu-derived threshold, removing temporally redundant content. At the mid-to-long boundary, Spatial Domain Consolidation (SDC) builds a sparse 8-connected graph over the TAS-retained tokens, groups them via union-find into connected components, and replaces each component with its mean anchor to eliminate spatial redundancy. All thresholds are computed per-frame at runtime using Otsu's method on the relevant score distribution, making the compression rate self-adaptive to scene dynamics without any manual tuning or learnable parameters. The TAS backward scores also serve as a zero-overhead proactive response trigger, detecting scene switches when the fraction of novel tokens exceeds a sensitivity parameter γ.

## Results

FluxMem achieves 76.4 on StreamingBench real-time and 53.3 on OVO-Bench overall, improving over the Qwen2.5-VL baseline by +2.5 and +3.5 respectively while compressing ~70% of visual tokens. On offline benchmarks, it reaches 65.3 on VideoMME (w/o sub.), 73.1 on MLVU, and 61.1 on LongVideoBench, surpassing the baseline by +2.0, +5.2, and +0.4. Efficiency gains are substantial: 69.9% latency reduction and 34.5% peak GPU memory reduction on OVO-Bench, with only 4.1 ms per-frame overhead for the memory update.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among training-free methods, FluxMem outperforms all competitors across every benchmark: it beats LiveVLM (72.9→76.4 on StreamingBench, 57.3→65.3 on VideoMME, 66.3→73.1 on MLVU), ReKV (69.1→76.4 on StreamingBench), and StreamMem (62.1→65.3 on VideoMME). Against training-based online methods, FluxMem surpasses TimeChat-Online (75.3→76.4 on StreamingBench, 47.6→53.3 on OVO-Bench, 65.4→73.1 on MLVU) and Dispider (67.6→76.4 on StreamingBench, 41.8→53.3 on OVO-Bench), but trails StreamForest by a narrow margin on both online benchmarks (76.4 vs. 77.3 on StreamingBench; 53.3 vs. 55.6 on OVO-Bench). On offline long-video tasks, FluxMem achieves the best scores among all methods: 73.1 on MLVU (vs. StreamForest 69.6, TimeChat-Online 65.4), 65.3 on VideoMME (vs. Qwen2.5-VL 63.3), and 61.1 on LongVideoBench (vs. Qwen2.5-VL 60.7, TimeChat-Online 57.7). Notably, FluxMem is training-free and plug-and-play, whereas StreamForest and TimeChat-Online require task-specific fine-tuning, making FluxMem's competitive online performance particularly strong given its zero-training constraint.

## Relation to Prior Work

FluxMem sits at the intersection of three prior research threads: visual token compression for MLLMs (TimeChat-Online, DTD, Token Merging), KV-cache management for streaming video (ReKV, LiveVLM, StreamMem), and hierarchical memory inspired by cognitive science (Atkinson & Shiffrin). It departs from prior token-compression work by replacing a single global pruning policy with a two-stage, hierarchy-aware pipeline—Temporal Adjacency Selection at the short-to-mid boundary and Spatial Domain Consolidation at the mid-to-long boundary—each governed by per-frame Otsu-derived thresholds borrowed from classical image segmentation. The framework is explicitly training-free and plug-and-play, positioning it as a drop-in module atop any pretrained MLLM rather than a new model architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that a training-free, adaptive memory mechanism can match or exceed training-based streaming MLLMs while cutting latency by ~70% and peak GPU memory by ~35%, FluxMem offers a practical path to deploying video understanding on resource-constrained edge devices (smart glasses, robots, autonomous vehicles). The repurposing of Otsu's method for per-frame token-threshold calibration is a simple but effective idea that eliminates a common hyperparameter-tuning burden. Open questions include whether the fixed three-level hierarchy generalizes to arbitrarily long streams or multi-modal inputs, whether the 3×3 spatial window in TAS/SDC is optimal for high-resolution encoders, and whether the approach transfers cleanly to audio-visual or embodied settings.
