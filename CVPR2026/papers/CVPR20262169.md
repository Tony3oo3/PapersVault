## Problem

Conventional cameras are bandwidth-limited to 30–60 FPS, making them unable to capture rapid scene motion without severe motion blur. Existing computational high-speed imaging methods overcome this by encoding temporal information into a single exposure, but they require specialized per-camera hardware (coded apertures, diffraction gratings, spinning optics) that restricts them to single-view capture, precluding volumetric 3D reconstruction of fast dynamics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262169/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-view color-strobed frames from 8 global-shutter cameras (1280×1024 at 60 FPS), where each frame encodes N=10 high-speed sub-frames as a linear color mixture, along with COLMAP-calibrated camera poses and a per-experiment strobe color dictionary. Background subtraction and per-camera color calibration (via a Spyder color checker) are applied before optimization. The output is a dynamic Gaussian Splatting model parameterized by initial Gaussian states and time-varying deformations, which can render high-speed novel views at 600 FPS (10× temporal upscaling) from arbitrary camera positions.

## Method

The key insight is to encode temporal information in the scene itself by strobing it with a sequence of N distinct RGB colors (generated via PWM pulse-width modulation of three LEDs) during each camera exposure, so that each captured frame is a linear mixture of N high-speed sub-frames with known, distinct colors. For reconstruction, they extend Gaussian-Flow (dynamic 3DGS with polynomial + Fourier deformation) by modifying the rendering function to output single-channel intensity per Gaussian (exploiting the uniform-albedo assumption) and replacing the standard photometric loss with a color-unmixing objective: the estimated captured image is computed as the weighted sum of rendered intensity frames at each strobe timestamp, using the known strobe color coefficients, and L1 loss is minimized against the actual RGB frame. A total-variation depth regularization term is added for geometrically meaningful 3D structure. Strobe colors are selected by sampling LED intensities uniformly on a circle in αβγ space (three 120°-phase-shifted sine waves), and the entire system is synchronized by a single Arduino driving both LED PWM and camera hardware triggers.

## Results

The method achieves a 10× temporal upscaling, decoding 60 FPS camera captures into 600 FPS volumetric renderings using N=10 color-strobed interframes per exposure. In simulation-based evaluation (MAE metric), reconstruction quality degrades noticeably near N=28 interframes due to color overlap, and shows a roughly linear increase in error with ambient light level. The method reliably synthesizes novel views with as few as 6 cameras (8 used in real experiments), and handles non-white albedos with moderate degradation. Real-world experiments with 8 global-shutter cameras at 1280×1024 resolution demonstrate artifact-free high-speed novel-view renderings of spinning disks, Nerf darts, and flying chess pieces.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not include quantitative head-to-head comparisons against prior methods, as it presents first-of-a-kind high-speed volumetric reconstructions. The closest prior works—SpinCam (Chan et al. [3]) and diffraction-based sparse-scene imaging (Sheinin et al. [38])—are compared only qualitatively: the authors note their method requires no mechanically moving parts (unlike SpinCam's rotating diffraction grating), no specialized optics (unlike [38]), and supports multi-view volumetric reconstruction (unlike both, which are single-view). Jaques et al. [13] achieved only 3× temporal upscaling with RGB channels, whereas this method achieves 10× (and potentially more) across multiple simultaneous views. The simulation analysis (Fig. 6) serves as an internal ablation rather than a baseline comparison, characterizing sensitivity to interframe count, ambient light, albedo, camera count, and motion complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Gaussian-Flow [24] for dynamic 3D Gaussian Splatting and 3DGS [14] as the base scene representation, but departs fundamentally from the high-speed computational imaging literature by encoding temporal information in the scene illumination rather than in the camera hardware. Prior high-speed imaging methods (coded apertures, spinning diffraction gratings, per-pixel control) all require augmenting each camera individually, limiting them to single-view capture; here, a single RGB LED strober illuminates the scene, making the encoding invariant to camera optics and trivially scalable to arbitrary multi-view setups. The closest prior work is Chan et al. [3], which also uses color to multiplex interframes, but modulates the camera's PSF via a rotating grating rather than the scene's appearance, and Jaques et al. [13], which uses RGB channels for 3× temporal upscaling but only in a single-view microscopy setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to unify compressive high-speed imaging with volumetric rendering, opening a path toward multi-view high-speed 3D capture using only commodity cameras and a simple LED driver. However, the method is constrained to uniform-albedo objects against a dark background with relatively simple (near-rigid) motion, limiting immediate practical deployment. Key open questions include extending to spatially varying reflectance, handling non-dark backgrounds, and scaling to more complex or articulated motion where the Gaussian-Flow deformation model may be insufficient. The simulation showing reliable novel-view synthesis with as few as six cameras and graceful degradation up to ~28 interframes provides useful design guidance for future hardware prototypes.
