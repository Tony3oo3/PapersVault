## Problem

Conventional multi-projector calibration requires projecting and capturing structured light patterns sequentially for each projector, causing calibration time to scale linearly with the number of projectors. This scalability bottleneck has remained unsolved for over four decades and severely hinders deployment of large-scale systems such as high-brightness stacking, superresolution, light-field, and shadow-suppression displays where dozens or hundreds of projectors share a common projection region.

## Input / Output

The method takes structured light patterns (Gray-code, line-shift, and a binary temporal ID sequence) projected simultaneously from all M projectors onto a planar calibration board with N embedded wide-angle cameras (e.g., 4× Raspberry Pi Camera Module 3 Wide at 4608×2592), captured across multiple board poses. It produces the full intrinsic and extrinsic parameters of every projector simultaneously, along with per-projector homography transformations for alignment. A key unusual aspect is that the 'sensor' is physically embedded in the calibration target itself, and an offline-estimated per-camera homography (M_n) must be applied to map raw camera pixel coordinates to the true board-plane intersection points before feeding correspondences into Zhang's algorithm.

## Method

The core insight is to embed cameras so their optical centers lie on the calibration board plane, converting the board from a diffuse reflector into a directional light collector: light from projector m reaching camera n strikes a specific sensor pixel determined by the incident ray direction, so overlapping patterns from all M projectors remain optically separable in a single capture. Each camera decodes its per-projector pixel coordinate p_m(n) by thresholding intensity, identifying the emitting projector via a binary temporal ID sequence, and decoding Gray-code plus line-shift patterns for subpixel accuracy. A critical technical contribution is a homography-based misalignment compensation: because compound optics prevent the optical center from lying exactly on the board surface, the ray from each projector intersects the board at a projector-dependent point x_n(m) rather than a fixed x_n; this mapping M_n is estimated offline by projecting single-line patterns from one projector at K positions and fitting a projective transform via RANSAC. The resulting 2D-2D correspondences across multiple board poses are fed into Zhang's method to recover all projector parameters simultaneously, reducing the required pattern count from M×(⌈log₂W⌉+⌈log₂H⌉+L) to ⌈log₂M⌉+⌈log₂W⌉+⌈log₂H⌉+L, i.e., from linear to nearly constant in M.

## Results

For three-projector calibration (Table 1), the proposed method with optical-center misalignment compensation achieves RMS reprojection errors of 0.89–0.91 pixels, compared to 0.34–0.39 pixels for the conventional 108-corner method and 0.65–0.76 pixels for the conventional 4-corner method. In the 25-projector scalability experiment, the proposed method requires only 54 simultaneous projection patterns versus 1,100 sequential patterns (95% reduction) for the conventional approach, while producing a sharper aligned image than the conventional setup. Under ~70 klux outdoor sunlight, the proposed method successfully decodes and aligns two simultaneously projecting projectors, whereas the conventional external-camera method fails entirely.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two variants of the conventional external-camera calibration: a 108-corner condition (all 9×12 checkerboard corners) and a 4-corner condition (matching the 4 embedded cameras). The proposed method with compensation (0.89–0.91 px) is less accurate than the conventional 108-corner method (0.34–0.39 px) but more accurate than the conventional 4-corner method (0.65–0.76 px), which is the fairer comparison given the same number of correspondences. Without the misalignment compensation, the proposed method degrades to 2.18–2.47 px, confirming the compensation step is essential. In the 25-projector experiment, the proposed method with compensation actually outperformed the conventional setup, attributed to the external camera being mounted near the ceiling (long distance to board) and thus losing apparent spatial resolution. The key differentiator is scalability: the proposed method reduces pattern count from O(M) to O(1) (54 vs. 1,100 patterns for 25 projectors), a 95% reduction, while maintaining sub-pixel accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Zhang's pinhole-camera calibration framework [33] as its mathematical backbone but departs fundamentally from all prior multi-projector calibration approaches, which necessarily calibrate projectors sequentially to avoid pattern interference on diffusely reflecting surfaces. The key conceptual departure is an inversion of the imaging geometry: instead of external cameras observing structured light reflected off a calibration board, cameras are embedded *into* the board facing the projectors, so that incident direction encodes projector identity on the image sensor. This draws on two prior threads—pinhole-array pattern separation for single-device calibration [26, 28] and embedded light sensors for single-projector registration [14, 16, 24]—but combines them into a new paradigm that, to the authors' knowledge, is the first to enable simultaneous estimation of both intrinsic and extrinsic parameters for multiple projectors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method reduces calibration pattern count from O(M) to O(1) with respect to the number of projectors (demonstrated as a 95% reduction with 25 projectors), directly addressing a scalability bottleneck that has hindered deployment of dense projector arrays for light-field displays, superresolution, and shadow-suppression systems. Practical robustness to extreme ambient light (70 klux outdoor) where conventional external-camera methods fail entirely is a significant operational advantage. Key limitations remain: all projectors must share a common projection region (restoring linear scaling for wide-area non-overlapping systems), non-planar targets are restricted to polyhedral shapes, and ambient-light robustness has only been validated with two projectors. Open questions include whether the directional information captured by the embedded cameras can serve as an additional constraint to reduce the number of required board poses, and whether the approach extends to curved surfaces or larger camera arrays.
