## Problem

Current audio-driven talking face generation methods achieve reasonable lip synchronization but produce uniform, uncontrollable facial motion. They lack fine-grained control over speaking style (e.g., mouth openness, lip pursing, articulation width) and emotional expression (e.g., intensity modulation, region-specific compound emotions), limiting their utility for personalized digital humans and expressive content creation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262934/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method accepts a reference image or video (for identity and pose) plus driving audio at 16 kHz, and optionally a speaking-style reference video or preset style code, and an emotion source (categorical label, audio intonation, text semantics, or reference image). It produces a 512×512 lip-synced talking face video at 30 fps, with adjustable lip-movement scale, per-articulation style editing, emotion intensity scaling, and region-specific compound emotion composition. For video inputs, pose is extracted from the source video; for image inputs, pose is sampled from predefined templates.

## Method

PC-Talk uses implicit keypoints (bound to 2D facial landmarks) as an intermediate representation and decomposes facial animation into a lip-sync deformation D_l and a pure emotional deformation D_e, which are additively combined with the original keypoints before warping and decoding. The LAC module employs a style-aware auto-regressive Transformer (inspired by FaceFormer) that predicts lip-sync deformations conditioned on a style embedding—projected from either a reference video's expression sequence or a preset one-hot code into a shared style space—alongside a custom audio-visual encoder pretrained on 2D audio-visual synchronization. Lip movement scale is controlled by a multiplicative factor on D_l, and per-articulation style editing is achieved by projecting D_l onto articulation-specific deformation vectors (e.g., lip pursing, mouth widening) and rescaling. The EMC module isolates pure emotional deformation by predicting combined deformations for both the target emotion and neutral expression with the same audio, then subtracting the neutral component; this decomposition extends naturally to region-specific compound emotions by independently generating deformations on semantically grouped keypoints (e.g., mouth vs. eyebrows) and composing them. Training uses a Wav2Lip-style sync loss, keypoint L1 loss, regularization, velocity loss, and a style discriminative loss, with the LAC and EMC modules trained separately on HDTF and MEAD respectively.

## Results

On HDTF, PC-Talk achieves the best lip-sync score (LSE-C) of 9.37 with image input and 9.03 with video input, surpassing dedicated lip-sync methods like Wav2Lip (8.64/8.65) and LatentSync (8.92). It also attains the lowest FVD (100.85 video / 205.55 image) and competitive FID (15.51 video / 33.07 image) on HDTF. On MEAD-Neutral, PC-Talk leads in LSE-C (8.29 video / 8.19 image), FID (24.88 video / 35.22 image), and FVD (170.56 video / 153.81 image). For emotional talking face generation (Table 2), PC-Talk achieves the highest emotion accuracy (Acc_emo) of 72.32 on MEAD and 46.19 on HDTF, along with the best E-FID of 1.88 on MEAD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PC-Talk outperforms all 11 baselines on lip-sync (LSE-C) across both HDTF and MEAD-Neutral. Against video-input methods, it beats Wav2Lip by 0.38 LSE-C (9.03 vs 8.65) and LatentSync by 0.11 (9.03 vs 8.92) on HDTF, while also achieving substantially lower FVD (100.85 vs 183.99 and 175.23 respectively). Among image-input methods, PC-Talk leads Sonic (9.37 vs 8.64 LSE-C) and Hallo-v2 (9.37 vs 7.53) on HDTF lip-sync, with FID of 33.07 competitive with Hallo-v2's 33.15. For emotional talking face generation, PC-Talk surpasses ED-Talk (Acc_emo 72.32 vs 57.45 on MEAD) and EAT (72.32 vs 68.21) in emotion accuracy, while also achieving the best E-FID (1.88 vs 2.10 and 2.54). In the user study, PC-Talk scores 4.6–4.9 across all dimensions versus 3.8 (best competitor, Hallo-v2) for lip-sync and 3.8 for overall realism. The method also runs at 30.13 FPS, far exceeding diffusion-based baselines like EchoMimic (0.84 FPS) and Hallo-v2 (0.69 FPS).

## Relation to Prior Work

PC-Talk sits within the multi-stage audio-to-motion-to-image pipeline for talking face generation, adopting the implicit keypoint intermediate representation popularized by Face-vid2vid and LivePortrait, where keypoints are semantically anchored to 2D facial landmarks. It departs from prior keypoint-based methods (EAT, VASA, Ditto) by introducing semantically enriched lip-specific keypoints and a style-aware auto-regressive Transformer (inspired by FaceFormer) for lip-sync prediction, while borrowing the SyncNet-based lip-sync loss from Wav2Lip. The key conceptual departure is framing talking face controllability as two orthogonal problems—lip-audio alignment control and emotion control—both solved through explicit manipulation of keypoint deformations, rather than through end-to-end generative models or fixed motion coefficients.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in digital human and film production pipelines: prior methods achieve lip-sync but offer no fine-grained control over speaking style (per-articulation editing, volume scaling) or emotion (intensity, region-specific compound expressions). The decomposition of pure emotional deformation by subtracting a neutral prediction from an emotional one is a clean, generalizable trick that could transfer to other animation tasks. Limitations include the 512×512 resolution ceiling, dependence on a limited style space learned from training data, and the fact that compound emotion control relies on the semantic structure of implicit keypoints, which may not generalize to all facial configurations. Open questions include whether the keypoint-deformation control paradigm scales to higher resolutions, longer sequences, or multi-person scenarios without degrading temporal consistency.
