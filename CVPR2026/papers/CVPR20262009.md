## Problem

Existing AI-generated video detection datasets are limited in scale, rely on outdated or narrowly scoped generative models, and prioritize quantity over semantic diversity and scenario coverage. Moreover, current benchmarks largely stop at dataset creation, leaving fundamental questions—such as whether temporal or spatial artifacts matter more, how generation task type affects detectability, and whether higher-quality generators are harder to detect—unsystematically explored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262009/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each video is preprocessed by uniformly sampling 32 frames from the first 128 frames (or all available frames), center-cropping along the shorter side, and resizing to 256×256 PNG; only the first 8 of the 32 frames are used during training and inference. Detectors output probability scores evaluated via AUC, while VLMs produce binary real/fake decisions evaluated via accuracy. The evaluation follows a cross-detection protocol: a detector trained on videos from one generator is tested on outputs from all other generators, with all videos standardized to H.264 codec to eliminate compression-format bias.

## Method

AIGVDBench introduces a standardized construction pipeline with three key components: (1) an Attribute Balancing Selection Algorithm that categorizes 400K prompts from OpenVid-HD along spatial/temporal content and attribute dimensions, then iteratively selects a balanced 20K-prompt subset by prioritizing single-attribute prompts and resolving multi-label overlaps; (2) a curated set of 31 generation models (20 open-source, 11 closed-source) spanning T2V, I2V, V2V, and real-world scenarios, yielding over 440K videos (20K per open-source model, 2K per closed-source model); and (3) systematic evaluation of 33 detectors across four paradigms—video classification networks, frame-level image detectors, dedicated video detectors, and VLMs—producing 1,500+ evaluations. The benchmark surfaces 8 in-depth analyses and 4 novel findings, including that generation task type significantly impacts detector performance, that generation model quality does not monotonically determine detectability, and that current VLMs lack reliable detection capability.

## Results

On the closed-source models (the harder setting), Effort achieves the highest average AUC of 94.05%, followed by ForgeLens1 (85.03%) and UniFormerV2 (87.07%) among video classifiers. On open-source models, ForgeLens1 leads with 91.82% average AUC, while Effort reaches 87.49%. Vision-Language Models perform at or near chance level (~50% ACC) across nearly all models, with the notable exception of DeepSeek-VL2, which achieves 75.35% ACC on closed-source models. The dedicated video detection methods DeCoF (82.56% open / 72.90% closed) and DeMamba (80.99% / 69.43%) trail the best image-detection and video-classification baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the 10 video classification baselines, UniFormerV2 (87.07% AVG AUC) and TimeSformer (86.50%) dramatically outperform the rest on closed-source models, while on open-source models X3D (84.07%) and UniFormerV2 (83.27%) lead; older architectures like VideoSwin (52.47% closed) and TSM (56.08%) remain near chance. Among the 9 image-detection baselines, Effort achieves the best closed-source AUC (94.05%), surpassing ForgeLens1 (85.03%) by ~9 points and D3 (76.58%) by ~17 points; on open-source models ForgeLens1 (91.82%) edges out Effort (87.49%). The two dedicated video-detection methods, DeCoF (82.56% open / 72.90% closed) and DeMamba (80.99% / 69.43%), consistently trail the best image-detection and video-classification baselines by 5–20 AUC points. Among the 10 VLM baselines, all perform at or below chance (~50% ACC) on open-source models, with Intern-VL-8B (55.48%) being the best; on closed-source models, DeepSeek-VL2 is the sole outlier at 75.35% ACC, while Qwen2.5-VL-3B (57.52%) and InternVL3-8B (56.36%) are the next best, and LLaVA-v1.5-7B, DeepSeek-VL-7B, and DeepSeek-VL2-S remain at exactly or near 50%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of deepfake detection, AI-generated image detection, and video understanding, positioning AI-generated video detection (AIGVD) as a distinct task that has lagged behind its image and face-swap predecessors. It departs from prior dataset construction efforts (GVD, GVF, GenVideo, GenVidBench, GenBuster-200K, GenWorld) by introducing an attribute-balancing selection algorithm to eliminate prompt distribution bias, rather than relying on random sampling or manual curation. The evaluation framework repurposes detectors from four unrelated families—classic video classifiers, image forgery detectors, dedicated video forgery detectors, and vision-language models—under a unified cross-model protocol, rather than proposing a new detection architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With 440K+ videos spanning 31 generation models and 33 evaluated detectors, AIGVDBench is likely to become the de facto standard benchmark for AI-generated video detection, filling a critical gap where prior datasets covered at most 20 models and 100K videos. The finding that generation quality (per VBench rankings) does not predict detectability challenges the assumption that better generators are automatically harder to detect, and the near-chance performance of even large VLMs (Qwen2.5-VL-32B at ~51% ACC) raises urgent questions about whether current multimodal models can ever serve as reliable authenticity judges. Open questions include whether temporal artifacts remain exploitable as generators improve, whether the attribute-balancing protocol generalizes to future model generations, and how to build detectors that are robust to the rapid evolution of synthesis technology.
