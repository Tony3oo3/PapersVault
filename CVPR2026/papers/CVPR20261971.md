## Problem

Camera control for portrait video generation is hampered by two issues: scene-agnostic extrinsic camera representations suffer from monocular scale ambiguity (the same parameter change produces wildly different visual effects depending on unknown scene scale), and reconstruction-based methods amplify small 3D estimation errors into visible facial distortions. Additionally, acquiring paired training data where the same dynamic human scene is captured under different camera trajectories remains extremely difficult.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261971/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single monocular portrait video and a target camera trajectory, which is specified not as numeric extrinsics but as a sequence of rasterized 2D facial landmark maps rendered from a generic 3D Gaussian head along the desired path. The source video and landmark maps are each encoded by a VAE into latents; the source latent is concatenated with the noise latent via frame conditioning while the camera-conditioning latent is injected via channel conditioning into the diffusion transformer. The output is a new video of the same subject from the target viewpoint, preserving identity, expressions, and fine-grained dynamics such as hair motion.

## Method

FaceCam replaces extrinsic camera parameters with a face-tailored, scale-aware representation: 2D projections of 3D facial landmarks under the target pose, which are inherently scale-invariant because perspective division cancels any global similarity transform, thereby resolving the monocular scale ambiguity. The model is a fine-tuned Wan video diffusion transformer (DiT) trained with a flow-matching loss, where the source video latent and camera-conditioning latent are injected via frame and channel conditions respectively. To overcome the lack of dynamic-camera training data, the authors develop two augmentation strategies on static multi-view NeRSemble captures—synthetic zoom/pan motion and multi-shot stitching of clips from different viewpoints—plus in-the-wild videos with synthetic camera motion to improve generalization. At inference, a generic 3D Gaussian head (FaceLift) is rendered along the user-specified trajectory, MediaPipe extracts per-frame landmarks, and the resulting landmark maps condition generation, allowing the model trained on discrete pose changes to produce smooth continuous camera motion.

## Results

On the Ava-256 static-camera benchmark, FaceCam achieves PSNR 15.85, SSIM 0.7208, LPIPS 0.2521, and ArcFace 0.8574, substantially outperforming ReCamMaster (PSNR 9.73, ArcFace 0.7014) and TrajectoryCrafter (PSNR 10.32, ArcFace 0.5220). On 100 in-the-wild portrait videos with dynamic camera trajectories, FaceCam attains 97% camera-motion correctness, 83.94 ArcFace similarity, and 73.49 imaging quality (VBench), surpassing both baselines on identity preservation and visual quality. The ablation variant FaceCam* (using a generic 3D head for conditioning instead of the target video) still achieves ArcFace 0.8073, confirming the representation's robustness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FaceCam decisively outperforms both baselines on the Ava-256 ground-truth benchmark, improving PSNR by ~6 dB over ReCamMaster (15.85 vs. 9.73) and ~5.5 dB over TrajectoryCrafter (15.85 vs. 10.32), while also achieving the best SSIM (0.7208), lowest LPIPS (0.2521), and highest ArcFace identity score (0.8574 vs. 0.7014 and 0.5220). On in-the-wild dynamic-camera evaluation, FaceCam leads on ArcFace similarity (83.94 vs. 78.92 for ReCamMaster and 49.79 for TrajectoryCrafter) and imaging quality (73.49 vs. 69.05 and 71.37), though TrajectoryCrafter edges it slightly on camera-motion correctness (99% vs. 97%). ReCamMaster shows the weakest camera control (83% correctness) and produces blurry outputs under zoom-in, while TrajectoryCrafter suffers from facial distortions due to point-cloud estimation errors, resulting in the lowest identity score. The ablation (FaceCam without in-the-wild data) achieves near-perfect camera correctness (100%) but lower identity and quality scores, confirming that the in-the-wild augmentation trades a small amount of trajectory precision for substantially better visual fidelity.

## Relation to Prior Work

FaceCam builds on the Wan video diffusion transformer as its generative backbone and adopts the video-to-video training strategy from ReCamMaster, but departs fundamentally from both prevailing camera-conditioning paradigms: scene-agnostic extrinsic parameter encodings (ReCamMaster, CameraCtrl, AC3D) and 3D-reconstruction-based approaches (TrajectoryCrafter, ReCapture). Instead, it introduces a face-tailored scale-aware representation using rasterized 2D facial landmark maps as the camera signal, grounded in classical multi-view geometry (fundamental matrix / essential matrix recovery from correspondences). The training pipeline also departs from prior work by synthesizing dynamic camera training pairs from static multi-view studio captures (NeRSemble) via multi-shot stitching and synthetic zoom/pan, rather than relying on 4D synthetic data or per-scene optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The scale-aware landmark conditioning offers a clean, interpretable, and deterministic alternative to extrinsic-parameter or depth-reconstruction conditioning that is particularly well-suited to the human face, where small geometric errors are perceptually amplified. The practical finding that discontinuous multi-shot-stitching training generalizes to smooth continuous camera trajectories at inference is a useful data-efficiency trick that could transfer to other view-synthesis settings. Limitations include restriction to portrait-scale subjects, dependence on a generic 3D Gaussian head proxy for inference-time landmark rendering, and evaluation against only two baselines; extending the approach to full-body or scene-level camera control, and validating against a broader set of camera-control methods, remain open.
