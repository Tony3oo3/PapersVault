## Problem

CLIP-like models trained with InfoNCE or NCE objectives implicitly learn similarity scores proportional to log density ratios between conditional and marginal image-text distributions, yet this probabilistic structure has not been systematically examined or exploited beyond embedding and retrieval tasks. The paper asks whether these pretrained models can serve as off-the-shelf, general-purpose density ratio estimators for downstream applications such as domain adaptation, semantic diversity measurement, and data curation without any additional training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262777/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes pretrained CLIP ViT-L-14 embeddings (from LAION2B) of images and text captions, plus a single natural-language prompt (e.g., 'A photo of pets') to specify a target domain. It produces per-sample density ratio estimates via scaled inner products, importance weights for reweighting pretraining losses, KL divergence scalars quantifying how much conditioning on one modality shifts the other's distribution, and a filtered subset (top 25% by KL) of a pretraining pool. The logit scale is reduced from ~100 to 10 to prevent overflow in mixed-precision training, and candidate sample sets D_T and D_I (10k each) are drawn from the data pool for finite-sample KL estimation.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution is a unified derivation showing that both CLIP's InfoNCE and SigLIP's sigmoid NCE objectives optimize similarity scores that equal the log of the density ratio p(t|i)/p(t) (or symmetrically p(i|t)/p(i)), up to a normalizing constant. From this, two applications follow: (1) Importance-Weighted Learning, where a single prompt embedding t† provides per-sample weights exp(a⟨v_i, v_{t†}⟩) that reweight the standard CLIP contrastive loss to perform soft domain adaptation without dedicated datasets; and (2) KL divergence estimation via two complementary strategies — a direct Monte Carlo approximation using sample data (Eqs. 10–13), and a closed-form exponential-family approximation (D_W, D_C) that treats the conditional distribution as an exponential family with the image embedding as natural parameter, yielding a²‖v_i − v̄‖²-type expressions. The exponential-family view connects to Oyama et al.'s word-embedding information gain result, extended here to high-dimensional multimodal embeddings.

## Results

For Importance-Weighted Learning (IWL), re-weighting CLIP pretraining with a single domain prompt improves zero-shot accuracy by 2–8 points and F1-score by 3–7 points over the unweighted baseline on Food101, Oxford-IIIT Pet, and Flowers102 (up to 7 points F1 gain). For KL-based data curation on DataComp (small scale, top-25% retention), the best variant D_KLR on text achieves 3.25% ImageNet-1k zero-shot accuracy and 0.1344 mean across 38 downstream tasks, matching LAION-2B filtering (3.1%, 0.133) while using only one-quarter of the data. Text-based KL metrics consistently outperform image-based ones, with D_KLR (text) the strongest variant. CLIPScore remains a substantially stronger filter (5.1% IN1k, 0.173 mean), indicating KL-based curation is complementary rather than a replacement for alignment-based filtering.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LAION-2B filtering [11], the best KL variant (D_KLR on text) slightly edges out the baseline on both IN1k (0.0325 vs 0.031) and mean (0.1344 vs 0.133), confirming the paper's claim of competitive performance. Against Basic heuristics [11], D_KLR (text) wins on IN1k (0.0325 vs 0.030) but trails on mean (0.1344 vs 0.142), so the comparison is mixed. Against CLIPScore [14], the gap is large: CLIPScore achieves 0.051 IN1k and 0.173 mean versus 0.0325 and 0.1344 for the best KL method, and the authors explicitly note that KL-based selection alone does not surpass CLIPScore, positioning it as a complementary signal. In the metric-correlation analysis (Table 1), D_C is shown to be mathematically equivalent to the negative of the Conformity metric [18] (Pearson r = −1.000), while it correlates moderately with Whitened CLIP log-likelihood [2] (r = −0.626) and Raw Norm [9] (r = −0.731), but D_KL and D_KLR show near-zero correlation with all three, indicating they capture distinct distributional information beyond frequency or alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets the contrastive training objectives of CLIP (InfoNCE) and SigLIP (sigmoid NCE) through the lens of Noise Contrastive Estimation theory, showing that their learned similarity scores are proportional to log density ratios between conditional and marginal distributions. While the NCE-to-density-ratio connection was previously established for word embeddings (Gutmann & Hyvärinen; Levy & Goldberg) and the exponential-family KL approximation was derived for word2vec (Oyama et al.), no prior work systematically applied this interpretation to high-dimensional vision-language models or exploited it for downstream tasks. The paper thus bridges classical density ratio estimation (KLIEP, uLSIF, LogReg) with modern contrastive pretraining, positioning pretrained CLIP-like models as off-the-shelf, general-purpose density ratio estimators rather than merely embedding extractors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified density-ratio perspective offers a principled, low-cost mechanism for domain adaptation (via importance weighting with a single prompt) and data curation (via KL-based informativeness scoring) that complements alignment-based filtering like CLIPScore. However, the experiments are conducted at relatively small scale (CC12M, DataComp 'small' pool), the density ratio estimates inherit biases from the pretraining distribution, and the KL-based curation does not surpass CLIPScore, leaving open whether the approach scales to billion-pair datasets or can be effectively combined with alignment scores. A key open question is whether the exponential-family approximation and finite-sample KL estimators remain accurate at the scale and diversity of modern web-scale corpora.
