## Problem

Learning-based color constancy methods overfit to the spectral response of the training camera, causing significant performance degradation when applied to images from different sensors. Existing cross-camera approaches either require target-camera calibration data, labeled samples, or pre-calibrated color correction matrices, and none exploit the semantic scene understanding that human observers naturally use when iteratively correcting white balance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261665/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a RAW image and the camera's 3×3 color correction matrix (camera-to-XYZ) as input. At each of up to 20 iterations, the raw image is white-balanced by the current illuminant estimate and mapped through the CCM and a fixed XYZ-to-sRGB matrix to produce a pseudo-sRGB image (shorter side resized to 448 px) that is fed to the VLM. The VLM outputs a single discrete cast label (red, green, or blue) per iteration; the final illuminant is the normalized geometric mean of the last three RGB estimates, with the step angle linearly decaying from 3° to 0.1°.

## Method

VLM-CC reframes color constancy as an iterative perceptual-feedback loop rather than a single-pass RGB regression. Starting from a Gray-World initialization, each iteration white-balances the raw image, converts it to pseudo-sRGB, and queries a LoRA-finetuned Qwen2.5-VL 7B to predict the dominant residual color cast (red, green, or blue) conditioned on a structured color-prior prompt listing 2–6 scene objects with their expected intrinsic colors. The predicted cast is mapped to a unit direction vector, and the illuminant estimate is rotated toward that axis by a decaying step angle in RGB space; convergence is triggered when three distinct cast labels appear, at which point step angles are halved and iteration stops upon a second triangle or a 20-iteration cap. The VLM is trained with LoRA adapters (rank 8) on the vision tower, projector, and language model using a causal LM loss on the single cast token, with training data augmented by perturbing the ground-truth illuminant by up to ~17.5° before sRGB conversion. The key design choice is predicting a discrete categorical cast rather than continuous RGB values, which aligns with the VLM's strength in qualitative color judgment and avoids the numerical instability of token-based regression.

## Results

On the primary leave-one-out cross-dataset protocol, VLM-CC achieves a mean angular error of 1.52° on Gehler-Shi, 1.83° on NUS-8, and 1.51° on Cube+, outperforming the strongest prior method CCMNet (2.23°, 2.32°, 1.68° respectively). The largest relative gains appear in the Worst-25% metric, where VLM-CC reduces worst-case errors from 5.46° (CCMNet) to 3.29° on Gehler-Shi and from 5.18° to 3.88° on NUS-8. Under the NUS-8 cross-sensor protocol (7 cameras train, 1 test), VLM-CC reaches 1.49° mean error, again the best among all learning-based methods. On the standard three-fold cross-validation of Gehler-Shi, VLM-CC achieves 1.34° mean, matching C4 (1.35°) and surpassing the recent generative method GCC (1.91°).

## Baselines & Comparisons

VLM-CC outperforms all 26 baselines on mean angular error across every leave-one-out and cross-sensor evaluation. The closest competitor is CCMNet [44], which leverages pre-calibrated color correction matrices; VLM-CC improves mean error by 0.71° on Gehler-Shi (1.52° vs 2.23°), 0.49° on NUS-8 (1.83° vs 2.32°), and 0.17° on Cube+ (1.51° vs 1.68°). The most dramatic advantage is in worst-case robustness: Worst-25% error drops from 5.46° (CCMNet) to 3.29° on Gehler-Shi and from 5.18° to 3.88° on NUS-8, indicating VLM-CC handles challenging scenes far better. On the Best-25% metric, CCMNet retains a slight edge (0.36° vs 0.41° on Gehler-Shi; 0.38° vs 0.41° on Cube+), suggesting VLM-CC's iterative refinement is most beneficial for harder cases rather than already-easy ones. In the single-dataset three-fold cross-validation on Gehler-Shi (Table 5), VLM-CC (1.34°) essentially ties C4 (1.35°) and clearly beats GCC (1.91°), confirming competitiveness even without cross-dataset training. Statistical baselines (Gray-World, Shades-of-Gray, Gray-Edge) are consistently 2–3× worse in mean error, underscoring the benefit of learned and semantic approaches over hand-crafted assumptions.

## Relation to Prior Work

VLM-CC departs from the dominant paradigm in learning-based color constancy, where a network directly regresses the illuminant from raw pixels in a single forward pass (e.g., CCMNet, C5, GCC). Instead, it repurposes a LoRA-adapted vision-language model as a qualitative perceptual evaluator that judges residual color cast in a pseudo-sRGB domain, framing the problem as iterative feedback rather than one-shot regression. Conceptually, it draws on the observation that humans adjust white balance by referencing recognizable objects and iterating, and it leverages the rich semantic color priors already encoded in VLM pretraining. Among cross-camera methods, it is the first to operate in a shared sRGB-like space for evaluation rather than in raw sensor space, and the first to use a generative language model's reasoning capability for low-level illumination estimation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a 7B VLM, with only LoRA adapters trained, can serve as a camera-agnostic illuminant evaluator that generalizes across sensors without per-camera calibration or retraining, addressing a persistent weakness of prior learning-based CC methods. The consistent Worst-25% improvements across all benchmarks suggest the semantic feedback loop is particularly effective in challenging scenes where statistical or purely learned methods fail. However, the approach requires up to 20 VLM forward passes per image at inference, raising practical latency concerns, and the 3-class (red/green/blue) discretization inherently limits angular precision. Open questions include whether the framework extends to mixed-illuminant or nighttime scenes, whether smaller VLMs or distilled classifiers can retain the benefit, and whether the iterative loop can be parallelized or shortened without sacrificing convergence.
