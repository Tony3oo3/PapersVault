## Problem

Fisheye cameras are being rapidly adopted for wrist-mounted robotic manipulation (UMI, RDT2, GEN-0, π0.5), yet their downstream effects on imitation learning policy performance remain largely unexplored. Specifically, how the wide FoV and radial distortion of fisheye lenses impact spatial localization, scene generalization, and cross-hardware transfer has never been systematically quantified, leaving practitioners without evidence-based guidance for large-scale fisheye data collection.

## Input / Output

The policy is deliberately state-free: it consumes only wrist-mounted RGB camera images (fisheye at 235° FoV or pinhole at 90°/60° FoV) with no proprioceptive state and no third-view camera, forcing all spatial reasoning to come from visual cues alone. In simulation, a ResNet-18 encoder processes the image; in the real world, a pre-trained CLIP ViT encoder is used. The output is a relative action chunk—delta actions between consecutive frames in simulation, or relative transformations to the first frame of the chunk in real-world deployment—consistent with UMI-style action parameterization.

## Method

The paper is an empirical study built on Diffusion Policy (U-Net noise predictor, DDIM scheduler) and contributes two enabling components: (1) a fisheye camera simulator in MuJoCo that renders via a two-stage pipeline—six virtual cameras produce a cubemap, which is projected to an equirectangular intermediate and then reprojected into the fisheye domain using a configurable lens model—and (2) Random Scale Augmentation (RSA), which samples a uniform scale factor s ∈ U(0.7, 1.3) per training image, center-crops to that scale, and pads with black when s > 1, compelling the network to learn relative object-to-gripper scale rather than absolute pixel size. The experimental design systematically varies four factors (camera model, scene complexity, scene diversity N, and camera intrinsics) across six simulation tasks from Robomimic/MimicGen and three real-world tasks, with a proprioception-prediction probing protocol to quantify learned spatial representations and a normalized multi-stage scoring metric for real-world evaluation.

## Results

In simulation (Table 1), a single fisheye camera (235° FoV) achieves 0.57 average success rate on feature-poor backgrounds and 0.66 on feature-rich backgrounds, compared to 0.31 and 0.34 for a single pinhole camera (90° FoV). With dual cameras, the gap widens: Fisheye(Double) reaches 0.72/0.75 vs. Pinhole(Double) at 0.38/0.45. In the real world, the fisheye's advantage over feature-rich backgrounds is +0.39 normalized score versus +0.18 for pinhole. For scene generalization (RQ2), the fisheye policy's zero-shot success rate on unseen scenes exceeds 95% when trained with just 8 diverse scenes, while the pinhole policy plateaus at a lower level.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study rather than a method-vs-method comparison, so the 'baselines' are the standard pinhole camera configurations (single and dual, 90° FoV in simulation, 60° FoV in real world) against which the fisheye camera (235°/180° FoV) is evaluated. The fisheye camera consistently outperforms the pinhole baseline across all six simulation tasks and both single/dual camera setups, with the largest gaps on spatially demanding tasks like Threading (+0.26 SR) and Assembly (+0.24 SR) under feature-rich backgrounds. In the real world, the fisheye's advantage is amplified by the CLIP encoder, yielding a +0.39 normalized score gain from feature-rich backgrounds versus only +0.18 for pinhole. For hardware generalization (RQ3), the baseline policy (trained with standard augmentation) suffers a sharp performance drop on unseen fisheye lenses, while the proposed Random Scale Augmentation (RSA) recovers most of the lost performance, confirming that scale overfitting is the root cause of cross-camera failure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2024) as its core algorithm and on the UMI (Chi et al., 2024) paradigm of wrist-mounted fisheye cameras for in-the-wild data collection, but departs from both by isolating and quantifying the specific optical properties (wide FoV, radial distortion) rather than treating the camera as a fixed hardware choice. It positions itself against the growing but under-analyzed trend of fisheye adoption in large-scale robotics datasets (RDT2, GEN-0, π0.5), filling a gap where prior work provided application-level insights but no systematic study of how lens characteristics affect policy learning. The fisheye simulation pipeline adapts classical two-stage projection methods (OmniCV-Lib, equirectangular re-projection) to MuJoCo, and the proposed Random Scale Augmentation is a novel data-centric technique specifically targeting cross-lens scale overfitting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the robotics community scales toward large-scale fisheye-based datasets for training VLA models, this paper provides the first empirical grounding for how to collect and use such data effectively: prioritize feature-rich environments, maximize scene diversity, and apply scale augmentation. The finding that fisheye's localization advantage is contingent on visual complexity—and that it can actually overfit more in simple scenes—challenges the assumption that wider FoV is universally beneficial. Open questions include whether these findings transfer to language-conditioned VLA policies, how they interact with multi-camera or third-view setups, and whether the RSA strategy generalizes beyond the Diffusion Policy architecture tested here.
