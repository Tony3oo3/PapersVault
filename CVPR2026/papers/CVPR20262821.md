## Problem

Existing articulated object generation methods are fundamentally limited: optimization-based reconstruction is slow, per-object, and restricted to single-joint objects, while retrieval-based approaches assemble parts from fixed libraries, producing repetitive geometry with poor generalization. Critically, no prior method jointly reasons over part geometry and kinematic structure, leaving a disconnect between a part's visual semantics and its intended mechanical role.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262821/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a uniformly sampled point cloud of 32,768 surface points with normals (extracted from a watertight mesh); for image or text inputs, an off-the-shelf 3D generator (Hunyuan3D 2.5 or TripoSG) first produces the mesh. The output is a complete articulated asset: a tokenized URDF specifying part bounding boxes, joint types (revolute, continuous, prismatic, screw), connectivity, axes, origins, and motion limits, followed by high-fidelity part geometries synthesized by XPart and refined by a physics-based collision-free limit correction step.

## Method

ArtLLM reformulates articulation prediction as autoregressive language modeling: a Point Transformer v3 encoder (initialized from P3SAM part-segmentation weights) encodes the point cloud, a two-layer MLP projector aligns features to a Qwen3 0.6B LLM backbone, which then generates a structured token sequence of part AABBs followed by joint definitions. All continuous parameters are discretized—coordinates into 128 bins over [-1,1], rotational limits into 48 bins over [-2π,2π], translational limits into 64 bins over [-2,2], and joint axes into a 128-entry hierarchical codebook (axis-aligned directions plus FPS on a Fibonacci sphere)—enabling stable next-token prediction. Training uses a multi-task, multi-stage SFT strategy: stage 1 trains only part-layout prediction to ground the encoder, then stage 2 jointly fine-tunes on part layout, kinematic prediction (conditioned on GT parts), and end-to-end articulation prediction. The predicted bounding boxes condition XPart for geometry synthesis, with a bounding-box expansion step ensuring full point-cloud coverage, and a final physics-based correction identifies collision angles via hierarchical search to enforce physically plausible joint limits.

## Results

On the PartNet-Mobility test set (7 categories, 77 objects), ArtLLM achieves a part mIoU of 0.6884 and Chamfer Distance of 0.028, substantially outperforming all baselines. For articulation prediction, it attains 90.8% joint type accuracy, 0.127 joint-axis error, 0.080 joint-pivot error, and 77.4% kinematic graph accuracy. Inference takes only 19 seconds per object, roughly 4–27× faster than competing methods (84–522 s). The sole metric where ArtLLM trails is joint-range IoU (0.740 vs. 0.865 for Articulate-Anything), which benefits from rule-based limit prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ArtLLM dominates on part geometry: its mIoU of 0.6884 nearly doubles Articulate-Anything (0.3381) and SINGAPO (0.4330), and is 5.6× better than URDFormer (0.1225), reflecting that retrieval-based methods cannot match the input geometry while URDFormer's fixed frame-and-interior assumption produces poor shapes. On joint prediction, ArtLLM achieves 90.8% type accuracy (vs. 84.6% ArtAny, 76.5% SINGAPO, 60.7% URDFormer) and reduces joint-axis error to 0.127 (vs. 0.453, 0.245, 0.738), indicating far more accurate rotation-axis localization. The kinematic graph accuracy gap is especially stark: ArtLLM reaches 77.4% versus 61.4% (ArtAny), 45.6% (SINGAPO), and only 7.9% (URDFormer). The one metric where ArtLLM underperforms is joint-range IoU (0.740 vs. 0.865 for ArtAny), which the authors attribute to ArtAny's rule-based limit heuristic; ArtLLM's physics-based post-processing narrows but does not close this gap. Retraining SINGAPO on the same dataset (Singapo*) improves its metrics substantially (e.g., type accuracy 76.5%→90.7%) yet still trails ArtLLM on mIoU, axis error, and graph accuracy, confirming the architectural advantage. Inference speed is a clear differentiator: ArtLLM completes in 19 s versus 84 s (SINGAPO), 183 s (URDFormer), and 522 s (ArtAny).

## Relation to Prior Work

ArtLLM sits at the intersection of 3D large language models (PointLLM, ShapeLLM, SpatialLM) and part-aware 3D generation (XPart, OmniPart), repurposing the autoregressive token-prediction paradigm of LLMs to output a structured articulation blueprint (part bounding boxes + joint parameters) from a point cloud. It departs from two dominant prior paradigms for articulated object generation: optimization-based reconstruction (PARIS, ArtGS, VideoArtGS), which is slow and limited to single-joint objects, and retrieval-based feedforward methods (SINGAPO, URDFormer, Articulate-Anything), which are constrained to a fixed part library and produce repetitive geometry. The key conceptual move is framing URDF-style kinematic structure as a discrete language sequence with quantized continuous parameters, enabling a single 0.6B LLM to jointly reason over part layout and joint topology in one autoregressive pass.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical pipeline for rapidly producing simulation-ready articulated assets from a single image, which is directly relevant to scaling robot learning in simulation and building digital twins. The real2sim validation with a Franka Panda arm demonstrates that generated assets faithfully reproduce real-world articulation behavior, bridging a gap that prior retrieval-based methods could not close due to geometric mismatch. Key limitations remain: the model is trained on only 43 categories (primarily household items), does not jointly predict physical properties (mass, friction), and the 128-bin quantization introduces a granularity ceiling on joint precision. Open questions include whether open-vocabulary articulation (e.g., via Kinematify-style approaches) can extend coverage to vehicles and robots, and whether physics-aware training could eliminate the need for the post-hoc collision-based limit correction.
