## Problem

Existing medical multimodal benchmarks are dominated by perception-level VQA (describing images or retrieving obvious facts), leaving complex clinical reasoning capabilities—such as temporal prediction, causal chains, long-tail generalization, and multi-source integration—largely unmeasured. No prior benchmark systematically decomposes medical reasoning into fine-grained visual and inference subdimensions, nor provides expert-validated, clinically challenging questions spanning diverse organs and modalities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260720/cropped_page2_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark item pairs a medical image (from 12 imaging modalities including radiology, pathology, ophthalmology, and endoscopy, across 11 organ systems) with a text question. Questions come in two formats: 16,655 five-option multiple-choice items scored by exact match, and 3,998 open-ended items scored via a weighted formula S = (1·Consistency + 1·Coherence + 4·Visual Accuracy + 4·Ground-truth Correctness) / 10, normalized to 0–100. The heavy weighting on visual and ground-truth dimensions (4× vs 1×) reflects the clinical priority of correct visual grounding over fluent but ungrounded reasoning.

## Method

Med-CMR decomposes medical reasoning complexity into seven dimensions—three visual (small-object detection, fine-detail discrimination, spatial understanding) and four reasoning (temporal prediction, causal reasoning, long-tail generalization, multi-source integration)—each mapped to a dedicated question category with five subtypes. Data is sourced from authentic journal case reports (JMCR, NEJM); medical-background annotators design 10–20 question templates per category, and GPT-5-mini-2025-08-07 assists in template selection and answer extraction from human-annotated captions. Distractors are constructed via a human-in-the-loop pipeline where three MLLMs (GPT-5-Mini, Qwen3-VL-Plus, Claude-Sonnet-4) each generate four candidates, from which three medical annotators select four final distractors satisfying difficulty, correctness exclusion, visual dependence, and clinical plausibility criteria. A two-stage filter removes trivial items: healthcare practitioners pre-screen images for dimension alignment, and post-generation, questions answered correctly by all three of Lingshu-7B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, and LLaVA-Med-v1.5-Mistral-7B are discarded. Open-ended evaluation employs DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp as an independent LLM-as-a-Judge under a controlled protocol, validated against human expert rankings (Spearman ρ > 0.78 across all four scoring dimensions).

## Results

On Med-CMR, GPT-5 achieves the best overall performance with 57.81% MCQ accuracy and a 48.70 open-ended score, followed by Gemini-2.5-Pro (49.87% MCQ, 45.98 OE) and the top open-source model Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B (49.34% MCQ, 42.62 OE). Long-Tail Generalization is the most challenging dimension across all models, with GPT-5 reaching only 55.19% and all open-source models below 46%. The gap between GPT-5 and the best open-source model is 8.47 points on MCQ but narrows to just 0.82 points on open-ended scoring (48.70 vs. InternVL3.5-241B-A28B's 47.88), indicating that fluent reasoning is more mature than visual grounding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-5 leads all 18 models on every MCQ dimension and the overall MCQ score (57.81%), outperforming Gemini-2.5-Pro by 7.94 points and the best open-source model Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B by 8.47 points. On open-ended scoring, GPT-5 (48.70) edges out InternVL3.5-241B-A28B (47.88) by only 0.82 points, while Gemini-2.5-Pro (45.98) and Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B (42.62) trail further behind. A key finding is that medically fine-tuned models (Medgemma-4B/27B, Lingshu-7B/32B) consistently underperform their respective base models (Gemma3, Qwen2.5-VL) on MCQ accuracy—for example, Lingshu-32B scores 30.47% vs. Qwen2.5-VL-32B's 33.36%, and Medgemma-27B scores 28.91% vs. Gemma3-27B's 37.07%—suggesting medical fine-tuning degrades general multimodal reasoning. On open-ended tasks the gap narrows or reverses for Lingshu-32B (43.02 vs. 41.22), but Medgemma-27B still lags its base (31.49 vs. 35.36). Among general open-source models, scaling consistently helps MCQ accuracy (Qwen2.5-VL: 31.06→33.36→42.17 from 7B→32B→72B) but yields diminishing returns on visual accuracy and ground-truth correctness in open-ended scoring.

## Relation to Prior Work

Med-CMR carves new ground in medical multimodal evaluation by decomposing 'complex medical reasoning' into seven explicit sub-dimensions (three visual, four reasoning) rather than treating it as a monolithic score, a granularity absent from prior benchmarks like OmniMedVQA, GMAI-MMBench, and MedXpertQA-MM, which remain dominated by perception-level VQA. It also introduces a unified MCQ + open-ended evaluation protocol with LLM-as-a-Judge scoring along four weighted criteria, going beyond the single-metric accuracy used by most existing medical VQA datasets. The benchmark's data curation pipeline—dimension-guided collection from journal case reports, template-based question generation, model-assisted difficulty filtering, and multi-stage human review—repurposes standard VQA construction techniques but applies them with explicit clinical-complexity targeting that prior work did not systematize.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that specialized medical MLLMs (Medgemma, Lingshu) do not reliably outperform their general base models on complex reasoning MCQs, and that long-tail generalization is the dominant failure mode even for GPT-5 (top score only 55.19%), pointing to a fundamental gap in rare-case robustness. The finding that medical fine-tuning degrades fine-grained visual perception and cross-modal evidence integration—while improving open-ended medical semantics—raises open questions about how to preserve general reasoning capacity during domain adaptation. With 20,653 questions across 11 organ systems and 12 modalities, Med-CMR is positioned to become a standard stress test for clinical MLLM deployment, though its reliance on a single LLM judge (DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp) for open-ended scoring and the absence of longitudinal or multi-patient context limit its scope.
