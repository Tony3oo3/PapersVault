## Problem

Despite substantial progress of MLLMs on diverse multimodal benchmarks, no unified counting dataset exists to rigorously evaluate counting ability across image, text, and audio simultaneously. Existing counting datasets use heterogeneous annotation formats (points, boxes, density maps, timestamps), lack MLLM-ready QA templates, and suffer from inconsistent evaluation protocols (varying splits, prompts, decoding settings, and matching rules), making cross-modal and cross-model comparison infeasible.

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs a modality-specific stimulus (RGB images at 234×180 to 6736×4640 px, text documents of 584 to 8,052,974 characters, or WAV audio clips of 3.0 to 2,497.9 s) with a deterministic counting question. The model must return a single numeric value; a priority-based parser extracts it from answer tags, standalone trailing numbers, or the first parseable token. Ground truth is evidence-first, storing both gt_count and structured gt_evidence (point coordinates, character spans, or timestamps), enabling verification beyond the scalar answer.

## Method

UNICBench organizes all 14,301 QA pairs into a three-level capability taxonomy (Pattern: direct instance counting; Semantic: attribute filtering and identity deduplication; Reasoning: rule-driven compositional counting) crossed with three difficulty tiers (Easy 1–10, Medium 11–100, Hard >100), yielding a 3×3 stratified evaluation grid per modality. The evaluation protocol fixes system prompts, temperature (0.0), max_tokens (4096), and seeds across 45 MLLMs, with modality-specific answer-extraction rules and a unified metric suite (Success Rate, MAE, MSE, Hit Rate at 100%/90%/80% tolerance). The corpus aggregates 5,300 images from 8 established counting datasets plus manual annotations, 872 self-collected text documents spanning code, LaTeX, legal, and literary corpora, and 2,069 audio clips from DESED and AliMeeting, all verified through dual independent annotation with 100% consistency. An open evaluation toolkit provides the parsing pipeline, stratified reporting, and adapter interfaces to enable reproducible, comparable counting assessment.

## Results

Across 45 MLLMs evaluated on UNICBench, performance varies sharply by modality and difficulty. On the image track, only three models exceed 50% Hit Rate @80% (InternVL3-5-241B-A28B: 57.1%, Gemini-2.5-Pro-Thinking: 51.3%, GPT-5-mini: 50.5%), with GPT-5-mini achieving the lowest overall MAE (29.8). On the text track, Gemini-2.5-Pro-Thinking leads with 63.3% Hit Rate @100% and MAE 30.5, while DeepSeek-R1-0528 follows closely (59.7%, MAE 34.6). On the audio track, Qwen2.5-Omni-7B and Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B-Instruct achieve 100% extraction success, and GPT-Audio-mini attains the lowest MAE (0.6) among audio-specialized models, though its success rate is only 63%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, UNICBench does not propose a new method but evaluates 45 MLLMs under a unified protocol. On the image track, GPT-5-mini achieves the lowest overall MAE (29.8) and ties with GPT-o3 for the best Reasoning-level MAE (5.3 vs. 4.4), while InternVL3-5-241B-A28B leads on Hit Rate @80% (57.1%) and @100% (21.9%), indicating superior exact-count accuracy among open-source models. On the text track, Gemini-2.5-Pro-Thinking dominates with the best Hit Rate @100% (63.3%) and lowest MAE (30.5), outperforming DeepSeek-R1-0528 (59.7%, MAE 34.6) and o4-mini (59.6%, MAE 47.1); notably, GPT-5-mini performs poorly here (MAE 1665.2) due to extreme overestimation on high-count samples. On the audio track, Qwen2.5-Omni-7B and Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B-Instruct are the only models with 100% success rate, and Qwen2.5-Omni-7B achieves the best combined profile (MAE 29.2, 100% success), whereas GPT-Audio-mini achieves the lowest MAE (0.6) but only 63% success due to frequent refusals. Across all three modalities, Reasoning-level tasks consistently produce the largest inter-model variance and the hardest performance ceiling, with no model exceeding 50% Hit Rate @80% on Hard-difficulty examples in any modality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by unifying counting evaluation across image, text, and audio under a single MLLM-ready QA framework, whereas prior work treated each modality in isolation: image counting relied on density maps and point annotations (ShanghaiTech, FSC-147, NWPU-MOC), text counting appeared as subproblems in document QA (DocVQA, ChartQA), and audio counting was embedded in sound event detection (DESED, AudioSet). The key departure is the introduction of a three-level capability taxonomy (Pattern → Semantic → Reasoning) and three-level difficulty stratification applied uniformly across all three modalities, combined with evidence-first ground truth and a standardized evaluation protocol (fixed prompts, seeds, matching rules) that prior modality-specific benchmarks lacked. The paper does not propose a new counting method but repurposes existing datasets and models into a common evaluation harness, positioning itself against general MLLM benchmarks (MMMU, MMBench, SEED-Bench) that do not isolate counting as a distinct capability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

UNICBench fills a clear gap by providing the first cross-modal, multi-level counting benchmark with 45 SOTA MLLMs evaluated under a unified protocol, yielding the first systematic quantification of where MLLMs fail at counting (Reasoning level, Hard difficulty, high-density scenes). The audio-track finding that refusal behavior artificially deflates MAE (models with ~64% success rate show MAE < 1 while 100%-success models show MAE ~30) exposes a metricological pitfall that could mislead future evaluations. Open questions include whether hybrid detector-MLLM pipelines can close the gap on dense/occluded scenes, how cross-modal alignment failures can be addressed, and whether the capability taxonomy generalizes beyond the three levels defined here. Limitations include dataset bias toward certain domains, the relatively small audio track (2,069 samples), and the fact that some models' low success rates on audio may reflect API limitations rather than true inability.
