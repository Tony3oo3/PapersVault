## Problem

Existing training-free NAS proxies rely on fragmented, isolated heuristics—gradient magnitude, activation variance, NTK conditioning—without addressing the fundamental question of what makes an architecture trainable. No unified, theoretically grounded criterion exists that captures the interplay between representational geometry and gradient propagation stability, leaving practitioners to combine ad-hoc metrics without principled justification.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263958/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a neural architecture specification (DAG cell or MobileNetV2 block configuration) and a single synthetic batch of 64 Gaussian images at 64×64 resolution with randomly generated target labels (categorical for classification heads, random tensors for spatial outputs). It produces a scalar intrinsic trainability score I(A) that ranks candidate architectures; when plugged into an evolutionary search loop (InTrain-NAS), it yields a full architecture under a user-specified FLOP budget (450M, 600M, or 1000M) with no access to real training data.

## Method

InTrain decomposes trainability into two components coupled multiplicatively. Geometric capacity γ(A) is computed as the log-product of per-layer participation ratios (PR = (Tr C)²/Tr(C²)) of activation covariance eigenspectra, capturing effective representational dimensionality; a bottleneck layer with collapsed variance suppresses the entire product. Optimization resilience o(A) is the cumulative sum over all parameters of a variance-to-maximum gradient-health ratio h(θ_i) = min(1, σ(∇θ_i)/(max|∇θ_i|+ε)), measuring how uniformly gradient magnitude distributes across parameter elements. The two are combined as I(A) = γ(A)·(1+o(A))/log(L+1), where the multiplicative gating encodes the hypothesis that capacity without stable gradients (or vice versa) yields degenerate trainability, and the log-depth normalization enables fair cross-depth comparison. The entire evaluation requires one forward and one backward pass on synthetic data with a task-agnostic loss (cross-entropy or MSE against random targets), making it fully training-free and dataset-independent.

## Results

On NAS-Bench-201, InTrain achieves Kendall's τ of 0.669, 0.671, and 0.675 (Spearman ρ of 0.857, 0.861, 0.859) on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet16-120 respectively, outperforming all single-metric zero-cost proxies. On NAS-Bench-101, InTrain reaches Kendall's τ of 0.56 and Spearman ρ of 0.75. In the full NAS pipeline on ImageNet, InTrain-NAS achieves 78.9%, 79.9%, and 81.3% Top-1 accuracy under 450M, 600M, and 1000M FLOP budgets, matching or exceeding all prior zero-shot methods while requiring only 0.4 GPU days.

## Baselines & Comparisons

InTrain outperforms all single-metric zero-cost proxies on NAS-Bench-201: it exceeds ZiCo (KT 0.607→0.669 on CIFAR-10), SynFlow (0.561→0.669), NASWOT (0.571→0.669), VKDNW_single (0.618→0.669), and Jacov (0.616→0.669) by 5–11 points in Kendall's τ. The only method that surpasses InTrain is the ensemble-based AZ-NAS (KT 0.712 vs. 0.669 on CIFAR-10), which aggregates multiple heterogeneous proxies without a unified theoretical basis. On the ImageNet search pipeline (Table 2), InTrain-NAS achieves the best Top-1 accuracy at all three FLOP budgets (78.9% at 450M, 79.9% at 600M, 81.3% at 1000M), edging out AZ-NAS (78.6%, 79.9%, 81.1%) and ZiCo (78.1%, 79.4%, 80.5%) while using the same 0.4 GPU-day budget. InTrain also substantially outperforms traditional complexity metrics (FLOPs: KT 0.623) and activation-only proxies (Zen-score: KT 0.102), confirming that the multiplicative coupling of geometric capacity and gradient health captures a more discriminative signal than either component alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by unifying two previously separate lines of zero-cost NAS proxies—gradient-based methods (SynFlow, ZiCo, GraSP) and activation-based methods (NASWOT, ZENNAS)—under a single theoretical framework called 'intrinsic trainability.' Rather than building on any single prior proxy, it repurposes the participation ratio (from random matrix theory and generalization analysis) as a geometric capacity measure and introduces a variance-to-maximum gradient-health metric, then couples them multiplicatively. The key conceptual departure from prior work is the explicit hypothesis that capacity and resilience interact through a gating mechanism rather than contributing additively, which the ablation (additive combination underperforming both components) is designed to validate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a theoretically motivated single-metric alternative to ensemble-based proxies like AZ-NAS, which is practically appealing for its simplicity and interpretability (layer-wise diagnostics of capacity bottlenecks and gradient pathologies). However, the 'theoretical' grounding is largely post-hoc: the multiplicative coupling is hypothesized from limiting-case arguments rather than derived from first principles, and the Rényi entropy connection to the participation ratio, while correct, is a standard identity rather than a new insight. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes beyond CNNs to transformers or other architectures, whether the synthetic-input protocol (Gaussian noise, random targets) captures meaningful architecture-specific signal or merely measures initialization statistics, and whether the depth-normalization by log(L+1) is principled or empirically tuned.
