## Problem

Existing MIL frameworks for Whole Slide Image diagnosis treat all misclassifications uniformly, ignoring that in clinical multiclass WSIs the severity of an error is inherently asymmetric—misclassifying a malignant case as benign is far more dangerous than the reverse. Prior mistake-severity methods either assume priority only between distinct labels or define severity purely by class distance, failing to capture the directionality of risk and the implicit within-sample hierarchies that arise because pathologists assign only the single most urgent diagnosis to a WSI.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a bag of 256×256 patches (at 1.0 MPP) extracted from H&E-stained WSIs, encoded by a pretrained feature extractor, together with a predefined class hierarchy (finest classes grouped into coarser sets up to a single root). The WSI-level label is the maximum-severity class among all patches, not a per-patch annotation. Output is a multiclass WSI prediction at each hierarchy level, evaluated with both standard metrics (ACC, AUC) and the proposed asymmetric severity metrics AsCC and AsMC, which penalize high-to-low priority errors more heavily than low-to-high errors.

## Method

PAMS trains a set of MIL classifiers (TransMIL or DTFD-MIL) at each hierarchy level end-to-end with a composite loss L = λ₁·L_MSCE + λ₂·L_HA. The Mistake Severity Cross-Entropy (MSCE) replaces standard CE with a directional weight matrix M where the (i,j) entry is α|i−j| when class i is more urgent than class j, and 1 otherwise, so the loss explicitly penalizes predicting a lower-priority class when the truth is higher-priority. Hierarchical alignment (L_HA) enforces consistency between consecutive levels via Jensen-Shannon divergence on aggregated fine-level probabilities. Semantic Feature Remix (SFR) augments training by clustering instances from two WSIs of different priorities, identifying top-k clusters dominated by the higher-priority WSI's unique patches, and injecting them into the lower-priority bag to synthesize complex multi-finding samples labeled with the higher priority—mimicking a pathologist's diagnostic workflow without requiring pixel-level annotations.

## Results

On the BRACS dataset (TransMIL), PAMS achieves 47.59% ACC, 80.61% AUC, 64.92% AsCC, and 55.65% AsMC, outperforming all baselines on every metric. On the In-house dataset (TransMIL), PAMS reaches 92.27% ACC, 99.31% AUC, 94.84% AsCC, and 56.20% AsMC, with the largest gains in AsMC over the next-best method (Hong et al., 53.10%). The DTFD-MIL-AFS architecture shows similar trends, with PAMS recording 48.28% ACC and 54.64% AsMC on BRACS and 92.19% ACC and 51.78% AsMC on In-house. On CIFAR-10 (natural domain, ResNet-18), MSCE alone achieves 85.64% ACC and 35.70% AsMC, surpassing CDW-CE (84.11% ACC, 34.63% AsMC) and CO2 (83.97% ACC, 34.73% AsMC).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PAMS outperforms all eight baselines on both AsCC and AsMC across both medical datasets and both MIL architectures. The largest AsMC gap is against CE (55.65 vs. 50.18 on BRACS-TransMIL, +5.47) and against Hong et al. τ=10 (55.65 vs. 45.54, +10.11), confirming that random remix without semantic curation is substantially less effective at suppressing severe errors. On the In-house dataset, PAMS leads Hong et al. (τ=10) by 3.10 points in AsMC (56.20 vs. 53.10) and by 1.15 in ACC (92.27 vs. 91.22), while also achieving the highest AsCC (94.84 vs. 94.02). Chang et al. [6] is the closest competitor on BRACS ACC (47.51 vs. 47.59) but trails PAMS by 4.63 in AsMC (51.02 vs. 55.65), and degrades further under DTFD-MIL. On CIFAR-10, MSCE alone beats CDW-CE by 1.53 ACC and 1.07 AsMC, and CO2 by 1.67 ACC and 0.97 AsMC, while Weighted CE (1:10) actually underperforms plain CE on all metrics, highlighting that naive class weighting is insufficient for asymmetric severity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the hierarchical mistake-severity line of work initiated by Bertinetto et al. (CVPR 2020) and Garg et al. (ECCV 2022), which organize multiclass problems into coarse-to-fine hierarchies with per-level classifiers. It departs from prior MS methods in two key ways: (1) it introduces a directionally asymmetric penalty (MSCE) that recognizes misclassifying a high-priority class as a low-priority one is clinically worse than the reverse, and (2) it replaces the random patch remix of Hong et al. (MICCAI 2025) with a semantic clustering-based remix (SFR) that selectively extracts high-priority patches. The asymmetric Mikel's Wheel metric is a direct modification of the symmetric version from Zhao et al. (NeurIPS 2024), adding a penalty term for priority-violating errors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap in clinical AI deployment: conventional accuracy metrics and symmetric severity measures cannot distinguish a model that confuses invasive carcinoma with normal tissue from one that confuses the reverse, yet the former is a catastrophic diagnostic failure. The asymmetric metric (AsCC/AsMC) provides a more clinically meaningful evaluation tool that could influence how medical AI models are selected and validated. Limitations include reliance on manually defined class hierarchies, evaluation on only two medical datasets (one in-house and non-public), and the CIFAR-10 generalizability check being a single-architecture experiment. Open questions include whether the hierarchy structure can be learned automatically and whether the approach transfers to other safety-critical domains with different error-cost structures.
