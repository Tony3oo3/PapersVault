## Problem

Existing 3DGS acceleration methods fail to properly regulate Gaussian count during training, producing redundant primitives that waste computational budget. Their densification and pruning strategies enforce multi-view consistency only indirectly—through Gaussian-associated scores or gradient magnitudes—rather than measuring each Gaussian's actual contribution to multi-view rendering quality, leading to either excessive redundancy or degraded reconstruction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262382/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sparse SfM point cloud for initialization and multi-view RGB training images for supervised optimization. During training, K=10 views are randomly sampled each densification/pruning step to compute per-pixel L1 loss maps, which drive the multi-view consistency scoring. The output is a compact 3D Gaussian scene representation (as few as 0.22M primitives versus 2.63M for vanilla 3DGS) that renders novel views at 579–714 FPS, with training completing in roughly 100 seconds on an RTX 4090.

## Method

FastGS replaces the vanilla 3DGS adaptive density control with two multi-view consistency strategies. Multi-view consistent densification (VCD) projects each Gaussian onto K sampled views, counts high-error pixels (above a threshold on the normalized per-pixel L1 loss map) within its 2D footprint, and only densifies Gaussians whose average high-error count exceeds a threshold—ensuring new primitives target regions under-reconstructed across multiple views. Multi-view consistent pruning (VCP) weights the same high-error pixel count by each view's photometric loss (L1 + SSIM), removing Gaussians whose score indicates they contribute to multi-view rendering degradation. A compact box (CB) further reduces Gaussian-tile pairs during rasterization by pruning pairs with negligible Mahalanobis-distance contribution. Because VCD and VCP precisely identify which Gaussians to add or remove, no budgeting mechanism is needed, making the framework directly pluggable into diverse 3DGS backbones.

## Results

On Mip-NeRF 360, FastGS completes training in 1.93 minutes (≈116 s) with PSNR 27.56, SSIM 0.797, and 0.40M Gaussians, achieving a 3.29× speedup over DashGaussian (6.35 min) and a 10.8× speedup over vanilla 3DGS (20.93 min). On Deep Blending, training finishes in 1.28 minutes with PSNR 30.03, a 15.45× acceleration over 3DGS. The FastGS-Big variant (densification every 100 iterations) reaches PSNR 27.93 on Mip-NeRF 360 in 3.58 minutes, surpassing DashGaussian by 0.2 dB while using fewer than half the Gaussians (1.15M vs. 2.40M). Across dynamic, surface, sparse-view, large-scale, and SLAM tasks, FastGS delivers 2–6× training speedups while preserving or slightly improving rendering quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FastGS outperforms all five static-scene baselines in training time while maintaining comparable or superior rendering quality. Against DashGaussian [4] (the prior SOTA), FastGS is 3.29× faster on Mip-NeRF 360 (1.93 vs. 6.35 min) and FastGS-Big achieves 0.2 dB higher PSNR (27.93 vs. 27.73) with fewer than half the Gaussians. Versus Taming-3DGS [23], FastGS is 2.78× faster with similar PSNR, while Taming-3DGS retains 0.68M Gaussians vs. FastGS's 0.40M. Speedy-Splat [11] is 6.9× slower and suffers a notable quality drop (PSNR 26.91 vs. 27.56 on Mip-NeRF 360), confirming the paper's critique of its indirect multi-view pruning. Mini-Splatting [7] and vanilla 3DGS [16] are 9× and 11× slower respectively. For generality, FastGS plugged into Deformable-3DGS [40] yields a 2.84× speedup on dynamic scenes (Neu3D: 9.29 vs. 26.16 min, with improved PSNR 29.29 vs. 26.74), into PGSR [3] a 2–6× speedup on surface reconstruction, into DropGaussian [27] a 2.56× speedup on sparse-view LLFF, into Octree-GS [30] a 2.19× speedup on BungeeNeRF, and into Mip-Splatting [42] / Scaffold-GS [22] an 8.8× / 3.6× speedup on Mip-NeRF 360, all with negligible quality loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

FastGS builds directly on the 3DGS pipeline (Kerbl et al., 2023) and its acceleration lineage, specifically integrating per-splat parallel backpropagation from Taming-3DGS (Mallick et al., 2024) and precise tile-intersection from Speedy-Splat (Hanson et al., 2025). The key conceptual departure is in adaptive density control: prior methods (Taming-3DGS, DashGaussian, Speedy-Splat, Mini-Splatting) rely on Gaussian-associated proxy scores—opacity, scale, gradient magnitude, Hessian approximations—to decide when to densify or prune, whereas FastGS evaluates each Gaussian's actual contribution to multi-view reconstruction quality via per-pixel L1 loss maps. This shifts the control signal from indirect geometric/photometric properties to a direct measure of rendering error, eliminating the need for budget mechanisms that prior methods require to prevent Gaussian explosion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Training a 3DGS scene in ~100 seconds (15× over vanilla 3DGS) removes the primary practical barrier to deploying 3DGS in interactive and real-time applications, and the framework's generality—delivering 2–6× speedups across dynamic scenes, surface reconstruction, sparse views, large-scale scenes, and SLAM—suggests multi-view-consistent quality-based control is a broadly applicable principle rather than a task-specific trick. A notable limitation is that the method struggles with post-training of feed-forward 3DGS outputs, where the initial Gaussian count is already very dense and pruning within a few thousand iterations degrades quality. Open questions include whether the quality-based scoring can be adapted to amortized/feed-forward settings and whether the per-pixel loss-map approach can be made differentiable to enable end-to-end optimization of the densification schedule.
