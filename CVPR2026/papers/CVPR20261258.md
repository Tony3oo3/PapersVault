## Problem

Existing photo-realistic style transfer and personalized image enhancement methods rely on explicit reference images or well-defined source-target pairs, but real user aesthetic preferences are implicit and only revealed through comparative judgments between differently-processed versions of the same scene. This paper identifies the absence of a large-scale preference dataset, effective learning methods for ambiguous comparative signals, and a suitable evaluation framework as the three key barriers to personalized photographic style learning.

## Input / Output

At inference, the model receives a single RGB query image plus N preference reference triplets (scene, preferred, non-preferred) drawn from the user's stored comparisons. The output is a full-resolution stylized RGB image whose color and tonal rendering reflects the user's implicit aesthetic. During training, one image from each preference pair is randomly selected as input, and the prediction is supervised against both the preferred and non-preferred images simultaneously, with a curriculum that shifts weighting from equal (w=0.5) toward the preferred target (w=1.5).

## Method

The paper explores three baseline architectures rather than proposing a single model: (a) a shared EDSR encoder with per-user HIIF implicit-neural-representation decoders trained at inference time, (b) a global encoder-decoder conditioned on a compact User Preference Embedding computed by summing and subtracting DINOv2 features across N preference pairs and fusing them with a shallow Transformer, and (c) an adapted PIE-MSM transformer that consumes preference triplets as in-context examples to predict a style embedding for a query. All three share a dual-loss training objective that penalizes deviation from both the preferred and non-preferred images, with a linear curriculum on the balance weight w to first learn the style difference before committing to the preferred target. For evaluation, the paper introduces the Comparative Quality Score (CQS), which multiplies a geometric-mean Base Fidelity Score (penalizing poor performance on either target) by a Comparative Margin Ratio (measuring directional bias toward the preferred target), addressing the inadequacy of standard single-target metrics for preference-based tasks.

## Results

On PPSD (50 validation users, N=16 references), Model (b) (User Preference Embedding) achieves the best Comparative Quality Score (CQS) across all four metrics: 0.204 on ΔE₀₀, 9.99 on LPIPS, 36.63 on PSNR, and 0.879 on SSIM. Model (c) (Exemplar-based Inference) ranks second on ΔE₀₀ (0.197) and SSIM (0.867), while Model (a) (User-specific Decoder) trails significantly on ΔE₀₀ (0.154) due to poor absolute fidelity (BFS 0.146). All three methods yield positive CMR values, confirming that each successfully learns to distinguish preferred from non-preferred styles, validating the feasibility of PPS learning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three of its own baseline architectures rather than external methods, since PPS is a newly defined task. Model (b) (User Preference Embedding) wins on CQS for all four metrics, with the largest margin on PSNR (36.63 vs. 25.52/25.72) and LPIPS (9.99 vs. 7.00/7.24). Model (c) (Exemplar-based Inference) achieves the highest preference alignment (CMR 0.108 on ΔE₀₀) but its absolute fidelity is comparable to Model (b), yielding a slightly lower CQS (0.197 vs. 0.204). Model (a) (User-specific Decoder) is clearly the weakest: its BFS of 0.146 on ΔE₀₀ is far below the other two (0.189, 0.196), and the CQS correctly penalizes this despite its moderate CMR of 0.050. The authors highlight that CQS is necessary over naive Δ or average metrics: Model (a) has a larger preference margin than Model (c) on ΔE₀₀ (CMR 0.050 vs. 0.011 for LPIPS) but its poor fidelity makes it inferior overall, a distinction only CQS captures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new problem space between Photo-realistic Style Transfer (PST), which requires an explicit reference image, and Personalized Image Enhancement (PIE), which relies on well-defined source-target pairs or degradation models. The key conceptual departure is that PPS learning has no single reference to mimic and no explicit degradation to reverse; the learning signal is purely comparative, drawn from pairwise user judgments across diverse scenes. Methodologically, the exemplar-based inference approach directly adapts PIE-MSM's two-stage transformer pipeline to handle preference triplets (scene, preferred, non-preferred) rather than input-output pairs, while the other two baselines repurpose LIIF/HIIF implicit decoders and DINOv2 features in a preference-conditioned setting. The comparative preference framing borrows from NLP (HH-RLHF, UltraFeedback) and vision (Pick-a-Pic, HPDv3) datasets, but applies it to the perceptual domain of color and tone rather than content or text-to-image quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By providing the first large-scale pairwise preference dataset for photographic style (767 users, ~60K judgments) along with a purpose-built evaluation metric (CQS), this paper establishes the infrastructure needed to make personalized color/tone retouching a researchable problem. The positive CMR values across all three baselines validate that implicit preference learning from comparative judgments is feasible, but the modest absolute fidelity scores (e.g., ΔE00 ~5 for the best model) indicate significant room for improvement. Open questions the authors highlight include whether fully automated retouching is the right UX paradigm, how to collect preferences naturally over time rather than in concentrated sessions, and whether diffusion-model preference optimization (DPO, DiffusionDPO) can be adapted to this setting. The dataset's reliance on a single web-based collection protocol and the limited scope to color/tone (excluding content-level edits) are notable constraints for generalization.
