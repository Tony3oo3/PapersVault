## Problem

Existing defenses against model stealing in vertical federated learning—perturbation-based (InvL-ENP/DNP) and decoupling-based (Model Rake)—render standard pointwise embedding-regression attacks ineffective by distorting or splitting coordinates. However, these defenses cannot erase the local neighborhood geometry that split models must preserve for the server's top model to maintain accuracy, leaving a residual structural signal that remains exploitable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263792/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The attacker (an honest-but-curious server) issues auxiliary queries in the target client's feature space and records the returned 128-dimensional embeddings. From these (input, embedding) pairs, VENOM produces a surrogate bottom model whose architecture matches the client's (e.g., 3-block ResNet for images, 3-layer FC for tabular/multimodal data). The auxiliary set can be in-distribution or near-OOD (e.g., CIFAR-100 or Tiny-ImageNet for a CIFAR-10 victim), with performance degrading as distributional shift (measured by FID) increases.

## Method

VENOM operates in three stages. First, it trains a contrastive encoder (3-layer linear base + 2-layer projection head) on server-observed embeddings using instance discrimination: two perturbed views of each embedding form a positive pair while all other views in the batch serve as negatives, with a temperature-scaled cosine-similarity objective. After training, the projection head is discarded and the encoder is frozen, yielding a denoised representation space where local neighborhoods are clearer than in the raw, defense-perturbed coordinates. Second, it mines k-nearest (KNN) and k-farthest (KFN) neighbors for each anchor in this contrastive space. Third, it trains the surrogate with a composite loss combining (i) an L2 term and a KL feature-shape term for pointwise alignment in the original embedding space, and (ii) an attraction term pulling the surrogate's contrastive-space output toward its KNN and a repulsion term pushing it away from its KFN, weighted by α=0.5. The key insight is that the neighborhood geometry is a utility-preserving invariant that defenses cannot remove without degrading the top model, making it a stable supervision signal even under aggressive coordinate obfuscation.

## Results

VENOM is evaluated on six datasets (Bank, SUSY, Diabetes, MNIST, CIFAR-10, NUS-WIDE) under eight defense settings, reporting Stealing Accuracy (S-ACC) and Agreement (AGR). Under no defense, gains over the pointwise Steal baseline are modest (e.g., CIFAR-10 S-ACC 65.14→69.97, MNIST 95.05→96.62). Under the strongest defenses the gap widens dramatically: under Model Rake, VENOM lifts S-ACC from 17.44 to 68.52 on MNIST (+51.1 pp) and from 12.84 to 52.58 on CIFAR-10 (+39.7 pp); under InvL-DNP, CIFAR-10 S-ACC rises from 52.16 to 61.59 and AGR from 50.49 to 68.84. VENOM recovers roughly 85–93% of the defended pipeline's accuracy under Model Rake, whereas the baseline retains only 20–64%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the standard pointwise embedding-matching attack (Steal, from PISTE [28]), which regresses a surrogate's outputs directly to server-observed embeddings via an ℓ₂ loss. VENOM outperforms Steal on every dataset–defense combination for both S-ACC and AGR. Under light defenses (Noisy, Pruning, Projection, DP-SGD) the margin is 2–10 pp in S-ACC. Under spectrally shaped noise defenses (InvL-ENP, InvL-DNP) the gap widens to 7–10 pp on tabular data and 8–12 pp on images (e.g., MNIST S-ACC 81.61→90.85 under InvL-ENP). The largest separation appears under Model Rake, where Steal collapses to near-chance on images (MNIST 17.44%, CIFAR-10 12.84%) while VENOM recovers 68.52% and 52.58% respectively — gains of +51.1 and +39.7 percentage points. In AGR terms, the advantage is equally pronounced: under Model Rake on MNIST, AGR jumps from 13.53% to 69.37%, confirming that VENOM's surrogate not only achieves higher accuracy but also aligns its predictions with the defended pipeline's decisions far more faithfully.

## Relation to Prior Work

VENOM extends the PISTE [28] framework for stealing split-learning bottom models via pointwise embedding regression by replacing fragile coordinate matching with geometry-aware supervision in a learned contrastive space. Conceptually, it draws on Cont-Steal [20], which uses contrastive alignment to steal image encoders, and adapts that idea to the VFL setting where the attacker only observes server-side embeddings. The paper positions itself against two defense families—spectrally shaped noise (InvL-ENP/DNP [29]) and two-branch decoupling (Model Rake [39])—arguing that both preserve the local neighborhood structure required for the top model's utility, and that this residual structure is the exploitable signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central implication is that current VFL defenses are fundamentally limited: any defense that preserves utility must retain local embedding geometry, and VENOM shows this geometry is recoverable even under aggressive perturbation or multi-branch obfuscation. This raises the open question of whether a defense can break neighborhood structure without destroying the top model's accuracy—a tension the paper does not resolve. Limitations include the dependence on in-distribution or near-OOD auxiliary queries (far-OOD collapses the attack) and the two-client evaluation setting; scalability to many clients and to defenses that explicitly randomize neighborhood structure remain unaddressed.
