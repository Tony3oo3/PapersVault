## Problem

Video diffusion models struggle to synthesize realistic videos with highly dynamic motions (e.g., breakdancing) or extreme camera movements because such examples are scarce in large-scale training datasets, and accurate camera pose estimation for rapid viewpoint changes is unreliable. Existing work that augments training with rendered synthetic videos introduces an appearance domain gap, causing models to reproduce artificial textures and lighting rather than realistic visuals.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261163/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a text prompt and, for camera-controlled synthesis, camera parameters converted to Plücker embeddings as input, and produces 121-frame RGB videos at 704×1280 resolution. Internally, the pipeline first generates optical flow maps (encoded into the VAE latent space via HSV mapping so the RGB-trained VAE can be reused) and then conditions video generation on those flow maps. The motion generator and video generator are both built on the Wan2.2-5B backbone with a VACE-style control branch for injecting conditioning signals.

## Method

DynaVid is a two-stage diffusion framework that decouples motion synthesis from appearance synthesis: a motion generator produces optical flow maps, and a motion-guided video generator renders RGB frames conditioned on that flow. The key insight is to train the motion generator on synthetic optical flow rendered from Blender's Cycles engine (which encodes only motion, not appearance) rather than on rendered videos, thereby capturing highly dynamic motion patterns without inheriting the artificial look of synthetic renders. The motion generator is pre-trained on real-world flow (extracted via WAFT) and fine-tuned on a mixture of real and synthetic flows using a Flow Matching objective, while the video generator is trained on real video-flow pairs filtered by flow cycle consistency (threshold at the 90th percentile, 1.19 px) to reduce estimation errors. For camera control, Plücker embeddings computed from camera parameters are injected into the motion generator's transformer blocks via pixel-wise addition through the VACE control branch, so the generated flow reflects the specified camera trajectory, which then propagates to the video stage.

## Results

On the DynaVid-Camera test set (extreme camera motion), DynaVid achieves a mean rotation error of 0.9289°, substantially outperforming AC3D (1.1529°) and GEN3C (1.1852°), while also reporting the best I-Qual (0.6713) and A-Qual (0.4501) among all methods. On the DynaVid-Human test set (vigorous human motion), DynaVid achieves FVD of 1351.94 with the highest I-Qual (0.7352) and M-Smooth (0.9931), surpassing CogVideoX-5B (FVD 2238.68) and Wan2.2-5B (FVD 1775.99). On common-scene benchmarks (Pexels, RE10K), DynaVid matches or slightly trails baselines on FVD but leads in imaging quality (I-Qual 0.7342 on Pexels, 0.7224 on RE10K).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CogVideoX-5B and Wan2.2-5B (general text-to-video models), DynaVid achieves lower FVD and higher I-Qual on both Pexels and DynaVid-Human, with the gap widening substantially on highly dynamic scenes (FVD 1351.94 vs. 2238.68 and 1775.99 respectively). HyperMotion, a pose-guided human animation method, reports lower FVD on both datasets (420.82 on Pexels, 391.22 on DynaVid-Human) but this is an artifact of using the first frame as input; on fair visual-quality metrics, DynaVid outperforms HyperMotion in I-Qual (0.7352 vs. 0.6265 on DynaVid-Human) and A-Qual on Pexels (0.5807 vs. 0.5578). For camera control, DynaVid significantly outperforms AC3D on extreme trajectories (mRotErr 0.9289° vs. 1.1529° on DynaVid-Camera) while remaining competitive on moderate RE10K scenes (0.1136° vs. 0.1347°). GEN3C achieves the lowest mRotErr on RE10K (0.0809°) but degrades sharply on dynamic camera motion (1.1852°), where DynaVid is 22% better; DynaVid also produces substantially higher visual quality (I-Qual 0.6713 vs. 0.5659) on DynaVid-Camera, whereas GEN3C exhibits visible artifacts in unseen regions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Wan2.2-5B video diffusion backbone and the VACE conditional architecture, extending them into a two-stage pipeline (motion generator + motion-guided video generator) analogous to prior flow-then-video approaches like MoVideo and LFDM. Its key conceptual departure is from prior synthetic-data training methods (Sharma et al., Zhao et al., Shuai et al.) that render full RGB videos: here, only optical flow is rendered from Blender/Cycles, decoupling motion supervision from appearance and avoiding the well-known domain gap of rendered footage. For camera control, the paper extends the Plücker-embedding conditioning used by CameraCtrl and AC3D by training the motion stage on synthetic NURBS-interpolated trajectories, addressing the scarcity of extreme-motion camera data that limits those prior models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical recipe for injecting highly dynamic motion patterns into video diffusion models without sacrificing visual realism, which is a persistent bottleneck given the underrepresentation of such motions in large-scale training corpora. The ablation confirming that rendered synthetic videos actively degrade appearance quality (not merely fail to help) while rendered flow does not is a useful design principle for future synthetic-data pipelines. However, the current scope is limited to single-person human motion and camera trajectories; generalizing to multi-person scenes, novel object categories, or more complex physical interactions remains open, and the reliance on an off-the-shelf flow estimator (WAFT) for real-data pairs introduces a quality ceiling that the filtering step only partially mitigates.
