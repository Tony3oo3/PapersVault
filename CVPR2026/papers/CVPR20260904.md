## Problem

Existing knowledge distillation methods for medical image segmentation align teacher-student features or prediction distributions but neglect to internalize and integrate the student's own semantic information after knowledge transfer. This causes the student to deviate from its own useful capabilities under teacher guidance, leading to degraded generalization on transfer datasets in 58% of cases across 12 mainstream KD methods.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260904/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 2D medical image slices (512×512 for Synapse multi-label, 256×256 for binary segmentation datasets) and produces pixel-wise segmentation masks. The teacher is a frozen SAM-L vision transformer with LoRA, and the student is a lightweight CNN (EfficientNet-B1 by default, also tested with MobileNetV2 and TinyViT). For multi-label tasks the output is a C-channel probability map per pixel; for binary tasks it is a single-channel mask.

## Method

IAD is a two-stage distillation framework. In the Knowledge Infusion Stage (KIS), soft-label distillation (L2 distance between teacher and student predictions) is combined with a class-weighted prototype alignment strategy that computes per-class prototypes from both predictions and aligns them with weights inversely proportional to class size, mitigating class imbalance while transferring class-level semantics. In the Knowledge Assimilation Stage (KAS), a contrastive semantic self-optimization loss forms positive-negative sample pairs from the student encoder output and an inverted prediction to sharpen foreground-background boundaries and reshape class relative positions in feature space, while a reverse feature constraint (L2 + Dice loss between a projected encoder feature and the prediction) enforces encoder-decoder semantic consistency. KAS is plug-and-play and can be appended to any existing KD method. The two stages are trained sequentially with separate loss weightings, and ground-truth supervision is maintained throughout both stages.

## Results

On the Synapse multi-label segmentation dataset, IAD achieves 84.17% DICE and 12.94 mm HD95, improving over the student baseline (EfficientNet-B1) by +4.32% DICE and +6.22 mm HD95, and surpassing the best KD baseline (KD, 81.75% DICE) by 2.42 points. On ACDC, IAD reaches 89.29% DICE (+1.85 over student). On the four Polyp binary segmentation datasets, IAD attains 77.98% average DICE (+2.42) and 69.70% average mIoU (+2.38). On the four transfer datasets (ISIC2018, PH2, BUSI, STU), IAD delivers a 4.16% average DICE and 4.23% average mIoU gain over the student, outperforming the second-best method (VL2Lite) by 0.96% DICE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IAD outperforms all 12 KD baselines on every dataset evaluated. On Synapse, IAD (84.17% DICE) beats the strongest baseline KD (81.75%) by 2.42 points and the second-best CRD (81.49%) by 2.68 points; notably, three baselines (CATKD, SinKD, VL2Lite) actually degrade below the non-distilled student baseline. On ACDC, IAD (89.29%) surpasses the best competitor CrossKD (88.53%) by 0.76% DICE, while SIKD and CATKD drop 3.19% and 2.75% below the student. On the four Polyp datasets, IAD (77.98% avg DICE) leads the best baseline AT (77.17%) by 0.81 points, with CATKD and SIKD performing worse than the student. On the transfer/generalization benchmark (ISIC2018, PH2, BUSI, STU), IAD achieves 76.56% avg DICE, exceeding VL2Lite (75.60%) by 0.96% and DIST+ (75.15%) by 1.41%, while 7 of 12 baselines (KD, AT, OFD, CrossKD, SIKD, SinKD, CILD) fall below the student baseline, confirming the paper's central claim that existing KD methods suffer from poor knowledge assimilation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the knowledge distillation (KD) paradigm for medical image segmentation by introducing a two-stage 'infusion to assimilation' framework that departs from the dominant single-stage alignment approach. While prior response-based (KD, LSKD, SinKD), feature-based (AT, OFD, CATKD), and hybrid (DIST+, VL2Lite) methods focus on aligning teacher-student predictions or features, IAD adds a post-transfer assimilation stage (KAS) that uses contrastive semantic self-optimization and reverse feature constraints to help the student retain its own useful representations. The class-weighted prototype alignment in KIS draws on prototype-based distillation ideas but applies them at the prediction level specifically to address class imbalance in multi-organ segmentation. The work also builds on the SAM-as-teacher trend in medical imaging, using SAM-L with LoRA as the frozen teacher and lightweight CNNs (EfficientNet-B1, MobileNetV2, TinyViT) as students.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and underappreciated failure mode: 58% of mainstream KD methods degrade student generalization on unseen transfer datasets, suggesting that naive teacher-to-student alignment can suppress the student's own inductive biases. The plug-and-play KAS component (showing 2–5 DICE point gains when appended to existing KD methods) offers a practical tool for the community without requiring redesign of the distillation pipeline. Limitations include evaluation restricted to 2D slices, a single teacher-student architecture pair for most experiments, manually tuned class weights per dataset, and the absence of 3D or end-to-end validation. Open questions include whether the assimilation principle generalizes to 3D volumetric segmentation, classification, or detection tasks, and whether the two-stage training can be unified into a single end-to-end objective.
