## Problem

Pretrained neural networks are typically treated as monolithic units that process every input through all layers uniformly, yet it remains unclear whether simple images reach stable predictions much earlier in the network. Prior early-exit work requires architectural modifications and specialized training, leaving the intrinsic decision-making timeline of unmodified, deployed models uncharacterized. This paper asks: in existing pretrained classifiers, at which layer does each image's prediction naturally stabilize?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261413/cropped_page4_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes standard 224×224 RGB images from the full ImageNet validation set (50,000 images) and passes them through frozen pretrained networks (ResNet-18/50/101, ViT-B/16, ConvNeXt-Tiny) without any weight modification. For each image, it produces a per-image decision layer (the first of 12 anchor points where the probe prediction persists unchanged across k consecutive anchors), a per-layer probe accuracy curve, an early/mid/late decider assignment based on relative depth thresholds (30%/70%), and a cumulative decision curve. The terminal anchor doubles as a fallback bucket for samples whose predictions never stabilize, so the decision-layer distribution is not a pure density over genuine stabilization points.

## Method

The core technique is a forensic linear-probing analysis: lightweight linear classifiers (W·h + b, trained with SGD, momentum 0.9, lr 0.1, 100 epochs with early stopping) are fit at 12 matched anchor layers spanning stem through final pooling, using 70% of validation data for training and 30% for evaluation. The decision layer for each image is defined as the first anchor l where the predicted class remains identical across a persistence window of k consecutive anchors (k swept over {1,2,3,4}); if no such anchor exists, the terminal anchor is assigned by convention. This 'when does the prediction crystallize' framing differs from standard probing (which asks what information is encoded) and from early-exit training (which adds learnable exit branches). The authors separately track probe accuracy at each anchor to distinguish stable-but-wrong predictions at low-accuracy layers from stable-and-correct ones, and they partition the decision-layer distribution into early (<0.3T), mid, and late (≥0.7T) bins to quantify bimodality and architectural heterogeneity.

## Results

Under k=2 stability, ResNet-50 achieves a final probe accuracy of 73.7% with a mean decision layer of 5.51, while ViT-B/16 reaches 80.8% at mean depth 4.98 and ConvNeXt-Tiny reaches 79.3% at mean depth 9.51. The decision distributions are strongly bimodal: ResNet-50 splits into 39% early / 42% late / 18% mid deciders, whereas ConvNeXt-Tiny is heavily late-skewed (87% late, 62% never-stable). Mean decision depth shifts 2.4–3.4× as the stability window tightens from k=1 to k=4 (e.g., ResNet-50: 2.86 → 9.10), and the practical efficiency gain is minimal—stability-only gating yields only 34.68% accuracy at 28.73 ms versus 76.15% at 49.46 ms for full-depth inference.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a forensic empirical study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the primary contrasts are cross-architecture and cross-criterion. Across architectures, ViT-B/16 exhibits the shallowest mean decision depth (4.98) and highest final probe accuracy (80.8%), while ConvNeXt-Tiny is the most late-skewed (87% late, 62% never-stable) despite comparable final accuracy (79.3%), indicating that decision-depth statistics are architecture-dependent rather than dataset-determined. Within the ResNet family, deeper models (50/101) distribute decisions more evenly (33–39% early) compared to ResNet-18's pronounced late-heavy bias (54% late vs. 23% early), reflecting capacity constraints. On the practical efficiency front, the paper compares three gating strategies against full-depth inference: stability-only gating (34.68% acc, 28.73 ms) is far below baseline (76.15% acc, 49.46 ms), while confidence thresholding (73.65% acc, 49.13 ms) nearly matches baseline accuracy but provides negligible latency savings, yielding a nearly vertical Pareto frontier. Under mild corruptions (noise, blur, brightness), mean decision depth shifts by only +0.01 to +0.71 anchors, confirming that decision timelines are relatively robust to small distribution shifts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes linear probing methodology—standardly used to ask *what* information is encoded at a given layer—into a forensic tool that asks *when* predictions naturally stabilize during a forward pass, without modifying the base architecture. It explicitly departs from early-exit literature (ClassyNet, JEI-DNN, nested prediction sets) that requires trainable exit branches and specialized training, and from representation-similarity analyses (CKA, SVCCA) that measure layer-to-layer similarity without tracking per-sample prediction formation. The contribution is thus a new diagnostic lens on unmodified pretrained networks rather than a new architecture or training procedure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central negative result—that clear bimodal decision heterogeneity (33–39% early deciders) does not translate into practical latency savings because early stability is predominantly stable-but-wrong—redirects the early-exit research agenda away from naive stability gating toward learned readiness predictors, confidence calibration, or architectures with multiple semantic consolidation points. The 15× accuracy phase transition in final residual stages challenges the gradual feature-composition narrative and has implications for where compression or capacity investment should target. Open questions include whether the findings generalize beyond ImageNet, whether learned gating can close the stability-correctness gap, and whether the bimodal structure persists under domain shift or fine-tuning.
