## Problem

Current deep learning methods for automatic tooth alignment predict transformation matrices using only point-to-point geometric constraints, ignoring the clinically specific distribution characteristics (e.g., coupled translation-rotation correlations, biomechanically bounded value ranges) that emerge from the evolutionary biomechanical forces shaping malocclusion. This geometric-only supervision fails to capture the latent statistical structure of valid orthodontic transformations, limiting prediction accuracy and physiological plausibility.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-orthodontic 3D tooth point cloud of 4,096 points organized into 32 sub-point clouds (128 points per tooth) as input and outputs a set of 32 per-tooth 4×4 rigid transformation matrices (each encoding a 3×3 rotation and 3×1 translation). Applying these matrices to the input point cloud yields the aligned post-orthodontic arrangement. The diffusion module operates on the reshaped 32×4×4 matrix tensor rather than raw point cloud data, reducing input dimensionality for the denoising process.

## Method

TAlignDiff unifies a PointNet-based regression network (PRN) with a DDPM-based transformation matrix denoising module (DTMD) in a joint optimization framework. The PRN uses two PointNet encoders (global and local) with an MLP decoder to regress transformation matrices under point-wise reconstruction and centroid offset losses. The DTMD learns the latent distribution of clinically valid transformation matrices via forward noising and reverse denoising, and a novel contrastive denoising loss compares noise estimates from predicted versus ground-truth matrices to enforce that predictions reside on the same gradient flow as the true distribution. Training follows a staged strategy: PRN and DTMD are trained separately for 200 epochs, then DTMD is frozen and its contrastive denoising loss guides PRN refinement for 200 additional epochs. At inference, only the lightweight PRN is used, so the diffusion module adds no computational overhead to prediction.

## Results

On the ISICDM 2024 challenge test set (30 cases), TAlignDiff achieves a Target Registration Error (TRE) of 0.725±0.216 mm and an Absolute Arch Error (AAE) of 0.646±0.207 mm, outperforming all baselines. On an independent orthodontic dataset (30 unseen cases), the method further reduces TRE to 0.716±0.188 mm and AAE to 0.645±0.179 mm, confirming cross-domain generalization. Statistical significance tests (p < 0.01) confirm the improvements over all compared methods on both datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TAlignDiff outperforms all six baselines on both the challenge test set and the independent orthodontic dataset. Against the closest competitor PSTN (TRE 0.779, AAE 0.705 on test), TAlignDiff reduces TRE by 0.054 mm and AAE by 0.059 mm. The prior diffusion-based method TADPM [13], which the paper builds upon, performs worst among methods with test-set results (TRE 0.890, AAE 0.821), suggesting that directly regressing matrices from high-dimensional geometric features is less effective than the proposed two-stage regression-then-denoise pipeline. On the independent dataset (Table 3), TAlignDiff (TRE 0.716, AAE 0.645) beats the next-best TADPM (TRE 0.747, AAE 0.679) and PointMLP (TRE 0.758, AAE 0.685), with the largest margin over PointNet++ (TRE 0.899, AAE 0.831). TANet and LETA report only validation-set results due to a closed challenge test server, but their validation numbers (TRE 0.885 and 0.777, respectively) are still higher than TAlignDiff's validation TRE of 0.690. All improvements are statistically significant (p < 0.01).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the TADPM framework (Lei et al., 2024), which first introduced diffusion probabilistic models for learning tooth transformation matrix distributions, by decoupling the regression and distribution-modeling stages. Rather than conditioning a diffusion process on high-dimensional point cloud and mesh features as TADPM does, TAlignDiff first predicts initial transformation matrices via a PointNet-based regression network (PRN) and then applies a lightweight diffusion denoising model (DTMD) directly on the low-dimensional transformation matrices. The key architectural departure is the introduction of a contrastive denoising loss that compares noise estimates from predicted versus ground-truth matrices, creating a bidirectional feedback loop between geometric regression and distribution refinement—a mechanism absent in prior tooth alignment methods that rely solely on point-wise geometric constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work addresses a genuine clinical gap: geometric constraints alone cannot capture the biomechanically coupled, distributionally constrained nature of real tooth movements (e.g., correlated translation-rotation patterns, physiological angle limits). The practical advantage that DTMD is training-only (not needed at inference) makes the approach deployable without added latency. However, the evaluation is limited to 124 patients from a single challenge dataset plus 30 cases from one independent set, leaving open questions about generalization across malocclusion types, population diversity, and whether the learned distribution truly encodes biomechanical priors or merely memorizes dataset-specific statistics. Scaling to larger, multi-center clinical datasets and validating with orthodontists on treatment plan quality remain important next steps.
