## Problem

Vision-language models frequently over-rely on textual cues, hallucinate unsupported visual details, and produce chain-of-thought explanations that are inconsistent with the actual reasoning process. While saliency maps can reveal which image regions drive a model's predictions, existing techniques are computationally expensive (requiring gradients or multiple forward passes) and have not been integrated into the training loop to actively enforce faithful visual grounding.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260724/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives an RGB image (encoded into visual tokens via a ViT) and a text question, and produces a structured response consisting of a thinking trace enclosed in  tags followed by a final answer. During both inference and training, the framework additionally generates per-token saliency maps over the image patch grid by extracting first-order logit contributions from visual tokens, requiring no extra forward or backward passes. For training, each sample in the saliency-r1-8k dataset includes a human-annotated bounding box marking the image region essential for answering the question, which serves as the alignment target.

## Method

Saliency-R1 decomposes each generated token's logits into first-order contributions from all context tokens (following the transformer circuits framework), isolates the contributions from visual tokens, applies ReLU to discard negative values, and arranges them into a spatial saliency map over the image patch grid. To capture the full reasoning trajectory, the method performs attention rollout through thinking tokens as a bottleneck: it multiplies the visual-to-thinking attention matrix by the thinking-to-answer attention matrix at each layer and head, then aggregates the resulting transitional contributions into a holistic saliency map. This alignment score (ratio of saliency mass inside the annotated bounding box to total saliency mass) is combined with accuracy and format rewards in a GRPO objective, where the policy is optimized via LoRA to maximize the composite reward across a group of sampled responses. The key design choice is that the saliency maps are computed entirely from quantities already available in the KV cache and attention weights, making the reward signal computationally free and compatible with real-time RL training.

## Results

On the 7B model, Saliency-R1 improves over the Qwen2.5-VL-7B base by +1.4 on POPE (88.1 vs 86.7), +4.2 on ChartQA (88.2 vs 84.0), +4.2 on MME-RealWorld (62.9 vs 58.7), and +14.6 on SalBench (63.7 vs 49.1). Against closed-source models, Saliency-R1-7B surpasses GPT-4o on MME (2385 vs 2329), MME-RealWorld (62.9 vs 45.2), and ScienceQA (94.3 vs 89.2), and outperforms Claude-3.5 Sonnet on MME (2385 vs 1920) and MME-RealWorld (62.9 vs 51.6). On the saliency-r1-8k interpretability test set, Saliency-R1-7B achieves 30.08% energy-PG and 38.63% PG, a +10.82% and +14.14% improvement over the base model respectively. The saliency map technique itself achieves competitive faithfulness (deletion 70.96% at 5% on COCO) while requiring no extra forward or backward passes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Saliency-R1-7B outperforms GPT-4o on MME (2385 vs 2329), MME-RealWorld (62.9 vs 45.2), and ScienceQA (94.3 vs 89.2), but trails on MMMU-Pro (37.6 vs 54.0) and MMStar (62.6 vs 63.9). Against Claude-3.5 Sonnet, it wins decisively on MME (2385 vs 1920) and MME-RealWorld (62.9 vs 51.6) but loses on ChartQA (88.2 vs 90.8). Among open-source models, Saliency-R1-7B beats InternVL2.5-8B on MME (2385 vs 2344) and MME-RealWorld (62.9 vs 58.1) but falls short on POPE (88.1 vs 90.6) and MMBench (81.8 vs 84.6). Compared to the closest reasoning-model baseline Vision-R1-11B, Saliency-R1 gains +195 on MME (2385 vs 2190) and +1.5 on MME-RealWorld (62.9 vs 61.4). The ablation in Fig. 4 confirms that the saliency alignment reward contributes +1.5% average accuracy over standard GRPO (Vision-R1 protocol), and that using only the saliency reward (Saliency-R1-pure) retains 99.4% of the full model's performance while eliminating the need for an LLM judge.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) saliency-map interpretability for transformers (building on logits decomposition from Elhage et al. and attention rollout from Abnar & Zuidema), (2) VLM reasoning via RL (building on Vision-R1 and the GRPO framework from DeepSeekMath), and (3) attention manipulation in VLMs (PASTA, ADAPTVIS, INSTABOOST). The key conceptual departure is repurposing saliency maps from post-hoc explanation tools into a differentiable training signal: by computing token-level visual contributions via first-order logits decomposition (requiring no extra forward or backward passes), the authors use the overlap between these maps and human-annotated bounding boxes as a GRPO reward, thereby aligning the model's internal visual attention with task-relevant regions during post-training rather than steering attention at inference time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a persistent trustworthiness gap in VLMs—unfaithful chain-of-thought and hallucinated visual grounding—by making interpretability a training objective rather than a diagnostic. The finding that the saliency alignment reward alone (Saliency-R1-pure) nearly matches the full multi-reward objective suggests the visual-attention signal is the dominant driver of improvement, which could simplify future training pipelines by eliminating LLM-judge calls. However, limitations temper the impact: training data is small (8k samples for RL), bounding boxes are coarse compared to pixel-level segmentation, and evaluation is restricted to 3B/7B models, leaving open whether the approach scales to larger architectures or benefits from finer spatial supervision (e.g., SAM 3 masks).
