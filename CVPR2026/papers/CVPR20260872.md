## Problem

Vision-language models achieve strong multimodal performance, yet how computation is organized across populations of neurons remains poorly understood. Existing interpretability analyses rely on local signals—attention patterns, saliency maps, patch attribution—that identify influential inputs but cannot characterize the large-scale population-level structure through which multimodal behavior is actually realized. This paper asks whether within-layer neuron correlation topology carries recoverable behavioral signal, how it evolves across modalities and depth, and whether it identifies causally influential internal components.

## Input / Output

The method takes an image-question pair, runs a frozen VLM forward pass, and records hidden activations H^(ℓ) ∈ R^{d×N} at each transformer layer. Crucially, the downstream analysis module never receives raw activation magnitudes or token semantics; it operates solely on the Pearson correlation structure between neuron activation profiles across tokens, represented as a sparse weighted graph (top-k edges, density ≤ 0.2). The output is a fixed-dimensional graph-level structural signature h^(ℓ) per layer, which supports task prediction (classification and regression), hallucination status classification, cross-modal alignment scoring, and identification of hub neurons for causal intervention.

## Method

Each transformer layer is represented as a complete weighted graph whose nodes are neurons and whose edge weights are Pearson correlations of neuron activation profiles across all multimodal tokens in a single inference pass. To analyze these graphs without exposing hidden-state content, each neuron is assigned a learnable one-hot identity embedding projected into a low-dimensional space, and a GCN propagates information over the correlation graph to produce topology-dependent node representations, which are then aggregated via mean and max pooling into a per-layer structural signature. Probes (linear and GCN-based) are trained on these signatures to predict task outputs, and a symmetric InfoNCE objective is used to contrastively align modality-specific graph embeddings (vision-only, text-only, multimodal) into a shared structural space. Hub neurons are identified by graph degree (sum of absolute edge weights), and their causal importance is validated through targeted ablation, edge-level activation swapping (identical/opposite/random), and direct scaling perturbations, all of which produce substantially larger performance drops than activation-magnitude-based selection.

## Results

Graph-based (GCN) probes consistently outperform linear probes on neuron-correlation topology across three VLMs (InternVL3-1B, Qwen2.5-VL-3B, LLaVA-1.5-7B) and six benchmarks. The largest gains appear on CLEVR counting, where GCN improves accuracy by 7.7% on LLaVA (67.9% vs. 60.2%), 4.3% on Qwen2.5-VL (96.3% vs. 92.0%), and 1.3% on InternVL3 (99.3% vs. 98.0%). For hallucination detection on MHaluBench, GCN probes reach 78.9–91.0% accuracy, substantially exceeding text-only baselines (50.0–66.4%). On CLEVR counting regression, GCN probes reduce MSE by 37–65% over linear probes (e.g., LLaVA: 0.379 vs. 0.605) while improving R² from 0.884 to 0.928.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between GCN probes and linear probes operating on the same graph-derived layer representations. GCN wins on 11 of 18 model-dataset pairs (Table 1), with the most consistent and largest margins on grounded reasoning tasks (CLEVR, TDIUC) and smaller or mixed margins on harder open-ended benchmarks (MMMU, EMMA). For hallucination detection (Table 3), GCN probes (78.9%, 91.0%, 90.8% across the three VLMs) outperform both text-only baselines—mean word2vec embedding (66.4%, 65.4%, 64.9%) and token length (50.0%, 63.3%, 64.2%)—by 12–41 percentage points, confirming that topological structure carries hallucination signal beyond shallow lexical cues. In the causal intervention experiments, graph-degree-ranked neuron ablation causes the largest accuracy drop compared to random selection or activation-magnitude selection, and edge-level 'opposite' intervention (negating a partner neuron's activation) degrades performance more than random replacement, indicating that the sign and alignment of correlated activity matter beyond node identity alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the neuron correlation topology framework from Zheng et al. [50], which applied GCN-based probing to LLM layers, to the multimodal VLM setting. It departs from the dominant local-attribution paradigm in VLM interpretability (attention flow, Grad-CAM, patch saliency) by operating at the population level: each layer is treated as a neuron-neuron correlation graph rather than a collection of individually interpretable units. The modality-specific subgraph decomposition and cross-modal contrastive alignment (InfoNCE) are new analytical tools not present in the LLM predecessor, and the causal intervention section (hub ablation, edge-level perturbation) connects to the mechanistic interpretability tradition of causal mediation testing [17, 31] but applies it to topology-defined rather than sparsity-defined components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes neural topology as a practical intermediate scale for VLM interpretability—richer than per-token attribution but far more tractable than full circuit recovery—and demonstrates that this scale carries recoverable behavioral signal, identifies causally influential hub neurons, and reveals depth-dependent cross-modal consolidation. Key limitations temper the claims: correlation graphs are not causal wiring diagrams, performance on harder reasoning benchmarks (MMMU, EMMA) is mixed and sometimes below linear baselines, and the hub sets are small (top 1%) so the causal evidence, while consistent, is narrow in scope. Open questions include whether topology-defined interventions scale to targeted model editing, whether hub identity is stable across fine-tuning or continued pre-training, and whether the framework generalizes to non-transformer multimodal architectures.
