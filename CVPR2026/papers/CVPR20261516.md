## Problem

Existing text-to-motion generation methods rely on sequence-level or action-level descriptions and lack fine-grained, temporally-aligned body-part annotations, making it impossible to independently control individual body parts or compose novel motions from atomic part-level elements. This gap stems from both the absence of structured part-level motion datasets and model architectures that cannot jointly reason over spatial (which body part) and temporal (when) dimensions.

## Input / Output

The model accepts three hierarchical text conditions: a single sequence-level description, W non-overlapping action-level prompts aligned to temporal windows, and sparse per-frame, per-part prompts for K predefined body parts (head, arms, legs, spine, trajectory). Unknown part labels are common due to the sparse annotation structure. The output is a T-frame 3D motion sequence in a rotation-invariant representation comprising SMPL pose parameters (6D), pelvis Z-coordinate, linear and angular velocities, and local joint positions.

## Method

FrankenMotion is a transformer-based DDPM diffusion model that fuses multi-granularity text into a joint latent space: CLIP text embeddings for action and part labels are PCA-reduced to 50 dimensions, expanded to per-frame resolution, concatenated with the noisy motion input, and passed through an MLP to produce fused motion-text tokens; a sequence-level CLIP embedding and diffusion timestep are appended as global tokens. Training uses standard DDPM loss with Beta-distribution stochastic masking over part labels to handle sparsity and improve robustness to incomplete conditioning. The key enabling contribution is the FrankenStein dataset, where FrankenAgent (a Deepseek-R1 LLM) infers temporally-aligned, atomic body-part annotations from existing sequence and action labels in BABEL, KIT-ML, and HumanML3D, producing 46.1k part-level annotations across 39 hours of motion. This hierarchical conditioning allows the model to learn atomic part-to-motion mappings and compose them into coherent whole-body motions guided by higher-level semantics.

## Results

On the FrankenStein test set, FrankenMotion achieves the best semantic correctness across all three granularity levels: 47.21% R@1 and 58.97% R@3 at the part level, 48.10% R@1 and 65.79% R@3 at the action level, and 65.27% R@1 and 85.62% R@3 at the sequence level. In terms of realism, FrankenMotion attains the lowest FID scores (0.04 per-action, 0.06 per-sequence) and highest diversity (53.82 per-action, 48.60 per-sequence) among all baselines. The closest competitor, UniMotion, trails by 1.5–2.6 points on R@1 across levels and by 0.02–0.04 on FID.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FrankenMotion outperforms all three adapted baselines on every reported metric. Against UniMotion (the strongest baseline), FrankenMotion gains +1.49 on Part R@1, +1.61 on Part R@3, +0.52 on Action R@1, +3.61 on Seq R@1, and +3.54 on Seq R@3, while also achieving lower FID (0.04 vs 0.05 per-action; 0.06 vs 0.08 per-sequence). STMC, a post-hoc stitching method, lags significantly behind on all semantic correctness metrics (e.g., 40.67 vs 47.21 Part R@1) and produces the worst realism (FID 0.10/0.20), reflecting its inability to compose parts into coherent whole-body motion. DartControl, an autoregressive model, suffers from error accumulation and repetitive segments, yielding the lowest Part R@1 (38.67) and highest Seq FID (0.28) among baselines, though it is competitive on Seq R@1 (54.28) due to its strong sequence-level text following. Qualitatively, STMC fails to follow fine-grained instructions like 'turn around,' UniMotion ignores detailed part-level cues, and DART produces repeated motion segments, whereas FrankenMotion faithfully composes all hierarchical prompts into a single coherent motion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the text-to-motion generation line (MDM, TEMOS, UniMotion, DART) by introducing a unified hierarchical conditioning scheme that simultaneously controls sequence-level semantics, atomic actions, and individual body parts at fine temporal resolution. It departs from STMC, which composes part motions post-hoc via stitching, and FineMoGen, which enforces synchronized temporal windows across parts, by learning end-to-end part-aware generation with asynchronous, semantically distinct part movements. The dataset contribution (FrankenStein) repurposes the LLM reasoning capability of Deepseek-R1 to automatically decompose existing high-level annotations (from BABEL, KIT-ML, HumanML3D) into structured per-frame body-part labels, addressing the annotation bottleneck that has limited prior fine-grained motion work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The FrankenStein dataset (39 hours, 138.5k labels, 46.1k part-level annotations) provides the first large-scale resource with temporally aligned, atomic part-level text, enabling a new class of experiments in fine-grained motion control and editing that were previously infeasible. The compositional property—generating novel motions like 'sitting while raising the left arm' that were never observed in training—suggests a path toward open-ended motion synthesis from structured descriptions. However, the model cannot yet generate minute-long sequences in a single pass, and the performance gains over adapted baselines (e.g., ~1.5 points R@1 over UniMotion at part level) are modest, leaving open whether the hierarchical design's advantage grows with scale or more diverse training data.
