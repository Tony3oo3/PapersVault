## Problem

Existing spatial intelligence benchmarks for MLLMs are task-centric and fragmented, treating spatial capabilities as isolated or overlapping skills without revealing their underlying structure. This makes it impossible to understand how spatial abilities emerge, interact, and transfer across levels of complexity. The paper asks whether a compact, capability-centric hierarchy can expose the dependencies and transfer dynamics among 27 fine-grained spatial sub-abilities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263185/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes RGB images, multi-view image sets, video clips, and text prompts at varying resolutions depending on the task level. Outputs are level-specific: L1–L2 tasks yield classification labels, numerical estimates (distance, size, orientation), or spatial captions; L3 tasks produce chain-of-thought reasoning chains and step-by-step navigation plans; L4 tasks output discrete key-mouse action sequences derived from an action-mapping strategy that converts embodiment-specific controls (game characters, robot grippers, human hands) into unified high-level motion primitives. Agentic L4 data is additionally reformatted into multi-step multiple-choice tasks to facilitate MLLM evaluation.

## Method

SpatialTree organizes spatial intelligence into a four-level hierarchy (L1 Perception → L2 Mental Mapping → L3 Mental Simulation → L4 Agentic Competence) and instantiates it as SpatialTree-Bench, a 27-sub-ability benchmark built by reorganizing prior datasets and filling gaps with a Spatial Engine that chains expert models (DepthAnything3, SpatialTracker, GeoCalib, OrientAnything) through level-specific QA generation pipelines. The key empirical contribution is a training-intervention study: SFT experiments reveal negative transfer within L1 but strong cross-level transfer from low to high abilities with multi-ability synergy, while RL experiments (GRPO) expose a dichotomy where naive 'thinking' rewards help complex reasoning but degrade intuitive perception. This motivates a hierarchy-aware auto-think reward mechanism that suppresses deliberation for L1 perception tasks (length penalty, no thinking reward) while amplifying reasoning rewards for L3–L4 tasks, yielding consistent gains across all levels.

## Results

On SpatialTree-Bench, Gemini 3 Flash achieves the highest overall score of 57.8, followed by Gemini 3 Pro (56.5) and Seed1.8 (50.3). Among open-source models, Qwen3VL-235B leads with 40.0, substantially ahead of Qwen3VL-30B (35.3) and Qwen2.5VL-72B (33.0). A key structural finding is that L1 perception sub-abilities (Geometry, Motion, Relation, Localization, Orientation) show weak mutual correlations and are largely orthogonal, while L3–L4 capabilities (causal reasoning, sequential planning, goal execution, open exploration) exhibit strong positive correlations, indicating increasing interdependency at higher levels. RL with a hierarchy-aware 'auto-think' strategy improves Qwen2.5-VL-7B from 27.3 to 30.8 average, with the largest gains in Goal Execution (+8.3) and Open Exploration (+5.8).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini 3 Flash (57.8) outperforms all other models by a wide margin, beating Gemini 3 Pro (56.5) by 1.3 points and Seed1.8 (50.3) by 7.5 points on the overall average. Among open-source models, Qwen3VL-235B (40.0) leads, surpassing Qwen3VL-30B (35.3) by 4.7 points and Qwen2.5VL-72B (33.0) by 7.0 points, though it trails the top proprietary models by 17–18 points. GPT-4o (31.9) ranks 13th, below even the 72B open-source Qwen2.5VL, and is notably weak on Geometry (23.9) and Localization (24.2) despite competitive Motion (38.6) scores. Specialized spatial-intelligence models (VST-base 26.2, SpaceR-base 28.1, SenseNova-base 30.2) all underperform their general-purpose base models on the full benchmark, suggesting narrow spatial tuning does not transfer broadly. In the RL experiments, the hierarchy-aware auto-think strategy improves Qwen2.5-VL-7B from 27.3 to 30.8 average, outperforming naive full-level RL (30.1) and all single-level RL variants, with the largest relative gains in L4 Goal Execution (+8.3 vs. +5.8 for naive RL) and L1 Geometry (+3.3 vs. +1.1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the task-centric trajectory of prior spatial benchmarks (BLINK, SpatialEval, 3DSR-Bench, VSI-Bench, MMSI-Bench, Omnispatial, SITE, IR3D-Bench) by proposing a capability-centric hierarchical taxonomy inspired by cognitive science (Piaget, Tolman, Kuipers). Methodologically, it builds on GRPO (DeepSeekMath) for reinforcement learning but introduces a hierarchy-aware reward mechanism that differentiates between intuitive perception and complex reasoning, and on Visual Spatial Tuning (VST) for data mixing recipes and as a baseline specialized model. The key conceptual departure is treating spatial intelligence as a structured tree with measurable inter-level dependencies rather than a flat collection of independent tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SpatialTree-Bench provides the first unified, capability-organized evaluation across 27 sub-abilities, giving the field a common language and diagnostic tool for spatial MLLMs. The finding that naive RL with extensive 'thinking' degrades low-level perception while helping high-level reasoning has direct practical implications for post-training pipelines of spatial MLLMs, and the auto-think strategy offers a simple, broadly applicable fix. Open questions include whether the four-level hierarchy is the correct granularity, how L4 agentic competence scales beyond the three embodiments tested, and whether the cross-level transfer dynamics observed in SFT and RL hold across model architectures and scales beyond Qwen2.5-VL-7B.
