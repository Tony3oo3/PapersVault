## Problem

Aligning autonomous driving agents with human cognitive expectations remains difficult: conventional RLHF relies on slow, indirect manual preference ranking, while existing EEG-guided RL methods require real-time brain-signal collection during inference, limiting scalability. This paper asks whether human cognitive responses (specifically ERP amplitudes) can be predicted directly from visual scene data and used as an implicit reward signal, eliminating the need for a human in the loop at test time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20264039/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy network consumes a temporal sequence of three single-channel semantic segmentation frames (90° FOV, from CARLA's front camera) and outputs a continuous longitudinal control value in [-1, 1] (brake to throttle) plus a time-to-collision estimate clipped to [0, 5] s. The auxiliary EEG feature prediction model takes the same segmentation frames and outputs a binary probability of ERP induction, which is fed back into the RL reward as a scalar cognitive penalty. All inference operates purely on segmentation images—no EEG, gaze, or other physiological signal is required at test time.

## Method

The framework has three stages. First, EEG is recorded from 20 participants in a VR driving simulator (HTC Vive Pro Eye + CARLA) during emergency-braking and left-turn scenarios; ERP (P3) peaks are extracted and thresholded (1.7 µV peak-to-peak) to form high/low-ERP trial labels. Second, a lightweight three-convolutional-layer CNN with average pooling is trained via binary cross-entropy to predict ERP presence from segmentation images, achieving 82 % five-fold accuracy at 204 fps—faster than ResNet-18, Swin-ViT, or ConvNeXt baselines. Third, the predicted ERP probability is injected into the TD3 reward function as a negative cognitive-load term (β = −1), combined with collision (−100), idle (−1), and time-gap rewards. The policy network encodes the three-frame state with a shallow CNN, applies a self-attention layer to enlarge the receptive field, and uses two MLP heads to jointly predict the control action and TTC (the latter regularized with an MSE auxiliary loss, α = 0.1). This design lets the agent internalize human cognitive salience during training while remaining fully autonomous at inference.

## Results

On the emergency braking scenario, the proposed cognitive-reward RL framework achieves a route completion of 85±43, driving score of 79±31, and infraction score of 0.84±0.14, outperforming all baselines. On the left-turn scenario, it reaches 67±8 route completion, 57±10 driving score, and 0.77±0.05 infraction score. The lightweight EEG feature prediction model attains 82% mean five-fold cross-validation accuracy at 204 FPS, surpassing ResNet-18 (81%, 73 FPS), Swin-ViT (79%, 62 FPS), and ConvNeXt (80%, 74 FPS) in both accuracy and inference speed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all five RL baselines on every metric in both scenarios. In emergency braking, it leads the closest competitor (manual RLHF) by 12 points in route completion (85 vs. 73) and 13 points in driving score (79 vs. 66), while also achieving the highest infraction score (0.84 vs. 0.80). In the left-turn scenario, the margin is smaller but consistent: 4 points in route completion over RLHF (67 vs. 63) and 8 points in driving score (57 vs. 49). Behavior Cloning, which excels in emergency braking route completion (65) but collapses in left-turn driving score (29), is the weakest structured baseline. TD3-lag, the safe-RL variant, underperforms vanilla TD3 in left-turn route completion (40 vs. 60), suggesting the Lagrangian constraint is overly conservative. The EEG prediction model itself also edges out heavier architectures (ResNet-18, Swin-ViT, ConvNeXt) in both accuracy (82% vs. 79–81%) and throughput (204 vs. 62–74 FPS), saving approximately 2.1 hours of RL training time over ResNet-18 at 1M steps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLHF paradigm (Christiano et al., 2017) by replacing explicit human preference rankings with implicit neural signals, specifically event-related potentials (ERPs) recorded via EEG. It builds on prior ErrP-based RL work (Wang et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2021) that used brain signals for shared autonomy, but departs critically by eliminating the need for real-time EEG collection during inference—instead training a lightweight CNN to predict ERP occurrence directly from semantic segmentation images, drawing on the broader literature on predicting brain responses from visual stimuli (Cichy et al., 2017; Gifford et al., 2022). The driving task itself builds on CARLA-based RL and E2E-AD work, with the cognitive reward signal integrated into a TD3 policy network augmented with self-attention.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first study to introduce EEG-based reward modeling for autonomous driving, and the proposed dataset (EEG + gaze + active control from a VR simulator) is the first of its kind, providing a foundation for future neuro-cognitive driving research. The key practical advantage—eliminating real-time EEG at inference—makes the approach far more scalable than prior ErrP-based methods. However, significant open questions remain: the model is scenario-specific (trained on emergency braking, tested on left turns), the participant pool is small (20), and generalizability to diverse driving conditions, larger populations, and real-world deployment is untested. Whether predicted ERP signals can serve as a general-purpose cognitive reward across arbitrary driving scenarios, or whether they remain a narrow signal tied to specific hazard types, is the central open question.
