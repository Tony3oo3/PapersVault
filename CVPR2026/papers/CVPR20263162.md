## Problem

Existing LVLM post-training quantization methods estimate token sensitivity only at the modality level (vision vs. text), implicitly assuming uniform sensitivity within each modality. However, activation analysis reveals substantial intra-modality token variability across layers and sub-layers, meaning modality-level weighting cannot capture which specific tokens are most vulnerable to quantization noise. This granularity gap limits the accuracy of quantized LVLMs, particularly under aggressive bit-widths like W3A16.

## Input / Output

The method operates on LVLMs (LLaVA-onevision-7B, InternVL2-8B/26B, Qwen2-VL-7B) that consume RGB images and text prompts to produce text responses. During calibration, 128 image-caption pairs from COCO Caption are formatted into each model's conversational prompt style; the quantized model then processes the same multimodal inputs (visual patch tokens, text tokens, and special tokens) to generate text outputs. The quantization itself is applied per-token for activations and per-channel for weights, with group size 128 for asymmetric weight quantization.

## Method

The core contribution is Quantization-aware Integrated Gradients (QIG), which shifts the attribution objective from explaining the model's absolute prediction to explaining the output gap between the full-precision model f(x, w) and the quantized model f(x, w^q). Specifically, QIG integrates the gradient of f(x_α, w) − f(x_α, w^q) along the straight-line path from the quantized reference input x^q to the actual input x, yielding a per-token score that quantifies how much restoring that token reduces the quantization-induced error. Raw QIG values are heavy-tailed, so IQR-based clipping suppresses extreme outliers before normalizing into importance coefficients λ_i that sum to one. These coefficients reweight the channel-wise equalization (CWE) objective, biasing the scale search toward tokens empirically most sensitive to quantization while keeping the overall WA quantization framework unchanged. The method is training-free, requires no additional calibration data, and adds only ~2 minutes of GPU time over the MBQ baseline.

## Results

Under W3A16 weight-only quantization, QIG achieves 72.04% average accuracy on LLaVA-onevision-7B, improving over MBQ (70.44%) by 1.60% and reducing the gap to FP16 (73.37%) to just 1.33%. Under W4A8 weight-activation quantization, QIG reaches 70.23% on LLaVA-onevision-7B and 72.04% on InternVL2-8B, consistently outperforming all baselines. Across all six model-bitwidth configurations in Table 2, QIG achieves the highest average accuracy, with gains of approximately 0.5% over the strongest modality-level baseline (MBQ) and 2–4% over generic LLM PTQ methods (GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant).

## Baselines & Comparisons

QIG consistently outperforms all four baselines across every model and bitwidth configuration. Against MBQ (the strongest modality-level baseline), QIG gains approximately 0.5% on average accuracy, with the largest margin on LLaVA-onevision-7B W3A16 (72.04 vs. 70.44, +1.60%) and InternVL2-8B W4A8 (72.04 vs. 71.38, +0.66%). Against generic LLM PTQ methods, the gap is much larger: GPTQ trails QIG by 2.0–4.1% under W3A16, AWQ by 1.7–2.8%, and SmoothQuant by 3.3–4.3% under W4A8. Notably, GPTQ and SmoothQuant—designed for pure LLMs—actually underperform naive RTN on two of three models, confirming that cross-modal statistical characteristics are critical and that directly porting LLM quantization to LVLMs is insufficient. On the most challenging benchmarks (VizWiz, MMMU), QIG's advantage over MBQ widens to roughly 1%, suggesting token-level weighting better preserves visually sensitive and reasoning-critical tokens.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the post-training quantization (PTQ) line for LVLMs initiated by MBQ, which reweights reconstruction errors at the modality level, and by SmoothQuant/GPTQ, which handle channel-wise equalization and second-order error minimization for LLMs. The key conceptual departure is repurposing axiomatic attribution (Integrated Gradients) from mechanistic interpretability to measure token-level quantization sensitivity, shifting the attribution objective from the model's absolute prediction to the prediction gap between full-precision and quantized outputs. Unlike prior LVLM quantization methods that assume uniform sensitivity within a modality, QIG assigns per-token importance weights derived from the quantization error itself, bridging the granularity gap between modality-level balancing and the computationally prohibitive leave-one-out perturbation approach.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in achieving consistent accuracy gains (up to 1.6% over RTN under W3A16) with only ~2 minutes of additional calibration time, making token-level sensitivity weighting deployable at scale. The finding that standard gradient and attention proxies are misaligned with quantization error—while a perturbation-based signal (efficiently approximated by QIG) is well-aligned—offers a useful diagnostic for why existing LVLM quantization methods plateau. Open questions include whether the token-level sensitivity landscape is stable across different calibration sets and prompt formats, and whether the same attribution principle transfers to other compression paradigms such as pruning or distillation for multimodal models.
