## Problem

Existing visual rearrangement methods treat the goal and initial room states as two isolated distributions and directly infer rearrangement targets from their difference, making them sensitive to perception noise and unable to reason about the evolutionary process between states. This paper redefines the task through the lens of nonequilibrium thermodynamics, showing that the shuffle/unshuffle process conforms to a diffusion equation and can be modeled as a forward/reverse diffusion bridge between two structured states rather than between data and pure noise.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262314/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes differential point clouds extracted from 480×480 egocentric RGB-D observations during the walkthrough (goal) and unshuffle (initial) stages, where only moved and protruding parts are retained. These point clouds are fitted into Gaussian Mixture Models whose components correspond to individual objects (mean = center coordinate, covariance = positional range), with the number of components determined by clustering. The output is the predicted goal-state GMM parameters, from which per-object displacement vectors (μ_i → μ̃_i) are derived as the rearrangement targets.

## Method

The core mechanism is a Brownian Bridge Diffusion Model conditioned on both the goal state x₀ and initial state x_T, where the forward process interpolates between the two structured states with controlled variance (δ_t = 2s(m_t − m_t²)) rather than collapsing to Gaussian noise. A 6-layer encoder-only Transformer with 8 attention heads serves as the denoising network, taking GMM parameters as tokens (encoded via a 2-layer MLP to 512-d features) and predicting the noise ε_θ(x_t, t) at each step, which corresponds to the directional trend of object movement. Training minimizes the ELBO-derived loss ‖x_t − x₀ − ε_θ(x_t, t)‖², while inference applies 200 DDIM reverse steps to iteratively denoise from x_T back to the predicted goal state x₀*. The GMM representation is chosen because the product of a GMM and a Gaussian remains a GMM, ensuring all intermediate diffusion states retain the same parametric form and align with the IOU-threshold-based task objective.

## Results

On the RoomR test set, Diffusion Rearrangement achieves 17.8% Success rate, 38.8% Fixed Strict, 0.641 Misplaced, and 0.643 Energy Remaining, outperforming all three baselines across every metric. On the more challenging ProcTHOR dataset (multi-room, procedurally generated scenes), the method reaches 8.4% Success and 24.7% Fixed Strict, again leading all competitors. The improvement over the strongest baseline CAVR is +3.6% in Success and +5.7% in Fixed Strict on RoomR, and +3.5% in Success and +7.7% in Fixed Strict on ProcTHOR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three modular baselines: MaSS (search-based 3D mapping), TIDEE (scene-graph-based spatial reasoning), and CAVR (point-cloud matching). Diffusion Rearrangement wins on all four metrics against every baseline on both RoomR and ProcTHOR. Against the strongest baseline CAVR, the gains are +3.6% Success and +5.7% Fixed Strict on RoomR, and +3.5% Success and +7.7% Fixed Strict on ProcTHOR, with Misplaced and Energy Remaining also improved by roughly 0.06–0.04. The gap over TIDEE and MaSS is substantially larger (e.g., 17.8% vs. 11.7% and 4.7% Success on RoomR), indicating that distribution-based representation and iterative denoising provide a clear advantage over both scene-graph and instance-matching strategies. The authors attribute the gains to modeling the evolutionary process between goal and initial states rather than performing a single-shot differential comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Brownian Bridge Diffusion Model (BBDM) framework, which defines a stochastic process between two structured endpoints rather than between data and pure Gaussian noise, and repurposes it for the visual rearrangement task. It departs from the dominant modular rearrangement approaches (TIDEE, MaSS, CAVR) that treat goal and initial states as two isolated distributions to be compared via scene graphs, instance matching, or point-cloud alignment; instead, the paper models the transition between them as a learnable diffusion bridge, with a transformer denoising network operating in the latent space of Gaussian Mixture Models fitted from differential point clouds. The GMM-based scene representation is itself a departure from prior work's use of scene graphs or raw coordinates, motivated by the IOU-threshold success criterion of the task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The diffusion-bridge reframing offers a principled, step-by-step denoising procedure that avoids the large inference span of direct comparison methods, and the GMM representation naturally constrains predictions to the acceptable-state region defined by the task's IOU threshold. However, the method remains tightly coupled to point-cloud quality (Gaussian noise on depth causes measurable degradation), is validated only in AI2-THOR and ProcTHOR simulations, and the absolute success rates (17.8% on RoomR) indicate substantial room for improvement. Open questions include whether the diffusion perspective generalizes to other embodied manipulation tasks, how the approach scales to rooms with many more objects, and whether real-world sensor noise and partial observability will erode the gains seen in simulation.
