## Problem

Existing test-time adaptation methods for OOD detection with vision-language models rely on a finite, fixed set of external OOD labels to represent the open-ended OOD semantic space, making them unable to adapt to OOD samples that fall outside these predefined semantic scopes. This paper asks whether directly learning OOD textual semantics from the unlabeled test stream—rather than aligning specific labels with OOD distributions—can yield better and more generalizable adaptation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263490/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an unlabeled stream of RGB images (no external OOD labels or ID training data required), processed by a frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 image encoder. It produces a calibrated OOD detection score that fuses a base detector score (MCM) with a calibration term computed from the OOD Textual Knowledge Bank, yielding a binary ID/OOD decision. The learnable OOD prompts are initialized with the template 'a photo of a {class label}' for each of the N ID classes, and only the prefix portion is updated during test-time adaptation.

## Method

TTL introduces N learnable OOD prompts (one per ID class) that are optimized during test-time using pseudo-labels from a base OOD detector. An OOD-focused minority-balanced loss (L_OMB) amplifies the cosine similarity between pseudo-OOD image features and OOD text features, while an OOD Knowledge Purification loss (L_OKP) splits pseudo-OOD samples into high-confidence and low-confidence (ID boundary) subsets to suppress noise by decreasing similarity between OOD prompts and ID boundary samples. A fixed-capacity OOD Textual Knowledge Bank (K=2048) accumulates the most discriminative OOD text features across batches, selected by their minimum distance to all ID text features, and is used at inference to calibrate the final detection score via a weighted fusion with the base detector. Only the prefix of OOD prompts is learnable; the ID prompts, class-label tokens, and both CLIP encoders remain frozen throughout.

## Results

On the ImageNet-1k benchmark, TTL achieves an average FPR95 of 12.46% and AUROC of 97.29%, outperforming the best test-time adaptation baseline AdaNeg (19.22% FPR95, 96.17% AUROC) by 6.76% in FPR95 and 1.12% in AUROC. On the CIFAR-100 benchmark, TTL reaches an average FPR95 of 2.36% and AUROC of 99.26%, surpassing the strongest TTA method AdaND (20.95% FPR95, 92.50% AUROC) by a large margin. The paper reports average gains of 12.67% FPR95 and 3.94% AUROC over state-of-the-art methods across both benchmarks, and notably requires no external OOD labels or labeled ID training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TTL outperforms all 17 baselines on both benchmarks without requiring any external OOD labels or labeled ID training data. Among test-time adaptation methods, TTL surpasses AdaNeg (the closest competitor, which uses external OOD text labels) by 6.76% FPR95 and 1.12% AUROC on ImageNet-1k, and by 18.59% FPR95 and 6.76% AUROC on CIFAR-100. Against the best training-based method MoFE (no external resources), TTL improves FPR95 by 7.56% and AUROC by 2.40% on ImageNet-1k. Among post-hoc methods, TTL beats the strongest baseline CSP (which uses external OOD labels) by 5.05% FPR95 and 1.53% AUROC. On the more challenging CIFAR-100 benchmark, TTL's advantage is even more pronounced: it achieves 2.36% average FPR95 versus 20.95% for AdaND and 36.11% for FA, with particularly large gains on Places365 (6.65% vs. 65.71% FPR95 for AdaND). The only scenario where TTL does not dominate is when combined with a visual knowledge bank (TTL-V variant), which further improves FPR95 to 9.19% at the cost of doubled storage (8 MB vs. 4 MB).

## Relation to Prior Work

TTL builds on two key threads: MCM (Ming et al., NeurIPS 2022) as the base CLIP-based OOD scoring function, and CoOp-style prompt learning (Zhou et al., IJCV 2022) repurposed from training-time to test-time adaptation. It departs most directly from AdaNeg (Zhang et al., NeurIPS 2024), which aligns a fixed set of external OOD text labels to the test distribution on the visual side; TTL inverts this by making the text side learnable and adapting it to the visual distribution, eliminating the need for predefined OOD labels. The OOD Textual Knowledge Bank draws conceptual inspiration from OODD's visual feature dictionary (Yang et al., CVPR 2025) but operates in the textual embedding space with a discriminative scoring criterion for retention.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine practical limitation: real-world OOD semantics are open-ended, yet prior test-time methods (AdaNeg, OODD) rely on finite label sets or visual-only memory, capping their adaptability. The purification strategy and knowledge bank are practical contributions that make pseudo-label-driven test-time adaptation more robust to the noise that plagues such settings. Limitations include dependence on pseudo-label quality (inherited from the base detector), higher per-image inference cost (~11.4 ms vs ~8.2 ms for non-adaptive methods), and evaluation restricted to CLIP ViT-B/16 on two benchmarks. Open questions include whether the text-side adaptation generalizes to larger or non-CLIP VLMs, and whether the approach extends to video or multi-modal OOD detection.
