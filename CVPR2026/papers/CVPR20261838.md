## Problem

Translating open-ended, maneuver-level passenger instructions (e.g., 'I feel unsafe') into safe, executable driving behaviors in L4–L5 autonomous vehicles remains unsolved: existing HMIs target driver intervention rather than non-driving passengers, current LLM-based driving methods handle only standardized navigation commands, and no high-fidelity closed-loop benchmark exists for evaluating instruction realization in realistic urban traffic.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261838/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a single natural-language instruction and a textual scene description (providing lane topology, traffic density, and environmental constraints) at the LLM stage, while the MPC planners operate directly on raw 3D detections and HD maps. The LLM is invoked exactly once to emit a human-readable scheduling script that encodes an ordered sequence of five atomic behaviors (lane keeping, left/right lane change, accelerate, brake) together with asynchronous trigger conditions; the script then drives a fast schedule-plan-control loop that outputs planned trajectories and continuous LQR control signals at high frequency without further LLM queries.

## Method

The proposed scheduling-centric framework decouples semantic reasoning from vehicle control across timescales: a large LLM (e.g., DeepSeek-V3) interprets the open-ended instruction against a textual scene description and, in a single call, generates an executable script that schedules multiple MPC-based motion planners using coroutine mechanisms and event-triggered asynchronous switches (e.g., 'when gap > 20 m, switch from deceleration to right lane change'). This 'Mode III' hybrid scheduling combines the low query overhead of static configuration with the contextual responsiveness of continuous LLM decision-making, avoiding the incoherence and latency of per-step LLM queries. At the low level, behavior-specific MPC planners perform receding-horizon trajectory optimization over an explicit vehicle model, and an LQR tracker converts trajectories into continuous steering, throttle, and brake signals. The LLM never emits numerical control values; all executed actions originate from safety-constrained atomic planners, creating a transparent, auditable chain from language to control and confining hallucination risk to the scheduling stage only. The authors also introduce POINT, a closed-loop benchmark of 1,050 instruction–scenario pairs built on the nuPlan hybrid simulator, with metrics spanning intent recognition, instruction realization, collision avoidance, TTC, drivable-area compliance, speed-limit adherence, and direction consistency.

## Results

On the POINT benchmark (1,050 instruction-scenario pairs in nuPlan closed-loop simulation), the proposed scheduling-centric framework achieves an Instruction Realization score of 0.84 with a single LLM query, outperforming the best instruction-realization baseline DiLu++ (0.51) by 65% and Diffusion-ES (0.28) by 200%. On safety metrics, the framework attains 0.99 Collision Avoidance and 0.88 TTC, matching or exceeding specialized AD methods (PDM-Closed: 0.97/0.86; DiLu+: 1.00/0.84) while executing risky instruction-driven maneuvers. Expert Trajectory Progress is lower (0.82 vs. 0.91–1.00 for specialized methods), reflecting expected deviations from global paths when following passenger instructions. The framework also demonstrates considerable tolerance to LLM inference latency, with safety metrics remaining stable under added delays.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the two instruction-realization baselines, the proposed framework dominates: it improves Instruction Realization from 0.28 (Diffusion-ES) and 0.51 (DiLu++) to 0.84, while also surpassing both on Collision (0.99 vs. 0.82/0.92), TTC (0.88 vs. 0.80/0.73), and Drivable Area (0.97 vs. 0.80/0.96). DiLu++ edges out the proposed method only on Expert Progress (0.87 vs. 0.82), likely because its Mode-II continuous LLM queries allow closer adherence to the global path at the cost of incoherent decisions (e.g., redundant lane changes). Against specialized AD methods that do not follow instructions, the framework matches or exceeds safety: Collision of 0.99 surpasses PDM-Closed (0.97) and LogReplay (0.86), and TTC of 0.88 exceeds PDM-Closed (0.86) and DiLu+ (0.84), though DiLu+ achieves a perfect 1.00 Collision score. The trade-off is Expert Progress (0.82 vs. 0.91–1.00), which is expected since instruction-driven maneuvers (lane changes, overtaking, pulling over) necessarily deviate from the logged expert trajectory. Overall, the framework is the only method that simultaneously achieves high instruction realization and safety comparable to specialized planners, validating the scheduling-centric decoupling of LLM semantic reasoning from MPC-based control.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes control-theoretic design principles—hierarchical decoupling, timescale separation, and event-triggered scheduling—from classical control into the LLM-in-the-loop driving setting, creating a scheduling-centric architecture where an LLM produces a one-shot script that coordinates predefined MPC-based motion planners. It departs from end-to-end VLA methods (LMDrive, AutoVLA) by confining LLM involvement to high-level discrete scheduling rather than continuous control, and from prior LLM-based AD methods (DiLu, LanguageMPC) by introducing a Mode-III hybrid that achieves Mode-I's low query overhead with Mode-II's contextual adaptability via asynchronous triggers. The POINT benchmark extends nuPlan with 1,050 open-ended instruction-scenario pairs, filling a gap the authors identify in the absence of closed-loop, high-fidelity evaluation for instruction-realization tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The POINT benchmark is the first high-fidelity, closed-loop testbed for open-ended, maneuver-level instruction realization in urban traffic, providing the field a missing evaluation standard. The scheduling-centric architecture offers a practical design pattern for LLM-in-the-loop AD systems that preserves safety by keeping all vehicle control within verified atomic planners, directly addressing ISO 26262 traceability concerns. Key open questions remain: the framework is limited to five predefined atomic behaviors and textual scene descriptions (no visual integration), nuPlan lacks ego-view rendering for VLA evaluation, and the expressiveness of asynchronous triggers bounds scheduling generality—scaling to richer instruction spaces will require adaptive re-invocation or learned trigger policies.
