## Problem

Existing audio-visual benchmarks for MLLMs focus predominantly on passive perception tasks with short, single-source audio clips, lacking a structured cognitive progression from basic recognition to complex reasoning and goal-directed interaction. In particular, no prior benchmark systematically evaluates multi-turn, memory-dependent audio-visual interaction or distinguishes the role of audio as an explicit instruction versus supplementary context, leaving a critical gap in assessing whether models can truly integrate audio into sequential decision-making.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263932/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample provides a tri-modal input of an image, an audio segment (either a spoken instruction generated via Azure TTS or human reading, or a contextual audio clip from natural scenes, music, or long-form discourse), and optionally a text question. Perception and reasoning tasks yield a single multiple-choice or open-ended answer, while interaction tasks require the model to produce a sequence of free-form actions across multiple turns, with each turn's response validated against a natural-language success criterion on a task graph. The benchmark comprises 2,451 curated samples spanning indoor/outdoor, static/dynamic, and multi-source acoustic conditions.

## Method

HAVE-Bench organizes evaluation along two orthogonal axes: a three-level cognitive hierarchy (Perception → Reasoning → Interaction) and two audio roles (Audio-as-Instruction vs. Audio-as-Context), yielding a structured taxonomy of subtasks. The interaction level is the key novelty: tasks are modeled as multi-turn task graphs G=(V,E) where each node v_i carries an observation o_i=(I_i, A_i) and each edge e_ij has a natural-language success criterion c_ij; a fixed LLM judge validates whether the model's free-form response satisfies c_ij to accept the state transition, and a task instance succeeds only if the judged transitions form a valid path to a goal state. Three interaction scenarios—audio-guided navigation, rule-based puzzle solving, and music reproduction—are hand-crafted to stress cross-turn memory and planning while keeping per-step perceptual demands comparable to lower levels. Data construction converts existing image-text VQA datasets into speech via TTS and human reading for AaI tasks, uses MLLM-assisted filtering and fully manual annotation for AaC tasks, and employs GPT-4o for quality-based downsampling and speech-friendly rewriting. Evaluation uses a unified LLM-as-judge protocol (GPT-4o) for open-ended and interaction tasks, with all scores averaged over three independent runs under the VLMEvalKit framework.

## Results

On HAVE-Bench, Gemini2.5-Flash achieves the highest overall scores at 68.9 (Perception), 60.8 (Reasoning), and 30.4 (Interaction), while Qwen2.5-Omni is the best open-source model at 68.4, 52.6, and 18.0 respectively. The open-closed performance gap widens substantially up the cognitive hierarchy: only +0.5 points on Perception, growing to +8.2 on Reasoning and +12.4 on Interaction. All models exhibit consistent degradation from Perception to Reasoning to Interaction, and no model exceeds 40% success rate on any Interaction subtask, with the strongest (Gemini2.5-Flash) reaching only 37.7% on Music Reproduction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the seven evaluated models, Gemini2.5-Flash dominates at the Reasoning and Interaction levels, leading on every subtask except Math Reasoning (where Qwen2.5-Omni edges ahead by 1.0 point) and Instructed Recognition (where Qwen2.5-Omni leads by 4.6 points). At the Perception level, the gap between the best closed-source (Gemini2.5-Flash, 68.9) and best open-source (Qwen2.5-Omni, 68.4) model is negligible, but this widens to 8.2 points on Reasoning and 12.4 points on Interaction. Qwen2.5-Omni consistently outperforms other open-source models (Ming-Lite-Omni, MiniCPM-o, Ola, VITA-1.5, Megrez-3B-Omni) across all three levels, with the largest margins on Interaction (18.0 vs. 11.6 for Ming-Lite-Omni). MiniCPM-o shows a notable weakness on Text-Rich QA (62.1 vs. 86–89.5 for other models), while Megrez-3B-Omni trails significantly on all subtasks, scoring only 3.8 on Interaction. On Interaction tasks specifically, even the strongest model (Gemini2.5-Flash) achieves only 30.4% average success, and all models fail to follow the required multi-step workflows (e.g., Menu→Learn→Solve for puzzles), indicating that cross-turn memory and planning—not per-step perception—remain the primary bottleneck.

## Relation to Prior Work

HAVE-Bench carves new ground in audio-visual MLLM evaluation by introducing a three-level cognitive hierarchy (Perception → Reasoning → Interaction) and a two-axis audio role distinction (Audio-as-Instruction vs. Audio-as-Context), neither of which prior benchmarks like AVQA, OmniBench, or AV-Odyssey provide. It departs from existing audio-visual datasets that focus on passive, single-turn perception or narrow scenario-specific reasoning by pioneering multi-turn, memory-dependent interaction tasks (navigation, puzzle solving, music reproduction) and by incorporating longer, multi-source, and sequential audio. The data construction pipeline repurposes established image-text VQA datasets (MME, ChartQA, MathVista, etc.) by converting questions to speech via TTS and human reading, while the interaction tasks are entirely novel constructions with hand-crafted task graphs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that the open-closed performance gap widens dramatically with cognitive level (from ~+0.5 on Perception to +12.4 on Interaction), and that all models—regardless of scale or access—fail on multi-turn audio-visual interaction (best score: 30.4%), with the bottleneck identified as cross-turn memory rather than per-step multimodal competence. The consistent text-image over speech-image performance gap across all models highlights an unresolved transfer problem: models cannot fully leverage their strong VQA reasoning when the question arrives as speech. Open questions include how to train models to maintain coherent state across multi-turn audio-visual dialogues, how to close the speech-image reasoning gap, and whether richer long-form, multi-source audio training data can narrow the open-closed divide.
