## Problem

Existing multimodal judge benchmarks assign a single overall preference label per response pair, leaving unexplored whether LMM-based judges can follow diverse, fine-grained evaluation criteria and recognize when different criteria yield conflicting judgments for the same pair. This coarse formulation masks critical trade-offs (e.g., a concise but correct answer vs. a visually grounded yet slightly hallucinated one) and provides no signal on criterion-level reliability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263225/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance presents the judge with an RGB image, a textual query, two candidate model responses, and a single named criterion (e.g., Completeness, Grounding, No Hallucination, Logic, Efficiency). The judge must output a binary preference (response a or b) plus a brief textual justification for that criterion alone. Critically, the same response pair is evaluated independently under each of 5 criteria, and the benchmark specifically retains pairs where ground-truth preferences conflict across criteria (68.9% of open-ended and 86.5% of reasoning prompts exhibit such conflicts).

## Method

Multi-Crit is built through a rigorous curation pipeline: prompts are drawn from 8 sources spanning open-ended generation (ImageInWords, DOCCI, WildVision) and verifiable reasoning (MathVerse, MM-K12, EMMA-mini, VisualPuzzles); responses are generated by 11 LMMs and filtered via length normalization, correctness-matching for reasoning, and ensemble disagreement among three strong judges to retain only challenging pairs. Nine PhD annotators label each criterion independently with Cohen's κ of 0.718 (open-ended) and 0.805 (reasoning), and preferences are aggregated with strict majority-plus-tie rules. Three complementary metrics assess pluralistic adherence: Pluralistic Accuracy (PAcc) requires correctness on all criteria simultaneously; Trade-off Sensitivity (TOS) checks whether the judge detects at least one ground-truth conflict within an instance; and Conflict Matching Rate (CMR) demands exact resolution of each specific conflicting criterion pair, providing a strictly finer-grained signal than TOS.

## Results

On the open-ended split, o4-mini achieves the highest Pluralistic Accuracy (PAcc) of 32.78%, followed by Claude-3.7-Sonnet (31.77%) and GPT-5 (29.77%); on the verifiable reasoning split, o4-mini leads with 53.17% PAcc, GPT-5 reaches 45.24%, and o3 achieves 44.44%. The best open-source model, InternVL3.5-38B, attains 30.43% PAcc (open-ended) and 37.30% (reasoning), trailing the top proprietary models by 2.4 and 15.9 points respectively. Finetuned judge models (e.g., R1-Reward at 17.73% open-ended PAcc, LLaVA-Critic-R1 at 18.39%) consistently underperform their base models on pluralistic criteria, with R1-Reward being the sole exception that improves over Qwen2.5-VL-7B (9.41→17.73) on open-ended PAcc.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary models, o4-mini leads on PAcc in both splits (32.78% open-ended, 53.17% reasoning) and on average criterion-level accuracy (69.67%, 80.85%), while GPT-4o achieves the highest Trade-off Sensitivity in reasoning (84.40%) and o3 excels on Hallucination (84.21%) and Exploration (84.62%) criteria. No single model dominates all criteria: GPT-4o leads in Expressiveness (76.17%) but lags in Hallucination (65.75%), whereas o3 is strong on Hallucination (72.93%) but weak on Expressiveness (63.21%). Open-source models trail proprietary ones by approximately 9.4 points in CMR on open-ended (43.11→33.73) and 18.1 points on reasoning (65.84→47.69), far exceeding the 4–11 point gaps in criterion-level accuracy, indicating a specific weakness in resolving criterion-level conflicts. Finetuned judges (LLaVA-Critic, R1-Reward, LLaVA-Critic-R1, UnifiedReward) fail to consistently outperform their base models on pluralistic metrics; R1-Reward is the only one that improves PAcc over Qwen2.5-VL-7B (9.41→17.73 open-ended, 16.67→19.05 reasoning), yet its gains in TOS and CMR are inconsistent. All Qwen-based finetuned judges show uniform improvements in visual grounding accuracy (e.g., R1-Reward: 51.7→60.7 open-ended, 46.9→58.0 reasoning), confirming that critic fine-tuning primarily enhances grounding rather than pluralistic criterion-following.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LMM-as-a-Judge paradigm and existing multimodal judge benchmarks (MLLM-as-a-Judge, VL-RewardBench, MM-RLHF) that evaluate judges via a single overall preference label per response pair. It also builds on recent text-only criteria-following work (Pitis et al., Wang et al.) that embeds criterion-level differences into responses or summarizes from human justifications, explicitly extending that line to the multimodal setting. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of *pluralistic* criteria-following: each response pair is annotated with multiple criterion-level preferences that can conflict, and three new metrics (PAcc, TOS, CMR) are proposed to measure adherence, trade-off sensitivity, and conflict resolution—dimensions absent from all prior multimodal judge benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Multi-Crit is the first benchmark to systematically expose how poorly current multimodal judges handle conflicting evaluation criteria, revealing that even the strongest proprietary model (o4-mini) achieves only 32.78% pluralistic accuracy on open-ended tasks. The finding that critic fine-tuning—designed specifically to improve judgment—only enhances visual grounding and fails to generalize to pluralistic criteria-following challenges the assumption that holistic preference training transfers to fine-grained, multi-dimensional evaluation. Open questions include how to design training signals that teach judges to recognize and resolve criterion-level conflicts, whether scaling open-source models with high-quality multi-criterion annotations can close the gap to proprietary models, and whether the human-aligned upper bound observed in proprietary models represents a fundamental ceiling or a data-scaling artifact.
