## Problem

CLIP-based open-vocabulary predictors are typically evaluated under closed-set assumptions, yet real-world deployment requires the model to remain stable as the vocabulary expands with unseen distractor classes and to stay extensible to novel categories. Existing fine-tuning methods (prompt-tuning, adapter-based tuning) are designed for stationary closed-set conditions and degrade significantly as the target vocabulary scales, with no principled guidance on how to preserve openness. The paper formalizes a vocabulary scaling law that lower-bounds stability and extensibility by performance on the full class-name universe, revealing why current approaches fail.

## Input / Output

The model takes an RGB image and a candidate vocabulary of class names (e.g., 'a photo of a [class]') and outputs a predicted class label via softmax over cosine similarities between the image embedding and prompt embeddings. During fine-tuning, the input includes 16-shot training images per target class in V^(T) plus a large pool of open-set class names from the universe U (no training images for U/V^(T)). Evaluation progressively expands the vocabulary by adding batches of 20–25 new distractor classes at each step, measuring accuracy on both known (stability) and newly introduced (extensibility) classes across 10 random scaling sequences.

## Method

SVFT (Submodular-Vocabulary Fine-tuning) is a bi-level optimization framework that fine-tunes only class-name embeddings (not context embeddings) while enforcing an orthogonality constraint between embeddings of target classes V^(T) and open-set classes in U/V^(T). Since the full universe U is too large to optimize over directly, the outer level selects a small informative subset S of at most K classes from U/V^(T) by casting the selection as a monotone submodular maximization under a cardinality constraint, solved via linear greedy search with a (1−1/e) approximation guarantee. The inner level then minimizes a cross-entropy loss over V^(T)∪S plus a λ-weighted orthogonality penalty that discourages the tuned class-name embeddings from aligning with open-set class embeddings. This alternating loop—greedy submodular selection followed by class-name embedding update—iterates until convergence, yielding a model that simultaneously improves stability (resistance to distractor classes) and extensibility (zero-shot generalization to novel classes).

## Results

On the vocabulary scaling law verification (Table 1), the proposed Name-based PT+Orth(U) strategy achieves the best closed-set accuracy and smallest performance gaps across all three benchmarks: 87.8% Acc-C on CIFAR-100 (extensibility gap −4.1, stability gap −2.6), 90.2% on ImageNet Entity13 (−1.9, −2.9), and 86.2% on ImageNet Living17 (−0.1, −0.9). On the more challenging Birds and Rare Species benchmarks, SVFT maintains accuracy within a 2-point drop as negative vocabularies expand from 20 to 400 classes, while the best baseline (CLIP-Adapter) degrades by ~15 points. On adversarial classes selected by SVFT's submodular objective, SVFT achieves 91.06% (Birds) and 87.50% (Rare Species), far exceeding MAPLE (54.46%, 7.14%) and CLIP-Adapter (73.63%, 80.36%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SVFT (equivalent to Name-based PT+Orth(U) in Table 1) outperforms all baselines on both stability and extensibility across all three benchmarks. Compared to the strongest neural-scaling baseline PE (ViT-B/16), SVFT gains +8.2% Acc-C and reduces the extensibility gap from −7.0 to −4.1 on CIFAR-100, and from −2.1 to −1.9 on ImageNet Entity13. Against context-based prompt tuning (CoOp [35]), tuning class-name embeddings on the full universe U reduces the CIFAR-100 extensibility gap from −10.9 (Context-based PT(U)) to −4.1, confirming Takeaway 2. On the Birds and Rare Species fine-tuning benchmarks, SVFT surpasses CoOp [35], CoCoOp [34], CLIP-Adapter [12], and MAPLE [15] in both stability (within 2-point drop vs. ~15-point drop for CLIP-Adapter) and extensibility. In the full-class-name comparison where baselines are given access to U/V^(T) class names, SVFT still dominates on adversarial classes: 91.06% (Birds) and 87.50% (Rare Species) versus 54.46%/7.14% for MAPLE and 73.63%/80.36% for CLIP-Adapter, revealing the fragility of adapter and multi-modal prompt methods under targeted vocabulary perturbation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on CLIP's dual-encoder architecture (Radford et al., 2021) and the stability/extensibility evaluation framework introduced by Ren et al. (2023), which exposed how CLIP degrades as the candidate vocabulary grows. It departs from the dominant prompt-tuning lineage (CoOp, CoCoOp, CLIP-Adapter, MAPLE) by arguing these methods are fundamentally misaligned with open-world evaluation because they optimize only over the target training vocabulary. The class-name embedding fine-tuning mechanism is adopted from Chen et al. (2025), but the key conceptual departure is the formal vocabulary scaling law and the submodular maximization formulation for selecting which open-class names to include during fine-tuning — a cross-pollination from combinatorial optimization that has no direct precedent in the CLIP fine-tuning literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The vocabulary scaling law provides the first theoretical explanation for why CLIP-based predictors lose accuracy as the vocabulary expands, and it yields actionable design principles (tune class-name embeddings, enforce orthogonality, account for the full universe) that are validated empirically. The submodular connection offers a principled, near-optimal way to avoid the intractable cost of fine-tuning over all open classes, making the approach scalable. Key limitations remain: the framework assumes a finite, pre-defined vocabulary universe; the greedy selection becomes a bottleneck for very large universes; and the orthogonality constraint is a tractable surrogate rather than a sufficient condition for extensibility. Open questions include extending the scaling law to detection and segmentation, handling truly unbounded vocabularies via LLM-based dynamic construction, and integrating SVFT into continual learning loops.
