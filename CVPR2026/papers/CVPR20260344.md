## Problem

Existing deepfake detectors trained on limited domains achieve high in-domain AUC but fail at definitive frame-by-frame real/fake classification when test inputs come from unspecified, mixed domains, because inter-domain discrepancies dominate the feature space over the subtle real/fake distinction. The paper formalizes this gap as the Multi-In-Domain Face Forgery Detection (MID-FFD) task and shows that high per-domain AUC does not translate to reliable binary accuracy under domain-unspecified conditions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260344/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single 256×256 (or 224×224 for ViT-based backbones) RGB face crop with no domain label and produces a single real/fake confidence score. Before the detector sees the image, a developer network generates a pixel-level perturbation whose magnitude (the 'dose') is adaptively scaled per image by a dictionary-based reconstruction error, so uncertain or hard-fake inputs receive stronger forgery-trace amplification while easy or out-of-domain inputs receive minimal perturbation.

## Method

DevDet is a two-stage, model-agnostic framework. In Stage 1, a Face Forgery Developer (FFDev) generator G(·) is trained to output a perturbation δ_dev added to the input (x̃ = x + ε·δ_dev), optimized via cross-entropy on hard-fake samples (pushed toward fake) and easy-real samples (preserved as real) plus a total-variation smoothing loss, with the detector frozen. In Stage 2, Dose-Adaptive Fine-Tuning (DAFT) fine-tunes the pretrained detector on FFDev-processed images while a DoseDict—a sparse dictionary learned on hard-fake feature vectors via alternating optimization—dynamically sets the per-image dose ε_a = Norm(1 − e(x)), where e(x) is the reconstruction error against the dictionary. This sequential design (FFDev first, then DAFT) amplifies real/fake differences to dominate over domain discrepancies in the latent space, enabling confident binary judgment on domain-unspecified inputs while preserving the backbone's original out-of-domain generalization.

## Results

On Protocol 1 (FF++, CDF, DFDCP, WDF), DevDet achieves a Summarized AUC (S-AUC) of 0.9332 and a Multi-domain Accuracy (M-ACC) of 0.8764, outperforming the strongest baseline Effort (S-AUC 0.8951, M-ACC 0.7839) by 3.81% and 9.25% respectively. On the broader Protocol 2 (adding DF40 and CDF3 with diffusion-based forgeries), the method reaches M-ACC 0.9053 versus 0.8486 for Effort. The model-agnostic enhancement (Table 3) shows consistent MID-FFD gains of 8–13% in per-dataset ACC across Xception, Effnb4, SPSL, and Effort backbones while preserving or slightly improving cross-dataset generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DevDet outperforms all ten baselines on both S-AUC and M-ACC under Protocol 1. The largest gap is against the strongest baseline Effort (ICML'25): DevDet improves M-ACC from 0.7839 to 0.8764 (+9.25%) and S-AUC from 0.8951 to 0.9332 (+3.81%), indicating that the domain-dominant feature-space issue severely limits even the best generalizable detector on frame-by-frame binary judgment. Against frequency-domain methods (F3Net, SPSL), the M-ACC advantage is 10.5–12.5%, reflecting their inability to resolve cross-domain decision boundaries. CLIP, the strongest ViT-based baseline, trails by 10.6% in M-ACC (0.7707 vs 0.8764). On Protocol 2 with diffusion-based forgeries (DF40, CDF3), DevDet extends its lead to 5.67% M-ACC over Effort (0.9053 vs 0.8486), confirming robustness to advanced generative methods. The model-agnostic ablation (Table 3) further shows that plugging DevDet onto Xception, Effnb4, SPSL, or Effort yields 8–13% MID-FFD ACC gains while cross-dataset generalization is preserved or marginally improved, confirming the method's plug-and-play nature.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant generalizable face forgery detection paradigm (Xception, CLIP, SPSL, Effort, etc.), which trains on limited single or few domains and relies on learned invariance to unseen forgeries, arguing that such an approach is idealistic given the diversity of real-world deepfakes. It also positions itself against incremental face forgery detection (IFFD) methods, contending that their efficiency gains are trivial for the inherently low-cost FFD task while suffering from catastrophic forgetting. The proposed DevDet framework repurposes concepts from photographic development (a learned perturbation that 'exposes' hidden traces) and sparse dictionary learning (DoseDict) as a model-agnostic preprocessing and fine-tuning pipeline atop any pretrained generalizable backbone, rather than proposing a new detection architecture from scratch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and practically important gap: high in-domain AUC does not guarantee reliable frame-by-frame real/fake accuracy when inputs come from unspecified, mixed domains, a scenario that mirrors real-world deployment. The model-agnostic design (FFDev + DAFT) means the approach can be bolted onto any existing pretrained detector without retraining from scratch, and the DoseDict mechanism explicitly preserves out-of-domain generalization while improving multi-in-domain accuracy. Open questions include whether the two-stage pipeline scales to dozens of domains or video-level detection, the computational overhead of running FFDev plus DoseDict at inference, and whether the fixed 0.5 confidence threshold assumption holds under class imbalance in production settings.
