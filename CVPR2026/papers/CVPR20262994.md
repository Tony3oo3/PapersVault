## Problem

Existing video reward models evaluate visual quality, motion dynamics, and text alignment but systematically overlook structural distortions such as deformed limbs, duplicated body parts, and mesh penetration, leading to high scores for videos with severe anatomical or physical defects. Image-based artifact detectors cannot be directly transferred because video distortions manifest as blurry, temporally fragmented regions rather than the sharp, well-defined artifacts seen in generated images, creating a critical domain gap.

## Input / Output

REACT takes a single video frame (sampled adaptively via a two-stage dynamic mechanism at 2 fps) together with the corresponding text prompt as input, and produces a point-wise quality score on a 1–5 scale (two decimal places), a set of attribution labels drawn from an eight-category structural distortion taxonomy (limb deformation, extra limbs, limb incompleteness, torso deformation, facial deformation, non-animal collapse, motion blur, mesh penetration), and a free-form Chain-of-Thought reasoning trace explaining the assessment. The frame-level granularity is a deliberate design choice: distortions are spatially localized within individual frames, and frame-level annotation is far more scalable than video-level labeling.

## Method

REACT is built on Qwen2.5-VL-7B and trained in two stages. The first stage is supervised fine-tuning on 6K synthesized Chain-of-Thought instances (generated by prompting Gemini-2.5-Pro with human-drawn distortion bounding boxes), where a masked-loss strategy is used: the first epoch trains on full reasoning traces, while the second epoch computes loss only on the final attribution labels and scores to inject domain knowledge without overfitting to specific reasoning paths. The second stage applies Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with a composite pairwise reward comprising a format reward, an attribution-accuracy reward (weighted F1-style scoring of predicted vs. ground-truth labels), and a preference reward derived from the Bradley-Terry-with-ties (BTT) probability model that compares predicted point-wise scores of paired frames against human preference annotations. At inference, a dynamic two-stage sampling mechanism first scores frames at half the target fps, then adaptively densifies sampling around low-scoring frames to maximize the probability of capturing distorted content within a fixed frame budget.

## Results

On REACT-Video (human preference alignment), REACT achieves 0.610 accuracy with ties and 0.813 without ties, outperforming the strongest video evaluator UnifiedReward (0.416 / 0.701) by 20–40% relative. On REACT-Frame (distortion recognition), REACT attains an F1-score of 0.845 for distorted frames and 0.671 for normal frames, substantially exceeding the best MLLM baseline Gemini-2.5-Pro (0.650 / 0.335) and the best image evaluator MagicAccessor (0.554 / 0.285). Ablations confirm that both the SFT stage (with masked loss) and the GRPO stage with pairwise BTT-based rewards are necessary for these gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

REACT wins on every comparison across both tasks. In human preference alignment (Table 1), the strongest video evaluator UnifiedReward [51] reaches 0.416 (w/Tie) / 0.701 (w/o Tie), while REACT achieves 0.610 / 0.813—a gap of roughly 0.19 and 0.11 respectively. VideoScore2 [10] and VideoReward [27] trail even further (0.342/0.521 and 0.415/0.551). Among general MLLMs, Qwen2.5-VL-32B [2] is the best at 0.364/0.583, and Gemini-2.5-Pro [8] and Gemini-2.5-Flash [7] score 0.370/0.534 and 0.384/0.553, all well below REACT. Image evaluators Q-Insight [23] (0.384/0.559) and VisualQuality-R1 [55] (0.376/0.610) also fall short, confirming the domain gap between image and video distortions. In distortion recognition (Table 2), REACT's F1 of 0.845 on distorted frames surpasses Gemini-2.5-Pro [8] (0.650), GPT-o3 [35] (0.641), GPT-4o [13] (0.488), and MagicAccessor [44] (0.554); on normal frames REACT (0.671) similarly outperforms all baselines, whose F1-scores range from 0.285 to 0.379.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the video reward model line (VideoScore, VideoReward, VideoScore2, UnifiedReward) by isolating structural distortion as a first-class evaluation axis that prior models conflate into generic visual or motion quality scores. It departs from image-based artifact detectors (MagicMirror, Q-Insight, VisualQuality-R1) by explicitly addressing the domain gap—video distortions are blurry and temporally fragmented rather than sharp and well-defined—and by operating at the frame level rather than the full-video level. The training pipeline repurposes GRPO (from DeepSeek-R1/DeepSeekMath) and the BTT-with-ties preference formulation, combining them with a masked SFT stage to inject domain knowledge without overfitting reasoning trajectories.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Structural distortions (deformed limbs, mesh penetration, duplicated parts) are a persistent failure mode in T2V generation that current reward models systematically miss, meaning RLHF pipelines guided by those rewards will not correct these artifacts. The REACT-Bench benchmark and the eight-category distortion taxonomy provide the community with a concrete, reproducible evaluation target that was previously absent. Key limitations include the frame-level scope (no temporal reasoning, so flash effects and sudden object disappearances remain undetectable) and the reliance on a self-constructed benchmark, which limits external validation. Extending to spatio-temporal distortion detection and integrating REACT into actual T2V training loops are natural next steps.
