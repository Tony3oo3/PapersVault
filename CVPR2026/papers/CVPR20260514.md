## Problem

Video-language models suffer a sharp accuracy drop on long videos because their limited context windows force sparse temporal sampling, creating an imbalance where most computational capacity is spent on spatial detail rather than temporal reasoning. Existing remedies add architectural complexity or require fine-tuning, yet many fail to consistently outperform base models. This paper asks whether a simple, training-free visual prompting strategy can consistently improve long-video understanding across diverse VLMs without modifying their architecture.

## Input / Output

The method takes a video (sampled to T frames) and a multiple-choice text question, and outputs a single answer letter. The key input transformation is that T = αβC sampled frames are each downsampled to H/α × W/β and then stacked αβ-at-a-time into panel images of the original H × W resolution, so the VLM receives exactly C images of its expected input shape. The number of sampled frames T is chosen dynamically: T = C for short videos (no paneling) and T = αβC for long videos where frames would otherwise be spaced more than γ apart, with γ set to the video's frame rate.

## Method

The core mechanism is a two-step visual prompting pipeline: (1) dynamic frame sampling that triggers paneling only when the video is long enough that uniform sampling would space frames more than γ apart, and (2) panel construction where αβ consecutive downsampled frames are concatenated into a single H×W image in left-to-right, top-to-bottom order, effectively extending temporal coverage by a factor of αβ while preserving the VLM's expected input dimensions. With the default α=β=2, four frames are packed into one 2×2 grid, doubling the temporal resolution at the cost of halving per-frame spatial resolution. The approach is entirely training-free and model-agnostic, requiring no architectural changes or additional parameters. Optionally, fine-tuning the projector and LLM on the original training data re-represented as panels yields further gains, confirming that panels are a more effective long-video representation beyond the zero-shot setting.

## Results

The paneling approach consistently improves video QA accuracy across all 8 VLMs and 5 benchmarks. On the longest-video benchmark (TimeScope Long), VideoLLaMA 3 7B improves from 39.1% to 46.7% accuracy (+7.6 points, +19.4%), the largest single gain reported. Average improvements range from +0.3 to +1.0 for small-context models (8–16 frames), +1.2 to +4.1 for medium-context models (32–64 frames), and +0.8 to +2.7 for long-context models (180 frames). Notably, LLaVA-Video 7B with panels (60.7 avg) outperforms all long-context base VLMs without panels, and commercial models GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4.1 also benefit (+2.5 and +1.1 on VideoMME respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method is applied as a drop-in visual prompting layer on top of 8 existing VLMs (Video-LLaVA, VideoChat2-HD, LLaVA-OV 0.5B/7B/72B, Qwen-2.5VL, LLaVA-Video 7B/72B, Qwen-2VL, VideoLLaMA 3) plus 2 commercial models (GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4.1), and improves the average accuracy in every case. The largest absolute gain is on VideoLLaMA 3 7B for TimeScope Long (39.1→46.7, +7.6 pts), while the largest relative gain is on LLaVA-Video 7B (+4.1 avg, driven by a +14.4 jump on TimeScope Short from 64.8→79.2). LLaVA-OV 7B and Qwen-2.5VL 7B (32-frame) both gain +3.4 on average, with the biggest sub-gains on TimeScope Short (+10.8 and +8.0 respectively). A notable cross-context finding: LLaVA-Video 7B with panels (60.7 avg) surpasses all 180-frame long-context base VLMs (54.5–59.7), suggesting that existing long-context models do not fully exploit their larger windows. The only consistent losses are minor (≤0.6 pts) on individual sub-benchmarks, such as VideoLLaMA 3 on MF2 (58.9→58.3) and Qwen-2.5VL 180-frame on TimeScope Long (37.6→35.6), but these are offset by larger gains elsewhere.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of long-video understanding work that modifies model internals—token compression, memory bridges, summarization tokens (VideoLLaMB, Video-XL, SF-LLaVA)—by operating entirely at the input level through visual prompting. It repurposes the composite-image idea previously explored for short-video action recognition and comic-book understanding, but is the first to apply it to long-video VLMs and to show that frame-level paneling outperforms token-level pooling for increasing temporal resolution. The approach also connects to the broader visual-prompting literature (bounding boxes, colored regions, numerical tags) but introduces a structurally different prompt: a spatial rearrangement of temporal information rather than an attentional cue.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Because the method is training-free, parameter-free, and model-agnostic, it can be adopted by any VLM user immediately without retraining, making it a low-cost, high-impact intervention for the long-video understanding community. The consistent gains across 8 architectures, 5 benchmarks, and context windows from 8 to 180 frames suggest a general principle: VLMs underutilize their spatial token budget on long videos, and rebalancing toward temporal coverage is broadly beneficial. Open questions include whether explicit temporal ordering cues (e.g., numerical tags) can close the small gap on ordering tasks, and whether the finding that a 64-frame model plus panels rivals 180-frame models implies that current long-context VLMs are not efficiently leveraging their larger windows.
