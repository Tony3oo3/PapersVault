## Problem

Existing dense crowd simulations rely on 2D particle or disk representations that capture push propagation at a macro level but cannot model how forces are transmitted through the body at the limb level, missing critical mechanisms of balance recovery, stepping, and hand-based stabilization. No prior work has performed full-body, physics-based simulation of dense crowds where agents must maintain balance under external perturbations and unavoidable contact, making it impossible to study force amplification, falls, and crush hazards with biomechanical fidelity.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes a state vector comprising the agent's body-link positions, orientations, and linear/angular velocities over the last 4 frames (t−3 to t), augmented during adaptation with heuristic-computed hand target positions and orientations. External pushes are applied as horizontal forces at the shoulder level with magnitudes 70–200 N and durations 0.7–1.3 s, sampled from experimental observations. The output is a set of target joint postures fed into stable PD servos that compute joint torques, producing full-body motions including omnidirectional stepping and hand-to-shoulder contacts with neighboring agents.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage RL pipeline for physically simulated full-body agents. Stage 1 (pretraining) uses a GAN-like adversarial imitation framework with PPO, where a discriminator scores pose trajectories against a single-subject motion capture dataset of balance recovery motions, while additional reward terms enforce CoM/CoP tracking (with a momentum-based CoP target incorporating linear and angular momentum) and penalize foot sliding, heading drift, and excess kinetic energy. Stage 2 (adaptation) finetunes the frozen pretrained policy via the AdaptNet architecture—adding embedding layers for the expanded state and parallel MLPs for new contact behaviors—while introducing an online hand-contact heuristic that predicts 1-second shoulder trajectories, detects collision risks against neighbor shoulders within 5 m, and selects targets by reachability and collinearity with the agent's linear momentum. Social interaction rewards penalize cumulative contact force and maximum hand placement error during contact, encouraging brief, mechanically efficient shoulder-level pushes rather than sustained or poorly placed contacts.

## Results

The pretrained policy π_pretrain maintains balance under pushes up to ~230 N (vs. only 21 N when the balance reward is removed), with heading deviation of 5.93° and foot sliding of 22 cm over 80 evaluation trials. After adaptation, π_adapt reduces the cumulative transmitted impulse during hand-shoulder contact to 40 Ns (vs. 75 Ns without the hand-placement reward and 74 Ns without the social reward), while keeping final hand height at the neutral 0.81 m. In the 5-person queue experiment reproducing [10], the simulated push velocity and propagation distance match the experimental linear regression across three interpersonal distances (0.4 m, 0.6 m, 0.8 m), confirming that the simulation captures the energy-dissipation trends observed in human subjects.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against explicit competing methods; instead, it validates its simulation against two external references. First, the pretraining stage is validated against a single-subject XSens motion-capture dataset of balance-recovery motions, with CoM trajectories and velocities closely tracking the reference data across push magnitudes of 60–240 N (Figure 3). Second, the multi-agent adaptation is validated against the controlled human experiment in Feldmann & Adrian [10], where a 5-person queue is pushed from behind at three interpersonal distances. The simulated push-velocity-vs-impulse relationship reproduces the experimental linear regression, and propagation distances (which agents are affected) match the observed patterns: at 0.8 m only the first agent reacts, at 0.6 m the first two dissipate energy, and at 0.4 m momentum accumulates to produce a strong push on the last agent. Ablation studies (Tables 1–2) further confirm that each reward component is necessary: removing the balance reward drops the survivable push threshold from ~230 N to 21 N, and removing the social reward increases transmitted impulse by ~85%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines adversarial imitation learning (AMP-style GAN framework) for single-agent balance recovery with AdaptNet-based policy adaptation to extend the learned policy to multi-agent dense crowd settings. The balance reward formulation draws on momentum-based CoP targets from Zordan and Mordatch, while the overall architecture departs from prior dense-crowd work that relied on 2D particle models by introducing full-body physics-based agents. The hand-contact heuristic is a novel engineering contribution that bridges the gap between the single-agent pretraining policy and socially plausible multi-agent interactions, filling a gap the authors identify as unaddressed in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is, to the authors' knowledge, the first full-body physics-based simulation of dense crowds where push propagation emerges from individual balance-recovery behaviors rather than being prescribed by a 2D model. The successful replication of empirical push-propagation trends (matching linear regression from Feldmann & Adrian) validates the approach for safety-critical applications like crowd-crush prediction. Key limitations include reliance on a single-subject motion capture dataset, a hand-engineered heuristic for contact targeting, and restriction to static (non-locomoting) scenarios; extending to locomotion and replacing the heuristic with a learned module given richer multi-person interaction data are the most pressing open questions.
