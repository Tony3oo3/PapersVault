## Problem

Existing text-to-video evaluation metrics such as VideoScore were designed for short clips (seconds to tens of seconds), yet text-to-long-video (T2LV) systems now produce videos spanning minutes to hours. No benchmark exists to meta-evaluate whether these evaluation systems can reliably assess long-video quality, leaving a critical validation gap for the T2LV research community.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262051/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation system receives a text prompt (concatenation of per-clip captions averaging ~57,883 characters) and a pair of long videos (ranging from ~19 min average to ~3 h maximum, sampled at 1 fps with 512-px longer side). Due to context-length limits, VLM-based judges consume only center frames from FFmpeg-detected clip boundaries, while VideoScore is capped at 48 frames. The system outputs a binary preference (which video is higher quality), aggregated across all pairs into a per-aspect accuracy score with 95% confidence intervals.

## Method

SLVMEval constructs controlled high-quality vs. low-quality video pairs by starting from the Vript dense video-captioning dataset and applying aspect-specific synthetic degradations to 5 randomly sampled clips per video across 10 aspects (4 video-quality: aesthetics, technical quality, appearance style, background consistency; 6 video-text consistency: temporal flow, comprehensiveness, object integrity, spatial relationship, dynamics degree, color). Degradation operations range from simple pixel-level changes (contrast reduction, downscaling, style transfer) to semantic manipulations (clip reordering, clip removal, object inpainting via Grounding DINO + Stable Diffusion, horizontal flipping, motion freezing, color editing via QwenImage-Edit). Crowdsourced A/B/C ratings from 5 workers per pair filter the dataset to retain only clearly perceptible degradations, yielding 3,932 final videos. Meta-evaluation uses pairwise comparison accuracy, and eight baseline systems (GPT-5, GPT-5-mini, Qwen3-VL-235B in both video-based and text-based VLM-as-a-judge modes, CLIPScore with Jina CLIP v2, and VideoScore-v1.1) are assessed against human evaluators.

## Results

On the SLVMEval benchmark, human evaluators achieve 84.7%–96.8% accuracy across all 10 aspects, confirming the benchmark's validity. The best automatic system, GPT-5 (video-based), reaches 98.9% on Background Consistency and 90.1% on Aesthetics but drops to 35.3% on Dynamics Degree and 50.3% on Temporal Flow. Across 9 of 10 aspects, all automatic systems lag behind humans by 6.3 to 43.2 percentage points, with the largest gaps on semantic and temporal consistency aspects. Accuracy for nearly all automatic systems decreases as video duration increases, as measured by Spearman rank correlation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-5 (video-based) is the strongest automatic system, exceeding 85% accuracy on all four Video Quality aspects (Aesthetics 90.1%, Technical Quality 85.8%, Appearance Style 88.9%, Background Consistency 98.9%) but still trailing humans by 5.5–13.1 points. On Video-Text Consistency aspects, GPT-5 drops sharply: Dynamics Degree (35.3%, below chance), Temporal Flow (50.3%), and Comprehensiveness (51.3%) are all near or below random. CLIPScore is the only non-VLM system that meaningfully outperforms chance on semantic aspects, reaching 76.0% on Object Integrity and 57.4% on Comprehensiveness, likely due to CLIP's contrastive text-image pretraining; however, it remains at chance level (50.5–51.7%) on temporal aspects like Dynamics Degree and Temporal Flow. Qwen3-VL-235B performs near chance in video-based mode (38.5–55.7%) but improves substantially in text-based mode, gaining 23.3 points on Background Consistency (49.7→73.0) and 12.5 points on Object Integrity (38.5→51.0). VideoScore falls below chance on Technical Quality (33.8%), Color (33.8%), and Comprehensiveness (34.5%), which the authors attribute to a mismatch between its five predefined criteria and the 10 SLVMEval aspects. Overall, no automatic system matches human performance on more than one aspect, and the gap widens as video duration increases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the pairwise-comparison meta-evaluation framework established by VBench and UVE from short clips (~10 s) to long videos (up to ~3 hours), addressing a critical gap as text-to-long-video generation emerges. Rather than comparing outputs of different T2V models (as VBench does), it constructs controlled high-quality/low-quality pairs via synthetic degradation of dense video-captioning data from Vript, enabling fine-grained probing of 10 specific quality aspects. The evaluation systems tested (VLM-as-a-judge, CLIPScore, VideoScore) are all designed for short-video settings, making the length mismatch the central experimental variable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As T2LV systems (Phenaki, StreamingT2V, SkyReels-V2) begin producing arbitrarily long videos, the field lacks any validated evaluation infrastructure for that regime; SLVMEval fills this gap by providing a human-validated testbed where the correct answer is known. The finding that all automatic systems underperform humans in 9 of 10 aspects—and that accuracy degrades with duration—quantifies how far current metrics are from being usable for long-video generation research. The observation that manual filtering is not strictly necessary (high Pearson correlation before/after filtering) suggests the benchmark can be scaled to new aspects or domains without prohibitive annotation cost. Open questions include whether the degradation-based paradigm generalizes to failure modes that only appear in actual T2LV outputs, and how to design evaluation systems that maintain accuracy over hours-long temporal contexts.
