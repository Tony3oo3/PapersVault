## Problem

Most LVLM video reasoning systems rely on a batch inference paradigm that requires the full video before any reasoning begins, introducing ~10.6s Time-to-First-Token latency and causing temporal drift where the model loses track of early visual cues. This 'wait-and-see' approach is fundamentally misaligned with real-world video that arrives as a continuous stream, making it unsuitable for latency-sensitive applications like robotics, autonomous driving, and live surveillance.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a video stream arriving progressively at 2 FPS (resampled via timestamp-based alignment to preserve annotated keyframes) along with a text query about previously observed content. It produces streaming chain-of-thought reasoning tokens incrementally as frames arrive, with each reasoning sentence delimited by <EOT> boundary tokens and temporally grounded to specific keyframes. Unlike batch systems that output a single final answer, TaYS generates a continuous sequence of frame-aligned reasoning units that evolve in tandem with the visual stream, enabling queries at arbitrary time steps.

## Method

TaYS is a supervised fine-tuning framework built on Qwen2.5-VL that introduces three architectural innovations to enable true parallel streaming reasoning. First, a streaming attention mask enforces temporal causality by restricting each reasoning token at position i to attend only to visual tokens within a sliding window relative to the current temporal step, preventing information leakage from future frames. Second, a modality-decoupled positional encoding assigns independent positional axes to visual tokens (pos = s) and reasoning tokens (pos = t), eliminating the dynamic RoPE index shifts that occur when the visual sequence length grows continuously during streaming. Third, a parallel dual KV-cache maintains separate video cache C_v and text cache C_r, implementing a recursive merge-generate-split loop where new frames are asynchronously absorbed into C_v while C_r performs autoregressive decoding, so reasoning is never stalled by visual encoding. Training uses streaming Video CoT trajectories constructed from VideoEspresso with GPT-4o-generated frame-aligned triplets and BGE-M3 consistency filtering, fine-tuning the model to produce causally ordered, frame-consistent reasoning conditioned only on preceding observations.

## Results

On the extended VideoEspresso benchmark, TaYS (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) achieves 36.86% overall accuracy, outperforming the strongest batch baseline (Batch SFT, 30.38%) by +6.5% and the Interleaved SFT baseline (34.98%) by +1.9%. In GPT-5 subjective evaluation, TaYS attains a 43.7% normalized win rate versus 31.4% for Batch and 21.7% for Interleaved. On latency, TaYS reduces Time-to-First-Token from ~10.6 s (Batch) to ~10⁻⁶ s while maintaining a stable end-to-end delay of ~12 s across all frame rates (1–5 FPS), whereas Interleaved delay grows from 12.9 s to 20.1 s. Temporal grounding improves markedly: mean reasoning-event deviation drops from 1.52 s (Interleaved) to 0.69 s, with 86.0% of reasoning steps falling within 1 s of annotated keyframes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TaYS is compared against four baselines: Batch w/o thinking, Batch with thinking, Batch SFT, and Interleaved SFT (sequential frame-reasoning alternation without parallel caching). On the 7B model, TaYS (36.86%) clearly surpasses all four baselines in overall accuracy, with the largest margin over Batch SFT (+6.5%) and a +1.9% edge over Interleaved SFT (34.98%). On the 3B model, TaYS (33.45%) is marginally below Interleaved SFT (33.96%) on objective accuracy but substantially ahead of all batch variants (≤29.18%). In GPT-5 subjective evaluation, TaYS dominates with a 43.7% win rate, nearly doubling Interleaved SFT (21.7%) and exceeding Batch (31.4%), indicating superior reasoning coherence despite the small objective-accuracy gap at 3B. On latency, TaYS achieves near-zero TTFT (~10⁻⁶ s) versus ~10.6 s for Batch and ~0.03 s for Interleaved, and maintains a flat ~12 s end-to-end delay across 1–5 FPS while Interleaved delay escalates to 20.1 s at 5 FPS. Temporal alignment further favors TaYS: 0.69 s mean keyframe deviation (vs. 1.52 s for Interleaved) and 86.0% of reasoning within 1 s of events (vs. 62.4%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Qwen2.5-VL architecture, modifying its attention mechanism, positional encoding, and KV-cache management to support streaming inference. It departs from the dominant batch CoT paradigm for video reasoning (VideoEspresso, Video-of-Thought) and from prior streaming video understanding work (VideoLLM-Online, StreamChat) that focuses on captioning or QA continuity rather than explicit stepwise reasoning. The key architectural departure from naive interleaved streaming is the parallel dual KV-cache design, which decouples visual encoding from token generation—a contrast with the monolithic cache used in both batch and interleaved settings. The decoupled positional encoding also extends prior RoPE-based approaches by assigning independent positional axes to visual and reasoning tokens, addressing a cross-modal index conflict that prior streaming LLM work (e.g., group position encoding) handled differently.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-zero TTFT and stable ~12s end-to-end delay across frame rates make streaming video reasoning practically viable for latency-sensitive applications like robotics teleoperation and live surveillance, where the 10.6s batch latency is prohibitive. However, the evaluation is confined to a single benchmark (VideoEspresso) and one model family (Qwen2.5-VL 3B/7B), with no ablation isolating the individual contributions of the streaming mask, decoupled PE, and dual cache. Open questions include whether the approach scales to much longer streams (minutes to hours), whether it transfers to other LVLM architectures with different visual encoders, and whether the modest +2.9% accuracy gain over interleaved baselines holds on more diverse or longer-horizon reasoning tasks.
