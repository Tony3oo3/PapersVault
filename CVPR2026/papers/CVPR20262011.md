## Problem

Standard LIME-based saliency methods for medical imaging suffer from three critical gaps: zero or mean masking pushes perturbed images off the learned data manifold, making local surrogate fitting unreliable; superpixel-based partitioning introduces domain-specific instability; and existing XAI methods ignore the evidence-based medicine paradigm where clinicians reference similar historical cases. Additionally, gradient-based methods require white-box access that is often unavailable in clinical settings due to privacy constraints.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a medical image (chest X-ray or breast ultrasound), a frozen pre-trained binary classifier (InceptionV3, ViT, or SwinViT), the image-level abnormality label, and a training set of labeled images for evidence retrieval. The image is partitioned into fixed-size non-overlapping square patches (replacing superpixels), and the output is a pixel-level saliency map with values in [0,1] that highlights spatial regions driving the abnormality classification. Evaluation is performed against ground-truth segmentation or bounding-box maps using AUPRC, with the method operating in a fully black-box setting with respect to the classifier.

## Method

MedLIME augments the LIME surrogate-fitting pipeline with three modules. First, Generative Masking replaces zero/mean patch masking with reconstruction via a pretrained Masked Autoencoder, ensuring perturbed samples remain on the data manifold while probing local sensitivity. Second, Supervised Test-Time Adaptation learns per-sample photometric transformation parameters (Gaussian Blur, HSV Shift, RGB Shift) by minimizing cross-entropy on geometrically augmented copies of the test image while keeping the backbone frozen, calibrating the input to the model's learned distribution. Third, Evidence-based Regularization retrieves k nearest training samples in the classifier's feature space and computes a Nadaraya-Watson kernel-weighted reference saliency map that acts as an L2 regularizer in the surrogate objective. The final saliency map is obtained by minimizing a locality-weighted loss combining the standard LIME squared prediction error, an L1 sparsity term, and the L2 distance to the NW reference, yielding a patch-level importance vector that is upsampled to pixel resolution.

## Results

MedLIME achieves the highest average AUPRC across all four datasets and three model architectures: 0.418 on RSNA, 0.314 on ChestX-Det10, 0.451 on CheXlocalize, and 0.464 on BUID. The gains over the strongest baseline range from ~2% absolute AUPRC on BUID (0.464 vs. XRAI's 0.445) to ~8% on ChestX-Det10 (0.314 vs. XRAI's 0.234). On the challenging BUID dataset with extremely small ground-truth abnormality regions, MedLIME outperforms baselines by 2–30% AUPRC depending on the architecture. MedLIME also achieves the best MoRF (0.28) and LeRF (0.36) faithfulness scores among all compared methods on the ViT model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MedLIME outperforms all nine baselines on the average AUPRC across every dataset. The closest competitor is XRAI (gradient-based, region-level), which leads on individual cells (e.g., 0.571 GradCAM on RSNA/SwinViT, 0.554 XRAI on BUID/SwinViT) but trails MedLIME on the per-dataset averages by 1.9–8.0 AUPRC points. Among LIME variants, SLICE is the strongest (avg 0.263 on RSNA, 0.317 on CheXlocalize) yet still lags MedLIME by 15.5 and 13.4 points respectively; G-LIME and BayLIME are consistently the weakest LIME-based methods, scoring below 0.20 on most dataset-architecture combinations. CAM-based methods (GradCAM, LayerCAM, FinerCAM) show high variance across architectures—LayerCAM reaches 0.471 on RSNA/ViT but drops to 0.154 on ChestX-Det10/SwinViT—whereas MedLIME maintains a tighter performance band (0.272–0.504) across all 12 dataset-architecture pairs. On the faithfulness metrics (MoRF, LeRF) reported in Table 2, MedLIME achieves the best MoRF (0.28) and LeRF (0.36) scores, confirming that its saliency maps are not only more accurate in localization but also more faithful to the model's actual decision process.

## Relation to Prior Work

MedLIME extends the LIME paradigm by replacing its conventional zero/mean masking with generative reconstruction via a pretrained Masked Autoencoder, ensuring perturbed samples remain on the data manifold—a key departure from prior LIME variants (LIME-C, D-LIME, SLICE, BayLIME, GLIME) that either operate in latent space or rely on superpixel segmentation. The paper introduces two components unexplored in the explainability literature: supervised test-time adaptation (STTA) that calibrates inputs to a frozen model's distribution, and evidence-based regularization (EBR) that borrows the clinical practice of referencing similar historical cases via k-NN retrieval and Nadaraya-Watson kernel estimation. Unlike gradient-based methods (GradCAM, IG, XRAI), MedLIME remains fully model-agnostic, and unlike standard LIME it avoids superpixel algorithms by using fixed-size square patches, reducing modality-specific dependencies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

In high-stakes medical imaging where white-box access is often restricted by privacy or regulatory constraints, a robust model-agnostic explanation method that consistently outperforms both gradient-based and perturbation-based baselines across diverse architectures (CNN, ViT, Swin) and datasets is practically valuable. The EBR component's grounding in evidence-based medicine offers a principled inductive bias that could generalize to other clinical decision-support settings. However, the method still assumes a linear surrogate can approximate the local decision boundary, which may fail for highly non-linear models, and the reliance on a pretrained MAE introduces an additional model dependency that must be domain-matched. Open questions include whether the framework extends to 3D volumetric medical imaging, whether the MAE can be replaced by lighter-weight reconstructors, and how the method behaves under severe domain shift where the training set for EBR retrieval is insufficient.
