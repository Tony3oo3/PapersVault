## Problem

Existing causal representation learning methods for domain generalization perform point estimation of the structural causal model, selecting a single set of invariant causal features whose quality degrades sharply under limited or noisy data. No prior work performs Bayesian inference over the causal graph structure itself (as opposed to model parameters or latent representations) and leverages the resulting structural uncertainty to guide prediction aggregation for out-of-distribution generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263503/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images from multiple labeled training domains (e.g., 30k images per domain on CMNIST, 9,991 images across 4 domains on PACS, or biaxial loading images on BLT) along with domain identifiers. At inference, for each test image it samples S latent representations from the trained encoder and evaluates L=5 graph-specific predictors, producing a final prediction (class label or regression value) weighted by per-graph epistemic uncertainty. It additionally outputs three uncertainty signals: single-graph prediction uncertainty, Bayesian inference uncertainty over the full ensemble, and causal graph uncertainty measuring CMB variability across sampled graphs.

## Method

CGU-BAYES is a three-phase pipeline: (1) an NF-iVAE learns component-wise identifiable latent representations Z from images; (2) DAG-GFlowNet performs Bayesian causal discovery over {Z, Y, U} to sample L causal graphs from the posterior p(G|D), retaining only those satisfying structural assumptions; (3) for each sampled graph, the Causal Markov Blanket of Y is extracted and a dedicated predictor p(y|z_cmb^G) is trained. The key distinction from prior Bayesian DG methods is that the posterior is over the causal graph structure rather than model parameters, so each sampled graph yields a different CMB feature set and predictor. At inference, the epistemic uncertainty of each single-graph predictor—computed as the entropy gap between total and aleatoric uncertainty over sampled CMB representations—serves as a proxy for alignment between the test input and that graph, and is exponentiated into a weight that adaptively upweights predictors whose causal structure best matches the unseen domain.

## Results

On the challenging real-world BLT regression dataset, CGU-BAYES achieves 54.2% average OOD relative MSE, outperforming the best causal baseline CMBRL (62.9%) by 8.7 percentage points, with gains up to 15.8% on the most shifted domain (1:0.1). On standard classification benchmarks, CGU-BAYES reaches 82.5% on VLCS, 89.5% on PACS, and 69.5% on OfficeHome, matching or exceeding the strongest baselines (CMBRL: 82.3/89.1/67.1). The boosted variant CGU-BAYES++ achieves the best overall results at 82.9% (VLCS), 90.5% (PACS), and 69.5% (OfficeHome). Under limited-data and noisy settings on CMNIST, CGU-BAYES outperforms the Bayesian baseline BITEBAYES by 8.3% (500 images) and 5.4% (σ²=10 noise).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the challenging BLT regression dataset, CGU-BAYES (54.2% avg. relative MSE) substantially outperforms all causal baselines—CMBRL (62.9%), ICARL (66.7%), CTRANS (64.1%), and CAUSALREP (69.8%)—as well as Bayesian methods BITEBAYES (64.6%) and PTG (68.1%), with the largest gains (9.3–15.8%) on domains with strong distribution shift. On clean CMNIST, CGU-BAYES (74.1% OOD) ties with CMBRL (74.1%) and slightly trails CTRANS (72.5% is actually lower; CTRANS is 72.5%), while CGU-BAYES++ (74.2%) edges out all baselines by a narrow margin, consistent with the paper's observation that sufficient clean data reduces the benefit of graph sampling. On VLCS, CGU-BAYES (82.5%) beats CMBRL (82.3%) and ICARL (81.8%); on PACS, CGU-BAYES (89.5%) surpasses CMBRL (89.1%) and ICARL (88.7%) but is slightly below CIRL (90.1%), whereas CGU-BAYES++ (90.5%) leads all methods. On OfficeHome, CGU-BAYES (69.5%) achieves the best result, exceeding the next-best SAGNet and Mixup (68.4%) and CMBRL (67.1%). The uncertainty-guided weighting is critical: removing it (CGU-BAYES w/o UG) degrades BLT performance from 54.2% to 59.7%, and a uniform ensemble of CGU-BAYES++'s base models scores only 55.7/80.5/87.2 on CMNIST/VLCS/PACS versus 74.2/82.9/90.5 with the learned weights.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal representation learning line (ICARL, CMBRL) by replacing point-estimation of a single causal graph with Bayesian inference over the graph posterior, sampling multiple plausible structures via DAG-GFlowNet and constructing an ensemble of CMB-based predictors. It departs from prior Bayesian DG methods (BITEBAYES, PTG) that learn posteriors over model parameters or invariant features, instead placing the Bayesian machinery on the causal structure itself. The uncertainty quantification draws on epistemic neural networks (Osband et al.) and the entropy decomposition of total vs. aleatoric uncertainty, but the key novelty is using single-graph epistemic uncertainty as alignment weights in the aggregation, a mechanism not previously applied to DG prediction in vision.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical limitation of causal DG methods—reliance on a single, potentially inaccurate graph estimate under scarce or noisy data—and provides a principled way to quantify when the causal structure is uncertain and to downweight unreliable predictors. The introduction of the BLT dataset (a real-world, multi-domain regression task with heteroskedastic noise) fills a gap in DG benchmarks beyond synthetic or standard image classification. However, on clean, data-sufficient benchmarks (PACS, VLCS, OfficeHome), standalone CGU-BAYES does not consistently surpass deterministic baselines, and the boosted variant CGU-BAYES++ (which aggregates multiple SOTA methods) outperforms it, suggesting the core contribution is the uncertainty-guided aggregation framework rather than the specific graph-sampling pipeline. Open questions include scalability to larger latent spaces, sensitivity to the BCD method choice, and whether the alignment-weighting mechanism transfers to non-causal ensemble settings.
