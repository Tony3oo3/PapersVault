## Problem

Vision-Language-Action models for robotic manipulation are brittle because they are trained exclusively on trajectory-monotonic, success-biased human demonstrations, causing them to overfit spurious correlations between robot pose and environment state. This leaves them unable to recover from common execution errors: minor pose deviations (in-distribution errors) where the task is still valid, or catastrophic failures like a toppled object (out-of-distribution errors) where the environment state itself is unrecoverable by the task policy. No prior framework provides a unified, data-centric mechanism to handle both error regimes.

## Input / Output

The system consumes RGB images from top and wrist-mounted cameras (RealSense D435i in real-world deployment) along with a language instruction that specifies either the main task (e.g., 'make coffee') or a specific reset skill (e.g., 'reset the cup'). The VLA outputs action chunks of length K in the robot's action space, while the online MLLM monitor produces a discrete arbitration decision—continue task execution, invoke a specific reset skill adapter, or confirm environment restoration. In real-world settings, object poses are estimated via Any6D from visual input alone, eliminating the need for privileged simulator state logs.

## Method

FLARE introduces a dual Retry/Reset paradigm grounded in an ID/OOD error taxonomy. For ID errors, a perturbation-bridging augmentation injects random pose perturbations (up to 45° rotation, 0.5 m translation) between task segments, followed by kinematic bridging segments that reconnect the robot to the valid starting pose of the next subtask; the VLA is trained on bridging-to-task subsequences so it learns to execute correct actions from diverse, out-of-distribution robot poses. For OOD errors, an offline MLLM (Gemini-2.5-Pro) analyzes failure videos to classify errors, identify the reset target object, and extract the failure timestamp, bootstrapping a compact library of object-centric reset demonstrations (10–20 per skill) that are then massively expanded via the same perturbation-bridging augmentation. Training uses LoRA adapters on a shared π0.5 VLA backbone—one adapter for the main task and a separate adapter per reset skill—preventing gradient interference while enabling modular scaling. At inference, an online MLLM monitor observes execution and dynamically swaps the active LoRA adapter: ID errors are handled autonomously by the VLA's learned retry robustness, while OOD errors trigger a switch to the appropriate reset skill adapter, after which the system seamlessly resumes the main task.

## Results

On 9 contact-rich manipulation tasks in RoboMimic, FLARE achieves a mean success rate of 84.0%, surpassing the VLA backbone π0.5 (72.2%) by 11.8% and the prior leading self-reflection method Phoenix (57.8%) by 26.2%. FLARE attains state-of-the-art performance on 8 out of 9 tasks, including 100% on Stack_D0, Stack_D1, and StackThree_D0. In real-world validation on a Piper arm, FLARE improves over π0.5 by 12.5% on Stack Three Blocks (75.0% vs. 62.5%) and by 10% on Insert U-shaped Block (55.0% vs. 45.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLARE outperforms all three primary baselines across the 9-task RoboMimic benchmark. Against the VLA backbone π0.5 (72.2% mean), FLARE adds 11.8% absolute improvement, with the largest gains on high-randomization tasks (e.g., Coffee_D1: 78% vs. 56%; ThreePieceAssembly_D1: 58% vs. 46%). Against Phoenix (57.8%), the previous leading self-reflection method, FLARE improves by 26.2% and even surpasses Phoenix-Human (78.9%), which is supplied with oracle human instructions for subgoal correction. Against OpenVLA (38.0%), the gap is 46% in mean success rate, with particularly dramatic improvements on long-horizon tasks (StackThree_D1: 90% vs. 20%). The sole task where FLARE does not achieve the best result is Threading_D0 (72% vs. Subgoal Self-reflection's 80%), though it still substantially exceeds π0.5 (42%) and Phoenix (68%) on that task.

## Relation to Prior Work

FLARE builds directly on π0.5 as its VLA backbone and extends MimicGen's segment-stitching data augmentation with a novel perturbation-bridging strategy that decouples robot pose from environment state. Conceptually, it departs from prior self-correction work (Phoenix, REFLECT, SeRO) by reframing error recovery as a data-centric problem rather than an architecture or RL problem, introducing an ID/OOD failure taxonomy that unifies two previously separate recovery mechanisms (retry and reset) under a single training pipeline. The dual use of an MLLM—offline as a failure analyst to mine OOD states and online as a closed-loop monitor to arbitrate between task and reset adapters—goes beyond prior MLLM-based systems that rely on fixed skill libraries or template-based correction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FLARE addresses a fundamental and widely acknowledged limitation of VLA models—their brittleness to execution deviations—through a practical, data-centric mechanism that does not require RL or architectural changes, making it broadly applicable to existing VLA pipelines. However, the approach is currently validated on only 9 simulation tasks and 2 real-world tasks with a single arm, and reset skill success rates for complex objects (coffee pod, U-shaped block) remain low (20–24%), highlighting that dexterous in-hand pose adjustment remains an open challenge. Open questions include whether the perturbation-bridging strategy scales to higher-DoF or multi-arm systems, how the reset skill library grows with task diversity, and whether the MLLM monitor's latency and reliability are sufficient for real-time deployment beyond controlled lab settings.
