## Problem

Existing training-free video motion transfer methods rely on intermediate outputs (attention maps, diffusion features) of pre-trained T2V models, requiring gradient backpropagation through internal layers and incurring heavy GPU memory and time costs. This architectural dependency also limits flexibility across different model backbones. The paper asks whether motion transfer can be achieved by operating purely on the prediction outputs of flow-based T2V models, eliminating internal-layer access entirely.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262885/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a source video (480×720, 49 frames) and a text prompt describing the desired target scene. The source video is encoded into latent space and forward-noised (no iterative inversion), while the target video is generated via a flow-based T2V model (Wan2.1-1.3B or Wan2.2-5B) with 50 denoising steps, where optimization is applied only to the initial 10 steps. The output is a target video that inherits the source's motion patterns (object trajectories, camera movement, complex actions) while rendering entirely new subjects and scenes per the prompt.

## Method

FlowMotion's key insight is that early latent predictions (ẑ₀(t) = z_t − t·v_t) of flow-based T2V models inherently encode rich temporal information with minimal appearance detail. The proposed flow guidance loss has two components: Latent Alignment (LA) directly aligns source and target latent predictions for global motion consistency, and Difference Alignment (DA) aligns frame-wise latent differences to emphasize temporal dynamics while suppressing static appearance cues. A velocity regularization strategy decomposes each velocity update into a projection along the accumulated flow direction and an orthogonal component, then decays the orthogonal part (γ=0.1) to prevent abrupt directional shifts and over-alignment. Because guidance operates solely on model prediction outputs rather than internal features, no gradient propagates through the network's layers, reducing peak GPU memory to 19.3 GB (vs. 63–89 GB for prior training-free methods) and inference time to 213 s per video.

## Results

On a 50-video benchmark (480×720, 49 frames), FlowMotion achieves the highest Motion Fidelity (0.850) and Temporal Consistency (0.986) among all compared methods, while also attaining strong Text Similarity (0.347). In terms of efficiency, FlowMotion requires only 213 s inference time and 19.3 GB GPU memory per video—less than half the memory of the most memory-hungry training-free baseline (SMM at 89.4 GB) and no per-video training time, compared to 1,170–8,100 s for training-based methods. In a 20-participant user study, FlowMotion also scores the highest overall preference (4.446/5), outperforming the next-best method DeT (3.465/5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FlowMotion outperforms all four training-based baselines (LoRA Tuning, MotionDirector, MotionInversion, DeT) on Motion Fidelity and Temporal Consistency while also matching or exceeding their Text Similarity, and it eliminates the 1,170–8,100 s per-video training cost entirely. Among training-free methods, FlowMotion dominates on Motion Fidelity: it surpasses DiTFlow (0.850 vs. 0.691), SMM (0.850 vs. 0.762), MotionClone (0.850 vs. 0.786), and MOFT (0.850 vs. 0.582) by large margins. The only metric where FlowMotion trails a baseline is Text Similarity, where DiTFlow edges ahead (0.350 vs. 0.347), though DiTFlow's Motion Fidelity is substantially lower. In terms of efficiency, FlowMotion uses 19.3 GB GPU memory—roughly 2.7× less than MotionClone (51.5 GB), 3.9× less than MOFT (75.0 GB), and 4.6× less than SMM (89.4 GB)—and completes inference in 213 s, faster than all training-free baselines except DiTFlow (349 s). The user study confirms these quantitative advantages, with FlowMotion scoring 4.514 on Motion Preservation and 4.523 on Temporal Consistency, well above the next-best DeT (3.874 and 3.831 respectively).

## Relation to Prior Work

FlowMotion departs from both training-based motion transfer (which fine-tunes temporal attention or LoRA parameters per video) and prior training-free methods (MotionClone, MOFT, SMM, DiTFlow) that extract motion guidance from intermediate features such as temporal attention maps, diffusion features, or cross-frame attention flows. Its key conceptual shift is to operate entirely on the model's predicted outputs—specifically, one-step latent predictions from flow matching—rather than requiring gradient propagation through internal layers or architecture-specific attention blocks. This is enabled by the empirical observation that early latent predictions in flow-based T2V models (e.g., Wan) already encode rich temporal structure, making them a natural and sufficient motion representation without any inversion or internal feature access.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: FlowMotion reduces GPU memory from 51–89 GB (training-free baselines) to ~19 GB and inference time from 349–1839 s to 213 s, while achieving the best motion fidelity and temporal consistency in quantitative and user evaluations. The approach is architecture-agnostic within the flow-based T2V family and generalizes across model scales (Wan2.1-1.3B to Wan2.2-5B), suggesting broad applicability as flow matching becomes the dominant video generation paradigm. Limitations include restriction to flow-based backbones (not applicable to standard DDPM/EDM diffusion models), the somewhat ad-hoc velocity regularization, and the fact that the clean-latent alternative (z_0^src) can outperform latent predictions on fine-grained motion at the cost of text alignment—pointing toward future adaptive guidance strategies.
