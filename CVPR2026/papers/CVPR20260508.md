## Problem

Autoregressive models dominate biomedical vision-language models, yet masked diffusion models (MDMs) such as LLaDA, which have shown competitive performance in general-domain language generation, remain largely unexplored for biomedical image understanding. The paper addresses how to effectively adapt language diffusion models to the biomedical domain, why they are promising for this setting, and what design principles (initialization, fine-tuning, inference configuration) are essential for building effective biomedical diffusion VLMs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260508/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a biomedical image (processed by a SigLIP2 vision encoder into visual embeddings) and a text prompt (biomedical query formatted as a dialogue turn) as input, and produces a fixed-length text response generated via iterative denoising from a fully masked sequence. Unlike autoregressive baselines where output length is unreliable (e.g., LLaVA-Med averages only ~36 words despite a 256-token limit), LLaDA-MedV explicitly controls response length through the generation parameter L (256 tokens for open-ended conversation, 64 for VQA), yielding substantially longer and more informative outputs (~166 words per response). For VQA tasks, closed-form answers are scored by accuracy and open-form answers by token-level recall.

## Method

LLaDA-MedV adapts the LLaDA-8B-Instruct masked diffusion language model to biomedical visual understanding by extending its masked prediction objective (Eq. 3) to condition on visual features and user prompts (Eq. 4), using a SigLIP2 vision encoder and a two-layer MLP projector to inject image embeddings into the bidirectional-attention Transformer. Training follows a three-stage pipeline: (1) biomedical semantic alignment (freeze vision tower and language backbone, train only the projector on 600k image-text pairs), (2) end-to-end vision instruction tuning (fine-tune language backbone and projector on 60k multi-turn dialogues, keeping the vision tower frozen), and (3) dataset-specific fine-tuning on VQA-RAD, SLAKE, and PathVQA training sets. Inference employs semi-autoregressive generation with low-confidence remasking: the response of length L is divided into L/B blocks processed left-to-right, each undergoing Z·B/L sampling steps where only low-confidence tokens are remasked. A key finding is that initializing from LLaDA-V (general-domain diffusion VLM) degrades biomedical performance and causes repetitive outputs, making domain-appropriate initialization critical.

## Results

On the open-ended biomedical conversation benchmark (193 questions), LLaDA-MedV achieves an overall GPT-4-judged score of 52.605, surpassing LLaVA-Med (44.750) by 7.855% and LLaDA-V (50.738) by 1.867%. On closed-form VQA, LLaDA-MedV sets new state-of-the-art accuracy: 84.93% on VQA-RAD, 92.31% on SLAKE, and 95.15% on PathVQA. On open-form VQA, performance is less competitive (e.g., 45.60 recall on VQA-RAD vs. 79.19 for Q2ATransformer), attributed to the lack of post-training in the LLaDA backbone. The model also generates substantially longer responses (~166 words vs. ~36 for LLaVA-Med) with explicit length control via sampling steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LLaDA-MedV outperforms all nine autoregressive and diffusion baselines on the open-ended biomedical conversation benchmark, with the largest margins over LLaVA-Med (+7.855 overall) and LLaMA (+24.781), and a narrower but consistent lead over the closest competitor LLaDA-V (+1.867). On closed-form VQA, LLaDA-MedV achieves the highest accuracy across all three benchmarks, beating LLaVA-Med by 0.74% (VQA-RAD), 6.97% (SLAKE), and 3.94% (PathVQA), and surpassing the previous best (M2I2) on SLAKE by 1.21%. However, on open-form VQA, LLaDA-MedV trails several baselines substantially (e.g., 45.60 vs. 79.19 on VQA-RAD open-form vs. Q2ATransformer), which the authors attribute to the LLaDA backbone lacking RLHF or other post-training for answer-formatting. Among the VQA-specific baselines (VL Encoder-Decoder, Q2ATransformer, PubMedCLIP, BiomedCLIP, M2I2), LLaDA-MedV wins decisively on closed-form accuracy but loses on open-form recall, suggesting the diffusion paradigm excels at constrained classification but struggles with free-form token recall without additional fine-tuning. The comparison with LLaDA-V (the general-domain diffusion VLM) is particularly informative: LLaDA-MedV's biomedical-specific training yields a 1.867-point gain, confirming the value of domain adaptation for diffusion-based VLMs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLaDA masked diffusion language model [33] to the biomedical visual domain by adopting the LLaVA-style visual instruction tuning architecture [24] (SigLIP2 vision encoder + MLP projector + LLaDA-8B backbone) and MaskGIT's low-confidence remasking strategy [5] for inference. While LLaDA-V [56] demonstrated that diffusion models can handle general-domain visual understanding, this work is the first to apply the paradigm to biomedical image understanding, departing from the autoregressive models (LLaVA-Med, MedVLM-R1, Med-Flamingo) that have dominated the biomedical VLM landscape. A notable departure from prior practice is the rejection of LLaDA-V initialization weights, which the authors found to degrade biomedical performance and induce repetitive outputs, in favor of LLaDA-8B-Instruct with domain-specific alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first diffusion-based biomedical VLM, this work opens a new architectural direction for medical image-language understanding and demonstrates that masked diffusion enables explicit, reliable control over response length (166 vs. 36 words average) — a practical advantage for clinical applications requiring detailed, context-rich answers that ARMs struggle to produce even with prompt engineering. However, significant open questions remain: the model's open-form VQA performance lags behind ARMs due to insufficient post-training, token repetition under low sampling steps is a persistent failure mode, and inference is roughly 6× slower per word. Future work on adaptive step allocation, RLHF-based post-training, and more efficient remasking schedules will be needed before diffusion-based biomedical VLMs can match the maturity of their autoregressive counterparts.
