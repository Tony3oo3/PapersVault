## Problem

Existing digital hematopathology models are restricted to single-task, single-disease, or single-modality settings, and cannot provide unified multi-task, multi-modal reasoning across the full complexity of blood film analysis. Pathology foundation models are optimized for whole-slide images at low magnification, while hematology demands fine-grained cell-level interpretation at 40×–1000× magnification; hematology-specific foundation models in turn are limited to single-cell tasks and a narrow set of cell types. No prior framework jointly handles detection, segmentation, classification, morphology prediction, and visual-textual reasoning across multiple hematological diseases in one model.

## Input / Output

The model consumes two input streams: (1) hematological microscopy images at varying magnifications (40×, 100×, 1000×), both as single-cell crops and full field-of-view (FoV) frames containing multiple overlapping cells, and (2) structured text prompts whose format depends on the task—detection/segmentation prompts follow the template 'This image is for the detection of <disease> of cells,' VQA prompts are prefixed with 'Q:', and MLM prompts use 'mask:' tokens. Outputs are task-specific: bounding boxes with class labels for detection, binary segmentation masks, six morphological attribute predictions per cell in FoV images, free-text answers for VQA, and completed masked sentences for MLM. Notably, single-cell classification operates purely on visual features without any text conditioning.

## Method

Uni-Hema pairs a ResNet-50 backbone with a six-layer DINO-inspired spatial image encoder and a T5-based text encoder-decoder, bridged by the proposed Hema-Former module containing four sub-modules: Cross-Modal Fusion (CMF) aligns text and image embeddings via sequential cross-attention for language tasks; Text-Guided Visual Refinement (TGVR) injects disease-specific textual context into top-K objectness queries to produce disease-informed decoder embeddings for detection and morphology; a Single-Cell Feature Extractor (SCFE) aggregates mean-pooled encoder features with a learnable query for text-independent classification; and a Query-Guided Mask Former (QGMF) fuses backbone spatial features with encoder embeddings and decoder queries (following Mask DINO principles) to predict segmentation masks. Training proceeds in six sequential stages—pre-training the visual branch on classification, pre-training the text branch on semi-synthetic MLM/QA data, joint vision-module training across classification/segmentation/detection, then targeted fine-tuning of the image decoder, QGMF, and finally CMF+text decoder—over 46 heterogeneous datasets (~700K images, 21K QA pairs) in roughly 8 days on a single RTX 4090. The key design insight is that hierarchical, task-appropriate feature granularity (global for classification, token-level for detection, pixel-level for segmentation, sentence-level for language) is generated within a single shared encoder-decoder stack rather than requiring separate task-specific networks.

## Results

On cell detection, Uni-Hema achieves a mean mAP50 of 61.1 across 12 datasets, outperforming DINO (60.4) and YOLO (59.4), with the largest gains on LeukemiaAttri subsets (e.g., 49.8 vs. 44.4 for AttriDet on H_100x_C2). For single-cell classification, it reaches a mean F1 of 92.5, surpassing DinoBloomS (91.5) and DINOv3S (90.8). On segmentation, Uni-Hema attains a mean Dice of 91.7, competitive with TransNetR (93.7) but well above U-Net (84.5). For field-of-view morphology prediction, it achieves a mean F1 of 77.2 versus 62.6 for the multi-task AttriDet baseline, and reports BLEU-4 scores of 56.4 (VQA) and 79.8 (masked language modeling) on its curated text tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On detection, Uni-Hema clearly outperforms the multi-task AttriDet [59] on all four LeukemiaAttri subsets (e.g., 49.8 vs. 44.4 mAP50 on H_100x_C2) and beats DINO on H_1000x malaria (83.1 vs. 79.8), but trails DINO/YOLO on smaller or easier datasets such as Sickle Cell (67.0 vs. 73.6), Parasites (36.2 vs. 46.1), BCCD (87.8 vs. 89.5), and TXL (94.0 vs. 95.3), which the authors attribute to limited training samples for those classes within the large joint corpus. For single-cell classification, Uni-Hema's SCFE features surpass DinoBloomS on both Raabin (98.8 vs. 98.0 F1) and BMC (86.2 vs. 85.0), and substantially outperform DINOv2S (81.0 mean) and ResNet (76.4 mean). On segmentation, TransNetR retains a slight edge in mean Dice (93.7 vs. 91.7), with Uni-Hema losing on 5 of 6 datasets but winning on Elsafty (99.9 vs. 99.5); the gap is attributed to the use of a simple upsampler rather than a SOTA decoder. The most decisive win is in field-of-view morphology prediction, where Uni-Hema achieves 77.2 mean F1 versus 62.6 for AttriDet, with gains of 10–22 points across all four LeukemiaAttri subsets. On unseen datasets (Table 2), Uni-Hema leads in classification (90.8 vs. 89.9 DinoBloomS mean F1) and single-cell morphology (91.6 vs. 91.4 DINOv3S), confirming cross-domain generalization without task-specific retraining.

## Relation to Prior Work

Uni-Hema composes three well-established architectural components—DINO-DETR for the image encoder/decoder, T5 for the text encoder/decoder, and Mask DINO for the segmentation head—into a single unified pipeline, with the novel Hema-Former module serving as the cross-modal fusion bridge. It departs from prior hematology foundation models (DinoBloom, RedDino) that are restricted to single-cell, single-task settings, and from generalist medical AI (GMAI) that relies on natural-language supervision and cannot handle detection or segmentation. The paper also extends the multi-task hematology line (CelloType, AttriDet/LeukemiaAttri) by scaling from two tasks on one disease to six tasks across six disease categories within one architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that a single model trained once on 46 heterogeneous datasets can match or exceed task-specific SOTA across detection, segmentation, classification, morphology, VQA, and MLM, Uni-Hema offers a practical path toward deployable, multi-disease hematological diagnostics—particularly relevant in low-resource settings where specialist hematologists are scarce. Key limitations include the semi-synthetic nature of the VQA/MLM data (generated by BioMistral and Gemini), reliance on a single GPU for training, and the fact that the unified model underperforms dedicated detectors on some malaria and sickle-cell detection benchmarks. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to additional hematological conditions, whether the semi-synthetic language data introduces biases that affect clinical reliability, and whether the progressive six-stage training protocol can be simplified or parallelized.
