## Problem

Despite strong high-level reasoning, current vision-language models struggle with fine-grained visual perception tasks that require mapping physical scales to numeric values, such as reading pressure gauges, rulers, or clocks. Existing fine-grained benchmarks focus on text reading, chart reasoning, or artificial low-level vision tests, but none systematically evaluate instrument reading across diverse device types and readout designs. This paper identifies and quantifies this persistent gap in VLM capabilities.

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs an instrument image (real-world photo or synthetic render) with a natural-language question asking for the reading. The expected output is a numeric value graded against a closed tolerance interval (not a single exact value) plus an optional unit string; answer extraction handles integers, decimals, scientific notation, fractions, and time patterns (hh:mm:ss), taking the rightmost scalar when multiple appear. The benchmark spans 26 instrument types across four readout designs—dial, digital, linear, and composite—with 2,442 total image-question pairs.

## Method

The paper introduces MeasureBench with a modular synthesis framework that treats each instrument as a plug-and-play generator under a unified interface, randomizing scales, readings, materials, lighting, backgrounds, and camera pose while enforcing semantic validity. Two complementary rendering backends are provided: a 2D programmatic renderer where LLMs draft code from prompt templates for full control over fonts and geometry, and a 3D Blender renderer producing photorealistic scenes with realistic lighting, reflections, and occlusions to narrow the sim-to-real gap. The benchmark comprises 1,272 human-annotated real-world images and 1,170 synthetic images (30 per each of 39 instrument appearances). Evaluation of 18 VLMs reveals that unit recognition exceeds 90% accuracy while value accuracy remains below 31%, exposing a fundamental fine-grained spatial grounding limitation. Preliminary reinforcement fine-tuning with GRPO on 3,900 synthetic samples yields over 3× improvement on in-domain synthetic data and moderate gains on real-world images, primarily for simpler single-needle and digital instruments.

## Results

On MeasureBench, the best-performing VLM (Gemini 2.5 Pro) achieves only 30.2% overall accuracy on real-world images and 26.3% on synthetic images, revealing that instrument reading remains a major challenge for current VLMs. Unit recognition is consistently strong (93–96% across top models), while value accuracy is the primary bottleneck (e.g., Gemini 2.5 Pro: 96.2% unit vs. 30.7% value on real images). Digital readouts are substantially easier (up to 80.2% for Gemini 2.5 Pro) compared to dial (31.5%), linear (21.9%), and composite (3.8%) instruments. After GRPO reinforcement fine-tuning on synthetic data, Qwen2.5-VL-7B improves from 14.6% to 19.7% on real images (+34.9%) and from 10.9% to 35.2% on synthetic images (+222.9%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares 18 general-purpose VLMs against two prior special-purpose gauge-reading systems (Reitsma et al. [29] and Shu et al. [34]) on the dial-meter subset of MeasureBench. VLMs substantially outperform both special-purpose pipelines: Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves 30.2% overall accuracy on real images versus 8.5% for Reitsma et al. and 4.2% (value-only) for Shu et al., while on synthetic images the gap widens to 26.3% vs. 12.5% and 0.0% respectively. The special-purpose systems fail to generalize beyond their narrow training distributions, breaking down at individual pipeline stages (gauge detection, pointer segmentation, OCR). In terms of unit recognition, VLMs also dominate: Gemini-2.5-Pro reaches 96.2% unit accuracy on real images compared to 17.8% for Reitsma et al. Among the VLMs themselves, no single model dominates all readout types—Gemini-2.5-Pro leads overall, but GPT-5-Mini slightly outperforms GPT-5 (22.0% vs. 19.8% real), and Qwen2.5-VL-7B matches Qwen2.5-VL-72B, indicating that larger language backbones do not reliably improve fine-grained visual perception.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the fragmented line of instrument-reading research—prior work has addressed narrow subsets such as clocks, rulers, pressure gauges, and household meters—into a unified benchmark spanning 26 instrument types and four readout designs (dial, digital, linear, composite). Methodologically, it builds on GRPO (from DeepSeekMath) for reinforcement fine-tuning experiments and draws on programmatic/3D rendering pipelines for data synthesis, but departs from prior special-purpose CV pipelines (e.g., Reitsma et al., Shu et al.) by evaluating general-purpose VLMs rather than hand-crafted detection-OCR-segmentation chains. In the broader VLM benchmark landscape, it fills a gap left by text-reading and chart-reasoning benchmarks that do not require mapping physical scales to numeric values.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a fundamental and persistent limitation of current VLMs: strong OCR and object recognition (>90% unit accuracy) does not translate into fine-grained spatial grounding for pointer localization and tick interpolation (~30% value accuracy even for frontier models). The finding that extended 'thinking' tokens provide negligible benefit and that larger language backbones do not improve fine-grained perception when the visual encoder is unchanged challenges the prevailing assumption that scaling reasoning or language capacity will close the gap. Open questions include whether better visual encoders or architecture-level changes (rather than more data or more reasoning tokens) are needed, and whether the sim-to-real gap in synthetic training data can be further narrowed to enable robust generalization to unseen instrument types.
