## Problem

Existing backdoor attacks on object detection rely on static, trigger-specific visual patterns (patches, noise, fixed shapes) that are inflexible, visually detectable, fragile to standard defenses, and confined to isolated digital or narrow physical conditions. No prior method exploits the natural, dynamic spatial interactions between real-world objects as a trigger mechanism, leaving a gap in understanding how post-processing components like NMS can be hijacked through purely label-level manipulation in realistic, time-varying scenarios.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images from MS-COCO 2017 and PASCAL VOC 07/12, where the 'trigger' is not a pixel-level pattern but a spatial configuration of natural objects (e.g., a person overlapping a sheep). Poisoning is performed exclusively at the annotation level—appending bounding box entries to label files—requiring no pixel modification. The output is the standard NMS-filtered detection set (bounding boxes, class labels, confidence scores), but under triggered conditions the NMS selection is flipped to produce misclassification, mislocalization, disappearance, or false appearance of objects.

## Method

Phantom hijacks the Non-Maximum Suppression step through a two-stage label-poisoning procedure. Stage 1 injects additional bounding box labels into training annotations whose IoU with the victim's ground-truth box exceeds the NMS threshold γ, placing them in the same NMS cluster; the number and class of injected labels vary by attack variant (e.g., one target-class box for misclassification, two boxes for object appearing, none for object disappearing). Stage 2 enforces a hierarchical confidence ranking (c_trigger > c_victim > c_target) together with geometric constraints ensuring the trigger overlaps the victim but not the target, so that under benign conditions the victim naturally wins NMS while under triggered conditions the trigger suppresses the victim and the target box survives. Training jointly optimizes a standard detection loss plus a confidence-ranking loss, embedding the desired NMS behavior end-to-end. Because the attack is entirely label-driven and independent of pixel-level features, it remains effective under input transformations (Gaussian blur, JPEG) and evades model-centric defenses like ODSCAN and data-centric defenses like Detector Cleanse.

## Results

On YOLOv5 with MS-COCO, Phantom achieves ASR of 96.87% (misclassification), 100% (mislocalization), 96.29% (object disappearing), and 72.72% (object appearing), while maintaining mAP50 within 0.5% of the clean baseline (59.84 vs. 60.64). On Faster R-CNN with MS-COCO, ASR ranges from 62.99% (misclassification) to 91.84% (object disappearing), with mAP50 dropping by less than 0.5 points. On PASCAL VOC, ASR values are consistently higher, reaching 94–100% for most attack variants. Phantom is the only method among all tested attacks capable of performing mislocalization, achieving 88.21% (Faster R-CNN) and 100% (YOLOv5) ASR on COCO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against BadDet variants (RMA, GMA, OGA, ODA) [3], Phantom consistently achieves higher ASR while preserving mAP50: on YOLOv5/COCO misclassification, Phantom reaches 96.87% ASR vs. 59.40% (RMA) and 48.50% (GMA), with mAP50 of 59.82 vs. 55.20 and 54.22 respectively. For object disappearing on Faster R-CNN/COCO, Phantom (91.84% ASR, 58.86 mAP50) substantially outperforms UT (60.11% ASR, 48.27 mAP50) and Clean-label (85.83% ASR, 25.86 mAP50), which severely degrades clean performance. For object appearing on Faster R-CNN/COCO, Phantom achieves 91.88% ASR vs. 75.66% (OGA) and 69.80% (Clean-label). Notably, Phantom is the only method capable of performing mislocalization attacks (88.21–100% ASR), a capability absent from all baselines. The one exception is YOLOv5/COCO object appearing, where Phantom (72.72% ASR) underperforms OGA (77.91%) and Clean-label (83.94%), though it still maintains the highest mAP50 (60.10 vs. 59.82 and 60.23).

## Relation to Prior Work

Phantom extends the object-detection backdoor attack line initiated by BadDet (Chan et al., ECCV 2022) and subsequent clean-label variants, but departs fundamentally from their reliance on static visual triggers (patches, patterns, or fixed natural objects). Instead of modifying pixels or appending fixed trigger images, Phantom repurposes the NMS post-processing step as the attack surface, using only label-level poisoning and confidence-ranking constraints to create a trigger that is the spatial overlap between two real objects at inference time. This is conceptually adjacent to DAEDALUS (Wang et al.), which also targets NMS, but DAEDALUS requires adversarial pixel perturbations whereas Phantom operates entirely in the annotation space, making it orthogonal to pixel-level defenses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Because NMS is a near-universal component of both one-stage and two-stage detectors, the vulnerability identified here is architecturally broad and not tied to any specific backbone or head design. The demonstrated bypass of ODSCAN, Detector Cleanse, and input-transformation defenses highlights a significant gap: current backdoor defenses assume pixel-level or trigger-pattern signatures, and interaction-based triggers that emerge from natural scene geometry are invisible to them. Open questions include whether defenses can be designed around NMS decision boundaries rather than input features, and whether the same label-poisoning + post-processing-exploitation paradigm transfers to other pipeline stages (e.g., anchor assignment, class-agnostic NMS). The physical-world validation with moving objects underscores the practical threat to autonomous driving and surveillance deployments.
