## Problem

VLMs struggle with metrically precise spatial reasoning required for embodied applications, and while tool-augmented agentic paradigms promise to close this gap, prior approaches rely on handcrafted prompting, fixed tool pipelines, or single-tool RL. Naively scaling RL to multiple heterogeneous tools creates a combinatorially large action space where exploration fails to discover effective policies, leaving a fundamental gap in how to train VLMs to coordinate diverse vision and robotic tools.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260317/cropped_page0_idx9.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives an RGB image paired with a text query (or a robot camera image with a manipulation instruction) and produces a multi-turn dialogue structured with  tags for reasoning,  tags for tool invocations, and <answer> tags for the final response. Tool calls can target segmentation, pointing, monocular depth, 3D cuboid fitting, grasp generation, cropping, perspective projection, or robot actions (image/depth capture, grasp, place), and the final answer may be a multiple-choice selection, 2D bounding box, pointing coordinate, 3D pose (eight projected corners), grasp pose (five 2D contact points), or a robot action command.

## Method

DIRL (Double Interactive Reinforcement Learning) decomposes multi-tool learning into two phases to avoid the combinatorial explosion of naive multi-tool RL. In the teaching phase, a single pointing tool is first trained via interactive RL (constrained search space), and its traces are combined with multi-tool demonstrations from a frontier model (Claude Sonnet 4.5) to form an 8k SFT dataset; the base model (Qwen2.5-VL-3B) is then fine-tuned on this mixture. In the exploration phase, the SFT-initialized policy undergoes full multi-tool interactive RL using GRPO with KL regularization, interacting with all tools through Toolshed—a distributed platform that decouples compute-intensive tool serving (SAM2, Depth Pro, RoboRefer, GraspGen, robot controllers) from the training loop via asynchronous parallel workers and resource isolation. This two-round IRL design ensures the model first acquires grounded single-tool behavior before refining complex multi-tool chaining strategies through interactive feedback.

## Results

SpaceTools-3B achieves state-of-the-art performance across all five spatial reasoning benchmarks. On RoboSpatial-Home it reaches 70.00% overall accuracy (vs. 62.50% for the best proprietary model, Gemini-ER 1.5), 90.32% on BLINK depth, and 53.07% on RefSpatial. On BOP-ASK, it attains 34.37% pose IoU and 43.06% Grasp-MACE, substantially outperforming GPT-5 (9.03% pose, 39.59% Grasp-MACE) and Claude Sonnet 4.5 (1.67% pose, 40.12% Grasp-MACE). Compared to tool-free fine-tuning of the same Qwen2.5-VL-3B base model on identical 8k samples, SpaceTools gains +12% (vs. SFT) and +16% (vs. RL) on RoboSpatial.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SpaceTools-3B outperforms all proprietary models on every benchmark: it leads Gemini-ER 1.5 by +7.5% on RoboSpatial (70.00 vs. 62.50), +21.1% on BLINK (90.32 vs. 69.23), and +11.4% on RefSpatial (53.07 vs. 41.72); it surpasses GPT-5 by +25.3% on BOP-ASK pose (34.37 vs. 9.03) and +3.5% on Grasp-MACE (43.06 vs. 39.59); and it exceeds Claude Sonnet 4.5 by +32.7% on pose (34.37 vs. 1.67) and +2.9% on Grasp-MACE. Against spatial VLMs, SpaceTools dominates: it beats RoboRefer-8B-SFT (the strongest spatial baseline) by +10.6% on RoboSpatial and +4.7% on RefSpatial, while achieving 34.37% pose IoU where all spatial VLMs score 0%. Versus general open-source models (Qwen2.5-VL-32B, LLaVA-NeXT-8B), SpaceTools leads by 28–44 points on RefSpatial and 34 points on pose. Most notably, compared to tool-free fine-tuning of the identical Qwen2.5-VL-3B base on the same 8k samples, SpaceTools gains +12% (vs. SFT) and +16% (vs. RL) on RoboSpatial, confirming that interactive multi-tool RL—not merely more data or a different loss—drives the improvement. On real-robot manipulation (Table 3), SpaceTools achieves 86% pick-and-place success with 10 s time-to-first-movement, matching Claude Sonnet 4.5+Toolshed (79%) and far exceeding GPT-5+Toolshed (65%) and π0.5 (0%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends ViGoRL's demonstration that interactive RL can teach a VLM to use a single visual tool (cropping) to the multi-tool setting, where the combinatorial action space makes naive RL exploration intractable. It departs from prior tool-augmented spatial reasoning approaches (SpatialPIN, APC, Visual Programming) that rely on handcrafted prompting or fixed pipelines, and from TIGeR which uses pre-computed tool outputs, by enabling the model to learn tool coordination through interactive exploration with real tool feedback. The two-phase DIRL framework—first grounding via single-tool IRL, then refining multi-tool coordination via full IRL—addresses the key systems challenge of scaling interactive tool use during training, supported by the Toolshed infrastructure for serving compute-heavy vision tools asynchronously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a 3B VLM with learned multi-tool coordination can outperform frontier proprietary models (GPT-5, Claude Sonnet 4.5) on spatial reasoning benchmarks and achieve 86% success on real robot manipulation, suggesting that tool-augmented training is a scalable alternative to architectural modification or large-scale data engineering for embodied spatial understanding. The finding that tool-augmented training on 8k samples substantially outperforms tool-free fine-tuning on the same data (+12–16%) highlights the value of interactive tool feedback over static supervision. Open questions include whether the approach scales to even larger tool sets, whether the reliance on a frontier model for universal teacher demonstrations limits the method's accessibility, and whether the observed out-of-domain generalization from single-tool IRL (e.g., RoboSpatial → RefSpatial) is robust across task families.
