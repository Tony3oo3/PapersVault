## Problem

Existing VLM-based safety guardrails are trained and evaluated under a single fixed safety policy, causing severe overfitting: models perform well on the seen policy but collapse on unseen ones and even lose basic instruction-following ability. No prior benchmark systematically measures cross-policy generalization, and no training method explicitly optimizes for policy adaptability rather than static classification.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262286/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives an RGB image paired with an arbitrary natural-language policy description (e.g., a platform's content rules specifying which categories of content are prohibited). It outputs a binary safe/unsafe judgment accompanied by policy-grounded reasoning that explicitly references the relevant policy rules. Unlike prior guardrails that lock output into a fixed JSON-style safety rationale, SafeGuard-VL can follow diverse output formats (e.g., multiple-choice, free-form) while still incorporating policy context into its decision.

## Method

SafeGuard-VL uses a two-stage paradigm that decouples semantic understanding from policy-specific safety recognition. Stage 1 (SFT) employs a self-recaption pipeline: Qwen2.5-VL generates an initial caption that suppresses unsafe details due to its built-in safety filters, then Gemma 27B performs minimal edits to recover the suppressed harmful semantics while preserving the original sentence structure, yielding ~100K augmented captions that teach the model to describe unsafe elements rather than classify them. Stage 2 (RL) applies GRPO with verifiable rewards (RLVR) on the LlavaGuard training set, where the ground-truth safe/unsafe label under a given policy serves as the reward signal and the model must generate reasoning that justifies its decision by referencing the specific policy text. This RL-based policy alignment avoids the distribution-matching overfitting inherent to SFT, enabling the model to adapt its judgments to different policy definitions at test time without retraining.

## Results

On UnsafeBench, SafeGuard-VL-Full achieves the highest overall F1-score of 72.2%, substantially outperforming general-purpose VLMs (Qwen2.5-VL-7B: 41.7%, GLM-4V-9B: 56.5%) and safety-specialized models (QwenGuard-7B: 43.6%, ShieldGemma2: 47.3%). On the authors' SafeEditBench, SafeGuard-VL-RL+SafeEditTrain reaches 49.43% macro-F1 across five policy levels, edging out Qwen2.5-VL-7B (48.68%) and significantly surpassing QwenGuard-7B (32.76%). On LlavaGuardBench, QwenGuard-7B retains its home-benchmark advantage (84.57 vs. 71.78 for SafeGuard-VL-RL), but SafeGuard-VL-RL preserves general capability (57.02% average on MMMU/RealWorldQA/BLINK/MMT) while QwenGuard-7B drops to 35.98%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SafeGuard-VL-Full decisively outperforms all five baselines on UnsafeBench overall F1 (72.2%), with the largest margins over Llama Guard (+49.5) and QwenGuard-7B (+28.6). Against general-purpose VLMs, the gain is more moderate but consistent: +15.7 over GLM-4V-9B (56.5%), +16.3 over InstructBLIP (55.9%), and +30.5 over Qwen2.5-VL-7B (41.7%). On SafeEditBench, the advantage narrows: SafeGuard-VL-RL+SafeEditTrain (49.43%) only marginally beats Qwen2.5-VL-7B (48.68%) but still leads LlavaGuard-7B (44.16%) and QwenGuard-7B (32.76%) by 5–17 points. The one clear loss is on LlavaGuardBench, where QwenGuard-7B scores 84.57 versus SafeGuard-VL-RL's 71.78, reflecting over-specialization to its own training distribution; however, SafeGuard-VL-RL recovers 21 points on general benchmarks (57.02 vs. 35.98), indicating QwenGuard's high safety score comes at the cost of catastrophic general-capability degradation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the VLM-based safety guardrail line (LlavaGuard, QwenGuard, Llama Guard, ShieldGemma) by identifying and addressing a critical blind spot: all prior methods are trained via SFT under a single fixed policy, making them brittle to policy changes. It repurposes RL (specifically GRPO/RLVR, borrowed from the DeepSeekMath lineage) as an alternative to SFT for policy alignment, and introduces a self-recaption data construction pipeline that leverages a more permissive model (Gemma 27B) to recover suppressed unsafe semantics from a baseline VLM's output. The SafeEditBench benchmark is novel in using image-editing models (Nano Banana) to create minimally-edited safe-unsafe pairs, enabling controlled cross-policy evaluation that no prior safety benchmark supports.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that SFT-trained guardrails collapse to 0% F1 on unseen policies (Table 3) is a stark demonstration that current safety systems lack genuine policy understanding, with direct implications for any deployment where safety rules evolve. However, the practical improvement from RL is modest (SafeEditBench overall F1 rises from ~45 to ~49), and the benchmark itself is small (128 image pairs, 5 policies), limiting confidence in the generalization claims. Open questions include whether the two-stage SFT+RL recipe scales to dozens of policies or truly novel policy formulations, whether the self-recaption mechanism generalizes beyond the Qwen2.5-VL/Gemma pairing, and whether the modest RL gains persist at larger model scales or with more diverse training data.
