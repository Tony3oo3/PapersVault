## Problem

Existing 3D active mapping and reconstruction pipelines assume static scenes, making them unable to accurately capture non-cooperative moving objects that reshape the environment in real time (e.g., construction equipment, vehicles). This paper introduces the novel task of active mapping of moving objects, where an autonomous agent must plan its own observation trajectory while simultaneously compensating for the target's independent motion, a setting that has not been formally addressed or benchmarked before.

## Input / Output

The agent receives per-frame RGB-D observations and a binary segmentation mask of the target object (obtainable via background subtraction or moving-object segmentation), along with its own known camera pose from a localization system. The mask is back-projected using depth and intrinsics to yield 3D object points in the camera frame, which are then transformed into the object's local reference frame for reconstruction. The system outputs an incrementally refined 3D Gaussian Splatting model of the object in its local frame, a planned agent trajectory (sequence of motion primitives), and a continuous SE(3) pose estimate of the object, all produced online at 8 FPS.

## Method

Paparazzo is a learning-free, dual-mode framework that alternates between Object Tracking Mode (when EKF confidence is low) and Object Mapping Mode (when confidence is high), governed by state uncertainty and Normalized Innovation Squared thresholds on an SE(3) Extended Kalman Filter. In Tracking Mode, the agent keeps the object centered in the FOV at a fixed apparent size, measuring object pose via KISS-Matcher + Colored ICP registration against the accumulated Gaussian Splatting model. In Mapping Mode, candidate viewpoints are distributed in a foveated configuration around the object and propagate with its EKF-predicted trajectory; the next viewpoint is selected by minimizing a composite cost B(x,i) = −w_eig·EIG(x) + w_sync·C_sync(x,i), where EIG is the FisherRF information gain from the 3DGS model and C_sync is the temporal mismatch (in A*-planned agent steps) between the agent's arrival at the viewpoint and the object's predicted pose at that future time step. This trade-off ensures the agent does not chase maximally informative but temporally infeasible views, and the system reverts to Tracking Mode whenever the EKF detects unreliable prediction (e.g., abrupt direction changes), maintaining a reactive loop between tracking and mapping.

## Results

Across six indoor scenes and four motion patterns, Paparazzo achieves an average 3D coverage of 81.5% under Bouncing Ball motion, 70.7% under Curved Bouncing Ball, 67.7% under Forward & Backward, and 68.0% under Stop & Go, consistently outperforming the strongest baseline (Tracking-Only) by 5–7 percentage points in coverage. In terms of AUC (coverage efficiency), Paparazzo reaches 0.75 (BB), 0.67 (CBB), 0.62 (FB), and 0.65 (SG), exceeding the best baseline by at least 0.03 in every setting. Completeness (mean distance to nearest reconstructed point) ranges from 0.77 cm (BB) to 1.23 cm (FB), with Paparazzo achieving the lowest values in 3 of 4 motion types.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Paparazzo is compared against three baselines: Random Walk (RW), Random Informative Selection (RIS), and Tracking-Only (TO). Paparazzo outperforms all three baselines in coverage and AUC across every motion type. Against the strongest baseline (TO), Paparazzo gains +5.6 pp coverage on Bouncing Ball (81.5% vs 75.9%), +2.6 pp on Curved Bouncing Ball (70.7% vs 68.2%), +5.7 pp on Forward & Backward (67.7% vs 62.1%), and +7.2 pp on Stop & Go (68.0% vs 60.8%). The gap is largest under Stop & Go, where the object's intermittent pauses make passive tracking ineffective and Paparazzo's ability to exploit stationary phases for multi-view observation is most valuable. RIS, which selects informative viewpoints but ignores temporal synchronization, underperforms TO in most settings (e.g., 48.6% vs 60.8% coverage on Stop & Go), confirming that motion-aware feasibility reasoning is critical. RW, which ignores object position entirely, is the weakest baseline, achieving only ~50% coverage across all motion types.

## Relation to Prior Work

Paparazzo extends the active mapping literature—which has long assumed static scenes (frontier exploration, NBV selection, and recent Gaussian-Splatting-based methods like ActiveGS and ANM)—by introducing the non-cooperative moving-object setting. It repurposes FisherRF's information-gain criterion and SplaTAM's online Gaussian Splatting backbone, originally designed for static reconstruction, by expressing the scene representation in the object's local frame and coupling it with an EKF on SE(3) for motion prediction. The closest prior work, in-hand scanning, shares the goal of reconstructing a moving object but assumes cooperative motion; Paparazzo departs by requiring the agent to plan its own trajectory against an independently moving target.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first benchmark and protocol for active mapping of moving objects, providing a standardized evaluation that the field previously lacked. The learning-free design (EKF + FisherRF + A*) means the method generalizes to unseen objects and scenes without retraining, which is attractive for deployment. Key limitations include the assumption of a known object mask, evaluation restricted to a single rigid object in simulation, and the discrete Habitat action space; open questions include multi-object scenes, non-rigid deformation, and real-world sensor noise. The finding that unpredictable bouncing motion is actually easier to map than predictable linear motion (because wall bounces provide free viewpoint diversity) challenges the intuition that predictability always aids reconstruction.
