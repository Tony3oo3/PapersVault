## Problem

Previous data-driven floorplan generation methods (diffusion-based, GAN-based) are designed for specific conditional tasks and rely on suboptimal representations—raster-to-vector conversion, multi-stage pipelines, or fixed-size vertex outputs—that prevent a single model from generalizing across boundary, graph, number-of-rooms, and completion tasks. This paper addresses the lack of a unified representation and architecture capable of handling all these generation tasks in one framework.

## Input / Output

All conditions (site boundary polygon, room adjacency graph, target room count, or a partial floorplan) are encoded as a prefix of a single FML token sequence, with 2D coordinates discretized into 1D values z = x + y·W in a 256×256 grid. The model autoregressively generates the remaining tokens to produce a complete vector floorplan with variable-length room polygons (each room has its own vertex count), interior door lines, and a front door line—no fixed-size constraints or post-hoc room allocation steps are needed.

## Method

The core contribution is Floorplan Markup Language (FML), a tag-structured grammar that encodes floorplans and their conditions as a single token sequence using four token types (tags, coordinates, room indices, room types), casting all generation tasks into next-token prediction. The model (FMLM) is a standard autoregressive transformer with LLaMA-3 architecture, trained with cross-entropy loss applied only to floorplan tokens while conditions are randomly dropped (50% each) to support all task combinations in one checkpoint. Two key design choices enable generalization: (1) room indices are written in descending order so the model can naturally stop after generating the conditioned number of rooms, and (2) a room-permutation data augmentation teaches permutation-equivalency. At inference, constrained decoding zeros out invalid token probabilities to enforce grammar rules (e.g., exactly two door vertices, room vertices outside existing rooms), eliminating the overlapping-room and misaligned-door artifacts seen in unconstrained generation.

## Results

On the RPLAN dataset, FMLM achieves FID 14.17, GED 1.24, and IoU 97.59 (averaged over 5–8 rooms) on the most complex boundary+graph conditional task, substantially outperforming Graph2Plan (FID 22.87, GED 3.43, IoU 92.96). On graph-conditional generation, FMLM reaches FID 3.41 and GED 1.21 (all room counts), compared to HouseDiffusion's FID 29.31 and GED 1.55. For boundary-only generation, FMLM attains FID 4.61 and IoU 98.06 versus GSDiff's FID 11.26 and IoU 97.21. On unconditional generation, FMLM achieves FID 7.22 versus GSDiff's 15.02.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FMLM outperforms all four baselines across every task it is compared on. Against Graph2Plan on boundary+graph generation, FMLM reduces FID from 22.87 to 14.17, GED from 3.43 to 1.24, and improves IoU from 92.96 to 97.59 (Table 5); on boundary-only generation, FID drops from 34.20 to 6.51 and IoU rises from 95.87 to 97.86 (Table 2). Against GSDiff on boundary generation, FMLM achieves FID 4.61 vs. 11.26 and IoU 98.06 vs. 97.21 (Table 3), and on unconditional generation FID 7.22 vs. 15.02. On graph-conditional generation, FMLM beats HouseDiffusion on FID (3.41 vs. 29.31) and GED (1.21 vs. 1.55) averaged over all room counts, though it slightly underperforms HouseDiffusion on GED for 8-room plans (1.96 vs. 1.87), likely due to fewer training samples in that category. FMLM also surpasses HouseGAN++ by a wide margin on graph generation (FID 3.41 vs. 48.44, GED 1.21 vs. 2.57). Notably, FMLM is the only method that handles all task types—unconditional, boundary, number, graph, boundary+graph, and completion—within a single model, whereas each baseline is restricted to one or two specific tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant diffusion-based and GAN-based paradigms for vector floorplan generation (HouseDiffusion, GSDiff, HouseGAN++) by repurposing the autoregressive transformer architecture from large language models, paired with a novel markup-language representation (FML) that encodes rooms, doors, boundaries, and adjacency graphs as a single tagged token sequence. Rather than building on a specific prior architecture, it reframes the entire generation problem as next-token prediction, sidestepping the multi-stage pipelines and fixed-vertex-count constraints that limit generalization in prior diffusion and GAN methods. The LLaMA-3 transformer backbone and sinusoidal positional encoding for coordinates are borrowed directly from the LLM literature, while the constrained decoding mechanism draws on grammar-guided generation ideas.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unification of boundary-conditional, graph-conditional, number-conditional, completion, and editing tasks into a single autoregressive model is practically significant: it eliminates the need for task-specific architectures and multi-stage pipelines, and the markup representation naturally supports interactive editing and future integration with LLMs for natural-language-driven design. Key limitations remain: the model is restricted to single-story floorplans, and the RPLAN dataset (80k samples) is relatively small, so scalability to larger, more diverse architectural vocabularies is untested. Open questions include whether the FML grammar can be extended to multi-story buildings, whether the approach transfers to commercial or industrial layouts, and how well it generalizes beyond the RPLAN distribution.
