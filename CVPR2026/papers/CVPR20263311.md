## Problem

Existing VLA models for end-to-end autonomous driving rely on either autoregressive decoding (sequential, slow, exposure-bias-prone) or diffusion-based planners (parallel but lacking explicit reasoning interpretability). Discrete flow matching offers fully parallel bidirectional denoising with a tunable coarse-to-fine compute-accuracy trade-off, yet it remains unexplored for VLA policies in driving. The paper tackles three concrete barriers to applying DFM here: the lack of road-scene competence in generic VLM backbones, the inability of standard text tokenizers to encode metric structure for high-precision numerical regression, and the absence of explicit safety/comfort enforcement in closed-loop control.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a single front-view camera image (aspect-ratio preserved, zero-padded to 384×384, encoded by SigLIP into 576 visual tokens), a natural-language navigation command with a system prompt, and scalar ego-vehicle states (position, heading, velocity, acceleration). Continuous scalars are discretized into a 20,001-entry uniform codebook over [−100, 100] at 0.01 resolution, expanding the Janus-1.5B vocabulary to 122,401 tokens. The output is a sequence of 8 waypoints spanning 4 seconds, generated via parallel denoising where all token positions are updated simultaneously at each step rather than sequentially.

## Method

WAM-Flow formulates trajectory planning as discrete flow matching over a structured token space, adapting the Janus-1.5B backbone from causal autoregressive decoding to non-causal parallel denoising. A metric-aligned numerical tokenizer discretizes continuous scalars into a shared codebook and learns embeddings via a triplet-margin ranking loss so that Euclidean distances in latent space monotonically reflect scalar differences, enabling stable coarse-to-fine refinement. The flow objective uses a geometry-aware Gibbs probability path (with coordinate-specific dissimilarities: tokenizer-induced for numbers, circular for angles, semantic for text) realized by a CTMC whose transition rate steers states toward the target, trained with a conditional cross-entropy loss. A four-stage curriculum—numerical embedding pretraining on nuPlan, 6.5M-sample VQA pretraining for road-scene competence, supervised flow fine-tuning, and simulator-guided GRPO with a composite safety×performance reward—progressively aligns the model. At inference, Euler-discretized parallel denoising with 1–10 steps provides a flexible speed-accuracy trade-off (89.1 PDMS at 1 step, 90.3 at 5 steps on NAVSIM-v1).

## Results

On NAVSIM-v1, WAM-Flow achieves a PDMS of 90.3 with 5-step denoising and 89.1 with 1-step denoising, outperforming the strongest baseline RecogDrive (89.6 PDMS) by 0.7 points. On the more comprehensive NAVSIM-v2 benchmark, WAM-Flow attains an EPDMS of 84.7, surpassing RecogDrive (83.6) and Artemis (83.1). The 1-step inference runs in 0.09 s, a 4.67× speedup over RecogDrive's 0.42 s, while 5-step inference (0.48 s) matches RecogDrive's latency at higher accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WAM-Flow outperforms all 11 baselines on NAVSIM-v1 PDMS, with the largest margins over end-to-end non-VLA methods (VADv2: +9.4, TransFuser: +6.3) and a clear lead over the closest VLA competitors RecogDrive (+0.7) and AutoVLA (+1.2). On safety-critical sub-metrics, WAM-Flow achieves the best No-Collision rate (99.2) and Drivable Area Compliance (98.3), exceeding RecogDrive by 1.0 and 0.8 points respectively. On NAVSIM-v2, WAM-Flow leads in EPDMS (84.7 vs. 83.6 for RecogDrive) and in Driving Direction Compliance (99.5) and Lane Keeping (97.4), though it trails RecogDrive on Extended Comfort (73.9 vs. 87.7). In terms of efficiency, the 1-step WAM-Flow inference (0.09 s) is 4.67× faster than RecogDrive (0.42 s) while still achieving 89.1 PDMS, and the 5-step setting (0.48 s) matches RecogDrive's latency at higher accuracy. Notably, WAM-Flow uses only a single front-view camera, whereas several baselines rely on 3–6 cameras or LiDAR inputs.

## Relation to Prior Work

WAM-Flow adapts the Janus-1.5B autoregressive VLM backbone into a non-causal discrete flow matching model, drawing on the CTMC-based probability transport framework from Gat et al. (NeurIPS 2024) that was previously applied to language models (LLaDA, DREAM-7B) and multimodal generation (FUDOKI) but not to driving VLA. It departs from both autoregressive VLA planners (EMMA, DrivingGPT, AutoVLA) that commit to tokens sequentially, and diffusion-based planners (DiffusionDrive, RecogDrive) that denoise in continuous latent space, by performing fully parallel, bidirectional denoising over a structured discrete token space. The GRPO alignment stage extends the DeepSeek-R1-style group-relative policy optimization to the discrete flow matching setting, which the authors note is the first such application in autonomous driving.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes discrete flow matching as a viable third paradigm alongside autoregressive and diffusion-based VLA for end-to-end driving, with the practical advantage of a tunable compute-accuracy trade-off (1-step at 0.09s vs. 5-step at 0.48s) that no prior method offers. The 4.67× inference speedup over RecogDrive at competitive accuracy, achieved with a smaller 1.5B backbone versus 8B, suggests the paradigm could enable real-time deployment on resource-constrained hardware. Open questions include whether the metric-aligned tokenizer and GRPO alignment generalize to multi-agent or long-horizon planning, whether the coarse-to-fine property holds under distribution shift, and whether the approach scales to higher-resolution or multi-view inputs without the single-front-camera limitation.
