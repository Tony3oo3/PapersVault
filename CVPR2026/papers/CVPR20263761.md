## Problem

Diffusion-based neural rendering methods produce photorealistic images from G-buffers but suffer from two critical limitations: the iterative denoising process (20–50 steps) introduces latency incompatible with real-time or interactive use, and the inherent stochasticity of sampling causes flickering and temporal inconsistencies that fail studio-quality standards. No prior method achieves both single-step inference speed and deterministic, physically faithful rendering from structured G-buffer inputs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263761/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The forward renderer consumes a set of spatially aligned G-buffers (albedo, normal, depth, and a three-channel Disney BRDF material buffer) at 512×512, a Reinhard tone-mapped environment map rotated into camera view space, and optionally sparse path-traced keyframes for guidance. All inputs are encoded into a shared latent space via a 3D causal VAE before processing. The inverse renderer takes a single RGB image plus a text prompt specifying the target intrinsic (e.g., 'albedo', 'normal') and outputs the corresponding G-buffer channel, reusing the same frozen backbone.

## Method

RenderFlow reformulates neural rendering as a single-step conditional flow matching problem, learning a velocity field that transports the albedo latent directly to the fully-shaded image latent in one evaluation, rather than iteratively denoising from Gaussian noise. The architecture is built on the Wan2.1 video DiT backbone: spatially aligned G-buffer latents are added element-wise to albedo tokens, the environment map is injected per-block via adaptive normalization (FiLM-style scale and shift), and an optional Keyframe Adapter adds a cross-attention branch with RoPE-encoded temporal distances and LoRA in the feedforward layer. Training uses a stochastic bridge matching objective in latent space (Brownian bridge interpolation with σ=0.005) combined with pixel-space LPIPS and gradient losses for perceptual fidelity and high-frequency detail. At inference, the final latent is recovered in closed form as ẑ₁ = z_t + v_θ(z_t, t)(1−t), yielding deterministic output with zero variance across runs. For inverse rendering, the frozen forward backbone is augmented with LoRA on self-attention, a prompt-conditioned cross-attention branch, and per-intrinsic MLP heads, requiring training of only adapter parameters.

## Results

On the 512×512 test set, RenderFlow without keyframe guidance achieves PSNR 24.214, SSIM 0.874, and LPIPS 0.113, outperforming both diffusion-based baselines (RGB-X: 20.984/0.793/0.165; DiffusionRenderer: 23.758/0.863/0.128) while rendering a frame in ~0.19 s on a single RTX 4090—roughly 10× faster than RGB-X (~2.19 s) and 7× faster than DiffusionRenderer (~1.40 s). With sparse keyframe guidance enabled, the full model reaches PSNR 26.663, SSIM 0.883, and LPIPS 0.101 at ~0.24 s per frame. A key practical advantage is determinism: RenderFlow exhibits zero variance across repeated inference runs, whereas both diffusion baselines show significant run-to-run variance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RenderFlow is compared against two state-of-the-art diffusion-based neural rendering methods, both fine-tuned on the same UE5 dataset for fair comparison. Against RGB-X (950M params, 50 sampling steps), RenderFlow without keyframes wins on every metric—PSNR by +3.2 dB (24.214 vs 20.984), SSIM by +0.081, and LPIPS by −0.052—while being approximately 11× faster (0.19 s vs 2.19 s). Against DiffusionRenderer (1.7B params, 30 sampling steps), the margins are tighter but still favorable: PSNR +0.46 dB, SSIM +0.011, LPIPS −0.015, with a ~7× speedup (0.19 s vs 1.40 s). Enabling keyframe guidance widens all gaps substantially (e.g., PSNR 26.663 vs 23.758 for DRender) at only a 0.05 s cost. A distinctive qualitative advantage is determinism: RenderFlow produces identical outputs across runs (zero variance in Fig. 6), whereas both diffusion baselines exhibit visible flickering and metric variance, making RenderFlow more suitable for production pipelines requiring temporal stability.

## Relation to Prior Work

RenderFlow departs from the dominant diffusion-based neural rendering paradigm (RGB-X, DiffusionRenderer) by replacing the Gaussian noise prior with the albedo buffer as the flow's starting point, enabling a single-step conditional flow rather than 20–50 iterative denoising steps. The mathematical foundation draws on rectified flow and stochastic interpolants (bridge matching), while the architecture repurposes the pretrained Wan2.1 video DiT backbone and borrows VACE's progressive inference and decoupled attribute embedder design. The key conceptual shift is treating rendering as a residual flow from a structured, low-level signal (albedo) to a high-level target (shaded image), rather than a generative process from unstructured noise, which preserves geometric integrity by construction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10× speedup over diffusion baselines (0.19 s vs. ~2.19 s per 512×512 frame) combined with zero inference variance makes neural rendering viable for interactive and production pipelines where stochastic flickering is unacceptable. However, the method is validated only on synthetic Unreal Engine 5 data at 512×512 resolution, and the VAE encoder/decoder accounts for ~90% of inference time, limiting the practical speedup for higher-resolution or real-time deployment. Open questions include whether the albedo-to-shaded flow generalizes to real-world captures, whether the tone-mapped LDR environment map assumption holds for extreme lighting, and whether the keyframe guidance mechanism scales to longer sequences without accumulating drift.
