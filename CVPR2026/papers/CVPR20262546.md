## Problem

While procedural data generated from formal grammars has been shown to improve large language models, it remains unclear whether vision transformers can acquire useful inductive biases from data that is entirely non-visual and non-semantic. Existing work on synthetic or abstract images for vision still relies on 2D visual structure, leaving open the question of whether a ViT can 'learn to see' from purely symbolic sequences with no correspondence to image properties.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262546/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

During the procedural warm-up phase, the ViT consumes fixed-length sequences (N = H×W tokens) of abstract symbols drawn from a 128-token vocabulary, with the standard patch-embedding layer replaced by a frozen random embedding lookup table and positional encodings held constant; the model predicts masked tokens (50% of structurally informative positions). In the subsequent standard phase, the token embeddings and prediction head are discarded, and the full ViT processes 224×224 RGB images (ViT-B/16) or 32×32/64×64 images (ViT-T/16) to produce class labels across benchmarks ranging from CIFAR-10 to ImageNet-1K.

## Method

The method is a two-stage pipeline: (1) a brief procedural warm-up in which a ViT is trained for masked-token prediction on sequences generated from formal grammars spanning the Chomsky hierarchy (regular WW, context-free k-DYCK, context-sensitive k-DYCK SHUFFLE), with the patch embedding and positional encoding frozen so that only attention and MLP layers are updated; (2) standard supervised image training where the procedural-specific components are removed and the full architecture is trained with conventional hyperparameters. The key design choice is freezing the embedding layer to prevent the model from solving the objective through input representations, forcing it to encode the data-generating algorithm (e.g., stack-based bracket matching) in its internal weights. Ablations show the benefit depends on precise hierarchical structure in the data (shuffling tokens eliminates gains), manifests in both attention and MLP layers, and is concentrated in the final third of transformer blocks—contrasting with standard visual pretraining that primarily shapes early layers. Allocating just 1% of the total training budget to procedural data yields a +1.7% top-1 accuracy gain on ImageNet-1K, equivalent to saving 28% of the image data.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K, procedural warm-up (k-DYCK, k=64) achieves 79.21% top-1 accuracy, a +1.72% absolute improvement over default random initialization (77.49%). Across six benchmarks (ImageNet-1K, Tiny-ImageNet, Food-101, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, STL-10), the method yields an average +3.4% absolute gain over default initialization. Notably, allocating just 1% of the total training budget (~3.8M symbolic examples) to procedural data matches the performance of full ImageNet-1K pretraining while using 28% fewer natural images (~108M fewer).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares procedural warm-up against three initialization/pretraining baselines: default random initialization, Mimetic initialization (structured attention weights), and FractalDB warm-up (procedural visual data). Procedural warm-up outperforms default random initialization on all six benchmarks, with the largest gains on STL-10 (+5.96%) and Food-101 (+4.95%). Against Mimetic initialization, procedural warm-up wins on 5 of 6 datasets (e.g., +0.53% on ImageNet-1K, +1.26% on CIFAR-100), with a near-tie on CIFAR-10 (92.81% vs. 92.89%). Against FractalDB, procedural warm-up dominates on every benchmark, with particularly large margins on CIFAR-100 (+7.37%) and STL-10 (+7.86%), despite FractalDB containing actual visual structure. This suggests that non-visual, symbolic data provides a richer and more transferable inductive bias than handcrafted abstract images designed to mimic natural image statistics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the procedural pretraining paradigm from LLMs (Jiang et al. 2026; Hu et al. 2025) to vision transformers, adapting formal-grammar-based symbolic data (k-DYCK, k-DYCK SHUFFLE, WW) as a warm-up stage before standard image training. It departs from prior synthetic-visual-data approaches (FractalDB, fractals, wave patterns) by entirely forgoing 2D visual structure, and from structured initialization methods (Mimetic, I-BiT) by acting on the full network (attention + MLP) rather than only attention weights. The key conceptual move is treating the problem as one of instilling generic computational priors—stack manipulation, hierarchical composition—rather than imitating any visual property.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 1% of the training budget on purely symbolic data substitutes for 28% of ImageNet-1K images offers a practical data-efficiency lever, and the observation that benefits concentrate in late layers (contradicting the standard view that early layers carry the most transferable visual features) suggests a qualitatively distinct training signal. Open questions include whether the effect generalizes beyond standard ViTs (Swin, XCiT), whether a closed-form initialization can capture the learned weight structure, and whether the late-layer mechanism interacts differently with visual features during fine-tuning. Limitations include evaluation on a single architecture family and classification-only benchmarks.
