## Problem

Large Vision-Language Models suffer from object hallucination that worsens as generation length increases, a phenomenon termed long-term decay where visual grounding weakens and language priors dominate. Existing training-free methods such as Contrastive Decoding (VCD, ICD, M3ID) fail to sustain visual grounding across long sequences and require two forward passes per decoding step, doubling inference latency. No prior training-free approach simultaneously addresses both the long-term decay problem and the inference efficiency cost.

## Input / Output

FLB operates on standard RGB images paired with a text prompt (e.g., 'Please describe this image in detail') and produces a free-form text caption with reduced object hallucination. The method intervenes purely at the logit level during autoregressive decoding: it stores the first token's logit vector once and adds a temporally weighted copy to every subsequent token prediction, with an adaptive plausibility threshold (β=0.1) that zeros out tokens whose original probability falls below a fraction of the maximum. No modification to the model weights, architecture, or input preprocessing is required.

## Method

FLB exploits the observation that the first generated token's logit is the most visually grounded because it is produced immediately after the image tokens, before RoPE-induced positional drift weakens cross-modal attention. The first-token logit l₀ is stored once and added to each subsequent decoding step with a temporal weight w_t = γ(1 − e^{−λt}) that grows with sequence position to counteract long-term decay. An adaptive plausibility constraint, borrowed from VCD, restricts the boosted distribution to tokens whose original probability exceeds β times the maximum, preventing implausible token selection. The mechanism yields two complementary effects: a direct visual grounding effect where the stored logit acts as a persistent visual anchor reinforcing ground-truth object logits, and an implicit 'The' effect where boosting the sentence-initial article 'The' reduces prediction entropy and encourages the model to reference previously grounded entities rather than sampling novel hallucinated objects. Unlike CD methods requiring dual forward passes, FLB uses only a single forward pass per step with negligible overhead.

## Results

On the AMBER benchmark with LLaVA-1.5, FLB reduces CHAIR from 11.5 (baseline) to 6.1, Hal from 48.9 to 31.6, and Cog from 4.6 to 2.7, while maintaining Cover at 50.4 (vs. 50.1 baseline). On InstructBLIP, FLB achieves CHAIR 9.0 and Hal 43.8, outperforming all contrastive decoding baselines. On the CHAIR benchmark, FLB reduces CHAIR_s from 57.5 to 43.5 (LLaVA-1.5) and from 59.0 to 52.5 (InstructBLIP), with CHAIR_i dropping from 17.3 to 12.0 and 18.5 to 15.8 respectively. FLB also improves on MMHalBench (2.230 vs. 1.944 baseline) and ConvBench win rates across all three turns.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLB outperforms all three contrastive decoding baselines (VCD, ICD, M3ID) on every hallucination metric across both LLaVA-1.5 and InstructBLIP. On LLaVA-1.5 AMBER, FLB achieves CHAIR 6.1, beating the best baseline ICD (9.1) by 33% and the baseline (11.5) by 47%; on Hal, FLB (31.6) surpasses ICD (40.6) by 9 points. On InstructBLIP, FLB (CHAIR 9.0) beats VCD (10.2) and M3ID (11.5), while ICD actually worsens performance (12.1) relative to baseline. FLB also leads on the CHAIR benchmark, reducing CHAIR_s by 14 points (LLaVA-1.5) and 6.5 points (InstructBLIP) over baseline, whereas ICD and M3ID degrade InstructBLIP's CHAIR_s to 65.7 and 69.8 respectively. Crucially, FLB achieves these gains with a single forward pass per decoding step, whereas VCD, ICD, and M3ID each require two forward passes, roughly doubling inference time.

## Relation to Prior Work

FLB sits within the training-free hallucination mitigation lineage for LVLMs, specifically the contrastive decoding (CD) family (VCD, ICD, M3ID), but departs from it in a fundamental way: rather than requiring a second forward pass on a perturbed input to compute a contrastive logit, FLB reuses the single first-token logit as a persistent visual anchor across all subsequent decoding steps. It borrows the adaptive plausibility threshold mechanism from VCD [14] to prevent implausible token selection, but the core decoding rule is entirely different. The paper explicitly positions FLB as addressing the 'long-term decay' problem that prior CD methods fail to resolve, where RoPE-induced positional drift causes visual grounding to weaken as generation progresses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FLB's practical appeal lies in achieving state-of-the-art hallucination reduction with near-zero inference overhead, making it immediately deployable in latency-sensitive multimodal systems where CD methods' 2× cost is prohibitive. The discovery that the 'The' token effect (implicit visual referencing) contributes more to hallucination reduction than the direct visual grounding effect the method was designed for opens an interesting question about how linguistic initialization shapes cross-modal attention. However, the authors acknowledge that FLB does not eliminate the underlying RoPE-based decay mechanism and cannot adapt to evolving visual semantics mid-sentence, leaving room for decay-aware positional or token-level grounding approaches as future directions.
