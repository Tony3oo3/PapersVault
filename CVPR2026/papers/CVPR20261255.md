## Problem

Vision-language-action (VLA) models excel at following external language commands but remain largely unaware of in-situ guides—text and symbols physically inscribed on objects (e.g., 'PULL', 'Squeeze and Turn')—that humans intuitively rely on for safe and correct manipulation. No standardized benchmark exists to measure this capability, leaving the gap between what robots can perceive on objects and what they can act upon unquantified.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261255/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives multi-view RGB images at 224×224 from three cameras (left-shoulder, wrist, right-shoulder) plus a dedicated guide-perception camera, along with semantic segmentation masks isolating the in-situ guides, depth maps, and proprioceptive states (joint angles, velocities, gripper status). In the LI-VLA condition, an additional language instruction string is appended, generated at evaluation time by an external VLM that parses the visual guide from the observation. The policy outputs an 8-dimensional action vector (7-DoF target joint positions + gripper command) at 10 Hz, and task success is a binary determination based on whether a target joint exceeds a calibrated threshold within a 10-second episode.

## Method

INSIGHT Bench formalizes in-situ guide grounding through a taxonomy of five information types (contact affordance, in-part contact specification, action directional, procedural guidance, target disambiguation) instantiated across three manipulation tasks (Cabinet, Door, Bottle) totaling 783 procedurally generated scenarios. The key technical mechanism is programmatic guide-to-physics grounding: each visual guide is coupled to a corresponding physical constraint in simulation (e.g., a 'squeeze' guide locks the cap's revolute joint until the end-effector applies the specified force and orientation), transforming the task from visual pattern matching into causal reasoning. A scalable, skill-based data generation pipeline (GRASP → ROTATE → PULL primitives executed via CuRobo motion planning) produces 14,076 demonstration trajectories with extensive domain randomization and guide spatial randomization, stored in LeRobot format. The empirical study fine-tunes three VLA models (π0, SmolVLA, GR00T N1.5) under three conditions—no-guide (NG-VLA), guided-visual (G-VLA), and language-instructed (LI-VLA)—revealing that G-VLA policies fail to outperform NG-VLA baselines while LI-VLA policies achieve substantial gains, pinpointing the bottleneck to visual guide perception rather than action execution.

## Results

On the INSIGHT Bench overall task success rate, G-VLA policies (fine-tuned with visual guides) show no clear improvement over NG-VLA baselines: π0 rises only marginally from 12.1% to 14.1%, GR00T N1.5 from 17.2% to 18.5%, and SmolVLA actually drops from 10.1% to 7.4%. In contrast, LI-VLA policies (same guided data plus language instructions) achieve substantially higher success: GR00T N1.5 reaches 24.3% and π0 reaches 18.5%, confirming the bottleneck is visual guide interpretation rather than action execution. Per-task, G-VLA excels on Cabinet (target disambiguation; GR00T >3× over NG-VLA) but fails on Bottle-Standard (directional) and Door (procedural), where LI-VLA is consistently best. Real-world validation with GR00T confirms the gap: LI-VLA outperforms G-VLA on Bottle-Std (65% vs 45%) while G-VLA slightly edges LI-VLA on Cabinet (50% vs 45%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three VLA models—π0 (PaliGemma 3B backbone), GR00T N1.5 (Eagle-2.5 2.1B backbone), and SmolVLA (SmolVLM-2 0.5B backbone)—each under three fine-tuning conditions (NG-VLA, G-VLA, LI-VLA) on the same INSIGHT Bench tasks. The central finding is that visual guides (G-VLA) do not reliably improve manipulation: π0 gains only 2 points, GR00T gains 1.3 points, and SmolVLA loses 2.7 points relative to NG-VLA, suggesting guides act as visual distractors for smaller backbones. LI-VLA consistently outperforms both other settings, with GR00T N1.5 achieving the highest overall success (24.3%) and π0 reaching 18.5%, demonstrating that the models can execute the required actions when guide semantics are decoded into language. On the Cabinet task specifically, G-VLA outperforms NG-VLA by a large margin (GR00T >3×), showing that simple target-disambiguation cues are groundable visually, while on Door and Bottle-Standard tasks, visual guides underperform the no-guide baseline. GR00T N1.5 is the most robust model across settings, attributed to its Eagle-2.5 backbone's enhanced grounding capabilities, whereas SmolVLA struggles across nearly all conditions, particularly failing Bottle-Squeeze entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant VLA paradigm, where models follow externally supplied language commands or human demonstrations, by introducing the problem of grounding visual symbols and text physically embedded on objects (in-situ guides). It builds on the Isaac Lab simulation framework for environment construction and CuRobo for skill-based trajectory generation, but its conceptual contribution is orthogonal to prior visual prompting or goal-image conditioning work, which relies on externally supplied cues rather than object-attached information. In the manipulation benchmark lineage (LIBERO, CALVIN, ManiSkill2, ARNOLD), all prior benchmarks condition on instructions external to the scene; INSIGHT Bench is the first to couple on-object visual guides with guide-conditioned physical constraints, creating a new evaluation axis for compositional visual-to-action reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that current VLA models (π0, GR00T N1.5, SmolVLA) fail to ground in-situ visual guides—training with guides yields no improvement over training without them, and guides can even act as visual distractors—while the same information delivered as language instructions substantially improves performance. This localizes the bottleneck to visual perception and semantic decoding rather than action execution, pointing to a concrete research gap: building models that natively interpret object-embedded text and symbols without external language scaffolding. Limitations include the narrow task scope (4 task types, 37–102 assets), simulation-centric evaluation with only a small real-world validation (15 trajectories per variation), and reliance on a single external VLM for the LI-VLA instruction generation, which may overestimate the practical benefit of language decoding.
