## Problem

Despite strong performance of Video-LLMs on video question answering, their internal processing mechanisms remain largely unexplored. Unlike image-level VLMs, where interpretability studies have revealed staged information flow and attention patterns, the high-dimensional video domain lacks systematic analysis of how video tokens interact with language tokens across transformer layers. This paper addresses the gap by asking: how do Video-LLMs internally extract, process, and reason over video content to produce answers?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260729/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on standard Video-LLM pipelines: 32 uniformly sampled video frames (224×224 to 512×512 resolution) encoded by a visual encoder (e.g., CLIP-L-14) and projected into the LLM's token space, concatenated with text question tokens. The 'output' is not a generated answer per se but a set of performance scores (accuracy on Video-MME, MVBench, EgoSchema) measured under controlled attention perturbations, plus a reduced FLOPs configuration. The unusual aspect is that the method modifies the attention mask at specific layer ranges rather than altering inputs or outputs, making it a purely internal diagnostic tool.

## Method

The paper decomposes the standard causal attention in Video-LLMs into three components—Language-to-Video (text attending to video tokens), Video Temporal (across-frame attention), and Video Spatial (within-frame attention)—and designs three corresponding attention knockouts that selectively zero out each component. These knockouts are applied under two settings: a global setting (blocking LV-K beyond a cutoff layer to probe staged processing, or blocking one type across all layers to compare global contributions) and a fine-grained setting (applying a knockout within a sliding window of 4 layers to identify critical layers). Experiments span five models (LongVA-7B, InternVideo2.5-8B, LLaVA-Video-7B, LLaVA-OneVision-7B, LLaVA-NeXT-Video-32B) across three benchmarks with over 500 data points. Key findings reveal a two-stage pattern (video extraction in early ~60% of layers, reasoning in later layers), dominance of language-to-video attention over temporal/spatial self-attention, and a small set of critical intermediate layers. These insights are then leveraged into a vision-token early-exit strategy that drops video tokens after layer 18 and suppresses temporal attention in non-critical early layers, reducing attention FLOPs to ~38% with minimal accuracy loss.

## Results

The study reveals a clear two-stage processing pattern in Video-LLMs: blocking language-to-video attention beyond 60% of layers causes negligible performance drop across all five tested models (LongVA-7B, InternVideo2.5-8B, LLaVA-Video-7B, LLaVA-OneVision-7B, LLaVA-NeXT-Video-32B) on Video-MME, MVBench, and EgoSchema. Globally, a full language-to-video knockout causes a substantially larger performance drop than temporal or spatial knockouts, confirming that spatial-temporal modeling relies primarily on language-guided retrieval. The proposed efficiency strategy (vision token early exit after layer 18 + restricting temporal attention in layers 1–8) reduces attention FLOPs from 100% to ~37.5–37.6% with minimal accuracy loss (e.g., LLaVA-Video: 62.4→60.0 on Video-MME, 61.1→60.8 on MVBench, 58.4→58.2 on EgoSchema).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical interpretability study rather than a method comparison paper, so there are no competing baselines in the traditional sense. The key internal comparisons show that language-to-video attention knockout (LV-K) consistently produces a much larger performance drop than video temporal knockout (VT-K) or video spatial knockout (VS-K) across all models, benchmarks, and frame lengths (8–64 frames), indicating that current Video-LLMs rely far more on cross-modal retrieval than on intra-video self-attention. In the fine-grained setting, specific intermediate layers (e.g., layers 12–16 in a 28-layer model) act as critical outliers where knockout causes substantial degradation, while most other layers contribute minimally. The efficiency strategy built on these findings (exit + window) achieves ~62% FLOPs reduction with only 0.2–2.4 point accuracy loss, outperforming the simpler exit-only strategy (35.7% FLOPs reduction, 0.4–1.0 point loss) in computational savings at a small additional accuracy cost. Results are consistent across five models of varying size (7B to 32B) and three benchmarks spanning short, medium, and long video durations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the attention knockout technique from Geva et al. [14]—originally developed for dissecting factual recall in autoregressive LLMs—to the Video-LLM setting by decomposing causal attention into three targeted types (language-to-video, video temporal, video spatial). It extends the growing interpretability literature on image-level MLLMs (which has examined information flow, logit distributions, and visual token redundancy) to the higher-dimensional video domain, where prior work has been largely absent. The study also complements the recent behavioral analysis of Video-LLMs by [42], which documented external performance patterns (e.g., sensitivity to language vs. video perturbations) but left internal mechanisms unexplored.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that Video-LLMs rely predominantly on language-guided retrieval rather than expensive video-video self-attention for spatial-temporal modeling directly informs efficient inference strategies, demonstrated by a ~60% reduction in attention FLOPs with minimal accuracy loss via vision token early exit. However, the critical-layer identification is task-dependent and the current efficiency strategy uses a fixed layer subset, leaving open the question of whether a dynamic, task-adaptive knockout schedule can further improve the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff. Broader open questions include whether the two-stage pattern and language-dominance finding generalize to architectures with different video encoders, longer context windows, or video generation tasks.
