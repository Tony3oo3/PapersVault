## Problem

Medical image segmentation models suffer from poor generalization across unseen domains due to confounders such as equipment differences, imaging modes, and procedure artifacts. Existing domain generalization strategies (adversarial training, feature disentanglement, meta-learning) do not explicitly model or remove these confounders, leaving spurious correlations that degrade performance on new clinical sites. This paper asks whether causal inference can be integrated with vision-language models to explicitly eliminate confounder effects while preserving anatomical structure.

## Input / Output

The method takes 224×224 RGB endoscopic images from multiple source domains (bronchoscopy, laryngoscopy, laparoscopy) and produces pixel-wise segmentation masks over K lesion classes (Polyps, Tumors, Inflam, Nodules, Cyst). Beyond the image, the method consumes two sets of text prompts: class-level prompts (e.g., 'A Polyps in an endoscopic image') for lesion region selection, and domain-level prompts (e.g., 'An endoscopy image with blur') to build a 12-entry confounder dictionary. The visual encoder outputs a dense H×W×d feature map, from which only the top-α fraction of spatial locations (ranked by cross-modal cosine similarity) are retained as the compact representation F ∈ R^{N×d} fed into the causal intervention module.

## Method

MCDRL has two core components. First, Multimodal Target Region Selection (MTRS) computes cosine similarity between CLIP's dense visual features and class text embeddings, then masks out all but the top-α spatial locations to form a compact, lesion-focused representation. Second, Causal-Driven Representation Learning (CDRL) constructs a confounder dictionary Z ∈ R^{M×d} (M=12) from text prompts describing five categories of domain variation (view quality, illumination, imaging technique, distance, surface interference), and applies a cross-attention operation A(F, Z) as a tractable approximation of the do-operator E_z[A(F,z)], effectively marginalizing over confounders to yield a domain-invariant feature F_ca. Training combines a pixel-wise cross-entropy segmentation loss, a causal loss that aligns the pooled intervened feature with class-domain composite text embeddings, and a contrastive loss that fine-tunes CLIP's vision encoder for disease-class discrimination. The causal intervention module is activated after the 10th epoch in a 50-epoch progressive schedule, and the entire pipeline is evaluated under a multi-source-to-single-target domain generalization protocol across five clinical sites.

## Results

Using the ViT-L/14 backbone, MCDRL achieves an average mDice of 81.6% across five clinical sites, representing a 6.5% improvement over the CLIP baseline (75.1%) and a 2.0% gain over the strongest competitor BiomedCoOp (79.4%). The method consistently leads on every individual site, with the largest per-site Dice of 84.1% on Site A (BM-BronchoLC) and the smallest of 79.6% on Site D (ETIS). Across lesion types, MCDRL reaches 82.4% average mDice, with the largest gain on nodules (+11.4% over baseline) and cysts (+5.2%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MCDRL outperforms all three baselines (CLIP Baseline, StyLIP, BiomedCoOp) on every site and every backbone tested. Against the strongest competitor BiomedCoOp, MCDRL gains 2.0% mDice with ViT-L/14 (81.6 vs. 79.4), 2.0% with ViT-B/16 (80.0 vs. 78.0), and 1.9% with ResNet-50 (78.6 vs. 76.7). The gap over StyLIP is larger, at 3.3% mDice (ViT-L/14), and the gap over the raw CLIP baseline reaches 6.5%. On the lesion-type breakdown (Table 2), MCDRL leads BiomedCoOp by 3.9% average mDice (82.4 vs. 78.5), with the most pronounced advantage on nodules (84.9 vs. 78.5, +6.4%) and cysts (80.8 vs. 75.6, +5.2%). No comparison shows MCDRL losing or tying on any reported metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) VLM-based medical imaging, where CLIP and its successors (StyLIP, BiomedCoOp) have been adapted for zero-shot or few-shot segmentation; (2) causal inference for robust recognition, where do-calculus and confounder marginalization (Pearl, Arjovsky/IRM) provide a formal language for removing spurious correlations; and (3) domain generalization for medical segmentation, which has relied on adversarial training, feature disentanglement, and meta-learning. MCDRL departs from prior DG methods by explicitly constructing a text-based confounder dictionary via CLIP's text encoder and implementing the causal do-operation as a cross-attention marginalization over that dictionary, rather than learning domain-invariance implicitly through adversarial or meta-learning objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work offers a theoretically grounded (causal) recipe for domain-invariant medical segmentation that is relatively simple to implement—12 hand-crafted text prompts plus a cross-attention module—making it accessible to clinical imaging groups without large-scale domain-specific pretraining. However, several open questions remain: the confounder dictionary is manually designed and limited to endoscopic/cavity imaging, so its transferability to radiology, pathology, or other modalities is untested; the approximation in Eq. 5 (averaging features before the prediction head rather than averaging predictions) is a computational shortcut whose fidelity to true interventional inference is not formally bounded; and the causal framing, while elegant, is implemented through a standard cross-attention mechanism whose 'causal' properties are not validated beyond correlation-based metrics. Whether the approach scales to open-vocabulary or fully automatic confounder discovery would be a natural next step.
