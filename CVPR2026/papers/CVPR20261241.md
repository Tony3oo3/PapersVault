## Problem

Existing diffusion-based inverse problem solvers (DPS, DAPS, DDRM, etc.) combine the observation likelihood and the diffusion prior through ad hoc score approximations that accumulate errors along the sampling trajectory, yielding suboptimal MAP estimates and poor measurement consistency. No prior method provides a principled optimization framework that provably converges to a fixed point while faithfully enforcing data fidelity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261241/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a noisy observation vector y, a differentiable forward model A(·) (linear or nonlinear, e.g., downsampling, blur, phase measurement), and a pretrained diffusion model (pixel-space or latent-space, 256×256 RGB). It outputs a single reconstructed image x₀ representing the MAP estimate, evaluated on FFHQ and ImageNet validation sets. Unlike posterior sampling methods that produce multiple samples, DDiff returns one solution per run, and the residual ‖y − A(x)‖² is reported as a data-consistency diagnostic.

## Method

DDiff formulates the MAP problem as an ADMM-style dual ascent with three alternating updates: a linearized gradient step for data fidelity (x-update), a denoising step using the pretrained score model (z-update), and a standard dual variable update. The key technical insight is that the score network is only accurate on the diffusion manifold at time t, so the naive z-update (applying the score to x+u) is unreliable; instead, DDiff constructs x_t recursively via a DDIM reverse step where the current x estimate serves as the predicted x₀, then adds the dual variable u re-scaled to match the signal level at t−1. This ensures the score is always evaluated at a manifold-consistent point while the dual variable accumulates constraint violations across iterations. The paper proves fixed-point convergence of all three iterate sequences under bounded denoiser and gradient assumptions without requiring a convex prior, and extends the framework to latent diffusion models for reduced compute.

## Results

On FFHQ, DDiff achieves the best PSNR on 5 of 8 tasks (e.g., 30.07 dB on 4× super-resolution vs. 29.34 for DAPS; 33.08 dB on random inpainting vs. 30.76; 31.48 dB on nonlinear deblurring vs. 28.45) and the best LPIPS on 7 of 8 tasks. On ImageNet, DDiff leads in PSNR on super-resolution (25.81 vs. 25.44), random inpainting (28.39 vs. 27.32), nonlinear deblurring (29.68 vs. 27.28), and HDR (26.50 vs. 26.10). DDiff consistently reports the lowest residual error (data-consistency metric) across nearly all tasks, e.g., 0.0026 on Gaussian deblurring (FFHQ) and 0.0027 on nonlinear deblurring, indicating more faithful adherence to the observations than any baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDiff is compared against seven pixel-space baselines (DAPS, DMPlug, DPS, DiffPIR, DCDP, DDRM, RED-diff) and three latent-space baselines (LatentDAPS, PSLD, ReSample). DAPS is the strongest competitor: it outperforms DDiff in PSNR on Gaussian deblurring (29.63 vs. 28.87 dB on FFHQ; 25.90 vs. 22.29 on ImageNet), motion deblurring (29.17 vs. 28.24 on FFHQ; 26.61 vs. 24.16 on ImageNet), and HDR (27.39 vs. 26.05 on FFHQ), as well as on LPIPS for 4× super-resolution (0.190 vs. 0.211). However, DDiff dominates DAPS on LPIPS for 6 of 8 tasks and on residual error for 7 of 8 tasks, and achieves substantially higher PSNR on inpainting (random: +2.32 dB on FFHQ), nonlinear deblurring (+3.03 dB on FFHQ), and phase retrieval on FFHQ. DPS achieves the lowest LPIPS (0.079) and residual (0.0033) on box inpainting (FFHQ) but trails DDiff by 1.2 dB in PSNR. DiffPIR and DCDP are consistently the weakest baselines, with PSNR gaps of 5–20 dB and residual errors 2–10× higher than DDiff, particularly on nonlinear tasks where DiffPIR drops to ~10 dB PSNR on phase retrieval. The paper also notes that DDiff is faster than DAPS at equal NFEs due to the absence of MCMC equilibration steps and reduced backpropagation overhead.

## Relation to Prior Work

DDiff repositions diffusion-model-based inverse problem solving from the posterior-sampling paradigm (DPS, DDRM, DAPS) and half-quadratic splitting (DiffPIR, DCDP, PnPDM) into a dual-ascent MAP framework rooted in ADMM. The key methodological departure is the z-update: rather than applying the score network directly to x+u (as in naive Diff-PnP-ADMM) or approximating the conditional score via Dirac deltas (as in DPS/DAPS), DDiff inserts a DDIM-style noising step that keeps the score evaluation on its training manifold, then re-injects the dual variable at the correct signal scale. This yields the first fixed-point convergence proof for a diffusion-based MAP solver and, by removing the dual update, exactly recovers DiffPIR, establishing a clean lineage from HQS to ADMM-style methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in DDiff's substantially better noise robustness (e.g., 3× lower LPIPS than DAPS at σ=0.3 on phase retrieval) and tighter data consistency (lower residuals), which directly reduces hallucination from the generative prior—critical for applications like low-dose CT or astronomy where measurement fidelity is paramount. The convergence guarantee and the latent-space extension (LatentDDiff) make the framework more trustworthy and scalable. Open questions include extension to 3D/video data, handling of non-Gaussian noise models, and whether the fixed-point structure can be exploited for adaptive step-size or early-stopping strategies.
