## Problem

Current video understanding benchmarks are overwhelmingly Western-centric and English-dominant, with multilingual efforts relying on direct translation of English annotations while retaining Western visual content. This bias masks the inability of Video-LLMs to operate in diverse global cultural contexts. Additionally, prior cultural benchmarks evaluate only final answers without reasoning traces, making it impossible to diagnose where and why models fail in complex multi-step video reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262557/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes long-form culturally-rich videos (1 minute to over 1 hour) with native-language audio as visual input, paired with complex open-ended questions written in the video's native language. Each question requires at least two reasoning skills (e.g., temporal ordering, cause-and-effect, spatial perception) plus a mandatory visual cultural understanding skill. Outputs are free-form text answers scored on a 0–2 scale by an LLM judge (Gemini-2.5-Flash), and the diagnostic pipeline additionally produces a structured error log mapping each failure to a specific node in the evidence graph with a tagged error type (Temporal Localization, Spatial Grounding, Attribute Misidentification, Spurious Object/Event, Reasoning, or Knowledge Issue).

## Method

CURVE introduces two core technical contributions beyond the dataset itself. First, an Evidence Graph formalizes each human-authored reasoning trace as a Directed Acyclic Graph where nodes represent atomic evidences (visual observations at specific timestamps, external facts, or logical inferences) and edges encode prerequisite dependencies. Second, Iterative Error Isolation is a three-stage loop that (1) traverses the graph comparing model reasoning against each node, (2) tags the first failure(s) using a fine-grained perception/reasoning/knowledge taxonomy, and (3) generates corrective hints from the identified errors, prunes the graph to unevaluated nodes, and re-queries the model. This counterfactual re-evaluation continues until the full reasoning chain is solved, uncovering approximately 22% of total errors beyond the first iteration and revealing that 75% of all failures stem from cultural visual perception rather than logical reasoning.

## Results

On the CURVE benchmark (2,400 open-ended questions across 540 videos in 18 locales), the best-performing model, Gemini-2.5-Pro, achieves only 45.07% aggregate accuracy (on a 0–2 LLM-judge scale), compared to a human baseline of 95.22%—a gap of over 50 percentage points. GPT-5 follows at 42.20%, while open-weight models lag substantially: Qwen-3-VL scores 21.50% and Qwen-2.5-VL just 12.75%. Performance varies dramatically by locale: the top model reaches 64.29% on Korean (ko-KR) but drops to 28.00% on Telugu (te-IN) and 31.60% on Tamil (ta-IN), exposing severe cultural and linguistic bias.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All seven model variants fall far short of the human baseline (95.22%), with the best model (Gemini-2.5-Pro, 45.07%) trailing by over 50 points. Among proprietary models, Gemini-2.5-Pro outperforms GPT-5 (42.20%) by ~3 points on aggregate, while GPT-5-mini (36.64%) and Gemini-2.5-Flash (35.84%) cluster in the mid-30s. Claude-Sonnet-4 (23.36%) underperforms both GPT and Gemini variants by roughly 12–22 points across most locales. Open-weight models trail significantly: Qwen-3-VL (21.50%) scores about half of Gemini-2.5-Pro's aggregate, and Qwen-2.5-VL (12.75%) is the weakest overall, scoring below 15% on 10 of 18 locales. The most striking disparity is cultural rather than architectural: Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves 64.29% on ko-KR but only 28.00% on te-IN, a 36-point swing that no single model variant can close, confirming that the bottleneck is cultural visual perception rather than raw model scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

CURVE extends the VideoQA evaluation landscape by introducing a multicultural, multilingual, long-video reasoning benchmark with fully native-language annotations, departing from prior efforts (ViMUL-Bench, CVQA, CulturalVQA) that relied on translated annotations or Western-centric visual content. Methodologically, it builds directly on MINERVA's use of human reasoning traces for error categorization, but advances this into a graph-based framework (Evidence DAG + Iterative Error Isolation) that enables fine-grained, step-level diagnosis of model failures rather than coarse final-answer accuracy. The LLM-as-judge evaluation protocol follows standard practice for open-ended QA, while the evidence graph construction and iterative correction loop represent a novel diagnostic pipeline not present in prior video reasoning benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 50-point gap between human performance (95.22%) and the best model (45.07%) provides a stark, quantified measure of the cultural blind spot in current Video-LLMs, and the finding that 75% of failures stem from cultural visual perception rather than reasoning suggests that simply improving chain-of-thought or scaling inference compute will not close the gap—models need fundamentally better cultural visual grounding. The benchmark's extensible 18-locale design and the evidence-graph diagnostic methodology offer a reusable framework for future multicultural evaluation. Open questions include whether the LLM-based error tagging introduces systematic biases, whether the cultural perception gap can be addressed through targeted data augmentation, and whether the 1.4× error multiplier for low-resource languages reflects a data-coverage issue or a deeper architectural limitation.
