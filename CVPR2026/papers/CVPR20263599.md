## Problem

State-of-the-art instruction-based image editing models (e.g., FLUX-Kontext, Qwen-Image-Edit) apply each instruction in a prompt with a fixed, all-or-nothing strength, offering no mechanism to continuously adjust the intensity of individual edits. Prior slider-based control methods target text-to-image generation and require separate training per attribute, leaving a gap for fine-grained, disentangled, continuous control over multi-instruction real-image editing.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263599/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an original RGB image, a multi-instruction text prompt (e.g., 'make the dragon exhale fire, turn the mountain to lava'), and a per-instruction slider value β_i ∈ [0,1] (or equivalently a LoRA scaling α_i) that independently controls each edit's strength from full suppression to amplification. It produces a single edited image at the chosen strengths, or a smooth continuous trajectory of images as any slider is varied. The pipeline operates entirely within the VAE latent space of the base MMDiT model, with the prompt encoded via T5 into text token embeddings that interact with image tokens through shared attention layers.

## Method

SliderEdit's key insight is that instruction semantics are localized in specific text token embeddings within the MMDiT architecture, enabling targeted modulation. It introduces Selective Token LoRA (STLoRA), which applies low-rank adaptation matrices (rank 16) only to the token embeddings corresponding to a target instruction, and Globally Selective Token LoRA (GSTLoRA) for single-instruction edits where updates span all image and text tokens. Training uses the Partial Prompt Suppression (PPS) loss, which requires the adapted model's output on the full prompt to match the base model's output with the target instruction removed, teaching the adapter to neutralize that instruction's visual effect. Continuous control is then achieved by smoothly scaling the learned LoRA weights (α = 1 − β), yielding a continuum from suppression through full application to exaggeration. The entire adapter is trained on just 1k–8k samples for ~1000 iterations, making it extremely lightweight and plug-and-play on top of frozen base models.

## Results

On single-instruction edits (γ=1, δ=15), GSTLoRA achieves the highest continuity scores across all VLMs (CLIP 0.2998, SigLIP 0.3062, BLIP 0.2227), substantially outperforming Explicit CFG (0.1993/0.2263/0.1803) and Implicit CFG (0.1547/0.1906/0.1513). For disentanglement, GSTLoRA yields the lowest identity drift (ID 0.2675) and LPIPS-AlexNet (0.1868), compared to Explicit CFG (0.3415, 0.2465) and Concept Slider (0.7091, 0.2174). On extrapolation, Explicit CFG leads on BLIP (0.5717 vs. 0.4862 for GSTLoRA), but the gap is modest relative to the large continuity and identity-preservation advantages of SliderEdit. In multi-instruction settings (γ=2,3), STLoRA on Qwen-Image-Edit achieves the best continuity (0.2813 at γ=2, 0.4345 at γ=3) while FLUX-Kontext better preserves identity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Concept Slider [10], SliderEdit (GSTLoRA) wins decisively on continuity (CLIP 0.2998 vs. 0.1803, a 66% relative improvement) and identity preservation (ID 0.2675 vs. 0.7091), while Concept Slider edges ahead on BLIP extrapolation (0.4680 vs. 0.4862 is actually a SliderEdit win; the closest is CLIP extrapolation 0.2581 vs. 0.2648). Against Continuous Attribute Control [3], SliderEdit dominates on all continuity metrics (e.g., BLIP 0.2227 vs. 0.1114) and identity (0.2675 vs. 0.5519), though it trails on BLIP extrapolation (0.4862 vs. 0.2082 is actually a SliderEdit win; CLIP extrapolation 0.2380 vs. 0.2648 favors SliderEdit). The most competitive comparison is with Explicit CFG [15]: SliderEdit achieves markedly smoother trajectories (continuity CLIP 0.2998 vs. 0.1993, a 50% gain) and better identity preservation (ID 0.2675 vs. 0.3415), but Explicit CFG reaches higher peak edit strength on BLIP extrapolation (0.5717 vs. 0.4862). Against Implicit CFG, SliderEdit wins on every metric, with the largest gaps in continuity (CLIP 0.2998 vs. 0.1547) and identity drift (0.2675 vs. 0.2748). Overall, SliderEdit's core advantage is smooth, disentangled control rather than maximum edit amplitude.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) instruction-based image editing models (FLUX-Kontext, Qwen-Image-Edit) that apply edits in an all-or-nothing fashion, and (2) continuous attribute slider methods (Concept Sliders, Baumann et al.) that enable smooth control but require per-attribute LoRA training and target text-to-image generation rather than real-image editing. SliderEdit departs from both by introducing Selective Token LoRA (STLoRA) and Globally Selective Token LoRA (GSTLoRA), which apply a single set of low-rank adapters trained with a Partial Prompt Suppression loss to achieve disentangled, per-instruction continuous control in multi-instruction editing—eliminating the need for per-attribute retraining that prior slider methods required. The MMDiT architecture's localized instruction-token embeddings provide the interpretability foundation that makes selective token modulation feasible.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SliderEdit provides a lightweight, plug-and-play mechanism (1k–8k training samples, ~1000 iterations) to convert any MMDiT-based editing model into a continuously controllable system, which is practically significant for interactive editing applications. The consistent trade-off observed between continuity, extrapolation, and disentanglement across all configurations suggests a fundamental tension in how edit strength is modulated, an open question for future work. Limitations include evaluation on a single dataset (GPT-Image-Edit subset) and two base models, and the method's behavior on very long or highly compositional prompts remains unexplored. The framework also opens the door to narrative-style sequential editing and zero-shot personalization with continuous control, suggesting broader creative applications.
