## Problem

Voxel-based fMRI models such as TFF and SwiFT are constrained by prohibitive memory demands, limiting them to processing only ~20 temporal frames at once and thus missing informative long-range temporal dynamics (e.g., infraslow BOLD-LFP coupling, global arousal waves). The paper asks whether a 2D autoencoder pre-trained on natural images can serve as a training-free tokenizer for 4D fMRI volumes, enabling substantially longer-sequence modeling with a lightweight Transformer.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261233/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 4D fMRI time series where each 3D volume is 96×96×96 (single channel, min-max normalized to [-1,1]); the single channel is duplicated to three channels to simulate RGB before tokenization. Each 3D volume is compressed into exactly 27 continuous tokens of dimension 3072, and the Transformer processes T=256 frames (i.e., 256×27 = 6,912 tokens) per forward pass. Outputs are a sex class label, a continuous age estimate, a continuous intelligence score (CogTotalComp-AgeAdj), or an ADHD diagnosis label, all read from a [CLS] token.

## Method

The core insight is that a 2D Deep Compression Autoencoder (DCAE) trained on natural images preserves both fine-grained spatial detail and high-level functional connectivity in fMRI volumes without any fine-tuning on medical data. Tokenization proceeds by slicing each 3D volume along all three axes, encoding each 2D slice with the DCAE encoder (32× spatial compression), then aggregating tokens from the three slicing variations at matching spatial positions to yield 27 tokens per frame. A 12-layer Transformer encoder with grouped query attention (14 query heads, 2 KV heads), rotary positional encoding, and F.scaled.dot_product_attention processes the token sequence. For self-supervised pre-training, a masked token modeling objective replaces 50% of tokens with a learnable [MASK] token using a tube-masking strategy (same spatial mask repeated across frames) and trains a linear head to reconstruct masked tokens via L1 loss. This yields 7.33× memory savings and 3.8× faster training versus SwiFT at T=50, extending the processable temporal window to T=384 under the same ~30 GB budget.

## Results

On UKB, TABLET (T=256) achieves 97.7% ACC / 0.998 AUC for sex classification and 0.340 MSE / 0.814 ρ for age regression, outperforming SwiFT (T=50) on age (0.364 MSE, 0.802 ρ) while trailing slightly on sex (98.1% ACC). On HCP, TABLET reaches 93.8% ACC / 0.987 AUC for sex, 0.473 ρ for age, and 0.392 ρ for intelligence prediction, beating SwiFT (T=50) on all three tasks. On ADHD-200, TABLET achieves 65.8% ACC / 0.729 AUC / 0.630 F1 for diagnosis, surpassing SwiFT (T=50) by ~2% ACC and ~3% AUC. TABLET is 7.33× more memory-efficient and 3.8× faster per epoch than SwiFT at T=50, and can process T=384 frames within the same ~30 GB budget where SwiFT is limited to T=40.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TABLET (T=256) outperforms the strongest voxel-based baseline SwiFT (T=50) on UKB-Age (MSE 0.340 vs 0.364; ρ 0.814 vs 0.802), ADHD-200 diagnosis (ACC 65.8 vs 63.9; AUC 0.729 vs 0.701), HCP-Sex (ACC 93.8 vs 92.2; AUC 0.987 vs 0.972), HCP-Age (ρ 0.473 vs 0.460), and HCP-Intelligence (MSE 0.835 vs 0.865; ρ 0.392 vs 0.354). TABLET trails SwiFT (T=50) marginally on UKB-Sex (ACC 97.7 vs 98.1; AUC 0.998 vs 0.999) and on HCP-Age MSE/MAE (0.773/0.705 vs 0.764/0.699), though it still leads on Pearson ρ. Against the older voxel-based TFF (T=20), TABLET wins on every reported metric across all three datasets. All ROI-based baselines (XGBoost, BrainNetCNN, BNT, meanMLP, Brain-JEPA) are substantially outperformed by TABLET on every task, with the largest gaps on age regression (e.g., UKB-Age ρ: 0.814 vs 0.685 for BNT) and ADHD diagnosis (ACC: 65.8 vs 63.6 for BNT). The authors note that gains over SwiFT are modest on resting-state fMRI because the tasks rely more on structural than temporal information, and they show larger advantages on the more dynamic HBN-Movie task-fMRI data in supplementary experiments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the voxel-based fMRI modeling line (TFF, SwiFT) by replacing direct 4D volume processing with a compact tokenization step, enabling a standard Transformer encoder to handle substantially longer temporal sequences. The tokenizer is the encoder of a Deep Compression Autoencoder (DCAE) pre-trained on natural images, repurposed here without any fMRI-specific fine-tuning—a deliberate departure from the domain-specific 3D autoencoder one might expect. The self-supervised pre-training component adapts the masked image modeling paradigm (SimMIM, VideoMAE) to operate on the compressed token space rather than raw voxels, removing the need for an image-level decoder during pre-training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 7.3× memory reduction and 3.8× speedup over SwiFT at matched input scale unlock temporal windows (~10× longer) that were previously infeasible on standard hardware, opening the door to studying slow neural dynamics (infraslow BOLD-LFP coupling, global arousal waves) that unfold over tens of seconds. However, the authors note that gains on resting-state tasks are modest, and the key open question is which neuroscientific phenomena and task-fMRI settings will most benefit from the extended temporal context. Limitations include independent per-frame tokenization that may disrupt fine temporal dynamics, and the absence of explicit spatial or temporal structure in how the 27 tokens are processed by the Transformer.
