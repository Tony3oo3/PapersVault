## Problem

Existing procedural planning methods for instructional videos rely on large-scale models (diffusion, LLMs, transformers) that learn procedural structure implicitly, resulting in poor sample-efficiency and high computational cost. Prior work that does use explicit procedural knowledge graphs applies them only as post-processing decoders or conditioning signals at inference, rather than integrating them into the training loop. This paper asks whether explicitly embedding a Procedural Knowledge Graph end-to-end via a differentiable decoding layer can relieve the model from memorizing procedural rules, enabling a far simpler and more parameter-efficient planner.

## Input / Output

The model takes two short video clips (start and goal visual states) and a pre-computed Procedural Knowledge Graph (PKG) with action nodes and fixed transition-probability edges, plus a target plan length T. A frozen visual backbone extracts features from each clip, projected to a shared embedding space. The output is a soft plan distribution (T×N matrix over the action taxonomy) produced by the Differentiable Viterbi Layer; at inference, standard Viterbi Decoding converts this into a discrete action sequence of length T. The PKG introduces no trainable parameters—it is estimated from action co-occurrence statistics in the training set and held fixed.

## Method

ViterbiPlanNet decomposes planning into four stages: encoding the PKG as a fixed transition matrix, encoding start/goal clips with a frozen backbone plus learnable projection, predicting emission probabilities (action–observation compatibility) via a transformer encoder and MLP with sigmoid, and decoding through a Differentiable Viterbi Layer (DVL). The DVL replaces the non-differentiable max and argmax operators of standard Viterbi with log-sum-exp and softmax relaxations, producing a soft plan and soft backpointer distributions that allow gradients from the planning MSE loss to flow back into the emission network. Crucially, the DVL is used during training (not merely at inference), so the model learns structure-aware emission probabilities rather than memorizing full plans; the PKG's transition probabilities are fixed and introduce zero additional trainable parameters. Training combines the plan loss with a visual-semantic alignment loss and a task classification loss, all weighted equally. At inference, standard Viterbi Decoding is applied to the learned emissions to recover a discrete, graph-consistent plan.

## Results

On CrossTask (T=3), ViterbiPlanNet achieves 38.45% SR, 63.07% mAcc, and 83.89% mIoU with only 5.57M parameters, outperforming the next-best methods (SCHEMA at 37.24% SR, PlanLLM at 36.84% SR) by 1.2–1.6 points. On COIN (T=3), it reaches 33.99% SR and 50.87% mAcc, and on NIV (T=3) it attains 32.37% SR and 46.96% mAcc, all with statistically significant margins over prior methods. The model uses 2–3 orders of magnitude fewer parameters than competing approaches (e.g., ~5.5M vs. 385M for PlanLLM, 1.08B for MTID, 30–100B for LLMs). In the cross-horizon consistency test (trained at T=6, tested at T=3/4/5), ViterbiPlanNet leads by up to +8% SR over all competitors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ViterbiPlanNet achieves the highest Success Rate across all three datasets and both horizons (T=3, T=4), with statistically significant gains over SCHEMA (+1.21% SR on CrossTask, +1.10% on COIN) and PlanLLM (+1.61% on CrossTask, +0.55% on COIN). On NIV, the margin is largest: +2.37% SR over PlanLLM and +4.81% over KEPP. On mAcc and mIoU, ViterbiPlanNet is within 0.1–0.8 points of SCHEMA and PlanLLM on CrossTask and COIN (statistically inconclusive), indicating comparable step-level accuracy while winning on the stricter full-sequence SR metric. Against diffusion-based baselines PDPP and KEPP, ViterbiPlanNet leads on SR by 1.7% and 3.5% respectively on CrossTask, while PDPP retains a small mIoU edge on COIN. Compared to LLM/VLM baselines (Qwen2.5-VL-32B, Qwen2.5-32B, Qwen3-30B, Gemini 2.5 Pro), ViterbiPlanNet outperforms all by 9–27 points in SR, confirming that in-context prompting is insufficient for structured procedural planning. Against MTID (1.08B params), ViterbiPlanNet (5.5M params) achieves comparable SR (39.75 vs. 40.45 at T=3) but substantially better mIoU (76.92 vs. 69.17), demonstrating that the parameter-efficient DVL-based approach captures procedural structure without the capacity of a billion-parameter diffusion model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the differentiable dynamic programming framework of Mensch and Blondel (2018), adapting their log-sum-exp and softmax relaxations to the Viterbi decoding problem, and on pre-computed Procedural Knowledge Graphs (PKG) from action co-occurrence statistics (Ashutosh et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2023). It departs from prior procedure planning methods (SCHEMA, PlanLLM, P3IV, KEPP) that use Viterbi decoding or PKGs solely as inference-time post-processing or conditioning signals, by embedding the Viterbi algorithm directly into the training loop via a Differentiable Viterbi Layer. The visual encoding follows SCHEMA's frozen-backbone-plus-projection design, and the overall probabilistic formulation (emission + transition decomposition) is standard HMM structure, but the end-to-end differentiability of the decoding step is the key architectural departure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~5–7M parameter model outperforming 30B+ LLMs and 1B diffusion models on procedure planning is a strong efficiency result with practical implications for on-device and resource-constrained assistive agents. The cross-horizon consistency finding (trained at T=6, tested at T=3/4/5) suggests that structure-aware training captures transferable procedural logic rather than horizon-specific memorization, which is an open question for most prior methods. Limitations include reliance on a pre-computed PKG (which may not generalize to open-vocabulary or novel procedures), evaluation on relatively short horizons (T≤6), and the modest absolute SR gains on standard benchmarks. The unified evaluation protocol with bootstrapped confidence intervals is a useful community contribution that addresses long-standing inconsistencies in the field.
