## Problem

MLLMs excel at holistic scene understanding but remain weak at fine-grained visual perception and precise spatial reasoning, often overlooking subtle cues like small object changes, color variations, or missing elements. Existing RL-based approaches either overspecialize on specific grounding tasks or rely on costly manual annotation, while alternative proxy tasks (jigsaw puzzles, hallucination detection) do not explicitly target fine-grained perceptual sensitivity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262730/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a pair of visually similar rendered images (I_a, I_b) and a text instruction prompting it to find all discrepancies, and produces a free-form textual response that is deterministically parsed into a list of axis-aligned bounding boxes [x_min, y_min, x_max, y_max]. Critically, the model is never told the number of differences, so it must infer completeness on its own. The image pairs are synthesized via Blender 3D rendering with programmatically controlled modifications (shape, color, size, material changes, or object addition/removal), yielding automatic pixel-accurate ground-truth boxes without manual annotation.

## Method

DiG formulates fine-grained perception as a differential grounding task and trains MLLMs via GRPO reinforcement learning with a three-component reward: a binary format reward for parseable output, and an accuracy reward combining detection F1 (via Hungarian bipartite matching between predicted and ground-truth boxes) and mean IoU for spatial precision. To enable scalable training, a fully automated Blender-based pipeline generates ~4.8K paired images with controllable discrepancy count, type, and location, eliminating manual labeling. A key challenge is reward sparsity—early in training the model rarely localizes any difference correctly, causing RL rewards to collapse near zero. This is addressed by a curriculum scheduling strategy that progressively increases difficulty from single-difference to double-difference to mixed-difference (up to four discrepancies) instances, shaping the reward landscape from dense to sparse supervision and enabling stable convergence. The approach is applied to Qwen3-VL-4B/8B-Thinking backbones using the EasyR1 framework.

## Results

On perception benchmarks, DiG-8B (Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking + DiG) achieves an average score of 80.5, improving over the base model (79.3) by +1.2, with the largest gains on HalBench (+3.4 to 76.7) and V* (+2.1 to 81.2). The 4B variant similarly improves from 78.9 to 79.9 average, with +3.8 on HalBench and +1.7 on HRBench-8K. On general multimodal benchmarks, DiG-8B gains +2.2 on MMBench (87.2), +17.5 on MME (1665.9), and +2.0 on TextVQA (77.5) over its base. On grounding benchmarks, DiG-8B improves RefCOCO val@50 from 86.9 to 90.0 and RefCOCO+ val@50 from 81.8 to 84.2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the same-family base models (Qwen3-VL-4B/8B-Thinking), DiG achieves consistent improvements across nearly all perception benchmarks, with the most notable gains on HalBench (+3.4/+3.8) and V* (+2.1 for 8B), confirming that differential grounding directly strengthens fine-grained visual discrimination. Compared to the larger Qwen2.5-VL-72B, DiG-8B substantially outperforms on HalBench (76.7 vs. 55.2), V* (81.2 vs. 85.9 — actually loses here), and CV-Bench (85.8 vs. 83.0), but trails on HRBench-8K (70.4 vs. 77.1) and MMVP (78.7 vs. 81.7), indicating that the 72B model retains an edge on some holistic perception tasks. Against InternVL2.5-78B, DiG-8B wins decisively on HalBench (76.7 vs. 57.1) and V* (81.2 vs. 75.9) but is outperformed on POPE (88.0 vs. 90.8) and MMVP (78.7 vs. 83.0). On general multimodal benchmarks (Table 2), DiG-8B surpasses LLaVA-NeXT on every metric and edges out Qwen2.5-VL-72B on MMStar (72.7 vs. 70.8) and AI2D (84.1 vs. 83.9), though it remains below the much larger InternVL3.5-38B on MMBench, MMVet, and TextVQA. On grounding benchmarks, DiG-8B improves RefCOCO val@50 by +3.1 (90.0 vs. 86.9) and RefCOCO+ val@50 by +2.4 (84.2 vs. 81.8) over its base, demonstrating that the fine-grained spatial skills learned through differential grounding transfer effectively to referring expression comprehension.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GRPO (DeepSeekMath) as its RL optimization backbone and positions itself within the growing line of RL-based post-training for MLLMs (Vision-R1, MM-Eureka, ReasonRFT, R1-VL, Perception-R1). It departs from prior proxy-task approaches (jigsaw puzzles, hallucination detection) by introducing differential grounding—a task that explicitly targets fine-grained spatial perception rather than coarse compositional reasoning—and from direct grounding fine-tuning (e.g., Perception-R1) by avoiding task-specific overfitting through a synthetic, automatically verifiable formulation. The automated Blender-based 3D rendering pipeline for data generation is a practical departure from the manually annotated grounding datasets (RefCOCO family) that constrain prior work in scale and diversity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is a fully automated, annotation-free data generation pipeline that makes fine-grained perception training scalable without the cost of human labeling, and the curriculum scheduling strategy addresses a real optimization bottleneck (reward sparsity) that plagues RL on sparse-signal tasks. The demonstrated transferability from synthetic 3D-rendered pairs to real-world grounding (RefCOCO) and general multimodal benchmarks suggests the learned perceptual skills are not overfit to the synthetic domain. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond Qwen3-VL backbones, whether it extends to video or higher-resolution inputs, and whether the 4.8K-pair dataset is sufficient or whether scaling the synthetic data further would yield additional gains. The reliance on geometrically simple 3D objects also raises questions about applicability to natural-image fine-grained perception.
