## Problem

Multilingual text-to-image models frequently produce culturally neutral or English-biased images when prompted in non-English languages, failing to reflect the socio-cultural context of the target language. The paper shows this stems not from missing cultural knowledge in the training data but from insufficient activation of culture-related internal representations, a gap that prior cross-lingual alignment work has overlooked.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262540/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a short 'noun-only' text prompt in one of 15 target languages (e.g., 'a traditional building' in Portuguese or Turkish) and produces a single RGB image whose visual elements should be statistically associated with that language's cultural context. Evaluation is performed via CultureVQA, a single-choice VQA task where Qwen3-VL or Gemini-2.5-Flash must attribute the generated image to one of 15 cultural regions, testing cultural recognizability from visual cues alone without any textual prompt.

## Method

The paper first localizes culture-sensitive signals through a two-stage probing pipeline: (1) comparing attention from culture-style modifiers to target nouns across text-encoder layers to identify the single most culturally sensitive layer, and (2) applying a Top-K Sparse Autoencoder at that layer to extract sparse features and selecting neurons with high weighted frequency scores (activation frequency × mean magnitude) for cultural prompts relative to noun-only prompts. Guided by these findings, two complementary alignment strategies are proposed: a zero-training neuron amplifier that scales the identified culture-sensitive neuron activations by (1+λ) at inference time via SAE encode-modulate-decode, and a fine-tuned layer enhancer that inserts a small residual MLP (W₂σ(W₁h)) only into the culturally sensitive layer, trained with pixel-level MSE against ground-truth cultural reference images while freezing all backbone parameters.

## Results

On the CultureBench test subset with 'noun-only' prompts, the fine-tuned variant achieves a CultureVQA score of 36.63, surpassing the strongest baseline (StableDiffusion 3.5 at 25.13) by 11.5 points. The zero-training variant attains the best CLIPScore (0.291) and ImageReward (0.33), while both variants improve LPIPS over all baselines (0.654 and 0.661 vs. best baseline 0.673). The zero-training method adds +12.32 CultureVQA over PEA-Diffusion, and the fine-tuned method adds +14.98, demonstrating that targeted cultural neuron activation yields substantial gains without sacrificing visual fidelity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against six SOTA multilingual T2I models: StableDiffusion XL, FLUX.1-dev, Show-o2, PEA-Diffusion, AltDiffusion, and StableDiffusion 3.5. On the primary cultural metric (CultureVQA), both proposed variants decisively outperform all baselines: the fine-tuned method (36.63) exceeds the best baseline StableDiffusion 3.5 (25.13) by 11.5 points, and the zero-training method (33.91) exceeds it by 8.8 points. On text-image alignment (CLIPScore), the zero-training variant (0.291) edges out AltDiffusion (0.282), the previous best, while the fine-tuned variant (0.290) is nearly tied. On perceptual preference (ImageReward), the zero-training variant (0.33) dramatically surpasses AltDiffusion (−0.11), the only baseline with a positive score. On reconstruction fidelity (LPIPS, lower is better), the zero-training variant (0.654) improves over PEA-Diffusion (0.673), the best baseline. Overall, the proposed methods win on all four metrics simultaneously, with the largest margins on CultureVQA and ImageReward, confirming that cultural neuron activation improves cultural consistency without degrading standard generation quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on two multilingual T2I diffusion systems—PEA-Diffusion and AltDiffusion—as its base architectures, and draws methodologically from the neuron-interpretability literature (Top-K SAE, FEMN-style causal probing) that has been applied to object/attribute concepts in CLIP and LLMs but not yet to cultural representations. It departs from prior cultural-bias work (SCoFT, ViSAGe, Stable Bias), which focuses on fairness and debiasing with an English-centric lens, by reframing the problem as cross-lingual cultural consistency and introducing a diagnostic-plus-intervention pipeline: first localizing culture-sensitive layers and neurons via attention contrast and SAE decomposition, then amplifying or fine-tuning only those units. The CultureBench benchmark and CultureVQA metric fill a gap the authors identify: no prior work provides a unified, multilingual, multi-country evaluation of whether generated images carry the target culture's visual cues.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a zero-training, plug-and-play neuron-amplification step can close most of the cultural-consistency gap without retraining the diffusion backbone, which is attractive for deploying multilingual T2I systems at scale. CultureBench (15 countries, 7,932 samples) and CultureVQA offer the community a reusable evaluation protocol that prior fairness-oriented benchmarks lacked. Open questions remain: whether the 'insufficient activation' diagnosis generalizes beyond the two tested architectures and 15 cultures, whether VLM-based cultural attribution (Qwen3-VL, Gemini-2.5-Flash) is sufficiently reliable as a proxy for human judgment, and how cultural enhancement interacts with anti-stereotyping constraints—amplifying culture-specific neurons could inadvertently reinforce reductive cultural tropes if not carefully bounded.
