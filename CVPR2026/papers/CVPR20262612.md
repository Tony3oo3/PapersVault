## Problem

Existing WSI retrieval methods operate at fixed patch or slide granularity, misaligned with clinical workflows where pathologists examine regions of arbitrary orientation and size. No prior approach can represent or match such arbitrary regions while preserving semantic and structural consistency under rotation and scale variations, leaving a fundamental gap in region-level retrieval for computational pathology.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262612/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a query region specified by a 5-tuple descriptor (x, y, w, h, θ) on a WSI and a pre-indexed database of 224×224 foreground patches (at 5× magnification, encoded by the UNI foundation model and stored in a Milvus HNSW index with spatial metadata). It outputs a set of candidate region descriptors (center position, width, height, rotation angle) ranked by semantic similarity, where the geometric parameters are estimated from the affine relationship between query and retrieved anchors rather than directly observed.

## Method

URICA introduces semantic tessellation, which partitions an arbitrary region into overlapping square patch anchors at a fixed step size t, and an affine identifier, a vector between anchor pairs whose magnitude ratio and angle difference invariantly encode scale and rotation shifts (Property 1). At retrieval time, each query anchor triggers a top-k nearest-neighbor search in the patch database; the resulting anchor correspondences yield pairwise (Δθ, Δd) estimates, from which URICA selects the most uniform subset via variance minimization to recover the global transformation. A theoretical bound (Eq. 7) connects the tessellation-based similarity to the ideal pixel-level semantic mask objective, parameterized by a coincidence degree α estimated empirically. For efficiency, K-Means anchor selection reduces the number of database queries, and a Bag of Shifting (BoS) strategy replaces exponential subset enumeration with O(k_a·k²) interval-based identification.

## Results

On slide-level retrieval across 24,811 TCGA WSIs (29 cancer subtypes, 10 anatomical sites), URICA achieves 98.38 ± 1.17% mMV@5, substantially outperforming all published baselines (best prior: HSHR at 90.87 ± 10.3%). On the novel inter-region retrieval task, URICA achieves mSim@5 of 0.9402 and mIoU@5 of 0.6416 on pulmonary tissue, compared to 0.6812/0.1656 (Slide Method), 0.8582/0.3163 (Sample Method), and 0.1671/0.0262 (Adjacent Method). URICA attains the best mIoU@5 across all reported sites and subtypes, with gains most pronounced in pulmonary, urinary, and gastrointestinal tissues where spatial overlap improves by 2–3× over the strongest baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

URICA outperforms all four published slide-retrieval baselines on the overall mMV@5 metric: Yottixel (87.73%), SISH (84.96%), RetCCL (87.00%), and HSHR (90.87%), with URICA reaching 98.38%. The margin is largest on gastrointestinal tissue (97.90% vs. 69.22% for HSHR) and pulmonary tissue (99.26% vs. 84.27% for RetCCL), where prior methods struggle most. URICA is slightly behind HSHR on melanoma (97.36% vs. 97.52%) and prostate/testis (97.92% vs. 99.10%), but these are near-parity cases. On the novel inter-region retrieval task (Table 2), URICA dominates all three internal baselines: it achieves the best mIoU@5 in every reported subtype (e.g., 0.6416 vs. 0.3163 for Sample Method on Pulmonary), while also attaining the best mSim@5 in most cases. The Adjacent Method performs worst overall (mIoU@5 ≈ 0.02), confirming that rigid local-neighborhood reconstruction cannot capture region-level structure under rotation and scale variation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant WSI retrieval paradigms—patch-based indexing (Yottixel, DRA-Net) and slide-level contrastive retrieval (SISH, RetCCL, HSHR)—by introducing a region-level formulation that neither prior line addresses. It repurposes foundation-model patch embeddings (UNI, DINO-family) not as terminal retrieval units but as building blocks for a novel 'semantic tessellation' structure, and borrows affine geometry from oriented object detection (ReDet, AO2-DETR) to construct a transformation-invariant region signature. The theoretical bounding section draws on probabilistic similarity analysis to connect the sparse tessellation representation to an ideal pixel-level mask objective, a bridge absent from prior retrieval work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By being the first method to retrieve arbitrary WSI regions under rotation and scale, URICA opens a capability gap that directly mirrors how pathologists actually examine tissue—selecting regions of variable orientation and size rather than fixed patches or whole slides. The theoretical approximation bounds provide a principled way to choose tessellation granularity, reducing what was previously a hyperparameter search to a derived quantity. Key open questions include whether the approach generalizes to truly irregular (non-rectangular) regions, whether encoder-agnostic variants can reduce the dependency on UNI-quality features, and whether the method's advantage persists on smaller archives where the indexed patch database is less informative.
