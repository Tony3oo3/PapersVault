## Problem

Existing VLA models rely on monolithic action decoders trained on aggregated data, which limits scalability for long-horizon multi-step tasks and causes catastrophic forgetting when incrementally acquiring new skills. The paper addresses both the lack of structured task decomposition within a single model and the inability to expand a robot's skill repertoire without degrading previously learned capabilities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263634/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes multi-camera RGB observations, a natural-language task instruction, and the robot's proprioceptive state. It adaptively switches between two output modes via special [think] and [act] tokens: in thinking mode it emits a textual task chain with progress tracking and a scalar atomic skill abstraction (σ ∈ [0,100]); in acting mode it produces a fine-grained action chunk computed as a weighted blend of a shared expert and one skill-specific expert. The skill abstraction is deterministically mapped to a fixed high-dimensional embedding that drives expert routing, so the same skill always activates the same expert regardless of task context.

## Method

AtomicVLA builds on the π0/π0.5 flow-matching VLA backbone and introduces a Skill-Guided Mixture-of-Experts (SG-MoE) action decoder comprising a shared expert (preserving pretrained generalization), multiple atomic-skill experts, and a sparse skill router. Each atomic skill is encoded as a noise-level-inspired scalar σ, embedded via Z_σ = E(norm(log(σ))) into a high-dimensional vector that conditions the router; only the top-scoring expert is activated and its output is combined with the shared expert through a weighted sum F_out = (1−w_k)·F_share(x_t) + w_k·F_k(x_t). For continual learning, introducing a new skill requires only adding one expert module and extending the router (initialized by copying original weights plus small random values for the new branch), leaving all existing experts frozen to prevent interference. A unified Think-Act loop lets the VLM decide at each timestep whether to plan (generating the task chain and skill abstraction) or to act (routing to the appropriate expert), and a principal-axis-analysis pipeline with InternVideo2.5 automatically segments trajectories into atomic action segments for training data annotation.

## Results

On the LIBERO benchmark, AtomicVLA achieves 96.6% average success rate (97.8% for AtomicVLA*), outperforming π0 by 2.4% and π0.5 by 0.9%, with a notable 10% gain on the most challenging LIBERO-LONG suite (95.2% vs. 85.2%). On CALVIN (ABC→D), AtomicVLA reaches an average task length of 4.09 (0.22 over π0), while AtomicVLA* reaches 4.27 (0.25 over π0.5). In real-world Franka experiments, AtomicVLA* improves long-horizon task success by 18.3% over π0.5 and reduces continual-learning degradation from −15.0% (π0.5) to only −1.3%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AtomicVLA outperforms all seven baselines on the LIBERO benchmark. Against the strongest baselines π0 and π0.5, the key advantage is on LIBERO-LONG (95.2% vs. 85.2% and 92.4% respectively), where the atomic skill decomposition and expert routing provide the largest gains. On CALVIN, AtomicVLA* (4.27 avg. task length) surpasses both π0 (3.87) and π0.5 (4.02), with the gap widening at later stages (77.6% at step 5 vs. 59.4% and 71.0%). In real-world long-horizon tasks, AtomicVLA* (63.3% avg.) beats π0.5 (45%) by 18.3 points and π0 (36.7%) by 26.6 points. For continual learning, AtomicVLA* suffers only a −1.3% average drop after adding a new skill, compared to −15.0% for π0.5, demonstrating that the SG-MoE architecture effectively prevents catastrophic forgetting. The ablation against MoDE [35] (timestep-conditioned routing) shows a 5.7% gap on LIBERO-LONG, confirming that skill-level routing is superior to token-level or denoising-step routing.

## Relation to Prior Work

AtomicVLA builds directly on the π0 and π0.5 VLA foundation models, extending their flow-matching action decoder with a Skill-Guided Mixture-of-Experts (SG-MoE) architecture. It departs from the dominant two-stage planner-controller paradigm (e.g., Hi Robot, Gemini Robotics) by unifying high-level task planning and low-level action execution within a single end-to-end model, and from prior MoE-in-robotics work (e.g., MoDE, VER) by routing experts on semantically meaningful atomic skill abstractions rather than on individual action tokens or denoising timesteps. The continual learning mechanism—adding new experts and extending the router without retraining existing ones—addresses a gap that prior VLA models, which rely on a single monolithic decoder, have largely left unaddressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The SG-MoE architecture offers a concrete, modular recipe for scaling VLA models to long-horizon tasks and lifelong skill acquisition, which is critical for real-world deployment where robots must accumulate capabilities over time. The continual learning result (only 1.3% average degradation vs. 15% for π0.5) is practically significant, suggesting that structured skill decomposition can mitigate catastrophic forgetting without experience replay. However, the evaluation is limited to a single Franka arm, a small set of manipulation primitives (5–8 skills), and relatively simple tasks; open questions include whether the approach scales to high-dimensional dexterous manipulation, larger skill libraries, or multi-robot settings, and how the fixed noise-level-to-skill mapping generalizes to continuous or open-vocabulary skill spaces.
