## Problem

Open-vocabulary segmentation (OVS) lags behind fully supervised segmentation due to two fundamental mismatches: VLMs are trained with coarse image-level supervision yet must produce fine-grained pixel-level predictions, and natural language prompts are semantically ambiguous for dense localization. The paper asks whether a small set of pixel-annotated support images, combined with textual class names, can close this gap while preserving the open-vocabulary property of predicting arbitrary categories at test time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260044/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a test RGB image, a set of textual class names (or descriptions) defining the vocabulary, a small support set of pixel-annotated images (B images per class, where B can be as low as 1), and optionally SAM 2.1 region proposals. The support set is dynamically expandable and may be partial—some classes can lack visual examples or textual names. Output is a full-resolution pixel-level segmentation mask, obtained by upsampled patch-level (h×w ViT grid) or region-level (SAM mask) predictions from a per-image linear classifier.

## Method

RNS (Retrieve and Segment) is a retrieval-augmented test-time adapter that, for each query image, retrieves k nearest-neighbor visual support features from a pre-extracted pool of per-image class features, then trains a lightweight linear classifier on the union of these retrieved features and fused class features (convex combinations of textual and visual class prototypes across multiple mixing coefficients λ). The key distinction from prior work is that fusion between modalities is learned per-query through the classifier's training objective rather than applied as a hand-crafted late combination of independent predictions. Class relevance weights, estimated via image-level similarity to text features, suppress irrelevant retrieved classes, and a pseudo-label KL-divergence loss handles classes lacking visual support by leveraging zero-shot predictions on the test image itself. The entire test-time training completes in under one second on an A100 GPU, and the support set can be expanded incrementally without retraining the backbone.

## Results

On the six OVS benchmarks (VOC, Cityscapes, ADE20K, PASCAL Context-59, FoodSeg103, CUB-200), RNS with B=20 support images per class achieves an average mIoU of 61.9, narrowing the gap to fully supervised segmentation (73.4) to just 11.5 points. With only B=1, RNS already reaches 49.9 average mIoU, a +34 improvement over the DINOv3.txt+SAM zero-shot baseline (27.9). The method consistently outperforms all OVS competitors across every dataset and support budget, with the largest gains on fine-grained datasets like CUB (65.2 vs. 22.9 for CAT-Seg at B=20) and FoodSeg103 (52.2 vs. 33.3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RNS outperforms kNN-CLIP [18] across all support budgets (B=1 to B=20) on both OpenCLIP and DINOv3.txt backbones; kNN-CLIP's hand-crafted fusion helps at B=1 but hinders performance after B=5, while RNS's learned per-image fusion scales better. FREEDA [2] shows minimal gains from combining textual and visual support, staying close to its w/o-text variant, whereas RNS consistently benefits from the fusion. Against CAT-Seg [11], RNS B=20 surpasses it by 14.1 mIoU on average while using only 964 pixel annotations versus CAT-Seg's 118k COCO annotations. RNS B=1 (49.9 avg) already exceeds LPOSS+ [63] (33.2) by 16.7 and CorrCLIP [83] on all overlapping datasets (e.g., VOC 73.2 vs. 76.4 is the only loss, but RNS leads on City 59.1 vs. 49.9, ADE 37.3 vs. 28.8, C-59 52.7 vs. 47.9). Compared to the DINOv3.txt+SAM zero-shot baseline [61], RNS B=1 improves average mIoU by 34 points (49.9 vs. 27.9), with the largest relative gains on fine-grained datasets (CUB: 34.0 vs. 5.8; Food: 42.8 vs. 27.2). SAN [78] is only reported on four datasets; RNS B=20 exceeds it on ADE (47.8 vs. 32.1), Food (52.2 vs. 24.5), and CUB (65.2 vs. 19.3), though SAN leads on C-59 (57.7 vs. 62.5 is actually RNS winning).

## Relation to Prior Work

RNS carves new ground at the intersection of open-vocabulary segmentation (OVS), few-shot learning, and retrieval-augmented adaptation. It departs from prior retrieval-based OVS methods like kNN-CLIP [18] and FREEDA [2], which rely on hand-crafted late fusion or generated prototypes, by introducing a learned per-image linear classifier that fuses textual and visual support features through a mixing coefficient. The few-shot OVS setting itself—combining textual class names with pixel-annotated visual examples in a dynamically expanding support set—is a deliberate generalization of the standard zero-shot OVS formulation, drawing conceptual inspiration from retrieval-augmented generation and test-time adaptation literature while operating on frozen VLM features rather than retraining backbones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RNS narrows the gap to fully supervised segmentation to 11.5 mIoU on average with only 20 support images per class, a substantial reduction from the ~34 mIoU zero-shot baseline, while preserving open-vocabulary generalization. The dynamic support mechanism enables personalized segmentation of specific instances without architectural modifications, opening a practical pathway for domain-specific adaptation (medical imaging, remote sensing) where naming is non-trivial. Open questions include scalability to very large support sets, whether the approach transfers to truly out-of-domain settings beyond the evaluated benchmarks, and whether the 11.5 mIoU residual gap can be closed further without sacrificing the open-vocabulary property. The reliance on pixel-annotated support images, while fewer than supervised training, still represents a non-trivial annotation cost that limits fully automated deployment.
