## Problem

One-shot Transformer Architecture Search (TAS) methods train a weight-entangled supernet where all subnets share the same parameters, causing a feature collapse problem: subnets fail to learn distinct, architecture-specific feature representations and perform significantly worse than independently trained networks. This limits the practical utility of one-shot TAS, as the shared weights are optimized for generic features across all subnets rather than specialized features tailored to each architecture.

## Input / Output

The method operates on RGB images (224×224) for ImageNet classification and transfer learning benchmarks. The search space is parameterized by block-level attributes (number of attention heads, MLP ratio per block) and subnet-level attributes (embedding dimension, depth), defining a combinatorial set of candidate ViT architectures. The output is an optimal subnet architecture whose weights (supernet + merged LoRA) produce class labels at inference with zero additional computational overhead beyond the base supernet.

## Method

TAS-LoRA freezes a pretrained weight-entangled supernet and trains a Mixture-of-LoRA-Experts (MoLE) module applied to all linear layers (QKV projections and MLPs) in each transformer block. Instead of assigning a unique LoRA per subnet (infeasible due to the enormous number of configurations), a lightweight LSTM-based router takes the subnet's architectural attributes (heads, MLP ratio, embedding dim, depth) as input and outputs per-layer expert weight distributions over K LoRA experts, enabling subnet-specific feature refinement. A group-wise router initialization strategy groups transformer blocks by architectural similarity and biases the router so each expert is initially assigned to a distinct group, preventing early-training redundancy. Training proceeds in two phases: a 5-epoch warm-up updating only LoRA experts, followed by 45 epochs of joint router and expert optimization. At inference, the selected subnet's LoRA experts are merged into the supernet weights (W_merged = W + Σ p_k E_k), eliminating any routing overhead.

## Results

On ImageNet, TAS-LoRA achieves 75.7% top-1 accuracy in the AutoFormer-T space (5.9M params, 1.3G FLOPs), 81.9% in AutoFormer-S (22.9M, 5.0G), and 82.6% in AutoFormer-B (54.0M, 11.0G), consistently outperforming the base AutoFormer supernet by 1.0–1.2 points. When combined with DYNAS's training strategy, TAS-LoRA reaches 75.9% in the Tiny space, a +1.1 point gain over DYNAS alone. On transfer learning benchmarks, TAS-LoRA-S achieves 99.1% on CIFAR-10, 91.0% on CIFAR-100, 98.2% on Flowers, 92.3% on CARS, and 78.0% on INAT-19, matching or exceeding PreNAS-S and AutoFormer-S across all datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TAS-LoRA consistently outperforms the weight-entangled supernet baselines it builds upon: it improves AutoFormer by +1.0 (Tiny), +0.2 (Small), and +0.2 (Base) points, and improves DYNAS by +1.1 points in the Tiny space, confirming that the gains come from subnet-specific LoRA features rather than the training strategy. Against other one-shot TAS methods, TAS-LoRA-S (81.9%) edges out FocusFormer-S (81.6%) and ViTAS-F (80.5%), and TAS-LoRA-B (82.6%) matches PreNAS-B (82.6%) while using the same 54M parameters. The one notable loss is in the Tiny space, where PreNAS-Ti (77.1%) surpasses TAS-LoRA-Ti (75.7%) because PreNAS restricts the supernet to only 6 subnets, partially alleviating feature collapse at the cost of flexibility under new hardware constraints. On transfer learning, TAS-LoRA-S matches PreNAS-S on CIFAR-10 (99.1%) and CARS (92.3 vs 92.2), slightly trails on CIFAR-100 (91.0 vs 91.2), but leads on Flowers (98.2 vs 97.6) and INAT-19 (78.0 vs 76.4). Compared to hand-designed ViTs, TAS-LoRA-B achieves 82.6% with 54M parameters, outperforming ConViT-B (82.4%, 86M) and DeiT-B (81.8%, 86M) with roughly 37% fewer parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of one-shot transformer architecture search (AutoFormer, VITAS, FocusFormer, DYNAS) and parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA, Mixture-of-LoRA-Experts). It departs from prior one-shot TAS methods that rely on a single weight-entangled supernet by identifying the feature collapse problem—where shared weights prevent subnets from learning architecture-specific features—and addressing it with a lightweight Mixture-of-LoRA-Experts layer and an LSTM-based router that uses subnet architecture (rather than input features) as the routing signal. Compared to MoS, which trains multiple supernets to reduce gradient conflicts, TAS-LoRA trains a single supernet plus a small set of LoRA experts, and unlike PreNAS, which reduces the subnet count, it retains the full search space while enabling per-subnet specialization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of feature collapse as a first-class problem in one-shot TAS provides a clear diagnostic for why weight-entangled supernets underperform independently trained subnets, and the LoRA-based solution is practical: no retraining is needed when hardware constraints change, and LoRA parameters merge into the supernet at inference with zero overhead. However, the absolute performance gains are modest (0.1–1.0 points over AutoFormer across scales), and the method still depends on a pretrained supernet, making it a two-stage pipeline. Open questions include whether the approach scales to much larger search spaces or non-ViT architectures, whether the LSTM router is optimal versus simpler alternatives, and whether the group-wise initialization strategy generalizes beyond the AutoFormer family.
