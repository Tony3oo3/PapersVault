## Problem

Existing rs-fMRI-based brain graph learning methods construct node features and adjacency matrices solely from pairwise functional correlations, lacking biological and cognitive semantic information at both the node and topological levels. This limits representational capacity, discriminative power, and—critically—biological interpretability, as the learned connectivity patterns cannot be grounded in established neuroscience or clinical knowledge.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260532/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each subject is represented as a 116-node graph (AAL116 atlas, 200 time points) where the adjacency is a Pearson correlation FC matrix and node features are per-region correlation vectors. The method additionally consumes LLM-generated structured descriptions of each brain region (produced by ChatGPT-4o from anatomy/function/pathology prompts) and a large corpus of PubMed literature (35,133 depression or 32,617 autism papers, title+abstract encoded by BERT at 256-token length). Beyond the binary disease label, the framework outputs a refined LLM-instructed connectivity matrix and per-region top-k literature associations with semantic relevance weights, enabling automated interpretability.

## Method

IEBGL introduces two complementary modules that anchor brain graph construction in external knowledge. The LITR module uses LLM-generated region descriptions as initial node features, then applies masked contrastive learning (random feature masking at p=0.2 plus symmetric Bernoulli edge perturbation at γ=0.2) within a two-layer GCN graph autoencoder; the decoder reconstructs an optimized adjacency via a learnable bilinear mapping with sigmoid activation, which is fused with the original FC matrix through a learnable scalar α. The LASA module encodes the full literature corpus with BERT, projects both node features and document embeddings into a shared space, and aggregates top-k relevant documents per node using scaled dot-product attention modulated by a sparse, entropy-regularized weight matrix, then fuses the result with projected node features via an MLP. The resulting interpretability-enhanced graph is classified by GBMN, which combines a GatedGCN branch for local topology with a Bi-directional Mamba branch for long-range dependencies. LITR is pretrained with reconstruction plus InfoNCE contrastive loss and then frozen; the end-to-end training minimizes cross-entropy classification loss plus L1 sparsity and attention-entropy regularization on the LASA modulation weights.

## Results

On the REST-meta-MDD dataset, IEBGL achieves 79.93% accuracy, 75.31% F1, and 84.22% AUC, outperforming the best baseline (BrainNetTF, 75.42% ACC / 81.53% AUC) by 4.51% in accuracy. On the ABIDE dataset, IEBGL reaches 81.43% accuracy, 80.22% F1, and 83.92% AUC, surpassing the strongest competitor (Graph-Mamba, 78.46% ACC / 80.63% AUC) by 2.97% in accuracy. The improvements are statistically significant (p < 0.05) against 10 of 11 baselines on both datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IEBGL outperforms all 11 baselines on both datasets across accuracy, F1, and AUC. On MDD, the largest gap is over BrainNetTF (75.42% ACC) and BrainGNN (75.22% ACC), with IEBGL achieving 79.93% ACC — a 4.51% improvement. On ABIDE, IEBGL (81.43% ACC) surpasses Graph-Mamba (78.46% ACC) by 2.97% and SK-GNN (77.40% ACC) by 4.03%. The most relevant comparison is SK-GNN, the only other method incorporating external literature; IEBGL improves over it by 5.04% ACC on MDD and 4.03% ACC on ABIDE, with statistically significant differences (p < 0.05). Classical GNNs (GCN, GIN, GraphSAGE) lag substantially, with GCN trailing by over 14% ACC on MDD. The paper reports p-values for each comparison, with 10 of 11 baselines showing significant differences on both datasets (the exceptions being BrainGNN on MDD at p=0.0732 and Graph-Mamba on ABIDE at p=0.0518).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the brain graph learning line initiated by GNN-based classifiers (BrainGNN, STAGIN, BrainNetTF) and recent knowledge-injection approaches (SK-GNN, BrainGFM) by unifying two previously separate ideas: LLM-generated topological priors and large-scale biomedical literature semantics. It departs from SK-GNN, which uses Gumbel-Softmax to select knowledge fragments from a smaller literature set, by instead employing LLM reasoning (ChatGPT-4o) to reconstruct the adjacency matrix via a masked-contrastive graph autoencoder (LITR) and aggregating over 35k PubMed papers with attention-based semantic modulation (LASA). The downstream classifier leverages the GBMN backbone (GatedGCN + Bi-directional Mamba), building on the state-space model trend in graph learning (Graph-Mamba, Brain-GM) but applying it to an interpretability-enhanced graph rather than the raw FC graph.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical recipe for making brain graph classifiers interpretable by automatically linking discriminative regions to specific biomedical publications, which could accelerate clinical validation and hypothesis generation. However, several open questions remain: the LITR module depends on a single LLM (ChatGPT-4o) and a fixed prompt set, raising concerns about robustness to prompt engineering and LLM version changes; the literature retrieval strategy (keyword-based PubMed queries) may introduce domain bias; and the interpretability claims rest on post-hoc attribution (gradient-based) rather than the model's internal representations being inherently explainable. The finding that small literature sets (500–1k papers) actually degrade performance below the no-literature baseline is a useful practical caveat for future work on knowledge-augmented neuroimaging models.
