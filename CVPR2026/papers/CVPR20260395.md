## Problem

State-of-the-art video-language models downsample input videos to 1 FPS or less, and the dominant evaluation benchmarks (Video-MME, MVBench, EgoSchema) are solvable at similarly low frame rates, leaving a critical blind spot: no existing benchmark tests whether models can perceive and reason about rapid, high-frequency temporal events that are simply invisible at low sampling rates. FPS-Bench addresses this gap by constructing questions that are, by construction, unanswerable below a measured minimum frame rate.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260395/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark item pairs a short video clip (~10 s average, native resolution, drawn from YouTube-8M across five visual domains) with a multiple-choice question offering four plausible options plus a 'None of the above' escape. The model must output the selected option letter along with a textual justification. Evaluation protocol adapts per model: raw-video models (e.g., Gemini) receive the full mp4 at their default sampling rate, while frame-based models receive as many frames as their architecture allows (up to 512 for GPT-4o, 64 for LLaVA), with the largest FPS that covers the entire clip.

## Method

The core contribution is the minFPS metric and the benchmark construction pipeline around it. For each video-question pair, an annotator sequentially increases the sampling rate from 1 FPS in integer steps until the correct answer becomes unambiguously verifiable; any rate below that threshold must make the answer impossible to confirm. All 1000 questions (from 554 videos) are curated to have minFPS ≥ 4, with a dataset average of 6.67 FPS and a median of 6.0 FPS. Questions span nine temporal-reasoning categories (e.g., Blink and Miss, Instance Count, Action Order, Synchronization Assessment), each requiring perception of rapid or transient events. Annotation is entirely human-based rather than LLM-generated, with a multi-stage quality review: dual minFPS measurement (final value is the minimum of two annotators' measurements), clarity checks, three external reviewers answering blind, and an LLM-based check (Gemini-1.5 Pro run four times without video) to flag questions solvable without watching the clip.

## Results

On FPS-Bench (1000 multiple-choice questions requiring ≥4 FPS, average 6.67 FPS), the best-performing model GPT-4o achieves only 31.8% overall accuracy, barely above the 25% random baseline, while human experts achieve 72.2%. The open-source Oryx (31.3%) and Qwen-3-VL-32B (30.7%) follow closely, while Gemini 2.5 Pro lags at 28.9%. The hardest category is Instance Count (best: GPT-4o at 32.1%), and the easiest are Action Order and Causality Detection (best: Oryx at 48.6% and GPT-4o at 39.8%), consistent with their lower average minFPS of ~5.3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All 14 evaluated VLMs (open-source, closed-source, and image-only) fall in a narrow 24–32% accuracy band, far below the 72.2% human performance, confirming that high-FPS temporal reasoning is a fundamental blind spot. GPT-4o leads at 31.8% overall, with particular strength in Instance Count (32.1%) and Causality Detection (39.8%), but is weakest in Synchronization (23.9%, below random). The open-source Oryx (31.3%) outperforms the closed-source Gemini 2.5 Pro (28.9%), suggesting the open/closed gap is narrowing for video understanding. Scaling does not reliably help: InternVL-3.5-14B (27.9%) underperforms its 8B counterpart (28.7%), while Qwen-3-VL-32B (30.7%) does improve over Qwen-3-VL-8B (28.0%). Ablations show that even when videos are slowed to guarantee models see all key frames (Table 2), accuracy improves by only 1–3 points (e.g., Qwen-32B: 30.7%→33.7%), indicating the bottleneck is not merely token budget but an inability to perceive and reason over rapid transient events.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in video QA benchmarking by introducing the minFPS metric, which quantifies the minimum frame rate needed to answer a question, departing from the temporal certificate concept in EgoSchema that measures duration rather than frame density. It positions itself against existing general-purpose benchmarks (MVBench, Video-MME, EgoSchema) that can be solved at <1 FPS, and against narrow high-speed benchmarks (MotionBench, AirLetters, DIVE) that require only ~2 FPS or focus on a single domain. Unlike MVBench and EgoSchema, which rely on LLM-generated questions from captions, FPS-Bench uses entirely human-annotated questions sourced from YouTube-8M, ensuring each example genuinely demands high-frame-rate perception.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FPS-Bench exposes a stark capability gap: even the strongest SOTA VLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Pro) achieve only ~30% accuracy versus 72% for humans, and this gap persists even when videos are slowed to guarantee models see all necessary frames, suggesting a fundamental limitation in temporal reasoning rather than a simple token-budget issue. The minFPS metric provides a reusable diagnostic for characterizing temporal difficulty across benchmarks. Key limitations include the relatively small scale (1000 questions), bias toward short high-speed events, and the open question of whether architectural changes (e.g., training at 16+ FPS as in F-16) or new temporal attention mechanisms can close this gap.
