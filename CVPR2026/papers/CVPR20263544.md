## Problem

Production text-to-image samplers rely on a single globally fixed timestep schedule for all prompts and noise seeds, which is unlikely to be optimal across the diverse range of test-time conditions. While prior work (e.g., TPDM) explores learned schedules, it does so autoregressively with O(L) policy calls and targets early stopping rather than optimal reallocation of a fixed step budget. This paper addresses both gaps: learning instance-level schedules in a single pass and tackling the high-variance gradient estimation that makes high-dimensional policy learning impractical.

## Input / Output

The scheduler policy takes a text prompt embedding and the initial noise latent x_T as input, processed through multiscale convolutional blocks and cross-attention, then outputs (L+1) Dirichlet parameters via an MLP head. These parameters define a distribution over L+1 nonnegative intervals that partition [0,1]; a single sample from this Dirichlet yields the complete descending timestep sequence t_1 > ... > t_L in one shot, with the (L+1)-th interval acting as a learnable stopping margin. The resulting schedule is executed by the frozen sampler (SD-XL, SD-3.5, or Flux-Dev) to produce the final image, with the policy network itself being only ~20M parameters (<1% of the backbone).

## Method

The core contribution is a single-pass Dirichlet scheduler trained with REINFORCE, where the key innovation is a James-Stein (JS) shrinkage reward baseline that provably reduces gradient estimation variance beyond standard RLOO. The JS baseline models contextual rewards under a two-level random effects hierarchy and adaptively interpolates between the per-context RLOO mean and the cross-context batch mean via a data-dependent shrinkage coefficient α̂_c, estimated from within- and between-context reward variances each iteration. Theorem 3.2 shows this convex combination is the empirical Bayes posterior mean and achieves strictly lower MSE than RLOO for any finite number of rollouts. Training uses no KL regularization and initializes the scheduler from scratch, with the policy updated by averaging REINFORCE gradients weighted by (r − b_JS) across all rollouts in a mini-batch. This design amortizes the scheduling cost to a single forward pass at inference, unlike autoregressive alternatives that require O(L) policy calls.

## Results

On HPD v2 held-out prompts, the JS-scheduled Flux-Dev achieves HPSv2 scores of 29.21 (5 steps), 30.86 (10), 31.12 (20), 31.23 (40), and 31.36 (80), consistently outperforming the default schedule (23.73–31.04) and RLOO (26.48–31.10) across all budgets. Notably, the 5-step JS schedule (29.21) nearly matches the deliberately distilled Flux-Schnell sampler (29.42) without any backbone retraining. On GenEval, JS improves overall scores from 0.62→0.68 (SD-3.5-M) and 0.64→0.70 (Flux-Dev), with the largest gains in counting (0.49→0.65 and 0.58→0.77). For text rendering, JS boosts OCR-Recall from 49.77→58.58 on Flux-Dev at 40 steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The JS-scheduled policy dominates all four baselines (Default, Cross-Context RLOO, RLOO, and TPDM-style PPO) on HPSv2 across every backbone and step budget. Against TPDM-style PPO on Flux-Dev, the gap is dramatic: JS scores 29.21 at 5 steps versus TPDM's 15.73, a 13.5-point margin, and 31.36 vs. 18.65 at 80 steps, indicating that the autoregressive next-timestep formulation with PPO fails to learn effective schedules under the same compute budget. Compared to the simpler RLOO baseline, JS yields the largest relative gains at low budgets (e.g., +2.73 HPSv2 at L=5 on Flux-Dev) and maintains a consistent 0.1–0.3 point advantage even at L=80 where discretization error is minimal. The Cross-Context RLOO baseline is notably unstable, sometimes scoring below the Default schedule (e.g., 26.70 vs. 29.61 at L=40 on SD-3.5-M), whereas JS never degrades below Default. On GenEval, JS improves the overall score by +0.06 (SD-3.5-M) and +0.06 (Flux-Dev) over Default, with the counting sub-task showing the largest absolute gain (+0.16 and +0.19 respectively).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the REINFORCE score-function estimator (Williams, 1992) and the RLOO baseline (Kool et al., 2019) by reinterpreting RLOO as a Monte Carlo approximation to the variance-optimal baseline and then applying James-Stein shrinkage (James & Stein, 1961) to provably reduce gradient estimation variance. It departs from the contemporaneous TPDM approach (Ye et al., 2025), which autoregressively predicts the next timestep for early stopping, by instead formulating schedule learning as a single-pass Dirichlet policy that outputs the entire timestep allocation in one shot, targeting the quality-vs.-steps Pareto frontier rather than variable-length sampling. The work also positions itself as orthogonal to distillation-based few-step methods (progressive distillation, consistency models, DMD), which retrain the backbone, whereas here the backbone remains frozen and only a lightweight 20M-parameter scheduler is trained.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a model-agnostic, low-overhead post-training lever: a single forward pass through a tiny scheduler replaces the entire backbone, making it applicable to any frozen diffusion or flow-matching model without retraining. The JS shrinkage baseline is a general-purpose, drop-in variance-reduction primitive for policy-gradient training that could benefit RLHF and other long-horizon one-shot settings beyond T2I. Key limitations include the reliance on a single reward model (HPSv2 or rule-based GenEval), only 2 rollouts per context, and a simple encoder-decoder scheduler; open questions include adaptive early stopping, multi-objective rewards, and extension to video, flow, and 3D generative pipelines.
