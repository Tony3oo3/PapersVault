## Problem

Existing weakly-supervised action segmentation methods operate on visual-level representations where rapid appearance fluctuations are easily mistaken for action transitions, causing over-segmentation and noisy boundaries. Humans instead perceive actions through hierarchical structures with a few key transitions across multiple abstraction levels, but no prior method models this hierarchy with theoretical guarantees that the high-level action variables are identifiable from observations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260982/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a T-frame video and an ordered action transcript (a sequence of M action labels indicating the order of segments, including background, but without frame-level temporal alignment). It outputs a per-frame action label sequence of length T. The transcript is the sole supervision signal; no frame-level ground truth is available during training, and the model must infer segment boundaries purely from the ordering constraint.

## Method

HAL formulates a hierarchical causal generative process in which slowly evolving high-level latent action variables govern the dynamics of rapidly changing low-level visual variables, augmented with pseudo-states and deterministic transitions to align their sequence lengths. A pyramidal transformer backbone extracts multi-level features, and variational inference (ELBO with reconstruction and KL terms) trains visual and action encoders/decoders. A smoothness transition constraint (L_s) enforces the temporal asymmetry by L2-normalizing both latent layers, computing SoftMAX-weighted frame-to-frame change magnitudes, and penalizing via ReLU whenever action-variable changes exceed visual-variable changes, plus an additional smoothness penalty on action transitions. Under mild assumptions (bounded densities, injective linear operators, positive density, non-singular Jacobians), the paper proves block-wise identifiability of the latent action variables by matching the joint distribution of five consecutive frames, extending prior single-layer identifiability results to the two-layer hierarchical setting.

## Results

On the Breakfast dataset, HAL achieves 56.3±1.3 MoF, 42.6±1.9 IoU, and 62.4±2.5 IoD, outperforming the strongest baseline ATBA (53.9±1.2 MoF, 41.1±0.7 IoU, 61.7±1.1 IoD) by 2.4 MoF and 1.5 IoU points. On CrossTask, HAL matches CtrlNS on MoF (54.0) but substantially exceeds it on IoU (21.6 vs. 15.7) and MoF-Bg (35.0 vs. not reported). On GTEA, HAL leads all baselines on every metric (45.2 MoF, 25.6 IoU, 49.2 IoD), surpassing ATBA by 3.0 MoF and 1.2 IoU. On Hollywood Extended, HAL achieves the best IoU (33.4) and IoD (56.8), though CtrlNS edges it slightly on MoF (52.9 vs. 51.0).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HAL consistently outperforms the strongest prior method ATBA [68] across all four datasets: +2.4 MoF and +1.5 IoU on Breakfast, +0.7 IoU and +3.7 MoF-Bg on CrossTask, +2.5 IoU and +1.0 IoD on Hollywood, and +3.0 MoF and +1.2 IoU on GTEA. Against the recent causal-representation baseline CtrlNS [61], HAL ties on CrossTask MoF (54.0) but wins decisively on IoU (21.6 vs. 15.7) and MoF-Bg (35.0 vs. not reported); on Hollywood, CtrlNS edges HAL by 1.9 MoF (52.9 vs. 51.0) while HAL leads on IoU (33.4 vs. 32.7) and IoD (56.8 vs. 52.4). On GTEA, HAL surpasses CtrlNS by a wide margin (45.2 vs. 33.3 MoF, 25.6 vs. 18.9 IoU). Earlier baselines such as AdaAct [74], DP-DTW [7], CDFL [41], TASL [46], POC [47], and MuCon [62] are all outperformed by 4–15 MoF points on Breakfast, with the largest gaps on IoU (e.g., 42.6 vs. 36.3 for AdaAct) and IoD (62.4 vs. 46.4 for AdaAct). The paper attributes the gains primarily to the smoothness transition constraint L_s, which enforces slower evolution of high-level action variables and reduces over-segmentation caused by transient visual fluctuations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal representation learning and nonlinear ICA literature (particularly the single-layer identifiability results of Hu & Schennach [20]) to a two-layer hierarchical setting, proving block-wise identifiability of high-level action variables under mild assumptions. It builds directly on ATBA [68] for the backbone architecture and segmentation loss, but departs from prior weakly-supervised action segmentation methods by constraining smoothness on latent action variables rather than predicted labels, and by introducing an augmented generative process with pseudo-states to align the different temporal rates of visual and action latents. The pyramidal transformer with a variational inference framework positions the work at the intersection of hierarchical VAEs and causal temporal representation learning (e.g., CtrlNS [61], TDRL [71]), but unlike those single-layer approaches, HAL explicitly models multi-scale temporal dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a principled, theoretically grounded explanation for why over-segmentation occurs in transcript-based action segmentation (visual features change faster than semantic actions) and provides a mechanism—sparse smoothness constraints on hierarchical latents—that provably recovers the action variables. However, the practical gains over strong baselines like ATBA and CtrlNS are modest (1–3 points on MoF/IoU), and the identifiability assumptions (injective linear operators, non-singular Jacobians, five-frame matching) may be difficult to verify in real-world video. Open questions include whether the hierarchical causal framework generalizes beyond transcript supervision to other weak labels, whether the pseudo-state augmentation is necessary or an artifact of fixed-length encoders, and whether the approach scales to longer videos or more complex action taxonomies. The authors note extending the idea to video generation as a future direction.
