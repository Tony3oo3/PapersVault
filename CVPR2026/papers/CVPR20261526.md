## Problem

Existing visual grounding benchmarks (RefCOCO series, Ref-L4) are saturated with state-of-the-art MLLMs achieving 90%+ accuracy, making them unable to differentiate true grounding ability from keyword-matching shortcuts. Critically, no prior benchmark systematically evaluates the model's ability to reject descriptions that contain subtle factual errors, a capability essential for safety in real-world applications. The paper asks whether MLLMs can truly ground with human-like sophistication across fine-grained discrimination, complex spatial reasoning, occlusion, and rejection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261526/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs a high-resolution RGB image (square-root-of-area ranging from 1,500 to 7,680 pixels, sourced from SA-1B and HR-Bench 8K) with a natural-language description of 18–58 words structured as complete sentences or short paragraphs. The model must output either a single bounding box (scored by IoU@0.5) or a null rejection when no object in the image matches the description. Descriptions are deliberately longer and more compositional than prior benchmarks (vs. 3.6 words average in RefCOCO+), and the target instances are notably small relative to the image (area ratio quartile: 0.16%–2.7%).

## Method

GroundingME is constructed via a three-stage human-in-the-loop pipeline: (1) automated bounding-box annotation using RAM++ for class detection and GroundingDINO for localization with a customized NMS rule, (2) description generation by Gemini-2.5-Flash prompted with red-box visual grounding, and (3) manual filtering and refinement enforcing uniqueness, subject clarity, task specificity, and factual accuracy. The 1,005 samples are organized into four L-1 challenge dimensions (Discriminative, Spatial, Limited, Rejection) with twelve L-2 subcategories. Beyond the benchmark, the paper proposes two improvement strategies: a Test-Time Scaling method that generates 16 thinking trajectories and uses a judge model (text-only or multimodal) to perform Best-of-16 selection based on reasoning quality, and a Data Mixture Training approach that fine-tunes Qwen3-VL-8B on RefCOCOg augmented with 30,000 negative samples at varying ratios to instill rejection capability.

## Results

On GroundingME (1,005 samples, Accuracy@0.5), the best model Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B achieves only 45.1% overall accuracy, with the majority of 25 evaluated MLLMs scoring between 10% and 40%. Performance is stratified across the four challenge dimensions: Discriminative (best avg: 75.0% for Qwen3-VL-32B), Spatial (best avg: 58.7% for Seed-1.6-Vision), Limited (best avg: 54.0% for Qwen3-VL-A22B), and Rejection (best avg: 7.0% for Gemini-2.5-Pro, with most models at 0%). Enabling thinking mode yields a 4.7–7.4% gain, and test-time scaling with a judge model adds up to 4.5% further improvement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark reveals that no model exceeds 50% accuracy, exposing a significant gap versus the >90% scores these models achieve on RefCOCO/Ref-L4. Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B leads at 45.1%, narrowly ahead of the commercial Seed-1.6-Vision (42.6%), while Gemini-2.5-Pro (20.7%) and Gemini-2.5-Flash (18.7%) perform at mid-range open-source levels, contradicting the expectation that commercial models dominate. Within the Qwen3-VL family, scaling from 2B (21.1%) to A22B (45.1%) yields a 24-point gain, confirming that model size remains a critical factor. The Rejection dimension is the most revealing failure: 22 of 25 models score 0%, with only Gemini-2.5-Pro (7.0%), Qwen2.5-VL-72B (3.0%), and Seed-1.6-Vision (1.0%) showing any rejection behavior. On Discriminative tasks, larger models pull ahead sharply (Qwen3-VL-32B: 75.0% vs. Qwen3-VL-2B: 44.6%), while on Spatial tasks the gap is more moderate, suggesting that relational reasoning benefits less from scale than fine-grained appearance discrimination.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the long line of visual grounding / referring expression comprehension benchmarks (RefCOCO series, CLEVR-Ref+, RefCrowd, Ref-ZOM, Ref-L4, HC-RefLoCo, Ref-Adv) by unifying four previously fragmented challenge dimensions—discriminative, spatial, limited, and rejection—into a single 1,005-sample benchmark. For the test-time scaling method, it adapts the generate-then-select paradigm from Brown et al. and Snell et al. to the specific context of thinking-trajectory quality in multimodal grounding, and for the data mixture training it builds on prior observations about negation awareness deficits in vision-language models. The key departure from prior benchmarks is the explicit inclusion of rejection samples and the multi-dimensional taxonomy, whereas earlier works addressed at most one or two of these axes in isolation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a stark capability gap: the best model scores only 45.1% while existing benchmarks report 90%+, and most models score 0% on rejection, revealing that MLLMs fundamentally cannot say 'no object matches'—a critical safety gap for real-world deployment. The data mixture training strategy (adding negative samples to RefCOCOg) is a simple, practical fix that lifts rejection from 0% to 27.9%, but the out-of-domain degradation on non-rejection tasks (38.8% → 33.0%) highlights that rejection capability does not transfer freely to harder scenarios, leaving open how to build robust, generalizable rejection without sacrificing positive grounding accuracy. The benchmark is likely to become a standard diagnostic tool as MLLM grounding capabilities continue to advance.
