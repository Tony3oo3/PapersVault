## Problem

Existing LVLM jailbreak methods either require white-box access for gradient-based perturbation optimization, rely on typography-based concealment that OCR filters increasingly neutralize, or demand iterative parameter tuning (shuffle order, mixup rate) per sample. This paper identifies an underexplored vulnerability—semantic slot filling—where LVLMs automatically complete missing slot values with harmful content even when each slot type appears locally benign, and shows this can be exploited in a single black-box query without any optimization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261566/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The attack takes a single harmful text query (e.g., from AdvBench-M or SafeBench) and produces a structured visual prompt rendered via Python/Matplotlib scripts (mind map, table, or sunburst diagram) with small random perturbations (positional jitter, angular rotation), paired with a fixed completion-guided text instruction. The LVLM's output is a text completion that fills in the missing slot values with the target harmful content. No natural images or adversarially optimized pixel perturbations are involved; the visual input is a programmatically generated schematic diagram.

## Method

StructAttack consists of two optimization-free modules. First, Semantic Slot Decomposition (SSD) uses a Decomposer LLM (DeepSeek-Chat with a role-playing prompt) to reformulate a harmful query into a central topic plus a set of malicious slot types that are individually benign (e.g., 'Making Process', 'Raw Materials') yet compositionally reconstruct the original intent, along with two distractor slot types that dilute malicious density and divert safety attention. Second, Visual-Structural Injection (VSI) renders these slots into a structured visual layout (mind map, table, or sunburst diagram) via template-specific Python scripts and applies a random perturbation operator for structural variation. The perturbed image is then paired with a fixed completion-guided instruction that implicitly prompts the LVLM to reassemble the slot branches and fill in the missing values, exploiting the model's reasoning/completion bias to generate harmful content without triggering safety filters. The entire pipeline is single-shot and layout-agnostic, requiring no gradient access or iterative refinement.

## Results

On AdvBench-M, StructAttack (Mind Map variant) achieves an ASR of 69.0% on GPT-4o, 69.9% on Gemini-2.0-Flash, 52.3% on Gemini-2.5-Flash, 59.3% on Qwen3-VL-Flash, 88.4% on Qwen2.5VL-7B, and 87.0% on InternVL-3-9B, with corresponding HF scores ranging from 5.3 to 6.7. On SafeBench, ASR values are 56.0% (GPT-4o), 67.7% (Gemini-2.0-Flash), 50.9% (Gemini-2.5-Flash), 56.0% (Qwen3-VL-Flash), 77.7% (Qwen2.5VL-7B), and 74.6% (InternVL-3-9B). Overall, StructAttack achieves an average ASR of ~90% on open-source LVLMs and ~66% on closed-source LVLMs, all with a single query (no iterative optimization).

## Baselines & Comparisons

StructAttack outperforms all five baselines on every model and dataset. On GPT-4o (AdvBench-M), StructAttack reaches 69.0% ASR versus 32.9% for the strongest baseline SI-Attack and only 10.7% for FigStep-Pro, a ~2× margin over the best competitor. On open-source models the gap widens further: StructAttack achieves 88.4% (Qwen2.5VL-7B) and 87.0% (InternVL-3-9B), surpassing FigStep-Pro (64.4% / 86.1%) and JOOD (33.3% / 54.2%). Among typographic methods, FigStep-Pro is the strongest but still trails StructAttack by 25–30 percentage points on closed-source models. OOD-based methods (SI-Attack, JOOD) show high variance across models (e.g., SI-Attack drops from 47.7% on Gemini-2.0-Flash to 3.7% on Qwen3-VL-Flash), whereas StructAttack maintains consistent 50–90% ASR across all six models. StructAttack also achieves the highest HF scores (5.3–7.0) compared to baselines (0.8–6.4), indicating not just more frequent jailbreaks but more harmful outputs.

## Relation to Prior Work

StructAttack repurposes semantic slot filling (SSF), a well-studied NLU task, as an attack vector against LVLMs, departing from both typographic attacks (FigStep, HADES) that embed harmful text into images and OOD-based attacks (SI-Attack, JOOD) that require iterative optimization of visual perturbations. Rather than exploiting visual perception vulnerabilities, it targets the model's reasoning and completion capability by presenting individually benign slot types in structured layouts (mind maps, tables, sunburst diagrams) that the model reassembles into harmful semantics. The method is positioned as an optimization-free, single-query alternative to prior approaches that demand white-box access or multiple iterations per sample.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The one-shot, black-box nature of StructAttack makes it practically concerning: no gradient access, no iterative tuning, and no white-box model parameters are needed, lowering the barrier for real-world exploitation. Its robustness to system-prompt defenses (retaining 47.2% ASR while typographic and OOD attacks drop to near zero) suggests that current defensive strategies targeting visual content verification are insufficient against semantically structured attacks. Open questions include whether the reliance on an external LLM (DeepSeek-Chat) for slot decomposition can be eliminated, whether the vulnerability persists as models improve their contextual safety reasoning, and what effective defenses look like when the attack surface is the model's own completion behavior rather than its visual encoder.
