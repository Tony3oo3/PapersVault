## Problem

Existing multimodal fusion methods project all modalities into a single latent space or decouple shared/private factors at only one semantic level, ignoring the hierarchical structure of multimodal data where shallow, mid, and deep features carry different granularities of meaning. This uncontrolled cross-level mixing induces semantic misalignment, error propagation, and leakage of modality-private information into shared channels, degrading representation quality and downstream task performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262061/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

CLCR consumes three modalities—linguistic token sequences (BERT), visual frame features (TCN), and acoustic features (TCN)—and optionally optical flow for UCF101. Each modality is mapped to a unified feature width d at three semantic levels, producing level-wise token sequences. The final output is a task representation formed by concatenating a fused shared descriptor (weighted across modalities) and a confidence-gated private descriptor, fed into a classifier (class label) or regressor (sentiment score in [-3,3]).

## Method

CLCR organizes each modality into a three-level semantic hierarchy (shallow/mid/deep) via BERT layer selections or multi-stage TCNs, then applies two interaction domains. IntraCED factorizes each level's tokens into shared and private subspaces using orthonormal projectors on the Stiefel manifold, restricts cross-modal attention to the shared subspace only, and gates participation with a learnable token budget projected onto a truncated simplex to enforce sparsity. InterCAD compresses shared and private streams into fixed-size summaries, computes level-importance weights from cross-modal anchors via a learned MLP, performs modality selection on the shared path through scaled dot-product attention, and aggregates private information through per-modality confidence gates. Two regularizers—whitened cross-correlation-based intra-level separation and inter-level penalties on private redundancy, shared-private leakage, and incompatible anchor mixing—stabilize the disentanglement and level selection during end-to-end supervised training.

## Results

On the four acoustic-visual benchmarks, CLCR achieves 77.92% accuracy on CREMA-D, 75.41% on Kinetics-Sounds, 73.82% on AVE, and 83.64% on UCF101, outperforming the strongest baseline (ARL) by 1.46%, 1.32%, 1.21%, and 0.58% in accuracy respectively. On multimodal sentiment analysis, CLCR reduces MAE to 0.678 on CMU-MOSI and 0.511 on CMU-MOSEI, improving Acc-2 by 2.65% and 2.54% and F1 by 2.59% and 2.72% over the best prior method (EMOE). All results are reported as mean ± std over 5 random seeds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLCR outperforms all 16 baselines across all six benchmarks. On the acoustic-visual tasks, the closest competitor is ARL [27], which CLCR surpasses by 1.21–1.46% in accuracy; MLA [35] and D&R [5] trail by roughly 4–4.5% on CREMA-D. On MSA, EMOE [7] is the strongest baseline (MAE 0.710 on MOSI, 0.536 on MOSEI), and CLCR improves MAE by 0.032 and 0.025 respectively while gaining 2.5–2.7% in F1. Disentanglement-based methods (MISA [10], FDMER [30], DMD [12], DLF [26]) are consistently outperformed by 0.02–0.06 MAE on MSA, confirming that single-level shared/private decomposition is insufficient compared to CLCR's three-level hierarchy. Gradient-modulation approaches (OGM-GE [20], AGM [11], CGGM [8]) and calibration methods (MMPareto [28], PMR [6]) show the largest gaps, with CLCR exceeding them by 5–13% in accuracy on acoustic-visual tasks, indicating that dynamic reweighting alone cannot compensate for cross-level semantic misalignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLCR sits at the intersection of two established lines of multimodal fusion research: feature disentanglement (MISA, FDMER, DMD, DLF) that decompose representations into shared and modality-private subspaces, and dynamic calibration methods (ARL, EMOE, MLA) that reweight modality contributions. The key conceptual departure is that all prior methods assume cross-modal interaction occurs at a single, undifferentiated semantic level, whereas CLCR explicitly stratifies each modality into a three-level hierarchy (shallow/mid/deep) and confines exchange to matched levels via budgeted token selection in a learned shared subspace. Technically, it repurposes Stiefel-manifold parameterization for orthonormal shared/private projectors, simplex-constrained token budgets for sparse attention, and whitened cross-correlation for identifiability regularization—each borrowed from adjacent subfields but combined in a level-wise architecture that has no direct predecessor.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By isolating cross-level semantic asynchrony as a distinct failure mode beyond generic modality heterogeneity, CLCR provides a principled explanation for why naive multi-scale fusion degrades and a structured remedy that generalizes across emotion recognition, event localization, sentiment analysis, and action recognition. The token-budget mechanism offers an interpretable knob for controlling cross-modal information flow, and the robustness-under-noise results suggest the level-wise confinement of exchange has practical value in noisy real-world settings. Open questions include whether the three-level hierarchy is optimal or whether the framework scales gracefully to more levels or modalities, whether the Stiefel-based decomposition remains stable at larger feature widths, and whether the moderate but consistent gains (1–2.5% over strongest baselines) persist on larger, more diverse benchmarks beyond the six tested here.
