## Problem

Pre-trained vision encoders such as DINOv2 produce features that are poorly aligned across modalities of the same scene: the cosine similarity between an RGB image and its corresponding depth map is nearly identical to that between two unrelated images. The paper asks how to make a vision encoder 'omnivorous'—mapping RGB, depth, and segmentation views of a scene to nearly identical points in a shared feature space—without collapsing the discriminative semantics that make the foundation model useful for downstream tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261185/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The encoder accepts any of RGB, depth, or segmentation images (and at test time, NOCS images) as input. During training, depth and segmentation maps are first colorized using a 64-bin palette quantized from the corresponding RGB image, then randomly blended with up to 50% of the RGB image (modality mixup) to span a continuous modality space. The output is a modality-invariant embedding computed from both the CLS token and 64 subsampled dense patch tokens, all L2-normalized, which can be used directly for retrieval or as frozen features for linear/DPT readout heads on depth, segmentation, or classification tasks.

## Method

The method uses a parameter-efficient teacher-student architecture on top of a frozen DINOv2 ViT: the first 8 of 12 blocks (ViT-B) are frozen as the shared backbone f*, while the last 4 blocks form a trainable adapter g (the student head), with the teacher's head g* kept frozen as a stable anchor. Training combines two objectives: (1) a symmetric InfoNCE alignment loss computed over all three modality pairs (RGB–Depth, Depth–Seg, Seg–RGB) to pull same-scene embeddings together while pushing apart different scenes, and (2) a cosine-distance anchoring (distillation) loss that keeps each modality's student embedding close to the corresponding teacher embedding, preventing representational collapse. Two data-centric strategies prevent trivial alignment: colorizing depth/segmentation maps with an RGB-derived natural palette (so the network cannot shortcut via channel statistics) and stochastic modality mixup (blending up to 50% RGB into depth/seg per sample) to encourage invariance across a continuous modality spectrum. The total loss is L_align + 10·L_anchor, with a learned temperature for InfoNCE clipped to [0, 100].

## Results

On cross-modal retrieval (the paper's primary evaluation), the Omnivorous adapter dramatically improves alignment over frozen DINOv2: on ScanNet (GAP features), R@1 jumps from 4.6% to 46.1% and Median Rank drops from 401.8 to 2.0; on MOVi, R@1 goes from 15.5% to 86.2%. Downstream, the method matches or exceeds DINOv2: linear-probe ImageNet accuracy improves from 80.4% to 83.8%, NYUv2 depth RMSE (Linear) drops from 0.405 to 0.377, and ADE20k mIoU (Linear) rises from 0.463 to 0.475. For zero-shot cross-modal transfer, feeding segmentation maps into an RGB-trained depth head yields RMSE 0.532 vs. 1.536 for DINOv2, and on out-of-distribution NOCS inputs the gap is 1.075 vs. 1.996.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline throughout is frozen DINOv2 (ViT-B/14 and ViT-L/14). Omnivorous wins decisively on the paper's core task—cross-modal retrieval—improving R@1 by 40+ points on ScanNet and 70+ points on MOVi, while reducing Median Rank from ~400 to ~2. On downstream tasks, the method wins with linear readouts (ImageNet +3.4 pp, NYUv2 depth RMSE −0.028, ADE20k mIoU +0.012, Cityscapes mIoU +0.010) but is at parity with DINOv2 when using the more expressive DPT decoder (e.g., NYUv2 RMSE 0.297 for both; Cityscapes mIoU 0.737 vs. 0.732, a slight loss). For zero-shot cross-modal transfer, the advantage is dramatic: an RGB-trained depth head fed segmentation maps achieves RMSE 0.532 vs. 1.536 for DINOv2, and on unseen NOCS inputs 1.075 vs. 1.996. k-NN classification on ImageNet is essentially tied (81.97% vs. 81.94%), confirming the anchoring loss prevents representational drift, though Omnivorous shows small regressions on fine-grained datasets (iNaturalist −1.0 pp, GLDv2 −1.8 pp) likely due to its training mix including simulated multi-object data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against two main lines of work: (1) unified co-training approaches (Omnivore, ImageBind, Unified-IO) that train a single backbone from scratch to handle multiple modalities, and (2) RGB-D pretraining schemes (CLIP2Point, CoMAE, Mask3D) that require paired data during the pretraining phase. The key departure is a post-hoc, parameter-efficient strategy: freeze a strong unimodal foundation model (DINOv2) and fine-tune only the last 4 of 12 ViT blocks as a modality-agnostic adapter, using a symmetric InfoNCE alignment loss coupled with a cosine-distance anchoring (distillation) loss to prevent representational collapse. The data-centric contributions—colorizing depth/segmentation maps with the RGB palette to block low-level shortcut learning, and stochastic modality mixup to span a continuous modality space—are distinctive relative to prior contrastive alignment work that typically relies on standard colormaps and discrete modality pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a lightweight adapter (4/12 blocks) on DINOv2 enables zero-shot cross-modal transfer (e.g., train a depth head on RGB, deploy on segmentation or NOCS inputs) without retraining, which is directly useful for robotics, AR/VR, and 3D scene understanding pipelines. The finding that cross-modal alignment *improves* linear-probe classification (80.4% → 83.8% on ImageNet) rather than degrading it challenges the assumption that alignment must come at a semantic cost. Open questions include whether the approach benefits from high-resolution fine-tuning (as DINOv2 itself uses), whether aligning modalities during pretraining rather than post-hoc would yield deeper gains, and whether the slight regressions on fine-grained datasets (iNaturalist, GLDv2) can be addressed through training-mix adjustments. The authors also note potential for generative applications (e.g., image-to-depth conditioning on Omnivorous features).
