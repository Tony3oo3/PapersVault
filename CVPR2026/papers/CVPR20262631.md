## Problem

Descriptive image captions used to train Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) suffer from insufficient precision and granularity: human-annotated captions (e.g., COCO) are incomplete and omit fine-grained details, while captions from SoTA LMMs still miss important objects, 3D spatial relationships, and fine-grained attributes. This limits the vision-language alignment and downstream reasoning performance of LMMs trained on such data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262631/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a single RGB image as input and produces a comprehensive natural-language caption that integrates structured visual attributes: fine-grained object categories (across 891k animal species, 427k plant species, food, logos, landmarks, aircraft, and celebrities), low-level properties (depth, bounding-box size, facial emotion), OCR text, and relational information (human-object interactions, 2D absolute/relative positions, and 3D depth-based spatial ordering). The output is a single coherent paragraph rather than a list of attributes, with object grounding information (bounding boxes) embedded to support region-level understanding.

## Method

Cap-Workflow is a multi-stage pipeline that first localizes objects using a combination of in-domain (Group DETR, Conditional DETR) and open-world detection models, merging boxes with confidence >0.5 and applying NMS at IoU 0.75. It then extracts instance-level attributes via dedicated specialist models (fine-grained classifiers, depth estimation, emotion recognition, OCR) and relational attributes via an HOI detector and geometric/depth-based spatial reasoning. A large language model (Qwen2-72B-AWQ) is prompted in two stages: first to fuse per-object attributes into region-level captions (using a reference caption from InternVL2-26B), then to integrate relational attributes (HOI, 2D/3D spatial relations, object counts) into a final image-level caption. The key insight is that off-the-shelf visual specialists—originally trained for tasks like depth estimation or fine-grained classification—provide structured, hallucination-free attributes that a generalist LMM cannot reliably produce, and structured prompt engineering can weave these into fluent, information-dense captions.

## Results

On seven VQA benchmarks, LLaVA-v1.5 trained with Cap-Workflow-1M achieves 80.9 on VQAv2, 64.2 on GQA, 71.0 on ScienceQA, and 59.4 on Ai2D, outperforming the LLaVA-v1.5 baseline (78.5, 62.0, 66.8, 55.5) and ShareGPT4V (80.6, 63.3, 68.4). The high-resolution LLaVA-NeXT* variant reaches 82.4 on VQAv2, 78.8 on DocVQA, 65.2 on GQA, and 71.2 on Ai2D, surpassing the LLaVA-NeXT baseline (81.8, 74.4, 64.2, 66.6) and Monkey (80.3, 66.5, 60.7, 62.6). On LMM benchmarks, LLaVA-NeXT* achieves 69.3 on MMBench, 40.1 on MM-Vet, 72.2 on SEED, and 65.7 on SEED-Bench, leading all compared methods. The captioning-specific comparison (Table 6) shows Cap-Workflow outperforming human COCO annotations, InternVL2-26B, and LLaVA-NeXT-34B across OKVQA, GQA, ScienceQA, TextVQA, MMBench, MM-Vet, and SEED-Bench for both LLaVA-v1.5 and LLaVA-NeXT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Cap-Workflow consistently outperforms all captioning-method baselines (ShareGPT4V, DenseFusion, DCI, DOCCI, IT) on both LLaVA-v1.5 and LLaVA-NeXT, with the largest margins over DCI (e.g., +4.9 on MMBench, +8.0 on MM-Vet for LLaVA-v1.5) and DOCCI (+4.2 on MMBench, +5.9 on MM-Vet). Against the LLaVA-NeXT baseline, Cap-Workflow gains +0.6 on VQAv2, +4.4 on DocVQA, +1.0 on GQA, and +4.6 on Ai2D, while also leading on MMBench (+1.9), MM-Vet (+6.2), SEED (+2.0), and SEED-Bench (+1.0). Compared to Monkey, the strongest high-resolution competitor, Cap-Workflow leads by +2.1 on VQAv2, +12.3 on DocVQA, +4.5 on GQA, and +8.6 on Ai2D. In the captioning-specific ablation (Table 6), Cap-Workflow beats human COCO annotations by +2.0 on OKVQA and +1.6 on MMBench for LLaVA-v1.5, and surpasses InternVL2-26B and LLaVA-NeXT-34B on all seven benchmarks for both model variants. The only notable weakness is MMBench-CN, where the absence of Chinese data in Cap-Workflow-1M limits performance, and POPE, where detection noise slightly degrades hallucination resistance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of generating training captions via a single generalist LMM (e.g., ShareGPT4V, DenseFusion) or human annotation (COCO, LAION) by instead composing captions from structured attributes extracted by multiple off-the-shelf visual specialists (detection, depth, emotion, fine-grained classification, HOI, OCR) and then fusing them with an LLM. Conceptually, it repurposes models originally trained for narrow perception tasks into a captioning pipeline, treating the LLM as a cognitive integrator rather than a perceptual engine. The approach is positioned as complementary to prior captioning methods (DCI, DOCCI, IT, ReCap) rather than a direct extension of any single one, and the two released datasets (Cap-Workflow-1M, Cap-Workflow-118K) are built on top of existing image collections (DenseFusion, COCO).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value lies in providing a scalable, cost-effective captioning pipeline that outperforms both human and single-LMM annotations without requiring expensive API calls, and in releasing a 1.1M-image dataset that practitioners can immediately use for LMM pretraining. However, the paper acknowledges several limitations: detection noise degrades performance on hallucination-sensitive benchmarks (POPE), the open-source OCR model constrains TextVQA gains, and the absence of multilingual data hurts MMBench-CN. Open questions include whether the pipeline can be extended to video, whether adding more specialist models yields diminishing returns, and how to reduce the noise introduced by imperfect detection without sacrificing recall.
