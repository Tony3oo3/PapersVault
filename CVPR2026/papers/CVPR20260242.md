## Problem

Existing multimodal reasoning benchmarks evaluate only forward chain-of-thought reasoning, yet real educational scenarios require teachers to retrospectively trace the underlying errors behind a student's incorrect answer and generate targeted corrective feedback. This educational diagnostic reasoning capability—reconstructing plausible erroneous reasoning chains and producing error-prevention principles—remains entirely unexplored in large multimodal models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260242/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a multimodal input tuple (image, question text, a specific incorrect answer, and a correct step-wise rationale as reference) and must produce two outputs in a single pass: (1) a stepwise erroneous reasoning chain (averaging ~6.2 steps) that logically and coherently derives the given incorrect answer through a representative student misconception, and (2) corrective feedback comprising a question-specific error explanation plus a high-level generalizable principle. The erroneous chain must not merely contain a random mistake but must capture commonly occurring errors (visual perception, calculation, reasoning, knowledge, or misinterpretation) at the appropriate step position, with the final step matching the provided incorrect answer.

## Method

EduDiag is constructed via a two-stage AI-assisted annotation pipeline: GPT-4o identifies tricky steps in correct rationales, introduces one of five error types at those steps, and generates five candidate erroneous chains with corresponding incorrect answers; three human annotators then verify logical accuracy, representativeness of the error, and diversity, retaining up to three chains per question. Evaluation uses three independent LLMs (Gemini-2, Claude-3.7, Seed-1.6) to score erroneous chains on a 0–3 scale (consistency with ground truth, logical coherence, relevance to the incorrect answer) and feedback on a 1–5 scale, plus a correction accuracy metric where a fixed Qwen2-VL-7B student model answers a constructed multiple-choice question using the generated principle. Beyond the benchmark, the paper applies GRPO with two reward functions—R1 (BERTScore alignment of feedback) and R2 (accuracy of autonomously predicting the incorrect answer without it being provided in input)—and finds that R2 significantly improves error-tracing quality (S_e=3 proportion nearly doubles) and downstream correction accuracy, while R1 alone yields no gain over SFT.

## Results

On the EduDiag benchmark, GPT-5 achieves the best overall performance among all 24 evaluated LMMs with S_e=2.67, S_f=3.83, and Acc_p=52.22%, yet still falls 10.45% short of the ground-truth principle (62.67% Acc_p). Among open-source models, GLM-4.1V-Thinking-9B leads zero-shot (S_e=2.07, Acc_p=51.66%), while Qwen3-VL-8B achieves the best fine-tuned result (S_e=2.09, Acc_p=51.81%). Supervised fine-tuning yields only ~1% marginal gains in Acc_p across most models, and GRPO with reward R_2 (accuracy on candidate incorrect answers) provides the largest improvement, boosting Qwen3-VL-8B to 53.85% Acc_p and increasing the proportion of S_e=3 chains from 14.52% to 32.18%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 24 LMMs (18 open-source, 6 proprietary) as baselines on the EduDiag benchmark. Among proprietary models, GPT-5 leads with 52.22% overall Acc_p, outperforming GPT-4o (51.48%), Claude-3.5-Sonnet (51.92%), and Gemini-2.5-Pro (50.87%); however, all proprietary models remain 10+ points below the ground-truth principle (62.67%). Among open-source zero-shot models, GLM-4.1V-Thinking-9B (51.66%) and Llama-3.2-90B (50.76%) are the strongest, with the latter benefiting from scale (Llama-3.2-11B achieves only 49.89%). After SFT, Qwen3-VL-8B reaches 51.81% Acc_p, a mere ~1% gain over its zero-shot counterpart (51.23%), confirming that SFT alone is insufficient. GRPO with reward R_2 (predicting the incorrect answer from self-constructed chains) yields the largest improvement: Qwen3-VL-8B jumps to 53.85% Acc_p and Qwen2.5-VL-7B to 53.46%, surpassing all SFT-only results. In the mathematics domain specifically, even GPT-5 achieves only S_e=2.38 and 33.94% Acc_p, indicating that mathematical error tracing remains the hardest sub-task across all models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two prior lines of work. First, multimodal reasoning benchmarks (M3CoT, MMMU, MATH-Vision) evaluate forward chain-of-thought reasoning but contain no annotations of erroneous reasoning processes or corrections. Second, prior LMM-based error correction work (VISCO, FIRE) assumes the student's full reasoning chain is available for diagnosis, whereas this paper introduces the harder retrospective setting where only the incorrect answer is given and the model must reverse-engineer the underlying misconception. The task is grounded in cognitive science literature on educational diagnostic reasoning (Corbett; Liu et al.), but no prior benchmark operationalizes this for multimodal models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that error tracing—not feedback generation—is the fundamental bottleneck, with even GPT-5 achieving only 52.22% correction accuracy versus 62.67% for ground-truth principles, and SFT yielding merely ~1% gains in correction accuracy despite large improvements in chain quality scores. The finding that GRPO with an accuracy reward on candidate incorrect answers (R2) outperforms both SFT and BERTScore-based feedback rewards (R1) suggests that incentivizing natural derivation of errors is more effective than surface-level feedback alignment. Open questions include whether the gap to ground truth can be closed with more targeted RL, whether the findings generalize beyond the three domains tested, and whether the distractor-generation application scales to real educational item writing.
