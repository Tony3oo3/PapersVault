## Problem

Existing 3D reconstruction kernels (Gaussians, Student's t, exponential functions) act as non-ideal low-pass filters whose frequency responses decay gradually rather than dropping to zero at a cutoff, causing high-frequency aliasing leakage in discrete sampling. No prior kernel simultaneously achieves the ideal low-pass property (instantaneous spectral truncation) and practical spatial efficiency for rasterization-based rendering.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263765/cropped_page4_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view RGB images with known camera intrinsics and extrinsics, and produces a 3D scene representation parameterized by Jinc or modulated kernels (position, covariance, color) rendered via differentiable rasterization. Low-resolution experiments use 64×64 and 128×128 images downsampled from NeRF-Synthetic via Lanczos interpolation; the Jinc kernel's effective spatial range is fixed at α=30. For high-resolution evaluation, modulated kernels are dropped into existing 3DGS and SSS pipelines with only the kernel function replaced, preserving all other training configurations.

## Method

The paper derives the Jinc kernel as the inverse Fourier transform of a 3D spherical ideal low-pass filter, yielding a spatial response proportional to j₁(r)/r (spherical Bessel function) that drops to zero exactly at the cutoff frequency. Jinc Splatting is then formulated with a closed-form ray integral (Eq. 11), projective coordinate transformation to an elliptical rendering footprint, and analytic gradient backpropagation through the Bessel-function-based integral. Because the Jinc kernel decays only as r⁻¹ in space (causing rectangular truncation artifacts and high memory cost), the authors introduce a frequency modulation strategy: multiplying the base kernel by ω + (1−ω)cos(f₀x) with f₀ set to half the FWHM, which shifts spectral energy to approximate the ideal filter while preserving the base kernel's fast spatial decay (e⁻ʳ² for Gaussian, r⁻² for Student's t). This modulation is applied to both 3DGS and SSS, yielding consistent PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS gains across Mip-NeRF360, Tanks & Temples, and Deep Blending.

## Results

On Mip-NeRF360, the modulated SSS kernel (Ours(SSS)) achieves 29.96 dB PSNR, 0.893 SSIM, and 0.143 LPIPS, outperforming the SSS baseline (29.90 dB) and 3DGS (28.69 dB). On Tanks & Temples, Ours(SSS) reaches 24.95 dB PSNR, surpassing SSS by 0.08 dB and 3DGS by 1.81 dB. On Deep Blending, Ours(SSS) attains 30.17 dB PSNR with 0.911 SSIM, the best among all compared methods. In low-resolution settings (64×64), the Jinc kernel achieves 29.87 dB PSNR, a 5.86 dB improvement over 3DGS and 0.70 dB over SSS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The modulated 3DGS kernel (Ours(3DGS)) consistently outperforms the original 3DGS [18] across all three datasets, with the largest gain on Tanks & Temples (+0.72 dB PSNR) and Deep Blending (+0.43 dB), while also improving SSIM and LPIPS. The modulated SSS kernel (Ours(SSS)) achieves marginal but consistent improvements over SSS [53] (+0.06 to +0.10 dB PSNR), which the authors attribute to SSS's already-slow frequency-domain decay limiting additional modulation benefit. Against implicit rendering (Mip-NeRF [2]), Ours(SSS) leads by 0.73 dB on Mip-NeRF360 and 2.73 dB on Tanks & Temples. Compared to other Gaussian-based methods, Ours(SSS) surpasses 3DHGS [25] by 0.40 dB on Mip-NeRF360, Fre-GS [48] by 2.11 dB, Scaffold-GS [29] by 1.12 dB, and 3DGS-MCMC [20] by 0.07 dB on Mip-NeRF360. On Deep Blending, Ours(SSS) achieves the best PSNR (30.17 dB) and SSIM (0.911), edging out Scaffold-GS [29] (30.21 dB PSNR but lower SSIM 0.906) and SSS [53] (30.07 dB). The Jinc kernel in low-resolution experiments (Table 2) shows a dramatic 5.86 dB PSNR advantage over 3DGS at 64×64, confirming the theoretical anti-aliasing benefit of the ideal low-pass filter design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) and 3D Student Splatting (SSS) by replacing their reconstruction kernels with signal-processing-motivated alternatives. Its key conceptual departure is reframing explicit 3D primitives as low-pass filters in the frequency domain, positioning Gaussians, exponential functions, and Student's t distributions as imperfect filters that leak high-frequency energy, and introducing the Jinc kernel (inverse Fourier transform of the 3D ideal low-pass filter) as the theoretically correct reconstruction kernel. The modulation strategy—multiplying base kernels by a cosine term to shift and reshape their spectral response—sits at the intersection of classical anti-aliasing filter design (Mip-NeRF, Mip-NeRF360, Zip-NeRF) and the Gaussian splatting rendering pipeline, bridging a gap that prior splatting methods addressed only heuristically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The signal-processing lens provides a principled, quantitative framework for evaluating and designing 3D reconstruction kernels, moving beyond ad-hoc choices toward filter-theoretic criteria (cutoff sharpness, energy concentration, decay speed). However, the Jinc kernel's slow spatial decay (∝ r⁻¹) and inherent ringing limit its practical utility, and the modulation strategy, while effective, only partially closes the gap to ideal filtering. Open questions include whether a kernel can simultaneously achieve ideal frequency-domain cutoff and fast spatial decay, whether the modulation approach extends to dynamic 4D scenes or feed-forward pipelines, and whether generative models that effectively increase sampling rates could obviate the need for aggressive low-pass filtering altogether.
