## Problem

Multi-modal contrastive models like CLIP exhibit a persistent modality gap—image and text embeddings occupy linearly separable regions of the shared embedding space—despite the contrastive loss explicitly rewarding alignment. Prior work has not established a clear causal explanation for the gap's formation nor determined whether closing it helps or hurts downstream performance. This paper asks whether the gap is a bug or a feature by linking it directly to model robustness under embedding-space perturbations.

## Input / Output

The method operates on pre-computed, L2-normalized embeddings (e.g., 512-d for ViT-B-16, 768-d for ViT-L-14) from any multi-modal model, requiring no access to raw images or text. It takes the full set of text embeddings and image embeddings, computes the global gap vector and the principal components of the retrieved modality, and outputs a single translated set of text embeddings (shifted by the projected gap vector) that can be dropped into any cross-modal nearest-neighbor pipeline. Robustness is measured as the fraction of queries whose nearest-neighbor assignment is unchanged after adding noise (Gaussian, quantization, or rephrasing) to the embeddings.

## Method

The paper proves two theorems explaining gap formation: (1) when modalities are initialized as tight, separated clusters, gradient descent on the contrastive loss first shrinks within-modality variance along the initial gap direction (Theorem 3.1), and (2) once a zero-variance direction exists, double-stochasticity of the softmax assignment matrices ensures the gap vector becomes orthogonal to both modalities and remains constant (Theorem 3.2). It then proves that under this orthogonality, translating one modality toward the other along the gap vector monotonically increases robustness to zero-mean noise (Theorem 3.4) while preserving all cross-modal nearest-neighbor assignments (Theorem 3.5), so clean accuracy is unchanged. The practical algorithm projects the empirical gap vector onto the orthogonal complement of the retrieved modality's PCA subspace (Eq. 11) and translates the retrieved modality by this projected vector—a single matrix-vector operation applied as a post-processing step with no retraining, fine-tuning, or architectural modification.

## Results

The paper's main empirical finding is that closing the modality gap via a simple post-processing translation monotonically increases robustness (measured as the probability that nearest-neighbor retrieval is unchanged under noise) while preserving clean accuracy, across CLIP (ViT-L-14, ViT-B-16) and SigLIP on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and A-OKVQA. Under Gaussian noise (σ²I), robustness improves substantially as the gap is closed (α increases) and degrades when the gap is expanded, with clean zero-shot and VQA accuracy remaining unchanged as predicted by Theorem 3.5. For quantization noise, robustness also improves when the gap is minimized in the quantized space. For text rephrasing noise (a setting where theoretical assumptions are violated), accuracy under noise still increases when the gap is closed, with no change in clean accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against other gap-closing or robustness methods in a head-to-head benchmark; instead, it compares each model's original embedding space (baseline) against the same model after applying the proposed gap-closing post-processing step. The key comparison is: original model vs. gap-closed model, measured on both clean accuracy and robustness under noise. The gap-closed version matches the original on clean accuracy (zero-shot classification, VQA, retrieval) while showing consistent and monotonic improvements in robustness under Gaussian noise, quantization, and text rephrasing. The paper positions its method as complementary to prior gap-closing approaches (e.g., [17, 18, 19, 23]) by requiring no retraining, no architectural changes, and being applicable as a drop-in post-processing step for any cross-modal nearest-neighbor task. The theoretical contribution (Theorems 3.1–3.5) builds on and extends Zhang et al. [43], showing that dimensionality collapse is neither necessary nor sufficient for gap formation and highlighting the role of double-stochasticity in the convergence dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical analysis of Zhang et al. [43] on the modality gap by showing that dimensional collapse is neither necessary nor sufficient for gap formation, instead attributing it to the interaction between tight-cluster initialization and contrastive loss dynamics. It builds on Liang et al. [12] who first characterized the gap empirically, and on the broader CLIP/SigLIP contrastive learning literature, but departs from prior explanations (information imbalance, dimensional collapse) by providing a more nuanced gradient-dynamics account. The key conceptual departure is connecting the gap to robustness: prior works [6, 12, 22, 43] debated whether the gap helps or hurts downstream performance without reaching consensus, while this paper proves a monotonic relationship between gap size and vulnerability to embedding-space perturbations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway — that a single post-processing translation of one modality toward the other's mean can substantially improve robustness to quantization, Gaussian noise, and text rephrasing without sacrificing clean accuracy — is immediately applicable to any system using cross-modal nearest-neighbor retrieval (RAG, zero-shot classification, retrieval-augmented generation). However, the theoretical guarantees rely on the orthogonality assumption and tight-cluster initialization, which the paper acknowledges do not hold for all noise types (e.g., rephrasing noise lies within the text subspace). Open questions include whether the algorithm generalizes to non-orthogonal gap directions, to multi-modal settings beyond image-text, and to noise models where the gap-closing direction interacts with the noise covariance.
