## Problem

High-content cellular imaging (e.g., Cell Painting) produces rich morphological profiles that capture whole-cell responses to genetic and chemical perturbations, but existing integration methods treat these phenotypes as node features, overlooking that they primarily encode relational signals about how perturbations reshape molecular interactions. This node-centric treatment introduces substantial noise and fails to extract the edge-level, cellular-scale evidence needed for accurate genotype-phenotype mapping across tasks like GRN inference, DTI prediction, and subtype-specific subnetwork discovery.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-extracted cellular morphological embeddings (from Cell Painting/JUMP-CP imaging), a heterogeneous molecular interaction graph (e.g., STRING-based regulatory edges, drug-protein links), and optionally single-cell transcriptomic profiles. A notable practical constraint is that morphological data is often incomplete—only ~39% of test links in the hESC dataset have full images—so the framework must degrade gracefully under missing modalities. Outputs are binary interaction predictions (TF-gene links, drug-target scores) and, for the subnetwork task, induced subgraphs per disease subtype with quantified structural divergence (GED, DCS, CDV).

## Method

KERNEL's core insight is an edge-centric phenotypic knowledge extraction module: for each pair of molecular types (e.g., TF–gene, drug–protein), it computes cosine similarity between their morphological embeddings and adds the top-K% most similar cross-type pairs as new edges of a dedicated relation type r_morph, weighted by the similarity score. These augmented edges are then processed by a relation-specific heterogeneous GNN that uses GAT convolutions for edges originating from one entity class and GIN convolutions for the other, with morphology-derived weights modulating message passing. A retrieval-style contrastive loss aligns morphological embeddings so that known interacting pairs are pulled together and random pairs pushed apart, while a structure-aware alignment loss (based on Jaccard neighborhood overlap) enforces local topological consistency in the learned node embeddings. The overall training objective jointly optimizes the contrastive morphological alignment, the structural consistency regularizer, and a supervised cross-entropy link-prediction loss, with hyperparameters λ₁ and λ₂ balancing the auxiliary terms.

## Results

On GRN inference (the primary task), KERNEL achieves AUPR of 0.381 on hESC (TFs+500 genes) and 0.424 on hHEP (TFs+1000 genes), improving AUPR by 56.8% and 38.1% over the strongest baseline GENELink, respectively. On DTI prediction, KERNEL reaches AUC 0.950 and F1 0.700 on CRISPR, surpassing MOTIVE (AUC 0.842, F1 0.509) by 12.8% and 37.5%. For BRCA subtype-specific subnetwork discovery, KERNEL produces networks with substantially larger structural divergence between subtypes (GED 0.659 vs. GeSubNet's 0.285) and higher coverage (CDV 0.251 vs. 0.189).

## Baselines & Comparisons

KERNEL outperforms all nine baselines across every task and metric. In GRN inference, the strongest baseline GENELink [8] (GAT-based, using scRNA-seq and known regulatory edges) is surpassed by KERNEL by 56.8% AUPR on hESC-500 and 38.1% on hHEP-500, with even larger gaps at 1000 genes (171% AUPR improvement on hESC-1000). GCLink [46] (graph contrastive learning) trails GENELink and is further behind KERNEL, particularly on Hit@500. Tree-based methods (GENIE3 [13], GRNBoost2 [22]) and deep-learning methods (GNE [14], CNNC [47], DeepSEM [34]) all achieve AUPR below 0.06, far below KERNEL's 0.38–0.42 range. For DTI prediction, MOTIVE [2] (the strongest DTI baseline, incorporating multi-modal data with static edges) is outperformed by KERNEL by 12.8% AUC and 37.5% F1 on CRISPR, and by 14.0% AUC and 18.9% F1 on ORF; GNN baselines (GraphSAGE, GATv2) and simpler models (BiLinear, MLP) are further behind. In subnetwork discovery, GeSubNet [43] produces less distinct subtype networks (GED 0.285 vs. KERNEL's 0.659) and lower coverage (CDV 0.189 vs. 0.251), indicating KERNEL captures more biologically meaningful subtype-specific regulatory modules.

## Relation to Prior Work

KERNEL departs from the dominant node-centric paradigm in knowledge-aware molecular interaction learning (e.g., MOTIVE, PolyGene), where phenotypic data are attached as node features, by instead using phenotypic similarity to dynamically augment the graph with confidence-weighted edges. It builds on heterogeneous GNN architectures (GAT, GIN) and contrastive representation learning, but repurposes morphological embeddings as relational evidence rather than descriptive attributes. The framework also extends subnetwork discovery beyond correlation-based or static-graph methods (ARACNe, WGCNA, GeSubNet) by incorporating phenotype-driven edge construction that highlights subtype-specific regulatory modules.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By treating cellular morphology as edge-level relational signal rather than node-level feature, KERNEL offers a principled denoising mechanism for the inherently high-dimensional and noisy phenotypic data produced by Cell Painting and JUMP-CP assays, which is a practical bottleneck in high-content profiling. The demonstrated robustness under missing modalities and cold-start drug scenarios suggests the framework could be deployed in real-world pipelines where imaging data are incomplete. Open questions include whether the edge-centric formulation generalizes to additional biological modalities (spatial transcriptomics, proteomics), how the learned morphological edges map to known mechanistic pathways, and whether the approach scales to genome-wide networks beyond the ~1000-gene scale tested here.
