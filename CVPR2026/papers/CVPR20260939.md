## Problem

Adversarial robustness evaluations for multimodal LLMs have focused on simple tasks like coarse-grained classification and short captioning, using inconsistent datasets and success criteria (keyword matching, CLIP similarity, LLM-as-judge) that hinder fair comparison. It remains unclear whether existing transfer-based attacks generalize to the open-ended, question-conditioned VQA and OCR tasks that MLLMs are actually deployed for, where targets are implicit in the question-answer format rather than explicitly provided as a category or caption.

## Input / Output

The attack takes an RGB image, a natural-language question (multiple-choice for VQA or open-ended for OCR), and a designated targeted wrong answer, and produces an L∞-bounded perturbation (ε ∈ {8/255, 16/255}) to add to the image. For OCR, the perturbation is spatially restricted to an expanded bounding box around the relevant text region rather than applied globally. The perturbed image is then queried against a black-box MLLM (e.g., GPT-4.1, Claude 3.7, Gemini 2.0) with the original question; success requires the model to output the targeted wrong answer, evaluated over three independent runs per example.

## Method

Omni-Attack optimizes a perturbation against an ensemble of surrogate CLIP models (ViT-H-14-378/DNF, ViT-SO400M/SigLIP, ViT-H-14-CLIPA/Datacomp1B) to maximize similarity to a target while minimizing similarity to the ground truth. A key innovation is a target-construction pipeline that uses multiple LLMs to generate question-conditioned textual captions (describing what the image would look like if the target answer were correct) and corresponding visual targets via a text-to-image model, with cycle verification (feeding the generated caption back to an LLM with the question to confirm it yields the intended option) and multi-target ensembling to reduce variance. For OCR, a location-aware pipeline uses PaddleOCR to detect text bounding boxes, identifies the question-relevant box by erasing it and checking whether the MLLM's answer changes, then restricts optimization to that expanded region before pasting the modified patch back. Transferability is further improved through four regularization/augmentation techniques: DropPath (stochastic residual-block skipping in the surrogate), PatchDrop (random patch discarding in ViT surrogates), exponential moving average of the perturbation, and random differentiable JPEG compression.

## Results

On AdvRobustBench, Omni-Attack achieves up to 71.8% targeted ASR on GPT-4.1 (MMBench split, ε=8/255) and 80.1% at ε=16/255, with comparable performance on GPT-4o (69.8%) and Gemini 2.0 (65.8%). The Claude family is notably more robust, with only 15.5% ASR on Claude 3.7 at ε=8/255. OCR tasks are the hardest setting, peaking at 25.2% ASR on GPT-4.1 (ε=8/255), while on the NIPS 2017 captioning benchmark Omni-Attack reaches 81.2% ASR on GPT-4.1 at ε=8/255.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Omni-Attack substantially outperforms all four prior baselines across every victim model and benchmark. On the MMBench split (ε=8/255), it achieves 71.8% ASR on GPT-4.1 versus 3.4% (AttackVLM), 6.9% (SSA-CWA), 9.5% (AnyAttack), and 2.8% (M-Attack) — a 7–25× improvement. The gap is even more pronounced on Claude 3.7, where Omni-Attack reaches 15.5% while all baselines stay below 3.2%, and on Gemini 2.0 (65.8% vs. ≤7.3%). On the NIPS 2017 captioning benchmark, Omni-Attack scores 81.2% on GPT-4.1, nearly doubling the best prior method (SSA-CWA at 42.3%) and tripling AnyAttack (36.3%). Even against non-CLIP auto-regressive victims (Emu3, Fuyu-8B), Omni-Attack leads by 18–20 points over the strongest baseline (AnyAttack). The consistent dominance across all settings indicates that the target-construction pipeline and location-aware OCR strategy are the primary drivers of the performance gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the transfer-based black-box adversarial attack paradigm (optimizing perturbations against surrogate CLIP-family models) to the open-ended, question-conditioned VQA and OCR setting, where prior work (AttackVLM, SSA-CWA, AnyAttack, M-Attack) operated on simpler targets like object categories or single-sentence captions. The key conceptual departure is the target-construction pipeline: because AdvRobustBench targets are implicit in question-answer pairs rather than explicit sentences or images, the authors use LLMs to generate question-conditioned textual and visual targets with cycle verification and multi-target ensembling. For OCR, the location-aware pipeline (using PaddleOCR bounding boxes to restrict optimization to question-relevant regions) is a new component not present in prior VLM attack methods. The regularization techniques (DropPath, PatchDrop, perturbation EMA, random JPEGify) are repurposed from the broader adversarial transferability literature rather than introduced here.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The standardized AdvRobustBench benchmark addresses a real gap: prior MLLM adversarial evaluations used inconsistent datasets, target definitions, and success criteria, making cross-method comparison nearly impossible. The finding that GPT-4.1 achieves 71.8% targeted ASR at ε=8/255 while Claude 3.7 stays below 16% under the same conditions highlights that adversarial robustness varies dramatically across proprietary models and is not simply a function of model scale or capability. Open questions include whether the Claude robustness gap stems from specific training choices (the authors speculate about divergent visual training pipelines), whether the OCR difficulty reflects a fundamental limitation of CLIP-based surrogates for text, and what defenses (if any) can close the gap without degrading clean performance. The paper does not propose defenses, leaving the practical security question unresolved.
