## Problem

Existing Generalized Category Discovery (GCD) methods assume labeled and unlabeled data share the same distribution, an assumption that breaks down when models face simultaneous domain shifts and novel category emergence. Prior cross-domain GCD approaches (HiLo, CDAD-Net) rely on heuristic feature separation or adversarial alignment without modeling the underlying causal mechanisms of domain shift, leaving no principled formulation of causal risk or identifiability guarantees for robust category discovery.

## Input / Output

The method takes a labeled source-domain set of RGB images with known-category labels and an unlabeled target-domain set containing both known and novel categories, where the target may originate from a different domain (e.g., clean→corrupted in SSB-C, or Real→Painting/Sketch/Quickdraw/Clipart/Infograph in DomainNet). A DINO-pretrained ViT-B/16 backbone (fine-tuning only the last transformer block) processes each image, and the model outputs cluster assignments for all unlabeled samples. Evaluation uses the Hungarian algorithm to compute optimal label-to-cluster matching, reporting overall, old-class, and new-class clustering accuracy on both source and target domains.

## Method

CausalGCD decomposes latent representations into semantic causal features U and domain-specific factors S (assumed independent), then introduces a Causal Dependency Risk (CDR) that measures prediction sensitivity to counterfactual interventions on U via an MLP-based intervention module with VIB-style Gaussian parameterization. The paper derives a theoretically computable upper bound (Theorem 2) linking target-domain CDR to source-domain quantities through Expected Collaborative Deviation, Expected Relational Inconsistency, and β-divergence, making the risk optimizable without target labels. Exogeneity is enforced by maximizing mutual information between source and target class prototypes via a cosine-similarity alignment loss, while a Causal Geometric Manifold Constraint (CGMC) uses Fourier-based amplitude/phase style transfer to generate cross-domain samples and then minimizes the discrepancy in top-m eigenvector-based geometric correlations between every known–unknown category pair across domains. The overall objective jointly optimizes source and target CDR, a KL-based empirical-to-expected risk bridge, the exogeneity loss, CGMC, and a semantic separation margin, all trained end-to-end with SGD and cosine-annealed learning rate.

## Results

On the DomainNet benchmark (Real→Painting), CausalGCD achieves 69.9% overall clustering accuracy on the source domain and 48.0% on the target domain, outperforming the strongest baseline FREE (67.7% / 45.6%) by 2.2% and 2.4% respectively. On the SSB-C corruption benchmark, CausalGCD leads on all three datasets under corruption: 57.8% on CUB-C, 41.5% on Scars-C, and 37.2% on FGVC-C, exceeding FREE by 2.1%, 2.6%, and 2.2% respectively. The method also improves on clean (source) domains, e.g., 62.2% vs. 60.4% on CUB-C original, confirming cross-domain stability beyond just the shifted target.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CausalGCD outperforms all 10 baselines on the majority of domain pairs across both benchmarks. Against the strongest cross-domain baseline FREE, CausalGCD gains 2.2–2.4% on DomainNet target domains (e.g., 48.0% vs. 45.6% on Painting, 31.2% vs. 29.3% on Clipart) and 2.1–2.6% on SSB-C corrupted domains. The previous best cross-domain method HiLo is surpassed by a larger margin of 5.9–6.4% on DomainNet target accuracy (e.g., 48.0% vs. 42.1% on Painting). Non-domain-aware GCD methods (SimGCD, SPTNet, RLCD) and NCD methods (RankStats+, UNO+) suffer severe degradation under domain shift, with target-domain accuracies often below 20%, while CausalGCD maintains 17–48% depending on the domain gap. On the source (clean) domain, CausalGCD also leads or matches FREE (69.9% vs. 67.7% on Real+Painting), indicating that causal regularization does not sacrifice in-domain performance. The only minor exception is on Real+Infograph where CausalGCD scores 17.5% vs. FREE's 18.1% on the target, a 0.6% gap likely attributable to the extreme style difference of the Infograph domain.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two active research threads: cross-domain Generalized Category Discovery (GCD) and causal representation learning for domain invariance. Prior cross-domain GCD methods (HiLo, CDAD-Net, FREE) address domain shift through heuristic mechanisms—adversarial alignment, semantic-domain disentanglement, or frequency-domain perturbations—without a principled causal account of why domain-specific features are spurious. Meanwhile, causal domain adaptation work (IRM, iCaRL, EAGLE, Lv et al.) provides theoretical tools for invariant representation learning but has not been applied to the category discovery setting. CausalGCD carves new ground by being the first to define a structured causal graph for cross-domain GCD, introduce a counterfactual-based Causal Dependency Risk with a theoretically derived upper bound, and couple it with a geometric manifold constraint that preserves known-unknown category relationships across domains.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: real-world deployment of category discovery systems inevitably encounters simultaneous domain shifts and novel category emergence, yet prior methods assume a single distribution. The causal-theoretic framing (identifiability of counterfactual risk, exogeneity/monotonicity conditions) provides a principled lens that could inform future work beyond GCD, and the CGMC offers a reusable mechanism for preserving inter-category geometry under distributional change. Limitations include reliance on strong causal assumptions (S ⊥ U, S ⊥ Y|U) that may not hold in all settings, a Fourier-based style transfer that is specific to natural images, and evaluation limited to two benchmarks with ViT-B/16. Open questions include whether the framework extends to video, 3D, or multimodal discovery, and whether the causal assumptions can be relaxed or verified empirically.
