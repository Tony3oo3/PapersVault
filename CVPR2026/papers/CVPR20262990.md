## Problem

Existing text-to-image models struggle with compositional synthesis—accurately rendering scenes with multiple objects, diverse attributes, and intricate spatial-semantic relationships. While reinforcement learning offers a promising training signal without requiring ground-truth images, large-scale RL for compositional T2I faces severe stability challenges due to the heterogeneous nature of compositional sub-skills (object existence, attribute binding, relational understanding, counting). The paper asks whether a principled, difficulty-aware curriculum can stabilize and accelerate compositional RL training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes natural-language text prompts synthesized from scene graphs of controlled compositional difficulty (ranging from single-object scenes to multi-object compositions with multiple attributes and relations). It produces RGB images that are evaluated not by pixel-level metrics but by a suite of binary visual question-answer pairs (object presence, object count, attribute binding, relation confirmation) scored by a multimodal LLM, yielding a scalar reward in [0,1]. No ground-truth reference images are required at any stage of training or evaluation.

## Method

CompGen defines a multiplicative difficulty criterion over scene graphs—|O| · max(1, |A|/|O|) · max(1, |R|/|O|)—that captures combinatorial complexity from object count, attribute density, and relational connectivity. An adaptive Metropolis-Hastings MCMC sampler with an energy function measuring deviation from a target difficulty interval generates scene graphs at prescribed complexity levels, using add/delete graph operations with a simulated-annealing temperature schedule. Each sampled graph is instantiated with concrete semantics, converted to a text prompt via a constrained LLM (DeepSeek-V3), and paired with programmatically generated binary VQA questions that serve as fine-grained reward signals through an MLLM (LLaVA-v1.6-13B). Training integrates this curriculum into Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), where curriculum scheduling strategies (easy-to-hard, Gaussian, random) dynamically weight rewards across difficulty levels, and group-normalized advantages drive policy updates with KL regularization against a reference model.

## Results

On five compositional T2I benchmarks (GenEval, DPG, TIFA, T2I-CompBench, DSG), CompGen applied to Stable-Diffusion-1.5 (0.9B) achieves a 66.62% average score, an 11.72-point improvement over the untrained baseline (54.90%). Applied to SimpleAR (0.5B), CompGen reaches 71.27% average, a 7.61-point gain over the SFT baseline (63.66%), establishing a new state-of-the-art that surpasses the 14B-parameter Emu3 (62.31%). The largest per-benchmark gains are on DPG (+16.43 pts for SD1.5, +2.72 pts for SimpleAR) and DSG (+15.59 pts for SD1.5, +14.13 pts for SimpleAR).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CompGen (SD1.5, 0.9B) achieves 66.62% average, surpassing the larger Playground-V2 (66.08%, 2.6B) and SDXL (65.74%, 2.6B), as well as the same-scale SD2.1 (59.51%) and Lumina-Next (61.36%, 1.7B). On the autoregressive side, CompGen (SimpleAR, 0.5B) reaches 71.27%, outperforming Emu3 (62.31%, 14B) by nearly 9 points, Show-o (63.11%), SimpleAR-SFT (63.66%), LlamaGen (48.16%), and minDALL-E (44.45%). The method wins on 4 out of 5 individual benchmarks for both backbones; the sole exception is DSG where SDXL (83.40%) and Show-o (77.00%) retain an edge over the diffusion and autoregressive CompGen variants respectively, though CompGen (SimpleAR) still leads DSG at 86.11%. Against the VQAScore-only baseline (no curriculum, 61.44%), full CompGen gains an additional 5.18 points, isolating the contribution of the curriculum-based MCMC sampling and GRPO scheduling.

## Relation to Prior Work

CompGen positions itself against three families of compositional T2I methods—attention-based inference modifications (DenseDiffusion, CONFORM), planning-based layout guidance, and learning-based fine-tuning—and departs from all of them by requiring neither ground-truth images, intermediate skeletons, nor architectural changes. Its methodological lineage draws on scene-graph-based compositional evaluation (Krishna et al., Gao et al.), curriculum reinforcement learning with GRPO (Parashar et al.), and VQAScore-style MLLM rewards (Lin et al.), but the key conceptual contribution is the coupling of a principled scene-graph difficulty metric with an adaptive MCMC sampler to construct a synthetic training curriculum that drives RL without any paired image supervision.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a 0.9B diffusion model or 0.5B autoregressive model, when trained with curriculum-based RL on synthetically generated scene-graph prompts, can match or exceed models 3–28× larger on compositional benchmarks, suggesting that compositional weakness is partly a data-distribution problem rather than a capacity problem. The finding that curriculum scheduling strategy (Gaussian > easy-to-hard > random) materially affects scaling behavior provides actionable guidance for future RL-based T2I training. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger models and more complex scenes, whether the MLLM reward bottleneck (LLaVA-v1.6-13B) limits ceiling performance, and whether the difficulty metric can incorporate semantic or visual-realism dimensions beyond structural graph properties.
