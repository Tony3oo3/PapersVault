## Problem

Existing 4D urban scene reconstruction methods (NeRF, 3DGS-based) can only reproduce the weather conditions present during data capture, making them unable to simulate challenging scenarios like rain, snow, or fog needed for autonomous driving training. Meanwhile, image-level weather editing via diffusion models introduces content hallucinations (warped vehicles, distorted buildings) and offers poor fine-grained control over weather intensity, failing to meet the structural consistency requirements of 4D scene generation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263140/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a sequence of 30 consecutive raw images (1920×1080 on Waymo, 1600×900 on nuScenes), a text prompt specifying the target weather condition, and LiDAR-projected sparse depth maps as geometric supervision. It produces a 4D dynamic Gaussian scene graph (with sky, background, rigid, and non-rigid nodes) parameterized by shared 32-dimensional appearance features and per-weather MLP decoders, from which multi-weather rendered images and real-time dynamic weather simulations (25.67 FPS) can be generated. The Qwen-Image edited sequences serve as photometric supervision during training rather than as direct output.

## Method

WeatherCity introduces a Weather Gaussian Representation where each Gaussian primitive stores a shared appearance feature vector f_i ∈ R^32 encoding intrinsic texture, and a separate weather-specific two-layer MLP decoder φ_w maps this feature to the RGB color c_i^w for each weather condition w, thereby disentangling scene geometry from weather-dependent photometric appearance. A content consistency loss computed via VGG-19 relu_4_1 features between rendered multi-weather images and the original raw images enforces structural coherence, correcting per-frame artifacts introduced by the Qwen-Image editing step. For dynamic effects, raindrops are modeled as single elongated Gaussian ellipsoids and snowflakes as three concentric Gaussians at 60° angles, each following physics-driven velocity equations (fall + wind + turbulence for snow), while fog is rendered via the Beer-Lambert law with adjustable density and color. All weather particles are integrated as additional nodes in the dynamic Gaussian scene graph and rasterized together with scene Gaussians in a unified alpha-blending pass, ensuring correct occlusion and seamless composition.

## Results

On the Waymo Open Dataset, WeatherCity achieves CLIP-S 0.872, CLIP-DS 0.303, and Sem-CS 0.915, outperforming all baselines across every metric. On nuScenes, it reaches CLIP-S 0.870, CLIP-DS 0.302, and Sem-CS 0.968, with the largest margin over the strongest baseline (Qwen-Image) in semantic consistency (+6.6 points). In terms of runtime, WeatherCity renders at 25.67 FPS, which is 180–780× faster than the image/video editing baselines (0.033–0.142 FPS), making it suitable for real-time simulation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WeatherCity outperforms all four baselines on every metric across both datasets. Against the strongest image-editing baseline Qwen-Image, it gains +8.7 points in CLIP-S and +7.2 points in Sem-CS on Waymo, and +6.6 points in Sem-CS on nuScenes, confirming that lifting 2D editing into a 3D-consistent Gaussian representation substantially improves content preservation. TurboEdit, while competitive in CLIP-S (0.830 on Waymo), lags significantly in CLIP-DS (0.220 vs. 0.303) and Sem-CS (0.801 vs. 0.915), indicating weaker alignment with target weather prompts and poorer structural fidelity. ControlNet and FRESCO trail by the largest margins (e.g., ControlNet scores only 0.634 CLIP-S on Waymo), reflecting their limited ability to preserve scene geometry under weather transformation. In runtime, WeatherCity's 25.67 FPS rendering speed is roughly two orders of magnitude faster than the slowest baseline (ControlNet at 0.033 FPS) and ~180× faster than the fastest (FRESCO at 0.142 FPS), enabling real-time interactive simulation that none of the diffusion-based baselines can match.

## Relation to Prior Work

WeatherCity builds directly on OmniRe's dynamic Gaussian graph (sky, background, rigid, non-rigid nodes) and standard 3D Gaussian Splatting rendering, extending them with a Weather Gaussian representation that replaces per-Gaussian RGB with a shared appearance feature decoded by weather-specific MLPs. It departs from prior 3D weather editing (ClimateNeRF, WeatherGS, RainyGS), which handle only static effects or rain removal, by introducing physics-driven particle systems for dynamic rain/snow and Beer-Lambert fog, all unified within the same splatting pipeline. The pipeline also repurposes a 2D text-guided editing model (Qwen-Image) as a supervision signal generator rather than a direct editing tool, bridging the gap between flexible 2D weather synthesis and geometrically consistent 4D reconstruction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For autonomous driving simulation, the ability to generate temporally consistent, multi-weather 4D scenes at 25+ FPS with fine-grained intensity control (rain amount, snow density, fog thickness) addresses a concrete gap in closed-loop testing pipelines that currently rely on captured-weather-only reconstructions. The content consistency loss is a practical remedy for the well-known frame-flickering problem of per-frame 2D editing, and the unified rendering of weather particles as Gaussian nodes (enabling occlusion-correct compositing) is an elegant design choice likely to be adopted in follow-up work. Key open questions include automating the currently manual weather particle parameter tuning, extending to more weather types (hail, sleet, extreme fog), and validating whether the learned weather decoders generalize to unseen scene geometries beyond the five evaluated driving sequences.
