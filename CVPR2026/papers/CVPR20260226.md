## Problem

Existing ChartQA benchmarks evaluate only final-answer correctness, treating the intermediate reasoning chain as a black box—accepting correct answers produced through flawed reasoning and failing to diagnose where multi-step reasoning collapses. No prior benchmark systematically assesses whether MLLMs maintain coherent reasoning under visual degradations such as blur, noise, watermarking, or label removal, leaving open whether models genuinely understand charts or rely on superficial textual cues.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260226/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a chart image (one of five versions: original, Gaussian-blurred, noise-added, watermarked, or annotation-removed) with a single complex question that is internally decomposed into 4–10 sub-questions forming a chain- or network-structured dependency graph. The model is queried step-by-step, and the evaluation framework produces four accuracy metrics (ISA, CSA, FAA, CFA), four corresponding robustness metrics (AR per metric), plus per-task-type error rates (SER) and first-error ratios (FER) that localize where in the reasoning chain failures originate.

## Method

ChartR is constructed via a multi-stage pipeline: 1,000 charts across 20 types are generated with GPT-4o and rendered via Matplotlib/Plotly, then filtered to 200 by three human reviewers scoring visual readability and data rationality. For each chart, 4–10 sub-questions are selected from eight task types (Value Extraction, Color Identification, Position Recognition, Value Comparison, Conditional Processing, Trend Identification, Sequential Ordering, Numerical Calculation) with explicit dependency annotations, then composed by GPT-4o into a single complex question; all QA pairs undergo three-annotator verification. The evaluation framework's key distinction is between individual-step accuracy (ISA/FAA) and chained accuracy (CSA/CFA), where a step counts as correct only if all its predecessors are also correct—quantifying error propagation. Robustness is measured via AR_M = (M_original − mean M_perturbed) / M_original across four visual perturbations, and task-level diagnostics (SER, FER, and immediate error-dependency recovery rates) identify which sub-task types trigger cascading failures.

## Results

On the ChartR benchmark (averaged across original and four perturbed variants), Gemini-2.0-flash achieves the highest scores: ISA=83.01%, CSA=63.29%, FAA=78.20%, and CFA=50.60%. Chart-specialized models perform markedly worse, with TinyChart-3B (ISA=28.29%, CFA=0.30%) and ChartGemma-2.4B (ISA=27.78%, CFA=0.00%) trailing all general-purpose models. Across all 12 models, CFA (requiring every reasoning step to be correct) remains below 10% for 10 of 12 models, revealing that consistent multi-step chart reasoning remains an open challenge. Under annotation removal, performance drops sharply (e.g., Gemini-2.0-flash CFA falls from 54.50% to 28.00%), indicating heavy reliance on textual labels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper, so the 'comparison' is across 12 MLLMs evaluated on ChartR. Gemini-2.0-flash dominates all four metrics, outperforming the second-best general-purpose model (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) by 11 points on ISA and 23 points on CFA. Surprisingly, all three chart-specialized models (ChartMoE-8B, TinyChart-3B, ChartGemma-2.4B) underperform every general-purpose model on CFA, with ChartGemma-2.4B scoring 0.00% and TinyChart-3B only 0.30%, suggesting narrow training domains hurt multi-step reasoning generalization. Among general-purpose models, Qwen2.5-VL-7B is the clear runner-up (CFA=27.60%), while DeepSeek-VL-7B and Janus-Pro-7B are the weakest (CFA ≤ 0.70%). The ISA–CSA gap (e.g., 19.72 for Gemini vs. 29.84 for MiniCPM-o-2.6-8B) quantifies error propagation: models with larger gaps fail to maintain correctness across dependent reasoning steps. Under annotation removal, even Gemini-2.0-flash drops 26.5 points in CFA, while smaller models like InternVL2.5-2B and ChartGemma-2.4B drop to 0%, confirming that current models depend heavily on visible text labels rather than true visual structure understanding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant CQA evaluation paradigm (FigureQA, DVQA, ChartQA, ChartX, CharXiv), which treats the reasoning chain as a black box and scores only final-answer correctness via EM or ANLS. It introduces a fundamentally different evaluation axis: decomposing each question into 4–10 dependent sub-questions spanning information-extraction and reasoning types, then scoring step-wise and chained accuracy separately. The visual-perturbation robustness axis (blur, noise, watermark, annotation removal) has no direct precedent in prior CQA benchmarks, which evaluate only clean inputs. Conceptually, the work bridges the gap between single-step VQA evaluation and the multi-step reasoning demands that modern MLLMs are expected to handle.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that multi-step chart reasoning is far harder than final-answer metrics suggest: most models achieve under 10% CFA (all steps correct), and value extraction errors cascade through downstream steps, making early-stage accuracy the critical bottleneck. The finding that annotation removal causes the largest performance drops across all models challenges the assumption that MLLMs perform genuine visual understanding, pointing instead to heavy reliance on embedded textual cues. Limitations include a modest chart pool (200 base charts, 20 types), a single-GPU evaluation protocol, and only four perturbation types; future work on multilingual, dynamic, and more diverse chart formats would strengthen generalizability. Open questions include whether improving early-step extraction (e.g., via dedicated OCR or grounding modules) can close the ISA-to-CFA gap, and whether robustness under perturbations can be improved without sacrificing clean-input accuracy.
