## Problem

Current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models entangle visual understanding with task-specific action policies, requiring exhaustive full-trajectory data collection per task and substantial fine-tuning when camera-arm configurations change. The paper argues that decoupling how a robot sees (egocentric viewpoint alignment) from how it acts (manipulation) is a missing primitive that would reduce training redundancy, enable cross-task transfer, and eliminate hardware-specific calibration.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260608/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

EgoRoC consumes a wrist-mounted (first-person) RGB image, a single rendered top-view target image (generated once per task via VGGT point-map construction and pix2pix-turbo inpainting from a third-person camera), and a natural-language instruction. It outputs a discretized 6-DoF relative SE(3) transform (percentile-binned into 256 tokens per dimension) plus a three-state align token (aligning/aligned/searching), exposing only this thin pose interface to the downstream VLA without any architectural modification.

## Method

EgoRoC comprises two sequential modules: (1) EVAM, a LoRA-adapted Qwen2.5-VL-7B that predicts the relative pose between the current egocentric view and the next target view, trained on static image pairs with known relative poses rather than full manipulation trajectories; and (2) DHCM, a conditional diffusion generator (Concatsquash MLPs) that converts and corrects the EVAM pose into the end-effector frame by fusing COTR-based 4×4 grid cross-image correspondence features with executed action parameters through a Q-Former-inspired cross-attention mechanism. DHCM is regularized by Rank-N Contrast (aligning embedding distances to rotation-angle label distances) and geometric constraints (unit-sphere, orthogonality, minimal-adjustment losses) on four-point SE(3) encodings. A two-stage training protocol first learns view alignment and calibration separately, then performs end-to-end refinement with frozen EVAM so DHCM adapts to residual EVAM bias during deployment.

## Results

Attaching EgoRoC as a plug-and-play head consistently improves success rates across all five VLA baselines (OpenVLA, TinyVLA, SpatialVLA, Pi-0, OpenVLA-OFT) on four manipulation tasks in both simulation and real-world settings, with gains most pronounced on long-horizon tasks (Task-PA, Task-PD) where alternating alignment→manipulation reduces cumulative perception-control drift. In the hand-eye calibration comparison (Table 2), full DHCM training achieves 72.0%/52.0%/43.0%/20.0% success on Task-L/Po/PA/PD, surpassing the classical Tsai-Lenz method (67.0%/52.0%/40.0%/17.0%) on all four tasks. In data-efficient fine-tuning experiments, EgoRoC raises success rates for both OpenVLA and Pi-0 at every demonstration budget (75–300 samples), with the gap widening on out-of-distribution tasks (Task-Po, Task-PA).

| DHCM (first-stage only) | 66.0 | 49.0 | 37.0 | 15.0 |

## Baselines & Comparisons

EgoRoC is evaluated as an additive plug-and-play head on five VLA baselines rather than as a standalone replacement. Against OpenVLA [23], TinyVLA [51], SpatialVLA [41], Pi-0 [4], and OpenVLA-OFT [24], attaching EgoRoC yields consistent success-rate gains across all four tasks in both Isaac Sim (Franka Panda) and real-world (UMS Eco-bot) settings, with the largest improvements on long-horizon tasks (Task-PA, Task-PD) where repeated alignment prevents drift. In the data-efficient fine-tuning study, both OpenVLA and Pi-0 show higher success rates at every demonstration count (75–300) when EgoRoC is attached, and the advantage is larger on out-of-distribution tasks. For hand-eye calibration specifically, the proposed DHCM module (full two-stage training) outperforms the classical Tsai-Lenz [48] method on all four tasks (e.g., 72.0% vs 67.0% on Task-L, 43.0% vs 40.0% on Task-PA), while the first-stage-only DHCM is slightly below Tsai-Lenz, confirming that the second-stage end-to-end refinement is critical for correcting EVAM prediction bias. The shared-LMM variant (Table 1) shows that using OpenVLA's Llama-2-7B as the EVAM backbone yields a marginal 0.3% average improvement over the default Qwen2.5-VL-7B, confirming plug-and-play flexibility without architectural changes.

## Relation to Prior Work

EgoRoC positions itself as a missing upstream primitive in the VLA pipeline, building on LMMs (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) for egocentric pose estimation and on diffusion models for hand-eye calibration, while explicitly departing from classical AX=XB calibration (Tsai-Lenz, Horaud) by performing online, first-person calibration without external markers or CAD. It draws on correspondence methods (COTR) and grid-based geometric encoding to decouple spatial reasoning from semantic content, and on LoRA-based adapter tuning to keep downstream VLA policies (OpenVLA, Pi-0, TinyVLA, etc.) architecturally unchanged. The key conceptual departure from prior VLA work is treating egocentric visual alignment as a reusable, task-agnostic capability trained on static image pairs rather than full manipulation trajectories, in contrast to the end-to-end entanglement of perception and action in RT-2, OpenVLA, and Pi-0.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By reducing training data requirements from full manipulation episodes to static wrist-target image pairs with relative poses, EgoRoC addresses a practical bottleneck in multi-task VLA deployment and offers a hardware-agnostic path to cross-platform transfer via its diffusion-based online calibration. The plug-and-play 6-DoF interface means existing VLA investments are preserved, which is attractive for practitioners maintaining diverse robot fleets. However, the reliance on static image pairs and a single third-person camera for target rendering may limit applicability to highly dynamic or occluded environments, and the paper acknowledges that temporal alignment and latency reduction remain open. Broader questions include whether this decoupling generalizes beyond manipulation to navigation or dexterous tasks, and whether the alignment head's quality ceiling is bounded by the LMM backbone's spatial reasoning.
