## Problem

Existing RF spatial spectrum synthesis methods such as NeRF² and NeWRF adapt vanilla NeRF to the RF domain but require scene-specific training, overfitting to the training layout and necessitating time-intensive retraining for every new scene. This limits scalability for practical applications like network planning and sensing, where spectra must be synthesized across diverse and changing environments.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263389/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes the 360×90 spatial spectrum matrices (one-degree azimuth/elevation resolution over the upper hemisphere) of L geographically nearest neighboring transmitters, along with their 3D positions and the target transmitter's 3D position. It produces a single 360×90 power matrix representing the received signal power at the fixed receiver across all surrounding directions. Unlike NeRF², which queries an MLP at arbitrary voxel coordinates, GRaF's input is entirely spectral and geometric—no CAD model, DoA measurements, or scene geometry are required.

## Method

GRaF rests on an interpolation theorem proving that a transmitter's spatial spectrum can be approximated as a weighted combination of its L nearest neighbors' spectra, with error bounded by O(δ²) where δ is the neighborhood radius. A geometry-aware Transformer encoder processes each neighbor's spectrum through a ResNet-18 feature extractor and encodes relative positions via positional embeddings; cross-attention layers then dynamically weight the most influential neighbors to produce a latent variable Z that captures scene-level RF propagation characteristics (path loss, multipath, shadowing). A neural ray tracing algorithm traces Q=32,400 directional rays from the receiver, sampling S points along each ray and computing voxel features by combining Z with positional encodings of the sample location, ray direction, and transmitter position. At each voxel, separate MLPs predict complex-valued radiated signal and attenuation, which are aggregated along the ray with free-space path loss (λ/4πd) and propagation-delay phase shifts to yield the received signal, whose magnitude squared gives the spectrum value. Training minimizes MSE between predicted and ground-truth spectra in a supervised fashion, and the model generalizes to unseen scenes and layouts without retraining.

## Results

In the single-scene setting (Table 1), GRaF achieves PSNR 21.94 dB, MSE 0.038, LPIPS 0.136, and SSIM 0.766, outperforming NeRF² by 10.2% in PSNR and 50.4% in LPIPS. In the unseen-scene setting (Table 2), GRaF maintains PSNR 20.96 dB while NeRF² drops to 17.36 dB (a 12.9% degradation for NeRF² vs. only 4.5% for GRaF). In the most challenging unseen-layout setting (Table 3), GRaF achieves PSNR 17.81 dB, substantially ahead of NeRF² (12.76 dB, a 35.9% drop from its single-scene score) and KNN-DL (14.94 dB, a 28.2% drop).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRaF is compared against three baselines: KNN (simple averaging of L nearest-neighbor spectra), KNN-DL (learnable per-pixel weight matrices over neighbors), and NeRF² (scene-specific neural radiance field with per-scene training). In the single-scene setting, GRaF outperforms NeRF² by 10.2% in PSNR (21.94 vs. 19.93 dB) and 50.4% in LPIPS (0.136 vs. 0.274), and beats KNN-DL by 5.5% in PSNR. In the unseen-scene setting, GRaF's advantage over NeRF² widens to 20.7% in PSNR (20.96 vs. 17.36 dB), as NeRF²'s scene-specific training causes a 12.9% PSNR drop while GRaF degrades only 4.5%. In the unseen-layout setting, the gap is most dramatic: GRaF achieves 17.81 dB PSNR versus 12.76 dB for NeRF² (a 39.6% relative improvement) and 14.94 dB for KNN-DL, confirming that geometry-aware cross-attention and neural ray tracing are critical for cross-layout generalization. KNN remains relatively stable across settings (PSNR 14.77–15.32 dB), validating the interpolation theory but lacking the modeling capacity to capture complex propagation effects.

## Relation to Prior Work

GRaF extends the RF radiance field line initiated by NeRF² (Zhao et al., MobiCom 2023), which adapted vanilla NeRF's per-scene MLP to synthesize spatial spectra via ray aggregation. The key departure is replacing scene-specific training with a geometry-aware Transformer encoder that encodes spectra from L nearest neighboring transmitters into a scene-independent latent radiance field, followed by a neural ray tracing algorithm that models complex-valued signal and attenuation per voxel. This draws conceptually on generalizable NeRF work in the optical domain (MVSNeRF, WaveNeRF) but is grounded in a new interpolation theorem specific to RF propagation, which justifies approximating a target spectrum from geographically proximate transmitters. Unlike NeWRF (which requires DoA measurements) or WiNeRT (which requires CAD models), GRaF needs only neighboring spectra and positions, making it more practical for deployment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for wireless network planning and sensing: eliminating per-scene retraining reduces the cost of site surveys and enables rapid spectrum synthesis for new layouts. The interpolation theorem provides a theoretical foundation that validates the use of local spatial correlations, and the downstream AoA estimation results (61.6% error reduction with GRaF-augmented training) demonstrate tangible benefits for localization. However, cross-frequency generalization remains weak (PSNR drops from ~24.5 to 11.6–14.8 dB when transferring across bands), and cross-layout performance degrades noticeably, suggesting the latent field still encodes layout-specific structure. Open questions include whether the approach scales to outdoor/urban-scale scenes, whether the interpolation theory holds at larger neighborhood radii, and how to handle dynamic environments where object configurations change over time.
