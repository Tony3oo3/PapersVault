## Problem

Existing instructional video editing methods rely on non-causal spatiotemporal attention that requires fixed-length inputs and scales poorly with video length, making them impractical for streaming or resource-constrained deployment. While autoregressive generation offers variable-length efficiency, it remains under-explored for video editing. Additionally, current benchmarks based on CLIP text similarity fail to capture edit faithfulness, leading to sub-optimal method rankings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262418/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model accepts a variable-length video clip (no fixed frame count) and a natural-language instruction prompt (e.g., 'remove the object', 'turn the object red'), producing an edited video clip generated sequentially frame-by-frame. Each output frame is conditioned on the model's own previous-frame prediction, so the computation per frame is constant regardless of total video length. The backbones are SD1.5 and SD3.5(M), and the model is trained on paired (input, output) video clips from the Señorita-2M dataset covering global style transfer, local style transfer, object removal, object addition, and object swap.

## Method

RFDM adapts a 2D image-to-image diffusion backbone to autoregressive video editing through two key mechanisms: (i) conditioning each frame's denoising on the previous frame's clean prediction as an extra input, adding no extra compute, and (ii) a novel residual-flow forward diffusion process that shifts the sampling-noise mean toward the previous prediction, so the model learns to predict only the inter-frame residual rather than the full frame. This residual formulation explicitly separates already-edited regions (which need only a small shift) from new regions requiring fresh editing, yielding stronger temporal consistency and lower error accumulation. To handle exposure bias, the model uses Diffusion Forcing during training—sampling independent noise levels per frame and using the model's own (noisy) prediction as the conditioning signal—rather than teacher forcing on ground-truth frames. Trained on 2M paired real-world videos with standard MSE denoising loss and classifier-free guidance at inference, RFDM matches I2I-model compute while achieving faithfulness competitive with 3D V2V models.

## Results

On TGVE and TGVE+, RFDM3.5 achieves the highest ViCLIPout (0.284 / 0.250) and CLIPFrame (0.965 / 0.970) scores among all compared methods, leading in 4 of 6 metrics across the two benchmarks. On the Señorita benchmark, RFDM3.5 attains the best DVS (0.48), MLLM-Judge (7.37/10), and lowest ViDreamSim (0.20) scores, outperforming all I2I-based baselines. In terms of efficiency, RFDM3.5 generates 16 frames in 13 s using only 6 GB RAM, matching Fairy's latency while using ~13× less memory and reducing latency by ~4× compared to TokenFlow (128 s) and RAVE (92 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RFDM3.5 outperforms all I2I-based baselines (Fairy, TokenFlow, RAVE, VidToMe) on the Señorita benchmark across DVS, MLLM-Judge, and ViDreamSim, with the largest gaps over Fairy (DVS 0.48 vs 0.32; MLLM-Judge 7.37 vs 2.87) and VidToMe (DVS 0.48 vs 0.32; ViDreamSim 0.20 vs 0.46). Against the 3D V2V model EVE, RFDM3.5 achieves slightly lower PickScore (20.66 vs 20.76 on TGVE; 20.52 vs 20.88 on TGVE+) but higher ViCLIPout and CLIPFrame, which the authors attribute to EVE's larger 4.4B-parameter backbone and 34M-video training set. On TGVE/TGVE+, RFDM3.5 leads in ViCLIPout and CLIPFrame over all baselines, including TokenFlow (0.284 vs 0.257 ViCLIPout) and RAVE (0.284 vs 0.254), while remaining competitive on PickScore. In efficiency, RFDM3.5 uses ~13× less RAM than Fairy (6 GB vs 77 GB) at the same 13 s latency, and is ~10× faster than TokenFlow (13 s vs 128 s) and ~7× faster than RAVE (13 s vs 92 s). The smaller RFDM1.5 variant remains competitive with TokenFlow and RAVE on TGVE/TGVE+ while being 12–16× faster and using 4–5× less RAM.

## Relation to Prior Work

RFDM extends the InstructPix2Pix I2I diffusion formulation to video by making it causal: each frame's denoising is conditioned on the model's own previous-frame prediction, adding no extra compute. The core architectural novelty—the residual flow forward process that shifts the sampling-noise mean toward the previous prediction—draws directly from ResShift's residual-shifting idea for image super-resolution, repurposed here to focus denoising on inter-frame changes rather than full-frame reconstruction. For handling the train-inference distribution gap (exposure bias), the paper adopts Diffusion Forcing's per-frame random noise-level sampling rather than standard teacher forcing. This positions RFDM as a lightweight, autoregressive alternative to the dominant non-causal 3D V2V approaches (EVE, Fairy, VidToMe) that require fixed-length inputs and heavy spatiotemporal attention.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: RFDM achieves video editing at image-model compute cost (8 s / 2 GB for 16 frames on A100) while scaling independently of video length, opening the door to real-time streaming and on-device deployment that 3D V2V models cannot support. The Señorita benchmark and its faithfulness-oriented metrics (ViDreamSim, DVS, Error Accumulation) address a genuine evaluation gap, showing that CLIP-similarity-based benchmarks can misrank methods. The acknowledged limitation—short temporal memory preventing motion-constrained edits like action changes—points to KV-caching or longer-context mechanisms as a clear next step, and the residual-flow formulation's superior tracking (validated on DAVIS) suggests it could serve as a building block for more complex temporal editing pipelines.
