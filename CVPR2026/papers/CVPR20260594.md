## Problem

AI-generated images inherently contain generative traces, but no trace-free counterpart exists to provide ground-truth supervision for learning to extract them. Existing approaches treat these traces as generic noise residuals and rely on hand-crafted filters or task-agnostic denoisers, which fail to isolate model-specific artifacts and generalize poorly across unseen generative architectures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260594/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (resized to 256×256, with a 224×224 center crop for the CLIP branch) and produces two outputs: a binary real/fake classification score and a spatial generative signature (GenSign) map of the same resolution as the input. The GenSign map is a residual-like image where real images yield noise-like patterns while AIGIs exhibit coherent spatial patches revealing model-specific artifacts.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-stage surrogate supervision framework. First, a Dynamic-Architecture Image Reconstructor (DAIR) reconstructs real images using stochastically varied decoder architectures—randomly selecting upsampling operations, normalization layers, activations, and a Context-Conditioned Dynamic Convolution (CCDC) that blends K=4 base kernels via input-dependent routing weights—so that the reconstruction residual R_gt = I − R(I) serves as a synthetic ground-truth generative signature. Second, a fully convolutional extractor is trained with MSE loss to predict R_gt from the reconstructed image, learning to isolate generative signatures in a supervised manner. Third, a dual-stream detector fuses CLIP ViT-L/14 RGB features (fine-tuned via LoRA, rank 4) with EfficientNet-B0 features extracted from the GenSign map through an MLP classifier; the frozen DAIR is repurposed as a self-augmentation module during detector training, assigning all reconstructed images a fake label to force the model to rely on generative traces rather than content biases. The key insight is that sufficient architectural diversity in the simulation space enables effective transfer to real-world generators, resolving the absence of ground-truth signatures.

## Results

On UniversalFakeDetect, the method achieves 99.37% mean AP, surpassing the best baseline FatFormer (98.16%) by 1.21%. On AIGCDetectBenchmark it reaches 98.04% mean AP (vs. PatchCraft 96.07%), and on AIGIBenchmark it attains 89.96% mean AP, a 14.6-point margin over the next-best AIDE (75.36%). On the GenImage benchmark (trained on SDv1.4), it achieves 96.6% mean accuracy, outperforming Effort (91.1%) and FatFormer (88.9%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms all 18 baselines across all four benchmarks. On UniversalFakeDetect, it leads FatFormer (98.16%) by 1.21% and NPR (92.76%) by 6.61% in mean AP, with near-perfect scores (>99%) on most GAN and diffusion generators. On AIGCDetectBenchmark, it surpasses PatchCraft (96.07%) by 1.97% and LNP (91.29%) by 6.75%, maintaining >99% AP on 12 of 17 generators where competitors like LNP collapse to 42.75% on WFIR. The largest margin appears on AIGIBenchmark, where the method achieves 89.96% mean AP versus AIDE's 75.36% (+14.6%), driven by strong generalization to face-swap models (e.g., 87.10% on InSwap vs. AIDE's 43.00%). On GenImage, it beats Effort (91.1%) by 5.5% and DRCT (89.5%) by 7.1% in mean accuracy, with the largest single-model gap on Midjourney (99.3% vs. 82.4% for Effort). Notably, older frequency-domain methods (F3Net, FreDect) and reconstruction-based approaches (DIRE) show severe degradation on diffusion and commercial models, while the proposed GenSign-based detector maintains consistent performance across all model families.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in the AIGI detection literature by reframing the generative-trace extraction problem as a supervised learning task through a surrogate supervision strategy. Prior work on generative fingerprints (Yu et al., Marra et al.) established that GANs and diffusion models leave instance-specific traces, but extraction relied on hand-crafted filters or generic denoisers optimized for noise removal rather than model-specific artifacts. The Dynamic-Architecture Image Reconstructor (DAIR) departs from both the reconstruction-bias paradigm (DIRE, AeroBlade, which require access to the source model) and the foundation-model paradigm (UniFD/CLIP, which avoid forensic feature learning entirely) by creating synthetic ground-truth residuals on real images through stochastically varied architectures, enabling true supervised training of a signature extractor. The Context-Conditioned Dynamic Convolution and the self-augmentation strategy (re-purposing the frozen reconstructor to generate 'fake' training samples) are novel architectural and training contributions not present in prior AIGI detection work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The surrogate supervision paradigm offers a general recipe for the 'no ground truth' problem that plagues forensic feature extraction: if you can simulate the artifact on clean data, you can train a supervised extractor. This could extend beyond AIGI detection to other forensic tasks where the signal of interest has no clean counterpart. However, the approach still shows notable weaknesses on Midjourney (81.17% AP) and some face-swapping models, suggesting that not all generative traces are captured by the simulated distribution. Open questions include whether the reconstructor's diversity is sufficient for future generative paradigms (e.g., video, 3D, or autoregressive image models), whether the ~90-hour total training pipeline (reconstructor + extractor + detector) is practical for rapid deployment as new generators emerge, and whether the GenSign representation is robust to adversarial perturbations specifically designed to erase generative traces.
