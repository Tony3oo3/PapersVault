## Problem

Despite rapid progress in video generation, there is no comprehensive benchmark for evaluating controllable human video synthesis. Existing datasets (TikTok, TED-Talks, HumanVid) are extremely narrow—containing only 16–128 videos of a single person dancing or talking in a static close-up shot—making them insufficient to assess the full range of human actions, multi-person interactions, dynamic scenes, and camera motion that real-world generation must handle.

## Input / Output

The benchmark evaluates pose-conditioned and depth/edge-conditioned image-to-video models at 16 fps on 1,544 reference videos (1.5–15 s, portrait and landscape). Each model receives the first frame as a reference image plus a conditioning signal (2D pose keypoints from DWPose/OpenPose, depth or edge maps, or LLM-generated text captions) and must produce a temporally coherent video of the human actor(s) performing the specified motion. Evaluation additionally requires manually curated per-actor segmentation masks (from SAM2, corrected over 1,000 rater-hours) and 2D pose keypoint annotations for 100 representative videos to compute human-specific metrics.

## Method

The paper introduces WYD, a benchmark of 1,544 videos curated from Kinetics, DiDeMo, and Oops through a multi-stage filtering pipeline (shot detection, pose visibility, VLM caption similarity, manual quality review over 500+ hours) and annotated with nine fine-grained categories (56 sub-categories) covering actor count, occlusion, actions, scene, interactions, locomotion, camera motion, and video motion. The evaluation protocol adapts existing metrics to the human level: pICD computes DINOv2 cosine distance restricted to person-masked patches for appearance consistency, and pAPE computes 1−mAP between reference and generated poses using DWPose with segmentation-mask-based actor matching for multi-person pose adherence. Video-level quality and motion are measured with FVD (I3D backbone) and optical-flow endpoint error (RAFT), respectively. The key design insight is that manually curated segmentation masks enable repurposing whole-video metrics into person-specific ones, and the fine-grained category labels allow systematic slicing to diagnose where models fail (e.g., multi-person blending, animal interactions, dynamic camera, full-body locomotion). All eight evaluated SOTA models (MagicPose, MimicMotion, ControlNeXt, VACE, Control-A-Video, Ctrl-Adapter, TF-T2V) show 1.8–12.3× higher errors on WYD than on prior benchmarks, confirming its greater difficulty.

## Results

On WYD₁₆, VACE achieves the best overall performance among pose-conditioned models, being on-par with MagicPose on human-level metrics (pICD, pAPE) while also obtaining competitive video-level scores (FVD, OFE) with MimicMotion and ControlNeXt. WYD is consistently harder than prior benchmarks, yielding error rates 1.8–4.6× higher for pICD and 1.8–12.3× higher for pAPE compared to TikTok and TED-Talks. The proposed human-centric metrics validate well against human preferences: pICD achieves 72.67% pairwise accuracy (vs. 67.33% for frame-wide ICD), pAPE reaches 71.95% (vs. 61.45% for pOFE), and FVD achieves 96.36% Spearman rank correlation with human video-quality judgments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the four pose-conditioned models, VACE emerges as the strongest overall, matching MagicPose on human-level metrics (pICD, pAPE) while also achieving competitive FVD and OFE scores comparable to MimicMotion and ControlNeXt-SVD-v2. MagicPose suffers from a notably high FVD due to flickering artifacts, despite competitive pose adherence. MimicMotion and ControlNeXt achieve strong video-level quality but both re-scale and re-center generated humans (a preprocessing artifact), which pAPE detects as a systematic failure. Among depth/edge-guided models, TF-T2V tends to outperform Control-A-Video and Ctrl-Adapter, the latter two exhibiting low visual quality (high FVD and pICD) with distorted colors and artifacts despite following the driving signal well. Across all models, performance degrades significantly for multi-person scenes (2+ actors), complex animal interactions, non-static camera motion, full-body locomotion, and rapid actions—failure modes invisible on the narrower TikTok and TED-Talks benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a necessary correction to the narrow evaluation landscape for controllable human video generation, where the field has relied on TikTok (16 single-person dance clips) and TED-Talks (128 single-person talking clips). It repurposes two existing metric families — DINOv2-based subject consistency from Consisti2v [60] and pose average-precision from COCO/Follow-Your-Pose [42, 50] — by restricting them to manually segmented human regions, yielding the human-specific pICD and pAPE metrics. The dataset itself is curated from Kinetics, DiDeMo, and Oops, filtered and annotated with 2,500+ rater hours, deliberately expanding the evaluation scope from single-person close-ups to multi-actor, multi-environment, dynamic-camera scenarios.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WYD is likely to become the de facto standard benchmark for controllable human video generation given its scale (1,544 videos), fine-grained 9-category taxonomy, and open-source release from Google DeepMind. The 1.8–12.3× error inflation relative to prior benchmarks concretely demonstrates that SOTA models (MagicPose, MimicMotion, ControlNeXt, VACE) have been overfit to a narrow distribution of close-up, single-person, static-camera videos — a limitation invisible on TikTok or TED-Talks. Open questions remain: FVD fails to detect object disappearance in human-object interactions, DWPose accuracy degrades on poor-quality generations, and no existing metric adequately captures multi-person identity preservation or interaction fidelity, all of which the authors flag as future work.
