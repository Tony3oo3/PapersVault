## Problem

Despite the critical role of micro-actions (subtle, spontaneous body movements reflecting emotional states) in affective computing, no dedicated benchmark exists to evaluate Multimodal Large Language Models on fine-grained micro-action understanding. Existing video QA benchmarks target daily activities, long-form videos, or general motion, while prior micro-action datasets (iMiGUE, SMG, MA-52) were designed for traditional recognition pipelines rather than probing MLLM perception, relational reasoning, and interpretive explanation of short-duration body-part dynamics.

## Input / Output

Each video averages 2.12 seconds (max 5.01 s) and is consumed as 8 sampled frames paired with a text prompt. Closed-ended tasks (CMAR, FMAR, SAD, MAS, MMAD, PPR) yield a single class label or YES/NO answer; open-ended tasks (MADU, MARE) require free-text motion descriptions or multi-step reasoning chains that name both the coarse body-level and fine action-level labels. Open-ended outputs are scored on a 0–5 scale by a GPT-4o LLM judge across three sub-dimensions (L1: core semantics, L2: spatial/directional accuracy, L3: temporal/causal consistency).

## Method

The benchmark is built via a three-stage semi-automatic pipeline. First, a micro-motion tracker combines CoTracker3 dense optical flow (four directional components), YOLOv8x-Seg human segmentation, and skeleton data to produce per-body-part motion descriptors (vectors + coordinates). Second, LLMs (DeepSeek-v3.2 / DeepSeek-R1) convert these descriptors into structured micro-action captions describing each body part's motion pattern. Third, a QA generation loop uses structured templates to reformulate captions into task-specific questions, followed by LLM-based reflection verification and multi-round human rectification, yielding 12,000 QA pairs across eight tasks in a Perception–Comprehension–Reasoning hierarchy. For training, MA-Bench-Train (20.5K videos from 166 non-overlapping participants) provides the same structured captions as supervision; fine-tuning Qwen3-VL-8B via LoRA on this corpus improves closed-ended accuracy from 46.97% to 50.68% and open-ended scores from 0.75 to 1.69, surpassing all proprietary baselines.

## Results

On MA-Bench, the best proprietary model (Gemini-2.5-Flash) achieves 50.70% average accuracy on closed-ended tasks, while the best zero-shot open-source model (VideoChat-Flash-Qwen2-7B) reaches 49.87%. After fine-tuning Qwen3-VL-8B on MA-Bench-Train, the model attains 50.68% closed-ended accuracy and a substantially higher 1.69/5 open-ended score, compared to 0.76/5 for Gemini-2.5-Flash. Notably, all models score near the random baseline (50%) on relational comprehension tasks (SAD, MAS, PPR), indicating that fine-grained body-part dynamics remain a major unsolved challenge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The fine-tuned Qwen3-VL-8B + MA-Bench-Train (50.68% closed-ended, 1.69 open-ended) essentially matches Gemini-2.5-Flash (50.70%) on closed-ended accuracy but dramatically outperforms it on open-ended reasoning (1.69 vs. 0.76), a gap of over 120%. It surpasses GPT-4o (44.87%) by ~5.8 points and Grok-2-Vision (43.25%) by ~7.4 points on closed-ended accuracy. Among zero-shot open-source models, VideoChat-Flash-Qwen2-7B (49.87%) and VideoLLaMA3-7B (48.48%) are the strongest, but both are outperformed by the fine-tuned Qwen3-VL-8B. A striking finding is that nearly all models hover around the 50% random baseline on relational comprehension tasks (SAD, MAS, PPR), with no model exceeding 60.30% on SAD, indicating that inter-part spatial-temporal reasoning remains largely unsolved. The ablation (Table 4) further shows that fine-tuning the LM decoder alone (50.63%) is nearly as effective as full-model fine-tuning (50.68%), while vision-encoder-only fine-tuning (48.62%) yields smaller gains, suggesting that language-side adaptation is the primary driver of improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two established lines of work: micro-action recognition (iMiGUE, SMG, BBSI, MA-52) and MLLM-based video understanding benchmarks (MVBench, Video-MME, MotionBench, FAVOR-Bench). While prior micro-action datasets provide action-level and body-level labels, none offer the structured motion descriptions or multi-tier QA formulations needed for MLLM evaluation. The annotation pipeline repurposes existing tools (CoTracker3 for optical flow, YOLOv8x-Seg for segmentation, skeleton trackers) in a novel combination to generate body-part motion descriptors, which are then fed to LLMs to produce structured captions and QA pairs. The paper does not build on a specific prior MLLM method but rather establishes the first evaluation infrastructure for this domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MA-Bench fills a clear gap by providing the first systematic evaluation of MLLMs on fine-grained micro-action understanding, revealing that even frontier proprietary models achieve only ~50% accuracy on closed-ended tasks and very low scores (≤0.76/5) on open-ended reasoning. The accompanying MA-Bench-Train corpus (20.5K videos) is practically important because it demonstrates that domain-adaptive fine-tuning of a small open-source model (Qwen3-VL-8B) can surpass much larger proprietary models, suggesting a viable path for resource-constrained labs. Open questions include whether the structured caption pipeline generalizes beyond the MA-52 domain, how performance scales with video length and action complexity, and whether the three-tier evaluation architecture can be extended to other fine-grained motion domains (e.g., facial micro-expressions, gait).
