## Problem

Imaging individual scintillation events with a single-photon camera is extremely challenging because each event emits only a small number of photons, making depth-from-defocus methods susceptible to dark counts and yielding poor 3D localization. Existing camera-based scintillator designs lack sufficient light collection per event to achieve sub-millimeter spatial resolution, especially in larger scintillator volumes where the probability of particle interaction is higher but depth estimation is harder.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single 1-bit SPAD camera frame (512×512 pixels, 16 μm pitch, 1.5 μs integration) containing tens to hundreds of photon arrivals from one scintillation event plus its mirror reflections, along with known kaleidoscopic geometry (square pyramid with four specular faces) and calibrated thin-lens camera parameters. It outputs the 3D position (x, y, z_w) of the event within the scintillator volume, along with the number and identity of mirror reflections present in the image. The algorithm also assigns each photon to a component (event or specific mirror reflection) via posterior probabilities.

## Method

The paper proposes a square-pyramid scintillator with four specular mirror faces that creates known mirror reflections of each event, effectively providing multiple views of the same point source and increasing total light collected by the camera. A thin-lens imaging model with circle-of-confusion defocus blur and finite-mirror truncation zones is derived to predict where each reflection's photons land on the sensor. The image is modeled as a Gaussian Mixture Model with K+1 components (event plus up to four first-order mirror reflections), where all component means and variances are analytically constrained by the single unknown event position p₀ via reflection transformations and perspective projection. An EM algorithm maximizes the weighted log-likelihood with a density-based photon weighting scheme (q-nearest-neighbor kernel) to suppress dark counts, a regularization term anchoring component means to initialization centroids, and a weighted-KMeans initialization step that determines which mirror reflections are present. The approach achieves ~0.14 mm 3D error at 30 photons per component, roughly 6× better than depth-from-defocus baselines.

## Results

In simulations with calibrated camera parameters, the kaleidoscopic method achieves a 3D localization error of 0.14 mm at N₀=30 photons, 0.16 mm at N₀=20, and 0.29 mm at N₀=10, with per-axis resolutions of 0.14–0.31 mm across all brightness levels. This represents a 5–6× improvement over the non-kaleidoscopic version of the same algorithm (0.79–0.95 mm 3D error) and a 2–3× improvement over the prior noise-removing depth-from-defocus method [8] (0.64–0.80 mm). Experimental cross-validation on 1,606 test images yields a median event-location agreement of 0.10 mm (mean 0.17 mm, std 0.22 mm) across 17,666 pairwise measurements with mirror reflections removed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: (1) a non-kaleidoscopic version of the same GMM+EM algorithm that estimates depth from defocus using only the direct event image, and (2) the prior method of Bocchieri et al. [8] that classifies and removes dark counts before solving for the maximum-likelihood event location. The kaleidoscopic method decisively outperforms both on every metric and at every brightness level. At N₀=30, the kaleidoscopic 3D error of 0.14 mm is 5.9× lower than the non-kaleidoscopic version (0.83 mm) and 4.6× lower than the noise-removing baseline (0.64 mm). At the lowest brightness (N₀=10), the kaleidoscopic method still achieves 0.29 mm 3D error versus 0.95 mm and 0.80 mm for the two baselines, demonstrating robustness to photon starvation. Per-axis resolutions follow the same pattern, with the kaleidoscopic method maintaining sub-0.31 mm resolution in all axes even at N₀=10, while both baselines degrade to 1.1–1.4 mm. The advantage is attributed to the multiple mirror-reflection views providing redundant depth cues that are robust to dark counts and low photon counts, whereas depth-from-defocus alone is highly sensitive to noise in the photon-starved regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes kaleidoscopic mirror geometry—previously studied for multiview 3D reconstruction of extended objects with abundant light [2,3,6,10,20,25,29,32]—for the fundamentally different problem of localizing a point light source in a photon-starved regime. It departs from prior scintillation event imaging that estimates 3D position via depth-from-defocus with a single camera [8] or stereo vision with two objectives [11], both of which suffer from limited light collection. The GMM+EM framework is standard, but the key conceptual move is constraining all Gaussian components to a single 3D point source through known mirror reflection geometry, turning a multi-view problem into a single-camera, single-exposure measurement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~6× reduction in 3D localization error (0.14 mm vs. 0.83 mm at N₀=30) and sub-millimeter resolution even at N₀=10 photons make 3D event localization practical with a commercial SPAD camera, directly enabling Compton cameras, neutron scatter cameras, and PET/SPECT with improved spatial resolution. Key limitations include testing only first-order reflections in a single pyramid geometry, single-event-per-image operation, and the need for a wide-aperture scintillator (120° apex angle) to suppress higher-order reflections. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multiple simultaneous events, different kaleidoscopic geometries, and whether internal reflections at the scintillator base can be exploited rather than avoided.
