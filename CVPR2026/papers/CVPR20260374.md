## Problem

Emotion editing in talking face video is constrained by three existing paradigms: label-based methods are limited to discrete emotion categories, audio-based methods fail to disentangle emotion from linguistic content, and image-based methods require curated frontal reference images that are unavailable for extended emotions (e.g., sarcasm, charisma). The core open problem is bridging the domain gap between audio and visual emotion representations to enable cross-modal emotion transfer, particularly for emotions never observed during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260374/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a neutral talking face video (256×256 frames) and an emotional speech audio clip (16 kHz) as inputs, where the video supplies identity, lip motion, and head pose while the audio supplies only the emotion signal. It produces an emotional talking face video in which facial expressions are modified to match the target emotion while preserving the original identity and lip synchronization. At inference, the model operates on 5-frame temporal windows with zero-padded references and autoregressive prediction, and can be plugged into any disentanglement-based generator as a lightweight replacement for the heavy facial expression encoder.

## Method

C-MET models emotion as a semantic vector defined as the difference between two emotional embeddings, computed separately in audio space (via emotion2vec+large) and visual space (via EDTalk's disentangled facial expression encoder). A multimodal transformer encoder takes concatenated tokens from reference visual semantic vectors, the audio emotion semantic vector, and input visual embeddings—distinguished by learnable type embeddings and sinusoidal positional encodings—and regresses the target visual semantic vector. Training combines three losses: a bidirectional contrastive loss that aligns audio and visual token spaces, an MSE reconstruction loss on the predicted semantic vectors (in both forward and reverse directions), and a direction loss that enforces the forward and reverse vectors to be antiparallel. The predicted visual semantic vector is added to the input visual embedding and decoded by EDTalk's pretrained generator to synthesize the emotional frame. Because the method operates on relative differences rather than absolute embeddings, it generalizes to extended emotions synthesized via expressive TTS (e.g., Gemini TTS) that were never seen during training.

## Results

On MEAD, C-MET achieves the highest emotion classification accuracy (Acc_emo) of 55.91%, outperforming the best baseline EDTalk (41.99%) by 14 percentage points. On CREMA-D, C-MET reaches 43.47% Acc_emo versus EAT's 39.97% and EDTalk's 29.69%. In terms of visual quality, EDTalk retains a slight edge in FID (76.42 vs. 90.80) and FVD (293.90 vs. 329.86) on MEAD, while C-MET achieves comparable SyncNet confidence (7.9996 vs. 8.0529). C-MET also reduces inference time (AITV 2.643 s/video) relative to image-based methods EDTalk (2.827) and EAMM (3.745), though it is slower than the audio-based FLOAT (1.434).

## Baselines & Comparisons

C-MET decisively outperforms all four baselines on the primary metric Acc_emo: 55.91% on MEAD vs. EDTalk (41.99%), EAT (41.56%), EAMM (18.81%), and FLOAT (13.21%), and 43.47% on CREMA-D vs. EAT (39.97%), EDTalk (29.69%), FLOAT (29.11%), and EAMM (19.15%). Against the closest competitor EDTalk (image-based), C-MET gains 14 pp on MEAD and 13.8 pp on CREMA-D in emotion accuracy, while EDTalk retains a marginal advantage in FID (76.42 vs. 90.80) and FVD (293.90 vs. 329.86) on MEAD, reflecting a trade-off between emotional expressiveness and pixel-level reconstruction fidelity. Compared to the audio-based FLOAT, C-MET improves Acc_emo by 42.7 pp on MEAD and 14.4 pp on CREMA-D, demonstrating that explicitly modeling cross-modal emotion semantic vectors resolves the entanglement between speech content and emotion that plagues FLOAT. In inference speed, C-MET (2.643 s/video on MEAD) is faster than both image-based methods (EDTalk 2.827, EAMM 3.745) but slower than FLOAT (1.434), owing to the autoregressive transformer decoding over 5-frame windows. On CREMA-D, C-MET also surpasses EDTalk in AITV (1.561 vs. 1.590) while achieving substantially higher emotion accuracy (43.47 vs. 29.69), confirming that the plug-and-play transformer module is both more accurate and marginally faster than the heavy facial expression encoder it replaces.

## Relation to Prior Work

C-MET builds on the two-stage emotion editing framework of Sun et al. [45] and the disentanglement-based talking face generators (EDTalk, PD-FGC), repurposing their facial expression encoders and decoders as frozen components. The core idea of emotion semantic vectors—taking the difference between two emotional embeddings—draws directly from Emoknob's voice emotion control, but C-MET is the first to explicitly learn a cross-modal mapping between audio-space and visual-space emotion semantic vectors via a transformer. It also leverages emotion2vec as a large-scale pretrained audio encoder, departing from prior audio-based methods (FLOAT, EAT) that entangle emotion with linguistic content, and from image-based methods (EDTalk, EAMM) that require curated frontal-view reference videos.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to synthesize extended emotions (sarcasm, charisma, empathy) without collecting new audio-visual paired data addresses a practical bottleneck in expressive talking face generation, and the plug-and-play design means the module can be dropped into future disentanglement frameworks as they improve. However, the method still depends on EDTalk's decoder for visual quality (its FID/FVD scores are slightly worse than EDTalk's), and the extended-emotion evaluation relies on user studies with Gemini TTS-generated audio rather than ground-truth video, leaving open questions about robustness to TTS artifacts and generalization beyond the six tested extended emotions. A key open question is whether the cross-modal semantic vector mapping generalizes to emotions with very different facial kinematics (e.g., full-body expressions) or to multi-speaker scenarios.
