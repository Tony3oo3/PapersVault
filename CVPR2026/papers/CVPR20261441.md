## Problem

Existing assembly datasets and benchmarks are dominated by simplified IKEA-style furniture with easily distinguishable parts and straightforward attachments, failing to capture the geometric complexity and sophisticated maneuvers (e.g., insertion with twisting) required for industrial objects like motors, gearboxes, and appliances. Moreover, prior evaluation protocols only check final pose alignment via point-cloud metrics, leaving the physical feasibility of intermediate assembly trajectories unverified.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261441/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives an unordered set of N 3D part point clouds (each resampled to a fixed point count) and a step-by-step instruction manual where each step consists of a 2D line-drawing diagram (fixed parallel projection, IKEA-style) and a free-form text description. It outputs a part permutation (assembly order), and for each part a sequence of T=12 SE(3) poses (rotation as quaternion + translation) representing its 6-DoF motion trajectory from initial to final assembled position. Notably, the diagram encoder operates on difference images between consecutive steps to isolate the newly added part, rather than on full step renderings.

## Method

AssemblyDyno is a transformer-based architecture that jointly predicts assembly order and 6-DoF trajectories in a single forward pass. Feature extraction uses a PointNet variant for 3D parts, DINOv3 on difference images of consecutive instruction diagrams (split into K patches), and Qwen-3 embeddings for text, all projected to a shared dimension D. Part order is obtained via a similarity matrix between part and instruction features followed by Hungarian matching, producing a permutation matrix. For trajectory prediction, the permutation matrix with positional encoding is added to part features and passed through a self-attention transformer decoder, then cross-attended with position-encoded instruction features to yield an N×T×D latent, decoded by a pose head into quaternions and translations. Training is fully supervised (no simulator-in-the-loop) with a composite loss combining chamfer distance on the final assembled point cloud, per-step translation L2, per-step rotation chamfer distance (to handle part symmetries), and frame-to-frame velocity smoothing terms for both translation and rotation.

## Results

On AssemblyBench (standard setting, model predicts its own part order), AssemblyDyno achieves 34.64% Success Rate (SR) and 71.21% Part Assembly Correctness (PA) for final pose estimation, with a Kendall's Tau of 0.819 for ordering. In the physics simulator evaluation, AssemblyDyno reaches 13.57% SR and 42.76% PA, while the baseline ManualPA achieves only 1.79% SR and 23.24% PA. With ground-truth part orders, AssemblyDyno's simulator SR jumps to 33.57% (vs. 2.14% for ManualPA), and the median ACD drops from 104.66×10⁻³ to 9.97×10⁻³, demonstrating that the model's trajectories are physically feasible when ordering errors are removed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is ManualPA (Zhang et al., ICCV 2025), a state-of-the-art method for instruction-diagram-guided part assembly. On final pose estimation, AssemblyDyno edges out ManualPA by a narrow margin: 34.64% vs. 33.57% SR and 71.21% vs. 70.04% PA in the standard setting, and 44.29% vs. 39.28% SR with GT orders. The decisive gap appears in the physics-simulator evaluation: AssemblyDyno achieves 13.57% SR versus ManualPA's 1.79% (≈7.6×) in the standard setting, and 33.57% vs. 2.14% (≈15.7×) with GT orders. The median ACD in the simulator is 34.77×10⁻³ for AssemblyDyno versus 192.79×10⁻³ for ManualPA, indicating that ManualPA's heuristically generated trajectories (simple outward translation from the predicted final pose) are largely physically infeasible. The ablation 'w/o trajectory' (replacing AssemblyDyno's trajectory module with the same heuristic) collapses to 1.43% simulator SR, confirming that the dedicated trajectory prediction head is the primary source of the advantage over ManualPA.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ManualPA line of work [43]—which predicts assembly order and final 6D poses from IKEA-style instruction diagrams—to a substantially harder regime: industrial objects with complex geometries, full 6-DoF motion trajectories, and physics-feasibility verification. The ground-truth trajectories are generated via the Assemble-Them-All physics-based disassembly pipeline [31, 32], while the instruction manuals are produced by a VLM (GPT-4.1) annotation pipeline that is novel in its handling of occlusions and repeated part types. Architecturally, AssemblyDyno composes off-the-shelf encoders (PointNet, DINOv3, Qwen-3) with a transformer decoder and Hungarian-matching order predictor, departing from prior work primarily in its joint trajectory prediction head and its physics-simulator-based evaluation protocol rather than in any single encoder or attention mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AssemblyBench is the first large-scale assembly benchmark to include ground-truth 6-DoF trajectories alongside multimodal manuals for non-furniture industrial objects, filling a clear gap between simplified IKEA-style datasets and the geometric complexity of real manufacturing. The physics-simulator evaluation protocol is a meaningful step toward closing the sim-to-real gap: it exposes compounding collision and infeasibility errors invisible to point-cloud metrics, and the 10× simulator success-rate gap (33% vs. 3%) between AssemblyDyno and ManualPA underscores how much prior methods' trajectories fail under physical constraints. Key limitations remain—performance is still far from deployment-ready, the dataset is entirely synthetic, and order prediction is identified as the dominant bottleneck in the standard setting—leaving open questions about real-world transfer, incorporation of gravity/friction, and whether the implicit physics learned from supervised trajectories generalize beyond the training distribution.
