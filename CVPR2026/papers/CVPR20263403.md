## Problem

Multimodal interactions (redundancy, uniqueness, synergy) vary dynamically across individual samples, yet conventional paradigms handle them implicitly with fixed inductive biases: joint learning is dominated by the most salient modality under redundancy, while modality ensembles cannot capture synergy. This paper provides the first systematic information-theoretic analysis showing that effective multimodal learning requires capturing the full spectrum of sample-specific interaction compositions, and identifies the resulting interaction deficit in existing approaches.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263403/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes pairs of modalities (audio+video, RGB+optical flow, image+text, visual+textual) encoded by ResNet18 or ViT backbones into compact representations Z. Rather than producing a single fused prediction, the model explicitly decomposes each representation into four interaction components (redundancy R, two uniqueness terms U¹ and U², and synergy S), each projected to a preliminary prediction, which are then dynamically weighted by a sample-specific gating network to yield the final class label.

## Method

DMIL performs a two-step variational decomposition: first, an intra-modality decomposition factorizes each modality's representation into a directly predictive component M and a residual inter-modality component N (via a variational information bottleneck objective); second, a consistency decomposition separates M into a shared redundant component R and modality-specific unique components U. The synergy component S is constructed by fusing the residual factors N¹ and N² through a multi-level fusion mechanism designed to capture non-linear (e.g., XOR) cross-modal interactions. Training follows a three-stage strategy: Stage 1 learns the intra-modality decomposition, Stage 2 freezes those modules and optimizes the consistency decomposition while constructing S, and Stage 3 jointly fine-tunes all parameters with a gated aggregation that dynamically weights each interaction component per sample. A theoretical lower bound (Theorem 1) motivates the design by showing that maximizing learned information I(Z;Y) requires both effective learning across all interaction compositions and representations that explicitly encode each sample's interaction composition.

## Results

DMIL achieves state-of-the-art performance across all five evaluation datasets and both backbone architectures (ResNet18 and ViT). On CREMA-D, DMIL reaches 77.02% ACC and 85.13% mAP, surpassing the strongest baseline MCR (76.34% / 84.59%). On UCF101, DMIL attains 85.01% ACC and 90.53% mAP, a substantial margin over the next-best MLB (83.25% / 89.53%). Under the ViT backbone on Kinetic-Sounds, DMIL achieves 74.20% ACC and 81.83% mAP, outperforming the ensemble baseline (71.80% / 79.03%) by over 2 points in accuracy. On CMU-MOSEI (text+visual sentiment), DMIL scores 81.12% ACC and 82.17% mAP, again leading all competitors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DMIL outperforms all 10 baselines on every dataset and metric, with no losses or ties. The closest competitor is MCR [20] (game-theoretic regularization), which leads among baselines on most datasets but is still surpassed by DMIL by 0.3–2.2 points in accuracy (e.g., 77.02 vs. 76.34 on CREMA-D; 86.72 vs. 86.41 on KS). MLB [19] (multi-loss gradient modulation) is the strongest baseline on UCF101 (83.25% ACC) but trails DMIL by a notable 1.76 points. On the ViT-based KS setting, the Ensemble paradigm is the best baseline (71.80% ACC), yet DMIL still leads by 2.4 points, confirming the advantage of explicit interaction decomposition over simple prediction averaging. Regularization-based methods (OGM [32], PMR [12], AGM [19]) and interaction-focused methods (MMIB [28], QMF [43], FCL [24], MMML [38], I2M2 [27], MMTM [35]) are consistently outperformed by 1–4 points in accuracy across datasets, with the largest gaps appearing on UCF101 and the ViT-based KS experiments where architectural differences amplify the benefit of targeted interaction learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the information decomposition framework of Liang et al. (ICLR 2023), which formalized multimodal information into redundancy, uniqueness, and synergy components, and on the Deep Variational Information Bottleneck (Alemi et al., 2016) for the variational mechanism used to factorize representations. It departs from prior work by shifting the analysis from dataset-level aggregate interaction quantities to sample-specific interaction composition, proving a theoretical lower bound (Theorem 1) that connects learned information to per-sample interaction structure. While prior interaction-focused methods (MCR, FCL, MMIB) implicitly handle these components through regularization or contrastive objectives, DMIL explicitly decomposes them via a two-stage variational architecture and learns each component through a dedicated three-stage training protocol.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic information-theoretic account of why no single fusion paradigm (joint learning or ensemble) can handle all interaction compositions, which explains previously observed but unexplained performance discrepancies across datasets. The practical impact is a flexible, architecture-agnostic module (compatible with ResNet, ViT, 2- or 3-modality settings) that consistently improves over strong baselines by 1–3% across diverse tasks. Key open questions remain: how the framework scales to more than three modalities where the information decomposition becomes combinatorially complex, and whether the explicit decomposition transfers to large multimodal language models where interactions are far more heterogeneous. The interaction composition variable C is a theoretical construct that is never directly observed in practice, so the degree to which the learned decomposition truly recovers the 'true' interaction structure remains an open interpretability question.
