## Problem

Supervised fine-tuning of LLMs for medical reasoning typically relies on large, unfiltered datasets containing redundant and low-quality samples, incurring substantial computational cost without proportional gains. Existing data selection methods are one-dimensional—either difficulty-based or influence-based—overlooking the critical interplay between a sample's intrinsic reasoning complexity and its gradient-based optimization utility. The paper identifies that influence alone favors easy-to-optimize samples with shallow reasoning chains, while difficulty alone selects noisy cases with weak gradient signals that destabilize training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a medical reasoning dataset in instruction-following format (question q paired with a reference answer a containing a reasoning trace), a pre-trained LLM checkpoint (base model before SFT), and a small validation set (default 20 samples per downstream task, 9 tasks total). It produces a selected subset S at a user-specified retention ratio r (e.g., 1% or 10%), which is then used for LoRA fine-tuning (rank 8, 3 epochs, 8192-token context) to yield a medical reasoning model. The difficulty score is a scalar from a BiomedBERT classifier on a 5-point Likert scale, and the influence score is a scalar Dot value computed via gradient inner products projected to 4096 dimensions via Johnson-Lindenstrauss random projection.

## Method

DIQ (Difficulty Influence Quadrant) assigns each training sample two orthogonal scores: a model-agnostic difficulty score from a fine-tuned BiomedBERT classifier (one of Knowledge, Reasoning, or Overall dimensions on a 5-point Likert scale) and a model-dependent Dot influence score defined as the inner product between the sample's per-example gradient and the mean validation gradient at the base checkpoint. A first-order Taylor expansion of gradient descent shows that Dot(z) approximates the expected one-step decrease in average validation loss up to O(η²), making it a principled proxy for optimization utility without requiring Hessian computations. The dataset is partitioned into four quadrants using a percentile threshold on difficulty and the median of Dot, then selected by priority Q1 (high-difficulty, high-influence) → Q2 → Q3 → Q4, with samples within each quadrant sorted by Dot descending (ties broken by difficulty descending) until the target retention ratio is met. The entire scoring pass requires only O(|D_val| + |D|) backward passes and no Hessian-vector products, making it 1.85–9.80× cheaper than a single full fine-tuning run and reusable across multiple model configurations.

## Results

On Llama3.1-8B-Instruct fine-tuned with LoRA, DIQ-selected subsets achieve the best average accuracy (AvgA) across all four dataset–budget combinations: 42.78% (Huatuo 1%), 44.04% (Huatuo 10%), 41.46% (FineMed 1%), and 39.36% (FineMed 10%). Notably, DIQ at 10% on Huatuo (44.04%) surpasses full-data fine-tuning (43.44%), and DIQ at 1% on FineMed (41.46%) exceeds the full-data model (38.57%) by +2.89 points. On standard tasks (AvgS), the gains are especially large: DIQ at 1% on FineMed lifts AvgS to 58.14% versus 42.52% for full-data training (+15.62). Across nine benchmarks (MedQA, MedMCQA, MMLU-Pro, HLE, MedBullets, MedXpertQA, MedGUIDE, MetaMedQA), DIQ consistently outperforms all baselines at matched budgets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DIQ outperforms all three baselines (PPL, Similarity, LESS) on AvgA at every dataset–budget combination tested. On Huatuo at 1%, DIQ (42.78%) beats the strongest baseline LESS (40.54%) by +2.24 points and PPL (38.99%) by +3.79; at 10%, DIQ (44.04%) leads Similarity (41.73%) by +2.31 and LESS (40.89%) by +3.15. On FineMed at 1%, DIQ (41.46%) surpasses LESS (39.20%) by +2.26 and PPL (37.05%) by +4.41; at 10%, DIQ (39.36%) edges out PPL (38.32%) by +1.04 and LESS (37.78%) by +1.58. The margin over LESS is particularly informative because LESS also uses gradient-based influence (TracIn) but selects only by influence without the difficulty signal, confirming that the two-dimensional DIQ criterion is necessary. DIQ also surpasses full-data fine-tuning at 10% on Huatuo (+0.60) and at 1% on FineMed (+2.89), demonstrating that principled selection with minimal data can exceed brute-force scaling.

## Relation to Prior Work

DIQ sits at the intersection of two prior threads: gradient-based influence functions (TracIn, LESS) that measure a sample's expected effect on validation loss, and difficulty-based selection heuristics (Thapa et al., MedReason) that score samples by knowledge or reasoning complexity. The key departure is that prior methods treat these signals in isolation—either selecting by difficulty alone or by influence alone—whereas DIQ explicitly models their interaction via a 2D quadrant partition, revealing that the two axes are not redundant but capture complementary (and sometimes conflicting) properties. The 'less is more' data-efficiency paradigm (LIMA, LiMo) motivates the goal, while the BiomedBERT-based difficulty classifier and Johnson-Lindenstrauss gradient projection are borrowed tools repurposed for the medical SFT setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: matching full-dataset performance with 1% of the data and exceeding it at 10% translates directly into reduced compute for medical VLM fine-tuning, and the one-time selection cost is an order of magnitude cheaper than a single fine-tuning run. The quadrant analysis exposes a non-obvious tension—high-influence samples tend to be shallow while high-difficulty samples produce weak gradients—that should inform data curation beyond the medical domain. Open questions include whether static influence scores computed on a base model remain valid as training progresses, how DIQ behaves at ≥70B scale, and whether the difficulty classifier generalizes to clinical subdomains not represented in its training data.
