## Problem

Complex image editing instructions involving multiple combinatorial operations or inter-step dependencies are poorly handled by existing paradigms: single-turn editing fails to parse and execute compounded instructions accurately, while sequential editing, though it simplifies each step, suffers from severe error accumulation that degrades final fidelity. The paper asks whether sequential decomposition can be made robust enough to consistently outperform single-turn editing as task complexity grows, and how to balance the benefits of decomposition against its compounding-error drawbacks.

## Input / Output

The model receives a source RGB image and a complex text instruction that concatenates multiple atomic editing operations (e.g., add, remove, replace, translate, rotate, color change, viewpoint change), potentially containing inter-step dependencies where later operations reference earlier edits by description rather than explicit object name (e.g., 'the object that was rotated' or 'where the chair originally was'). At inference, the model produces an interleaved sequence of K decomposed text instructions and their corresponding intermediate/final edited images, where K is user-specified to control decomposition granularity. The final output is the last generated image, with intermediate images serving as contextual guidance for subsequent steps.

## Method

The approach builds on BAGEL, a unified multimodal model, and finetunes it with three joint objectives: (1) autoregressive instruction decomposition predicting per-step instructions between consecutive images, (2) rectified-flow in-context editing on intermediate and target images conditioned on decomposed instructions and prior images (deliberately excluding the overall complex instruction to decouple generation from the full prompt), and (3) single-turn editing to retain direct editing capability. Training data comes from a Blender-based synthetic pipeline that procedurally constructs scenes, applies L atomic operations with random parameters, and injects inter-step dependencies (operation reference and position reference) with probability p_d, yielding 94K editing sequences with guaranteed ground-truth decompositions. At inference, the key contribution is Context-Guided Sequential Editing (CGSE), which computes a base velocity that edits the source image directly using all decomposed instructions up to step i, then adds a separate context-guidance term from selected historical editing results weighted by a tunable coefficient γ_ctx, allowing explicit control over how much prior (potentially error-laden) results influence the current edit. Sim-to-real transfer is achieved by co-training on a mixture of synthetic sequences and real single-turn editing pairs from Pico-Banana for only 1,000 steps.

## Results

On synthetic tasks with inter-step dependencies (Table 2), finetuned BAGEL with 5-step Context-Guided Sequential Editing (CGSE) achieves the best GPT-5 score of 4.14, outperforming its single-turn counterpart (3.91) and all baselines. On DINO-I similarity, CGSE (K=3) reaches 0.779, surpassing Gemini 2.5 Flash Image (0.698 sequential) and GPT-4o (0.507 sequential). On the real-world Complex-Edit benchmark (Table 5), co-trained BAGEL-SR with CGSE (K=2, γ_ctx=0.5) achieves the best Instruction Following (8.25) and Identity Preservation (6.38) scores, improving over zero-shot BAGEL (7.88 IF, 5.22 IP) and real-data-only finetuning (8.20 IF, 6.00 IP).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Qwen-Image, finetuned BAGEL dominates across all metrics in both single-turn and sequential settings (e.g., GPT-5: 3.91 vs. 2.02 single-turn; 4.14 vs. N/A sequential). Against GPT-4o*, BAGEL (Finetuned) achieves substantially higher DINO-I (0.791 vs. 0.579) and DINO-D (0.578 vs. 0.388) in single-turn editing, and the gap widens dramatically in sequential editing where GPT-4o* degrades to 3.09 GPT-5 while BAGEL CGSE reaches 4.14. Against Gemini 2.5 Flash Image, BAGEL (Finetuned) leads on DINO-I (0.791 vs. 0.712) and DINO-D (0.578 vs. 0.408) in single-turn, and on sequential GPT-5 (4.14 vs. 3.28), though Gemini's single-turn GPT-5 (3.23) is closer to BAGEL's (3.57). A notable finding is that GPT-4o* and zero-shot BAGEL both suffer severe performance drops when sequential editing is applied naively (GPT-4o*: 3.77→3.09; BAGEL: 2.17→2.03), whereas the finetuned model's sequential paradigms consistently improve over its own single-turn baseline, demonstrating that learned decomposition is essential for robust sequential editing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the in-context editing framework established by VINCIE [24] and the unified multimodal model BAGEL [10], which natively supports interleaved text-image generation. It departs from prior complex-editing work [40] that attempted sequential decomposition and observed severe compounding errors, by proposing a controlled synthetic data pipeline (Blender-based) that provides deterministic, high-quality decomposition supervision and a new inference paradigm (Context-Guided Sequential Editing) that explicitly modulates the influence of prior editing results via a tunable guidance coefficient. Unlike prior data construction approaches [3, 29, 42, 43] that rely on pretrained editing models (whose quality is upper-bounded by their own failure modes), this pipeline guarantees authentic edits through predefined 3D scene operations, enabling scalable task curation at arbitrary complexity without quality degradation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the long-standing assumption that sequential editing inevitably degrades due to error accumulation can be overcome with proper paradigm design and training data, offering a practical recipe (CGSE with modulated context guidance) for complex multi-edit tasks. The sim-to-real transfer via brief co-training (1,000 steps) is promising but limited: perceptual quality drops, full-context sequential editing fails to generalize to real images, and gains on real-world benchmarks are modest. Open questions include whether the synthetic-to-real gap can be closed with more diverse synthetic scenes, whether the approach scales to open-domain real-world editing beyond the Complex-Edit benchmark, and how to preserve perceptual fidelity during sequential execution without sacrificing decomposition accuracy.
