## Problem

Existing backdoor attacks on lane detection suffer from two critical weaknesses: triggers are placed at random or fixed positions that ignore spatial sensitivity of the model, yielding suboptimal attack success rates, and the trigger patterns are visually conspicuous and easily flagged by forensic detectors. This paper addresses both gaps by asking how to place triggers where the model is most susceptible and generate them in a way that blends seamlessly into driving scenes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261705/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes RGB driving scene images (CULane at 1640×590, TuSimple at 1280×720), a text prompt describing the desired trigger (e.g., 'Add a small and sparse brown mud'), and a pre-trained lane detection model of the same architecture as the victim. SAM2 segments the road region, and a 40×40 mask (~900 pixels) is placed at the heatmap-identified optimal position. The output is a set of poisoned training images (10% poisoning rate) that, when used to train the LD model, produce a backdoored detector whose predictions shift (offset by 60 px, disappear, or rotate by 9°) only when the trigger is present at inference.

## Method

DBALD operates in three stages. First, SAM2 segments the drivable road area and a sliding window enumerates ~100–400 candidate trigger positions. Second, a strategy-specific gradient attention map is computed by backpropagating the task-relevant loss (focal loss for lane-disappearance, L1/GLIoU for offset/rotation) through a pre-trained LD model; the candidate with the highest summed absolute gradient magnitude is selected as the optimal trigger location. Third, a mask-guided diffusion inpainting process (based on UltraEdit's alternating even/odd-step scheme) synthesizes the trigger within the selected region, guided by the text prompt and constrained by two auxiliary losses: an MSE term (L_lane) that preserves lane-marking geometry and an SSIM term (L_env) that maintains photorealistic consistency of surrounding traffic elements. This dual-loss design suppresses the structural artifacts that vanilla diffusion inpainting introduces near mask boundaries, making the trigger visually natural and evading CLIP-based and gradient-based forensic detectors.

## Results

DBALD achieves the highest average Attack Success Rate (ASR) across all four lane detection models on both CULane and TuSimple, with an average improvement of +10.87% over the strongest baseline. On CULane, DBALD reaches 76.41% ASR (LaneATT), 66.50% (ADNet), 70.74% (SCNN), and 73.39% (RESA), while on TuSimple it reaches 90.84%, 80.66%, 93.54%, and 95.43% respectively. Clean accuracy is well preserved: poisoned models retain within 1–3% of their benign ACC (e.g., LaneATT drops from 75.01% to 74.20% on CULane). In physical experiments with cone triggers, DBALD achieves 57% ASR under LOA, with only modest drops under rain (45%), blur (48%), and occlusion (49%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DBALD outperforms all four baselines on every model–dataset combination. Against BadNets (fixed white square) and Blended (fixed image blending), DBALD gains 10–30+ percentage points in ASR, as these static triggers are easily ignored by the model. The closest competitors are LD-Attack (fixed pattern + image overlay) and BadLane (random mud-like texture): on TuSimple, LD-Attack reaches 89.22–92.76% ASR and BadLane reaches 89.51–91.83%, while DBALD achieves 90.84–95.43%, a modest but consistent 1–17% edge. The gap widens substantially on the more complex CULane dataset, where BadLane drops to 54.56–68.85% ASR and LD-Attack to 63.34–69.07%, whereas DBALD maintains 66.50–76.41%. Notably, DBALD also preserves clean accuracy better than most baselines (e.g., ADNet poisoned ACC of 82.37% vs. 74.37% for Blended), and in stealthiness evaluation (Table 2), UniDetection flags only 2.72% of DBALD samples versus 59.31% for BadLane, confirming superior visual naturalness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of backdoor attacks on lane detection initiated by LD-Attack (Han et al., 2022) and BadLane (Zhang et al., 2023), which relied on fixed or randomly placed triggers with limited stealth. It departs from these by repurposing the UltraEdit region-based diffusion editing framework for trigger synthesis and adapting gradient-attention analysis (previously used in classification via Grad-CAM) to the hybrid lane-existence/coordinate-regression losses of LD models. The two novel loss terms (lane-structure MSE and environment SSIM) are specific to the driving-scene inpainting setting and have no direct analogue in prior diffusion editing or backdoor literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical threat is underscored by physical experiments showing 57% ASR with simple traffic cones and the near-total failure of existing defenses (fine-tuning, neuron pruning, and even a VLM anomaly detector catching only 3–6% of triggers). The stealthiness gap—UniDetection flags 59% of BadLane triggers but under 3% of DBALD's—suggests current forensic tooling is ill-equipped for diffusion-generated, contextually blended triggers in driving scenes. Open questions include whether task-specific distilled diffusion models can reduce generation cost, whether quantization-based defenses (mentioned as future work) can close the gap, and whether the findings generalize beyond the four tested architectures and two datasets.
