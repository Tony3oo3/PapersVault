## Problem

Conventional lesion segmentation collapses all non-lesion pixels into a single homogeneous 'background' class, discarding the rich anatomical context (organs, tissues, structures) in which lesions appear. This binary formulation is a fundamental limitation shared across architectures, leading to false positives and unstable predictions for small, sporadic lesions. The paper asks whether sub-dividing the background into semantically meaningful auxiliary classes can improve segmentation, and provides the first rigorous information-theoretic justification for why it does.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262419/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard 3D medical volumes (CT, MRI, or PET) and produces a per-pixel lesion segmentation mask at inference. During training, the model receives multi-class pixel labels comprising the target lesion class plus one or more auxiliary background structures (e.g., kidney parenchyma, pancreas, multiple organs), which can be manually annotated, automatically generated by pretrained organ segmentation models (TotalSegmentator, VIBE-Segmentator), or interactively produced via nnInteractive with 7–10 clicks. At inference, only the target-class output is used; the auxiliary heads are discarded, so there is no increase in inference cost.

## Method

BackSplit is a training paradigm that replaces the single background class with multiple auxiliary anatomical classes and jointly optimizes all classes via standard multi-class softmax supervision, requiring no architectural modification. The theoretical core (Theorem 1) proves that the expected Fisher Information of the full multi-class score dominates that of the collapsed binary score in the Loewner order, with the gap equal to the conditional variance of the score across the collapsed classes; Proposition 1 gives a closed-form softmax decomposition showing the gap is non-zero whenever at least two non-target classes carry distinct gradient directions. Corollary 1 then shows the multi-class MLE is asymptotically more efficient, yielding smaller prediction variance for the target-class posterior via the delta method. Empirically, the paradigm is validated across five datasets (KiTS23, PANTHER-MR, NSCLC-Radiomics, AutoPET, MSWAL) and three architectures (U-Net, ResEncU-Net, SegResNet), demonstrating consistent Dice and HD-95 improvements even when auxiliary labels are automatically or interactively generated with noise.

## Results

On KiTS23 (cyst segmentation), BackSplit nearly doubles Dice across all three architectures: U-Net improves from 0.179 to 0.457, ResEncU-Net from 0.253 to 0.461, and SegResNet from 0.291 to 0.429. On PANTHER-MR (pancreatic tumor), Dice gains are more modest (e.g., U-Net 0.478→0.525), while on NSCLC-Radiomics (GTV), improvements are consistent but smaller (e.g., ResEncU-Net 0.502→0.539). HD-95 decreases and NSD increases in the majority of settings, confirming sharper boundaries and better surface agreement. All gains are achieved without adding model parameters or changing inference cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is standard binary (target-vs-background) training with identical architecture, hyperparameters, and data splits. BackSplit wins on Dice in 17 out of 18 dataset–architecture combinations, with the largest gains on KiTS23 where Dice nearly doubles (e.g., +0.28 for U-Net). NSD improves in 16/18 settings, and HD-95 decreases in 15/18. The two exceptions are PANTHER-MR with ResEncU-Net (HD-95 worsens from 66.3 to 70.5 despite a marginal Dice gain) and NSCLC-Radiomics with SegResNet (NSD drops from 0.392 to 0.384 while Dice still improves). No comparison against other published segmentation methods is made; the paper positions BackSplit as a training-paradigm change orthogonal to architecture choice rather than a new model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the well-known multi-class segmentation formulation by reframing it as a structured background supervision strategy (BackSplit) for lesion segmentation, rather than treating it as a new architectural or loss-function design. Conceptually, it builds on the label-coarsening literature and the Missing Information Principle from statistical learning theory, providing the first rigorous Fisher Information decomposition showing that collapsing non-target classes into a single background label provably reduces expected information. It also connects to prior context-aware and auxiliary-supervision methods (organ masks, anatomy-guided reasoning, multi-task losses) but departs from them by requiring no additional network branches or handcrafted priors—only a richer label space during training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BackSplit offers a drop-in training modification (add auxiliary organ/tissue labels to the softmax output) that is architecture-agnostic, requires no inference-time overhead, and works even with automatically or noisily generated auxiliary masks from pretrained models like TotalSegmentator or interactive tools like nnInteractive. The Fisher Information theorem gives practitioners a principled reason to prefer multi-class background supervision over binary training, and the consistent gains across five datasets and three backbones strengthen practical confidence. Open questions include which specific auxiliary structures contribute most, whether language-driven or proxy representations of anatomical context can replace explicit segmentations, and how the approach behaves in genuinely small-data regimes where the large-sample theoretical guarantees may not hold.
