## Problem

Physical adversarial attacks on visible-thermal (RGB-T) object detectors have been largely overlooked, and existing RGB-T attack methods suffer from two key limitations: they either use 2D patches restricted to narrow viewing angles (e.g., −30° to 30°) or deploy overlapping RGB-T patterns (ORP) with low-E films that reduce light transmittance by 30%, diminishing the visibility of the printed adversarial pattern. No prior work has achieved full-view (0°–360°) physical attacks across multiple RGB-T fusion architectures using a low-cost, physically realizable pattern.

## Input / Output

The method consumes aligned 3D RGB-T models of a human body and clothing (built from publicly available 3D RGB meshes extended with captured thermal textures), a target RGB-T detector, and background image pairs from the FLIR-aligned dataset. The output is a non-overlapping adversarial pattern (NORP) where each pixel is either a printed RGB color on fabric or a region covered with 0.1 mm aluminum film, rendered onto 3D models for digital evaluation and manufactured as physical shirt-and-pants clothing with 25 mm × 25 mm pixel resolution.

## Method

The core design is the Non-Overlapping RGB-T Pattern (NORP), which assigns each clothing pixel to either printable fabric (RGB controllable, thermal fixed by body temperature) or aluminum film (RGB and thermal both fixed by film properties), eliminating the light-reduction problem of overlapping patterns. To optimize this mixed discrete-continuous variable space, the paper proposes Spatial Discrete-Continuous Optimization (SDCO) with Spatially-Random Discretization (SRD): at each gradient step, a random Bernoulli mask (α = 0.7) discretizes a subset of thermal pixels (freezing their p_i and optimizing the corresponding RGB values) while the complementary subset keeps thermal continuous (freezing RGB and optimizing p_i), so every pixel is updated in both roles over iterations. The optimized textures are rendered onto 3D clothing meshes with EOT augmentation to simulate physical perturbations, and a fusion-stage ensemble loss (weighted sum over early-, mid-, late-fusion, and independent detectors) is used to improve transferability to unseen architectures.

## Results

In the digital world, the proposed NORP + SDCO method achieves an average ASR of 99.6% across four RGB-T detector architectures (Prob-E, Prob-M, Prob-L, YOLOv11 RGB/T), with individual ASRs of 100.0%, 100.0%, 99.8%, 98.8%, and 99.4%. In the physical world, the adversarial clothing achieves an average ASR of 71.0% (ranging from 61.2% to 79.2% across detectors), evaluated over 5,220 image pairs captured at full 0°–360° viewing angles and 2–15 m distances. The fusion-stage ensemble method further improves transferability to unseen detectors, reaching 99.0% ASR on D-DETR (a black-box late-fusion detector) versus 48.6–91.8% for single-model-optimized patterns.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method decisively outperforms all three prior RGB-T physical attack methods. MAP [19] (2D multispectral patch) peaks at 37.2% ASR on Prob-M and drops to 4.2% on YOLOv11-T, reflecting its limited −30° to 30° attack angle. MIC [20] (overlapping RGB-T pattern with low-E films) reaches at most 26.2% ASR on Prob-E, constrained by the 30% light transmittance loss from the laminated films. UAP [35] (unified adversarial patch for independent detectors) achieves at most 27.8% ASR on Prob-M and only 22.2% on YOLOv11-T in the physical world, versus 64.4% for the proposed method. Among alternative optimization strategies, STE [3] is the closest competitor (96.8% on Prob-M) but still trails SDCO by 3.2 points, while Gumbel-Softmax [16] lags further behind (87.8% on Prob-M), confirming that spatially-random discretization better matches the spatial interdependency of visible and thermal variables in NORP.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of RGB-T physical adversarial attacks initiated by AdvB (bulb-based), MAP and UAP (2D patches), and MIC (low-E film clothing). It departs from prior work in two key ways: replacing 2D limited-angle patches with 3D full-view (0°–360°) adversarial clothing, and replacing the overlapping RGB-T pattern (ORP) used by MIC with a non-overlapping design (NORP) that uses spatially distinct materials (printed fabric vs. aluminum film) to avoid the 30% light-transmittance loss. The SDCO optimization method is novel in addressing the spatial discrete-continuous coupling inherent to NORP, where each pixel is either a continuous RGB variable or a fixed thermal value, a constraint not present in prior single-modality or overlapping-pattern attacks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The systematic demonstration that a single adversarial clothing pattern can evade all four fusion architectures (early, mid, late, independent) at full viewing angles and distances up to 20 m exposes a fundamental vulnerability in multimodal pedestrian detection that is critical for autonomous driving safety. The 71% physical-world ASR (vs. 99.6% digital) and the fact that eight defense methods still leave ≥70% ASR indicate that current defenses are insufficient against this class of attack. Open questions include whether the NORP design generalizes to other multimodal pairs (e.g., RGB-LiDAR), whether the 3D modeling pipeline can be automated for arbitrary body shapes, and whether the fusion-stage ensemble strategy transfers to other multimodal detection tasks beyond pedestrian detection.
