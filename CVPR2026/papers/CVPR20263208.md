## Problem

Retrieval-Augmented Generation has proven effective for offline video comprehension, but streaming video introduces fundamentally different challenges: unbounded continuous data influx, strict real-time latency constraints, and queries whose answers depend on specific moments in an ever-growing stream. Existing RAG pipelines assume a complete, static video and cannot adaptively segment events, extract knowledge with low latency, or modulate retrieval scope based on query temporal sensitivity.

## Input / Output

The system consumes video frames incrementally in a sliding window of size w=8 for boundary detection, and receives a free-form text query at any point during the stream. Under strict causality (no future frames accessible), it outputs a text answer grounded in retrieved knowledge, while simultaneously maintaining a growing knowledge base of event-level captions. The framework is plug-and-play: it wraps around an existing MLLM (e.g., Qwen2-VL-7B or ViSpeak) without architectural modifications, injecting retrieved captions as augmented context into the generation prompt.

## Method

StreamRAG comprises three modules operating in sequence. (1) Stream Event Segmentation computes ViT [CLS]-token similarity between adjacent frames, derives depth scores within a sliding window, and triggers a boundary when a score exceeds μ+τ·σ, partitioning the stream into semantically coherent events without ground-truth annotations. (2) The Knowledge Extraction Accelerator concatenates the previous event's caption embeddings with the new event's multimodal video-text encoding, performs a single forward pass to obtain the full output probability matrix, then identifies a divergence point where log-probability drops below a dual threshold (relative α and absolute β) relative to a moving average; it preserves the verified caption prefix and autoregressively regenerates only the divergent suffix, cutting latency by ~27% at ~18% token reuse. (3) Query-aware Dynamic Knowledge Integration scores each query's temporal urgency via an LLM classifier and its historical relevance via cosine similarity to the top-3 retrieved captions, combining both into a composite gating score that selects among three retrieval tiers (latest-only, moderate k₁, or broad k₂ historical context), ensuring time-sensitive queries receive fresh evidence while historical queries access broader context.

## Results

On OVOBench, StreamRAG plugged into Qwen2-VL-7B (1 fps) raises the Real-Time Visual Perception average from 60.65 to 69.96 (+9.3 pts) and the Backward Tracing average from 48.58 to 53.08 (+4.5 pts), yielding an overall score of 42.30 vs. 40.55. When attached to the stronger streaming model ViSpeak, StreamRAG lifts the overall OVOBench score from 54.25 to 54.80 and the Backward Tracing average from 57.52 to 60.78. On StreamingBench, Qwen2-VL-7B+ours improves the Contextual Understanding average from 30.34 to 44.27 (+13.9 pts) and the Real-Time average from 71.15 to 77.33, while ViSpeak+ours reaches 41.00 CU-Avg vs. 37.80 for ViSpeak alone. The Knowledge Extraction Accelerator reduces captioning latency from 15.24 s to 11.12 s (27 % speedup) at ~18 % token reuse while simultaneously improving accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

StreamRAG consistently outperforms both existing RAG frameworks when attached to ViSpeak: ViSpeak+Ours (54.80 overall) beats ViSpeak+VideoRAG (53.82) by 1.0 pt and ViSpeak+StreamChatRAG (49.53) by 5.3 pt on OVOBench, with the largest gap on Backward Tracing (60.78 vs. 51.43 and 59.39 respectively). Against the strongest offline proprietary model Gemini 1.5 Pro (57.15), ViSpeak+Ours remains 2.4 pt lower overall but narrows the gap on Real-Time Perception (69.68 vs. 69.32) and Backward Tracing (60.78 vs. 62.54). Compared to the other online streaming baselines, ViSpeak+Ours leads Dispider (34.72) by 20.1 pt, Flash-VStream (45.09) by 9.7 pt, and VideoLLM-online (20.79 RT-Avg) by a wide margin. On StreamingBench, ViSpeak+ours (41.00 CU-Avg) surpasses ViSpeak alone (37.80) by 3.2 pt and Qwen2-VL-7B+ours (44.27 CU-Avg) shows a 13.9-pt gain over the un-augmented Qwen2-VL-7B (30.34). The blind GPT-4-turbo baseline (27.90 RT-Avg) confirms that visual access is essential, while Qwen2-VL-72B offline (49.30) is still outperformed by the 7B model augmented with StreamRAG on the online setting, underscoring the plug-and-play value of the framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

StreamRAG sits at the intersection of two threads: streaming video understanding (ViSpeak, Dispider, Flash-VStream) and retrieval-augmented video comprehension (VideoRAG, Goldfish, DrVideo). The closest prior work is StreamChat, which maintains a hierarchical memory updated at fixed intervals during streaming; StreamRAG departs from this by replacing fixed-rate updates with adaptive semantic event segmentation (borrowing depth-score analysis from VideoLLaMB), substituting input-side token compression with output-side caption token reuse for latency reduction, and introducing query-aware dynamic retrieval that modulates retrieval scope based on temporal sensitivity rather than relying solely on cosine similarity. The framework is explicitly positioned as a plug-and-play module that enhances existing streaming MLLMs (Qwen2-VL, ViSpeak) without architectural modification, distinguishing it from prior work that redesigns the model itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first RAG framework explicitly designed for streaming video QA, addressing a gap where offline RAG methods (VideoRAG, Goldfish) cannot handle unbounded temporal extent and real-time latency constraints. The finding that indiscriminate RAG (VideoRAG) actually degrades streaming model performance highlights that naive knowledge injection is counterproductive in time-sensitive settings, motivating the query-aware retrieval design. Open questions include scalability to multi-hour streams, integration of audio and other modalities into the knowledge base, and whether the token-reuse strategy generalizes to non-continuous video domains (e.g., multi-camera surveillance). The plug-and-play nature makes it practically deployable for applications like autonomous driving and AI glasses, but evaluation is limited to two benchmarks and two base models.
