## Problem

Current VideoQA benchmarks overwhelmingly test questions answerable through explicit visual content—objects, actions, and events directly observable in individual frames or short clips. However, human video understanding relies heavily on inferring implicit relationships that are never shown simultaneously: spatial arrangements across viewpoints, causal chains spanning off-screen events, and temporal continuity across discontinuous frames. No existing benchmark systematically probes this implicit relational reasoning, leaving a critical gap between what models are evaluated on and what true video comprehension requires.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262674/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question is paired with a movie clip (sourced from 107 films across 15 genres and 7 decades) presented as 8, 16, or 32 sampled frames, with all audio and subtitles stripped to enforce purely visual reasoning. The model receives a four-option multiple-choice question and must select the correct answer. Questions are specifically constructed so that no single frame or short clip contains sufficient evidence; the correct answer requires integrating cues distributed across the full temporal span of the clip, such as inferring an object's position relative to another that is never co-visible.

## Method

The paper introduces VRR-QA, a 1K-question benchmark built through a custom curation pipeline: expert annotators use a purpose-built FrameQuiz tool to select temporal segments from creative movie clips, author multiple-choice questions requiring implicit inference, and craft plausible distractors, with all annotations cross-verified among the authors. A separate ImplicitEval tool is used by non-expert annotators to establish a human baseline (83% accuracy). The dataset is organized into 9 reasoning categories (lateral/vertical spatial, depth/proximity, viewpoint/visibility, motion/trajectory, motivational, inferred counting, physical/environmental context, social interaction), and GPT-4.1-assisted initial tagging is refined by expert relabeling. Evaluation spans 14+ models (open-weight 7B-scale LLaVA, Qwen, InternVL, Gemma; proprietary GPT-4.1/O3/5.2, Gemini 2.5/3 Flash, Claude 4/4.5 Sonnet) with category-wise and macro-average accuracy, plus ablations on frame count, model scale, and structured reasoning prompts.

## Results

On VRR-QA (16 input frames), the best model GPT-O3 achieves 64.1% overall accuracy and 68.6% macro-average, still 19 points below the human baseline of 83.0% (85.6% macro). Among non-reasoning proprietary models, Gemini 3 Flash leads at 61.8% avg / 67.6% macro, followed by GPT-4.1 at 54.3% / 58.6%. No open-source model exceeds 50% average accuracy; the best (InternVL3) reaches only 43.3% avg / 50.2% macro. The hardest categories for all models are Inferred Counting (best: 48.3% by Gemini 3 Flash) and Relative Depth & Proximity (best: 55.3% by GPT-O3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-O3 (reasoning model) is the top performer at 64.1% avg, outperforming GPT-4.1 (54.3%) by 9.8 points, confirming that explicit reasoning capability yields substantial gains on implicit video reasoning. Gemini 3 Flash is the strongest non-reasoning proprietary model at 61.8% avg, narrowly trailing GPT-O3 but surpassing GPT-5.2+ (56.8%) and GPT-5.2 (50.8%). Among open-source models, Qwen2-VL (44.9%) and InternVL3 (43.3%) lead, but none crosses the 50% threshold, highlighting a clear proprietary-vs-open gap. Claude models (45.4–48.6% avg) consistently underperform GPT and Gemini variants across most categories. All 14 evaluated models fall 19+ points below the human baseline (83.0%), with the largest gaps in Lateral Spatial Reasoning (best model 52.8% vs. human 85.4%) and Inferred Counting (best 48.3% vs. human 65.9%), indicating these remain the most challenging implicit-reasoning dimensions.

## Relation to Prior Work

VRR-QA carves new ground within VideoQA by targeting a dimension that prior benchmarks explicitly exclude: questions whose answers are never directly visible in any single frame or short clip. Existing benchmarks (MVBench, TempCompass, TemporalBench, VideoMME, TVQA, CinePile) focus on explicit perception—recognizing objects, actions, or events observable in-frame—or on temporal manipulation of existing clips. VRR-QA departs by sourcing questions from cinematic movie clips where storytelling conventions (off-screen action, implied causality, viewpoint shifts) naturally produce implicit relational reasoning tasks, and by stripping audio/subtitles to force purely visual inference. The dataset is expert-annotated rather than template- or LLM-generated, distinguishing it from the automated pipelines used in most prior VideoQA benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a substantial and persistent gap: even the strongest model (GPT-O3) achieves only 64% accuracy versus an 83% human baseline, and no open-source model crosses 50%, suggesting that current architectures lack the representational machinery for sustained cross-frame implicit inference. The finding that increasing frame count (8→32) and model scale yield only modest gains points to a need for architectural or training-paradigm innovations rather than brute-force scaling. Open questions include whether explicit spatial/temporal reasoning modules, world-model pretraining, or longer-context training could close the gap, and whether the 1K scale is sufficient to track progress reliably as models improve.
