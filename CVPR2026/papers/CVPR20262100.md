## Problem

Monocular dynamic scene reconstruction is inherently illposed due to limited multiview constraints, making accurate recovery of object geometry and temporal coherence difficult. Existing Gaussian Splatting-based methods for dynamic scenes rely on per-frame deformation models that are memory-intensive, slow to train, and produce artifacts in complex motion regions, while inferring scene dynamics purely from photometric cues during optimization is both inefficient and unreliable in monocular settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262100/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a monocular RGB video sequence, per-frame camera intrinsics and extrinsics, and per-frame depth maps (from LiDAR sensors or learned monocular depth estimators) as input. It produces a 3D Gaussian Splatting scene decomposed into a static set and a dynamic set of Gaussians, where each dynamic Gaussian stores Poly-Fourier trajectory coefficients for position and rotation offsets. As a by-product of the reconstruction, the method yields temporally consistent per-instance segmentation masks for dynamic objects, enabling zero-cost scene editing operations such as object removal, isolation, or repositioning during rendering.

## Method

The core contribution is an advanced four-stage pre-processing pipeline that estimates preliminary 3D scene dynamics before photometric optimization: (1) dynamic region detection via dense optical flow (RAFT) combined with Sampson epipolar error thresholding, (2) instance-level segmentation and tracking using SAM2 with forward and reverse propagation, (3) per-point 3D scene flow extraction via point tracking (BootsTAPIR) refined with per-instance rigid transformations (Kabsch + RANSAC) to fill occluded regions, and (4) initialization of dynamic Gaussians by solving a linear system to fit Poly-Fourier curve coefficients to the refined trajectories. The scene is disentangled into static and dynamic Gaussian sets with separate densification strategies, and dynamic Gaussians encode motion as compact time-dependent Poly-Fourier offsets to mean position and rotation (the latter handled via quaternion normalization rather than direct offset addition). Photometric optimization uses a combined L1 + SSIM + Pearson-correlation depth loss, and unlike Gaussian Flow, no time-varying color deformations are modeled, improving compactness and preventing the network from compensating motion errors through artificial color changes.

## Results

On the DyCheck dataset, MOSAIC-GS achieves a mean PSNR of 18.4 and mean LPIPS of 0.255, placing it second in PSNR behind MoSca (19.32) while setting a new state-of-the-art in LPIPS across most scenes. On the NVIDIA Dynamic Scene (original) dataset, it scores 26.26 PSNR and 0.060 LPIPS (best LPIPS), and on the Gaussian Marbles variant it achieves 23.79 PSNR and 0.069 LPIPS (best PSNR). Training time is 10.5 minutes and rendering speed is 180 FPS, compared to 50 min / 38 FPS for MoSca and 5–9 h / 200 FPS for Gaussian Marbles.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MOSAIC-GS trails MoSca [16] in mean PSNR on DyCheck (18.4 vs. 19.32) but surpasses it in mean LPIPS (0.255 vs. 0.264), indicating better perceptual quality, while training 4.8× faster (10.5 min vs. 50 min) and rendering 4.7× faster (180 vs. 38 FPS). Against Shape of Motion [39], MOSAIC-GS leads in both PSNR (+1.08) and LPIPS (−0.040) on DyCheck. On the NVIDIA original dataset, MOSAIC-GS achieves the best LPIPS (0.060) but slightly lower PSNR than MoSca (26.26 vs. 26.72). On the Gaussian Marbles variant, MOSAIC-GS achieves the best PSNR (23.79 vs. 23.68 for Gaussian Marbles [35]) with identical LPIPS (0.069) while training in 7 minutes versus 3.5–8 hours. Compared to Gaussian Flow [20], MOSAIC-GS improves PSNR by ~2.2 dB and LPIPS by ~0.05 on DyCheck, with roughly 2× faster training. Overall, MOSAIC-GS is the only method that simultaneously achieves near-SOTA reconstruction quality, best-in-class perceptual metrics, and the fastest training/rendering among all compared approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

MOSAIC-GS builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al.) as its scene representation and on Gaussian Flow (Lin et al.) for Poly-Fourier trajectory encoding, but departs from both by initializing deformation coefficients from a multi-stage geometric preprocessing pipeline rather than learning them during photometric optimization. The preprocessing chain—epipolar-error-based dynamic detection, SAM2 segmentation/tracking, TAPIR point tracking with Kabsch-based rigidity refinement—draws on recent advances in each sub-task but combines them in a novel way to produce motion priors before optimization begins. The scene disentanglement into static and dynamic Gaussian sets, along with the decision to forgo time-varying color deformations (unlike Gaussian Flow), distinguishes it from prior monocular dynamic GS methods such as MoSca and Gaussian Marbles, which rely on per-frame deformation storage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 5× reduction in training time (10.5 min vs. 50 min for MoSca) and 3–5× speedup in rendering (180 FPS vs. 38–52 FPS) make high-fidelity dynamic scene reconstruction practical on resource-constrained platforms like robotics and embedded devices, where prior methods were prohibitive. The free byproduct of temporally consistent segmentation and editing (object removal, isolation, color/motion manipulation) at zero additional cost broadens the utility beyond pure view synthesis. Key limitations remain: heavy dependence on external model quality (SAM2, TAPIR, depth estimation), the rigidity assumption that fails for highly non-rigid objects, and degradation when an entire dynamic object is invisible from the training viewpoint—open questions for future work.
