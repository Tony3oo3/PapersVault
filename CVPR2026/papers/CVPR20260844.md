## Problem

While LLMs and LVLMs are known to struggle with counting, prior work has largely assessed this through behavioral accuracy or prompt-based evaluations without investigating the internal mechanisms. There is a lack of mechanistic interpretability studies that identify where numerical information is stored, how it is updated across tokens and layers, and what causal role specific activations play in producing count outputs in both text and vision-language settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260844/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Inputs are controlled synthetic stimuli: text lists of 1–9 repeated items (monotypic or polytypic, with list-first or question-first prompt configurations) and synthetic images containing 1–9 geometric shapes at varying resolutions (3×3, 6×6, 10×10 patch grids), each paired with a counting question. Beyond the final digit prediction, the method produces per-token and per-region decoded count probabilities (softmax over digits 1–9) at every layer, as well as a Causal Influence (CI) score that jointly measures the increase in probability of the expected answer and the decrease in probability of the competing answer under intervention.

## Method

The core tool, CountScope, constructs a minimal target context containing one or more placeholder tokens followed by a counting question, then transfers activations from a source context's tokens, regions, or layers into the placeholder via online or offline activation patching; the resulting softmax over digits reveals what count information is encoded at the probed location. The paper layers three analytical tiers—behavioral accuracy, observational geometry (PCA projections and cross-task cosine similarity of item embeddings), and causal mediation (zero patching, interchange intervention, and the CountScope decoding protocol)—to localize count signals across tokens, layers, and modalities. Key mechanistic findings include: a memoryless internal counter that continues from the source's end when items are patched (rather than recomputing a full sum), type-specific resetting counters in polytypic lists, layerwise emergence where lower layers encode small counts and deeper layers are required for larger ones, approximate linear additivity of position-difference vectors in embedding space, and a strong reliance on separator tokens as structural shortcuts whose perturbation degrades counting far more than equivalent perturbations of element tokens.

## Results

The paper's central causal finding is that an internal counter mechanism operates in a memoryless, Markov-like fashion: when the final k items from a source context are patched into a target, the (k+1)-th item encodes N_source+1 rather than k+1, with CI scores of 0.90±0.07 (LLM, question-first, k=2) and 0.51±0.07 (LVLM, system-prompt, k=3). Separator tokens act as structural shortcuts—patching all separators to the first separator's activation causes a 0.75±0.39 (monotypic) and 0.97±0.05 (polytypic) drop in ground-truth probability. As a practical implication, imposing spatial partitioning on images improves counting accuracy for 10–20 objects from 10.0% to 50.4%, outperforming chain-of-thought prompting (15.3%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic interpretability study rather than a method-comparison paper, so there are no external baselines to beat or match. The internal comparisons are across model families and prompt configurations. The continued-counting hypothesis is strongest in the question-first setting for LLMs (CI up to 0.90) and in the system-prompt setting for LVLMs (CI up to 0.51), while the question-last and user-prompt configurations show substantially weaker effects (CI ≤ 0.16), indicating that the counter mechanism is most transparent when the counting instruction precedes the items. For the maximum-latent-count hypothesis (Table 3), LVLMs show consistently higher CI scores (0.66–0.89) than LLMs (0.05–0.80), suggesting vision-language models more reliably retrieve the maximum count from context. The linear additivity test (Table 4) shows LLMs support position-difference interventions more robustly (CI 0.60–0.85) than LVLMs (CI 0.25–0.62), reflecting the noisier numerical representations in visual embeddings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability toolkit—specifically the PatchScope framework [10]—into a specialized causal decoding tool (CountScope) tailored for numerical representations, after finding that standard probes like Logit Lens and Tuned Lens are too noisy for count-based information. It departs from the dominant prior work on LLM/LVLM counting, which has focused almost exclusively on behavioral accuracy and prompt-based evaluations [3, 5, 9, 30], by providing the first systematic causal-mediation analysis of where and how count information is encoded, updated, and read out across layers and modalities. The layerwise emergence of numerical representations connects to broader findings on progressive feature abstraction in transformers, while the practical implication of distributing counting across visual tokens offers an orthogonal perspective to chain-of-thought strategies [13, 15, 30].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a unified mechanistic account of counting in both LLMs and LVLMs, revealing that the process is structured, layerwise, and heavily dependent on structural shortcuts (separators) rather than genuine item-by-item accumulation. The finding that counting capacity is fundamentally bounded by transformer depth, and that visual tokens can serve as additional computational substrate, points toward concrete architectural and prompting strategies for improving numerical reasoning in vision-language models. Key open questions remain: a complete circuit-level discovery of the counting mechanism is still missing, the behavior under system-2 setups (e.g., CoT decoding) is not fully characterized, and whether the separator-shortcut reliance can be mitigated through training or architectural changes is unexplored.
