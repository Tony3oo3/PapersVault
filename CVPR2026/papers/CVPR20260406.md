## Problem

Existing caching-based acceleration methods for diffusion and rectified-flow models measure redundancy between consecutive timesteps using raw feature distances, which entangle content-carrying signal with stochastic noise. This design overlooks the well-documented spectral evolution of denoising—where low-frequency structure emerges early and high-frequency detail is refined later—causing cache refresh decisions to be driven by noise rather than meaningful content change.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260406/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

SeaCache operates as a plug-and-play module on the timestep-modulated input features (I_t) at the pre-attention input of the first transformer block, requiring no architectural modification. It produces a dynamic per-timestep refresh/skip schedule that gates whether the full denoiser runs or cached outputs are reused, ultimately yielding generated images (1024×1024) or videos (480p, 65 frames). The only tunable hyperparameter is the accumulated-distance threshold δ, which controls the refresh ratio (fraction of timesteps computing a full forward pass).

## Method

SeaCache derives a timestep-dependent Spectral-Evolution-Aware (SEA) filter from the frequency response of the optimal linear MMSE denoiser, which takes a Wiener-like form G_t(f) = a_t·S_x(f) / (a_t²·S_x(f) + b_t²) under a power-law natural image spectrum. This filter is density-normalized to enforce constant mean gain over radial frequency bins, ensuring comparable feature magnitudes across timesteps. At each step, input features are passed through FFT → element-wise multiplication by G_t^norm → iFFT to obtain a spectrally reweighted representation that amplifies content-relevant low-frequency signal while suppressing high-frequency noise. The relative ℓ₁ distance between consecutive SEA-filtered features replaces the raw distance in TeaCache's accumulated-distance refresh rule, yielding a spectrum-aware gate that automatically concentrates refreshes on early timesteps without manual step-counting. The approach is training-free, network-agnostic, and sampler-agnostic, depending only on the shared noise-schedule coefficients (a_t, b_t).

## Results

On FLUX.1-dev (200 DrawBench prompts, 1024×1024), SeaCache (δ=0.3, ~50% refresh) achieves PSNR 26.29, LPIPS 0.106, SSIM 0.893 at 9.4 s latency and 1098 TFLOPs, outperforming TeaCache (PSNR 20.76) and TaylorSeer (PSNR 22.78) by 5.5 and 3.5 dB respectively while also being faster. Under the more aggressive ~30% budget, SeaCache (δ=0.6) reaches PSNR 21.33 at 6.4 s, versus 17.21 for TeaCache and 19.97 for TaylorSeer. On video models, the gains are even larger: on HunyuanVideo, SeaCache (δ=0.19) achieves PSNR 32.39 vs. 23.40 for TeaCache (+9 dB), and on Wan2.1 1.3B, PSNR 26.60 vs. 20.84 for TeaCache (+5.8 dB), all at lower latency and TFLOPs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SeaCache consistently outperforms all five baselines on every metric (PSNR, LPIPS, SSIM, latency, TFLOPs) at matched refresh ratios. Against TeaCache [30], the closest dynamic-caching baseline, SeaCache gains +5.5 dB PSNR at ~50% refresh (26.29 vs. 20.76) and +4.1 dB at ~30% (21.33 vs. 17.21), while also reducing latency by ~1.5–2 s. Against TaylorSeer [33], SeaCache improves PSNR by +3.5 dB at ~50% and +1.4 dB at ~30%, with lower FLOPs. Against the static-schedule methods Δ-DiT [8] and ToCa [75] (both evaluated only on FLUX at ~50%), SeaCache achieves substantially higher PSNR (26.29 vs. 17.40 and 18.40) at roughly half the latency (9.4 s vs. 15.5/15.9 s). Against DiCache [5], the paper demonstrates plug-and-play compatibility: applying the SEA filter to DiCache's block-level features yields consistently higher PSNR at the same refresh ratio (Fig. 9), confirming the spectral metric generalizes beyond input-side features. On CycleReward perceptual ranking, SeaCache achieves the best (lowest) average rank at both ~50% (1.91) and ~30% (1.96) budgets, ahead of TeaCache (2.01/2.07) and TaylorSeer (2.08/1.98).

## Relation to Prior Work

SeaCache extends the dynamic caching line initiated by TeaCache, which introduced accumulated-distance refresh scheduling on timestep-modulated input features, and the spectral-diffusion analysis (Spectral Diffusion, [67]) that characterizes how optimal linear denoisers shift their frequency response across timesteps. The key departure is that all prior caching methods (TeaCache, TaylorSeer, DiCache, Δ-DiT) measure redundancy in raw feature space, implicitly treating content and noise as interchangeable; SeaCache instead derives a Wiener-filter-based, timestep-dependent SEA filter from the linear-MMSE denoiser and applies it before computing distances, so the cache gate responds to signal alignment rather than stochastic high-frequency variation. The method is explicitly positioned as a drop-in replacement for the distance metric in any existing caching policy, preserving the accumulated-threshold refresh logic unchanged.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Because SeaCache is training-free, architecture-agnostic, and sampler-agnostic (works for both DPM and rectified-flow models), it can be attached to any existing caching pipeline with a single FFT-based filtering step, making it immediately adoptable by practitioners accelerating FLUX, HunyuanVideo, Wan2.1, or future DiT-based generators. The ~8 dB PSNR gain over the strongest baseline on HunyuanVideo and consistent improvements across all three evaluated models suggest the spectral prior is a broadly applicable inductive bias, not an artifact of a particular architecture. Open questions include whether the linear-denoiser and power-law-spectrum assumptions that underpin the SEA filter remain valid for very deep or non-stationary networks, and whether the same spectral-evolution framing can inform other acceleration decisions beyond caching (e.g., step selection, token pruning).
