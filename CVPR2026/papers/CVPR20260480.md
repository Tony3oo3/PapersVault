## Problem

Multi-view 3D reconstruction methods are highly sensitive to photometric inconsistencies from camera optics and ISP variations, yet existing compensation strategies (GLO vectors, affine corrections, bilateral grids) lack physical grounding, are non-interpretable, and conflate sensor-intrinsic properties with per-frame capture settings. This makes it unclear how to assign appearance parameters for novel views, forcing evaluation protocols to rely on ground-truth target images that are unavailable in practice.

## Input / Output

The method operates as a reconstruction-agnostic post-processing step: it takes the raw radiance image rendered from any 3D representation (3DGUT, 3DGS, Zip-NeRF) along with per-frame camera poses, and optionally EXIF metadata (e.g., relative exposure offsets) concatenated to the controller input. It outputs a photometrically corrected image and interpretable per-frame ISP parameters (exposure offset in EV, four chromaticity offsets, vignetting polynomial coefficients, and CRF parameters). The controller itself is a lightweight network (1×1 convolutions pooling to a 5×5 grid followed by an MLP) that maps the rendered radiance to per-frame exposure and color-correction parameters at inference time.

## Method

PPISP decomposes the camera image formation into four physically-motivated sequential modules: a per-frame exposure offset (base-2 scale), per-channel radial vignetting (cubic polynomial in squared radius), a 3×3 color homography on RG chromaticities with intensity normalization (parameterized by four chromaticity offsets), and a piecewise power-curve camera response function with gamma correction. The key design principle is disentangling sensor-intrinsic effects (vignetting, CRF — shared across frames) from capture-dependent settings (exposure, color correction — per-frame), with regularization that enforces physically plausible parameter ranges. A separate controller network, trained in a second stage with the scene and per-camera parameters frozen, predicts the per-frame exposure and color-correction parameters directly from the rendered radiance, mimicking auto-exposure and auto-white-balance in real cameras. This two-stage training (30k iterations for joint scene+ISP optimization, then 5k iterations for the controller) enables novel-view rendering without access to ground-truth target images.

## Results

On the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset with 3DGUT, PPISP (with controller) achieves 28.15 PSNR, 0.821 SSIM, and 0.264 LPIPS, outperforming BilaRF (24.97 PSNR) and ADOP (26.42 PSNR). On the more challenging Tanks and Temples dataset, PPISP reaches 24.62 PSNR versus 19.78 for BilaRF and 20.28 for ADOP. Notably, PPISP's raw PSNR often exceeds BilaRF's PSNR-CC (which assumes privileged access to ground-truth novel views), e.g., 24.12 vs. 25.63 on the BilaRF dataset and 24.62 vs. 23.46 on Tanks and Temples. The controller consistently adds 0.5–3.1 dB PSNR over the base model without a controller across all datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PPISP (with controller) outperforms BilaRF on PSNR across all five datasets with 3DGUT, with the largest margin on Tanks and Temples (24.62 vs. 19.78, +4.84 dB) and the BilaRF dataset (24.12 vs. 21.41, +2.71 dB). Against ADOP, PPISP wins on every dataset, e.g., 28.15 vs. 26.42 on Mip-NeRF 360 and 24.62 vs. 20.28 on Tanks and Temples. A particularly strong result is that PPISP's raw PSNR (without any GT-based color correction) exceeds BilaRF's PSNR-CC on Tanks and Temples (24.62 vs. 23.46) and the BilaRF dataset (24.12 vs. 25.63 is the one exception where BilaRF-CC still leads), demonstrating that the controller faithfully reproduces auto-exposure and white-balance behavior without needing the target image. GLO (NeRF-W style latent vectors) performs worst among baselines, e.g., 21.26 PSNR on Mip-NeRF 360 with 3DGS versus PPISP's 27.97. The only dataset where the controller shows a notable gap to PSNR-CC is the BilaRF dataset, which the authors attribute to manual exposure overrides in the metadata that the controller cannot infer from radiance alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

PPISP builds most directly on ADOP's differentiable post-processing framework (exposure, white balance, CRF, vignetting as explicit calibration parameters) but departs by more cleanly disentangling exposure offset from chromatic correction and using a more compact CRF model. The individual modules draw on classical imaging literature—Goldman's vignetting polynomial, Finlayson's color homography, and Grossberg-Nayar's piecewise power CRF—rather than learning unconstrained per-frame transforms as in GLO or BilaRF. The key conceptual departure is the PPISP controller, which predicts per-frame ISP parameters from rendered radiance for novel views, analogous to auto-exposure/auto-white-balance in real cameras, eliminating the need for GT novel-view access during evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical evaluation gap: prior methods require GT novel views to estimate corrective mappings, masking inter-method differences and being infeasible in deployment. The controller closes this gap and consistently approaches PSNR-CC levels, validating that physically-grounded, low-capacity corrections generalize better than high-capacity bilateral grids (Table 5). Open questions include handling spatially-varying effects (localized tone-mapping, lens flares) that the linear-then-CRF pipeline cannot model, and the controller's reliance on meaningful radiance-to-ISP correlations that break down under manual camera overrides. As a reconstruction-agnostic post-processing step, PPISP is immediately applicable to any radiance field or Gaussian splatting pipeline.
