## Problem

Existing procedural sequence generation methods either focus on a single domain or require multiple expert networks (e.g., asymmetric LoRAs) for different domains, leading to poor generalization to unseen processes, high parameter redundancy, and a fixed number of output frames that cannot adapt to varying process complexity. This paper tackles all three limitations simultaneously within a unified framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263279/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt describing a multi-step process (e.g., 'Step-by-step bamboo basket weaving, <image-1> Bamboo strips are soaked...') and optionally two reference images as conditioning. It first generates a single 1024×1024 grid image (4 or 9 sub-frames) via the DiT backbone, then applies a sliding-window post-processing stage that adaptively inserts or removes intermediate frames based on computed visual and semantic differences, yielding a variable-length procedural sequence rather than a fixed grid.

## Method

ProcessMaker is built on the Flux.1 DiT backbone and operates in two stages. Stage 1 introduces Sparse Masks for LoRA—a single LoRA whose per-domain masks (with the last 70% of values zeroed) replace multiple domain-specific LoRAs, reducing trainable parameters to ~7.3% of the SOTA—combined with Self-supervised Representation Alignment, which extracts frozen representations from appointed DiT blocks (4th, 9th, 14th, 19th, 29th) as a teacher and aligns the fine-tuned student via cosine + MSE distance, all under a flow-matching objective. Stage 2 employs a sliding window over the generated grid frames, computing a joint difference from CLIP global features, DINO local patch features, and CLIP text embeddings, then using adaptive thresholds (mean ± k·σ) to decide where to synthesize complementary intermediate frames or delete redundant ones via a second LoRA conditioned on two reference images.

## Results

On the MakeAnything dataset (21 domains, 50 sequence groups each), ProcessMaker achieves the highest or near-highest CLIP Alignment and GPT-4o Coherence scores across nearly all domains. For example, on Painting it reaches 34.47 Alignment / 4.85 Coherence, on LEGO 34.53 / 5.00, and on Oil Painting 37.32 / 5.00, consistently outperforming both Flux.1 and MakeAnything. In the specialized-domain comparison (Table 2), ProcessMaker beats domain-specific models: 34.47/4.85 vs. ProcessPainter's 26.43/4.85 (Painting), 31.59/3.90 vs. LayerTracer's 31.43/3.55 (Icon), and 34.47/4.50 vs. CoCook's 32.28/4.15 (Cook). The method achieves these results with only 306.32M trainable parameters (7.3% of the 4.19B base model).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ProcessMaker outperforms Flux.1 on all 21 domains in both Alignment and Coherence, with the largest gaps in Coherence (e.g., +2.15 on LEGO, +1.15 on Icon), confirming that the base DiT alone cannot produce coherent multi-step sequences. Against MakeAnything (the closest multi-domain competitor), ProcessMaker wins on 19 of 21 domains in Alignment and 17 in Coherence; the two exceptions are SandArt (tie at 4.70 Coh) and Ink Painting (tie at 4.90 Coh), where margins are within 0.06. In the specialized-domain comparison, ProcessMaker decisively beats ProcessPainter on Painting (34.47 vs. 26.43 Alignment, a +8.04 gap), LayerTracer on Icon (31.59 vs. 31.43 Align, 3.90 vs. 3.55 Coh), and CoCook on Cook (34.47 vs. 32.28 Align, 4.50 vs. 4.15 Coh), demonstrating that a single generalized model can match or exceed domain-specific experts. Ideogram is compared only qualitatively (Figure 3); the paper reports that Ideogram produces inconsistent or illogical intermediate frames in unseen domains, whereas ProcessMaker maintains procedural coherence. Overall, ProcessMaker achieves these gains while using only 306.32M trainable parameters versus MakeAnything's 4.19B, a 92.7% reduction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the MakeAnything framework for multi-domain procedural sequence generation on Diffusion Transformers, but departs from its asymmetric LoRA (shared A with multiple task-specific B matrices, akin to Mixture-of-Experts) by replacing it with a single LoRA modulated by domain-specific sparse masks. It also introduces a self-supervised representation alignment loss that uses frozen pretrained DiT layer outputs as a teacher signal during fine-tuning, a technique borrowed from knowledge distillation but applied here to preserve generalization. The sliding-window adaptive-step mechanism is novel within this task, drawing on CLIP and DINO feature distances to decide where to insert or remove intermediate frames rather than generating a fixed grid.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in achieving multi-domain procedural generation with only 7.3% trainable parameters relative to MakeAnything, making the approach far more accessible for practitioners who cannot afford multiple expert LoRAs. The adaptive-step capability addresses a real usability gap: fixed-length grids either waste frames on trivial transitions or miss critical ones. However, the grid-image formulation inherently limits per-frame resolution, and generalization to truly unseen domains still relies entirely on the pretrained Flux.1 backbone without any domain-specific guidance, leaving open the question of how well this scales to highly specialized or novel process types. Future work on super-resolution and richer conditioning (sketches, video clips) is needed to close the fidelity gap.
