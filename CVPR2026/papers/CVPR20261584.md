## Problem

Estimating the full articulated structure of a 3D object—its movable parts, kinematic hierarchy, and motion constraints—from a single static mesh remains unsolved in a general, efficient manner. Prior approaches either require slow per-object optimization, are restricted to a handful of object categories, assume the kinematic structure is known a priori, or rely on retrieving parts from a fixed library, limiting their generality and speed.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 3D mesh (vertices and faces) as input, which is converted to a dense point cloud (102,400 points at inference) augmented with per-point surface normals and PartField semantic features. It outputs a complete articulated structure parameterized as a 4-tuple (P, S, K, M): the number of parts, a face-to-part segmentation, a directed kinematic tree rooted at a base part, and per-joint motion constraints (type, prismatic direction, revolute axis as direction+point, and angular/linear ranges). The output is directly convertible to URDF format for physics simulation, and inference completes in approximately 10 seconds.

## Method

PARTICULATE uses a Part Articulation Transformer with 8 attention blocks that alternates query self-attention and query-to-point cross-attention between N point tokens and P_max=16 learnable part queries, avoiding expensive point-to-point self-attention. Multiple decoder heads predict different articulation attributes: an MLP on (point, part) token pairs yields per-point part logits; an MLP on part-token pairs produces a soft kinematic adjacency matrix; and separate MLPs on individual part tokens predict motion type, ranges, and prismatic direction. The revolute axis is over-parameterized—its direction comes from a part-token MLP while its location is regressed per-point and aggregated via coordinate-wise median, which empirically reduces axis drift. Training is fully supervised end-to-end with a DETR-style Hungarian matching between predicted and ground-truth parts, and a multi-task loss combining cross-entropy (segmentation, motion type), binary cross-entropy (kinematic tree), and L1 losses (ranges, directions, axis locations). At inference, Edmonds' algorithm extracts the maximum spanning arborescence from the predicted kinematic matrix to recover the tree, and connected-component refinement snaps faces within the same mesh component to a single part.

## Results

On the PartNet-Mobility test set, PARTICULATE achieves gIoU 0.880, PC 0.003, and mIoU 0.884 for part segmentation (with mesh connectivity), substantially outperforming all baselines. On the more challenging Lightwheel benchmark (243 diverse objects, 14 categories), PARTICULATE reaches gIoU 0.332, PC 0.168, and mIoU 0.576, again leading all competitors. For full articulated motion prediction (Table 3), PARTICULATE attains gIoU 0.843 and OC 0.003 on PartNet-Mobility and gIoU 0.305 and OC 0.009 on Lightwheel, surpassing Articulate AnyMesh (which is given ground-truth motion ranges) by a wide margin. Inference takes approximately 10 seconds per object, compared to 10–20 minutes for prior VLM-based or optimization-based methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PARTICULATE outperforms all four baselines on both datasets across every metric. On the Lightwheel benchmark, the closest competitor is Articulate AnyMesh [43] (gIoU 0.172 vs. 0.332 for PARTICULATE), a VLM-based method that struggles with internal parts invisible from external views; SINGAPO [33] (gIoU −0.050) and P3SAM [39] (gIoU 0.122) perform even worse, with SINGAPO's negative gIoU indicating that its retrieved part geometries are worse than treating the object as a single part. PartField [35], a semantic part segmentation model, achieves the lowest gIoU (0.079) on Lightwheel because its part definitions do not align with articulated parts. On PartNet-Mobility, the gap widens dramatically: PARTICULATE (gIoU 0.880) nearly doubles the best baseline, Articulate AnyMesh (0.383), and SINGAPO (0.271) and PartField (0.183) trail further behind. For motion prediction (Table 3), PARTICULATE also leads on the whole-object Chamfer distance (OC 0.009 on Lightwheel, 0.003 on PM), beating Articulate AnyMesh (OC 0.010 and 0.022 respectively) even though the latter is given ground-truth motion ranges for each predicted part.

## Relation to Prior Work

PARTICULATE departs from the dominant per-instance optimization paradigm (NeRF/GS-based reconstruction from posed images) and from generative approaches (NAP, CAGE, SINGAPO) that are limited to a few categories, assume known kinematic structure, or rely on part retrieval from a database. It also contrasts with recent VLM-based methods (Articulate-Anything, Articulate AnyMesh) that perform slow per-object reasoning and struggle with internal parts. Architecturally, it builds on DETR-style learnable queries and cross-attention for set prediction, PartField for 3D semantic features, and Edmonds' algorithm for kinematic tree extraction, but combines these into a single feed-forward transformer that predicts all articulation attributes (segmentation, kinematic tree, motion constraints) simultaneously from a static mesh in one pass.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The feed-forward formulation reduces articulation estimation from minutes to ~10 seconds and enables a practical pipeline where off-the-shelf 3D generators (e.g., Hunyuan3D) produce static meshes that PARTICULATE immediately converts into physics-simulatable articulated assets (URDF export). The new Lightwheel benchmark and revised evaluation protocol (penalizing unmatched parts) expose that prior metrics were uninformative—the Naive Baseline of treating an object as a single part outperformed all baselines under the old protocol. Key limitations remain: training data is small (~3,800 objects), the model fails on kinematic structures far from training distribution, and generated assets exhibit inter-part penetrations, leaving open the question of whether post-training with simulation feedback can close the gap to physically plausible, sim-to-real-ready articulated assets.
