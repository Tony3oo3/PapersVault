## Problem

Existing event-based autofocus methods assume the sharpest focus corresponds to the maximum event rate, but this assumption fails at perfect focus where spatially compact edges trigger fewer events. Prior polarity-symmetry approaches offer heuristic cues without explaining the physical origin of event-rate behavior around the focal plane. This paper identifies and theoretically explains a counterintuitive dual-peak-valley structure in the event-rate curve, showing the true focus lies at the inter-peak minimum rather than at a peak.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an asynchronous event stream (x, y, t, polarity) recorded during a continuous motorized focus sweep, partitioned into fixed 1 ms temporal windows to compute per-position event counts. It requires no reconstructed images, no supervision, and no GPU; all processing is single-pass on CPU. The output is a scalar estimated focus position f* (in motor-step or timestamp units) together with a confidence score S that quantifies the contrast between the valley and its bounding peaks, serving as a reliability indicator for downstream focus control.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution derives, from the event-generation threshold condition and a Gaussian defocus-blur model, that the activation-region measure (and hence event rate) is non-monotonic in blur scale: it rises from a strict local minimum at σ=0, peaks at some σ*>0, then falls, producing a dual-peak-valley profile across a one-way focus sweep. The ESVA algorithm exploits this structure through three sequential signal-processing modules: (1) Gaussian structural smoothing to suppress stochastic event noise while preserving global curvature, (2) consistency filtering that projects samples with normalized inter-step deviation above a threshold onto a locally linear manifold to remove transient spikes, and (3) a dual-peak constraint that detects the two dominant local maxima and restricts valley search to the interval [P1, P2], preventing false minima from noise or secondary oscillations. The focus estimate is the argmin of the regularized curve within that interval, and the entire pipeline runs in O(N) time with no learned parameters, no image reconstruction, and no iterative optimization.

## Results

On the DAVIS real-world dataset, ESVA achieves an average focusing timestamp error of 1.30 ms, outperforming the next-best method ELP (2.04 ms) by 36% and ER+EGS (26.20 ms) by 20×. On the challenging EAD dataset (extreme low-light <1 Lux with motion), ESVA achieves 65.38 μm average distance error, improving over ER+EGS (92.30 μm) by ~30% and PBF (972.81 μm) by 93%. On the synthetic SYN benchmark, ESVA reaches 6.62 ms average error versus 8.68 ms for ELP, and on the high-resolution EVK4 dataset it achieves 4.22 ms versus 5.33 ms for ELP. Runtime is 1.43–1.68 ms per sequence on CPU, faster than all baselines except OLE'23.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ESVA wins on every dataset and every condition against all four baselines. The closest competitor is ELP [3] (one-step event-driven autofocus, CVPR 2025), which uses polarity-inversion detection; ESVA improves over ELP by 36% on DAVIS (1.30 vs 2.04 ms), 24% on SYN (6.62 vs 8.68 ms), 21% on EVK4 (4.22 vs 5.33 ms), and 86% on EAD (65.38 vs 475.87 μm). ER+EGS [21] (event-rate maximization with golden-section search, CVPR 2022) performs worst on DAVIS (26.20 ms) because it stops at the first local peak before the true focus, and is competitive only on EAD light-static (84.57 vs 65.00 μm). OLE'23 [11] (polarity-symmetry microscopy autofocus) generalizes poorly beyond its microscope setting, degrading to 17.79 ms on EVK4 dark-static and 329.64 μm on EAD dark-static. PBF [2] (polarity-balance focus metric) suffers catastrophic failures on EAD (972.81 μm average) due to polarity imbalance under extreme illumination, while remaining competitive on DAVIS bright conditions (4.94 ms average).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs fundamentally from the event-based autofocus lineage initiated by Lin et al. (ER+EGS, CVPR 2022), which assumed the sharpest focus corresponds to maximum event rate, and from subsequent polarity-symmetry methods (Bao et al. PBF, Ge et al. OLE'23) that refined the metric without questioning the underlying monotonicity assumption. Rather than building on any single prior architecture, the authors repurpose the physics of event generation (Gaussian blur spreading, threshold-crossing dynamics) to derive a non-monotonic event-rate model, revealing that the true focus sits at a local minimum between two peaks. This inverts the core heuristic of all prior event-autofocus work and reframes the problem from peak-finding to valley-localization within a structurally constrained interval.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dual-peak-valley characterization provides a physically interpretable, supervision-free focus metric that is robust across illumination and motion conditions, and its O(N) runtime makes it practical for real-time neuromorphic systems. The main limitation is the single-dominant-depth-layer assumption: in multi-layer scenes the event-rate curve may develop additional peaks or asymmetric valleys, requiring spatial or task-specific constraints not yet explored. Open questions include whether the valley structure persists under extreme motion blur, whether it can be combined with polarity or spatial cues for multi-plane focusing, and whether the same non-monotonicity principle transfers to other event-based depth or sharpness estimation tasks beyond autofocus.
