## Problem

Designers using text-to-image models struggle to transfer specific visual attributes (shape, material, color, pose, camera angle, style) from a reference image into new generations, because articulating nuanced design intentions purely through text is imprecise and existing image-conditioned methods either reconstruct entire objects, require thousands of training samples, or need pre-processing like edge detection. No prior method can selectively extract a single attribute-level concept from one image and apply it across diverse contexts without pulling in unrelated visual features.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single reference image (no dataset or pre-processing required) and a target concept specified as a short text phrase (e.g., 'colors of the bubbles'), and produces an optimized token embedding that encodes only that attribute. The embedding is then inserted into arbitrary text prompts to generate images carrying the target attribute in new contexts. Implementation uses Stable Diffusion 3 with three text encoders, optimizing four token embeddings per encoder over ~5,000 iterations on a single RTX 3090 GPU.

## Method

Building on Textual Inversion, the method introduces three complementary mechanisms to isolate a target attribute from entangled visual features. First, a VLM constructs a custom training prompt that explicitly describes all non-target attributes, so the token embedding is pressured to represent only the target. Second, a distilled embedding is extracted by passing '[*] [category]' through the text transformer, exploiting self-attention so that the category token's forwarded representation captures only the target-category features from the placeholder token, structurally excluding undescribed attributes. Third, a residual embedding (obtained by passing '[R] image' through the transformer) absorbs all remaining attributes, preventing the distilled embedding from being forced to encode them and thereby stabilizing optimization; a cosine similarity loss pushes the residual embedding away from the target concept to avoid feature leakage. The final training objective combines the standard diffusion denoising loss with this cosine penalty, and the resulting distilled embedding can be dropped into any prompt to inject the learned attribute.

## Results

On the proposed Attribute-Level Concept Learning Dataset (30 reference images, 60 evaluation prompts across 6 categories: shape, material, color, pose, camera shot/angle, and style), the method generates 1,800 images per baseline for quantitative comparison. In the user study (28 participants, 540 responses), the proposed method achieves a Prompt Fidelity of 0.815, surpassing TokenVerse (0.573), U-VAP (0.207), OmniGen2 (0.613), and TI (0.604), and nearly matching ProSpect (0.820). For Concept Similarity, ProSpect scores highest (93.7%) but at the cost of very low Concept Exclusiveness (25.2%), while the proposed method achieves the best overall balance of CS and CE as reported in the quantitative ablation (Fig. 8).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all baselines on the critical balance of Concept Similarity and Concept Exclusiveness: ProSpect achieves the highest CS (93.7%) but the lowest CE (25.2%), meaning it fails to exclude non-target attributes; U-VAP has the highest CE (75.9%) but the lowest CS (54.0%) and Prompt Fidelity (0.207), tending to nearly reconstruct the reference image rather than transferring a single attribute. TokenVerse (CS 84.0%, CE 40.0%) and TI (CS 69.2%, CE 56.0%) both struggle with selective extraction, absorbing untargeted attributes into the token. OmniGen2 (CS 65.3%, CE 68.0%) is moderate on both metrics but lags in Prompt Fidelity (0.613). On Prompt Fidelity, the proposed method (0.815) is essentially tied with ProSpect (0.820) and clearly ahead of all other baselines. In qualitative comparisons with GPT Image 1 and Gemini 2.5 Flash Image (Nano Banana), both general-purpose models struggle with implicit concepts such as camera shot/angle and tend to incorporate non-target attributes (background, color) alongside the target material, confirming the continued relevance of dedicated attribute-level extraction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Textual Inversion (TI) from learning an entire object's appearance to learning a single attribute-level concept (color, shape, material, pose, camera angle, style) from one reference image. It builds on the observation from subject-driven generation that descriptive training prompts can steer token embeddings toward specific attributes, and departs from prior attribute-extraction methods (ProSpect, U-VAP, TokenVerse) by requiring no pre-processing, no multi-image datasets, and no architecture-specific modulation. The core technical departure is the distilled embedding, which exploits transformer cross-attention to structurally isolate category-specific features from the optimized token, paired with a residual embedding that absorbs remaining attributes to stabilize training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a practical, single-image workflow for designers who want to transfer a specific visual attribute (e.g., a particular material or camera angle) into new generations without iterative prompt engineering, and the newly constructed dataset with six attribute categories provides a reusable benchmark for this underexplored task. Limitations are tied to the base model (SD3's weak human anatomy, limited context range), and the authors note that scaling to larger models like FLUX is needed for broader applicability. The comparison with GPT Image 1 and Nano Banana—both of which still struggle with implicit attributes like camera shot—suggests that explicit attribute-level control remains an open problem even for frontier generative systems.
