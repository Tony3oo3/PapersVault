## Problem

Existing Gaussian Splatting-based inverse rendering methods operate on static scenes where it is inherently ambiguous to distinguish intrinsic material color from illumination effects such as shadows. This limits accurate separation of albedo from lighting, degrading relighting quality in real-world conditions with significant direct illumination. The paper hypothesizes that scene dynamics—where the same surfaces are observed under varying incident light due to motion—provide a natural supervisory signal to resolve this ambiguity.

## Input / Output

The method takes a video sequence of a dynamic scene with one view per timestep (D-NeRF-like captures) and known camera poses, assuming static but unknown illumination. It produces a 2D Gaussian Splatting geometry with per-Gaussian albedo and roughness, a single environment illumination map shared across the scene, and the ability to render the scene under arbitrary novel lighting. The canonical color learned in Stage 1 serves as the initial albedo estimate, and the deformation network's color output is discarded in Stage 2 so that all color variation arises from the rendering equation.

## Method

LumiMotion is a two-stage pipeline. Stage 1 jointly learns 2DGS geometry and a deformation MLP that predicts per-Gaussian position, rotation, and multiplicative color deltas (but not opacity or scale, to prevent objects from appearing/disappearing). A key contribution is a per-Gaussian Binary Concrete variable P that softly assigns each Gaussian to a static or dynamic group, with an L1 penalty encouraging static assignment; this separation is critical because moving Gaussians that encode shadows cannot receive a stable albedo in Stage 2. Stage 2 freezes the geometry and deformation, then performs inverse rendering via deferred shading: albedo, roughness, and normal maps are rasterized, and the Disney BRDF is evaluated per-pixel using stratified Monte Carlo sampling over the environment map with 2D Gaussian ray tracing for visibility and indirect light. The environment map, per-Gaussian albedo, and roughness are jointly optimized against ground-truth pixels with an L1 loss, while an L2 penalty on the lower hemisphere of the environment map regularizes the illumination estimate.

## Results

On the new LumiMotion synthetic benchmark (5 scenes × 4 lighting conditions, static and dynamic variants), LumiMotion achieves the best albedo estimation across all metrics in all three train-test lighting configurations, with PSNR up to 30.84 (Chapel Day→Golden Bay) and LPIPS as low as 0.036. For relighting, LumiMotion achieves the best LPIPS in all three configurations (0.060, 0.041, 0.048), corresponding to an average 15% improvement over the next-best baseline (IR-GS). The paper reports a 23% average LPIPS improvement for albedo estimation over IR-GS. On the real-world ENeRF dataset, LumiMotion qualitatively removes sharp shadows from albedo and produces artifact-free specular renders, whereas IR-GS retains shadow artifacts in the albedo.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LumiMotion is compared against three static-scene Gaussian relighting baselines: R-3DGS (Gao et al., ECCV 2024), GI-GS (Chen et al., ICLR 2025), and IR-GS (Gu et al., CVPR 2025). On albedo estimation, LumiMotion decisively outperforms all three baselines on every metric in all three lighting configurations, with the largest gains over IR-GS (the strongest baseline): e.g., PSNR 30.84 vs. 23.77 and LPIPS 0.036 vs. 0.053 on Chapel Day→Golden Bay. For relighting, LumiMotion achieves the best LPIPS in all three configurations (0.060, 0.041, 0.048 vs. IR-GS's 0.064, 0.046, 0.060), but IR-GS retains a slight edge on relighting PSNR in one of three settings (26.18 vs. 26.04 on Dam Wall→Harbour Sunset) and on SSIM across all settings (e.g., 0.953 vs. 0.928). R-3DGS and GI-GS are consistently the weakest baselines, with GI-GS particularly struggling on relighting (PSNR as low as 17.64). On the real-world ENeRF dataset, LumiMotion qualitatively surpasses IR-GS by removing sharp dynamic shadows from the albedo and avoiding specular rendering artifacts that IR-GS exhibits.

## Relation to Prior Work

LumiMotion builds directly on 2D Gaussian Splatting for geometry representation (providing the surface normals essential for shading) and on MLP-based deformation networks from 4DGS/Deformable 3D Gaussians for modeling temporal dynamics. Its inverse rendering pipeline adopts the deferred shading and ray-tracing strategy established by IRGS and GI-GS, applying the rendering equation after rasterization rather than per-Gaussian. The key conceptual departure is from all prior Gaussian-based inverse rendering methods (IRGS, GI-GS, R-3DGS), which are restricted to static scenes, and from dynamic relighting methods (Gaussian Codec Avatars, IntrinsicAvatar), which are confined to human avatars with pose priors. LumiMotion is positioned as the first method to perform inverse rendering of arbitrary, non-avatar dynamic scenes under unknown natural lighting, using scene motion itself as a supervisory signal to disentangle material from illumination.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core insight—that temporal motion of the same surfaces under static lighting provides a natural disambiguation signal between intrinsic albedo and incident illumination—offers a principled way to address the long-standing material-lighting ambiguity in inverse rendering without requiring multi-illumination captures or object-category priors. The released synthetic benchmark (5 scenes × 4 lighting conditions, static and dynamic variants) is the first to enable systematic evaluation of inverse rendering in dynamic settings, filling a gap identified in Table 1. However, the approach remains sensitive to camera pose accuracy, sparse view configurations, and complex dynamics where the simple Binary Concrete separation can misclassify shadow regions as dynamic Gaussians; the authors note that stronger supervision (e.g., optical flow) may be needed. Open questions include whether the method generalizes to scenes with genuinely changing illumination, whether the two-stage pipeline can be unified, and whether the separation strategy scales to highly articulated or deformable objects.
