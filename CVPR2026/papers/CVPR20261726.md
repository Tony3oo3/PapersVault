## Problem

Existing video instance segmentation (VIS) methods are not robust to the noise, blur, and illumination degradations inherent in low-light video, and no domain adaptation framework has been proposed for VIS in the dark. Compounding this, publicly available synthetic low-light pipelines were designed for still images and fail to model temporal degradations such as motion blur from long shutter speeds, while no dedicated low-light VIS benchmark exists.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261726/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes sRGB low-light video sequences and outputs per-instance segmentation masks with class labels and tracking identities, following the standard VIS protocol. As an auxiliary output, the enhancement decoder reconstructs the corresponding normal-light frames, which are used only during training via an L1 reconstruction loss. Training videos are synthesized on-the-fly by applying the degradation model to normal-light YouTube-VIS 2019 clips using degradation profiles estimated by VDP-Net from real low-light datasets (SDSD, DID, BVIRLV, LMOT).

## Method

ELVIS comprises three components: (1) a physics-based synthetic low-light video pipeline that models illumination via exposure scaling (2^ε), combined motion and defocus blur via a rotated multivariate Gaussian PSF parameterized by (σ_Hx, σ_Hy, θ_H), and four noise types (Gaussian read, Poisson shot, uniform quantization, and orientation-selectable Gaussian banding); (2) VDP-Net, a lightweight ResNet-18-based network with a temporal fusion block and dual MLP heads that estimates the full degradation profile φ from real low-light video in an unsupervised manner (trained with L1 loss plus a cosine angular loss for the blur angle); and (3) a multiscale deformable-attention pixel decoder head integrated into Mask2Former-based VIS architectures (MinVIS, GenVIS, DVIS++), which reconstructs normal-light frames and thereby forces the backbone to disentangle scene content from degradation features. During VIS training, degradation parameters are sampled from a pre-collected set of VDP-Net-estimated profiles rather than random values, ensuring realistic and diverse degradation conditions.

## Results

On the synthetic low-light YouTube-VIS 2019 validation set, ELVIS consistently improves all three SOTA VIS methods: DVIS++ (ViT-L) gains +1.7 AP (55.2→56.9) and +3.2 AP75 (62.1→65.3), GenVIS (SWIN-L) gains +1.6 AP (53.7→55.3) and +4.5 AP50 (74.8→79.3), and MinVIS (SWIN-L) gains +2.4 AP (51.8→54.2). On real low-light ELVIS-S, ELVIS achieves 58.0 AP, outperforming the best two-stage baseline (StableLLVE, 57.3 AP) by +0.7 AP; on the more challenging LMOT-S, ELVIS reaches 6.7 AP versus 3.9 AP for the best two-stage baseline (+2.8 AP). The synthetic pipeline also outperforms competing physics-based pipelines (Lv et al., Cui et al., Lin et al.) by 1.0–1.4 AP on ELVIS-S and 0.9–1.5 AP on LMOT-S.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ELVIS is applied as a plug-in enhancement to three SOTA VIS methods (MinVIS, GenVIS, DVIS++) and improves all of them across every metric on synthetic YouTube-VIS 2019, with the largest AP gain of +3.7 for DVIS++ (ResNet-50: 38.8→42.5) and the largest AP75 gain of +3.2 for DVIS++ (ViT-L: 62.1→65.3). Against two-stage baselines (SDSD-Net, StableLLVE, DarkIR followed by GenVIS), ELVIS wins on both real datasets: +0.7 AP on ELVIS-S (58.0 vs. 57.3) and +2.8 AP on LMOT-S (6.7 vs. 3.9), with the gap widening substantially on the harder LMOT-S benchmark. Against competing synthetic low-light pipelines (Lv et al., Cui et al., Lin et al.), the proposed VDP-Net-guided pipeline achieves the best AP on both ELVIS-S (54.5 vs. 53.5/51.1/35.1) and LMOT-S (6.6 vs. 5.1/5.7/2.7), and also outperforms a random-sampling ablation of its own pipeline (39.9/4.7 AP), confirming the value of learned degradation profiles. On the LLVE side (Table 6), the synthetic pipeline trains SDSD-Net to the best LPIPS on both SDSD (0.198) and DID (0.164), and the best PSNR/SSIM on DID (14.266/0.676), validating the realism of the generated degradations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of synthetic low-light data generation (Lv et al., Wei et al., Cui et al.) by introducing a video-specific degradation model that incorporates multivariate Gaussian blur alongside illumination and physics-based noise, addressing a gap where prior pipelines were image-centric and ignored temporal blur from long shutter speeds. It departs from existing domain-adaptation approaches for low-light detection and segmentation (Cui et al., Du et al., Chen et al.) by being the first to target video instance segmentation, integrating an enhancement decoder head into Mask2Former-based VIS architectures to disentangle degradation from scene content. The calibration-free VDP-Net builds on prior noise-estimation networks (Jin et al., Zou et al., Lin et al.) but adds temporal fusion and a cosine angular loss to handle blur orientation, moving beyond image-level parameter estimation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ELVIS fills a clear gap by providing the first end-to-end framework for low-light VIS, a task with direct applications in autonomous driving, surveillance, and wildlife monitoring where existing VIS models fail. The demonstration that two-stage enhancement-then-segmentation pipelines underperform an integrated approach with an enhancement decoder offers a practical design principle for degraded-domain adaptation. However, the real-world evaluation remains preliminary (250 manually annotated frames in ELVIS-S, pseudo-labels in LMOT-S), and the authors explicitly call for properly annotated low-light VIS benchmarks as critical future work. The modest absolute performance on real low-light data (AP ~6–7 on LMOT-S) underscores how far the task is from being solved.
