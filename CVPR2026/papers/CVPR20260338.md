## Problem

Existing frame selection methods for long video understanding select frames based solely on high query-frame relevance, producing disjointed sets that ignore the video's narrative structure and fail to capture process, causality, or storyline. This paper argues that effective video understanding hinges on detecting semantic shifts—pivotal moments of narrative change—rather than merely sampling the most relevant individual frames.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260338/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a long video (uniformly subsampled at 1 FPS, yielding N candidate frames) and a text query as input. It produces a chronologically ordered subset of K frames (K ≪ N, typically 8–64) that preserves semantic segment structure. The selected frames are then fed as visual context to a frozen LVLM (e.g., LLaVA-Video-7B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B) alongside the query to generate a video understanding response. The entire pipeline is training-free and operates as a single-pass preprocessing step before LVLM inference.

## Method

WFS-SB reframes semantic boundary detection as a signal processing problem. It first computes per-frame Image-Text Matching scores via BLIP-2's ITM head, forming a noisy temporal relevance signal. A multi-level Discrete Wavelet Transform (Daubechies-4, adaptively chosen decomposition depth) decomposes this signal, and only the coarsest-scale detail coefficients are retained and inverse-transformed to yield a clean semantic change signal whose local extrema mark narrative boundaries, segmenting the video into coherent clips. A two-stage selection then follows: (1) adaptive budget allocation assigns frames to each segment via a softmax-weighted composite importance score (duration, mean/peak relevance, internal variance), and (2) within each segment, Maximal Marginal Relevance selects a diverse yet relevant frame set. The key differentiator is that wavelet multi-resolution analysis inherently suppresses high-frequency noise from model uncertainty and visual artifacts, isolating genuine macro-level semantic transitions that naive gradient or threshold methods miss.

## Results

WFS-SB delivers consistent plug-and-play improvements across all four LVLM backbones and three long-video benchmarks, with average gains of +3.9% on VideoMME, +8.8% on MLVU, and +5.4% on LongVideoBench. The most striking results come with LLaVA-Video-7B at K=8 frames: +5.5% on VideoMME (56.2→61.7), +9.5% on MLVU (57.4→66.9), and +6.2% on LongVideoBench (54.9→61.1). With Qwen2.5-VL-7B at K=32, WFS-SB achieves the largest single-benchmark gain of +10.7% on MLVU (59.7→70.4). Gains are most pronounced under tight frame budgets (K=8), confirming that boundary-aware selection is most critical when frame resources are constrained.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WFS-SB outperforms all seven baselines across every benchmark and LVLM backbone tested. Against the strongest training-free competitor AKS, WFS-SB leads by +1.1% on VideoMME, +2.4% on MLVU, and +1.0% on LVB with LLaVA-OV-7B, and by +0.4%, +3.2%, and +1.2% respectively with Qwen2.5-VL-7B. Against the iterative method A.I.R. (which requires multiple LVLM inference passes), WFS-SB achieves higher accuracy on all three benchmarks with both Qwen2.5-VL-7B (64.4 vs 65.0 on VideoMME is a loss, but 70.4 vs 67.5 on MLVU and 64.4 vs 61.4 on LVB are wins) and InternVL-3-8B (67.4 vs 68.2 on VideoMME is a slight loss, but 74.8 vs 74.5 on MLVU and 62.9 vs 62.8 on LVB are wins). Against training-based methods Frame-Voyager and FrameOracle, WFS-SB achieves substantially larger gains (e.g., +5.2% vs +4.2% on VideoMME with LLaVA-OV-7B), while requiring no training. The gap is most dramatic on MLVU, where WFS-SB's +9.5% gain with LLaVA-Video-7B exceeds the next-best method (KFC at +6.4%) by over 3 percentage points, highlighting the advantage of semantic-structure-aware selection over pure relevance-based sampling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant frame-selection paradigm that treats each frame's query-relevance score independently (BOLT, KFC, AKS, MDP3, A.I.R.) by reframing the problem as signal processing: the ITM score sequence is a noisy, non-stationary temporal signal whose macro-level transitions encode semantic boundaries. The wavelet transform, well-established in image compression and turbulence analysis, is repurposed here for the first time to denoise and segment a query-relevance signal in the video-understanding pipeline. The intra-segment selection step borrows Maximal Marginal Relevance from information retrieval, and the overall two-stage (segment-then-sample) structure is a conceptual departure from flat, relevance-ranked sampling, though it shares the training-free, plug-and-play philosophy of BOLT and MDP3.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WFS-SB offers a practical, zero-training enhancement that consistently improves four architecturally diverse LVLMs across three benchmarks, making it immediately deployable for long-video QA systems. The main computational bottleneck is ITM signal extraction (19.4 s, ~79% of pipeline time), which the authors acknowledge could be accelerated via batching or distillation but leave unaddressed. An open question is how the method behaves on very long videos (tens of minutes to hours) where the adaptive decomposition level and the assumption of a small number of coherent semantic segments may break down. Additionally, because the approach is query-conditioned, it cannot produce a single reusable video representation, limiting its applicability to multi-query or open-ended summarization settings.
