## Problem

Prior vision-based sound recovery methods have focused on objects with favorable vibration responses (speaker membranes, thin chip bags), leaving solid everyday objects with weak or highly resonant impulse responses largely unaddressed. Fusing multi-point vibration signals from such objects is non-trivial because surface points are coupled by complex, frequency-dependent mechanical wave propagation rather than simple time delays, causing naive averaging or global delay-and-sum to cancel important spectral components.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a 10×10 grid of two-axis speckle shift signals (200 total 1D channels) captured at 22,000 fps from a single object's surface, preprocessed with a 7th-order Butterworth bandpass filter (50–10,000 Hz). The output is a single denoised, spectrally 'equalized' 1D audio signal that approximates the original scene sound source, with the object's resonant timbre suppressed. The object is placed ~50 cm from the camera and the sound source ~150 cm away, with a single clap recording used as broadband excitation for mode extraction.

## Method

The paper derives a physics-guided forward model (Eq. 8) that expresses each measured two-axis speckle shift as a weighted sum over the object's vibrational modes, where each mode contributes a spatial gradient of its shape convolved with a second-order oscillator impulse response. Mode frequencies and shape gradients are extracted from the data via a three-stage robust procedure: cross-point spectral standard deviation peak detection, correlation-based pruning of redundant modes, and total-variation-based rejection of physically inconsistent high-spatial-frequency outliers. Sound recovery then proceeds by jointly optimizing the latent sound signal s(t) and per-mode coupling coefficients α_k to minimize the reconstruction error across all 200 channels, with a smoothness regularizer on s(t), solved via Adam gradient descent for 10,000 steps. This inversion simultaneously denoises the signal and 'equalizes' the resonant coloration by correctly aligning frequency-dependent phase relationships across the grid, outperforming both single-point capture and classical beamforming baselines.

## Results

The paper presents primarily qualitative (spectrogram-based) comparisons across diverse objects (drum, chip bag, laptop, binder, guitar, picture frame, yoga block) rather than a single quantitative benchmark table; the authors note that 'extensive quantitative evaluation is provided in the supplementary.' Key practical metrics include a recovery time of ~27 s on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, mode detection reliably identifying frequencies below 5 kHz, and a 10×10 speckle grid captured at 22,000 fps. The method's recovered signal closely matches a calibrated inverse-filter baseline (which requires a separate reference chirp recording), confirming that the physics-guided model captures most of the recoverable frequency content without any scene intervention.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four fusion strategies on the same multi-point speckle recordings: (1) single-point capture, (2) naive averaging across all grid points, (3) delay-and-sum beamforming with a globally estimated time delay, and (4) the proposed modal-guided inversion. Single-point measurements on resonant objects (e.g., drumhead) are noisy and strongly 'colored' by the object's modal frequencies. Naive averaging performs worse than single-point because random phase misalignments across points cause destructive interference at many frequencies. Delay-and-sum reduces high-frequency noise but still loses higher-frequency modal content because a single constant delay cannot align the frequency-dependent phase shifts induced by different vibration modes. The proposed method outperforms all three by explicitly modeling the frequency-dependent phase and magnitude coupling through the object's modal basis, yielding a denoised, spectrally richer reconstruction with reduced resonant timbre. Against the calibrated baseline (which inverts per-point impulse responses using a known reference chirp), the proposed method closely matches the recovered frequency content without requiring any additional reference signal or scene intervention, though the highest-frequency modes remain slightly underrepresented.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the vision-based sound recovery line (Davis et al. 2014; Sheinin et al. 2022; Cai et al. 2025) from single-point or favorable-object measurements to multi-point fusion on challenging solid objects. The measurement pipeline builds directly on Kichler et al. [26] for 2D speckle-grid vibrometry, while the modal identification procedure draws on classical frequency-domain decomposition methods (Brincker et al. [7, 8]). The key conceptual departure is the derivation of a physics-guided forward model (Eq. 8) that links multi-point, two-axis speckle shifts to the underlying sound source through the object's vibrational modes, replacing the ad-hoc signal-merging strategies (single-axis playback, global delay assumptions) used in prior multi-point work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first principled, physics-based framework for fusing multi-point speckle vibrations to recover sound from objects with poor or highly resonant impulse responses, a regime where prior single-point and naive-averaging methods fail. The close match to a calibrated 'optimal' baseline (Fig. 7) without any reference signal suggests the modal model captures the dominant physics, but the approach remains limited by mode-extraction accuracy (modes above ~5 kHz are hard to detect) and performs best on stiff, planar objects. Open questions include more robust high-frequency mode identification, handling strongly non-planar or heterogeneous geometries, and whether learned modal priors could replace the current empirical extraction pipeline.
