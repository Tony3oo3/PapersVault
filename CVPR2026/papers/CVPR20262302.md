## Problem

Despite over 150 foundation models proposed for Earth observation, no foundation model has been developed for Mars remote sensing, leaving planetary scientists reliant on ImageNet pre-training that fails to capture Martian surface characteristics. The core challenge is integrating data from sensors with vastly different spatial resolutions (0.25 m to 100 m/pixel) and coverage (HiRISE <3% vs. CTX ~100%), where direct multi-sensor data stacking or joint training is impractical due to resolution mismatch and scalability constraints.

## Input / Output

The model consumes Mars orbital imagery patches from three sensors spanning a 400× resolution range: HiRISE (0.25 m/pixel, ~4M samples), CTX (5 m/pixel, ~4M samples), and THEMIS (100 m/pixel, ~4M samples), with stratified sampling by USGS geologic map units to preserve surface diversity. A single unified model produces representations fine-tunable for both classification (4 tasks, reported as weighted F1) and semantic segmentation (5 tasks, reported as mIoU and Object F1), eliminating the need for separate per-sensor models.

## Method

MOMO pre-trains independent masked autoencoder models on each sensor's data using a custom multi-term reconstruction loss combining MSE, SSIM, LPIPS, and a gradient penalty to preserve structural details like crater rims. The key novelty is the Equal Validation Loss (EVL) checkpoint selection strategy: rather than merging at final or early-stopping checkpoints, EVL searches for a tuple of epochs (one per sensor) where validation losses are mutually within a tolerance ε, then selects the tuple minimizing average normalized distance to each sensor's early-stopping epoch. The selected checkpoints are fused via task arithmetic (parameter addition), producing a single model that integrates all three sensors. This approach ensures models are merged at compatible convergence stages, avoiding overfitted or underfitted contributions, and supports modular extension to new sensors without full retraining.

## Results

MOMO achieves the best average rank (1.67) across all 9 Mars-Bench orbital tasks, outperforming all baselines. On segmentation tasks, MOMO leads with mIoU of 0.20 (Boulder), 0.71 (ConeQuest), 0.54 (Crater Binary), 0.12 (Crater Multi), and 0.57 (MMLS). On classification, MOMO scores 0.96 (AtmosDust), 0.92 (DoMars16k), 0.98 (Frost), and 0.91 (Landmark) F1, matching or exceeding the strongest baselines. The largest gains over ImageNet pre-training appear in segmentation (~4% mIoU on Boulder) and in the AtmosDust classification task (~4% F1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MOMO outperforms ImageNet pre-training on 7 of 9 tasks, with an average ~1.25% F1 gain on classification and ~1% mIoU gain on segmentation; ImageNet only wins on Landmark by a narrow 1% margin. Among Earth-observation foundation models, SatMAE (avg rank 3.00) and DINOv3 (3.67) are the closest competitors on classification, but MOMO decisively leads on all five segmentation tasks—e.g., 0.20 vs. 0.05 mIoU on Boulder vs. SatMAE, and 0.57 vs. 0.32 on MMLS. CROMA and Prithvi, the two explicitly cited baselines, perform substantially worse than MOMO across nearly all tasks (avg ranks 5.89 and 6.11 respectively), with CROMA scoring only 0.41 F1 on DoMars16k versus MOMO's 0.92. Compared to sensor-specific pre-training, MOMO improves by 1.75% on classification and 4.2% on segmentation while offering a single unified model instead of per-sensor models. Against the Data Merge (joint pre-training) baseline, MOMO gains 15.25% on classification and 5.4% on segmentation, and additionally supports modular addition of new sensors without full retraining.

## Relation to Prior Work

MOMO builds on the Masked Autoencoder pre-training paradigm (He et al., 2022) and the task-arithmetic model merging framework (Ilharco et al., 2022), extending both to the multi-sensor, multi-resolution setting of Mars orbital imagery. It departs from Earth-observation foundation models (SatMAE, CROMA, Prithvi, TerraFM, DINOv3) that assume co-registered or similar-resolution inputs, and from prior Mars-specific work (Jiang et al., 2021; Purohit et al., 2024) that used single sensors. The key methodological departure is the Equal Validation Loss (EVL) checkpoint selection strategy, which is the first systematic criterion for choosing *when* to merge independently trained models rather than simply merging at final or early-stopping checkpoints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MOMO fills a clear gap as the first foundation model for Mars remote sensing, providing planetary scientists a unified, multi-resolution backbone that eliminates the need to maintain separate models per sensor. The EVL strategy is a general, lightweight technique applicable to any multi-sensor or multi-resolution merging scenario, and the modular design allows new sensors to be integrated without full retraining. Open questions include whether the linear mode connectivity assumption holds for highly divergent distributions (e.g., adding hyperspectral or radar sensors), whether alignment-based techniques (Git Re-Basin, layer-wise editing) can further improve merged-model quality, and whether the approach scales beyond three sensors. The paper's reliance on a single architecture (ViT-MAE) and five-epoch training limits confidence in the generality of the EVL findings.
