## Problem

Reward models (RMs) are deployed throughout text-to-image pipelines for dataset filtering, evaluation, and reward-guided optimization, yet their robustness and fairness as scoring functions remain largely unexamined. While prior work has studied reward hacking and mode collapse, no systematic audit has tested whether RMs encode demographic biases that cause disparate effects—such as hypersexualization of female subjects or convergence toward White-presenting outputs—during optimization, or whether their scores systematically favor certain racial or gender groups on counterfactual image sets.

## Input / Output

In the optimization experiments (Part I), the input is an initial noise vector ε paired with a text prompt, fed through a one-step T2I base model (SDXL-Turbo, Pixart-α DMD, or SD-Turbo); the output is the optimized image, from which NSFW classification (binary via a pretrained classifier) and skin-exposure ratio (body-skin pixels / total visible person area) are extracted, along with demographic labels via CLIP cosine similarity to Chicago Face Database anchor embeddings. In the counterfactual scoring experiments (Part II), the input is a set of matched images that vary only in race or gender for a fixed prompt, and the output is a scalar reward score per image, which is then decomposed via OLS regression (race, gender, race×gender interaction) and averaged rank per demographic group.

## Method

The paper conducts a two-part empirical audit of five widely used RMs (PickScore, ImageReward, HPS, VQAScore, CLIP, and Aesthetic Score) plus an Incompression baseline. Part I uses the ReNO framework to perform gradient-ascent noise optimization (Eq. 2) with each RM across three base models, then quantifies disparate hypersexualization (ΔNSFW, Δskin-exposure stratified by gender) and demographic convergence (transition heatmaps of perceived race/gender before vs. after optimization, using CLIP anchor classification grounded in self-identified Chicago Face Database demographics). Part II scores counterfactual image sets from CausalFace, SocialCounterfactuals, and PAIRS under four prompt families (SCM, ABC, DALL-Eval Descriptor, Occupation), fitting OLS regressions of normalized reward scores on race, gender, and their interaction, and computing average rank per demographic group; occupation-prompt effect sizes are further correlated with U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics female-employment shares to test whether RM scoring mirrors real-world demographic frequency priors rather than quality-relevant dimensions.

## Results

Across all reward and base model combinations, PickScore produces the largest hypersexualization effect, increasing NSFW classification rate by 19% for female subjects versus 7% for male subjects (2.7× disparity), with the worst case (PickScore + Pixart-α DMD) reaching a 25% NSFW rate increase. For demographic convergence, HPS causes 89.2% of non-White images to reclassify as White after optimization, ImageReward causes 76.1%, and CLIP causes 36.2%. Additionally, ImageReward and CLIP reclassify female subjects as male 39% and 26% of the time, respectively. In counterfactual scoring, HPS and ImageReward consistently rank White subjects highest and Asian subjects lowest across all prompt types, while VQAScore uniquely reverses its racial ranking for negative prompts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper audits six RMs (PickScore, ImageReward, HPS, VQAScore, CLIP, Aesthetic Score) rather than proposing a new method, so 'comparisons' are cross-RM disparities. PickScore is the most problematic for hypersexualization, producing 2.7× the NSFW rate increase for female vs. male subjects and the largest absolute increase (25% with Pixart-α DMD). HPS is the most severe for racial convergence, shifting 89.2% of non-White images to White-presenting outputs, followed by ImageReward (76.1%) and CLIP (36.2%); under negative optimization (λ=−1), HPS and ImageReward instead push non-Black images toward Black classification. In counterfactual scoring, HPS and ImageReward rank White subjects highest and Asian subjects lowest consistently across all four prompt sets and three datasets, a structural prior that persists even for negative prompts. VQAScore is the notable exception, ranking White subjects highest for positive prompts but Black subjects highest for negative prompts, suggesting its alignment objective interacts differently with demographic priors. CLIP generally ranks Black subjects last relative to other racial groups, while Aesthetic Score shows similar gender disparities in NSFW rate on SD-Turbo but is less studied in the paper's analysis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the growing body of work on demographic bias in text-to-image generation [4, 9, 10, 20, 40, 45] by shifting the focus from generative models to the reward models (RMs) that increasingly mediate T2I pipelines for filtering, evaluation, and fine-tuning. It builds on the ReNO noise-optimization framework [14] and prior identifications of RM failure modes (reward hacking, mode collapse [31, 44, 54, 63]), but departs from the two closest prior studies of T2I RMs [8, 45] by conducting a systematic, multi-model audit using both optimization trajectories and counterfactual scoring across three datasets and four prompt families. The use of the Chicago Face Database for anchor-based demographic classification and the correlation with U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics occupational data are methodological contributions not present in prior RM bias analyses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Because RMs are embedded in nearly every modern T2I pipeline (best-of-N selection, reward-guided fine-tuning, dataset curation, safety filtering), the finding that they systematically favor White subjects and disproportionately sexualize female subjects means that any downstream system using these RMs inherits a demographically skewed filter that cannot be attributed to image quality. The paper leaves the remediation question open—no corrected RM training procedure is proposed—and the authors acknowledge that their counterfactual datasets are not strict counterfactuals, so the magnitude of effects may be somewhat inflated. A key open question is whether the demographic priors identified here (which mirror real-world frequency distributions) can be disentangled from genuine quality signals during RM training, and whether the findings generalize to the broader class of non-demographic robustness failures the authors hint at.
