## Problem

Existing task-aware infrared-visible image fusion methods, whether loss-function-driven or task-semantic-guided, perform well only on the specific downstream tasks used during training and degrade significantly on untrained tasks. This limits their practical utility in multi-task scenarios where a single fused image must serve diverse objectives such as object detection, semantic segmentation, and salient object detection simultaneously without retraining the fusion network.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263365/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes paired infrared and visible images (256×256 patches during training) along with semantic features extracted from frozen downstream task networks (YOLOv5s, SegFormer-B2, CTDNet-18) as additional input to the compensation module. It produces a task-customized fused image tailored to a specific downstream task, with the RSC module contributing only 0.46M trainable parameters and 174.06G FLOPs at inference. No gradient updates are applied to any network during inference; the RSC module simply receives task semantic features and performs forward-pass compensation on the frozen fusion network.

## Method

CLDyN uses a two-stage training procedure: first, a Vision-guided Fusion Network (VFN) is trained with pixel and gradient losses for visual quality; second, the VFN is frozen and a Requirement-driven Semantic Compensation (RSC) module is trained via a closed-loop optimization mechanism. The RSC module contains a Basis Vector Bank (BVB) storing 32 learnable orthogonal basis vectors per convolutional configuration to generate task-specific kernels, and an Architecture-Adaptive Semantic Injection (A2SI) block that uses four frozen orthogonal convolutional prototypes (1×1, 3×1, 3×2, 3×3) to adaptively select kernel configurations per branch based on task semantics and scene context. A reward-penalty strategy compares task performance before and after semantic compensation, rewarding the RSC module under GT supervision when performance improves and applying an additional penalty when it degrades, thereby preventing harmful semantic drift. CAGrad is employed to mitigate gradient conflicts across the multiple downstream tasks during multi-task optimization of the RSC module.

## Results

On the multi-task evaluation (Table 2), CLDyN achieves mAP50→95 of 0.6304 (OD), mIoU of 60.34 (Seg), mFβ of 0.8129 (SOD), and Em of 0.9087 (SOD), ranking first or second on all four metrics while using only 0.46M trainable parameters and 174.06G FLOPs—orders of magnitude fewer than joint-training baselines (e.g., SMiF: 45.60M params, 526.20G FLOPs). For fusion quality (Table 1), CLDyN ranks first on most metrics across M3FD, FMB, and VT5000 (e.g., QAB/F of 0.6900/0.7124/0.6519 and QCV of 472.62/316.41/331.15). Cross-detector generalization to DETR yields a +2.0 mAP improvement (0.5610→0.5810) without retraining the RSC module.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the 10 'task network retraining' baselines, CLDyN wins on SOD mFβ (0.8129 vs. IRFS 0.8114) and is within 0.0016 of the best OD mAP (OCCO 0.6320) and SOD Em (IRFS 0.9091), while trailing TIMF by 0.52 mIoU on Seg. The efficiency gap is dramatic: CLDyN uses 0.46M trainable parameters versus 46.52M for TIMF and 45.60M for SMiF, with 174.06G FLOPs versus 526.20G for SMiF. In the 'joint training' comparison (Table 3), CLDyN outperforms IRFS on OD (0.6304 vs. 0.6180) and SOD mFβ (0.8129 vs. 0.8319—actually IRFS leads here), while MRFS leads on Seg mIoU (61.48 vs. 60.34) but lags on OD (0.5959 vs. 0.6304) and SOD. Against the instruction-driven IDF-TDDT, CLDyN significantly outperforms on both quantitative and qualitative metrics across all three tasks, while avoiding the LLaMA-based instruction encoding overhead. For fusion quality (Table 1), CLDyN leads on QAB/F, QCB, and QCV across all three datasets, with only TIMF exceeding it on MI (2.5942 vs. 2.5265 on M3FD).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of task-aware image fusion and dynamic network design. Prior task-aware fusion methods fall into two camps—loss-function-driven (SeAFusion, TDAL, MetaFusion) and task-semantic-guided (DetFusion, MRFS, SAGE)—but both are locked to the specific downstream task(s) used during training and degrade on untrained tasks. CLDyN repurposes the closed-loop control paradigm from robotics and the dynamic convolution idea from dynamic neural networks, applying them to fusion for the first time: a lightweight RSC module receives semantic feedback from frozen downstream task networks and generates task-specific convolutional parameters on the fly, decoupling fusion quality from any single task's training signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a 0.46 M-parameter, 174 G-FLOP module can adapt a frozen fusion network to object detection, semantic segmentation, and salient object detection simultaneously, and even generalize across detector architectures (YOLOv5 → DETR) without retraining—something joint-training methods with 40–135 M parameters cannot do. Open questions remain: the RSC is trained on a fixed task set, so truly novel downstream tasks are out of scope; the reward-penalty strategy requires ground-truth supervision during training, limiting fully unsupervised deployment; and the modest absolute gains over the strongest single-task baselines (e.g., mAP 0.6304 vs. 0.6320 for OCCO on OD) suggest the closed-loop mechanism may plateau as task diversity grows. Whether the BVB + A2SI design transfers to other fusion modalities (e.g., multi-focus, multi-exposure) is an unexplored direction.
