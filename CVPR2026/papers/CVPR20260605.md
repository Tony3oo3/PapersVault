## Problem

Statistically consistent noise-correction methods (e.g., Forward Correction) are theoretically guaranteed to converge to the Bayes-optimal clean classifier, yet in practice they are outperformed by empirical sample-selection approaches. The prevailing hypothesis attributes this gap to imperfect estimation of the noise transition matrix T. This paper decisively refutes that hypothesis by showing that even with a perfect oracle T, correction methods still suffer performance collapse during prolonged training, and provides a unified three-pillar diagnosis (macroscopic convergence states, microscopic gradient dynamics, and information-theoretic limits) explaining the root cause.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, variable resolution for Clothing1M) paired with noisy labels, along with an instance-dependent noise transition matrix T(x) ∈ [0,1]^{K×K} that maps clean to noisy label probabilities per input. The output is a K-dimensional logit vector producing a predicted class label and a calibrated probability distribution over classes. Evaluation uses both classification accuracy and Expected Calibration Error (ECE), with the paper arguing that ECE is essential for distinguishing the true benefits of consistency-based correction from mere accuracy gains.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a three-pillar theoretical diagnosis of why Forward Correction (FC) fails despite a perfect T: (1) Macroscopic analysis partitions the input space into X_correct and X_error regions, proving FC achieves Bayes-optimal accuracy in the ideal population-minimizer state but collapses to a T-biased hard-vertex one-hot prediction under empirical memorization; (2) Microscopic gradient analysis shows FC's per-sample gradient provides only a transient 'softening effect' that guides the model toward the ideal posterior early in training, but the global empirical minimum is still a pathological vertex e_{k_FC*}; (3) An information-theoretic argument via the Data Processing Inequality proves the noise channel T(x) irreducibly contracts the mutual information between the true hypothesis and the observed label, setting a fundamental statistical difficulty floor. Practically, the authors propose FEC (frozen pretrained encoder + Mixup + FC) and JEC (jointly trained encoder + Mixup + FC) as lightweight regularization frameworks that prevent the memorization collapse, pushing FC toward its ideal generalization state and achieving competitive accuracy with state-of-the-art sample-selection methods on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and Clothing1M.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with 50% symmetric noise, JEC (Joint-Enhanced Correction) achieves 88.8% accuracy, substantially outperforming the unregularized Forward Correction baseline (79.8%) and approaching DivideMix (94.6%). At the more challenging 80% symmetric noise level, JEC reaches 78.5% vs. Forward's 63.3%, while on CIFAR-100 with 50% symmetric noise, JEC achieves 64.9% vs. Forward's 46.6%. On the real-world Clothing1M dataset, JEC attains 72.24% accuracy, surpassing Forward (69.84%), T-Rev (71.01%), and DualT (71.49%), though remaining below DivideMix (74.76%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between the unregularized Forward Correction (FC) and the proposed JEC/FEC variants, demonstrating that adding self-supervised pretraining and Mixup regularization recovers FC's theoretical advantage: JEC improves over Forward by 9.0 points on CIFAR-10 (50% sym), 15.2 points on CIFAR-100 (50% sym), and 2.4 points on Clothing1M. Against sample-selection baselines, JEC is competitive but not dominant: it trails DivideMix by 5.8 points on CIFAR-10 (50% sym) and 9.7 points on CIFAR-100 (50% sym), yet matches or exceeds LossModel on CIFAR-100 (64.9% vs. 66.1% at 50% sym, 50.1% vs. 48.2% at 80% sym). On Clothing1M, JEC (72.24%) outperforms T-Rev (71.01%), DualT (71.49%), and Mixup (71.29%) but remains below DivideMix (74.76%). Co-teaching and PENCIL are substantially outperformed by JEC across all noise levels, confirming that the regularization framework closes most of the gap between noise correction and state-of-the-art sample selection without requiring complex hybrid architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the noise transition matrix (T-matrix) framework for Learning with Noisy Labels, particularly Forward Correction as formalized by Patrini et al. [56] and Natarajan et al. [53], which guarantee asymptotic classifier consistency. However, it departs sharply from the dominant research trajectory that has focused on improving T-estimation (anchor points, volume minimization, clusterability) by demonstrating that estimation accuracy is not the binding constraint. The three-pillar diagnostic framework—contrasting ideal vs. overfitted convergence states, analyzing per-sample gradient dynamics, and applying the Data Processing Inequality to quantify information loss—repurposes tools from statistical learning theory and information theory in a way that prior LNL work has not attempted, positioning the paper as a foundational re-examination rather than an incremental methodological contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By proving that even an oracle T cannot prevent performance collapse in high-capacity networks, this paper redirects the LNL research agenda from refining T-estimation toward designing robust optimization and regularization strategies. The information-theoretic bound (Theorem 4.4) establishes a fundamental per-sample information ceiling that no correction method can exceed, providing a principled stopping point for method design. Open questions include whether the proposed regularization framework (FEC/JEC with Mixup and pretrained encoders) generalizes to higher noise ratios and more complex architectures, and whether the information-scaling insight (multi-label augmentation) can be made computationally practical at scale. The finding that calibration (ECE) degrades more severely than accuracy in the overfitted regime also suggests the field should adopt richer evaluation metrics beyond top-1 accuracy.
