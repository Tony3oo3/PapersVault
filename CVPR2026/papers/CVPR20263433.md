## Problem

Automated agentic architecture search (e.g., MaAS) learns from absolute performance scores on individual queries, which conflates an architecture's intrinsic quality with the query's difficulty, and from single-execution outcomes that capture stochastic execution artifacts rather than consistent capability. These two instabilities produce noisy, biased optimization signals that prevent reliable discovery of genuinely effective multi-agent workflows.

## Input / Output

The system takes a text query (math word problems, code prompts, or multi-step tool-use tasks) and an LLM backbone (GPT-4o-mini or Qwen-2.5-72B) as input. It outputs a query-conditioned probabilistic architecture distribution over an L-layer agentic supernet, which is then instantiated into a concrete multi-agent workflow that produces a final task answer. The search itself operates on cohorts of N sampled architectures per query, each executed K independent times, so the effective per-query compute is N×K LLM workflow runs.

## Method

RAAS introduces two synergistic evaluation modules within the Agentic Supernet paradigm. Contextual Architecture Orchestration (CAO) samples N candidate architectures per query, evaluates them all on the identical query, computes a cohort mean as a contextual baseline, and derives zero-centered merit signals (each architecture's deviation from the baseline) that factor out task difficulty. Multi-Trial Assessment Synthesis (MTAS) runs K independent executions per architecture and aggregates outcomes via a trimmed mean to suppress execution variance. The resulting contextual merit serves as a reward-like signal in a merit-weighted adaptation step: the supernet's per-layer operator probability distributions are updated in proportion to each architecture's contextual merit, weighted by its influence gradient, so that superior design patterns are reinforced while inferior ones are suppressed. This closed-loop process iterates over queries to progressively sharpen the architecture distribution toward high-utility workflows.

## Results

On the gpt-4o-mini backbone, RAAS achieves 60.87% on MATH, 95.16% on GSM8K, 96.31% on HumanEval, and 84.18% on MBPP, averaging 84.13% across the four benchmarks. This represents an average improvement of +5.41 points over the strongest baseline MaAS (78.72%), with the largest gain on MATH (+8.79) and MBPP (+5.47). On the GAIA multi-step tool-use benchmark, RAAS reaches 20.84% average accuracy, outperforming MaAS (18.06%) by +2.78 points. The method also maintains strong results with the qwen-2.5-72b backbone, achieving 83.59% average on the four primary benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RAAS outperforms all 12 compared baselines across every benchmark. Against single-agent methods, RAAS exceeds CoT (74.23% avg) by +9.90 and Self-Consistency (75.39%) by +8.74 on average. Among hand-crafted multi-agent systems, LLM-Debate (75.60%) and DyLAN (76.55%) are the strongest, yet RAAS still leads by +8.53 and +7.58 respectively. Among automated agentic systems, AFlow (77.91%) is the closest competitor before MaAS, with RAAS gaining +6.22 over AFlow. The most direct comparison is against MaAS (78.72%), the agentic supernet baseline that RAAS builds upon: RAAS improves by +5.41 on average, with the largest margin on MATH (+8.79) where task-difficulty variance is highest. On GAIA, RAAS (20.84%) beats MaAS (18.06%), GPTSwarm (14.59%), and AgentSquare (15.49%), though it trails TapeAgent (16.61%) on Level 1 specifically while leading on the overall average. ADAS (71.37%) and AutoAgents (73.20%) underperform even the vanilla single-agent baseline, highlighting that naive automated search without stable evaluation signals can be counterproductive.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Agentic Supernet paradigm introduced by MaAS [39], which reframes multi-agent architecture design as optimization over a probabilistic operator space. Its core departure is methodological: rather than improving the search algorithm itself, RAAS targets the evaluation signal that drives that search, drawing a conceptual parallel to GRPO's [30] group-relative advantage estimation for the peer-comparison mechanism (CAO) and to classical ensemble/statistical aggregation for the multi-trial synthesis (MTAS). The paper positions these as fixes for two specific instabilities inherited from MaAS and prior automated agentic systems—task-difficulty entanglement in absolute scores and execution variance in single-trial assessments—rather than as a new search paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that evaluation-signal quality (not search-algorithm sophistication) is the primary bottleneck in agentic architecture search is a useful reframing for the field, and the +5.41 average gain over MaAS at lower cost is practically meaningful. However, the evidence base is somewhat narrow: most benchmarks are single-turn reasoning or coding tasks, the multi-step tool-use evidence comes from a single benchmark (GAIA) with smaller gains (+2.78), and only two LLM backbones are tested. Open questions include whether the peer-comparison and multi-trial mechanisms transfer to richer multi-turn interactive settings, whether adaptive allocation of cohort size N and trial count K can further reduce cost, and whether theoretical convergence guarantees exist for the merit-weighted adaptation rule.
