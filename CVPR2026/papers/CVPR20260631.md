## Problem

Existing video object removal methods rely heavily on input masks and fail to discover and erase object-induced side effects such as shadows, reflections, lighting changes, and deformation that extend beyond the masked region. This limitation is compounded by the absence of a large-scale, publicly available dataset that systematically captures these diverse effects across dynamic, multi-object video scenes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260631/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

For removal, the method takes a video clip (832×480, 81 frames) with the target object present plus a binary object mask, and outputs a temporally coherent video where the object and all its induced effects are erased. For insertion, it takes a background video and a foreground object (specified via mask), producing a composite video with the object naturally integrated along with realistic shadows, reflections, and lighting. The two modes are switched at inference by modifying a single task token, requiring no architectural change or retraining.

## Method

EffectErase is built on the Wan 2.1 DiT backbone fine-tuned with LoRA (rank 256) and jointly learns removal and insertion as inverse tasks sharing a common denoising path. A Task-Aware Region Guidance (TARG) module encodes a CLIP image embedding of the foreground object and a language-model task token, then injects them via cross-attention into every DiT block to model spatiotemporal correlations between the object and its effect regions. An Effect Consistency (EC) loss computes KL divergence between soft affected-region maps (derived from max-pooled cross-attention of both branches) and a normalized difference-map prior, forcing removal and insertion to localize the same effect areas. The full training objective combines denoising losses for both tasks with the EC term, and the model is trained on the newly introduced VOR dataset of 60K paired videos (145 hours, 366 object classes) spanning camera-captured and Blender-synthesized content.

## Results

EffectErase achieves state-of-the-art performance across all three evaluation benchmarks. On ROSE-Benchmark (with GT), it reaches PSNR 32.16, SSIM 0.948, LPIPS 0.039, and FVD 55.58, outperforming the previous best (ROSE) by 1.04 dB in PSNR and 16.6 in FVD. On VOR-Eval, it scores PSNR 23.75, SSIM 0.806, LPIPS 0.170, and FVD 342.87. On the in-the-wild VOR-Wild set (without GT), it attains the highest QScore of 9.28 and user rating of 7.20/10, surpassing ROSE (9.24 / 6.38).

## Baselines & Comparisons

EffectErase outperforms all seven baselines on every metric across all three benchmarks. The closest competitor is ROSE [19], the only other method explicitly modeling side effects; EffectErase improves over it by +1.04 dB PSNR and −16.6 FVD on ROSE-Benchmark, and by +0.78 dB PSNR and −40.2 FVD on VOR-Eval, while also leading on VOR-Wild user ratings (7.20 vs. 6.38). MinMax-Remover [40], which implicitly learns effects via minimax optimization, trails EffectErase by 5.4 dB PSNR on ROSE-Benchmark and 196.6 FVD on VOR-Eval. Video inpainting methods (ProPainter [39], DiffuEraser [15], VACE [11]) perform substantially worse, with VACE [11] being the weakest overall (PSNR 20.8 on ROSE, user score 1.50 on VOR-Wild), confirming that generic inpainting without effect-aware modeling fails to remove object-induced artifacts. Image-level methods (ObjectClear [38], OmniPaint [35]) applied per-frame show the largest FVD gaps (742–810 on ROSE vs. 55.6 for EffectErase), highlighting the importance of temporal modeling for video object removal.

## Relation to Prior Work

EffectErase builds on the Wan 2.1 rectified-flow video generation backbone and extends the video object removal line of work (ROSE, MinMax-Remover) by reframing removal and insertion as inverse tasks trained jointly on a shared denoising network. It departs from prior methods that either implicitly learn side effects (MinMax-Remover) or rely on binary difference masks (ROSE) by introducing a Task-Aware Region Guidance module that uses cross-attention to model spatiotemporal object–effect correlations, and a soft Effect Consistency loss that aligns attention maps across the two inverse tasks. The VOR dataset itself extends prior efforts (RORD, ROSE, SVOR) by combining camera-captured and 3D-synthesized pairs with dynamic cameras, dynamic objects, and multi-object interactions, covering five effect types that earlier datasets largely omitted.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 60K-pair VOR dataset (145 hours, 366 object classes, 5 effect types) and the two benchmarks (VOR-Eval with ground truth, VOR-Wild in-the-wild) fill a critical gap that has limited progress in effect-aware video object removal, giving the community a standardized training and evaluation resource. The joint removal–insertion training paradigm is a clean and reusable recipe: the same model handles both tasks by swapping a task token, and the Effect Consistency loss provides a general mechanism for enforcing cross-task geometric alignment. The main open limitation is the reliance on an input mask; the authors note that text- or speech-driven interaction is a natural next step, and whether the approach generalizes to longer videos or higher resolutions beyond the 832×480 / 81-frame training regime remains untested.
