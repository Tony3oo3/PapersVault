## Problem

3D Gaussian Splatting's monolithic alpha-blending formulation inherently conflates geometry and appearance across multiple radiance paths, making it fundamentally unable to model scene-scale transparency such as glass panels, windows, and display cases. Existing transparent-surface methods are either object-centric requiring segmentation masks or restricted to first-surface reconstruction, failing to recover transmitted radiance in complex multi-depth scenes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262109/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

GLINT takes multi-view RGB images with SfM point cloud initialization, plus frame-consistent depth, normal, and albedo priors extracted from a pre-trained video relighting model (DiffusionRenderer) encoder. The output is a decomposed 3D Gaussian scene with three explicit primitive sets (interface, transmission, reflection), from which novel views, per-pixel transparency maps, normal/depth maps, and TSDF-fused meshes can be extracted. Notably, transparency localization is bootstrapped during optimization from inter-component depth differences and albedo cues, eliminating the need for manual segmentation masks.

## Method

GLINT introduces a decomposed Gaussian representation that partitions primitives into interface (G_intr), transmission (G_trans), and reflection (G_refl) sets, resolving the transparency-depth dilemma inherent in monolithic alpha-blending. A hybrid rendering pipeline rasterizes the interface component via 2DGS to produce a G-buffer (depth, normal, transparency, specularity), which then guides OptiX-based ray-traced queries into the transmission and reflection sets for secondary paths. Outgoing radiance is computed through a BSDF-inspired, transparency-gated decomposition: an opaque branch blends diffuse base color with Fresnel-weighted specular reflection (Schlick approximation), while a transparent branch balances transmitted background radiance against reflected environment radiance under the optically thin assumption. Training combines photometric losses (L1, SSIM, LPIPS) with geometric regularization from the DiffusionRenderer encoder and a bootstrapped transparency supervision signal derived from thresholding inter-component depth differences and albedo values.

## Results

On the real-world DL3DV-10K dataset, GLINT achieves the best photometric quality with PSNR 30.21, SSIM 0.92, and LPIPS 0.11, outperforming the strongest baseline EnvGS (PSNR 29.65). On the synthetic 3D-FRONT-T benchmark, GLINT reaches PSNR 34.50, SSIM 0.96, and LPIPS 0.05, while also achieving the best geometric accuracy with Normal MAE 7.96°, Depth AbsRel 0.04, Chamfer Distance 0.34 dm, and F1-score 0.836. These results demonstrate consistent superiority in both appearance and geometry across all evaluated metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GLINT outperforms all five baselines on every metric across both datasets. Against the strongest general-purpose baseline EnvGS [32], GLINT gains +0.56 dB PSNR on DL3DV-10K and +0.79 dB on 3D-FRONT-T, while also achieving substantially better geometry (Normal MAE 7.96° vs. 14.37°, Depth AbsRel 0.04 vs. 0.13). The dedicated transparent-surface method TSGS [19] achieves the best geometry among baselines (Normal MAE 9.89°) but suffers severe photometric degradation (PSNR 25.94 on DL3DV-10K, 28.80 on 3D-FRONT-T), confirming its inability to recover transmitted radiance. Planar-constrained methods 2DGS [12] and PGSR [3] show moderate performance but lack any explicit transparency modeling, resulting in ghost-like reflections and missing glass geometry. Ref-GS [39] performs worst on geometry (Normal MAE 41.55°), indicating that directional factorization alone is insufficient for transparent surfaces. Overall, GLINT's decomposed representation consistently resolves the transparency-depth trade-off that limits all prior methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

GLINT builds directly on 2DGS for its base surface representation and on EnvGS's ray-tracing pipeline (BVH-accelerated triangle pairs) for secondary radiance queries, while borrowing geometric and material priors from DiffusionRenderer's video diffusion encoder. It departs from prior radiance decomposition methods (DeferredGS, 3DGS-DR, EnvGS) that model only opaque reflection, and from transparent-surface reconstruction methods (TSGS, TransparentGS) that are object-centric and require segmentation masks. The key conceptual departure is the explicit three-way partition of Gaussians into interface, transmission, and reflection sets, combined with a BSDF-inspired transparency-gated radiance transport that unifies rasterization and ray tracing in a single pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a fundamental limitation of the Gaussian splatting paradigm—the monolithic alpha-blending that forces a trade-off between visual plausibility and geometric accuracy for transparent surfaces—making it relevant to digital-twin construction, robotic manipulation, and scene understanding where accurate glass geometry is essential. The introduction of 3D-FRONT-T provides the first synthetic benchmark for scene-scale transparency, though its small scale (5 scenes) limits statistical confidence. Key open questions include extending the formulation to multi-bounce and nested transparency (currently limited to first-order, optically thin interactions), scaling to larger real-world scenes with complex refraction, and whether the decomposition generalizes to non-dielectric transparent materials.
