## Problem

Standard supervised training for deepfake detection applies uniform loss weights to all samples, which fails to prioritize the hard-but-learnable examples most critical for generalization. Existing curriculum learning approaches rely on static, pre-defined difficulty schedules that cannot adapt to the detector's evolving learning state, leaving a persistent gap in out-of-distribution robustness against unseen manipulation techniques.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261420/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The Student detector consumes face images or video frames (pre-processed per DeepfakeBench/DF40 protocols) and outputs a real/fake binary classification with a confidence score, evaluated at the video level via AUC. The Tutor agent, however, operates on a per-sample state vector that concatenates the Student's intermediate-layer deep features, current prediction confidence, a correctness one-hot indicator, the normalized EMA loss over past epochs, and a normalized forgetting-event count—producing a continuous scalar weight in [0,1] that modulates each sample's cross-entropy loss.

## Method

TSRL formalizes the training loop as a Markov Decision Process where a PPO-based Tutor agent learns a dynamic sample-weighting policy for a Student deepfake detector. At each step, the Tutor observes a history-aware state (deep features, confidence, correctness, EMA loss, forgetting count) and outputs a sigmoid-constrained weight that re-scales the sample's cross-entropy loss, effectively creating a real-time curriculum. The reward is a dense state-change signal that gives +1 for flipping an incorrect prediction to correct, −1 for the reverse, and a confidence-delta term for unchanged predictions, directly incentivizing immediate learning progress. Training proceeds in three phases: behavioral cloning from a heuristic expert policy for stable initialization, a Student warmup with uniform weights to accumulate meaningful state statistics, and full PPO optimization with clipped surrogate objectives. The framework is model-agnostic and was validated by plugging the Tutor into six diverse detector backbones (IID, CLIP, CORE, UCF, ProDet, Effort), yielding consistent AUC gains of 2–4% in cross-dataset and cross-method generalization.

## Results

On the cross-method evaluation (DF40), Effort+TSRL achieves the highest average AUC of 0.942, a +2.2% improvement over the Effort baseline (0.920). CORE+TSRL shows the largest relative cross-method gain (+4.1%, from 0.814 to 0.855), while CLIP+TSRL shows the largest relative cross-dataset gain (+3.6%, from 0.732 to 0.768). All six retrained baselines (IID, CLIP, CORE, UCF, ProDet, Effort) show consistent AUC improvements when augmented with TSRL, with gains ranging from +0.5% to +4.1% depending on the backbone and evaluation protocol.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TSRL consistently improves all six retrained baselines across both evaluation protocols. In cross-method evaluation, Effort+TSRL (0.942 avg AUC) surpasses the strongest SOTA baseline SLADD (0.865) by 7.7 points, and CORE+TSRL (0.855) outperforms RECCE (0.844) and LSDA (0.835). In cross-dataset evaluation, Effort+TSRL (0.903) edges out CDFA (0.901), the best non-TSRL method, while CLIP+TSRL achieves the largest relative gain (+3.6%) among all baselines. The gains are most pronounced for weaker backbones: CLIP improves from 0.653 to 0.674 in cross-method, and CORE jumps from 0.814 to 0.855, suggesting TSRL is particularly effective at rescuing underperforming architectures. Notably, TSRL does not degrade any baseline on any individual dataset, with the only exceptions being minor single-dataset drops (e.g., CORE+TSRL on e4s: 0.658→0.537) that are more than offset by large gains elsewhere.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the curriculum learning paradigm (Bengio et al., 2009) from static, pre-defined difficulty schedules to a fully dynamic, RL-learned policy for sample weighting during training. It departs from prior deepfake-specific curriculum approaches (CDFA [17], dynamic curriculum [28]) that rely on fixed heuristics or monotonic pacing, and from RL-for-augmentation work [21], by framing per-sample loss re-weighting as a sequential decision problem solved with PPO. The history-aware state design (EMA loss, forgetting counts) and state-change reward are novel components that distinguish this from generic RL curriculum methods, though the MDP formulation itself is standard.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The TSRL module offers a practical, plug-and-play training strategy that consistently improves cross-dataset and cross-method generalization across six diverse backbones, addressing the field's central generalization gap without architectural changes. However, the computational overhead of maintaining per-sample learning histories and running PPO updates at each epoch is non-trivial and not fully quantified. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger training sets, whether the RL training stabilizes across seeds, and whether the dynamic curriculum idea transfers to other detection or classification tasks beyond face-based deepfakes. The notable AUC drop on the e4s method for CORE+TSRL (0.658→0.537) hints at potential fragility on certain forgery types that warrants further investigation.
