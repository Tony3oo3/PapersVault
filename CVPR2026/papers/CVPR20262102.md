## Problem

Learning-based super-resolution for smartphone digital zoom requires paired LR-HR training data, but collecting real sensor-specific pairs is impractical. Existing synthetic data pipelines apply generic degradation pools (random Gaussian blurs, parametric noise) in the sRGB domain, creating a domain gap because real blur and noise occur earlier in the imaging pipeline where the sensor response is linear. No prior work has calibrated device-specific SR blur kernels and sensor noise models directly in the mosaicked RAW domain to close this gap.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262102/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The SR model takes a single mosaicked RAW image (single-channel Bayer CFA, low-resolution) as input and produces a 4× upscaled high-resolution sRGB image. During training, LR RAW images are synthesized by unprocessing HR linear RGB images: applying a calibrated per-channel blur kernel, subsampling at 4×, mosaicking according to the sensor's CFA pattern, and adding calibrated heteroscedastic Gaussian noise at a randomly sampled ISO level. The network internally converts the mosaicked input into 4-channel GBRG by stacking 2×2 pixel blocks, then upsamples before the final convolutional block to account for the 2× resolution reduction from this conversion.

## Method

The core contribution is a calibration-based degradation modeling framework that replaces generic degradation pools with device-specific profiles. For blur, they display random patterns on a color-calibrated monitor, capture them with the target camera, and jointly optimize per-patch, per-color-channel SR kernels and a homography alignment mapping via L1 regression in the mosaicked RAW domain—avoiding the common PSF-then-bicubic model and eliminating the need for heuristic kernel priors. For noise, they calibrate heteroscedastic Gaussian parameters (signal-dependent and signal-independent variance) per ISO level and per CFA channel using burst captures of a color-checker chart, then fit interpolation curves to handle uncalibrated ISO values. These calibrated degradations are used to unprocess HR sRGB images into LR mosaicked RAW training pairs, on which an RRDBNet architecture is trained with L1 loss for RAW-to-sRGB 4× super-resolution. The framework is validated both in a camera-specific setting (training and testing on the same device) and a cross-camera blind setting (training on seven cameras, testing on held-out devices), demonstrating that degradation profiles overlap across smartphones and that accurate modeling outperforms generic pools.

## Results

In the camera-specific setting (Table 1), the proposed method achieves the best MTF50 across all four tested cameras (1.50, 2.00, 1.72, 1.45), with the S24U Tele 2 result representing a 200% increase in the spatial frequency at which 50% contrast is reproduced. PSNR and SSIM are also best or near-best on all four cameras (e.g., 33.59 dB / 0.961 on S23U Tele 1). In the cross-camera blind SR setting (Table 2), the model trained on seven cameras generalizes to two unseen devices, achieving the highest MTF50 (1.19 on Pixel 6 Main, 0.42 on Mi 11 Main), PSNR (32.41, 36.11 dB), and SSIM (0.905, 0.919) over all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method consistently outperforms all six baselines on MTF50 (detail recovery) across every camera in both the camera-specific and cross-camera settings. In the camera-specific evaluation, KernelGAN and Degradation-Transfer are the closest competitors on MTF50 (e.g., 1.42 vs. 1.50 on S23U Tele 1), but the calibrated kernels yield a clear margin, especially on S24U Tele 2 where Ours reaches 2.00 versus 1.62 for KernelGAN. MANet shows high variance across cameras (MTF50 ranges from 0.29 to 1.66), reflecting the instability of per-patch kernel estimation, while Real-ESRGAN and bicubic downsampling remain the weakest baselines. In the cross-camera (blind SR) setting, BSRAW and RAWSR are the strongest competitors on MTF50 (0.97 and 0.96 on Pixel 6 Main), but Ours achieves 1.19, a ~22% improvement, and also leads on PSNR by 3.4 dB over BSRAW (32.41 vs. 28.79) and 3.4 dB over RAWSR (32.41 vs. 28.99), indicating substantially better structural fidelity. On Mi 11 Main, Ours leads PSNR by 2.46 dB over RAWSR (36.11 vs. 35.71) and 2.46 dB over Real-ESRGAN (36.11 vs. 35.08), while BSRAW and RAWSR show degraded MTF50 scores (0.34 and 0.14), suggesting poor generalization of their generic degradation pools to unseen sensors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant strategy in blind SR data synthesis—sampling from broad pools of parametric degradations (Gaussian kernels, Poisson-Gaussian noise) as in Real-ESRGAN, BSRAW, and RAWSR—by instead calibrating device-specific SR kernels and heteroscedastic Gaussian noise models directly from each camera. The SR kernel estimation via a display-based joint optimization of homography alignment and per-channel convolution matrices in the mosaicked RAW domain is a new calibration procedure not present in prior kernel-estimation work (KernelGAN, MANet, Degradation-Transfer), which either estimate kernels from natural images or from 1× lens PSF charts. The unprocessing pipeline (inverting ISP to synthesize RAW from sRGB) follows established work by Brooks et al., Nam et al., and Seo et al., but the paper's contribution is in making the degradation step physically accurate rather than generic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The calibrated kernel-and-noise dataset for eight modern smartphones is a practical resource that reduces the barrier to realistic RAW-domain SR training, and the cross-camera generalization result (trained on seven cameras, tested on two unseen ones, still outperforming baselines) suggests that smartphone degradation profiles share enough structure to enable a single model to serve multiple devices. However, the approach requires a specialized display-and-camera capture setup for calibration, is validated only under good lighting, and the PSNR/SSIM gains over strong baselines are modest (a few tenths of a dB), with the more dramatic improvements appearing in no-reference MTF metrics. Open questions include whether the methodology extends to low-light conditions, larger-sensor DSLR cameras, and whether the kernel-overlap assumption holds across more diverse sensor architectures.
