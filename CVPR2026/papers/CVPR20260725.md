## Problem

Prior diffusion-based approaches to hard combinatorial and geometric optimization problems operate in parametric or symbolic representations (indicator vectors, graph structures), requiring specialized architectures and domain-specific adaptations. This paper asks whether a standard visual diffusion model can instead solve such problems directly in pixel space, treating each geometric instance as an image generation task, and demonstrates this on three NP-hard or open geometric problems: the Inscribed Square Problem, the Euclidean Steiner Tree Problem, and the Maximum Area Polygonization Problem.

## Input / Output

For the Inscribed Square Problem, the input is a 128×128 binary image of a Jordan curve, and the output is a binary image of an inscribed square, refined by a rigid snapping step that maximizes corner-to-curve alignment. For the Steiner Tree and Maximum Area Polygon problems, the input is a rasterized point set image, and the output is a grayscale image that must be decoded into a symbolic structure (node/edge graph or polygon edge set) via thresholding, connected-component detection, and line-segment pixel-fraction tests. The conditioning image is concatenated as an additional channel to the noisy input at every denoising timestep, so the model sees the problem instance as a clean color channel throughout sampling.

## Method

The method uses a standard U-Net backbone with self-attention layers (analogous to Stable Diffusion) trained as a conditional denoiser with 100 steps, a standard noise schedule, and MSE loss. Training data are synthetic: for each problem, valid solutions are generated (curves with inscribed squares, SMTs via GeoSteiner, optimal polygons via exhaustive DFS) and rasterized into images, exploiting the observation that constructing solution examples is far easier than solving a specific instance. The same unmodified architecture is applied across all three problems, varying only the training data and the lightweight decoding step. At inference, DDIM sampling from different noise seeds yields diverse valid solutions, and a task-specific post-processing stage (rigid snapping for squares, node/edge extraction for trees, edge validation and cycle search for polygons) recovers the discrete geometric structure from the generated image.

## Results

On the Steiner Tree Problem, the model achieves a mean length ratio of 1.0008±0.0005 relative to the optimal solution for 10–20 input points (99.6% valid rate), and generalizes to 41–50 points with a ratio of 1.0092±0.0055 (33.4% valid rate), consistently outperforming MST (1.036–1.052) and random trees (1.86–1.90). On the Maximum Area Polygon Problem, the model reaches a mean area ratio of 0.988±0.020 for 7–12 points (95.3% valid, 57.4% exact-optimal rate) versus 0.771±0.136 for random polygons. For the Inscribed Square Problem, snapping post-processing improves alignment from −1.60 to −0.90 (GT: −0.14) while maintaining squareness at 0.891 (GT: 0.924).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against other learned diffusion or neural solvers; instead it benchmarks against two simple structural baselines. On the Steiner Tree Problem, the diffusion model's best-of-10 solution is within 0.08–0.92% of the optimal SMT length across all point counts, substantially beating the MST baseline (3.6–5.2% above optimal) and random planar trees (86–90% above optimal). On the Maximum Area Polygon Problem, the model achieves 98.8% of optimal area for 7–12 points (57.4% of the time recovering the exact optimum), far exceeding random simple polygons at 77.1%. The valid-rate drop for Steiner trees at 41–50 points (33.4%) and for polygons at 13–15 points (62.0%) reflects the increasing combinatorial difficulty, but whenever a valid solution is produced its quality remains very close to optimal. The Inscribed Square results are evaluated against ground-truth squares rather than a competing method, with snapping closing most of the alignment gap (−0.90 vs. GT −0.14).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior diffusion-based solvers for combinatorial optimization (DIFUSCO, T2T, Fast T2T), which operate in symbolic or graph-based parameter spaces, by formulating geometric problems entirely in pixel space with a standard U-Net diffusion model. It also differs from the closest pixel-space work (Graikos et al. on TSP, which uses stochastic optimization with a differential renderer, and a Sudoku solver using patch-level noise scheduling) by employing a straightforward conditional DDIM sampling pipeline with the problem image simply concatenated as an extra channel. The paper repurposes the standard text-to-image diffusion architecture (U-Net with self-attention, MSE loss, Gaussian noise schedule) without any task-specific architectural modifications, positioning itself as a demonstration that generative image models can double as geometric solvers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a general paradigm—pixel-space diffusion as a unified framework for approximating hard geometric problems—where the same unmodified architecture handles three structurally different NP-hard tasks, and inference time remains constant regardless of input size (unlike polynomial or exponential classical solvers). Key limitations include the pixel discretization ceiling, validity rates that drop sharply as problem complexity increases (e.g., 33% for 41–50-point Steiner trees), and the fact that specialized solvers remain superior for any single problem. Open questions include whether the approach extends to 3D (volumetric or video-based models), whether it scales to substantially larger instances, and whether the observed generalization beyond training distribution size holds more broadly.
