## Problem

Video relighting with background replacement requires simultaneously preserving temporal consistency, structural fidelity, and illumination naturalness, but existing methods fail to balance these goals. Training-based approaches are resource-intensive and produce limited lighting diversity, while training-free methods like AnyPortal and Light-A-Video introduce temporal flickering from per-frame relighting and structural misalignment from weak condition control. The paper identifies the root cause as the absence of a framework that systematically enforces Condition Consistency—ensuring output changes are driven solely by intended condition changes.

## Input / Output

The method takes a real-world video clip and a text prompt describing the target lighting and background as primary inputs. It additionally extracts a foreground mask (via BiRefNet + MatAnyone), structural conditions (HED, depth, and Canny maps), and relights the first frame with IC-Light to serve as a reference frame condition. The output is a temporally coherent relit video with a newly generated background, produced in the latent space of Wan2.1 over 55 denoising steps (50 generation + 5 residual reuse) on a single 80 GB A100 GPU in 3–5 minutes, supporting diverse resolutions.

## Method

FlowPortal is a training-free framework built on the Wan2.1 flow-matching model whose core mechanism is the Residual-Corrected Flow: it constructs a Consistency Residual Velocity V_t^res = V_0 − V_t^src (where V_0 is the ideal linear restoration path from noise to the source video) and adds it to the target-condition flow, yielding V_t^edit = V_t^tar + V_t^res, which provably guarantees perfect reconstruction when source and target conditions are identical. A Decoupled Condition Design separates illumination-specific inputs (IC-Light-relit reference frame, lighting-specific text prompts) from illumination-agnostic inputs (ControlNet structural conditions from depth/edge/Canny maps) to enforce directional change. A High-Frequency Transfer step injects source-video Fourier high-frequency components into the target latent at each denoising step to preserve fine texture, and a foreground mask restricts both the residual correction and frequency transfer to the subject region, leaving the background for unconstrained generation. The residual velocity is reused every r=10 steps, reducing total computation from 2T to (1+1/r)T steps while maintaining quality.

## Results

On a test set of 69 real-world video clips, FlowPortal achieves the best video-text alignment (CLIP-T 0.3271), temporal smoothness (CLIP-I 0.9828), motion consistency (0.8944), and detail consistency (41.20 PSNR), while matching the training-based Lumen on structural consistency (0.8804 vs. 0.8809) and identity consistency (0.7328 vs. 0.7392). In user studies with 24 participants, FlowPortal dominates all four preference criteria (User-Pmt 57.4, User-Tmp 58.4, User-Fg 52.2, User-Lit 54.2) over the next-best method Lumen (22.7, 28.1, 35.5, 24.4). Inference takes only 3–5 minutes per video on a single A100 GPU, roughly 5–10× faster than AnyPortal and Light-A-Video (20–30 min).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the training-free AnyPortal, FlowPortal wins on every metric: CLIP-T improves from 0.3196 to 0.3271, structural consistency from 0.8530 to 0.8804, and identity consistency from 0.4310 to 0.7328, while user preference scores are roughly 3–6× higher (e.g., User-Pmt 57.4 vs. 15.3). Against Light-A-Video(A), the gap is even wider, with FlowPortal leading on all objective metrics and user scores exceeding 10× (e.g., User-Tmp 58.4 vs. 3.7). Against the training-based Lumen, FlowPortal achieves comparable structural (0.8804 vs. 0.8809) and identity consistency (0.7328 vs. 0.7392) while significantly outperforming on video-text alignment (0.3271 vs. 0.3055), detail consistency (41.20 vs. 40.42), and all four user preference criteria by 20–35 points, indicating that Lumen's near-identity foreground comes at the cost of insufficient lighting change. FlowPortal also runs 5–10× faster than the other training-free baselines (3–5 min vs. 20–30 min per video).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is directly inspired by FlowEdit (Kulikov et al., ICCV 2025), which proposed inversion-free editing with flow models, and proves theoretical equivalence under a fixed-noise assumption while making two key modifications: using a single shared noise throughout the process (avoiding FlowEdit's multi-noise averaging that causes blur) and reformulating the edit as a generation-from-noise process rather than an edit-from-source process (enabling spatial masking for background replacement). It departs from prior training-free video relighting methods (AnyPortal, Light-A-Video) that apply IC-Light per-frame and rely on video diffusion to fix temporal inconsistencies, instead performing holistic video-level relighting. The Decoupled Condition Design (reference frame from IC-Light + ControlNet structural condition + illumination-specific text) is a novel engineering contribution that operationalizes the paper's central 'Condition Consistency' principle, which has no direct analogue in prior relighting work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Condition Consistency principle and Residual-Corrected Flow provide a general, training-free recipe for converting any flow-based video model into a controllable editing model, with the authors explicitly noting applicability to stylization, colorization, and object manipulation beyond relighting. The residual-reusing acceleration (recomputing the consistency residual every r steps) offers a practical efficiency gain that brings total inference to roughly 1.1× a single forward pass, making the approach accessible on consumer hardware. However, the method remains bounded by the quality of its two external components (IC-Light for the reference frame and Wan2.1 for generation), and the evaluation is limited to a single 69-clip test set on one GPU, leaving open questions about robustness to longer videos, more extreme lighting changes, and non-human subjects at scale.
