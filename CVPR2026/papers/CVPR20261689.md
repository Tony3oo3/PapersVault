## Problem

Open-vocabulary object detectors fail to localize camouflaged objects because (1) fine-grained textual descriptions from multimodal models contain redundant and decorative modifiers that introduce noise into cross-modal matching, and (2) the visual embeddings of camouflaged objects are nearly indistinguishable from their surrounding background, preventing the model from learning a clear decision boundary. Additionally, no dedicated benchmark exists for evaluating open-vocabulary detection specifically on camouflaged targets.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an RGB image alongside a set of K fine-grained text sub-descriptions per category (e.g., color, texture, shape attributes generated by Qwen3-VL-Plus), rather than a single category name. These sub-descriptions are first decorrelated via SVD before entering the text encoder. The output consists of standard axis-aligned bounding boxes with class labels, evaluated under both open-set (trained on 40 base classes, tested on 40 base + 47 novel classes) and closed-set (all 87 classes) protocols on the OVCOD-D benchmark.

## Method

SDDF builds on a YOLOv8 backbone with PAN and a YOLO-World-style embedding head, adding three key mechanisms. First, a sub-description principal component contrastive fusion module applies SVD to decorrelate noisy text sub-descriptions, then computes importance weights as the difference between each object embedding's cosine similarity to a sub-description and the global average embedding's similarity, producing a softmax-weighted fused text representation that emphasizes class-specific semantics. Second, a specificity-guided regional weak alignment loss (coverage-based) encourages the predicted box of the most text-similar region to spatially overlap the ground-truth box, tightening the link between fine-grained semantics and localization. Third, an SF-GLU (Spatially Focused Gated Linear Unit) layer generates a spatial gating signal by combining per-patch text-region similarity scores with L2 distance to the highest-scoring patch, multiplicatively enhancing visual features in the object domain while preserving background features via a 1+σ(·) offset. Training uses transfer learning from O365/GoldG pre-trained weights with contrastive alignment between visual and textual embeddings.

## Results

Under the open-set evaluation setting on OVCOD-D, SDDF-L achieves an AP of 56.4 (AP50: 76.4, AP75: 60.7), outperforming the strongest baseline DOSOD-L (AP 53.4) by 3.0 points. The medium variant SDDF-M reaches AP 54.3, surpassing DOSOD-M (51.8) by 2.5 AP. Under the closed-set setting, SDDF-L further improves to AP 59.4 (AP75: 63.4), a 5.8-point gain over DOSOD-L, while maintaining real-time inference at 33 FPS on an RTX 4090.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SDDF consistently outperforms all baselines across model scales. Against the strongest competitor DOSOD [13], SDDF gains +3.3 AP at the small scale (48.1 vs 44.8), +2.5 AP at medium (54.3 vs 51.8), and +3.0 AP at large (56.4 vs 53.4) in the open-set setting; the gap widens to +5.8 AP at large scale in the closed-set setting (59.4 vs 53.6). Transformer-based grounding models (GLIP-T, GLIPv2-T, Grounding DINO-T) perform substantially worse (AP 22.6–34.8), which the authors attribute to BERT tokenization fragmenting dense multi-attribute sub-descriptions. YOLO-World [2] and YOLOE [33] trail SDDF by 8–18 AP points, confirming that the specificity-guided fusion and SF-GLU modules provide meaningful gains over standard open-vocabulary YOLO pipelines. YOLOv8 trained from scratch [32] achieves only 18.6–23.7 AP, underscoring the importance of large-scale pre-training for camouflaged object detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

SDDF builds directly on the YOLO-World and DOSOD architectures (both YOLOv8-based open-vocabulary detectors) and extends them with three specificity-driven modules: SVD-based decorrelation of text sub-descriptions, a contrastive fusion mechanism that weights sub-descriptions by their discriminative power relative to background, and an SF-GLU spatial gating layer. Conceptually, the paper carves out the intersection of two previously separate research threads—open-vocabulary object detection (GLIP, Grounding DINO, YOLO-World) and camouflaged object detection (COD10K, NC4K, CAMO)—arguing that neither community has addressed the other's core challenge: OVOD methods lack fine-grained visual discrimination under camouflage, while COD methods lack open-vocabulary text grounding. The coverage-based weak alignment loss and the use of Qwen3-VL-Plus-generated fine-grained sub-descriptions represent a departure from the single-category-label prompting convention used by all prior OVOD baselines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The OVCOD-D benchmark is the first to provide detection-ready annotations and fine-grained textual prompts for camouflaged objects in an open-vocabulary setting, filling a clear gap that the authors document through the substantial AP drop of YOLO-World on camouflage versus LVIS. The finding that token-level grounding models (GLIP, Grounding DINO) degrade sharply on dense multi-attribute sub-descriptions—attributed to BERT tokenization fragmenting semantic concepts—raises an open question about how to interface rich textual prompts with cross-modal attention architectures beyond YOLO-style detectors. Limitations include the small dataset scale (87 classes), the reliance on a single VLM for description generation, and the fact that 'novel' classes are still drawn from a fixed pool rather than truly open-world categories, so the open-vocabulary claim is weaker than in LVIS-style evaluations. Future work could explore whether the SF-GLU and contrastive fusion ideas transfer to segmentation-based COD or to other visually challenging domains (occlusion, low contrast).
