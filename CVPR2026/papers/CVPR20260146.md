## Problem

Existing controllable video generation methods provide geometric foundations (bounding boxes, camera poses, object tracks) but largely ignore physically-accurate lighting, producing mismatched shadows, reflections, and ambient occlusion in realistic materials. This paper addresses the gap by explicitly disentangling layout, lighting, and camera trajectory as independent, physically grounded control signals for video synthesis.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt and a 9-channel scene proxy (a stack of three 2D render passes—pure diffuse, glossy GGX, and rough GGX—each 3-channel RGB, rendered at 720×1280 for 81 frames) as conditioning, along with a camera trajectory and an HDR environment map. The scene proxy is produced by a renderer-based agent that parses the text into a 3D scene graph, retrieves mesh assets, selects or generates an HDR map, and plans a camera path, then renders the passes via Blender. The output is an 81-frame photorealistic video at 704×1280 resolution with physically consistent lighting, faithful scene layout, and aligned camera motion.

## Method

LiVER builds on the Wan2.2-5B video diffusion backbone (flow-matching formulation) and introduces a lightweight 2D convolutional proxy encoder that maps the 9-channel render-pass stack to compact spatial features at 1/16 resolution, which are injected into the video latent space as a learnable spatial residual (z' = z + α·z^y, with α initialized to zero for stable warm-up). A renderer-based agent decomposes the user's text into a structured scene graph, retrieves PBR mesh assets from Objaverse-XL, selects an HDR environment map from Poly Haven, and plans a spline-interpolated camera trajectory, then renders the conditioning proxy through Blender. Training follows a three-stage schedule: (1) freeze the backbone and train only the proxy encoder and adapter for 10 epochs, (2) jointly fine-tune LoRA layers on the backbone together with the encoder/adapter for 10 epochs, and (3) continue joint LoRA fine-tuning with a 1:1 mix of real and synthetic data to expand lighting diversity. The synthetic subset (LiVER-Syn) is rendered with dynamically rotating HDR maps (180°–240° yaw) to provide the diverse illumination variability absent from real footage, which ablations confirm is essential for correct lighting control.

## Results

On the LiVER-Real test set, LiVER achieves the best scores across all metrics: FVD 32.45 (16-frame) / 32.56 (81-frame), FID 42.32 / 129.56, CLIP 29.62 / 30.97, ATE 1.30 / 2.48, RPEt 0.81 / 0.71, RPEr 1.16° / 0.50°, lighting error (LE) 0.05 / 0.04, lighting instability (LI) 0.02, and mIoU 0.86 / 0.87. In the user study, LiVER is preferred by 83.4% of participants for video quality, 83.3% for scene control, 72.1% for camera trajectory control, and 59.3% for lighting control, substantially outperforming all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LiVER outperforms all three baselines on every reported metric. Against CameraCtrl (16-frame comparison), LiVER reduces FVD from 48.03 to 32.45 (−32%), FID from 98.29 to 42.32 (−57%), and mIoU improves from 0.68 to 0.86, while also halving the camera pose error (ATE 1.30 vs. 2.15). Against MotionCtrl, the gap is even larger: FVD drops from 63.13 to 32.45, and the per-step rotation error (RPEr) falls from 7.32° to 1.16°, indicating far more stable camera trajectory adherence. In the 81-frame comparison against VideoFrom3D, LiVER achieves a lower FVD (32.56 vs. 36.94) and dramatically better FID (129.56 vs. 157.89), while VideoFrom3D's ATE of 17.55 is roughly 7× worse than LiVER's 2.48, reflecting VideoFrom3D's weaker camera consistency. The user study confirms these quantitative advantages, with LiVER preferred by 83.4% of participants for video quality versus 8.7% for VideoFrom3D and 4.3% for CameraCtrl.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the 3D-grounded video generation line (CameraCtrl, MotionCtrl, VideoFrom3D, CineMaster) by adding physically-accurate lighting as a first-class, disentangled control signal, which prior geometric-conditioning methods largely ignore. It also departs from the relighting literature (DiLightNet, GenLit, Light-A-Video, LumiSculpt) by rendering lighting cues from an explicit 3D scene proxy via PBR passes (diffuse, GGX) rather than learning lighting edits in a data-driven, entangled manner. The renderer-based agent that translates text into a structured 3D scene graph and camera trajectory is a novel orchestration layer not present in prior controllable video generation pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LiVER provides a practical bridge between high-level text descriptions and physically consistent video output, with the renderer-based agent lowering the barrier to 3D scene authoring and the editable proxy enabling a hybrid CGI workflow. The LiVERSet dataset (11K annotated videos with geometry, HDR maps, and camera poses) is a useful resource for the community. Key limitations include the coarse initial 3D reconstruction (making output quality prompt-sensitive) and the dependency on a 3D engine (Blender) in the generation loop, which constrains real-time applicability. Open questions include whether the PBR-pass conditioning generalizes to more complex multi-object dynamic scenes and whether the agent can handle more nuanced or contradictory lighting descriptions.
