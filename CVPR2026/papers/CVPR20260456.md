## Problem

Existing test-time hallucination mitigation methods for LVLMs predominantly target language-induced hallucinations (e.g., via textually perturbed inputs), leaving vision-induced hallucinations comparatively underexplored. This paper asks whether hallucination directions rooted specifically in the visual modality can be identified and suppressed at inference time without retraining or architectural changes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260456/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, CIPHER takes a standard RGB image and a text instruction (e.g., 'describe this image in detail') and produces a generated caption or VQA answer, operating within a single forward pass with no additional decoding overhead. The offline phase consumes 5,000 MSCOCO image-caption pairs, perturbing captions via GPT-3.5 and images via Stable Diffusion v1.5 (partial forward diffusion to t_h = 0.5T, reverse denoising conditioned on the hallucinated caption, classifier-free guidance scale 7.5) to produce 25,000 counterfactual images. The projection is applied at upper transformer layers (16–32) on mean-pooled caption-token hidden states before each autoregressive decoding step.

## Method

CIPHER operates in two phases. In the offline phase, it constructs OHC-25K by pairing diffusion-edited counterfactual images (structurally faithful but semantically altered) with their original ground-truth captions, then extracts hidden-state differences between clean and counterfactual representations across all samples and layers; SVD on the stacked difference matrix yields a low-rank hallucination subspace (top r right-singular vectors, r chosen via grid search: 8 for LLaVA, 64 for MiniGPT-4, 32 for mPLUG-Owl2). At inference, CIPHER projects each intermediate hidden state onto the orthogonal complement of this subspace via h_clean = (I − V_r V_r^T) h_test, nullifying hallucination-aligned components while preserving core semantics. The key insight is that visual counterfactuals produce stronger, more layer-stable representation shifts than textual perturbations (confirmed by linear probing: 0.86–0.89 F1 vs. 0.74–0.78), making the resulting subspace more discriminative for hallucination suppression.

## Results

On the CHAIR benchmark, CIPHER achieves the lowest hallucination rates across all three LVLMs: CHAIR_S of 13.05% (LLaVA-1.5), 18.48% (MiniGPT-4), and 13.60% (mPLUG-Owl2), outperforming the strongest baseline Nullu by 2.15%, 2.92%, and 2.00% respectively. CIPHER also attains the best CHAIR_I scores (4.53%, 8.33%, 4.92%) while maintaining or improving BLEU scores (15.82, 15.10, 16.25), confirming that hallucination suppression does not sacrifice generation quality. On OPOPE, CIPHER achieves the highest accuracy, precision, and F1 across all three models (e.g., 80.05% accuracy and 92.11% F1 on LLaVA-1.5). CIPHER matches the throughput of standard greedy decoding (0.70 items/s), whereas decoding-based methods like OPERA and HALC drop to 0.10 and 0.05 items/s.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CIPHER outperforms all seven baselines on every CHAIR metric across all three LVLMs. The closest competitor is Nullu (CVPR'25), which also uses feature-level projection but targets text-induced hallucinations; CIPHER improves CHAIR_S by 2.15% on LLaVA-1.5 (13.05 vs. 15.20), 2.92% on MiniGPT-4 (18.48 vs. 21.40), and 2.00% on mPLUG-Owl2 (13.60 vs. 15.60). Decoding-based methods (DoLa, OPERA, VCD, HALC) show modest or inconsistent gains over the greedy baseline and incur 3–14× higher inference latency (0.05–0.42 items/s vs. CIPHER's 0.70 items/s). Post-processing methods Woodpecker and LURE achieve competitive CHAIR_I on MiniGPT-4 but degrade CHAIR_S on LLaVA-1.5 (23.85% and 19.48% respectively vs. CIPHER's 13.05%) and rely on external models for detection and rewriting. On OPOPE, CIPHER achieves the best F1 across all models (92.11%, 92.58%, 90.95%), surpassing Nullu by 0.32%, 0.51%, and 0.15% respectively, confirming consistent gains in object-level hallucination detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

CIPHER directly extends the feature-level intervention paradigm introduced by Nullu (CVPR'25), which extracts hallucination directions by contrasting hidden states from textually perturbed inputs. The key departure is shifting the perturbation from the text modality to the visual modality: CIPHER uses Stable Diffusion to generate counterfactual images whose semantics contradict the ground-truth caption, then applies the same SVD-based subspace extraction and orthogonal projection at inference. This positions CIPHER within the broader test-time hallucination mitigation literature (DoLa, VCD, OPERA, HALC) but distinguishes itself by targeting vision-induced hallucinations specifically, a gap the authors identify as underexplored relative to language-induced ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is strong: CIPHER is training-free, requires no architectural changes, and matches greedy decoding throughput (0.70 items/s), making it immediately deployable alongside existing LVLMs. The OHC-25K counterfactual dataset and the finding that visual perturbations produce more structured, layer-stable representation shifts than textual ones (linear probing accuracy 0.86–0.89 vs. 0.73–0.80) provide a useful empirical foundation for future work on modality-specific hallucination. A key limitation is the fixed, offline-estimated subspace, which the authors acknowledge may not adapt to input-specific hallucination patterns; whether dynamic or per-input projection strategies can further improve suppression remains an open question. Generalization beyond the three evaluated LVLMs (LLaVA-1.5, MiniGPT-4, mPLUG-Owl2) and to larger-scale models is also untested.
