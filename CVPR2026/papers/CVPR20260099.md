## Problem

Deformable medical image registration (DMIR) processes two images simultaneously, causing feature combination relationships to grow exponentially with image resolution — a combinatorial explosion that forces models to consider many irrelevant feature pairings. Existing registration networks rely on static receptive fields and static weights, which fix the search space shape and search direction, preventing adaptive focus on truly relevant feature relationships. No prior method has successfully made both the receptive fields and the weights dynamic in a unified manner.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pair of fixed and moving medical images (3D cardiac CT at 144×144×128, 3D brain MRI at 160×160×128, or 2D brain MRI at 160×160) and produces a deformation field that warps the moving image to align with the fixed image. Registration is performed bidirectionally: deformation fields are estimated in both a→b and b→a directions, and a symmetric loss enforces consistency between the two mappings. The network is trained unsupervised using only intensity-based similarity and smoothness losses, requiring no ground-truth displacement fields.

## Method

DySNet stacks Dynamic Stream Blocks (DSB), each combining two modules that jointly decompose a convolution kernel into a receptive field and spatial/channel weights, then introduce dynamics to each. The Adaptive Stream Basin (AdSB) module predicts per-pixel offsets via a small network on concatenated fixed/moving features, deforming the static receptive field into a data-dependent shape that focuses on correlated regions and reduces interfering features. The Dynamic Stream Attention (DySA) module computes softmax-normalized dot-product attention scores between the query at each position and the deformed keys within the dynamic receptive field, yielding input-dependent spatial weights that adapt the search direction. The resulting dynamic kernel aggregates deformed value features to produce attention outputs, and the full network is instantiated within Xmorpher (DySNet-X) and ModeT (DySNet-M) architectures, trained with a bidirectional registration loss combining local normalized cross-correlation and deformation smoothness regularization.

## Results

DySNet-M achieves the highest average DSC of 82.0% across three registration tasks (3D Cardiac CT, 3D Brain MRI, 2D Brain MRI), with per-task DSCs of 84.1%, 79.7%, and 82.2% respectively. DySNet-X achieves 78.4% average DSC (74.5%, 77.8%, 83.0%). Both variants maintain low Jacobian negative volume (≤0.98%), indicating topology-preserving deformations, while the strongest baselines (VoxelMorph, XMorph) achieve 77.2% and 73.4% average DSC respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DySNet-M outperforms its base architecture ModeT [38] on all three tasks (+0.5% on 3D Cardiac, +2.3% on 3D Brain, +0.3% on 2D Brain), and DySNet-X improves over XMorpher [33] by +2.1%, +6.6%, and +6.5% respectively, with the largest gains on fine-grained brain structures. Against the strongest CNN baseline VoxelMorph [2], DySNet-M leads by 7.1% on 3D Cardiac CT and 3.8% on 3D Brain MRI. DySNet-M slightly trails SACB [7] on 2D Brain MRI (82.2% vs 82.5%) and TransMorph [5] (82.2% vs 82.3%), but dominates on both 3D tasks. Swin-VM [47] fails on 3D tasks (marked invalid) and ties DySNet-M only on 2D Brain. LKU-Net [19] and ViT-V-Net [4] are consistently outperformed by 10+ DSC points across all tasks, confirming that dynamic receptive fields and attention provide substantial gains over static convolutional or patch-based attention mechanisms.

## Relation to Prior Work

DySNet unifies two previously separate lines of dynamic feature modeling—deformable convolution/attention (which adjusts receptive field geometry) and attention-based weight computation (which adjusts aggregation direction)—into a single framework for deformable medical image registration. Prior registration networks (VoxelMorph, Xmorpher, ModeT, Swin-VM) rely on static receptive fields and static post-training weights, while earlier dynamic approaches like deformable convolution, DAT, and SACB-Net introduced dynamics in only one of these two components. The paper's kernel-decomposition formulation (Eq. 3) provides a clean algebraic bridge between convolution and attention, positioning AdSB and DySA as complementary halves of a unified dynamic kernel rather than independent modules.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that jointly adapting both the search space (receptive field shape) and search direction (attention weights) yields consistent gains across three anatomical scales and two base architectures (Xmorpher, ModeT), the paper offers a modular drop-in block for DMIR practitioners seeking to improve fine-structure alignment without redesigning the full network. The 'combinatorial explosion' framing, while conceptually useful, remains largely qualitative—the exponential growth argument (Eq. 1) is illustrative rather than empirically validated, and the actual DSC improvements (0.3–6.6 percentage points) are moderate. Open questions include whether the dynamic mechanism scales to higher-resolution 3D volumes, whether the offset prediction network adds meaningful latency in clinical deployment, and whether the gains persist on more challenging inter-subject or cross-modality registration tasks.
