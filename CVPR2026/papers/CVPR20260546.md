## Problem

Existing interaction datasets are split along a fundamental axis: human-centric video benchmarks (Ego4D, EpicKitchens) capture long-horizon activities but provide no force or tactile signals, while robotics datasets (RH20T, DROID) focus on short-horizon primitives and rarely cover articulated furniture. For articulated objects specifically, no public dataset couples multi-view visual observations with end-effector forces and tactile sensing across both human and robotic embodiments performing the same interactions, making it impossible to study how visual appearance relates to physical force patterns or how human-demonstrated skills transfer to robot morphologies.

## Input / Output

The dataset provides 3048 time-aligned sequences where each frame carries synchronized RGB-D (from Project Aria egocentric and two iPhone 13 Pro exocentric cameras), 6-DoF force-torque readings, GelSight Digit tactile images, hand poses, eye gaze, and SLAM trajectories, all spatially registered to Leica RTC360 laser-scanned point clouds. For the three benchmark tasks, inputs range from a single RGB image (GPT-5 articulation type) or egocentric video (ArtiPoint/ArtGS) to tactile sensor images (Sparsh force estimation) to RGB-D plus a text goal like 'open the drawer' (ForceSight). Outputs include articulation type and 3D axis, per-axis normal/tangential force magnitudes in Newtons, and a 3D interaction force vector, with ground truth derived from the force-torque sensor, motor torque-current calibration, and manual annotation.

## Method

The core contribution is a data capture pipeline centered on a custom open-source Hoi! gripper: a 2-finger antipodal parallel gripper (inspired by ALOHA) with two GelSight Digit tactile sensors, a Bota SensONE 6-DoF force-torque sensor at the wrist, a Dynamixel XM430-W350-T motor, and a calibrated load cell in the handle that translates operator grip into controlled gripping force. Each of 381 articulated objects across 38 real-world environments is operated under four embodiments (bare hand, hand + wrist camera, UMI gripper, Hoi! gripper), with a small subset also recorded on a teleoperated Spot robot. Temporal alignment is achieved via QR codes encoding Unix timestamps at 25 Hz displayed to all camera streams (yielding ~10–25 ms accuracy), while spatial alignment uses visual localization (hloc) against the Leica point clouds to register all device trajectories into a shared world frame. The evaluation benchmarks three tasks—articulation estimation, tactile force estimation, and visual force estimation—by applying existing methods (ArtiPoint, ArtGS, GPT-5, Sparsh with DINO/DINOv2, ForceSight) in zero-shot settings, revealing significant performance degradation in the wild compared to their original benchmarks.

## Results

On the Hoi! dataset, existing articulation estimation methods degrade substantially: ArtiPoint drops from 98% revolute recall on Arti4D to 57.1% on Hoi!, while ArtGS achieves 0% revolute recall on both datasets. For tactile force estimation, Sparsh (DINO/DINOv2) yields combined force RMSE of 3.86 N / 4.11 N on Hoi!, orders of magnitude worse than its millinewton-level accuracy on the original benchmark. For visual force estimation, ForceSight degrades from 0.40 N RMSE on its original dataset to 2.23 N (projected) / 2.57 N (raw) on Hoi!, with the worst per-location error of 3.53 N in kitchen_7 (fridge/oven). GPT-5, evaluated zero-shot, is surprisingly robust for articulation type prediction (89.7% revolute recall on Hoi!), outperforming both ArtGS and ArtiPoint on this sub-task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ArtGS [Liu et al., 2025] fails catastrophically on Hoi! with 0% revolute recall and 49–58° axis angle errors, attributed to non-robust object segmentation in cluttered in-the-wild scenes; it performs identically poorly on Arti4D. ArtiPoint [Werby et al., 2025] drops from 98% revolute recall on Arti4D to 57.1% on Hoi!, with axis error nearly tripling (17°→64°), because scaled monocular depth (via MapAnything) introduces stochastic noise that breaks its 3D lifting and trajectory filtering. Sparsh [Higuera et al., CoRL 2024] degrades from millinewton-level accuracy on its controlled benchmark to 3.86–4.11 N combined force RMSE on Hoi!, due to out-of-distribution contact geometries (real handles, edges, furniture parts vs. simple indenters) and uncontrolled load regimes. ForceSight [Collins et al., 2023] shows a 5–6× increase in RMSE (0.40 N → 2.23–2.57 N) when transferred zero-shot to Hoi!, struggling most with stiff, force-demanding mechanisms (magnetic drawers, fridges) that are underrepresented in existing datasets. Overall, the paper demonstrates that all four baselines lose significant performance on Hoi! relative to their original benchmarks, validating the dataset as a challenging and underexplored testbed for force-grounded manipulation research.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of articulation understanding (ArtiPoint, ArtGS, Ditto), egocentric video datasets (Ego4D, EgoExo4D), and force-centric robotics data (ForceMimic, RH20T), none of which jointly provide force/tactile signals, multi-view coverage, and aligned human-robot embodiments for the same articulated objects. The Hoi! gripper repurposes the UMI handheld concept and ALOHA antipodal mechanism, augmenting them with GelSight Digit tactile sensors and a Bota SensONE force-torque sensor to close the sensing gap. Conceptually, the paper departs from the prevailing split between 'what is seen' (video understanding) and 'what is felt' (robotics force control) by treating them as a single unified observation space across embodiments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset fills a concrete gap: no prior public resource couples articulated manipulation with end-effector forces, tactile contact, and multi-embodiment alignment in realistic indoor scenes, making cross-embodiment transfer and force-from-vision research previously intractable at scale. The evaluations reveal that methods achieving strong results on controlled benchmarks (ArtiPoint, Sparsh, ForceSight) degrade substantially in-the-wild, suggesting that current articulation and force models are overfit to clean, simulated, or tabletop conditions. Key open questions include whether the human-operated gripper's hybrid embodiment truly captures robot kinematic constraints, whether the limited mechanical complexity (no rare edge-case mechanisms) limits generalization, and how to move from these perception benchmarks toward end-to-end policy learning that jointly optimizes perception and action.
