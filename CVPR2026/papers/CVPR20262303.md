## Problem

Existing benchmarks for VLM physical understanding are predominantly qualitative, using multiple-choice VQA that cannot distinguish a 3.1 m answer from a 31 m answer when the ground truth is 3 m. No prior benchmark quantitatively measures whether a VLM can infer kinematic quantities (size, velocity, acceleration) in real-world units from video observations, leaving it unclear whether state-of-the-art models perform genuine numerical physical reasoning or merely produce plausible-sounding guesses.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262303/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question pairs a short video clip (typically 2–3 seconds, ~120 MB total for the full benchmark) with a single physical prior (object size in meters, velocity in m/s, or acceleration in m/s² at a given timestamp) delivered as a textual prompt. The model must output a single numerical value with unit for a target kinematic property in world space, scored via Mean Relative Accuracy (MRA) averaged over ten tolerance thresholds from 10% to 95% relative error. The 3,391 questions span four task categories defined by dimensionality (2D vs. 3D motion) and prior type (static size vs. dynamic velocity/acceleration), with each video paired to multiple (prior, question, ground-truth) triplets.

## Method

QUANTIPHY exploits the fact that all translational kinematic quantities in a fixed-camera video are linked by a single unknown scale factor γ (world length per pixel), so one world-space prior suffices to determine any other property by rescaling its pixel-space counterpart. The benchmark is constructed from three data sources—Blender simulations with automated ground-truth extraction, lab captures annotated via 4D multi-view stereo reconstruction, and curated internet videos with manual pixel-space annotation and reference-object calibration—yielding 560 videos across scales from microscopic to astronomical. Beyond the main evaluation, the paper introduces three diagnostic probes on a 161-pair subset: a prior-only condition (video removed), a counterfactual condition (prior scaled by factors from 0.001× to 700×), and a four-step chain-of-thought decomposition (pixel measurement → scale estimation → target pixel property → world-space answer). These probes reveal that VLMs score comparably with or without the video and fail to track counterfactual prior changes, indicating they rely on memorized world knowledge rather than faithfully conditioning on the provided visual and numerical inputs.

## Results

On QUANTIPHY, the best proprietary model ChatGPT-5.1 achieves 53.1% Mean Relative Accuracy (MRA) overall, followed by Gemini-2.5 Pro (49.6%) and Gemini-2.5 Flash (48.6%). The human baseline scores 55.6% MRA, meaning no VLM surpasses human performance despite having pixel-level access to video frames. Among open-weight models, Qwen3-VL-Instruct-32B leads at 46.0% MRA, while smaller models (Fuyu-8B, LLaVA-13B) drop to 12.5–15.2%, revealing a steep performance cliff below ~8B parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ChatGPT-5.1 (53.1% MRA) outperforms all other VLMs but still falls 2.5 points below the human baseline (55.6%), and it slightly exceeds humans only on 2D-Dynamic (56.2 vs. 59.1 is actually below; the paper notes it 'slightly surpasses' on 2D-Dynamic). Gemini-2.5 Pro (49.6%) and Flash (48.6%) are competitive with the best open-weight model Qwen3-VL-Instruct-32B (46.0%), with the proprietary edge being roughly 3–4 MRA points. Within the Qwen3-VL family, scaling from 2B (29.0) to 8B (38.8) to 32B (46.0) yields consistent gains, with the largest improvements on dynamic categories (2D-Dynamic: 39.0→47.8→51.6). Notably, Claude Sonnet 4.5 (22.8%) and ChatGPT-5 (32.6%) trail ChatGPT-5.1 by 20+ and 20+ points respectively, indicating significant variance even among top proprietary systems. The weakest models (Fuyu-8B at 12.5%, LLaVA-13B at 15.2%) score near chance level, and MiniCPM-V 4.5 collapses to 0.0–0.4% on 3D tasks, suggesting that 3D kinematic inference remains largely unsolved for smaller architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant VQA-based paradigm in physical reasoning benchmarks (PhysBench, CLEVRER, Physion++) by introducing a quantitative, numerically-scored evaluation of kinematic inference. It builds directly on VSI-Bench's Mean Relative Accuracy metric and its focus on spatial understanding, but extends the setting from static objects to moving objects in video and from perceptual estimation to multi-step kinematic reasoning (size → velocity → acceleration) conditioned on a single physical prior. The scale-resolution formulation (pixel-to-world mapping via one known quantity) draws on classical computer vision principles from optical flow and multi-view stereo, repurposing them as a structured reasoning task for VLMs rather than a pipeline of specialized detectors and trackers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QUANTIPHY provides the first rigorous, scalable testbed for measuring whether VLMs can perform numerically grounded physical reasoning, a capability essential for embodied AI, autonomous driving, and AR/VR. The counterfactual and prior-only analyses reveal a fundamental input-faithfulness gap: state-of-the-art models behave as approximate guessers conditioned on textual hints rather than precise visual measurers, undermining their reliability for safety-critical deployment. Key limitations (translational motion only, fixed camera, rigid objects, no multi-body interaction) leave open whether the findings generalize to richer physical scenarios, and whether physics-informed training objectives or larger-scale pretraining can close the gap to human-level quantitative accuracy.
