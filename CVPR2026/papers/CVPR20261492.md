## Problem

Current text-to-video models suffer from unrealistic dynamics, temporal inconsistency, and unstable semantic alignment, and existing preference alignment approaches (RLHF with reward models or DPO with human/VLM-annotated pairs) are bottlenecked by the high cost and inherent ambiguity of video preference labeling. This paper asks whether preference supervision for video generation can be constructed entirely without human annotation or auxiliary reward models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261492/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a text prompt and a partial reference video (the first N₁ or N₂ latent frames of a ground-truth clip) as conditioning and produces a full 49-frame video at 288×512 resolution. During training, the continuation model receives a binary frame mask distinguishing conditioned frames (zero timestep, clean latent) from target frames (noised latent), along with learnable frame-level task-prompt embeddings that signal which frames to generate versus preserve.

## Method

The core insight is that video continuation inherently induces a preference ordering: for a fixed total length, a continuation conditioned on more reference frames contains less generated content and is therefore higher-fidelity than one conditioned on fewer frames, yielding annotation-free preference pairs. The authors extend a pretrained DiT-based rectified-flow model into a latent-space continuation model via per-frame timestep masking and learnable frame-level task prompts, trained with LoRA adapters while freezing the backbone. They then propose Asymmetrical DPO, which partitions the two continuation samples into a shared prefix, an asymmetric region, and a fully generated region, and computes the Flow-DPO loss only over the latter two regions (where the samples actually differ), normalized by continuation length to prevent gradient imbalance. This concentrates alignment signals precisely where preference divergence arises, eliminating the need for human labels, VLM scoring, or explicit reward models.

## Results

On VBench, DynamicsBoost achieves the best scores on Aesthetic Quality (59.92), Motion Smoothness (99.21), Dynamic Degree (44.92), and Overall Consistency (25.64), outperforming the strongest DPO baseline (Flow-DenseDPO) by up to +4.82 on Dynamic Degree. On PhysGenBench, it leads in Aesthetic Quality (48.21), Imaging Quality (55.14), Motion Smoothness (99.44), and Overall Consistency (23.68). On VideoGen-Eval, it achieves the highest Dynamic Degree (56.31) and Overall Consistency (25.12), with a notable +6.86 margin over Flow-DenseDPO on Dynamic Degree.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the pretrained Wan base model, DynamicsBoost improves Dynamic Degree by +10.20 (44.92 vs 34.72) and Overall Consistency by +1.16 on VBench, confirming that continuation-based DPO substantially enhances motion realism. Compared to Flow-DPO [19], which relies on VLM-scored preference pairs, DynamicsBoost wins on every VBench metric except Background Consistency (97.53 vs 98.15), with the largest gap on Dynamic Degree (+13.70). Against Flow-StructuralDPO [33], which uses guided paired generation, DynamicsBoost leads on Aesthetic Quality (+1.84), Motion Smoothness (+3.06), Dynamic Degree (+9.67), and Overall Consistency (+1.46). Against Flow-DenseDPO [33], which segments videos and scores each with a VLM, DynamicsBoost wins on Aesthetic Quality (+0.97), Motion Smoothness (+1.05), Dynamic Degree (+4.82), and Overall Consistency (+0.62), while Flow-DenseDPO retains a narrow edge on Imaging Quality (67.91 vs 66.81). Notably, DynamicsBoost achieves these gains without any human or VLM preference annotation, whereas all three DPO baselines require VideoReward or VLM scoring.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Flow-DPO lineage (itself derived from Diffusion-DPO and the broader DPO paradigm for LLMs) to video generation, but departs fundamentally from all prior video DPO methods (Flow-DPO, Flow-StructuralDPO, Flow-DenseDPO) by eliminating the need for human or VLM preference annotation. Instead of relying on externally labeled win-loss pairs, it repurposes the video continuation task as an automatic preference-pair generator, exploiting the monotonic quality degradation as generated content length increases. The Asymmetrical DPO loss is a targeted modification of the standard Flow-DPO objective, restricting supervision to non-shared continuation regions and normalizing by continuation length to handle variable-length pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By removing the annotation bottleneck, this approach enables scalable preference alignment for video models without the costly and ambiguous human/VLM labeling that has limited prior DPO-based video work. The method is practically attractive: it requires only LoRA fine-tuning, no architectural changes, and no reward model, making it easy to adopt on top of existing DiT-based video generators. Open questions include whether the monotonic continuation-quality assumption holds for static or low-dynamicity videos, how the approach generalizes to much longer sequences, and whether the preference signal remains informative as base models improve and the gap between generated and ground-truth frames narrows.
