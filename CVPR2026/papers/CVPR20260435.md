## Problem

Few-step diffusion and flow matching models suffer from degraded inversion quality because large time-step updates amplify approximation errors from local linearization, making DDIM-based inversion unreliable. Existing few-step editing methods work around this by attaching auxiliary inversion networks or task-specific modules to pretrained generators, which limits scalability and generalization across architectures. The paper asks whether a few-step model can directly learn its own inversion process within a single unified framework.

## Input / Output

For inversion-based editing, the model takes a source image, a source prompt, and a target prompt, and produces an edited image in 1, 4, or 50 NFEs. For text-to-image generation, it takes a text prompt and produces an image. The model operates in the latent space of a pretrained flow matching backbone (e.g., Stable Diffusion 3's MMDiT), and an additional time-interval embedding encoding (t, t′−t) is injected alongside the standard timestep conditioning. Fine-tuning uses LoRA on the backbone with a zero-initialized interval embedding for warm-up.

## Method

BiFM extends the MeanFlow Identity to model average velocity fields u(x_t, t, t′) over continuous time intervals in both the noise→image (generation) and image→noise (inversion) directions, constrained by a shared instantaneous velocity field. The key insight is that the backward average velocity over [t′, t] must be the negative of the forward average velocity over [t, t′] along the true ODE trajectory, which is enforced through a bidirectional consistency loss L_BiFM = D(u_θ(x_t, t, t′), −u_θ(x_t′, t′, t)). The full training objective combines the MeanFlow regression loss with a time-weighted consistency term, and a lightweight MLP-based time-interval embedding (encoding t and t′−t) is injected into the backbone identically to the original timestep embedding. This formulation allows BiFM to be trained from scratch with a predefined velocity schedule or fine-tuned from any pretrained multi-step flow matching model where the instantaneous velocity is the model's own prediction, without requiring explicit access to the velocity field.

## Results

On PIE-Bench (the primary editing benchmark), BiFM achieves the best overall performance across all sampling budgets: at 4 NFE it reaches LPIPS 67.25, SSIM 87.29%, PSNR 28.92, and CLIP-Edited 23.58, outperforming the strongest few-step baselines (InstantEdit: LPIPS 44.39 but SSIM 86.44%; ReNoise: LPIPS 136.52). At 1 NFE, BiFM attains SSIM 85.88% and PSNR 28.46, surpassing SwiftEdit (SSIM 81.05%, PSNR 23.33). For image generation, BiFM reduces FID to 4.57 on MSCOCO-256 (vs. 6.05 vanilla MMDiT, 4.73 MMDiT+REPA) and achieves the best 1-NFE FID of 2.75 on CIFAR-10 (vs. 2.85 sCT, 2.92 MeanFlow).

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the few-step (4 NFE) editing setting, BiFM wins on SSIM (87.29% vs. 86.44% InstantEdit, 84.63% TurboEdit), PSNR (28.92 vs. 27.96 InstantEdit, 25.51 TurboEdit), and CLIP-Edited (23.58 vs. 22.82 InstantEdit, 21.82 TurboEdit), while InstantEdit achieves a lower LPIPS (44.39 vs. 67.25) and MSE (34.94 vs. 54.92), indicating a trade-off between perceptual and structural fidelity. In the one-step regime, BiFM substantially outperforms SwiftEdit on SSIM (+4.83 pp), PSNR (+5.13), MSE (58.22 vs. 66.58), and both CLIP scores, at the cost of a marginally higher LPIPS (92.30 vs. 91.04). For multi-step editing (50 NFE), BiFM achieves the best LPIPS (47.01) and PSNR (29.89), beating PnP Inv (49.25, 27.22) and NT Inv (60.67, 27.03), though EditFT attains a higher SSIM (91.50%) at 30 NFE. On image generation, BiFM improves FID over MMDiT+REPA (4.57 vs. 4.73) and MMDiT+MeanFlow (4.57 vs. 5.02) on MSCOCO-256, and sets the best 1-NFE FID on CIFAR-10 (2.75 vs. 2.85 sCT, 2.92 MeanFlow, 4.85 Rectified Flow). For image reconstruction, BiFM reduces MSE to 87.72×10⁴ and LPIPS to 89.49×10³, outperforming PnP Inv (105.66, 95.95) and RF-Solver (94.80, 106.15) across all four metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

BiFM extends the MeanFlow Identity (Geng et al., 2025), which links average and instantaneous velocity fields for few-step generation, by applying the same identity in both time directions within a single model. This departs from prior inversion approaches that either reverse DDIM update equations numerically (training-free methods like PnP, NT Inv) or attach auxiliary inversion networks (SwiftEdit, TurboEdit, iCD). The key conceptual shift is treating inversion not as a separate learned mapping or a solver reversal, but as the natural backward integration of the same flow matching ODE, constrained by a bidirectional consistency loss that enforces reversibility of the learned average velocity field.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BiFM provides a practical path to one-step and few-step inversion-based editing on large pretrained flow matching models (e.g., Stable Diffusion 3) without auxiliary modules or architectural changes beyond a lightweight time-interval embedding, which is significant for real-time interactive editing applications. The approach's generality—applicable to both training from scratch and LoRA fine-tuning of existing backbones—makes it broadly adoptable. Open questions include whether the bidirectional consistency constraint remains beneficial at higher resolutions or for video generation, and whether the current one-step editing quality (competitive but not dominant on all metrics vs. SwiftEdit) can be further closed with better interval sampling or longer fine-tuning.
