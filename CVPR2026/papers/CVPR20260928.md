## Problem

Existing infrared physical adversarial attacks are designed for a fixed target scale, causing them to fail when the same pedestrian moves closer or farther away. This single-scale limitation arises because hand-crafted or uniformly generated perturbations have a fixed receptive field and cannot simultaneously disrupt the hierarchical feature pyramids that modern detectors use to process targets at different sizes. The paper asks whether a perturbation design can natively encode multi-scale structure to remain effective across varying distances without re-optimization.

## Input / Output

The method takes a clean infrared image (e.g., 384×288 from a HIKMICRO FH25 camera), the target pedestrian's bounding box, and a black-box detector whose only observable output is the objectness confidence score. It produces a set of fractal parameters (center coordinates, initial length ratio, per-layer rotation angles, and line lengths) that define an H-pattern perturbation to be rendered as cold patches (maintaining 24 °C) within the bounding box. The optimized pattern is physically fabricated and deployed, with the attack succeeding when the detector's confidence drops below 0.5.

## Method

AdvFractal generates adversarial perturbations by recursively constructing H-pattern fractals: starting from an initial H-structure, each layer scales, rotates, and superimposes child H's at the endpoints of the parent, with line width halved at every recursion level to create a progressive coarse-to-fine hierarchy. This self-similar geometry simultaneously perturbs global contours (coarse layers) and local textures (fine layers), matching the multi-scale feature pyramid of the detector. Parameters are optimized via Particle Swarm Optimization under the Expectation over Transformation (EOT) framework, where each particle encodes a full fractal configuration and the fitness is the expected detector confidence over a distribution of physical transformations (scaling, rotation, brightness, noise). The black-box setting requires only detector outputs, and the final 3-layer fractal with a 0.4 length ratio is deployed as thermally cold patches that are visually stealthy in the visible spectrum while producing strong infrared perturbations.

## Results

AdvFractal achieves a 97.54% average attack success rate (ASR) in physical-world tests across distances of 5.2 m to 9.4 m, and a 99.16% average ASR in cross-dataset evaluation (FLIR V1_3, FLIR V2, LLVIP, MSRS, KAIST) against YOLOv3. In the digital domain, the method attains an 89.35% average ASR across 10 detectors (YOLOv3, DETR, Mask R-CNN, Faster R-CNN, Libra R-CNN, RetinaNet, YOLOF, YOLOX, YOLOv8, Deformable DETR) with an average of 238.12 queries per image. Cross-detector transfer yields 100% ASR on Libra R-CNN, YOLOF, and Deformable DETR, while DETR and RetinaNet remain the most robust targets at ~60% ASR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AdvFractal outperforms all four black-box baselines in both digital and physical settings. In the physical domain, the gap is most dramatic: AdvFractal (97.54%) surpasses AdvIB (86.30%) by 11.24 points, AdvICRS (70.01%) by 27.53 points, HCB (55.21%) by 42.33 points, and AdvIC (53.70%) by 43.84 points. In the digital domain, AdvFractal (89.35%) leads AdvICRS (87.20%) by a narrower 2.15-point margin, while maintaining a higher query count (238 vs. 156), suggesting a slight efficiency trade-off. In cross-dataset evaluation (Table 3), AdvFractal achieves 99.16% average ASR versus 90.40% for AdvICRS, 73.18% for AdvIC, 44.28% for AdvIB, and 36.94% for HCB, with the largest gains on complex datasets like MSRS (98.70% vs. 76.70% for AdvICRS) and LLVIP (100% vs. 93.10%). The physical multi-scale comparison (Figure 5b) further shows that baselines exhibit unstable ASR across distances, whereas AdvFractal maintains near-constant effectiveness from 5.2 m to 9.4 m, confirming its core multi-scale advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of black-box physical adversarial attacks on infrared pedestrian detectors (HCB, AdvIB, AdvIC, AdvICRS) by replacing their fixed-granularity perturbation shapes with H-pattern fractal geometry whose self-similarity is argued to naturally align with the multi-scale feature pyramids of modern detectors. The optimization strategy (PSO under EOT) follows the standard black-box physical-attack recipe, but the key conceptual departure is the progressive width-halving across fractal layers, which the ablations show is critical for long-range effectiveness. The work also draws on the broader adversarial-attack literature's use of EOT for physical robustness and PSO for gradient-free parameter search, but applies them to a fractal parameterization that had not previously been explored in the adversarial-attack setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that current infrared detection systems remain highly vulnerable to physically realizable, multi-scale attacks, with a 97.54% ASR across 5.2–9.4 m and 99.16% cross-dataset ASR, which is concerning for safety-critical applications like autonomous driving. The fractal-depth ablation (depth-1 collapsing to 0.01% at 9.4 m while depth-3 retains 98.67%) provides a clear mechanistic explanation for why single-scale patches fail at range. Limitations include the narrow scope (pedestrian detection only, cold patches only, single camera model), the fact that all black-box baselines are from the same research group, and the relatively modest stealthiness score (second-best). Open questions include whether the fractal principle generalizes to other modalities or detection tasks, and whether targeted defenses (beyond the tested AT and DW) can close the multi-scale gap.
