## Problem

Existing video temporal grounding (VTG) benchmarks contain alarmingly high error rates (e.g., 20–35% of samples in Charades-STA), producing misleading model rankings that inflate open-source results while underestimating proprietary ones. Additionally, despite many proposed architectural and training designs for VTG, no systematic study has established which timestamp encoding, optimization paradigm, and RLVR training recipes actually work best under controlled conditions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263810/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a video (sampled into frames) and a natural-language text query describing an event, and outputs a temporal segment as start and end timestamps in seconds (e.g., "The event occurs from 3.2s to 7.8s"). Timestamps are encoded as interleaved raw-text tokens (e.g., "10.2s") inserted before each frame's visual tokens in the LLM's input sequence, rather than via position embeddings or visual overlays. Evaluation uses R1@0.3/0.5/0.7 and mIoU across three re-annotated benchmarks spanning short indoor clips (Charades), medium-length web videos (ActivityNet), and long highlight videos (QVHighlights).

## Method

TimeLens is built on Qwen2.5-VL-7B or Qwen3-VL-8B and trained exclusively with thinking-free RLVR (GRPO) on a 100K-sample dataset re-annotated via an automated multimodal pipeline, with no preceding SFT phase. The key design choices are: (1) interleaved textual timestamp encoding using raw timestamps as text tokens before each frame's visual tokens, which outperforms position-embedding and visual-overlay alternatives; (2) a pure RLVR paradigm without explicit chain-of-thought reasoning, which matches or exceeds SFT and thinking-based RLVR at lower training cost; and (3) two RLVR recipes—early stopping when the temporal IoU reward and within-group reward standard deviation plateau, and difficulty-based Gaussian sampling of training samples (selecting those with IoU in a high-difficulty range relative to the current model). These components are validated on TimeLens-Bench, a rigorously re-annotated evaluation suite where manual auditing corrected errors in query uniqueness, event existence, clarity, and timestamp precision.

## Results

On TimeLens-Bench, TimeLens-8B (built on Qwen3-VL-8B) achieves mIoU of 55.2, 53.2, and 65.5 on Charades-TimeLens, ActivityNet-TimeLens, and QVHighlights-TimeLens respectively, setting a new state-of-the-art among open-source models. TimeLens-7B (built on Qwen2.5-VL-7B) achieves mIoU of 48.8, 46.2, and 56.0, substantially improving over its baseline (39.3, 31.4, 31.6). The paper also demonstrates that re-annotated benchmarks dramatically re-rank models: proprietary models like Gemini-2.5-Pro score far higher on refined benchmarks while open-source models drop significantly, revealing that legacy benchmarks inflated open-source results and underestimated proprietary ones.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TimeLens-8B surpasses GPT-5 by a wide margin on all three benchmarks (mIoU: 55.2 vs 40.5 on Charades, 53.2 vs 42.9 on ActivityNet, 65.5 vs 56.8 on QVHighlights) and also outperforms Gemini-2.5-Flash on all three (55.2 vs 48.6, 53.2 vs 52.5, 65.5 vs 64.3). However, TimeLens-8B still trails Gemini-2.5-Pro on ActivityNet (53.2 vs 58.1) and QVHighlights (65.5 vs 70.4), though it leads on Charades (55.2 vs 52.8). Among open-source models, TimeLens-8B leads all competitors by a substantial margin, e.g., over Time-R1-7B (49.2 mIoU on QVHighlights) and MiMo-VL-7B (41.5 mIoU on QVHighlights). TimeLens-7B delivers a dramatic improvement over its Qwen2.5-VL-7B baseline (+9.5, +14.8, +24.4 mIoU across the three benchmarks) and even surpasses the larger Qwen3-VL-8B on Charades (48.8 vs 48.3). The paper's key finding is that legacy benchmarks (Charades-STA, ActivityNet Captions, QVHighlights) produce misleading rankings where open-source models appear competitive with proprietary ones, while the refined TimeLens-Bench reveals a much larger gap that TimeLens models substantially narrow.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a systematic, incremental study rather than a novel method, building directly on Qwen2.5-VL and Qwen3-VL as base MLLMs and on the RLVR paradigm introduced by Time-R1. It departs from prior work in two critical ways: first, it challenges the assumed reliability of standard VTG benchmarks (Charades-STA, ActivityNet Captions, QVHighlights) by exposing 20–35% error rates and producing rigorously re-annotated replacements; second, it provides the first controlled comparison of timestamp encoding strategies, training paradigms (SFT vs. thinking-based RLVR vs. thinking-free RLVR), and RLVR recipes under a unified, quality-assured protocol. Conceptually, it extends the MLLM-for-VTG line of work by treating data curation as a first-class research contribution alongside algorithmic design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discovery that widely-used VTG benchmarks contain alarmingly high error rates—and that correcting them dramatically re-ranks models (proprietary models like Gemini-2.5-Pro actually outperform open-source models, reversing prior leaderboard narratives)—calls into question the validity of much prior VTG research and provides the field with a trustworthy evaluation standard. The algorithmic findings (interleaved textual timestamp encoding, thinking-free RLVR superiority, early stopping, difficulty-based sampling) offer concrete, reproducible best practices that lower the barrier to building competitive VTG models. Open questions include whether the data-quality pathology extends to other video understanding tasks (e.g., video QA, action recognition), whether the thinking-free RLVR finding generalizes beyond VTG to other perception-heavy multimodal tasks, and how performance scales with further data or model size under the corrected evaluation.
