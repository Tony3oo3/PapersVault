## Problem

CLIP and other vision-language models are highly vulnerable to adversarial perturbations, yet existing test-time defenses apply uniform adaptation to all inputs, creating a fundamental conflict between preserving clean accuracy and improving adversarial robustness. The recent detect-then-adapt approach (TTC) attempts to resolve this but suffers from poor detection accuracy and limited generalization across datasets and model architectures, severely constraining its practical utility.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261635/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image as input and produces a class label via CLIP's zero-shot classification. For clean inputs (detected via a cosine-similarity threshold of 0.8 on CLIP embeddings before and after fixed spatial padding), the image is passed directly to the frozen CLIP predictor. For adversarial inputs, the method generates 64 augmented views (via AugMix), applies a trainable padding module (padding size 32, parameters initialized in [0,10] corresponding to pixel values [0,255]), and outputs a weighted ensemble prediction. No model weights are modified at any stage; all adaptation occurs in the input pixel space.

## Method

TTP is a three-stage detect-then-adapt pipeline operating entirely in the input space. First, a fixed spatial padding is applied and the cosine similarity between CLIP image embeddings before and after padding is computed; clean samples exhibit minimal shift (similarity > 0.8) while adversarial samples show pronounced shifts, enabling a universal, dataset-agnostic detection threshold. Second, for detected adversarial inputs, a lightweight trainable padding module is optimized via a single gradient step that minimizes average prediction entropy over a subset of low-entropy augmented views, restoring attention patterns disrupted by the perturbation. Third, a similarity-aware ensemble assigns each augmented view a weight proportional to exp(α_i − β_i), where α_i is the cosine similarity between the view's padded embedding and the padded adversarial embedding (favoring views that recover clean-like attention) and β_i is the similarity to the original adversarial embedding (penalizing views still close to the corrupted representation), yielding a robust final prediction without any text prompt tuning or architectural modification.

## Results

On the primary ViT-B/32 backbone under 100-step PGD (ε=4/255), TTP achieves an average adversarial accuracy of 39.7% across eight fine-grained datasets, compared to 35.3% for the SOTA test-time defense R-TPT and 0.0% for vanilla CLIP. Clean accuracy is preserved at 57.1% (vs. 57.4% for CLIP), confirming that the detect-then-adapt strategy does not sacrifice zero-shot performance. Scaling to ViT-B/16 and ViT-L/14, TTP reaches 42.9% and 51.6% average adversarial accuracy respectively, consistently outperforming R-TPT by 3.0 and 2.0 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the zero-shot CLIP baseline, TTP matches clean accuracy (57.1% vs. 57.4% average) while lifting adversarial accuracy from 0.0% to 39.7%, confirming that the detection gate prevents clean-sample degradation. Compared to TTC, which also uses a detect-then-adapt paradigm, TTP achieves a dramatic 32.9-point average robustness gain (39.7% vs. 6.8%), attributed to TTP's near-100% detection accuracy versus TTC's unstable L2-distance threshold that fluctuates across datasets and backbones. Against the SOTA test-time defense R-TPT, TTP wins by 4.4 points on average adversarial accuracy (39.7% vs. 35.3%) on ViT-B/32, with consistent 2–3 point margins on ViT-B/16 (42.9% vs. 39.9%) and ViT-L/14 (51.6% vs. 49.6%). Versus the MTA test-time adaptation baseline, TTP trades 1.2 points of clean accuracy (57.1% vs. 58.3%) for a 4.7-point robustness gain (39.7% vs. 35.0%), and the gap widens further on larger backbones where MTA's robustness drops to 27.4% on ViT-B/16. The simple Ensemble baseline is outperformed by TTP on both clean accuracy (57.1% vs. 50.1%) and robustness (39.7% vs. 34.3%), showing that targeted adaptation after detection is more effective than uniform averaging across all views.

## Relation to Prior Work

TTP extends the detect-then-adapt paradigm introduced by TTC [46], which first proposed separating clean and adversarial inputs before applying targeted defense to CLIP. However, TTP departs from TTC's L2-distance-based detector (which suffers from poor cross-dataset and cross-backbone generalization) in favor of a padding-induced cosine-similarity shift that yields a single universal threshold. Conceptually, TTP also diverges from the dominant test-time prompt-tuning line (R-TPT, TAPT, TPT) by operating entirely in the input pixel space via spatial padding rather than adapting text prompts, making it architecture-agnostic and requiring no model-internal access. The entropy-minimization training loop for the padding module borrows the marginal-entropy objective from the broader TTA literature (TENT, TPT).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The detect-then-adapt framing resolves a fundamental tension in test-time defenses: uniform adaptation degrades clean accuracy while skipping adaptation sacrifices robustness, and TTP's near-perfect detection allows both to be optimized independently. The practical appeal of a single universal threshold (τ=0.8) across all tested datasets and three CLIP backbones makes the method easy to deploy without per-domain calibration. Limitations include evaluation restricted to CLIP and fine-grained classification at a relatively mild attack budget (ε=4/255, 100-step PGD); whether the padding-based mechanism transfers to other VLM architectures, larger perturbation budgets, or multi-modal (text-side) attacks remains open. The added inference cost from generating 64 augmented views and computing entropies also warrants attention for latency-sensitive applications.
