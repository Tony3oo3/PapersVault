## Problem

State-of-the-art Multi-Modal Diffusion Transformers (MMDiTs) such as FLUX.1 (12B) and Qwen-Image (20B) incur prohibitive computational costs that impede deployment in resource-constrained settings. Existing structured pruning methods for diffusion models lack generalizability to MMDiT architectures, provide limited flexibility in layer pruning configurations, and offer insufficient understanding of inter-layer dependencies, making it difficult to achieve high compression ratios without significant quality degradation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261994/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained MMDiT teacher model and a calibration dataset of 100,000 image-text pairs (from LAION-2B-en with Qwen2.5-VL captions) to produce a compressed student model that accepts text prompts and generates images. A distinctive feature is the plug-and-play inference: at deployment, any pruned layer can be swapped back to its corresponding teacher layer without retraining, enabling on-the-fly trade-offs between inference speed and generation fidelity. The compressed models are evaluated at multiple parameter budgets (e.g., 20B→10B, 12B→8B, 8B→6.5B) on standard T2I benchmarks.

## Method

PPCL operates in two stages. For depth-wise pruning, it trains a lightweight linear probe per MMDiT block to model each layer's input-output mapping, then identifies contiguous redundant layer intervals by tracking the first-order difference of CKA similarity between teacher outputs and probe outputs—intervals where this difference decreases monotonically indicate substitutable layers. Distillation is non-sequential: each student layer receives its input directly from the teacher's preceding layer rather than from the previous student layer, breaking the error-propagation chain and enabling independent optimization of each pruned interval. For width-wise pruning, redundant text streams and FFNs (identified via CKA heatmaps and MSE analysis) are replaced with compact linear projectors, further reducing parameters while preserving cross-modal alignment. The plug-and-play design means the final student model can selectively invoke original teacher layers at inference, supporting arbitrary compression ratios from a single trained checkpoint.

## Results

On Qwen-Image (20B), PPCL achieves a 50% parameter reduction (20B→10B) with only a 3.29% average performance drop across DPG, GenEval, LongText-Bench, OneIG-Bench, and T2I-CompBench, while nearly doubling inference speed (2625→1462 ms) and cutting GPU memory by ~33%. On FLUX.1-dev (12B→8B), PPCL yields a 4.03% average drop, substantially outperforming TinyFusion (13.80%) and HierarchicalPrune (13.38%) at the same 8B budget. The plug-and-play property enables constructing 12B and 14B variants from the trained 10B model without retraining, with the 14B variant showing only a 0.42% average drop. Further pruning FLUX.1 Lite (8B→6.5B) incurs a mere 0.07% performance loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TinyFusion and HierarchicalPrune at matched parameter budgets, PPCL consistently achieves the lowest average performance drop: on FLUX.1-dev at 8B, PPCL (4.03%) outperforms TinyFusion (13.80%) and HierarchicalPrune (13.38%) by roughly 9–10 percentage points; on Qwen-Image at 14B, PPCL (0.42%) beats TinyFusion (8.75%) and HierarchicalPrune (6.49%) by 6–8 points. Against FLUX.1 Lite (8B), PPCL(8B) trades slightly lower DPG (80.0 vs 82.1) and GenEval (0.605 vs 0.623) for substantially better OneIG-EN (0.456 vs 0.378) and T2I-CompBench S-CoT (78.15 vs 77.21), yielding a lower overall drop (4.03% vs 6.09%). Chroma1-HD achieves the lowest drop (1.02%) on FLUX.1-dev but at a severe latency cost (1714 ms vs PPCL's 535 ms, over 3× slower). Dense2MoE, while fastest (312 ms), suffers the largest quality degradation (21.52% drop) and is not directly comparable since it retains 12B parameters. The plug-and-play property further distinguishes PPCL: the 12B and 14B Qwen-Image variants are obtained by swapping student layers back to teacher layers in the 10B model with zero additional training, a flexibility none of the baselines offer.

## Relation to Prior Work

PPCL sits at the intersection of structured pruning and knowledge distillation for diffusion transformers, building on prior MMDiT compression efforts such as TinyFusion (differentiable layer sampling), HierarchicalPrune (position-aware pruning with PWP), and FLUX.1 Lite (distillation-based compression). It departs from these by introducing a linear-probing-plus-CKA-first-order-difference mechanism to identify contiguous redundant intervals, and by adopting a non-sequential inter-layer distillation scheme that breaks the error-propagation chain inherent in sequential distillation. The width-wise pruning component (replacing text streams and FFNs with linear projectors) draws on the observation that MMDiT's dual-stream architecture exhibits asymmetric redundancy, a direction partially explored by Dense2MoE but here applied via lightweight linear replacements rather than MoE routing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play property—enabling arbitrary layer swapping between student and teacher at inference without retraining—offers a practically valuable deployment knob for trading inference speed against fidelity, which prior pruning methods do not provide. The empirical finding that contiguous layer removal consistently outperforms non-contiguous removal reveals a depth-wise continuity in MMDiT redundancy that could inform future architecture design. Limitations include the dependence on a 100K-image training set for distillation, the requirement to retain teacher weights for the plug-and-play mechanism, and evaluation restricted to T2I generation; whether the contiguous-interval detection and non-sequential distillation transfer to video diffusion or other generative tasks remains open.
