## Problem

Existing adversarial attack studies on multi-agent systems largely reduce to single-agent vulnerabilities—perturbing one agent's message or corrupting shared tools—while overlooking how vulnerabilities propagate through multimodal perception, communication topology, and collective reasoning dynamics. The robustness of multi-modal multi-agent systems (MM-MAS) under coordinated, multi-layer adversarial perturbations remains substantially unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263955/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes image-question pairs (5,984 samples from GQA spanning ten semantic categories) fed into a multi-agent configuration of one master agent and six specialized sub-agents (image understanding, object detection, segmentation, etc.) with 13 distributed tools. Adversarial perturbations are generated externally: textual attacks via GPT-4o and visual attacks via the Nano Banana model. Outputs are quantified through four metrics—Attack Success Rate (ASR), Task Success Rate (TSR), Hallucination Error Rate (HER), and Cross-Modal Consistency (CMC) computed as average CLIP cosine similarity between attacked image-text pairs—rather than a single scalar score.

## Method

HAM³ formalizes a MM-MAS as a tree of agents where each agent applies three sequential mappings (perception → communication → reasoning), and defines attack-specific perturbations δ_A^(l) at each layer. At the perception layer, the Cross-Modal Injection Attack (CMA) jointly perturbs visual inputs (semantic edits, text overlay) and textual inputs to mislead all agents at the entry point. At the communication layer, four attacks target structural dependencies: Agent Spoofing forges identities in the communication graph, Structural Blocking creates cyclic waiting deadlocks, Shared Memory Pollution injects falsified history into agent memory, and Shared Context Injection inserts a common adversarial prior into system prompts. At the reasoning layer, the Chain-of-Thought Injection Attack (CIA) inserts or replaces intermediate reasoning steps, exploiting the fact that corrupted CoT traces propagate through shared reasoning in ReAct, Plan-and-Solve, and Reflexion paradigms. The key finding is that reasoning-layer attacks achieve the highest ASR (up to 78.3%) and produce predominantly systemic errors (>58% of failures involve multiple agents reaching consistent wrong conclusions), because once the inference trace is corrupted, downstream collaboration cannot self-correct.

## Results

On the GQA benchmark, the proposed Chain-of-Thought Injection Attack (CIA) achieves the highest Attack Success Rate of 78.3% under the ReAct paradigm with Qwen2.5-VL-7B, surpassing the best communication-layer attack (SBA, 65.0%) by 13.3 points and the best perception-layer attack (CMA, 60.8%) by 17.5 points. Across all three reasoning paradigms (ReAct, Plan-and-Solve, Reflexion) and five foundation models, reasoning-layer attacks consistently yield the highest ASR, with Reflexion showing the strongest robustness (CIA ASR drops to 61.7% vs. 78.3% for ReAct). Larger models (GPT-4o, o1-mini) reduce CIA ASR to 65.0% and 71.5% respectively under ReAct, confirming a scalability benefit. More than half of successful attacks produce systemic (multi-agent consistent) errors across all three layers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its proposed hierarchical attacks against four baselines: Visual Injection Attack (VIA, Nagaraja et al.), Textual Injection Attack (TIA, Zhang et al./ASB), Tool Spoofing Attack (TSA, Shi et al.), and Role Manipulation Attack (RMA, Zheng et al.). In the perception layer, the proposed Cross-Modal Injection (CMA) outperforms both single-modality baselines: CMA achieves 60.8% ASR vs. VIA at 57.7% and TIA at 55.2% under ReAct/Qwen-7B, confirming that joint visual-textual perturbation is more effective than either modality alone. In the reasoning layer, the proposed CIA (78.3%) narrowly beats the strongest baseline TSA (76.7%) by 1.6 points but substantially outperforms RMA (65.5%) by 12.8 points under the same setting. Notably, TSA is the closest competitor to CIA across all models, suggesting that tool-level manipulation is nearly as damaging as direct reasoning-chain corruption. The proposed communication-layer attacks (SBA, SCIA, SMPA, ASA) have no direct baselines in the comparison table, as existing work does not address topological or shared-memory disruptions in multi-agent settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two previously separate adversarial-attack threads: multi-agent communication attacks (InjecAgent, ASB, CORBA) and multimodal VLM jailbreaks (typographic/compositional visual prompts). Rather than building on a single prior method, HAM³ synthesizes attack primitives from both lines into a unified three-layer taxonomy (perception, communication, reasoning) and is the first to evaluate them jointly on a multimodal multi-agent system. The key conceptual departure from prior multi-agent attack work is the explicit modeling of structural/topological perturbations (agent spoofing, link blocking) alongside content-level manipulations, and the explicit modeling of cross-modal fusion as an attack surface.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that reasoning-layer CoT injection achieves 78.3% ASR and that over half of successful attacks produce *systemic* (multi-agent-consistent) errors challenges the implicit assumption that multi-agent redundancy provides inherent robustness. Practically, this gives system designers a concrete vulnerability hierarchy to prioritize defenses against. Open questions include whether findings generalize beyond the GQA benchmark and the specific OxyGent 1+6 agent topology, whether the relatively simple prompt-injection-style attacks used here represent the full threat surface, and what defensive mechanisms (e.g., reasoning verification, topology-aware consensus) can mitigate the identified cascading failure modes.
