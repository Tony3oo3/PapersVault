## Problem

Existing end-to-end autonomous driving systems optimize for generic safety and efficiency objectives or offer only a few preset modes (e.g., sport, comfort, eco), failing to capture individual drivers' long-term habits or interpret real-time natural language intent such as 'I'm in a rush.' No prior work systematically combines long-term preference alignment from driver profiles with short-term style instruction adaptation in complex, closed-loop driving scenarios involving merging, overtaking, and emergency braking.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262861/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a front-view RGB image, ego-vehicle kinematic state, a free-form language instruction expressing short-term style preference, navigation route waypoints, and a structured driver profile (demographics, driving history, typical purposes). The driver profile is encoded into a fixed-length user embedding via DeBERTaV3, while the instruction is processed by the VLA language backbone. The output is a continuous control action (throttle, brake, steering) computed as the sum of a base action derived from predicted route and speed waypoints and a discrete personalized residual (speed change + steering change) produced by a residual decoder, all passed through a PID controller.

## Method

DMW builds on the SimLingo VLA backbone (InternViT-300M + Qwen2-0.5B) and introduces two complementary personalization mechanisms. For long-term alignment, a contrastive learning objective (InfoNCE) trains a DeBERTaV3-based profile encoder and a temporal behavior encoder to produce aligned user embeddings in a shared latent space, which condition the policy via LoRA-adapted fine-tuning. For short-term adaptation, GRPO reinforcement fine-tuning uses a residual decoder that outputs discrete speed and steering adjustments on top of the base waypoint-derived action, with a style-aware reward whose safety, efficiency, and comfort weights are dynamically mapped from the instruction and scenario context (inferred by an LLM and refined by expert review). During training, trajectories are augmented by conditioning on the least-similar driver's profile to encourage the policy to learn a smooth manifold of distinct driving styles rather than collapsing to a single behavior.

## Results

On Bench2Drive closed-loop evaluation, DMW-Vanilla (fixed reward weights) achieves the highest Driving Score (82.19/81.96/81.48) and Success Rate (70.97/70.63/71.05) across Aggressive/Neutral/Conservative instructions, outperforming SimLingo (DS 78.56/78.15/78.18) and StyleDrive (DS 75.68/76.26/77.02). The full DMW with style-aware rewards produces the largest behavioral differentiation: under Aggressive instructions it boosts Efficiency to 281.56 (an 18.77% gain over SimLingo's 3.70%) and Acceleration to 6.01 m/s², while under Conservative instructions it raises Comfort to 34.62 and Headway to 30.05 m. For long-term preference alignment, DMW achieves an Alignment Score of 0.92 (in-distribution) and 0.83 (out-of-distribution) with human ratings of 8.7/8.3 (ID) and 7.8/8.0 (OOD), substantially exceeding MORL-PD's 0.42/0.25 AS and 5.1/3.9 ratings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SimLingo (the VLA backbone without personalization), DMW-Vanilla improves DS by 3.3–3.6 points and SR by 4.8–5.5 points across all three style conditions, confirming that the safety-aware RL fine-tuning alone yields substantial gains. The full DMW with style-aware rewards produces far larger behavioral shifts than SimLingo: under Aggressive instructions, Efficiency rises from 247.60 to 281.56 (+13.7%) and Acceleration from 5.39 to 6.01 m/s², whereas SimLingo's metrics barely move across styles (DS range only 78.15–78.56). Compared to StyleDrive, which maps instructions to three fixed style labels, DMW achieves higher DS (82.72 vs 77.02 under Conservative; 82.19 vs 75.68 under Aggressive) and more pronounced metric differentiation, indicating that continuous style-aware reward adaptation outperforms discrete style conditioning. For long-term preference alignment, DMW dominates MORL-PD by a wide margin: Alignment Score of 0.92 vs 0.42 (in-distribution) and 0.83 vs 0.25 (out-of-distribution), with human ratings of 8.7 vs 5.1 (ID) and 8.0 vs 3.5 (OOD), demonstrating that the learned user embeddings from textual profiles generalize far better than a fixed preference vector for capturing individual driving styles.

## Relation to Prior Work

DMW builds on the SimLingo VLA backbone for language-grounded driving planning and extends it with a personalization layer that has no direct predecessor. Conceptually, it bridges two previously separate lines: data-driven style-conditioned policies (StyleDrive, MAVERIC) that learn from demonstrations but lack natural-language interaction, and LLM-based instruction-following systems (Talk2Drive) that handle short-term commands but ignore long-term driver habits. The key departure is the contrastive-learning-based user embedding that encodes a driver's profile into a latent vector, combined with GRPO-based reinforcement fine-tuning using a residual decoder, which together enable simultaneous long-term alignment and short-term style adaptation within a single end-to-end policy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Personalization is a recognized but largely unsolved gap in autonomous driving, where current systems offer only a handful of preset modes; DMW demonstrates that a compact VLA model can produce driving behaviors that human evaluators recognize as belonging to specific individuals, a step toward human-centered autonomy. However, all validation is confined to the CARLA simulator with 30 drivers and 20 scenarios, leaving the sim-to-real transfer, scalability to large user populations, and robustness to conflicting or evolving preferences as open questions. The PDD dataset, while small, is the first multi-modal collection explicitly designed for preference-conditioned policy learning and could serve as a useful benchmark for future work.
