## Problem

Creativity in visual design has been treated as a single undifferentiated quantity, and existing metrics (FID, CLIPScore, Rarity Score, Surprise) either measure generic image quality or conflate distinct creative dimensions. No prior work decomposes creativity into interpretable, type-specific axes grounded in the image formation pipeline, nor provides a lightweight reward model that can both assess and steer creative generation along those axes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260804/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

For assessment, CREward takes a single RGB image and outputs four scalar creativity scores (geometry, material, texture, overall) via a frozen SigLIP encoder and a 5-layer MLP head. For preference learning during training, it consumes image pairs (5,000 pairs across 5 objects, each generated by one of five T2I models) with LVLM-derived preference labels. For guided generation, a text prompt plus LoRA slider weights (one per creativity type) drive a diffusion model to produce images with amplified creativity in the selected dimension, and Grad-CAM applied to the reward head yields per-type attention maps.

## Method

The core insight is that large vision-language models (Gemma-3, Gemini-2.5) exhibit strong rank correlation with human creativity judgments—sometimes exceeding inter-human agreement—making their preference labels a scalable proxy for human supervision. CREward is trained with a pairwise logistic loss on 5,000 image pairs labeled by Gemma-3, using a frozen SigLIP vision backbone with only a 5-layer MLP head (ReLU, dropout 0.2) optimized for 20 epochs, yielding a lightweight model that achieves second-highest rank correlation with humans across all four creativity types. For generation, type-specific LoRA adapters are trained on a one-step extrapolation objective that couples a reward-increasing term (−f_θ^(c)(x̂₀,t)) with the standard diffusion noise-prediction loss, producing controllable sliders that selectively amplify geometry, material, or texture creativity during denoising without retraining the base model.

## Results

On the CREBench-Human benchmark, CREward (trained solely on Gemma-3-generated labels) achieves rank correlations of 0.59, 0.72, 0.76, and 0.61 for Geometry, Material, Texture, and Overall creativity respectively, with preference accuracies of 72%, 77%, 74%, and 72%. Notably, CREward surpasses the closed-source Gemini-2.5-Flash on Texture rank correlation (0.76 vs. 0.74) and exceeds inter-human agreement on Material (0.72 vs. 0.63) and Texture (0.76 vs. 0.46). The Surprise baseline [34] lags behind on all four types, with rank correlations ranging from 0.42 to 0.56.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Surprise score [34], CREward wins decisively on all four creativity types for both rank correlation and preference accuracy (e.g., 0.76 vs. 0.49 on Texture rank correlation; 77% vs. 71% on Material accuracy). Compared to the open-weight Gemma-3-27B-it, CREward substantially outperforms on Material (0.72 vs. 0.66) and Texture (0.76 vs. 0.60) rank correlation, despite being trained on Gemma-3's own labels—suggesting the lightweight SigLIP+MLP architecture distills the LVLM's judgments more effectively than the LVLM's direct pairwise comparisons. Against the closed-source Gemini-2.5-Flash, CREward trails on Geometry (0.59 vs. 0.80) and Overall (0.61 vs. 0.68) rank correlation but edges ahead on Texture (0.76 vs. 0.74), making it the only open-weight method to match or exceed the proprietary model on any type. CREward also exceeds inter-human agreement on Material (0.72 vs. 0.63) and Texture (0.76 vs. 0.46), indicating that the learned reward model captures these dimensions more consistently than individual human annotators.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reward-model paradigm from ImageReward [36]—which learns a single scalar preference score from human annotations—to a type-specific creativity setting, decomposing the monolithic creativity signal into geometry, material, and texture axes grounded in the 3D rendering pipeline. For the generation side, it adapts the one-step extrapolation and LoRA-based steering scheme from Sliderspace [8] to steer diffusion models along individual creativity dimensions. Conceptually, it departs from prior creativity metrics (Rarity Score, Surprise score, FID/CLIPScore) that treat creativity as an undifferentiated quantity, and from LVLM-as-judge work that has not previously been validated on fine-grained creative perception.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the first practical, interpretable, and type-specific tool for both assessing and steering creative image generation, with demonstrated utility in design-inspiration workflows and LoRA-based controllability. Key limitations include cross-type entanglement in the LoRA sliders, a bias toward novelty over value (semantically weak images can score high), and evaluation restricted to five single-object categories. Open questions include whether the geometry/material/texture decomposition generalizes to multi-object scenes, noisy real-world images, or other creative dimensions (e.g., color palette, composition), and whether stronger disentanglement can yield truly independent creative controls.
