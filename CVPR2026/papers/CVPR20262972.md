## Problem

Existing generated video attribution methods either embed watermarks during/after generation (degrading quality or requiring deployment changes) or train dedicated source-tracing models (needing large datasets and retraining for each new generator). No practical solution exists for the scenario where an auditor has only white-box access to a model's autoencoder and a handful of reference videos, making reliable, training-free source tracing an open problem.

## Input / Output

SWIFT takes a test video (any length, with frame count divisible by the target model's temporal compression ratio K, typically 4 or 8), white-box access to that model's 3D VAE autoencoder, and a small calibration set of belonging videos (as few as 20) to estimate a decision threshold. It outputs a binary attribution label (belonging vs. non-belonging) derived by comparing a scalar attribution signal—the average per-frame MSE loss ratio over overlapping frames between two reconstructions—against a KDE-estimated threshold. The method requires no gradient optimization, no watermark embedding, and no post-processing of generated videos.

## Method

SWIFT exploits the 'Pixel Frames(many) ↔ Latent Frame(one)' temporal mapping inherent in 3D VAEs: within each chunk of K consecutive frames, the VAE encodes all K pixel frames into a single latent frame, creating a distributional signature that belonging videos satisfy but non-belonging videos do not. The method applies a fixed-length sliding window of K(N−1) frames to perform two reconstructions via the target VAE: a normal window W₀ aligned to chunk boundaries and a corrupted window W_{K−1} shifted backward by K−1 frames, which disrupts both frame composition within chunks and positional alignment. The attribution signal is the average MSE loss ratio of the overlapping frames between the two reconstructions; belonging videos yield a signal well below 1 (normal reconstruction is faithful while corrupted reconstruction is poor), whereas non-belonging videos yield a signal near 1 (both reconstructions are equally unfaithful). A kernel density estimate over the calibration set's signals provides a model-specific threshold without assuming a parametric distribution, enabling few-shot (≈20 samples) or even zero-shot (τ=1) attribution.

## Results

SWIFT achieves a 94.0% average attribution accuracy across five SOTA video generation models (HunyuanVideo, Wan2.1, EasyAnimate, LTX-Video, Wan2.2), substantially outperforming the AEDR baseline (73.6%) by +20.4 percentage points. Per-model accuracy ranges from 85.3% (LTX-Video, which incorporates a denoising step in its VAE) to 98.4% (Wan2.1). With only 20 belonging video samples for threshold calibration, SWIFT already reaches 90.2% average accuracy, and zero-shot attribution (threshold τ=1) achieves 90.1%, 89.3%, and 98.4% on HunyuanVideo, EasyAnimate, and Wan2.2 respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is AEDR [27], a training-free image attribution method that reconstructs inputs through the target model's VAE and compares latent feature consistency. SWIFT wins 23 out of 25 model-pair comparisons, with the largest margin being +47.8 pp (EA target vs. LTX source, 97.8% vs. 50.0%). AEDR's weakness is most pronounced when the target model is HYV or EA, where it drops to 50–65% accuracy due to insufficient separation between belonging and non-belonging videos in the spatial-only reconstruction. SWIFT's two losses are both with LTX-Video as the target (vs. EA: 94.5% vs. 97.1%; vs. Real: 92.2% vs. 95.6%), attributable to LTX's VAE incorporating a final denoising step that narrows the reconstruction gap and reduces temporal-cue sensitivity. In terms of efficiency, SWIFT is also faster than AEDR by 4–32% because it reconstructs only a sliding window rather than the full video.

## Relation to Prior Work

SWIFT extends the training-free autoencoder reconstruction paradigm from image attribution (AEDR [27]) to the video domain by exploiting the temporal compression structure inherent in modern 3D VAEs. While prior video attribution work falls into either active watermark-based methods or training-based passive classifiers, SWIFT carves out a new middle ground: a passive, training-free approach that requires only a small set of belonging samples for threshold calibration. The key conceptual departure from image methods is the recognition that spatial-only reconstruction consistency is insufficient for video; the 'Pixel Frames(many) ↔ Latent Frame(one)' temporal mapping within each VAE chunk provides a natural corruption signal that image attribution methods cannot access.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SWIFT offers a practical attribution mechanism for model owners who cannot embed watermarks or afford large-scale training, requiring only 20 samples (or zero for some models) and white-box access to the VAE alone. However, robustness remains a significant open question: attribution accuracy drops sharply under common post-processing (Flip to 52%, Noise to 60.3%), and the method is inapplicable to models using 2D VAEs or those with denoising-augmented decoders (LTX drops to 85.3%). Future work must address robustness to adversarial perturbations and extend the temporal-corruption signal to architectures where the VAE's distributional signature is further attenuated.
