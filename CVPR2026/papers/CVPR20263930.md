## Problem

Current MLLMs lack systematic evaluation and training frameworks for complex spatial reasoning that integrates geometric understanding, viewpoint imagination, fine-grained perception, and occlusion reasoning. Wide-baseline matching (WBM)—deciding whether two views separated by large baselines depict the same scene element—exposes a stark gap: on a difficult 90-sample subset, humans achieve 84.0 F1 while the best existing MLLM reaches only 37.2, and no scalable, verifiable training paradigm exists to close this gap.

## Input / Output

The model receives two RGB images with pre-marked point sets rendered as visual prompts (indexed dots), where each point set is dynamically sampled at inference time from a verified correspondence pool and partitioned into matchable and distractor subsets. The output is a discrete textual mapping f̂: {1,…,n} → {1,…,m} ∪ {∅}, assigning each query point either a target index or an explicit 'no match' label, rather than a continuous similarity score matrix. Point sets are not fixed offline; their cardinality and spatial arrangement are adaptively constructed per sample to control difficulty.

## Method

DCRL (Dynamic Correspondence Reinforcement Learning) trains Qwen3-VL-8B via GRPO-based RLVR with a holistic matching reward that scores correctness over all query regions including unmatched points, eliminating the ambiguity of partial bipartite matching. Training follows a dual-level curriculum: an outer Image-Level Viewpoint Progression partitions the dataset into 10 overlap-score bins and advances sequentially once accuracy exceeds 0.8, permanently discarding mastered bins; an inner Point-Level Correspondence Curriculum dynamically adjusts both cardinality (L1 unambiguous → L2 selective → L3 partial matching) and spatial distribution (sparse → moderate → dense sampling) within each viewpoint stage. Data is generated at scale from RGB-D videos (CO3D, uCO3D, ScanNet) and SfM reconstructions (DL3DV, RealEstate10K), with correspondences verified via depth reprojection or COLMAP landmarks and subsampled into spatially separated pools of 10–50 pairs. This verifiable-reward RL approach outperforms SFT by +19.5 F1 on ReasonMatch-Bench and transfers positively to OmniSpatial, MindCube, and SAT without degrading general visual understanding.

## Results

On ReasonMatch-Bench (2,810 image pairs), DCRL (Qwen3-VL-8B + DCRL) achieves 70.5 F1 (70.3 Precision, 71.1 Recall), outperforming the strongest proprietary baseline GPT-5-mini (57.9 F1) by 12.6 points and Gemini-2.5-Pro (42.8 F1) by 27.7 points. On a 90-sample high-divergence human-study subset, human annotators reach 84.0 F1 while DCRL achieves 52.0, indicating substantial remaining headroom. DCRL also transfers positively to spatial benchmarks: +5.27 on OmniSpatial (48.87 vs. 43.60 base), +3.51 on MindCube (43.52 vs. 40.01), and +5.30 on SAT (75.30 vs. 70.00), while showing no degradation on general visual understanding (e.g., MMStar 62.5 vs. 59.8, V*Bench 85.9 vs. 84.8).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DCRL decisively outperforms all proprietary MLLM baselines on ReasonMatch-Bench: GPT-5-mini (57.9 F1), GPT-5-Chat (51.5), Gemini-2.5-Pro (42.8), Claude-4.5-Sonnet (41.7), and GPT-4o (33.5), with the largest margin over Gemini-2.5-Pro (+27.7 F1). It also surpasses the much larger Qwen3-VL-235B (49.2 F1) by 21.3 points, demonstrating that targeted RL training on an 8B model exceeds a 235B model's zero-shot spatial matching ability. On OmniSpatial, DCRL (48.87) beats GPT-4-turbo (34.06), LLaVA-1.5-7B (34.97), and Qwen2.5-VL-7B (39.25), but trails Gemini-2.5-flash (52.12) and InternVL3-78B (49.33). On MindCube, DCRL (43.52) outperforms GPT-4o (38.81), RoboBrain (37.38), and all open-weight baselines. On SAT, DCRL (75.30) substantially exceeds GPT-4o (57.50) and Qwen2.5-VL-7B (56.33). Notably, SFT on CoT-annotated data achieves only 51.0 F1 on ReasonMatch and degrades SAT to 41.3, whereas DCRL reaches 70.5 and 75.3 respectively, confirming that RLVR yields more transferable spatial reasoning than supervised imitation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the classical computer vision task of wide-baseline matching (Pritchett & Zisserman, 1998) as a testbed for MLLM spatial reasoning, departing from prior spatial benchmarks (OmniSpatial, VSI-Bench) that probe isolated capabilities like relative positioning or viewpoint prediction. Methodologically, it extends the RLVR paradigm from DeepSeek-R1 (GRPO with verifiable rewards) to the multimodal spatial domain, where matching correctness provides a natural verifiable signal without requiring explicit chain-of-thought supervision. It also contrasts with prior MLLM spatial training approaches (SAT, RoboSpatial, Multi-SpatialMLLM) that rely on SFT or limited task formats, by introducing a dual-level curriculum (image-level viewpoint progression + point-level correspondence adaptation) that dynamically adjusts difficulty during RL training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates a substantial and persistent gap between current MLLMs and humans on wide-baseline correspondence (52.0 vs 84.0 F1 on the hardest subset), with object-centric matching remaining particularly challenging (27.8 vs 62.1). The finding that SFT actually degrades SAT performance (70.0 → 41.3) while RL improves it (75.3) suggests that teacher-forced imitation overfits to surface correspondence patterns rather than building transferable geometric reasoning. Open questions include whether the curriculum design generalizes across model architectures and scales, whether the 43-point gain on ReasonMatch-Bench holds for larger models, and whether the modest transfer to other spatial benchmarks (+3–5%) indicates a truly general spatial capability or task-specific adaptation.
