## Problem

Fine-tuning vision-language models on long, detail-rich captions remains challenging because pre-trained encoders are constrained by token-length limits and existing long-text alignment methods rely heavily on semantic patterns from pre-training corpora, which degrade in domains with scarce or poorly represented visual-linguistic cues. No prior approach leverages the fundamental geometric structure of images (edges) as a complementary alignment signal to reinforce vision-language correspondence beyond appearance-based semantics.

## Input / Output

At inference, StructXLIP takes standard RGB images (resized to 224×224) and text captions as input, producing image and text embeddings in a shared d-dimensional space for cross-modal retrieval ranking (Recall@K in both T→I and I→T directions). During fine-tuning only, it additionally consumes edge maps extracted from the original images and structure-centric captions obtained by filtering out color and material terms via an LLM-generated lexicon. No edge extraction or text filtering is required at test time, so inference cost is identical to standard CLIP.

## Method

StructXLIP augments the standard symmetric InfoNCE image-text contrastive loss with three structure-centric auxiliary losses: (i) a global contrastive loss aligning edge-map embeddings with structure-centric text embeddings, (ii) a local multi-positive contrastive loss matching SAM-segmented edge regions to sentence-delimited text chunks, and (iii) a cosine-similarity consistency loss anchoring edge-map representations to original image representations to prevent modality drift. Structural views are produced by applying an edge detector (Canny, LoG, HED, etc.) to images and an LLM-guided lexicon filter to remove appearance terms from captions. Theoretically, the auxiliary losses maximize a lower mutual information bound (by the Data Processing Inequality), making them an inherently harder, information-reduced task that introduces gradient diversity and acts as an implicit regularizer, steering optimization toward more robust and semantically stable minima. The losses are plug-and-play and can be added to any CLIP-based fine-tuning framework without architectural changes.

## Results

StructXLIP achieves the best Recall@1 on all four datasets in both retrieval directions, with the largest gains on SKETCHY (69.86% T→I, 68.22% I→T) and DOCCI (83.04% T→I, 81.59% I→T). On the fine-grained fashion benchmark SKETCHY, it outperforms the second-best method GOAL by +6.65% (T→I) and +5.78% (I→T) at R@1. On the challenging Insect dataset (rare domain, 81-word captions), StructXLIP still improves R@1 T→I by +1.12% (a 12.7% relative gain) over GOAL. The method also demonstrates plug-and-play compatibility, boosting LoRA and DoRA by ~4–5% R@1 on SKETCHY when the structure-centric losses are added.

## Baselines & Comparisons

StructXLIP outperforms all seven baselines (Long-CLIP, FineLIP, SmartCLIP, GOAL, SigLIP2, LoRA, DoRA) on R@1 in both retrieval directions across all four datasets, with the sole exception noted on DCI R@5 I→T. The strongest competitor is GOAL [CVPR'25], which jointly models global-local correspondences; StructXLIP beats it by +6.65% R@1 on SKETCHY T→I, +3.57% on DOCCI T→I, and +3.26% on DCI T→I. FineLIP [CVPR'25] performs weakest overall (40.59% R@1 on SKETCHY), suggesting that multi-stage fine-tuning with adaptive attention fusion is less effective than structural cue injection for long-text alignment. SmartCLIP [CVPR'25] is competitive on DOCCI (74.92% R@1) but lags significantly on SKETCHY (50.73%), indicating its modular token routing does not capture fine-grained structural details as well. As a plug-and-play module, the structure-centric losses L* boost LoRA by +5.01% and DoRA by +3.43% R@1 on SKETCHY, and improve SigLIP2 by +4.58% R@1, confirming broad compatibility across full-parameter and parameter-efficient fine-tuning paradigms.

## Relation to Prior Work

StructXLIP extends the CLIP-based contrastive fine-tuning paradigm (InfoNCE loss) by introducing structural cues—edge maps and lexicon-filtered captions—as auxiliary alignment signals during training. It departs from the long-text alignment line (LongCLIP, GOAL, SmartCLIP, FineLIP), which primarily extends token capacity or reinforces global-local correspondences through learned mechanisms, by instead grounding alignment in classical edge detection (Canny, LoG) and deterministic text filtering. The local alignment loss draws on LISM-style multi-positive contrastive learning from GOAL, while the theoretical analysis leverages the Data Processing Inequality and mutual information lower bounds from contrastive representation learning. Crucially, the structural signals are used only during fine-tuning; inference remains identical to standard CLIP, positioning StructXLIP as a training-time augmentation rather than an architectural change.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature of the three structure-centric losses—demonstrated across full-parameter fine-tuning, LoRA, DoRA, and multiple SOTA methods—makes this a broadly applicable recipe for improving VLM alignment without architectural modifications. The strong performance in low-data regimes (5–50% of training data) and robustness to noisy structural inputs (incorrect edge maps, severe word reduction) suggest the structural inductive bias provides genuine regularization beyond what semantic contrastive learning alone offers. The authors explicitly raise the open question of whether training a VLM from scratch with structural cues could yield fundamentally different representations, though they acknowledge the computational cost. A potential limitation is that the structural emphasis may be less beneficial in domains where appearance (color, texture, material) is the primary discriminative signal rather than shape and layout.
