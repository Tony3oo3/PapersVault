## Problem

Frame selection, a standard efficiency technique in VideoLLM pipelines, removes intermediate frames and breaks temporal continuity, causing models to misinterpret event order and causal relations. Unlike humans who infer progression from sparse cues, VideoLLMs frequently fail at temporal reasoning under sparse sampling, and existing remedies (enhanced temporal encoding, spatiotemporal modelling) require complex architectural changes or extensive training.

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of N uniformly or adaptively sampled video frames (minimum 3 per video) and a natural-language text query. Each frame is modified in pixel space by overlaying a frame-index label (e.g., 'frame #01') at the bottom-left corner, with font size scaled as min(width, height)/s to adapt to resolution. The query is additionally rewritten to embed mapped frame indices as explicit temporal anchors before being fed to the frozen VideoLLM, which outputs a standard natural-language answer.

## Method

VIKEY is a training-free, plug-and-play framework combining two components. First, Sequential Visual Prompting inserts a numbered label into the bottom-left corner of each sampled frame, exploiting a discovered positional bias where the model attends more strongly to lower-region cues (achieving 100% reverse-lookup accuracy vs. 60–79% for top placements). Second, Keyword-Frame Mapping (KFM) extracts salient keywords from the query via an LLM, computes CLIP cosine similarity between each keyword and every frame, and rewrites the query to include the highest-scoring frame index (above a threshold τ) as an explicit temporal anchor. The key insight is that frame indices function as dictionary-like keys: the VideoLLM can perform both lookup (describe frame k) and reverse lookup (which frame contains X) when indices are visually present, and layer-wise attention analysis confirms that VP shifts attention toward image tokens in mid-to-late layers responsible for spatiotemporal integration.

## Results

On the 64-frame (1 FPS) setting, VIKEY improves TempCompass total accuracy by up to +9.6 points (GPT-4.1: 70.74 → 80.37) and MVBench total by up to +1.9 points (Qwen2.5-VL: 85.75 → 87.68). Under the sparser 20%-frame setting, gains are larger: LLaVA-Video-7B jumps from 65.53 to 72.21 on TempCompass and from 44.07 to 50.28 on VideoMME TR, and in some cases the 20%-frame + VP result exceeds the 64-frame baseline without VP (e.g., LLaVA-Video VideoMME TR: 50.28 vs. 47.46). The method is training-free and plug-and-play, requiring no architectural changes or additional supervision.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GPT-4.1, VIKEY yields the largest single gain on TempCompass (+9.6 pts at 64 frames, +7.3 pts at 20% frames) while MVBench remains essentially unchanged, suggesting the benefit is most pronounced for event-order and attribute-change reasoning. Against Qwen2.5-VL-7B, improvements are consistent and moderate across all four benchmarks in both settings (e.g., MVBench 85.75 → 87.68, VideoMME TR 38.42 → 41.25 at 64 frames). Against LLaVA-Video-7B, the method shows mixed results at 64 frames (MVBench drops slightly from 82.50 to 81.25) but delivers strong gains at 20% frames, where VideoMME TR jumps from 44.07 to 50.28—exceeding the 64-frame baseline of 47.46. Against LLaVA-OneVision-7B, gains are seen on TempCompass and LongVideoBench but MVBench and VideoMME TR show small drops at 64 frames, while the 20% setting recovers and surpasses the dense baseline on LongVideoBench (52.53 vs. 48.25). Compared to FrameFusion (token-pruning), VIKEY consistently outperforms across all sampling ratios from 20% to 90% on TempCompass, indicating that frame-level visual prompting is more effective than token-level compression for preserving temporal continuity.

## Relation to Prior Work

VIKEY repurposes visual prompting—a technique previously confined to spatial grounding within single images (e.g., drawing circles or bounding boxes)—as a temporal cue mechanism across sparsely sampled video frames. It departs from the dominant line of temporal-reasoning work (enhanced temporal encodings, spatiotemporal graph self-training, time-gating) by requiring no architectural modification or additional training, instead treating frame-index overlays as lightweight ordinal signals. The Keyword-Frame Mapping module builds on the empirical observation that VideoLLMs can treat frame numbers as dictionary-like keys, a capability the paper shows is nearly absent without explicit visual indexing. Conceptually, the paper bridges the efficiency literature (frame selection, token pruning) and the prompting literature, positioning VP as a bridge that restores temporal continuity lost during aggressive frame subsampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free, plug-and-play nature of VIKEY makes it immediately deployable on top of any VideoLLM pipeline that already performs frame selection, offering a low-cost remedy for the temporal degradation that frame sparsification introduces. The discovery of a strong positional bias (bottom-corner prompts achieving 100% reverse-lookup accuracy versus ~60–79% for top corners) reveals a previously uncharacterized asymmetry in how VideoLLMs attend to visual annotations, with practical implications for prompt design. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to very long videos (hundreds of frames) where CLIP-based keyword mapping may become ambiguous, whether the positional bias is model-specific or universal, and whether the modest 1–5 point gains on standard benchmarks translate to meaningful improvements in downstream agentic or multi-step video tasks.
