## Problem

Sparse IMU-based motion capture suffers from accumulated drift and physically implausible artifacts (foot sliding, penetration, floating) because it cannot directly measure absolute position. Existing pressure-sensor approaches are restricted to foot-only configurations or lack mechanisms to enforce physical plausibility in reconstructed poses. GRIP addresses this by combining just four IMUs with insole pressure data and driving a physics-simulated humanoid to produce globally accurate, physically consistent full-body motion.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260909/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes acceleration and orientation from four IMUs (two wrists, two insoles) transformed into a global frame, plus a compact per-foot pressure representation (vertical GRF, 2D center-of-pressure, and binary forefoot/rearfoot contact labels) designed for cross-device compatibility. A 25×25 environmental height map sampled over a 1.5 m × 1.5 m area provides spatial context. The output is not a static pose but a sequence of joint torques applied to a torque-driven humanoid in a physics simulator, yielding full-body joint positions (24 SMPL joints), 6D-rotation joint angles, key-joint velocities, and physically constrained trajectories.

## Method

GRIP uses an observer–controller architecture with two modules. KinematicsNet, a multi-stage unidirectional LSTM, progressively estimates leaf-joint positions, full-body joint positions, joint angles, and key-joint velocities from sensor data, trained with per-module MSE losses. DynamicsNet, a PPO-trained MLP policy, controls the simulated humanoid using a State Difference representation that encodes orientation, velocity, and root-relative position discrepancies between the KinematicsNet estimates and the simulated state—deliberately avoiding integrated global positions to prevent drift propagation. The reward combines an Adversarial Motion Prior for realism, an imitation term for reference tracking, and an energy penalty for torque regularization. A fall-recovery mechanism reinitializes the simulator from a history buffer of KinematicsNet outputs when balance is lost, replacing the corrupted segment with kinematic predictions to maintain continuity without auxiliary forces.

## Results

GRIP achieves the best global pose accuracy (MPJPE) across all three datasets: 182.44 mm on PRISM, 218.09 mm on UnderPressure, and 118.60 mm on PSU-TMM100, using only 4 IMUs plus insole pressure. It also attains the lowest foot penetration (FP) on every dataset (5.77 mm, 0.00 mm, and 0.73 mm respectively), demonstrating the benefit of physics-simulation-based control. For local pose metrics (PEL-MPJPE, PA-MPJPE, MPJRE), the 6-IMU GlobalPose method remains slightly ahead, while GRIP with 4 IMUs + pressure is competitive with 6-IMU methods. On the slow-motion PSU-TMM100 dataset, GRIP also achieves the best vertical ground reaction force error (316.06 N), outperforming all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRIP (4 IMUs + pressure) outperforms all five baselines on global MPJPE across all three datasets, with the largest margins over FoRM (e.g., 182.44 vs. 199.60 mm on PRISM) and SolePoser (which reports no global position). Against the 6-IMU physics-optimization methods PIP and GlobalPose, GRIP wins on MPJPE and foot penetration (FP) but trails on local pose metrics (PEL-MPJPE, PA-MPJPE, MPJRE), where GlobalPose's pelvis-mounted IMU and gravity-pose correction give it an edge. MobilePoser (3 IMUs) is outperformed by GRIP on MPJPE and FP in all settings, though MobilePoser achieves slightly lower vGRF on PRISM (248.54 vs. 258.40 N). FoRM (2 IMUs + pressure, no physics) is consistently the weakest baseline on global metrics and foot penetration, confirming the importance of physics simulation. On the slow-motion PSU-TMM100 dataset, GRIP uniquely achieves the best vGRF (316.06 N), as the optimization-based baselines miss fine-scale foot contact events during slow weight shifts.

## Relation to Prior Work

GRIP sits at the intersection of three research threads: sparse-IMU motion capture (DIP, TransPose, PIP, GlobalPose, MobilePoser), insole-pressure-assisted pose estimation (SolePoser, FoRM, PressInPose), and physics-based humanoid control (PHC, AMP, QuestSim). It departs from prior IMU methods by replacing post-hoc physical optimization with an online physics-simulation controller (DynamicsNet) driven by a State Difference representation that avoids drift from integrated positions. It also departs from prior physics-based avatar work (QuestSim, PHC) by operating without absolute positional inputs from cameras or HMDs, relying instead on relative motion cues from four IMUs and insole pressure. The two-stage KinematicsNet→DynamicsNet architecture with a fall-recovery mechanism is a novel integration of these threads.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The PRISM dataset (1,275 sequences, 3.5 hours, six subjects, multi-modal) fills a gap in publicly available data that simultaneously captures IMUs, insole pressure, optical MoCap, and environmental models for diverse motions including sports and object interactions. The finding that four IMUs plus pressure sensors outperform six IMUs alone in stability (94.5% vs 87.0% success rate) suggests pressure is a more effective complement than additional inertial sensors, which has practical implications for sensor design. Limitations include higher vGRF error in high-momentum motions (due to the absence of floating-base residual forces), a fall-recovery mechanism that replaces physics with kinematic estimates during falls, and a dataset limited to six subjects. Open questions include extending to multi-person scenarios, dynamic object interactions, and integrating additional modalities (cameras, UWB) to further reduce drift.
