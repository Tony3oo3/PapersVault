## Problem

Multimodal large reasoning models (MLRMs) produce hallucinations that stem not only from under-utilized visual evidence but also from an internal imbalance in how perception and reasoning are allocated across transformer layers. Existing mitigation strategies assume the root cause is insufficient visual grounding and compensate via stronger supervision, finer alignment, or external priors, leaving the cross-layer functional misalignment—specifically perceptual bias in shallow layers and reasoning drift in deeper layers—unaddressed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261918/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The plugin operates on off-the-shelf MLRMs (Kimi-VL A3B-Thinking, Ocean-R1 7B-Instruct, R1-OneVision 7B) that take a standard RGB image and text prompt and produce a text answer with a reasoning chain. No changes to input format, resolution, or output structure are required; the intervention is entirely internal to the attention computation during inference. The method is training-free and plug-and-play, adding roughly 2 seconds of latency over a ~101-second baseline on HallusionBench (200 samples), compared to 1.2×–6.6× overhead for competing baselines.

## Method

The proposed two-step plugin first performs Functional Head Identification: it materializes per-head attention matrices, computes a modality attention ratio (fraction of attention mass on visual vs. textual tokens) for each head, and applies depth-aware layer boundaries (ℓ_perc, ℓ_reas) together with ratio thresholds (τ_perc, τ_reas) to partition heads into perception-oriented (shallow, high visual ratio) and reasoning-oriented (deep, low visual ratio) groups. Second, Class-Conditioned Rescaling assigns multiplicative gains g_perc ≥ 1 and g_reas ≥ 1 to the identified heads' outputs (applied after per-head computation but before the output projection W_O), while all other heads remain at gain 1. The guiding principle is that only a small subset of dominating heads drives reasoning, so amplifying functional heads increases their likelihood of entering this critical set. The intervention is layer-agnostic in its gain values, introduces no architectural change or retraining, and adds less than 1% extra computation, yielding an average 4.2-percentage-point accuracy gain across five benchmarks and three MLRMs.

## Results

Across three MLRMs (Kimi-VL A3B-Thinking, Ocean-R1 7B-Instruct, R1-OneVision 7B) and five benchmarks, the proposed Functional Head Identification + Class-Conditioned Rescaling plugin achieves an average 4.2-percentage-point accuracy gain over the vanilla backbone, with gains reaching up to 7% on the most challenging tasks (e.g., +6.09% on MathVision for Ocean-R1, +7.01% on MMStar for Kimi-VL). The method attains the best score on nearly 95% of individual task-metric pairs. Efficiency is preserved: the plugin adds only ~2 s of inference time (≈1% extra computation) versus 1.2×–6.6× overhead for the strongest baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all three baselines on the vast majority of task-metric pairs. Against AGLA (the strongest baseline), the plugin wins on 14 of 15 individual metrics across the three models, with the sole exception being a near-tie on R1-OneVision MathVista (60.09 vs. 60.21, within one standard deviation); the largest AGLA gaps are on HallusionBench (+6.83% for Kimi-VL, +8.44% for Ocean-R1) and MMStar (+2.73% for Kimi-VL, +3.53% for R1-OneVision). Against VCD, gains are more uniform: the plugin leads by 2.5–7.0 points on Kimi-VL and 3.1–6.5 points on Ocean-R1, while on R1-OneVision the margin narrows to 0.4–5.7 points. CGD is consistently the weakest baseline, underperforming even the vanilla model on most tasks (e.g., 46.81 vs. 54.58 on Ocean-R1 MathVista; 37.76 vs. 59.76 on Ocean-R1 SEED-Bench), suggesting that CLIP-guided decoding can actively harm reasoning in long-chain MLRMs. Notably, the plugin's advantage is most pronounced on visual-reasoning and multimodal-integration benchmarks (HallusionBench, MMStar, SEED-Bench), where it simultaneously improves both perception and reasoning, whereas VCD tends to trade math accuracy for visual gains and AGLA shows the opposite pattern.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant 'compensate for vision' paradigm in multimodal hallucination mitigation (stronger supervision, finer-grained alignment, external visual priors) by reframing the problem as a cross-layer allocation imbalance between perception and reasoning stages. It builds directly on interpretability findings that MLRMs exhibit a staged attention division—shallow layers attend to visual tokens while deeper layers shift to textual reasoning—and on the observation that only a small subset of attention heads carries decisive influence. The proposed functional head identification and class-conditioned rescaling is positioned as a training-free, architecture-agnostic plugin that intervenes on this staged structure, contrasting with decoding-time baselines (VCD, CGD, AGLA) that operate at the output level rather than reshaping internal information flow.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature (no retraining, <1% extra computation, ~2s overhead vs. 1.2×–6.6× for baselines) makes this approach immediately deployable on existing MLRMs, which is practically significant for high-stakes applications where retraining is infeasible. The non-additivity finding—that combining perception and reasoning head amplification yields gains that neither achieves alone, and that either alone can even hurt performance on certain tasks—suggests hallucination is genuinely a cross-stage interaction problem rather than a single-capability failure. Open questions include generalizability beyond the three tested architectures, whether the task-dependent boundary bands (ℓ_perc, ℓ_reas) can be predicted automatically rather than swept manually, and whether the approach extends to longer reasoning chains or multi-image settings.
