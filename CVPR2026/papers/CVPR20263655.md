## Problem

Existing category-level articulated object pose estimation methods regress poses in continuous spaces, creating a fundamental mismatch with the discrete, non-uniformly sampled point cloud inputs and requiring exhaustive search over large domains. Additionally, part-wise approaches estimate each rigid component independently, ignoring the kinematic constraints imposed by joint structures, which leads to physically inconsistent predictions and poor robustness under self-occlusion.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263655/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a partial point cloud observation (1024 uniformly sampled points) of an articulated object's rigid parts as input and produces per-part 6D poses (3D rotation in SO(3) and 3D translation) along with joint axis descriptors (unit direction vector and pivot point for revolute joints; unit direction vector for prismatic joints). Each part's pose is internally represented as a sequence of 6 discrete tokens—three Euler angles and three translation coordinates—uniformly binned into 360 integer states, with an additional [MASK] token extending the state space to 361 for structured corruption during diffusion.

## Method

DICArt formulates articulated pose estimation as a conditional discrete diffusion process where a forward Markov chain progressively corrupts the GT pose into noise and a learned reverse process denoises it back. Two key design choices shape the diffusion: a block-diagonal transition matrix that isolates rotation and translation tokens into separate subspaces to prevent semantic mixing, and a [MASK]-augmented state space (inspired by D3PMs) for structured categorical corruption. The core novelty is a reformulated reverse process with a flexible flow decider: at each denoising step, Gumbel-Softmax-sampled binary indicators determine whether each token should be denoised toward its GT value or reset to a noisy state, enforcing synchronized convergence among semantically correlated tokens (e.g., the three Euler angles of a part) and avoiding the asynchronous convergence that degrades standard discrete diffusion. Finally, a hierarchical kinematic coupling mechanism estimates the parent part's pose via diffusion, then recovers child part poses through spatial kinematic reasoning using predicted joint axis descriptors and motion axes constrained to be orthogonal to the joint axis, reducing the prediction space and improving robustness under self-occlusion.

## Results

On the ArtImage synthetic dataset, DICArt achieves the best per-part rotation error across all five categories, with headline numbers of 3.2°/3.9° (Laptop), 3.5°/5.1°/5.3° (Eyeglasses), 2.9°/3.7° (Dishwasher), 1.7°/2.2° (Scissors), and 1.7° (all four Drawer parts). Translation errors are similarly dominant, e.g., 0.041 m/0.091 m/0.083 m on Eyeglasses and 0.041 m–0.091 m on Drawer. On the semi-synthetic ReArtMix dataset, DICArt reduces rotation error by roughly 50% over ReArtNet (e.g., 1.5°/1.6° vs 3.4°/3.9° on Drawer) and translation error to as low as 0.007 m. On the real-world 7-part RobotArm dataset, DICArt achieves an average rotation error of 8.2° and translation error of 0.105 m, substantially outperforming A-NCSH (average ~12.4° rotation, ~0.154 m translation).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DICArt outperforms all four ArtImage baselines (A-NCSH, GenPose, OP-Align, ShapePose) on every category in both rotation and translation error. The largest margins appear on Eyeglasses, where DICArt's 5.1°/5.3° child-part rotation error beats A-NCSH's 22.3°/23.2° by more than 17°, and on Drawer, where DICArt's uniform 1.7° rotation error is roughly 1.1°–3.6° lower than the best baseline (A-NCSH at 2.8°–3.9°). On the semi-synthetic ReArtMix dataset, DICArt halves the rotation error of ReArtNet across all five categories (e.g., 1.5° vs 3.4° on Drawer, 2.5° vs 5.1° on Stapler) and reduces translation error by 50–70%. On the real-world 7-part RobotArm dataset, DICArt achieves an average rotation error of 8.2° versus A-NCSH's ~12.4°, with the gap widening on distal parts (Part 7: 15.1° vs 23.5°). Axis descriptor prediction also favors DICArt consistently, e.g., 0.8° angle error on Laptop vs 1.7° for A-NCSH and 3.3° for ShapePose.

## Relation to Prior Work

DICArt builds directly on the discrete diffusion framework of D3PMs (Austin et al., NeurIPS 2021) and the multinomial diffusion reverse-process formulation (Hoogeboom et al., ICLR 2022), extending them with a Gumbel-Softmax reparameterized flow decider (inspired by Zheng et al., 2023) to handle correlated pose tokens. It departs from the dominant continuous-diffusion paradigm for pose estimation (GenPose, DiffPose) by arguing that the discrete, non-uniformly sampled nature of point-cloud inputs is fundamentally mismatched with continuous pose regression. It also moves beyond part-wise independent estimation (A-NCSH, OP-Align) by introducing a hierarchical kinematic coupling mechanism that explicitly encodes parent–child joint constraints, a structural prior that prior category-level articulated pose methods largely ignored.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work offers a principled argument that discretizing the pose space and respecting kinematic structure can simultaneously improve accuracy and robustness to self-occlusion, which is a persistent pain point in articulated object manipulation and tracking. However, the evaluation is limited to relatively small-scale datasets (ArtImage, ReArtMix, a 7-part RobotArm) with a narrow set of baselines, and the method requires known kinematic topology (parent/child assignment, joint type), limiting its applicability to fully unknown articulations. Open questions include whether the discrete binning granularity can be made adaptive, whether the framework scales to objects with dozens of parts or continuous joint spaces, and whether the kinematic coupling can be learned end-to-end rather than specified a priori.
