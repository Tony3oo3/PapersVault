## Problem

Existing hallucination detection methods for (M)LLMs treat the task as standard binary classification on surface features or token probabilities, lacking a theoretical account of why and where hallucinations arise. This paper identifies that hallucinations are not randomly distributed but concentrate at knowledge boundaries—transitional zones between stable factual regions and unstable speculative regions in the model's representation space—and that no prior method explicitly models these stability transitions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261930/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The probe consumes hidden states from three strategically selected transformer layers (early, middle, late) concatenated with an explicit scalar perturbation magnitude δ, computed as 1 − cos(h, h_δ) between the unperturbed and perturbed representation. It produces a single factual-confidence score in [0, 1] via a sigmoid output, where values near 1 indicate a stable, factually grounded state. For MLLMs, the perturbation pipeline additionally injects Gaussian noise (σ = 0.1) into visual features alongside textual perturbations.

## Method

The core architecture is a lightweight probe comprising a transformer-based HiddenProcessor (self-attention over the multi-layer hidden state sequence) followed by a 2-layer feature projector and a 3-layer MLP Classifier. The key training innovation is a composite loss L_total = L_BCE + λ·L_Lyapunov, where the Lyapunov constraint loss penalizes non-negative derivatives ∂V(h,δ)/∂|δ|, enforcing that predicted confidence decays monotonically as perturbation magnitude increases—directly encoding the Lyapunov stability condition. Training proceeds in two stages: first optimizing BCE alone to establish a factual baseline, then gradually increasing λ to impose the monotonicity constraint. Perturbations are multi-scale, combining semantic variations (word substitution, token insertion, structural rephrasing) with representational perturbations (Gaussian noise injected into hidden states), and the probe is evaluated across LLMs (Llama-2/3, Qwen-3, Falcon-7B) and MLLMs (LLaVA-1.5, Qwen-2.5-VL) on eight benchmarks.

## Results

Using AUPRC as the primary metric, Lyapunov Probes achieve an average improvement of 6.2% over the strongest probe-based baseline (Probe [37]) and 18.5% over probability-based methods across four LLMs and four benchmarks. The largest gains appear on open-ended factual QA: on TriviaQA with Llama-3-8B, the method reaches 86.46 AUPRC (vs. 78.82 for Probe), and on PopQA it achieves 67.08 (vs. 60.77). For MLLMs, the method delivers a 2.1% average improvement over base probes, with the most notable gain of 6.2% on VizWiz-VQA for LLaVA-1.5, where degraded visual inputs challenge existing approaches.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Lyapunov Probes consistently outperform all four baselines across nearly every model–dataset combination. The closest competitor is the standard supervised Probe [37], which our method surpasses by an average of 6.2% AUPRC; the gap is largest on TriviaQA (e.g., 86.46 vs. 78.82 for Llama-3-8B) and smallest on MMLU (80.00 vs. 79.26), where the multiple-choice format limits the benefit of stability-based detection. The three probability-based baselines—Verbalized [43], Surrogate [50], and Seq. Prob. [25]—are substantially weaker, particularly on PopQA where they score only 10–27 AUPRC compared to 61–67 for our method, indicating that token-probability and prompt-based confidence signals fail to capture knowledge-boundary instability. On CoQA (dialogue), gains over Probe [37] are modest (1.5% average), likely because multi-turn context provides inherent coherence cues. The only near-tie is Qwen-3-4B on PopQA (64.02 vs. 64.41 for Probe [37]), a 0.4-point margin that is within noise. Overall, the Lyapunov stability constraint provides a clear and consistent advantage over both discriminative probes and uncertainty-scoring methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the probe-based hallucination detection line initiated by Mahaut et al. [37], which trains supervised classifiers on hidden states, by adding a Lyapunov-inspired monotonicity constraint that enforces confidence decay under perturbation. Conceptually, it departs from treating hallucination detection as a standard binary classification problem and instead frames it through dynamical systems stability theory, where factual knowledge corresponds to stable equilibrium regions and hallucinations arise at unstable knowledge boundaries. The perturbation-based training strategy (semantic and representational perturbations with two-stage optimization) is a practical instantiation of this stability framing, building on adversarial robustness ideas but applied to the epistemic rather than adversarial domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The cross-domain generalization results (probes trained on TriviaQA transferring to CoQA and PopQA with only 5–16 point gaps) suggest that stability-based signals capture more universal knowledge-boundary properties than dataset-specific patterns, which is practically valuable for deployment where labeled data per domain is expensive. However, the actual performance gains over the strongest probe baseline are modest (2–8% AUPRC on LLMs, ~2% on MLLMs), and the ablation reveals that the Lyapunov constraint contributes only 3–5 points while multi-layer fusion is the dominant factor, raising the question of whether the dynamical systems framing provides genuine mechanistic insight beyond a well-motivated monotonicity regularizer. Open questions include whether the approach scales to much larger models (70B+), whether the three-region partition (stable known, stable unknown, unstable boundary) can be made more formally operational, and whether the stability signal persists in reasoning models with chain-of-thought generation.
