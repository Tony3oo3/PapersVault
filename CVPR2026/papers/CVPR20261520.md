## Problem

Manual Jiangnan garden construction requires 3–4 expert designers and 3–4 weeks, relying on document search, asset modeling, and specialized layout knowledge. Existing scene generation methods target either indoor rooms or unstructured landscapes and cannot capture the water-centric terrain logic, winding exploratory paths, and culturally grounded aesthetic constraints that define Jiangnan gardens. Additionally, no 3D dataset with stylistic coherence and cultural annotation exists for this domain.

## Input / Output

The system takes a free-form text prompt (e.g., specifying garden style, balance, or atmosphere) and a curated 3D asset library (GardenVerse, 132 assets across Rock/Plant/Architecture) enriched with expert-annotated garden knowledge (visual attributes, spatial compositions, seasonal suitability, contextually appropriate placements). The garden is generated on a 20×15 grid corresponding to a 200×150 m² site, and the output is a complete 3D scene comprising four terrain types, spline-based roads, and object positions/rotations, plus a 2D structural layout export for physical construction. Non-expert users can produce a garden via text input within one minute in a Unity interface.

## Method

GardenDesigner chains four agents that sequentially build the garden: (1) a Terrain Distribution Agent parses the text prompt via GPT-5 into four terrain parameters and runs a genetic algorithm on a 2D grid, guided by a water-centric fitness loss that penalizes insufficient water coverage; (2) a Road Generation Agent scores grid borders using cultural priors (reach most areas, follow boundaries, avoid straightness) and outputs smooth spline curves; (3) an Asset Selection Agent retrieves objects from GardenVerse by querying a vector store of expert-annotated garden knowledge conditioned on area information; (4) a Layout Optimization Agent encodes eight spatial constraints into five semantic categories (Global, Position, Distance, Alignment, Rotation) as differentiable loss terms and solves for object placements via Depth-First Search. The key insight is translating abstract Jiangnan aesthetic principles—water as organizational soul, winding discovery paths, asymmetrical balance, and cultural symbolism—into concrete procedural losses and knowledge-embedded retrieval, rather than relying on learned scene priors.

## Results

GardenDesigner achieves a Pathway Score of 8.1±2.5 versus 0 for the baseline, indicating that key architectures and plants are reachable along generated roads. Class Diversity reaches 68.3±5.6 (vs. 21.8±1.6), reflecting a much wider range of garden object types. The Fractal Dimension of 1.36±0.1 falls within the range of real Jiangnan gardens (1.123–1.329), while CLIP-Score is 27.6±0.1 (vs. 27.4±0.1). On VLM-based aesthetic metrics, GardenDesigner scores 54.2 (CLIP-A), 32.5 (VLM-S), and 53.8 (QA-Quality), all substantially above the baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is Liu et al. [24] (Controllable Procedural Generation of Landscapes, ACM MM 2024), a procedural landscape generator that does not incorporate Jiangnan-specific aesthetic rules or a domain asset library. GardenDesigner decisively outperforms the baseline on Pathway Score (8.1 vs. 0), meaning the baseline produces layouts where key structures are unreachable from roads, and on Class Diversity (68.3 vs. 21.8), showing the baseline generates far fewer object categories. On Fractal Dimension, GardenDesigner scores 1.36 versus 1.42; although numerically lower, the authors argue 1.36 is closer to the measured range of authentic Jiangnan gardens (1.123–1.329), making it a more natural spatial structure. On VLM-based aesthetic evaluation (Table 2), GardenDesigner leads on all three metrics—CLIP-Aesthetic (54.2 vs. 52.9), VLM-Score (32.5 vs. 24.9), and Q-Align QA-Quality (53.8 vs. 43.8)—with the largest gap on VLM-Score, suggesting the chain-of-agents pipeline produces gardens that human-like evaluators perceive as more coherent and aesthetically pleasing. In human evaluation (11 experts + 32 non-experts), GardenDesigner is preferred over the baseline across all four criteria (Overall Quality, Text Relevance, Spatial Layout, Cultural Atmosphere), with selection ratios of 45–74% versus under 10% for the baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of procedural scene generation and LLM-guided layout, but for a culturally specific domain (Jiangnan gardens) that prior work has not addressed. It repurposes established procedural tools—genetic algorithms for terrain, grid-based scoring for paths, and depth-first search for layout—while wrapping them in a chain-of-agents architecture that encodes domain-specific aesthetic principles (water-centricity, winding paths, asymmetrical balance) as explicit computational constraints. The closest prior work, Liu et al. [24], performs LLM-guided procedural landscape generation but targets general terrain without cultural or stylistic constraints; GardenDesigner departs by introducing a knowledge-embedded retrieval mechanism over an expert-annotated asset library (GardenVerse) and five categories of spatial-aesthetic loss functions that have no analogue in prior landscape or indoor scene generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic computational framework for a specific cultural heritage scene type, demonstrating that abstract aesthetic principles can be operationalized as optimization objectives and that expert-annotated knowledge meaningfully improves layout quality over generic LLM reasoning. Practical impact extends to VR/AR tourism, film/game asset creation, and physical garden construction via 2D layout export. However, the evaluation rests on a single external baseline, a modest 132-asset dataset, and a fixed 20×15 grid, leaving open questions about scalability to larger or more diverse garden typologies, robustness to varied user prompts, and whether the chain-of-agents architecture generalizes to other culturally constrained scene types without substantial re-engineering.
