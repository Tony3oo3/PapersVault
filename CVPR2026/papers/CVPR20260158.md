## Problem

Existing causal discovery methods (LiNGAM, NOTEARS, CAM) assume simple parametric noise or linear relationships, making them unreliable on industrial sensor data with multi-modal, heavy-tailed, or time-varying noise distributions. Moreover, these methods produce only point estimates of causal structure without quantifying uncertainty in the discovered relationships. This paper bridges the gap between polynomial chaos uncertainty quantification and causal discovery to handle arbitrary noise distributions while providing principled confidence assessment.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an unlabelled observational data matrix X ∈ R^{N×d} along with user-specified PCE truncation order P, a choice of orthonormal polynomial basis family (Hermite, Legendre, etc.) matched to the expected noise distribution, and sparsity thresholds τ_s and τ_i. It outputs a directed acyclic graph Ĝ with estimated edge coefficients, per-variable PCE noise coefficients, first-order and total-order Sobol sensitivity indices for each edge, and bootstrap confidence intervals (B=150 resamples) on all variance-attribution quantities. The basis type must be selected a priori per variable, and the algorithm operates recursively by peeling off sink nodes, so the computational cost scales with the number of variables and the PCE order.

## Method

PCE-LiNGAM embeds finite-order polynomial chaos expansions directly into the noise terms of structural equations, so each variable's exogenous noise is represented as a sum of orthonormal polynomial basis functions of latent standardized variables. The key identifiability insight is that each variable's noise carries a unique sparse 'fingerprint' in its PCE coefficients, and this fingerprint propagates through the causal graph: if X_i is a parent of X_j, then X_j's global expansion necessarily contains cross-terms involving X_i's noise basis, which cannot be reproduced by any alternative DAG. The practical algorithm (Algorithm 1) recursively identifies sink nodes by finding the variable with the most unique PCE signature, then tests candidate parents via a signature contamination score that measures whether regressing out a candidate parent cleans up the residual's PCE signature relative to the parent's own signature. For uncertainty quantification, the learned PCE coefficients yield closed-form variance decompositions and Sobol indices, with bootstrap resampling providing confidence intervals. When the PCE order is large, sparse PCE with compressed sensing (ℓ₁-regularized projection) keeps the coefficient estimation tractable.

## Results

Across six benchmark datasets (DS1–DS6) with extreme non-Gaussian noise (Cauchy, Weibull, Laplace, mixture-of-Laplacians, etc.), PCE-LiNGAM achieves the highest average F1 of 0.756 ± 0.185 and the lowest average structural Hamming distance (SHD) of 3.7 ± 3.1, outperforming all four baselines on every individual dataset. On the primary illustration dataset DS1 (diamond DAG, 4 variables, four distinct non-Gaussian noise families), PCE-LiNGAM recovers the true graph perfectly with F1 = 1.000 and SHD = 0. On the hardest dataset DS5 (7-node sparse graph, Weibull noise), PCE-LiNGAM achieves F1 = 0.667 while DirectLiNGAM and CAM collapse to F1 = 0.000.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PCE-LiNGAM outperforms all four baselines on every dataset and every metric (F1, Precision, Recall, SHD). The closest competitor is ICA-LiNGAM (F1 = 0.502), which trails by 0.254 F1 points on average and by 0.428 on DS1; ICA-LiNGAM's ICA-based mixing-matrix recovery degrades under the heavy-tailed and multimodal noise distributions that PCE handles natively. NOTEARS (F1 = 0.483) is competitive on precision (0.606) but suffers from low recall (0.412), missing many true edges, and matches PCE-LiNGAM only on the industrial-sensor dataset DS6 (both F1 = 0.571). DirectLiNGAM (F1 = 0.253) and CAM (F1 = 0.274) are substantially outperformed, with both collapsing to F1 = 0.000 on DS5 (7-node Weibull-noise graph), indicating that their iterative exogeneity tests and greedy BIC searches fail under the sparsity and non-Gaussianity conditions PCE-LiNGAM is designed for.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LiNGAM identifiability framework (Shimizu et al., 2006) by replacing the simple non-Gaussian noise assumption with structured Polynomial Chaos Expansion (PCE) representations of noise in structural equations, drawing on the Wiener-Askey polynomial chaos tradition (Wiener, 1938; Xiu, 2002) that has been used extensively for forward uncertainty propagation in engineering but never for the inverse problem of causal discovery. The key conceptual departure is from both neural-network-based structure learners (NOTEARS, DAG-GNN) and Bayesian variational approaches (DiBS, BCD Nets): rather than learning flexible function approximators or assuming parametric noise, PCE-LiNGAM exploits the orthonormal polynomial structure to create traceable 'fingerprints' that propagate through the causal graph, yielding a constructive identifiability proof under sparsity assumptions on chaos coefficients. The method also subsumes LiNGAM (linear, P=0 non-Gaussian) and standard linear Gaussian models as special cases, positioning itself as a unifying generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a genuine gap for industrial applications where sensor noise is multi-modal, heavy-tailed, or time-varying—conditions under which ICA-based and score-based methods degrade substantially. The identifiability theorem (Theorem 2.3) provides theoretical grounding that many competing methods lack, and the PCE representation naturally yields Sobol sensitivity indices and bootstrap confidence intervals without additional modeling assumptions. However, all experiments are on small synthetic datasets (4–7 variables, 1000–1500 samples), and the motivating industrial data (DS6) is still a simulation; no real-world industrial sensor data is evaluated. Open questions include whether the sparsity assumption on dominant chaos coefficients (Assumption 2.2) holds in practice for high-dimensional systems, how the method scales beyond ~10 variables given the combinatorial growth of multivariate PCE terms, and whether the basis-type selection (Hermite vs. Legendre vs. other) can be automated from data.
