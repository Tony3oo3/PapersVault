## Problem

Event-based human action recognition degrades significantly under the combined conditions of low illumination and unconstrained 6-DoF handheld camera motion, yet no existing benchmark jointly captures these challenges with synchronized IMU data, and no effective motion compensation method has been developed for event streams in this regime. Prior work addresses low-light or camera motion in isolation, leaving a substantial performance gap when both are present simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260235/cropped_page0_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes asynchronous event streams from a DAVIS-346 sensor (346×260 resolution) where each event is a quadruple (x, y, t, p) with microsecond-level timestamps, alongside high-frequency IMU readings (3-axis accelerometer and gyroscope) that are extrinsic-calibrated to the camera frame. The IMU data is not fed directly into the recognition network but is used upstream to drive adaptive temporal segmentation and non-linear coordinate warping. The output is a single action class label from 62 categories (30 single-person actions and 32 two-person interactions), predicted via a cross-entropy loss over the pooled HSTS embedding.

## Method

The EIS-HAR framework comprises three sequential stages. First, Adaptive IMU-based Motion Compensation (AIMC) partitions the angular velocity signal into frequency-domain groups using local extrema and median displacement, then applies a non-linear warping function (derived from Euler-angle rotation with a tan-based displacement model) to each event, with a dynamic scaling factor γ_group = γ_min + (γ_max − γ_min)/(a·N_imu + b) that resolves the integer-rounding failure at microsecond intervals. Second, Iterative Greedy Sampling (IGS) selects K keyframes from the dense compensated sequence by iteratively maximizing a composite score S_comb weighted by relevance, quality, temporal uniformity, and visual diversity, with dynamic suppression of redundant or clustered candidates after each selection. Third, the Hybrid Spatio-Temporal Swin Transformer (HSTS) processes the K keyframes through a four-stage architecture where each stage combines a patch-merging layer, a refinement block (parallel 2D spatial and 1D temporal depthwise-separable convolutions), and two hybrid blocks each containing three parallel paths—Swin windowed attention for global structure, a local spatial path, and a local temporal path—fused via learnable scalar weights, ultimately producing a class logit through global average pooling and a linear head.

## Results

On the proposed DarkShake-DVS dataset, EIS-HAR achieves 91.35% top-1 accuracy with AIMC (88.61% without), outperforming the best baseline Swin-T (88.86%) by 2.49 points. On HARDVS, the method reaches 53.21% top-1 / 60.45% top-5, surpassing TSM (52.63%) and Swin-T (51.91%). On DailyDVS-200, it achieves 51.99% top-1 / 78.13% top-5, exceeding Evmamba (49.65%) and Swin-T (48.06%). Notably, SSM-based models (Vision Mamba, VMamba, VideoMamba) degrade severely on DarkShake-DVS, scoring only 61–67%, attributed to their sensitivity to camera jitter.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EIS-HAR outperforms all 20 compared baselines across all three datasets. On DarkShake-DVS, the 2.49-point margin over Swin-T (91.35% vs. 88.86%) is the largest gap among transformer-based methods, while SSM-based models (Vision Mamba 66.77%, VMamba 65.11%, VideoMamba 61.44%) collapse by 20+ points, which the authors attribute to their sensitivity to camera jitter. On HARDVS, the advantage is narrower (53.21% vs. TSM's 52.63%), and on DailyDVS-200 the gap over Evmamba is 2.34 points (51.99% vs. 49.65%). The AIMC motion compensation module provides consistent gains across all baselines (1.5–4.5 points), with TAM benefiting most (+4.52%), confirming that the preprocessing step is broadly applicable. The only exception is VMamba, which slightly degrades with AIMC (67.73→65.11), suggesting its architecture may already partially absorb motion artifacts. In terms of efficiency, the IMU-based compensation completes in 70 ms versus 210 ms (optical flow [11]) and 300 ms (4-DOF [29]), while achieving higher pixel-event density on all three rotation axes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the IMU-based event motion compensation framework of Zhao et al. [55] by introducing an adaptive, frequency-domain segmentation strategy and a dynamic scaling mechanism that addresses the integer-rounding failure mode of fixed-window compensation. The recognition backbone builds on the Video Swin Transformer [26] and depthwise-separable convolutional refinement, while the sampling strategy draws on keyframe selection ideas from video understanding. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior event-based HAR work by treating low-light and 6-DoF ego-motion as a joint, co-occurring challenge rather than separate problems, and by coupling IMU data directly into the compensation pipeline rather than using it only for post-hoc correction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DarkShake-DVS fills a clear gap: no prior event-based HAR benchmark simultaneously captures low illumination, authentic 6-DoF handheld motion, and synchronized IMU data, making it a practical reference for nighttime monitoring, handheld, and drone-mounted deployments. The 70 ms compensation latency is well within real-time budgets, and the finding that SSM-based models (Vision Mamba, VMamba, VideoMamba) degrade substantially under camera jitter—while transformer-based methods remain robust—raises open questions about the temporal inductive biases of state-space architectures under non-stationary ego-motion. Limitations include the constant-depth assumption (no depth sensing), the observation that some action categories remain indistinguishable even after compensation under extreme jitter, and evaluation on only three datasets, leaving generalization to depth-varying or multi-object scenes untested.
