## Problem

Humanoid loco-manipulation from pure RGB perception remains largely unsolved: existing door-opening systems rely on depth sensing, object-centric features, hard-coded motion primitives, or wheeled platforms, and none produce a scalable, generalizable policy for diverse articulated objects. Two fundamental barriers are identified: (i) the learning algorithm must coordinate vision and whole-body control under partial observability across long-horizon, contact-rich tasks, and (ii) the visual sim-to-real gap spans a vast space of appearance and physics variation that demands broad, heterogeneous synthetic data rather than a few curated scenes.

## Input / Output

The student policy consumes a single egocentric RGB frame (processed by a ResNet vision encoder) concatenated with proprioceptive state (joint angles, joint velocities, and root angular velocity), passed through a two-layer LSTM (512 units) and a three-layer MLP (512→256→128) to produce target joint positions for all 43 body and hand joints. These targets are tracked by low-level PD motors at 50 Hz, and the policy is built on top of a pretrained whole-body controller (HOMIE) that handles legged locomotion, so the learned policy focuses on upper-body manipulation and balance coordination during contact.

## Method

DoorMan uses a three-phase teacher-student-bootstrap pipeline in IsaacLab. Phase 1 trains a privileged teacher via PPO with stage-conditioned rewards and a staged-reset exploration scheme: a rolling buffer of 100 simulation snapshots is maintained at each stage transition, and at reset the environment is randomly initialized from a later-stage snapshot, formally reweighting the discounted occupancy measure toward downstream states to overcome the low-probability bridge-crossing problem in long-horizon tasks. Phase 2 distills the teacher into an RGB student via DAgger under aggressive domain randomization (5 door types with randomized dimensions, hinge damping, latch dynamics, handle placement; PBR textures, 5233 dome-light maps, and camera intrinsics/extrinsics). Phase 3 fine-tunes the student with GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) using a binary success signal plus light shaping penalties, allowing the student to discover compensatory behaviors absent from the teacher—such as keeping the handle centered in view and adjusting end-effector poses for visibility—thereby closing the partial-observability gap without additional real-world data.

## Results

DoorMan achieves an 83% success rate on real-world door-opening tasks, matching the expert human teleoperator (80%) and substantially outperforming the non-expert teleoperator (60%). In terms of task fluency, DoorMan completes door-opening interactions 23.8% faster than expert teleoperators and 31.7% faster than non-experts, using the same whole-body controller (HOMIE). In simulation, the best visual randomization configuration (100% PBR textures + dome-light randomization) yields 85.8% (PushLever), 80.8% (PullLever), and 85.0% (PushBar) success rates across the three door sub-tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baselines are human teleoperators using the same HOMIE whole-body controller, split into experts (3+ months full-time experience) and non-experts (<1 day experience). DoorMan matches the expert teleoperator in success rate (83% vs. 80%) while exceeding them by 23.8% in task completion time. Against non-expert teleoperators, DoorMan improves success rate by 23 percentage points (83% vs. 60%) and reduces completion time by 31.7%. The paper attributes the teleoperator gap to difficulty gauging spring-loaded handle forces, tracking the revolving door path under kinematic constraints, and maintaining whole-body balance simultaneously—information that is learnable in simulation but beyond current VR teleoperation feedback. No other learned-policy baselines are directly compared; the paper positions its contribution as the first end-to-end RGB sim-to-real humanoid loco-manipulation policy for articulated objects.

## Relation to Prior Work

DoorMan builds on the HOMIE whole-body controller [4] as a pretrained locomotion backbone, then layers a teacher-student distillation pipeline (PPO teacher → DAgger RGB student) on top, a structure inherited from dexterous manipulation work like Dextrah-RGB [38]. Its key departures are twofold: (i) a staged-reset exploration scheme that exploits simulator recoverability to stabilize long-horizon privileged-policy training, and (ii) repurposing GRPO [36]—originally designed for LLM reasoning—as a lightweight RL fine-tuning step to close the partial-observability gap in the vision student. Unlike prior door-opening systems that rely on depth, object-centric features, hard-coded primitives, or wheeled platforms, and unlike VBC [24] or LeVERB [45] which either decouple locomotion from manipulation or target coarser vision-language tasks, DoorMan produces a unified, end-to-end RGB+proprioception policy for diverse articulated loco-manipulation on a humanoid.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that a humanoid can perform diverse articulated-object loco-manipulation (multiple door types, handles, and latching mechanisms) from pure RGB via sim-to-real RL, outperforming human teleoperators by up to 31.7% in completion time. The staged-reset exploration and GRPO bootstrapping are presented as drop-in techniques applicable to any loco-manipulation task with a nonzero-success base policy, broadening their utility beyond doors. Open questions include reducing dependence on hand-crafted stage-based reward engineering (the authors suggest high-capacity behavioral-cloning teachers as an alternative) and extending the framework to the full range of everyday whole-body interactions (drawers, latches, gates). The 10%-vs-100% texture randomization finding also raises the question of how much visual diversity is truly necessary for robust transfer.
