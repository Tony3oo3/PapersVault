## Problem

Existing fine-grained image retrieval methods rely on one-hot labels derived from category names, which compress rich semantic information into a single global identifier and fail to model comparability among cross-category visual details. This semantically sparse supervision causes local features of visually similar parts across unseen classes to collapse into indistinguishable representations, severely limiting generalization to categories not seen during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262912/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The retrieval model takes RGB images (resized to 256×256, randomly cropped to 224×224 during training) and produces a single embedding vector for cosine-distance-based retrieval. Beyond the image input, the framework consumes category names as semantic anchors: an LLM generates ~20 attribute-oriented descriptions per class, which a frozen CLIP text encoder maps into a shared vision-language embedding space. The final supervision signal is a category-specific linguistic prototype (a d-dimensional vector) constructed from a sparse set of Top-K attributes selected from a dataset-wide vocabulary, replacing the conventional one-hot label.

## Method

LaFG redefines each category name as a semantic anchor rather than an index. A frozen LLM (GPT-4) generates diverse attribute-oriented descriptions per class, which a frozen CLIP text encoder embeds into a vision-aligned space; K-means clustering across all classes then induces a compact attribute vocabulary that simultaneously denoises raw LLM outputs and borrows complementary attributes from visually related categories. A hand-crafted prompt ('a photo of [CLASS]') queries this vocabulary to select Top-K relevant attributes, which are fused via softmax-weighted summation with the class embedding to form a category-specific linguistic prototype. Training uses a symmetric KL-divergence alignment loss between two modality-specific projectors (one for visual embeddings, one for linguistic prototypes) with exponential moving average parameter updates to prevent premature convergence, combined with an auxiliary contrastive loss for cross-category comparability. Crucially, the alignment operates on distributions rather than individual embeddings, allowing retrieval vectors to preserve instance-specific cues while remaining consistent with linguistic attribute descriptions.

## Results

LaFG achieves state-of-the-art Recall@1 of 87.2% on CUB-200-2011, 91.5% on Stanford Cars 196, and 87.1% on Stanford Online Products, all with a ViT backbone. On CUB, it surpasses the previous best ViT-based method DDML (86.0%) by +1.2% and SEE (85.8%) by +1.4%. On Stanford Cars, it leads DDML (89.5%) by +2.0% and DV (90.7%) by +0.8%. On SOP, it outperforms HypViT (85.9%) by +1.2% and DDML (86.1%) by +1.0%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LaFG outperforms all 15 compared baselines on every reported metric across all three benchmarks. On CUB-200-2011, it leads the closest ViT-based competitors DDML (86.0%) and VPTSP-G (86.6%) by +1.2% and +0.6% Recall@1 respectively, while the gap over R50-based methods (e.g., PNCA++ at 72.2%) exceeds 15 points. On Stanford Cars 196, LaFG (91.5%) surpasses DV (90.7%) by +0.8% and DDML (89.5%) by +2.0%, and notably outperforms DPHM (84.1%) by a large margin of +7.4%. On Stanford Online Products, LaFG (87.1%) edges out SEE (86.3%) and DDML (86.1%) by +0.8% and +1.0% Recall@1, and leads HypViT (85.9%) by +1.2%. The consistent gains across fine-grained (CUB, Cars) and coarse-grained (SOP) settings confirm that the language-driven attribute supervision generalizes beyond fine-grained domains.

## Relation to Prior Work

LaFG departs from the dominant paradigm in fine-grained image retrieval, where one-hot category labels serve as the sole supervisory signal (e.g., DDML, NIA, VPTSP-G), by reinterpreting category names as semantic anchors that can be expanded into attribute-level descriptions. It repurposes two existing toolkits—LLMs (GPT-4) for generating fine-grained textual descriptions and frozen VLMs (CLIP) for projecting them into a vision-aligned embedding space—in a pipeline that had not previously been combined for retrieval supervision. The distribution-alignment loss with EMA-stabilized projectors draws on ideas from contrastive and knowledge-distillation literature but applies them to align visual embeddings with language-derived prototypes rather than with teacher networks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the semantic content of category names, when properly expanded and aligned, provides a richer supervisory signal than one-hot labels for cross-category generalization, offering a practical recipe (LLM → VLM → clustering → prototype) that other FGIR methods could adopt. However, the approach depends on access to a powerful LLM for description generation and a frozen VLM for alignment, raising cost and reproducibility concerns; it also remains untested on very large-scale or open-vocabulary settings where the attribute vocabulary would need to be rebuilt. An open question is whether the gains persist when the LLM is replaced by a smaller or open-source model, and whether the dataset-specific vocabulary can be made transferable across domains.
