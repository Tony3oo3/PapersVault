## Problem

Text-based human motion editing must simultaneously modify target regions per a language instruction and preserve unedited regions for temporal coherence, yet existing diffusion-based approaches rely on coarse global conditioning or heuristic similarity cues that cause motion distortion and suboptimal semantic alignment. The core gap is the absence of a unified supervision paradigm that explicitly balances change and invariance from both positive (correct modification, structural preservation) and negative (semantic disambiguation) perspectives.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262523/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a source 3D human motion sequence (207-dim per frame: global velocity, global orientation, joint rotations, local joint positions) and a free-form natural-language editing instruction as input, and produces an edited motion sequence that may differ in length from the source. The editing objective is formalized with a per-frame preservation factor m ∈ [0,1]^F that explicitly interpolates between source frames and edited content, making the change/invariance trade-off a first-class modeling target rather than an implicit side effect.

## Method

OmniME builds on a Fusion Transformer (4 layers) that integrates CLIP-encoded text with source motion features, followed by an 8-block Diffusion Transformer (DiT) for denoising. Three complementary supervision mechanisms are layered on top of the standard diffusion loss: (1) Retrospective Feature Supervision attaches lightweight projection heads to the 2nd, 4th, and 6th DiT blocks, each regressing the full motion via MSE, so intermediate representations are pulled toward the target distribution in a coarse-to-fine manner; (2) a Motion Preservation Mechanism computes frame-wise rotation-and-location similarity with a sliding window, derives a MotionSNR ratio (top-κ vs. bottom-κ similarity frames), and applies an additional reconstruction loss only to high-SNR samples where the edit involves subtle changes; (3) Triplet-based Semantic Alignment mean-pools the final DiT block features into a motion embedding and applies a margin-based triplet loss (α=0.2) against positive and negative CLIP text embeddings to enforce cross-modal correspondence. The total objective sums the diffusion loss, a classification loss, and the three auxiliary losses with learned weights (λ_retro=1, λ_triplet=0.01, λ_preserve=0.2/0.1), trained for 1500 epochs with 300-step cosine-schedule diffusion and two-way guidance (scale 2).

## Results

On the MotionFix dataset, OmniME achieves R@1=77.29% and AvgR=1.79 (batch) and R@1=32.02% and AvgR=13.06 (full test set), outperforming the strongest baseline SimMotionEdit* (R@1=71.04%, AvgR=2.22 batch; R@1=26.88%, AvgR=20.88 test set). On the STANCE Adjustment dataset, OmniME reaches R@1=43.75% and AvgR=4.66 (batch) and R@1=22.45% and AvgR=22.77 (test set), surpassing MotionReFit (R@1=42.45%, AvgR=5.12 batch) and SimMotionEdit* (R@1=36.46%, AvgR=5.71 batch; R@1=12.76%, AvgR=29.05 test set). The headline improvement is a reduction in AvgR from 20.88 to 13.06 on MotionFix and from 29.05 to 22.77 on STANCE Adjustment, indicating substantially better retrieval alignment between edited and target motions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OmniME outperforms all four baselines on every reported metric across both datasets. Against MDM [66] (ICLR'23), which generates motion from text alone without source conditioning, OmniME improves batch R@1 from 4.03% to 77.29% on MotionFix, confirming that source-motion conditioning is essential. Against MDM-BP and TMED [6] (SIGGRAPH Asia'24), which incorporate source motion and text instructions within a diffusion framework, OmniME gains +14.39 and +14.39 points in batch R@1 respectively, and reduces test-set AvgR from 56.63 to 13.06. Against MotionReFit [28] (CVPR'25), which uses dynamic motion augmentation (MCM), OmniME leads by +0.92 in batch R@1 on MotionFix and +1.30 on STANCE Adjustment. The closest competitor is SimMotionEdit* [34] (CVPR'25), which predicts source-target similarity for auxiliary supervision; OmniME still surpasses it by +6.25 in batch R@1 and by 7.82 in test-set AvgR on MotionFix, and by +7.29 in batch R@1 and 6.28 in test-set AvgR on STANCE Adjustment, demonstrating that the combined positive-negative omni-supervision (retrospective features + preservation + triplet alignment) provides a consistent advantage over similarity-based supervision alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

OmniME extends the SimMotionEdit (CVPR 2025) and MotionFix/TMED (SIGGRAPH Asia 2024) line of diffusion-based text-driven motion editing by reframing the supervision design as a unified positive-negative learning paradigm. Where SimMotionEdit introduced a single similarity-based auxiliary loss for motion preservation, OmniME decomposes supervision into three complementary branches: multi-level retrospective feature supervision across DiT transformer blocks (borrowing the retrospective supervision idea from 3D segmentation), a MotionSNR-gated preservation loss, and a triplet contrastive loss for text-motion alignment. The architectural backbone (Fusion Transformer + 8-block DiT with CLIP text encoding) follows the MotionFix/TMED recipe, so the contribution is primarily in the loss design and training strategy rather than in the generative architecture itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, well-motivated recipe for improving diffusion-based motion editing by explicitly separating 'change' supervision (editing the right regions) from 'invariance' supervision (preserving the rest), which is the central unsolved tension in the task. The cross-dataset generalization result (trained on MotionFix, tested on STANCE) suggests the supervision principles are not overfit to a single dataset's characteristics. However, the evaluation is limited to two single-person datasets with relatively small triplet counts (~6.7K and ~4.4K), and the paper does not address multi-person editing, long-horizon temporal consistency, or interactive/iterative editing—areas the authors themselves flag as future work. The triplet loss component contributes the smallest individual gain in the ablation, raising the question of whether negative supervision is truly necessary or merely a regularizer.
