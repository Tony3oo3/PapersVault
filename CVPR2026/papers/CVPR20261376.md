## Problem

Despite CNNs, Vision Transformers, and MLP-Mixers achieving similar aggregate ImageNet accuracies (72–84%), it remains unclear where and why these architecture families systematically diverge in their predictions. Prior work identified trivial and impossible images that fail to differentiate models, but no study has explicitly mapped the subset of images where architectural inductive biases produce structured, family-specific disagreement patterns.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes full softmax probability distributions from 12 pretrained models (7 CNNs, 3 ViTs, 2 MLP-Mixers) run on all 50,000 ImageNet validation images at 224×224 resolution, with no fine-tuning or additional training. Beyond per-image disagreement scores, it produces a Controversy Score quantifying the separation between top-10% and bottom-10% disagreement tails, a pairwise agreement matrix revealing family block structure, an ADER metric measuring concentration of inter-family divergence, and a sequential gating routing decision that adaptively escalates through ConvNeXt-Tiny → ResNet-50 → ViT-Base based on confidence thresholds.

## Method

The core mechanism computes a per-image disagreement score D_i as the standard deviation of maximum softmax probabilities across all 12 models, then partitions images into controversial (top 10% D) and consensus (bottom 10% D) subsets to expose where architectural biases manifest. Pairwise top-1 agreement matrices are analyzed for intra- versus inter-family structure, revealing that CNNs and ViTs form distinct clusters while MLPs show systematically lower alignment (3.3 pp intra-family advantage). The ADER metric (24.6) quantifies that inter-family disagreements on the 10% controversial tail account for 24.6× more total disagreement share than the data fraction alone would predict. Isotonic calibration amplifies the Controversy Score from 4.46 to 18.65, confirming the separation reflects genuine architectural differences rather than miscalibration artifacts. A sequential gating policy leverages this structure to achieve 83.7% accuracy with only 1.18 average model calls (90% compute reduction) by routing to more expensive architectures only when base-model confidence falls below a tunable threshold.

## Results

Across 12 pretrained models (7 CNNs, 3 ViTs, 2 MLPs) on the full ImageNet validation set (50K images), the Controversy Score is 4.46, meaning the top 10% most controversial images exhibit ~4.5× higher mean disagreement (0.255) than the bottom 10% consensus images (0.057); after isotonic calibration this separation amplifies to 18.65×. Mean per-model accuracy is 79.9% (range 72.6–84.0%), yet intra-family agreement (83.5%) exceeds inter-family agreement (80.2%) by 3.3 pp, and the ADER metric of 24.6 shows inter-family disagreements on the 10% controversial tail account for 24.6× more total disagreement share than expected by data share alone. A sequential gating policy (ConvNeXt-Tiny → ResNet-50 → ViT-Base) achieves 83.7% accuracy with only 1.18 average model calls (90% compute reduction) and ECE of 0.028. OOD validation on ImageNet-A (CS₁₀=2.89, ADER₁₀=9.82) and ImageNet-R (CS₁₀=3.34, ADER₁₀=10.14) confirms the controversy-space phenomenon persists under distribution shift.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new architecture but compares 12 pretrained models across three architecture families (CNNs: ResNet-50/101, Wide-ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B0/B2, MobileNetV3-Large, ConvNeXt-Tiny; ViTs: ViT-Base/16, ViT-Small/16, DeiT-Base; MLPs: MLP-Mixer-B/16, gMLP-S16) on identical ImageNet-val inputs. CNNs and ViTs form distinct agreement clusters with high intra-family consistency (ResNet variants >0.88 mutual agreement; ViT-Base and DeiT-Base at 93.4%), while MLP-based models show systematically lower cross-family agreement (MLP-Mixer averages 78.8%, gMLP 83.5%), indicating their lack of local connectivity or global attention creates fundamentally different decision boundaries. ConvNeXt-Tiny achieves the highest overall pairwise agreement (85.3%) and the best single-model accuracy (84.0%), consistent with its hybrid design incorporating transformer-inspired components. Cross-family CNN–ViT agreement averages 84.2%, a 3.3 pp gap below intra-family levels, confirming that architectural inductive biases leave measurable signatures even when aggregate accuracy is comparable. The disagreement–diversity correlation (r=0.82) further shows that controversial images elicit genuinely different class predictions rather than merely split confidence, reinforcing that family diversity—not model scale—drives ensemble complementarity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two prior threads: Meding et al.'s dichotomous data difficulty analysis (which identifies trivial vs. impossible images for individual models) and Conwell et al.'s large-scale finding that training diet dominates architecture for brain alignment. Rather than extending either directly, it repurposes the concept of cross-model disagreement—previously treated as noise or a secondary diagnostic—into a structured forensic framework that maps *where* in the image distribution architectural inductive biases produce divergent predictions. The Controversy Score and ADER metrics are new analytical tools, but the underlying comparison of pretrained model families on ImageNet is a well-trodden empirical setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that architectural choice matters most on a concentrated 10% subset of images, enabling targeted evaluation and cost-effective ensemble strategies (the sequential gating result of 83.7% accuracy at 1.18 model calls is directly deployable). However, the analysis is limited to the ImageNet family (val, -A, -R) and does not isolate *which* visual properties (texture, global context, fine-grained shape) drive the disagreement, leaving the mechanistic explanation open. Broader validation on non-ImageNet datasets and controlled perturbation experiments would be needed to confirm that the controversy space generalizes beyond the 1,000-class taxonomy.
