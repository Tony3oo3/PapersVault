## Problem

Traditional electronic speckle pattern interferometry (ESPI) requires precision phase-stepping hardware, multiple captures per measurement, and assumes static scenes, making it impractical for capturing transient or dynamic surface deformations. This paper asks whether dense, per-pixel displacement and motion can be recovered from a single speckle image without any specialized phase-stepping equipment, while also accounting for motion occurring during the camera's finite exposure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263068/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes two types of monochrome speckle images (1280×1024): a calibration set of at least 5 frames captured with unknown global phase shifts (induced by manually translating one light source on a linear stage), and a single target image of the displaced object. It produces a per-pixel complex phasor whose angle encodes wrapped displacement (convertible to metric units given the optical geometry) and whose magnitude encodes the amount of motion during the exposure. Processing a single 1280×1024 frame takes ~0.15 s in MATLAB or under 5 ms with a parallelized GPU implementation.

## Method

The core insight is that the speckle interferometry intensity model l_jk = a_j cos(b_j − φ_k) + c_j can be written as the inner product of a spatial speckle vector v_j ∈ ℝ³ and a temporal displacement vector u_k ∈ ℝ³, reducing the problem to a rank-3 matrix factorization. During calibration, the J×K intensity matrix is decomposed via SVD into V and U, and the gauge ambiguity is resolved by enforcing the constraints u_k3 = 1 and u_k1² + u_k2² = 1 (unit-circle constraint), recovering the per-pixel speckle vectors without any knowledge of the actual phase shifts. For reconstruction from a single image, the method solves for per-pixel phasor components (u_j1, u_j2) by minimizing a Horn-Schunck-style energy that balances data fidelity (v_j · u_j = l_j) against a spatial smoothness prior, initialized by a locally-constant linear solve and refined with the classic iterative scheme. The phasor angle yields displacement while its magnitude (a sinc or Bessel function of the phase range) quantifies motion during exposure, enabling simultaneous recovery of both quantities from one frame.

## Results

CSPI achieves sub-micrometer per-pixel displacement measurement, verified against a motorized translation stage with 29 nm step-size control and 40 μm travel accuracy. The method captures acoustic vibrations at 1801 fps (recovering piano notes C3–C4 with visible second harmonics matching a reference microphone spectrogram) and mechanical vibrations at 600 fps (tuning fork fundamental at ~286 Hz, Chladni plate modes at 134 Hz and 150 Hz). Processing a 1280×1024 image takes ~0.15 s in MATLAB, ~1 s in Python, and under 5 ms with a parallelized GPU implementation. Increasing the laser baseline from 5.2 cm to 9.2 cm improves sensitivity to sensor noise at the cost of increased phase-wrapping ambiguity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CSPI against two prior ESPI methods, primarily through visual comparison in Fig. 4 rather than quantitative metrics. Against the traditional double-exposure ESPI of Butters & Leendert [1], CSPI produces a full per-pixel displacement map from a single image, whereas the baseline requires two captures and yields only binary fringe patterns (zero-crossings at 2π multiples) that are inherently noisy and require heavy post-processing. Against the DC-(5,1) algorithm of Kao et al. [10], CSPI avoids the per-pixel phase ambiguities visible in Fig. 4(c) and the residual low-quality phase maps of their correlation-based correction in Fig. 4(d); the proposed Horn-Schunck-style reconstruction (Fig. 4(e)–(f)) yields visibly smoother and more accurate displacement estimates. Additionally, unlike Kao et al., CSPI does not require extra optical components for controlled phase shifting and explicitly accounts for motion during the camera's finite exposure via phasor magnitude, a capability neither baseline provides.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets classical Electronic Speckle Pattern Interferometry (ESPI) through a matrix-factorization lens borrowed from uncalibrated photometric stereo, replacing the need for precision phase-stepping hardware with an SVD-based calibration. The reconstruction step directly adapts the Horn-Schunck optical flow energy functional to the phasor domain, a conceptual bridge between two traditionally separate subfields. It departs from prior single-image ESPI methods (e.g., Kao et al.'s (5,1) algorithm) by eliminating extra optical components and by explicitly modeling motion during finite exposure through the phasor magnitude, rather than assuming instantaneous capture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By removing the dependency on precision phase-stepping hardware and enabling single-shot per-pixel displacement and motion recovery, CSPI makes dense interferometric measurement more practical for dynamic and transient events that were previously inaccessible without scanning or long exposures. Key limitations remain: the method requires active coherent illumination, assumes spatially smooth displacement (a strong prior for textured or multi-object scenes), and is bounded by phase-wrapping for large displacements. Open questions include extending the framework to 3D displacement recovery, handling speckle decorrelation over larger motions, and whether the smoothness prior can be relaxed or replaced with learned priors for more complex scenes.
