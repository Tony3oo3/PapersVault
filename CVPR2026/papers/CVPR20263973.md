## Problem

AI systems trained on existing visual physics datasets (typically dozens to thousands of examples with simplified objects and clean backgrounds) cannot learn the breadth of everyday physical phenomena needed for true world simulation. The field lacks a large-scale, high-quality dataset with multiobject interactions, complex backgrounds, and comprehensive ground-truth annotations spanning diverse physical laws, making it impossible to train or rigorously evaluate models on realistic physics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263973/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each of the 153,810 scenes is rendered at 1120×1120 resolution, 30 FPS (60 FPS for 8,780 laser scenes), averaging 5.2 seconds, from 12 fixed cameras (elevation 30°–60°) plus 1 smoothly moving monocular camera, yielding 2 million videos total. Annotations are structured across five axes—geometry (3D meshes, trajectories), semantics (per-frame 2D masks), motion (dynamic object paths), physical properties (material parameters like Young's modulus, viscosity, friction angle), and text (manually proofread ~64-word descriptions). For downstream tasks, video generation consumes a text prompt plus initial frame, future prediction consumes a video clip, property estimation consumes multiview video observations, and motion transfer pairs a source video with a target image.

## Method

The dataset is constructed by first identifying 71 basic physical phenomena across mechanics, optics, fluid dynamics, and magnetism, then composing them into 3,284 multiphysics activities (71 single, 943 double, 2,270 triple). Scenes are built from 2,231 real-world 3D objects, 623 materials, and 528 backgrounds, then simulated using UE5 Chaos Physics for rigid-body dynamics, MPM (Taichi) for deformable and granular objects, and SPH (Doriflow) for liquids. For applications, the paper introduces a Physical Motion Fidelity (PMF) metric based on DFT energy comparison between generated and reference videos, fine-tunes SVD/CogVideoX/Wan2.2 via LoRA, SFT, and final-layer tuning on 83,650 text-video pairs, and benchmarks six future-prediction methods, two property-estimation methods (PAC-NeRF, GIC), and two motion-transfer methods across seen and novel viewpoints.

## Results

Fine-tuning SVD with SFT on PhysInOne improves PMF from 2.753 to 3.147 and reduces FVD from 203 to 143, while Wan2.2-5B SFT boosts PMF from 2.041 to 2.978 and FVD from 258 to 190, confirming the dataset's value for physics-aware video generation. For long-term future frame prediction, DefGS achieves the best seen-viewpoint PMF (3.980) and PSNR (22.85), but all 4D methods degrade substantially under novel viewpoints (e.g., DefGS drops to PMF 3.347, PSNR 17.95). Physical property estimation remains highly inaccurate: GIC estimates log₁₀(E) for elastic solids at 49.76±8.78 vs. PAC-NeRF's 117.18±68.44, and both methods fail to reliably recover viscosity and bulk modulus for fluids. Motion transfer methods (MotionPro PMF 3.484, GoWithTheFlow PMF 3.309) maintain visual fidelity (PSNR ~19–20) but fail to accurately propagate complex multi-object physical motions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among video generation baselines, SVD+SFT achieves the highest overall PMF (3.147) and lowest FVD (143), while Wan2.2-5B+LoRA shows the largest relative PMF gain (+0.744) and human rating jump (2.26→4.80) over its base model; CogVideoX+LoRA shows negligible improvement (PMF 2.869 vs. 2.877), suggesting limited benefit from physics data for that architecture. For future frame prediction, DefGS and FreeGave lead among 4D methods on seen viewpoints (PMF 3.980 and 3.897 respectively), while MAGI-1 achieves the best overall PMF (4.086) but only for seen views; all methods degrade 15–30% under novel viewpoints, exposing a critical gap. In physical property estimation, GIC outperforms PAC-NeRF on elastic solids (log₁₀(E): 49.76 vs. 117.18) and Newtonian fluid viscosity (log₁₀(μ): 8.78 vs. 42.64), but PAC-NeRF is more accurate for non-Newtonian fluid yield stress (log₁₀(τ_γ): 339.20 vs. 28.78); both methods achieve resimulation PSNR of 24–27, indicating partial but incomplete property recovery. For motion transfer, MotionPro slightly edges GoWithTheFlow on PMF (3.484 vs. 3.309) and PSNR (20.28 vs. 18.98), yet both fail to faithfully transfer complex multi-object physical dynamics, confirming that current optical-flow-based approaches are insufficient for physics-rich motion propagation.

## Relation to Prior Work

PhysInOne extends the synthetic-physics-dataset paradigm established by CLEVRER, Physion, ShapeStacks, and similar works, but departs from them in scale and complexity by orders of magnitude: where prior datasets cover 1–12 phenomena with simplified objects on clean backgrounds, PhysInOne instantiates 71 phenomena across 153,810 multiobject, multiphysics scenes with realistic 3D assets and complex backgrounds. The simulation pipeline repurposes existing engines (UE5 Chaos Physics, Taichi MPM, Doriflow SPH) rather than introducing new physics solvers, and the application experiments fine-tune or evaluate existing video-generation, 4D-reconstruction, and property-estimation models (SVD, CogVideoX, Wan2.2, DefGS, PAC-NeRF, GIC, etc.) on the new data. Conceptually, the paper positions itself as the 'ImageNet moment' for visual physics, filling the data-scarcity gap that has kept prior physics-learning models confined to toy scenarios.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

At 2 million annotated videos spanning 71 phenomena, PhysInOne is the largest visual-physics dataset by a wide margin and is likely to become the default training/evaluation substrate for physics-aware video generation, world models, and embodied AI. The experiments reveal a persistent gap: even after fine-tuning strong foundation models, physical plausibility remains far from satisfactory for mechanics and optics, and property estimation / motion transfer methods fail on complex multiobject scenes, underscoring that scale alone does not close the physics-reasoning gap. Open questions include whether the synthetic-to-real transfer gap (UE5-rendered vs. real-world physics) limits downstream utility, whether the PMF metric generalizes beyond the controlled test setting, and whether the 8:1:1 split with asset-level isolation is sufficient to prevent subtle leakage across the 2,231 objects.
