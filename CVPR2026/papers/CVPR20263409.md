## Problem

Instruction-tuning VLMs is expensive, requiring large annotated datasets and substantial compute, yet it remains unclear whether the full corpus is necessary. Prior data-efficient selection methods are static (one-time, non-adaptive), rely on auxiliary reference VLMs, assume full ground-truth annotations upfront, or incur prohibitive gradient-computation costs. The paper asks whether a VLM can indicate what it can most effectively learn at a given training stage, enabling dynamic, self-paced sample selection without external supervision.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an unlabeled pool of image-question pairs (I, Q) and a fixed answer budget b (typically 16–20% of the pool size). Answers A are queried only on a need basis for the selected samples, rather than being available for the entire dataset upfront. The trained VLM produces a text answer for a new image-question pair, evaluated across 14 multimodal benchmarks spanning perception, OCR, reasoning, multilingual understanding, and scientific diagrams.

## Method

PROGRESS is a two-stage pipeline. First, Multimodal Concept Categorization extracts features via a frozen DINO vision encoder and BERT question encoder, concatenates and L2-normalizes them, then applies spherical k-means to partition the unlabeled pool into K concept clusters in a fully unsupervised, model-agnostic manner. Second, Prioritized Concept Learning tracks the relative change in per-cluster accuracy between training steps (Δ_k = (Acc_k^(t) − Acc_k^(t−γ)) / (Acc_k^(t−γ) + ε)) and samples from clusters using a temperature-controlled softmax over Δ_k, balancing informativeness (high-improvement clusters) against diversity (broad skill coverage). A small random exploration fraction (δ%) prevents mode collapse. Annotations are queried only for the selected samples, and the model is fine-tuned with LoRA on this growing labeled set, yielding a self-paced curriculum that controls both which skills are acquired and the order in which they are learned.

## Results

On LLaVA-665K with a 20% data budget, PROGRESS (accuracy objective) achieves 98.8% relative performance for LLaVA-v1.5-7B, outperforming all baselines including COINCIDE (97.8%) and Random (95.0%), while requiring only 20% annotations versus 100% for COINCIDE. For the larger LLaVA-v1.5-13B model, PROGRESS reaches 96.8% relative performance, again the best among all methods. On the VisionFlan-191K dataset under a stricter 16.7% budget, PROGRESS achieves 99.0% relative performance, surpassing COINCIDE (95.8%) and Random (95.0%). PROGRESS also exceeds full-data finetuning on several individual benchmarks (VizWiz, SQA-I, MME, ChartQA, CMMMU) despite using only 20% of the data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PROGRESS (98.8% Rel.) outperforms all eight baselines on the primary LLaVA-665K / LLaVA-v1.5-7B setup. The closest competitor is COINCIDE (97.8%), which requires a separately trained auxiliary VLM (TinyLLaVA) and 100% ground-truth annotations, whereas PROGRESS uses only 20% need-based labels and no auxiliary model. Among static scoring methods, EL2N (93.4%) and Perplexity (91.1%) trail PROGRESS by 5.4 and 7.7 percentage points respectively; CLIP-Score (90.6%) and Self-Filter (90.1%) are even further behind. Deduplication-based SemDeDup (94.1%) and graph-based D2-Pruning (94.8%) also fall short by 4–5 points. Random sampling (95.0%) serves as a strong diversity baseline but still lags PROGRESS by 3.8 points. Notably, PROGRESS exceeds full-data finetuning on five individual benchmarks (VizWiz 53.4 vs 47.8, SQA-I 69.9 vs 68.4, MME 1483.2 vs 1476.9, ChartQA 17.3 vs 16.4, CMMMU 24.6 vs 22.1), suggesting that progress-driven selection can sharpen niche skills that uniform training dilutes.

## Relation to Prior Work

PROGRESS builds directly on COINCIDE (Lee et al., EMNLP 2024), which introduced concept-skill transferability-based coreset selection for VLMs, and adopts its warmup sampling strategy using transferability and density metrics. However, it departs from COINCIDE in three fundamental ways: (1) replacing static one-shot selection with dynamic, progress-driven sampling that adapts to the model's evolving learning state; (2) eliminating the need for an auxiliary reference VLM by using unsupervised DINO+BERT features for clustering; and (3) operating under a need-based annotation budget rather than assuming full ground-truth labels. The method also draws on self-paced learning and curriculum learning principles, but is the first to apply these ideas to large-scale VLM instruction tuning using the model's own relative-error signal as the pacing mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: reducing annotation cost by 80% while achieving 98–100% of full-data performance addresses a major bottleneck for academic labs and smaller organizations that cannot afford large-scale labeling or proprietary model queries. The curriculum analysis revealing that the model naturally consolidates prerequisite skills (e.g., OCR before translation/code) before tackling harder abilities provides actionable insight into how VLMs should be trained. Open questions include whether the approach extends to pretraining or continued pretraining settings, how it behaves with fundamentally different architectures (e.g., diffusion-based VLMs), and whether the modest ~1% gap over COINCIDE widens or narrows at even larger scales. The reliance on an LLM judge for accuracy-based variants also introduces a practical dependency that the loss-based variant avoids but at the cost of slightly lower performance.
