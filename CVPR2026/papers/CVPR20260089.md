## Problem

Existing human activity recognition (HAR) datasets for industrial environments suffer from three critical limitations: they lack truly synchronized multimodal data combining wearables, vision, and audio; they rely on highly constrained, protocol-driven tasks that fail to capture the open-ended, procedural nature of real industrial work; and they focus on short, isolated actions rather than extended multi-step workflows. OpenMarcie addresses these gaps by providing the largest multimodal dataset for industrial action monitoring, featuring 37+ hours of synchronized egocentric/exocentric video, wearable sensors, and audio across two contrasting assembly scenarios.

## Input / Output

The dataset provides 282 raw channels across 17 modalities at 9 body positions, including wrist IMUs, chest-mounted LiDAR depth, stereo microphones, thermal cameras, and spectrometers, all temporally aligned with egocentric RGB-D video and three exocentric ZED X AI stereo cameras. For the HAR benchmark, inputs are 12-second windows of egocentric video, right-hand IMU, and instrumental sound, producing one of 12 activity class labels under subject-disjoint splits. Captioning outputs are free-form natural language sentences decoded from predicted sentence embeddings via Vec2Text retrieval, while cross-modal alignment produces shared embedding vectors enabling retrieval across any modality pair.

## Method

The core contribution is a dataset with a two-stage annotation pipeline: human annotators produce intent-aware verb-object-tool labels (supporting concurrent multi-label actions like 'walking while carrying'), which are then refined into persistent hard labels via Deepseek-r1 for class identification and GPT-4o for structured-to-caption translation, validated by bidirectional consistency checks (Macro F1=0.715, METEOR=0.531). For benchmarking, HAR uses modality-specific encoders (ViT for video, DeepConvLSTM for IMU, EnCodec for audio) fused via a late-fusion transformer; captioning regresses sentence embeddings from narrations using the OV-HAR framework with Vec2Text decoding; and cross-modal alignment employs ImageBind-inspired contrastive learning with multi-modal InfoNCE loss over synchronized positive pairs. The two experimental settings—ad-hoc bicycle assembly (12 participants, open-ended) and sequential collaborative 3D printer assembly (24 valid participants, instruction-guided with handoff between workers)—capture both goal-oriented problem-solving and procedural knowledge acquisition with error correction.

## Results

On the primary HAR task (12-class, subject-disjoint), Inertial+Vision (I+V) achieves the highest Macro F1 of 0.882 (Scenario a) and 0.773 (Scenario b) without the null class, outperforming all other modality combinations. Inertial alone is the strongest single modality (0.834 / 0.750), while Acoustic alone is the weakest (0.489 / 0.425). For open-vocabulary captioning, I+V again leads with cosine similarity of 0.561 (Scenario a) and 0.655 (Scenario b). In cross-modal alignment, I+V+Text yields the best retrieval performance (R@1 = 0.485, R@5 = 0.803, Top-1 = 0.587 in Scenario a; R@1 = 0.467, R@5 = 0.787, Top-1 = 0.570 in Scenario b).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a dataset paper, so comparisons are between modality configurations rather than external methods. Inertial+Vision (I+V) consistently dominates all other pairwise and triple combinations on HAR, captioning, and cross-modal alignment across both scenarios, achieving 0.882 Macro F1 (Scenario a) versus 0.859 for the full I+A+V fusion, indicating that adding Acoustic to the I+V pair actually degrades performance. Acoustic alone is the weakest modality in every setting (0.489 Macro F1 on HAR, 0.361 cosine similarity on captioning), and adding it to other modalities provides only marginal or negative gains. Vision alone (0.757) substantially outperforms Acoustic alone (0.489) on HAR, a gap of ~34 percentage points, underscoring the limited discriminative power of instrumental sound in this controlled test-bench environment. The authors note that the absence of authentic industrial noise (machinery, vibrations) likely suppresses audio's contribution, and that audio becomes more useful in multimodal fusion contexts. No external HAR or captioning baselines from prior literature are directly compared; the evaluation focuses on establishing per-modality and fusion baselines for the community.

## Relation to Prior Work

OpenMarcie positions itself against a lineage of industrial HAR datasets (InHARD, LARa, OpenPack, Assembly101, HA4M, HA-ViD, IndustReal) that individually address subsets of sensing modalities or task types but none combine wearables, egocentric+exocentric video, multi-action labels, and full industrial coverage. Methodologically, the benchmarking pipeline draws on OV-HAR [22] for open-vocabulary captioning via embedding regression, ImageBind [13] for contrastive cross-modal alignment with InfoNCE loss, and standard per-modality encoders (ViT, DeepConvLSTM, EnCodec) fused via a late-fusion transformer. The annotation pipeline is novel in its use of LLMs (Deepseek-r1, GPT-4o) as structured translators from human narration to hard labels, a partially supervised approach not seen in prior industrial datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OpenMarcie fills a critical gap by providing the first truly multimodal, multi-action industrial dataset with both ad-hoc and procedural task structures, enabling research on procedural knowledge acquisition, collaborative assembly, and sensor substitution that prior single-modality or scripted-action datasets cannot support. Key limitations include collection in a controlled test-bench rather than a real factory (lacking authentic industrial noise), a predominantly right-handed engineering cohort, and the finding that audio contributes minimally to recognition performance in this setting. Open questions include whether the LLM-assisted annotation pipeline generalizes to noisier real-world narrations, how performance shifts under genuine factory conditions, and whether the multi-action labeling scheme scales to more complex concurrent workflows.
