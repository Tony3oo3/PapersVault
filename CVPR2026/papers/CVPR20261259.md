## Problem

Multimodal survival prediction models assume complete data availability, yet clinical settings frequently lack one or more modalities (e.g., expensive genomic profiling or missing tissue samples). Existing missing-modality methods—feature alignment, imputation, and joint distribution learning—enforce output similarity or synthesize pseudo-features without explicitly identifying what information is uniquely modality-specific versus what is cross-modally inferable, leaving no principled mechanism for deterministic recovery of shared information.

## Input / Output

The method consumes whole-slide images at 20× magnification (patched and encoded via UNI2 into D=256-dim tokens) and genomic profiles (RNA-seq, CNV, SNV) organized into six cancer-hallmark functional groups processed by group-specific MLPs into the same 256-dim token space. It outputs discrete hazard probabilities over K time intervals and a scalar survival risk score via a lightweight MLP head, trained with a negative log-likelihood that naturally handles both censored and uncensored observations.

## Method

MUST decomposes each modality's global representation into a modality-specific component and a cross-modal shared component through bidirectional cross-attention and self-attention with modality-specific class tokens, all projected into a learned low-rank shared subspace (rank r=64 in D=256). Three algebraic constraints—shared consistency (c_P←G = c_G←P), inter-modal orthogonality (u_P ⊥ u_G), and intra-modal orthogonality (u_m ⊥ c_m)—enforce that the shared component is deterministically recoverable from any available modality via simple subtraction (c = g_P − u_P). Training proceeds in two stages: a warm-up phase with Gaussian noise injection to learn task-relevant encoders, followed by joint optimization of the survival loss with decomposition, shared-consistency, and orthogonality losses. For missing modalities, the shared component is recovered algebraically, and the truly modality-specific residual is generated by a conditional latent diffusion model (4-layer transformer, 50-step DDIM) conditioned on the recovered shared component and the structural class token, isolating stochastic generation to only the irreducible modality-specific information.

## Results

On five TCGA cancer datasets, MUST achieves a state-of-the-art overall C-index of 0.742 with complete pathology and genomics data, outperforming the best multimodal baseline CMTA (0.724) by 2.5%. Under missing genomics, MUST retains a C-index of 0.716 (3.5% degradation), surpassing LD-CVAE (0.697) and SMIL (0.624). Under missing pathology, MUST achieves 0.739 (only 0.4% degradation), a scenario that LD-CVAE cannot handle at all due to its unidirectional architecture.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MUST outperforms all nine baselines across every evaluation scenario. Against unimodal baselines (ABMIL, TransMIL for pathology; MLP, SNN for genomics), MUST gains 6.0–6.6% C-index on complete data, confirming the value of multimodal integration. Among complete-data multimodal methods, MUST beats CMTA (0.742 vs. 0.724, +2.5%) and SurvPath (0.742 vs. 0.713, +2.9%), with the paper attributing the gap to CMTA's lack of explicit algebraic decomposition that prevents modality collapse. For missing genomics, MUST (0.716) surpasses LD-CVAE (0.697, +1.9%), M³Care (0.689, +2.7%), ShaSpec (0.676, +4.0%), and SMIL (0.624, +9.2%); the large SMIL gap is attributed to implicit similarity enforcement being insufficient without explicit identification of missing information. For missing pathology, MUST (0.739) leads SMIL (0.687, +5.2%), M³Care (0.683, +5.6%), and ShaSpec (0.676, +6.3%), while LD-CVAE cannot handle this scenario at all due to its unidirectional architecture. Notably, MUST shows asymmetric degradation (0.4% for missing pathology vs. 3.5% for missing genomics), with some datasets (BRCA, GBMLGG, LUAD) even improving under missing pathology, which the authors attribute to LDM denoising filtering high-frequency noise from noisy WSI patches.

## Relation to Prior Work

MUST extends the multimodal survival prediction line (SurvPath, CMTA, Pathomic Fusion) by adding an explicit algebraic decomposition layer that prior cross-attention methods lack, and it departs from existing missing-modality approaches (SMIL, M³Care, ShaSpec, LD-CVAE) by enforcing algebraic invertibility in a learned low-rank shared subspace rather than relying on implicit alignment or joint posterior learning. The disentanglement literature (Lee & Pavlovic, Daunhawer et al.) informs the shared/specific split, but MUST's key distinction is that the decomposition is algebraically recoverable—shared components are deterministically derivable from any available modality—rather than merely statistically separated for interpretability. The use of conditional latent diffusion to generate only the truly modality-specific residual (conditioned on recovered shared information and structural class tokens) is a novel application of LDMs in learned embedding space for medical multimodal tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a clinically critical gap: genomic profiling is expensive and historical cohorts often lack molecular data, yet existing multimodal survival models degrade severely or simply cannot operate when a modality is absent. MUST's algebraic framework provides a principled, deterministic recovery path for shared information, isolating stochastic generation to the irreducible modality-specific residual, which is a cleaner inductive bias than prior imputation or alignment strategies. Key limitations include the requirement for paired training data to enforce strict algebraic constraints (though preliminary results with 50% unpaired data are promising), and the asymmetric degradation pattern (0.4% missing pathology vs. 3.5% missing genomics) suggests the framework's benefits are modality-dependent. Open questions include extending the algebraic decomposition to more than two modalities, handling training-time missingness more robustly, and validating the approach on larger, more diverse clinical cohorts beyond TCGA.
