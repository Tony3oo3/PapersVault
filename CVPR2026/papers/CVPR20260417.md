## Problem

Existing test-time adaptation methods for 3D human pose prediction treat the full body as a homogeneous entity, applying uniform parameter updates across all anatomical segments. However, domain shifts in human motion are inherently heterogeneous—distinct body parts (arms, legs, torso) exhibit divergent kinematic signatures and shift at different rates. This anatomical-agnostic adaptation causes over-adaptation of stable regions and under-adaptation of shift-prone articulations, degrading prediction quality under continuously evolving target distributions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a 1-second sequence of 17-joint 3D coordinates (normalized to [-1,1]) as input and predicts the next 1-second pose sequence in the same format. It operates in a continual test-time adaptation setting where the model processes a stream of incoming test batches sequentially, adapting parameters between batches before making predictions. The backbone is the siMLPe MLP architecture, and the model is initially trained in a supervised manner on the source domain (H3.6M) before being deployed for adaptation.

## Method

TT-HA decomposes the siMLPe backbone's parameters into five anatomical subsets (left/right arms, left/right legs, torso) plus a shared set using an information-theoretic stability metric that measures each parameter's KL-divergence response to perturbations relative to each body segment. During inference, it tracks per-segment domain dynamics via instance normalization statistics maintained with exponential moving average, computing Earth Mover's Distance between consecutive Gaussian distributions to quantify segment-wise shift magnitude. A z-score-based peak detection algorithm on a sliding window identifies sudden domain changes: for major shifts, only the affected segment's parameters are restored to source-trained values (selective knowledge restoration), while for minor shifts, segment-specific parameters are fine-tuned using self-supervised temporal consistency and spatial bone-length losses. This dual strategy ensures targeted adaptation without full parameter resetting, preserving stable regions while correcting shift-prone articulations.

## Results

Under the standard evaluation setup (Setup-1), TT-HA achieves the best MPJPE across all four datasets at the 1000 ms horizon: 97.4 mm on H3.6M, 68.5 mm on CMU MoCap, 127.5 mm on GRAB, and 119.4 mm on RICH, outperforming the prior SoTA HoCoTTA by 4.0, 2.6, 4.3, and 7.8 mm respectively. The largest relative gain appears on RICH (6.1% MPJPE reduction over HoCoTTA), where scene-contact interactions create the most pronounced anatomical domain shifts. TT-HA also leads on P-MPJPE (e.g., 95.5 mm vs. 108.8 mm on RICH) and PCK@150 (78.7% vs. 73.1% on RICH), confirming consistent improvements in both absolute and Procrustes-aligned accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TT-HA outperforms all five baselines on every dataset and metric. Against the closest competitor HoCoTTA (the prior SoTA CTTA method), TT-HA reduces MPJPE by 4.0 mm on H3.6M, 2.6 mm on CMU MoCap, 4.3 mm on GRAB, and 7.8 mm on RICH at the 1000 ms horizon; the P-MPJPE gap on RICH is even larger (13.3 mm), indicating that anatomical-targeted adaptation particularly benefits Procrustes-aligned accuracy where global pose errors are removed. Against the non-TTA baselines (LTD, PGBIG, siMLPe), the advantage is substantially wider—e.g., 16.9 mm MPJPE reduction over LTD on RICH—confirming that test-time adaptation is essential for cross-distribution prediction. On the most challenging cross-dataset setup (H3.6M → GRAB, Setup-4), TT-HA also leads HoCoTTA by 2.8–4.4 mm MPJPE across all five GRAB action categories, with the largest margin on 'drinking' (4.2 mm at 1000 ms), where arm-specific domain shifts are most pronounced. TT-HA additionally achieves the highest PCK@150 on every dataset (e.g., 78.7% vs. 73.1% on RICH), demonstrating that the anatomical decomposition translates into more joints falling within the 150 mm accuracy threshold.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends HoCoTTA (ECCV 2024), the sole existing continual test-time adaptation framework for 3D human pose prediction, by challenging its assumption of full-body homogeneous adaptation. While HoCoTTA partitions parameters into globally 'sensitive' vs. 'invariant' groups, TT-HA argues that domain shifts in human motion are anatomically localized—certain segments (e.g., arms during object interaction) shift while others (e.g., legs) remain stable—and decomposes parameters into five anatomical subsets plus a shared set using an information-theoretic sensitivity metric. The method also departs from prior TTA work (TENT, NOTE, TTN) that relies on batch normalization statistics, replacing them with instance normalization and Earth Mover's Distance for per-segment, per-sample domain monitoring.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The anatomical heterogeneity insight provides a principled rationale for why uniform full-body adaptation can over-adapt stable segments and under-adapt shift-prone ones, offering a more targeted adaptation strategy for real-world deployment where domain shifts are non-uniform. However, the fixed five-segment partition may not capture all relevant anatomical boundaries (e.g., hands, feet, head), and the approach is validated primarily on a single MLP backbone (siMLPe), leaving open whether the decomposition generalizes to GCN or transformer architectures. The empirically tuned peak-detection threshold (12 standard deviations) and the reliance on a single momentum coefficient for IN statistics also raise questions about robustness across diverse deployment scenarios.
