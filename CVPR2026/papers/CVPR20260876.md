## Problem

Existing red-teaming methods for generative models (LLMs, VLMs, text-to-image) treat outputs as static and cannot capture safety risks that emerge from temporal dynamics in video generation. A textually innocuous prompt can elicit a policy-violating video when harmful content is distributed across a sequence of individually benign events whose temporal composition reconstructs the unsafe action. This paper addresses the gap by proposing the first automated red-teaming framework specifically designed to exploit temporal vulnerabilities in T2V models.

## Input / Output

The system takes a seed prompt (harmful or safe) and a target harmful category as input, and produces a ~100-token adversarial text prompt structured as a chronological sequence of individually benign event descriptions connected by temporal markers (e.g., 'First,' 'After two seconds'). The generated prompt must pass all three textual safety filters (Toxicity Detector, NSFW detector, LlamaGuard) while causing the target T2V model to produce a 5–8 second video (at resolutions from 1024×704 to 1360×768) that is classified as unsafe by a GPT-4o-based video judge. Generation uses beam search with beam width 16.

## Method

TEAR formulates T2V red-teaming as a Markov Decision Process and optimizes a Llama-3-based generator (LoRA) in two stages. First, the generator is supervised-fine-tuned on a rule-based dataset where harmful prompts are decomposed via three rewriting rules—temporal deconstruction, sequential enforcement, and temporal-space synthesis—into sequences of benign descriptions whose composition reconstructs the unsafe action. Second, the generator undergoes temporal-aware online preference learning via PPO with two rewards: a prompt-level reward combining a hate-speech classifier score and cosine similarity to reference temporal-pattern embeddings, and a temporal consistency reward measuring global video-text alignment and inner temporal coherence of the generated video using pre-trained video encoders. A KL penalty regularizes against the initial generator to prevent over-optimization. A Qwen-3-VL refine model then iteratively revises generated prompts using structured feedback (quantitative scores, qualitative explanations, actionable suggestions) from both the prompt and video judgment systems, closing the loop until the red-teaming objective is met.

## Results

TEAR achieves an Attack Success Rate (ASR) of 82.3% on Hunyuan-Video and 80.5% on Wan 2.2, substantially outperforming the best baseline FLIRT (57.2% and 56.4% respectively). The method simultaneously maintains high prompt safety, with NSFW filter pass rates of 96.5% (Hunyuan-Video) and 97.4% (Wan 2.2), confirming that generated prompts remain textually innocuous. In a seed-free setting, TEAR still achieves 79.2% and 76.9% ASR, and transferability across five T2V models yields an average cross-model ASR of 76.4%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TEAR decisively outperforms all four baselines on both open-source T2V models. Against FLIRT (the strongest baseline), TEAR gains +25.1 pp ASR on Hunyuan-Video (82.3% vs. 57.2%) and +24.1 pp on Wan 2.2 (80.5% vs. 56.4%). Against ART, the margins are +29.7 pp and +30.8 pp respectively. The T2V-specific baselines (T2VSafetyBench at 40.8%/37.2% and UVD at 29.0%/31.0%) lag far behind, confirming that static-image red-teaming strategies are poorly suited to temporal video dynamics. Notably, TEAR also achieves the highest prompt pass rates (~95–97%) among all methods that exceed 50% ASR, whereas FLIRT and T2VSafetyBench sacrifice prompt safety (PSR ≈ 50–54%) to achieve their lower attack rates. This dual superiority in both attack effectiveness and prompt stealthiness is the paper's central empirical claim.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the automated red-teaming paradigm—well established for LLMs (GPTFuzzer, DiverCT), VLMs (TRUST-VLM), and text-to-image models (ART, FLIRT)—to the text-to-video domain, where the key departure is the explicit modeling of temporal dynamics. Prior T2V safety work (T2VSafetyBench, UVD) is limited to static evaluation or treats video as a sequence of independent frames, lacking mechanisms to exploit the fact that individually benign events can compose into harmful sequences over time. Methodologically, TEAR repurposes the PPO-based preference learning framework from LLM alignment (RLHF) and combines it with a rule-based temporal decomposition strategy (deconstruction, sequential enforcement, temporal-space synthesis) that has no direct analogue in prior red-teaming literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that commercial T2V services achieve ~98% prompt-filter pass rates while still yielding >85% attack success rates exposes a fundamental architectural gap: current safety filters operate on static text or individual frames and are blind to temporal aggregation attacks. The high cross-model transferability (76.4% average ASR) suggests a shared, structural vulnerability in how T2V models process sequential prompts, implying that per-model safety tuning is insufficient. Open questions include whether temporal-aware safety filters can be retrofitted to existing T2V pipelines, whether the vulnerability persists for longer videos (>8 seconds) where more complex event chains are possible, and whether the specific three-rule temporal decomposition is the only effective attack surface or one of many.
