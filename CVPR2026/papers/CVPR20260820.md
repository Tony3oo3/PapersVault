## Problem

Existing GCD pipelines rely primarily on visual features and treat supervised learning on known categories and discovery of novel categories as loosely coupled or disjoint processes. This decoupling weakens the transfer of prior knowledge to unlabeled data and produces brittle boundaries between visually similar yet semantically distinct categories, especially on fine-grained benchmarks. The paper asks whether the human cognitive strategy of analogical reasoning—retrieving relevant prior concepts and drawing structural analogies—can be formalized to sharpen novel-category separation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a labeled set of images with known-category labels, an unlabeled set containing both known and novel categories, and short textual descriptions of each known category (fed through a CLIP text encoder). The ATCG is a plug-and-play module that attaches to existing GCD pipelines (SimGCD-CLIP, CMS-CLIP, SelEx-CLIP) without altering their overall architecture; it produces per-sample analogical text embeddings that are linearly blended with visual embeddings (via a tunable coefficient α) and projected through a small MLP to yield the final fusion embedding used for contrastive learning and parametric classification. The backbone is CLIP ViT-B/16 with only the last encoder layers and the fusion head fine-tuned.

## Method

The core contribution is the Analogical Textual Concept Generator (ATCG), a multi-layer attention module that generates text embeddings for unlabeled samples by attending over a visual-textual knowledge base built from labeled data. The Initial Layer uses a Text & Image-Analogical Attention (TIAA) mechanism where the unlabeled image embedding serves as Query, labeled image embeddings as Keys, and labeled text embeddings as Values, producing an initial analogical text embedding. Subsequent Stacked Layers alternate a Text Self-Attention (TSA) step for internal textual coherence with a second TIAA step that concatenates the refined text embedding with the image embedding as Query against concatenated labeled text+image Keys, iteratively sharpening the analogy. Training is two-stage: first, ATCG is trained via a pseudo-GCD procedure that randomly splits known categories into pseudo-known and pseudo-unknown subsets and minimizes cosine distance between generated and ground-truth text embeddings; second, the frozen ATCG generates text embeddings for all samples, which are fused with visual features and optimized jointly with contrastive representation learning and parametric classification (with self-distillation) in the host GCD pipeline.

## Results

AL-GCD (ATCG plugged into SimGCD-CLIP) achieves 74.7% overall accuracy on CUB-200, 78.3% on Stanford Cars, 58.6% on FGVC Aircraft, 84.7% on CIFAR-100, 92.6% on ImageNet-100, and 50.3% on Herbarium19. Aggregated across all six benchmarks, attaching ATCG yields an average gain of +5.0% overall, +4.2% known-category, and +5.5% novel-category accuracy (Table 2a). The gains are most pronounced on fine-grained datasets, where the average improvement reaches +7.1% overall and +7.6% on novel categories. Under the unknown-category-count setting, AL-GCD still improves CMS-CLIP by +4.0% overall and +4.6% on novel categories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AL-GCD consistently outperforms all eight baselines across every dataset and metric. Against the strongest CLIP-based baselines, SelEx-CLIP + AL-GCD achieves 84.1% on CUB (vs. 74.2% for SelEx-CLIP alone, +9.9%) and 66.6% on FGVC Aircraft (vs. 56.7%, +9.9%), while SimGCD-CLIP + AL-GCD reaches 92.6% on ImageNet-100 (vs. 89.9%, +2.7%) and 84.7% on CIFAR-100 (vs. 81.1%, +3.6%). Compared to the recent CLIP-based methods GET (77.0% CUB, 78.5% Cars) and CPT (70.1% CUB, 74.2% Cars), AL-GCD-plugged pipelines surpass them by 7–14 points on fine-grained benchmarks. Against DINO-based methods (GCD, GPC, SimGCD-DINO, CMS-DINO, RLCD), the gap is even larger: e.g., on CUB, SimGCD-CLIP + AL-GCD (74.7%) exceeds RLCD (70.0%) by 4.7 points and GCD (51.3%) by 23.4 points. On the challenging long-tailed Herbarium19, AL-GCD lifts SimGCD-CLIP from 47.9% to 50.3% overall and from 39.9% to 43.1% on novel categories, outperforming all other methods including GET (49.7% overall) and CPT (45.3%). The largest relative gains appear on fine-grained, look-alike categories (CUB, Stanford Cars, FGVC Aircraft), confirming that analogical textual concepts are most beneficial where visual similarity is high.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GCD line of work initiated by Vaze et al. (CVPR 2022) and the parametric classification framework of SimGCD (Wen et al., ICCV 2023) by introducing a cognitive-science-inspired analogical reasoning mechanism. While recent CLIP-based GCD methods (CPT, GET) have begun leveraging language priors, they still treat visual and textual branches as loosely coupled; AL-GCD departs from this by explicitly constructing a visual-textual knowledge base from labeled data and using attention-based analogy to generate textual concepts for unlabeled samples, effectively bridging supervised knowledge and unsupervised discovery. The ATCG module is designed as a plug-and-play component that attaches to existing pipelines (SimGCD-CLIP, CMS-CLIP, SelEx-CLIP) without altering their overall architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play design and consistent gains across six benchmarks (especially +7.1% on fine-grained datasets) make this a practically useful addition to the GCD toolkit, addressing the well-known weakness of visual-only methods on look-alike categories. The cognitive science framing (hippocampal memory retrieval + prefrontal analogical reasoning) provides a principled motivation, though the actual mechanism—attention over a stored knowledge base—is relatively straightforward. Open questions include whether the knowledge base scales to very large category counts, whether the method degrades when text descriptions are noisy or unavailable, and whether integrating richer semantic sources (e.g., LLMs) as the authors suggest would yield further gains. The reliance on CLIP's text encoder quality and the need for category-level text descriptions remain practical constraints.
