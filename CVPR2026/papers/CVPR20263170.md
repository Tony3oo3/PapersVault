## Problem

Current MLLMs and VLMs excel at recognizing what UI elements are present but fundamentally struggle with discerning how they are displayed—distinguishing partial rendering from intentional occlusion, detecting sub-pixel text truncation, or verifying semantic consistency across multi-page flows. No systematic benchmark exists to evaluate these fine-grained display defect detection capabilities on realistic, complex commercial interfaces, as prior evaluations rely on older, simpler datasets like RICO that lack the diverse styling defects found in modern apps.

## Input / Output

For single-interface tasks, the model receives one UI screenshot and outputs a binary defect-present/absent judgment per defect type, with bounding boxes localizing the defect region. For the sequence-interface Text Inconsistency task, the model receives an ordered set of UI screenshots (averaging 6.8 per sequence) and produces an open-ended recall of inconsistencies, which is scored against a structured ground-truth answer set specifying the offending interface, violated specification, and problem description. All screenshots are real captures from 11 commercial apps across 21 devices in varied modes (dark, large-font, rotated), with a balanced 1:1 positive-to-negative ratio for single-interface tasks.

## Method

UI-Lens is a benchmark rather than a new model: it contributes a curated dataset of 4,759 Chinese (and 3,392 English) expert-annotated UI screens spanning six defect subtypes organized into three categories (element-boundary, content-missing, semantic-inconsistency). Because genuine display defects are scarce in production apps, eight senior UI/UX design experts synthesized 1,030 additional positive examples via Figma, validated by a Pearson correlation of r=0.88 between synthetic and real defect distributions. Annotation follows a two-stage expert protocol (initial bounding-box marking followed by cross-validation and arbitration), achieving Krippendorff's alpha of 0.8417. Evaluation is conducted zero-shot with chain-of-thought prompting across 9 mainstream MLLMs/VLMs, using binary classification metrics (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1) for single-interface tasks and a judge-model-based open-ended recall protocol for sequence-interface tasks.

## Results

Across 9 mainstream MLLMs/VLMs evaluated zero-shot on UI-Lens, task-average accuracy on fine-grained boundary tasks is near random: 50.04% (Text Overflow), 54.07% (Cropped Content), and 54.14% (Container Overlap). The best-performing models, Gemini-2.5-Pro and Seed1.6-Vison, achieve model-average F1 scores of approximately 66% and 65%, respectively, but still fall far short of practical usability. The sequence-interface Text Inconsistency task is the most challenging, with a task-average F1 of only 11.44%, while content-missing tasks (Undisplayed Content at 57.78% and Abnormal Text Ellipsis at 75.24% F1) are comparatively more tractable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini-2.5-Pro leads on Text Overflow F1 (64.67%), Container Overlap F1 (65.05%), and Text Inconsistency F1 (24.94%), and achieves the highest model-average recall (81.41%) but the lowest model-average precision (56.85%), reflecting an aggressive detection strategy. Seed1.6-Vison is the most balanced performer, ranking top-2 on five of six single-interface tasks (e.g., 62.31% F1 on Cropped Content, 77.30% on Abnormal Text Ellipsis) and achieving the second-highest overall F1. Seed1.5-VL adopts a highly conservative strategy, attaining the best model-average precision (75.7%) but the worst recall (27.07%), rendering it nearly useless for coverage-critical QA. GPT-5 and GPT-4o show moderate performance on content-missing tasks (81.13% and 74.42% F1 on ATE, respectively) but collapse to near-random F1 on fine-grained boundary tasks (6.80% and 38.77% on Text Overflow). Open-source models (Qwen3-VL-235B, GLM-4.5V) trail closed-source counterparts across all tasks, with Qwen3-VL-235B achieving only 14.10% F1 on Text Overflow and GLM-4.5V reporting N/A on Container Overlap. Prompting strategies (self-correction, FGVP, one-shot CoT) yield at most +2.48 F1 improvement, confirming that the gap is architectural rather than promptable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of UI understanding and MLLM evaluation. Prior UI defect detection systems (Owl Eyes, Nighthawk, AutoConsis) relied on older, simpler datasets like RICO and focused on limited defect types, while broader MLLM evaluation work (WebUI, Ferret-UI, UIBert) targets component recognition and OCR on clean interfaces rather than display anomalies. UI-Lens departs from both lines by introducing a realistic, multilingual, expert-annotated benchmark spanning six fine-grained defect subtypes across commercial-grade applications, and by systematically probing 9 SOTA MLLMs/VLMs in a zero-shot setting—a combination absent from prior literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a critical and previously undocumented gap: SOTA models that excel at object recognition and UI component identification perform at or near random-guess levels on fine-grained boundary defects (Text Overflow F1 ≈ 22%, Container Overlap F1 ≈ 34%) and cross-interface semantic consistency (Text Inconsistency F1 ≈ 11%), undermining the near-term prospect of automating UI display QA with general-purpose MLLMs. Open questions include whether dedicated fine-grained boundary perception modules, interactive context modeling (e.g., scrollability awareness), or path-level state tracking can close these gaps, and whether the synthetic positive examples (validated via Pearson r = 0.88) fully capture the distribution of real-world defects. The benchmark's multilingual scope (Chinese + English) and coverage of 21 devices and challenging modes (dark, large-font) make it a practical reference for industry QA pipelines.
