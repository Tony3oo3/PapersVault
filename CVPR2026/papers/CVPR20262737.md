## Problem

Existing brain tumor segmentation methods degrade severely when one or more of the four standard MRI modalities (FLAIR, T1, T1c, T2) are unavailable. Structured architectures (CNNs, Transformers) have fixed computation paths that assume complete cross-modality correspondence, forcing prior work into either exponentially many per-combination models or two-stage training pipelines (knowledge distillation, generative reconstruction) that add cost and inadequately handle interference from missing inputs.

## Input / Output

The method accepts any non-empty subset of the four 3D MRI volumes (FLAIR, T1, T1c, T2), each of shape 1×D×H×W, and produces a voxel-wise segmentation map with three sub-region labels: whole tumor (WT), tumor core (TC), and enhancing tumor (ET). During training, all four modalities are always fed to the modality-specific encoders, and missing-modality scenarios are simulated by zeroing out selected feature nodes and updating graph connectivity; at inference, the corresponding encoders and edges for absent modalities are simply skipped. Full-resolution inference uses a sliding-window strategy over 128×128×128 crops.

## Method

The framework treats each modality's encoder features as graph nodes and fuses them via a Graph Attention Network (GAT) with two key modifications. First, zero-initialized learnable virtual nodes are appended to each modality's basic nodes, acting as modality-agnostic anchors that preserve invariant information even when the corresponding modality is entirely absent. Second, a dynamic edge-connectivity mechanism rewrites the adjacency matrix at each forward pass: when a modality is dropped, all incoming edges to its basic nodes are severed (e_ji = 0) while outgoing edges remain, so information flows unidirectionally from available modalities into the missing ones without contaminating existing features. The GAT itself is heterogenized—both the linear projection W and the attention vector a are modality-specific—allowing the network to learn distinct interaction patterns per modality pair. Training uses random modality dropout (0 to N−1 modalities masked per sample) with a combined Dice loss over a modality-specific decoder and a multimodal decoder, enabling one-stage supervised learning without any full-modality teacher or generative auxiliary network.

## Results

On BraTS 2018, the method achieves mean DSC of 66.0% (ET), 81.4% (TC), and 86.7% (WT) across all 15 modality combinations, outperforming the strongest baseline MMCFormer by 1.3%, 2.1%, and 0.9% respectively. On BraTS 2020, mean DSC reaches 65.8% (ET), 80.8% (TC), and 88.4% (WT), surpassing the strongest baseline LS3M by 2.2%, 1.0%, and 0.2%. The method achieves the best DSC in 12/15 modality combinations on BraTS 2018 and 11/15 on BraTS 2020 for ET, with the largest gains (3.65% ET, 4.3% TC) occurring when the critical T1c modality is missing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MMCFormer (customized, one model per modality subset) on BraTS 2018, the proposed method wins on mean DSC for all three subregions (ET +1.3%, TC +2.1%, WT +0.9%) while using a single unified model instead of 15 separate ones. Against mmFormer (one-stage Transformer-based) on both datasets, the method achieves consistent gains of 6.1% ET and 8.4% TC on BraTS 2018, and 7.8% ET and 5.9% TC on BraTS 2020, demonstrating the advantage of graph-based over Transformer-based modality interaction. Against M³AE (two-stage generative reconstruction), the method outperforms by 6.1% ET and 4.0% TC on BraTS 2018, and 4.5% ET and 3.2% TC on BraTS 2020. Against LS3M (state-space model, BraTS 2020 only), the method leads by 2.2% ET, 1.0% TC, and 0.2% WT, with the gap being smallest on WT where all methods perform well. Against U-HVED (variational autoencoder), the method dominates by large margins (19.2% ET, 16.6% TC on BraTS 2018), reflecting the superiority of explicit graph-based fusion over latent-space reconstruction. The method achieves the best DSC in 12/15 combinations on BraTS 2018 and 11/15 on BraTS 2020 for ET, with the largest absolute gains (3.65% ET, 4.3% TC) in scenarios where T1c is missing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Graph Attention Network (GAT) [27] from its original single-modality graph learning context into a multimodal fusion architecture, where each MRI modality becomes a node rather than a spatial unit. It departs from the dominant structured-architecture paradigm (CNNs, Transformers) that underlies most prior missing-modality methods, which rely on fixed computation paths and typically require two-stage training (knowledge distillation or generative reconstruction). The key conceptual departure is treating modality absence as a graph-topology change (removing nodes/edges) rather than as a data corruption to be repaired, enabling a unified one-stage training framework with dynamic connectivity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The one-stage, plug-and-play graph formulation eliminates the exponential training cost of per-combination models and the complexity of two-stage distillation pipelines, which is practically significant for clinical deployment where modality availability is unpredictable. However, the performance gains over strong baselines (MMCFormer, LS3M) are modest (1–2% Dice on average), and the framework is validated only on 4-modality brain MRI; whether the virtual-node and dynamic-edge strategy generalizes to other modalities, other organ segmentation tasks, or larger modality sets remains open. The paper also does not ablate the interaction between virtual nodes and the heterogeneous weight matrix, leaving unclear how much of the gain comes from each design choice in isolation.
