## Problem

Existing humanoid locomotion perception modules—depth cameras and elevation maps—provide only partial, locally flattened views of the environment, failing to capture overhead constraints, lateral clutter, and multi-level structures simultaneously. No prior method achieves a single policy that handles ground-level obstacles, lateral obstacles, and overhanging structures with efficient, real-time perception suitable for onboard deployment.

## Input / Output

The actor consumes a binary 32×32×40 voxel grid (5 cm resolution, 1.6 m × 1.6 m × 2.0 m perception volume) built from dual torso-mounted Hesai JT128 LiDARs at 10 Hz, concatenated with 5-step proprioceptive history (joint positions/velocities, root angular/linear velocity, gravity vector) and 4-step action history, plus goal position and elapsed/remaining time. The critic additionally receives a height map and root velocity as privileged information. The output is a vector of whole-body joint actions for the 29-DoF Unitree G1, executed at 50 Hz on an onboard NVIDIA Orin NX.

## Method

Gallant voxelizes LiDAR returns into a robot-centric binary occupancy tensor and processes it with a z-grouped 2D CNN that treats the 40 height slices as input channels, exploiting the fact that most (x,y) columns contain only 1–2 occupied z-slices; this reduces compute by roughly a factor of k relative to a 3D CNN while preserving vertical structure through channel mixing. A parallelized raycast-voxelization pipeline built on NVIDIA Warp simulates realistic LiDAR scans over both static terrain and the robot's own moving links, with domain randomization on pose, hit position, 100–200 ms latency, and 2% voxel dropout to bridge the sim-to-real gap. Training uses PPO with a goal-reaching reward (position-based rather than velocity-tracking) that fuses locomotion and local navigation into a single end-to-end policy, and a curriculum over 8 terrain families (plane, ceiling, forest, door, platform, pile, upstair, downstair) with progressive difficulty interpolation. The asymmetric actor-critic design feeds the height map only to the critic, leveraging it for credit assignment during training while keeping the deployed policy free of latency-sensitive reconstruction.

## Results

In simulation, Gallant achieves 96–100% success rate across seven of eight terrains (Plane 100%, Ceiling 97.1%, Forest 84.3%, Door 98.7%, Platform 96.1%, Upstair 96.2%, Downstair 97.9%), with Pile being the hardest at 82.1%. In real-world deployment on the Unitree G1, Gallant consistently outperforms both the HeightMap and NoDR (no domain randomization) baselines across all terrains over 15 trials each. The paper reports over-90% success on stair climbing and platform stepping—tasks previously considered unstable for elevation-map-based controllers—and near-100% success on ground-only terrains with fewer collisions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary quantitative comparisons are ablations rather than external baselines, but the perceptual-interface ablation serves as the closest baseline comparison. Only-Height-Map (elevation map as sole input) catastrophically fails on overhead and lateral terrains—5.3% on Ceiling and 10.2% on Door—while Gallant achieves 97.1% and 98.7% respectively, confirming that 2.5D representations cannot capture multi-layer structure. Only-Voxel-Grid (no height map in critic) is consistently worse than Gallant across all terrains (e.g., 75.9% vs 84.3% on Forest, 72.3% vs 82.1% on Pile), validating the asymmetric actor/critic design. Among alternative architectures, the standard 3D-CNN is competitive on Ceiling (97.5% vs 97.1%) but underperforms on Forest (73.9% vs 84.3%) and Pile (65.3% vs 82.1%), while Sparse-3D-CNN is weakest overall (52.4% on Pile). In real-world deployment, the HeightMap baseline fails on Ceiling and Door entirely and is outperformed even on ground-level terrains due to noisy elevation reconstruction and LiDAR jitter; the NoDR variant (no domain randomization) drops significantly on ground-level terrains due to unmodeled sensor latency, while Gallant maintains high success across all scenarios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant elevation-map paradigm in humanoid perceptive locomotion (Long et al., Wang et al., Ren et al.), which flattens 3D geometry into 2.5D height fields and discards vertical/multi-layer structure. It also moves beyond depth-camera approaches (Zhuang et al.) limited by narrow FoV, and addresses the computational infeasibility of raw point-cloud inputs (Wang et al.) for real-time onboard inference. The reward structure is inherited from HOMIE (Ben et al.), while the position-based navigation formulation follows prior quadruped work (He et al., Yang et al.), but the combination of voxel-grid perception, a z-grouped 2D CNN, and a full LiDAR simulation pipeline for humanoid whole-body control is novel. Voxel grids had been explored for cross-modal scene understanding on legged robots (Cui et al., Zhang et al.) but not as a direct locomotion-control input.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Gallant demonstrates that a single end-to-end policy can handle ground-level, lateral, and overhead constraints simultaneously on a real humanoid, a capability previously out of reach for elevation-map or depth-based methods. The z-grouped 2D CNN offers a practical efficiency win (roughly k× less compute than 3D CNNs) that makes real-time onboard inference feasible, and the high-fidelity LiDAR simulation with domain randomization is a reusable component for future LiDAR-based robot learning. The main open bottleneck is LiDAR latency (10 Hz, >100 ms delay), which caps Pile-terrain success around 80%; the authors suggest lower-latency sensors or a reactive policy as future directions. Broader implications include whether voxel-grid representations can be extended to manipulation or multi-robot settings where full 3D awareness is equally critical.
