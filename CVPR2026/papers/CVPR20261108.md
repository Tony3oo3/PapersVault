## Problem

Existing DeepFake detection benchmarks rely on binary real/fake labels and lack the fine-grained annotations needed to evaluate whether MLLM-based detectors genuinely perceive manipulation artifacts, produce grounded explanations, or hallucinate non-existent evidence. No prior benchmark simultaneously measures perception (artifact identification), detection (classification accuracy), and hallucination (explanation reliability) across diverse manipulation types, generators, and modalities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261108/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark feeds paired real-synthetic media (images, videos, audio) alongside structured text prompts in three formats: True-False questions probing single artifact/location presence, Multiple-Choice questions with a 'none of the above' option and multi-select support, and Open-Ended questions (Type-A for known-fake artifact enumeration, Type-B for blind real/fake classification with explanation). Model outputs are parsed via an external LLM (Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite) that maps free-text responses to a predefined artifact vocabulary, producing per-sample scores for Cover (explanation completeness), CHAIR/Hal (hallucination frequency), F^0.5 (joint precision-recall of explanation quality), and binary detection accuracy.

## Method

TriDF constructs a 65K-sample benchmark from 51 generators across 16 DeepFake manipulation types (partially manipulated: face swap, lip-sync, voice conversion, etc.; fully synthetic: talking-head synthesis, text-to-human generation, voice cloning) with rigorous one-to-one real-fake pairings and automatic quality control. The key technical contribution is a two-level artifact taxonomy—quality artifacts (blurriness, flicker, banding) grounded at specific spatial locations, and semantic artifacts (anatomical inconsistencies, abnormal motion, prosody anomalies) requiring common-sense reasoning—annotated by humans rather than LLMs to avoid self-preference bias. Evaluation decomposes into three dimensions: Perception (TFQ/MCQ on manipulated samples only), Detection (Type-B OEQ on mixed real/fake), and Hallucination (CHAIR, Hal, and F^0.5 computed from both Type-A and Type-B OEQ responses on all samples). The F^0.5 metric weights precision twice as heavily as recall (β=0.5) to penalize false-positive artifacts, and a penalty of CHAIR=1 is assigned when the model produces no mapped artifacts or misclassifies a fake as real.

## Results

On TriDF's perception evaluation (TFQ), GPT-5 achieves the highest average accuracy (60.19%), followed by Qwen3-VL-30B (59.85%) and Gemini 2.5-Pro (59.63%), all only moderately above the 50% random baseline. For interpretable detection (Type-B OEQ), Gemini 2.5-Pro achieves the best accuracy (73.11% image, 59.84% video) with the highest artifact coverage (Cover 0.4208 image, 0.1857 video). Hallucination remains severe across all models: CHAIR scores range from 0.53 to 1.0, and Hal (fraction of responses containing hallucinations) exceeds 0.87 for nearly every system. Claude Sonnet 4.5 attains the best MCQ perception (0.21 avg) and highest Type-A Cover (0.6410 image) but suffers from elevated CHAIR (0.6241) due to over-elaborate, hallucinated explanations roughly twice as long as other models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary models, Gemini 2.5-Pro leads on detection accuracy (73.11% image, 59.84% video) and artifact coverage, outperforming GPT-5 (65.73%/63.12%) and Claude Sonnet 4.5 (62.40%/49.67%) by 7–13 points on image accuracy. Claude Sonnet 4.5 excels in perception (best MCQ score 0.21, highest Type-A Cover 0.6410) but its longer responses introduce more hallucinated artifacts (CHAIR 0.6241 vs. Gemini's 0.5571). Among open-source models, Qwen3-VL-30B-Instruct is the strongest overall (68.94% image Acc, 0.3661 Cover), closely followed by Qwen3-Omni-30B (69.42% Acc, 0.4143 Cover), both substantially ahead of LLaVA-OV-72B (53.74% Acc) and InternVL variants (41–52% Acc). Purpose-built detectors FakeShield and FakeVLM perform poorly on TriDF's open-ended evaluation (Acc. 40.45% and 47.36%, Cover < 0.03), indicating that task-specific training does not transfer to the broader, multi-generator, multi-modality setting. A critical finding is that no model achieves CHAIR below 0.53, meaning hallucination is pervasive regardless of detection accuracy, and the best F0.5 score (0.4258, Gemini 2.5-Pro image) still reflects substantial unreliability in generated explanations.

## Relation to Prior Work

TriDF extends the line of MLLM-based DeepFake detection benchmarks (FakeBench, LOKI, DD-VQA, AIGI-Holmes, SID-Set) by introducing the first evaluation framework that jointly measures fine-grained artifact perception, binary detection accuracy, and explanation hallucination in a single unified protocol. Where prior benchmarks relied on LLM-generated rationales or similarity-based interpretability scores, TriDF grounds evaluation in human-annotated, location-specific artifact taxonomies (quality vs. semantic) and uses a lightweight external LLM for artifact mapping rather than a powerful closed-source judge, reducing self-preference bias. The benchmark also departs from earlier detection corpora (FaceForensics++, DFDC) that provided only binary labels by constructing rigorous real-fake pairs across 51 generators and 16 manipulation types spanning image, video, and audio.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that strong perceptual ability does not guarantee reliable detection when hallucinated artifacts contaminate the reasoning chain—reframes how the community should evaluate and design MLLM-based detectors, suggesting that hallucination suppression is as critical as improving artifact recognition. The benchmark's scale (65K test samples, 3 modalities, 16 manipulation types) and its explicit hallucination metrics (CHAIR, Hal, F^0.5) fill a gap that no prior resource addresses, making it a likely standard for future interpretable detection work. Open questions include whether the artifact taxonomy generalizes to next-generation generators, how to architecturally reduce hallucination without sacrificing coverage, and whether the perception–detection coupling observed here holds under distribution shift to truly unseen manipulation types.
