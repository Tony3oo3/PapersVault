## Problem

Existing dataset quantization methods rely on a bin generation step whose cubic complexity O(CM³) makes them infeasible for large-scale datasets (e.g., classes with >200K samples cause out-of-memory failures). Additionally, the fixed drop ratio used in patch dropping assumes uniform redundancy across samples, which rarely holds in practice and degrades the representational quality of the quantized coreset. The paper asks whether bin generation is truly indispensable and whether a lighter, adaptive alternative can match or exceed its performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260469/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a labeled RGB image dataset (e.g., CIFAR-10/100 at 32×32, ImageNet-1K at 224×224, Fakeddit at variable resolution) and produces a quantized coreset where each retained sample has a variable number of patches removed according to its own adaptive drop ratio. The compressed samples are paired with a pre-trained masked autoencoder that reconstructs dropped patches during downstream training. The coreset fraction ρ is user-specified (10–30% for standard benchmarks, 1–3% for Fakeddit), and the output sample weight w_samples accounts for the variable patch retention when computing the effective compression ratio.

## Method

BGFDQ replaces bin generation with a three-stage pipeline: (1) Neighbor Identification computes K=20 nearest neighbors per sample within each class using squared Euclidean distance in ResNet-18 feature space; (2) Neighbor-aware Coreset Selection iteratively picks a random sample into the coreset and probabilistically removes its neighbors with decay probability p_k = e^(−ραk), a mechanism theoretically shown to achieve strictly better coverage and lower redundancy than bin-generation-based random selection; (3) Semantic-shift Patch Dropping (SPD) evaluates each sample at nine candidate drop ratios (10%–90%), computes the semantic shift via feature-space distance, and selects the largest ratio whose shift remains below a class-specific threshold λ_C (the mean of the 50 smallest intra-class distances). This reduces overall complexity from O(CM³) to O(CM²) and enables processing of datasets with >200K samples per class where all bin-generation baselines fail.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 at ρ=30%, BGFDQ (w/ SPD) achieves 70.2% validation accuracy, surpassing the best bin-generation baseline DQ (67.7%) by 2.5% and DQAS (66.2%) by 4.0%. On CIFAR-10 at ρ=20%, BGFDQ (w/ SPD) reaches 91.6% vs. 89.3% for ADQ and 87.6% for DQ. On the large-scale Fakeddit dataset (classes with >200K samples), all bin-generation-based methods (DQ, DQAS, ADQ) fail with out-of-memory errors, while BGFDQ (w/ SPD) achieves 84.8% at ρ=2%, outperforming random sampling (84.5%) by 0.3%. BGFDQ (w/o SPD) reduces total runtime by 15–35% compared to baselines while still achieving >1% higher accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BGFDQ (w/ SPD) outperforms all five baselines across every dataset and compression level where comparisons are available. Against the closest dataset-quantization competitor DQAS (ECCV 2024), BGFDQ gains +1.4% on CIFAR-10 (ρ=20%) and +5.0% on CIFAR-100 (ρ=30%), while DQAS cannot scale to Fakeddit. Against DQ (ICCV 2023), the original dataset quantization method, BGFDQ improves by +4.0% on CIFAR-10 (ρ=10%) and +2.5% on CIFAR-100 (ρ=30%). ADQ (AAAI 2025) is outperformed by +2.3% on CIFAR-10 (ρ=20%) and +3.1% on CIFAR-100 (ρ=20%). The non-quantization baselines GradMatch (ICML 2021) and DM (WACV 2023) are substantially weaker, trailing BGFDQ by 25.6% and 13.1% respectively on CIFAR-10 at ρ=10%, confirming that the quantization paradigm with adaptive patch dropping is far more effective than gradient-matching coreset selection or distribution-matching distillation. Critically, all three bin-generation-based quantization methods (DQ, DQAS, ADQ) fail entirely on Fakeddit due to OOM, whereas BGFDQ handles classes with >200K samples without issue.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the dataset quantization paradigm established by DQ [31], which introduced the bin generation → coreset selection → patch dropping pipeline, and its follow-ups DQAS [29] and ADQ [11] that refined coreset selection within that framework. The key conceptual departure is replacing the submodular bin generation step with a lightweight KNN-based neighbor identification, reframing the problem from global cluster partitioning to local semantic redundancy analysis. The neighbor-aware coreset selection draws on ideas from coreset selection literature (geometry-based and scoring-based methods) but is specifically designed to exploit precomputed neighborhood structure, while the semantic-shift patch dropping generalizes the fixed-ratio patch dropping inherited from DQ into an adaptive, per-sample mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for scalability: eliminating the cubic-complexity bin generation step enables dataset quantization on classes with 200K+ samples where all prior methods fail with out-of-memory errors, opening the door to quantizing truly large-scale datasets. However, the SPD module introduces substantial runtime overhead (2219s vs 806s for standard patch dropping on CIFAR-10), and the method still depends on a pre-trained feature extractor and a masked autoencoder for reconstruction, limiting its end-to-end novelty. Open questions include whether the neighbor-based selection generalizes to non-image modalities (text, audio) as the authors note, whether the theoretical coverage/redundancy guarantees hold under distribution shift, and whether the adaptive drop ratio can be computed more efficiently than the current 9-candidate search.
