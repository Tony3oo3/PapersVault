## Problem

Existing learning-based 3D symmetry detectors are trained and evaluated almost exclusively on clean, object-centric datasets (e.g., ShapeNet, Objaverse) and fail to generalize to in-the-wild scenes with complex backgrounds, occlusions, and varying illumination. Moreover, the inherent scale ambiguity of monocular inputs makes localizing 3D symmetry planes ill-posed, so most prior methods only predict plane orientation without a meaningful 3D offset.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260217/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image of an architectural landmark as input and outputs a set of 3D reflectional symmetry planes, each specified by a unit normal and a signed distance offset in the camera coordinate frame. Internally, the model predicts per-pixel signed distance maps relative to VGGT's predicted point map; at inference, explicit planes are recovered by solving a constrained least-squares fit over high-confidence 3D points. The target symmetries are global (applying to an entire landmark or complete facade), not local architectural elements like individual windows.

## Method

The paper introduces two coupled contributions. First, an automated annotation pipeline that extracts symmetry planes from SfM reconstructions by matching each image against its own horizontal reflection (within-view) and against reflected versions of visually similar images from the same scene (cross-view, leveraging the 'doppelganger' matching failure mode); matched 2D correspondences are unprojected to 3D via depth maps, candidate planes are fitted by minimizing reflection residuals, and DBSCAN clustering aggregates thousands of noisy candidates into high-confidence annotations, yielding the ArchSym dataset (93 scenes, 34,177 images). Second, a single-view detector that finetunes a frozen VGGT backbone with a two-stage symmetry head: a transformer decoder with learnable instance queries identifies candidate planes, and a DPT-style dense prediction head with FiLM conditioning regresses per-instance signed distance maps. The key design choice is parameterizing symmetries as signed distances to the model's own predicted geometry, which provides a stable, scale-consistent supervision signal that resolves monocular scale ambiguity; training uses a confidence-aware bipartite matching loss (Hungarian algorithm), and at inference planes are extracted by solving a unit-normal-constrained least-squares problem over the predicted point map and signed distances.

## Results

On the ArchSym benchmark (19 unseen test scenes), the proposed method achieves a geodesic distance of 3.71° and F@1° of 0.25, substantially outperforming the finetuned Reflect3D baseline (Geo 10.46°, F@1° 0.07) and the direct-regression baseline (Geo 5.06°, F@1° 0.16). For full-plane evaluation, the method achieves a dense symmetry error of 0.13, compared to 0.18 for the direct baseline; Reflect3D does not report this metric as it predicts only plane normals. The method also demonstrates strong qualitative generalization across architectural eras, viewpoints, and styles unseen during training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Reflect3D (finetuned on ArchSym for fair comparison), the proposed method wins decisively on all normal-only metrics: geodesic distance drops from 10.46° to 3.71° (a 64% reduction), and F@1° improves from 0.07 to 0.25 (3.6×). Reflect3D also produces redundant detections and misses partially visible symmetries in qualitative comparisons. Against the direct-regression baseline (which uses the same frozen VGGT backbone but regresses plane parameters directly), the proposed method wins on every metric, with the largest gap at F@1° (0.25 vs. 0.16) and dense symmetry error (0.13 vs. 0.18), confirming that the signed-distance-map parameterization better grounds planes in 3D geometry. For annotation quality, the paper's pipeline is compared qualitatively against LANGEVIN (a Riemannian Langevin dynamics method); LANGEVIN fails on incomplete point clouds (e.g., Isa Khan's Tomb, Frauenkirche) and detects architecturally implausible planes (e.g., horizontal planes on the Arc de Triomphe), while the proposed pipeline consistently extracts semantically correct symmetries.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on VGGT, a 3D foundation model that predicts scene geometry from a single image, by attaching a symmetry prediction head that operates on its frozen backbone features. It departs from prior single-view symmetry detectors (NeRD/NeRD++, Reflect3D) in two key ways: those methods were trained exclusively on clean, object-centric datasets (ShapeNet, Objaverse) and predicted only plane normals, leaving 3D localization to downstream optimization. The conceptual novelty lies in (1) repurposing the 'doppelganger' failure mode of SfM image matchers as an automated symmetry annotation signal, and (2) parameterizing symmetries as signed distance maps relative to predicted geometry to resolve the scale ambiguity that plagues monocular symmetry detection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ArchSym provides the first large-scale benchmark (34K images, 93 landmark scenes) for evaluating 3D-grounded symmetry detection in realistic, in-the-wild conditions, filling a gap where prior methods had no appropriate testbed. The signed-distance-map parameterization offers a clean, general recipe for grounding any 3D prediction in a scale-consistent frame, which could transfer beyond symmetry to other geometric tasks. However, the approach is limited to reflectional (not rotational) symmetries, global (not partial) structures, and architectural landmarks; its accuracy is also tightly coupled to VGGT's geometry quality, and the reliance on SfM reconstructions for annotation introduces a dependency on reconstruction fidelity that may not hold for all scene types.
