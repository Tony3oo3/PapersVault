## Problem

Ghost points from multi-path laser reflections off glass and reflective surfaces cause spurious 3D structures that degrade LiDAR-based SLAM and object detection. Prior ghost removal methods rely on geometric consistency across dense, static point clouds and fail on mobile LiDAR with sparse, single-frame data. No dataset or learning-based method existed for ghost detection from full-waveform LiDAR (FWL) data in mobile scenarios.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261046/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw FWL histograms of shape (H, W, T) = (512, 400, 700) with ~1 ns temporal resolution and 105 m max range, preprocessed to (128, 128, 256) by discarding ceiling/floor bins and internal noise, then downsampled and cropped. It outputs per-voxel class probabilities over four categories (Glass, Ghost, Object, Noise) at every (x, y, t) coordinate, from which ghost-labeled peaks are removed to produce a cleaned 3D point cloud for downstream SLAM or detection.

## Method

The framework has two stages. First, FWL-MAE performs self-supervised pre-training: spatial patches are randomly sampled in the (x, y) plane and all temporal bins along T within each patch are masked; a 6-block, 6-head Transformer encoder reconstructs the masked volume via MSE loss while a linear head simultaneously regresses the dominant peak's position, amplitude, and width with L1 losses, forcing the latent representation to encode physically meaningful waveform structure. Second, the frozen FWL-MAE encoder feeds a lightweight two-layer linear classification head trained with focal loss (to handle the extreme Ghost-vs-Noise class imbalance) to assign per-voxel class probabilities. The key distinction from prior MAE-based approaches (e.g., MARMOT for transient imaging) is the explicit peak-property regression, which captures the temporal and intensity signatures that differentiate multi-path ghost reflections from direct returns in sparse mobile LiDAR data.

## Results

On the Ghost-FWL dataset, the proposed FWL-MAE-based framework achieves 0.751 ghost peak recall and 0.918 ghost removal rate, outperforming the MARMOT-pretrained baseline (0.746 recall, 0.910 removal rate) and the no-pretraining variant (0.704 recall, 0.900 removal rate). In downstream SLAM, the method reduces ATE to 0.245 m and RTE to 0.245 m, a 66–84% improvement over Dual-Peak and Multi-Peak baselines. For 3D object detection, the Ghost False Positive Rate drops to 1.34%, a 50× reduction compared to Multi-Peak (67.9%) and Dual-Peak (75.8%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines across two evaluation axes. For the core ghost-removal task (Table 2), the proposed FWL-MAE framework marginally outperforms MARMOT [34] (recall 0.751 vs. 0.746; removal rate 0.918 vs. 0.910), confirming that domain-specific peak-aware pretraining yields better representations than a general transient-imaging MAE. For downstream SLAM (Table 3), the method dramatically outperforms both Dual-Peak [15,28] (ATE 0.245 vs. 0.715 m, a 66% reduction) and Multi-Peak [24] (ATE 0.245 vs. 1.547 m, an 84% reduction), with similar gains on RTE. For 3D object detection (Table 4), the Ghost FP Rate drops from 67.9% (Multi-Peak) and 75.8% (Dual-Peak) to just 1.34%, a roughly 50× reduction in ghost-induced false positives. The ablation (Ours w/o FWL-MAE) shows that removing the self-supervised pretraining costs 4.7 points in recall, validating the contribution of the peak-aware masked autoencoder.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: geometry-based ghost removal (Yun & Sim, Lee et al.), which relies on dense multi-frame point cloud consistency and fails on sparse mobile LiDAR, and full-waveform LiDAR (FWL) processing (Scheuble et al.), which has been exclusively 'ranging-centric' for distance estimation. The authors repurpose the masked autoencoder paradigm from MAE (He et al.) and its transient-imaging extension MARMOT (Shen et al.) to learn FWL-specific representations, but shift the task from range estimation to physical-cause classification of reflection peaks (Object, Glass, Ghost, Noise). This creates a genuinely new task formulation—learning-based ghost detection from FWL in mobile scenarios—that neither prior line of work had addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 7.5B peak-level annotations across 24K frames make this the first practically usable benchmark for mobile FWL ghost detection, filling a gap that synthetic data generation (computationally expensive, physically inaccurate multi-path modeling) could not address. Downstream gains (66% SLAM trajectory error reduction, 50× false-positive reduction) demonstrate real safety relevance for autonomous driving, though comparisons are against factory-default peak-selection settings rather than learned baselines. Key limitations include static multi-viewpoint annotations only (mobile sequences remain unlabeled), focus on glass-induced ghosts in clear weather, and a single sensor configuration—extending to other reflective materials, adverse conditions, and temporal/tracking scenarios remains open.
