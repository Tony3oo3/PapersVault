## Problem

Visual counting research has been dominated by rigid, homogeneous objects like crowds and vehicles, leaving fine-grained, taxonomy-aware plant counting largely unexplored. Plants exhibit non-rigid morphologies, dramatic phenotypic plasticity across growth stages, and a hierarchical Linnaean class space of hundreds of species, making them fundamentally harder to count than generic objects. No prior benchmark integrates plant taxonomy with counting annotations to enable hierarchical reasoning and species-aware generalization evaluation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263278/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each test image (resolution ranging from 165×127 microscopy to 6,000×4,000 UAV imagery) is paired with K visual exemplar patches (1 or 3) cropped from the same image, and optionally a text prompt encoding the full 7-level taxonomic hierarchy (Kingdom through Species). Regression-based methods output a density map whose integral yields the count, while detection-based methods output per-instance bounding boxes. The ground truth consists of 678,050 point annotations at structural centers plus 30,000 bounding boxes across 10,000 images, with the minimal countable unit defined as a species-organization pair (e.g., 'Oryza sativa leaf' vs. 'Oryza sativa whole plant').

## Method

The core contribution is the TPC-268 dataset and its evaluation protocol rather than a new counting architecture. The dataset embeds every instance in the full Linnaean hierarchy (2 kingdoms, 242 species, 268 species-organization categories) and spans four biological organization levels (tissue, organ, organism, population) across observation scales from microscopy to canopy-level remote sensing. Data splits are formulated as a Mixed-Integer Linear Programming problem enforcing three constraints: taxonomic disjointness at the species-organization level, balanced instance density across subsets, and coverage of all observation scales in each split. The paper benchmarks 11 state-of-the-art CAC methods (CNN, Transformer, and DETR-based) under 1-shot and 3-shot settings, revealing that local-structure-matching models (LOCA) outperform global-attention models on the test set, that detection-based approaches struggle with severe occlusion, and that appending taxonomic text prompts to CountGD consistently reduces MAE. Cross-dataset transfer experiments confirm that models trained on generic objects (FSC-147) degrade substantially on plants, while models trained on TPC-268 generalize to generic objects, establishing TPC-268 as a harder, biologically grounded testbed.

## Results

On the TPC-268 test set (3-shot), LOCA achieves the best overall performance with MAE 17.51, RMSE 38.37, and R² 0.78, followed closely by DAVE (MAE 17.61, R² 0.75). Regression-based methods consistently outperform detection-based approaches (C-DETR: MAE 22.68; CountGD: MAE 19.52), indicating that holistic density estimation is more effective than explicit instance localization under severe occlusion. In the 1-shot setting, DAVE leads with MAE 19.47 and R² 0.72, while TasselNetV4 achieves the best validation R² (0.85) but generalizes poorly to the test set (R² 0.67), highlighting a val-test gap for global-attention models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LOCA [49] (ICCV'23) achieves the best 3-shot test MAE (17.51) and R² (0.78), outperforming DAVE [42] (MAE 17.61) by a narrow margin and CACViT [53] (MAE 22.04) by 4.5 MAE points, confirming that local structural matching with iterative prototype adaptation is critical for dense, morphologically diverse plant scenes. TasselNetV4 [21], a plant-specific vision foundation model, attains the best validation R² (0.83) but drops to 0.60 on test, a 0.23 gap that the authors attribute to overfitting of global-attention hyperparameters to validation data. Detection-based methods C-DETR [41] (MAE 22.68) and CountGD [1] (MAE 19.52) underperform all top regression models, with C-DETR's R² of 0.74 being misleadingly high relative to its MAE, suggesting it localizes some instances but fails on dense, occluded regions. Cross-dataset transfer (Table 3) reveals that models trained on FSC-147 degrade severely on TPC-268 (e.g., LOCA MAE jumps from 10.72 to 24.70, +130%), while the reverse transfer (TPC-268→FSC-147) shows negligible or even improved MAE (LOCA: 17.51→15.16, −13%), indicating TPC-268 is a harder, more generalizable training regime. Incorporating taxonomic text prompts into CountGD [1] reduces MAE from 19.52 to 16.90 (full taxonomy), a 13.4% improvement, validating the utility of the hierarchical annotations as an inductive bias.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of class-agnostic counting (CAC) and plant science. While CAC has been well-studied on generic objects (FSC-147, FSCD-LVIS) and plant counting has been addressed in isolated, species-specific datasets from the phenotyping community (TasselNet, GWHD, StomataCounter), no prior benchmark has unified these threads by embedding the Linnaean taxonomic hierarchy into the counting problem. The paper repurposes the exemplar-based CAC paradigm and its standard evaluation protocol (MAE/RMSE/R² on density maps) but introduces a taxonomy-aware data split (species-organization level) and multi-scale observation range (microscopy to UAV) that have no precedent in existing counting benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TPC-268 exposes a significant gap: SOTA CAC methods (best test MAE ~17.5 by LOCA) struggle substantially with plant counting, and the t-SNE analysis reveals that learned visual features fail to cluster along taxonomic lines, suggesting current architectures cannot capture phylogenetic structure from appearance alone. The cross-dataset asymmetry (TPC-268-trained models transfer to FSC-147 but not vice versa) confirms that plants pose a fundamentally harder counting challenge than generic objects. Open questions include how to explicitly model phylogenetic similarity in visual representations, how to handle the extreme 4-order-of-magnitude scale range, and whether the long-tailed species distribution (53.5% of species have <20 images) can be mitigated through hierarchical transfer.
