## Problem

Physical AI systems like autonomous driving are evaluated on multiple competing rule-compliance metrics, and different data sources influence these metrics at heterogeneous rates. Existing data mixture optimization methods assume well-defined, homogeneous domains with uniform influence, overlooking the metric-dependent improvement rates that arise when data from different contexts (e.g., curvy suburban roads vs. dense urban traffic) affect different subsets of driving rules at distinct speeds.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pool of driving video clips segmented into fixed 10-second virtual clips (20 frames at 2 Hz), along with a feature representation for clustering (geolocation metadata or TF-IDF vectors from VLM-generated captions), small-scale pilot training results to fit per-cluster scaling curves, and a selection budget B. It produces an ordered sequence of selected clips and an implicit cluster-wise allocation, where each sample is drawn from the cluster currently offering the highest marginal utility gain, always prioritizing the highest-importance (lowest EPDMS) sample within that cluster.

## Method

MOSAIC is a three-stage data selection framework: (i) cluster the data pool into M domains and rank samples within each cluster by an importance score defined as the model's EPDMS on that individual clip; (ii) fit a saturating exponential scaling law ΔU_i(n) = a_i(1 − e^{−n/τ_i}) per cluster using two small pilot runs, where a_i captures asymptotic utility gain and τ_i governs saturation rate; (iii) iteratively allocate one sample at a time to the cluster with the highest first-difference marginal gain δ_i(b_i) = ΔU_i(b_i+1) − ΔU_i(b_i), drawing the next-highest-importance sample from that cluster. The key technical assumption is a linear separable approximation that each domain's contribution to overall utility improvement is independent, reducing the joint mixture optimization to per-cluster concave functions. This greedy first-difference procedure mimics gradient-based optimization of the data mixture without requiring explicit gradient computation over the discrete selection space.

## Results

On OpenScene, MOSAIC achieves 84.25 EPDMS at 4000 clips, outperforming the next-best baseline (Coreset, 83.63) and Random (80.38) by 0.62 and 3.87 points respectively, while requiring over 80% fewer samples to match Random's performance (BRMR = 0.18). On Navtrain, MOSAIC reaches 90.18 EPDMS at 1600 clips, surpassing Chameleon (89.50) and Coreset (89.30) by 0.68 and 0.88 points, with a BRMR of 0.37 indicating 63% data savings. Across all budgets on both datasets, MOSAIC consistently achieves the highest EPDMS and lowest BRMR, with the largest absolute gains observed in the DAC (driving area compliance) metric.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MOSAIC outperforms all three baselines across every budget on both OpenScene and Navtrain. Against Uncertainty (entropy-based active learning, [24]), MOSAIC wins decisively: on OpenScene at 250 clips, MOSAIC scores 77.38 vs. 70.78 EPDMS, and the Uncertainty method's BRMR reaches 14.58, meaning it needs 14.6× more data to match Random. Against Coreset (diversity-based selection, [46]), MOSAIC leads by approximately 1.1 EPDMS points on OpenScene (84.25 vs. 83.63 at 4000 clips) and 0.88 points on Navtrain (90.18 vs. 89.30 at 1600 clips), with a substantially lower BRMR (0.18 vs. 0.25 on OpenScene). Against Chameleon (kernel-ridge mixture weighting, [54]), MOSAIC achieves 1.33 points higher EPDMS on OpenScene at 4000 clips (84.25 vs. 82.92) and 0.68 points higher on Navtrain at 1600 clips (90.18 vs. 89.50); notably, Chameleon degrades under caption-based clustering while MOSAIC remains robust, suggesting Chameleon's kernel weighting is sensitive to domain structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

MOSAIC sits at the intersection of data mixture optimization (DoReMi, DOGE, Chameleon, ADO) and active learning / data pruning (CoreSet, influence-based selection), but departs from both in a key way: prior mixture methods assume pre-defined, homogeneous domains with consistent per-metric influence, while prior selection methods operate on individual samples without modeling how performance scales with domain-specific data volume. MOSAIC repurposes scaling-law fitting (borrowed from LLM pretraining literature) at the cluster level and combines it with importance-based ranking and iterative first-difference allocation to handle the multi-metric, heterogeneous-domain setting characteristic of physical AI. The closest conceptual ancestor is ADO [23], which also fits scaling estimators during training, but MOSAIC pre-fits per-cluster curves via pilot runs and explicitly optimizes over an aggregate utility of competing metrics rather than a single loss.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For autonomous driving and broader physical AI, data collection is the dominant cost, and the ability to reach full-training performance with 42% fewer clips (or match random selection with 80% fewer) has direct operational impact. The framework is general enough to transfer to any multi-metric learning problem with heterogeneous data sources, and the ablations confirm that both clustering and ranking contribute in complementary regimes (ranking dominates in low-data, clustering in high-data). Key limitations remain: the linear-separability assumption (Eq. 3) ignores cross-cluster interactions, the method requires pilot runs that add upfront compute, and performance is sensitive to clustering quality. Open questions include whether the saturating-exponential scaling form is universally appropriate, how the approach behaves with more than a handful of clusters, and whether the framework extends to online or streaming data collection settings.
