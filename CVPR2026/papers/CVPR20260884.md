## Problem

Autonomous humanoid loco-manipulation—coordinating locomotion and manipulation under onboard perception over long horizons—remains largely unsolved, with existing systems limited to blind locomotion, static tabletop manipulation, or heavy reliance on teleoperation. The paper asks whether large-scale visual sim-to-real, trained entirely in simulation, can produce an RGB-based policy that transfers zero-shot to a real humanoid for continuous walking, placing, grasping, and object transport.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260884/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The deployed student policy consumes a single RGB frame from an Intel RealSense D435i camera (encoded via a DINOv3 vision backbone) together with real-robot proprioception (base linear/angular velocities, projected gravity, joint positions and velocities, and fingertip forces). It outputs delta commands—incremental linear velocity (x,y), yaw rate, and joint-position deltas for arms and fingers—that are accumulated and passed to a pre-trained HOMIE whole-body controller, which handles low-level motor execution. The privileged teacher, by contrast, receives full exteroception including exact object/table transforms, placement targets, and task-stage indicators, but this information is never available at deployment.

## Method

VIRAL uses a two-phase teacher-student pipeline. The teacher is a goal-conditioned PPO policy trained on 16 GPUs with a delta action space over the HOMIE WBC command interface, stage-based rewards, and reference-state initialization from 200 teleoperated simulation demonstrations to bootstrap long-horizon exploration. The student is distilled from the teacher via a DAgger-and-BC mixture (alpha=0.5) on 64 GPUs with tiled rendering in Isaac Lab, using DINOv3 features fused with proprioception and a history-aware policy head. Sim-to-real transfer is achieved through extensive visual domain randomization (dome lighting, materials, camera extrinsics, image quality, sensor delay), system identification of the high-gear-ratio dexterous hand, and real-to-sim camera extrinsics calibration. A key empirical finding is that compute scale is a hard requirement: both teacher and student training fail or plateau in low-GPU regimes, while 8-64 GPUs yield reliable convergence and near-expert real-world success (54/59 cycles).

## Results

On a continuous loco-manipulation task (walking between tables, placing, grasping, and turning), VIRAL achieves 54/59 (91.5%) success across consecutive real-world trials on a Unitree G1 humanoid, with a 20.2 s cycle time. This matches near-expert teleoperation performance (100% success, 21.4 s cycle time for a 1000+ hour expert) while being ~1.2 s faster per cycle, and substantially outperforms a non-expert teleoperator (~1 hour experience) who reaches only 73% success with significantly slower execution. The policy also generalizes zero-shot to variations in tray position, robot start pose, table height/type/color, lighting, and object category without any real-world fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against other sim-to-real loco-manipulation methods in a head-to-head table; instead, it benchmarks against human teleoperators using the same HOMIE whole-body controller for a near-apple-to-apple comparison. VIRAL achieves 91.5% success (54/59), approaching the expert's 100% while executing 1.2 s faster per cycle (20.2 s vs. 21.4 s). Against the non-expert teleoperator (~1 hour of experience), VIRAL wins on both reliability (91.5% vs. 73%) and speed (20.2 s vs. significantly slower). The paper positions this as evidence that autonomous RGB-based loco-manipulation can reduce human workload in assisted teleoperation settings, though it acknowledges that matching the expert's perfect success rate remains an open challenge.

## Relation to Prior Work

VIRAL sits at the intersection of three largely separate research threads: sim-to-real legged locomotion (which typically ignores manipulation), sim-to-real tabletop manipulation (which assumes a fixed base), and humanoid loco-manipulation (which has relied on teleoperation or non-onboard sensors). Methodologically, it builds on the teacher-student privileged learning paradigm of Chen et al. [9] and the HOMIE whole-body controller [6] as a low-level API, while extending the DAgger/BC distillation recipe and domain randomization tools from prior manipulation sim-to-real work [2, 3, 62] to the mobile, long-horizon setting. A notable departure from the legged-RL literature is the use of a delta action space rather than absolute joint targets, and the integration of reference-state initialization from teleoperated demonstrations to bootstrap long-horizon RL exploration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is, to our knowledge, the first demonstration of zero-shot, RGB-only, onboard-sensor humanoid loco-manipulation trained entirely in simulation and deployed without real-world fine-tuning, achieving 54 consecutive task cycles at near-expert teleoperation reliability. The finding that compute scale (tens to dozens of GPUs) is not merely beneficial but *necessary* for both teacher and student training is a practically important and somewhat counterintuitive result that will shape how the community budgets resources for visual sim-to-real. Open questions include whether the recipe generalizes beyond the single walk-place-grasp-turn task, how it interacts with foundation-model or imitation-learning approaches the authors themselves flag as complementary, and whether the heavy simulation compute requirement can be reduced through better data efficiency or curriculum design.
