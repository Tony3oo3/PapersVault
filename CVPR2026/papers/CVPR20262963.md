## Problem

The standard F-measure optimization approach for multi-label classification (Dembczynski et al., 2013) has two practical limitations: the prediction step requires an O(q³) matrix multiplication that becomes prohibitive for large label sets, and the required multinomial estimators are trained on a label transformation that partitions already sparse positive samples into q categories, producing a long-tailed distribution that is difficult to learn. This paper addresses both issues with a computational reformulation and a sampling-based estimation strategy.

## Input / Output

The method takes a feature vector x (tabular features or ResNet-50 embeddings for image datasets) and, during autoregressive sampling, each binary estimator also receives the previously sampled labels y_<j as additional input. The output is the predicted binary label vector h(x) ∈ {0,1}^q, obtained by applying the F-measure Bayes rule to the estimated probability matrix V̂(x) ∈ [0,1]^{q×q} and empty-set probability p̂₀(x). The matrix V̂(x) is not predicted directly but estimated from m sampled label vectors via Monte Carlo counting, and the final prediction step uses FFT-accelerated convolution rather than explicit matrix multiplication.

## Method

The paper makes two key modifications to the Dembczynski et al. framework. First, it observes that the matrix U in the prediction step is a Hankel matrix (constant skew-diagonals), allowing the q×q matrix multiplication to be reformulated as q convolutions computed via FFT, reducing complexity from O(q³) to O(q² log q). Second, it replaces the q multinomial estimators with q binary estimators trained via binary cross-entropy, where the j-th estimator models P(Y_j=1 | Y_<j, x) using both the feature vector and preceding labels as input. These binary estimators form an autoregressive model that generates m sampled label vectors, from which V(x) is estimated by simple Monte Carlo counting (Equation 10). Theorem 2 provides a Frobenius-norm bound on the estimation error, decomposing it into a sampling term (O(√(log(1/δ)/m))) and a training-error term (O(√(qε_n))). The framework is further shown to be flexible: the same sampled labels can be used to optimize Hamming accuracy or subset accuracy via different prediction rules, enabling a one-model-for-many-metrics deployment.

## Results

The proposed SMN method achieves the best F1-measure across all six benchmark datasets, with scores of 66.02 (yeast), 56.44 (enron), 53.81 (medical), 44.98 (bibtex), 78.24 (COCO), and 86.08 (VOC). The most substantial gains appear on challenging datasets: medical (+15.27 over DMN) and bibtex (+7.13 over DMN), where sparsity issues are most severe. FFT-based prediction computation reduces runtime by ~23% compared to matrix multiplication (e.g., 1.03s vs 1.35s on COCO), and total SMN inference time stays within 20% of DMN across most datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SMN outperforms all six baselines on every dataset. The closest competitor is DMN [12] (direct multinomial estimation), which SMN beats by 0.13 on yeast, 1.11 on enron, 15.27 on medical, 7.13 on bibtex, 0.97 on COCO, and 1.07 on VOC. CCS [41] (convex calibrated surrogates) performs notably worse than DMN, especially on bibtex (21.06 vs 37.85) and medical (23.94 vs 38.54), confirming that the label transformation used in both methods exacerbates sparsity. ASL [32] (asymmetric loss) is competitive on medical (51.22) but still trails SMN by 2.59 points, and lags significantly on bibtex (38.33 vs 44.98). SF1 [3] (sigmoid F1 loss) achieves the best non-Bayes-rule result on COCO (77.87) but remains below SMN by 0.37. BR and CC [31] are consistently the weakest, as they do not optimize the F-measure Bayes rule; CC slightly outperforms BR on bibtex (34.15 vs 29.70) due to its exploitation of label dependencies, but both are far below SMN.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Bayes rule for F-measure optimization established by Dembczynski et al. (ICML 2013), which has served as the standard approach for over a decade. It makes two targeted modifications: (1) exploiting the Hankel structure of the prediction matrix to replace O(q³) matrix multiplication with O(q²log q) FFT-based convolution, and (2) replacing the direct multinomial estimation of q²+1 probabilities with an indirect sampling-then-estimation framework that trains only q binary classifiers. The sampling approach draws conceptual inspiration from Bayesian inference, the discriminative-vs-generative classifier literature (Ng & Jordan), and classifier chains, while the FFT acceleration follows a technique previously applied to Dice-optimized segmentation (Dai & Li).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The FFT acceleration is broadly applicable to any method built on the Dembczynski Bayes rule, making it a drop-in improvement for the entire F-measure optimization literature. The sampling-based estimation addresses a long-overlooked practical limitation (sparsity from label transformation) with theoretical consistency guarantees, and the one-to-many metric optimization capability (F1, HA, SA from a single model) offers practical value for deployment scenarios where evaluation criteria shift. Limitations include reliance on a simple feedforward backbone, modest sampling overhead, and the fact that the framework remains tied to the specific structure of the F-measure Bayes rule; whether the sparsity advantage persists with deeper architectures or very large label spaces (q > 1000) remains open.
