## Problem

RL-trained VLMs consistently outperform SFT-trained VLMs on out-of-distribution benchmarks, yet the prevailing explanation attributes this to RL's optimization objective (exploration, reward feedback). This paper asks whether the generalization gap can instead be explained by a data-centric mechanism: RL's group-relative advantage naturally zeros out gradients for easy and hard samples, implicitly restricting learning to medium-difficulty examples, while SFT's uniform treatment of all samples—especially hard ones—degrades OOD robustness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261927/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images (resized to a maximum of 896×896 pixels, i.e., 802,816 pixels) paired with text prompts. For image classification the output is a single-word class label (exact match, case-insensitive); for visual grounding it is a bounding box (correct if IoU ≥ 0.5); for reasoning tasks it is a multiple-choice text answer. A distinctive aspect of the pipeline is a pre-training generation step where the model produces G=8 responses per training instance (temperature 0.9, top-p 1.0) solely to assign a difficulty label before any fine-tuning begins.

## Method

DC-SFT (Difficulty-Curated SFT) explicitly replicates RL's implicit data filtering within a standard SFT pipeline. For every training prompt, the initialized model generates 8 responses; instances where all responses are correct are labeled easy, all incorrect are labeled hard, and mixed outcomes are labeled medium-difficulty. Two variants are proposed: SFT-M trains exclusively on medium-difficulty samples (mimicking RL's zero-advantage filtering of easy and hard items), while SFT-EM retains both easy and medium samples and discards only hard ones. The key insight is that in GRPO, uniform rewards across a group yield zero normalized advantage (A^k = 0), so easy and hard instances contribute negligible gradients—RL effectively trains only on medium-difficulty data. By removing hard samples (which produce disproportionately large gradient norms and drive overfitting to noisy patterns), DC-SFT achieves OOD performance exceeding GRPO while being 3–5× more computationally efficient and substantially more stable during training.

## Results

On the Qwen2.5-VL-7B model, SFT-EM (the proposed DC-SFT variant training on easy + medium-difficulty data) achieves 62.10% average OOD accuracy, surpassing standard SFT (57.62%) by 4.48% and GRPO (59.48%) by 2.62%. The SFT-M variant (medium-difficulty only) reaches 60.41% OOD Avg, still exceeding GRPO by 0.93%. For the 3B model, SFT-EM achieves 59.44% OOD Avg vs. 56.50% (SFT) and 58.31% (GRPO). DC-SFT also delivers 4.9× higher training efficiency on ImageNet and 3.2× on RefCOCO compared to GRPO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DC-SFT (SFT-M and SFT-EM) against two baselines: standard SFT on the full dataset and GRPO (the RL algorithm from DeepSeekMath [30]). SFT-EM consistently outperforms GRPO on average OOD accuracy across both model sizes (62.10% vs. 59.48% for 7B; 59.44% vs. 58.31% for 3B), while SFT-M also exceeds GRPO (60.41% vs. 59.48% for 7B). Both DC-SFT variants substantially beat standard SFT on OOD (by 2.52–4.48%) at the cost of a modest ID drop (1.5–2.5%). GRPO shows training instability with sharp performance drops during training (e.g., on ImageNet-R and Ref-L4), whereas DC-SFT maintains stable performance curves throughout. On the reasoning benchmarks (Table 5), SFT-M also outperforms both SFT and GRPO, achieving 55.32% average on the 7B model vs. 53.88% (SFT) and 53.12% (GRPO).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the well-documented empirical observation that RL post-training generalizes better than SFT for VLMs (Chu et al. [7], Chen et al. [5]), but departs from the prevailing algorithmic explanation (RL's exploration and reward-driven optimization) by proposing a data-centric mechanism: RL's GRPO-style advantage normalization implicitly zeroes out gradients for easy and hard samples, concentrating learning on medium-difficulty examples. It also connects to the data curation literature (rejection sampling, distillation) and the test-time scaling line of work (DeepSeek-R1 [9], Kimi K1.5 [33]), positioning DC-SFT as a bridge that lets SFT-based pipelines match or exceed RL's generalization without the algorithmic complexity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a trivial data-filtering step (removing hard samples) can match or surpass RL's OOD generalization at 3–5× lower compute cost and with far greater training stability, which is immediately actionable for teams post-training VLMs. However, the evidence is confined to ≤7B models, two task families (classification, grounding), and two model families (Qwen2.5-VL, MiniCPM-V-4), leaving open whether the difficulty-filtering mechanism holds at larger scale, for more complex multi-turn or agentic tasks, or when difficulty is defined by task-specific rather than model-specific criteria. The finding that even 5% hard data causes measurable OOD degradation is a useful diagnostic for practitioners, but its transferability to other modalities and training regimes remains untested.
