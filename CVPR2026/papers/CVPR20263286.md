## Problem

Google Earth imagery lacks orthorectification, causing inherent 2D positional offsets between open-source building footprint labels and their true roof locations in images. Training segmentation networks on these misaligned labels severely degrades performance, yet existing correction methods either rely on synthetic offset simulation with limited real-world generalization or on template matching whose effectiveness is tightly coupled to hand-crafted template design. The paper asks whether high-quality label correction can be achieved with minimal manual annotation, making large-scale dataset construction from freely available imagery practical.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 1024×1024 patches of 0.5 m-resolution non-orthorectified RGB satellite imagery from Google Earth Pro, raw binary building footprint masks from open databases (Microsoft Global ML or BRIGHT), and a small set of manually annotated ground-truth offsets covering less than 1% of the dataset. It produces per-instance 2D translation vectors (dx, dy) that are applied to the raw masks via a differentiable translation operator, yielding spatially corrected binary segmentation masks. The offset regression network operates at the object level using ROI-pooled features from a DeepLabV3+ backbone rather than predicting pixel-level corrections, preserving sharp building boundaries.

## Method

OMAF is a three-stage pipeline. First, a self-alignment module estimates each building's offset by minimizing a composite loss—edge alignment (mean distance from mask boundary to nearest image edge via a distance transform), intra-mask intensity variance, and L2 regularization—optimized via differentiable bilinear interpolation with a coarse-to-fine Gaussian pyramid and 9-point non-maximum-suppression-style initialization to escape local minima. Second, a statistical prior (2D Gaussian fitted from the <1% manual offsets) assigns a confidence score to each estimated offset, suppressing structurally similar but incorrect alignments. Third, an instance-level offset regression network (DeepLabV3+ with ResNet-D-101, ROI pooling, and normalized centroid positional encoding) is trained with a confidence-weighted L1 loss to refine the pseudo-labels, after which the corrected masks supervise standard segmentation models in a fully supervised manner.

## Results

On the Islahiye dataset, OMAF-corrected labels boost mIoU by 11.2–40.6 percentage points across seven segmentation architectures, with the largest gain seen in UNetFormer (35.8% → 76.4%, +40.6%). On Antakya, improvements range from +11.1% (SegMAN-B) to +23.2% (UNetFormer). Against domain adaptation baselines, OMAF with ≤1% manual labels achieves 77.3% mIoU on Islahiye and 67.7% on Antakya, outperforming the best semi-supervised DA method (DSTC at 20% labels: 70.8%/61.6%) by 6.5 and 6.1 points respectively, while requiring 20× less annotation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OMAF is a label-correction framework rather than a new segmentation architecture, so it is evaluated by training seven representative models (DeepLabV3+, UNetFormer, SegFormer-B, FeedFormer-B, VWFormer-B, VMamba-B, SegMAN-B) on raw vs. corrected labels. Every model improves substantially: mIoU gains range from +11.1% (SegMAN-B, Antakya) to +40.6% (UNetFormer, Islahiye), with AUC consistently reaching 98–99% after correction. Against domain adaptation baselines (Table 3), OMAF with ≤1% manual labels (77.3% mIoU Islahiye, 67.7% Antakya) outperforms the best UDA method BUS (62.1%/42.0%) by 15.2 and 25.7 points, and beats the best SSDA method DSTC at 20% labels (70.8%/61.6%) by 6.5 and 6.1 points, demonstrating that minimal annotation plus geometric correction is more cost-effective than scaling up labeled data for domain adaptation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of label-noise correction and remote sensing segmentation, departing from two dominant prior paradigms: (1) template-matching methods (Yuan, Vargas-Muñoz) that require hand-crafted templates per building type, and (2) simulated-offset training (Girard, Zampieri) that adds synthetic displacement to ground-truth labels. OMAF instead formulates correction as a differentiable, object-level optimization problem combining edge-distance and texture-variance constraints, then refines estimates with a learned instance-level regression network. The use of a small manually-annotated prior (<1%) to fit a Gaussian offset distribution and weight training losses is a novel weakly-supervised element not present in prior correction frameworks, which either assume unbiased majority labels or require full ground truth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: it unlocks large-scale, low-cost dataset construction from freely available Google Earth imagery paired with open footprint databases, a pipeline that is otherwise infeasible due to the lack of RPC metadata and DSMs. The finding that <1% manual annotation outperforms semi-supervised domain adaptation methods using 20% labeled data (by 6–7 mIoU) is a compelling cost-efficiency result for practitioners in underdeveloped regions. However, the method is explicitly limited to translational offsets and cannot handle shape mismatches or spurious annotations (scenarios b and c in Fig. 2), and the evaluation is confined to two self-constructed datasets of modest size (592 and 130 patches), leaving open whether the approach generalizes to other object categories or more complex geometric distortions.
