## Problem

Existing multimodal reasoning benchmarks treat images as static inputs and restrict intermediate reasoning to text-only chain-of-thought, yet many real-world problems (spatial layout, geometric construction, physics simulation) are intrinsically visual and require humans to 'draw to think.' No prior benchmark systematically evaluates whether MLLMs can leverage or generate intermediate visual representations (sketches, diagrams, path drawings) as part of their reasoning process, leaving a fundamental gap between perception-oriented evaluation and true visual reasoning.

## Input / Output

Each of the 546 problems provides a problem image (geometry diagram, physics setup, puzzle board, etc.) paired with a text question. Under the Visual-CoT evaluation level, the model additionally receives human-annotated intermediate visual images (e.g., force-body diagrams, convex hull sketches, die-tracking sequences) alongside a textual thinking prompt. The model outputs a single text answer, which is extracted via a tiered pipeline (rule-based parsing of <answer> tags, regex heuristics, then LLM-as-judge fallback) and scored as micro-averaged accuracy across four task domains (Euclidean Geometry, Physics-Based Reasoning, Abstract Spatial & Logical Puzzles, Causal Transformations).

## Method

MIRA introduces a three-level diagnostic evaluation protocol that decouples the contribution of visual information from textual generation: Level 1 (Direct) gives only the image and question; Level 2 (Text-CoT) adds a step-by-step thinking prompt; Level 3 (Simulated Visual-CoT) provides both human-annotated intermediate visual images and a textual CoT prompt. The dataset is constructed via a hybrid pipeline combining manual authoring by graduate researchers (inspired by puzzle communities), programmatic generation via Python scripts for fine-grained difficulty control, and image editing (GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Flash) for visual clarity, followed by cross-review to ensure single unambiguous ground-truth answers. To probe model upper bounds, the paper additionally reports pass@k (k=1,2,4,8) and majority voting over 8 samples, and designs task-specific Text-CoT prompts aligned with Visual-CoT reasoning paths to isolate the marginal contribution of visual cues beyond textual guidance.

## Results

On the MIRA benchmark (546 problems across 4 categories), no model exceeds 20% accuracy under direct input (D); the best performer, GPT-5.2, reaches only 20.9% (D) / 19.6% (T) / 39.5% (V). The average across all 22 evaluated models is 14.9% (D), 14.7% (T), and 21.0% (V), with Visual-CoT yielding an average relative gain of 33.7%. Text-CoT actively degrades performance for several strong models: Gemini 2.5 Pro drops from 16.9% to 13.8% and o3 from 16.4% to 14.1%, while the Puzzles category is the hardest (9.5% average under D vs. 16.1% on other categories).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 22 MLLMs across three tiers. Among closed-source models, GPT-5.2 leads with 39.5% under Visual-CoT, followed by Gemini 3 Flash (34.4%) and Gemini 3 Pro (29.3%); notably, Gemini 3 Pro actually *degrades* from 27.0% (D) to 29.3% (V), suggesting multi-image reasoning limitations. Open-weight understanding models (Qwen2.5-VL, GLM 4.5V, Qwen3-VL) trail significantly, averaging 13.0% (V) versus 21.0% for closed-source, with Qwen3-VL (235B) the strongest at 26.2%. Unified open-weight models (Bagel 7B, Janus-Pro 7B) perform worst at 8.8% and 7.2% (V), indicating that generation capability alone does not translate to effective visual reasoning. Text-CoT consistently underperforms or matches direct input: it reduces accuracy for Gemini 2.5 Pro (−3.1 pp), o3 (−2.3 pp), and GPT-4o (−2.2 pp), while specialized Text-CoT prompts (Table 2) recover only +1.4% average over generic prompts—far below the +4.7% average gain from Visual-CoT. Pass@8 and majority voting yield diminishing returns (only +3.0% from k=4 to k=8), confirming that failures stem from a fundamental capability gap rather than sampling variance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant text-only Chain-of-Thought paradigm (Wei et al., Kojima et al.) by arguing that many real-world reasoning problems are intrinsically visual and cannot be faithfully decomposed into verbal steps. It builds conceptually on Visual CoT (Shao et al.) and tool-augmented sketching methods (Visual Sketchpad, VisProg, ViperGPT), but shifts the focus from algorithmic tool-use to a benchmark-level evaluation of whether intermediate visual images are *necessary* for correct reasoning. Unlike prior multimodal benchmarks (MMMU, MMStar, EMMA, VisuLogic) that treat images as static inputs with text-only intermediate steps, MIRA explicitly requires models to process sequences of annotated intermediate visual states, introducing a three-level diagnostic protocol (Direct → Text-CoT → Visual-CoT) that isolates the contribution of visual information from textual generation ability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MIRA exposes a fundamental capability gap: even the strongest proprietary models (GPT-5, o3, Gemini 2.5 Pro) score below 20% on direct input, and Text-CoT actively *degrades* performance for several of them, challenging the assumption that verbalized reasoning universally helps. The 33.7% average relative gain from providing intermediate visual clues, combined with the minimal improvement from pass@k or majority voting, suggests that current MLLMs lack a core 'thinking while drawing' skill rather than merely suffering from sampling noise. The open question is whether unified models with native image generation (Bagel, Janus-Pro) can close this gap through training, or whether a new architectural paradigm is needed to tightly couple visual generation with subsequent reasoning steps.
