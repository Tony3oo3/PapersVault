## Problem

Existing exemplar-free class-incremental continual learning (ExfCCL) methods with Vision Transformers either freeze the backbone and learn task-specific prompts (high stability, low plasticity) or uniformly adapt all layers (high plasticity, low stability), but neither adapts computation to task difficulty or inter-task synergy. The Skip operation (bypassing layers for easier tasks) and New operation (introducing entirely new layers for dissimilar tasks) remain unexplored, and tiny backbones are under-studied. This paper addresses these gaps by learning task-tailored dynamic backbones that balance stability and plasticity through a principled exploration-exploitation mechanism.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images from a stream of tasks with significantly varying class counts and training set sizes (e.g., MNIST with 10 classes vs. FGVC Aircraft with 100 classes). At inference, the model first infers the task ID of an unseen test sample via an external task-centroid memory (requiring an additional forward pass through the initial backbone), then routes through the task-specific learned architecture to produce a class label. The learned architecture itself is task-dependent: different tasks yield different FLOPs (e.g., 56 GFLOPs for MNIST vs. 66 GFLOPs for Cars on MTIL with ViT-Base) because layers are selectively skipped, reused, adapted, or replaced.

## Method

CHEEM represents internal parameter memory as a Mixture-of-Experts at selected ViT layers (MLP^Down or attention projection), where each expert is a tuple of parameters and a task-specific mean CLS token. For each new task, a Supernet is constructed offering four operations per layer—Reuse (share existing expert), Adapt (add LoRA low-rank update), New (initialize a fresh layer), and Skip (bypass the entire FFN or MHSA block). The Supernet is trained via HEE-NAS, a hierarchical exploration-exploitation sampling strategy: a first-level categorical distribution over experts is computed from normalized cosine similarity of pooled CLS tokens, and a second-level Bernoulli decision (sigmoid of similarity) selects Reuse vs. Adapt; an auxiliary expert with negative-max similarity handles New/Skip. After Supernet training, a compute-aware evolutionary search (crossover and mutation with a performance-tolerance threshold) selects the target architecture balancing validation accuracy and FLOPs, which is then retrained from scratch. An epoch-wise schedule blends pure uniform exploration with the hierarchical exploitation strategy to balance diversity and convergence.

## Results

On the MTIL benchmark with ViT-Base, CHEEM achieves 85.9% average accuracy with 1.7% average forgetting and 62.3 FLOPs, closely approaching the full fine-tuning upper bound of 88.1%. On VDD with ViT-Base, CHEEM reaches 86.7% average accuracy with only 0.4% forgetting. With the weaker DEiT-Tiny backbone, CHEEM achieves 74.5% (MTIL) and 76.18% (VDD) average accuracy, nearly matching the full fine-tuning upper bounds of 75.3% and 76.21% respectively. The proposed Figure-of-Merit (FoM) metric shows CHEEM is 10–24× better than prompting-based baselines on MTIL with ViT-Base.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CHEEM outperforms all eight baselines on both MTIL and VDD across both backbone sizes. Against the strongest prompting-based method S-Prompts, CHEEM gains 4.3% average accuracy on MTIL (85.9% vs 81.6%) and 8.1% on VDD (86.7% vs 78.6%) with ViT-Base, while also reducing FLOPs (62.3 vs 67.6 G). Against DIKI, which uses the lowest FLOPs among competitive methods (42.5 G on MTIL), CHEEM achieves 9.5% higher accuracy at a modest 47% FLOPs increase. The closest competitor is LoRA-CL (a special case of CHEEM without NAS), which achieves 84.7% on MTIL ViT-Base vs CHEEM's 85.9%, but requires 68.2 G FLOPs vs 62.3 G since it cannot skip modules. On the weaker DEiT-Tiny backbone, the gap widens: CHEEM reaches 74.5% on MTIL vs 71.1% for LoRA-CL and 67.3% for S-Prompts, demonstrating that HEE-NAS is especially beneficial when backbone capacity is limited. The FoM metric (Table 1) quantifies this: CHEEM is 24.2× better than CODA-Prompt and 31.1× better than L2P on MTIL with ViT-Base, while on VDD with DEiT-Tiny the advantage reaches 2811× over CODA-Prompt due to near-parity with the full fine-tuning upper bound.

## Relation to Prior Work

CHEEM extends the learn-to-grow paradigm from L2G [20] (which applied DARTS-based structure search to ConvNets) to Vision Transformers, replacing uniform DARTS with a hierarchical exploration-exploitation (HEE) sampling strategy that leverages task-synergy signals from pooled CLS tokens. The supernet training builds on SPOS [14], and the evolutionary target-network selection adapts Regularized Evolution [32] with a compute-aware ranking criterion. For task ID inference, CHEEM adopts the external task-centroid memory from S-Prompts [40], decoupling it from the internal parameter memory that is the paper's core contribution. Conceptually, the paper departs from both prompting-based methods (which freeze the backbone and lack plasticity) and uniform parameter-tuning methods (which adapt every layer identically) by introducing four asymmetric operations—Reuse, Adapt, New, Skip—that allow per-layer, per-task structural decisions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CHEEM demonstrates that task-difficulty-aware dynamic backbones can approach full fine-tuning upper bounds (within ~2% on MTIL and VDD) while allocating fewer FLOPs to easier tasks, offering a practical path for deploying continual learning on edge devices with limited compute. The proposed Figure-of-Merit metric provides a more holistic comparison axis than accuracy alone, and the finding that prompting-based methods (CODA-Prompt, DualPrompt, L2P) degrade severely on imbalanced benchmarks like MTIL and VDD challenges the assumption that frozen-backbone prompting is universally robust. Open questions include scalability to longer task streams (beyond 10 tasks), generalization to non-ViT architectures or other modalities, and whether the NAS training overhead can be further reduced for real-time deployment.
