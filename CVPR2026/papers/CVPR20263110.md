## Problem

Multimodal autoregressive (AR) models match diffusion models in general text-to-image generation but fail at subject-driven image generation: direct LoRA or end-to-end fine-tuning on a few reference images either cannot capture the subject's appearance or severely degrades the model's semantic following capability. This gap limits AR models' utility in the highly user-relevant task of personalizing generation to a specific subject.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263110/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small set of reference images of a target subject (following DreamBooth's 9-subject benchmark with 4 live and 5 inanimate objects) and text prompts in the format 'a S* {category}', where S* is a predefined token bound to the subject. It outputs novel RGB images of that subject at 768×768 resolution (Lumina-mGPT) or comparable resolution (LlamaGen-XL). During inference, the subject token S* is embedded in the prompt to trigger subject-specific generation, and 4 images are produced per prompt across 25 test prompts per subject.

## Method

Proxy-Tuning is a three-stage pipeline: (1) a diffusion model (SDXL, SD3, SD3.5, or FLUX) is fine-tuned via LoRA on the subject's reference images using the DreamBooth paradigm; (2) the fine-tuned diffusion model generates a diverse proxy dataset by sampling prompts of the form 'a S* {category}'; (3) the AR student (LlamaGen or Lumina-mGPT) is fine-tuned with LoRA on this synthesized proxy data. The key finding is a weak-to-strong generalization phenomenon: the AR student consistently surpasses its diffusion supervisor in subject fidelity (CLIP-I, DINO) and prompt adherence, a behavior absent when the same pipeline is applied to diffusion students. The authors attribute this to AR models' discrete token representation, which allows them to fit the primary token distribution encoding the subject's local appearance while filtering out biased tokens introduced by the diffusion model's exposure bias and attribute bias.

## Results

With SDXL as the diffusion supervisor, Proxy-Tuned LlamaGen achieves CLIP-I 0.8152 and DINO 0.7436, surpassing the supervisor's own scores of 0.8002 and 0.7272 respectively, while Proxy-Tuned Lumina-mGPT reaches CLIP-I 0.8074 and DINO 0.7834. This weak-to-strong generalization holds across all four diffusion supervisors tested (SDXL, SD3, SD3.5, FLUX), with AR students consistently outperforming their teachers on subject fidelity metrics (CLIP-I, DINO) while maintaining competitive prompt adherence (CLIP-T). Direct LoRA fine-tuning of AR models on the original DreamBooth dataset yields substantially lower scores (e.g., CLIP-I 0.6752, DINO 0.5088 for LlamaGen), confirming that Proxy-Tuning is essential for effective subject-driven AR generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Proxy-Tuning decisively outperforms direct DreamBooth-style fine-tuning (LoRA or end-to-end) on AR models: LlamaGen with Proxy-Tuning achieves CLIP-I 0.8152 vs. 0.6752 for direct LoRA, and DINO 0.7436 vs. 0.5088, a gap of over 14 points in subject fidelity. Against the diffusion supervisors themselves, Proxy-Tuned AR students win on both CLIP-I and DINO across all four architectures tested (e.g., LlamaGen w/ Proxy-Tuning beats SDXL by +0.015 CLIP-I and +0.016 DINO; Lumina-mGPT w/ Proxy-Tuning beats FLUX by +0.008 CLIP-I and +0.024 DINO), demonstrating the weak-to-strong generalization phenomenon. On CLIP-T (prompt adherence), the AR students are slightly below their diffusion supervisors (e.g., 0.2772 vs. 0.3225 for LlamaGen vs. SDXL), though the user study (Table 6) shows human evaluators rate Proxy-Tuned AR models higher in prompt fidelity (4.52 vs. 4.33 for direct diffusion fine-tuning), suggesting CLIP-T underestimates actual prompt adherence. When Proxy-Tuning is applied to diffusion students instead (Table 4), all four diffusion models degrade relative to their own DreamBooth baselines (e.g., SDXL drops from 0.8002 to 0.7941 CLIP-I), confirming the weak-to-strong effect is unique to AR architectures. In multi-subject personalization (Table 5), a single LlamaGen model jointly learning multiple subjects matches or exceeds individually-tuned diffusion supervisors (e.g., LlamaGen(SD3) achieves DINO 0.7335 vs. SD3's 0.6965), while diffusion models trained jointly on multiple subjects exhibit severe subject mixing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DreamBooth paradigm of subject-driven image generation (binding a subject to a learned token) from diffusion models to multimodal autoregressive models, and adapts the weak-to-strong generalization concept previously observed in NLP (Burns et al., 2023) to the image generation domain. It departs from direct fine-tuning approaches (LoRA or end-to-end) that fail for AR models by introducing a proxy data synthesis stage where a fine-tuned diffusion model generates training pairs for the AR student. The method sits at the intersection of the AR image generation line (LlamaGen, Lumina-mGPT, Chameleon, Emu3) and the diffusion-based personalization line (DreamBooth, DisenBooth, IP-Adapter), bridging the two architectures through a teacher-student pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first empirical evidence that weak-to-strong generalization exists in image generation, showing AR students consistently surpass their diffusion supervisors in subject fidelity across four different diffusion architectures (U-Net and DiT at 2B–12B scale) and two AR models. This has practical implications for personalization pipelines, suggesting AR models can be efficiently adapted via proxy data rather than direct fine-tuning, and that a single AR model can handle multiple subjects simultaneously where diffusion models degrade. Open questions include a theoretical explanation for why the discrete token prediction mechanism filters out diffusion-induced bias, automated selection of proxy dataset size, and whether the phenomenon extends beyond subject-driven generation to other conditional tasks.
