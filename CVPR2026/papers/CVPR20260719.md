## Problem

CLIP-based continual learning suffers from two underexplored issues: a severe text distribution gap between rich pretraining captions and stark class-name labels used during adaptation, and a performance mismatch where the vision encoder alone outperforms the full dual-encoder model on fine-grained tasks while CLIP dominates on natural images. Existing approaches either discard the text encoder or rely on external multimodal LLMs for caption generation, failing to exploit the complementary knowledge already encoded within CLIP's own components.

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes an RGB image and standard class-name text templates ('a photo of a [class name]') and outputs a class label via the original CLIP architecture, requiring no architectural modification. During training, the input is augmented with sample-specific learnable pseudo-caption tokens (K=3 per image) optimized in CLIP's continuous embedding space, and a mixed batch combining target data with COCO Captions reference samples for contrastive richness. A temporary prototypical vision classifier, initialized from pretrained vision encoder features, serves as an auxiliary training head but is discarded before deployment.

## Method

Learning from Itself (LfI) mines CLIP's internal knowledge through two mechanisms. First, it generates pseudo-captions by freezing CLIP and optimizing per-sample learnable tokens to minimize the contrastive loss, regularized by supervised contrastive loss (preserving class-level coherence) and vocabulary alignment loss (preventing adversarial drift), effectively bridging the pretraining-finetuning text distribution gap without external models. Second, it introduces adaptive mutual distillation between the CLIP dual-encoder head and a prototypical vision classifier, where a sigmoid-weighted mixing coefficient dynamically determines which head teaches more based on their instantaneous cross-entropy losses, enabling the stronger branch to guide the weaker one. The total training objective combines supervised cross-entropy for both heads, an auxiliary CLIP loss using the generated tokens, and the adaptive bidirectional KL distillation, with all CLIP parameters unfrozen (except temperature) during adaptation.

## Results

On OpenCLIP ViT-B/16, LfI achieves state-of-the-art continual learning accuracy across all five evaluated datasets: 83.82% (last) / 88.65% (avg) on CIFAR-100 (10-task), 83.57% / 86.25% on ImageNet-R, 81.74% / 87.23% on CUB-200, 91.42% / 94.41% on Stanford Cars (10-task), and 89.44% / 92.47% on Food-101. On OpenAI CLIP ViT-B/16, LfI reaches 82.69% / 87.84% on CIFAR-100, 83.97% / 88.08% on ImageNet-R, and 80.68% / 88.29% on ImageNet-100, consistently outperforming all baselines by 1–5%. Gains are most pronounced on fine-grained tasks (e.g., +1.54% over ENGINE on CUB-200, +1.34% on Stanford Cars) and on Food-101 (+5.55% over ENGINE), while on natural images LfI approaches the joint-training upper bound (83.82% vs. 89.44% on CIFAR-100).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LfI outperforms the strongest VLM-based baseline ENGINE [58] on every dataset and protocol: +4.60% last on CIFAR-100 (83.82 vs. 79.22), +3.20% on ImageNet-R (83.57 vs. 80.37), +1.54% on CUB-200 (81.74 vs. 80.20), +1.34% on Stanford Cars (91.42 vs. 90.08), and +5.55% on Food-101 (89.44 vs. 83.89). Against naive finetuning, LfI wins on CIFAR-100 (+2.91%), ImageNet-R (+2.28%), CUB-200 (+10.68%), and Food-101 (+1.96%), but trails slightly on Stanford Cars 50-10 protocol (85.57 vs. 83.01 for 50-10, though LfI wins on the 10-task protocol). Prompt-based methods (L2P, DualPrompt, CODA-Prompt) and CoOp lag substantially, with CODA-Prompt catastrophically failing on ImageNet-100 (34.76% vs. LfI's 80.68% on OpenAI CLIP). Vision-only prototypical methods (SimpleCIL, APER) show strong zero-shot performance on fine-grained tasks but cannot match LfI's post-training gains (e.g., SimpleCIL 79.57% vs. LfI 81.74% on CUB-200). On OpenAI CLIP, LfI also surpasses the most recent VLM methods MG-CLIP [16] (+2.12% on CIFAR-100, +1.30% on ImageNet-R) and RAPF [15] (+3.65% on CIFAR-100), confirming robustness across CLIP variants.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on CLIP's dual-encoder architecture (Radford et al., 2021) and the prototypical classifier paradigm from SimpleCIL (Zhou et al., 2025), but departs from the dominant trend in CLIP continual learning that either discards the text encoder or relies on external knowledge sources (e.g., ENGINE's GPT-4 injection). It repurposes the classical mutual learning formulation (Zhang et al., 2018) into an adaptive asymmetric variant where the stronger branch dynamically teaches the weaker one, and introduces a novel pseudo-caption generation mechanism that optimizes learnable tokens within CLIP's frozen embedding space to bridge the pretraining-finetuning text distribution gap. Conceptually, the paper positions itself against both vision-only adaptation methods and external-knowledge-injection approaches, arguing that CLIP's internal components already contain complementary knowledge that can be reorganized without architectural modification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification and quantification of the vision-text performance mismatch (20% gap on fine-grained tasks) provides a concrete diagnostic for practitioners choosing between vision-only and full CLIP pipelines, and the adaptive mutual distillation offers a principled way to combine both without ensembling. The self-contained nature—requiring no external captioning models or GPT-4 access—makes the approach more deployable in resource-constrained or privacy-sensitive settings. However, the method adds substantial training complexity (separate token generation phase, dual-head training, multiple loss terms), and results are limited to ViT-B/16 backbones and class-incremental settings; whether the pseudo-caption mechanism and adaptive distillation scale to larger models or other continual learning paradigms (domain-incremental, task-incremental) remains open. The modest 1–3% margins over strong baselines like ENGINE also leave room for question about whether the added complexity is justified in all deployment scenarios.
