## Problem

Current deepfake detection benchmarks treat all misclassification errors as equally significant, measuring only 'how accurate' a detector is rather than 'how socially beneficial' it is. A model that achieves 95% accuracy while missing the most socially damaging 5% of forgeries may cause more real-world harm than one with 85% accuracy that reliably catches high-impact fakes, yet no existing evaluation framework captures this distinction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260717/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Inputs are video clips spanning face forgery (3–30 s, ≤1280×720) and AI-generated sources (2–120 s, up to 3840×2160), uniformly sampled to 32 frames with faces extracted via dlib and resized to 256×256. Beyond standard real/fake classification and AUC/Accuracy/EER/AP metrics, the framework produces a per-sample SMI score (combining model confidence, human perceptual ratings from 10 non-AI participants, and a normalized length/resolution content factor) and an SMI-weighted accuracy (Acc-SMI) that reweights each sample's contribution by its societal risk. The SMI-critical dataset comprises 17,653 false-negative samples (5,673 face forgery + 11,980 AIG) annotated with these SMI labels.

## Method

DeepfakeImpact is a two-stage benchmark. Stage I establishes standardized technical baselines by evaluating 33 SOTA detectors (CNN, ViT, GNN-based) across 12 datasets under a unified preprocessing, training, and evaluation pipeline supporting both frame-level and video-level models. Stage II introduces the Social Misjudgment Impact (SMI) metric, which averages three complementary signals—model-based confidence (mean predicted 'real' probability across all models that misclassify a sample), human-based confidence (mean perceived-realism score from 10 lay participants), and a content factor (normalized video duration and resolution)—to quantify the social harm of each false negative. During training, an SMI-weighted cross-entropy loss prioritizes high-SMI samples, and during evaluation, Acc-SMI weights each sample's correct/incorrect indicator by its SMI score, shifting the optimization target from raw accuracy to social risk mitigation. The framework also includes ablations on data augmentations, backbone architectures, and t-SNE visualizations showing that SMI-based loss improves feature separation between detected and undetected forgeries.

## Results

On face forgery cross-domain evaluation (trained on FF++, tested on 4 other datasets), SSTGNN achieves the best video-level average AUC of 0.8912, while Effort leads frame-level cross-domain with 0.8534. On AIG datasets (cross-domain, trained on Youku-mPLUG + ZeroScope), SSTGNN attains 0.8591 average AUC at video level and Effort achieves 0.8583 at frame level. In-domain performance is substantially higher (e.g., SSTGNN 0.9766 on FF++ video-level), with a 7–12% average drop in cross-domain settings. The SMI-critical dataset contains 17,653 misclassified fake samples (5,673 FF + 11,980 AIG), and Acc-SMI evaluation reveals that top AUC performers like UIA-ViT and FTCN degrade notably on socially critical samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SSTGNN (graph neural network, video-level) dominates cross-domain evaluation on both face forgery (0.8912 avg AUC) and AIG datasets (0.8591), outperforming the next-best video-level methods FTCN (0.8828) and MINTIME (0.8762) on FF by 0.8–1.5 AUC points. Effort (orthogonal subspace decomposition, frame-level) is the strongest frame-level method on AIG datasets (0.8583 avg AUC), narrowly trailing SSTGNN and significantly outperforming EfficientNet (0.8348) and XceptionNet (0.8042). On in-domain FF evaluation, PCL (0.9595) and UIA-ViT (0.9578) lead frame-level, while SSTGNN (0.9766) and DSPFWA (0.9601) lead video-level. A critical finding is the divergence under SMI-aware evaluation: UIA-ViT and FTCN, which rank highly in conventional AUC, show substantially degraded Acc-SMI on the SMI-critical dataset, whereas Effort and SSTGNN maintain their leading positions, indicating that technical accuracy does not guarantee social robustness. Video-level methods generally exhibit smaller in-domain-to-cross-domain drops than frame-level methods, suggesting temporal modeling provides regularization against domain shift.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DeepfakeBench [54] lineage of standardized deepfake detection benchmarks by adding a second evaluation stage that incorporates social impact. Prior benchmarks (FaceForensics++, DFDC, DeepfakeBench, DeepfakeBench-MM) and reliability-oriented studies [49, 47] focus on technical accuracy, transferability, or robustness, treating all detection errors as equally weighted. DeepfakeImpact departs from this by introducing the SMI metric, which re-weights errors based on model confidence, human perceptual convincingness, and video content factors, explicitly linking detection failures to potential societal harm rather than algorithmic robustness alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central finding—that models ranking highly on conventional AUC (e.g., UIA-ViT, FTCN) can degrade substantially on socially critical samples while moderate performers (e.g., EfficientNet) excel—challenges the assumption that technical accuracy proxies real-world protective value. However, the SMI metric's construction is somewhat ad hoc: it relies on only 10 human raters, uses a simple average of three heterogeneous factors, and defines 'socially critical' samples circularly from existing models' false negatives, raising questions about whether the metric captures genuine societal risk or merely model-specific blind spots. Open questions include how to scale human-based SMI annotation, whether the content factor (duration/resolution) meaningfully proxies social harm, and whether SMI-weighted training generalizes beyond the specific false-negative distribution it was constructed from.
