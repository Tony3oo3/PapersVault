## Problem

Commonly used image quality metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, DISTS, NIQE, etc.) exhibit limited or even negative correlation with human perceptual preferences when evaluating modern super-resolution models. The paper identifies three intrinsic challenges: distortion-oriented FR metrics regress toward averaged/blurry solutions, perception-oriented FR metrics fail when ground-truth images are of suboptimal quality, and existing metrics struggle to discriminate subtle perceptual differences among high-quality SR outputs.

## Input / Output

The RQI model takes an ordered pair of RGB images — the SR output (target) and the GT reference — and outputs a single scalar score representing the relative perceptual quality of the target with respect to the reference. The score is signed: positive values indicate the target is perceptually superior to the reference, negative values indicate it is inferior. The framework is asymmetric, so swapping the input order flips the sign of the prediction. At evaluation time, the SR output is always placed first in the input order, and scores are compared across models to rank perceptual quality.

## Method

RQI reformulates IQA training from absolute quality regression to relative quality discrepancy prediction. Given a degradation sequence {I_1,...,I_n} with MOS scores, instead of pairing each distorted image with the pristine reference, RQI constructs dense pairwise comparisons between all image pairs {I_i, I_j} (i≠j), using the MOS difference (q_i − q_j) as the regression target. This design allows any image — including distorted ones — to serve as the reference, naturally teaching the model to handle imperfect GTs. Training uses Huber loss for stable gradients on small score differences, and the final regression layer removes its activation function to permit negative outputs. The framework is architecture-agnostic: it is applied to existing IQA backbones (AHIQ, MANIQA, TOPIQ) trained on standard IQA datasets (Kadid-10K, PieAPP, PIPAL) without collecting SR-specific data, and the best model (MANIQA trained on PIPAL) achieves the highest SRCC with human judgments across all tested SR benchmarks.

## Results

On the user-study benchmarks, RQI (MANIQA trained on PIPAL under the RQI scheme) achieves the highest SRCC with human perception across all four datasets: 0.744 on DIV2K, 0.504 on RealSR, 0.529 on DRealSR, and 0.664 on Set5&Set14, outperforming the next-best metric (DeQA-Score at 0.613 on DIV2K) by a substantial margin. Traditional distortion metrics (SSIM, PSNR) exhibit *negative* SRCC (e.g., −0.348 and −0.079 on DIV2K), confirming they contradict human preference. On the broader IQA benchmarks (Table 3), RQI maintains stable performance, achieving SRCC_all of 0.901 on BSD-SR, 0.910 on QADS, 0.609 on SRIQA-Bench, and 0.666 on Kadid-10K, while also serving as an effective auxiliary loss that improves perceptual scores (Clip-IQA, DeQA) of SwinIR, SeeSR, and PiSA-SR without sacrificing structural fidelity (SSIM, PSNR).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RQI decisively outperforms all ten baselines on the primary user-study evaluation. Distortion-oriented FR metrics (SSIM, PSNR) are the worst performers, showing *negative* SRCC on every dataset (e.g., SSIM = −0.348, PSNR = −0.355 on DRealSR), confirming they reward blurry averages over perceptually rich textures. Perception-oriented FR metrics (DISTS, LPIPS) achieve moderate SRCC on DIV2K (0.610, 0.415) but collapse to near-zero or negative values on RealSR (0.048, 0.008) and DRealSR (−0.102, −0.141), where GT quality is suboptimal. NR metrics (NIQE, PI, Clip-IQA, MANIQA) show mid-range consistency (SRCC 0.19–0.59) but lack reference-based fidelity assessment. The two strongest baselines—AFINE (SRCC 0.581 on DIV2K, 0.484 on DRealSR) and DeQA-Score (0.613 on DIV2K, 0.437 on DRealSR)—are both surpassed by RQI (0.744 and 0.529 respectively), with RQI achieving a 0.131 SRCC gap over DeQA-Score on DIV2K and a 0.095 gap over AFINE on DRealSR. On the broader IQA benchmarks (Table 3), RQI remains competitive or best: it leads on SRIQA-Bench (SRCC_all 0.609 vs. AFINE 0.574) and Kadid-10K (0.666 vs. LPIPS 0.741 on SRCC_mean but RQI is the only method evaluated on all four criteria simultaneously), while SSIM/PSNR dominate only on the older BSD-SR and QADS datasets where degradations are more pronounced. When used as an auxiliary training loss, RQI improves perceptual scores (Clip-IQA, DeQA) of SeeSR and PiSA-SR by 0.01–0.03 over their baselines, whereas AFINE as a loss yields smaller or inconsistent gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on existing IQA architectures (MANIQA, AHIQ, TOPIQ) but introduces a new training paradigm—Relative Quality Index (RQI)—that reformulates the supervision signal from absolute quality scores to pairwise relative quality discrepancies. It departs from traditional FR-IQA (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, DISTS) by rejecting the assumption of perfect references, and from NR-IQA by retaining reference-based fidelity measurement. The closest prior work is AFINE [9], which also relaxes the perfect-reference assumption, but RQI differs by requiring no SR-specific training data, using a regression (rather than probability-based) formulation, and constructing denser pairwise comparisons that naturally cover subtle quality differences. The paper also positions itself as a general framework applicable to any existing IQA model, rather than proposing a new architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that PSNR and SSIM exhibit negative correlation with human perception on modern SR benchmarks is a strong empirical warning that the field's default evaluation metrics are actively misleading for current-generation models. The RQI framework's simplicity—requiring only a relabeling of training pairs and a Huber loss—makes it immediately adoptable by practitioners without architectural changes, and its dual utility as both an evaluation metric and an SR training loss broadens its practical impact. Limitations include evaluation restricted to ×4 SR, reliance on existing IQA datasets with multiple degradation levels per scene, and the fact that the best results (MANIQA_RQI on PIPAL) still achieve only moderate SRCC (~0.5–0.7) on the hardest benchmarks. The authors note potential generalization to deblurring, denoising, and deraining, but these remain untested.
