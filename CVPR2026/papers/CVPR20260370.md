## Problem

Existing automated circuit-discovery pipelines assign importance scores to edges and then impose a manually fixed top-k budget to select the final circuit, making circuit size an ex ante hyperparameter rather than a consequence of retained behavior. This fixed-budget design makes circuits highly sensitive to the chosen budget, induces a trade-off where overly small circuits lose sufficiency and necessity while overly large circuits become unselective, and does not transfer cleanly across tasks or models.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained transformer language model (GPT-2, Qwen-2.5, Gemma-2, Llama-3.1) and a calibration split of task data. It computes a signed importance score for every directed edge in the model via integrated gradients over a continuous edge gate, then outputs a sparse subgraph of edges (the circuit) and the scalar global threshold τ that induced it. The circuit is further stabilized by a narrow buffer band around τ and repaired for input-to-logits reachability if thresholding severs all paths.

## Method

The core mechanism replaces fixed top-k truncation with a retention-calibrated global threshold: after scoring all edges with sign-preserving integrated gradients (EAP-IG), the method binary-searches over sorted score magnitudes to find the smallest threshold τ such that the induced circuit C(τ) = {e : |I_e| ≥ τ} satisfies a target performance-retention criterion (e.g., ΔNLL_suff below a tolerance). A buffer ratio β then conservatively includes a fixed fraction of near-threshold edges in [τ(1−β), τ) to suppress brittleness at the boundary, and a graph-theoretic reachability check repairs any broken input-to-logits path. The key distinction from prior work is that circuit size is determined endogenously by the retained-behavior target and the observed score distribution rather than imposed in advance, so the same rule adapts automatically across tasks and model scales without task-specific budget tuning.

## Results

On the Mechanistic Interpretability Benchmark (MIB), the proposed retention-calibrated global thresholding achieves best or second-best CPR across most task–model pairs: CPR 3.72 on Gemma-2 IOI (vs. 3.20 for EAP-IG-inputs), 1.75 on Gemma-2 ARC-Easy (vs. 1.70 for EAP-IG-activations), and 1.40 on Llama-3.1 ARC-Challenge (vs. 0.98 for the next-best EAP-IG-inputs). On GPT-2 IOI, EActP(CF) edges out Ours on CPR (2.30 vs. 2.01), but Ours extends to all four models and attains CMD 0.03–0.04 across IOI settings. On InterpBench, Ours reaches AUROC 0.79, second only to EAP-IG-activations (0.81). On GPT-2 IOI probability-space diagnostics, Ours improves sufficiency (ΔNLL_suff 0.758 vs. 1.009 for EAP-IG-inputs) while slightly increasing NLL-based necessity (8.431 vs. 8.328).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against EAP-IG-inputs (CF), the strongest EAP-IG variant, Ours wins on Gemma-2 IOI (CPR 3.72 vs. 3.20), Gemma-2 ARC-Easy (1.75 vs. 1.53), Llama-3.1 ARC-Easy (1.25 vs. 1.04), and Llama-3.1 ARC-Challenge (1.40 vs. 0.98), but loses on Llama-3.1 IOI (1.96 vs. 2.08) and Qwen-2.5 IOI (1.92 vs. 1.63 is a win, but EAP-IG-activations ties at 1.63). Against EActP (CF), Ours trails on GPT-2 IOI (2.01 vs. 2.30) but EActP is only available for two models, whereas Ours extends to all four. On MCQA, Ours trades a small amount of CPR (e.g., 0.73 vs. 1.16 on Qwen-2.5) for substantially lower CMD (0.14 vs. 0.08 for EAP-IG-inputs, and 0.05 vs. 0.29 for NAP-IG on Gemma-2), indicating more faithful probability-space behavior. Against IFR and UGS, Ours dominates across nearly all available columns (e.g., IOI GPT-2 CPR 2.01 vs. 0.58 for IFR; IOI Qwen-2.5 CPR 1.92 vs. 0.98 for UGS). On CMD, Ours matches or beats EAP-IG variants on most IOI settings (0.03–0.04 vs. 0.01–0.04) and is dramatically lower than IFR (0.04 vs. 0.75 on Gemma-2 IOI), confirming that the retention-calibrated threshold produces circuits that are both selective and faithful.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the EAP-IG scoring framework from Hanna et al. [13] and the attribution-patching methodology of Syed et al. [29], using sign-preserving integrated gradients over a continuous edge gate to compute per-edge importance. Its conceptual departure lies in the score-to-circuit mapping: rather than the standard fixed top-k budget or other manually chosen sparsification rules used by most attribution-based pipelines, it selects a single global threshold calibrated by a performance-retention criterion, making circuit size an emergent property of retained behavior. This positions the work within the automated circuit-discovery literature but shifts the design question from 'how to score edges' to 'how to map scores into a circuit without an ex-ante budget.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The fixed top-k budget is a persistent practical pain point in circuit discovery, and replacing it with a retention-calibrated threshold offers a cleaner, more transferable operating principle that avoids task-specific hyperparameter tuning. However, the empirical gains are modest: the method is competitive (best or second-best on CPR/CMD) rather than dramatically superior, and MCQA results reveal a trade-off where it sacrifices some accuracy for lower CMD. Open questions include whether the retention target itself is merely a reparameterized budget, how the method behaves on tasks with less concentrated score distributions, and whether the approach extends to node-level or feature-level circuit discovery beyond edge-level thresholding.
