## Problem

Diffusion model sampling requires dozens to hundreds of expensive denoiser forward passes, creating a critical latency bottleneck for interactive applications. Existing feature caching accelerators (naive reuse, Taylor expansion) rely on local time-domain approximations whose errors compound rapidly with the skip horizon, causing severe quality degradation at high speedup ratios. No prior training-free method can simultaneously achieve large speedups and preserve sample fidelity because local predictors fail to capture the long-range temporal dynamics of latent features along the sampling trajectory.

## Input / Output

Spectrum operates as a training-free acceleration wrapper around any existing diffusion model (FLUX.1, SD3.5-Large, Wan2.1-14B, HunyuanVideo), taking the same text prompt and initial Gaussian noise latent as the base model and producing the same 1024×1024 images or video clips. The method reduces the number of actual denoiser evaluations from 50 to as few as 10–14 via an adaptive schedule, while the ODE solver step still runs at every timestep using the forecasted features as a surrogate for the full network output. It is agnostic to the choice of ODE solver and noise schedule.

## Method

Spectrum views each channel of the denoiser's latent feature vector as a scalar function of the diffusion timestep and approximates it with a truncated Chebyshev polynomial series of degree M (default M=4). At each actual network evaluation, the Chebyshev coefficients are refitted online via a closed-form ridge regression solution (Eq. 13) over all previously cached features, and at skipped timesteps the feature is forecast by evaluating the polynomial at the projected timestep τ=2t−1. The key theoretical advantage is that the approximation error is bounded by the polynomial degree M (exponential in M via the Bernstein ellipse) rather than by the skip step size, in contrast to Taylor expansion where worst-case error scales as (Δt)^(P+1). Two practical extensions further improve performance: an adaptive timestep scheduler that allocates more actual passes early in sampling and increases forecast usage later, and a last-block-only caching strategy that caches and forecasts only the final attention block output, reducing memory and compute overhead while matching or exceeding per-module caching quality.

## Results

On FLUX.1 (DrawBench, 50-step reference), Spectrum achieves a 4.79× speedup (α=3.0, 10 network evaluations) with PSNR 22.21, SSIM 0.788, LPIPS 0.261, and ImageReward 1.00, while at 3.47× speedup (α=0.75, 14 evaluations) it reaches PSNR 24.32 and SSIM 0.854. On Wan2.1-14B (VBench), Spectrum delivers a 4.67× speedup with PSNR 21.24 and VBench Quality 82.21, compared to 17.24 PSNR for TaylorSeer at a lower 3.94× speedup. The method is training-free and requires only negligible overhead (M=4 Chebyshev basis, λ=0.1 regularization) beyond the cached features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Spectrum consistently outperforms all four baselines on both speedup and quality. Against TaylorSeer (the closest competitor), Spectrum at α=3.0 achieves 4.79× speedup vs. 4.14× for TaylorSeer (N=6, O=1) on FLUX.1, while also improving PSNR from 20.24 to 22.21 and LPIPS from 0.294 to 0.261; on Wan2.1-14B the gap widens further (PSNR 21.24 vs. 17.24, LPIPS 0.265 vs. 0.367). Against FORA (naive cache reuse), Spectrum at α=0.75 achieves 3.47× speedup vs. 3.19× with substantially better PSNR (24.32 vs. 15.15) and SSIM (0.854 vs. 0.651) on FLUX.1. Against TeaCache (dynamic caching), Spectrum at α=3.0 reaches 4.79× vs. 3.85× speedup with higher PSNR (22.21 vs. 16.71) and lower LPIPS (0.261 vs. 0.406). Against ToCa (token-wise caching), Spectrum dominates on both speedup (4.79× vs. 1.95×) and quality (PSNR 22.21 vs. 16.49) at the high-speedup setting. The advantage is most pronounced at higher speedups, where TaylorSeer's local approximation error compounds (e.g., ImageReward drops from 0.82 to 0.32 on SD3.5) while Spectrum's spectral error bound remains stable.

## Relation to Prior Work

Spectrum sits within the training-free feature caching paradigm for diffusion acceleration, directly building on TaylorSeer's cache-then-forecast framework but replacing its local discrete Taylor expansion with a global spectral approximation via Chebyshev polynomials. The key conceptual departure is shifting from time-domain local predictors (naive copying, finite-difference Taylor) to function-space orthonormal basis fitting, motivated by the theoretical observation that Chebyshev truncation error is bounded by polynomial degree rather than forecasting horizon. The paper also draws on the broader landscape of diffusion acceleration (distillation, ODE solvers, quantization) to position itself as orthogonal and composable with those approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free nature and negligible overhead make Spectrum immediately deployable on existing diffusion models without retraining, which is critical for practitioners seeking real-time generation. The theoretical guarantee that error does not compound with skip size addresses a fundamental limitation of prior local forecasters and provides a principled justification for large-step acceleration. Open questions include whether the spectral approach generalizes to non-stationary or highly oscillatory feature trajectories, whether the adaptive scheduling can be made fully automatic rather than heuristic, and how well Spectrum composes with other acceleration techniques (quantization, token pruning) in practice.
