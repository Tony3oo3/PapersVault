## Problem

Existing Single Image Reflection Separation methods suffer from progressive transmission-reflection confusion, particularly in deep decoder layers, where implicit fusion mechanisms and inadequate multi-scale coordination cause layer-specific features to become inseparable. This is exacerbated under nonlinear mixing (e.g., specular highlights, over-exposure), where simple additive or weighted linear models fail to capture inter-layer interactions, and increasing network depth further degrades feature distinguishability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261404/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single mixed RGB image (resized to 384×384) and produces three outputs: a transmission layer, a reflection layer, and a residual layer that captures nonlinear inter-layer interactions beyond simple additive mixing. Training data is synthesized using a 'screen' blending mode with randomly sampled attenuation coefficients (γ₁∈[0.8,1.0], γ₂∈[0.4,1.0]) to model real-world nonlinear superposition, combined with real paired data from PASCAL VOC, Real20, and Nature datasets.

## Method

ReflexSplit is a dual-stream decoder framework built on three components. First, Cross-scale Gated Fusion (CrGF) adaptively aggregates global semantic priors (Swin Transformer), local texture details (MuGI-based CNN), and decoder context via bidirectional channel-splitting gates at each decoder level, stabilizing gradient flow across hierarchical depths. Second, Layer Fusion-Separation Blocks (LFSB) alternate between early fusion (bidirectional cross-stream projection to extract shared degradation structure) and differential dual-dimensional attention, where self-attention and cross-attention maps from transmission and reflection streams are combined via cross-stream subtraction (A^t − σ(λ_ℓ)A^r), extending the Differential Transformer's noise-cancellation principle to explicit layer disentanglement. Third, curriculum training progressively strengthens the differential separation coefficient λ through depth-dependent initialization (deeper layers receive stronger separation) and epoch-wise warmup (linearly increasing from 0.1 to 1.0 over 30 epochs), allowing the network to learn holistic reconstruction before enforcing layer-specific disentanglement.

## Results

On the five synthetic benchmarks (Real20, Objects, Postcard, Wild, Nature; 540 images total), ReflexSplit achieves the best average PSNR of 26.40 dB and best SSIM on every individual dataset, edging out RDNet (26.38 dB) and DSIT (25.93 dB). On the real-world OpenRR-1K dataset, ReflexSplit attains the highest SSIM (0.9372), lowest LPIPS (0.1087), lowest NIQE (2.9764), and lowest DISTS (0.0684), while DSIT marginally leads on PSNR (26.67 vs. 26.58). The model uses 174M trainable parameters—34% fewer than RDNet (266.4M)—while outperforming it on both PSNR and SSIM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReflexSplit outperforms all 11 baselines on average PSNR across the five synthetic benchmarks, with the closest competitor being RDNet (26.38 vs. 26.40 dB) despite RDNet using 52M more parameters. On the real-world OpenRR-1K dataset, ReflexSplit wins on four of five metrics (SSIM 0.9372, LPIPS 0.1087, NIQE 2.9764, DISTS 0.0684) but trails DSIT by 0.08 dB on PSNR (26.58 vs. 26.67), suggesting DSIT preserves slightly more pixel-level fidelity while ReflexSplit better preserves perceptual structure. Among the dual-stream Transformer baselines, DSIT (NeurIPS 2024) is the strongest PSNR competitor on synthetic data (25.93 dB) but suffers from the transmission-reflection confusion that ReflexSplit's differential attention explicitly targets. DSRNet (ICCV 2023) and Zhong et al. (CVPR 2024) report higher average SSIM (0.908 and 0.914) but lag by 0.68–0.72 dB in PSNR, indicating they trade reconstruction accuracy for structural similarity. The lightweight DExNet (9.6M params) achieves 25.04 dB average PSNR, confirming that ReflexSplit's 174M parameter budget yields a meaningful 1.36 dB gain over the most parameter-efficient baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

ReflexSplit builds directly on the dual-stream SIRS lineage initiated by YTMT and refined by DSRNet (MuGI gating) and DSIT (dual-dimensional attention), while drawing a key conceptual bridge from Differential Transformer's attention-cancellation mechanism for noise suppression in single-stream architectures. Its primary departure is repurposing that cancellation principle as cross-stream subtraction (A^t − λA^r) to enforce layer-specific disentanglement, and extending MuGI's single-scale gating into a cross-scale bidirectional fusion (CrGF) that aggregates semantic priors, texture details, and decoder context across hierarchical depths. The curriculum training strategy with depth-dependent λ initialization further distinguishes it from prior methods that apply uniform separation strength throughout the decoder.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and targets a concrete failure mode—progressive transmission-reflection confusion in deep decoder layers—that plagues existing dual-stream SIRS methods, and the differential cross-stream attention mechanism offers a principled, interpretable remedy. However, quantitative gains over strong baselines (DSIT, RDNet) are modest (≈0.1–0.5 dB PSNR, ≈0.01–0.02 SSIM), and the 174M parameter count limits practical deployment. Open questions include whether the differential separation principle generalizes to other layer-disentanglement tasks (e.g., foreground-background, haze removal), whether the curriculum schedule is essential or merely a training stabilizer, and whether the approach scales to higher-resolution or video reflection separation without the current 384×384 constraint.
