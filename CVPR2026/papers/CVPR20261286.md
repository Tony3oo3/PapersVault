## Problem

Existing self-supervised methods for human movement representation learn fixed-length encodings at frame, clip, or video granularity, which are either too fine-grained and redundant, misaligned with semantic boundaries, or gloss over reusable temporal patterns. Capturing the compositional structure of body movements—atomic motions and their recurring temporal compositions as semantically meaningful, reusable units—requires resolving two entanglements: segmentation and semantics are mutually dependent, and representations and their temporal compositions must be learned jointly. No prior method learns variable-length, semantically grounded movement segments in a fully self-supervised manner.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 3D SMPL pose sequences (downsampled to 5 fps, 30-second windows) and first segments them into variable-length Action Atom segments using kinematic trajectory discontinuities, then groups these into longer Action Motif segments via frequent pattern mining. Each variable-length segment is consolidated into a single latent token via cross-attention, so the number of tokens per sequence is data-dependent rather than fixed. Downstream, these latent tokens support action recognition (zero-shot k-NN or 1-layer Transformer head), autoregressive motion prediction (next latent token + segment length, decoded to 3D poses), and motion interpolation (infilling masked latent positions).

## Method

A4Mer is a two-stage nested latent Transformer that learns hierarchical movement representations bottom-up. The first stage segments poses by nonlinear joint-trajectory changes (Action Atoms) and the second stage discovers recurring Action Atom patterns via the GSP algorithm to define Action Motif segments. Both stages share an Encoder (Transformer decoder with segment-wise self-attention and cross-attention consolidation of variable-length segments into single tokens) and a LatentFormer (self-attention over latent tokens for inter-segment reasoning). Training uses a JEPA-style pretext task: randomly masked segments are predicted in latent space with a stop-gradient target and EMA-updated target network. A key loss decomposition splits each prediction error into a local component (deviation from sequence mean) and a global component (sequence mean), with dynamic weighting (λ_k = sg(L_k^l / L^g), α=0.05) that prioritizes the local term to encourage context-independent, reusable segment representations.

## Results

On the AMD dataset, A4Mer achieves 31.7% top-1 / 59.0% top-3 k-NN action recognition accuracy and 38.1% with a trained head, outperforming all six baselines. For long-term motion prediction (3-second horizon), A4Mer reaches 150 mm MPJPE on AMD and 120 mm on HiK (zero-shot), the best in both settings. For motion interpolation (4–8 s gaps), A4Mer achieves 126 mm MPJPE on AMD and 110 mm on HiK, again the lowest error among all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

A4Mer outperforms all six baselines on every reported metric. Against the closest competitor USDRL (contrastive, frame-level), A4Mer gains +6.0 pp on k-NN top-3 (59.0 vs 43.0), +8.0 pp on the trained head (38.1 vs 30.1), and reduces prediction MPJPE by 21 mm on AMD (150 vs 171) and 35 mm on HiK (120 vs 155). MacDiff (denoising, clip-level) is the only method that ties A4Mer on HiK interpolation (both 110 mm) but trails by 16.9 pp on k-NN top-3 and 7.8 pp on the head. BehaveMAE (MAE, clip-level) is competitive on the head (35.6 vs 38.1) but degrades severely on HiK prediction (288 mm) and interpolation (362 mm), suggesting its fixed-length clip representations fail to generalize across datasets. MotionBERT (2D→3D lifting, frame-level) is the weakest baseline, with near-zero k-NN accuracy (1.77% top-1) and the highest prediction error on AMD (237 mm), highlighting the limitations of frame-wise representations for capturing compositional motion semantics.

## Relation to Prior Work

A4Mer builds directly on the JEPA framework (Assran et al., CVPR 2023) for its training paradigm—masked latent token prediction with stop-gradient and EMA targets—but departs from prior self-supervised human motion learning in a fundamental way: instead of learning fixed-length representations at frame, clip, or video granularity (as in MotionBERT, USDRL, BehaveMAE, MacDiff, H2OT), it discovers variable-length semantically meaningful segments through a bottom-up hierarchical process. The two-stage architecture (Action Atoms → Action Motifs) with GSP-based pattern mining and cross-attention consolidation is novel, and the local/global loss decomposition is a new mechanism to enforce context-independence. The AMD dataset with foot-mounted cameras and ChArUco markers for occlusion-robust SMPL annotation also carves new ground in 3D pose dataset construction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The hierarchical Action Motif representation addresses a core gap in human behavior modeling: prior methods treat motion as a flat sequence of fixed-length units, missing the compositional structure that makes human movement interpretable and predictable. The AMD dataset (14.2 hours, 50 subjects, 24 cameras, real home) with its clever foot-camera annotation technique provides a practical resource for training and evaluating such representations in naturalistic settings. Open questions include whether the GSP-based segmentation generalizes to substantially larger or more diverse datasets, whether the approach extends to multi-person or object-interaction scenarios, and whether the 512-cluster discretization is optimal. The zero-shot transfer results suggest the learned representations are genuinely semantic, but validation on more diverse action vocabularies would strengthen the claim.
