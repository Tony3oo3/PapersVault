## Problem

Existing RGB-A video generation methods suffer from entangled RGB and alpha distributions in the latent space, producing inaccurate transparency, visual artifacts, and degraded RGB quality. Prior approaches either adapt 2D image VAEs (LayerDiffuse) that lack temporal modeling, or duplicate backbones with cross-attention (TransPixeler) that still fail to effectively disentangle the two channels, especially for semi-transparent objects like hair, veils, and smoke.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260513/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a text prompt (via umT5) and optionally a Gaussian ellipse mask specifying the approximate shape and position of the transparent region, producing an RGB-A video at 480×832 resolution with 81 frames at 16 FPS in just 4 sampling steps. For the image-to-video variant, a transparent input image plus text prompt drives the animation. The Gaussian ellipse mask is derived from the alpha channel's point cloud via PCA-based ellipse fitting, and a scalar factor μ (default 0.05) controls the strength of the mean shift, giving users a simple knob for transparency placement without requiring per-pixel masks.

## Method

The core insight is to shift the alpha distribution outward while preserving the RGB distribution, operating in both the latent space (end of diffusion) and the noise space (start of diffusion). In the latent space, a transparency-aware bidirectional diffusion loss (TABD) trains the VAE using a frozen DiT: a binary mask reverses the rectified-flow loss sign in semi-transparent and transparent regions, implicitly increasing RGB likelihood while decreasing alpha likelihood so the DiT can better distinguish the two. In the noise space, a transparency-guided mean sampler constructs a Gaussian ellipse mask from the alpha channel and shifts the noise mean (ε̃ ~ N(G·μ, I)) in transparent regions, providing both training guidance and inference-time controllability. The VAE uses a frozen encoder with a feature-merge block and separate RGB/alpha decoders (LoRA rank 128), while the DiT is fine-tuned with DoRA (rank 32) on the Wan2.1-T2V-14B base, requiring no architectural modification to the backbone.

## Results

On the main quantitative benchmark (Table 1), the proposed method achieves the highest scores across all five metrics: Text Alignment 4.00, Aesthetic Quality 0.649, Naturalness 3.19, Motion Smoothness 0.9949, and Temporal Flickering 0.9941. In the user study (Table 2, lower rank = better), it ranks first in both transparency correctness (1.23) and overall quality (1.11). Inference speed is also a major advantage: the model generates 81 frames at 480×832, 16 FPS in 128 seconds using only 4 sampling steps, compared to TransPixeler's 32 minutes for 49 frames at 480×720, 8 FPS (~15× faster).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms all four baselines on every quantitative metric in Table 1. Against the strongest baseline TransPixeler (Close), it gains +0.55 in Text Alignment (4.00 vs 3.45), +0.076 in Aesthetic Quality (0.649 vs 0.573), and +0.12 in Naturalness (3.19 vs 3.07), while also leading in Motion Smoothness (0.9949 vs 0.9907) and Temporal Flickering (0.9941 vs 0.9822). LayerDiffuse+AnimateDiff, which simply grafts an image-level RGB-A VAE onto a video model, shows the weakest motion and text alignment (2.67/3.15), confirming that image-based transparency adaptation is insufficient for video. LayerFlow produces distorted objects and scores lowest on Text Alignment (2.67) and Naturalness (2.35). In the user study (Table 2), the proposed method is ranked first in transparency correctness (1.23 vs 2.51 for TransPixeler-Open) and overall quality (1.11 vs 2.71 for TransPixeler-Open). Additionally, a qualitative comparison against the Wan+MatAnyone pipeline (Fig. 6) shows that the two-stage generate-then-mat approach fails to remove all background and cannot handle semi-transparent effects like lighting, whereas the proposed end-to-end model produces accurate transparency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RGB-A video generation line initiated by LayerDiffuse (image-level alpha embedding) and TransPixeler (duplicated backbone with cross-attention), but departs from both by reframing the core challenge as a distributional separation problem. Rather than entangling RGB and alpha in a shared latent space or duplicating the entire network, the authors introduce a 'shiftable RGB-A distribution' principle: the alpha distribution is shifted outward in both the latent space (via a transparency-aware bidirectional diffusion loss that inverts the Rectified Flow objective in transparent regions) and the noise space (via a Gaussian ellipse mask that shifts the initial noise mean). The base architecture is Wan2.1-T2V-14B, and the training framework builds on Rectified Flow/Flow Matching, with DoRA for the DiT and LoRA for the VAE decoders.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RGB-A video generation is directly relevant to creative industries (game design, VFX, UI) where transparent assets are essential, yet prior methods produced unstable or incorrect transparency. The key practical contribution is that the approach preserves the base model's RGB generation quality while adding controllable transparency, and the Gaussian ellipse mask gives users an intuitive spatial control mechanism. Limitations include a very small generation dataset (301 videos), reliance on a specific base model (Wan2.1), and evaluation limited to a handful of qualitative scenes; open questions include scalability to multi-layer compositing, longer video durations, and more diverse semi-transparent phenomena (e.g., volumetric fog, caustics).
