## Problem

Prior IMU-vision contrastive methods compress each clip into a single global embedding optimized for coarse action-category discrimination, causing segments that differ only by phase shifts or repetition boundaries to collapse to nearby codes and making the representations insensitive to true sub-second temporal synchrony. Additionally, multi-sensor IMU configurations (each attached to a different body part) are typically naively concatenated, failing to capture their spatial and temporal specificity, and the highly repetitive nature of human motion produces abundant near-duplicate synchronization cues that create ambiguous alignments—challenges not addressed by audio-video synchronization techniques.

## Input / Output

The method consumes N body-mounted IMU streams (each F frames × C channels) and a video from which 2D skeletal joint coordinates are extracted via RTMPose/MMPose; both are processed in fixed 5-second windows yielding T=25 temporal tokens with 256-dimensional embeddings. The video is never used as raw pixels—only the estimated pose sequence enters the model, and the full-body skeleton is decomposed into N part-specific joint subsets matched to their corresponding IMU sensors. Outputs include cosine-similarity rankings for bidirectional retrieval, a temporal offset estimate (via weighted histogram of per-window offset votes), subject and body-part assignments, and action class labels under 1-NN or fine-tuned classification.

## Method

MoBind employs a hierarchical contrastive framework with three alignment levels: token-level InfoNCE matching individual temporal tokens across modalities for sub-second synchrony, local-level contrastive alignment pairing each IMU sensor's mean-pooled representation with its corresponding body-part pose representation, and global-level alignment of the aggregated multi-sensor IMU embedding with the full-body pose embedding. Both modalities use the same encoder architecture—1D convolutional blocks preserving temporal resolution, followed by non-overlapping temporal patching and a Transformer layer producing T tokens—while the pose stream is split into N body-part segments before encoding. A Masked Token Prediction auxiliary task (75% masking ratio, MSE loss) is applied to the IMU token stream to regularize the model and retain action-level semantics that pure fine-grained alignment would under-represent. The total loss combines bidirectional InfoNCE at all three levels (weighted λg=1.0, λl=1.0, λt=0.5) with the MTP term (λmtp=0.3), trained end-to-end with Adam at lr 1e-4.

## Results

On cross-modal retrieval (Table 1), MoBind achieves R@1 of 0.94 (IMU→Video) and 0.92 (Video→IMU) on mRi, 0.87/0.68 on TotalCapture, and 0.83/0.83 on EgoHumans, outperforming the strongest baseline SyncNet (0.77/0.75 on mRi) by 17+ points. For temporal synchronization (Table 2), MoBind reduces MAE to 0.47 s on mRi, 0.05 s on TotalCapture, and 0.04 s on EgoHumans, with 200 ms-tolerance accuracy of 0.88, 0.98, and 1.00 respectively—far exceeding SyncWISE (MAE 3.31–4.07 s) and IMU2CLIP (MAE 1.17–3.13 s). On IMU-to-person identification in multi-person EgoHumans scenes (Table 3), MoBind reaches 98.12% accuracy and 98.01% F1, surpassing VIPL (90.14% / 89.33%). For action recognition (Table 4), MoBind attains 0.98 (finetune) and 0.87 (1-NN) on mRi, and 0.72/0.71 on TotalCapture, leading all IMU-encoder baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoBind outperforms all nine baselines across every task and dataset. Against the contrastive retrieval baselines, MoBind leads SyncNet [5] by 17 points R@1 on mRi (0.94 vs 0.77) and by 36 points on TotalCapture (0.87 vs 0.51); IMU2CLIP [27] and DeSPITE [23] are substantially weaker, with TotalCapture R@1 of only 0.06 and 0.03 respectively, indicating that global-only embeddings collapse under highly dynamic whole-body motion. For temporal synchronization, MoBind (MAE 0.04–0.47 s) dominates SyncWISE [46] (MAE 3.31–4.07 s) by roughly an order of magnitude, and also beats the offset-classification baseline IMUSync (MAE 0.72–1.01 s) on all three datasets. On multi-person IMU-to-person identification, MoBind (98.12% Acc) surpasses VIPL [34] (90.14%) by 8 points, confirming that local body-part contrastive learning adds spatial grounding beyond global alignment. For action recognition, MoBind (0.98 finetune / 0.87 1-NN on mRi; 0.72/0.71 on TotalCapture) edges out SyncNet (0.97/0.85; 0.68/0.63) and clearly beats dedicated IMU encoders UniMTS [45] (0.95/0.36; 0.66/0.53), ImageBind [12] (0.95/0.40; 0.72/0.47), EVI-MAE [44] (0.54/0.43; 0.63/0.66), and Primus [9] (0.82/0.80; 0.57/0.67), with the largest gap on TotalCapture where the MTP auxiliary task contributes nearly 20% improvement over MoBind without MTP.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoBind synthesizes ideas from three distinct research threads without directly building on any single prior method: (1) global contrastive IMU-video alignment (IMU2CLIP, DeSPITE, ImageBind), which the paper departs from by replacing single-vector embeddings with hierarchical token-local-global representations; (2) audio-visual synchronization (SyncNet, DiVAS, SyncFormer), whose sub-second alignment strategies are repurposed for the IMU-pose setting with the key adaptation of body-part decomposition to handle multi-sensor configurations; and (3) IMU-to-subject association (VIPL, VIT, Vi-Fi), which the paper subsumes by adding body-part-level localization on top of person-level matching. The authors explicitly note that no prior work targets fine-grained, sub-second IMU-video alignment with structured multi-sensor modeling, positioning MoBind as a new formulation that unifies these threads under a single hierarchical contrastive objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: calibration-free temporal synchronization of IMU and video streams removes a major barrier to collecting and using multimodal motion data outside controlled lab settings, and the single learned representation simultaneously supports retrieval, synchronization, and spatial localization without task-specific heads. Key limitations include reliance on estimated 2D keypoints (RTMPose) rather than ground-truth 3D pose, which may introduce systematic errors in occluded or fast-motion scenarios, and the requirement of known IMU mounting positions for body-part decomposition. Open questions include whether the hierarchical design transfers to 3D pose or other wearable sensor types (e.g., EMG, force plates), how performance scales to longer sequences beyond the 5-second training window, and whether the approach generalizes to actions substantially different from those in the three evaluation datasets.
