## Problem

Imitation learning policies in driving simulators plateau well below expert performance because privileged experts rely on information unavailable to sensor-based students: they react to occluded actors (visibility asymmetry), use noise-free kinematic states to plan with minimal safety margins (uncertainty asymmetry), and the student's navigation intent is under-specified as a single target point (intent asymmetry). These misalignments produce demonstrations that are non-causal, dangerously aggressive, or encourage shortcut learning toward the goal point, systematically degrading closed-loop driving performance.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes multi-view camera images (140° FOV), a LiDAR point cloud, and object-level radar features from four radar units (75 detections each, pre-processed by a lightweight learned module). Navigation intent is provided as a three-point GNSS route (previous, current, future target) normalized to [-1,1], alongside ego vehicle status. The radar features bypass the sensor-fusion encoder and are injected directly into the planning decoder as additional context tokens. The model outputs a steering command and a target speed, produced by separate learned queries attending over a unified BEV token space.

## Method

The paper introduces LEAD, a student-centric expert derived from PDM-Lite that constrains planning inputs to the student's observable sensor frustum (excluding occluded actors, capping speed to locally inferable limits) and injects conservative safety margins (braking near observable hazards, reduced speed in low visibility, enlarged collision boxes for unprotected turns), while maintaining equivalent expert performance. On the student side, TFv6 removes the GRU bottleneck that previously conditioned only steering on the target point, instead representing the target as an explicit token alongside BEV tokens in a unified transformer decoder with learnable planning queries. Navigation conditioning is densified from a single target to a three-point route (previous, current, future) with a reduced switching threshold, so the policy receives earlier and richer path information. Trained on 73 hours of LEAD-generated data across diverse towns, weather, and sensor configurations, TFv6 achieves 95 DS on Bench2Drive and more than doubles prior performance on Longest6 v2 and Town13, with consistent sim-to-real gains on NAVSIM and Waymo via perception co-training.

## Results

TFv6 (RegNetY-032, 140° FOV camera + LiDAR + Radar) achieves a new state-of-the-art 95.2 ± 0.3 Driving Score on Bench2Drive and 62 ± 1 DS on Longest6 v2, more than doubling the prior best (23 ± 4 DS for TFv5). On the challenging Town13 validation benchmark (unseen town, ~12.4 km routes), TFv6 reaches 4.04 NDS, nearly doubling TFv5's 2.12 NDS. On real-world benchmarks, the LEAD pre-training strategy adds consistent gains: +1.0 PDMS on NAVSIM v1, +3.1 EPDMS on NAVSIM v2, and +0.25 RFS on WOD-E2E over LTFv6 without synthetic pre-training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TFv6 decisively outperforms all prior closed-loop baselines. On Bench2Drive, it surpasses HiP-AD (86.8 DS) by +8.4 DS and SimLingo (85.1 DS) by +10.1 DS, while also improving Success Rate from 69.1% to 86.8%. On Longest6 v2, the gains are even more dramatic: TFv6 reaches 62 DS versus 7 for HiP-AD and 22–23 for SimLingo/TFv5, a more-than-doubling improvement that highlights the difficulty of long-horizon evaluation. On Town13 validation (unseen town), TFv6 achieves 4.04 NDS versus 2.12 for TFv5, though both remain far below the PDM-Lite expert (58.50 NDS), underscoring the remaining learner-expert gap. The LEAD expert itself matches PDM-Lite's driving performance (96.8 vs. 97.0 DS on B2D), confirming that reducing learner-expert asymmetry does not sacrifice expert competence. On real-world benchmarks, LTFv6 + LEAD pre-training consistently beats LTF (83.8 → 86.4 PDMS on NAVSIM v1; 23.1 → 31.4 EPDMS on NAVSIM v2), demonstrating transferable benefits of synthetic data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the TransFuser/TransFuser++ (TFv5) architecture and the PDM-Lite rule-based expert that underpin the Learning by Cheating paradigm in CARLA. It departs from the prevailing assumption that, once an expert reaches strong benchmark performance, further gains must come from student model improvements; instead, it argues the expert itself is the bottleneck and systematically redesigns it to reduce visibility, uncertainty, and intent asymmetries. On the student side, it extends the target point bias analysis of Jaeger et al. [25] by showing the bias persists even with strong scene representations and is better explained by under-specified intent conditioning and late GRU-based injection, rather than weak perception.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that constraining a privileged expert to sensor-observable information yields an 11-point DS gain on Longest6 v2 without any architectural change reframes expert design as a first-class lever in simulation-based imitation learning, a perspective the field largely abandoned after early expert-quality gains. The released LEAD dataset and training pipeline, combined with SOTA results across five benchmarks (including more than doubling prior performance on Longest6 v2 and Town13), provide a practical foundation for the CARLA community. Open questions remain: the residual gap to the expert (73 vs. 54 DS on Longest6 v2) likely requires closed-loop training (DAgger, RL); sim-to-real transfer is demonstrated only in open-loop form; and whether the alignment principles generalize beyond rule-based experts to learned or human demonstrations is untested.
