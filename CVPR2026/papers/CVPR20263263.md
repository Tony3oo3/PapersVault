## Problem

Existing 4D Gaussian Splatting methods for dynamic novel view synthesis are limited to short video sequences (<10 s), require large storage (>500 MB), and consume substantial GPU VRAM, making them impractical for long-duration scenes and bandwidth-constrained deployment. While Temporal Gaussian Hierarchy (TGH) extends 4D Gaussians to longer videos, it still fails to meet low-bandwidth requirements (<1 MB/s) critical for mobile and streaming applications, and its fixed-segment structure hinders effective compression.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263263/cropped_page0_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-view video sequences with known camera poses as input; evaluation datasets range from 10-second clips at 1352×1014 (N3DV, 19–21 cameras) to 1–10-minute sequences at 4K/60 FPS (SelfCap, 18–22 cameras). It produces a compressed layered 4D Gaussian representation that supports real-time novel view rendering at arbitrary timestamps, with compressed variants achieving up to 22.3× storage reduction (e.g., 8.8 MB for a 10-second scene) while sustaining 660+ FPS on an RTX 3090.

## Method

L4DRotorGS organizes 4D-Rotor Gaussians into a layered hierarchy (L = ⌈log₂n⌉+1 layers) based on temporal extent τ, with each layer further split into left/right temporal buckets that allow Gaussians to span boundaries, enabling selective loading of only relevant buckets at render time. Training uses a triple-buffer strategy (GPU double buffer + CPU bucket buffer) to minimize CPU-GPU transfer overhead, combined with a Dynamic-Aware Rotor Learning Rate that assigns smaller temporal-rotor learning rates to long-τ Gaussians to prevent position drift in static regions. Compression applies three techniques: Factorized Covariance Quantization decomposes the 4D covariance into a scale factor (scalar quantized), normalized scale (VQ), and separate spatial/temporal rotor components (each VQ) to handle the >10-order-of-magnitude dynamic range; Layered Compression quantizes distribution-sensitive components per-layer while using global VQ/SQ for SH and opacity; and Residual Codebook Quantization adds block-specific codebooks via lightweight residual vectors to refine quantization without expanding the global codebook.

## Results

On N3DV, the uncompressed variant (Ours) achieves the best PSNR of 32.23 dB, outperforming the closest baselines Ex4DGS (32.11) and STG (32.05), while the compressed Ours Large variant reaches 662 FPS with only 13.8 MB storage (13.1× compression, 0.17 dB PSNR drop). Ours Small pushes compression to 20.5× (8.8 MB, <1 MB/s bitrate) at 661 FPS with 31.84 dB PSNR. On the longer SelfCap sequences (1–10 min), Ours Large achieves 1190 FPS at 48.4 MB storage with 24.49 dB PSNR, a 40% FPS gain over the uncompressed model (854 FPS, 928.8 MB).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On N3DV, Ours achieves the highest PSNR (32.23 dB), narrowly beating Ex4DGS (32.11) and STG (32.05), while also training fastest (0.5 h vs. 0.6 h for Ex4DGS and 1.3 h for STG). The compressed variants dominate on the speed–storage trade-off: Ours Large (662 FPS, 13.8 MB) surpasses TGH (550 FPS, 90 MB) in both rendering speed and storage by a wide margin, and Ours Small (8.8 MB) is the smallest model in the table, beating MEGA (25.05 MB) by 2.8×. Against NeRF-based methods (DyNeRF, K-Planes, MixVoxels), Ours Large is 40–8000× faster in rendering (662 vs. 0.015–16.7 FPS) while matching or exceeding their PSNR. On SelfCap (1–10 min sequences), no external baselines are available for long-video comparison; the paper reports only internal comparisons, where Ours Large achieves a 19.1× storage reduction (928.8→48.4 MB) with a mere 0.15 dB PSNR drop and a 40% FPS increase (854→1190). The main trade-off is that Ours Small shows minor artifacts in highly dynamic regions on SelfCap due to the tighter storage budget, and its LPIPS (0.156) is slightly higher than MEGA's (0.057, though measured under a different protocol).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on 4D-Rotor Gaussian Splatting [4] for the 4D Gaussian formulation and Temporal Gaussian Hierarchy (TGH) [34] for organizing Gaussians into temporal layers, but departs from TGH by introducing a layer-bucket structure that allows Gaussians to span bucket boundaries, yielding more temporally consistent groups for compression. The compression pipeline adapts C3DGS [23]'s vector quantization strategy to the 4D setting, but replaces direct covariance VQ with Factorized Covariance Quantization (FCQ) after observing that the 10+ order-of-magnitude dynamic range of 4D covariances makes naive quantization infeasible. The overall contribution is a unified C++/CUDA system integrating layered representation, an efficient triple-buffer training framework, and three complementary compression techniques (FCQ, Layered Compression, RCQ) into a single pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a practical bottleneck for dynamic scene deployment: existing 4D Gaussian methods scale poorly to minute-long sequences due to storage and VRAM costs, limiting use on bandwidth-constrained or mobile platforms. Achieving 20×+ compression with <0.2 dB PSNR loss and 660+ FPS rendering makes long dynamic scenes viable for real-world applications like AR/VR and interactive entertainment. Key limitations include the still-time-consuming offline compression stage (~4 min) and the lack of online/streaming training support, which restricts adaptability in live capture scenarios. Open questions include whether the layered compression strategy generalizes to even longer sequences (hours) and whether the factorization approach can be extended to other 4D representations beyond rotors.
