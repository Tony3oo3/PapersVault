## Problem

Complex text-based image editing—where a target prompt specifies multiple independent editing targets—remains poorly handled by existing flow-matching methods. Single-round approaches struggle with long-text semantic following (losing or entangling editing effects), while multi-round approaches accumulate source inconsistency across iterations. No existing method achieves a strong simultaneous balance between semantic alignment with all targets and preservation of non-edited source structure in a single pass.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261124/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a source RGB image, its corresponding source text prompt, and a complex target prompt containing n editing targets (e.g., four in Complex-PIE-Bench). An LLM decomposes the complex prompt into n intermediate prompts, each cumulatively incorporating the first i targets. The output is a single edited image produced in one round of ODE integration (28 timesteps on FLUX.1 dev), with no attention manipulation or iterative re-editing required.

## Method

FlowDC builds on the inversion-free editing velocity formulation of FlowEdit and introduces two training-free mechanisms. First, Progressive Semantic Orthogonalization (PSO) constructs n parallel editing trajectories guided by the intermediate prompts, then applies a Gram-Schmidt-style orthogonalization (PVO) to the resulting velocity or displacement vectors, yielding an orthogonal basis where each vector isolates the semantic contribution of one editing target. Second, Velocity Orthogonal Decay (VOD) projects the original editing velocity onto the time-varying subspace spanned by this basis, retains the in-subspace component (λ_sub = 1), and applies a piecewise-linear decay to the orthogonal component (λ_orth from 0.1 to 0.64), suppressing unstable editing-irrelevant structural changes. The two-stage subspace construction uses reference velocities at an early guidance timestep to avoid collapse when displacements are zero, then switches to displacement-based orthogonalization, and finally to a single displacement vector in the late stage to reduce computational cost.

## Results

On Complex-PIE-Bench (700 samples, 4 editing targets each), FlowDC achieves the best source consistency across all three metrics: CLIP-I 87.72%, DINO 71.69%, and LPIPS 23.72%, while also securing second-best semantic alignment (CLIP-T 27.69%, behind RF-Edit's 28.79%). On PIE-Bench++ (700 samples, mixed 1–3+ targets), FlowDC again leads on all source-consistency metrics (CLIP-I 88.76%, DINO 72.33%, LPIPS 22.09%) and matches FlowEdit on CLIP-T (25.13% vs. 25.12%). The ablation confirms that removing Velocity Orthogonal Decay (VOD) collapses source consistency (DINO drops from 71.69% to 47.58%, LPIPS from 23.72% to 49.89%), while removing Progressive Semantic Orthogonalization (PSO) reduces CLIP-T from 27.69% to 27.30%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FlowDC outperforms the strongest single-round inversion-free baseline, FlowEdit [20], on every metric on both benchmarks (e.g., on Complex-PIE-Bench: DINO 71.69% vs. 70.84%, LPIPS 23.72% vs. 23.86%, CLIP-T 27.69% vs. 26.91%). Against the inversion-based baselines (SDEdit, RF-Inv, ODE-Inv), FlowDC leads on all four metrics, with the largest gaps on DINO (e.g., 71.69% vs. 70.55% for SDEdit) and LPIPS (23.72% vs. 44.10% for SDEdit). The multi-round baseline MultiTurn [46] is clearly inferior, scoring 25.03% CLIP-T and 61.51% DINO on Complex-PIE-Bench, confirming the cumulative-inconsistency problem the paper identifies. RF-Edit [37] achieves the highest CLIP-T (28.79%) but at the cost of severe over-editing, with DINO collapsing to 47.62% and LPIPS rising to 50.80%, making it the worst source-consistency performer. ParallelEdit [14] is compared qualitatively in Figure 4 and partially quantitatively in the appendix; the paper notes it requires complex prompt engineering and its official implementation only handles a subset of PIE-Bench++, so it is excluded from the main quantitative table.

## Relation to Prior Work

FlowDC builds directly on the inversion-free flow editing paradigm established by FlowEdit and FlowAlign, which construct direct editing trajectories between source and target images without DDIM inversion. It departs from the two dominant strategies for complex (multi-target) editing—single-round attention manipulation (Prompt-to-Prompt, ParallelEdit) and iterative multi-round editing (MultiTurn, EMILIE)—by instead operating in velocity space: decomposing the editing velocity onto an orthogonal basis derived from progressively decomposed prompts, then selectively decaying the orthogonal component. The use of an LLM for prompt decomposition and the progressive orthogonalization of parallel editing trajectories are the key conceptual departures from prior attention-based or iterative approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Complex multi-target editing is a practical requirement that current flow-based editors handle poorly, and FlowDC offers a training-free, single-round solution that avoids both the long-text following failures of single-round methods and the cumulative drift of multi-round methods. The introduction of Complex-PIE-Bench (700 samples, exactly four editing targets each) fills a gap in evaluation, since PIE-Bench++ contains only 19% of samples with three or more targets. Open questions include whether the orthogonal-velocity-decay principle generalizes beyond FLUX.1 to other flow architectures, whether the LLM-based prompt decomposition can be replaced by learned modules, and whether the approach extends to video or multi-modal (image+text) guidance as the authors note.
