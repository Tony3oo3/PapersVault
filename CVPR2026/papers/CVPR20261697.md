## Problem

State-of-the-art instruction-based image editing models (e.g., FLUX.1 Kontext, Qwen-Image) maintain reliable quality for only ~5 sequential edits before suffering catastrophic degradation including subject deformation, edge over-sharpening, and texture collapse. The paper identifies progressive loss of high-frequency information across editing iterations as the root cause, as the generative model increasingly regresses toward canonical training-data representations when fine-grained identity anchors degrade.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261697/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each editing turn, the method consumes the context image (output of the previous turn) and a natural-language text instruction, operating entirely in the latent space of a pre-trained flow-matching model with 28 denoising steps. For FLUX.1 Kontext specifically, the original first-turn image is additionally used during the final 30% of denoising steps for quality-guided noise suppression. The method is training-free and model-agnostic, requiring no fine-tuning or additional parameters beyond a small set of hyperparameters (α₀, γ, n, λ).

## Method

FreqEdit is a training-free framework built on three synergistic mechanisms operating in the velocity field of a rectified-flow model. First, a reference velocity is constructed from the context image via a straight-line trajectory in latent space, and its high-frequency components are extracted via 2-level discrete wavelet transform (db4) and injected into the editing velocity using a CFG-style linear extrapolation in the frequency domain. Second, an adaptive injection strategy spatially modulates the injection strength by computing the L2 divergence between editing and reference velocities, normalizing, inverting, and applying exponential scaling so that semantically consistent regions receive strong high-frequency preservation while edited regions retain transformation flexibility. Third, a path compensation mechanism accumulates the velocity difference between the corrected and editing trajectories over n=4 consecutive steps and periodically applies a correction to realign the denoising path toward the intended editing direction, eliminating ghosting artifacts from residual high-frequency conflicts. For models exhibiting noise accumulation (FLUX.1 Kontext), a quality-guided refinement blends the editing velocity with an auxiliary velocity from the original image during final denoising steps.

## Results

Qwen-Image + FreqEdit achieves the highest human preference score (7.393) and the best consistency at Turn 10 (0.807) among all eight methods. FLUX.1 Kontext + FreqEdit attains the lowest LPIPS at Turn 1 (0.115) and Turn 4 (0.218), indicating superior perceptual fidelity to the source. Both FreqEdit variants substantially outperform their base models in human preference (Qwen-Image: 5.177→7.393; FLUX.1 Kontext: 4.920→6.910) while incurring only a marginal instruction-following drop (e.g., FLUX.1 Kontext: 0.803→0.790 at Turn 10).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the strongest closed-source baseline Nano Banana, Qwen-Image + FreqEdit wins on human preference (7.393 vs. 7.271) and Turn-10 consistency (0.807 vs. 0.806), while Nano Banana retains an edge in instruction following at Turn 10 (0.835 vs. 0.784). Against MTC, both FreqEdit variants dominate on instruction following (0.784–0.790 vs. 0.554 at Turn 10) and LPIPS (0.291–0.330 vs. 0.449), though MTC scores slightly higher on Turn-10 quality (0.790 vs. 0.712–0.729). Against VINCIE, Seedream 4.0, and Bagel, FreqEdit variants lead on every metric at Turn 10, with the largest gaps in LPIPS (e.g., 0.291 vs. 0.546–0.635) and human preference (7.393 vs. 4.148–4.830). Compared to their own base models, FreqEdit improves Turn-10 consistency by +0.040 (Qwen-Image) and +0.036 (FLUX.1 Kontext) and reduces LPIPS by ~0.27–0.28, at the cost of only a 0.013–0.025 drop in instruction following.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the rectified-flow editing paradigm (FLUX.1 Kontext, Qwen-Image) and the growing line of multi-turn editing methods (MTC's trajectory control, VINCIE's block-causal video diffusion, Emu Edit's corrective processing), but departs from them by identifying a specific mechanistic cause—progressive high-frequency degradation—rather than treating cumulative error as a black box. The injection mechanism is inspired by classifier-free guidance but operates in the wavelet frequency domain, a connection to prior frequency-based editing work (FDS, FreeDiff) that those methods did not explore for multi-turn consistency. Unlike prior multi-turn approaches that require training or architectural modifications, FreqEdit is entirely training-free and model-agnostic, requiring only a 2-level DWT decomposition and a spatially-adaptive blending step at inference time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By isolating high-frequency loss as the root cause of multi-turn degradation and showing that a simple wavelet-based injection can restore fidelity across 10+ iterations, this work provides both a diagnostic framework and a practical, zero-cost fix for any flow-matching editing model. The finding that a training-free post-hoc correction can bring open-source models to parity with the much larger Nano Banana in multi-turn scenarios suggests that the bottleneck in iterative editing is not model capacity but a specific information-loss mechanism. Limitations include dependence on the source image's inherent high-frequency content and reduced effectiveness for edits spanning large spatial regions; whether the wavelet-based approach is optimal compared to other frequency decompositions (e.g., Fourier, Gabor) or whether the mechanism generalizes to video editing remain open questions.
