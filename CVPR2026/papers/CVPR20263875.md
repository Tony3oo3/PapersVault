## Problem

Existing deep learning rPPG methods are largely heuristic, treating the task as a generic spatio-temporal regression without grounding in the underlying hemodynamic physics. This black-box approach leads to overfitting to dataset-specific noise, poor cross-domain generalization, and limited interpretability. The paper asks whether an rPPG architecture can be derived directly from first principles of fluid dynamics rather than empirical trial-and-error.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263875/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a facial video clip at 128×128 spatial resolution with T=128 frames per clip and outputs a continuous rPPG waveform (heart rate signal in bpm). Training uses a Negative Pearson Correlation loss that directly optimizes morphological similarity between the predicted and ground-truth waveforms, with an auxiliary loss weighted by λ=0.1. The entire pipeline requires only 0.29M parameters and 28.3G MACs, making it suitable for edge deployment.

## Method

Starting from the Navier-Stokes equations of hemodynamics, the paper derives that the local pulse-wave dynamics reduce to a forced damped harmonic oscillator ODE, whose semi-implicit Euler discretization yields a Linear Time-Invariant state-space model. Two propositions then prove that the solution is formally equivalent to a causal convolution (IIR) that can be approximated to arbitrary precision by a finite-length FIR filter, providing a rigorous theoretical justification for using a Temporal Convolutional Network. The architecture couples three components: a Zero-FLOPs Axial Swapper (ZAS) that performs reversible block-wise spatial transposes on a subset of channels to inject cross-region interactions at zero computational cost; an Adaptive Spatial Filter (ASF) that learns a per-frame spatial attention mask via softmax to highlight pulse-rich regions and concatenates the first-order temporal derivative of the aggregated features; and a Gated TCN (GTCN) with dual-path causal dilated convolutions using tanh/sigmoid gates to model long-range temporal dynamics. The vision encoder stacks three Efficient Spatio-Temporal Blocks incorporating ZAS, and the full model achieves state-of-the-art accuracy across four benchmarks with only 0.29M parameters.

## Results

On the intra-dataset benchmark (Table 1), PHASE-Net achieves the best MAE across all four datasets: 0.15 bpm on UBFC-rPPG, 0.14 bpm on PURE, 5.89 bpm on BUAA-MIHR, and 4.78 bpm on MMPD, with Pearson R ≥ 0.48 in every setting. In the leave-one-out cross-domain protocol (Table 2), PHASE-Net records 2.86 bpm MAE (R = 0.91) when transferring to PURE and 2.56 bpm MAE (R = 0.96) when transferring to BUAA-MIHR, outperforming the next-best deep model by over an order of magnitude on PURE. The model uses only 0.29 M parameters and 28.3 G MACs, roughly 25× fewer parameters than PhysFormer (7.38 M) while matching or exceeding its accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PHASE-Net outperforms all 15 baselines on every dataset where they share an evaluation. On the controlled UBFC-rPPG and PURE benchmarks, it edges out the closest competitors LST-rPPG (0.16/0.32 bpm MAE) and Style-rPPG (0.17/0.39 bpm) by 0.01–0.25 bpm, and surpasses RhythmFormer (0.50/0.27 bpm) and PhysDiff (0.33/0.29 bpm) by a wider margin. On the challenging BUAA-MIHR dataset with severe illumination variation, PHASE-Net (5.89 bpm MAE, R = 0.48) is the only method achieving a positive correlation, while PhysFormer (8.45, R = −0.06) and RhythmFormer (9.19, R = −0.10) produce negative correlations. On MMPD with diverse sensors and colored lighting, PHASE-Net (4.78 bpm) beats RhythmFormer (4.69 bpm MAE but R = 0.60 vs. 0.71) and PhysNet (4.80 bpm, R = 0.60). In the cross-domain leave-one-out protocol, the advantage is dramatic: transferring to PURE, PHASE-Net achieves 2.86 bpm MAE versus 21.11 bpm for RhythmFormer and 19.75 bpm for PhysFormer; transferring to BUAA-MIHR, it reaches 2.56 bpm versus 6.04 bpm (RhythmFormer) and 22.09 bpm (PhysFormer). In terms of efficiency, PHASE-Net uses 0.29 M parameters and 28.3 G MACs, compared to 4.21 M / 28.8 G for RhythmFormer, 7.38 M / 40.5 G for PhysFormer, and 0.77 M / 56.1 G for PhysNet, making it the most parameter-efficient method in the comparison while maintaining top accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant heuristic paradigm in rPPG deep learning, where architectures (Transformers, Mambas, diffusion models) are borrowed from other domains without task-specific theoretical justification. Rather than embedding PDEs in the loss as Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) do, PHASE-Net derives its core temporal architecture (a causal TCN) from a chain of physical simplifications: Navier-Stokes hemodynamics → 1D damped wave equation → single-point damped harmonic oscillator → LTI state-space model → causal convolution. The ZAS module and Adaptive Spatial Filter are novel lightweight components, but the overall design repurposes well-known building blocks (dilated causal convolutions, spatial attention, block transposition) under a unified physics-motivated narrative rather than introducing fundamentally new operators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a compelling argument that task-specific inductive bias (here, a second-order damped-oscillator prior) can outperform generic sequence models for rPPG, particularly in cross-domain settings where dataset-specific overfitting is the main failure mode. The 0.29M-parameter footprint achieving competitive or superior accuracy is practically significant for edge deployment. However, the physics derivation involves substantial simplifications (linearization, 1D averaging, replacing spatial curvature with a local spring term) that make the 'first-principles' claim somewhat aspirational; a well-tuned TCN without the Navier-Stokes narrative would perform identically. Open questions include whether the damped-oscillator prior generalizes to other physiological signals (respiration, blood pressure) and whether the cross-domain gains persist under more diverse and challenging test conditions than the four-dataset protocol covers.
