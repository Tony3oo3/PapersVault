## Problem

Current long video benchmarks evaluate VLMs with limited frame inputs, but when key frames are missing, models that honestly refuse to answer are marked incorrect while models that guess may coincidentally produce the right answer. This binary evaluation inadvertently rewards speculation and penalizes honesty, producing misleading accuracy scores and encouraging untrustworthy model behavior rather than uncertainty-aware responses.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263570/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance feeds a uniformly sampled video clip (64, 128, 256, 512, or 1024 frames, downsampled from 1 FPS, with max resolution 512×512 pixels per frame) alongside an open-ended text question. The model must produce either a grounded answer or an explicit refusal stating the video lacks sufficient information. Ground-truth labels are provided per frame level, so the same question may have a definite answer at 1024 frames but be labeled unanswerable at 64 frames, enabling fine-grained assessment of when a model should and should not answer.

## Method

VirtueBench is constructed by collecting 3,042 videos and 33,400 questions from six existing long video benchmarks, converting all multiple-choice questions to open-ended format, and applying multi-stage quality filtering (removing long answers, option-dependent questions, timestamp references, and trivially answerable items) to yield ~2,500 QA pairs. Each video is then uniformly sampled into clips at five frame levels (64–1024), and a dual-annotator pipeline with Gemini-2.5-Pro reference answers produces per-clip ground truths, labeling clips as unanswerable when key visual evidence is absent. Evaluation uses an LLM-as-a-judge (GPT-4o) in two stages: first detecting whether the model refuses, then verifying semantic correctness of the answer against the reference. The benchmark's key design insight is assigning separate ground truths per frame level, so that a model is scored correctly only if it refuses on unanswerable clips and answers accurately on answerable ones, directly measuring trustworthiness under uncertainty.

## Results

On VirtueBench, Gemini-2.5-Flash achieves the best overall accuracy at 58.96% (64 frames), while Qwen3-VL-32B leads among open-source models at 50.83%. Refusal accuracy—the paper's central metric—varies dramatically: Qwen2.5-VL-72B reaches 80.20% at 64 frames, GPT-4o reaches 70.98%, while LLaVA-Video-7B, Keye-VL-8B, and VideoChat-Flash-7B all score below 1%. A general decline in accuracy is observed as frame count increases (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-72B drops from 49.32% at 64 frames to 31.52% at 1024 frames), contrasting with prior benchmarks that report monotonic improvement with more frames.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 25 VLMs on VirtueBench, revealing stark differences in trustworthiness. Qwen2.5-VL-72B achieves the highest refusal accuracy among open-source models (80.20% at 64 frames), substantially outperforming LLaVA-Video-72B (0.98%), Keye-VL-8B (0.20%), and VideoChat-Flash-7B (0.98%), which show virtually no refusal behavior. Among commercial models, GPT-4o (70.98% refusal at 64F) and Gemini-2.5-Flash (58.04%) demonstrate strong honesty, while GPT-5-chat (19.80%) and GPT-5 (33.53%) show markedly lower refusal rates despite higher overall accuracy on answerable questions. Qwen3-VL-32B (60.20% refusal) is the strongest open-source model overall (50.83%), narrowing the gap to Gemini-2.5-Flash (58.96%). A key finding is that removing the explicit honesty instruction from the prompt causes refusal accuracy to drop by nearly half for most capable models, indicating that current VLMs do not inherently refuse under uncertainty unless prompted to do so.

## Relation to Prior Work

VirtueBench repurposes question-video pairs from existing long video benchmarks (MLVU, LVBench, LongVideoBench, Video-MME, ALLVB) but introduces a fundamentally different evaluation protocol: per-frame-level ground truths that explicitly label unanswerable cases, rather than comparing model outputs to a single full-video answer. Conceptually, it departs from video hallucination benchmarks (VideoHallucer, HallusionBench, ELV-Halluc, VIDHALLUC) that diagnose specific hallucination types, instead targeting the broader question of whether models honestly acknowledge insufficient evidence. The paper aligns with Kalai et al.'s analysis of why language models hallucinate, arguing that current binary right/wrong evaluation inadvertently rewards guessing and penalizes honest refusal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a critical blind spot in current long-video evaluation: models that guess correctly are indistinguishable from those that genuinely perceive the evidence, while honest refusals are penalized. The finding that removing an explicit honesty instruction nearly halves refusal accuracy for most models suggests that current VLMs are not inherently uncertainty-aware but behave as 'test-takers' optimized to always produce an answer. Open questions include how to integrate refusal training into VLM pretraining or RLHF pipelines, whether the per-frame-level ground truth construction generalizes to other modalities or shorter videos, and whether LLM-as-a-judge evaluation (GPT-4o) introduces systematic biases in detecting refusal vs. hedging. The benchmark's reliance on a single judge model and its focus on open-ended answers (converting all MCQs) are practical limitations that may affect reproducibility.
