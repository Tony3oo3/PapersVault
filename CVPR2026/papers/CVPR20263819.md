## Problem

Despite reported gains from RL-based post-training on multimodal reasoning models, it remains unclear whether these improvements stem from genuine visual understanding or merely from strengthened textual reasoning priors within the LLM. No prior work has systematically investigated how hallucination—where models generate reasoning from corrupted or missing visual inputs—interacts with the RL training process itself, leaving a critical mechanistic gap in understanding what RL actually teaches multimodal models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263819/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes RGB images (e.g., geometric diagrams) paired with text prompts specifying conditions and questions as input, and produces a full chain-of-thought reasoning trajectory ending in a final answer. The distinguishing feature is that during both training and evaluation, modality-specific corruptions are applied: blank image replacement removes all visual content, random image replacement substitutes mismatched images, and textual information removal strips conditions and queries via rule-based matching. Despite these corruptions, the model is still required to generate complete reasoning chains and answers, forcing reliance on internally hallucinated content.

## Method

The Hallucination-as-Cue framework introduces three modality-specific corruptions (Blank Image Replacement, Random Image Replacement, Textual Information Removal) applied during both GRPO training and inference to systematically probe the role of hallucination in RL post-training. During hallucination-inductive training, GRPO samples rollout trajectories from corrupted inputs; a small subset of hallucinated responses coincidentally produce correct answers and receive positive rule-based rewards, which the algorithm then reinforces, teaching the model reasoning patterns derived from hallucinated content. The framework defines eight evaluation settings (S1–S8) that cross normal/corrupted training with normal/corrupted testing to disentangle whether improvements come from visual grounding or textual reasoning amplification. Experiments across Qwen2.5-VL-3B and 7B models on Geometry3K, MMR1-V0, and CLEVR training data, evaluated on MathVision, MathVerse, MathVista, and WeMath, reveal that hallucination-inductive training can match or surpass standard RL training, with larger models benefiting more strongly from hallucinated trajectories.

## Results

On the four multimodal math reasoning benchmarks (MathVision, MathVerse, MathVista, WeMath), standard GRPO training on Geometry3K improves Qwen2.5-VL-3B from 39.72% to 44.74% average accuracy, and Qwen2.5-VL-7B from 50.89% to 53.52%. Strikingly, for the 7B model, hallucination-inductive training with Random Image Replacement (GRPO-RI) achieves the highest average score of 54.23%, surpassing standard GRPO by 0.71 points. For the 3B model, all hallucination-inductive variants (BI: 42.25%, RI: 42.45%, TR: 42.34%) remain within ~2.5 points of standard GRPO while still improving over the base model by 2.5–2.7 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares hallucination-inductive GRPO training (BI, RI, TR corruptions) against two baselines: the untrained Qwen2.5-VL base model and standard GRPO training on clean image-text pairs. Against the base model, all RL variants (standard and hallucination-inductive) achieve substantial gains of 2.5–5.5 points in average accuracy across benchmarks, confirming that even severely corrupted training data still benefits the model. Against standard GRPO, the 3B model shows standard training winning by ~2.3 points on average (44.74% vs. 42.25–42.45%), while the 7B model reveals a reversal: GRPO-RI outperforms standard GRPO by 0.71 points (54.23% vs. 53.52%), and GRPO-TR ties it (53.51% vs. 53.52%). This scaling effect—where larger models benefit more from hallucinated trajectories—suggests that RL primarily amplifies the underlying LLM's reasoning capacity rather than teaching genuine visual grounding. The authors further show this pattern generalizes across training datasets (Geometry3K, MMR1-V0, CLEVR) in Table 2, where BI-corrupted training on MMR1-V0 yields 46.78% (3B) and 54.79% (7B), remaining competitive with standard GRPO on the same data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the GRPO algorithm from DeepSeek-R1, applying it to multimodal reasoning models (Qwen2.5-VL) following the recent wave of RL-based MLLM post-training (Vision-R1, VLM-R1, etc.). It departs from prior hallucination research—which has focused on mitigation, detection, or benchmarking—by repurposing hallucination as a diagnostic signal: rather than suppressing it, the authors intentionally induce it through modality-specific corruptions (blank images, random images, text removal) to probe what RL is actually learning. The framework also connects to concurrent work showing MLLMs can retain accuracy without images, but extends the analysis into the training dynamics rather than just inference-time behavior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that RL post-training with completely blank or mismatched images still improves—and in some cases surpasses—standard training challenges the core assumption that current RL methods teach MLLMs to ground reasoning in visual information, suggesting instead that they primarily amplify the underlying LLM's textual reasoning capacity. This has direct implications for designing modality-aware reward functions and training curricula that genuinely enforce visual grounding. Key limitations include the focus on a single model family (Qwen2.5-VL), primarily geometry/math tasks, and the GRPO algorithm specifically; open questions include whether these findings generalize to other RL paradigms (e.g., latent-space reasoning), other model architectures, and non-mathematical visual reasoning tasks.
