## Problem

Despite widespread adoption of the 'pre-train projector then fine-tune' paradigm for VLMs, there is no principled criterion for selecting which vision encoder to pair with a given LLM. The paper shows that conventional heuristics—choosing the largest encoder or the one with highest zero-shot accuracy—consistently fail to identify the optimal encoder, exhibiting only weak-to-moderate correlation with final VLM performance. This raises the fundamental question of what property of a vision encoder actually determines its suitability for VLM alignment.

## Input / Output

The method operates on 1,000 randomly sampled image-text pairs from the alignment dataset. For each candidate vision encoder, it extracts CLS tokens from the last layer as visual features, and for the target LLM, it extracts second-to-last-layer hidden representations as textual features. Both are reduced to pairwise angular distance matrices, which are then scale-normalized via median-ratio matching before GW computation. The output is a single GW distance scalar per encoder; the encoder with the minimum GW distance is selected as optimal, requiring no training or fine-tuning of any VLM.

## Method

The core idea is to measure structural similarity between vision and text representation spaces—two heterogeneous metric-measure spaces that cannot be compared via naive pointwise distance—using the Gromov-Wasserstein distance, which compares internal pairwise-distance geometry up to isometry. Concretely, the method computes pairwise angular distance matrices for both modalities, applies an affine median-ratio normalization to unify their scale, then solves for the optimal transport plan via a conditional-gradient scheme (iteratively solving linear OT subproblems with the GW gradient as cost matrix). The resulting GW discrepancy quantifies how well the within-space distance structures align under the optimal coupling. Theoretically, the paper proves that the Lipschitz constant of the Bayes-optimal cross-modal projector is upper-bounded by a function of the ∞-norm GW distance, establishing that smaller GW distance implies the optimal mapping can be realized by a lower-complexity hypothesis, thereby linking GW distance directly to learnability.

## Results

Using Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct as the base LLM on the 'Large' vision encoder group, GW distance selects the optimal encoder achieving 66.43 average score across 9 benchmarks, outperforming the best baseline (Accuracy/MutualNN at 64.63) by 1.8 points. In the correlation analysis across 14 encoders, GW achieves the strongest association with final VLM performance: Pearson r = 0.657, Spearman ρ = 0.534, and R² = 0.431, substantially exceeding all baselines (next best: MutualNN with r = 0.608 but ρ = 0.178). GW also matches the optimal model in all four main experimental settings (two LLMs × two size groups), while baselines fail to do so consistently.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GW distance outperforms all four baselines in the 'Large' encoder group with Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct, selecting SigLIP-SO400M-384 (66.43 avg) over DFN5B-ViT-H-378 chosen by both Accuracy and MutualNN (64.63 avg), a 1.8-point gap. In the 'Small' group, GW ties with the Accuracy baseline (both at 64.36) but still dominates RSA (61.31) and CCA (63.51). With LLaMa-3.1-8B-Instruct, CCA coincidentally matches GW in the 'Large' group (both at 61.38), but CCA's correlation with VLM performance is near zero (r = 0.043), indicating its success is an outlier rather than a reliable signal. In the correlation analysis, GW is the only metric exceeding 0.5 on both Pearson and Spearman correlations (0.657 and 0.534 respectively), while MutualNN's apparently strong Pearson (0.608) is misleading due to a negative Spearman (0.178) and inverted sign, suggesting spurious correlation. RSA and CCA consistently underperform, with CCA showing virtually no correlation to final performance (R² = 0.002).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Gromov-Wasserstein distance framework (Mémoli 2011; Peyré et al. 2016) and repurposes it as a cross-modal structural similarity metric for vision encoder selection in VLMs. Conceptually, it is closest to the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al. 2024), which also measures implicit alignment between visual and textual representations, but departs from its MutualNN metric by replacing fixed 1-to-1 neighborhood matching with an optimal-transport coupling that is invariant to isometry. The empirical setting follows the LLaVA/Visual Instruction Tuning paradigm (Liu et al. 2023, 2024), and the baselines (RSA, CCA, zero-shot accuracy) represent the standard heuristics practitioners currently rely on for component selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a ~1-minute inference-only computation can replace 68 GPU-hours of full VLM training to identify the best vision encoder, and the finding that conventional metrics (model size, zero-shot accuracy) show only weak correlation with final VLM performance challenges a widely-held heuristic in the community. The theoretical bound linking GW distance to the Lipschitz constant of the optimal cross-modal projector provides a formal justification for why structural compatibility matters. However, the scope remains limited to 18 encoders, 2 LLMs, and the LLaVA-style two-stage recipe; whether the metric generalizes to other VLM architectures (e.g., those with different projector designs), other modalities, or larger model pools remains an open question.
