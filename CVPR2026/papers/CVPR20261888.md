## Problem

Most Theory of Mind (ToM) evaluations for large language models rely on text-based inputs, leaving video-only, egocentric scenarios largely unexplored despite their ecological validity for real-world social reasoning. Furthermore, existing approaches treat multimodal large language models (MLLMs) as black boxes, rarely probing how internal attention mechanisms behave during multiple-choice ToM reasoning, and the impact of MLLM hallucinations on such tasks remains underexplored from an interpretability perspective.

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw egocentric video (24 frames) together with a text-based ToM question and multiple-choice options, deliberately omitting any textual annotations or scene descriptions to isolate visual reasoning. For the multiple-choice setting it outputs a single Top-1 answer across goal, belief, and action inference subtasks; for the open-ended setting it generates free-form natural-language explanations of agents' mental states, scored via a TruthfulQA-style rubric (truthfulness, informativeness, and their conjunction) adjudicated by two independent DeepSeek-R1 judges. Inference runs in a zero-temperature, zero-shot regime with no additional prompts beyond the video, question, and options.

## Method

VisionToM is a backbone-frozen activation-steering framework that injects learned intervention vectors into selected attention heads of an MLLM (LLaVA-Next-Video or Qwen2.5-VL) immediately after multi-head attention computation but before the output projection. It decomposes the task into two modules: (1) a visual-attention module that uses ℓ∞-bounded PGD adversarial perturbations on video frames to construct positive/negative sample pairs and computes a mean activation offset vector encouraging the model to attend to visual evidence rather than linguistic priors; and (2) a ToM-reasoning module that fixes the visual input, treats the correct answer as positive and incorrect answers as negative, clusters the negative representations per attention head (via silhouette, elbow, and Calinski-Harabasz criteria), and trains cluster-specific two-layer encoders to produce direction-specific correction vectors. Linear probes identify the top-K=64 most task-sensitive heads (shared across tasks for visual attention, task-specific for ToM reasoning), and at inference the combined direction Δ = δ_V + δ_T is added to the attention output scaled by α=1.0. The entire calibration (probe + encoder training) takes roughly 1.2 hours with the MLLM backbone frozen, and the resulting vectors are reused at inference without any fine-tuning, handcrafted prompts, or external language annotations.

## Results

On the EgoToM benchmark (video-only, 24 frames), VisionToM applied to LLaVA-Next-Video-7B improves top-1 accuracy by 13.0 pp on Goal (61.5→74.5%), 6.4 pp on Belief (38.9→45.3%), and 5.7 pp on Actions (24.0→29.7%). For Qwen2.5-VL-7B, the same intervention yields 88.9% on Goal (matching the human baseline of 88–89%), 42.0% on Belief, and 37.6% on Actions. All MLLMs remain well below human performance on Belief (72%) and Actions (78%), indicating that ToM reasoning in these dimensions is still far from solved.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VisionToM consistently outperforms the unmodified baselines of both backbone models: LLaVA-Next-Video-7B gains 13.0 pp on Goal and 6.4 pp on Belief, while Qwen2.5-VL-7B gains 2.0 pp on Goal and 6.4 pp on Belief. After intervention, Qwen2.5-VL-7B reaches 88.9% on Goal, matching the human baseline (88–89%) and surpassing Gemini-2.5-Flash (86.0%) and GPT-4-Turbo (83%). On Belief, LLaVA-Next-Video+VisionToM (45.3%) approaches Video-Llama2-72B (46%) and Gemini-2.5-Flash (46.7%), while Qwen2.5-VL+VisionToM (42.0%) still trails the strongest closed-source models. On Actions, all MLLMs—including those with VisionToM—remain substantially below the human baseline of 78%, with the best result being Qwen2.5-VL+VisionToM at 37.6%. Ablation rows confirm that removing either the visual-attention or ToM-reasoning component degrades performance, and reversing the intervention direction (−αΔ) causes large drops (e.g., LLaVA Goal falls to 50.6%), validating that the learned directions are meaningful rather than arbitrary.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends GridToM (Li et al., ICML 2025), which first demonstrated that attention-head intervention can improve ToM reasoning in MLLMs, by shifting the intervention target from textual to visual representations and replacing GridToM's binary logistic-regression coefficient directions with a clustering-based, per-cluster encoder approach. It also draws on the broader activation-steering / representation-engineering literature (e.g., inference-time intervention) and adversarial-perturbation methods for probing model internals, but applies them specifically to the video-only, egocentric ToM setting where no textual annotations are available. The use of PGD-generated adversarial frames as negative samples for visual-attention direction estimation departs from prior work that relied on random noise or handcrafted prompts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap: most ToM evaluations for MLLMs still rely on textual input or simulated environments, whereas real-world social reasoning is grounded in dynamic, first-person video. The backbone-frozen, calibration-once design makes the method lightweight and broadly applicable without retraining, which is attractive for deployment. However, the gains on the harder Belief and Actions subtasks remain modest (≈6% absolute), the evaluation is confined to a single benchmark (EgoToM) and two 7B models, and generalization to multi-agent, longer-horizon, or out-of-distribution video scenarios remains untested. Open questions include whether the cross-task visual-attention consistency holds for larger or architecturally different MLLMs, and whether the clustering-based encoder approach scales to open-ended (non-multiple-choice) ToM reasoning without a fixed answer set.
