## Problem

LiDAR localization and 3D object detection are traditionally handled in separate pipelines, causing computational redundancy and preventing cross-task knowledge transfer. The core difficulty is that these tasks demand conflicting feature representations—localization relies on stable static geographic structures while detection requires fine-grained dynamic object features—so naive multi-task feature sharing introduces mutual interference. Furthermore, no existing dataset simultaneously provides multi-traversal LiDAR sequences for localization evaluation and dense 3D bounding box annotations for detection, blocking joint training and benchmarking.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single LiDAR point cloud P = {p_i} in the local coordinate frame (voxelized at 0.2 m resolution over a 120 m × 120 m × 8 m grid) and produces two outputs: a 6-DoF ego-pose (position and orientation) estimated via scene coordinate regression followed by RANSAC refinement, and 7-DoF 3D bounding boxes (x, y, z, l, w, h, θ) for vehicles, pedestrians, and cyclists. Both tasks share the same BEV feature map from the backbone, with the localization head predicting world-aligned coordinates and the detection head regressing center-based boxes.

## Method

TACO consists of two stages: a Task-Agnostic Feature Extraction (TAFE) stage using LiSA's sparse 3D backbone to extract shared point-cloud features, and a Task-Aware Feature Contrastive Learning (TFCL) stage with three modules. ITCL decouples static geographic features (top-N by L2 norm) from movable object features (extracted via GT boxes) by minimizing their pairwise cosine similarity, preventing the locator from latching onto dynamic objects and the detector from confusing curbs with vehicles. IDCL performs symmetric contrastive learning between dynamic (v > 0.5 m/s) and static (v ≤ 0.5 m/s) objects, pulling same-category features together and pushing different-category features apart regardless of motion state. ILCL separates true geographic-discriminative features from pseudo-geographic features (stationary objects visually resembling infrastructure) to harden localization against temporarily static clutter. The total loss is a weighted sum of the detection loss, localization loss, and the three contrastive losses, trained end-to-end with Adam (lr=0.01, batch=100, 100 epochs).

## Results

On the OxfoLD test set, TACO achieves a mean position error of 0.72 m and mean orientation error of 0.85°, improving over the strongest SCR baseline LiSA (0.95 m, 1.14°) by 24.21% and 25.44% respectively. For 3D detection, TACO reaches 81.60% Vehicle AP@0.5, 52.32% Pedestrian AP@0.3, and 57.46% Cyclist AP@0.3, surpassing the CenterPoint baseline by 28.77% on vehicles. On nuScenes, TACO attains 0.92 m / 0.86° localization error and 85.72% / 86.93% / 52.30% detection AP, outperforming LiDARFormer across all metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TACO outperforms all 11 baselines on the primary localization task: it beats the best APR method (DiffLoc, 1.86 m / 0.87°) by 61% in position error, and the best SCR methods LiSA (0.95 m / 1.14°) and LightLoc (0.83 m / 1.12°) by 24% and 13% in position error respectively, while also achieving the lowest orientation error (0.85° vs. 1.12° for LightLoc). For 3D detection, TACO surpasses CenterPoint (63.37% Vehicle AP) by 18.23 points and PillarNeXt (72.54%) by 9.06 points on Vehicle AP@0.5, though it trails FSHNet (78.31%) by 3.29 points on vehicles while leading on pedestrians (52.32% vs. 50.22%). Among multi-task architectures, TACO dominates both Cross-stitch (1.97 m / 2.01°) and LiDARFormer (0.89 m / 1.06°) on localization, and achieves higher detection AP across all three categories compared to LiDARFormer (72.54% / 47.21% / 51.00%). On nuScenes, TACO improves over LiDARFormer by 8.91% in localization (0.92 m vs. 1.01 m) and by 2.27% / 0.84% / 3.93% in vehicle / pedestrian / cyclist detection AP. On KITTI-360, TACO achieves 1.79 m / 1.67° / 53.47% on seq.09 versus LiDARFormer's 1.84 m / 1.77° / 53.21%, confirming consistent gains across datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

TACO builds directly on LiSA (CVPR 2024) for its sparse-convolution 3D backbone and scene-coordinate-regression localization formulation, and on CenterPoint (CVPR 2021) for its center-based 3D detection head. It departs from prior multi-task LiDAR networks (Cross-stitch, LiDARFormer) that rely on shared backbones with task-specific heads or cross-stitch gating, by introducing explicit contrastive losses (ITCL, IDCL, ILCL) to decouple the inherently conflicting static-geographic and dynamic-object feature spaces. The paper also carves new ground in the data landscape by extending the Oxford RobotCar multi-traversal localization dataset with dense 3D bounding boxes (OxfoLD), filling a gap that prevented prior joint training of these two tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The OxfoLD dataset is a practically important contribution: it is the first to combine multi-traversal LiDAR sequences with per-frame 3D object annotations, enabling rigorous evaluation of joint localization-detection systems across time and weather. The task-aware contrastive learning paradigm offers a general recipe for resolving feature conflicts in multi-task settings where tasks demand opposing semantic priorities (global static structure vs. local dynamic objects). The authors acknowledge a key limitation—reliance on multi-traversal data with rich 3D boxes—and leave open whether the approach generalizes to single-traversal or weakly supervised settings, which would be critical for real-world deployment where repeated scans are unavailable.
