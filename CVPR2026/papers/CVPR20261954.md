## Problem

Prior computational shadow art methods treat the shadow as the sole medium and require a predefined visual target to optimize toward. This paper tackles a fundamentally different and more open-ended problem: given an arbitrary 3D object, jointly discovering scene parameters (pose and lighting) and generating a partial line drawing such that the cast shadow completes the drawing into a coherent, recognizable image—without any a priori target. The key challenges are that the subject is unknown, raw shadow renderings provide weak structural conditioning for generative models, and shadow-drawing training examples are extremely scarce.

## Input / Output

The input is a single 3D mesh whose longest dimension is normalized to standardize shadow size; the canvas is a ground plane and the light is a spotlight at fixed distance from the canvas center, leaving three free degrees of freedom (light azimuth, elevation, and object vertical rotation). The output is a pair: (i) a partial line drawing rendered in black strokes, and (ii) the full scene parameter set (light azimuth θ, elevation φ, object polar position (r, γ), and object rotation α) that, when rendered, produces a gray shadow silhouette that spatially completes the drawing. Generation is constrained as an outpainting problem with a binary object mask so that no strokes are placed over the object's footprint.

## Method

The core insight is replacing the raw shadow image with its 2D boundary contour as the conditioning signal for a FLUX.1-Canny LoRA line-drawing generator, which yields tighter geometric alignment and enables scalable synthetic data construction by extracting closed contours from GPT-4o-generated line drawings (10K samples). Scene parameters are optimized via PyTorch3D differentiable silhouette rendering to maximize the fractal dimension of the shadow contour, initialized from 48 diverse configurations with local-neighborhood constraints to preserve physical plausibility. A VLM with chain-of-thought prompting generates detailed subject descriptions from each shadow contour, and a VQA-based coherence check (overlaying the contour in red and asking whether it outlines the described component) filters misaligned results. Final ranking multiplies improvement scores across CLIP similarity, ImageReward, and Human Preference Score between the full composition and the shadow-removed variant, retaining only compositions where the shadow meaningfully contributes.

## Results

On a 200-object evaluation set (YCB, Objaverse-LVIS, Objaverse, real-world scans, and MeshLRM-generated assets), ShadowDraw achieves a mean CLIP score of 32.41 and a concealment score of 3.0059, substantially outperforming all baselines. In user studies, participants preferred ShadowDraw outputs over Gemini (shadow contour) in 70.3% of pairwise comparisons, and 96.8% of top-4 ranked results contained at least one rated satisfactory by 8 evaluators. The concealment metric (CLIP difference between full and shadow-removed compositions) is the most discriminative: ShadowDraw scores 3.0059 versus 0.2421 for the best baseline, confirming the shadow serves as a critical structural element rather than a redundant component.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper constructs three baselines using state-of-the-art image generators (Gemini Flash 2.5 and GPT-Image) conditioned on either the object-shadow composite or the shadow contour, paired with the same VLM-generated text prompts. ShadowDraw wins on every metric across all three baselines: CLIP improves by 0.76–1.26 points, and concealment improves by 2.76–3.29 points, indicating that off-the-shelf generators fail to make the shadow a meaningful structural contributor. In user preference studies (10 participants, pairwise), ShadowDraw is preferred over Gemini (shadow contour) in 70.3% of cases and over GPT-Image (shadow contour) in 98.5% of cases. The Gemini (object-shadow) baseline performs worst, with a negative concealment score (−0.2840), meaning the shadow actually degrades the composition relative to removing it. The authors attribute the gap to weak conditioning signals in composite images and the inability of general-purpose generators to capture the nuanced shadow-drawing interplay that their dedicated contour-conditioned LoRA model learns.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the computational shadow art lineage, where prior work (e.g., [32, 36, 39]) optimizes geometry or lighting to reproduce a *predefined* target shadow, by instead jointly discovering both the scene configuration and the target subject. For line drawing generation, it builds on FLUX.1-Canny with a LoRA adapter and adopts the masked outpainting formulation from [30], but repurposes edge-conditioned generation in a new way: the conditioning signal is a physically rendered shadow contour rather than a semantic edge map. The VLM-based prompt proposal and VQA-based coherence check draw on recent vision-language model capabilities but apply them to a novel compositional verification problem that has no direct precedent in the shadow art or line drawing literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the first end-to-end pipeline that turns an arbitrary 3D object into a deployable shadow-drawing artwork, requiring only a spotlight and a planar surface for physical reproduction, which significantly lowers the barrier to this art form. Limitations include the restriction to single-light, textureless setups, the fact that some objects naturally produce uninformative shadows, and the reliance on diffusion inference that adds runtime overhead. Open questions include whether the approach extends to textured or translucent materials, multi-light configurations, and whether the automatic ranking can fully replace human curation at scale.
