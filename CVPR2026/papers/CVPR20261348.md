## Problem

Rehearsal-based online class-incremental learning suffers from gradient conflict between current-task and memory-buffer gradients, and recent hypergradient methods mitigate this by aligning gradients toward a shared meta-objective. However, this alignment is inherently myopic: it corrects gradient directions only at the current parameter state without anticipating how the update will reshape the loss landscape, potentially steering the model into sharp regions that harm future learning. The paper asks whether a forward-looking, multi-directional exploration of the loss landscape can yield more robust updates that simultaneously preserve plasticity and stability.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for TinyImageNet) in an online class-incremental stream where each task introduces new classes and is processed for a single epoch. At each step, the model receives a current-task mini-batch and a replayed mini-batch sampled from a fixed-size memory buffer (ranging from 200 to 5k samples), and produces a single class-label prediction over all classes seen so far without task identity at inference. The memory buffer is updated after each step using a standard replay strategy.

## Method

LOR replaces the single corrective gradient step with a multi-directional lookahead exploration. At each training step, it computes the current-task gradient g_n and memory-buffer gradient g_M, then samples K preference vectors from a Dirichlet distribution to span the stability–plasticity trade-off spectrum. For each vector, it takes a frozen lookahead step θ'_k = θ_t − η_L(w_{k,n}·g_n + w_{k,M}·g_M) and evaluates the loss at that future state on a combined meta-batch. The K lookahead losses are aggregated via a Log-Sum-Exp (LSE) smooth maximum, and the resulting gradient is a softmax-weighted sum of the outer gradients—effectively emphasizing the worst-performing lookahead directions. This soft worst-case objective steers the model toward flatter, more generalizable regions of the loss landscape, reducing the disagreement risk with the ideal past-data classifier and thereby mitigating catastrophic forgetting.

## Results

On Seq-CIFAR10 (|M|=1k), LOR achieves 74.22% AAA, surpassing the strongest baseline POCL (66.23%) by 8.0 points and ER (54.91%) by 19.3 points. On the more challenging Seq-CIFAR100 (|M|=5k), LOR reaches 43.26% AAA versus POCL's 36.34% and CLSER's 23.25%. On Seq-TinyImageNet (|M|=5k), LOR attains 31.36% AAA, outperforming POCL (25.48%) by nearly 6 points. In terms of forgetting, LOR achieves FM of −11.12 on Seq-CIFAR100 (|M|=5k) compared to POCL's −17.55, indicating substantially better knowledge retention.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LOR outperforms all nine baselines across every dataset and buffer configuration. The closest competitor is POCL (Wu et al., ICML 2024), which LOR surpasses by 8.0 AAA points on Seq-CIFAR10 (|M|=1k: 74.22 vs 66.23), 6.9 points on Seq-CIFAR100 (|M|=5k: 43.26 vs 36.34), and 5.9 points on Seq-TinyImageNet (|M|=5k: 31.36 vs 25.48). Among rehearsal-based methods, LOR beats CLSER (Arani et al., ICLR 2022) by 10.95 AAA points on Seq-CIFAR10 (|M|=1k) and 20.01 points on Seq-CIFAR100 (|M|=5k), and outperforms DER++ (Buzzega et al., ICLR 2020) by 13.21 points on Seq-CIFAR10 (|M|=1k). Gradient-projection methods (GEM, A-GEM) and regularization-based EWC (Kirkpatrick et al., PNAS 2017) are substantially weaker, with LOR exceeding them by 25–35 AAA points on Seq-CIFAR100. On the full ResNet-18 backbone (Table 7), LOR achieves 25.45% Acc on Seq-CIFAR100 (|M|=5k), surpassing VR-MCL (Wu et al., ICLR 2024) at 19.68% and CLSER at 19.45%. In forgetting measure, LOR consistently achieves the least negative FM (e.g., −11.12 vs POCL's −17.55 on Seq-CIFAR100 |M|=5k), confirming superior knowledge retention.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of rehearsal-based continual learning, hypergradient/meta-learning methods (MER, La-MAML, VR-MCL, POCL), and lookahead optimization (Lookahead optimizer, extra-gradient methods). Its key conceptual departure is twofold: it provides the first formal analysis showing that hypergradient-based OCIL methods succeed by implicitly reducing gradient conflict between current and past tasks, and it then argues this alignment is inherently myopic—correcting only the immediate gradient direction without anticipating loss-landscape geometry one step ahead. LOR repurposes the lookahead-optimization idea from general-purpose training (slow/fast weight interpolation, extra-gradient) into a structured exploration of stability-plasticity trade-offs, using a Log-Sum-Exp surrogate to make the resulting min-max objective first-order and tractable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: LOR achieves the largest reported margins over prior SOTA (POCL, VR-MCL) across all three benchmarks and buffer sizes, with the gap widening on a 40-task sequence—evidence that myopic errors compound over long horizons. The method is architecture-agnostic (validated on 3-layer DNN and full ResNet-18) and requires only K additional forward passes per step, making it deployable in memory-constrained online settings. Open questions include whether the Dirichlet-sampled preference vectors are optimal, how the approach extends to task-incremental or domain-incremental settings, and whether the K-lookahead cost becomes prohibitive at very large model scales. The theoretical link between min-max robustness and reduced disagreement risk (via domain-adaptation bounds) is suggestive but remains informal.
