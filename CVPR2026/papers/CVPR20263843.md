## Problem

Existing LMM-based document recognition methods store all knowledge implicitly in network weights, making it impossible to disentangle which spatial or stylistic factors drive recognition, limiting robustness under unseen layouts and requiring costly fine-tuning for new document domains. No prior work introduces an explicit, non-parametric memory mechanism that can rapidly absorb and retrieve recurring structural patterns across a document corpus.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263843/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

For document recognition, the model takes a full-page RGB document image and directly outputs full-page Markdown (OCR-free, end-to-end), with the ViT-Det encoder producing spatial feature tokens at three resolutions (64×64, 32×32, 16×16 pixel patches) that serve as queries to the memory banks. For handwriting recognition, input text lines have extreme aspect ratios (>12:1) and variable lengths, which precludes standard square-based multiscale memory; the model instead uses an inter-memory self-attention mechanism to capture global style across the entire line before producing a transcription.

## Method

DREAM introduces an explicit multiscale prototype memory with three independent banks (M^(64), M^(32), M^(16)), each storing 2048 prototype vectors that summarize recurring layout structures and stylistic patterns. The key mathematical insight is that the GMM posterior (responsibility) over prototypes is exactly representable as a learnable cross-attention: regional features act as queries, prototype means as keys and values, and the softmax attention weights approximate the GMM responsibilities. During training, prototypes are consolidated via attention-weighted exponential moving average with an exponentially decaying rate (η_t = η_0·e^(−κt)), while an entropy-based sparsity loss (local entropy + global negative entropy) enforces that each token activates only a few prototypes and prevents attention collapse. Retrieved prototype representations are concatenated with encoder features, projected into the language embedding space, and fed to the decoder, making the module a plug-and-play component between any encoder and decoder.

## Results

On the Fox dataset, DREAM (0.6B params) achieves the best Edit Distance of 0.101 (CN) / 0.082 (EN), F1 of 0.964 / 0.939, BLEU of 0.767 / 0.909, and METEOR of 0.881 / 0.942, outperforming all baselines including models 50× larger (e.g., Nougat 250B, Qwen-VL-Plus >100B). On the self-built DreamDoc test set, DREAM improves Edit Distance from 0.254 to 0.248 and METEOR from 0.815 to 0.819 over the baseline. For handwriting recognition, DREAM achieves 93.36% AR on SCUT-HCCDoc and 78.82% AR on SCUT-EPT, surpassing the strongest prior methods by 0.58 and 0.34 percentage points respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DREAM (0.6B) outperforms all 12 compared baselines on the Fox dataset across every reported metric. Against the closest same-scale competitor Vary (7B), DREAM reduces English Edit Distance from 0.092 to 0.082 and improves METEOR from 0.926 to 0.942, while using roughly 12× fewer parameters. Compared to the much larger Qwen-VL-Plus (>100B), DREAM achieves lower Edit Distance (0.082 vs 0.096 EN) and higher F1 (0.939 vs 0.931 EN). On the DreamDoc test set, DREAM marginally improves over the GOT-based baseline (Edit Distance 0.248 vs 0.254; METEOR 0.819 vs 0.815), confirming gains on in-domain data. For handwriting recognition, DREAM beats the strongest prior method (Yao et al. 2024) by 0.58 pp on SCUT-HCCDoc (93.36% vs 92.78% AR) and by 0.34 pp on SCUT-EPT (78.82% vs 78.48% AR), also surpassing SegCTC (92.08%) and Zhu et al. (75.97%) by wider margins.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GOT encoder-decoder framework for document recognition and on a ResNet-Transformer architecture for handwriting recognition, inserting a novel explicit multiscale prototype memory module between encoder and decoder. Conceptually, it bridges two previously separate lines of work: (1) the GMM/probabilistic modeling tradition, where the paper proves that posterior responsibilities are exactly representable as a biased cross-attention, and (2) the external-memory literature (Neural Turing Machines, Differentiable Neural Computers, prototype memory networks for captioning and few-shot generation), from which it borrows the EMA-based write mechanism and Key-Value storage. The key departure from prior LMM-based document recognition (GOT, Vary, Nougat, InternVL, etc.) is the introduction of a non-parametric, corpus-level memory that explicitly stores and retrieves recurring layout and style patterns, rather than relying solely on parametric weights.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a real interpretability and adaptability gap in LMM-based document recognition: by making structural knowledge explicit in retrievable prototype slots, it offers a path toward rapid adaptation to new document styles without costly fine-tuning, and the attention weights provide a natural interpretability signal. However, the EMA-based consolidation is acknowledged to be slow to adapt to distribution shifts, and it remains unclear how the decoder internally leverages the concatenated prototype representations. The plug-and-play design and the bilingual DreamDoc dataset (4,800 training pages across seven document categories) are practical contributions, but the absolute performance gains over the 0.6B baseline are modest (e.g., edit distance 0.112→0.101 on Fox-CN), and the parameter-efficiency claim against much larger models rests on a single evaluation dataset. Future work on integrating short-term dynamic clustering with long-term prototype consolidation could substantially strengthen the approach.
