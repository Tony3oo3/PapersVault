## Problem

Existing IP protection methods for VLMs define authorized domains statically at training time, requiring costly retraining whenever deployment requirements shift to new clients or data sources. They also produce opaque, high-confidence outputs on unauthorized inputs without signaling whether the input is legitimate, undermining user trust. This paper addresses both gaps by enabling user-controlled, on-demand domain authorization and providing a legality-aware verification signal alongside task predictions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262823/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method jointly consumes an image sample and a credential token (a 'domain key' generated by a private encryption projector held by the model owner) as input. The output is a dual-path prediction: a task prediction vector over N+1 classes (N task classes plus one dedicated 'unauthorized' class) and a binary legality-aware signal that flags whether the input–credential pair is authorized. A mismatched or missing credential token causes the model to route the prediction to the unauthorized class and trigger a security alert, while a valid key unlocks the corresponding domain's task performance.

## Method

AoD-IP builds on a frozen CLIP backbone (visual and text encoders) with three lightweight trainable projectors: an image projector, a domain projector, and an encryption projector. The image and domain projectors generate domain-specific tokens, while the encryption projector produces a unique credential token for the authorized domain; all three are concatenated into a prompt fed through the frozen text encoder to produce textual features compared against visual features. An extended domain is synthesized via RandAugment-style perturbations of authorized data to simulate diverse unknown domains and enforce robust authorization boundaries without external data. Training uses a unified loss combining authorized-domain classification, a penalty against misclassifying authorized samples as unauthorized, losses pushing extended and unauthorized samples to the 'unauthorized' class, and a KL divergence term to separate authorized and extended domain features in the text-encoder space. At inference, the encryption projector is retained privately by the model owner, who issues credential tokens as 'domain keys' that users can select or switch to activate different authorized domains without any backbone retraining.

## Results

On the target-specified IP protection task, AoD-IP achieves mean W_{u-a} of 79.27%, 63.47%, and 58.49% on Office-31, Office-Home-65, and Mini-DomainNet respectively, with unauthorized-domain accuracy drops (Drop_u) of 88.25%, 74.57%, and 69.80% while incurring negligible authorized-domain degradation (Drop_a ≤ 0.26%). Under the more challenging authorization-application setting, AoD-IP reaches D_{u-a} of 69.39%, 58.64%, and 56.59% on the three benchmarks, with unauthorized accuracy suppressed to 7.17%, 3.82%, and 4.54% while maintaining authorized accuracy of 86.19%, 77.45%, and 77.40%. Legality discrimination accuracy (R_a, R_e, R_u) consistently exceeds 90% across all settings, reaching up to 100% on Office-31.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AoD-IP outperforms all six baselines on the primary W_{u-a} metric across all three benchmarks: on Office-31 it leads IP-CLIP (74.84) by 4.43 points, on Office-Home-65 it surpasses IP-CLIP (55.10) by 8.37 points, and on Mini-DomainNet it exceeds IP-CLIP (54.68) by 3.81 points. In terms of unauthorized-domain suppression (Drop_u), AoD-IP achieves the highest values (88.25%, 74.57%, 69.80%) while maintaining the lowest authorized-domain degradation (Drop_a ≤ 0.26%), whereas HNTL achieves comparable Drop_u on Mini-DomainNet (74.38%) but at the cost of a severe 16.70% Drop_a, making it impractical. Under the authorization-application setting (Table 7), AoD-IP again leads on D_{u-a} (69.39%, 58.64%, 56.59%) and achieves the lowest unauthorized accuracy (A_u^{ip} = 7.17%, 3.82%, 4.54%), while HNTL's authorized-domain accuracy collapses to near-random levels (e.g., 7.00% on Mini-DomainNet Pa† and Sk†). IP-CLIP is the closest competitor on Drop_a (0.00% on Office-31) but consistently trails AoD-IP on both W_{u-a} and Drop_u, indicating weaker overall protection. Statistical significance (p < 0.05) is reported for AoD-IP's advantages over all baselines on the mean rows.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the applicability-authorization line of model IP protection (NTL, CUTI-Domain, CUPI-Domain, HNTL, SOPHON, IP-CLIP) by replacing the static, training-time-defined authorized domain with a dynamic, user-controlled authorization mechanism. It repurposes CLIP's prompt-learning paradigm (analogous to CoOp/CoCoOp) as the vehicle for IP protection, introducing a lightweight encryption projector that generates credential tokens acting as 'domain keys.' The dual-path inference (joint task prediction and legality signal) departs from prior methods that output only a single classification, adding an explicit authorization verification channel. Conceptually, the work bridges parameter-efficient tuning and model security, treating the prompt space as the locus of both capability and access control.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The authorize-on-demand formulation addresses a genuine deployment bottleneck: existing IP protection methods require costly retraining whenever the authorized domain changes, which is impractical in multi-client or evolving application settings. The legality-aware output also improves interpretability, letting users distinguish valid predictions from unauthorized inferences rather than receiving opaque high-confidence outputs. However, the evaluation is limited to image classification on CLIP with extended domains generated via style perturbations rather than truly novel data sources, and the security of the credential-token mechanism against adversarial extraction or replay is not rigorously tested. Open questions include scalability to generative tasks (VQA, image synthesis), robustness under distribution shifts beyond perturbation-based simulation, and whether the lightweight projectors can be reverse-engineered to forge valid credentials.
