## Problem

3D human motion generation is constrained by an unsatisfying tradeoff: small, high-fidelity motion capture datasets (e.g., HumanML3D, AMASS) are too limited to challenge modern generative models, while large-scale in-the-wild video collections (e.g., MotionMillion) are dominated by static, noisy, or artifact-prone sequences with minimal curation. Moreover, existing datasets lack structured semantic organization, making it impossible to perform fine-grained, per-category evaluation of generative models.

## Input / Output

The pipeline ingests raw web video clips (sourced from online platforms and public datasets like Kinetics, OpenHumanVid, HOIgen-1M, VideoUFO) and produces 820K 3D motion sequences in SMPL format (global translation, root orientation, 24 joint angles) resampled to 30 FPS, with each sequence capped between 30 and 600 frames (mean 165, median 114). Each sequence is paired with five natural-language captions and three-level taxonomy labels (category, subcategory, atomic action) drawn from a hierarchy of 54 categories, 2,065 subcategories, and 28,874 atomic actions. The overall pipeline distills 125K hours of raw footage (~14 years) down to approximately 1,238 hours of curated motion.

## Method

The core contribution is a taxonomy-aware, multi-stage filtering pipeline that uses a novel three-level semantic taxonomy (Category → Subcategory → Atomic-action) to guide both data collection and quality control. Video clips are processed through metadata filtering, scene detection, single-human detection (YOLOv8 + ViT-Pose), and semantic temporal segmentation via Qwen3-VL, which aligns segment boundaries to meaningful atomic actions rather than fixed-length slicing. 3D motion is estimated with GVHMR in SMPL format, and a proposed Dynamic Score—combining a temporal component (mean joint velocity magnitude) and a spatial component (per-joint trajectory range) with weights (0.7, 0.3)—serves as the final quality gate. Crucially, the filtering threshold is adaptive per category (e.g., lower for 'fishing', higher for 'acrobatics'), selecting the top-P percentile within each category to retain subtle but meaningful motions while removing static artifacts. This taxonomy-guided curation yields a dataset with 41% higher mean dynamic score than MotionMillion and 61.7% greater subcategory coverage, enabling the first per-category evaluation of motion generation models (MDM and MMGPT) that exposes blind spots invisible to global metrics.

## Results

On RoMo, the diffusion-based MDM model achieves superior Diversity (27.67 vs. 16.68) and physical plausibility (Foot Skating 1.70×10⁻³ vs. 92.0×10⁻³; Ground Penetration 0.0 vs. 3.55×10⁻⁵), while the autoregressive MMGPT model achieves better FID (12.80 vs. 20.63), Matching Score (22.08 vs. 12.06), and Dynamic Score (0.3268 vs. 0.2138). At the dataset level, RoMo achieves a mean Dynamic Score of 0.336, which is 41.4% higher than MotionMillion's 0.222, and covers 2,065 subcategories versus MotionMillion's 1,277 (a 61.7% increase). Per-category evaluation reveals that MDM's performance varies dramatically across taxonomy categories, exposing blind spots invisible under aggregated metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper trains two SOTA architectures on RoMo: MDM (a 50-step diffusion model) and MMGPT (a 3B-parameter autoregressive GPT with VAE tokenizer). MDM clearly wins on physical plausibility—its Foot Skating is 54× lower (1.70 vs. 92.0 ×10⁻³) and it has zero ground penetration—while MMGPT dominates on semantic alignment (Matching Score 22.08 vs. 12.06) and distributional fidelity (FID 12.80 vs. 20.63). The authors attribute MDM's physical advantage to its holistic, non-autoregressive denoising that avoids error accumulation, and MMGPT's semantic advantage to its token-based sequential modeling of text-to-motion mapping. At the dataset level, RoMo outperforms MotionMillion on every quality axis: 41.4% higher mean Dynamic Score (0.336 vs. 0.222), 61.7% more subcategory coverage (2,065 vs. 1,277), and a t-SNE analysis showing tighter, more semantically structured clusters. The per-category evaluation (Fig. 9) further demonstrates that aggregated metrics mask large performance gaps—MDM excels on common actions but degrades significantly on fine-grained categories with subtle interactions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of large-scale video-based motion extraction (MotionMillion, Motion-X) and the structured curation philosophy of mocap collections (AMASS, HumanML3D, BABEL). Rather than simply scaling up pose estimation from web video, it introduces a taxonomy-aware adaptive filtering pipeline and a three-level semantic taxonomy (54 categories, 2,065 subcategories, 28,874 atomic actions) that no prior motion dataset has provided. The pipeline repurposes existing components—GVHMR for 3D pose, Qwen3-VL for semantic segmentation and captioning, YOLOv8/ViT-Pose for detection—into a coherent curation system, while the Dynamic Score metric and per-category percentile filtering represent genuinely new quality-control mechanisms. The Motion Toolbox further standardizes evaluation metrics that prior works defined inconsistently.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RoMo is likely to become the de facto large-scale benchmark for text-to-motion generation, resolving the long-standing tradeoff between small clean mocap data and large noisy video collections. The per-category evaluation methodology it introduces exposes blind spots in SOTA models (e.g., MDM struggling on fine-grained interaction categories) that global FID/Diversity metrics mask, shifting the field toward more transparent, interpretable evaluation. Open questions include whether the taxonomy is sufficiently complete for long-tail activities, whether the aggressive 99% filtering discards useful subtle motions, and whether models trained on this data truly generalize beyond the 54 categories or merely memorize category-specific patterns. The 41% dynamic score improvement over MotionMillion and the 61.7% increase in subcategory coverage suggest prior 'million-scale' datasets were substantially overcounting usable content.
