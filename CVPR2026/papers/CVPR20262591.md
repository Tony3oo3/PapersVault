## Problem

Clinical brain tumor MRI scans frequently lack one or more modalities (e.g., T2 is often unavailable due to artifacts), yet existing segmentation methods must trade off among fine-grained structure capture, cross-modal complementarity modeling, and effective exploitation of available modalities. No prior architecture achieves all three goals simultaneously: CNN-based multi-encoder designs (HeMIS paradigm) are limited by local receptive fields, while pure ViT approaches lack the pixel-level precision needed for medical segmentation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 4-modal 3D MRI volume (FLAIR, T1ce, T1, T2) cropped to 96×96×96 voxels, where any subset of modalities may be absent (15 non-empty combinations are evaluated). Missing modalities are indicated by zero-filled channels; the network must produce a 3-class segmentation mask (whole tumor, tumor core, enhancing tumor) without requiring the missing data. During Stage 2 inference, the Uni-Encoder path randomly masks modalities to match the training distribution, while the CNN Multi-Encoder path simply omits features for unavailable modalities before fusion.

## Method

UniME decouples representation learning from segmentation via a two-stage heterogeneous design. Stage 1 pretrains a single ViT Uni-Encoder (16 layers, d=864, 12 heads) with masked self-supervised learning that combines modality-level (p=0.5) and patch-level (q=75%) random masking, using register tokens, 3D RoPE plus learnable positional embeddings, SwiGLU FFNs with dual layer norm, and a lightweight 3-block transposed-convolution decoder for full-volume reconstruction loss (discarded after pretraining). Stage 2 adds four parallel modality-specific CNN encoders (U-Net style, 4 stages, channels 16–128) that extract high-resolution multi-scale features, which are fused with the Uni-Encoder's global semantic representation via ECA-based fusion blocks at three scales. A weight-shared decoder provides per-modality auxiliary supervision to stabilize gradient flow under random masking, and a layer-wise learning rate decay (ω=0.85) preserves pretrained semantics during fine-tuning.

## Results

On BraTS 2023, UniME achieves average DSC of 90.38% (WT), 84.51% (TC), and 75.59% (ET) across all missing-modality combinations, improving over the second-best method by 1.40%, 1.53%, and 2.36% respectively. On BraTS 2024, the corresponding averages are 88.02% (WT), 77.49% (TC), and 75.12% (ET), with margins of 1.84%, 1.96%, and 2.93% over the runner-up. The advantage is most pronounced for the enhancing tumor (ET) region and in settings with only one or two available modalities, where baselines degrade sharply.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UniME outperforms all nine baselines across every modality combination and every tumor subregion on both BraTS 2023 and BraTS 2024. The closest competitors are M²SegMamba (second-best on WT for BraTS 2023 at 88.98% vs. 90.38%) and M³AE (second-best on TC and ET for BraTS 2023 at 82.98% and 73.23% vs. 84.51% and 75.59%). The gap widens substantially for the enhancing tumor region, where UniME leads M³AE by 2.36% on BraTS 2023 and 2.93% on BraTS 2024, reflecting the difficulty of segmenting small, irregular ET boundaries under missing modalities. The original HeMIS baseline, which uses four parallel CNN encoders with a shared decoder, collapses to DSC below 40% for TC and ET when only one modality is available, whereas UniME maintains DSC above 61% for ET in the same setting. Among the more recent Transformer- and Mamba-based methods (mm-Former, M²FTrans, LS3M, IM-Fuse), UniME consistently leads by 1–4% in average DSC, with the largest margins appearing in the most degraded (fewest-modality) scenarios.

## Relation to Prior Work

UniME sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: the HeMIS-style multi-encoder paradigm for multimodal brain tumor segmentation (Havaei et al., 2016) and the masked self-supervised pretraining approach introduced for this task by M3AE (Liu et al., 2023). Its key departure is the explicit two-stage heterogeneous design that decouples representation learning (a single ViT Uni-Encoder pretrained with modality- and patch-level masking) from segmentation (parallel modality-specific CNN encoders fused with the pretrained semantic features). Unlike M3AE, which uses a symmetric U-Net for both pretraining and fine-tuning, UniME discards the auxiliary decoder after pretraining and introduces CNN Multi-Encoders in Stage 2 to recover fine-grained spatial detail that a pure ViT cannot provide. The Uni-Encoder itself draws on recent ViT advances (EVA-02's dual-norm SwiGLU, 3D RoPE, register tokens) adapted to the 3D medical imaging setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The two-stage heterogeneous principle—learn a robust unified representation first, then add task-specific fine-grained extractors—offers a reusable design pattern for other incomplete-modality medical segmentation tasks beyond brain tumors. However, the performance gains over strong baselines (M²SegMamba, IM-Fuse) are modest (1.4–2.9% DSC), and the method is validated only on BraTS 2023/2024 with a single GPU, leaving open whether the approach generalizes to other organ/tumor types or to more extreme missing-modality regimes. The ablation showing that a moderate-scale Uni-Encoder outperforms a larger one under limited data is a useful practical finding but does not challenge the broader assumption that scale helps in representation learning. A key open question is whether the specific masking schedule (p_m=0.5, q_m=75%) and the heterogeneous fusion strategy transfer to other 3D medical imaging domains with different modality structures.
