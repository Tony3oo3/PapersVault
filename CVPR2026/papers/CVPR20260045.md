## Problem

High-fidelity 3D head reconstruction faces a fundamental three-way trade-off: photogrammetry achieves exceptional detail but demands 25–200+ synchronized views and expensive computation, while foundation models and optimization-based methods each sacrifice either geometric accuracy or capture simplicity. No existing approach simultaneously delivers photogrammetry-level surface detail, sparse-view capture (fewer than 10 cameras), and computationally efficient reconstruction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260045/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes sparse multi-view RGB images (as few as 3, typically 10) along with their camera extrinsics and intrinsics from a synchronized, controlled-lighting capture setup. It first produces geometrically consistent per-pixel surface normal maps in a single feed-forward pass (~1.5 s for 10 views), then refines these into a complete 3D head mesh aligned to a canonical coordinate system, recovering fine features such as wrinkles and skin folds in roughly 30 seconds total.

## Method

Skullptor is a two-stage pipeline. Stage 1 extends the monocular DAViD foundation model (a Dense Prediction Transformer) with view-aware cross-attention layers inserted between each transformer block and its fully connected layer, so that each view's query attends to concatenated keys and values from all input views; camera poses are encoded as 7-dimensional vectors (unit quaternion + translation) and added as positional embeddings to distinguish viewpoints. The model is fine-tuned on Triplegangers scans rendered from randomly sampled virtual cameras with a cosine-similarity normal loss. Stage 2 initializes a unit-sphere mesh and optimizes vertex positions via differentiable inverse rendering to match the predicted normals, using a per-pixel weighting scheme that prioritizes frontal-facing regions, Laplacian smoothness regularization, and continuous remeshing (edge splits, collapses, flips) at every iteration to prevent mesh degeneracies while adapting resolution to local geometric complexity.

## Results

On NPHM, Skullptor with 10 views achieves 2.36 mm depth error, 6.12° mean angular error, and 0.117 normal gradient error in 0.50 min, matching Meshroom's 2.54 mm depth error (23 views, 9.5 min) while using less than half the cameras and running ~19× faster. On Multiface, Skullptor (10 views) achieves 2.99 mm depth error and 6.46° angular error in 0.48 min, substantially outperforming 2DGS (5.73 mm, 10.7°) and SuGaR (5.54 mm, 11.5°) though trailing Meshroom (0.467 mm, 5.43°) due to a noted photogrammetry-correlated bias in the Multiface ground truth. For normal estimation, Skullptor achieves the lowest normal gradient error (0.166 on NPHM, 0.234 on Multiface) among all methods, indicating superior preservation of high-frequency geometric detail, while maintaining inference speed (1.5 s) comparable to DAViD (1.1 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Meshroom (photogrammetry), Skullptor with 10 views achieves comparable depth error on NPHM (2.36 vs. 2.54 mm) while using fewer than half the cameras (10 vs. 23) and running ~19× faster (0.50 vs. 9.5 min); on Multiface, Meshroom's 0.467 mm depth error appears inflated by a correlated photogrammetry bias in the ground truth, as the paper notes a 'broken nose' artifact penalizes Skullptor's correct reconstruction. Against 2DGS, Skullptor (10 views) wins decisively on all metrics on both datasets: depth error improves from 6.37→2.36 mm (NPHM) and 5.73→2.99 mm (Multiface), angular error from 6.64°→6.12° and 10.7°→6.46°, and runtime drops from 47–50 min to 0.48–0.50 min. Against SuGaR, the margin is even larger: depth error improves from 3.23→2.36 mm (NPHM) and 5.54→2.99 mm (Multiface), angular error from 9.03°→6.12° and 11.5°→6.46°, and normal gradient error from 0.232→0.117 and 0.324→0.157. For normal estimation, Skullptor outperforms DAViD on normal gradient error (0.166 vs. 0.190 on NPHM; 0.234 vs. 0.250 on Multiface) with only 0.4 s additional inference time, and substantially outperforms Sapiens 2B on gradient error (0.166 vs. 0.185 on NPHM) while being ~29× faster (1.5 s vs. 43.7 s).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate paradigms in 3D head reconstruction: data-driven foundation models (specifically DAViD, a monocular normal estimator built on Dense Prediction Transformers) and optimization-based inverse rendering with continuous remeshing. The key architectural contribution is extending DAViD with view-aware cross-attention layers that enforce geometric consistency across sparse viewpoints, transforming a monocular predictor into a multi-view one. The predicted normals then serve as strong geometric priors within a differentiable rendering optimization loop, a strategy that departs from prior optimization-based methods (2DGS, SuGaR) that rely solely on photometric consistency without learned shape priors, and from foundation models that lack explicit multi-view constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for production VFX and gaming pipelines: achieving photogrammetry-level fidelity from fewer than 10 cameras in ~30 seconds (vs. 25-200+ views and minutes for Meshroom) dramatically reduces capture cost and complexity. The finding that learned geometric priors can compensate for extreme view sparsity (3 cameras for frontal reconstruction) opens the door to practical sparse-view capture setups where traditional photogrammetry fails entirely. Key limitations remain: the method requires controlled lighting and synchronized cameras, does not capture appearance (albedo, materials), and has not been validated on uncontrolled in-the-wild imagery. Future work on joint normal-albedo prediction and relighting would extend the framework toward complete appearance capture.
