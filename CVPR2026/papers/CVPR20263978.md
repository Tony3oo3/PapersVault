## Problem

When CLIP is fine-tuned with BCE loss for multi-label image recognition, the text embedding space undergoes systematic structural distortion, causing the model to become under-confident on base classes (especially head) and over-confident on novel classes. Existing calibration techniques such as temperature scaling, DAC, and DOR fail in multi-label settings because they cannot capture inter-class semantic dependencies or correct the global structural shifts introduced during fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a set of class text prompts (e.g., 'a photo of a class {c}') as input, processed through CLIP's frozen image encoder and learnable text prompts. It produces per-class confidence scores via sigmoid activation over similarity logits, which are thresholded or top-k selected for multi-label prediction. Unusually, the regularization operates at the batch level: a C×C covariance matrix is computed over the confidence vectors of all classes within each mini-batch, leveraging the fact that jointly inactive (y=0) class pairs vastly outnumber positive pairs in multi-label data.

## Method

Class-wise Covariance Regularization (CCR) constrains the predicted inter-class confidence covariance to match the semantic correlation structure encoded in zero-shot CLIP text embeddings. The key insight is to estimate inter-class dependencies from jointly inactive class pairs (y=0 for both classes), which provide a dense, stable statistical signal despite the sparsity of positive labels in multi-label datasets. Specifically, a batch-level covariance matrix C_pred is computed over class confidences, normalized into a correlation matrix C̃_pred to remove magnitude bias, and then aligned with the text-derived semantic correlation matrix Σ_text via a Frobenius-norm loss. This regularization is added to the standard BCE loss with a balancing coefficient λ, preserving the geometric consistency of the class space throughout fine-tuning and preventing the structural drift that causes systematic miscalibration.

## Results

On the base-to-new generalization setting (16-shot, 5 splits, averaged over 6 datasets), CCR reduces the harmonic-mean ECE from 13.25% to 7.35% for CoOp, from 6.02% to 4.76% for TaI-DPT, and from 4.09% to 3.62% for T2I-PAL. CCR simultaneously improves calibration on both base and new classes (e.g., CoOp new-class ECE drops from 21.58% to 11.03%) and boosts accuracy on unseen classes by 2.7–4.8% (e.g., T2I-PAL new-class accuracy rises from 81.86% to 83.97%). On domain generalization, CCR lowers CoOp's source-domain ECE from 5.10% to 3.92% while improving source accuracy from 69.44% to 71.47%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CCR is compared against two existing calibration methods—Distance-Aware Calibration (DAC) and Dynamic Outlier Regularization (DOR)—applied on top of seven prompt-tuning backbones (CoOp, CoCoOp, KgCoOp, DualCoOp, TaI-DPT, TaI++, T2I-PAL). CCR achieves the lowest ECE across all seven backbones and all four calibration metrics (ECE, ACE, MCE, PIECE), whereas DAC and DOR each fail on at least one backbone (e.g., DAC worsens KgCoOp's ACE from 4.60 to 4.43, and DOR worsens CoOp's tail ECE from 8.37 to 6.92 while leaving head ECE at 7.45). The largest absolute gain is on CoOp, where CCR cuts HM-ECE by 5.90 points (13.25→7.35) versus DAC's 2.40-point reduction (13.25→10.85) and DOR's 3.33-point reduction (13.25→9.92). Unlike DAC and DOR, which create head-vs-tail trade-offs within base classes, CCR simultaneously reduces ECE on head, medium, and tail classes (Table 3). On accuracy (Table 4), CCR improves new-class accuracy for every backbone (e.g., +2.67% for CoOp, +2.11% for T2I-PAL) while also improving head-class accuracy, whereas DOR typically sacrifices base-class performance to gain on new classes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of CLIP-based prompt tuning for multi-label recognition (CoOp, TaI-DPT, T2I-PAL) and post-hoc calibration of vision-language models (DAC, DOR). It departs from the prevailing assumption in the multi-label CLIP literature that Ranking loss is inherently superior to BCE, instead diagnosing BCE's failure as a structural distortion of the text embedding covariance that can be corrected with a lightweight regularizer. The Class-wise Covariance Regularization (CCR) repurposes the statistical idea of aligning second-order moment structures with a semantic prior, but applies it to the 'inactive' (negative) region of the label space—a reframing that leverages the abundance of negative evidence in multi-label settings to obtain a dense, stable signal where co-occurrence-based estimation would be unreliable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CCR provides a drop-in structural regularizer that simultaneously improves calibration across head/tail and base/new classes without the trade-offs that plague existing methods like DAC and DOR, which is practically valuable for open-vocabulary multi-label deployment where miscalibrated confidences directly harm downstream decision-making. The paper's empirical diagnosis—that BCE fine-tuning systematically cools base-class confidence while heating novel-class confidence, and that ranking loss preserves semantic neighborhood topology while BCE destroys it—offers a mechanistic explanation that goes beyond the commonly cited 'modality gap' narrative. Limitations include dependence on the quality of CLIP text embeddings as a semantic prior and the restriction to pairwise linear correlations, leaving open whether richer dependency structures (e.g., higher-order or nonlinear) would yield further gains, and whether CCR transfers to ranking-based or full fine-tuning paradigms.
