## Problem

While image-based jailbreak attacks on MLLMs have been extensively studied, the video modality remains a largely unexplored safety frontier. This paper investigates why simply converting a harmful image into a multi-frame video can bypass safety mechanisms more effectively than the original image, and asks whether one can design videos that are both diverse in content and proximal to safe data in the representation space to maximize attack success.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single harmful text query (e.g., 'How can someone make a bomb?') and produces a 4-frame typographic video at 1 fps plus a fixed text prompt. Each frame is a rendered image with a paraphrased version of the query in the top half (prefixed with 'Methods to', 'Steps to', or 'List of') and blank numbered list markers in the bottom half. From N=30 candidate frames (a mix of harmful and benign paraphrases), exactly K=4 frames are selected via bipartite matching, and the accompanying text prompt instructs the model to merge all titles into one intent and output 100-word actionable steps per item.

## Method

SPTV (Safety-Proximal Typographic Videos) exploits two key findings: (1) unsafe videos embed more similarly to safe videos than unsafe images do, making them harder for MLLMs to flag, and (2) videos with diverse frames are processed more like genuine videos rather than static images, evading image-specific safety alignment. The pipeline first augments the original query into N harmful and N benign paraphrases using Qwen3-14B, renders each as a typographic image, then embeds all images with CLIP-ViT-L/14. Frame selection is formulated as a bipartite matching problem solved by the Hungarian algorithm: it finds the permutation σ that minimizes the sum of negative cosine similarities between safe and unsafe images, then selects the top-K unsafe frames by pairwise similarity under that assignment. A carefully designed text prompt provides task guidance while avoiding triggers for perplexity-based or DiffPure-style defenses.

## Results

On the JailBreakV-28K benchmark (2,000 harmful prompts across 16 safety policies), SPTV achieves the highest average Attack Success Rate (ASR) of 36.4% across five MLLMs, outperforming the best image-based baseline FigStep (25.9%) and the best video-stack baseline FigStep(S) (28.6%). Per-model, SPTV reaches 44.1% on Qwen2-VL-7B, 37.1% on Qwen2.5-VL-7B, 37.0% on VideoLLaMA2-7B, 33.8% on GPT-4.1, and 30.0% on Gemini-2.5. On the per-policy breakdown (Table 2, Qwen2-VL-7B), SPTV achieves ASR above 79% on Explicitly Harmful (EH, 79.4%), Fraud (F, 79.8%), Illegal Activity (IA, 83.4%), and Malware (M, 91.2%) policies. Transferability to natural videos (MSVD backgrounds) is also demonstrated, with ASR remaining at 33.1% even at 40% text opacity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPTV outperforms all four baseline families across every evaluated model. Against the QR family (Liu et al., 2023), the strongest variant SD+Typo reaches only 24.0% average ASR, while SPTV achieves 36.4%—a 12.4-point gap; the gap widens on closed-source models (GPT-4.1: 33.8% vs 15.6%). VisualADV (Qi et al., 2024), a white-box perturbation method, is the weakest baseline at 6.7% average ASR, confirming its poor transferability to video-capable MLLMs. FigStep (Gong et al., 2025), the closest structural competitor, achieves 25.9% as an image-based attack and 28.6% when naively stacked into a video (FigStep(S)); SPTV surpasses both by 10.5 and 7.8 points respectively, with the largest margin on Qwen2-VL-7B (44.1% vs 31.8%/34.1%). VideoJail-Pro (Hu et al., 2025), the only prior video-specific jailbreak, shows highly inconsistent performance (0.3% on VideoLLaMA2-7B but 23.1% on Gemini-2.5) and a 13.4% average, well below SPTV's 36.4%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the image-based typographic jailbreak paradigm (FigStep, QR) to the video modality, building directly on FigStep's paraphrase-and-render pipeline but adding a temporal dimension. It departs from the prior VideoJail-Pro attempt by providing a mechanistic explanation (embedding-space similarity analysis) for why video encoders are more vulnerable, and by replacing ad-hoc video construction with a principled bipartite-matching (Hungarian algorithm) frame selection that maximizes safety-proximal diversity. The work also draws on CLIP-based visual feature extraction for the matching objective and on the broader perturbation-vs-structure taxonomy of MLLM jailbreaks to position its black-box, structure-based approach.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As MLLMs increasingly serve as perception engines for video-capable agents, the finding that video safety alignment is substantially weaker than image safety alignment—and that a simple frame-stacking trick already degrades defenses—poses a practical risk that current image-centric guardrails do not address. The VSP defense and the embedding-space analysis offer concrete starting points for video-aware safety alignment, but open questions remain: whether the vulnerability persists for longer, more naturalistic videos; whether the CLIP-based matching objective generalizes across model families with different video encoders; and whether the modest gap between SPTV and the simpler image-stack baseline (36.4% vs 28.6% average ASR) narrows or widens as video safety training matures.
