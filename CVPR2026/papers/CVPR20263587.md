## Problem

Intrinsic image decomposition into albedo and shading is fundamentally ill-posed, and collecting ground-truth reflectance and shading for real-world scenes requires specialized equipment and controlled procedures. Existing learning-based methods rely on statistical priors from synthetic or limited indoor datasets, suffering from sim-to-real gaps, while physics-based approaches demand transient thermal video, active lighting control, or radiometric calibration. This paper asks whether a single steady-state thermal image can provide dense, physics-based self-supervision for decomposition without any learned priors.

## Input / Output

The method takes a co-registered pair of a visible HDR image (merged from 20 exposure-bracketed frames, 600×800) and a long-wave infrared thermal image (512×640, 8–14 μm band) of the same static scene. It outputs a 3-channel albedo map bounded to [0,1]³ and a single-channel non-negative shading map. The thermal image is consumed exclusively through ordinality-based losses (edge sign comparisons and point-pair intensity rankings) applied to the grayscale mean albedo, while the reconstruction loss operates on the full 3-channel visible image.

## Method

The key insight is that unreflected light is absorbed and re-emitted as heat, so visible-thermal intensity ordinalities between any two scene points directly encode whether the difference is albedo-driven (opposite signs) or shading-driven (same signs). The paper formalizes this via local edge constraints (gradient sign agreement/disagreement classifies edges as albedo- or shading-dominant) and non-local point-pair constraints (Poisson-disk-sampled pairs yield four ordinality classes enforced by hinge losses). These ordinality losses, combined with a standard visible-image reconstruction loss, optimize a Double-DIP architecture—two randomly initialized convolutional encoder-decoder networks with skip connections that parameterize albedo and shading respectively. No auxiliary training data or pre-trained weights are used; the network's architectural prior provides implicit regularization, and only the network weights are optimized while the random noise inputs remain frozen.

## Results

On the Color Chart under Different Illuminations benchmark, VT-Intrinsic achieves si-MSE of 0.37 (White LED), 1.06 (Incandescent), and 1.19 (Sunlight) for albedo, substantially outperforming all learning-based baselines (best competitor: CRefNet at 1.98–2.29) and physics-based baselines. On the JoLHT-Video dataset, the method achieves si-MSE of 11 (Painted Mask albedo), 0.10 (Painted Mask shading), and 2.7 (Color Charts albedo), approaching JoLHT-Video (8.4, 0.05, 2.0) which requires transient thermal video and controlled lighting. Ordinality validation on 1,063 expert-labeled point pairs across 100 real-world scenes yields 98.95% overall accuracy (96.96% albedo, 99.62% shading).

## Baselines & Comparisons

VT-Intrinsic decisively outperforms all learning-based baselines (IntrinsicDiffusion, RGB↔X, Intrinsic-v1/v2, CRefNet) on every Color Chart illumination condition, with si-MSE improvements of 2–4× over the best learning-based method (e.g., 0.37 vs. 1.25 on White LED albedo). Against the physics-based JoLHT-Video, which requires calibrated transient thermal video and controlled visible-only lighting, VT-Intrinsic achieves comparable performance (11 vs. 8.4 on Painted Mask albedo; 0.10 vs. 0.05 on shading) using only a single steady-state thermal image. NIR-Priors is inapplicable under White LED (no NIR emission) and unavailable on the Painted Mask scene, limiting its generality. Optimization-based baselines (RGB-Retinex, Opt-LocalSmooth) are consistently the weakest, with si-MSE values 2–4× higher than the proposed method. The method's advantage is most pronounced under incandescent and sunlight conditions where invisible spectral components are present, as JoLHT-Video and NIR-Priors cannot handle these cases at all.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from JoLHT-Video [34], which required transient thermal video, active lighting control, and radiometric calibration to estimate absolute absorbed light, by showing that a single steady-state thermal image suffices to extract ordinal (relative) constraints on albedo and shading. It also contrasts with learning-based IID methods (Intrinsic-v1/v2, IntrinsicDiffusion, RGB↔X, CRefNet) that rely on statistical priors from large synthetic or pseudo-labeled datasets, and with auxiliary-sensor approaches using NIR [8] or LiDAR [36] that face spectral-mismatch issues. The method parameterizes the solution with a Double-DIP network [16] but uses the thermal ordinality losses as the primary supervision signal, effectively replacing learned priors with physics-derived self-supervision.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ordinality framework provides a principled, hardware-accessible path to dense albedo/shading supervision for real-world scenes without synthetic data or human annotation, and the 600-pair VT-Intrinsic dataset can directly train learning-based IID models. However, the approach is constrained by the Lambertian assumption, the need for a co-located thermal camera, and sensitivity to non-light-absorption heat sources (engines, humans) and specular/transparent materials. Open questions include whether the ordinality principle extends to non-Lambertian BRDFs, dynamic scenes, or multi-illumination settings, and whether cheaper thermal sensors (e.g., smartphone-integrated) can eventually make this pipeline practical at scale.
