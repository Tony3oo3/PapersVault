## Problem

Convex decomposition of nonconvex 3D shapes is NP-hard, and classical search-based methods (V-HACD, CoACD) are computationally expensive. Prior learning-based approaches (BSP-Net, Cvx-Net) rely on fixed half-plane assemblies that limit them to narrow object categories and fail to generalize to open-world content. Additionally, most prior work assumes clean mesh inputs, while modern geometry increasingly arrives as imprecise representations like Gaussian splats.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a point cloud of 100,000 uniformly sampled surface points normalized to [-1,1]³ and outputs a 448-dimensional feature field encoded as a 512×512×128 triplane. At inference, the feature field is partitioned via recursive binary clustering (agglomerative for meshes, k-means for point clouds) until a user-specified concavity threshold is met, yielding a set of convex hulls whose union approximates the input. Because the network operates on point clouds, it generalizes at inference to meshes, CAD models, and 3D Gaussian splats without requiring watertight geometry.

## Method

The core insight is to reformulate convex decomposition as a self-supervised contrastive feature learning problem: instead of directly optimizing discrete primitives, the network learns a continuous feature field where feature proximity encodes which surface points should share a convex body. The loss is derived from the geometric definition of convexity—pairs of points whose connecting segment lies inside the shape (convex pairs) are pulled together, while nonconvex pairs are pushed apart—implemented as a log-normalized contrastive objective with hard negatives sampled inversely proportional to squared Euclidean distance. The architecture uses a PVCNN encoder to extract per-point features, projects them onto three axis-aligned triplanes via mean reduction, processes them through a 2D CNN and six-layer transformer, then upsamples to produce the final triplane feature field. At inference, a recursive divide-and-conquer algorithm repeatedly binary-clusters the most concave component until all parts meet the target concavity threshold, enabling multi-granularity decompositions from a single feature field.

## Results

On the V-HACD dataset, the method achieves the lowest concavity (0.0973) and reconstruction error (0.0180) with the fewest components (11.90), outperforming CoACD (0.1095 concavity) and V-HACD (0.1176 concavity). On PartObjaverse-Tiny, it reaches 0.1257 concavity and 0.0254 reconstruction error with 21.18 components, beating all baselines including Cvx-Net(Objaverse) at 0.2970 concavity. On ShapeNet, the method achieves 0.0656 concavity and 0.0142 reconstruction error with only 12.63 components, substantially outperforming Cvx-Net(ShapeNet) (0.1795 concavity) and BSP-Net(ShapeNet) (0.2107 concavity).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms all four baselines on every dataset and every metric (concavity and reconstruction error). Against the classical methods, it beats CoACD by 11% in concavity on V-HACD (0.0973 vs. 0.1095) and by 9% on PartObjaverse-Tiny (0.1257 vs. 0.1388), while also using fewer components; V-HACD is beaten by 17% in concavity on V-HACD (0.0973 vs. 0.1176). Against the learning-based baselines, the gap is much larger: Cvx-Net(ShapeNet) is beaten by 79% in concavity on V-HACD (0.0973 vs. 0.4673) and by 69% on PartObjaverse-Tiny (0.1257 vs. 0.5079), while BSP-Net(ShapeNet) is beaten by 57% on V-HACD (0.0973 vs. 0.2249). Even the Objaverse-trained variants of Cvx-Net and BSP-Net remain 3–4× worse in concavity, confirming that the reconstruction-based objectives of prior learned methods do not scale to open-world geometry. The paper also notes qualitatively that classical methods fail on inclined convex regions due to axis-aligned cut assumptions, while the learned baselines struggle to generalize beyond their training categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from both classical search-based convex decomposition (V-HACD, CoACD) and prior learning-based methods (BSP-Net, Cvx-Net) that directly optimize or assemble discrete primitives. Instead, it reformulates the problem as continuous feature learning on the shape surface, borrowing the triplane architecture from PartField [31] and the log-normalized contrastive loss structure from SimCLR [11], but introducing a novel self-supervised geometric loss derived directly from the definition of convexity (line segments between points must remain in the shape). The key conceptual shift is that clustering a learned feature field yields the decomposition, rather than explicitly searching over partitions or fitting half-planes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first feed-forward open-world model for convex decomposition, this work removes the per-shape optimization bottleneck and enables fast, generalizable inference across diverse 3D modalities (meshes, CAD, Gaussian splats), which is critical for the growing demand in robotics simulation and generative 3D pipelines. The 5× collision-detection speedup demonstrated in Newton is practically significant for real-time physical simulation. However, the model struggles with thin structures and was trained only on clean object-level geometry, leaving open questions about scene-scale robustness, noise tolerance, and whether semantically-aware convex proxies (adapted to expected object motions) can be learned within the same framework.
