## Problem

Single large multimodal models (LMMs) exhibit cognitive biases that lead to incorrect hateful meme classifications, and existing multi-agent ensemble strategies—voting-based and debate-based—fail to resolve inter-model bias because voting amplifies shared errors and debate relies on potentially biased judges. No prior framework formulates multi-agent cooperation as a game with an explicit consensus-driven objective that steers heterogeneous models toward a jointly optimal, bias-mitigated decision.

## Input / Output

The system takes a meme consisting of an RGB image and its overlaid text as input and produces a binary hateful/non-hateful label as output. Internally, three frozen LMMs (LLaVA-1.5-13B, Qwen2-VL-7B, Gemma3-4B) and a CLIP-based learnable agent each generate a 768-dimensional representation projected into a shared decision space, from which a temperature-scaled softmax policy yields a binary action. The final prediction is the argmax over the master agent's policy, which concatenates all four agent representations before its own policy head.

## Method

GECO models hateful meme classification as a cooperative normal-form game among five agents (three frozen LMMs, one CLIP-based learnable agent, and one master agent) and trains them via a Regularized Nash Advantage (RNA) loss. The key mechanism is a mixed bonus scheme with three terms—individual hit (α), pairwise hit (λ), and all-hit (β)—that jointly reward each agent for being correct and for agreeing with other correct agents, explicitly promoting consensus on the true label rather than merely individual accuracy. Efficient policy learning restricts sampling to correct actions and uses single-step updates with a stop-gradient centered advantage vector to reduce variance, while a symmetric KL regularizer toward an EMA reference policy and a penalty coefficient η ensure training stability and convergence to a Nash equilibrium.

## Results

GECO achieves state-of-the-art performance across all five benchmarks. On PrideMM, it reaches 82.84% accuracy, surpassing the previous best (RA-HMD) by +4.74%. On the low-resource MultiOff dataset (<500 samples), GECO attains 78.52% accuracy, exceeding RA-HMD by +7.41%. On HatefulMemes, MAMI, and HarMeme, GECO also leads with 84.35% Acc / 91.57 AUC, 81.50% Acc / 91.80 AUC, and 89.11% Acc / 93.95 AUC respectively, outperforming all prior methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GECO outperforms all 11 baselines on every dataset where comparisons are available. Against the strongest CLIP-based method RGCL [28], GECO gains +6.50% accuracy on PrideMM (82.84 vs 76.34) and +11.39% on MultiOff (78.52 vs 67.13). Among LMM-based methods, the previous SOTA RA-HMD [29] is surpassed by +4.74% on PrideMM, +2.25% on HatefulMemes, +1.60% on MAMI, +1.01% on HarMeme, and +7.41% on MultiOff. Debate-based ensembles ExplainHM [23] and M2KE [27] trail GECO by 8.75% and 8.59% on HatefulMemes accuracy respectively, confirming that fixed dialogue rules and biased judges limit their effectiveness. The largest absolute gap appears on the low-resource MultiOff dataset, where GECO's cooperative game-theoretic optimization yields a +7.41% accuracy improvement over RA-HMD, highlighting the advantage of multi-agent collaboration under limited supervision.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-agent ensemble paradigm for hateful meme detection (voting-based methods like Mod-Hate, debate-based methods like ExplainHM and M2KE) by recasting agent coordination as a cooperative normal-form game. It directly builds on the regularized Nash advantage learning framework of Meng et al. (ICML 2025) for approximating Nash equilibria in normal-form games, adapting the regularized advantage vector and stop-gradient loss to a multi-agent LMM setting. The key conceptual departure from prior ensemble work is the introduction of a mixed bonus scheme that explicitly rewards collective agreement on the correct label, moving beyond individual-utility maximization that standard game-theoretic formulations would prescribe.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and underexplored failure mode in multi-agent LLM systems: when a majority of agents share a common bias, both voting and debate mechanisms propagate rather than correct that bias. The game-theoretic framing with a consensus-driven reward offers a principled mechanism to break out of such equilibria, and the ablation showing that the master agent's removal causes the largest performance drop (82.84→62.88 on PrideMM) suggests the coordination structure itself is as important as individual model quality. Open questions include whether the approach scales beyond binary classification and five agents, whether the mixed bonus weights transfer across domains without re-tuning, and whether the framework's gains persist when the individual LMMs are of comparable (rather than heterogeneous) capability.
