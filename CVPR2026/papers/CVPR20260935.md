## Problem

Channel-wise smoothing PTQ methods like SmoothQuant compute a single scaling factor per channel, but in MLLMs the dominant modality (e.g., vision, with activations 10–100× larger) dictates that factor, catastrophically over-smoothing weaker modalities like audio or text—a failure the authors term smoothing misalignment. The natural fix of per-modality smoothing factors breaks computational invariance by requiring separate quantized weights per modality, defeating the memory savings quantization is meant to provide.

## Input / Output

The method consumes calibration data containing mixed-modality token sequences (text, image, and audio tokens) and produces a single set of low-precision quantized weights (using text-smoothed weights as the shared base) plus small low-rank compensation matrices (L1, L2 of rank r) for each non-text modality. Only the LLM decoder (Thinker) component of the MLLM is quantized; vision and audio encoders remain in full precision. At inference, text tokens use the base quantized weight directly, while other modalities additionally apply their low-rank correction, incurring only 2rd extra FLOPs and 2mrd extra memory per layer.

## Method

MASQuant combines two components. First, Modality-Aware Smoothing (MAS) learns a dedicated diagonal smoothing matrix S_m per modality by minimizing MAE reconstruction loss on modality-specific calibration data, replacing the closed-form β-parameterized formulas used by SmoothQuant/AWQ and eliminating cross-modality dominance at its source. Second, Cross-Modal Compensation (CMC) preserves a single quantized weight by exploiting the fact that, after SVD-based whitening of the non-text modality's smoothed activations, the residual weight difference ΔW = S_v W − Q(S_t W) exhibits strong low-rank structure; a truncated SVD of the whitened residual yields compact factors L1 = T⁻¹U_r and L2 = Σ_r V_rᵀ that approximate ΔW optimally in the weighted Frobenius sense (Theorem 2). The paper proves both the SQNR degradation under smoothing misalignment (Theorem 1) and the optimality of the whitened low-rank compensation, and demonstrates that text is the ideal base modality because it requires no compensation at inference, keeping the decoding path identical to standard quantized inference.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-VL-3B at W8A8, MASQuant achieves 70.9% average accuracy, matching FP16 (70.1%) and surpassing MBQ (70.1%) and SmoothQuant (69.9%). At the aggressive W4A8 setting, MASQuant reaches 64.7% average accuracy on 3B, outperforming MBQ (64.6%), SmoothQuant (57.4%), and dramatically exceeding RTN (5.1%). On the omni-modal Qwen2.5-Omni-3B at W4A8, MASQuant achieves 3.6 WER on LibriSpeech versus 77.4 for SmoothQuant and 9.5 for MBQ, while scoring 41.4 on OmniBench versus 27.3 (SQ) and 36.7 (MBQ). At W4A6 on 7B, MASQuant attains 63.0% average accuracy on VL benchmarks, edging out MBQ (62.9%) and SmoothQuant (61.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MASQuant consistently outperforms all three baselines across both vision-language and omni-modal settings. Against SmoothQuant, the advantage is most dramatic at aggressive bit widths: at W4A8 on Qwen2.5-Omni-3B, MASQuant achieves 3.6 WER on LibriSpeech versus 77.4 for SQ (a 21× gap), and 41.4 vs. 27.3 on OmniBench. Against MBQ, MASQuant wins on average accuracy at W4A8 (64.7 vs. 64.6 on VL-3B; 69.0 vs. 68.5 on VL-7B) and at W4A6 (63.0 vs. 62.9 on VL-7B), while also substantially outperforming on omni-modal audio (3.6 vs. 9.5 WER at W4A8 on Omni-3B). Against AWQ, MASQuant surpasses it at W4A16 on VL-3B (69.9 vs. 69.5 avg) and VL-7B (74.2 vs. 73.5 avg), and on Omni-3B OmniBench (46.9 vs. 43.8). At W8A8, MASQuant matches or slightly exceeds FP16 on VL-3B (70.9 vs. 70.1 avg), indicating near-lossless quantization when modality-specific smoothing is applied.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the channel-wise smoothing PTQ lineage (SmoothQuant, AWQ) to the multimodal setting, where prior work had only begun addressing modality-specific issues through hyperparameter tuning (MBQ adjusts β) or modality-specific quantization ranges (MQuant). The key conceptual departure is identifying 'smoothing misalignment' as a fundamental failure mode—where a single per-channel scaling factor dictated by the dominant modality catastrophically degrades weaker modalities—and resolving it by learning separate modality-specific smoothing factors. To maintain the single-weight constraint of computational invariance, the paper repurposes SVD-based whitening (previously used for weight compression in SVD-LLM v2) to show that cross-modal weight differences become low-rank after whitening, enabling lightweight low-rank compensation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of smoothing misalignment as a fundamental obstacle clarifies why naive transfer of LLM PTQ methods to MLLMs fails, particularly at aggressive bit widths where the dominant modality's influence becomes catastrophic (e.g., audio WER jumping 20× under SmoothQuant at W4A8). This has direct practical implications for deploying omni-modal models on edge devices. Open questions include whether the low-rank compensation overhead becomes prohibitive as the number of modalities grows, whether the approach extends to training-time quantization, and whether the text-as-base-modality choice generalizes when text is not the most frequent inference modality.
