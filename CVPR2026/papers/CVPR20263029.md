## Problem

Existing concept erasure evaluations for text-to-image diffusion models rely on explicit, single-word prompts and limited concept sets, making it impossible to determine whether a concept is truly removed from the model's semantic representations or merely hidden at the lexical surface level. No prior benchmark simultaneously covers diverse concept domains, challenging implicit prompts, visually similar non-target retention, computational efficiency, and demographic bias in a unified framework.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes domain-specific text prompts constructed via templates (e.g., 'an image of <c>' for objects, 'a photo in the style of <c>' for art styles) where <c> is substituted with the target concept name, prefixed variants, synonyms, or free-form descriptions generated by ChatGPT. For each prompt, 30 images are generated from the erasure-modified Stable Diffusion model and scored by domain-specific classifiers (ML-Decoder, GCD, NudeNet, YOLOv11, etc.). Output is a matrix of 13 scalar metrics—erasing/retaining ability scores, unlearning and inference times, compute budget, FID, and gender/ethnicity bias scores—averaged over all concept categories per domain.

## Method

EMMA is an evaluation benchmark rather than a new erasure algorithm; its core contribution is a structured taxonomy of 5 domains (objects, celebrities, art styles, NSFW, copyright) spanning 206 concept categories, assessed along 5 dimensions with 13 metrics. The key methodological innovation is the implicit erasing-ability axis, which uses synonym variants, concise descriptions, and long descriptive prompts to probe whether the concept's semantic representation survives beyond token-level removal. Retaining ability is extended beyond random non-target concepts to include the 5 most visually/semantically similar concepts identified via ChatGPT, exposing collateral damage to related concepts. Bias is quantified by computing SSIM and CLIP similarity between images from gender/ethnicity-neutral prompts and attribute-specific prompts, measuring deviation from the original model's demographic balance. Five SOTA methods (CA, ESD, UCE, MACE, FMN) are evaluated under identical protocols on Stable Diffusion v1.4/v2.1, with domain-specific classifiers determining concept presence in generated images.

## Results

On the Object domain, concept remapping methods dominate: MACE achieves the highest explicit EA (98.6% name, 97.8% prefix), while ESD leads on implicit prompts (82.1% variant, 67.6% short, 61.7% long). However, all methods show significant EA degradation on implicit prompts—MACE drops from 98.6% (name) to 70.5% (long descriptions)—revealing that erasure is largely superficial. For retaining ability, MACE retains 91.6% on random concepts but only 79.4% on similar ones (e.g., motorbike after erasing bicycle). Inference times increase 2–10× over the original SD model (e.g., MACE: 19.2s vs. 2.5s), while FID scores remain comparable (MACE: 43.90 vs. SD: 42.85).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MACE [23] achieves the strongest explicit EA across most domains (98.6% name on objects, 97.3% on celebrities, 96.4% on art styles) and the best RA on random concepts (91.6% objects, 89.9% celebrities), but its EA drops sharply on long descriptions (70.5% objects) and its RA on similar concepts falls to 79.4%. ESD [10] leads on implicit EA metrics (82.1% variant, 67.6% short on objects) and has the fastest inference (8.5s), yet suffers the largest RA degradation on similar concepts (74.6% objects, 20.0% art styles) due to its unconditioned prompt design. UCE [11] offers the fastest training (47.1s objects) and competitive implicit EA (78.0% short), with the best RA on similar concepts among remapping methods (86.0% objects). CA [20] and FMN [55], both optimization-based, substantially underperform remapping methods: CA achieves only 16.5% EA on object names, and FMN barely exceeds the unmodified SD baseline (5.8% vs. 5.7% name on objects) while degrading RA (75.0% random). The unmodified SD v1.4 [38] baseline confirms that low EA on implicit prompts partly reflects the model's inherent inability to interpret descriptive prompts, rather than successful erasure.

## Relation to Prior Work

EMMA extends the concept erasure evaluation landscape by unifying and expanding dimensions that prior benchmarks (SLD, CCE, Ring-A-Bell, UnlearnCanvas, HUB, EraseBench, SEE) each cover only partially. It builds directly on the gender bias measurement framework of Wu et al. [52] and generalizes it to ethnicity, while introducing implicit-prompt erasure testing (variants, short/long descriptions) and visually-similar non-target retention that no prior benchmark systematically combines. Conceptually, EMMA departs from the dominant evaluation paradigm—explicit-name prompts and random non-target concepts—by arguing that token-level erasure is insufficient and that semantic-level removal must be verified through paraphrased and descriptively indirect prompts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EMMA's central finding—that methods scoring >95% on explicit prompts drop to 60–80% on long descriptive prompts—challenges the field's assumption that high explicit erasure scores indicate genuine concept removal, suggesting current methods only sever the lexical link rather than the underlying representation. The discovery that erasure amplifies gender and ethnicity bias relative to the base model introduces a previously unmeasured safety risk for deployment. Open questions include whether latent-space-level erasure (rather than cross-attention remapping) can close the implicit-prompt gap, how to regularize against collateral damage to visually similar concepts, and whether bias monitoring can be integrated into the unlearning loop itself. As a 206-concept, 5-domain, 13-metric benchmark, EMMA is positioned to become the standard evaluation protocol for the field.
