## Problem

Existing multi-image benchmarks are insufficient for evaluating complex multi-image reasoning, typically featuring only 2–4 images, shallow inter-image dependencies, and uniform task formats that fail to stress-test cross-image structural reasoning. This paper asks whether current MLLMs can handle multi-image visual puzzles requiring strong inter-image relational modeling, temporal sequencing, and spatial assembly under high visual information density.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20264034/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question presents 2–14 RGB images alongside a text prompt, with images also serving as answer options in choice-based tasks to force cross-image analysis. Sorting and jigsaw tasks deliberately omit explicit textual instructions, compelling models to rely solely on visual understanding. For sorting tasks, a fine-grained adjacency-based scoring metric (Rule 2) replaces binary evaluation, computing the normalized fraction of correctly positioned neighboring pairs with directional encoding (up/down/left/right).

## Method

The benchmark is constructed through two image generation pipelines: real-world images collected via keyword-based search APIs, and synthetic puzzle images generated by simulators and solvers (Sudoku, 15-puzzle, Slitherlink) with injected noise to create causal chains and reasoning semantics. Images undergo multi-round manual grouping and filtering to ensure discernible structural, semantic, or temporal connections without textual prompts. The 4,000 questions span four major categories (Understanding, Reasoning, Subject, Jigsaw) and 15 subtasks classified by inter-image relationship type, with task design emphasizing vision-centric evaluation by using images as both prompts and options and by omitting instructions in sorting tasks to suppress linguistic priors. Evaluation is conducted via VLMEvalKit across 10 proprietary and 10 open-source MLLMs, with a custom adjacency-based scoring rule for sorting tasks that measures directional correctness of neighboring pairs rather than requiring exact sequence reconstruction.

## Results

On the MIRACLE benchmark (4,000 questions, 2–14 images each), the best proprietary model Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves 55.91% overall accuracy, followed by OpenAI o3 at 51.86% and Seed-1.6-Thinking at 45.30%. Among open-source models, Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Instruct leads at 32.15%, with all open-source models falling below 33%. Performance drops sharply in ultra-multi-image settings (>7 images): Gemini-2.5-Pro falls from 70.17% to 42.93%, while most open-source models drop below 20%. The hardest subtasks are Jigsaw-Strange Shape (Gemini-2.5-Pro: 16.65%) and Number-Constraint Reasoning (Gemini-2.5-Pro: 16.39%), where even top models struggle significantly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MIRACLE evaluates 21 MLLMs (10 proprietary, 11 open-source) as baselines on a unified multi-image reasoning protocol. Gemini-2.5-Pro (55.91%) outperforms OpenAI o3 (51.86%) by ~4 points overall, with the gap widening on MCQ (81.08% vs. 75.68%) and Jigsaw (36.82% vs. 29.90%). All proprietary models substantially outperform the best open-source model (Qwen2.5-VL-72B at 32.15%), with a gap of 23.76 points between the top proprietary and top open-source entries. Thinking-mode variants consistently beat their non-thinking counterparts within the same family (e.g., Seed-1.6-Thinking 45.30% vs. Seed-1.6 35.93%; Claude-4-Sonnet-Thinking 34.09% vs. Claude-4-Sonnet 29.66%). In ultra-multi-image settings (>7 images), the performance gap widens dramatically: Gemini-2.5-Pro retains 42.93% while most open-source models fall below 20%, and even GPT-4o drops to 24.64%. On the hardest subtasks (Jigsaw-Strange Shape and Number-Constraint Reasoning), all models score below 20%, indicating a shared capability ceiling in fine-grained spatial and structural reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

MIRACLE extends the multi-image benchmarking line (MuirBench, MMIU, Mantis-Eval, MileBench, MMDU) by deliberately increasing image count (2–14 per question), enforcing strong cross-image dependencies, and introducing sorting/jigsaw task formats absent from prior benchmarks. Rather than building on a specific model architecture, it repurposes the VLMEvalKit evaluation framework and draws on puzzle-game solvers and web-retrieved image sets to construct a 'strong-reasoning' evaluation axis that prior benchmarks (focused on understanding, perception, or long-context) do not systematically cover. The paper positions itself as filling the gap between shallow multi-image comparison tasks and the kind of structural, compositional, and sequential reasoning that real multi-image decision-making demands.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest proprietary model (Gemini-2.5-Pro) scores only 55.91% overall, and performance collapses on structural tasks like Jigsaw-Strange Shape (~17%) and Number-Constraint Reasoning (~11–19%), exposing a clear gap between perceptual recognition and compositional/spatial reasoning in current MLLMs. The consistent advantage of thinking-mode models and the sharp accuracy drop beyond 7 input images point to concrete bottlenecks—limited working-memory for visual state and immature structural assembly—that future architectures must address. Open questions include whether scaling alone can close the jigsaw/structural-reasoning gap, whether explicit spatial-reasoning pretraining or new tokenization schemes for multi-image inputs would help, and how to design training objectives that reward cross-image relational modeling rather than per-image recognition.
