## Problem

Existing Cinematic Audio Source Separation (CASS) methods are purely audio-based, ignoring the inherent audio-visual coupling in films where speech aligns with lip movements and sound effects align with visual actions. Moreover, no publicly available dataset provides synchronized film video with isolated source audio tracks, making audio-visual CASS training infeasible with real cinematic data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263648/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a mixed audio spectrogram (16 kHz mono) plus two video streams: cropped facial frames for speech cues and full scene frames for sound-effect cues. At inference on real films, facial regions are automatically extracted from a single video to form the face stream while the full frame serves as the scene stream, requiring no architectural change from the dual-stream training setup. The output is a concatenated tensor of three source spectrograms (speech, sound effects, music) that are converted back to waveforms via inverse STFT.

## Method

AV-CASS formulates CASS as conditional flow matching, where a U-Net-based vector field estimator maps Gaussian noise to the joint distribution of three source spectrograms, conditioned on the mixture spectrogram and a fused visual representation. Visual features are extracted by two frozen encoders (AVDiffuSS for faces, CAVP/Diff-Foley for scenes), projected into a shared space via MLPs, concatenated along the temporal axis, and injected into the U-Net through cross-attention. Training uses a two-stage procedure: an audio-only denoiser warm-up followed by full audio-visual training where visual cues are introduced via zero-initialized convolution layers (ControlNet strategy) to prevent early overfitting to visual signals. Timesteps are sampled from a logitnormal distribution to emphasize intermediate steps. A synthetic data pipeline pairs LRS3 speech clips (with facial video), SMAD-filtered VGGSound effect clips (with scene video), and FMA music clips, mixing them additively to create training samples with ground-truth stems and aligned dual-stream video.

## Results

On the AVDnR benchmark (1K audio-visual samples, 60 s each), AV-CASS achieves the best FAD (0.84), KL divergence (0.93), PESQ (2.26), and Wrong Placement Ratio (1.84%) among all compared methods, while attaining a competitive SI-SDRi of 12.32 dB (second only to BandIt's 14.40 dB). On real-world movie clips, AV-CASS obtains the highest MOS of 4.13 ± 0.09 (vs. BandIt 3.78, MRX 2.55) and the lowest WPR for dialogue (0.46%) and music (0.32%). In the audio-only setting on DnRv3, the audio-only variant still achieves the best WPR (1.91%) and FAD (2.62), outperforming BandIt (WPR 3.53%, FAD 4.79%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AV-CASS outperforms all six baselines on FAD, KL, PESQ, and WPR on the AVDnR benchmark. Against the strongest audio-only baseline BandIt, AV-CASS reduces FAD from 2.15 to 0.84 and WPR from 4.65% to 1.84%, but trails on SI-SDRi (12.32 vs. 14.40 dB), consistent with the known trade-off between generative and predictive models. Against the audio-visual baseline DAVIS-Flow, AV-CASS wins on every metric (FAD 0.84 vs. 5.94; WPR 1.84% vs. 12.14%), demonstrating that the dual-stream facial+scene conditioning and multi-source formulation are critical advantages over single-target AV separation. On real-world movie clips, AV-CASS achieves the lowest WPR for dialogue (0.46% vs. BandIt 0.60%) and music (0.32% vs. BandIt 0.35%), while DAVIS-Flow degrades severely on music (35.94% WPR). In the audio-only setting on DnRv3, the audio-only variant still leads on WPR (1.91% vs. BandIt 3.53%) and FAD (2.62 vs. 4.79), confirming the generative flow-matching formulation generalizes well even without visual input.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CASS task (formalized by the Divide and Remaster dataset and subsequent audio-only methods like MRX and BandIt) into the audio-visual domain, a direction explicitly identified as unexplored by the authors. The generative backbone adopts conditional flow matching (Lipman et al., ICLR 2023), which has been applied to audio generation and separation (FlowSep, VoiceFlow) but not previously to CASS. The visual conditioning pipeline repurposes two frozen encoders from prior audio-visual work — the facial encoder from AVDiffuSS (Lee et al., ICASSP 2024) and the scene encoder from Diff-Foley/CAVP (Luo et al., NeurIPS 2023) — and integrates them via a ControlNet-style zero-initialized strategy, a design borrowed from text-to-image diffusion. Unlike prior audio-visual separation methods (DAVIS, AudioScope) that condition on a single visual cue for a single target source, this work introduces a dual-stream (facial + scene) formulation tailored to the multi-source CASS setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most practically significant contribution is the synthetic training pipeline that pairs in-the-wild audio and video sources (LRS3, VGGSound, FMA) to create CASS training data without requiring source-separated film recordings — a data bottleneck that has largely blocked audio-visual CASS research. The finding that a model trained on this synthetic dual-stream setup generalizes to real single-video cinematic content without architectural changes is encouraging, though the real-world evaluation is limited to 30 clips with subjective MOS scoring. Open questions include whether the approach scales to longer films, additional source types (e.g., nonverbal sounds as in DnR-nonverbal), and whether the 128-step flow-matching inference can be accelerated for near-real-time use. The introduction of the WPR metric also provides a useful reference-free tool for evaluating stem-level contamination in separation tasks.
