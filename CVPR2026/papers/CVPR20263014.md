## Problem

Video-LLMs rely on uniform frame sampling or redundancy-reduction heuristics that ignore the specific user instruction, leading to missed key frames for temporal reasoning and instruction-following tasks. A critical gap is the absence of large-scale, instruction-conditioned temporal grounding data that teaches models to adaptively select frames based on what the query actually demands (static semantics, dynamic motion, or holistic coverage).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263014/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The VideoITG module receives up to 512 video frames encoded at 1 fps (16 visual tokens per frame via SigLIP) alongside a text instruction/question, and outputs a relevance score per frame from which the top-k (default 32) frames are selected. These selected frames are then passed to a downstream Video-LLM (e.g., LLaVA-Video-7B, Qwen2-VL, InternVL) which produces the final text answer. The system is plug-and-play: it sits between any ViT-based vision encoder and any Video-LLM without modifying the answering model.

## Method

The core contribution is a plug-and-play frame selection module trained on the VideoITG-40K dataset (40K videos, 500K instruction-grounded annotations) generated by the VidThinker pipeline, which automates annotation in three stages: instruction-conditioned clip captioning, LLM-based relevant clip retrieval, and per-frame binary relevance classification. Three model variants are explored—text-generation (next-token prediction), anchor-based causal attention (inserting a global-average anchor token per frame to mediate temporal cues under a causal mask), and full-attention pooling (removing the causal mask, averaging visual tokens per frame, and classifying instruction relevance with a classification head). The best-performing Variant C uses full bidirectional attention so all visual tokens can attend to the instruction, combined with average pooling and a lightweight classification head. Training follows a three-stage protocol: MLP projector pre-training on image data, video fine-tuning on LLaVA-Video, and final supervised training on VideoITG-40K at 1 fps with a dynamic token spatial size strategy.

## Results

On LLaVA-Video-7B with 32 selected frames, VideoITG-8B achieves an average score of 66.9 across LongVideoBench, MLVU, and VideoMME, outperforming uniform 64-frame sampling (64.7) and all competing frame-selection methods. With only 8 frames on LLaVA-OneVision-7B, VideoITG reaches 61.6 average versus 54.9 for uniform sampling (+6.7). On the broader set of Video-LLMs (Table 3), VideoITG-32 consistently improves over uniform-32 by +4.5 to +6.6 points in average score, with the largest gain on Qwen3-VL (+6.6) and MLVU (+13.1). Notably, 16 frames selected by VideoITG match 64-frame uniform sampling on VideoMME, demonstrating that fewer, better-chosen frames suffice.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VideoITG outperforms all six compared frame-selection baselines across every benchmark where results overlap. Against BOLT [27] on LLaVA-OneVision-7B (8 frames), VideoITG gains +4.0 average (61.6 vs 57.6), with the largest margin on MLVU (+5.3). Frame-VOYAGER [59] is the closest competitor at 8 frames (59.5 avg), but VideoITG still leads by +2.1 overall and by +3.4 on LongVideoBench. On Qwen2-VL, VideoITG edges out Q-Frame [67] by +0.6 average (60.3 vs 59.7), with the gap widening on MLVU (+1.2) and VideoMME-L (+0.9). At 32 frames on LLaVA-Video-7B, VideoITG surpasses AKS [45] by +2.0 (66.9 vs 64.9) and QuoTA [33] by +1.0 (66.9 vs 65.9), while using half the frames of QuoTA. Gen-S [56] reports only partial results (LongVideoBench 63.3, MLVU 73.4); on those two benchmarks VideoITG-32 is competitive (61.6, 74.6) but trails on LongVideoBench by 1.7 points, suggesting Gen-S may have an advantage on text-referring tasks. Across the extended evaluation in Table 3 (six different Video-LLMs), VideoITG-32 consistently beats uniform-32 by 4.5–6.6 points, confirming that the gains are not model-specific.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends video temporal grounding from single descriptive-query localization (as in SeViLA, TimeChat, Grounded-VideoLLM) to instruction-driven temporal reasoning that adapts frame selection to four distinct instruction types (semantic, motion, joint, and open-ended). It builds directly on LLaVA-Video's training recipe and architecture as its initialization, while departing from prior frame-selection methods (BOLT, Q-Frame, AKS, Frame-Voyager) that either reduce spatiotemporal redundancy or use question-centric scoring without explicit temporal modeling. The VidThinker annotation pipeline is a novel contribution that automates the three-step human reasoning process (clip captioning → retrieval → frame localization) using GPT-4o, producing a dataset 4× larger than prior temporal grounding resources.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The VideoITG-40K dataset (40K videos, 500K instruction-grounded annotations) substantially expands the scale and instruction diversity of temporal grounding supervision, and the plug-and-play VideoITG module consistently improves six different Video-LLMs (InternVL, Qwen, LLaVA-Video, Eagle) by 4–6 points without modifying their architectures. The practical finding that 16 VideoITG-selected frames match 64-frame uniform sampling on VideoMME offers a concrete efficiency win for long-video inference. Open questions include whether the four instruction types adequately cover all real-world query distributions, how the method scales to hour-scale videos beyond the 3-minute training range, and whether the full-attention variant's advantage over causal attention persists at much larger model scales.
