## Problem

Deep dehazing models trained on synthetic data suffer significant performance degradation on real-world haze scenes due to the synthetic-to-real domain gap. Two critical barriers hinder effective adaptation: the absence of paired ground-truth clear images for unsupervised training, and the prohibitive computational cost of full model fine-tuning for each target domain.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263638/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single hazy RGB image (processed as 256×256 patches during training) and outputs a dehazed RGB image of the same resolution. During adaptation, the H2C loss additionally consumes two fixed text prompts ('a photo with haze' and 'a clear photo') encoded by CLIP's text encoder to define the semantic transformation direction. The approach is plug-and-play: it wraps around any pre-trained dehazing backbone (DEA, MSBDN, DeHamer, ConvIR) without modifying its architecture, adding only lightweight LoRA adapters to a small number of selected layers.

## Method

BiLaLoRA combines two key components. First, the Haze-to-Clear (H2C) loss reformulates dehazing as a semantic alignment problem in CLIP's latent space: it computes the displacement vector between input and output image embeddings and maximizes cosine similarity with the direction defined by positive and negative text prompts, providing unsupervised guidance without paired data. Second, the bilevel layer-positioning mechanism treats LoRA injection as a differentiable architecture search: each candidate layer's LoRA module is gated by a learnable parameter α (sigmoid-constrained to (0,1)), and a bilevel optimization problem is solved where the upper level updates α using validation-set feedback while the lower level updates LoRA weights on the training set. The hypergradient for α is computed efficiently via a rank-one outer-product Hessian approximation, reducing the problem to first-order derivatives. Training proceeds in two stages—bilevel layer-positioning to rank and select the top-k most critical layers, followed by standard LoRA fine-tuning on those layers—achieving comparable performance to full fine-tuning with ~78% reduction in training time.

## Results

On the average of three real-world datasets (RTTS, URHI, Fattal), BiLaLoRA achieves FADE 0.638, BIQME 0.611, Entropy 7.572, and MUSIQ 64.40, ranking first on FADE, BIQME, and MUSIQ among all compared methods. Compared to the strongest real-dehazing baseline PHATNet (ICCV 2025), BiLaLoRA improves FADE by 7.4% (0.638 vs. 0.689) and MUSIQ by 6.5% (64.40 vs. 60.55). Against the best all-in-one restoration model FoundIR (ICCV 2025), BiLaLoRA reduces FADE by 55% (0.638 vs. 1.427) and increases MUSIQ by 8.6% (64.40 vs. 59.30). BiLaLoRA also reduces training time by 77.7% (0.94 h vs. 4.22 h) relative to full fine-tuning while maintaining comparable FLOPs (+1.18%) and runtime (+0.89%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BiLaLoRA outperforms all 16 baselines on FADE (lower is better), achieving 0.638 vs. the next-best PHATNet at 0.689 (7.4% improvement) and KANet at 0.692. On BIQME (higher is better), BiLaLoRA scores 0.611, surpassing CoA and RIDCP (both 0.602) and PHATNet (0.590). On MUSIQ, BiLaLoRA leads with 64.40, ahead of IPC (63.14), RIDCP (62.32), and PHATNet (60.55). The sole metric where BiLaLoRA does not rank first is Entropy, where CoA edges it out by 0.013 (7.585 vs. 7.572). Among all-in-one restoration models (PromptIR, DiffUIR, MoCE-IR, FoundIR), BiLaLoRA achieves substantially lower FADE (0.638 vs. 1.393–1.672) and higher MUSIQ (64.40 vs. 58.24–59.30), indicating that task-specific dehazing with parameter-efficient adaptation significantly outperforms general-purpose restoration pipelines on real haze. Against the closest real-dehazing competitors (PHATNet, KANet, CoA, IPC, RIDCP), BiLaLoRA consistently leads on at least three of four metrics, with the largest margins observed on FADE and MUSIQ.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA) and real image dehazing domain adaptation. It extends LoRA by replacing heuristic layer selection with a bilevel optimization formulation that automatically identifies bottleneck layers, drawing on the rank-one Hessian approximation from Liu et al. (TIP 2022) to make the hypergradient computationally tractable. The H2C loss repurposes CLIP's cross-modal embedding space as an unsupervised supervision signal, departing from prior dehazing domain adaptation methods (DAD, PSD, D4) that rely on GANs, physics priors, or full fine-tuning. Conceptually, the paper unifies two previously separate concerns—where to adapt and how to supervise—into a single bilevel framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature of BiLaLoRA (separate adapters for daytime/nighttime, rapid switching across target domains) addresses a practical deployment bottleneck in real-world dehazing where full fine-tuning is infeasible. The automatic layer-positioning mechanism generalizes across four different architectures, suggesting the bilevel formulation could transfer to other low-level vision tasks beyond dehazing. However, the approach still depends on CLIP's semantic space being well-aligned with perceptual haze removal, and the rank-one Hessian approximation may limit the precision of layer ranking in very deep or heterogeneous networks. Open questions include whether the bilevel formulation scales to transformer-based restoration backbones and whether more refined text prompts or multi-modal guidance could further close the synthetic-to-real gap.
