## Problem

Single-agent GUI models face two fundamental challenges on long-horizon tasks: (1) responsibility coupling, where a unified policy must simultaneously master high-level planning and low-level execution, leading to capability conflicts and performance collapse; and (2) lack of task state awareness, where agents must infer progress from ambiguous screenshots that are empirically shown to be insufficient representations of task state, causing progress loss over extended trajectories.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260473/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The CES loop consumes a high-level natural language instruction, the current GUI screenshot, and a compressed high-semantic state summary (natural language) at each timestep. The Coordinator produces a short atomic instruction targeting one specific GUI operation; the frozen Executor maps that instruction plus the screenshot to a standardized action (click at coordinates, scroll direction, or typed text); the State Tracker ingests the Executor's chain-of-thought and action output to emit an updated state summary. The Executor is never trained and is swappable, so the system's trainable outputs are limited to the atomic instruction and the state summary.

## Method

The CES framework structurally decouples GUI automation into three specialized agents—Coordinator (planning), Executor (execution), and State Tracker (state management)—inspired by an OS analogy of CPU, I/O device, and dynamic memory. Training uses a staged execution-feedback RL strategy with GRPO: Stage 1 optimizes the Coordinator (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) against a frozen Executor (GUI-R1-7B) using ground-truth state annotations, while Stage 2 freezes the Coordinator and optimizes the State Tracker (Qwen3-4B) so its generated state summaries are scored by the reward they ultimately produce through the fixed Coordinator-Executor chain. The reward is a composite Execution-Feedback Reward combining a format term and an executor term (action-type accuracy weighted 0.2 plus parameter accuracy weighted 0.8), ensuring high-level agents are trained toward verifiably effective outputs rather than subjective quality. A warm-up SFT phase precedes RL to establish role-specific output formats, and the entire high-level module is designed as a plug-and-play component that can be paired with any pretrained Executor.

## Results

On the three long-horizon GUI benchmarks (AITZ, AMEX, GUI-Odyssey), CES with the GUI-R1-7B executor achieves Type/GR/SR of 64.44/64.58/43.05, 77.57/61.64/48.48, and 79.24/63.82/53.69 respectively, improving Type accuracy by an average of 10.38% over the GUI-R1-7B single-agent baseline. On GUI-Odyssey (the longest-horizon benchmark, avg. 15.3 steps), CES reaches 53.69% SR, surpassing the strongest multi-agent baseline SWIRL (51.65% SR) and the best single-agent RL method GUI-R1-7B (38.79% SR) by 2.04 and 14.90 points respectively. The generalization study (Table 2) confirms that plugging the trained CES high-level modules onto different executors (UI-R1-3B, GUI-Owl-7B, GUI-Owl-32B) consistently yields 5–17% SR gains over each executor's standalone baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the strongest single-agent RL baseline GUI-R1-7B, CES (using the same model as frozen executor) improves Type accuracy by 11.71/10.31/13.75 points on AITZ/AMEX/GUI-Odyssey and SR by 12.46/4.79/14.90 points, confirming that decoupling planning from execution yields substantial gains. Compared to the multi-agent baseline SWIRL (which reports only GUI-Odyssey results), CES achieves higher Type (79.24 vs. 74.87) and SR (53.69 vs. 51.65), though SWIRL retains a slight edge on GR (66.39 vs. 63.82). Against the SFT baseline OS-Atlas-7B, CES leads by 25.92/22.46/18.82 points in Type across the three benchmarks, and against the zero-shot Qwen2.5-VL-7B the gap is even larger (30.21/18.05/23.64 Type points). The paper also shows that simply prompting GPT-5 as Coordinator/State Tracker (GUI-R1-7B + GPT-5) yields only a ~4% average Type improvement and even degrades some metrics, whereas the RL-trained CES modules deliver consistent, large gains. In the generalization study (Table 2), CES outperforms both the standalone baseline and the prompt-only CES-P variant across all three executor sizes (3B, 7B, 32B), with the largest SR gains on the smallest model (UI-R1-3B: +5.55 SR on GUI-Odyssey) and the 7B model (GUI-Owl-7B: +10.83 SR on GUI-Odyssey).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GRPO reinforcement learning algorithm from DeepSeekMath [26] and the GUI-R1 [23] executor model, but departs fundamentally from the single-agent paradigm that dominates prior GUI agent work (SFT-based [35,24] and RL-based [21,23,27]). While multi-agent frameworks like Mobile-Agent-v3 [43] and MobiAgent [45] have explored role decomposition, they rely on prompt engineering with general-purpose VLMs rather than dedicated training. The key conceptual departure is the execution-feedback RL strategy: instead of training a unified policy or using prompt-based role assignment, the paper freezes a powerful Executor and uses its verifiable action outputs as reward signals to train only the high-level Coordinator and State Tracker in a staged manner, structurally resolving the responsibility coupling problem that plagues single-agent designs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CES framework offers a practical, plug-and-play solution to long-horizon GUI automation that can enhance any existing Executor model without retraining it, which is significant given the cost of training large VLMs. The empirical temporal-reasoning experiment (showing even strong models fail to determine screenshot ordering in a trajectory) provides compelling evidence for the state-awareness bottleneck, and the capability-conflict finding (where prompting a 3B model for all roles degrades performance while a 32B model benefits) validates the architectural decoupling hypothesis. Open questions include whether joint training of all three agents could yield further gains, whether the approach generalizes to even longer horizons (beyond ~15 steps), and whether the frozen-Executor assumption remains valid as Executor models evolve. The paper acknowledges that the performance bottleneck has shifted to the Executor's perceptual limitations, suggesting future work on co-evolving all components.
