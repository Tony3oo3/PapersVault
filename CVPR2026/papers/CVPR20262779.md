## Problem

Current video-language models rely on frame-level spatial features to infer temporal information, making them unable to extract meaning when individual frames contain no recognizable spatial content. Existing temporal benchmarks entangle spatial and temporal cues, masking this architectural blind spot. The paper asks whether VLMs can genuinely process purely temporal patterns—where the signal exists only in the relationship between frames—or whether they are fundamentally 'time-blind.'
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262779/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Input consists of 960×540 pixel video sequences (average 7.11 s, ~333.5 frames) where every individual frame appears as random binary noise; the meaningful content (a word, object silhouette, or dynamic scene) is encoded exclusively in the temporal motion pattern across frames. Models receive these frame sequences along with a text prompt and must output a 1–5 word identification of the content. Evaluation uses exact-match accuracy, with flexible acceptable label sets for Object Images and Dynamic Scenes to accommodate semantic ambiguity (e.g., 'man playing basketball' accepts 'playing basketball', 'human', etc.).

## Method

SpookyBench encodes content through two temporal mechanisms: (1) opposing-motion animation where foreground and background binary noise shift in opposite directions (y+vt mod h vs. y−vt mod h) to make masked text or object silhouettes perceptible only through motion grouping, and (2) threshold-based depth-map animation where pixels within a brightness range receive time-varying noise offsets while others remain static. The benchmark spans three categories—Text (210 videos), Object Images (184 videos, segmented via SAM2 from Flux/DALLE-3 generations), and Dynamic Scenes (57 videos, depth maps from LaSOT/OTB2015 via Video Depth Anything)—with variable speckle sizes (1×1 to 3×3) and noise densities (10–90%). The paper then diagnoses the failure through five complementary experiments: systematic evaluation of 25+ open and closed-source VLMs under direct and chain-of-thought prompting, controlled human studies (6 annotators, 98% accuracy), frame-rate ablation (1–30 FPS), fine-tuning on SpookyBench data (still 0%), and motion-boundary augmentation via Farneback optical flow (restoring 51–59% accuracy), collectively isolating the architectural inability to perform internal frame-differencing and temporal integration.

## Results

On SpookyBench, human participants achieve 98.0% ± 0.6 weighted accuracy across three categories (Text, Object Images, Dynamic Scenes), while all 24 evaluated Video-VLMs — spanning 2B to 78B parameters and including closed-source systems like GPT-4o and Gemini 2.5 Pro — score exactly 0% under both direct prompting and chain-of-thought prompting. This uniform failure persists across frame rates (1–30 FPS), model architectures, and even after fine-tuning on 400 SpookyBench videos for 30 epochs. When motion boundaries are pre-computed via Farneback optical flow and overlaid as spatial cues, Qwen2-VL-7B jumps to 51.54% and GPT-4o to 59.10% overall, confirming the deficit is architectural rather than task-impossible.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central finding is a uniform 0% failure across all 24 compared Video-VLMs, making traditional win/lose comparisons inapplicable — no model, regardless of scale (2B to 78B), architecture family (LLaVA, Qwen, InternVL, InternVideo, TimeChat, VideoGPT+), or origin (open-source vs. closed-source commercial), can identify content encoded purely in temporal noise patterns. Notably, TimeChat-7B, specifically designed for fine-grained temporal understanding, fails identically to general-purpose models, and the largest open-source model (InternVL2.5-78B) performs no better than the smallest (VideoLLaMA3-2B). Closed-source systems GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Gemini 1.5 Pro, and Gemini 2.0 Flash all score 0%, ruling out the hypothesis that proprietary training data or scale alone resolves the issue. The motion boundary augmentation experiment (Table 5) provides the most informative comparison: when Farneback optical flow boundaries are pre-computed and overlaid as explicit spatial cues, Qwen2-VL-7B reaches 51.54% and GPT-4o reaches 59.10% overall, demonstrating that the information is computationally extractable but inaccessible to current architectures without external temporal-to-spatial conversion. Fine-tuning InternVL2.5-8B and Qwen2-VL-7B on 400 SpookyBench videos for 30 epochs also yields 0%, confirming the limitation is architectural rather than a data distribution mismatch.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing a benchmark that completely decouples temporal from spatial information in video understanding, departing from existing temporal benchmarks (TemporalBench, TVBench, VITATECS) that still allow models to succeed via per-frame spatial shortcuts. Conceptually, it parallels ARC-AGI's use of synthetic puzzles to isolate a single reasoning capability, but applied to temporal pattern extraction. The temporal encoding technique—opposing motion patterns in binary noise that make content visible only through temporal dynamics—draws on Gestalt common-fate principles from neuroscience but has no direct methodological predecessor in the video-VLM literature. The paper does not build on a specific prior architecture; rather, it positions itself as a diagnostic tool that exposes a blind spot shared across all current video-VLM families (LLaVA, Qwen-VL, InternVL, GPT-4o, Gemini).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 15+ state-of-the-art models (2B to 78B parameters, open and closed source) achieve 0% while humans reach 98%—and that fine-tuning on the exact data distribution still yields 0%—suggests a fundamental architectural limitation rather than a data or training gap. The motion-boundary augmentation experiment (0% → 51–59%) confirms the information is computationally extractable but inaccessible to current frame-level spatial encoders, pointing toward the need for dedicated temporal integration mechanisms. Open questions include whether any architecture (recurrent, attention-based, or inspired by distributed neural timing circuits) can natively decode such patterns, and whether this 'time blindness' manifests in real-world scenarios beyond synthetic noise stimuli. The benchmark is synthetic and somewhat artificial, so its ecological validity for natural video remains to be established, but the extreme and consistent failure across all tested systems makes it a compelling diagnostic for the field.
