## Problem

Existing social reasoning benchmarks for AI either evaluate fully visible scenes or focus on physical occlusion of inanimate objects, failing to capture how humans infer hidden information from others' behavioral cues (gaze, pose, orientation) under partial visual information. This paper asks whether current VLMs can perform this form of visually-grounded social inference—reasoning about what players attend to and intend—to localize a removed ball in sports images.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260073/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each test item is a single sports frame (soccer, basketball, or volleyball) with the ball removed via Stable Diffusion inpainting, overlaid with a 6×10 alphanumeric grid (rows A–F, columns 1–10). The model receives the image plus a text prompt (one of three strategies) and must output a single grid cell (e.g., "B6") along with free-form reasoning text. Ground truth may span multiple adjacent cells when the ball straddles grid boundaries, and predictions are sampled at temperature 0.6 (n=50 for Base/Cue prompts, n=20 for CoT) to capture distributional behavior.

## Method

The benchmark is constructed via a modular pipeline: YouTube sports footage is retrieved and decoded at ~1 FPS, frames are filtered using CLIP similarity against action-focused prompts, balls and players are detected with YOLOv8 (requiring exactly one ball proximal to non-overlapping players), and ball regions are inpainted with Stable Diffusion. A curated 150-image evaluation set (50 per sport) is complemented by 3,000 pipeline-generated soccer images. Evaluation compares four VLMs (Gemini-2.0-flash, GPT-4o-mini, LLaMA-3.2-Vision, Qwen-2.5-VL) under three prompting strategies (Base, Cue-Directed, Chain-of-Thought) against 150 human participants, using accuracy, Euclidean error, Wasserstein distance to human response distributions, and behavioral metrics (near-player rate, overlap rate, center ratio, normalized entropy) to characterize both performance and reasoning strategy.

## Results

Humans consistently outperform all four VLMs by a factor of 2–3 in localization accuracy: human accuracy ranges from 19–34% across sports, while all models remain at or below 17%. In terms of Euclidean error, humans achieve 68.5±40.8 px in basketball (their best sport) versus 127–162 px for the best models, and the gap widens in volleyball where LLaMA and Qwen reach 272–273 px under CoT prompting. Models exhibit a strong center bias (center ratio R > 1.2 in volleyball) and a near-player bias (90% of predictions within threshold of a player vs. 65–75% for humans), while humans show higher normalized entropy (0.855 vs. 0.698–0.808), indicating broader, cue-driven distributions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four VLMs (Gemini-2.0-flash, GPT-4o-mini, LLaMA-3.2-11B-Vision, Qwen-2.5-VL-7B) against 150 human participants (50 per sport) on the SPOT THE BALL benchmark. Humans win decisively on every sport and metric: their Euclidean error is roughly half that of the best model (e.g., 68.5 px vs. 127.7 px in basketball for GPT). Among models, proprietary systems (Gemini, GPT) consistently outperform open-weight systems (LLaMA, Qwen) by 10–30 px in Euclidean error, and their prediction distributions are closer to human distributions in Wasserstein distance. Prompting strategies yield inconsistent gains: Cue-Directed prompting reduces error by ~5–13 px for Gemini in basketball but slightly worsens GPT in soccer; CoT prompting sometimes degrades performance dramatically (LLaMA volleyball jumps from 172.8 to 272.6 px). The paper also notes that providing models with the same example images shown to humans actually decreased performance across all three sports, suggesting the bottleneck is social cue integration rather than task comprehension.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of social reasoning benchmarks and object localization. Prior social-reasoning evaluations for AI are either text-only (ToM, empathy, moral reasoning) or focus on fully observable visual scenes and physical occlusion of inanimate objects; none require inferring a hidden object's location purely from the gaze, pose, and orientation of human agents. The task is inspired by a classic newspaper puzzle and repurposes standard tools (CLIP for frame selection, YOLOv8 for detection, Stable Diffusion for inpainting) into a modular pipeline, but the core evaluation question—can a VLM perform visually grounded social inference under partial information—is, per the authors, unaddressed in existing literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a concrete, measurable gap (2–3×) between human and VLM performance on a task that is critical for embodied and safety-critical deployments (robot navigation, autonomous driving). The finding that neither cue-directed nor chain-of-thought prompting closes the gap—and that CoT sometimes degrades accuracy—suggests the bottleneck is architectural (lack of structured social-cue integration) rather than instructional, pointing toward the need for models that explicitly encode behavioral cues. Open questions include whether synthetic ablations (e.g., Google Research Football) can isolate pose vs. gaze contributions, whether video dynamics change the picture, and whether sport familiarity modulates human performance.
