## Problem

Existing controllable video generation methods are locked to a single control granularity (dense or sparse) and assume strict structural alignment between the input motion and the first frame, limiting practical applicability. While point-trajectory control could naturally express a continuous spectrum of granularity through adjustable sampling density, prior work remains fragmented across 2D dragging, sparse 3D, or dense 3D settings, with no unified framework that handles all granularities and unaligned inputs simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263680/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a first-frame RGB image, a set of 3D point trajectories where each point carries a trajectory ID (temporal correspondence), a segmentation ID (object instance), and an optional 3-channel color vector (appearance cue), plus a text prompt. Trajectories can be provided at any density—dense, spatially sparse (subset of points), temporally sparse (subset of frames), or spatially shifted (unaligned)—and are projected into two conditioning videos (an ID-coded video encoding SegID in red and TrajID in green-blue, and a color-cue video) that are VAE-encoded into compact latent tokens. The output is a temporally coherent video that follows the specified motion while respecting the first-frame appearance.

## Method

FlexTraj encodes point trajectories into two condition videos (ID-coded and color-cue), VAE-encodes them into latent tokens, and fuses them via a zero-initialized linear projection before concatenating with noisy latent and text tokens into a unified sequence. Instead of ControlNet-style injection, the paper uses an efficient sequence-concatenation strategy with LoRA adapters applied only to QKV projections when processing condition tokens, plus a condition mask that prevents condition tokens from attending to noise or text tokens—enabling KV caching at inference for roughly 50% speedup while inherently supporting unaligned inputs through attention rather than direct addition. Training follows a four-stage annealing curriculum: (1) dense aligned with full color, (2) dense with randomly omitted color, (3) gradual introduction of spatial and temporal sparsity, and (4) unaligned conditions with reduced learning rate, allowing the model to generalize smoothly from deterministic to flexible supervision.

## Results

On DAVIS, FlexTraj achieves the best FVD across all four control settings: 532.4 (Dense), 710.4 (Spatially Sparse), 837.0 (Temporally Sparse), and 622.3 (Unaligned), while also attaining the lowest trajectory error (0.017–0.031) on aligned tasks and the highest trajectory similarity (0.908) on the unaligned task. On FlexBench, the method similarly leads in FVD for Spatially Sparse (851.6) and Temporally Sparse (1144.8) settings. On MoveBench, the 5B-parameter FlexTraj model outperforms the 14B Wan-Move baseline in FID (10.9 vs. 12.2), FVD (77.9 vs. 83.5), and SSIM (0.69 vs. 0.64), while remaining competitive on PSNR (17.4 vs. 17.8) and EPE (2.7 vs. 2.6).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FlexTraj outperforms all seven baselines on FVD across every evaluated task. Against DAS (dense 3D point trajectories), FlexTraj reduces FVD from 714.3 to 532.4 on Dense and from 773.9 to 622.3 on Unaligned, while also improving trajectory error from 0.029 to 0.017 (Dense) and trajectory similarity from 0.861 to 0.908 (Unaligned). Against Go-with-the-Flow (dense 2D flow), FlexTraj achieves lower FVD (532.4 vs. 793.1 Dense; 622.3 vs. 1050.5 Unaligned) and substantially better trajectory error (0.017 vs. 0.044). Against MagicMotion (mask/box trajectories), FlexTraj wins on all three shared tasks: FVD drops from 705.3→532.4 (Dense), 860.5→710.4 (Spatially Sparse), and 1054.4→837.0 (Temporally Sparse), with trajectory error improving by 3–7×. Against ToRA and LeviTor (both spatially sparse), FlexTraj achieves the best FVD (710.4 vs. 1233.3 and 1337.3) and trajectory error (0.025 vs. 0.058 and 0.050). Against SparseCtrl (temporally sparse edges), FlexTraj nearly halves FVD (837.0 vs. 2533.4) and improves consistency (0.983 vs. 0.967). On MoveBench, the 5B FlexTraj model beats the 14B Wan-Move on FID (10.9 vs. 12.2), FVD (77.9 vs. 83.5), and SSIM (0.69 vs. 0.64), with only marginal losses on PSNR (17.4 vs. 17.8) and EPE (2.7 vs. 2.6).

## Relation to Prior Work

FlexTraj builds on the DiT-based video diffusion paradigm (CogVideoX, Wan) and the point-trajectory control line (DAS, LeviTor, GoFlow, ToRA), but departs from prior work in two key ways. First, it replaces the dominant ControlNet-style injection with an efficient sequence-concatenation scheme (inspired by EasyControl) augmented with LoRA and a condition mask, arguing that additive conditioning is suboptimal on DiT backbones and inherently enforces structural alignment. Second, it introduces a unified point representation (TrajID + SegID + optional color) that subsumes both flow/Gaussian-map encodings and first-frame color-propagation schemes, enabling a single model to handle dense, spatially sparse, temporally sparse, and unaligned trajectory inputs via an annealing training curriculum rather than task-specific architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By unifying four previously disjoint control granularities in one model, FlexTraj removes the need for practitioners to select and maintain separate pipelines for drag-to-video, motion interpolation, camera redirection, and mesh animation, which has practical value for both creative and CG production workflows. The annealing training strategy (dense→sparse→unaligned) is a reusable recipe for multi-granularity conditioning that could transfer to other DiT-based generators. Open questions include whether the approach scales to longer sequences and more complex multi-entity scenes, whether the SAM + SpatialTracker annotation pipeline introduces systematic biases, and whether the unaligned control quality (TrajSIM ~0.76–0.91) is sufficient for production-grade CG retargeting without manual correction.
