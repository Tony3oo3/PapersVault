## Problem

Large Multimodal Models are fundamentally text-centric, forcing visual reasoning through language tokens that lack the expressivity for spatially structured visual abstractions. Existing remedies—supervising intermediate steps with helper images, bounding boxes, or depth maps—impose restrictive human-designed priors, incur heavy annotation costs, and fail to generalize when useful intermediate representations are hard to specify. The paper asks whether an LMM can autonomously discover task-relevant visual abstractions without any explicit intermediate supervision.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261543/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives one or more RGB images and a text prompt (multiple-choice or open-ended question) and produces a standard text answer. During training, K=16 learnable latent tokens are appended to the prompt, and a modified attention mask is applied so that answer tokens (and optionally prompt tokens) cannot attend directly to image tokens, forcing all visual information to flow through the latent tokens. At inference, the standard attention mask is restored, so the model jointly attends to both original image tokens and the now-enriched latent tokens.

## Method

LIVR augments an LMM with K unshared latent tokens whose embeddings are randomly initialized and trained end-to-end. The key mechanism is visual bottlenecking: during Stage 1, an attention mask blocks both answer-to-vision and prompt-to-vision attention, so the only pathway for visual information to reach the answer is through the latent tokens, which are trained via standard NLL loss on answer tokens alone. Stage 2 reverts to a standard attention mask and continues training so the model learns to jointly use original image tokens and the enriched latents. The language backbone is fine-tuned with LoRA while the vision encoder and projector remain frozen. This two-stage, task-agnostic procedure requires no intermediate labels, helper images, or additional data beyond standard question-answer pairs, and the same protocol applies uniformly across single-task and multi-task fine-tuning.

## Results

On nine perception-heavy tasks (counting, jigsaw, localization, visual/semantic/functional correspondence, art style, relative reflectance, visual similarity), LIVR achieves a mean top-1 accuracy of 67.85% on Qwen2.5-VL-3B, outperforming Direct SFT (61.61%) by +6.24 points, with the largest gains on Jigsaw (+12.00) and Functional Correspondence (+13.02). On Qwen3-VL-4B and LLaVA-OneVision-1.5-4B, LIVR improves mean accuracy by +3.43 and +5.60 points over Direct SFT, respectively. In multi-task fine-tuning on Qwen3-VL-4B, LIVR reaches 72.37% mean accuracy vs. 69.60% for Direct SFT (+2.77). On spatial reasoning benchmarks, LIVR-3B achieves 85.6% on SAT Val and 59.5% on BLINK-3, substantially exceeding ViGoRL-3B (62.9% and 48.5%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ViGoRL (grounded RL with text CoT and bounding boxes), LIVR-3B achieves 85.6% on SAT Val vs. 62.9% and 59.5% on BLINK-3 vs. 48.5%, but trails slightly on RoboSpatial (66.7% vs. 67.1%). Compared to vanilla GRPO, LIVR wins decisively on SAT Val (85.6% vs. 50.0%) and BLINK-3 (59.5% vs. 46.5%) but loses on RoboSpatial (66.7% vs. 69.7%). On the 7B benchmark (Table 4), LIVR-7B outperforms Vision-R1 on all three metrics (MMVP 75.3 vs. 46.7, V* 80.1 vs. 70.2, BLINK-5 54.28 vs. 42.76) and PAPO on MMVP (75.3 vs. 54.3) and V* (80.1 vs. 36.1), though PAPO edges ahead on BLINK-5 (54.81 vs. 54.28). Against LVR (latent visual intermediates + RL), LIVR wins on MMVP (75.3 vs. 71.7) but is marginally behind on V* (80.1 vs. 80.6) and BLINK-5 (54.28 vs. 55.37). Against PixelReasoner (visual intermediates + RL), LIVR leads on MMVP (75.3 vs. 67.0), ties on V* (80.1 vs. 80.1), and is slightly behind on BLINK-5 (54.28 vs. 54.52). Notably, LIVR achieves these results without using any helper images, text CoT, explicit grounding coordinates, or RL, relying solely on implicit latent tokens trained with a visual bottleneck.

## Relation to Prior Work

LIVR carves out a distinct niche in the landscape of multimodal visual reasoning by combining ideas from latent-space computation (Coconut, Think Before You Speak) with the LLaVA-style LMM architecture, but departing sharply from prior latent-reasoning methods (Mirage, LVR) that require explicit supervision on intermediate visual targets such as helper images or crops. The key conceptual departure is the visual bottlenecking mechanism: by masking attention so that answer tokens can only access visual information through newly introduced latent tokens, the model is forced to learn task-relevant visual abstractions purely from the end-task NLL loss, without any hand-designed intermediate labels. This positions LIVR as a task-agnostic alternative to both text-CoT approaches (which are limited by verbalizability) and supervised visual-intermediate methods (which incur annotation costs and embed human biases about what 'useful' visual structure looks like).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that LIVR offers a simple, drop-in enhancement (K latent tokens + two-stage masking + LoRA) that consistently improves perception-heavy VQA across multiple backbones and tasks without requiring any additional supervision or data beyond standard question-answer pairs. This makes it particularly attractive for multi-task fine-tuning settings where designing task-specific visual intermediates is infeasible. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger models or more complex reasoning chains, how the number of latent tokens should be chosen for very different task families, and whether the two-stage training protocol can be simplified or replaced with a single-stage objective. The paper's evaluation is limited to nine BLINK-style tasks and three sub-5B backbones, leaving the behavior at scale and on open-ended generation tasks unexplored.
