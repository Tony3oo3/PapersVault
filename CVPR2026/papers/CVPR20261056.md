## Problem

State-of-the-art video reasoning models operate under a DIRECT paradigm, processing a large number of frames in a single turn regardless of video length or task complexity, which is resource-intensive and inflexible. Humans naturally adapt their reasoning strategy—skimming long videos iteratively while watching short ones in full—but no existing system trains video models to exhibit this any-horizon behavior. The paper asks whether it is possible to develop performant any-horizon video reasoning systems that flexibly choose between single-turn and multi-turn agentic reasoning.

## Input / Output

SAGE accepts 128 sampled frames at 2 FPS (with temporal pooling factor 2, 128–192 tokens per frame), video metadata (path and duration), tool definitions, and a free-form user query. The orchestrator SAGE-MM outputs structured JSON action strings: in Stage-1 it produces a video context summary, query intent, and either a final answer or a tool call; in Stage-2 (up to 10 iterative steps) it outputs whether the query is answerable, the next recommended tool, and the final answer. The system supports six tools including web search, website parsing, speech transcription (Whisper-large-v3), segment-level temporal grounding, video part extraction, and media analysis.

## Method

SAGE is a two-stage agentic system where an orchestrator VLM (SAGE-MM, based on Qwen3-VL-8B or similar) decides whether to answer directly or invoke tools in an iterative loop. Unlike prior agent systems that rely solely on whole-video temporal grounding, SAGE performs segment-level grounding over at most 10-minute windows and incorporates web search and speech transcription to leverage external knowledge and verbal signals. Training proceeds in two phases: a cold-start SFT stage on 417.7k state-action pairs generated by a Gemini-2.5-Flash-powered SAGE pipeline, followed by RL post-training using GRPO with a multi-reward scheme combining step-level rewards (format compliance, tool-call rationality judged by GPT-4o, argument repetition penalty, argument validity) and a trajectory-level accuracy reward computed via LLM-as-a-judge (GPT-4o) to handle open-ended answers. The RL recipe enforces any-horizon behavior by penalizing wrong answers that used tool calls while granting a bonus (+1.25 vs +1.0) for correct answers that employed visual tools, and uses a reduced max step count (N_max=6) for the first 100 RL steps for training stability.

## Results

On SAGE-Bench (1,744 samples, avg. duration 727s), SAGE-Flash (Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct + SFT + RL, with Gemini-2.5-Flash as tool backend) achieves 71.8% overall accuracy, 82.8% on MCQ, 62.4% on open-ended, and 69.9% on visual questions. The RL post-training stage improves the SFT model by 4.1% overall (63.9→68.0 for Qwen3-VL-8B) and yields up to 14.6% gains on videos in the 600–1200s duration bucket. On MINERVA, SAGE (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) improves long-video (>600s) accuracy from 25.8% to 28.4%, and on Video-MMMU, SAGE-Flash reaches 68.1%, outperforming the Qwen3-VL-8B baseline (65.3%) and Video-R1 (61.5%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SAGE-Flash (71.8% overall) narrowly surpasses GPT-4o in DIRECT mode (71.6%) and substantially outperforms Gemini-2.5-Flash DIRECT (68.1%), while also beating all open-source DIRECT baselines (Qwen3-VL-8B at 64.9%, Video-R1 at 57.6%, VideoRFT at 55.3%) by 7–21 points. On open-ended questions—the setting where existing AGENT systems struggle most—SAGE-Flash achieves 62.4%, compared to VideoChat-R1.5 (38.6%), VideoExplorer (35.1%), VideoMind (33.2%), and VideoAgent (32.9%), a gap of 24–30 points. On verbal-only questions, SAGE-Flash (79.1%) exceeds both GPT-4o (73.9%) and Gemini-2.5-Flash (71.6%), demonstrating the benefit of the web-search and speech-transcription tools. The RL stage consistently adds 3–5 points over SFT alone across all base models (e.g., Qwen3-VL-8B: 63.9→68.0; Qwen3-VL-4B: 64.6→68.4; Molmo2-8B: 63.2→66.1). On MINERVA long videos (>600s), SAGE (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) improves from 25.8% to 28.4%, outperforming VideoChat-R1.5 (31.8% overall but only 31.8% on long) and Video-R1 (25.8%). On Video-MMMU, SAGE-Flash (68.1%) beats Qwen3-VL-8B (65.3%) and Video-R1 (61.5%), though on Video-MME the perception-centric nature of the benchmark means disabling ground-event/extract-video-parts tools recovers competitive scores (66.2% vs. 63.5% with tools).

## Relation to Prior Work

SAGE extends the GRPO-based RL post-training paradigm from DeepSeek-R1/DeepSeekMath to multi-turn video agent reasoning, replacing verifiable-reward signals (option-matching, ROUGE) with LLM-as-a-judge accuracy rewards to handle open-ended questions. It departs from prior agent systems (VideoAgent, VideoMind, VideoExplorer, LongVT) that over-rely on a single temporal-grounding tool, instead equipping the orchestrator with web search, speech transcription, and segment-level grounding to enable knowledge-driven reasoning. The 'any-horizon' framing—where the model autonomously decides between single-turn direct answering and multi-turn tool use—is a deliberate generalization beyond both the DIRECT paradigm (Video-R1, VideoRFT, LongVILA-R1) and fixed multi-turn agent pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SAGE-Bench (1,744 open-ended samples, avg. 727 s) fills a notable gap: existing video reasoning benchmarks are predominantly MCQ and short-duration, poorly reflecting real entertainment use cases. The finding that existing agent systems substantially underperform DIRECT models on open-ended questions challenges the assumption that multi-turn tool use is universally beneficial and highlights the importance of training the orchestrator itself via RL. Open questions include whether the any-horizon RL recipe generalizes beyond entertainment domains, whether more advanced agent-centric policy optimization (e.g., group-in-group, entropy-balanced) further improves long-horizon video agents, and whether the system can dynamically synthesize new tools rather than relying on a fixed toolset.
