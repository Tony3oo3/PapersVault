## Problem

In sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) training, the router receives informative gradients only from the few experts selected per token, a phenomenon the authors term gradient blocking. This sparse, localized feedback makes it difficult for the router to learn well-calibrated expert-selection scores, leading to unstable routing dynamics—frequent changes in expert assignment across training steps (routing fluctuation)—that hinder expert specialization and degrade final accuracy. Existing remedies such as auxiliary load-balancing losses address expert-usage imbalance but do not effectively suppress this temporal routing instability.

## Input / Output

The model consumes standard RGB images (224×224 for ImageNet-1K pre-training; native resolutions for CIFAR-10/100 and Oxford-IIIT Pets) and outputs a single class label per image. During training, the method additionally requires intermediate feature vectors from a frozen pretrained dense teacher (DeiT-III) at the MoE layer positions to generate routing supervision; at inference, only the student MoE model is deployed, so no teacher access or extra computation is needed.

## Method

TGR-MoE attaches a lightweight teacher router to a frozen pretrained dense ViT (DeiT-III) and trains it with a load-balancing loss and an entropy loss so that it produces stable, well-distributed, and confident routing probabilities over the expert set. The student MoE router is then trained to match these teacher routing distributions via a KL-divergence distillation term, combined with the standard classification loss and the usual load-balancing regularizer. Because the teacher backbone is frozen and only the small router head is optimized, the teacher routing converges quickly and remains temporally stable, providing a consistent global prior that complements the sparse task-driven gradients the student router would otherwise receive. The MoE layers (replacing FFNs at layers 8, 10, and 12 of a DeiT-style ViT) use top-K expert selection with K=1 or K=2, and the entire framework incurs no additional inference cost since the teacher is used exclusively during training.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K pre-training, TGR-MoE achieves 78.78% (K=1) / 79.24% (K=2) top-1 accuracy for the Tiny model, 83.34% / 83.68% for Small, and 85.46% / 85.34% for Base, consistently surpassing the dense ViT and standard VMoE baselines at every scale. On CIFAR-100 fine-tuning, TGR-MoE reaches 87.03% (Tiny), 90.26% (Small), and 91.07% (Base), outperforming VMoE by 0.8–2.0 points. The method also scales reliably with expert count, maintaining steady gains up to E=128 (80.36% vs. 79.38% for VMoE on Tiny/ImageNet-1K) where conventional VMoE improvements taper off.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary baseline VMoE [32], TGR-MoE wins on virtually every metric: on ImageNet-1K it improves Tiny by +0.93 pp (K=1), Small by +0.71 pp, and Base by +1.49 pp; on CIFAR-100 the gains are +0.83 pp (Tiny), +1.58 pp (Small), and +2.03 pp (Base). Compared to SoftMoE [29], TGR-MoE is competitive but not uniformly superior: SoftMoE edges out TGR-MoE K=1 on Tiny ImageNet (79.31 vs. 78.78), yet TGR-MoE clearly wins on Small ImageNet (83.34 vs. 82.76) and on all CIFAR-100 entries (e.g., 90.26 vs. 88.45 for Small). Against Expert Choice MoE [48], TGR-MoE outperforms on ImageNet-1K at every scale (e.g., 83.34 vs. 82.09 for Small) and on CIFAR-100 (90.26 vs. 89.21 for Small), though Expert Choice retains a slight edge on Tiny Pets (91.21 vs. 91.78 is actually a TGR win; on Small Pets TGR wins 93.79 vs. 93.69). Versus z-loss regularization [49], TGR-MoE dominates on ImageNet-1K (85.46 vs. 84.03 for Base) and CIFAR-100 (91.07 vs. 89.99 for Base), with the gap widening at larger model scales.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the VMoE sparse MoE framework for vision transformers by introducing an external routing prior derived from a frozen dense teacher's intermediate features, rather than relying solely on task gradients and load-balancing regularizers. It departs from prior stabilization strategies—expert-choice routing, SoftMoE's continuous relaxation, z-loss, and differentiable top-k gating—which all modify the routing mechanism itself, by instead supplying the router with a stable, semantically structured supervision signal via KL-divergence distillation. The approach also distinguishes itself from prior router-distillation work (Read-ME, Dynamic Expert Specialization), which transfers routing patterns for model conversion or domain adaptation, by targeting the fundamental training instability (gradient blocking and routing fluctuation) that arises during pre-training from scratch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TGR-MoE offers a simple, inference-free mechanism to stabilize sparse MoE training that is directly applicable to any ViT-based MoE pipeline, and the routing-consistency analyses provide a useful diagnostic for practitioners monitoring expert specialization. However, the evaluation is confined to the DeiT architecture family and image classification, leaving open whether the approach transfers to NLP MoE, generative vision models, or non-Transformer backbones. The upper-bound experiment (80.19% teacher-routed vs. 77.81% TGR-MoE) reveals a substantial ~2.4% gap, suggesting that student routers still underutilize expert capacity and that richer distillation or co-adaptation strategies could close this gap. Scaling to larger datasets and expert counts beyond 128 remains untested.
