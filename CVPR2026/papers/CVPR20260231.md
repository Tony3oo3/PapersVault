## Problem

Selecting informative camera views for 3D scene reconstruction is critical, yet existing principled methods (e.g., FisherRF, uncertainty quantification) are computationally expensive, sensitive to training noise, and depend on nonstationary quantities like transmittance that shift rapidly during optimization. Heuristic strategies perform only marginally better than random sampling. The paper asks whether a simple, interpretable criterion can capture the essence of information gain without these practical drawbacks.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a set of candidate camera poses, the current 3DGS training state (per-primitive visibility booleans and accumulated viewing directions from all training cameras), and produces a single selected camera pose to add to the training set at each step. The coverage metric is computed by rendering an additional scalar channel alongside the standard color pass, making it directly visualizable as an image. In practice, per-Gaussian state is stored as a discretized boolean grid on the unit sphere, updated incrementally as new training views are added, requiring no custom CUDA kernels beyond those in Nerfstudio and gsplat.

## Method

CONVERGE derives its metric from a tractable approximation of the Fisher Information Gain for a least-squares regression of per-primitive attributes. The key insight is that maximizing FIG is equivalent (via Rayleigh quotients) to minimizing the norm of the candidate transmittance pattern projected onto all existing observations, which reduces to a per-primitive running norm that can be updated incrementally. The authors then extend this to view-direction-dependent color using a spherical Gaussian kernel model, and finally abstract away transmittance entirely by replacing it with binary visibility, yielding a coverage metric that only requires storing a discretized direction grid per Gaussian. The final metric scores each candidate view by summing, over visible primitives, a term proportional to the maximum angular similarity between the candidate's viewing direction and any previously observed direction for that primitive—favoring views that observe geometry from novel angles. A background compositing term allows tuning between exploration (penalizing empty space) and exploitation (rewarding foreground occlusion).

## Results

Across 15 real scenes (Tanks & Temples, Mip-NeRF360, and custom captures), CONVERGE achieves the best overall reconstruction quality: 22.12 PSNR, 0.71 SSIM, and 0.20 LPIPS at 30K training steps. This represents a +1.08 PSNR improvement over FisherRF (21.04) and a +0.32 PSNR improvement over the random baseline (21.80). In the embodied data-acquisition setting with sparse initialization, the gap widens to +1.5 PSNR over random (22.39 vs 20.89), suggesting stronger gains in robot deployment scenarios. CONVERGE also runs ~7× faster per view-selection step (3.5 s) than FisherRF (23.9 s) and ~10× faster than Bayes' Rays (37.1 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CONVERGE outperforms both state-of-the-art baselines on all three image metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) across every dataset. Against FisherRF, CONVERGE gains +1.08 PSNR overall (22.12 vs 21.04) and +0.11 LPIPS improvement (0.20 vs 0.25), with the largest per-dataset gap on Tanks & Temples (+1.26 PSNR). Against Bayes' Rays, the advantage is decisive: +6.37 PSNR overall (22.12 vs 15.75), which the authors attribute to Bayes' Rays inheriting the performance gap between NeRF and 3DGS backbones. Compared to the random baseline, CONVERGE's edge is smaller (+0.32 PSNR overall) because human-captured datasets are already well-distributed, but the gap widens substantially in the embodied + sparse-init setting to +1.5 PSNR (22.39 vs 20.89), indicating CONVERGE's coverage-aware selection becomes more valuable when initial coverage is poor and the robot must navigate continuously.

## Relation to Prior Work

CONVERGE builds directly on FisherRF (Jiang et al., 2023), which formulates active view selection as maximizing Fisher Information Gain over radiance field parameters. The key departure is a series of analytical relaxations—unit-norm constraints, per-primitive norm decomposition, and a transmittance-agnostic approximation—that reduce the expensive Fisher Information computation to a simple per-Gaussian directional coverage metric. This unifies two previously treated-as-separate objectives in the literature: information-theoretic view selection (FisherRF, Bayes' Rays) and spatial coverage optimization (Xiao et al., Xue et al., Xu et al.), showing they are equivalent under the derived approximation. The method is implemented within the Nerfstudio/gsplat pipeline, making it directly compatible with modern 3DGS training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for embodied and real-time deployment: CONVERGE runs in ~3.5 seconds per selection step versus ~24–37 seconds for FisherRF and Bayes' Rays, and requires no custom CUDA kernels, making it viable on robot hardware with tight compute budgets. The 1.5 PSNR gap over random in the embodied+sparse setting suggests meaningful gains when initialization is poor and the robot must navigate continuously. However, the method is illumination-agnostic and relies on intermediate reconstruction quality, so it may underperform in scenes with complex lighting or BRDFs. Open questions include extending to online trajectory-constrained planning on real hardware, incorporating photometric variation, and validating across a broader range of radiance field backbones beyond 3DGS.
