## Problem

Existing visual explanation methods (GradCAM, ScoreCAM, RISE, etc.) predominantly highlight positively contributing regions while ignoring negatively contributing ones, causing the confidence score derived from the explanation to diverge significantly from the original model's confidence. This paper identifies and formalizes 'confidence faithfulness' as a distinct explanation objective: the explained regions must reproduce not only the predicted class but also the exact confidence score, a requirement that current methods fail to meet, especially for low-confidence predictions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image and a pre-trained black-box classifier (only forward-pass access is required) and produces a binary saliency mask at a chosen spatial resolution (default 8×8 grid) that, when element-wise multiplied with the image, yields a confidence score within a small threshold ε of the original. The final explanation map is a weighted aggregation of all accepted masks, weighted by their associated confidence scores. MHE-pro additionally operates at four cascaded scales (5×5, 7×7, 9×9, 10×10), each with 1,000 iterations, producing a multi-resolution explanation.

## Method

MHE formulates explanation generation as a Metropolis-Hastings Markov chain over binary mask states, where each state is an h×w mask indicating which image regions are visible. The proposal distribution Q(i,j) flips each mask position with probability proportional to the L4 distance dis_i between the original and masked score vectors, so that low-importance samples are explored more aggressively while high-importance samples are retained. The acceptance rate α(i,j) = min(e^{-dis_{j+1}}·Q(j,i) / e^{-dis_i}·Q(i,j), 1) ensures the chain converges to a stationary distribution proportional to e^{-dis}, and a theoretical proof of detailed balance is provided. For the full explanation S^c (MHE), an additional threshold ε constrains the acceptable confidence difference, and ties are broken by preferring smaller-area masks for compactness. Two variants extend the framework: MHE-e replaces dis with the class confidence f_c(I') to isolate positive-contribution regions S^{c+}, and MHE-pro cascades MHE across multiple scales, using each scale's output as the initialization and base mask for the next, improving both search efficiency and explanation quality.

## Results

On ImageNet, MHE-pro achieves the lowest confidence-faithfulness error (dis) across all five models: 0.067 (VGG16), 0.063 (ResNet50), 0.066 (ViT), 0.123 (DINOv1), and 0.062 (CLIP), substantially outperforming RISE (0.131–0.243) and all CAM-based methods (0.108–2.067). MHE also leads on dis for ResNet50 (0.078) and ViT (0.079), while MHE-e achieves the highest AI score on DINOv1 (0.796) and CLIP (0.712). On the PNN metric, MHE-pro attains the best overall score (0.693–0.697) on VGG16 and ResNet50, confirming superior identification of positive, negative, and neutral contribution regions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MHE and MHE-pro consistently outperform all ten baselines on the dis metric (confidence faithfulness), which is the paper's primary evaluation criterion. Against RISE—the strongest black-box baseline—MHE reduces dis from 0.120 to 0.078 on ResNet50 and from 0.096 to 0.079 on ViT, while MHE-pro further narrows these to 0.063 and 0.066 respectively; on DINOv1 the gap is largest (RISE 0.243 vs. MHE-pro 0.123). All CAM-based methods (GradCAM, GradCAM++, ScoreCAM, HiResCAM, EigenCAM, EigenGradCAM, XGradCAM, KPCACAM, FinerCAM) exhibit substantially higher dis values, with CLIP scores ranging from 1.432 to 2.067, indicating severe confidence-faithfulness failure. KPCACAM is the best CAM method on ResNet50 (dis 0.108) and ViT (0.143) but still trails MHE-pro by 41% and 54% respectively. Sobol, the other black-box baseline, is outperformed by MHE on dis across all five models (e.g., 0.311 vs. 0.078 on ResNet50). On the PNN metric, MHE-pro achieves the top score in most scenarios (0.693–0.697 on VGG16/ResNet50), while RISE and MHE are competitive but slightly behind, and all CAM methods lag significantly (0.482–0.685), confirming that the MHE framework more effectively captures the full set of positive, negative, and neutral contribution regions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Metropolis-Hastings (MH) sampling framework [15] and positions itself within the perturbation-based explanation family, departing from random-sampling approaches like RISE [28] by introducing a structured proposal distribution that leverages previously accepted samples to guide exploration. Conceptually, it distinguishes 'confidence faithfulness' (matching the original score) from 'class faithfulness' (identifying the correct class), arguing that most prior methods—including the entire CAM family and RISE—only capture positive contribution regions S^{c+} while neglecting negative regions S^{c-}, which is the root cause of their confidence-score discrepancy. The PNN metric is introduced as a new evaluation tool that explicitly tests whether positive, negative, and neutral regions behave as expected, filling a gap in existing metrics (AD, AI, PG, EBPG) that do not decompose explanation quality by contribution sign.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The systematic demonstration that negative contribution regions are essential for confidence-faithful explanations reframes how practitioners should interpret saliency maps: a map that only highlights 'what the model sees' is incomplete without also showing 'what suppresses the prediction.' The PNN metric offers a more diagnostic evaluation framework that could be adopted by future explanation methods. Limitations include the computational cost of 4,000 forward passes per image (comparable to RISE but far exceeding CAM methods), the observation that 4,000 iterations may still be insufficient for challenging cases, and the fact that the method is restricted to classification confidence rather than extending to detection or segmentation. Open questions include whether the MH-based approach can be adapted to video or multi-label settings, and whether the confidence-faithfulness objective generalizes to non-softmax scoring functions.
