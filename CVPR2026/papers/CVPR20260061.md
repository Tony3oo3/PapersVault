## Problem

Diffusion models produce high-fidelity content but require slow, sequential iterative denoising, creating high latency that limits interactive use. Existing parallelization strategies are either architecture-specific (system-level methods like DistriFusion and AsyncDiff) or risk deviating from the original sampling distribution (mathematical ODE/SDE solvers like ParaDiGMS). The paper asks whether the standard diffusion framework itself contains exploitable inherent parallelism that can be unlocked without modifying the pretrained model.

## Input / Output

The method operates on any pretrained text-to-image diffusion model (SD2.1, SDXL, SD3) without architectural modification, taking a text prompt and initial Gaussian noise in latent space and producing a single generated image. Inference is distributed across up to 4 NVIDIA V100 GPUs, with the noise-prediction network evaluated in parallel at multiple timesteps per iteration. The method is model-agnostic, working identically for U-Net-based (DDPM/DDIM) and Transformer-based (Flow Matching/Euler ODE) architectures, and introduces only a small, stable memory overhead of 186–226 MB regardless of device count.

## Method

DRiffusion formalizes skip transitions as local operators within DDPM, DDIM, and Euler ODE frameworks, enabling direct jumps between arbitrary timesteps without altering the global schedule. At each anchor timestep t, the method uses these skip transitions to draft k approximate future states (x_{t-1}, …, x_{t-k}) in parallel, then feeds all drafts into the pretrained noise predictor simultaneously to obtain k noise estimates. These estimates are then used to replay the standard single-step denoising updates sequentially from the anchor, refining each state and producing an accurate next anchor (x_{t-k}, ε_{t-k}) that is cached to avoid redundant evaluation. Two modes are offered: an aggressive variant achieving O(1/N) latency scaling (up to N× speedup) by reusing the anchor noise for all drafts, and a conservative variant achieving O(2/(N+1)) scaling (up to (N+1)/2× speedup) by computing the anchor noise independently for higher fidelity. The key insight is that even though draft states are less precise due to larger step sizes, the pretrained network's strong generalization maps their neighborhoods to reasonable noise predictions, making the subsequent refinement step sufficient to recover full quality.

## Results

On MS-COCO, DRiffusion achieves 1.4×–3.7× wall-clock speedup across SD2.1, SDXL, and SD3 (up to 4 GPUs) while keeping FID and CLIP essentially unchanged from the single-device baseline (e.g., SD2.1 FID stays within 23.24–23.63 vs. 23.63 original; CLIP drops ≤0.02). PickScore and HPSv2.1 show only minor average drops of 0.17 and 0.43, respectively, across all configurations. Memory overhead is minimal and stable at +186–226 MB regardless of device count or mode.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is AsyncDiff, a system-level parallelization method that distributes sub-networks across devices with asynchronous denoising. At matched speedup levels (1.5×–3.6×), DRiffusion consistently outperforms AsyncDiff on all quality metrics: FID is lower (e.g., 23.87 vs. 24.13 at ~1.5×; 23.88 vs. 23.92 at ~2.4×), and PickScore is higher by a substantial margin (e.g., 22.37 vs. 22.34 at 2 devices; 22.26 vs. 22.02 at 4 devices). DRiffusion reduces the PickScore degradation gap relative to the original model by an average of 48.6% and up to 58.5% under the most extreme 4-device setting. On the efficiency side, DRiffusion matches or exceeds AsyncDiff's speedup at the same device count (e.g., 3.6× vs. 3.4× at 4 devices) while requiring far less extra memory (+186–226 MB vs. +494–574 MB). This memory advantage is practically significant: on a 32 GB node, DRiffusion runs SDXL at batch size 5 without issue, whereas AsyncDiff triggers OOM under the same setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

DRiffusion builds directly on the mathematical foundations of DDPM, DDIM, and Euler ODE solvers by deriving closed-form skip transition operators that allow jumping between arbitrary timesteps within each framework. It positions itself as a synthesis of two previously separate parallelization paradigms: system-level methods (AsyncDiff, DistriFusion) that exploit architectural parallelism, and mathematical methods (ParaDiGMS, ParaSolver) that redesign the sampling ODE/SDE. The key departure is treating skip transitions as a local, on-demand operator rather than a global schedule change, enabling a draft-and-refine scheduling pattern that consolidates multiple network evaluations into a single parallel step without altering the pretrained model or its sampling distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DRiffusion offers a model-agnostic, architecture-independent parallelization strategy that requires no retraining and introduces minimal memory overhead (186–226 MB vs. AsyncDiff's 494–574 MB), making it practical for high-resolution generation and multi-GPU deployment. The open questions include whether the quality gap widens at higher resolutions, with more aggressive step sizes, or beyond 4 devices, and how the method composes with other acceleration techniques like distillation or rectified flow. The observed HPSv2.1 drop of 1.50 for SD3 in aggressive 4-device mode hints at a quality ceiling that may constrain further scaling. Broader impact lies in providing a simple, drop-in parallelization primitive that could be integrated into existing diffusion pipelines without architectural changes.
