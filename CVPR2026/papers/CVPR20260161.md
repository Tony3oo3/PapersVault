## Problem

Existing pansharpening methods are trained and evaluated on small, fixed-resolution crops (200–256 px), yet real-world deployment requires inference on images up to 2000×2000 px. This creates two critical failures: Transformer-based models exhaust GPU memory at moderate resolutions (forcing tiled inference with visible block artifacts), and the distribution shift between training-scale and inference-scale inputs degrades spectral fidelity. No standardized dataset or benchmark exists to systematically evaluate cross-scale generalization in pansharpening.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a co-registered panchromatic image P ∈ R^{H×W×1} and a low-resolution multispectral image L_in ∈ R^{(H/r)×(W/r)×C} (where r is the upsampling ratio, typically 4 or 8). The LRMS is bicubic-interpolated to PAN resolution before processing. The output is a high-resolution multispectral image H_out at PAN spatial resolution. Crucially, the architecture is resolution-agnostic: the same trained model handles inputs from 200×200 to 2000×2000 px without retraining, with only the internal sequence length scaling linearly with image size while per-patch spatial dimensions remain fixed.

## Method

ScaleFormer reframes cross-resolution generalization as sequence-length extrapolation: images are tokenized into fixed-size spatial patches (via Scale-Aware Patchify), so increasing image resolution only lengthens the token sequence while keeping per-token statistics stable. During training, a bucketed sampling strategy randomly selects window sizes to expose the model to multiple effective sequence lengths, narrowing the train–test distribution gap. The architecture decouples modeling into a Spatial Transformer (intra-patch spatial features) and a Sequence Transformer (inter-patch dependencies along the scale axis), with corresponding Spatial-Cross and Sequence-Cross Transformers for bidirectional PAN–MS fusion. Rotary Positional Encoding (RoPE) is injected into sequence-dimension attention to enable extrapolation to unseen sequence lengths. This design reduces VRAM and GFLOPs at large scales compared to full-resolution self-attention while avoiding tiled-inference artifacts.

## Results

On the PanScale benchmark (averaged across multi-resolution test sets), ScaleFormer achieves PSNR of 39.29 / 41.04 / 44.65 dB and SSIM of 0.9761 / 0.9711 / 0.9827 on Jilin, Landsat, and Skysat respectively, outperforming the strongest baselines (HFIN, ARConv) by approximately 1 dB in PSNR and 0.005–0.01 in SSIM across all three datasets. In terms of efficiency, ScaleFormer uses only 0.52 M parameters and 20.57 GFLOPs at 200×200 input, roughly half the GFLOPs of HFIN (46.21 G) and ARConv (38.32 G), while maintaining the best fusion quality. On no-reference full-resolution evaluation (Table 3), ScaleFormer achieves the best QNR of 0.9569 on Landsat and 0.9408 on Skysat, confirming strong real-world generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ScaleFormer outperforms all 10 baselines across every metric (PSNR, SSIM, ERGAS, Q) on all three PanScale sub-datasets. The closest competitors are HFIN (CVPR 2024) and ARConv (arXiv 2025): on Jilin, ScaleFormer leads ARConv by +1.06 dB PSNR and HFIN by +1.29 dB; on Landsat, it leads HFIN by +0.83 dB and ARConv by +1.37 dB; on Skysat, it leads ARConv by +1.25 dB and HFIN by +0.69 dB. Traditional methods (GS, IHS, GFPCA) are outperformed by 13–19 dB in PSNR, confirming the gap between classical and learned approaches. Among deep-learning baselines, Pan-mamba (Information Fusion 2025) underperforms notably (35.55 dB on Jilin vs. 39.29 for ScaleFormer), while MSDCNN, SFINet, MSDDN, and PanFlowNet all trail by 2–4 dB. Notably, ARConv requires tiled inference due to GPU memory overflow at high resolutions, introducing visible block artifacts, whereas ScaleFormer handles full-resolution inputs natively with lower GFLOPs (20.57 G vs. 38.32 G for ARConv and 46.21 G for HFIN at 200×200).

## Relation to Prior Work

ScaleFormer repurposes two ideas from adjacent fields into the pansharpening setting: bucketed multi-resolution training (borrowed from video/image generation models like PixArt-α and SDXL) and Rotary Positional Encoding (from LLM architecture) to handle variable-length sequences. Rather than building on a specific prior pansharpening architecture, it reframes the problem by introducing a sequence axis that decouples intra-patch spatial modeling from inter-patch scale modeling, a design choice not present in prior Transformer-based fusion methods (HFIN, SFINet, ARConv). The paper also carves new ground by introducing the first cross-scale pansharpening dataset (PanScale) and benchmark (PanScale-Bench), filling a gap where prior resources (PanCollection, NBU, PAirMax) offered no multi-resolution or large-scale evaluation protocol.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The PanScale dataset and benchmark address a genuine practical gap: real-world satellite inference occurs at resolutions far exceeding training crops, yet no prior dataset or protocol systematically evaluated cross-scale generalization. The reframing of resolution changes as sequence-length changes offers a clean, generalizable principle that could transfer to other remote-sensing fusion tasks (hyperspectral, SAR-MSI). Open questions include how far sequence-length extrapolation can be pushed (beyond 2000×2000), whether the approach extends to 3D or video fusion, and whether the bucketed training strategy is sufficient for truly arbitrary scale shifts or requires additional inductive biases.
