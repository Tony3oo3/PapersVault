## Problem

Petabyte-scale whole-brain light-sheet fluorescence microscopy (LSFM) data at subcellular resolution lack scalable processing pipelines and generalizable detection models, as existing architectures struggle with the morphological heterogeneity of cells across brain regions and cell-type markers. No publicly available whole-brain LSFM benchmark with extensive ground-truth annotations existed to drive development of robust foundational models for volumetric biological imaging.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 3D LSFM volumes of intact mouse brains (1,600–1,850 z-slices at ~7,000×10,000 px/slice, 1.8×1.8×4 μm voxels, 65–140 GB compressed per marker) and produces discrete 3D cell centroid coordinates via a non-maximum-suppression step on a probability heatmap. For the self-supervised branch, input is reduced to small 3D crops (e.g., 16×32×32 voxels) centered on candidate cells, and the output is a 384-dimensional encoder feature vector per patch, which can be concatenated with detector features for post-hoc false-positive filtering.

## Method

The supervised baseline uses a ConvMixer backbone trained with focal loss per marker, augmented with a FindMaxima layer that applies 3D non-maximum suppression with a marker-specific distance threshold and confidence cutoff to convert the output heatmap into discrete cell centroids. For self-supervised representation learning, the authors adapt a DINOv2-style Vision Transformer with 3D Masked Autoencoder pretraining, introducing two key modifications: morphology-aligned crop/patch sizing (16×32×32 crop with 4×8×8 patches, maintaining a fixed 4×4×4 patch grid) and content-aware reconstruction weighting that scales the MAE loss by per-patch intensity variance to prioritize cell-containing regions over sparse background. A systematic sweep of 84 configurations revealed that lower mask ratios (0.15–0.55) substantially outperform the 0.75 standard for natural images, reflecting the higher semantic density of 3D cellular patches. The resulting all-markers MAE encoder produces transferable volumetric features that, when concatenated with detector features, yield up to +86% F1 improvement on morphologically challenging cell types like GFAP-positive astrocytes.

## Results

On the CANVAS benchmark, each ConvMixer baseline model performs best on its own marker dataset, with F1 scores of 0.78 (cFos), 0.81 (NeuN), 0.57 (TH), 0.63 (PV), 0.33 (GFAP), and 0.81 (IBA1). Cross-dataset generalization is severely limited: the TH model drops from F1 0.57 on its own data to 0.26 on cFos, and GFAP detection remains near-zero for all models except IBA1 (F1 0.61) and GFAP itself (F1 0.33). The 3D-MAE representation learning provides meaningful post-hoc refinement, improving GFAP mean F1 by +22.9% (up to +86.3% on region 3) and NeuN region_2 by +3.0%, demonstrating that self-supervised features capture complementary morphological information.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods; instead, it evaluates six ConvMixer baselines (one per marker) against each other to probe generalization. Each model dominates on its own marker, but cross-marker transfer is poor: the NeuN model surprisingly achieves F1 0.89 on PV (beating the PV model's 0.63), while the IBA1 model outperforms the GFAP model on GFAP data (0.61 vs. 0.33 F1), suggesting morphological overlap between microglia and astrocyte signals. GFAP is the hardest case, with near-zero F1 for four of six models, reflecting the complex, highly variable astrocyte morphology. The 3D-MAE self-supervised encoder, when concatenated with detector features, acts as a post-hoc false-positive filter: it yields the largest gains on GFAP (+22.9% mean F1, up to +86.3% on region 3) and modest gains on NeuN (+3.0%), confirming that label-free volumetric features complement supervised detection for morphologically challenging cell types.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Masked Autoencoder (MAE) framework and DINOv2-style Vision Transformer architectures, adapting both for 3D volumetric biological imaging through morphology-aligned patching and content-aware reconstruction weighting that accounts for the extreme sparsity of LSFM signals. For the supervised baseline, it adopts ConvMixer rather than the more common U-Net or ResNet backbones, adding a FindMaxima layer with non-maximum suppression to convert probability heatmaps into discrete cell centroids. Conceptually, the paper positions LSFM as a mesoscale bridge between fMRI (coarse, functional), EM (nanoscale, small volume), and transcriptomics (molecular, no morphology), and frames the core challenge as one of cross-marker and cross-region generalization in a data modality where existing architectures were not designed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CANVAS is the first and largest publicly available whole-brain LSFM benchmark with multi-marker annotations and a leaderboard, filling a critical gap for a data modality that is rapidly generating petabyte-scale volumes but lacks standardized evaluation resources. The empirical demonstration that marker-specific models fail to generalize across cell types and anatomical regions (e.g., GFAP near-zero F1 across most models, TH model F1 ranging 0.45–0.83 within its own dataset) underscores the need for truly generalizable foundational models for volumetric biological data. Open questions include whether self-supervised 3D-MAE representations can scale to full whole-brain inference, how active learning can reduce annotation burden, and whether the content-aware weighting strategy transfers to other sparse 3D imaging modalities.
