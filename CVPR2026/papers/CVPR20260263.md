## Problem

Thermal infrared (TIR) images suffer from complex, composite, and varying degradations (fixed-pattern noise, low resolution, blur, low contrast) that existing deterministic regression methods cannot handle robustly across different severity levels. While pre-trained diffusion models offer strong generative priors for restoration, they are trained on visible images and lack effective mechanisms to adapt to TIR-specific characteristics such as different intensity distributions and thermal textures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260263/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single degraded TIR image (512×512 crops during training) and produces an enhanced TIR image via a one-step Stable Diffusion Turbo pipeline: the input is encoded to latent space by a VAE, processed through a UNet with degradation-conditioned LoRA, and decoded back. A Degradation Estimation Module (DEM) with a DINOv2 ViT-Base backbone concurrently extracts a degradation vector from the same input, which dynamically modulates the LoRA update direction rather than being a fixed parameter.

## Method

HiDRA formulates TIR enhancement as a bi-level optimization: an upper-level Degradation Estimation Module (DEM) using DINOv2 features and a Mutual Interaction Attention (MIA) module learns degradation representations, while a lower-level degradation-aware LoRA (W + UΣV, where Σ is computed from the degradation vector via a two-layer MLP) fine-tunes a one-step SD Turbo model. The MIA module performs layer-wise self-attention across stacked multi-scale features to capture fine-grained TIR degradation patterns. A hierarchical adaptation strategy trains the DEM on validation sets with multiple degradation pipelines (varying type and severity) while the LoRA is optimized on training sets with randomly sampled degradations, using a first-order implicit gradient approximation for efficient single-loop optimization. This decomposition ensures the generative model adapts its update direction to specific degradation conditions rather than learning a single fixed low-rank update, improving robustness across varying degradation levels.

## Results

On the HM-TIR benchmark, HiDRA achieves the best scores across all five metrics for both FPN correction (LPIPS 0.127, DISTS 0.097, FID 45.08, NIQE 4.948, MANIQA 0.572) and blind super-resolution (LPIPS 0.119, DISTS 0.098, FID 36.80, NIQE 5.070, MANIQA 0.556). Under composite degradation at extreme severity, HiDRA maintains LPIPS 0.230 and MANIQA 0.555, while the next-best method (DifIISR) degrades to LPIPS 0.428 and MANIQA 0.474. On real-world datasets (TNO, RoadScene, MSRS), HiDRA attains the highest MI and VIF on all three, and on downstream tasks it reaches mAP 0.619 (object detection) and mIoU 49.4 (semantic segmentation), outperforming the strongest baselines PPFN (0.595 / 48.2) and DEAL (0.543 / 45.6).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HiDRA outperforms all 20 baselines on every metric in both FPN correction and blind super-resolution. The closest competitor in FPN correction is PPFN (LPIPS 0.159 vs. 0.127; FID 69.73 vs. 45.08), while traditional methods (GF, LRSID, SEID, UTV, WFAF) are 3–5× worse on LPIPS. In blind SR, CDFormer (LPIPS 0.207) and KXNet (0.214) are the strongest single-task baselines, yet HiDRA still leads by 0.088–0.095 LPIPS and 17.76–22.51 FID. Under composite degradation at extreme severity, PPFN and DEAL degrade sharply (LPIPS 0.550 and 0.741) while HiDRA stays at 0.230, and DifIISR (the best infrared-oriented SR method) reaches only 0.428. On real-world datasets, HiDRA surpasses PPFN on MI (e.g., 2.6126 vs. 2.5714 on TNO) and VIF (0.6077 vs. 0.6029), and clearly outperforms SD-based methods (DiffBIR, PASD, OSEDiff, PISA) which overfit to visible-domain priors. For downstream tasks, HiDRA achieves mAP 0.619 vs. PPFN 0.595 in object detection and mIoU 49.4 vs. PPFN 48.2 in semantic segmentation, confirming that the enhancement gains translate to practical perception improvements.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the one-step Stable Diffusion Turbo framework (Sauer et al., ECCV 2024) as a generative prior and the bi-level optimization formalism (Liu et al., TPAMI 2021) to solve a hierarchical adaptation problem. It departs from prior TIR enhancement methods (DEAL, PPFN) that treat degradation removal as deterministic regression, and from standard LoRA fine-tuning that uses fixed low-rank updates, by introducing a degradation-aware LoRA with a dynamic rank-r matrix Σ conditioned on estimated degradation vectors. The Degradation Estimation Module leverages DINOv2 features with a Mutual Interaction Attention mechanism, repurposing a self-supervised vision transformer backbone for degradation characterization rather than semantic understanding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical gap: visible-domain generative priors (Stable Diffusion) struggle with TIR's distinct color space, intensity distribution, and texture, and existing TIR methods lack robustness across varying degradation severities. The degradation-aware LoRA and hierarchical training strategy offer a reusable recipe for adapting large pre-trained generative models to sensor-specific domains beyond TIR. Limitations include training on only 1,503 images with a single A800 GPU, the M-pipeline ablation conducted at reduced resolution, and no analysis of inference latency for real-time deployment. Open questions include whether the bi-level formulation generalizes to multi-sensor fusion or video TIR enhancement, and whether the DINOv2-based DEM can be replaced by lighter-weight estimators for edge deployment.
