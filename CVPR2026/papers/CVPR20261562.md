## Problem

Diffusion and flow matching models suffer from a training-testing discrepancy (exposure bias): during training the prediction network receives ground-truth noisy interpolations, but during sampling it receives generated noisy data that has accumulated errors. This mismatch causes error accumulation and sampling drift, degrading generation quality. The paper identifies a specific structural pattern in this mismatch—the 'Slow Flow' phenomenon—where the generated noisy data at sampling timestep t is closest to the ground-truth interpolation at a higher-noise (slower) timestep, and uses this observation to design a targeted training fix.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261562/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

MixFlow is a post-training procedure applied to pretrained diffusion/flow matching models (SiT, REPA, RAE, SD 3.5), so the input and output formats are identical to the base model: class labels or text prompts plus Gaussian noise in, generated RGB images out (256×256 or 512×512 for ImageNet, 1024×1024 for SD 3.5). The only change is in how the training input is constructed: instead of feeding the single interpolation x_t at the sampled training timestep t, the network receives an interpolation x_{m_t} drawn from a range of higher-noise timesteps, while the timestep conditioning remains t. The sampling/inference procedure is unchanged from the base model.

## Method

MixFlow exploits the Slow Flow phenomenon: at sampling timestep t, the generated noisy data is nearest to the ground-truth interpolation at a slowed timestep m_t < t, with the gap growing as t increases. The training loss is modified so that the prediction network u_θ receives the interpolation x_{m_t} (computed with coefficients α_{m_t}, β_{m_t}) as its data input, while still being conditioned on the original training timestep t, and the target is the ground-truth velocity u*(x_{m_t}, m_t) at the slowed timestep. The slowed timestep m_t is sampled uniformly from [(1−γ)t, t], and the training timestep t is drawn from Beta(2,1) so that all (m_t, t) pairs are sampled uniformly. The mixture range coefficient γ (default 0.8) controls how far back in noise the slowed interpolations reach. Implementation requires only ~5 lines of code change to the standard training loop, and the method is applied as post-training (500K steps for SiT, 200 epochs for RAE) without modifying the architecture or sampling algorithm.

## Results

On ImageNet 256×256, MixFlow+RAE achieves a state-of-the-art gFID of 1.43 without guidance and 1.10 with guidance, outperforming all prior diffusion and autoregressive models. On ImageNet 512×512, MixFlow+RAE reaches 1.55 gFID (without guidance) and 1.10 gFID (with guidance), setting new records. For text-to-image generation on SD 3.5, MixFlow post-training improves GenEval average from 0.64 to 0.69 (40 steps) and DPG-Bench from 84.87 to 87.49, with consistent gains across all sub-metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MixFlow+RAE achieves the best gFID among all diffusion models on ImageNet 256×256 (1.43 w/o guidance, 1.10 w/ guidance), surpassing RAE (1.51/1.13), REPA-E (1.69/1.12), and REPA (5.84/1.28). On ImageNet 512×512 with guidance, MixFlow+RAE (1.10) edges out RAE (1.13), DDT (1.28), and EDM2 (1.25). In the direct exposure-bias comparison on SiT-B (Table 6), MixFlow (15.64/3.91 gFID) substantially outperforms Input Perturbation (17.01/4.32), Epsilon Scaling (17.18/4.39), and Time-Shift Sampler (18.12/4.49), with the gap widening at fewer sampling steps (e.g., 2.58 gFID gain at 20 steps vs. 2.33 at 250 steps). For text-to-image generation, MixFlow post-training of SD 3.5-ft-10K improves GenEval counting from 0.51 to 0.63 and T2I-CompBench spatial relation from 0.2855 to 0.3642 at 40 steps, outperforming the extra-training baseline SD 3.5-ft-20K on all six metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the flow matching framework (Lipman et al., ICLR 2022) and addresses the exposure bias problem that has been tackled through two prior lines: training modifications (Input Perturbation, Self Forcing) and sampling modifications (Epsilon Scaling, Time-Shift Sampler). MixFlow takes the training-modification route but departs conceptually by introducing the 'Slow Flow phenomenon' — the empirical observation that generated noisy data at timestep t is nearest to ground-truth data at a higher-noise (slowed) timestep — and uses this to construct a mixture of slowed interpolations as training inputs. Unlike Input Perturbation, which adds Gaussian noise to ground-truth data, or Self Forcing, which uses generated data (computationally expensive for many-step diffusion), MixFlow simply re-samples the interpolation coefficient from a range of higher-noise timesteps, requiring only ~5 lines of code change.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a trivially simple training modification (5 lines of code) that consistently improves FID across diverse architectures (SiT, REPA, RAE, SD 3.5) and achieves new SOTA on ImageNet (1.43/1.10 FID at 256×256, 1.55/1.10 at 512×512 with RAE). The finding that gains are larger with fewer sampling steps suggests MixFlow is particularly valuable for efficient generation settings. Open questions include whether the approach extends to video diffusion, whether the Beta(2,1) timestep distribution and γ=0.8 are truly optimal or merely convenient defaults, and whether combining MixFlow with inference-time corrections (e.g., Epsilon Scaling) yields further gains. The method's simplicity makes it a strong candidate for widespread adoption as a default post-training step.
