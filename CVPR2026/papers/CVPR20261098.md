## Problem

Large Vision-Language Models frequently omit or misrepresent critical visual details in generated image captions, yet quantifying this information loss across the image-to-text modality gap remains difficult. Existing approaches either rely on costly human annotations for fine-grained supervision or use VLM-based reward signals that are susceptible to reward hacking due to limited compositional reasoning. The paper tackles the open problem of measuring and minimizing cross-modal information loss without additional annotations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261098/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image as input and produces a free-form natural language caption as output. During training, each generated caption is used as a text query to retrieve top-K (K=5) image-text pairs from a corpus of ~1M samples (RefinedCaps augmented with DenseFusion-1M) via SBERT (MPNet-base) text encoding, while image embeddings are extracted with OpenCLIP ViT-H/14. The retrieved gallery images are then analyzed to compute the reward signal, making the retrieval corpus an implicit part of the input pipeline.

## Method

CIM formulates caption quality as a retrieval-based signal with two complementary metrics: Gallery Representation Consistency (GRC), which computes the L2 norm of the mean of ℓ2-normalized image embeddings of retrieved gallery images to measure caption detail richness, and Query-gallery Image Relevance (QIR), which computes an exponentially-decayed weighted sum of cosine similarities between the source image and retrieved images to measure caption precision. The combined reward Υ(v,c) = GRC(c) + β·QIR(v,c) is optimized via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), where G=5 captions are sampled per image and advantages are computed by normalizing rewards within each group. The key insight is that a fine-grained, accurate caption will retrieve a gallery of images that are both internally consistent (high GRC) and visually similar to the source (high QIR), effectively turning the modality conversion problem into an image-to-image similarity measurement without any human annotations or VLM-based reward models.

## Results

On COCO-LN500 with Qwen2.5-VL-7B, CIM achieves a CAPTURE score of 48.93, outperforming the base model (44.12) by 4.81 points and the strongest RL baseline CLIP-RL (46.71) by 2.22 points. The most dramatic gains appear on fine-grained sub-tasks: Attributes F1 improves from 39.90 (base) to 54.46 (+14.56), and Relations QA jumps from 23.76 to 44.15 (+20.39), a 20% relative improvement in relation reasoning. On DOCCI500, CIM reaches 63.46 CAPTURE (vs. 55.89 base) and 34.70 Relations QA (vs. 24.35 base), confirming consistent gains across both human-centric and non-human-centric benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CIM outperforms all five baselines across every metric on both benchmarks. Against the training-free methods VCD [24] and INTER [21], CIM leads by 9.17 and 4.88 CAPTURE points respectively on COCO-LN500, with the largest margins on Relations QA (+20.96 over VCD, +19.58 over INTER), indicating that inference-time decoding strategies cannot match the fine-grained detail captured by RL-trained captions. Among RL baselines, CLIP-RL [12] is the closest competitor on overall CAPTURE (46.71 vs. 48.93) but trails CIM by 5.07 on Attributes F1 and 20.35 on Relations QA, revealing that CLIP-based image-text similarity rewards are insufficient for compositional reasoning. Self Retrieval [18] similarly underperforms CIM by 2.84 CAPTURE and 18.11 Relations QA on COCO-LN500. Against SC-Captioner [83] (the strongest SFT+RL baseline in Table 4), CIM achieves +1.1 CAPTURE and +3.6 Relations QA on COCO-LN500 with Qwen2-VL-7B, and +1.0 CAPTURE and +5.8 Relations QA on DOCCI500, confirming that the retrieval-based reward signal provides complementary supervision beyond keyword-matching self-correction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RL-based image captioning line initiated by SC-Captioner (self-correction with keyword rewards) and CLIP-RL (image-text similarity rewards), but departs from both by replacing direct cross-modal similarity or VLM-based scoring with a retrieval-based proxy: the caption is used as a text query, and the consistency (GRC) and relevance (QIR) of the retrieved image gallery serve as the reward signal. This reframing sidesteps the reward-hacking problem identified in VLM-based metrics and avoids the annotation cost of supervised fine-tuning. The RL algorithm follows GRPO, consistent with recent practice, and the retrieval infrastructure (SBERT text encoder, OpenCLIP/DINOv3 image encoder) is standard, so the novelty lies in the reward formulation rather than the training pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The annotation-free retrieval-based reward offers a practical path to improving LVLM captioning without expensive human labeling, and the consistent gains across LLaVA, Qwen-VL, and InternVL architectures suggest broad applicability. However, the paper acknowledges entropy collapse as a limiting factor in RL training, and the method fails when all sampled captions are uniformly poor (optimization stagnation). An open question is whether the retrieval corpus can be scaled further to yield diminishing or accelerating returns, and whether the GRC/QIR formulation generalizes to video captioning or other modality-conversion tasks beyond image-to-text.
