## Problem

Large Vision-Language Models process hundreds of visual tokens, incurring high inference cost, and existing token pruning methods rely on attention or similarity criteria that suffer from positional bias and task-agnostic limitations. This paper asks whether the dynamic transition of token representations as they propagate through transformer modules can serve as a more reliable, bias-free signal of semantic importance for pruning decisions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261648/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

TransPrune operates on standard LVLM inputs: an RGB image encoded into a grid of visual tokens (e.g., 576 tokens for LLaVA-v1.5, more for LLaVA-Next) and a short text instruction. It produces a progressively pruned subset of visual tokens at designated layers (7, 9, 12), which then flow through the remaining LLM layers to generate a text response. The method is training-free, requires no retraining or fine-tuning, and is compatible with FlashAttention since TTV only accesses module inputs/outputs and IGA computes only instruction-to-image attention weights rather than full attention maps.

## Method

TransPrune estimates token importance via two complementary criteria. Token Transition Variation (TTV) measures how much each visual token's representation changes in magnitude (L2 norm ratio of output to input) and direction (cosine similarity) as it passes through the self-attention and FFN modules of each layer, computed as Softmax(1−|direction|)·magnitude per module and summed across both modules. Because TTV patterns are unstable in any single layer, an accumulation mechanism sums TTV scores across middle layers (7–12) up to each pruning layer, yielding a more stable importance signal. Instruction-Guided Attention (IGA) averages the attention weights from instruction tokens to each image token, providing task-relevance supervision that TTV lacks. The final pruning score is α·TTV + (1−α)·IGA (α=0.5), and tokens with the lowest scores are removed at each pruning stage. Crucially, TTV is computed per-token without inter-token dependencies, avoiding the positional bias inherent in attention-based scoring.

## Results

On LLaVA-v1.5-7B, TransPrune-High reduces inference to 40.8% of original TFLOPs while achieving MME 1540 (exceeding the full-model upper bound of 1506), GQA 61.4, and MMBench 66.0, outperforming all competing within-LLM pruning methods at comparable or lower compute budgets. TransPrune-Low further compresses to 31.2% TFLOPs while retaining MME 1491 and MMBench 65.6, surpassing PDrop (1468, 63.0) and SparseVLM (1441, 64.1) at similar budgets. On LLaVA-Next-7B, TransPrune-High at 40.0% TFLOPs achieves MME 1528 and POPE 86.9, and on Qwen2.5-VL-7B at 45.1% TFLOPs it reaches MME 1580 and POPE 87.5, both exceeding the respective full-model baselines on several metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

At the ~40–50% TFLOPs budget on LLaVA-v1.5-7B, TransPrune-High (40.8%) outperforms PDrop (46.6%) by +40 MME points (1540 vs 1500) and +1.1 MMBench points (66.0 vs 64.9), and beats SparseVLM (41.1%) by +56 MME points (1540 vs 1484) and +0.8 GQA points (61.4 vs 60.6), while using fewer TFLOPs than both. At the ~25–35% budget, TransPrune-Low (31.2%) surpasses PDrop (33.5%) by +23 MME points (1491 vs 1468) and +2.6 MMBench points (65.6 vs 63.0), and outperforms SparseVLM (33.5%) by +50 MME points (1491 vs 1441). On LLaVA-Next-7B, TransPrune-High (40.0%) leads PDrop (45.4%) by +17 MME points (1528 vs 1511) and +0.2 POPE points (86.9 vs 86.7) at lower compute. On Qwen2.5-VL-7B, TransPrune (45.1%) beats FastV (53.6%) by +17 MME points (1580 vs 1563) and +2.4 POPE points (87.5 vs 85.1) while using 8.5% fewer TFLOPs. Notably, TransPrune-High exceeds the full-model upper bound on MME (1540 vs 1506) and MMBench (66.0 vs 64.6) for LLaVA-v1.5, suggesting that pruning can occasionally improve performance by removing noisy tokens.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in LVLM token pruning, where importance is estimated via attention scores (FastV, SparseVLM, PDrop) or representational similarity (VisionZip, DivPrune, CDPruner). Rather than building on any single prior method, it introduces an orthogonal criterion—Token Transition Variation (TTV)—that measures how much a token's representation changes in magnitude and direction as it passes through self-attention and FFN modules, arguing that this dynamic signal is less susceptible to the positional bias and task-agnosticism that plague attention- and similarity-based approaches. The method is positioned as a within-LLM technique that complements (and can be stacked with) projector-based pruning, and its accumulation mechanism across middle layers draws on the observation that these layers integrate global and local context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TransPrune offers a practical, training-free recipe that cuts inference TFLOPs by more than half while preserving multimodal accuracy across three distinct LVLM architectures and a video model, and it remains compatible with FlashAttention—a property that makes it immediately deployable. The demonstration that a purely transition-based signal (TTV alone) is competitive without any attention computation challenges the assumption that attention is the primary or necessary route to token importance. Open questions include whether the middle-layer transition signal generalizes to architectures with very different depth or to non-visual modalities, whether the fixed accumulation window (layers 7–12) transfers without retuning, and whether the approach extends to dynamic or streaming settings where token counts vary per frame.
