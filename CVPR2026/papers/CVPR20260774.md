## Problem

Personalized Federated Learning must serve M clients with heterogeneous data distributions, but achieving optimal personalization ideally requires M distinct models on the Pareto front, which is infeasible at scale. Existing approaches either produce a single Pareto-optimal model without per-client personalization (FedMGDA, FedMTL) or resort to heuristic clustering/interpolation that lacks Pareto optimality guarantees. The paper asks: can a small set of K shared models (K ≪ M) achieve near-optimal personalization with rigorous convergence guarantees?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260774/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each of M clients holds a local dataset (32×32 or 64×64 RGB images, or text documents) drawn from a distinct distribution P_i, partitioned via Dirichlet(α=0.5) or natural institutional splits. The server maintains K models (K=3 in all experiments) and broadcasts them to all clients each round; clients compute gradients and losses for all K models locally and return them. Post-training, each client performs a forward pass with all K models on its local validation set and selects the single model with minimum loss as its personalized model, producing a class label at inference.

## Method

FedFew reformulates PFL as a K-for-M multi-objective optimization problem and solves it via a Smooth Tchebycheff Set Scalarization (STCH-Set), which applies log-sum-exp smoothing at two levels to make the nested max-min objective fully differentiable. The resulting gradient decomposes into an outer weight α_i (implementing hard-sample mining by upweighting clients poorly served by all models) and an inner weight w_ik (performing soft model selection by assigning higher weight to the model with lowest loss for each client). In the federated protocol, each client computes local gradients for all K models and sends them to the server, which aggregates using the STCH-Set weights to jointly update all K models. This two-level smoothing eliminates the need for manual client clustering or interpolation hyperparameters, allowing the optimization to automatically discover the optimal model diversity, with convergence to Pareto stationary points guaranteed by Theorem 4.2.

## Results

FedFew (K=3 server models) achieves 64.98% accuracy on CIFAR-100 (pathological, M=20), outperforming the best per-client baseline FedRep (61.46%) by 3.52%. Under practical heterogeneity, FedFew leads on CIFAR-100 (M=20) at 53.69% (+7.02% over FedRep), TinyImageNet at 30.31% (+3.07%), and AG News at 96.07% (+1.39%). On real-world medical imaging, FedFew attains 92.84% average and 83.90% worst-case accuracy on Kvasir, and 69.57% average with 55.40% minimum on FedISIC, substantially outperforming the clustering-based IFCA (82.05%/40.24% and 53.61%/23.23% respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedFew consistently outperforms all eight baselines across the majority of dataset-client configurations. Against the strongest per-client competitor FedRep, FedFew gains +3.52% on CIFAR-100 (pathological, M=20), +7.02% on CIFAR-100 (practical, M=20), +3.07% on TinyImageNet, and +1.39% on AG News, while matching FedRep's 100% on FEMNIST. The clustering-based IFCA, which also uses K shared models but relies on hard client-to-cluster assignment, is decisively outperformed: FedFew leads by +21.09% on CIFAR-100 (pathological, M=20) and by +10.82% average accuracy on Kvasir, with a dramatic worst-case improvement from 40.24% to 83.90%. Centralized baselines FedAvg and FedProx trail FedFew by 20–35 points on most datasets, confirming that a single global model cannot capture client heterogeneity. Among per-client methods, APFL and Ditto are competitive on CIFAR-10 (within ~1%) but fall 3–7 points behind FedFew on CIFAR-100 and TinyImageNet. FedMTL, the only other multi-objective approach, achieves comparable results on FEMNIST (100%) but lags FedFew by 2–7 points on image benchmarks, suggesting that its single Pareto-optimal model is insufficient for diverse client needs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Tchebycheff set scalarization (TCH-Set) from Lin et al. (ICLR 2025), originally designed for many-objective optimization, and adapts it to personalized federated learning via a 'few-for-many' (K-for-M) reformulation. It departs from the dominant PFL paradigms—clustering-based methods (IFCA, CFL) that require hard client partitioning, interpolation-based methods (APFL, Ditto) that blend global and local models with ad-hoc weights, and per-client methods (FedRep, FedBABU) that sacrifice collaboration—by instead jointly optimizing K shared server models through a smooth, differentiable objective. The key technical departure is a two-level log-sum-exp smoothing that transforms the discrete client-model assignment into a fully differentiable problem, enabling standard gradient-based federated training without manual clustering or hyperparameter tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The K-for-M framework provides the first PFL method with a clean error decomposition (Pareto coverage gap + statistical error) that gives system designers a principled knob (K) to trade personalization quality against communication cost, something heuristic methods lack. The finding that K=3 models consistently match or exceed methods maintaining M per-client models across seven datasets—including real-world medical imaging—suggests that most of the personalization benefit in PFL can be captured with very few shared models. Open questions include how the framework behaves under dynamic client populations (clients joining/leaving), whether the non-monotonic K-performance relationship observed on homogeneous data (where K=1 sometimes wins) persists on more heterogeneous benchmarks, and how the approach scales when M reaches thousands with highly skewed data distributions.
