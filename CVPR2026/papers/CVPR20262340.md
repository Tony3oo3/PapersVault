## Problem

Active search and persistent tracking of arbitrary targets by UAVs in cluttered environments remains challenging because existing methods either rely on heavy modular pipelines with explicit mapping (high computational cost, limited real-time agility) or end-to-end controllers that fail to generalize across targets and lack reliable recovery after tracking loss. Moreover, search and tracking are typically treated as independent tasks, ignoring their intrinsic interdependence where sustained long-term performance demands both robust in-frame tracking and active re-acquisition upon occlusion.

## Input / Output

The system consumes RGB-D observations (a single RGB frame and a depth frame from a forward-facing camera), the UAV's current velocity and acceleration in the camera frame, and a one-shot target template image extracted at initialization. It outputs a dynamically feasible 3D trajectory parameterized by a 5th-order polynomial over a short horizon T, from which position, velocity, and acceleration control commands are derived for low-level flight. The Rule-Based Point Search Policy additionally produces a 3D guidance point in the world frame that switches between tracking (from bounding box + median depth), Kalman-filter prediction (during brief occlusion), and exploration (spiral or farthest-point sampling during extended loss).

## Method

UAST couples a dual-branch perception module with a rule-based policy and a lightweight control network in a mapping-free pipeline. The Target Branch uses a shared-weight AlexNet backbone trained under the SiamFC++ protocol to perform template cross-correlation, yielding a bounding box and coarse mask encoded into a compact target feature; the Depth Branch uses ResNet-18 to encode geometric and navigability cues from the depth image. A Rule-Based Point Search Policy adaptively selects guidance points across three regimes—direct 3D back-projection when the target is reliably visible, Kalman-filter prediction under brief occlusion, and coverage-based spiral exploration upon extended loss—without any explicit map. The Control Network is a lightweight CNN operating on a V×H spatial grid that predicts per-cell end-state offsets and trajectory cost scores; the lowest-scoring cell defines the end state, and a 5th-order polynomial is fitted per axis to produce a smooth trajectory. Training employs a tracking-aware visibility loss combining differentiable SDF-based occlusion sampling along the UAV-target line of sight and FOV deviation penalties, alongside goal-alignment, smoothness, and safety terms, with an automated data pipeline that projects synthetic targets into real or simulated RGB-D scenes for diverse training.

## Results

On long-term tracking in cluttered environments, UAST achieves a 99% success rate at 3 m/s and 89% at 5 m/s, compared to 67% and 36% for the strongest baseline (Yopov2), representing a >50% improvement. The per-step cycle time is 8.6 ms, comparable to Yopov2's 7.0 ms and far faster than mapping-based methods (26–61 ms). For active search, UAST locates a target >100 m away in 54.5 s at 4.93 m/s average velocity, roughly 3× faster than RACER (176.6 s) and FALCON (146.7 s) while covering a similar path length.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UAST decisively outperforms both mapping-based trackers: Vis. [30] drops to 5% long-term success at 5 m/s and Elas. [15] to 3%, while UAST maintains 89%, owing to its mapping-free design and unified search-tracking recovery. Against the closest learning-based competitor Yopov2 [25], UAST improves long-term success from 67%→99% (3 m/s) and 36%→89% (5 m/s), with only a 1.6 ms increase in cycle time (8.6 vs. 7.0 ms); Yopov2's multi-target variant degrades further (63% at 5 m/s short-range). In active search, UAST completes the task in 54.5 s versus 176.6 s for RACER [46] and 146.7 s for FALCON [41], achieving ~3× speedup at a higher average velocity (4.93 vs. 1.66/1.81 m/s) with the lowest cycle time (8.6 vs. 43.1/37.2 ms). On short-range tracking (Table 1), UAST matches Yopov2 at 3 m/s (both 1.00) but leads at 4–6 m/s (1.00/0.93/0.88 vs. 0.90/0.90/0.80), while mapping-based methods collapse above 4 m/s.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two dominant paradigms in UAV tracking: (1) modular pipelines that maintain explicit voxel maps and perform multi-stage planning (Vis. [30], Elas. [15]), and (2) purely end-to-end learned controllers (Yopov2 [25]) that lack reliable recovery after target loss. It builds on SiamFC++ [37] for template-based target localization but repurposes it within a unified, mapping-free framework where a rule-based point search policy bridges the gap between tracking and exploration—something prior works treat as separate sub-problems. The tracking-aware visibility loss, which uses differentiable SDF sampling to enforce line-of-sight and FOV constraints during trajectory optimization, is a novel training objective not present in either the model-based or end-to-end baselines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical unification of active search and persistent tracking in a single mapping-free pipeline addresses a real deployment bottleneck: modular systems incur high memory and compute costs from explicit mapping, while end-to-end systems fail to recover from occlusion. The ~3× speedup over frontier-based exploration methods (RACER, FALCON) and the 50%+ tracking success improvement at high speeds suggest that tightly coupling perception, a lightweight rule-based policy, and a small control network can match or exceed heavier architectures. Key limitations include reliance on a single forward-facing RGB-D sensor (restricting safe retreat) and the search comparison being against general exploration methods rather than target-specific search baselines, leaving open whether the rule-based policy generalizes to multi-target or adversarial scenarios.
