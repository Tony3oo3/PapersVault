## Problem

The transition from mechanically scanned LiDARs to compact solid-state SPAD arrays introduces a severe new failure mode: internal-multipath glare, where light from bright or retroreflective surfaces scatters within the shared optics and spreads across the pixel array, creating phantom objects and obscuring real ones. Legacy scanning systems avoided this by design (dedicated optics per detector, one active detector at a time), so widely used datasets exhibit minimal glare and the problem has remained largely unaddressed. Existing learning-based corrections operate after point-cloud formation, where artifacts are already entangled with geometry, and require synthetic training data tied to specific reflector geometries.

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw per-pixel transient histograms from a dToF SPAD LiDAR (e.g., 192×256 array, 4096 timing bins per frame), from which it extracts up to K=3 echoes per pixel (intensity, mean ToF, ToF variance) via matched-filter peak detection. It also requires a pre-calibrated 2D GSF (measured with an IR flashlight at 49 FOV positions and interpolated) and sensor-specific timing parameters (dead time, transmitted pulse shape, background count rate). The output is a glare-suppressed depth map formed by selecting the highest-confidence echo per pixel, along with per-echo binomial-likelihood confidence scores that can be propagated downstream into existing LiDAR DSP pipelines.

## Method

The core insight is that internal glare in a dToF LiDAR is a linear, scene-independent operator—the Transient Glare Spread Function (TGSF)—that, because internal path-length differences are negligible relative to sensor time resolution, reduces to a time-independent 2D GSF acting independently on each time slice. The pipeline first corrects photon pileup (which co-occurs with glare at high flux) using a moments-based approach: it estimates the true echo intensity from the measured ToF variance via an inverse lookup table, then corrects the mean ToF shift, avoiding the saturation of Coates's method at 1–10 photons/pulse. Glare contribution at each echo is then predicted by a backprojection of pileup-corrected intensities through the GSF, weighted by a temporal overlap function o(δt) that accounts for non-aligned fitting windows across pixels. Finally, a binomial-likelihood confidence metric C = −ln P(Y; P_G, N) (when Y ≥ NP_G, else 0) quantifies whether each echo contains signal in excess of predicted glare, and the highest-confidence echo per pixel is selected to form the depth map—entirely training-free and operating prior to point-cloud formation.

## Results

The paper evaluates its method on a commercial SP-LiDAR (ADS6311, 192×256 SPAD array) across multiple scenes containing diverse retroreflectors (stop sign, license plate, traffic cones, high-vis vest, traffic drum). Quantitative metrics reported are RMSE (in meters, lower is better) and δ₁ (fraction of pixels within 1% relative depth error, higher is better), shown as insets in Figures 4 and 6. The method substantially suppresses phantom returns and ghost objects while preserving true scene structure, including a black mannequin (weak signal) occluded by bloom from a co-located stop sign and traffic drum. No single consolidated numerical table is provided in the text; quantitative values are embedded in figure insets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Two baselines are compared. First, the deep learning method of Cheong et al. [5] (ICCE-Asia 2024) models the GSF as an exponentially decaying function and is trained on synthetic data with simple retroreflector geometries (octagons, rectangles). The proposed method outperforms it on complex geometries such as traffic cones and high-vis vests, where the learning-based approach struggles to generalize; the proposed method is also training-free and scene-independent, whereas [5] requires explicit retroreflector geometry assumptions. Second, the photographic de-glare method of Talvala et al. [25] (ACM TOG 2007) is applied independently to each time slice of the datacube. When pileup is mitigated via a 4-OD neutral density filter (10⁴ attenuation), both methods successfully suppress glare. However, without the NDF, severe pileup induces range-walk error that shifts direct returns to earlier time bins while the fainter glare signal remains unshifted, causing the per-slice linear de-glare to over-predict glare in early slices and fail to remove true glare. The proposed method, operating on pileup-corrected echoes rather than raw time slices, successfully suppresses glare in both the attenuated and unattenuated (severe pileup) regimes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the linear Glare Spread Function (GSF) model from passive imaging (Talvala et al., 2007) into the transient (time-of-flight) domain, formulating glare as a scene-independent linear operator on SPAD histogram data. It departs from recent learning-based LiDAR blooming methods [5, 27] that operate post-point-cloud-formation on synthetic data, instead working pre-formation on raw echoes with a training-free, physics-based approach. The pileup correction step builds on moments-based estimation from Sheehan et al. [23], adapted to handle the extreme fluxes produced by retroreflectors where Coates's method saturates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Internal-multipath glare is a safety-critical failure mode for the next generation of solid-state LiDARs that legacy scanning systems avoided through hardware design, and this paper provides the first physically grounded, training-free algorithm to address it on unmodified commercial sensors. The demonstration that naive per-time-slice photographic de-glaring fails under pileup (due to range-walk separating aggressor and glare into different slices) clarifies why the problem cannot be reduced to standard HDR imaging. Open questions include robustness in outdoor traffic scenes with intense solar background, GSF degradation under dust/condensation, and whether the approach generalizes across sensor architectures beyond the single ADS6311 tested.
