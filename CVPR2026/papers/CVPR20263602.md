## Problem

Existing atmospheric turbulence synthesis methods treat exposure-time as a fixed or binary (short/long) variable, producing synthetic images with abrupt blur transitions that fail to capture the smooth, continuous blur dynamics observed in real imaging systems. This limitation results in unrealistic training data and poor generalization of turbulence mitigation models when deployed on real-world imagery with intermediate exposure settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263602/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The synthesis pipeline consumes clean RGB video frames (sourced from SVW and TSR-WGAN) along with seven physical parameters—propagation distance (30–1000 m), focal length (0.1–1 m), F-number, refractive index structure parameter C_n², altitude, wind speed, and a continuous exposure-time τ in [0.5, 40] ms—sampled across 12 predefined configurations. It outputs fully degraded turbulence videos, plus intermediate products of isolated geometric tilt and isolated blur, enabling multi-stage training. The resulting ET-Turb dataset comprises 5,083 videos (2,005,835 frames) split into 3,988 training and 1,095 test videos, alongside a curated ET-Turb-Real benchmark of 74 real-world videos from three distinct imaging devices.

## Method

The core contribution is an Exposure-Time-dependent MTF (ET-MTF) derived from Azoulay's finite-exposure theory, where the effective coherence length ρ_p(τ) = 1 + 0.35(r₀/(D + v_w·τ))^{1/3} smoothly interpolates between the short-exposure MTF (with aperture-limited high-frequency preservation) and the long-exposure MTF (pure Kolmogorov decay) as τ varies continuously. An inverse Fourier transform of the ET-MTF yields a tilt-invariant PSF that encodes pure blur independent of phase distortions. To capture spatial heterogeneity, the scalar blur-width ω is extended to a spatially varying field W(x, τ) modeled as a correlated Gaussian random field with mean and standard deviation prescribed by optical turbulence statistics (Eqs. 14–15), and position-dependent PSFs are applied via spatially varying convolution. For video sequences, inter-frame correlation is enforced through Taylor's frozen-flow hypothesis, displacing the degradation field along the wind direction by f·v_w·t/L between frames. This pipeline is used to generate ET-Turb, and experiments with four SOTA restoration models (TSR-WGAN, TMT, DATUM, MambaTM) demonstrate that continuous exposure modeling yields superior no-reference quality (NIQE, BRISQUE) on real turbulence data compared to fixed or binary exposure baselines.

## Results

Across four state-of-the-art turbulence mitigation models (TSR-WGAN, TMT, DATUM, MambaTM) evaluated zero-shot on the ET-Turb-Real benchmark, models trained on ET-Turb consistently achieve the lowest NIQE and BRISQUE scores. For example, MambaTM trained on ET-Turb reaches NIQE 4.212 and BRISQUE 55.050, outperforming the same model trained on TMT-dynamic (4.217 / 55.062) and ATSyn-dynamic (4.247 / 56.876). The largest gains appear on TSR-WGAN, where ET-Turb training yields NIQE 4.190 and BRISQUE 50.981 versus 4.231/52.502 (TMT-dynamic) and 4.224/54.462 (ATSyn-dynamic).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ET-Turb against two existing synthetic turbulence datasets: TMT-dynamic [53] and ATSyn-dynamic [52], using four restoration models as probes. ET-Turb wins on every model–metric combination: for instance, MambaTM trained on ET-Turb achieves NIQE 4.212 vs. 4.217 (TMT-dynamic) and 4.247 (ATSyn-dynamic), and BRISQUE 55.050 vs. 55.062 and 56.876 respectively. The advantage is most pronounced on TSR-WGAN, where ET-Turb reduces BRISQUE by ~1.5 points over TMT-dynamic and ~3.5 points over ATSyn-dynamic. On TMT, the gap over ATSyn-dynamic is the largest (NIQE 4.221 vs. 4.483; BRISQUE 56.691 vs. 59.707), suggesting ATSyn-dynamic's binary exposure modeling is particularly detrimental to that architecture. Overall, the margins are modest (0.01–0.3 in NIQE, 0.1–5 in BRISQUE) but uniformly in ET-Turb's favor, supporting the claim that continuous exposure-time modeling yields more realistic synthetic data and better cross-domain generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Azoulay's finite-exposure MTF theory [3] from its original two-regime (short/long exposure) formulation to a continuous exposure-time parameterization, building on the tilt-then-blur decomposition of Chan [10] and Charnotskii [11] and the stochastic tilt model of Schwartzman et al. [43]. It departs from the dominant Zernike-polynomial-based simulation approaches (TurbSim, P2S, DF-P2S) that are fundamentally constrained to binary exposure regimes, and from computationally prohibitive multi-phase-screen methods, by deriving a tilt-invariant PSF directly from the ET-MTF and coupling it with a spatially varying blur-width field whose statistics follow Du Bosq and Repasi [14]. The key conceptual shift is treating exposure-time as a continuous physical parameter that smoothly modulates the effective coherence length, rather than as a discrete label selecting between two fixed MTFs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ET-Turb dataset (5,083 videos, 2M+ frames) fills a practical gap: prior synthetic turbulence datasets either fix exposure or use binary short/long categories, producing training data whose blur statistics do not match the continuous range encountered in real imaging. By explicitly modeling exposure-time as continuous, the dataset narrows the synthetic-to-real domain gap, and the ablation confirms that this matters for downstream restoration quality. Open questions include whether the Taylor frozen-flow assumption for inter-frame correlation remains valid at the upper end of the 40 ms exposure range, whether the single-equivalent-screen approximation captures multi-scale turbulence adequately at very long propagation distances, and whether the approach extends naturally to anisotropic or non-Kolmogorov turbulence spectra.
