## Problem

Existing VLM benchmarks are limited to single-agent settings or text-only multi-agent environments, leaving unexplored how well VLMs handle the coupled challenges of multimodal perception and strategic interaction in multi-agent scenarios. Real-world multi-agent settings require theory-of-mind reasoning, decision-making under non-stationary dynamics, and balancing cooperation with competition—abilities that text-only or single-agent benchmarks cannot assess.

## Input / Output

Each VLM agent receives a multimodal observation consisting of a game frame image (rendered from the environment) and a text description of the current game state, formatted as a POMG observation. The model must produce one of three output types depending on the evaluation dimension: a structured label identifying visual elements (e.g., object positions, agent orientations), a predicted next action for another agent (selected from that agent's discrete action space), or its own action decision (a text action mapped to the environment's action space via a mapping function). All models use temperature 1.0 with up to 8k output tokens and 16k reasoning tokens for reasoning models.

## Method

VS-BENCH introduces ten vision-grounded multi-agent environments adapted from game theory and MARL literature, spanning cooperative (Hanabi, Overcooked, KAZ), competitive (Breakthrough, Kuhn Poker, Atari Pong, MPE), and mixed-motive (Coin Dilemma, Monster Hunt, Battle of the Colors) interactions, each modeled as a POMG with multimodal observation and text action spaces. The evaluation framework decomposes strategic ability into three measurable dimensions: perception via element recognition accuracy on 400 ground-truth samples per environment, strategic reasoning via next-action prediction accuracy on a curated balanced dataset, and decision-making via normalized episode return (random=0, oracle=100) through self-play or interaction with conventional agents. Fifteen VLMs—six commercial reasoning models, six commercial chat models, and three open-source models—are evaluated under identical protocols, with additional analyses on text-only vs. multimodal observations, CoT prompting as test-time scaling, social persona effects, and human baselines (26 participants) to contextualize model performance.

## Results

Across 15 VLMs evaluated on 10 multi-agent environments, the best-performing model (o3) achieves 84.9% overall perception accuracy, 46.6% strategic reasoning (next-action prediction) accuracy, and 31.4% normalized episode return for decision-making, all far below the 100% oracle. Four out of fifteen models perform worse than random on decision-making (overall normalized return ≤ 0), and even the top model surpasses only 12.9% of human participants (who average 62.7% normalized return). Perception is relatively strong (all models ≥ 67.8%), but strategic reasoning and decision-making reveal a significant capability gap, with the deficit most pronounced in video-game-derived environments (Overcooked, Pong, Monster Hunt).

## Baselines & Comparisons

o3 leads across all three evaluation dimensions (84.9% perception, 46.6% reasoning, 31.4% decision-making), ranking first in 6 of 10 environments for strategic reasoning. Commercial reasoning models (o3, gemini-2.5-pro, claude-3-7-sonnet, doubao-1-5-thinking-pro, qvq-max) consistently outperform their non-thinking counterparts and chat models, with o3 achieving a 6.2-point margin over the second-best (claude-3-7-sonnet at 40.4%) on reasoning. The three open-source models (Qwen2.5-VL-72B, InternVL3-78B, Llama-3.2-90B) average 29.2% reasoning accuracy, comparable to commercial chat models at 30.3%, but in decision-making, open-source models like InternVL3-78B (5.9% in Coin Dilemma) and Qwen2.5-VL-72B (19.6% in Monster Hunt) achieve results competitive with top reasoning models in specific mixed-motive games. On decision-making, the gap is stark: o3's 31.4% overall return is less than half the human average of 62.7%, and four models (qvq-max, gpt-4.1, gemini-2.5 w/o thinking, qwen-vl-max, claude-3-7 w/o thinking, doubao-1-5-vision-pro, grok-2-vision, Llama-3.2) score at or below random (0%). CoT prompting significantly improves chat models' decision-making but does not close the gap to reasoning models, confirming that test-time scaling helps but is insufficient to match dedicated reasoning architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established but previously disjoint lines of work: multi-agent LLM benchmarks (LLM-Coordination, GT-Bench, GAMA-Bench, MAgIC, LLMArena) that evaluate strategic reasoning in text-only settings, and VLM agent benchmarks (coding, GUI, game, embodied) that evaluate perception and action in single-agent settings. VS-BENCH repurposes well-known MARL and game-theory environments (Hanabi, Overcooked, KAZ, Breakthrough, Kuhn Poker, Pong, MPE, social dilemmas) and adapts them to provide multimodal (image + text) observations, thereby creating the first benchmark that jointly requires visual perception and multi-agent strategic reasoning from a single VLM. The problem formulation as a POMG with multimodal observation spaces and a text-to-action mapping is a natural but previously unexplored generalization of both the MARL evaluation setup and the VLM agent evaluation setup.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a stark and actionable gap: VLMs achieve 84.9% perception accuracy but only 46.6% strategic reasoning and 31.4% decision-making, with four of fifteen models performing below random in decision-making. This suggests that current VLMs' strong visual grounding does not transfer to the theory-of-mind and long-horizon planning required in multi-agent settings, pointing to a clear research direction for improving strategic capabilities. Open questions include whether test-time scaling (reasoning, CoT) can close the gap, how social personas and cooperative tendencies affect equilibrium selection in mixed-motive games, and whether the perception-reasoning gap narrows with future model generations. The human study (62.7% vs. 31.4% for o3) quantifies the remaining distance to human-level strategic play.
