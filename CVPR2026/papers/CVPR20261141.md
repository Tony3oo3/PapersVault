## Problem

Standard perception evaluation metrics (AP, IoU, F1) treat each detection independently and provide no information about how reliably a model detects objects as a function of distance. In autonomous driving, detection confidence fluctuates stochastically with range, and fixed-threshold detection leads to inconsistent decisions that directly affect safety-critical control. The paper asks: what is the maximum distance at which a perception system can be trusted to produce reliable detections, accounting for both mean performance degradation and output variability?

## Input / Output

The method consumes sequential front-view images (1920×1080) from the SensorRainFall dataset spanning 4–250 m under four controlled environmental conditions, paired with ground-truth bounding boxes, instance segmentation masks, and precise distance measurements. For each image, the perception model's output is reduced to a single scalar: the IoU × confidence score of the highest-confidence detection for the target class. The primary output is the PCD (a single distance in meters), and the aggregated aPCD is computed as the volume under the PCD surface over a grid of (p^thres, y^thres) threshold pairs, yielding a single scalar summary of overall perception reliability.

## Method

PCD is computed in two stages. First, a penalized cubic B-spline regression (K=10 basis functions, λ=0.6) is fitted to the IoU × confidence scores as a function of distance to capture the nonlinear mean trend. Second, variance change points are detected via a sequential hypothesis-testing procedure built on the Schwartz information criterion (Gao et al., 2019), which partitions the distance axis into segments of approximately constant variance; within each segment the scores are modeled as Gaussian with segment-level standard deviation. PCD is then the largest distance x_i at which the tail probability P(y_i > y^thres) exceeds a user-specified probabilistic threshold p^thres, jointly encoding both the expected detection quality and its local uncertainty. The aPCD aggregates PCD values across a range of (p^thres, y^thres) pairs by computing the volume under the resulting PCD surface, analogous to how AUC summarizes ROC performance across thresholds.

## Results

Across 16 experiments (4 environmental conditions × 2 target objects × 2 perception tasks), the paper evaluates 10 state-of-the-art models using both the proposed aPCD metric and traditional metrics (AP₅₀:₉₅, AP₅₀, AR, F1₅₀). In 11 of 16 experiments, aPCD rankings align with traditional metrics (e.g., Mask2Former achieves the highest aPCD of 107.1 m and highest AP₅₀:₉₅ of 0.423 for vehicle segmentation in clear daylight). In the remaining 5 experiments, aPCD reveals different model rankings: for vehicle detection in rainy night, GLIP achieves the highest aPCD (37.3 m) while DyHead leads on AP₅₀:₉₅ (0.144); for pedestrian segmentation in clear daylight, Mask2Former has the highest aPCD (23.5 m) while ConvNeXt-V2 leads on AP₅₀:₉₅ (0.068). These discrepancies demonstrate that aPCD captures distance-dependent reliability and output stability that frame-level metrics like AP and AR fail to reflect.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new perception model but a new evaluation metric (PCD/aPCD), so comparisons center on how model rankings shift between aPCD and traditional metrics. In Example 1 (vehicle segmentation, clear daylight), rankings are consistent: Mask2Former leads on both aPCD (107.1 m) and AP₅₀:₉₅ (0.423), while SOLOv2 trails on both (36.6 m aPCD, 0.233 AP₅₀:₉₅). In Example 2 (vehicle detection, rainy night), GLIP achieves the highest aPCD (37.3 m) despite DyHead scoring higher on all traditional metrics (AP₅₀:₉₅ 0.144 vs. 0.133, F1₅₀ 0.534 vs. 0.451); the paper attributes this to GLIP's lower output variance and shorter 'failure tail' beyond 200 m. In Example 3 (pedestrian segmentation, clear daylight), Mask2Former edges out ConvNeXt-V2 on aPCD (23.5 m vs. 23.4 m) while ConvNeXt-V2 leads on AP₅₀:₉₅ (0.068 vs. 0.055) and F1₅₀ (0.303 vs. 0.272), because Mask2Former produces more stable, lower-variance IoU×confidence scores at short ranges. Overall, aPCD reorders model rankings in 5 of 16 experiments, revealing that models with slightly lower aggregate AP can be more reliable in terms of consistent detection over distance—a distinction critical for safety-critical ADS operation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions PCD as a departure from static, frame-level perception metrics (AP, IoU, F1) and from recent uncertainty-aware extensions (PDQ, JIoU, GIoU) by explicitly modeling detection quality as a stochastic function of distance. It draws on variance change point detection (Hawkins & Zamba; Gao et al.) and penalized spline regression to segment the distance axis into statistically homogeneous regions, a technique borrowed from time-series and quality-control literature rather than from perception evaluation. The SensorRainFall dataset extends the line of controlled-environment driving benchmarks (SHRP 2, Virginia Smart Roads) but is distinguished by its focus on rainfall as the sole varied factor with all other conditions held constant.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PCD introduces a practical 'safety envelope' concept for ADS/ADAS operators, letting them set probabilistic and quality thresholds to determine the maximum reliable detection range under specific conditions. The SensorRainFall dataset fills a notable gap as the only publicly available controlled-environment dataset with multi-sensor (camera, radar, LiDAR) annotations across weather and illumination conditions. However, the evaluation scope is narrow—two target objects, a single ego vehicle, and four environmental conditions—limiting generalizability. Open questions include whether PCD's variance-change-point segmentation remains stable with more diverse object classes, longer sequences, and uncontrolled naturalistic driving data, and whether the metric's two-threshold parameterization can be simplified for routine deployment.
