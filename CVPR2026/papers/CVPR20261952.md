## Problem

Existing optimal coding-scheme designs for indirect time-of-flight (I-ToF) cameras either assume idealized sensors with unlimited power and bandwidth (Gupta et al.) or rely on computationally expensive deep-learning optimization (Li et al.), and neither accounts for the exclusive multi-tap mechanism that restricts only one demodulation gate to be active at any instant on commercial sensors. This paper asks whether one can derive a practical depth-variance metric and search for near-optimal coding schemes directly within the constrained hardware space, without large-scale training or idealized assumptions.

## Input / Output

The design procedure takes discrete hardware parameters (peak laser power, maximum modulation frequency yielding a minimum switchable duration t_min = 2/f_max, binary ON/OFF waveforms, and the exclusive multi-tap constraint) together with a scene albedo and ambient-light level that set the operating SNR. It outputs a pair of binary sequences — a modulation sequence S_M and three demodulation sequences S_D0, S_D1, S_D2 — each of length n = τ/t_min, where the demodulation sequences satisfy per-slot exclusivity (at most one tap active) and equal total ON-duration. At sensing time, the system captures three brightness measurements per pixel from a 3-tap sensor (e.g., 240×240 BEM80T04BB) and decodes depth via zero-mean normalized cross-correlation against a precomputed lookup table.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage framework: (1) a noise-propagation analysis that linearizes the ZNCC decoder around the true depth under a depth-dependent Gaussian noise model (photon shot + readout noise), yielding a closed-form depth-variance expression (Eq. 16) that depends on the projected correlation-curve velocity weighted by per-tap noise variance; and (2) a constrained combinatorial search over binary modulation/demodulation sequences that minimizes a range-integrated variance metric γ (Eq. 26) subject to peak-power scaling, bandwidth-imposed minimum pulse width, binary values, exclusive multi-tap, equal demodulation length, and non-ambiguity constraints. The key insight is that the optimal coding scheme is SNR-dependent rather than fixed, so the authors search separately for high-SNR and low-SNR optima by varying the ambient-light parameter. Because the hardware constraints reduce the search space to binary sequences of modest length (n ≈ 9–18 slots for 90–180 ns periods at 100 MHz), brute-force or simulated-annealing search is tractable, eliminating the need for deep-learning optimization or post-hoc approximation of ideal curves.

## Results

In the motion experiment (Sec. 5.4), the proposed-low scheme at 5 ms integration achieves MAE = 3.33 cm and STD = 1.87 cm on a static board at 90 cm, compared to MAE = 5.14 cm and STD = 3.89 cm for the original double-ramp scheme at 15 ms integration — a 35% MAE reduction with 3× shorter exposure. In simulation (Fig. 4), both proposed-high and proposed-low consistently outperform double ramp and Hamiltonian across all ambient-light levels (ā = 0–10), with the two proposed schemes swapping relative order at ā ≈ 0.2. On the Stanford bunny reconstruction (Fig. 5), proposed-high achieves the lowest MAE at high SNR (ā = 0) and proposed-low at low SNR (ā = 1), while both baselines show substantially larger errors in the low-SNR setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: the double-ramp coding scheme (Han et al. [12], the sensor's native scheme) and the practical Hamiltonian coding (Gutierrez-Barragan et al. [10]). In simulation, both proposed schemes outperform both baselines across the full range of ambient-light strengths; Hamiltonian slightly beats double ramp due to its 1/3 total power but is still inferior to the searched codes. In real experiments, the ranking shifts: Hamiltonian significantly outperforms double ramp in low-SNR conditions (contradicting simulation), yet both proposed schemes still achieve the best MAE and reconstruction quality in both high- and low-SNR settings. The most striking comparison is in the motion experiment, where 5 ms proposed-low (MAE 3.33 cm, STD 1.87 cm) beats 15 ms double ramp (MAE 5.14 cm, STD 3.89 cm) by a wide margin while using one-third the integration time, demonstrating superior integration-time efficiency and reduced motion blur.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three prior threads in I-ToF coding design: Gupta et al.'s [9] analytical depth-variance framework (coding curve length as a metric), Li et al.'s [20] practical depth-dependent noise model, and Gutierrez et al.'s [10] hardware constraint set (peak power, bandwidth, binary waveforms). The key departure is incorporating the exclusive multi-tap demodulation constraint—where only one tap can be active at any instant—directly into the design space rather than as a post-hoc approximation, and using the combined metric to drive a brute-force or simulated-annealing search over discrete binary sequences. Unlike Li et al., no deep-learning training or scene statistics are required; unlike Gupta et al., the metric accounts for brightness-dependent noise and non-ideal decoding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is that I-ToF sensor designers can now search for near-optimal coding schemes under real hardware limits without expensive simulation pipelines or large annotated datasets, and the finding that the optimal code is SNR-dependent (requiring different schemes for high vs. low ambient light) challenges the assumption of a single universal coding curve. The demonstration that a 5 ms integration with the proposed-low code outperforms a 15 ms integration with the sensor's native double-ramp code (MAE 3.33 vs. 5.14 cm) suggests meaningful FPS gains for motion capture. Open questions include whether the small-noise linearization holds under strong multi-path interference, whether non-periodic or segmented coding can further improve precision, and whether the approach generalizes to sensors with more than three taps or different exclusive-mechanism architectures.
