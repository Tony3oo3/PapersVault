## Problem

Separating reflected and emitted thermal radiation in long-wave infrared imaging is a long-standing under-determined problem, and existing approaches rely on assumptions (hot objects, static backgrounds, known emissivity) that break down in near-ambient scenes where both components are comparable in magnitude, time-varying, and spatially complex. No prior method jointly recovers emissivity, object temperature, and background radiation from dual-band thermal video without a priori emissivity knowledge in such dynamic settings.

## Input / Output

The method consumes dual-band thermal video (two spectral sub-bands within the 8–14 μm LWIR range, e.g., 8.5 μm and 9.5 μm filters) captured at 640×512 resolution with FLIR Boson microbolometer cameras, along with per-band Sakuma-Hattori calibration constants (a_m, b_m) that linearize the pixel-intensity-to-temperature mapping near ambient conditions. In the calibrated variant, pre-measured per-band emissivities from thermocouple-based calibration are additionally required. The output is a per-pixel decomposition into a time-varying emission video, a mean-subtracted reflection video, the object temperature trajectory T_o(t), the effective background temperature T_b(t), and (in the uncalibrated case) the per-band emissivity ε_m.

## Method

The core model is a per-pixel linear combination I_m(t) = ε_m·a_m·T_o(t) + (1−ε_m)·a_m·T_b(t) + b_m, where the near-ambient linearity of the Sakuma-Hattori function collapses the system to rank 2, making the uncalibrated case ill-posed. The key insight is a temporal asymmetry: object emission evolves smoothly per the heat-transport ODE (exponential decay), while background reflection changes abruptly and uncorrelated with the object. The uncalibrated algorithm exploits this via two ratio constraints—k_1 = ε_2/ε_1 from pixels with static background (differential form) and k_2 = (1−ε_2)/(1−ε_1) from the residual after subtracting a smooth emission-dominant signal—and jointly optimizes the smooth signal, emissivities, and a per-pixel noise term under a composite objective combining second-order smoothness, normalized Huber fidelity, MSE reconstruction, and L2/mean noise regularization. In the calibrated case, known emissivities yield a closed-form 2×2 linear solve for T_o and T_b per frame (Eq. 14), avoiding the optimization entirely.

## Results

On real-world thermal videos, the uncalibrated method achieves 1.72% temperature error on the wineglass scene and 5.34% on the coffee pot scene (validated against thermocouple contact measurements), while the calibrated method achieves 5.04% and 0.36% respectively. In simulation, the method consistently outperforms all baselines across noise levels, with the reconstruction loss contributing 90.10% of total error reduction in ablation. For emissivity calibration, the method recovers values within 0.02 of reference values for high-emissivity materials (e.g., 0.93 vs 0.95 for glass jar) and dramatically improves over the FLIR reflector method for low-emissivity surfaces (0.12 vs 0.43 for chrome ball).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Blackbody Channel Prior (BCP) [9], the uncalibrated method reduces wineglass temperature error from 14.6% to 1.72% (an 8.5× improvement) and coffee pot error from 6.62% to 5.34%; the calibrated variant further drops coffee pot error to 0.36%. Against the naive least-squares baseline, improvements are dramatic: 31.68% → 1.72% (wineglass) and 45.5% → 5.34% (coffee pot), confirming that the temporal smoothness and spectral ratio constraints are essential. In simulation (Fig. 2), the method outperforms both BCP and dual-wavelength pyrometry [39] across all noise levels, with the advantage most pronounced at moderate noise where baselines degrade sharply. For emissivity calibration, the proposed technique matches the FLIR reflector method [10] on high-emissivity materials (within 0.02–0.04) but substantially outperforms it on low-emissivity surfaces (chrome ball: 0.12 vs 0.43; aluminum cup: 0.10 vs 0.16), because the dual-band approach leverages specular reflections rather than a separate reflector plate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the thermal imaging decomposition literature from single-point pyrometry and static-background assumptions to spatially-varying video in near-ambient conditions, where reflected and emitted radiation are comparable in magnitude. It builds directly on Ramanagopal et al.'s [28] joint light-and-heat transport theory, which provides the exponential ODE prior for smooth object temperature evolution, and improves upon Nagase et al.'s [22] narcissus-effect correction by applying both gain and offset rather than offset-only. The key conceptual departure from prior dual-wavelength pyrometry [39] and the Blackbody Channel Prior [9] is the explicit joint exploitation of spectral emissivity ratios and temporal smoothness differences between heat transport (slow) and background reflections (abrupt), rather than assuming one component is negligible or parameterized.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration of physically-grounded reflection-emission separation in dynamic, near-ambient real-world scenes, validated against contact thermometry with sub-2% error on some objects. The uncalibrated formulation is particularly impactful because it removes the need for prior emissivity knowledge, making the approach applicable to arbitrary materials. Key limitations include the assumption that background changes are uncorrelated with object emission (which fails in uniformly warming rooms) and the SNR constraints of microbolometer sensors for low-emissivity surfaces. Open questions include whether the framework extends to more than two bands, whether the uncorrelated assumption can be relaxed with learned priors, and whether the approach generalizes to non-Lambertian or volumetric thermal scenes.
