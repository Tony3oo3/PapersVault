## Problem

Existing image memorability research either passively predicts a scalar score or uses generative editing models to alter images, but neither paradigm supports users at capture time with actionable guidance on how to make a photo more memorable. The paper formalizes Memorability Feedback (MemFeed), the task of generating natural-language, human-interpretable suggestions that a photographer can execute on-the-fly to increase an image's likelihood of being remembered.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261356/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single source photo (from a multi-shot scene) and a memorability feedback prompt as input, and produces concise natural-language instructions (typically 7–102 words, dominated by action verbs like 'Bring,' 'Stand,' 'Remove') describing concrete compositional or semantic changes. Evaluation applies the generated feedback to the source image via FLUX.1 Kontext as an in-context editor, then measures memorability improvement with a CLIP-based regressor; a perplexity metric on ground-truth feedback from the same captioning model provides a second evaluation axis.

## Method

MemCoach is a training-free, model-agnostic activation steering framework that distills memorability knowledge from a teacher MLLM into a student MLLM without any gradient updates. In a contrasting data generation step, the teacher (e.g., InternVL3.5) produces memorability-aware feedback by describing the transformation from the least-memorable to the most-memorable image in a scene, while the student produces its default neutral feedback on the same source image. A steering vector is then extracted per layer as the mean difference between student activations on the two feedback types (Eq. 3), capturing the latent-space direction associated with memorability-increasing suggestions. At inference, this vector is added to the student's intermediate activations scaled by a coefficient α (Eq. 4), shifting generation toward effective memorability guidance. The approach requires only ~1% of training data to match full-data fine-tuning performance and generalizes across multiple MLLM backbones (InternVL3.5, Qwen2.5-VL, LLaVA-OV, Idefics3).

## Results

On MemBench, MemCoach (using InternVL3.5 as backbone) achieves an Improvement Ratio (IR) of 0.80 and Relative Memorability (RM) of 7.21%, outperforming all zero-shot MLLM baselines and both aesthetics-specialized models. It surpasses the strongest zero-shot baseline GPT-5 MINI by +5% IR (0.80 vs. 0.75) and +0.18% RM (7.21 vs. 7.03), while improving over its own zero-shot backbone InternVL3.5 by +9.59% IR and +31.81% RM. On the perplexity metric, MemCoach achieves 4.99, lower than the zero-shot InternVL3.5 (5.49) and both aesthetics-specialized models, indicating better alignment with ground-truth memorability-aware feedback. MemCoach narrows the gap to teacher-oracle baselines (which have privileged access to destination images), reaching 0.80 IR versus the best oracle's 0.85.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MemCoach outperforms all zero-shot MLLM baselines on IR: it beats GPT-5 MINI (0.80 vs. 0.75), InternVL3.5 zero-shot (0.80 vs. 0.73), Idefics3 (0.80 vs. 0.73), LLaVA-OV (0.80 vs. 0.70), and Qwen2.5-VL (0.80 vs. 0.68). On RM, MemCoach (7.21%) edges out GPT-5 MINI (7.03%) and substantially surpasses all open-source zero-shot models, with the largest gain over Qwen2.5-VL (+2.95% RM) and InternVL3.5 (+1.74% RM). Against aesthetics-specialized models, MemCoach wins on both editing metrics: IR 0.80 vs. 0.73 for both AesExpert and Q-Instruct, and RM 7.21% vs. 6.67% (AesExpert) and 5.31% (Q-Instruct), while also achieving lower perplexity (4.99 vs. 5.97 and 5.36). MemCoach loses to teacher-oracle configurations on both IR (0.80 vs. 0.85 for InternVL3.5 oracle) and RM (7.21% vs. 11.92%), which is expected since oracles receive the destination image as privileged input. On perplexity, MemCoach (4.99) is competitive with the best zero-shot model (InternVL3.5 at 5.49) and far below Idefics3 (20.19) and Qwen2.5-VL (10.23), though it trails the best teacher oracle (Qwen2.5-VL at 2.34). The editing baseline (empty-string instruction to FLUX.1) is the weakest at IR=0.68 and RM=3.72%, confirming that meaningful feedback is necessary for memorability improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two established lines of memorability research: passive scalar prediction (Isola, Khosla) and generative editing that directly alters pixels (Siarohin, Sidorov), by introducing an intermediate task—generating natural-language actionable feedback at capture time. Methodologically, it repurposes the activation-steering paradigm from LLM interpretability (Turner et al., Rimsky et al.) for the first time on a perceptual MLLM task, using a teacher-student contrastive setup where the teacher has privileged access to a more memorable destination image. The benchmark builds on PPR10K's multi-shot scene structure, and the evaluation protocol borrows the in-context editing paradigm from FLUX.1 Kontext to operationalize 'did the feedback actually help?'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that state-of-the-art MLLMs exhibit near-zero Spearman correlation with human memorability judgments is a useful negative result that cautions against assuming multimodal pretraining captures fine-grained perceptual properties. The training-free, model-agnostic steering approach is practically attractive, but the evaluation's reliance on a single editing model (FLUX.1) as a proxy for real user behavior introduces a circularity concern, and the observed failure cases (removing distinctive elements) highlight an unresolved tension between normalization and distinctiveness in memorability. Open questions include whether the steering vector generalizes across domains beyond portraits, whether real-time latency is feasible for in-camera use, and whether the benchmark's scene-level pairing adequately captures the diversity of photographic decisions.
