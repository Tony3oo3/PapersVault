## Problem

Existing reconstruction-based detectors for AI-generated images rely on the first-order difference between an input image and its diffusion-model reconstruction, but this signal becomes dominated by stochastic perturbation noise as modern generators produce images increasingly indistinguishable from real ones. This paper addresses the gap by showing that a second-order difference (difference-in-differences) can cancel out the perturbation noise and amplify the weak detection signal that first-order methods miss.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262306/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single 256×256×3 RGB image, applies two consecutive forward-and-reverse diffusion reconstructions using a pre-trained ADM model (trained on LSUN-Bedroom), and computes pixel-wise first-order and second-order difference maps. These two difference maps are each fed into an independent ResNet-50 classifier; the final output is a binary real/synthetic label, where the image is classified as real only if both classifiers simultaneously predict real (a logical AND gate). Each classifier's threshold is set to 1−√0.5 ≈ 0.29 so the combined false-positive rate matches a single classifier at 0.5.

## Method

The core contribution is a difference-in-differences (DID) detection framework that performs two consecutive reconstructions x′ = R(x) and x″ = R(x′) with the same diffusion model, then computes both the first-order error Δ(x) = |x − x′| and the second-order error Δ²(x) = |x − x′| − |x′ − x″|. The key theoretical insight is that when real and synthetic images are highly similar (weak signal regime), the stochastic perturbation δ(x) ≈ δ(x′) cancels in the second-order difference, leaving Δ²(x) ≈ 0 for synthetic images while |Δ²(x)| ≈ |x − Π_M(x)| for real images, thereby isolating the true signal from noise. Two independent ResNet-50 classifiers are trained with standard cross-entropy loss on Δ(x) and Δ²(x) respectively, and the final decision requires both to agree the image is real. This dual-order design ensures robustness: the first-order term handles easy cases where generators are weak, while the second-order term handles hard cases where generators are strong and the first-order signal is buried in perturbation noise.

## Results

In the challenging setting (10k training samples, mismatched generator), DID achieves 96.42% total average accuracy when trained on LAION & SDXL, outperforming the second-best baseline DIRE (94.83%) by 1.6 points and LaRE² (82.78%) by 13.6 points. In the easier setting (40k training, matched generator), DID reaches 99.41% accuracy, essentially tying DIRE (99.37%). The improvement of DID over the strongest baseline ranges from 20% to 30% across evaluation subsets in the challenging setting, with the largest gains on LSUN-Bedroom and ImageNet test sets where the test distribution differs from the training distribution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DIRE (the closest reconstruction-based competitor), DID wins by 1.6 points on total average in the challenging LAION & SDXL setting (96.42% vs 94.83%), with the gap widening to 2.4 points on LSUN-Bedroom and 3.1 points on ImageNet evaluation subsets where the test distribution differs from training. In the easier 40k-sample setting with a matched generator, DID and DIRE are essentially tied (99.41% vs 99.37%), confirming that the second-order difference is most valuable when the first-order signal is weak. Against LaRE², DID achieves a large and consistent advantage of 13.6 points (96.42% vs 82.78%), as LaRE²'s latent-space projection fails to generalize across mismatched generators. AEROBLADE (training-free) and UFD (non-reconstruction-based) both perform near chance level (51.60% and 70.11% respectively) in the challenging setting, highlighting that DID's two-stage reconstruction approach is substantially more robust than either statistical or feature-space alternatives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reconstruction-based detection lineage initiated by DIRE, which classifies images by the first-order difference between an input and its diffusion-model reconstruction. The key conceptual departure is borrowing the difference-in-differences (DID) framework from econometrics: by performing two consecutive reconstructions and computing the difference between the two reconstruction errors (a second-order difference), the method cancels out stochastic perturbation noise that dominates when real and synthetic images are highly similar. While related variants like LaRE², AEROBLADE, and FIRE modify the reconstruction procedure or incorporate frequency-domain cues, none exploit this higher-order differencing structure, making the paper's contribution a principled extension rather than a new detection paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As generative models approach photorealistic fidelity, the reconstruction-error gap that underpins all first-order detectors shrinks, creating an urgent practical need for more sensitive detection signals. The 20–30% accuracy gains over DIRE in mismatched-generator and small-sample settings, plus robust generalization to GAN-generated images despite training only on diffusion outputs, make this a practically relevant improvement for deployment. Open questions remain: the optimal order of differencing (higher orders capture weaker signals but multiply reconstruction cost), whether the approach transfers to video or LLM-generated text detection, and how post-processing (compression, partial inpainting) affects the second-order signal.
