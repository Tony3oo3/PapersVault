## Problem

Counterfactual explanations (CFEs) have been extensively developed for image classifiers but remain largely unexplored for video classifiers. Existing image-based CFE methods perturb static features like textures and colors, which is fundamentally insufficient for videos where decisive features (motion, facial expression, human action) are dynamic and require temporally coherent, physically plausible edits across all frames. No principled framework existed that could generate video CFEs satisfying validity, proximity, actionability, realism, and spatiotemporal consistency simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The method takes an original video clip (224×224 for Shape-Moving, 512×512 for MEAD, 448×448 for NTU RGB+D), a target class label, and a black-box target video classifier. The first frame of the input video serves as the conditioning image for the I2V diffusion model, anchoring the counterfactual to start at the same moment as the original. The output is a full counterfactual video (same frame count and resolution as input) whose minimal, semantically meaningful spatiotemporal edits flip the classifier's prediction to the specified target class while preserving scene identity and layout.

## Method

BTTF employs the Wan-I2V latent diffusion model as a generator and directly optimizes its initial noise latent z_T via gradients from the target classifier, using a two-stage procedure. Stage 1 (inversion) minimizes an L1 reconstruction loss between the denoised latent and the VAE-encoded input video latent, anchoring the search near the original video with a single denoising step. Stage 2 (CFE generation) further optimizes z_T using a combined objective of cross-entropy loss with the target class and a translation-invariant video style loss (squared Frobenius norm of Gram matrix differences), with denoising steps progressively increasing from 1 to 15 to mitigate gradient vanishing. The diffusion model is LoRA fine-tuned on domain-specific data with a class-agnostic fixed text prompt, ensuring the CFE is guided purely by classifier feedback rather than linguistic priors. This design makes the explanation faithful to the target classifier's internal decision logic without relying on auxiliary robust classifiers or class-name prompts.

## Results

BTTF achieves a 99% flip rate on the non-robust E-swin classifier (MEAD), nearly matching PGD Attack's 100%. On standard perceptual metrics, PGD Attack dominates: SSIM 0.99 vs. 0.81, LPIPS 0.02 vs. 0.17, FID 0.68 vs. 19.03, and FVD 0.53 vs. 275.44. The paper attributes this gap to E-swin's zero robust accuracy (Table 1), meaning even sub-pixel noise suffices to flip predictions, making pixel-level metrics misleading for evaluating CFE quality. The target classifiers themselves achieve 100% (M-swin/Shape-Moving), 98.9% (E-swin/MEAD), and 54.2% (A-swinR/NTU RGB+D) standard accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole quantitative baseline is PGD Attack [34], a standard adversarial perturbation method. PGD wins decisively on every reported metric: it achieves a perfect 1.00 flip rate (vs. BTTF's 0.99), near-identical SSIM (0.99 vs. 0.81), and dramatically lower LPIPS (0.02 vs. 0.17), FID (0.68 vs. 19.03), and FVD (0.53 vs. 275.44). However, the paper argues this is an artifact of E-swin's zero robust accuracy—tiny, semantically meaningless pixel noise is sufficient to flip its prediction, so pixel-level similarity metrics trivially favor PGD. Qualitatively (Fig. 7), PGD's difference maps are noisy and uninformative, while BTTF's counterfactuals depict coherent, physically plausible actions (e.g., a person stepping backward instead of kicking) that reveal the classifier's actual decision logic. The authors conclude that no existing metric fairly captures the semantic and causal validity of video CFEs, and that BTTF's advantage is qualitative rather than quantitative under current evaluation protocols.

## Relation to Prior Work

BTTF extends the image-based counterfactual explanation (CFE) literature—where methods like DVCE and UVCE use diffusion models guided by classifier gradients or text prompts—to the video domain, introducing spatiotemporal constraints (physical plausibility, temporal coherence, smooth motion) that have no image analogue. It departs from prior image CFEs in two key ways: (1) it uses an Image-to-Video (Wan-I2V) latent diffusion model conditioned on the original first frame rather than a text-to-image or text-to-video generator, and (2) it avoids auxiliary guidance (robust classifiers, class-name prompts) so that the CFE is driven solely by the target classifier's gradients, preserving faithfulness. The two-stage inversion-then-optimization scheme and progressive denoising strategy are novel architectural choices specific to the video setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is, to the authors' knowledge, the first principled framework for generating video CFEs, opening a new sub-area of explainable video AI. The spurious-feature detection application (a classifier mistaking a 'dodge' for 'kicking') demonstrates practical debugging value, but the ~2-hour generation time per 4-second clip on an A100 and the requirement for domain-specific LoRA fine-tuning limit immediate adoption. A critical open problem the paper highlights is the absence of evaluation metrics that capture semantic/causal validity of video CFEs—standard metrics (FID, FVD, SSIM, LPIPS) paradoxically favor meaningless PGD noise over physically plausible counterfactuals. Future work on efficient inference, cross-domain generalization, and CFE-specific evaluation protocols will be essential for the field to mature.
