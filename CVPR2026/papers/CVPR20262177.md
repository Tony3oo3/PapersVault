## Problem

Modern vision-language pipelines inherit two structural inefficiencies from the pixel domain: transmitting dense RGB images from edge devices to the cloud is energy-intensive, and patch-based tokenization explodes sequence length, stressing attention budgets. This paper asks whether 2D Gaussian Splatting (2DGS)—a compact, spatially adaptive representation parameterizing images as colored anisotropic Gaussians—can serve as an effective visual substrate for large-scale vision-language alignment, and whether it can match or surpass pixel-based models in downstream multimodal reasoning.

## Input / Output

Images are pre-fitted into 2D Gaussian splat representations with configurable budgets of 400–3136 splats per image (each storing 8 FP16 parameters: 2D position, anisotropic covariance, and RGB color), yielding 3×–23.5× compression relative to 224×224 RGB pixels. The GS stem consumes these raw splat parameters via Fourier features and log scaling, and a Perceiver resampler produces a fixed number of visual tokens (98 or 196) that feed the frozen transformer. For the CLIP pipeline the output is a text-aligned CLS embedding for zero-shot classification; when integrated into an LLaVA-style VLM with Vicuna-7B, the output is a free-form text answer to a visual question.

## Method

The core architecture reuses a frozen RGB-pretrained ViT-B/16 backbone with a lightweight splat-aware input stem (Fourier features, log scaling, normalization, and linear projections) and a Perceiver resampler that maps variable-count Gaussian points to a fixed token budget. Training follows a two-stage procedure: Stage 1 distills the GS stem to match L2-normalized CLS embeddings of the RGB teacher (2 epochs, MSE loss), and Stage 2 performs parameter-efficient CLIP contrastive training (5 epochs) unfreezing only ~9.7–13.8% of total parameters (GS stem, first two transformer blocks, final norm/projection, and optionally symmetric text-side adapters). On the systems side, the authors introduce structured grid-based initialization (positions on a uniform grid, isotropic covariance, cell-average color), luminance-aware L1 pruning to sparsify low-contribution Gaussians, and batched CUDA kernels that achieve >90× faster fitting with 97% GPU utilization compared to prior 2DGS implementations, making million-scale GS encoding feasible for pretraining.

## Results

On the 38-dataset CLIP zero-shot benchmark, GS encoders with 3136 and 1600 splats retain 96–98% of the RGB ViT-B/16 (Small) baseline accuracy (relative accuracy 0.98 and 0.96 at 196 tokens) while compressing visual inputs by 3×–6× and accelerating data loading by up to 13.4×. Even the most aggressive 400-splat variant preserves ~91% of baseline accuracy at 23.5× compression. When integrated into an LLaVA-style VLM with Vicuna-7B, GS-3136 and GS-1600 encoders outperform the RGB baseline on 5 of 6 VQA benchmarks (e.g., SQA-IMG 64.70 vs. 63.81, MM-Vet 19.40 vs. 16.60, GQA 52.25 vs. 51.42), with the 98-token variant maintaining comparable performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is an RGB ViT-B/16 (Small) CLIP model trained on 12.8M DataComp pairs, which serves as the reference for both the zero-shot classification benchmark and the VLM integration. GS encoders trade a small accuracy drop (2–9% relative) for substantial compression (3×–23.5×) and data-loading speedups (6.7×–31.3×); the 3136-splat variant is nearly on par with RGB (98% relative accuracy) while halving input size. On the VQA side, GS-3136 and GS-1600 consistently match or exceed the RGB VLM across SQA-IMG, TextVQA, POPE, MME, MM-Vet, and GQA (e.g., MM-Vet 19.40 vs. 16.60 for GS-3136), whereas GS-900 and GS-400 show more degradation on MME and GQA. For the systems-level comparison, the paper benchmarks its batched CUDA kernels against the LIG [38] baseline, reporting up to 90.3× fitting speedup at batch size 4096 with 400 Gaussians per image and 97% GPU utilization. Overall, the GS approach wins on efficiency (compression, throughput) and ties or slightly wins on downstream VQA quality, while losing a small margin on the aggregate 38-dataset CLIP zero-shot score, particularly under sharp distribution shifts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends 2D Gaussian Splatting (originally developed for image compression in GaussianImage [36] and LIG [38]) from a rendering/compression primitive to a visual input representation for vision-language pre-training. It departs from the dominant paradigm of post-hoc token reduction (PruMerge, VisionZip, ToMe, SparseVLM), which compresses after patch-based encoding, by instead using Gaussians as the native input substrate. The two-stage training recipe (RGB→GS distillation followed by parameter-efficient CLIP adaptation) builds on the inductive biases of pretrained ViT-B/16 backbones, while the VLM integration follows the standard LLaVA recipe with Vicuna-7B. Prior small-scale efforts like GViT [12] and GaussianToken [5] explored Gaussians for classification or tokenization but did not scale to vision-language alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a compact, spatially adaptive Gaussian representation can retain 90–98% of RGB CLIP accuracy at 3–23.5× compression and even outperform RGB in a full VLM pipeline, suggesting that much of the semantic signal for multimodal reasoning is already condensed in a small set of anisotropic splats. Key limitations remain: GS performance is upper-bounded by the RGB teacher, the fitting step is still non-trivial (seconds per batch), and GS models show greater sensitivity to distribution shift. Open questions include whether GS-native architectures (without RGB distillation) can match or exceed current results, whether the two-stage recipe is fundamentally necessary, and how the approach scales to video or higher-resolution settings.
