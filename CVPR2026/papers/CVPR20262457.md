## Problem

Existing VideoQA benchmarks rely on multiple-choice formats or reference-answer evaluation, failing to capture the multimodal reasoning (visual + dialogue) required for real-world video understanding. Open-ended VideoQA evaluation is particularly difficult because standard semantic metrics (ROUGE, BERTScore) show near-zero model discrimination, and reference-based LLM judges inflate scores without verifying grounding in actual video content.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262457/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question is paired with four input video settings of varying granularity: the full-length movie (~107 min), the full recap video (~11 min, visual-only), ~14 min of aligned movie scenes, and ~1.2 min of aligned recap segments. In all cases, aligned movie subtitles are provided as text. The model produces a free-form text answer, which is then scored on a 0–5 integer scale for factuality and relevance by an LLM judge grounded in question-specific atomic facts extracted from the recap voiceover.

## Method

The pipeline leverages movie recap videos (YouTube voiceover summaries of films) as an intermediate representation: the transcribed voiceover (RecapSummary) is segmented and fed to GPT-4.1 to extract atomic facts—short, verifiable propositions about the film. QA pairs are then generated from these facts, with a simplification step to remove identifying details that would leak answers. Scene-level alignment between recap videos and full movies is achieved via SlowFast embeddings and cosine similarity over SceneDetect-segmented shots. The key evaluation contribution is a reference-free LLM judge (GPT-4.1 mini) that scores a model's answer on factuality and relevance by checking extracted claims against the question-specific atomic facts and subtitles, eliminating the need for a single gold-standard reference answer.

## Results

On the MOVIERECAPSQA benchmark (8,231 open-ended questions across 60 films), GPT-4o achieves the highest reference-free Factuality score (3.99 ± 2.01) and Relevance score (3.97 ± 1.92) among the seven MLLMs evaluated, while best human performance reaches 4.59 ± 0.63 on Factuality and 4.53 ± 0.76 on Relevance. Semantic metrics (ROUGE-L: 0.22–0.28, BERTScore: 0.63–0.69, BARTScore: 0.03–0.05) show virtually no model discrimination, and reference-based HELMET Correctness fails to separate models from humans (MiniCPM-o scores 1.27 vs. best human 1.26). The proposed reference-free metric produces a meaningful spread (Factuality: 2.96–3.99 across models) with higher per-model variance (1.97–2.35) than reference-based metrics (0.89–1.42), confirming it captures genuine answer-quality differences.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between three families of evaluation metrics rather than between models. Semantic baselines (ROUGE-L, BERTScore, BARTScore) fail to discriminate: per-model variance is ≤0.03 for all three, rendering them useless for ranking. Reference-based LLM judges (G-Eval, HELMET Correctness) provide better separation but misalign with human judgment—HELMET Correctness rates MiniCPM-o (1.27) above the best human (1.26) and LLaVA-NeXT-Video (0.98) on par with the average human (0.98), inflating model scores. The proposed reference-free metric (Factuality/Relevance via GPT-4.1-mini judge grounded in atomic facts) is the only metric that both separates models meaningfully (range 2.96–3.99) and preserves the human–model gap (best human 4.59 vs. best model 3.99). Among the seven MLLMs, GPT-4o leads on both Factuality (3.99) and Relevance (3.97), followed by Claude 3.5 Sonnet (3.76/3.92) and Amazon Nova Lite (3.53/3.93); Gemini 2.5 Flash, despite the highest HELMET Correctness (1.82), scores only 3.26 on Factuality, illustrating the reference-based metric's failure. Ablations further show that proprietary models' Factuality improves by +0.49 to +0.73 when visual input is removed, indicating their apparent multimodal advantage is driven by language priors rather than genuine visual understanding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of VideoQA benchmarking and open-ended evaluation. It departs from the dominant multiple-choice VideoQA paradigm (TVQA, MoVQA, CinePile) by introducing open-ended questions over long-form narrative video, and from text-based LLM-as-judge methods (G-Eval, HELMET, FactScore) by grounding the judge in recap-derived atomic facts rather than a single reference answer. The use of movie recap videos as an intermediate representation—bridging full-length films, aligned subtitles, and structured fact extraction—is a novel data-source repurposing not seen in prior VideoQA construction pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a critical and counterintuitive finding: current proprietary MLLMs achieve higher factuality when visual input is removed, suggesting that visual encoders actively degrade rather than improve answer quality. This reframes the multimodal integration problem from a reasoning challenge to a perception one, with implications for how future architectures should ground language in visual evidence. Open questions include whether the atomic-facts evaluation generalizes beyond movie recaps to other long-form video domains, and whether the visual-perception bottleneck persists as encoder quality improves. The reliance on GPT-4.1 for both dataset construction and (via GPT-4.1-mini) evaluation introduces a potential circularity that future work should address.
