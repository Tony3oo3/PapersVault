## Problem

Targeted transfer-based adversarial attacks still suffer from substantially lower Attack Success Rates than untargeted ones, largely because generated perturbations overfit the surrogate model. This paper identifies a previously overlooked root cause: adversarial examples rely excessively on a small subset of surrogate model parameters, limiting generalization to models with different parameter configurations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262608/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a clean RGB image (resized to 224×224 for Transformer-based surrogates) and a target class label, and produces an adversarial example constrained to an ℓ∞-norm ball of radius ε=16/255. The surrogate model is accessed in white-box mode (gradients available), while the resulting adversarial example is evaluated against black-box target models spanning both CNN and Transformer architectures. The method requires no retraining of the surrogate and operates with a fixed number of forward-backward passes per iteration for fair compute comparison.

## Method

RaPA introduces parameter-level randomization into the attack optimization loop by applying DropConnect-style Bernoulli masking to the weight and bias parameters of linear layers and the transformation parameters of normalization layers at every iteration. The key theoretical contribution shows that taking the expectation of the loss over random masks is equivalent (via second-order Taylor expansion) to adding an importance-equalization regularizer that penalizes concentrated parameter sensitivity, thereby flattening the importance distribution. In practice, fresh random masks are resampled at each of S inferences per iteration, creating diverse yet semantically consistent surrogate variants that act as a training-free self-ensemble. The method is architecture-agnostic (applied to both CNN and Transformer surrogates), requires no additional training, and integrates directly with existing input-transformation and gradient-stabilization techniques such as MI-FGSM and translation invariance.

## Results

On the most challenging CNN-to-Transformer transfer setting (ImageNet-Compatible dataset, ℓ∞=16/255), RaPA with ResNet-50 as surrogate achieves 45.0% average ASR across six Transformer targets, surpassing the previous SOTA FTM (33.3%) by 11.7 percentage points. With DenseNet-121 as surrogate, RaPA reaches 40.3% average ASR vs. FTM's 22.8% (a 17.5-point gain). In the CNN-to-CNN setting (Table 4), RaPA with ResNet-50 achieves 68.0% average ASR over ten targets, outperforming CFM (54.9%) by 13.1 points, and in the ViT-to-CNN direction it reaches 51.2% vs. CFM's 40.1%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RaPA outperforms all 14 compared baselines across every evaluation setting. Against the strongest input-transformation baselines, RaPA (45.0% avg ASR, RN50→Transformer) beats CFM (32.2%) by 12.8 points and FTM (33.3%) by 11.7 points; it also surpasses ODI (24.8%), Admix (17.1%), and BSR (17.7%) by wide margins. Among self-ensemble methods, RaPA dominates MUP (20.7%), DWP (12.1%), and SE-ViT (17.0% in ViT→CNN), confirming that random per-iteration masking is more effective than deterministic importance-based pruning or architecture-specific ensembling. Against training-based surrogate-enhancement methods, RaPA (training-free) already exceeds DSM (20.6%) and SASD-WS (42.2%) in the CNN→Transformer setting, and when combined with DSM it further lifts average ASR to 58.3%. In the CNN→CNN direction (Table 4), RaPA with RN50 reaches 68.0% average ASR, beating CFM (54.9%) by 13.1 points and ODI (39.5%) by 28.5 points; in the ViT→CNN direction it achieves 51.2% vs. CFM's 40.1%. The gap is largest on the hardest targets (e.g., CLIP: 15.6% vs. 9.3% for FTM; VGG-16: 41.9% vs. 27.3% for CFM), indicating RaPA's benefit is most pronounced where transfer is most difficult.

## Relation to Prior Work

RaPA extends the self-ensemble paradigm for transfer-based adversarial attacks by repurposing DropConnect (originally a training-time regularizer) as an attack-time randomization mechanism. Unlike prior self-ensemble methods that rely on training-based model enhancement (SASD-WS, DSM), deterministic importance-based pruning (MUP, DWP), or structural perturbations (Ghost Network), RaPA introduces parameter-level randomization that is training-free and architecture-agnostic. Conceptually, the paper departs from the dominant input-transformation and gradient-stabilization lines (DI, CFM, FTM, SI) by identifying a model-side cause—over-reliance on a small parameter subset—as the root bottleneck, and showing via a second-order Taylor expansion that random masking is equivalent to an importance-equalization regularizer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of parameter over-reliance as a previously overlooked cause of poor transferability reframes how practitioners think about adversarial generalization, and the DropConnect-based solution is simple, training-free, and easily plugged into existing attack pipelines. The 11.7% average ASR gain in the hardest CNN-to-Transformer transfer setting, combined with consistent improvements across 16 target models and 7 defenses, makes RaPA a practical tool for red-teaming. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to untargeted attacks, other modalities (e.g., audio, video), and whether the per-architecture tuning of DropConnect probability can be automated; the second-order Taylor approximation in the theoretical analysis also remains an approximation whose tightness is not formally bounded.
