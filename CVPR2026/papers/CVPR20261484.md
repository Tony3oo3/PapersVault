## Problem

Existing query-aware frame selection methods for long-form video understanding assume that complex search mechanisms are universally necessary, incurring significant computational overhead. This paper challenges that assumption by identifying a query typology (global vs. localized) and demonstrating that uniform sampling is both effective and efficient for global queries, while only localized queries truly benefit from query-aware selection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261484/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a long-form video (sampled at 2 fps for feature extraction) and a natural-language text query as input, and produces a set of selected frames (up to 256, each represented by 56 tokens) that are concatenated with the query and fed to an LMM for autoregressive answer generation. The framework is entirely training-free and operates as a preprocessing pipeline before the LMM inference step, with the LLM used for query classification and the LMM itself used for reward scoring of candidate frames.

## Method

DIG is a training-free, query-type-adaptive frame selection framework. An LLM first classifies the query as global or localized; global queries are handled by standard uniform sampling across the entire video. For localized queries, a three-stage pipeline is activated: (1) Content-Adaptive Frame Selection (CAFS) extracts DINOv2 features from 2-fps frames, computes pairwise cosine distances, identifies scene-transition peaks (prominence > 0.1) as segmentation boundaries, and selects midpoint r-frames from each segment; (2) the LMM assigns two-dimensional relevance rewards (direct query relevance plus potential for adjacent-frame supplementary information) to each r-frame; (3) an iterative reward-guided selection (thresholding below the running mean until the positive set stabilizes) identifies the most relevant r-frames, whose surrounding video segments (expanded by a temporal window wlen=2) are unioned into a refined video from which final input frames are uniformly sampled. This design avoids fixed Top-K hyperparameters and preserves fine-grained temporal context around query-relevant moments.

## Results

On MLVU with Qwen2.5-VL-7B and 32 input frames, DIG achieves 67.20% accuracy, a +7.68% improvement over uniform sampling (59.52%) and +2.13% over AKS (65.07%). On LongVideoBench under the same setting, DIG reaches 60.43% versus 56.92% (UNI) and 59.31% (AKS). Scaling to 256 frames, DIG maintains its lead at 72.46% on MLVU and 64.62% on LVB, while AKS degrades to 71.50% and 61.03% respectively. With the stronger Qwen2.5-VL-32B backbone, DIG achieves 75.20% on MLVU and 65.60% on LVB at 128 frames, outperforming all baselines across every tested configuration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DIG consistently outperforms both AKS [46] and Q-Frame [69] across all three benchmarks and all tested frame counts (8–256) for both Qwen2.5-VL-7B and Qwen2.5-VL-32B. The most striking finding is that at higher frame counts, both baselines degrade below uniform sampling: at 128 frames on LongVideoBench with Qwen2.5-VL-7B, AKS scores 60.36% and Q-Frame 59.76%, both below UNI's 61.86%, while DIG reaches 63.13%. At 256 frames, AKS drops to 61.03% on LVB (below UNI's 61.48%), whereas DIG maintains a 3.14% margin over UNI. On MLVU at 32 frames, DIG's 67.20% surpasses AKS (65.07%) by 2.13% and Q-Frame (60.03%) by 7.17%. The gap widens with frame count: at 192 frames on MLVU, DIG leads AKS by 2.39% (72.32 vs. 69.93), confirming that query-adaptive selection becomes increasingly critical as more frames are available.

## Relation to Prior Work

DIG positions itself against the growing family of query-aware frame selection methods (AKS, Q-Frame, BOLT, T*, Frame-Voyager) that assume complex search mechanisms are universally necessary for long-form video VQA. Rather than building on any single prior method, it repurposes existing components—DINOv2 for semantic segmentation, an LLM for query classification, and the LMM itself for reward scoring—into a new training-free pipeline whose central conceptual departure is the query typology (global vs. localized) that determines which strategy to deploy. The paper explicitly challenges the field's implicit assumption that query-aware selection always outperforms uniform sampling, showing the latter is both effective and efficient for a substantial class of queries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: DIG provides a simple, training-free framework that avoids the computational overhead of complex frame search for global queries while still delivering targeted selection where it matters, and it decouples the reward-assignment model from the inference backbone (enabling a stronger LMM for scoring without retraining). Limitations include reliance on an external LLM for query classification (error propagation risk), the DINOv2-based segmentation's sensitivity to video content type, and the residual wlen hyperparameter. Open questions include whether the global/localized dichotomy is sufficient for all query types, how the framework behaves under multi-turn or compositional queries, and whether the iterative reward-guided selection can be further simplified or made more robust.
