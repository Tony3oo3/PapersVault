## Problem

The exponential map on 3D meshes, computed via straightest geodesics, is the de-facto Riemannian operator for learning on surfaces, yet all existing implementations are non-differentiable and CPU-sequential. This prevents direct integration of geometrically accurate Riemannian operators into modern GPU-accelerated learning and optimisation pipelines, forcing practitioners to pre-compute operators or rely on slow numerical solvers that drift off the surface.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262795/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The core operator takes a mesh (N vertices, F triangular faces), a point defined intrinsically as a (face index, barycentric coordinates) pair, and a tangent vector in the local tangent plane, and returns the geodesic endpoint, the full traced curve, and the parallel-transported vector. Batches of tens of thousands of points across multiple heterogeneous meshes are processed simultaneously in a single CUDA kernel. Downstream applications consume these operators to produce body-part segmentation labels (per-face), generative samples from a target distribution on the mesh, and optimised Voronoi seed positions.

## Method

The paper derives two differentiation schemes for the straightest geodesics exponential map: (1) an Extrinsic Proxy (EP) that constructs a differentiable surrogate φ(p,v) = R^fix·(p+v) + t^fix using the cumulative rotation from parallel transport, yielding an analytical Jacobian for v but zeroing out unreliable p-derivatives; and (2) Geodesic Finite Differences (GFD) that perturbs v along the local orthonormal frame and p along barycentric axes, computing forward finite differences of Exp projected back via the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of the endpoint's local frame. Both schemes are agnostic to the forward geodesic computation and are parallelised on the GPU with one CUDA thread per geodesic path, avoiding inter-thread synchronisation. Built on this differentiable operator, the paper introduces three applications: Adaptive Geodesic Convolutions that learn per-channel patch sizes via differentiable Exp, MeshFlow which replaces RFM's ODE solver with K-step exponential maps for flow matching, and Mesh-LBFGS, a second-order Riemannian optimiser applied to centroidal Voronoi tessellation.

## Results

On body part segmentation (Composite dataset), AGC achieves 92.3% accuracy with HKS input and 91.7% with XYZ input, outperforming GCNN (86.4%), MDGCNN (89.5%), and DiffusionNet (91.7% HKS / 90.6% XYZ). For flow matching on Stanford Bunny and Spot the Cow, MeshFlow-1024 achieves lower KLD (e.g., 0.034 vs 0.084 on Spot k=100) and BCD (0.013 vs 0.014) than RFM while being 1.6×10⁴ times faster at inference and using 97% less GPU memory. The CUDA exponential map implementation is 3 orders of magnitude faster than pp3d on large batches and 5–6 orders of magnitude faster than PI on high-face-count meshes, with accuracy within 2.0e-5 (float32) of the reference implementation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AGC outperforms GCNN [53] by 5.3% (91.7% vs 86.4%) and MDGCNN [66] by 2.2% (91.7% vs 89.5%) on XYZ input, and beats DiffusionNet [76] by 1.1% (92.3% vs 91.7%) on HKS input. The only method exceeding AGC is FC* [57] at 92.9%, but it was trained on a modified dataset predicting vertex rather than face classes, making the comparison only partially fair. For flow matching, MeshFlow-1024 surpasses RFM [15] on KLD (0.034 vs 0.084 on Spot k=100) and BCD (0.013 vs 0.014) while reporting higher NLL (1.32 vs 1.26), though the authors note NLL is tied to the optimisation objective and thus only informative within MeshFlow variants. On the core exponential map, the CUDA implementation matches pp3d [74] in accuracy (2.0e-5 mean distance in float32) while being 3 orders of magnitude faster on large batches, and matches PI [51] in accuracy (5.4e-4 on sphere) while being 5–6 orders of magnitude faster on high-face-count meshes with large batches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper takes the straightest geodesics algorithm of Polthier & Schmies [65]—the de-facto standard for computing exponential maps on triangular meshes—and makes it differentiable and GPU-parallelised, two properties no prior implementation possessed. For applications, it extends GCNN [53] by replacing fixed patch sizes with learnable ones via the differentiable exponential map, replaces the ODE-based geodesic tracing in Riemannian Flow Matching [15] with direct back-propagation through Exp, and adapts the Riemannian L-BFGS framework of Hosseini & Sra [37] to meshes using their parallelised operators. The key conceptual departure from prior mesh-geometry libraries (geometry-central, potpourri3d) is treating the exponential map as a differentiable primitive within a PyTorch computation graph rather than a pre-computed or black-box lookup.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By providing the first differentiable, GPU-parallelised exponential map for meshes as a pip-installable library (digeo), this work removes a long-standing infrastructure bottleneck that forced mesh-learning methods to either pre-compute geodesic distances or rely on slow, non-differentiable numerical solvers. The three applications (adaptive geodesic convolution, MeshFlow, Mesh-LBFGS) demonstrate that the same primitive can serve supervised learning, generative modelling, and second-order optimisation, suggesting broad downstream utility. Open questions include whether the EP approximation suffices for tasks requiring accurate position gradients at scale, how the methods behave on very high-resolution meshes (beyond ~1.7M faces tested), and whether the straightest-geodesic formulation (as opposed to shortest-path) remains the optimal choice for all learning objectives.
