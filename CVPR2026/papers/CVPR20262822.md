## Problem

Existing AI-generated image attribution methods either require full access to generative models (watermarking) or treat attribution as a closed-set classification task requiring labeled or unlabeled images from all generators during training. This makes them inflexible and non-scalable to the rapidly evolving landscape of new and unseen image generators. The paper asks whether attribution can be reformulated as a retrieval problem that only needs a well-trained feature encoder and a small registered database of exemplars.

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard RGB image and converts it into a binary low-bit generative fingerprint by extracting the three least significant bit-planes of each channel and applying a sign threshold (yielding a 0/255 image). The fingerprint is fed to a modified ResNet-50 encoder that produces a feature vector. Attribution is performed by cosine-similarity retrieval against a registered database containing only 1-10 exemplar images per generator, and the top-ranked neighbor's generator label is assigned to the query. A secondary real/fake binary decision is made by comparing the query feature to a real-image prototype with a fixed similarity threshold.

## Method

LIDA consists of three stages: (1) Low-Bit Fingerprint Generation, which decomposes each RGB channel into bit-planes and combines the three LSB planes with a sign function to produce a binary noise image that discards semantic content while preserving generator-specific artifacts; (2) Unsupervised Pre-Training, where a ResNet-50 (with downsampling removed in lower layers to retain spatial resolution) is trained on ImageNet fingerprints using a standard image-classification pretext loss to learn transferable noise-structure features; (3) Few-Shot Attribution Adaptation, which fine-tunes the encoder on 1-10 images per generator using a center loss (for intra-class compactness in attribution) plus a real-prototype-based contrastive loss (for real/fake separation), deliberately avoiding cross-entropy to preserve the pretrained feature geometry. The retrieval-based formulation means new generators are accommodated simply by adding their exemplars to the database without any retraining, and the retrieved neighbors provide interpretable evidence for the attribution decision.

## Results

On the GenImage dataset under the cross-architecture attribution setting, LIDA achieves an average Rank-1 of 40.4% and mAP of 61.5% in the 1-shot setting, improving to 54.1% Rank-1 and 56.3% mAP at 5-shot, and 54.0% Rank-1 and 51.6% mAP at 10-shot. For AI-generated image detection, LIDA reaches 88.3% average accuracy in the 10-shot setting, surpassing the prior best few-shot method FSD (84.1%) by 4.2%. Under zero-shot detection (no fake images seen during training), LIDA achieves 86.3% average accuracy, outperforming all dedicated zero-shot detectors by at least 9.2%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LIDA dominates all three attribution baselines (ResNet-50, DIRE, ESSP) by large margins across every shot setting on both GenImage and WildFake; for example, in the 10-shot GenImage cross-architecture setting it leads ResNet by 32.6% Rank-1, DIRE by 36.8%, and ESSP by 31.6%. On few-shot detection (Table 4, 10-shot GenImage), LIDA achieves 88.3% average accuracy, beating LARE2 (72.5%) by 15.8% and FSD (84.1%) by 4.2%. In the zero-shot detection setting (Table 5), LIDA reaches 86.3% average accuracy, surpassing RIGID (69.8%) by 16.5%, AEROBLADE (48.8%) by 37.5%, Manifold (68.2%) by 18.1%, and FSD (77.1%) by 9.2%. On WildFake (10-shot cross-architecture), LIDA attains 62.3% average Rank-1, exceeding ResNet (29.9%), DIRE (31.5%), and ESSP (28.4%) by 32.4%, 30.8%, and 33.9% respectively. Notably, LIDA achieves 100% Rank-1 on the BigGAN subset in the 1-shot setting, a result no baseline approaches. The method wins every head-to-head comparison reported in the paper; no baseline matches or exceeds LIDA on any primary metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the authors' own prior work on bit-plane-based AI-generated image detection (LOTA, RAID) by showing that the same low-bit-plane artifacts that separate real from fake images also carry generator-specific signatures useful for attribution. It departs from the dominant classification-based attribution paradigm (both closed-set GAN fingerprinting and open-set pseudo-labeling approaches) by reframing attribution as instance retrieval against a small registered database, a formulation borrowed from general retrieval literature but not previously applied to generative image forensics. The training pipeline repurposes the center loss from face recognition and introduces a real-prototype contrastive loss for detection, deliberately avoiding cross-entropy to preserve pretrained feature geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The retrieval-based formulation offers a practical path to handling the rapid proliferation of new generators: adding a few exemplar images to the database suffices, eliminating the retraining burden that plagues classification-based methods. The low-bit fingerprint is a remarkably simple preprocessing (three LSBs per channel, thresholded) that yields large separability gains, suggesting that generative model identity is encoded in fine-grained noise structure rather than semantic content. Open questions include robustness to adversarial perturbations targeting the low-bit planes, scalability to video attribution, and whether the approach degrades when generators share very similar architectures (as seen with the DD/COM subsets in WildFake).
