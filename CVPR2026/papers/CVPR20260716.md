## Problem

Existing fake image detectors rely on backbones (ResNet, CLIP) designed for high-level semantic extraction rather than forgery artifact detection, leaving the embedding space suboptimal for distinguishing real from generated images. While Neighboring Pixel Relationships (NPR) capture intrinsic forgery clues from up-sampling artifacts, no prior work has jointly optimized a forgery-aware embedding space by leveraging both RGB and NPR modalities through representation learning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260716/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image as input and computes its NPR representation by dividing the image into non-overlapping 2×2 patches and subtracting the top-left pixel value per channel within each patch. Both the RGB image and its derived NPR are fed through separate encoders (Dinov2-ViT-L-14 with LoRA for RGB, ResNet-101 for NPR), and the concatenated features produce a binary real/fake classification score with a 0.5 decision threshold. At inference, only the two encoders are retained, so no additional computation is introduced beyond the standard forward pass.

## Method

The proposed SDID framework learns a forgery-aware embedding space via two complementary cross-modal representation learning objectives. Cross-Modal Contrastive Learning (CMCL) applies InfoNCE loss to pull the RGB and NPR features of the same image together while pushing them away from features of images in the opposite class (real vs. fake), thereby boosting inter-class separability. Cross-Modal Mutual Distillation (CMMD) extracts top-K nearest-neighbor similarity distributions in each modality's feature space and performs bidirectional KL-divergence distillation between RGB and NPR within the same class, learning compact intra-class representations. The two objectives run synchronously so that each modality learns a more comprehensive forgery-aware representation: CMCL handles inter-class discrimination while CMMD handles intra-class compactness. The total training loss combines cross-entropy classification, CMCL, and CMMD terms, and the approach generalizes across different RGB encoder backbones (Dinov2, CLIP) without architectural changes.

## Results

On GenImage, SDID achieves 98.4% average accuracy, outperforming the next-best method CoD (96.2%) by 2.2 points and the prior SOTA CoDE (93.5%) by 4.9 points. On DRCT-2M, SDID reaches 92.4% average accuracy, surpassing DLFE (90.9%) by 1.5 points. On Co-Spy-Bench, SDID achieves 96.1% average accuracy, exceeding the runner-up CO-SPY (87.1%) by a substantial 9.0 points, with most test sets above 95% except PG-v2-256 (90.5%), FLUX.1-sch (93.9%), and FLUX.1-dev (93.6%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SDID outperforms all 17 baselines across all three benchmarks. On GenImage, the closest competitors are CoD (96.2%) and DLFE (96.0%), both trailing SDID by roughly 2 points; the earlier contrastive-learning method CoDE (93.5%) is surpassed by 4.9 points, and reconstruction-error-based methods like DIRE (71.2%) and LaRE2 (86.2%) lag by 12–27 points. On DRCT-2M, SDID (92.4%) edges out DLFE (90.9%) and DRCT (90.5%) by 1.5–1.9 points, while showing particularly large gains on diffusion-reconstruction test sets (e.g., SDv2-DR: 75.9% vs. 51.8% for DLFE). On Co-Spy-Bench, the margin is most dramatic: SDID (96.1%) beats CO-SPY (87.1%) by 9.0 points and the NPR-only baseline [44] (65.7%) by 30.4 points, indicating that the cross-modal representation learning substantially amplifies the utility of NPR features beyond what they provide in isolation. Frequency-domain methods (FreqFD at 53.1%, FreqNet at 57.4%) and early GAN-era detectors (CNNSpot at 69.4%, GramNet at 72.2%) are consistently the weakest baselines, underscoring the difficulty of generalizing from GAN-specific or frequency-only cues to modern diffusion models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the NPR (Neighboring Pixel Relationships) artifact representation from [44], which captures up-sampling traces for source-invariant detection, and extends the contrastive learning line initiated by CoDE [2] (which used RGB-only contrastive learning) into a cross-modal setting. The two proposed losses—CMCL (InfoNCE-based cross-modal contrastive learning) and CMMD (relational knowledge distillation between modalities)—draw on established techniques from self-supervised contrastive learning (MoCo, SimCLR) and relational knowledge distillation (PKT, CompRess, SEED), but apply them jointly across the RGB and NPR modalities for the first time in the detection context. The framework departs from prior RGB-only detectors (UnivFD, DIRE, DRCT, Co-Spy) by arguing that semantic backbones are not inherently suited to forgery detection and that a purpose-built, cross-modal embedding space is needed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a relatively lightweight cross-modal representation learning recipe (CMCL + CMMD) can push detection accuracy to 98.4% on GenImage and 96.1% on Co-Spy-Bench, outperforming all prior methods by meaningful margins, and that the approach generalizes across different RGB encoders (Dinov2, CLIP). This suggests that the choice of embedding-space optimization strategy matters as much as the backbone architecture for detection generalization. However, performance still degrades on the most challenging subsets (PG-v2-256, FLUX.1-sch, FLUX.1-dev), and it remains open whether the NPR modality will retain its discriminative power as future generative architectures move away from the up-sampling patterns it exploits. The method's reliance on a fixed NPR preprocessing step also raises questions about robustness to compression, resizing, and other post-processing that may alter local pixel relationships.
