## Problem

Correcting unreliable neural network behaviors (backdoors, spurious correlations, feature leakage) typically requires labor-intensive data cleaning and full model retraining. Existing rank-one model editing was designed for domain adaptation or knowledge injection, where it suffers from out-of-span residuals and high sample complexity, and it defaults to editing a fixed (usually final) layer despite editability varying substantially across layers. This paper asks whether rank-one editing can be repurposed as a lightweight rectification tool that corrects misbehavior with minimal cleansed samples while preserving overall performance.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pair consisting of a corrupted sample x̃ (e.g., containing a backdoor trigger or spurious patch) and its manually cleansed counterpart x, together with the pre-trained model f and its ground-truth label y. It outputs the rectified model parameters (a rank-one weight update applied to a single identified layer) and the index of the suspect layer. A key practical detail is that the framework operates with as few as one clean-corrupted pair (n=1), and the dynamic variant iteratively re-identifies the suspect layer after each edit, checking that cumulative performance degradation stays within a user-specified budget ε.

## Method

The paper reformulates rank-one model editing as model rectification, showing that because the model was trained on both clean and corrupted inputs, the corrupted key k* lies in the span of learned key statistics (rectifiability) and paired supervision eliminates the sample-complexity burden of domain adaptation (span-aligned control). To overcome the fixed-layer bottleneck, the authors introduce an attribution-guided layer localization method: Integrated Gradients are computed along the linear path from x̃ to x at every internal layer, yielding layer-wise attribution maps M^l whose sums equal the global prediction change (Completeness axiom). Each M^l is then projected onto the rank-one editing direction (C^{-1}k*)^T, and the Frobenius norm of the result serves as a scalar proxy Ĝ_l for the first-order decrease in prediction gap. The dynamic rectification framework (Algorithm 1) iteratively selects the layer with the largest Ĝ_l, applies the rank-one update to remap k* to the cleansed value v*, and continues until the prediction gap falls below a target δ while overall accuracy degradation remains within budget ε.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, dynamic rectification with a single cleansed sample reduces the attack success rate (ASR) from 99.94% to 1.34% while preserving overall accuracy (OA) at 93.65% (vs. 93.67% for the Trojaned model). On ImageNet, the same single-sample setting drops ASR from 87.24% to 1.61% with OA at 66.77%. Scaling to 20 cleansed samples further reduces ASR to 0.26% (CIFAR-10) and 0.12% (ImageNet) with negligible OA loss. For spurious correlation mitigation on CIFAR-10, dynamic rectification (n=20) narrows the clean-vs-spurious accuracy gap from +5.58% (benign model) to +0.12%, and for feature leakage on BlockMNIST, a single sample reduces leakage from 3.597 to 2.417 while maintaining 99.03% accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed dynamic rectification with a single cleansed sample outperforms all baselines on both OA and ASR simultaneously. On CIFAR-10, it achieves OA=93.65% and ASR=1.34%, beating A-ClArC (n=20) which reaches OA=92.53% and ASR=6.32%, and P-ClArC (n=20) at OA=89.97% and ASR=6.21%—all while using 20× fewer samples. Fine-tuning with 20 samples (OA=91.58%, ASR=13.22%) and neuron patching (OA=89.70%, ASR=12.19%) are both dominated on both metrics. On ImageNet, the single-sample dynamic rectification (ASR=1.61%) surpasses A-ClArC (ASR=8.73%) and P-ClArC (ASR=8.09%) by roughly 5–7× in ASR reduction. For spurious correlation on CIFAR-10, dynamic rectification (n=20) achieves a clean/spurious gap of only +0.12% versus +10.65% for fine-tuning and +2.57% for A-ClArC, while P-ClArC collapses clean accuracy to 16.89%. On BlockMNIST feature leakage, a single-sample dynamic rectification (leakage=2.417, acc=99.03%) outperforms fine-tuning with 20 samples (leakage=2.929, acc=98.67%) and IG-SUM regularization (leakage=3.417, acc=94.14%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes rank-one model editing—originally developed for rewriting generative model rules (Bau et al., ECCV 2020) and later adapted for classifier domain adaptation (Santurkar et al., NeurIPS 2021)—into a model rectification framework for correcting unreliable behaviors. The key conceptual departure is that in rectification, the 'correct' activation already exists in the model for clean inputs, so the rank-one update merely reroutes non-robust cues rather than synthesizing a new association, yielding formal properties (rectifiability, span-aligned control) absent in the domain-adaptation setting. For layer selection, the paper departs from the fixed last-layer convention in prior editing work by introducing an attribution-guided estimator built on Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al., NeurIPS 2017), which quantifies first-order editability per layer and identifies the most responsible layer adaptively.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: achieving near-benign performance (e.g., ASR from 99.94% to 1.34% on CIFAR-10) with a single cleansed sample eliminates the large data-cleaning and retraining overhead that dominates current backdoor-defense and spurious-correlation-mitigation pipelines. The theoretical characterization of rectifiability and span-aligned control provides a principled explanation for why this works, distinguishing it from ad-hoc fine-tuning. Open questions include whether the attribution-based layer localization transfers to non-CNN architectures (e.g., vision transformers or LLMs), whether the method extends to adversarial robustness (explicitly excluded here), and how performance degrades when the cleansed sample is imperfectly aligned with the corrupted one.
