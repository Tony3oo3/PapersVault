## Problem

Cross-modal domain adaptation from richly labeled MRI to sparsely labeled PET for brain disorder diagnosis suffers from a fundamental tension: global feature alignment reduces domain shift but destroys modality-specific pathological discriminative information (structural vs. metabolic signals). Existing selective-alignment methods were designed for single-modal cross-dataset settings and do not account for the non-interchangeable physical semantics of MRI and PET features, making it difficult to simultaneously eliminate domain discrepancy and preserve the discriminative cues each modality uniquely provides.

## Input / Output

The method consumes three data streams: a large set of labeled structural MRI scans (source domain), a small subset of labeled PET scans (~20% of target), and a larger pool of unlabeled PET scans (target domain), with source and target samples being mismatched. Inputs are processed through a CNN feature extractor and parcellated via the AAL anatomical template into per-brain-region feature maps (zero-padded to a fixed size), which are then organized into graph structures where nodes represent brain regions and edges encode feature similarity. The output is a discrete brain disorder class label (AD/MCI/NC on ADNI and AIBL; PD/NC on PPMI).

## Method

DSDA introduces a dual-stream architecture with two coupled modules. The Pathology-aware Node Importance Alignment (PNIA) module applies graph convolution to brain-region features and an attention network to produce per-region importance scores, with a cross-domain constraint loss enforcing consistency of critical-region identification between MRI and PET. The Dual-Path Differential Feature Processing (DPDFP) module then splits regions into critical (top-K by importance) and non-critical, applying fundamentally different operations to each: non-critical regions undergo topology-weighted feature alignment, where a cosine-similarity alignment loss is modulated by cross-domain topological structural similarity (node-degree cosine) to suppress noise amplification; critical regions undergo high-confidence feature fusion, where Monte Carlo dropout estimates prediction uncertainty to filter low-confidence pseudo-labels, a second layer enforces cross-modal label consistency between PET pseudo-labels and MRI ground truth, and a progressive linear weight (θ = e/E) gradually injects MRI critical-region features into PET features to transfer discriminative pathological cues without early-training instability. The overall loss combines supervised classification losses on labeled MRI and PET with the unsupervised alignment, constraint, and pseudo-label terms, balanced by a hyperparameter α.

## Results

DSDA achieves state-of-the-art performance across all three datasets: 86.6% accuracy (AUC 0.962) on ADNI, 87.7% accuracy (AUC 0.962) on AIBL, and 88.9% accuracy on PPMI for brain disorder classification with only 20% labeled PET data. On ADNI, DSDA outperforms the strongest baseline DDSPSeg by 1.3% in accuracy and 0.4% in AUC, while on AIBL it surpasses the next-best method SLA by 3.5% in accuracy. The method also reports the lowest standard deviation (±0.01) among all approaches, indicating more stable training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DSDA outperforms all 11 baselines on every metric (ACC, SEN, F1, AUC) across both ADNI and AIBL. The closest competitor is DDSPSeg [37], which achieves 85.3% ACC on ADNI and 82.2% on AIBL; DSDA surpasses it by 1.3% and 5.5% respectively. SLA [33] is the second-closest on AIBL (84.2% ACC), trailing DSDA by 3.5%. Among unimodal semi-supervised methods, FixMatch [25] and MPL [20] lag significantly (74.1% and 71.4% on ADNI), confirming the benefit of cross-modal MRI guidance. CAN [29] performs worst (45.2% ACC on ADNI), which the authors attribute to its design for mild intramodal shifts in wafer-map data rather than large cross-modal distribution gaps. ODADA [26] and DDSPSeg [37] were originally unsupervised methods extended to the semi-supervised setting for fair comparison, yet still fall short of DSDA's differential alignment strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the selective feature alignment line of work (e.g., attention-guided DA [12], gradient-based meta-knowledge alignment [30,36]) by inverting the alignment target: rather than aligning classification-relevant features as prior methods do for single-modal cross-dataset shifts, DSDA aligns classification-irrelevant features and preserves classification-relevant ones, arguing that cross-modal pathological signals (structural in MRI vs. metabolic in PET) carry non-interchangeable physical semantics that forced alignment would destroy. The graph-convolutional brain-region modeling and Monte Carlo dropout uncertainty estimation are repurposed from standard GNN and Bayesian deep learning toolkits, but their combination into a dual-stream disentanglement-and-differential-processing architecture is new. The paper does not build on a single prior method but synthesizes ideas from domain adaptation, graph neural networks, and semi-supervised learning into a unified cross-modal framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important bottleneck—scarce expert-annotated PET data—by leveraging abundant MRI labels through a principled cross-modal transfer strategy, and the identified critical brain regions (hippocampus, posterior cingulate for AD; caudate, globus pallidus for PD) align with established neurology literature, lending clinical credibility. However, the evaluation is limited to three datasets with relatively small sample sizes (e.g., 120 MRI / 285 PET for ADNI), the top-K region selection is dataset-specific and hand-tuned, and the progressive linear weighting schedule is a simple heuristic whose sensitivity is not thoroughly explored. Open questions include whether the align-irrelevant/preserve-relevant principle generalizes to other cross-modal pairs (e.g., fMRI→PET, CT→MRI), whether the graph topology assumption holds for non-brain anatomical structures, and whether the framework scales to larger, multi-site datasets with greater domain heterogeneity.
