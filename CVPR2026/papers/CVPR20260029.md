## Problem

Existing backdoor attacks for Vision-Language-Action models target token-based discrete action generation (e.g., label flipping, token substitution), which cannot be transferred to flow-matching VLAs whose actions are produced by ODE-based vector-field integration. Moreover, prior triggers are visually conspicuous and prior attacks produce kinematically anomalous motions that are easily detected. This leaves the continuous dynamics of flow-matching VLAs as an unexplored and critical attack surface.

## Input / Output

The target model (π0) consumes a multi-modal observation o_t comprising an RGB visual frame, a natural-language instruction, and the robot's proprioceptive state, and outputs a continuous action chunk A ∈ R^{d×H} (d action dimensions over H timesteps) by integrating a learned vector field from Gaussian noise. The backdoor is activated by a context-aware visual trigger (e.g., an inverted cup or a background object) embedded in the RGB input; when absent, the model produces its normal action trajectory. The malicious action A* is either a fixed pose (Pose-Locking) or a small constant offset from the benign action (Initial-Perturbation).

## Method

FlowHijack implants a backdoor by corrupting the flow-matching vector field v_θ during fine-tuning, using a total loss L_total = (1−α−β)L_FM + αL_BD + βL_mimic. The key mechanism is a τ-conditioned injection: the Vector Field Hijacking loss L_BD is applied only when τ ∈ [0, τ₀] (τ₀=0.4), exploiting the fact that flow-matching models oversample small τ to learn the initial trajectory direction; the ODE solver then amplifies this small initial directional error into a large final-action deviation. A Dynamics Mimicry regularizer L_mimic enforces that the L2-norm of the malicious vector field matches the stop-gradient norm of the benign one, ensuring the resulting motion is kinematically indistinguishable from normal actions. Two malicious action strategies are supported: Pose-Locking (forcing a fixed unsafe pose) and Initial-Perturbation (a small constant offset that causes consistent task failure while preserving motion plausibility). Context-aware triggers (Object State predicates and Scene Semantic augmentations) replace the conspicuous pixel patches used in prior work, and the attack is shown to bypass both target-position filtering and downstream clean fine-tuning.

## Results

On the LIBERO benchmark (4 suites), FlowHijack achieves up to 100% Attack Success Rate (ASR) with context-aware triggers (Object State and Scene Semantic) while maintaining Success Rate (SR) degradation under 3.5% relative to the π₀ baseline. For example, with the Object State trigger on LIBERO-Goal, FlowHijack (IP) reaches 100% ASR with 97.8% SR, versus BadVLA's 11.2% ASR. With Scene Semantic triggers, FlowHijack achieves 100% ASR on both LIBERO-Goal and LIBERO-Spatial, where BadVLA drops to 11.7% and 15.3% respectively. The ablation confirms all three loss components (L_FM, L_BD, L_mimic) are necessary: removing L_BD yields 0% ASR, removing L_FM causes catastrophic forgetting (0% SR), and removing L_mimic produces kinematically detectable malicious trajectories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is BadVLA [37], adapted from its original token-based OpenVLA setting to the flow-matching π₀ model. On the conspicuous White Pixel trigger, both methods are comparable: BadVLA achieves 95–100% ASR across all four suites, and FlowHijack (PL) matches or slightly exceeds this (100% on LIBERO-10 and LIBERO-Object). The decisive gap appears with stealthy context-aware triggers: BadVLA's ASR collapses to 11.2% (LIBERO-Goal) and 13.4% (LIBERO-Spatial) for Object State, and 11.7%/15.3% for Scene Semantic, because its feature-space separation objective conflicts with the VLM's invariance to semantically similar states. FlowHijack, by targeting the downstream vector-field dynamics directly, achieves 100% ASR on LIBERO-Goal for both context-aware trigger types and 100% on LIBERO-Spatial for Scene Semantic, while preserving SR within 1–3% of the clean baseline. FlowHijack also consistently maintains higher SR than BadVLA under the same trigger conditions (e.g., 97.8% vs. 94.4% on LIBERO-Goal with Object State), indicating less collateral damage to benign performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the backdoor attack literature on VLA models from the token-based paradigm (BadVLA targeting OpenVLA's discrete action tokens) to the fundamentally different continuous action generation mechanism of flow-matching models like π0. Rather than manipulating VLM feature spaces or discrete outputs, FlowHijack directly corrupts the learned vector field v_θ via a τ-conditioned injection that exploits the oversampling of small τ values inherent to conditional flow matching training. The dynamics mimicry regularizer, which enforces kinematic similarity between malicious and benign vector fields, has no direct analogue in prior token-based backdoor work and addresses a stealth requirement specific to continuous control.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic demonstration that the vector field dynamics of flow-matching VLAs constitute a distinct and exploitable attack surface, with practical implications as these models (π0, RDT-1B) become the default for real-world robot control. The finding that existing defenses (target position filtering, 10k-step clean fine-tuning) fail to eliminate the backdoor underscores that current security tooling for VLA models is inadequate for continuous generative policies. Key open questions include whether dynamics-aware defenses (e.g., monitoring vector field statistics during inference) can detect such attacks, and whether the results generalize beyond π0 to other flow-matching or diffusion-based robot policies.
