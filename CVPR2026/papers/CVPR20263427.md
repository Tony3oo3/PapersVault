## Problem

Existing instruction-based image editing (IIE) benchmarks evaluate models through mixed task evaluations that obscure a critical failure mode: inconsistent performance across tasks of varying semantic scales (e.g., attribute modification vs. entity replacement). Additionally, prior multi-turn benchmarks are limited to 2–3 dialogue rounds and have not been validated by professional designers, creating a gap between benchmark scores and real-world design workflow applicability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263427/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation sample is a quadruplet (source image, text editing instruction, ground-truth target image, ground-truth mask), with all images resampled to 768×768 (lanczos for images, nearest for masks). The single-turn track contains 1,725 shared-context task pairs where the same source image receives both a low-semantic-scale (attribute) and a high-semantic-scale (entity replacement) instruction; the multi-turn track contains 260 dialogue groups averaging 4.35 turns (up to 16) with instructions dynamically alternating between semantic scales. The model's output is a single edited image per turn, evaluated against the GT image and mask.

## Method

Omni IIE Bench introduces a dual-track diagnostic design: (1) Single-turn Consistency, which pairs attribute-modification and entity-replacement instructions on the same source image to directly measure cross-scale performance degradation, and (2) Multi-turn Coordination, which constructs continuous dialogues (up to 16 turns) that interleave semantic scales to test error accumulation and contextual coordination. The data pipeline uses GPT-4o for scene captioning and instruction generation, Nano Banana (Gemini 2.5 Flash Image) for high-fidelity GT image synthesis, and a GroundingDINO + SAM pipeline (with GPT-4o as an instruction parser extracting core entity nouns) for automated mask generation. A rigorous three-stage human filtering process—12 CV graduate students scoring four dimensions (instruction following, realism, background preservation, modification satisfaction) with 50% cross-validation and arbitration, followed by 5 professional designers assessing industry relevance and aesthetics—yields a final acceptance rate of only ~36%. The evaluation framework decouples scoring into global image quality (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, CLIP), decoupled regional fidelity (foreground and background LPIPS/CLIP computed on mask-separated regions), and instruction compliance (GPT-4o-generated QA pairs verified by an MLLM referee with a strict all-or-nothing scoring policy).

## Results

On the single-turn track, Qwen-image-edit achieves the highest Overall score of 0.687, followed by Step1X (0.680) and ICEEdit (0.626), while HQEdit performs worst at 0.457. On the multi-turn track, Qwen-image-edit again leads with 0.676 Overall, but all models degrade substantially compared to their single-turn scores (e.g., MGIE drops from 0.520 to 0.404, a 22% relative decline). A key diagnostic finding is that nearly all models exhibit significant performance degradation when transitioning from low-semantic-scale (attribute modification) to high-semantic-scale (entity replacement) tasks, and background preservation (BG-LPIPS) deteriorates markedly in multi-turn settings due to error accumulation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Qwen-image-edit dominates both tracks, achieving the best Overall scores (0.687 single-turn, 0.676 multi-turn) and the highest QA instruction-compliance scores (0.744 / 0.818), indicating strong alignment between generated edits and textual instructions. Step1X is a close second on image fidelity metrics (best LPIPS-ALL of 0.379 and PSNR of 15.845 in single-turn) but trails Qwen on instruction compliance (QA 0.580 vs. 0.744). ICEEdit and FLUX occupy the middle tier (Overall ~0.61–0.63), with FLUX showing relatively stable background preservation (BG-LPIPS 0.428) but weaker overall quality. InstructPix2Pix and HIVE cluster around 0.53 Overall, with HIVE suffering from poor instruction compliance (QA 0.259). MGIE is a notable outlier: it achieves competitive image-generation quality (PSNR 14.733, SSIM 0.480) but has the worst instruction-following score (QA 0.070), meaning its outputs are nearly identical to the source images — a failure mode that benchmarks without explicit instruction-compliance metrics would miss. HQEdit is consistently the weakest model across all metrics (Overall 0.457 single-turn, 0.501 multi-turn), with the highest LPIPS (0.689) and lowest PSNR (9.259), indicating severe generation artifacts. Across all models, multi-turn performance drops by 5–22% relative to single-turn, with background preservation (BG-LPIPS) degrading most severely, confirming the paper's central diagnostic finding about error accumulation in iterative editing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against two lines of IIE benchmarks: single-turn task-coverage benchmarks (I2EBench, EditBench, EditVal, EmuEdit, AnyEdit) that mix tasks without diagnosing cross-scale stability, and multi-turn benchmarks (CompBench, MagicBrush, ImgEditBench, MuCIE) that test temporal consistency but cap at 2–5 turns and ignore semantic-scale switching. Omni IIE Bench carves new ground by introducing a dual-track diagnostic design—shared-context single-turn pairs that isolate the low-to-high semantic-scale gap, and multi-turn dialogues up to 16 turns that interleave attribute and entity edits—combined with a decoupled three-dimensional evaluation (global quality, regional fidelity, instruction compliance) and an industry-relevance review by professional designers that no prior IIE benchmark has employed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark quantifies, for the first time, a systematic and near-universal performance cliff when models shift from low-semantic-scale (attribute) to high-semantic-scale (entity replacement) edits, and shows that multi-turn error accumulation degrades background preservation across all eight evaluated models. This exposes a blind spot in existing benchmarks that report isolated per-task scores and thus mask cross-scale instability. Open questions include whether the semantic-scale gap can be closed through architectural changes (e.g., scale-aware conditioning) or training strategies, and whether the 16-turn multi-turn setting generalizes to even longer real-world design sessions. The rigorous human validation (correlation > 0.85 with expert rankings) strengthens confidence that the automated metrics capture practical quality.
