## Problem

Existing multimodal reasoning benchmarks either emphasize symbolic/mathematical reasoning where visual understanding plays a limited role, or focus on fine-grained perception without requiring spatial planning. The joint evaluation of fine-grained visual understanding and spatial reasoning over information-dense, high-resolution visual artifacts remains underexplored, leaving a gap in assessing whether MLLMs can genuinely ground their reasoning in visual content rather than relying on language priors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260613/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each instance pairs a high-resolution transit map (average 5,839×5,449 pixels, far exceeding typical VQA resolutions) with a text query specifying two stops and requesting a route. Questions come in two types—short (single fixed template) and long (two templates requiring via-stop counts or explicit stop enumeration)—yielding 1,008 QA pairs across 30 cities in 13 countries. The model must output a structured route (sequence of line names, departure/arrival stops, and transfer points), which is then scored on both binary correctness and a continuous map score (capped at 20 for short, 40 for long questions) that rewards partial segment-level matches.

## Method

REASONMAP is constructed via a semi-automated pipeline: MLLMs extract line/stop metadata from maps (manually corrected), questions are generated from predefined templates over randomly sampled stop pairs, and reference routes are collected from map-service APIs with routes that cannot be visually traced discarded. Evaluation uses a two-level framework: a correctness check (verifying stop validity, route existence, and transfer consistency) and a quality score (map score) that compares segment pairs, via-stop counts, and IoU of via-stop sets against reference routes, with difficulty-aware weighting to emphasize harder cases. For the training baseline, the authors apply GRPO reinforcement fine-tuning to Qwen2.5-VL-3B/7B-Instruct using two rule-based rewards—binary accuracy and format compliance—enabling cross-city generalization without supervised fine-tuning on non-differentiable metrics. A key empirical finding is that among open-source models, base variants outperform their reasoning-tuned counterparts (due to visual confusion in iterative self-reflection), while closed-source reasoning models benefit from stronger visual grounding that enables effective error correction within the reasoning chain.

## Results

On REASONMAP (1,008 QA pairs from 30 cities), the best open-source model Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Instruct achieves 26.65% weighted accuracy on short questions and 24.22% on long questions (map score 5.09/8.80), while the best closed-source model OpenAI o3 reaches 63.02% and 59.11% respectively (map score 9.53/17.96). A counterintuitive finding emerges: among open-source models, base variants consistently outperform their reasoning-tuned counterparts (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-72B at 26.65% vs. QvQ-72B at 9.03% on short questions), whereas the opposite holds for closed-source models (OpenAI o3 at 63.02% vs. OpenAI 4o at 41.15%). The GRPO-based RL training baseline improves Qwen2.5-VL-7B from 13.28% to 26.22% (short) and 7.12% to 26.04% (long) accuracy in a cross-city setting, while also reducing token usage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark evaluates 16 MLLMs across open-source and closed-source categories. Among open-source base models, Qwen2.5-VL-72B (26.65% short / 24.22% long) outperforms InternVL3-78B (25.35% / 19.62%) and Kimi-VL-A3B-Instruct (12.76% / 12.33%), confirming a scaling trend. However, all open-source reasoning models (Kimi-VL-A3B-Thinking at 5.47%, Skywork-R1V-38B at 6.86%, QvQ-72B at 9.03%) underperform their base counterparts by 5–21 percentage points, attributed to visual confusion during iterative self-reflection. In contrast, closed-source reasoning models dominate: OpenAI o3 (63.02% / 59.11%) surpasses OpenAI 4o (41.15% / 42.80%) by over 20 points, and Doubao-415 (43.14% / 46.09%) beats Doubao-115 (34.20% / 38.02%), suggesting stronger visual grounding enables effective error correction in reasoning chains. The GRPO training baseline further shows that RL fine-tuning on REASONMAP lifts Qwen2.5-VL-7B by +12.94% (short) and +18.92% (long) accuracy in a cross-city generalization setting, while halving token usage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two previously separate benchmark lineages: fine-grained visual understanding benchmarks (V*Bench, VisualPuzzles, VisuLogic) that require detailed perception but minimal spatial planning, and spatial reasoning benchmarks (MapBench, CityBench, DriveBench) that assess navigation but remain coarse in visual granularity. REASONMAP departs from both by jointly demanding pixel-level visual grounding and multi-step route planning over high-resolution, information-dense transit maps, a combination the authors argue is absent from prior evaluations. For the training baseline, the paper repurposes GRPO from the LLM mathematical reasoning literature (DeepSeekMath) and adapts its reward design to the rule-based, non-differentiable metrics of the proposed two-level evaluation framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The counterintuitive finding that open-source base MLLMs outperform their RL-reasoning-tuned counterparts on fine-grained visual tasks challenges the prevailing assumption that reasoning training universally improves multimodal performance, and the visual-masking experiments confirm that genuine visual grounding (not language priors) is necessary for robust map reading. The two-level evaluation (correctness via accuracy + quality via map score) offers a more nuanced assessment than binary VQA metrics, and the GRPO baseline demonstrates that RL can close part of the gap in a cross-city generalization setting. Open questions include whether the open-source reasoning underperformance is specific to map-like structured visual inputs or generalizes to other fine-grained domains, and whether the gap can be addressed through better visual grounding during RL training rather than more reasoning steps.
