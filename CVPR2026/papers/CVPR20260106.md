## Problem

Local basis function methods like 3D Gaussian Splatting and RayGauss achieve fast, high-quality rendering but lack an explicit geometric representation, while neural implicit surface methods (NeuS, VolSDF) provide continuous surfaces but rely on global MLPs or hash grids that are less efficient and less locally adaptive. Rasterization-based approaches further introduce artifacts and cannot exploit a continuous surface definition. The paper asks whether a global implicit surface built from sparse local RBFs and their derivatives can be learned end-to-end via differentiable volume ray marching, combining the locality and speed of kernel-based primitives with an analytically differentiable surface.

## Input / Output

The method takes posed multi-view RGB images plus a scene point cloud (from MVS or LiDAR, with positions and normals) used to initialize basis centers, scales, and HRBF coefficients. It produces a 3D mesh extracted via Marching Cubes at 512³ (DTU) or 1024³ (BlendedMVS) resolution, followed by removal of triangles invisible from all input cameras, and can render novel views from the learned implicit field. Training runs for 15,000 iterations on an RTX 4090, taking roughly 40 minutes per DTU scene and 15 minutes per BlendedMVS scene.

## Method

The core representation is an HRBF Implicit Field F(x) = Σⱼ(αⱼφⱼ(x) − βⱼᵀ∇φⱼ(x)) + p₀, where each φⱼ is a localized Gaussian or Wendland C² kernel centered at a learnable position μⱼ with a learnable scale Rⱼ, and the constant offset p₀ > 0 is proven necessary to prevent boundary artifacts as the sharpness parameter s grows. The implicit function is coupled to volumetric rendering via the Objects-as-Volumes density σ(x,ω) = |ω·∇v(x)|/v(x) with v(x) = Ψ(sF(x)), and the radiance field is an RBF-weighted blend of local primitives parameterized by spherical harmonics and spherical Gaussians. Rendering is performed by BVH-accelerated volume ray marching (adapted from RayGauss, implemented in NVIDIA OptiX), which restricts evaluation to locally relevant basis functions for efficiency. All parameters—positions, scales, HRBF coefficients, radiance, and s—are jointly optimized via differentiable rendering with a photometric loss (SSIM + L1) and L2 regularization on the HRBF coefficients, initialized from a block-diagonal approximation of the HRBF linear system solved on the input point cloud.

## Results

On the DTU dataset, the proposed HRBF method achieves the lowest mean symmetric Chamfer distance of 0.54 mm, outperforming the previous best results of 0.58 mm (PGSR and Fast Dipole Sums) and substantially beating neural SDF methods like NeuS (0.84) and VolSDF (0.86). On BlendedMVS, the method attains a mean Chamfer distance of 0.52 mm versus 0.65 mm for Fast Dipole Sums and 0.81 mm for NeuS2. Training takes approximately 39 minutes per DTU scene and 15 minutes per BlendedMVS scene on an RTX 4090, placing it in the mid-range of training times among compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed HRBF method achieves the best mean Chamfer distance on both DTU (0.54 mm) and BlendedMVS (0.52 mm), outperforming all 13 baselines. On DTU, it improves over the closest competitors PGSR (0.58) and Fast Dipole Sums (0.58) by 0.04 mm, and over Neuralangelo (0.61) by 0.07 mm; the gap widens substantially against neural SDF methods (NeuS 0.84, VolSDF 0.86) and rasterization-based Gaussian methods (Gaussian Surfels 0.88, 2DGS 0.80, VCR-GauS 0.80). On BlendedMVS, the advantage over Fast Dipole Sums (0.65) is 0.13 mm, and over NeuS2 (0.81) is 0.29 mm. In terms of training time (39 min on DTU, 15 min on BlendedMVS), the method is faster than NeuS (480 min), VolSDF (600 min), Neuralangelo (1080 min), GOF (136 min), and Fast Dipole Sums (42 min), but slower than NeuS2 (5 min), Gaussian Surfels (8 min), RaDe-GS (20 min), PGSR (26 min), 2DGS (27 min), and 3DGSR (30 min). The method wins on every individual DTU scene except scene 65 (0.87 vs. PGSR's 0.61) and scene 69 (0.56 vs. PGSR's 0.48), where PGSR retains a narrow edge, and scene 114 (0.39 vs. PGSR's 0.32), where PGSR is also slightly better.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: classical HRBF implicit surface reconstruction (Macêdo et al. 2009; Liu et al. 2016), which solves a linear system to fit radial basis functions to oriented point clouds, and differentiable-rendering-based surface reconstruction (NeuS, Objects as Volumes), which learns a global neural implicit field from multi-view images. The key conceptual departure is making the HRBF formulation learnable end-to-end via differentiable volume ray marching, while borrowing the BVH-accelerated sparse-primitive intersection algorithm from RayGauss (the authors' own prior work) to keep evaluation local and efficient. Unlike 3DGS-based surface methods (2DGS, PGSR, Gaussian Surfels) that extract meshes from discrete primitives, this paper learns a continuous implicit field whose zero level set is extracted via Marching Cubes, and unlike Neuralangelo or Fast Dipole Sums, it uses localized RBFs with learnable positions and scales rather than global dipole kernels or hash-grid encodings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a sparse, local RBF-based implicit field can match or exceed the accuracy of global neural SDF methods (NeuS, VolSDF) and 3DGS-derived surface methods while retaining the rendering efficiency of BVH-accelerated ray marching, achieving SOTA Chamfer distances on both DTU (0.54) and BlendedMVS (0.52). The theoretical analysis showing that the polynomial offset p0 must be strictly positive to avoid boundary artifacts in the asymptotic sharp-density regime is a useful design principle for any localized implicit representation coupled with volumetric rendering. Limitations include the dependence on a point-cloud initialization (from MVS or LiDAR), the Gaussian truncation introducing small discontinuities in F, and training times of ~40 minutes per DTU scene; open questions include whether the approach extends to unbounded outdoor scenes without dense initialization and whether the BVH structure can be updated dynamically during optimization.
