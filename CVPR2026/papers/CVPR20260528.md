## Problem

Existing NLOS imaging systems (optical and 77 GHz mmWave) are fundamentally limited to a few meters of hidden-scene range because each diffuse bounce incurs quadratic intensity falloff under eye-safe power budgets. This paper identifies that X-band radar (10 GHz, λ=30 mm) converts typical relay-wall roughness into predominantly specular reflections, enabling ~10× longer NLOS range, but the resulting long wavelength severely degrades angular resolution and makes standard radar reconstruction methods inapplicable to complex multi-bounce scenes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a complex-valued range-azimuth (RA) map of shape H×W produced by coherent detection of FMCW chirps from a 4×8 X-band antenna array, where each pixel encodes the complex amplitude at a specific range-azimuth bin. The first stage outputs a two-channel dense confidence map (H×W×2) with per-pixel probabilities for LOS and mirrored-NLOS (mNLOS) peaks; the second stage takes the detected peak locations and produces a 3D point cloud of hidden objects in true Cartesian coordinates by applying the relay-wall mirror geometry plus a learned residual offset.

## Method

The core insight is that at 10 GHz the relay wall acts as a quasi-mirror (specular-dominant BRDF approximated by a Dirac delta), collapsing the general multi-bounce integral to a single-path virtual-scene model, which both simplifies the forward model and motivates a geometry-aware reconstruction. The neural pipeline has two stages: (1) a Swin-UNet transformer encoder-decoder trained with Gaussian heatmap supervision and focal loss to produce dense LOS/mNLOS confidence maps, effectively upsampling the coarse angular resolution of X-band radar; (2) a geometry-aware head that anchors each mNLOS peak to its LOS cluster, estimates the local relay-wall normal via line fitting, computes the analytic mirror reflection, and then predicts a learned residual correction (via attention-based aggregation of neighboring wall points) to account for wall curvature and other non-idealities. Training uses focal loss for the dense prediction stage and an ℓ₁ loss on the residual for the geometry stage, with the entire system trained on a physics-informed simulator that integrates antenna radiation patterns, FDTD material reflectance, and multi-path ray tracing in Unreal Engine.

## Results

On simulated data, the proposed method achieves a Macro-F1 of 0.85 for dense prediction and a reconstruction F1 of 0.45 with Chamfer Distance of 8.2 m², outperforming the next-best baseline (RTN) by 32.9% in Macro-F1 and reducing reconstruction CD by 65.3%. On real-world X-band radar data, the method reaches a dense-prediction Macro-F1 of 0.47 and reconstruction F1 of 0.20, again leading all baselines. Cross-modality simulation shows X-band radar achieves roughly 10× longer NLOS reconstruction range than optical (SPAD-LiDAR) systems under identical 1.6 mW transmit power, and the prototype demonstrates accurate hidden-object reconstruction up to 40 m (80 m round-trip path).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines: NLOS-CFAR (Scheiner et al., CVPR 2020), which extends a CFAR detector with a known relay-wall assumption; RTN (Paek et al., NeurIPS 2022), a 4D radar object-detection network adapted for LOS/NLOS recovery; and Further Than CFAR (Roldan et al., RadarConf 2024), a LiDAR-supervised heatmap predictor. On simulated data, the proposed method dominates all baselines across every metric: it achieves 0.85 Macro-F1 for dense prediction versus 0.57 (RTN), 0.41 (Further Than CFAR), and 0.23 (NLOS-CFAR), and reduces reconstruction CD from 14.0 m² (RTN) to 8.2 m². On real data, the advantage narrows but persists: the proposed method leads in dense-prediction F1 (0.47 vs. 0.30 for Further Than CFAR) and reconstruction F1 (0.20 vs. 0.18). Notably, Further Than CFAR reports a slightly lower reconstruction CD on real data (21.7 vs. 24.3 m²), which the authors attribute to its inflated dense-prediction CD from false-positive points near the wall that artificially simplify the wall-fitting step. NLOS-CFAR performs worst overall, particularly on real data where its reconstruction F1 drops to 0.01, reflecting its reliance on a known relay-wall geometry that does not hold in uncontrolled outdoor scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant NLOS imaging lineage (optical transient imaging at 532–850 nm and 77 GHz mmWave radar) by shifting to a lower X-band frequency (10 GHz, λ = 30 mm) where the wavelength exceeds typical relay-wall roughness, converting diffuse inter-reflections into predominantly specular ones. Conceptually, it repurposes the well-known Rayleigh roughness criterion from radar cross-section theory to argue that a *longer* wavelength is advantageous for NLOS, inverting the usual resolution-vs-range tradeoff that drives mmWave radar design. Methodologically, it combines a Swin-UNet dense-prediction backbone (borrowed from medical segmentation) with a geometry-aware residual reconstruction head, and builds a physics-informed simulator (FDTD + Unreal Engine + ray tracing) to generate training data, rather than relying on measured multi-bounce signals or known relay geometries as prior radar NLOS methods do.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstrated 10× NLOS range advantage over optical systems under equal transmit power, and the 40 m experimental reconstruction, open a practical pathway for long-range hidden-scene perception in autonomous driving and outdoor robotics where optical NLOS is infeasible. Key limitations remain: the method assumes specular-dominant relay walls (ignoring highly rough or absorbing surfaces), does not yet exploit Doppler information for dynamic-scene disambiguation, and the experimental validation covers only 122 frames across 15 scenes. Open questions include robustness to non-specular relay materials, multi-bounce and corner-diffraction scenarios in cluttered urban environments, and whether the neural reconstruction generalizes to unseen relay geometries without retraining.
