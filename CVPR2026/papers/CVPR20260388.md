## Problem

Multi-task optimization methods face a fundamental tradeoff: gradient-based approaches directly resolve conflicts but lack stochasticity and can converge to poor local minima, while loss-based methods are more flexible but ignore gradient-level interactions that drive negative transfer. No existing framework simultaneously leverages both gradient and loss signals while retaining the exploration capacity needed to escape suboptimal solutions.

## Input / Output

Each task-specific agent receives a compact observation consisting of the T×T Gram matrix of task gradients (encoding per-task magnitudes on the diagonal and pairwise alignments off-diagonal) concatenated with the T-dimensional vector of empirical task losses, rather than the full T×|θ| gradient tensor. The agents jointly produce T scalar actions that are softmax-normalized into convex weights, yielding a single aggregated gradient G = Σ w_t g_t used to update the shared MTL model parameters via one gradient descent step.

## Method

TaskForce casts multi-task optimization as a cooperative Markov game trained with MADDPG, where each task is an agent with a decentralized policy and a centralized critic. The key design choice is the Gram-matrix observation (gg^⊤ ∈ R^{T×T}), which compresses the high-dimensional gradient information into a T×T summary that is computationally tractable for RL while preserving both magnitude and alignment signals. The hybrid reward combines a loss-based term (log-transformed relative loss improvement across all tasks) with a gradient-based term (negative squared norm of the aggregated gradient, reformulated from the MGDA convex minimization problem) to simultaneously encourage Pareto-convergent directions and immediate loss reduction. Training follows the CTDE paradigm: agents are trained off-policy from a replay buffer using centralized critics that see all agents' observations and actions, but at execution time each agent acts only on its own local observation, keeping inference lightweight. The next observation is generated from the subsequent data point rather than recomputing on the same point, avoiding an extra forward-backward pass per step.

## Results

On NYU-v2 (3 tasks), TaskForce achieves Δm = −6.47% and Δt = −9.96% relative to single-task learning, outperforming the strongest baseline Aligned-MTL (−4.93%, −8.40%) by 1.54 and 1.56 percentage points respectively. On Cityscapes (3 tasks), TaskForce reaches Δm = −0.65%, surpassing Aligned-MTL (−0.02%) and all other methods. On the challenging 11-task QM9 benchmark, TaskForce attains Δm = +59.0%, beating NashMTL (+62.0%) and Aligned-MTL (+81.9%), demonstrating robustness under high task complexity and large loss-scale disparities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TaskForce outperforms all 11 baselines on the overall Δm metric across all three datasets. On NYU-v2, the closest competitor Aligned-MTL [35] achieves Δm = −4.93% vs. TaskForce's −6.47%, a 1.54 pp gap; NashMTL [32] (−4.04%) and IMTL [26] (−0.59%) trail further behind. On Cityscapes, TaskForce (Δm = −0.65%) edges out Aligned-MTL (−0.02%) by 0.63 pp, while CAGrad [25] (+1.46%) and NashMTL [32] (+9.38%) show substantial degradation. On QM9, the gap widens dramatically: TaskForce (+59.0%) beats NashMTL (+62.0%) by 3 pp and Aligned-MTL (+81.9%) by 22.9 pp, indicating that the MARL-based approach scales better with task count and loss-scale disparity. Gradient-surgery methods (PCGrad [45], CAGrad [25], GradDrop [4]) and loss-weighting methods (DWA [28], UW [18], RLW [1]) consistently underperform, confirming the paper's claim that neither pure gradient nor pure loss signals suffice. The sole exception is Cityscapes semantic segmentation mIoU, where Aligned-MTL (67.06%) marginally exceeds TaskForce (66.63%), though TaskForce still leads on the aggregate Δm.

## Relation to Prior Work

TaskForce positions itself at the intersection of cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (building directly on MADDPG's centralized-training/decentralized-execution paradigm) and multi-task optimization, where prior work has relied on deterministic heuristic aggregation rules (PCGrad, CAGrad, GradDrop, MGDA) or loss-level reweighting (DWA, UW, RLW). The closest prior attempt to use RL for MTL is IGBv2, which is single-agent and operates solely at the loss level; TaskForce departs by introducing task-specific agents that observe gradient-level information via a compact Gram matrix representation and are guided by a hybrid reward combining convex multi-objective optimization objectives (from MGDA) with loss-convergence signals. The key conceptual departure from all existing MTO methods is replacing fixed aggregation heuristics with stochastic, learned cooperative policies, which the authors argue reduces the risk of converging to poor local minima.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that MARL can be made computationally practical for MTO through the Gram matrix observation trick, keeping training overhead comparable to conventional gradient-based methods. Consistent improvements across indoor (NYU-v2), outdoor (Cityscapes), and molecular (QM9) benchmarks suggest the approach generalizes across loss types and task complexities. Open questions include whether the stochasticity genuinely helps escape local minima at scale (the paper defers this to an appendix), how the method behaves with very large task sets beyond 11, and whether the learned policies transfer across architectures or must be retrained from scratch. The framework also raises the broader question of whether RL-based optimization can replace hand-designed heuristics in other multi-objective settings beyond MTL.
