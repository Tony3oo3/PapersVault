## Problem

Prior work shows OOD detection performance varies across artefact types, but the underlying causes of this variation remain largely unexplored. This paper identifies a previously unreported bias: for hard-to-detect (near-OOD) artefacts, detection performance systematically improves when the artefact shares visual similarity (e.g., colour, brightness) with the model's region of interest and degrades when it does not — a phenomenon the authors term the Invisible Gorilla Effect.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260041/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The study takes RGB images from three public datasets (CheXpert chest X-rays, ISIC dermatology, MVTec-AD industrial inspection) and produces per-image OOD detection scores evaluated via AUROC, AUCPR, and FPR@95. OOD artefacts (ink annotations, colour charts, synthetic squares) are manually annotated by colour across 11,355 images, segmented using SAM or ground-truth masks, and classified as similar or dissimilar to the model's ROI via linear Euclidean RGB distance. Colour-swapped counterfactuals are generated by per-channel mean shifting of segmented artefacts to control for dataset bias.

## Method

The paper conducts a large-scale empirical evaluation of 40 OOD detection methods (3,795 hyperparameter settings) across 7 benchmarks and 3 architectures (ResNet18, VGG16, ViT-B/32), finding that feature-based methods suffer significantly larger AUROC drops (7.1 ± 1.8 pp) than confidence-based methods (1.5 ± 1.1 pp) when artefacts are visually dissimilar to the ROI. To explain this mechanism, the authors perform PCA on the primary model's latent features and identify a 'nuisance subspace' — the top-k principal components with the highest colour-discrimination score I_k — showing a positive Spearman correlation (ρ=0.47) between component variance and colour sensitivity, meaning colour shifts project onto high-variance directions that methods like Mahalanobis underweight. As a mitigation, they project features orthogonally to this nuisance subspace (F_⊥ = (I − UUᵀ)F), which substantially narrows the similar/dissimilar performance gap for feature-based methods, outperforming colour jitter augmentation which showed inconsistent and sometimes counterproductive effects.

## Results

Across 40 OOD detection methods on the ISIC benchmark, feature-based methods exhibit a mean AUROC drop of 7.1 ± 1.8 pp between visually similar and dissimilar artefacts, compared to only 1.5 ± 1.1 pp for confidence-based methods. Mahalanobis Score achieves 76.98% AUROC on similar-colour ink artefacts but drops to 63.64% on dissimilar-colour ink (a 13.3 pp drop), while confidence-based methods like MCP drop only 1.1 pp (69.83% → 68.74%). The effect is consistent across ResNet18, VGG16, and ViT-B/32 architectures and across ISIC, MVTec-AD, and CheXpert benchmarks. DDPM-MSE is the only method that does not exhibit an AUROC drop (−2.32 pp), and subspace projection of the top-5 colour-sensitive PCA components reduces the gap for Mahalanobis (63.6→75.8), FeatureNorm (52.9→74.5), and NAN (48.5→76.8) on dissimilar ink.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study rather than a method-proposal paper: the 40 OOD detection methods are the subjects of analysis, not baselines to be beaten. The central finding is that feature-based methods (Mahalanobis, FeatureNorm, NAN, LOF, KNN, KDE, XOOD-M) suffer the largest AUROC drops (mean 7.1 pp) when artefact colour is dissimilar to the model's ROI, while confidence-based methods (MCP, ODIN, ViM, WeiPer, ASH) are largely unaffected (mean 1.5 pp). Among feature-based methods, NAN shows the worst drop (15.75 pp: 75.58→48.46 on ink) and NAC/NuSA are exceptions with minimal drops (<2 pp). External methods show large but high-variance drops (6.8 ± 7.7 pp), with Deep SVDD (13.10 pp) and FPI (13.90 pp) severely affected, while DDPM-MSE is the sole method showing no drop (−2.32 pp). The subspace projection mitigation recovers most of the lost performance for Mahalanobis (63.6→75.8), FeatureNorm (52.9→74.5), and NAN (48.5→76.8) on dissimilar ink, confirming that the effect is driven by colour-sensitive high-variance directions in the latent space.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a diagnostic study of existing OOD detection methods rather than proposing a new detector. It builds on prior observations that OOD detection performance varies across artefact types [2, 37, 74] and that feature-based methods are sensitive to layer choice [51], but departs from the standard near-OOD/far-OOD framing [67] which assumes a monotonic relationship between global similarity and detectability. The conceptual inspiration is drawn from inattentive blindness in cognitive psychology [10, 58], repurposing the idea that attended targets make similar distractors more salient to explain why OOD artefacts resembling the model's ROI are paradoxically easier to detect. The counterfactual methodology (colour-swapped artefacts) extends prior counterfactual OOD analysis [3] from a single artefact type to a controlled colour-similarity axis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that feature-based OOD methods (the most widely deployed category) suffer up to 15+ pp AUROC drops when artefacts are visually dissimilar to the ROI reveals a previously unacknowledged failure mode with direct safety implications in medical imaging and autonomous driving, where artefact appearance is unpredictable. The PCA-based mechanistic explanation (colour variation aligning with high-variance latent directions that Mahalanobis-like methods downweight) provides a concrete, testable account rather than a mere empirical observation. Open questions include whether the effect generalises beyond colour to texture, shape, or spatial-frequency similarity; whether it persists in foundation-model-based detectors (excluded here due to data leakage concerns); and whether the nuisance-subspace projection mitigation transfers across tasks without re-deriving the subspace. The paper also highlights that broad colour-jitter augmentation is an unreliable fix, leaving a gap for principled, task-agnostic mitigations.
