## Problem

Existing AI-generated image detectors suffer severe performance degradation when adapting to newly emerging generative models due to catastrophic forgetting, and most continual learning approaches rely on replaying historical data, which is infeasible in real-world deployment. Moreover, fixed classifiers cannot cover the dynamically evolving diversity of scene domains encountered in open-world settings, limiting generalization across both new generators and new content types.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260944/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image as input and produces a binary real/fake prediction. A VLLM first processes the image to extract a scene confidence distribution and a textual content description, which are combined with a scene label and a real/fake indicator into Scene-Aware Prompts (SAPs). These prompts are encoded by a frozen CLIP text encoder and paired with the CLIP image encoder output for contrastive alignment, while a shared classification head produces the final binary label.

## Method

SAIDO combines two components: a Scene-Awareness-Based Expert Module (SAEM) and an Importance-Guided Dynamic Optimization Mechanism (IDOM). SAEM uses a VLLM to dynamically classify each image into a scene category (or detect a new scene), then routes it to an independent lightweight LoRA expert on a shared CLIP ViT-L/14 backbone, with new LoRA experts allocated on-the-fly when novel scenes appear. IDOM computes per-neuron importance via the Fisher Information Matrix for both real and fake classes, partitions neurons into core and non-core groups using an α-quantile threshold, and applies a differentiated gradient update: core neurons receive an importance-scaled gradient that blends a parallel component (preserving real-image knowledge) and an orthogonal component (enabling new fake-pattern learning) relative to historical task gradients, while non-core neurons update freely. This neuron-level projection strategy achieves a stability-plasticity balance without any replay buffer, and the overall loss combines a CLIP-style contrastive objective with a weighted cross-entropy classification term.

## Results

On the 9-task continual learning protocol (Protocol 1), SAIDO achieves 95.61% Average Accuracy (AA) and 3.94% Average Forgetting (AF) without any replay buffer, yielding relative reductions of 44.22% in average detection error rate and 40.57% in forgetting rate compared to the second-best method (Tang et al.). In terms of plasticity, SAIDO reaches 97.27% New Accuracy, surpassing all baselines. On the open-world generalization protocol (Protocol 2) with six unseen generators, SAIDO achieves 91.35% average accuracy, improving over the second-best method by 9.47%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SAIDO outperforms all six baselines on every reported metric. Against the strongest continual-learning baseline Tang et al. [38] (which uses a replay buffer), SAIDO achieves 95.61% AA vs. ~94.5% AA and 3.94% AF vs. 4.32% AF, while also reaching 97.27% New ACC vs. 92.88%, and does so without any replay. Against RegO [3] (the closest no-replay continual-learning method), SAIDO improves New ACC from 90.04% to 97.27% and reduces AF from 11.30% to 3.94% on the final task. On open-world generalization (Protocol 2), SAIDO leads Tang et al. by 9.47% in average accuracy (91.35% vs. 81.88%) and RegO by 11.70% (91.35% vs. 79.65%), with particularly large gains on R3GAN (+10.82%) and FLUX1-dev (+13.60%). Non-continual baselines (Universe, NPR, AIDE) show severe degradation on later tasks (e.g., NPR drops to 67.94% AA on VQDM), confirming that continual learning is essential for multi-generator detection. The CLIP+LoRA upper bound (99.56% New ACC) indicates that SAIDO's 97.27% New ACC is close to the plasticity ceiling while maintaining far superior stability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gradient-projection-based continual learning framework of RegO [3] from audio deepfake detection to AI-generated image detection, replacing RegO's coarse threshold-based neuron partitioning with a Fisher Information Matrix–driven, neuron-level importance-guided strategy (IDOM). Architecturally, it builds on the CLIP+LoRA parameter-efficient fine-tuning paradigm but introduces a scene-aware mixture-of-experts structure (SAEM) where a VLLM dynamically routes images to scene-specific LoRA experts, departing from the content-agnostic adapter approach of Tang et al. [38] and the replay-dependent strategies of DFIL and EWC. The key conceptual departure from prior continual-learning detectors is the explicit separation of core vs. non-core neurons with differentiated gradient handling (parallel projection for real-image knowledge, orthogonal projection for fake-image knowledge), rather than a uniform regularization or projection applied to all parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As generative models proliferate (diffusion, autoregressive, hybrid), detectors must continuously absorb new forgery distributions without catastrophic forgetting or expensive retraining; SAIDO's replay-free, scene-routed expert design offers a practical path toward deployable, extensible detection systems. The observation that the scene set naturally stabilizes across diverse datasets is a useful empirical finding that validates the finite-expert architecture, though it also raises the open question of how the framework behaves when truly novel scene categories emerge beyond the VLLM's recognition scope. Limitations include the per-inference VLLM call for scene routing (adding latency and cost), the reliance on a fixed CLIP backbone that may not capture all forgery artifacts, and the evaluation being confined to a single task order in the main text. Future work could explore whether the importance-guided projection strategy transfers to other continual-learning domains beyond image forgery detection.
