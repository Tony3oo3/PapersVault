## Problem

Current VQA benchmarks overestimate VLM capabilities by emphasizing simple foreground reasoning in low-resolution scenes or needle-in-a-haystack retrieval, failing to probe fine-grained perception in dense, high-resolution environments. The authors argue that basic visual understanding is far from solved and that model performance degrades sharply under the visual complexity of real-world dense scenes, a regime that existing benchmarks do not capture.

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs a high-resolution (≥ Full HD, standardized to 4K) scan of a public-domain painting depicting a densely populated scene with a handcrafted text question. Answers take four formats: four-option multiple-choice, binary yes/no (always paired with a logical opposite to measure consistency), free-form integer counts, and free-form OCR text spanning English, Latin, Chinese, Dutch, and Greek. Ground-truth answers are held private; only images and questions are released, with scoring performed via a rate-limited public evaluation server to prevent test leakage.

## Method

The benchmark comprises 150 high-resolution paintings from museum collections (sourced via Google Arts & Culture) and 2,720 manually annotated QA pairs across six task categories: activity recognition, attribute recognition, counting, OCR, visual reasoning, and global scene classification. Questions are constrained to be image-grounded, solvable without external knowledge, and free of language priors; a quality-control pipeline uses blind QA (image ablated) with three strong models plus Gemini 2.5 Pro bias detection to remove questions answerable from text alone. Difficulty is stratified into three splits (easy/medium/hard) calibrated by the fraction of 37 evaluated models answering correctly. The evaluation protocol applies task-specific answer-extraction heuristics (option-letter mapping, integer extraction, diacritic/punctuation normalization for OCR) and reports accuracy, RMSE for counting, and normalized Levenshtein distance for OCR, supplemented by a logical-consistency analysis on paired binary questions.

## Results

On VisualOverload (2,720 QA pairs across 150 high-resolution paintings), the best model o3 achieves 69.5% overall accuracy but only 19.6% on the hardest split, while the strongest open-weight model InternVL3-38B reaches 67.6% overall and 7.2% on hard. Counting is the most challenging task, with the best accuracy (Gemini 2.0 Flash) at just 41.7%, and OCR peaks at 62.7% (o4-mini). Scene classification is comparatively easy for frontier models (~93–95%), with even 8B-parameter models reaching 93.4%, while reasoning tasks see o3 at 75.1% versus near-random performance for most other models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among 37 evaluated VLMs, o3 leads overall at 69.5% accuracy, narrowly ahead of o4-mini (69.1%) and Gemini 2.0 Flash (68.1%), but all three drop sharply on the hard split (19.6%, 17.2%, 19.1% respectively). The best open-weight model, InternVL3-38B (67.6% total), trails o3 by ~2 points overall but falls far behind on the hard split (7.2% vs. 19.6%). Counting is the weakest task across all models: Gemini 2.0 Flash achieves the best 41.7%, while most open-weight models score 20–35%; OCR shows a similar gap between proprietary (o4-mini 62.7%, Qwen2.5-VL-72B 72.9%) and open-weight models (typically 25–50%). Scene classification is the easiest task, with frontier models at 92–95% and even InternVL3-8B reaching 93.4%, yet several smaller models (PaliGemma 2 3B at 32.7%, LLaVA 1.5 7B at 43.2%) struggle significantly. Specialized high-resolution models (VILA HD, ILM-XC2) underperform comparably-sized general-purpose models, and counter-intuitive scaling is observed where larger variants (e.g., PaliGemma 2 28B at 41.5%) score below smaller ones (PaliGemma 2 3B at 29.0% is an exception, but InternVL3 78B at 66.8% trails InternVL3 38B at 67.6%). On reasoning, o3 is a clear outlier at 75.1%, while most other models hover near the 25–42% consistent-chance baseline, with some (PaliGemma 2 3B at 24.9%) performing at or below random.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in VQA benchmarking by targeting dense, high-resolution scenes rather than the low-resolution, globally-focused images that dominate existing datasets (VQA v2, GQA, SeedBench, MMVet). It departs from recent high-resolution benchmarks (VILA HD, V*, OtterHD) that model needle-in-a-haystack retrieval of small details, instead requiring models to process the full complexity of visually overloaded scenes across six task categories. The use of manually annotated public-domain paintings provides a fresh, copyright-free image source, avoiding the recycled-data and automated-annotation biases common in prior VQA benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a critical gap: even the strongest model (o3) achieves only 19.6% on the hard split and 69.5% overall on tasks the authors frame as 'basic, knowledge-free' visual understanding, suggesting that fine-grained perception in dense scenes remains far from solved. The finding that specialized high-resolution models underperform equally-sized general-purpose VLMs challenges the assumption that dedicated HD architectures are necessary, pointing instead to backbone and training quality as the bottleneck. A key limitation is the art-only image domain, which may not directly transfer to natural-image perception, and the private ground-truth setup limits reproducibility of error analysis. Open questions include whether the observed encoder compression limits can be overcome with architectural changes or whether the gap is fundamentally a multi-modal alignment problem.
