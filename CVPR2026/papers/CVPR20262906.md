## Problem

Existing RL frameworks for video understanding in MLLMs suffer from unstable training, prohibitively high data costs (e.g., Video-R1 requires 165k cold-start and 260k RL samples), and rely exclusively on textual chain-of-thought reasoning, which accumulates errors and hallucinations over long videos. This paper asks whether a data-efficient, multimodal-element-based reasoning framework can build robust video understanding from a base MLLM with only 8k total training samples.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262906/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a video (64 uniformly sampled frames) and a text question as input, with a task-specific prefix appended to the assistant prompt to select among three response modes: a direct multiple-choice answer, an event time interval expressed as a relative ratio [start_ratio, end_ratio] wrapped in learnable <|event_start|> and <|event_end|> tokens, or a textual description of key visual elements. At inference, the model first produces event intervals and key elements in parallel; the key elements are encoded via a text encoder and matched against visual embeddings of uniformly sampled frames (using a Perception Encoder) to retrieve keyframes, which are then densely sampled alongside sparsely sampled non-key frames and fed back into the model for the final answer.

## Method

The framework consists of a two-stage training procedure and a multimodal inference pipeline. Stage 1 is a multi-task supervised cold start (~3k samples) that teaches a base MLLM (Qwen2-VL-7B-Base) to produce three distinct response formats—video QA, event grounding with relative time ratios, and key element generation—under a unified system prompt with task-specific prefixes. Stage 2 introduces a novel multi-task GRPO algorithm (~5k samples) in which the same video-query pair is rolled out under different task prefixes (event grounding vs. video QA) within a single batch, with task-specific rewards (IoU + format compliance for grounding; binary accuracy for QA) that are zeroed out for the non-target task so the two objectives do not interfere. At inference, the trained model performs event grounding and keyframe detection in parallel, then constructs a hybrid frame set by densely sampling the identified key event interval and sparsely sampling the remaining video, which is fed back with the QA prefix to produce the final answer—effectively using multimodal elements (temporal segments and retrieved frames) as intermediate reasoning steps rather than relying on a long textual chain-of-thought.

## Results

Starting from Qwen2-VL-7B-Base, the proposed VideoReasoner framework (3k cold-start + 5k RL data) achieves 62.0 on Video-MME Overall, 55.0 on LongVB, 64.6 on MLVU, 42.8 on LVBench, and 44.1 on VideoEval-Pro, surpassing Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct on all five benchmarks and Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct on three (MLVU, LVBench, VideoEval-Pro). On temporal grounding (Charades-STA), the model reaches 59.1 mIoU and 70.4 R1@0.5, substantially outperforming Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct (43.6 mIoU, 42.9 R1@0.5). On video reasoning, Qwen2-VL-7B-Base + RL improves VSI-Bench from 28.9 to 33.7 and MMVU from 61.1 to 62.4, while the hallucination metric (VideoHallu) improves from 34.3 to 39.0, outperforming text-only CoT reasoning (6.6).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct, the proposed method wins on all five main benchmarks (Video-MME 62.0 vs 59.3, LongVB 55.0 vs 55.2, MLVU 64.6 vs 61.7, LVBench 42.8 vs 39.7, VideoEval-Pro 44.1 vs 39.6). Against the stronger Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, the method wins on MLVU (64.6 vs 63.0), LVBench (42.8 vs 37.7), and VideoEval-Pro (44.1 vs 40.3), but trails on Video-MME Overall (62.0 vs 62.4) and LongVB (55.0 vs 59.3). Compared to Video-R1 [12] applied on Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, the multi-task RL approach yields more stable gains: Video-R1 degrades VSI-Bench (38.1→37.8) and MMVU (67.5→64.3), while the proposed RL improves both (39.1 and 68.0). On temporal grounding, the method achieves 59.1 mIoU and 70.4 R1@0.5, far exceeding Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct (43.6 mIoU, 42.9 R1@0.5) and approaching TimeSuite's R1@0.3 (79.4 vs 81.9). On the Tomato temporal reasoning benchmark, the method outperforms Video-R1 (26.8 vs 19.5 on Qwen2.5-VL-Ins) and improves the base model (27.8 vs 27.3). On VideoHallu, the multimodal reasoning approach scores 39.0 versus 6.6 for text-only CoT, supporting the claim that multimodal element reasoning reduces hallucination relative to purely linguistic reasoning paths.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends GRPO (originally proposed in DeepSeekMath for mathematical reasoning) to a multi-task video understanding setting, where a single model is simultaneously optimized for video QA and temporal event grounding. It departs from prior video RL frameworks like Video-R1 and VideoFT, which rely exclusively on long textual Chain-of-Thought reasoning, by introducing multimodal element reasoning (event grounding intervals and keyframe/key-element detection) as intermediate reasoning steps. The choice to train from a Base MLLM rather than an Instruct Model is a deliberate departure from prior work, motivated by the observation that Instruct Models carry a strong direct-response prior that resists reasoning-format adaptation and requires substantially more data to overcome.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical contribution is demonstrating that a total of 8k training samples (3k cold start + 5k RL) can elevate a Base MLLM to performance competitive with or exceeding Instruct Models trained on orders of magnitude more data, which has direct implications for the cost of deploying video reasoning capabilities. The multimodal reasoning inference pipeline (parallel event grounding + keyframe retrieval + dense sampling) offers a concrete mechanism to reduce hallucination compared to purely textual CoT, as validated on VideoHallu. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes beyond the Qwen2-VL family, how to close the remaining gap with the latest Instruct models (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL), and whether the added inference latency from parallel reasoning and dense sampling is acceptable for real-time video applications.
