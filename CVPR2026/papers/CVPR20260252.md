## Problem

Diffusion-based image editing services produce results that are inherently difficult for third-party forensics to localize or detect, because active defenses (watermarks, traceable chains) rely on proprietary consensus and post-hoc enhancement methods (e.g., ReLoc) disrupt visual quality. The paper asks how to make edited images endogenously detectable by passive, prior-free forensic tools without compromising the user's editing requirements or requiring the server to share any secret parameters.

## Input / Output

The method operates as a plug-and-play module on a pre-trained Latent Diffusion Model (e.g., Stable Diffusion). At each denoising timestep it receives the current noisy latent z_t, the binary edit mask M, and the text prompt P as conditioning, and outputs a modified noise tensor ε_t that is fed back into the standard denoising update. The final output is a single edited RGB image whose edited and unedited regions carry amplified forensic discriminative cues detectable by off-the-shelf tools (TruFor, SAFIRE, MVSS, MTNet, MPC for localization; CNND, UFD, DRCT for image-level detection) without any server-provided key.

## Method

FFIM is a three-phase controllable denoising framework applied at every timestep of a standard LDM. Phase I (Controllable Projection) projects each sampled noise tensor onto the orthogonal complement of the current latent feature z_t and a cumulative buffer of prior-step orthogonal noise components, using an ℓ₂-based projection, so that the retained noise is maximally dissimilar to the existing image content and cross-step consistent. Phase II (Implicit Detection) decodes the intermediate latent through the LDM decoder and a pre-trained forensic feature extractor (e.g., TruFor) to obtain forensic-space features, then computes a cosine similarity between edited and unedited regions using a multi-scale Gaussian-blurred soft-weight mask; if the similarity exceeds a threshold τ, Phase III is triggered. Phase III (Explicit Guidance) performs gradient-based adversarial optimization: the forensic similarity loss is backpropagated through the forensic model, decoder, and denoising update to produce a gradient on the noise, which is subtracted (with a small learning rate) and then re-projected onto the orthogonal subspace from Phase I, iterating until the similarity drops below τ or a maximum iteration count is reached. The entire procedure is training-free and requires no modification to the pre-trained diffusion model weights.

## Results

On pixel-level localization, FFIM achieves up to +13.0% F1 improvement over the DDPM baseline (TruFor on AniCOCO: 0.700 vs. 0.570) and +6.6% F1 for the cross-domain forensic method SAFIRE (AniCOCO: 0.581 vs. 0.515). On image-level detection, FFIM delivers +14.8%, +13.5%, and +27.3% AUC gains for CNND, UFD, and DRCT respectively, compared to the baseline. These forensic gains are achieved without compromising visual quality: subjective user-study scores (4.15/5) and objective IQA metrics remain comparable to the standard DDPM output.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FFIM is compared against two baselines: standard DDPM denoising (no forensic awareness) and ReLoc (a post-hoc restoration module that enhances artifacts after generation). Against DDPM, FFIM consistently improves F1 across all four datasets and all five forensic algorithms, with the largest gains on in-domain TruFor (+13.0% on AniCOCO) and cross-domain SAFIRE (+6.6% on AniCOCO). Against ReLoc, FFIM also wins in nearly every setting; ReLoc's improvements are unstable and sometimes negative (e.g., −1.3% F1 for TruFor on AniCOCO, −0.8% F1 for SAFIRE on PIPE), whereas FFIM never degrades below baseline. On image-level detection, ReLoc achieves +5.9% and +11.8% AUC for CNND and UFD but drops −6.2% for DRCT, while FFIM delivers consistent positive gains across all three detectors (+14.8%, +13.5%, +27.3%). In summary, FFIM dominates both baselines on forensic-friendliness metrics while maintaining comparable visual quality, confirming that endogenic noise control during denoising outperforms post-hoc artifact enhancement.

## Relation to Prior Work

FFIM builds directly on the DDPM/LDM denoising framework, intervening at each timestep to reshape the sampled noise rather than modifying the output image post-hoc. It departs from two prior lines: (1) active-defense methods (watermarking, backdoors, traceable chains) that require proprietary consensus between the service provider and the verifier, and (2) post-hoc forensic enhancement like ReLoc, which amplifies artifacts after generation is fixed. The key conceptual shift is treating forensic detectability as an endogenic constraint on the denoising trajectory itself, using orthogonal projection to isolate noise components that maximize feature separation between edited and unedited regions, with an adversarial surrogate-forensic-model loop as a fallback.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap: current active defenses (watermarks, C2PA) are invisible to independent third-party forensics, leaving a trust vacuum in legal, journalistic, and public-discourse contexts. FFIM's plug-and-play design means service providers could adopt it without retraining, and the gains hold across both in-domain (TruFor) and cross-domain (SAFIRE, MVSS, MTNet, MPC) detectors, suggesting robustness. Key limitations include incompatibility with flow-matching or direct-prediction models (SD3, FLUX), dependence on a surrogate forensic model whose quality bounds the gains, and untested robustness to downstream compression or re-encoding. Open questions include whether the forensic signal survives real-world distribution pipelines and whether adversarial users could learn to suppress the orthogonal noise structure.
