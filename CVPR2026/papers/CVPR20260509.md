## Problem

Accurate depth estimation is essential for 3D reconstruction and surgical navigation in posterior segment ophthalmic surgery, yet no real-world depth-annotated dataset exists for this domain because structured light, CT, and stereo matching are all impractical under surgical microscopes with their confined fields of view and short baselines. The only prior synthetic ophthalmic dataset (SMDE) covers only the anterior eye, provides static monocular frames, and lacks stereo views, temporal sequences, and dynamic instrument-tissue interactions needed for real-time microscope-guided procedures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260509/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset consists of 44,800 synchronized stereo RGB pairs at 512×512×3 resolution, organized as 50-frame temporal sequences across 896 scenes, each accompanied by pixel-perfect metric depth maps, surface normal maps, instrument segmentation masks, and full camera intrinsics/extrinsics. A small stereo baseline mimics the surgical microscope's binocular geometry, and post-processing applies radial lens distortion (k=0.00001) and gradient blur (with instrument edges preserved via separate masks) to emulate real optical imperfections. The 50-frame sequences can be consumed as individual labeled images for single-frame training or as video clips for temporal depth estimation.

## Method

RetinalDepth is generated via a Real2Sim2Real pipeline in Blender. In the Real-to-Sim phase, a 3D sphere is textured with 10 distinct ultra-wide-field fundus images (representing different pathologies), with the optic cup and disc carved as geometric protrusions/depressions to introduce true depth variation; a glass sphere simulates the vitreum with randomized refractive index and roughness, and six instrument types (grasping/peeling/foreign-body forceps, intraocular magnet, gas hook, endolaser probe) are modeled with articulated joints and metallic materials. Small-scale trajectories (shifting, shaking, gentle manipulation) and dual-camera rendering with a disc aperture for pupil simulation produce photorealistic stereo sequences via the Cycles engine. In the Sim-to-Real phase, the paper fine-tunes monocular (Depth Anything, Marigold, MoGE, ZoeDepth), stereo (VGGT, DUSt3R, MASt3R), and video (VDA-L, DepthCrafter, DAV) depth models on the synthetic data, and introduces Temporal Depth Variance (TDV) as a novel metric that quantifies frame-to-frame stability on the static retinal background by computing mean squared depth differences between consecutive frames restricted to non-instrument pixels.

## Results

After fine-tuning on RetinalDepth, VGGT achieves the best whole-scene depth accuracy (d0.5=0.892, d1=0.956, d2=0.986, d3=0.993, AbsRel=0.061), while DA leads on d1/d2/d3 thresholds (0.975/0.995/0.998). On the instrument segment, DAv2 performs best (d0.5=0.730, AbsRel=0.097), followed by MoGE (d0.5=0.710) and DA (d0.5=0.637). In the video setting, fine-tuned single-image models dominate spatial accuracy (VGGT d0.5=0.892) but exhibit high temporal variance (TDV=5.870), whereas zero-shot video model VDA-L achieves the lowest TDV (0.044) at the cost of poor spatial quality (d0.5=0.119).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Fine-tuning on RetinalDepth dramatically improves all baselines over their zero-shot performance (e.g., DA jumps from d0.5=0.323 to 0.889; VGGT from 0.332 to 0.892). Among monocular models, DA achieves the best whole-scene d1/d2/d3 (0.975/0.995/0.998) and lowest AbsRel (0.055), while DAv2 leads on the instrument segment (d0.5=0.730, AbsRel=0.097), outperforming MoGE (0.710) and DA (0.637). Stereo models VGGT and MASt3R excel on whole-scene depth (VGGT d0.5=0.892) but fail on instruments (VGGT d0.5=0.197, MASt3R d0.139), likely due to reflective tool surfaces breaking stereo correspondences. DUSt3R and MASt3R remain the weakest stereo baselines even after fine-tuning (d0.5=0.760 and 0.727 on whole scene). In the video setting, zero-shot video models (VDA-L, DepthCrafter, DAV) show negligible spatial accuracy (d0.5 ≤ 0.119) compared to fine-tuned single-image models (d0.5 ≥ 0.710), though VDA-L achieves the best temporal consistency (TDV=0.044) versus VGGT's TDV=5.870, highlighting the spatial–temporal trade-off.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Blender-based synthetic data generation paradigm established for endoscopic surgery (EndoUCL, EndoSLAM, SimCol3D, C3VD) and the anterior-eye dataset SMDE, but transplants it to the entirely unexplored domain of posterior segment ophthalmic microsurgery. The Real2Sim2Real pipeline is a conceptual framing that borrows from domain-adaptation literature, but the specific anatomical modeling (ultra-wide-field fundus textures mapped onto a sphere with carved optic cup/disc, refractive aqueous humor simulation, and articulated instrument trajectories) is novel to this surgical context. The introduction of Temporal Depth Variance (TDV) as a background-stability metric for video depth estimation extends the standard per-frame evaluation paradigm (AbsRel, RMSE, δ thresholds) with a temporal-consistency dimension that prior surgical depth benchmarks lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RetinalDepth fills a critical gap: no prior dataset—real or synthetic—provides depth annotations for posterior segment fundus surgery, making it impossible to train or benchmark depth models for this safety-critical domain. The finding that fine-tuned single-image models (especially VGGT) dominate spatial accuracy over dedicated video methods, yet exhibit the worst temporal stability (TDV = 5.87), highlights an unresolved tension between per-frame precision and inter-frame coherence that will matter for real-time surgical navigation. Key limitations include the qualitative-only real-world evaluation, the 512×512 resolution, and the reliance on camera motion to approximate tissue deformation rather than explicit biomechanical modeling, all of which constrain confidence in the sim-to-real transfer. Open questions include whether the dataset's procedural diversity (896 scenes, 10 fundus textures) is sufficient to cover the full range of intraoperative variability, and whether TDV generalizes beyond the fixed-camera assumption used in its definition.
