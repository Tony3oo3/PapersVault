## Problem

Range-view (RV) based LiDAR diffusion models achieve 2D photo-realism but produce systematic 3D geometry artifacts—depth bleeding at object boundaries and wavy surfaces on planar regions—because projecting 3D geometry into 2D depth images inherently loses sparsity and self-occlusion cues. The paper identifies that 2D diffusion models are theoretically bounded in spatial gradient magnitude due to Lipschitz continuity in pixel space, making them incapable of generating the sharp, authentic 3D boundaries present in real LiDAR data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261821/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a diffusion-generated RV depth image (typically 64×1024 for 64-laser systems, overprovisioned to 64×1024 even for 32-laser nuScenes) which is back-projected via reverse range-view projection into a 3D point cloud, optionally augmented with a down-sampled segmentation color map as additional input channels. The output is a rectified 3D LiDAR point cloud where per-point offsets are projected onto the radial direction of each original point, correcting depth errors while preserving the angular structure of the LiDAR scan.

## Method

L3DR is a two-stage framework: first, a semantic-conditioned LiDAR diffusion model (LiDM) generates structurally similar point cloud pairs imbued with RV artifacts to serve as training data; second, a 3D residual regression network (RRN) built on SPUNet sparse convolution predicts point-level 3D offsets that cancel these artifacts. A key theoretical result (Theorem 1) shows that 2D DDIM outputs have bounded spatial gradients, while 3D models operating on back-projected points can produce arbitrarily sharp borders because large depth differences between adjacent pixels place them outside the 3D effective receptive field. The Welsch Loss—an upside-down bell curve ψ_ν(x) = 1 − exp(−x²/2ν²)—replaces standard L1/L2 losses to suppress high-bias regions (e.g., shifted walls, random foliage points) and focus gradient signal on local geometry artifacts. At inference, the RRN is diffusion-agnostic: it can rectify outputs from any LiDAR diffusion model with only ~20 ms and 38 M additional parameters.

## Results

On KITTI360 unconditional generation, L3DR with LiDM backbone achieves FSVD 35.8, FPVD 26.1, JSD 0.182, and MMD 3.27×10⁻⁴, improving over LiDM by 7.7%, 10.0%, 13.7%, and 14.8% respectively. With R2DM backbone, L3DR achieves the best JSD of 0.165 (1.8% improvement over R2DM). On semantic-conditioned generation, L3DR improves average metrics by 11.6% on nuScenes (e.g., FSVD 81.3 vs. 86.6) and 7.0% on Waymo (e.g., FSVD 18.3 vs. 21.4), while on SemanticKITTI it achieves an MMD of 1.69×10⁻⁴, a 46.5% reduction over LiDM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

L3DR consistently outperforms all six baselines across every dataset and metric. Against the strongest diffusion-based baselines, LiDM [34] and R2DM [28], L3DR improves FSVD by 7.7% and 2.4% respectively, FPVD by 10.0% and 8.7%, and JSD by 13.7% and 1.8% on KITTI360 unconditional generation. Against earlier GAN/VAE methods (LiDARGAN/VAE [3], ProjectedGAN [36]) and the original LiDARGen [56], the improvements are dramatic — e.g., FSVD drops from 183.4 (LiDARGAN) or 39.2 (LiDARGen) to 35.8. The only metric where L3DR does not achieve the absolute best score is MMD on KITTI360, where ProjectedGAN (2.88) and R2DM (2.92) slightly edge out Ours-R2DM (2.90) and Ours-LiDM (3.27), though the paper notes this is within a narrow margin. On conditional tasks (nuScenes, Waymo, SemanticKITTI), L3DR with semantic-map input to the RRN further widens the gap, achieving up to 46.5% MMD reduction over LiDM on SemanticKITTI.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RV-based LiDAR diffusion line (LiDARGen, R2DM, LiDM) by adding a post-hoc 3D rectification stage rather than modifying the diffusion model itself. The theoretical analysis (Theorem 1) provides a principled justification for why 2D diffusion outputs are inherently smooth while 3D networks can produce arbitrarily sharp boundaries, positioning the residual regression network as a geometrically motivated correction rather than an ad-hoc post-processing step. The Welsch Loss draws from robust statistics to handle anomalous training regions, a departure from the standard L1/L2 losses used in prior point cloud regression tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The negligible 19.65 ms overhead and diffusion-agnostic design make L3DR a practical plug-in for any existing RV-based LiDAR generator, lowering the barrier to geometry-realistic point cloud synthesis. An open question is how well the rectification generalizes to fundamentally different generation paradigms (e.g., 3D-native diffusion or occupancy-based methods) where the artifact structure differs from RV-specific issues. The reliance on a conditional diffusion model to produce training pairs also raises questions about scalability to datasets without reliable semantic annotations.
