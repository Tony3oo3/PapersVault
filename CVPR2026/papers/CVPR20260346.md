## Problem

Electronic Navigational Charts (ENCs) are safety-critical documents encoding regulatory, bathymetric, and route-constraint information through standardized IHO S-57 vector symbols, scale-dependent rendering, and precise geometric structure, yet no existing benchmark evaluates whether multimodal large language models can reliably interpret them. General vision, chart, document, and consumer-map benchmarks all lack the intersection of formal geospatial symbology, multi-scale cartographic reasoning, and multi-constraint safety decision-making that defines professional maritime navigation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260346/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs an ENC chart image (rendered from raw S-57 data via OpenCPN in one of three lighting modes—day, dusk, night—at one of six scale levels from 1:50k to 1:300k) with a natural-language question. Perception and decision-making tasks require a multiple-choice answer, while spatial reasoning tasks require free-form numeric outputs: decimal-degree or pixel coordinates (evaluated at 200 px tolerance), compass bearing in degrees 0–360° (20° tolerance), or distance in nautical miles (20% relative-error tolerance).

## Method

The benchmark is constructed through a four-stage semi-automated pipeline: (1) S-57 binary charts are rendered via OpenCPN across three ECDIS-compliant lighting modes and six scale levels, then parsed into GeoJSON features using GDAL with IHO lookup-table mapping; (2) bidirectional pixel-to-geographic coordinate conversion is established via affine transformation matrices derived from two manually labeled control points per chart; (3) point, linestring, and polygon features are annotated with graph-coloring overlap prevention and expert verification; (4) 20,490 QA pairs are generated from task-specific templates with ground truth computed using Haversine distance, arctangent bearing, and affine coordinate transforms, and distractors derived from common navigation errors. The evaluation framework organizes tasks into a three-tier cognitive hierarchy—Perception (4 tasks), Spatial Reasoning (3 tasks), and Maritime Decision-Making (3 tasks)—and assesses 10 state-of-the-art MLLMs under a unified zero-shot protocol, with additional ablations across lighting modes and scale levels to probe rendering robustness.

## Results

On ENC-Bench, the best model (Gemini-2.5-Pro) achieves only 47.88% average accuracy across all Perception and Decision-Making tasks, far below the near-perfect accuracy required in operational maritime navigation. Symbol Recognition is the most accessible task (69.53% for Gemini-2.5-Pro), while Anchorage Selection—the most complex multi-constraint task—yields a best score of just 30.50% (Qwen3-VL-235B-Thinking), barely above the 25% random baseline. On Spatial Reasoning, coordinate localization peaks at 21.43% Acc@200px (Gemini-2.5-Pro, pixel mode), bearing calculation reaches 55.64% Acc@20° (Qwen3-VL-235B-Thinking), and distance measurement tops out at 25.93% Acc@0.2 (Gemini-2.5-Flash) with mean relative errors exceeding 40% for all models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini-2.5-Pro leads overall at 47.88% average, narrowly ahead of Qwen3-VL-235B-Instruct (46.91%) and Qwen3-VL-235B-Thinking (46.68%), while GPT-4o trails significantly at 34.87%. On individual tasks, leadership is fragmented: Gemini-2.5-Pro dominates Symbol Recognition (69.53%), Qwen3-VL-235B-Thinking wins Track Direction (75.18%) and Safety Assessment (61.54%), GLM-4.5V edges out on Safety (65.67%), and Qwen3-VL-235B-Instruct leads Point Features (51.79%). In Spatial Reasoning (Table 4), Qwen3-VL-235B-Thinking achieves the best bearing accuracy (55.64% Acc@20°), while Gemini-2.5-Pro leads coordinate localization (21.43% Acc@200px in pixel mode); all models remain far below operational thresholds. The Thinking variants of Qwen3-VL consistently outperform their Instruct counterparts on reasoning-heavy tasks (Track, Safety, Anchor, Direction), confirming that extended chain-of-thought helps decomposable geometric problems but fails to close the gap on multi-constraint optimization like Anchorage Selection, where even the best model (30.50%) barely exceeds random chance (25%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first benchmark specifically targeting Electronic Navigational Chart (ENC) understanding, a domain that sits at the intersection of chart/document VQA (ChartQA, DocVQA), geospatial reasoning (MapQA, MapEval, RSVQA), and structured geometry (GeoQA, MathVista) but is not captured by any of them. Rather than building on a specific prior method, it repurposes existing rendering and geospatial tooling (OpenCPN, GDAL, Labelme) into a calibrated pipeline that generates expert-validated QA pairs from raw IHO S-57 vector data, explicitly positioning itself against the gap where no prior benchmark combines standardized maritime symbology, precise nautical geospatial computation, multi-scale cartographic rendering, and multi-constraint safety decision-making. The evaluation protocol (zero-shot, unified prompts, 10 frontier MLLMs) follows standard benchmark practice but applies it to a domain where near-perfect accuracy is operationally required.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ENC-Bench establishes essential evaluation infrastructure for a safety-critical professional domain where interpretation errors translate directly to maritime casualties, and its methodology is explicitly designed to extend to other standardized-symbolic domains (aviation charts, infrastructure blueprints, scientific visualization). The finding that the best model (Gemini-2.5-Pro) achieves only 47.88% overall—and barely exceeds random on multi-constraint anchorage selection (30.50% vs. 25% baseline)—quantifies a substantial gap between general MLLM capabilities and professional deployment readiness. Key open questions include: how to improve symbolic grounding for formal geospatial notation, whether explicit constraint-enumeration architectures can address multi-objective safety reasoning, and whether rendering-aware processing (adapting to lighting/scale) is learnable or requires architectural changes. The benchmark's zero-shot protocol also leaves open whether domain-specific fine-tuning or retrieval-augmented approaches could close the gap.
