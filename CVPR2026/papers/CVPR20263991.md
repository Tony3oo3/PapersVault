## Problem

Audio pre-training remains fragmented across speech, music, and environmental sound sub-fields, and is fundamentally bottlenecked by its reliance on weak, noisy, and scale-limited labels—current caption datasets barely exceed one million pairs and are often just fluent rewrites of sparse tags. The paper argues that, drawing from vision's ImageNet blueprint, the audio domain needs a large-scale, strongly-supervised, unified label system that bridges all three major audio domains before objective design can be meaningfully compared.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes raw audio clips (resampled to 16 kHz, converted to 80-dimensional log-Mel filterbank features with 25 ms window and 10 ms hop) and produces a frozen general-purpose audio representation from a Zipformer-M encoder. Supervision is derived from two sources generated by Qwen3-Omni-Captioner: (1) SOTA-quality natural-language captions (using the final summary paragraph) for audio-language objectives, and (2) a data-driven Unified Tag System (UTS) of up to 3,000 tags selected via TF-IDF for tag-oriented objectives. At evaluation time the encoder is frozen and paired with task-specific heads (linear classifiers, BERT/RoBERTa text encoders, BART decoders, or a Qwen2.5-7B LLM adapter).

## Method

The core contribution is a data-centric pipeline that first uses Qwen3-Omni-Captioner to generate rich captions for 400k diverse audio clips, then employs Qwen2.5-7B as an LLM tag parser to distill candidate tags from each caption, and finally applies TF-IDF scoring to select a fixed-size Unified Tag System (K ∈ {800, 1k, 1.5k, 2k, 3k}) that unifies speech, music, and environmental sounds. On this strong supervision source, the paper systematically compares four pre-training objectives: Multi-Tag Classification (BCE loss on multi-hot vectors), Parallel Decoding (non-autoregressive tag-sequence generation that forces the encoder to encode all tag information in a single vector), Contrastive learning (symmetric InfoNCE with BERT text encoder), and a Multi-Task combination of MTC + mixed AR/PAR captioning. The Zipformer-M audio encoder is paired with either a linear head (MTC), a BART-base decoder (PAR/Captioning), or a BERT-base text encoder (Contrastive), all trained from scratch on 8 V100 GPUs. The key empirical finding is that data quality and semantic coverage drive performance more than data volume (UTS models outperform AudioSet-trained baselines with 5× less data), while the choice of objective primarily dictates downstream task specialization rather than overall capability.

## Results

On linear probing (mean-pooling), the Multi-Task (Ours) model achieves the best in-class scores among proposed methods on general audio tasks (FSD-50k: 0.485, VggSound: 40.81, AS-Strong: 0.140), while MTC (Ours-UTS) leads on speech (VoxCeleb2: 37.10) and CREMA-D (66.01). For audio-language alignment, Contrastive-scratch (Ours) dominates retrieval (AudioCaps R@1: 29.66, ParaSpeechCaps: 55.3), and Multi-Task (Ours) leads captioning (AudioCaps: 45.17, ParaSpeechCaps: 46.01). On AIR-Bench open-formed QA, MTC (Ours-UTS) achieves the highest Music QA score (6.16) and near-perfect speech classification (92.64% gender, 86.59% age), decisively outperforming the MTC (AudioSet) baseline (5.61 Music QA, 45.06% gender).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MTC (AudioSet) [27], which uses 5× more data (~2M vs 400k clips), the UTS models lose on in-domain general audio (FSD-50k: 0.459 vs 0.656; VggSound: 37.70 vs 56.46) but dramatically win on out-of-domain speech: MTC (Ours-UTS) scores 37.10 on VoxCeleb2 vs 18.84, and on AIR-Bench Music QA (6.16 vs 5.61) and speech gender classification (92.64% vs 45.06%). Against the same-400k baselines from [74] (Contrastive-scratch and Captioning-scratch trained on lower-quality original captions), the paper's SOTA-caption models consistently outperform: Captioning-scratch (Ours) beats Captioning-scratch [74] on 8 of 10 alignment metrics (e.g., AudioCaps captioning 45.07 vs 43.58, ParaSpeechCaps 45.81 vs 42.85), and Contrastive-scratch (Ours) wins on CREMA-D (67.29 vs 63.74), MTAT (0.396 vs 0.384), and AudioCaps retrieval (29.66 vs 28.73). Against SSL models, BEATs [10] still leads on FSD-50k (0.565) and NSynth (75.90), while Wav2vec 2.0 [2] retains an edge on VoxCeleb2 (51.60 vs 37.10 with mean-pooling, though PAR reaches 60.97 with attention pooling); MERT [49] leads on NSynth (72.60) but is competitive on MTAT (0.402 vs 0.396). Overall, the paper's methods trade some in-domain audio classification strength for substantially better cross-domain generalization and audio-language alignment, with the Multi-Task model offering the most balanced profile across all three evaluation axes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper explicitly departs from the dominant audio-language pre-training paradigm (CLAP, captioning with weak/noisy labels) by drawing an analogy to vision's ImageNet and Multi-Tag Classification blueprint, arguing that audio needs its own large-scale strong supervision framework before scaling to web-scale VLM-style training. Methodologically, it builds on non-autoregressive sequence modeling (Gu et al., ICLR 2018) for the Parallel Decoding objective and on CapPA (Tschannen et al., NeurIPS 2023) for the mixed AR/PAR captioning strategy, but repurposes both in a tag-prediction context rather than free-form text generation. The data pipeline is novel in leveraging a high-fidelity audio captioner (Qwen3-Omni-Captioner) followed by LLM-based tag parsing to construct the first Unified Tag System bridging speech, music, and environmental sounds, a conceptual leap beyond AudioSet's 527-class ontology.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding that a 400k-clip UTS-trained model outperforms a ~2M-clip AudioSet baseline on out-of-domain generalization (especially speech) challenges the prevailing assumption that data volume is the primary scaling axis in audio pre-training, redirecting community attention toward supervision quality and semantic coverage. The systematic objective comparison (MTC vs. PAR vs. CLAP vs. Captioning vs. Multi-Task) provides practical guidance: no single objective dominates all downstream tasks, and the Multi-Task hybrid emerges as the most balanced choice. Key open questions remain: whether the quality-over-volume advantage persists at multi-million-clip scales, how to mitigate the single-captioner bias in UTS construction, and whether a unified objective can eliminate the observed task specialization without compromise.
