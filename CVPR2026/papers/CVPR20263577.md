## Problem

Evaluating HDR video color grading quality is difficult because existing HDR VQA methods target signal-level distortions (compression, noise) rather than high-level stylistic and semantic coherence, and pre-trained VLMs lack exposure to HDR photometry (PQ absolute-luminance encoding), causing them to misinterpret HDR brightness and color. Additionally, subjective quality labels are inherently noisy and multi-scale, making direct supervised alignment unstable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263577/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes HLG-encoded HDR video frames (4K sources sampled at 5 fps, resized to 960×540) paired with structured textual HDR descriptors (peak/average luminance, highlight-area ratio, shadow variance, mean chroma saturation, midtone contrast). It produces a short natural-language rationale followed by a scalar score in [-6, 10] via a fixed template (\texttt{<answer> s </answer>}), where 0 denotes parity with the SDR reference and negative values indicate degradation; video-level scores are obtained by averaging per-frame predictions.

## Method

HDR-VLM uses a two-stage pipeline on a Qwen2.5-VL 7B backbone. Stage 1 bridges the SDR-to-HDR domain gap by encoding all inputs into HLG (whose lower-brightness gamma segment is SDR-compatible, providing a natural curriculum) and applying progressive fine-tuning: first tuning only the ViT for HDR-sensitive cues, then jointly training ViT + projection MLP for multimodal alignment, and finally unfreezing all modules for instruction tuning on 40K dual-reference samples with factorized HDR descriptors. Stage 2 aligns the model with noisy human preferences via GRPO using a coarse-to-fine reward schedule: Step 1 (1500 steps) combines an endogenous Spearman rank-correlation reward over dynamic-K-means grade levels with a sign-consistency reward to learn robust inter-grade ordering; Step 2 (500 steps) adds an exogenous anchoring reward that replaces predicted per-grade means with ground-truth means plus a clipped distance reward to calibrate absolute score magnitudes without disturbing the learned order.

## Results

On the production-sourced HDR Color Grading Subjective Dataset, HDR-VLM achieves a PLCC of 0.9033 and SROCC of 0.8667, the only method to exceed 0.9 on PLCC. This surpasses the strongest pre-trained VLM (Gemini 2.5 Pro, PLCC 0.8194 / SROCC 0.8296) by +0.084 PLCC and the best RL-based competitor (VQAThinker, PLCC 0.8745 / SROCC 0.8386) by +0.029 PLCC. Traditional full-reference metrics (both SDR and HDR) perform poorly, with the best (ColorVideoVDP) reaching only PLCC 0.4275 / SROCC 0.5747, confirming that signal-level distortion metrics are ill-suited to creative color-grading assessment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HDR-VLM outperforms all 17 baselines on both PLCC and SROCC. Against traditional full-reference metrics, the gap is dramatic: the best SDR metric (SSIM, PLCC 0.4260) and best HDR metric (ColorVideoVDP, PLCC 0.4275) trail HDR-VLM by roughly 0.48 in PLCC, confirming that pixel-level and visibility-difference models cannot capture creative grading intent. Among deep VQA models, FuPiC [46] is the closest non-VLM competitor (PLCC 0.8621, SROCC 0.8287), but HDR-VLM still leads by +0.041 PLCC and +0.038 SROCC. Among pre-trained VLMs, Gemini 2.5 Pro [12] is the strongest (PLCC 0.8194, SROCC 0.8296), yet HDR-VLM's domain adaptation and RL alignment yield a +0.084 PLCC improvement. Among RL-based VQA methods, VQAThinker [6] (PLCC 0.8745, SROCC 0.8386) and VisualQuality-R1 [51] (PLCC 0.8418, SROCC 0.8212) benefit from the paper's Stage-1 HLG domain adaptation but lack the progressive ranking reward, leaving them 0.029 and 0.062 behind on PLCC respectively. The consistent margin across all categories indicates that both the HLG-based domain adaptation and the coarse-to-fine GRPO reward schedule contribute meaningfully to the final performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) HDR video quality assessment, which has historically focused on signal-level distortions (HDR-VDP, ColorVideoVDP, LGFM) rather than creative grading quality; (2) VLM-based IQA/VQA, where recent RL-augmented methods (Q-Insight, VisualQuality-R1, VQAThinker) have shown that GRPO-style policy optimization improves interpretability and generalization; and (3) domain adaptation of VLMs, where progressive unfreezing and staged objectives mitigate catastrophic forgetting. The key departure is the insight that HLG encoding serves as a natural SDR-to-HDR curriculum for VLMs, and the design of a two-step endogenous-to-exogenous ranking reward that addresses the multi-scale noise inherent in subjective grading data—neither of which appears in prior HDR QA or VLM-IQA work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper introduces the first production-sourced HDR color grading subjective dataset (700 videos, 9,750 pairwise judgments) and demonstrates that a VLM can be adapted to perceive HDR photometry and produce calibrated, interpretable quality scores—something signal-level metrics fundamentally cannot do. This opens a path toward automated, explainable quality control in HDR content pipelines. However, the evaluation is confined to a single dataset, a single display luminance (1,000-nit), and a single VLM backbone (Qwen2.5-VL 7B), leaving open whether the HLG-bridge strategy and progressive RL generalize across display types, HDR formats, or model scales. The authors themselves flag temporal awareness and display adaptation as key future directions.
