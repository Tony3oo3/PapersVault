## Problem

Unified diffusion editors process all editing conditions (text, mask, reference) through a fixed shared backbone with static concatenation or additive adapters, which cannot dynamically prioritize or suppress conflicting modalities. This leads to artifacts such as color bleeding at mask boundaries, identity or style drift, and inconsistent behavior when multiple conditions must be simultaneously satisfied, with the relative importance of each signal shifting across the diffusion trajectory.

## Input / Output

CARE-Edit accepts a text prompt, a base RGB image, an optional reference RGB image, and a user-defined binary or soft mask (all at the same spatial resolution), which are encoded by frozen text, DINO/VAE, and mask encoders into latent token sequences concatenated into a unified N×d representation. The output is a single edited RGB image. The mask is iteratively refined in latent space during denoising rather than used as a fixed spatial gate, and the reference image is optional depending on whether the task is instruction-based or subject-driven.

## Method

CARE-Edit inserts a lightweight latent-attention router into a frozen FLUX DiT backbone that assigns each diffusion token to a sparse top-K (K=3) subset of four heterogeneous experts—Text (cross-attention for semantic synthesis), Mask (convolutional adapter gated by the refined mask), Reference (FiLM modulation for identity/style transfer), and Base (cross-attention for global coherence)—conditioned on local token features, a global task embedding, and the diffusion timestep. Three complementary modules complete the pipeline: Mask Repaint iteratively refines the user mask via a residual field predicted from current latent and reference geometry; Latent Mixture fuses expert outputs through a convex combination weighted by routing confidence, then blends with the base expert via a timestep-adaptive gate; and a shared expert with fixed fraction λ_shared prevents routing collapse. Training uses LoRA adapters on the frozen backbone with a progressive curriculum (40K single-task iterations followed by 60K multi-task iterations), optimizing a combined loss of diffusion reconstruction, load-balancing, mask-boundary consistency, and mixture-smoothness regularizers.

## Results

On EMU-Edit, CARE-Edit achieves the best CLIPout (0.313) and DINO (0.835) scores, with CLIPim of 0.868, outperforming all unified editors and matching or surpassing task-specific methods. On MagicBrush, it attains the highest CLIPout (0.324) and a competitive L1 (0.052). On DreamBench++, CARE-Edit achieves the best scores across all metrics in both single-object (DINO-I 0.874, CLIP-I 0.792, CLIP-T 0.325) and multiple-object (DINO-I 0.568, CLIP-I 0.720, CLIP-T 0.327) settings, consistently outperforming the strongest baseline OmniGen2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CARE-Edit outperforms the strongest unified editor OmniGen2 on CLIPout (0.313 vs 0.306) and DINO (0.835 vs 0.832) on EMU-Edit, and on CLIPout (0.324 vs 0.306) on MagicBrush, though it trails slightly on MagicBrush CLIPim (0.894 vs 0.905) and DINO (0.885 vs 0.889). Against ACE++, CARE-Edit leads by a wide margin on all four EMU-Edit metrics (e.g., CLIPout 0.313 vs 0.256) and all four MagicBrush metrics (e.g., DINO 0.885 vs 0.823). On DreamBench++, CARE-Edit wins on all six metrics in both single- and multiple-object settings, with the closest competitor OmniGen2 trailing by 0.013 on DINO-I (0.874 vs 0.861) and 0.007 on CLIP-I (0.792 vs 0.784) for single-object, and by 0.008/0.007/0.008 for the multiple-object metrics. Compared to task-specific methods (PnP, Null-Text, InstructPix2Pix, EMU-Edit), CARE-Edit surpasses all on CLIPout and DINO for EMU-Edit while using a unified architecture. For subject-driven baselines (DreamBooth, BLIP-Diffusion, OminiControl, UNO), CARE-Edit achieves the highest DINO-I, CLIP-I, and CLIP-T in both DreamBench++ settings, with the largest gaps over DreamBooth (e.g., DINO-I 0.874 vs 0.552 single-object).

## Relation to Prior Work

CARE-Edit departs from the dominant ControlNet/OmniControl paradigm of static multi-condition fusion (concatenation or additive adapters) by introducing a Mixture-of-Experts routing mechanism adapted from Switch Transformers and EC-DiT, but applied to heterogeneous, modality-specific experts (text, mask, reference, base) rather than homogeneous capacity-scaling experts. It builds on the FLUX DiT backbone with LoRA fine-tuning, following the adapter-based fine-tuning recipe of OmniControl, and incorporates curriculum learning to guide expert specialization. The key conceptual shift is from treating all conditioning signals as equally weighted inputs to a shared pathway, to dynamically allocating computation per token and per timestep based on the editing task's needs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical pain point in unified image editing—conflicting multi-condition signals causing artifacts like color bleeding and identity drift—and provides a principled architectural solution with interpretable expert specialization. However, the performance gains over strong baselines like OmniGen2 are modest (e.g., 0.874 vs 0.861 DINO-I on DreamBench++), and the fixed set of four experts plus the top-K hyperparameter limit generality. Open questions include whether the expert set can be dynamically expanded for novel edit types, whether the routing mechanism generalizes beyond the FLUX backbone, and whether the curriculum training schedule is truly necessary or merely a training trick.
