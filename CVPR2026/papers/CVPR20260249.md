## Problem

Existing distribution matching distillation methods like DMD2 require thousands of GPU hours and naively combine distribution-matching and adversarial gradients at every timestep, introducing conflicting objectives that slow convergence. Additionally, applying reinforcement learning to few-step distilled models is notoriously unstable and prone to reward hacking, producing overexposed or oil-painted artifacts.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt as input and produces a generated RGB image in just 4 function evaluations (NFE), compared to 100+ steps for the teacher model. It is demonstrated on both score-based (SDXL) and flow-matching (SD3-Medium) backbones, trained on a filtered LAION 5B subset and evaluated on 10K COCO 2014 prompts using CLIP, ImageReward, PickScore, HPSv2, and MPS metrics.

## Method

Flash-DMD introduces a two-phase training framework. In Phase 1, it decouples the distribution-matching (DM) loss and adversarial loss via a timestep-aware strategy: DM loss is applied exclusively at high-noise timesteps to align the student with the teacher's global structure, while a SAM-based Pixel-GAN discriminator operates at low-noise timesteps on decoded images to enforce photorealism and counteract mode-seeking. The score estimator is stabilized by removing its dual discriminator role and using EMA updates with a reduced TTUR of 1–2 (vs. 5 in DMD2), achieving SOTA quality at only 2.1% of DMD2's training cost. In Phase 2, a DPO-style preference optimization using a Latent Reward Model (LRM) to score noisy latents at high-noise timesteps is jointly trained with the ongoing distillation loss, which acts as a regularizer that prevents reward hacking and policy collapse.

## Results

On COCO-10k with 4-step SDXL distillation, Flash-DMD (TTUR2-8k) achieves ImageReward 0.9740, PickScore 0.2327, HPSv2 0.2981, and MPS 12.71, surpassing the 100-NFE SDXL teacher on all preference metrics while using only 8.3% of DMD2's training cost. In the most extreme setting (TTUR1-1k, 2.1% of DMD2's cost), Flash-DMD still yields ImageReward 0.9509 and MPS 12.67, exceeding DMD2's 0.8748 and 12.41. In Phase 2 (joint RL), Flash-DMD achieves the highest PickScore (0.2346) and MPS (12.84) among all RL-tuned 4-step models while requiring only 12 H20 GPU hours versus 400 A100 hours for Hyper-SDXL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Flash-DMD outperforms all 4-step distillation baselines (LCM-SDXL, SDXL-Lightning, SDXL-Turbo, SDXL-SenseFlow, NitroSD-Realism/Vibrant, DMD2) on ImageReward, PickScore, HPSv2, and MPS, while matching or slightly trailing on CLIP score (e.g., 0.3298 vs. DMD2's 0.3302 at TTUR2-8k). Against the 100-NFE SDXL teacher, Flash-DMD wins on every preference metric (e.g., MPS 12.71 vs. 11.87) with 25× fewer NFE. In Phase 2 RL comparisons, Flash-DMD achieves the highest PickScore (0.2346) and MPS (12.84), beating PSO-DMD2 (Pick 0.2338, MPS 12.53) and LPO-SDXL (Pick 0.2342, MPS 12.58), but trails Hyper-SDXL on ImageReward (1.0035 vs. 1.085) and HPSv2 (0.2930 vs. 0.3030), which the authors attribute to Hyper-SDXL's reward-hacking artifacts (overexposed, oil-painted images). On SD3-Medium (Flow Matching), Flash-DMD (TTUR2-4k) surpasses the 28-NFE teacher on ImageReward (1.0193 vs. 1.0173) and HPSv2 (0.2976 vs. 0.2933) with only 4 NFE, outperforming SD3-Flash and SD3-TDM on all preference metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

Flash-DMD is a direct refinement of the DMD2 framework, targeting two specific inefficiencies: the naive summation of distribution-matching and adversarial gradients at every timestep, and the dual role of the score estimator as both a distribution tracker and a discriminator. The paper decouples these objectives via a timestep-aware strategy (DM loss at high-noise steps, Pixel-GAN at low-noise steps), replaces the latent-space discriminator with a SAM-based pixel-space GAN, and reduces TTUR from 5 to 1–2. For the RL phase, it builds on the Latent Reward Model (LRM) from Zhang et al. [54] but departs from prior few-step RL work (PSO, HyperSD) by performing stochastic sampling only at high-noise timesteps and jointly optimizing the preference loss alongside the ongoing distillation objective rather than applying RL as a separate post-training stage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 2.1% training cost relative to DMD2 (1k steps vs. 24k) is a substantial practical win that could make high-quality few-step distillation accessible to labs without large GPU clusters, and the joint RL+distillation design offers a principled remedy for the reward-hacking artifacts (oil-painting, overexposure) that plague standalone RL fine-tuning of few-step models. Limitations include evaluation on only two model families (SDXL, SD3-Medium), reliance on a specific reward model (LRM) for the RL phase, and the fact that some metrics (HPSv2, CLIP) still trail the strongest RL baselines. Open questions include whether the timestep-aware decoupling generalizes to video diffusion or larger models, and whether the Pixel-GAN's SAM backbone is necessary or whether lighter encoders suffice.
