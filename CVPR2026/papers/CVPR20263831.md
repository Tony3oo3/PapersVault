## Problem

Existing video misinformation detection (VMD) datasets rely on editing techniques that disrupt cross-modal consistency, producing unrealistic and easily detectable artifacts. In contrast, AI-generated video misinformation from models like Sora maintains high semantic consistency across modalities, creating a fundamental gap between what current datasets capture and what real-world deception looks like. No dedicated dataset or detection method addresses this realistic, generative form of video misinformation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263831/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a claim-video pair as input: a textual claim (headline or description) paired with a video clip that includes an audio track (background music and/or speech). The output is a binary veracity label (real/fake) accompanied by a structured reasoning path in the form of a directed acyclic graph (evidence graph), where nodes represent multimodal evidence and fact-checking results and edges encode their logical dependencies. The RAVM dataset further provides three annotation dimensions per pair—veracity, polarity (harmful vs. unharmful intent), and attribution—enabling fine-grained analysis beyond simple binary classification.

## Method

The paper introduces two core contributions: the RAVM dataset and the IEEG detection model. RAVM is constructed via a multi-agent-driven generative framework that manipulates claims guided by intent polarity (harmful vs. unharmful), generates semantically aligned videos through an iterative Agentic Feedback Video Refinement Module (using quality, alignment, and adversarial evaluators with a dynamic reward algorithm), forges videos via an Adaptive Keyframe-to-Video Forgery Module (image-to-video), and produces semantically consistent audio via a Semantics-Driven Audio Module. For detection, IEEG models multimodal evidence, fact-checking results, and their dependencies as a directed acyclic graph (evidence graph), inspired by the Graph-of-Thought reasoning paradigm. A 7B-parameter student model is trained via Agents-of-Thoughts Distillation, where Gemini 2.5 serves as the teacher to extract evidence, perform web-based fact-checking, and organize coherent reasoning paths over the graph, with the student optimized to reproduce these reasoning paths given the claim-video input.

## Results

On the proposed RAVM benchmark, IEEG (7B) achieves 75.99% Accuracy and 73.44% Macro-F1, substantially outperforming all 25 evaluated LLMs/MLLMs. The strongest baselines—Gemini 2.5 (68.89% Acc / 68.00% Macro-F1) and Qwen3-VL-Thinking 30B (68.53% Acc / 67.77% Macro-F1)—trail IEEG by roughly 7% in Accuracy and 5–6 points in Macro-F1. Notably, scaling model size from 1B to 72B among open-source models yields only marginal gains (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL goes from 58.33% at 7B to 66.67% at 72B), and even the 671B DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp reaches only 64.56% Accuracy, underscoring the difficulty of realistic AI-generated video misinformation detection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IEEG outperforms all 25 baselines on both Accuracy and Macro-F1. Against the strongest closed-source model, Gemini 2.5, IEEG gains +7.10% Accuracy and +5.44% Macro-F1, with a particularly large advantage on Fake F1 (81.66 vs. 62.67), indicating superior detection of manipulated content. Among open-source models, the best performer is Qwen3-VL-Thinking 30B (68.53% Acc), still 7.46 points below IEEG; scaling Qwen2.5-VL from 7B to 72B improves Accuracy by only 8.34 points while Macro-F1 actually drops, revealing diminishing returns. The 671B DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp (64.56% Acc) underperforms several 7–8B MLLMs, suggesting that raw text-reasoning capacity without dedicated video understanding is insufficient. In cross-dataset robustness experiments (Table 3), fine-tuning VideoLLaMA2-7B on RAVM yields +13.48% Accuracy on FakeSV, +36.65% on FakeTT, and +15.37% on FMNV over zero-shot, far exceeding gains from fine-tuning on each dataset's own training set, confirming RAVM's transferability. Conversely, fine-tuning on existing datasets (FakeSV, FakeTT, FMNV) degrades performance on RAVM (Table 4), with Macro-F1 dropping by 7–16 points, demonstrating that editing-based manipulation patterns do not transfer to realistic AI-generated misinformation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the video misinformation detection (VMD) line of work by shifting the manipulation paradigm from editing-based artifacts (as in FakeSV, FakeTT, FMNV) to AI-generated content produced via text-to-video and image-to-video models. Methodologically, it builds on the Graph-of-Thought reasoning paradigm and Agents-of-Thoughts Distillation to construct a 7B evidence-graph model (IEEG), departing from prior MLLM-based VMD methods that rely on linear Chain-of-Thought reasoning. The multi-agent iterative video refinement framework draws on recent video generation models (SkyReels-V2, LTX-Video) and commercial systems (Sora, Kling), but introduces intent-polarity-guided claim manipulation and adversarial evaluation as novel components absent from prior dataset construction pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RAVM is the first large-scale benchmark specifically targeting realistic AI-generated video misinformation, filling a critical gap between editing-based VMD datasets and the threat landscape posed by models like Sora. The finding that even >500B-parameter MLLMs (Gemini 2.5) and 671B LLMs (DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp) achieve only ~68% accuracy, while a 7B distilled model reaches ~76%, suggests that task-specific evidence-graph reasoning may be more effective than raw scale for this problem. Open questions include whether IEEG generalizes to unseen manipulation types, whether the multi-agent generation framework scales to longer or higher-resolution videos, and whether the 76% ceiling reflects a fundamental difficulty or a dataset-specific limitation. The negative transferability result (fine-tuning on existing datasets degrades RAVM performance) also raises concerns about the practical utility of current VMD training data.
