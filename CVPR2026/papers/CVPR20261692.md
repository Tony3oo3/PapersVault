## Problem

Existing two-stage 3D texturing pipelines rely on UV-based inpainting to complete textures after multi-view reprojection, but UV mapping introduces topological ambiguity: a single mesh admits multiple valid UV layouts, and generative meshes (extracted via MarchingCubes) exhibit diverse topologies that fall outside the training distribution of UV inpainting models. This domain gap causes fragmented, inconsistent textures on real-world 3D assets produced by generative pipelines.

## Input / Output

The system takes a single reference image and a textureless 3D mesh as input. The mesh is rendered into six orthographic views of normals and canonical coordinate maps (CCMs) at 512×512 resolution, which serve as geometric conditioning for the diffusion models. The output is a complete, continuous 3D texture field (Texture Function) defined over the entire spatial volume around the mesh, from which surface colors are queried via an MLP, rather than a traditional UV texture map.

## Method

UniTEX is a two-stage pipeline. Stage 1 fine-tunes two Flux diffusion transformers via LoRA to generate six orthographic shaded views and then delight them into diffuse color, using a novel drop-training strategy that retains only a subset of input tokens per training step, reducing memory by 22.5% and speeding training by ~44.5% while preserving quality. Stage 2 introduces the Large Texturing Model (LTM), a transformer operating on a triplane (3×32×32) + cube (8×8×8) token representation that fuses encoded multi-view images, CCMs, and geometry (via Shape2VecSet-style cross-attention on sampled point clouds) to predict a continuous Texture Function. The key training innovation is Texture Function supervision: surface colors are extrapolated into a thin volumetric shell (truncated at 0.025) around the mesh, providing dense 3D supervision analogous to SDF/UDF supervision in geometry generation, rather than the sparse surface-only or rendered-image supervision used by prior methods. The final texture is synthesized by blending the predicted texture function with the partially textured geometry from reprojection.

## Results

On artist-created meshes, UniTEX achieves the best scores across all four metrics: CMMD 0.826 (vs. 0.909 for Hunyuan3D-Paint), FID_CLIP 16.03 (vs. 20.38), CLIP-Score 0.844, and LPIPS 0.090. On generative meshes, UniTEX attains a CLIP-Score of 0.807 (vs. 0.800 for Hunyuan3D-Paint) and a user-preference rate of 65.91%, nearly 3× the next-best method (21.36%). In the refinement-stage comparison (Table 2), UniTEX reaches PSNR_uv of 23.01 and PSNR of 30.45, outperforming TexGEN (20.47 / 25.19) and Paint3D-UVpaint (18.61 / 23.90) by substantial margins.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UniTEX outperforms all five baselines on every reported metric. Against the closest competitor, Hunyuan3D-Paint, UniTEX improves CMMD by 9.2% (0.826 vs. 0.909) and FID_CLIP by 21.2% (16.03 vs. 20.38) on artist-created meshes, and wins the user-preference study by a wide 65.91% vs. 21.36% margin on generative meshes. Paint3D and TexPainter, both UV-based inpainting methods, trail significantly on generative meshes (CLIP-Score 0.720 and 0.709 vs. 0.807), confirming the topological-ambiguity limitation the paper targets. TexGaussians, a single-stage native 3D model, performs worst on generative meshes (CLIP-Score 0.681), underscoring that 3D-native approaches alone lack sufficient data for standalone generation. In the refinement-stage ablation (Table 2), UniTEX's LTM surpasses TexGEN by 2.54 dB in PSNR_uv and 5.26 dB in overall PSNR, and beats Paint3D-UVpaint by 4.40 dB in PSNR_uv, demonstrating that 3D-space regression handles fragmented UV layouts far more robustly than UV-space inpainting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines: (1) 2D diffusion-based multi-view texture generation (Paint3D, Hunyuan3D, TexPainter) and (2) 3D-native texture representations (Texture Fields, TexGaussian, TexGEN). It departs from the dominant two-stage paradigm by replacing the UV-based inpainting second stage—which prior methods like Paint3D and TexGEN rely on—with a Large Texturing Model that regresses textures directly in a 3D functional space (triplane-cube + transformer). The Texture Function supervision signal is conceptually borrowed from SDF/UDF representations in geometry generation, repurposed here to provide dense volumetric supervision for a surface-defined quantity. The drop-training strategy for DiT fine-tuning draws on LongLoRA and MVDiffusion++ insights about partial-token training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and addresses a concrete practical bottleneck: UV-based inpainting fundamentally cannot generalize to meshes produced by generative pipelines (MarchingCubes outputs with fragmented topologies), which is increasingly the dominant source of 3D assets. The Texture Function as a training objective is a clean conceptual contribution—extending a surface signal into a volumetric field for denser supervision—that could transfer to other surface-defined attributes (normals, roughness, etc.). Open questions include whether the triplane-cube representation scales to very high-frequency textures, whether the approach extends naturally to full PBR material generation (beyond albedo), and whether the 6-view conditioning is sufficient for highly concave or self-occluding geometries. The drop-training strategy is a practical plug-and-play efficiency gain for anyone fine-tuning large DiTs on multi-condition tasks.
