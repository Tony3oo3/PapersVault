## Problem

State-of-the-art intrinsic image decomposition models depend heavily on paired synthetic datasets with ground-truth intrinsic labels, which are inherently limited in scope and diversity, causing poor generalization to in-the-wild imagery. The paper asks whether the relative spatial and semantic reasoning capabilities of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) can substitute for absolute ground-truth supervision, enabling fine-tuning of intrinsic predictors on unlabeled real images.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a single RGB image as input and produces multiple intrinsic maps (albedo, depth, surface normals, irradiance) via a base diffusion or flow-matching model. During the GRPO fine-tuning stage, the MLLM judge consumes point-pair-annotated RGB images (with colored visual markers overlaid at sampled locations) alongside modality-specific text questions (e.g., 'which point is closer?'), and the base model is updated using only the RGB image and the judge's comparative reward—no ground-truth intrinsic maps are required. The base model generates G=8 stochastic samples per image via injected Euler-Maruyama noise, and the reward is the fraction of N=40 point pairs where the judge's judgment agrees with the analytically derived relation from the predicted intrinsics.

## Method

ReasonX is a two-stage framework: first, an open-source MLLM (InternVL2.5-4B) is fine-tuned on synthetic RGB-intrinsic pairs to answer modality-specific relative questions about point pairs (depth ordering, normal front-facingness, irradiance ratio, albedo color similarity), then frozen as a perceptual judge. Second, the base intrinsic model (PRISM or Marigold) is fine-tuned on 10,000 unlabeled COCO images using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), where the judge's comparative judgments serve as the reward signal. Because intrinsic prediction is RGB-conditioned and largely deterministic, the authors inject exploration by converting the denoising trajectory into a lightly stochastic one via Euler-Maruyama updates, enabling multiple plausible predictions per image for group-based advantage estimation. The GRPO objective includes a KL regularizer against a frozen reference model to prevent reward hacking (e.g., collapsing to near-constant intrinsics), and the transition distribution is Gaussian, allowing closed-form KL computation. This design makes the framework model-agnostic and requires no paired intrinsic supervision during fine-tuning.

## Results

On the IIW albedo benchmark, PRISM-X reduces WHDR 10% from 17.2 to 12.9 (+25% improvement over base PRISM), matching CRefNet (12.8), while Marigold-X improves from 16.7 to 15.2 (+9%). On zero-shot depth estimation, PRISM-X achieves a 45.8% AbsRel reduction on ETH3D (0.142→0.077) and 13.1% on NYUv2 (0.061→0.053) over base PRISM, with δ1 reaching 0.950 on ETH3D. For surface normals, PRISM-X attains the best mean angular error of 15.7° on NYUv2 and 14.5° on DIODE, outperforming specialized estimators like DSINE (16.4°/19.9°) and StableNormal (17.8°/19.3°). Cross-modal depth-normal alignment improves by 32% RMSE reduction on both ETH3D and COCO test sets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PRISM-X consistently outperforms its base model PRISM across all evaluated modalities: 25% WHDR reduction on IIW albedo, 45.8% AbsRel reduction on ETH3D depth, and 2.5% mean angular error reduction on NYUv2 normals. Against specialized depth estimators, PRISM-X (0.053 AbsRel) beats Marigold Depth v1.0 (0.055) and DPT (0.098) on NYUv2, and surpasses DPT (0.078) and HDN (0.121) on ETH3D, though it trails Depth Anything V2 (0.045/0.131) and Marigold Depth v1.0 (0.065) on ETH3D. On surface normals, PRISM-X (15.7°/14.5°) outperforms DSINE (16.4°/19.9°), GeoWizard (19.0°/24.7°), StableNormal (17.8°/19.3°), and Lotus-G (16.9°/21.2°) on both NYUv2 and DIODE, despite being trained without ground-truth normal supervision. On IIW albedo, PRISM-X (12.9 WHDR 10%) is essentially tied with CRefNet (12.8), a non-competing method trained directly on IIW, while clearly surpassing Careaga & Aksoy (16.8), RGB↔X (23.6), and OmniGen2 (17.8). The MLLM baseline OmniGen2 is outperformed by both ReasonX variants on every modality and dataset tested, confirming that structured GRPO fine-tuning of a dedicated intrinsic model is more effective than general-purpose MLLM generation for this task.

## Relation to Prior Work

ReasonX adapts the GRPO optimization framework from DeepSeekMath [35] and the stochastic exploration mechanism from Flow-GRPO [25] to the fundamentally different setting of deterministic, RGB-conditioned intrinsic prediction, where exploration must be artificially injected via Euler-Maruyama noise. Conceptually, it extends the ordinal/relative reasoning principle from Ordinal Shading [5]—which applied scale-invariant ordering to shading alone—into a cross-modal MLLM judge that performs pairwise point-pair comparisons across depth, normals, albedo, and irradiance. Unlike prior MLLM-as-reward-model work targeting generative tasks, ReasonX uses the MLLM as a frozen perceptual judge whose relative judgments are matched against analytically derived relations from the model's own predicted intrinsics, creating a self-consistent reward without any ground-truth maps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ReasonX addresses the core generalization bottleneck in intrinsic decomposition—reliance on synthetic paired data—by enabling fine-tuning on unlabeled in-the-wild images, and the model-agnostic design (validated on both flow-matching PRISM and diffusion-based Marigold) suggests broad applicability. The particularly large ETH3D depth gain (46%) over the more modest NYUv2 gain (13%) confirms the method specifically targets OOD generalization rather than in-distribution refinement. Limitations include the reliance on point-pair sampling (N=40), modality-wise rather than joint multi-modal rewards, and the MLLM judge's imperfect accuracy (87–96%), which caps the reward signal quality. Open questions include whether joint reconstruction-based or multi-modal rewards could further improve cross-channel consistency, and whether the approach extends to broader inverse rendering tasks beyond single-image decomposition.
