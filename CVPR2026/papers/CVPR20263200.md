## Problem

Existing unsupervised turbulence restoration methods estimate distortion and blur parameters independently for each frame, ignoring the strong cross-frame correlations that arise because all frames of the same scene are degraded by the same underlying turbulence process. This framewise independence makes the inverse problem highly ill-posed and leads to physically implausible solutions. The paper asks whether explicitly modeling the shared turbulence structure across frames can improve restoration quality without requiring paired training data.

## Input / Output

The method takes 20 turbulence-degraded RGB frames of a static scene (256×256 per patch) and produces a single restored sharp image. For high-resolution inputs such as RLR-AT (1920×1080), the image is partitioned into overlapping 256×256 patches processed independently and then merged. The INR representing the latent image is initialized by fitting the temporal mean of the observed frames, and at inference the restored image is generated by evaluating the INR on a uniform sampling grid.

## Method

TMFS decomposes each frame's distortion and blur parameters into a scene-shared autocorrelation function and per-frame noise maps, inspired by random-process turbulence simulation. Distortion is modeled by a 2D anisotropic autocorrelation represented as a weighted mix of two 1D correlation functions (isotropic and anisotropic), sampled via the power spectral density with a low-amplitude filter to prevent content leakage. Blur is modeled through higher-order Zernike coefficients with both inter-mode covariance and spatial autocorrelation, and the resulting spatially varying PSF is applied efficiently via P2S with an added delta-function basis. The latent image is represented by a 4-layer MLP with position encoding (INR), and the entire system is optimized per scene with a composite loss combining L1 reconstruction, a Canny-edge-guided gradient sharpening term, and a lucky-region regularizer that encourages each pixel to be sharp in at least one frame.

## Results

On the synthetic weak-turbulence dataset, TMFS achieves the best overall performance with SSIM 0.7505 and PSNR 25.70 dB, surpassing all supervised and unsupervised baselines. Under strong turbulence, TMFS attains the highest SSIM (0.4331) among all methods, though PSNR remains limited at 19.67 dB. On the real-world Heat Chamber dataset, TMFS achieves SSIM 0.7080 and PSNR 20.17 dB, outperforming all unsupervised baselines but slightly trailing the best supervised method DATUM (SSIM 0.7298). On the real RLR-AT dataset (1920×1080), qualitative results show TMFS produces more effective distortion removal than supervised methods trained on synthetic data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among unsupervised methods, TMFS consistently outperforms CLEAR, CDSP, and NDIR across all four evaluation settings. On weak turbulence, TMFS leads CLEAR (SSIM 0.7505 vs. 0.7008) and NDIR (0.7505 vs. 0.5841) by substantial margins; on strong turbulence, the gap widens further (SSIM 0.4331 vs. 0.3608 for NDIR and 0.1886 for CLEAR). Against supervised methods, TMFS achieves the best overall result on weak turbulence (PSNR 25.70 dB vs. 24.21 for TMT) and the best SSIM on strong turbulence (0.4331 vs. 0.4064 for DATUM), but is slightly outperformed by DATUM on middle turbulence SSIM (0.5786 vs. 0.5874) and Heat Chamber SSIM (0.7080 vs. 0.7298). The ablation (Table 2) confirms that TMFS's distortion module alone (T+L0) already surpasses NDIR and CDSP, attributing the gain to the random-process-based distortion modeling rather than the INR grid parameterization. On the real water-turbulence tank dataset, TMFS recovers finer textures and sharper edges than both NERT and the classical Oreifej et al. method, demonstrating cross-domain generalization without retraining.

## Relation to Prior Work

TMFS builds directly on the tilt-then-blur degradation model of Chan [7] and the random-process framework for sampling correlated Zernike coefficients from Chimitt et al. [8], repurposing a simulation tool for restoration. The key departure from prior unsupervised methods (NDIR [20], NERT [18], CDSP [29]) is the frame-correlated parameterization: instead of estimating distortion and blur independently per frame, TMFS decomposes each frame's degradation into a scene-shared autocorrelation structure plus per-frame noise, explicitly modeling the physical correlation of turbulence across short time intervals. For efficient spatially varying blur, it adopts the P2S phase-to-space transform [24] with an added delta-function basis, and uses an INR for the latent image representation following the NDIR/NERT line of work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central insight—that cross-frame turbulence correlations can be exploited to reduce the ill-posedness of unsupervised restoration—is physically well-motivated and yields a method that generalizes to real atmospheric and water turbulence where supervised methods trained on synthetic data degrade. However, the per-scene optimization requirement limits practical deployment, and performance under severe turbulence remains unsatisfactory. Open questions include whether the per-scene fitting can be amortized across scenes, whether the random-process parameterization can be learned end-to-end, and whether the approach extends to dynamic (non-static) scenes where the shared-correlation assumption breaks down.
