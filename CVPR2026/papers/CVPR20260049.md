## Problem

Hyperspectral imaging faces harsh trade-offs between spatial, spectral, and temporal resolution under inherently low-photon conditions. Existing compressive spectral cameras either require complex optics (dispersive prisms, coded apertures, custom DOEs) that block or spread light, or rely on heavy computation and data-driven reconstruction that risks hallucinating spectral content. No prior method simultaneously achieves state-of-the-art spectral accuracy, high photon efficiency, sub-second reconstruction, and physical interpretability with a simple optical design.

## Input / Output

The system captures N=5 defocused grayscale images (1920×1200, central 800×800 crop used, 9.7° FOV) by translating a 50 mm lens to five discrete axial positions, forming a chromatic focal stack where each frame is sharp at one wavelength and blurred at others. The output is a full hyperspectral cube X ∈ R^(H×W×C) with 29 spectral bands spanning 440–720 nm in 10 nm steps, reconstructed in under 0.64 s on an NVIDIA RTX A6000. Unlike snapshot methods, SfD is a multi-shot system, but its total exposure budget is matched to competitors for fair comparison.

## Method

The optical design exploits the natural longitudinal chromatic aberration of two off-the-shelf 50 mm lenses: translating the second lens to N positions makes each wavelength in focus at a different position, producing a structured sensing matrix H of wavelength-dependent 2D convolutions without any light-blocking elements. The inverse problem is solved with a modified plug-and-play ADMM that alternates between a physics-based Wiener-like deconvolution step and an off-the-shelf deep denoiser (Hybrid Spectral Denoising Transformer). Two task-specific augmentations enable speed and robustness: (1) spectra are projected onto a low-dimensional eigenspace (v principal components from the Harvard dataset) to exploit the low-rank structure of natural visible spectra, and (2) the large matrix inversion in the z-update is accelerated by exploiting the Block Circulant with Circulant Blocks structure of the convolutional submatrices, enabling sub-second runtime. The combination of photon-efficient optics (nearly all incident light preserved) and a physics-grounded iterative solver with a lightweight learned regularizer avoids both the photon loss of filter/dispersive systems and the hallucination risk of purely data-driven reconstruction.

## Results

On the Harvard dataset (30 images, 5 s total exposure), SfD achieves the best PSNR of 30.81 dB and tied-best SSIM of 0.92, with a SAM of 7.35° (second only to KRISM's 6.91°). Reconstruction completes in 0.64 s on an NVIDIA RTX A6000 using only 4 optical components, compared to up to 20 components and >15 min compute for other SOTA systems. On real-world Macbeth ColorChecker data, the prototype achieves 29.54 dB PSNR and 7.42° SAM, closely matching simulation performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SfD outperforms all seven baselines on PSNR, with the closest competitor being MST [7] (30.62 dB vs. 30.81 dB) and KRISM [45] (29.40 dB). On SAM, KRISM achieves a slightly better 6.91° vs. SfD's 7.35°, but at the cost of 20 optical components and per-scene SVD that introduces spectral bias. Against the DOE-based 2in1 Cameras [47], SfD wins on every metric (PSNR +1.7 dB, SSIM +0.05, SAM −1.37°, compute time 0.64 s vs. 1.74 s) while using the same number of optical components. Compared to the dispersive-element baselines Choi et al. [12] and MST [7], SfD achieves substantially faster reconstruction (0.64 s vs. >15 min and 1.19 s respectively) with fewer optical components (4 vs. 9). Against the other chromatic-aberration method, Zhan et al. [55], SfD improves PSNR by 16.7 dB (30.81 vs. 14.11) and SAM by 16.6°, demonstrating that robust iterative reconstruction is critical even when photon efficiency is high. Spectral-filter-based methods (S. DiffuserCam, S. DefocusCam, Tunable Filter) are far behind on all metrics due to their inherently low photon efficiency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the plug-and-play ADMM framework [10] for iterative reconstruction, augmenting it with principal-component projection and a BCCB-based fast matrix inversion to handle the chromatic focal stack forward model. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant CASSI and DOE-based compressive spectral imaging paradigms by exploiting the naturally occurring longitudinal chromatic aberration of standard refractive lenses as the spectral encoding mechanism, rather than custom dispersive or diffractive optics. It is closest in spirit to Spectral DefocusCam [17] (defocus-based encoding) and Zhan et al. [55] (chromatic aberration), but differs by using a grayscale sensor with spatially varying blur for spectral separation rather than binned super-pixels for spatial super-resolution, and by replacing naive inverse filtering or end-to-end deep networks with a physics-based iterative solver augmented by an off-the-shelf denoiser.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: two off-the-shelf lenses and a grayscale sensor (4 optical components) match or exceed systems requiring 9–20 components, with sub-second reconstruction and a physically interpretable model that avoids the spectral hallucination risk of purely data-driven approaches. Key limitations include a narrow 34 cm working range, a paraxial field of view, the need for 5 sequential exposures even for RGB output, and reduced accuracy at longer wavelengths where chromatic focal shift flattens. Open questions include extending the working volume through joint optical-algorithmic co-design, handling dynamic scenes via burst-photography techniques, and developing higher-fidelity noise models to fully exploit the method's photon efficiency advantage in low-light conditions.
