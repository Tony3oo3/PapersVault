## Problem

Reference-based sketch colorization suffers from a fundamental distribution shift: training uses semantically-aligned triplets (sketch and reference derived from the same ground truth), while inference encounters arbitrarily paired sketch-reference combinations. This mismatch causes the model to erroneously transfer spatial semantics from the reference into the output—a failure mode the authors term spatial entanglement—producing structural contradictions, color bleeding, and distorted geometry. Prior methods mitigated resulting artifacts (e.g., split cross-attention) but did not fundamentally close the training-inference distribution gap.

## Input / Output

The method takes a line-art sketch (extracted via four complementary edge/line extractors) and a color/style reference image as inputs, and produces a 1024×1024 colorized illustration. The reference image is consumed at two levels: high-level semantic attributes are extracted by a WD-Tagger and an OpenCLIP image encoder to condition the diffusion backbone, while low-level visual features are injected through a plugin adapter that runs only at timestep t=0 during inference. The sketch provides the sole structural/segmentation signal, and the output is expected to preserve sketch geometry while adopting the reference's color palette, texture, and global style.

## Method

The core contribution is a Dual-Branch Feature Alignment (DBFA) architecture with two weight-sharing branches: a semantic-aligned branch (sketch + ground-truth-derived reference, modeling training) and a semantic-misaligned branch (sketch + randomly sampled reference, modeling inference). A Gram Regularization Loss is computed on the final transformer-block feature maps of both branches, using a stop-gradient 'self-anchoring' mechanism that forces the misaligned branch's spatial correlations to match the aligned branch's, thereby enforcing P(X_spatial | I_r, I_s) = P(X_spatial | I_s). The diffusion backbone is SDXL (initialized from AnimagineXL), with the CLIP-L text encoder replaced by an anime-specific WD-Tagger (Swin Transformer v2) for fine-grained attribute control and the OpenCLIP-bigG text encoder replaced by its image encoder for cross-style visual embeddings. A separate plugin module, trained in a second 10K-step stage, injects low-level features for background texture and global style consistency. The Gram loss is activated after the first 33% of training steps (λ ramped 0→1) to avoid penalizing early, naturally low entanglement.

## Results

On the 50K-triplet validation set at 1024² resolution, the proposed method achieves a FID of 8.28, substantially outperforming the next-best baseline (Yan et al. at 12.09) and all other methods. It also leads in MS-SSIM (0.70) and CLIP score (0.912), while MangaNinja achieves a slightly higher PSNR (29.64 vs. 28.83) due to its limited generation range producing close-to-average outputs. A user study with 30 participants confirms the method is preferred over all six baselines with statistical significance (p < 0.01, Chi-Squared test).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms Yan et al. [44] (the closest competitor) by 3.81 FID points (8.28 vs. 12.09), 0.09 MS-SSIM, and 0.016 CLIP score, while also leading in PSNR (28.83 vs. 28.44). ColorizeDiff [45] trails on every metric, with a FID of 13.42 and MS-SSIM of only 0.57. Adapter-based methods (IP-Adapter, IP-Adapter-XL, T2I-Adapter) show severe degradation at 1024² resolution, with FID scores of 36–95, confirming their inadequate generation ability for high-resolution colorization. MangaNinja [21] is the only method that beats the proposed approach on PSNR (29.64 vs. 28.83), but this is attributed to its limited 512² resolution and inability to synthesize complex backgrounds, making its outputs closer to the mean and thus favorable for PSNR; it still loses on FID (42.85), MS-SSIM (0.67), and CLIP score (0.892). Cobra [54] is compared qualitatively and shows significant deterioration when the sketch style changes, requiring the authors to generate additional results using sketches extracted from their own outputs for a fair comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reference-based sketch colorization line of work (ColorizeDiffusion, MangaNinja, Cobra, IP-Adapter) by reframing the core challenge: rather than mitigating artifacts caused by the train/inference distribution shift as prior methods do, it explicitly models that shift with a Dual-Branch Feature Alignment architecture and a Gram Regularization Loss. The diffusion backbone builds on SDXL/AnimagineXL, and the anime-specific WD-Tagger (Swin Transformer v2) replaces the generic CLIP-L encoder for finer-grained attribute control, while a plugin module handles low-level texture transfer. Conceptually, the Gram loss draws on the DINOv3 observation that Gram matrices capture spatial semantic correlations, repurposing this for cross-branch feature alignment in a self-anchoring manner that avoids external networks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a root-cause problem (distribution shift) that prior reference-based colorization methods only treated symptomatically, and the dual-branch + Gram loss formulation is potentially transferable to other image-to-image tasks suffering from train/test distribution mismatch. However, the method is tightly coupled to anime-style content (WD-Tagger, AnimagineXL initialization, 6M anime training images), limiting immediate generalizability to broader illustration or photographic domains. Open questions include whether the Gram regularization approach scales to non-anime domains, whether the 30% training slowdown and 10% extra memory cost are acceptable at larger scales, and whether the plugin module's single-timestep execution at t=0 generalizes to more complex multi-region style transfers.
