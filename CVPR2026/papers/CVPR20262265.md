## Problem

Existing LoRA-based federated fine-tuning methods for Vision Transformers rely on a flat dual-adapter design (one global, one local) that ignores latent client group structures in real-world non-IID deployments. This causes gradient drift in the global adapter, overfitting in local adapters, and under-exploitation of subgroup-shared knowledge, leaving the personalization–generalization trade-off coarse-grained and performance gains unrealized.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images (32×32 CIFAR-100, 224×224 DomainNet) through a frozen ViT-Base backbone pretrained on ImageNet-21K, with LoRA adapters inserted into the query and value projections of each attention layer. It outputs a class label (100-way for CIFAR-100, 10-way for the DomainNet subset). For unseen clients at test time, a lightweight probe basis is extracted via a few local gradient steps, the client is routed to its nearest cluster by subspace similarity, and the leaf adapter is instantiated and refined over 5 local epochs before final evaluation.

## Method

HiLoRA decomposes each client's weight update into three orthogonally constrained low-rank components—Root (global), Cluster (subgroup-shared), and Leaf (client-specific)—enforcing pairwise orthogonality on the column spaces of the B matrices to disentangle knowledge at each tier. A LoRA-Subspace Adaptive Clustering mechanism discovers latent client groups by computing principal-angle distances between EMA-stabilized LoRA basis subspaces, converting them to a Gaussian-kernel affinity matrix, and applying spectral clustering with eigengap-based K selection. Training proceeds in a cascaded fashion: the Root adapter is trained globally with product-space aggregation and truncated SVD, then frozen; Cluster adapters are trained per-cluster with an orthogonality penalty against the frozen Root; finally, Leaf adapters are trained per-client with orthogonality penalties against both Root and Cluster. A tier-wise generalization bound (Theorem 1) shows that orthogonality narrows the restricted loss classes (tightening Rademacher complexity terms) while clustering reduces distributional discrepancy terms, jointly yielding a tighter client-level excess-risk upper bound.

## Results

On CIFAR-100, HiLoRA achieves the best mean and worst-case (10th-percentile) personalization accuracy across all three non-IID partitions: 84.6%/76.3% (GL-Dir 0.3), 93.4%/79.1% (SC-Dir 3), and 94.1%/82.3% (Patho-10). For unseen-client generalization, HiLoRA reaches 84.1%, 85.9%, and 94.0% test accuracy on the three partitions respectively. On DomainNet, HiLoRA attains 87.7% mean and 58.9% worst-case client accuracy, and 86.1% unseen-client accuracy, outperforming the next-best baselines (FedDPA-F at 86.0% mean, FlexLoRA at 83.9% mean) by 1.5–2.0% in mean accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HiLoRA outperforms all seven baselines on every reported metric across both CIFAR-100 and DomainNet. The closest competitor on CIFAR-100 personalization is FedDPA-T [37], which reaches 89.5% mean under SC-Dir(3) versus HiLoRA's 93.4% (a 3.9-point gap), while on Patho(10) the gap narrows to 1.3 points (92.8% vs 94.1%). On DomainNet, FedDPA-F [37] is the nearest baseline at 86.0% mean accuracy versus HiLoRA's 87.7%. For unseen-client generalization, HiLoRA's advantage is most pronounced: it leads FedALT [2] by 10.3 points on SC-Dir(3) (85.9% vs 75.6%) and by 8.0 points on Patho(10) (94.0% vs 86.0%), indicating that the hierarchical cluster-adapter reuse is particularly effective for new clients. Single-branch methods (FedIT [78], FlexLoRA [1]) and dual-adapter methods (FDLoRA [50], PF2LoRA [23]) consistently trail HiLoRA by 3–15 points in mean accuracy, confirming that the three-tier decomposition with subspace-based clustering captures both global and subgroup structure that flat or two-level designs miss.

## Relation to Prior Work

HiLoRA extends the dual-adapter paradigm for federated LoRA (e.g., FedDPA, FedALT) from a flat two-branch design (global + local) to a three-tier hierarchical structure (root + cluster + leaf), introducing a middle 'cluster' tier that captures subgroup-shared knowledge. The clustering mechanism departs from prior data-distribution-based or parameter-cosine-similarity approaches by operating on the principal-angle geometry of LoRA basis subspaces, which is reparameterization-invariant and privacy-preserving. Cross-tier orthogonality constraints on the B-matrices are a key architectural departure from prior dual-LoRA methods, which allow overlapping adaptation directions and suffer from cross-tier interference. The theoretical excess-risk bound formalizes how orthogonality and clustering jointly tighten generalization, a guarantee not provided by prior dual-adapter work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The hierarchical three-tier design offers a principled middle ground between fully global and fully local LoRA in federated settings, and the adaptive subspace clustering enables knowledge sharing among structurally aligned clients without requiring pre-defined groups or raw data exchange. The theoretical analysis (Theorem 1) provides the first tier-wise generalization bound for hierarchical LoRA in FL, giving practitioners a formal understanding of when and why the decomposition helps. Open questions include whether the fixed rank r across all tiers is optimal (the authors note different ranks per tier as future work), how the method scales to hundreds or thousands of clients, and whether the spectral clustering step remains stable under highly dynamic client populations. The extension to LoRA-MoE architectures is also flagged as a natural next step.
