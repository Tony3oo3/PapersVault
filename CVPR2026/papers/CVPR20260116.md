## Problem

Existing spatiotemporal lighting estimation methods fail to simultaneously provide accurate spatial grounding at arbitrary 3D positions, full HDR luminance accuracy, realistic high-frequency reflections, and temporal consistency. In particular, recent diffusion-based approaches like 4D Lighting produce overly smooth results that do not properly capture lighting dynamics or correctly ground predictions in the local scene context, leaving a gap for a unified method that addresses all five key capabilities (spatial grounding, temporal adaptation, HDR accuracy, near/far field sources, and high-frequency detail).

## Input / Output

The method takes a monocular RGB image or video sequence (512×512) plus a target 3D scene position and a text prompt encoding the exposure value (discrete EVs: 0, −3, −6, −9, −12) and sphere type (mirror or diffuse). Per-pixel depth is recovered via an off-the-shelf estimator (Video Depth Anything) and camera FoV via MoGe-2. At inference, the diffusion model is queried multiple times across all exposure/sphere-type combinations, and the resulting multi-bracket sphere images are fused through differentiable rendering into a single equirectangular HDRI map per 3D position, or a spatiotemporal HDRI volume for video.

## Method

LIMO fine-tunes Flux.1 Schnell (image) and Wan2.2 5B (video) diffusion models to inpaint a mirror or diffuse sphere at a specified 3D position, conditioned on the RGB image (sphere region masked to black), depth, sphere normal map, and two novel geometric maps (per-pixel direction and distance from scene surfaces to the sphere center) that provide spatial grounding beyond depth alone. The key insight is that depth conditioning is insufficient for correct spatial placement—these geometric maps let the network reason about occlusions and relative position. Training uses a large-scale synthetic Blender dataset with procedurally placed sphere grids across indoor/outdoor scenes and three animation scenarios (moving sphere, moving camera, dynamic lighting). At test time, a spatio-temporal HDRI volume is optimized via gradient descent with a PyTorch differentiable renderer (cosine + light multi-importance sampling), a Laplacian pyramid parameterization for fast convergence, and a temporal L1 consistency loss between neighboring frames.

## Results

On the Infinigen synthetic dataset, LIMO (image) achieves RMSE of 0.25 (mirror) and 0.16 (diffuse), substantially outperforming DiffusionLight (0.40/0.47) and 4D Lighting (0.34/0.36). On the real Laval Indoor SV dataset, LIMO (image) reaches RMSE 0.30/0.20 and SSIM 0.81/0.97, again leading both baselines. On the Laval Outdoor dataset, LIMO (image) achieves the lowest RMSE (0.27 mirror, 0.12 diffuse) and highest SSIM (0.79/0.99) across all methods. The video model trails the image model slightly on per-frame metrics (attributed to 5B vs 12B parameter capacity) but surpasses it on temporal consistency metrics (T-LPIPS-Diff, Warped Error).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LIMO (image) outperforms both DiffusionLight and 4D Lighting on every metric across all three single-image datasets. Against DiffusionLight, the advantage is most pronounced on the Infinigen dataset where RMSE drops from 0.40 to 0.25 (mirror) and 0.47 to 0.16 (diffuse), reflecting DiffusionLight's inability to ground predictions at specific 3D positions. Against 4D Lighting, LIMO (image) achieves roughly 25–30% lower RMSE and 5–10% higher SSIM on real indoor scenes (Laval Indoor SV). On the video/dynamic scene evaluation (Table 3), LIMO (video) matches or beats 4D Lighting on per-frame RMSE and SSIM, while decisively winning on temporal metrics: T-LPIPS-Diff for mirror spheres drops from 0.0418 (4D Lighting) to 0.0227 (LIMO video) on dynamic objects, indicating 4D Lighting's MLP-based implicit representation over-smooths lighting changes. The authors attribute 4D Lighting's lower warped L2 error in some cases to excessive smoothing rather than true accuracy, and note that LIMO better captures abrupt lighting discontinuities (e.g., entering/exiting shadow) that 4D Lighting fails to reproduce.

## Relation to Prior Work

LIMO extends the diffusion-based lighting estimation line initiated by DiffusionLight (which inpaints a single chrome ball) and 4D Lighting (which generates multiple spheres and fuses them via NeRF-like optimization) by fine-tuning a powerful pre-trained image/video diffusion model (Flux.1 Schnell / Wan2.2) to inpaint spheres at arbitrary 3D positions. It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) it introduces novel geometric conditioning maps (direction and distance from scene pixels to the sphere center) that go beyond the depth-only conditioning used by 4D Lighting, and (2) it jointly predicts both mirror and diffuse spheres across multiple exposure brackets, then merges them via differentiable rendering into a single HDRI, rather than relying on mirror spheres alone. The exposure-conditioning strategy is borrowed from GaSLight, and the overall IBL pipeline (mirror + diffuse probes) follows the classical Debevec framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LIMO is the first method to simultaneously address spatial grounding at arbitrary 3D positions, temporal consistency, full HDR accuracy, near-field and far-field sources, and high-frequency detail—capabilities that prior methods each addressed only partially. The practical impact for virtual production (compositing actors from light domes into real sets) is significant, as demonstrated in the paper's application examples. Key limitations include the non-zero sphere size violating the directional-lighting assumption (making HDRI optimization ill-posed for shadows on the sphere) and the failure to exploit lighting cues from human faces. Open questions include whether a truly point-based lighting representation could resolve the ill-posedness, and whether the geometric conditioning insight generalizes to other spatially-varying prediction tasks.
