## Problem

VLA models for robotic manipulation inevitably fail under out-of-distribution conditions but lack the ability to diagnose or recover from those failures. Existing failure datasets are predominantly generated via programmatic perturbation in simulation, suffering from a sim-to-real gap, while real-world failure data is difficult to annotate at scale. Moreover, prior correction methods rely almost exclusively on textual instructions, which current VLA models follow unreliably.

## Input / Output

ViFailback-8B consumes ego-centric video frames (head camera) plus a VQA text prompt and produces a structured multi-level output: failure detection, keyframe/subtask localization, failure type classification (4 categories), root-cause reasoning, low-level movement/rotation directions, high-level strategic advice, and drawing codes for 7 visual symbols (colored straight arrows, semi-circular arrows, dual crosshairs, crosshair, ON/OFF labels, prohibition icon, rewind icon). In the real-world deployment, these visual symbols are rendered onto the robot's camera view and fed alongside a textual correction prompt into the π0.5 VLA policy, which also receives wrist-camera observations that ViFailback-8B does not see.

## Method

The core contribution is a visual-symbol-based annotation framework that decomposes failure analysis into diagnosis (detection, localization, type, reason) and correction (low-level/high-level text + visual symbols), enabling efficient human annotation via mouse-drawn symbols on video frames rather than free-text labeling. A three-stage pipeline combines UI-based semantic filling, symbol drawing with predefined action categories, and VLM-assisted (Qwen3-VL-235B) open-ended description generation with manual verification, yielding 58,128 VQA pairs from 5,202 real-world ALOHA trajectories. ViFailback-8B is obtained by LoRA finetuning Qwen3-VL-8B for one epoch on this dataset, and it is deployed as an external supervisor that monitors the robot's video stream at a fixed interval, generates CoT-based diagnosis and visual symbol codes upon detecting a failure, and overlays those symbols on the camera feed to guide π0.5 recovery. Two integration strategies are evaluated: VSF (finetuning π0.5 on a visual-symbols-following dataset so it can end-to-end interpret the symbols) and PMC (a low-level motion controller that translates symbols into end-effector trajectories, using GraspNet for grasp pose estimation when needed).

## Results

On ViFailback-Bench Lite (closed-ended), ViFailback-8B achieves 93.70% average accuracy, surpassing the best baseline Gemini-2.5-Pro (54.64%) by 39.14 percentage points. On ViFailback-Bench Hard (open-ended), ViFailback-8B reaches 72.64% average, outperforming the best baseline GPT-4o (40.00%) by 32.64 points and its own zero-shot Qwen3-VL-8B counterpart (33.04%) by 39.6 points. In real-world ALOHA experiments, integrating ViFailback-8B as an external supervisor improves VLA task success rates by 20.6–23.8% over unassisted baselines across three manipulation tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ViFailback-8B decisively outperforms all 16 baselines on both benchmark splits. On Lite, it beats the strongest proprietary model Gemini-2.5-Pro (54.64%) by 39.14 points and the best open-source model Qwen2.5-VL-72B (50.61%) by 43.09 points; on Hard, it surpasses GPT-4o (40.00%) by 32.64 points. Among open-source VLMs, Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct is the strongest zero-shot baseline (47.79% Lite, 35.23% Hard), yet ViFailback-8B—despite being only 8B parameters—exceeds it by 45.91 and 37.41 points respectively. Embodied-specialized models (RoboBrain2.0-32B at 49.92% Lite, Cosmos-Reason1-7B at 38.06% Lite) trail general VLMs and are far below ViFailback-8B. The zero-shot Qwen3-VL-8B baseline (38.33% Lite, 33.04% Hard) shows that the 39.6-point Hard gain comes almost entirely from supervised finetuning on the ViFailback dataset rather than from base-model scale. In real-world deployment, the VSF method (73.0% avg success) and PMC method (74.6% avg success) both substantially outperform the unassisted π₀.₅ baselines (50.8–52.4%), confirming that visual-symbol-based correction transfers to physical recovery.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of VLM-based failure detection and recovery work (AHA, RACER, FailSafe, RoboFAC) by replacing simulation-generated failure data with real-world teleoperation and policy-rollout trajectories, and by introducing visual symbols as a correction modality alongside text. It also builds on the visual-prompting tradition in robot learning (RoVI, CrayonRobo, MOKA, RT-Sketch), but departs from those works by using symbols for real-time corrective intervention rather than initial task guidance. The annotation framework repurposes the VLM itself as a labeling assistant, drawing on the Qwen3-VL family for both the base model and the annotation pipeline, while the downstream integration with π_0.5 follows the external-supervisor pattern established by YAY and similar systems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 58K VQA pairs from 5,202 real-world trajectories constitute the first large-scale, real-world failure dataset for robotic manipulation, addressing a critical gap left by simulation-only approaches. The visual-symbol mechanism offers a more direct and interpretable corrective signal than pure text, which is practically important given the known fragility of VLA instruction-following. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond the ALOHA platform and 100-task scope, whether visual symbols can be learned end-to-end rather than requiring a separate finetuning or motion-controller pipeline, and how the rich action distributions in failure trajectories can be exploited for policy learning beyond the current external-supervisor setup.
