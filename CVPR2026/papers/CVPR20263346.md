## Problem

Deep detectors rely on aggressive downsampling to harvest high-level semantics, but successive strided convolutions and pooling act as low-pass filters that irreversibly destroy the weak thermal signatures of infrared tiny targets. Existing mitigations (skip connections, attention, tolerance-adjustable losses) compensate for information loss after the fact without resolving the fundamental non-injective nature of downsampling, leaving a critical performance bottleneck for scale-challenged infrared detection.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single-channel infrared image (H×W×1) and produces target detection boxes with center/size predictions via an anchor-free head. During training, the model additionally generates a reconstructed proxy of the input image from forward-pass feature latents through an inverse path, but this reconstruction is purely a training-time regularizer and is disabled at inference, so the deployed model outputs only detection results with no extra latency.

## Method

InvDet replaces the standard downsampling backbone with a hybrid invertible encoder: the first S_rev stages use Haar wavelet decomposition combined with bijective coupling InvBlocks (dense sub-networks φ, η, ρ) to guarantee exact invertibility, while deeper stages use conventional convolutions for semantic abstraction. An inverse path reconstructs the input from feature latents, making rescale-induced information loss directly observable and optimizable. A Target-Aware Reconstruction Modulation (TARM) module operates exclusively in the inverse path, gating high-frequency latents and applying a mild low-pass gain weighted by a GCTM-derived pixel-wise map, so reconstruction fidelity is strict on targets and relaxed on background without perturbing forward detection features. Training employs gradient-decoupled optimization with separate optimizers for the detection branch (neck + heads) and the reconstruction branch (invertible encoder), ensuring clean gradient flow. The GCTM metric fuses TAM-style geometric consistency with a radiometry-aware Bhattacharyya content term, producing scale-adaptive tolerance for tiny targets.

## Results

InvDet achieves F1 scores of 84.4% on IRSTD-1K (+4.1 over the second-best MA-Net at 80.3%), 87.4% on NUAA-SIRST (+3.5 over DNA-Net at 83.9%), and 86.2% on NUDT-SIRST (+1.5 over OSCAR at 80.9%). On the DUAB dataset, it attains the best F1 on spot targets (93.5%, +2.1) and extended targets (98.2%, +1.3). On IRSTD and DUAB-Point, MA-Net edges ahead (98.3 vs. 97.8 and 98.2 vs. 93.5), which the authors attribute to MA-Net's stronger in-domain fitting on larger datasets. Cross-dataset evaluation shows a mean F1 retention of 84.9%, with real-to-real transfer reaching 88–89%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

InvDet outperforms all eight earlier baselines (MDFAcGAN, ACM, ObjectBox, YOLO-FR, UIU-Net, DNA-Net, RDIAN, OSCAR) on F1 across every dataset, with the largest margins on IRSTD-1K (+4.1 over MA-Net's 80.3, +5.5 over DNA-Net's 78.9) and NUAA-SIRST (+3.5 over DNA-Net's 83.9). The closest competitor is MA-Net [30], which surpasses InvDet on IRSTD (98.3 vs. 97.8) and DUAB-Point (98.2 vs. 93.5), likely benefiting from the larger training set sizes of those benchmarks. On NUDT-SIRST, InvDet leads MA-Net by 1.5 points (86.2 vs. 84.7) and OSCAR by 5.3 points (86.2 vs. 80.9). The cross-dataset generalization study further distinguishes InvDet: it maintains 84.9% mean F1 retention when transferred across datasets without fine-tuning, suggesting the invertible encoder learns more transferable representations than the competing architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the invertible rescaling framework (IRN) from image super-resolution to object detection, repurposing Haar decomposition and bijective coupling blocks as the encoder backbone so that downsampling-induced information loss becomes an optimizable reconstruction error rather than an irrecoverable degradation. It departs from prior ISTD methods (DNA-Net, OSCAR, MA-Net) that compensate for information loss post-hoc via skip connections, attention, or loss redesign, by making the encoder itself invertible and constraining feature extraction through a reconstruction objective. The GCTM metric extends TAM's geometric tolerance with a radiometry-aware content term (Bhattacharyya coefficient normalized by local SNR and background entropy), and the TARM module introduces a gradient-decoupled, target-gated modulation that operates exclusively in the inverse path—unlike joint low-level/high-level frameworks (IA-YOLO, UniCD) that fuse a separate enhancement stream with the detection backbone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled mechanism to make rescale-induced information loss directly observable and optimizable, which is a conceptual advance over the field's prevailing strategy of architectural compensation. The strong cross-dataset generalization (84.9% mean F1 retention, with 88–89% retention between real-world datasets at 2× resolution difference) suggests the invertible constraint yields more transferable representations than purely detection-optimized backbones. However, MA-Net still outperforms InvDet on the two largest benchmarks (IRSTD, DUAB-Point), indicating the reconstruction-guided approach may trade some in-domain fitting capacity for generalization—a trade-off whose boundary conditions remain unclear. Open questions include whether the invertible constraint is truly necessary versus acting as a strong regularizer, how the method scales to sub-pixel targets or video sequences, and whether the TARM/GCTM design transfers to other low-signal detection modalities.
