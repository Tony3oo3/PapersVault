## Problem

Existing building wireframe reconstruction methods—whether vertex/edge detection or diffusion-based generation—struggle with geometric accuracy and topological correctness, particularly on noisy or incomplete point clouds, and rely on post-processing steps that prevent fully end-to-end training. The potential of combining autoregressive sequence modeling with reinforcement-learning-based post-training for holistic building wireframe reconstruction remains largely unexplored.

## Input / Output

The model takes a raw point cloud (4096 points) as input and produces a complete building wireframe as output, structured as an ordered sequence of edges spanning footprint, wall, and roof components. Vertex coordinates are normalized to a unit cube and 8-bit quantized (256 levels per axis), so each edge is represented by six discrete tokens. Generation terminates only at a token count that is a multiple of six, guaranteeing structurally complete edges before detokenization recovers continuous coordinates.

## Method

BuildingGPT reformulates wireframe reconstruction as autoregressive next-token prediction over a hierarchically organized token sequence: components are ordered footprint→wall→roof, edges within each component are sorted by their lowest vertex index, and vertices follow ascending z-y-x order. A Point Transformer–inspired encoder compresses the input point cloud into a fixed-length latent code (2048 tokens) that is prepended to the sequence, conditioning an OPT-based decoder-only Transformer (24 layers, 16 heads, 1536 hidden dim) on global geometric context. Training is two-stage: first, standard cross-entropy pre-training on the MunichWF dataset (267K samples); second, DPO post-training where preference pairs are constructed using a Preference Score Function (PSF = (Corner F1 + Edge F1) / Wireframe Edit Distance), with an auxiliary NLL loss on positive sequences to stabilize optimization. This combination of structurally-aware tokenization, autoregressive generation, and preference-aligned fine-tuning yields state-of-the-art results with demonstrated scaling behavior analogous to large language models.

## Results

On the MunichWF test set (5K samples), BuildingGPT achieves a Wireframe Edit Distance (WED) of 0.98×10⁻² and Average Corner Offset (ACO) of 0.88×10⁻², representing 29.5% and 33.3% reductions over the previous state-of-the-art EdgeDiff (1.39 and 1.32, respectively). For geometric accuracy, BuildingGPT attains Corner F1 of 97.4% and Edge F1 of 94.4%, improving by 3.5% and 2.8% over EdgeDiff. The model also demonstrates strong robustness, maintaining CF1 > 96% under 50% point removal and noise scale 0.02, and generalizes to the unseen AHN3 dataset without fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BuildingGPT outperforms all five baselines across every metric. Against the closest competitor EdgeDiff (diffusion-based, CVPR 25), BuildingGPT reduces WED by 29.5% (0.98 vs. 1.39) and ACO by 33.3% (0.88 vs. 1.32), while improving CF1 by 3.5 points (97.4 vs. 93.9) and EF1 by 2.8 points (94.4 vs. 91.6). PBWR (edge detection, CVPR 24) trails by 0.56 in WED and 5.5 points in EF1. BWFormer (vertex detection with Transformer, CVPR 25) is notably weaker, with WED 3.6× higher and EF1 6.5 points lower than BuildingGPT, largely due to its difficulty capturing edge connectivity for fine-grained structures. Point2Roof (vertex detection, JPRS 22) and PC2WF (vertex detection, ICLR 21) are substantially outperformed, with Point2Roof achieving only 43.9% EF1 and PC2WF a mere 0.8% EF1, reflecting the limitations of two-stage vertex-then-edge pipelines on incomplete point clouds.

## Relation to Prior Work

BuildingGPT departs from the dominant detection-based paradigm (vertex detection in PC2WF, Point2Roof, BWFormer; edge detection in PBWR) and the diffusion-based generation approach (EdgeDiff) by reformulating building wireframe reconstruction as an autoregressive sequence prediction problem. It borrows the edge-sorting tokenization mechanism from PolyGen and MeshGPT for 3D mesh generation, but introduces a hierarchical footprint-to-roof ordering that exploits building-specific structural and semantic regularities. The DPO post-training stage is directly adapted from LLM alignment (Rafailov et al., 2023) and recent mesh-generation work (DeepMesh, MeshRFT), but is here applied to a structured geometric reconstruction task with a custom Preference Score Function combining topological and geometric metrics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The introduction of MunichWF (267K samples) addresses a critical data bottleneck for holistic building wireframe reconstruction, moving beyond roof-only datasets and enabling meaningful scaling studies. The demonstration that autoregressive models exhibit LLM-like scaling behavior in this domain suggests substantial headroom for future building foundation models. However, the approach still struggles with complex local structures (windows, doors, stairs), and the DPO preference pairs are generated by the model itself rather than human annotation, raising questions about whether the alignment truly captures human geometric intuition. Open questions include whether finer-grained tokenization and more advanced RL methods can close the remaining gap on intricate architectural details.
