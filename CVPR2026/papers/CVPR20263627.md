## Problem

Existing negative-label-based OOD detection methods (e.g., NegLabel) mine negative labels solely by their cosine distance from ID labels, ignoring how those labels actually respond to OOD samples. Many mined labels exhibit poor activation on OOD test sets—sometimes even lower activation on OOD than on ID—introducing noise and degrading detection. No prior method dynamically selects negative labels based on their measured activation against the evolving test distribution.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB test images (encoded by a frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 image encoder), a set of ID class labels (e.g., ImageNet-1k's 1000 classes), and an external corpus of text labels (e.g., WordNet) from which negative labels are mined. It outputs a scalar OOD detection score S_aa(v) per image, compared against a threshold γ to assign ID or OOD. The method operates in a streaming fashion: it maintains FIFO queues (capacity L=300) of high-confidence positive and negative image features that are updated online as each batch arrives, requiring no training data or gradient updates.

## Method

TANL introduces an activation metric that quantifies how 'active' a corpus label is across a dataset by averaging its softmax probability over the associated images. During testing, it approximates this activation using cached high-confidence positive and negative samples in FIFO queues, then selects the top-M corpus labels with the highest differential activation (high on OOD, low on ID), yielding distribution-adaptive negative labels. A batch-adaptive variant blends historical queue activation with current-batch activation via a coefficient α=0.95 to capture temporal shifts. The activation-aware score function (Eq. 15) computes a cumulative sum over ranked negative labels, implicitly amplifying the contribution of strongly activated labels and improving robustness to the number of negative labels. Theoretical analysis (Eq. 16) shows that selecting labels with higher OOD-than-ID activation explicitly satisfies the condition p₁−p₂<0 required for adding more negative labels to reduce FPR.

## Results

On the ImageNet-1k benchmark with a ViT-B/16 CLIP encoder, TANL achieves an average FPR95 of 9.81% and AUROC of 97.97% across four OOD datasets (INaturalist, SUN, Places, Textures). This represents a 15.6% absolute reduction in FPR95 over NegLabel (25.40%) and a 7.7% improvement over the leading training-free method CSP (17.51%). TANL also outperforms the best training-based competitor SynOOD (FPR95 14.27%) while requiring no training, and achieves 527 FPS inference speed with zero learnable parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TANL decisively outperforms all training-free baselines: it reduces average FPR95 from 25.40% (NegLabel) to 9.81%, a 15.6-point drop, and beats the strongest training-free competitor CSP (17.51%) by 7.7 points. Among test-time adaptation methods, TANL surpasses AdaNeg (18.92%) and OODD (18.79%) by roughly 9 points in FPR95, with particularly large gains on SUN (3.53% vs. 9.50%) and Textures (13.38% vs. 31.27%). TANL also outperforms the best training-based method SynOOD (14.27%) by 4.5 points in FPR95 while requiring no training or fine-tuning. On individual datasets, TANL achieves the best FPR95 on INaturalist (0.42%), SUN (3.53%), and Textures (13.38%), though on Places (21.90%) it trails SynOOD (12.12%), suggesting scene-based OOD remains a harder case. Under the OpenOOD near-OOD setting (Table 2), TANL achieves 60.06% FPR95, outperforming NegLabel (69.45%) and AdaNeg (67.51%), confirming the advantage of activation-aware label selection in the most challenging regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends NegLabel [29], which mines negative labels by cosine distance from ID classes and uses them in a softmax-based OOD score. The key departure is replacing this static, distribution-agnostic selection with a dynamic, activation-based criterion that evaluates how strongly each candidate label responds to the actual test distribution, approximated online via high-confidence cached samples. It also relates to test-time adaptation methods like AdaNeg [82] and OODD [78], which cache features or build adaptive proxies, but TANL operates at the label level rather than the feature level, introducing an activation-aware score function (Eq. 15) that implicitly weights negative labels by their activation rank. The theoretical analysis in Eq. 16, following NegLabel's multi-label classification framework, provides a formal justification for why activated labels reduce FPR.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a non-obvious limitation of existing negative-label methods: many labels selected as 'distant from ID' actually exhibit lower activation on OOD samples than on ID samples, actively harming detection. This reframes the negative label selection problem from a geometric (distance-based) to a functional (activation-based) one, offering a principled lens for future work. Limitations include reliance on a large external corpus, sensitivity to the bootstrap phase before sufficient high-confidence samples accumulate, and the FIFO queue's limited memory of past distributional shifts. Open questions include whether the activation concept transfers to other VLM architectures beyond CLIP, how it behaves under rapidly non-stationary streaming distributions, and whether the activation-aware score generalizes to multi-modal or video OOD detection.
