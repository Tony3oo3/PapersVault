## Problem

Existing defect feature synthesis methods for few-shot industrial defect classification operate exclusively in Euclidean space, whose flat geometry limits the ability to capture the complex tree-like hierarchical relationships among defect types (e.g., leather defects branching into glue, fold, and color subcategories). No prior work has explored defect synthesis in hyperbolic space, despite its natural suitability for modeling such hierarchical structures under scarce data conditions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261535/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB defective images with pixel-level defect region annotations, processed through a pre-trained AlphaCLIP (ViT-L/14) encoder to extract both whole-image and cropped-region features. It operates under N-way K-shot settings (K ∈ {1,3,5}) where only a handful of labeled defect images per category are available for training. The output is a defect class label assigned by matching the adapted feature embedding against support-set prototypes, with the key intermediate artifact being synthetic defect features generated in the Poincaré ball that augment the limited real training data.

## Method

HypDFS first maps a small set of handcrafted-augmented defect prototype features (extracted by AlphaCLIP) into the Poincaré ball via the exponential map, then models each defect category's distribution using a wrapped normal distribution whose mean and covariance are estimated by a neural ODE (Runge-Kutta) to avoid bias from the tiny prototype set. Synthetic defect features are sampled from this hyperbolic distribution and mapped back to Euclidean space via the logarithmic map. A lightweight residual adapter fuses the pre-trained general features with both real and synthetic defect features through concatenation and a learnable linear projection with SiLU activation. Training is guided by a hierarchical defect contrastive loss that combines cross-entropy, a hyperbolic margin-based synthesis loss preventing synthetic features from collapsing onto prototypes or each other, and a contrastive term using hyperbolic distance between whole-image and cropped-region embeddings to enforce hierarchical structure.

## Results

On the MVTec-FS benchmark, HypDFS achieves mean classification accuracy of 79.3% (1-shot), 89.3% (3-shot), and 91.7% (5-shot), surpassing the strongest Euclidean baseline Zip-A-F by 5.6%, 3.2%, and 2.3% respectively. On the MTD dataset, the gains are even larger: 59.4% (1-shot), 78.6% (3-shot), and 86.4% (5-shot), outperforming Zip-A-F by 4.0%, 11.0%, and 7.8%. The method consistently outperforms all six Euclidean-space baselines across both datasets and all shot settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HypDFS outperforms all six Euclidean-space baselines on every dataset and shot setting. The strongest competitor is Zip-A-F (from the MVREC paper [29]), which itself substantially outperforms the other CLIP-based methods (CLIP-A, CLIP-ProtoNet, CLIP-KNN, CLIP-LinearProb, Tip-A-F) by 5–15% on MVTec-FS. Against Zip-A-F, HypDFS gains 5.6 points at 1-shot on MVTec-FS (79.3 vs. 73.7) and 11.0 points at 3-shot on MTD (78.6 vs. 67.6), with the gap narrowing to 2.3 points at 5-shot on MVTec-FS (91.7 vs. 89.4) as more real data becomes available. The weaker baselines (CLIP-ProtoNet, CLIP-KNN, Tip-A-F) cluster within 1–3% of each other, suggesting that the primary performance leap comes from the multi-view region-context strategy in Zip-A-F, while HypDFS adds a further 2–11% on top via hyperbolic feature synthesis. Notably, on MTD the advantage of HypDFS over Zip-A-F is largest at 3-shot (+11.0%), indicating that hyperbolic distribution modeling is most beneficial when data is most scarce.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the defect feature synthesis paradigm (previously confined to Euclidean space via methods like SimpleNet and CutPaste) into hyperbolic geometry, drawing on the wrapped normal distribution and neural ODE framework from Gao et al. (2022) for manifold-based feature augmentation. It also builds on the CLIP-adaptation line for few-shot defect classification (MVREC/Zip-A-F, CLIP-Adapter, Tip-Adapter) by introducing a lightweight residual adapter trained on both real and synthetic hyperbolic features. The key conceptual departure is the motivation that industrial defects exhibit tree-like hierarchical structure (e.g., leather → color/fold/glue → sub-variants), which hyperbolic space can model more naturally than flat Euclidean geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to apply hyperbolic space specifically to defect feature synthesis, opening a direction that could benefit other hierarchical industrial classification tasks. However, the curvature parameter remains heuristic (c=0.05), and the learnable-curvature variant actually underperformed the fixed setting, leaving the question of how to automatically select optimal curvature open. The evaluation is limited to two benchmarks (MVTec-FS, MTD) with a single GPU, so generalizability to larger-scale or more diverse industrial datasets remains unverified. Future work on adaptive curvature selection and broader benchmark coverage would strengthen the practical case for hyperbolic defect synthesis.
