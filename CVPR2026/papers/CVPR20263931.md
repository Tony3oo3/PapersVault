## Problem

Existing garment grasping methods assume stable illumination and, when extended to low-light via static RGB-depth fusion, treat non-RGB structural cues as a fixed supplement regardless of lighting level. This overlooks that illumination changes alter the relative informativeness of RGB versus depth features, causing depth signals to suppress still-useful RGB luminance cues and reducing grasping robustness. The paper identifies the need for illumination-conditioned, adaptive cross-modal compensation rather than uniform fusion.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a synchronized RGB image and depth map at 512x512 resolution captured under arbitrary illumination. It first applies a Retinex network to decompose the RGB into a luminance channel (H x W x 3) and a structural channel (H x W x 1), then produces two outputs: a semantic mask map over eight garment categories and a single optimal grasp point per garment, determined by a depth-optimal search that selects the closest-to-camera pixel nearest the region's geometric center. The MIGG benchmark provides 15,384 such triplets across four luminance bands (0-120) in two household scenes.

## Method

GraspALL introduces a learnable Parametric Luminance Curve (PLC) bank of 12 curves (256 sampling points each) that maps an input image to a discrete illumination index, replacing non-learnable histogram statistics. Two response libraries, a Luminance Response Library and a Structural Response Library, store encoder features indexed by that illumination level and are updated via exponential moving average during training. At inference, the retrieved luminance feature acts as a query in a cross-attention module over depth-encoded keys and values, producing illumination-adaptive structural compensation features that suppress depth cues incompatible with the current lighting. A Canny map extracted from the brightest reference image provides binary cross-entropy supervision for the structural decoder, while a spectral consistency loss drives the PLC parameters through chained backpropagation. Finally, enhanced luminance and structural features are concatenated, decoded into a semantic mask, and fed into a depth-optimal search strategy that selects the grasp point among the k nearest-surface pixels closest to the garment's geometric center.

## Results

On the MIGG dataset, GraspALL achieves a mean grasping success rate (mGSR) of 93.3% under bright illumination (80–120), 88.3% under medium (40–80), and 84.2% under extreme low-light (0–40), representing improvements of 32%, 36%, and 44% over the strongest baseline (DarkSeg) respectively. For semantic mask generation, GraspALL attains mIoU of 84.8%, 84.3%, 83.4%, and 82.8% across luminance bands 90–120, 60–90, 30–60, and 0–30, outperforming the best baseline (AMDA) by 14–20 percentage points. In real-world deployment on a Realman robot, GraspALL achieves 12–14/15 successful grasps across luminance levels 0–80, consistently exceeding all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GraspALL outperforms all seven baselines across every luminance level on both semantic mask (mIoU) and grasping (mGSR) metrics. Among semantic mask baselines, AMDA [42] is the strongest (77.3% mIoU at luminance 90–120), yet GraspALL still leads by 7.5 points, and the gap widens to 15.5 points at luminance 0–30 (82.8% vs. 67.3%). MRFS [33] and SegMiF [13] show steeper degradation under low light (e.g., MRFS drops 12% from bright to dark), while GraspALL maintains <2% fluctuation. For grasping, DarkSeg [41] is the closest competitor (78.3% mGSR at luminance 80–120), but GraspALL surpasses it by 15 points there and by 30.9 points at extreme low-light (84.2% vs. 53.3%). SAM-M [11] and ReKep [10], despite leveraging large foundation models, achieve only 43.3% and 42.4% mGSR at luminance 0–40, respectively, confirming that scale alone does not resolve illumination-induced degradation. BiFCNet [44], a traditional RGB-D segmentation approach, performs worst under low light (39.9% mGSR), highlighting the limitations of static multimodal fusion without illumination-aware adaptation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard paradigm in low-light garment grasping where non-RGB modalities (depth) are treated as static, illumination-invariant supplements to RGB. Prior methods (SegMiF, MRFS, AMDA, DarkSeg) perform fixed multimodal fusion regardless of lighting conditions, while GraspALL introduces a learnable Parametric Luminance Curve to estimate illumination level and conditionally modulates depth-derived structural features via cross-attention, making the fusion adaptive. Conceptually, it repurposes Retinex decomposition for luminance-structure separation, U-Net encoder-decoders for feature alignment, and EMA-based memory banks (LRL/SRL) for illumination-conditioned feature retrieval, but the specific combination of a parametric illumination estimator driving conditional cross-modal compensation is novel. The paper also introduces the MIGG benchmark, extending prior garment grasping datasets (Flat'n'Fold, GarmentPile) by adding controlled multi-level illumination variation and diverse household scenes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 32–44% grasping accuracy improvement over baselines under low light, validated in both simulation and a real-world Realman robot deployment, demonstrates that illumination-adaptive fusion is meaningfully superior to static multimodal approaches for deformable-object manipulation. The MIGG dataset fills a gap in the literature by providing a controlled, multi-illumination benchmark for garment grasping, though its simulation-based nature and single-illumination-factor modeling limit generalization claims. Open questions include whether the PLC generalizes to multi-source or mixed-spectrum lighting, whether the approach extends to other deformable objects beyond garments, and how well the method transfers to truly unstructured real-world scenes beyond the 1013-image real-world test set.
