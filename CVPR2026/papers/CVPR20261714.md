## Problem

Existing autoregressive trackers predict object states sequentially with a fixed inference capacity, which mismatches the highly dynamic temporal difficulty of real videos—stable segments with smooth motion require minimal reasoning, while sudden motion or heavy occlusion demands richer temporal modeling. This fixed-capacity assumption either wastes computation on easy frames or fails to cope with abrupt challenges, and heuristic workarounds like frame skipping break autoregressive consistency.

## Input / Output

The system consumes a template image, a current search frame, and a window of N historical trajectory states (bounding box coordinates). It produces per-frame object bounding boxes at variable temporal resolution: stable segments are processed in parallel batches of up to N frames using diffusion-predicted trajectory crops, while hard frames are handled sequentially with the high-capacity tracker. The diffusion estimator itself takes projected visual features and concatenated trajectory history as conditioning, and outputs a short-term future trajectory sample plus an implicit stability score that drives the mode-switching decision.

## Method

ARTrack-AC couples three cooperating components: a high-capacity AR tracker (ARTrackOT) for sequential inference on hard frames, a low-capacity AR tracker (FARTrack nano) for multi-frame parallel inference on stable segments, and a lightweight conditional diffusion model (single transformer layer, 4-step DDIM) that predicts N-frame future trajectories conditioned on recent observations. Difficulty is derived training-free from the convergence behavior of diffusion denoising: the maximum adjacent-step trajectory change within an early denoising window serves as a stability score, and a single amplitude threshold determines the length of the upcoming easy segment. For stable segments, diffusion-predicted trajectories guide search-region cropping to enable parallel batching; for hard frames, crops come from the previous tracked state and inference proceeds frame-by-frame. Only the diffusion model is trained (on GOT-10k, TrackingNet, LaSOT) with a combined MSE denoising loss plus L1 and SIoU geometric constraints, while both trackers remain frozen, making the system plug-and-play across tracker backbones.

## Results

ARTrack-AC_para achieves 66.7% AUC on LaSOT at 191 FPS, setting a new state-of-the-art accuracy-speed trade-off that surpasses all compared trackers. On TrackingNet it reaches 81.8% AUC, on GOT-10k it attains 72.3% AO (vs. 67.7% for the best baseline), and on LaSOText it scores 47.5% AUC. The sequential variant (ARTrack-AC_seq) matches the parallel variant's accuracy (66.5% vs. 66.7% AUC on LaSOT) while running at 131 FPS, and the 10-frame parallel variant (ARTrack-AC_para10) pushes speed to 291 FPS with a modest 3.2% AUC drop. Overall, the method runs 2.9× faster than its predecessor ARTrackOT (65 FPS) while improving accuracy by 7.3% AUC on LaSOT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARTrack-AC_para (191 FPS) outperforms the strongest baseline AsymTrack-B (135 FPS) by 2.0% AUC on LaSOT (66.7 vs. 64.7), 1.8% AUC on TrackingNet (81.8 vs. 80.0), 4.6% AO on GOT-10k (72.3 vs. 67.7), and 2.9% AUC on LaSOText (47.5 vs. 44.6), while also running 56% faster on GPU. Against HiT-Base (116 FPS), the gap widens to 2.1% AUC on LaSOT and 8.3% AO on GOT-10k. The sequential variant ARTrack-AC_seq matches the parallel variant's accuracy within 0.2% AUC on LaSOT but at a 32% speed cost (131 vs. 191 FPS), confirming that parallelism in stable segments is the primary efficiency driver. On the challenging LaSOText benchmark (dense distractors, small fast targets), all three ARTrack-AC variants surpass every efficient tracker, with the 10-frame parallel setting still achieving 45.2% AUC versus 44.6% for AsymTrack-B. The method also demonstrates robustness on low-frame-rate data: ARTrack-AC_seq matches AsymTrack-B's runtime on both GPU and CPU on GOT-10k (10 FPS) while exceeding it by 9.7% AO.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the autoregressive tracking lineage (ARTrack, ARTrackV2, FARTrack) by introducing adaptive capacity inference: rather than committing to a fixed computational depth per frame, the tracker dynamically switches between a high-capacity sequential mode and a low-capacity parallel mode based on predicted temporal difficulty. It departs from prior adaptive tracking methods (EAST, ABTrack, SGLATrack, ACFN) that rely on per-frame, appearance-driven reactive signals, instead using a forward-looking, motion-dynamics-based scheduling signal derived from a diffusion trajectory estimator. The diffusion model is repurposed not as a final predictor but as a trajectory prior and an implicit difficulty sensor, with its denoising convergence behavior serving as a training-free stability signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that aligning inference cost with temporal complexity—rather than optimizing a single fixed-capacity model—yields a superior accuracy-speed Pareto frontier (66.7% AUC at 191 FPS on LaSOT, 2.9× faster than its predecessor). The 'system-level autoregression' framing, where the tracker is autoregressive in both state prediction and capacity selection, offers a principled template for resource-aware inference in sequential vision tasks. A key limitation is sensitivity to frame rate: the parallel variant degrades at low FPS (10 fps on GOT-10k) due to reduced trajectory-prediction fidelity, and the paradigm's gains depend on having a meaningful gap between the two capacity modes. Open questions include whether the difficulty-aware scheduling generalizes to multi-object or video-level tracking settings and whether the diffusion-based estimator can be further compressed for edge deployment.
