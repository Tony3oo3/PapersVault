## Problem

Flattened feature representations from trained deep backbones for fundus image classification are highly redundant, containing correlated or weak dimensions that dilute discriminative signals, amplify dataset-specific noise, and hinder cross-device generalization. While channel pruning and token pruning have been explored, post-training pruning of the final flattened feature space—operating after the backbone is frozen and before the classifier—has received limited attention, particularly in medical imaging where small datasets make high-dimensional feature spaces prone to overfitting.

## Input / Output

The method consumes precomputed flattened feature vectors from a frozen backbone (2152-dim for EfficientNetV2, 768-dim for ViT, 1024-dim for CoAtNet) along with their training labels, and produces a binary mask selecting a compact subset of feature indices (reducing dimensionality by 4–96%). Fundus images are 224×224 RGB; the final pruned model outputs class labels for binary DR detection, glaucoma detection, multi-label disease classification, or multi-class retinal disease classification, evaluated via AUROC and AUPRC.

## Method

GFP (Greedy Feature Pruning) is a training-free, architecture-agnostic algorithm that partitions the L-dimensional flattened feature vector into contiguous groups of size n, computes a solo performance score for each group by zeroing all others and evaluating (AUROC+AUPRC)/2 on the training set, then ranks groups by score. Starting from the top K_min groups (enforcing a minimum keep ratio r_min for stability), it greedily adds one group at a time in rank order, tracking the subset that maximizes the metric. The final index set defines a binary mask applied to the classifier input, yielding a compact model with no retraining. The paper proves the exact subset selection problem is NP-hard (exponential in L) and shows GFP's time complexity is Θ(NCL²/n), making it tractable for typical feature dimensions around 10³.

## Results

GFP improves AUROC by up to 2.16 points and AUPRC by up to 3.73 points across five fundus datasets while reducing flattened feature dimensions by 4%–96% (e.g., CoAtNet 1024→32 on Messidor-2). The largest gains appear with CoAtNet: DDR AUROC/AUPRC rises from 95.17/96.60 to 96.73/97.37, and PAPILA AUPRC jumps from 80.70 to 84.43. Cross-dataset transfer (Messidor-2→DDR) shows the biggest single improvement: EfficientNetV2 AUROC increases from 80.83 to 82.99 (+2.16) with only 2152→1824 features retained.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GFP consistently outperforms or matches the unpruned backbone across all three architectures, while magnitude pruning (MP, Han et al. 2015) provides only marginal or negative effects: MP degrades EfficientNetV2 on RETINA (77.85→73.57 AUROC) and ViT on DDR (91.33→90.82), though it slightly helps CoAtNet on DDR (95.17→95.72) before GFP surpasses both (96.73). L1 regularization (Tibshirani 1996) is the weakest baseline, causing large drops on small datasets (e.g., EfficientNetV2 Messidor-2 AUROC falls from 87.66 to 83.25; CoAtNet Messidor-2 from 89.49 to 80.15). The ViT-specific token-pruning methods TRAM and LTMP underperform the plain ViT on four of five datasets; on RETINA, TRAM reaches only 89.96/78.39 and LTMP 83.17/67.87 versus ViT's 90.75/83.16, while GFP pushes ViT to 92.00/83.01. Overall, GFP is the only method that improves or preserves performance across all backbones and all five datasets simultaneously, confirming that post-hoc feature-space pruning is more robust than weight-level or token-level alternatives for fundus classification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant pruning literature, which targets intermediate channels (Ayinde & Zurada, Li et al., Duan et al.) or ViT tokens (TRAM, LTMP), by operating exclusively on the final flattened feature vector after the backbone is frozen. Unlike channel pruning or token pruning, which modify the computation graph and typically require fine-tuning, GFP is a training-free, architecture-agnostic post-hoc procedure that selects a compact subset of feature dimensions via a greedy build-up guided by a performance metric. The closest prior work, DeepFS, screens raw input features rather than learned deep representations, leaving the specific problem of pruning the backbone's output feature space largely unaddressed, particularly in medical imaging.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 4–96% of flattened features can be removed while often *improving* AUROC/AUPRC and cross-dataset transfer suggests that modern backbones encode substantial dataset-specific noise in their final representations, a practical insight for deploying compact, interpretable classifiers in clinical settings where retraining is costly. However, GFP does not reduce backbone inference cost (only the classifier head's input), uses a fixed group size that may miss non-contiguous feature interactions, and the greedy strategy offers no optimality guarantee. Open questions include whether adaptive or learned grouping, intermediate-layer sparsity, and extension to segmentation or detection tasks could further unlock the redundancy that GFP reveals.
