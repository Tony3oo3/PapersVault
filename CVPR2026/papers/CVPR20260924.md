## Problem

Offline diagrams exist only as images without structured data, making them non-editable and incomprehensible to LLMs. Existing stroke reconstruction methods handle character-level strokes but fail on complex diagram intersections, and current diagram recognition is limited to region-level cues or online-only settings. No prior framework bridges the gap between raw diagram images and LLM-capable structured representations for semantic reasoning and editing.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260924/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a single offline diagram image (handwritten or digital, containing flowcharts, mind maps, or state machines with 10+ symbols) and a natural-language query or editing instruction. It produces (1) reconstructed online strokes as coordinate sequences, (2) instance-level stroke class labels and symbol identifiers, and (3) a standardized node-edge data structure with attributes (ID, text, type, size, incoming/outgoing edges, child/parent nodes for nodes; length, direction, weight, start/end points for edges) that is fed alongside the original image to an LLM for Q&A or editing.

## Method

DiagramDiff comprises two stages. The reconstruction model skeletonizes the image via Guo-Hall thinning, detects joint points through 8-connected background component counting, classifies segments by endpoint topology, and merges segments via a maximum-weight matching over a connectivity score combining endpoint distance, angular alignment, and curvature similarity; a length-aware angular threshold with graph-cut optimization then splits erroneously merged strokes at sharp-angle points. The recognition model builds a graph where nodes are strokes (with class and instance attributes) and edges encode same/different-instance relations, extracts dual-channel features (GCN for geometric attributes, depthwise-separable convolution for image patches), and integrates a pre-trained diffusion model whose three independent DDIM intermediate denoising outputs serve as Query, Key, and Value in the GTN attention mechanism. This diffusion-based feature enhancement lets robust image features compensate for attribute bias introduced by reconstruction errors, avoiding the naive concatenation that degrades accuracy on reconstructed strokes.

## Results

On the primary diagram recognition task, DiagramDiff achieves SCA of 94.64% and 98.39% on CASIA-OHFC and OHSD respectively, outperforming the strongest baseline SpaceGTN (89.56% and 93.21%) by over 5 percentage points. On the FC_A and FC_B datasets, it reaches 97.24% and 97.92% SCA, again surpassing SpaceGTN (96.12% and 96.72%). For the stroke reconstruction sub-task, DiagramDiff attains IoU of 55.6% and 96.5% SRR on CASIA-OHFC, and 53.9% IoU and 96.2% SRR on OHSD, setting new state-of-the-art across all three metrics (IoU, SRR, HD). In the user study with 500 Q&A and editing tasks, DiagramDiff improves LLM accuracy by 10–20 percentage points over direct image input (e.g., GPT-4o editing: 71% → 90%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiagramDiff is compared against four offline recognition methods (DETR, DeDETR, Faster R-CNN, Mask R-CNN) and four online methods (ORSAD, EGAT, InstGNN, SpaceGTN). The offline methods are substantially outperformed, with the best (Mask R-CNN) reaching only 74.11% SCA on OHSD versus DiagramDiff's 98.39%. Among online methods, SpaceGTN is the strongest baseline, but DiagramDiff surpasses it by 5.08% SCA on CASIA-OHFC (94.64 vs. 89.56), 5.18% on OHSD (98.39 vs. 93.21), 1.12% on FC_A, and 1.20% on FC_B. The gap is largest on the more challenging CASIA-OHFC and OHSD datasets, where reconstructed strokes introduce attribute noise that the diffusion-based feature enhancement module specifically addresses. For stroke reconstruction (Table 3), DiagramDiff outperforms all three baselines (Liu et al., Chan et al., Mouss et al.) on every metric: e.g., on CASIA-OHFC it achieves 55.6% IoU vs. 50.5% (Mouss et al.) and 96.5% SRR vs. 92.3%, while also attaining the lowest Hausdorff distance (3.61 vs. 3.71). The ablation in Table 4 further confirms that the feature enhancement (FE) module contributes ~4% SCA improvement on reconstructed strokes, validating the diffusion-based fusion design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GTN/SpaceGTN lineage for online diagram recognition and the Guo-Hall thinning tradition for stroke extraction, but departs in two key ways: (1) it introduces a split-point detection module with graph-cut optimization to handle the merging of distinct strokes in offline diagrams, and (2) it integrates a pre-trained diffusion model into the GTN as a feature-enhancement mechanism to compensate for attribute bias introduced by stroke reconstruction. The broader positioning is novel: rather than treating diagram recognition as an end task, the paper frames it as a preprocessing pipeline that converts offline diagram images into standardized node-edge structures consumable by LLMs for semantic Q&A and editing — a use case absent from prior diagram recognition work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The introduction of DiagramQAE, the first offline diagram Q&A and editing benchmark, fills a clear gap for evaluating LLM-based diagram interaction, and the standardized data structure (Table 1) offers a practical schema for downstream diagram editing tools. However, the evidence base is limited: the dataset contains only 100 diagrams, the user study involves 10 participants, and LLM evaluation covers just four models, leaving open questions about scalability to highly complex diagrams (hundreds of nodes), generalization to diagram types beyond flowcharts/mind maps/state machines, and whether the diffusion-model feature enhancement transfers to other graph-based recognition settings. The 23 GB GPU memory requirement for the diffusion module also raises practical deployment concerns.
