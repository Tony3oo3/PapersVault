## Problem

Existing multi-shot video generation methods face a fundamental trade-off: end-to-end approaches are computationally expensive with limited user control, while keyframe-based methods that generate sparse single frames per shot fail to maintain cross-shot entity consistency and cannot model cinematic inter-shot transitions (e.g., shot/reverse shot, camera movement continuity). The paper identifies that the sparse keyframe formulation inherently lacks the structural information needed to anchor both intra-shot progression and inter-shot transitions.

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a free-form text-based story theme as input, which a Director Agent expands into a structured storyboard of per-shot text descriptions and cinematic attributes (shot scale, camera angle, camera movement, shot length). STEP² then iteratively produces start-end frame pairs (two RGB images per shot) that serve as visual anchors, and a Refiner Agent converts these into enhanced prompts for an off-the-shelf video model (WanX or Veo3.1) to synthesize the final clips. The training dataset (ConStoryBoard) consists of 100K start-end frame pairs extracted from >1080p movie clips with aesthetic scores above 5.5, annotated with story progression text and structured cinematic attributes.

## Method

The core contribution is STEP², which reformulates multi-shot generation as iterative start-end frame pair prediction using a flow-matching diffusion Transformer (MMDiT blocks). Three key mechanisms enable coherence: (1) a multi-shot memory pack that compresses all preceding shot frames into a single compact token via progressive spatial tiling with geometrically decaying compression rates (1/2^j), ranked by CLIP similarity to the current shot; (2) a dual-encoding strategy that VAE-encodes the start and end frames separately and concatenates them into a joint shot tensor, enabling implicit visual context sharing within each shot; and (3) a two-stage training scheme combining LoRA-based supervised fine-tuning (100K iterations) with DPO preference alignment (20K iterations) on manually curated preference tuples to learn valid cinematic transitions. A Qwen2.5-VL understanding model generates unified context tokens from the previous shot's end frame, text description, and cinematic attributes, which condition the generation model alongside the memory tokens.

## Results

STAGE achieves the best score across all 12 quantitative metrics in the main comparison. Headline numbers include Aesthetic Quality (AQ) of 0.7689, Overall Consistency (OC) of 0.2713, inter-shot Subject Consistency (SC-E) of 0.6917, and Transition Vector Similarity (TVS) of 0.2732. In LLM-based evaluation, STAGE scores 0.8929 on Overall Video Quality (OVQ), 0.6069 on Video-Text Consistency (VTC), 0.6985 on Inter-Shot Consistency (ISC), and 0.6255 on Shot Transition Smoothness (STS). In user studies, STAGE is preferred by 57.6%–72.8% of raters across four evaluation dimensions, far exceeding the next-best method (13.2%–24.4%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

STAGE outperforms all five baselines on every metric. Against the end-to-end method CineTrans [53], STAGE gains +0.20 in AQ, +0.07 in OC, +0.07 in SC-E, and +0.23 in TVS, indicating substantially better visual quality, text alignment, cross-shot consistency, and transition smoothness. Among keyframe-based methods, IC-LoRA+WanX [18,44] is the closest competitor on TVS (0.2090 vs. 0.2732) but lags significantly on VTC (0.3897 vs. 0.6069) and ISC (0.4901 vs. 0.6985). StoryDiffusion+WanX [63,44] produces the most cartoonish output and scores lowest on OVQ (0.5343) and VTC (0.2069) among keyframe methods. VideoGen-of-Thought [62] achieves the highest OVQ among baselines (0.8106) but has the weakest text-video alignment (VTC 0.1120) and poor transition quality (TVS 0.0966). MovieAgent [52] is the weakest overall, scoring lowest on OC (0.0711), TVS (0.0079), and all LLM-based metrics, reflecting severe text-fidelity and transition failures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reformulates the keyframe-based multi-shot video generation paradigm by replacing sparse single keyframes with start-end frame pairs, explicitly modeling both intra-shot progression and inter-shot transitions. It builds on the Qwen-Image diffusion transformer backbone and flow matching framework, extending DPO-based preference alignment from static image generation to the temporal/cinematic domain of multi-shot video. The multi-shot memory pack and dual-encoding strategy are novel architectural components, but the overall pipeline composes established techniques (MMDiT, LoRA, CLIP-based ranking, VAE encoding) in a new configuration tailored to the start-end pair formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ConStoryBoard dataset (100K annotated clips with cinematic attributes and preference tuples) fills a clear gap in multi-shot video training data, and the start-end pair formulation offers a clean, modular interface that decouples narrative planning from video synthesis. A key limitation acknowledged by the authors is the reliance on an off-the-shelf video generator for intra-shot infilling, which can introduce temporal inconsistencies that the storyboard anchors cannot fully control. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very long narratives (beyond the demonstrated 4–6 shots), whether the memory pack's progressive compression degrades over many shots, and whether the DPO alignment generalizes to diverse cinematic styles beyond the movie-clip distribution.
