## Problem

Existing gaze modelling methods collapse continuous eye movements into aggregated saliency maps or discrete fixation sequences, obscuring natural dynamics such as smooth pursuit and requiring unreliable fixation filtering for video stimuli. No prior work has applied graph-based simulation to model human attention as an autoregressive dynamical system in dynamic, task-driven video settings, particularly driving.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262190/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes T=20 timesteps (1 s) of egocentric driving video at 1280×640 (10 fps upsampled to 20 fps), with per-frame object detections (YOLOv8x), a drivable-area mask (YOLOPv2), monocular depth (monodepth2), VGG16 appearance crops, and historical gaze positions, all assembled into a spatio-temporal heterogeneous graph. The output is a 2D Gaussian mixture model (one component per last-timestep node) for the next gaze position; autoregressive sampling from this distribution yields raw gaze trajectories, which are post-processed training-free into scanpaths (EyeMMV fixation detection) and saliency maps (Gaussian-convolved fixation density).

## Method

The core architecture is a stack of Affinity Relation Transformer (ART) blocks that extend Heterogeneous Graph Transformers by injecting pairwise edge-affinity vectors (3D position deltas, timestep offsets, appearance cosine similarity) directly into key and value embeddings via two independent linear–BatchNorm–ReLU–linear encoders, replacing learned relative-position encodings with arbitrary relational features. A dedicated gaze node at each timestep encodes the driver's foveal view, and temporal edges across a multi-scale offset set {1,2,4,8,16} enable autoregressive conditioning on gaze history. The Object Density Network (ODN) head maps each last-timestep node to one Gaussian component of a mixture whose capacity K=|V_T| adapts to scene complexity, providing an interpretable fixation mechanism: high mixing weight on the gaze node maintains fixation, while high weight on environment nodes shifts attention toward relevant objects. Training uses negative log-likelihood on raw gaze positions without any fixation filtering, and simulation proceeds by iteratively sampling from the predictive GMM, updating the scene graph, and repeating to generate full gaze sequences.

## Results

On Focus100, ART achieves the best DTW (42.31 vs. 48.72 for GLC, 49.23 for DReyeVENet), LEV (1.23), fixation duration (0.41 s, closest to the human 0.44 s), fixation rate (1.64 fix/s, matching the human 1.61), and saliency metrics (NSS 4.864, IG 9.728, AUC 0.945), all outperforming every baseline. On MAAD, ART reaches a temporal correlation of 0.46 (exceeding the human leave-one-out baseline of 0.42), DTW of 2.70 (human: 2.65), and the best saliency scores (NSS 4.926, IG 9.778, AUC 0.953). The method is the only one that simultaneously matches human-level scanpath dynamics and produces state-of-the-art saliency maps without explicit saliency supervision.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ART outperforms all six baselines on the majority of metrics across both datasets. On Focus100, ART achieves the lowest DTW (42.31 vs. 48.72 for GLC, 49.23 for DReyeVENet, 51.76 for SCOUT) and LEV (1.23 vs. 1.24–1.45 for the deep baselines), and its fixation duration (0.41 s) and rate (1.64 fix/s) closely match human values (0.44 s, 1.61 fix/s), whereas all baselines produce far shorter fixations (0.12–0.21 s) and lower rates (0.05–0.57 fix/s). On saliency, ART leads on NSS (4.864 vs. 4.481 for GLC, 4.310 for ViNet), IG (9.728 vs. 9.471 for ViNet), and AUC (0.945 vs. 0.938 for ViNet) on Focus100, and on all three saliency metrics on MAAD (NSS 4.926, IG 9.778, AUC 0.953). The only metric where ART does not lead on Focus100 is temporal correlation (TC 0.22), which is marginally below DReyeVENet and ViNet (0.23) but tied with SCOUT. On MAAD, ART exceeds the human leave-one-out TC (0.46 vs. 0.42) and matches human DTW (2.70 vs. 2.65) and LEV (0.10 vs. 0.10), while ViNet achieves a slightly higher NSS (5.733) and IG (10.264) on that smaller dataset. Classical saliency methods (Itti, GBVS, Gaussian) are consistently far behind on all sequence and dynamics metrics, confirming that pixel-level saliency models cannot recover natural gaze dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends graph-based simulation (GBS), previously applied to physics (fluids, cloth) and traffic trajectory prediction, to the novel domain of gaze dynamics, framing driver attention as an autoregressive dynamical system on a heterogeneous spatio-temporal scene graph. The Affinity Relation Transformer (ART) generalises the Heterogeneous Graph Transformer (HGT) by injecting pairwise relational features (3D position differences, temporal offsets, appearance similarity) directly into key and value embeddings, replacing the free learned relative-position encodings common in vision transformers. The Object Density Network departs from fixed-component mixture density networks used in prior scanpath work by adapting the number of Gaussian components to the number of scene-graph nodes, making the predictive distribution object-centric and scene-adaptive. Unlike prior driving-attention methods that collapse gaze into aggregated saliency maps or discrete fixation sequences, this work operates on raw continuous gaze trajectories without fixation filtering.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified formulation—producing raw gaze sequences, scanpaths, and saliency maps from a single autoregressive model without task-specific heads—simplifies the modelling pipeline and avoids the artefacts introduced by fixation filtering on video stimuli. The Focus100 dataset (30 participants, 100 diverse driving clips, raw 60 Hz gaze, hazard annotations) addresses a real gap, as prior datasets like DR(eye)VE suffer from temporal misalignment and MAAD covers only a small subset. Key limitations include the controlled lab setting (57 cm monitor viewing rather than on-road), dependence on an upstream perception stack whose failures propagate to gaze prediction, and the absence of explicit driver-intent conditioning. Open questions include whether the graph-based simulation framing generalises to on-road or multi-driver settings, and whether the object-centric mixture head transfers to non-driving or non-structured scenes.
