## Problem

When text-to-image diffusion models receive underspecified prompts, they must make implicit decisions to fill in unspecified attributes, yet the internal mechanism governing these choices remains unexplored. Existing localization techniques rely on prompt injection to trace explicitly mentioned concepts through cross-attention layers, but this conflates explicit text conditioning with the model's intrinsic default decision-making. The paper asks: where in the architecture does the model resolve ambiguous, unspecified attributes, and can this knowledge enable more precise interventions?

## Input / Output

The method takes underspecified text prompts (e.g., 'a photo of a person') as generation input, extracts intermediate activations from all self- and cross-attention layers across denoising timesteps, and uses an external CLIP-based classifier to pseudo-label the generated images. It produces a ranked list of layer indices by linear-probe discriminability, normalized steering direction vectors (unit vectors derived from logistic regression weight vectors), and steered or fine-tuned generated images. Steering is applied from timestep t=15 onward on 2–4 selected self-attention layers, while the fine-tuning variant uses LoRA adapters trained on triplets of (image, general prompt, specific prompt).

## Method

ICM (Implicit Choice-Modification) localizes implicit decision layers via a three-stage probing pipeline: (1) generate images from a general prompt and collect average-pooled activations at every layer and timestep, (2) pseudo-label outputs with an external classifier to recover the model's actual attribute choices, and (3) fit logistic regression probes per layer to quantify linear separability of attribute classes. The key empirical finding is that self-attention layers exhibit substantially higher probe accuracy than cross-attention layers, revealing that implicit decisions are propagated from initial noise through self-attention rather than governed by text-conditioned cross-attention. For intervention, the method offers two modes: activation steering, which adds a scaled normalized probe weight vector (H' = H + αs) to selected layers at inference time, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning via LoRA applied exclusively to the identified layers. This selective targeting yields state-of-the-art debiasing (e.g., 0.087 FD for gender) while preserving image quality (122 FID) and prompt alignment, outperforming methods that intervene on all cross-attention layers or the UNet bottleneck.

## Results

On SD v1.5 debiasing (Table 1), ICM(Steering) achieves a Fairness Discrepancy (FD↓) of 0.087 for Gender, 0.133 for Age, and 0.266 for Race, while maintaining FID↓ of 122.08, 114.87, and 116.88 respectively—close to the original model's 120.06 FID. The method also preserves the highest CLIP-T prompt alignment (0.6140–0.6172) among all debiasing approaches. For the USA President steering task on SDXL (Table 5), targeting only the top-20 localized self-attention layers achieves 83.0% steering success with 0.893 CLIP-I, compared to 91.0% success but only 0.779 CLIP-I when steering all layers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ICM(Steering) does not always achieve the lowest FD: DIFFLENS [35] reaches 0.046 FD on Gender and 0.049 on Age, and Latent Direction [19] reaches 0.052 on Age and 0.175 on Race, all lower than ICM's corresponding values. However, ICM(Steering) substantially outperforms all baselines on the quality–bias trade-off: it maintains FID within ~2–5 points of the unmodified model (122.08 vs. 120.06 original) while Finetuning [34] inflates FID to 161.47 and Latent Editing [16] to 166–201. On CLIP-T prompt alignment, ICM(Steering) scores 0.6140–0.6172, exceeding every baseline (best competitor: DIFFLENS at 0.6090–0.6149). For the Race task, ICM(Steering) achieves the best CLIP-I (0.9099) and CLIP-T (0.6172) among all methods while still reducing FD from 0.558 to 0.266. The paper's central claim is that ICM provides the best overall balance—strong bias reduction without the severe quality degradation or prompt-alignment loss that broader interventions (all-layer steering, full finetuning) introduce.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant prompt-injection (causal tracing) paradigm for layer localization in diffusion models, which has consistently identified cross-attention layers as the locus of text-conditioned generation. Instead, it introduces a probing-based technique that operates on underspecified prompts and retrospectively labels outputs, revealing that implicit attribute decisions are governed by self-attention layers rather than cross-attention. For the debiasing application, it builds on activation steering methods (Li et al., Parihar et al., Shi et al.) and LoRA-based fine-tuning, but restricts interventions to a small subset of layers identified through the probing procedure, rather than applying them uniformly across all cross-attention layers or the UNet bottleneck.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that self-attention—not cross-attention—encodes implicit attribute decisions reframes how practitioners should think about where biases reside in diffusion models, with direct implications for targeted debiasing, concept editing, and interpretability. The approach scales across architectures (UNet-based SD/SDXL and Transformer-based SANA), suggesting the self-attention localization principle is not architecture-specific. Open questions include whether the mechanism generalizes to more complex or compositional implicit decisions, whether the fine-tuning variant can match the steering variant's quality preservation (Table 2 shows mixed results), and whether the identified layers remain stable across different training runs or model scales.
