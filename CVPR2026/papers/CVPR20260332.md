## Problem

Instruction-based image editing models are designed for non-high-resolution (≤1024×1024) inputs and degrade severely at ultra-high-resolution (≥4096×4096), producing distorted, noise-like outputs. The intuitive downsample–edit–upsample workaround inevitably loses high-frequency texture details that cannot be faithfully recovered. This gap is compounded by the absence of any large-scale, high-quality UHR editing dataset to train or evaluate such models.

## Input / Output

The method takes a UHR RGB image (minimum 4096×4096, dataset average 4656×4138) and a natural-language editing instruction as input, and produces a single edited UHR image at the same native resolution. Editing is performed directly in UHR latent space rather than via a downsample–edit–upsample pipeline, and the 13 supported edit types span local editing, global editing, camera movement, and personalized generation. The VINS-4KEval benchmark provides 509 UHR test samples across these 13 types for evaluation.

## Method

The paper introduces VINS-120K, a 120K-triplet dataset built primarily from 8K-UHD video frames (via CLIP-similarity and optical-flow filtering) augmented with curated long-tail samples from X2Edit and Nano-Consistent, all passed through a four-stage filtering pipeline (file validation, multi-dimensional quality scoring, VLM-guided instruction-adherence checking with object-level CLIP/L2 metrics, and dual-model aesthetic assessment) that retains only the top 20%. On top of this data, a High-Frequency-Aware Post-Adaptation strategy extends FLUX.1-Kontext-dev (fine-tuned with LoRA rank 32 at 4096×4096) to UHR via two mechanisms: (1) long-sequence stabilization through resolution-aware attention-score rescaling (temperature τ = log√(N_UHR/N_NHR)) and NTK-style RoPE base rescaling (b′ = b·√(N_UHR/N_NHR)) to counteract entropy shift and positional extrapolation failure; and (2) a Frequency-Focused Supervision (FFS) auxiliary loss that computes 2D-DFT spectral discrepancy between predicted and target latents, weighted by a dynamic function α_t that increases high-frequency emphasis as the noise level t decreases, added to the standard flow-matching objective with weight λ.

## Results

On VINS-4KEval, Kontext-dev with the proposed high-frequency-aware post-adaptation achieves a patch-FID (pFID) of 9.15, a 28% reduction over Seedream 4.0 (12.82) and a 28% improvement over the unmodified Kontext-dev base (12.66). The adapted model also slightly improves ImageJudge scores over the base (4.23/4.70/4.47 vs. 4.22/4.60/4.41 for Instruction Adherence / Editing Quality / Detail Preservation) while maintaining comparable VIEScore (7.44 overall vs. 7.43). Among all open-source baselines, the adapted model achieves the lowest pFID, outperforming the next-best (Kontext-dev at 12.66) by a wide margin.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the unmodified Kontext-dev base, the post-adaptation strategy improves pFID from 12.66 to 9.15 (−28%) while marginally increasing ImageJudge Editing Quality (4.60→4.70) and Detail Preservation (4.41→4.47), confirming that the adaptation enhances texture fidelity without sacrificing instruction following. Compared to Seedream 4.0 (the strongest commercial 4K editor), the adapted model loses on editing-ability metrics (ImageJudge overall ~4.47 vs. ~4.70; VIEScore 7.44 vs. 8.03) but achieves a substantially lower pFID (9.15 vs. 12.82), indicating superior fine-grained texture synthesis. Among open-source NHR baselines, the adapted model dominates on pFID: it outperforms Bagel (15.41), Step1X-Edit (15.37), ICEdit (16.69), AnyEdit (18.44), and OmniGen2 (18.73) by 3–10 points. On editing-ability metrics (ImageJudge, VIEScore), the adapted model is competitive with or slightly below the top open-source baselines (Bagel, Step1X-Edit), suggesting that the primary gain from VINS-120K training is in detail fidelity rather than semantic editing accuracy. The paper also demonstrates cross-backbone generality: applying the same post-adaptation to QwenImage-Edit-2511 reduces its pFID from 18.33 (Qwen+SR) to 11.38, confirming the strategy is not tied to the FLUX architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the instruction-based image editing line (InstructPix2Pix → FLUX.1-Kontext → DiT-based editors) into the ultra-high-resolution regime, where prior datasets cap at ~1.5K resolution and models trained at 1024×1024 degrade on 4K inputs. Methodologically, it repurposes long-context techniques from LLMs (NTK-aware scaled RoPE, attention temperature rescaling) into the image-token domain and introduces a frequency-domain auxiliary loss on top of the standard flow-matching objective. For data, it departs from synthetic image-to-image pipelines used by X2Edit and OmniEdit by sourcing pairs from real 8K video (UltraVideo), arguing that temporal continuity in video preserves fine-grained texture that fixed-resolution generation pipelines cannot.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

VINS-120K is the first large-scale 4K+ editing dataset, and the accompanying VINS-4KEval benchmark fills a gap in UHR evaluation; both are likely to become standard resources for the subfield. The post-adaptation recipe (attention rescaling + RoPE rescaling + frequency-focused loss) is simple, backbone-agnostic (validated on both Kontext and Qwen), and reduces pFID by 28% over Seedream 4.0, suggesting that targeted high-frequency supervision can close much of the resolution gap without architectural changes. Open questions include whether the approach scales to 8K/16K (exponentially longer sequences), whether the 20% data retention rate is optimal, and whether the frequency loss generalizes to video editing or other generative tasks.
