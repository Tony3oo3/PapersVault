## Problem

Direct-gradient fine-tuning of flow-matching models for preference alignment is blocked by two issues: backpropagating through long ODE sampling trajectories incurs prohibitive memory costs and causes gradient explosion, so existing methods can only update late timesteps. Early steps, which determine global image layout, remain untrained, and methods that do reach early steps (e.g., DRTune) discard the nested gradient term that captures cross-timestep interactions.

## Input / Output

The method fine-tunes a rectified flow matching model (Flux.1-dev) that takes a text prompt and produces a 720×720 RGB image via 25 ODE sampling steps with classifier-free guidance (scale 3.5). During training, a differentiable reward model (HPSv2.1, PickScore, or HPSv3) scores the actual generated image x_0, and the hinge-style loss drives reward maximization. Training uses 50,000 prompts from HPDv2 or MJHQ-30k for general preference alignment, or GenEval prompts for compositional alignment, with a batch size of 64 over 300 iterations on 16 GPUs.

## Method

LeapAlign constructs a two-step 'leap trajectory' from a full ODE sampling run: at each iteration, a complete trajectory is generated, two timesteps k > j are randomly sampled, and two one-step leaps (x_k → x̂_{j|k} and x_j → x̂_{0|j}) are formed using the rectified-flow prediction x̂_{j|k} = x_k − (k−j)·v_θ(x_k). A latent connector (stop-gradient correction) bridges each predicted state to the actual state, preserving differentiability while keeping memory constant. To stabilize training, a gradient discounting factor α scales down the nested gradient term (capturing cross-step interactions) rather than removing it as in DRTune, and a trajectory-similarity weighting up-weights leap trajectories whose predicted states closely match the true path. The reward is evaluated on the actual final image x_0 (not the one-step estimate), and a hinge loss max(0, λ − r(x_0)) prevents reward hacking.

## Results

On the HPDv2 test set (400 prompts, 4 images each), LeapAlign achieves the best in-domain HPSv2.1 score of 0.4092 (vs. 0.3078 for base Flux) and leads on all out-of-domain preference metrics: HPSv3 15.7678, PickScore 23.7137, UnifiedReward-IQ 3.7244, and ImageReward 1.5104. On the GenEval compositional benchmark (553 prompts), LeapAlign reaches an overall score of 0.7420, substantially outperforming the second-best method DRTune (0.7101), with particularly large gains on Attribute Binding (66.00 vs. 55.50) and Position (30.25 vs. 27.50).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LeapAlign outperforms all five baselines on the in-domain HPSv2.1 metric (0.4092), with the largest margin over DanceGRPO (+0.0641) and a clear lead over the closest direct-gradient competitor DRTune (+0.0210). Against MixGRPO, which jointly optimizes three reward models (HPSv2.1, PickScore, ImageReward), LeapAlign—trained only with HPSv2.1—still wins on HPSv2.1, HPSv3, PickScore, UR-Align, and UR-IQ, but trails on ImageReward (1.5104 vs. 1.6155), suggesting multi-reward training helps that specific evaluator. On GenEval, LeapAlign's advantage over DRTune is most pronounced on Attribute Binding (66.00 vs. 55.50, +10.5 pts) and Two Objects (96.46 vs. 93.69, +2.77 pts), confirming that the ability to update early generation steps (which determine layout) is critical for compositional tasks. ReFL and DRaFT-LV, which only update late steps, show minimal improvement on Position (26.75 and 24.00 vs. LeapAlign's 30.25) and Attribute Binding, further validating the leap-trajectory design. Overall, LeapAlign achieves the best score on 6 out of 7 preference metrics and all 6 GenEval sub-tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

LeapAlign extends the direct-gradient line of work for aligning flow-matching models (ReFL, DRaFT-LV, DRTune) by constructing a two-step leap trajectory that enables reward gradients to reach early generation steps while keeping memory constant. It departs from DRTune's approach of stopping gradients at the model input (which discards the nested gradient capturing cross-step interactions) by instead applying a discounting factor that scales down but preserves this term. The method also contrasts with GRPO-based approaches (DanceGRPO, MixGRPO) that treat flow-matching sampling as a stochastic policy, whereas LeapAlign exploits the native differentiability of the ODE trajectory for more stable and faster convergence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to stably update early generation steps—where global layout and composition are determined—is critical for compositional alignment, and LeapAlign demonstrates that this yields measurable gains on GenEval tasks like position and attribute binding that late-step-only methods cannot achieve. The finding that even the α=0 variant (no nested gradient) still outperforms DRTune suggests the leap trajectory construction itself is a valuable inductive bias beyond gradient preservation. Open questions include extending the method to non-differentiable reward models, scaling to video generation (noted as future work), and whether the two-step design generalizes to other flow-matching schedulers beyond rectified flow.
