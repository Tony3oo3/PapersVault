## Problem

Conventional video sensors rely on high-bit-depth ADCs that dominate power consumption (70–85% of total sensor energy), limit maximum frame rates, and generate massive data throughput, making ultra-low-power or extreme-speed imaging impractical. No prior work has demonstrated that a single bit per pixel, captured via time-varying thresholding, is sufficient to reconstruct full-bit-depth video, eliminating the ADC bottleneck entirely at the sensor.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260646/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a sequence of n binary frames (typically n=10, repeated twice for 20 input frames) where each pixel is 0 or 1 depending on whether the analog intensity exceeded the frame's threshold, along with the corresponding threshold sequence τ(k). Binary frames are scaled by their threshold values (or mapped to τ/2 and (1+τ)/2) before network input. The output is a full-bit-depth video (8-bit tone-mapped grayscale, or 12–14-bit RAW green channel), with an overlap strategy shifting the input window by n frames to improve boundary-frame quality.

## Method

The core acquisition mechanism replaces the multi-comparator ramp ADC with a single comparator whose DC reference is adjusted externally between frames, producing one bit per pixel per exposure. The key design choice is the threshold sequence: uniformly spaced thresholds are permuted to maximize the minimum distance between successive values (non-smooth ordering), which ensures frequent threshold crossings and preserves temporal resolution across frames. For reconstruction, the authors train existing video restoration networks (Aswin, EfficientSCI++, Shiftnet, Res2former) that deliberately omit optical-flow modules—since varying thresholds make pixel correspondences ambiguous—using an L1 loss between network output and ground truth. The network receives the threshold-scaled binary frames and the threshold sequence as conditioning, and is trained end-to-end in a supervised manner. They further explore threshold selection via empirical CDF quantiles, Beta-distribution fitting, and gradient-based learned thresholds (with sigmoid approximation for backpropagation through the non-differentiable Heaviside step).

## Results

On the GoPro dataset (250 fps, 8-bit), the best reconstruction network (Aswin) achieves 32.77 dB PSNR / 0.9239 SSIM with overlap and 32.24 dB / 0.9174 without overlap, recovering full 8-bit video from just 1-bit measurements. The model generalizes out-of-distribution to higher frame rates, reaching 35.09 dB / 0.9397 on XVFI (1000 fps) and 34.76 dB / 0.9329 on QUIVER (2000 fps), and successfully recovers video up to 5000 fps from i-SPEED 7 and iX cameras. For RAW video (14-bit), the method achieves 28.36 dB PSNR on Real-RawVSR (tone-mapped) and 32.14 dB on Real-RawVSR (linear RAW), with a notable drop to 18.27 dB in extreme-motion failure cases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks four state-of-the-art video restoration networks as reconstruction backends for the 1-bit measurement pipeline. Aswin [46] achieves the highest fidelity at 32.77 dB PSNR / 0.9239 SSIM (overlap), outperforming EfficientSCI++ [8] by 0.6 dB and Shiftnet [42] by 2.1 dB. EfficientSCI++ is a close second (32.17 dB), while Shiftnet (30.69 dB) and Res2former [71] (30.26 dB) trail by roughly 2 dB, indicating that lightweight transformer-based architectures with shifted-window attention are best suited for the highly ambiguous 1-bit-to-8-bit recovery task. The overlap strategy (shifting the input window by one threshold period and replacing boundary frames) consistently adds 0.4–0.5 dB across all networks, confirming that boundary artifacts from the repeating threshold sequence are the primary source of quality loss. Notably, all four networks are trained without optical-flow modules, since pixel correspondences are unreliable when the same motion-compensated pixel can be binarized to 0 or 1 across frames due to varying thresholds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing the well-studied video restoration task (Aswin, EfficientSCI++, Shiftnet, Res2former) to a radically new input modality: 1-bit frames produced by time-varying thresholding of conventional sensor outputs. It departs from compressive/snapshot video imaging, which requires specialized optical encoding hardware, and from 1-bit compressive sensing, which uses fixed thresholds or dithering on multiplexed measurements. The threshold-selection strategies draw on inverse transform sampling from signal processing and straight-through estimation from learned quantization, but their combination for frame-synchronous video capture on standard CMOS arrays is novel. Unlike event or spike cameras that record 1-bit data asynchronously and require auxiliary sensors, this approach operates on conventional synchronous frame capture with a single comparator per column or pixel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By replacing multi-bit ADC banks with simple comparators, the framework targets the dominant power, memory, and silicon-area bottlenecks in high-speed, high-resolution imaging, potentially enabling gigapixel/kilohertz capture on low-power hardware. Key limitations include the reliance on simulated 1-bit data for most experiments (the hardware proof of concept is a basic comparator on digitized video), performance degradation under high inter-frame motion (PSNR drops to ~18 dB in extreme cases), and the offloading of reconstruction to a neural network that itself consumes power. Open questions include scaling to very long threshold sequences, handling arbitrary motion without optical-flow priors, and demonstrating end-to-end performance on a real 1-bit sensor array at production frame rates.
