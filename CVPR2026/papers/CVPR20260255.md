## Problem

Image layer decomposition—simultaneously extracting a foreground object with occlusion recovery and producing a clean background with the object removed—remains underexplored in diffusion-based generative models. The only prior dedicated method (LAYERDECOMP) requires full fine-tuning of a closed-source text-to-image model on a large curated dataset, making it impractical for most users. This paper asks whether a pre-trained inpainting model can be efficiently repurposed for layer decomposition by exploiting the structural similarity between the two tasks.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 1024×1024 RGB image and a binary mask as primary inputs, and additionally derives edge (Canny), segmentation (SegFormer), and depth (Depth-Anything-V2) maps as auxiliary conditioning. It produces two outputs: an RGBA foreground layer with recovered occluded regions and a transparent alpha channel outside the object, and an RGB background layer with the object fully removed. The foreground and background are generated as two separate token sequences through the DiT, and the RGBA foreground requires a separately fine-tuned VAE decoder while the RGB background reuses the pre-trained VAE.

## Method

The core insight is that layer decomposition decomposes into background inpainting (filling the masked region) and foreground outpainting (extending visible content with an alpha channel), allowing a single pre-trained inpainting DiT (FLUX.1-Fill-dev) to be adapted via LoRA (rank 256) on attention and feed-forward layers plus a modified input projection. A Multi-Modal Latent Fusion module with linear attention complexity O(KN) compresses edge, segmentation, and depth tokens into a compact representation to preserve fine detail without quadratic cost. A dual image-mask context design—introducing a foreground context c_{I-M}^f alongside the standard background context c_{I-M}^b—serves as a control signal that balances content preservation against generation, reducing hallucination in the extracted foreground. Training uses flow matching loss on 100K synthetic triplets assembled from MULAN, LayerDiffuse-generated foregrounds, and OpenImages backgrounds, with intentionally imperfect masks to teach boundary inference.

## Results

On the MULAN test set (526 images), the proposed Outpaint-and-Remove method achieves the best background removal performance across all metrics: PSNR 27.30 dB, SSIM 0.93, LPIPS 0.08, and FID 25.97. Compared to its own base model FLUX.1-Fill-dev (PSNR 25.59, FID 35.96), the adaptation yields a +1.71 dB PSNR gain and a 9.99 FID reduction. For foreground extraction on 40 real-world images, the method achieves 59.51% user preference rate, substantially outperforming DiffMatte (32.34%) and MattingAnything (8.15%), though DiffMatte retains a slight edge on pixel-level metrics (PSNR 29.69 vs. 28.38).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against seven object-removal/inpainting baselines and two matting baselines. On background removal (Table 1), the proposed method outperforms all baselines on every metric, with the largest margins over GeoRemover (+10.11 dB PSNR, −66.80 FID) and Qwen-Image-Edit (+8.23 dB PSNR, −37.52 FID). Even against its own base model FLUX.1-Fill-dev, the lightweight LoRA adaptation improves PSNR by 1.71 dB and reduces FID by 9.99, confirming that the inpainting-to-decomposition transfer is effective. On foreground extraction (Table 2), DiffMatte achieves slightly better pixel-level fidelity (PSNR 29.69 vs. 28.38, FID 28.50 vs. 39.52), but the proposed method wins decisively on user preference (59.51% vs. 32.34%), suggesting its generated foregrounds are more practically useful despite marginally lower reconstruction scores. MattingAnything is outperformed on all metrics by both the proposed method and DiffMatte. Overall, the method establishes state-of-the-art on the novel layer decomposition task while also improving upon the original inpainting capability of the base model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes image layer decomposition as a unification of inpainting (background completion) and outpainting (foreground extraction with alpha), building directly on the FLUX.1-Fill-dev inpainting DiT as a frozen backbone. It departs from the closest prior work, LayerDecomp [37], which requires full fine-tuning on a large curated dataset, by instead using LoRA-based parameter-efficient fine-tuning with a novel multi-modal context fusion module (linear-attention complexity) and a hybrid training data strategy combining real (MULAN) and generated (LayerDiffuse) foregrounds. The conceptual lineage traces from diffusion-based inpainting through the observation that foreground generation with transparency constraints is structurally an outpainting problem, enabling a single backbone to handle both layers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in democratizing layer decomposition: by requiring only publicly available data (100K triplets from MULAN, OpenImages, LayerDiffuse) and lightweight LoRA adaptation rather than full fine-tuning on proprietary datasets, the method makes the capability accessible to resource-constrained users and creates a plug-and-play pathway that benefits from future inpainting model improvements. Key limitations include failures on cluttered scenes, large occlusions, and objects held in fingers, attributed to gaps in the synthetic training data. Open questions include whether the inpainting-to-decomposition transfer generalizes to video or 3D layer decomposition, and whether the multi-modal context design (depth, segmentation, edges) can be further simplified or made adaptive per scene complexity.
