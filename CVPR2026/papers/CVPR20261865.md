## Problem

Cancer-specific WSI prognosis models are trained on very limited data (~1,000 samples per cancer type) and struggle to generalize under high tumor heterogeneity. Existing remedies—multi-cancer joint training and representation-based knowledge transfer (e.g., ROUPKT)—either demand prohibitively expensive large-scale training or incur inference costs that grow linearly with the number of source models. The paper asks whether a single target model can efficiently absorb generalizable prognostic knowledge from other cancers without either of these overheads.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261865/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-extracted WSI patch features (via the UNI foundation model) and the weight parameters of a target cancer-specific MIL model plus 12 source cancer-specific models, all initialized from a shared pretrained backbone M_0. At inference, only the single merged model M_t* is used for survival prediction (C-Index), eliminating the multi-model forward passes required by representation-transfer baselines. The hypernetworks take the same WSI patch features as input to produce input-conditional mixing coefficients λ and aggregation weights w, making the merged model's behavior adaptive to each slide.

## Method

STEPH operates in three stages on task vectors (τ = M_t − M_0): (1) Task Vector Mixup (TVM) interpolates each source-target pair as τ_mix = λτ_t + (1−λ)τ_s, where an MIL-based hypernetwork H_mix outputs an adaptive λ per source, approximating training on vicinal mix data under the VRM principle; (2) a second hypernetwork H_agg (sharing the MIL encoder with H_mix but with an independent head) assigns non-negative weights w to all mixtures, and only the top-K are retained (sparse aggregation inspired by Mixture-of-Experts) to filter redundant or conflicting cross-cancer knowledge; (3) the final merged model is M_t* = M_0 + Σ_j w_j τ_mix,j. Only the two lightweight hypernetworks are trained (with a negative-likelihood survival loss plus auxiliary penalties on λ and w), while the cancer-specific models remain frozen, keeping additional training cost marginal.

## Results

On 13 TCGA cancer datasets (8,818 WSIs, 7,268 patients), STEPH achieves a mean C-Index of 0.6949 across 5-fold cross-validation, outperforming cancer-specific learning (Vanilla: 0.6609, Fine-tuned: 0.6611) by 5.14% on average and the representation-based knowledge transfer baseline ROUPKT (0.6812) by 2.01%. STEPH obtains the best C-Index on 12 out of 13 datasets compared to cancer-specific training, with the largest gain on BRCA (0.7408 vs. 0.6648, +11.4%). All generic model merging baselines (Model Avg., AdaMerging, TIES, Surgery, Iso-C) perform substantially worse, with averages ranging from 0.5689 to 0.6396.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against cancer-specific learning (Vanilla and Fine-tuned), STEPH wins on 12 of 13 datasets with a 5.14% average C-Index improvement, with the most dramatic gain on BRCA (+11.4%) and SARC (+7.1%). Against the representation-based knowledge transfer baseline ROUPKT [15], STEPH achieves a 2.01% average improvement and wins on 9 of 13 datasets, while also eliminating the need for multi-model inference at test time (a single merged model suffices). Among the four generic model merging baselines, TIES AM [37] is the strongest (0.6396 avg.), yet STEPH still outperforms it by 5.53%; the remaining merging methods (AdaMerging 0.5689, Surgery 0.5943, Iso-C 0.5699) all fall below even cancer-specific training, confirming that generic merging strategies designed for multi-task interference resolution are poorly suited to single-task knowledge transfer. The paper further shows that replacing the trainable aggregation weights in these merging baselines with STEPH's hypernetwork-driven weights boosts their average C-Index by 14.5%, underscoring the importance of input-conditional sparsity for this task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on task arithmetic (Ilharco et al., ICLR 2023), which introduced task vectors as a representation of learned knowledge, and repurposes them for cross-cancer knowledge transfer rather than the multi-task learning focus of most subsequent model merging work (TIES, AdaMerging, Surgery, Iso-C). It departs from the representation-based cross-cancer transfer paradigm (ROUPKT, 2025) by replacing multi-model inference at test time with a single merged model, and introduces hypernetworks to learn input-conditional mixup coefficients and sparse aggregation weights rather than relying on validation-set-derived or fixed parameters. The sparse aggregation step borrows the Mixture-of-Experts gating idea, and the mixup operation is grounded in Vicinal Risk Minimization theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in computational pathology: individual cancer types often have only ~1,000 WSI samples, limiting model generalizability. By producing a single merged model rather than requiring multi-model inference, STEPH offers a compelling efficiency advantage over representation-based transfer while achieving 5.14% C-Index improvement over cancer-specific baselines. Key limitations include the continued need for training data to optimize hypernetworks (ruling out fully training-free merging), reliance on TCGA with limited patient counts for some cancer types, and evaluation on a single MIL architecture. Open questions include whether training-free strategies can achieve comparable input-conditional weighting, and whether the approach generalizes to more advanced WSI architectures beyond attention-based MIL.
