## Problem

The modality gap in multimodal affective computing severely limits fusion effectiveness, as features from different modalities occupy non-aligned regions in feature space. Existing distribution-alignment methods (GANs, diffusion models, contrastive learning) typically perform one-to-one pairwise alignment without exposing each source data point to the global distribution of the target modality, resulting in brittle alignment that degrades under scarce paired data and ambiguous flow directions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262020/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-extracted acoustic, visual, and language feature sequences (U_m ∈ R^{T_m × d_m}) and produces a task-specific prediction (sentiment score, humor/sarcasm label). Language is designated as the dominant target modality; visual and acoustic unimodal representations (X_m ∈ R^d) are mapped into the language distribution via CaReFlow before being concatenated with the language feature and passed through a simple MLP fusion network. At inference, the rectified flow requires only two Euler steps (dt = 0.5) and does not access target-modality features, yielding a causal source-to-target information flow.

## Method

CaReFlow reformulates modality gap reduction as a distribution-mapping problem solved by a drift-force (velocity vector-field) network trained via rectified flow, with three key modifications over vanilla rectified flow. First, a one-to-many mapping strategy samples modality pairs across different samples (β times the same-sample pairs), allowing each source point to observe the global target distribution. Second, adaptive relaxed alignment introduces a margin η that is zero for same-sample pairs (strict alignment) and ε + ‖y_i − y_j‖² for cross-sample pairs (relaxed alignment scaled by label distance), resolving ambiguous flow directions without recursive retraining. Third, a cyclic backward rectified flow maps the forward-flow output back to the original source features, preserving modality-specific information. The drift force V is a simple MLP with sinusoidal positional time embeddings, and the total loss combines the downstream task loss with weighted forward and backward flow losses.

## Results

On CMU-MOSI, CaReFlow achieves 50.6% Acc7 and 89.8% Acc2, outperforming the strongest baseline DLF (49.4% Acc7, 88.7% Acc2) by over 1 point in both metrics. On CMU-MOSEI, CaReFlow obtains the best Acc2 (87.9%), F1 (88.0%), MAE (0.504), and Corr (0.799), with a near-best Acc7 of 55.7% (vs. 55.9% for AtCAF). On CH-SIMS-v2, CaReFlow leads all baselines by a wide margin, reaching 57.9% Acc5 (vs. 53.1% for the next-best KuDA) and 82.9% Acc2 (vs. 80.2%). On the humor and sarcasm detection tasks, CaReFlow surpasses the best baselines (AtCAF on UR-FUNNY, MO-Sarcation on MUStARD) by over 3 and 2.5 points respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On CMU-MOSI, CaReFlow outperforms all 9 baselines in Acc7 and Acc2, beating the strongest SOTA baseline DLF [44] by 1.2 points in Acc7 (50.6 vs. 49.4) and 1.1 points in Acc2 (89.8 vs. 88.7), while also achieving the lowest MAE (0.616 vs. 0.634 for Multimodal Boosting). On CMU-MOSEI, CaReFlow wins in Acc2, F1, MAE, and Corr, but trails AtCAF [15] by 0.2 points in Acc7 (55.7 vs. 55.9), making it the only metric where CaReFlow is not the best. On CH-SIMS-v2, CaReFlow dominates all 7 baselines by a substantial margin, improving Acc5 by 4.8 points over KuDA [9] (57.9 vs. 53.1) and Acc2 by 2.7 points (82.9 vs. 80.2). In the distribution-mapping ablation (Table 4), CaReFlow outperforms the best contrastive-learning baseline CLGSI [53] on all three datasets (e.g., 50.6 vs. 45.8 Acc7 on CMU-MOSI), and also surpasses GAN-based ARGF [27], MulT [41], Deep CCA [1], and Diffusion Bridge [17]. On the humor/sarcasm tasks, CaReFlow beats AtCAF by over 3 points on UR-FUNNY and MO-Sarcation [40] by over 2.5 points on MUStARD, confirming generalizability beyond sentiment analysis.

## Relation to Prior Work

CaReFlow adapts rectified flow (Liu et al., ICLR 2023), originally designed for generative modeling via straight ODE trajectories, to the problem of modality distribution mapping in multimodal affective computing. It departs from prior modality-gap reduction methods (GAN-based translation, diffusion bridges, contrastive learning) that perform one-to-one alignment within each sample, by introducing a one-to-many mapping strategy that exposes source-modality points to the global target distribution. The cyclic consistency component draws on the earlier cyclic-translation idea of Pham et al. (AAAI 2019), but is integrated into the flow framework to prevent information loss during distribution transfer. The adaptive relaxed alignment mechanism is novel, using label-distance-based margins to differentiate alignment strictness across same-sample, same-category, and cross-category pairs, thereby avoiding the recursive retraining that vanilla rectified flow requires for straighter paths.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By reformulating modality gap reduction as a distribution-mapping problem solvable with a single-shot rectified flow (two Euler steps, no recursive training), CaReFlow offers a computationally efficient alternative to diffusion-based or GAN-based alignment that achieves SOTA on five MAC benchmarks with only a concatenation-plus-MLP fusion head. This suggests that the quality of distribution alignment, rather than fusion complexity, is the dominant factor in multimodal performance for affective computing. Open questions include whether the language-as-target assumption generalizes to settings where no single modality dominates, whether the approach extends to more than three modalities or to continuous-valued regression tasks beyond sentiment scoring, and whether the one-to-many mapping strategy transfers to other domains where paired data is scarce (e.g., medical imaging with heterogeneous scanners).
