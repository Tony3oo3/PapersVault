## Problem

Existing multimodal benchmarks assess perception and single-turn reasoning but overlook multi-speaker, dialogue-centric settings that demand agentic reasoning—tracking who speaks, maintaining role continuity, and grounding events across time. No dedicated benchmark or alignment framework exists to evaluate and improve MLLMs' ability to decompose complex audio-visual interactions into planning, grounding, and reflection steps in realistic multi-party conversations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260398/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark uses 10–50 second audio-visual clips containing 2–10 visually and audibly active speakers, paired with diverse text question templates. In guided mode, auxiliary cues (face crops, voice segments, ASR transcripts, lip-sync signals) are explicitly provided per task; in agentic mode, the model must autonomously discover and invoke external tools (Whisper, Pyannote, InsightFace) without scaffolding. Outputs are structured as Plan-Act-Reflect phases yielding speaker identifications, temporal grounding intervals (evaluated via tIoU), dialogue summaries (BLEU/METEOR/CIDEr/GPT-judge), next-speaker predictions, and cross-scene narrative links scored by human-judged coherence.

## Method

RAFT (Reasoning-Acting-Feedback Training) is a model-agnostic alignment framework with two components. Reflective Reward Optimization (RRO) computes sequence-level rewards from four perceptual agents (sync, face, speech, diarization) and applies softmax-weighted reward-weighted regression—replacing GRPO's linear weighting for training stability—alongside a multimodal temporal-coherence loss that penalizes drift between audio, visual, textual, and reflective embeddings. Selective Reasoning Adaptation (SRA) restricts parameter updates to cross-modal inference layers only (rather than generic LoRA), improving interpretability and convergence under limited data. The final objective jointly optimizes structured alignment, temporal coherence, and reflective reward, operating within a Reason-Act-Feedback loop where the model extracts multimodal cues via perception tools and iteratively refines speaker-grounded, temporally consistent responses.

## Results

On the AMUSE benchmark, Qwen3-Omni with RAFT achieves 54.22% on AV Speaker Association, 56.73% on Next Speaker Prediction, and 62.53% on Speaker Re-identification (agentic mode), surpassing GPT-4o's zero-shot scores of 47.18%, 49.18%, and 54.63% respectively. The headline gain is up to 39.52% relative improvement in accuracy on Cross-Scene Narrative Linking for Qwen3-Omni (41.04% agentic → 57.26% agentic w/ RAFT). On AVDS summarization, Qwen3-Omni + RAFT reaches 54.54 BLEU and 6.62 GPT-as-a-Judge score, compared to GPT-4o's 43.52 BLEU zero-shot. Human performance remains substantially higher (e.g., 91.02% AVSA, 95.03% NSP), indicating significant headroom.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Qwen3-Omni with RAFT outperforms GPT-4o (the strongest closed-source baseline) on all three classification tasks: 54.22% vs 47.18% on AVSA (+7.04 pts), 56.73% vs 49.18% on NSP (+7.55 pts), and 62.53% vs 54.63% on SRID (+7.90 pts). Without RAFT, open-source models consistently trail closed-source ones; for example, Qwen3-Omni agentic (46.98% AVSA) underperforms GPT-4o zero-shot (47.18%), but RAFT closes and reverses this gap. Among open-source baselines, Qwen3-Omni + RAFT leads all competitors (e.g., 54.22% AVSA vs Video-SALMONN's 48.18% and CREMA's 45.81%). In the optimization ablation (Fig. 5), RAFT outperforms PPO, DPO, and GRPO on the STG task, with GRPO offering moderate gains while PPO and DPO lag due to weaker multimodal feedback signals. On AVDS summarization (Table 3), Qwen3-Omni + RAFT achieves 54.54 BLEU and 6.62 GPT-score, exceeding GPT-4o's zero-shot 43.52 BLEU, while GPT-4o still leads in guided mode (49.21 BLEU). Overall, RAFT acts as a model-agnostic enhancer that enables smaller open-source models to rival or surpass larger closed-source systems in multi-speaker agentic reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reward-weighted regression framework (Peters & Schaal, 2007) and GRPO-style group reward optimization (DeepSeekMath) into a multimodal agentic setting, replacing GRPO's linear reward weighting with a softmax formulation for training stability. Methodologically, RAFT's Selective Reasoning Adaptation is a constrained LoRA variant restricted to cross-modal inference layers, departing from generic parameter-efficient fine-tuning by targeting only the reasoning pathways. Conceptually, the paper positions itself against two gaps: (1) existing multi-speaker benchmarks (AVA, VoxConverse, AMI, Friends-MMC) evaluate perception-only tasks without structured reasoning, and (2) existing agentic evaluations (AgentBench, Agent-X) operate in text or tool-use domains rather than multi-party audio-visual dialogue. The three-mode evaluation protocol (zero-shot, guided, agentic) draws on the broader trend of measuring model autonomy but is the first to apply it systematically to multi-speaker audio-visual understanding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AMUSE is the first benchmark to explicitly require agentic reasoning (plan-act-reflect) over multi-speaker audio-visual content, filling a clear gap between perception-focused video benchmarks and dialogue-centric language evaluations. The consistent finding that model performance degrades with increasing autonomy—especially on temporal grounding and cross-scene narrative linking—highlights that current MLLMs rely on external scaffolding rather than internal temporal modeling, a limitation with direct implications for deploying these models as meeting assistants or conversational agents. RAFT's ability to close the open-vs-closed-source gap on this benchmark suggests agentic multi-speaker understanding is learnable with targeted alignment rather than requiring proprietary scale, though generalizability beyond AMUSE's 2,100 samples remains untested. Open questions include whether the softmax-over-GRPO modification and selective adapter strategy transfer to other agentic domains (embodied, long-horizon planning) and whether the benchmark's 10–50s clip length captures the full complexity of real multi-hour meetings.
