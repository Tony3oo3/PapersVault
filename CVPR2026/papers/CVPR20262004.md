## Problem

Existing CIL methods attribute the prediction bias toward new classes solely to intra-task class imbalance between old and new classes, correcting only at the classifier head. This paper identifies an overlooked factor—temporal imbalance—where earlier classes accumulate stronger negative supervision over the training process because their positive samples were seen earlier, leading to asymmetric precision and recall even among old classes with equal sample counts. No prior work has formally modeled this temporal dimension of supervision imbalance or provided a loss-level remedy.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-100, 224×224-scale for ImageNet-100 and Food101) and outputs a class label via a standard softmax classifier. The key operational detail is that TAL is a drop-in replacement for the cross-entropy loss during sequential task training: it requires no architectural changes, no additional data, and only tracks a per-class scalar Q_k updated in O(1) per step, adding roughly 0.8% computational overhead. The method is plug-and-play and can be combined with any existing CIL framework (iCaRL, DER, MEMO, etc.) that uses a cross-entropy-style loss.

## Method

TAL introduces a per-class temporal positive supervision strength Q_k[N], computed as a discrete convolution of the class's supervision polarity sequence (+1 for positive, −1 for negative) with an exponential decay kernel f[n] = λ^(n+1), updated recursively in O(1) as Q_k[N+1] = λ(Q_k[N] + a_k[N]). The TAL loss modifies cross-entropy by multiplying each non-true-class logit term e^{z_k} by a weight w(Q_k[N]) = (Q_k[N]/Q_max)^r, so classes with weak recent positive supervision (small Q_k) become less sensitive to negative pressure, while well-reinforced classes retain full sensitivity. A frequency alignment parameter α, determined analytically from the number of classes C and exponent r, guarantees that TAL degenerates exactly to standard cross-entropy under temporally uniform, class-balanced conditions. The Q update rule is also modified so that negative supervision is attenuated by w(·) in the recursion, keeping the tracked state consistent with the loss. Theoretically, the paper proves that under equal total positive sample counts, the class whose positives arrive later attains a strictly larger Q at the end of training (Theorem 1), formalizing the temporal imbalance that drives the precision-recall asymmetry observed empirically across baselines.

## Results

On the 10-task setting, TagFex+TAL achieves the best average accuracy (AMean) across all three datasets: 65.97% on CIFAR-100, 59.55% on ImageNet-100, and 54.73% on Food101. DER+TAL follows closely with 63.53% (CIFAR-100), 50.34% (ImageNet-100), and 52.25% (Food101). Notably, even the simplest baseline iCaRL+TAL (58.76% AMean on CIFAR-100 10-task) surpasses FOSTER+TAL and MEMO+TAL on ImageNet-100 (10-task) and Food101 (10-task), demonstrating that TAL's temporal reweighting provides substantial gains independent of the underlying CIL method's complexity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TAL is evaluated as a plug-and-play loss replacement integrated into five CIL baselines: iCaRL, FOSTER, DER, MEMO, and TagFex. Across all three datasets and both 10-task and 20-task configurations, TAL consistently improves every baseline, with the largest relative gains observed for simpler methods. On ImageNet-100 (10-task), iCaRL+TAL (55.98% AMean) surpasses both FOSTER+TAL (47.34%) and MEMO+TAL (48.93%), confirming the paper's claim that temporal reweighting can compensate for architectural sophistication. TagFex+TAL achieves the strongest overall performance, reaching 65.97% AMean on CIFAR-100 (10-task) and 54.73% on Food101, outperforming DER+TAL by 2.44 and 2.48 points respectively. DER+TAL shows particularly strong gains on Food101 (52.25% AMean), where its dynamically expandable representation benefits most from reduced negative-suppression pressure on early classes. The ablation (Table 2) further shows that TAL outperforms vanilla cross-entropy (59.96% AMean) across a broad range of \u03bb and r values, with the best configuration (\u03bb=0.995, r=1.0) reaching 63.36% AMean on CIFAR-100 10-task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant CIL framing that attributes prediction bias to intra-task class imbalance between new and old classes, arguing instead that the temporal ordering of positive supervision across the full training history is an independent and overlooked cause. It repurposes the exponential decay mechanism well-established in time-series forecasting (Holt-Winters), reinforcement learning (eligibility traces, TD(λ)), and online drift detection (ADWIN), but applies it at the loss-function level to model per-class supervision strength in CIL—a setting where, to the authors' knowledge, no prior work has explicitly modeled positive-negative supervision temporally. The resulting Temporal-Adjusted Loss (TAL) is designed as a plug-and-play replacement for cross-entropy that composes with existing CIL frameworks (iCaRL, FOSTER, DER, MEMO, TagFex) rather than replacing their architectural or regularization components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By identifying temporal imbalance as a distinct driver of forgetting—evidenced by the pervasive precision-recall asymmetry where earlier classes gain precision but lose recall even under balanced class counts—the paper opens a new axis of analysis for CIL that is orthogonal to the well-studied class-imbalance axis. The practical impact is notable: a simple O(1)-per-step loss modification yields consistent gains across three datasets and five baselines without architectural changes, and the theoretical guarantee that TAL degenerates to standard CE under balanced conditions makes it safe to adopt broadly. Key open questions include whether the fixed exponential decay (single λ) is sufficient for long-horizon or non-stationary settings, whether λ should adapt per-task or per-class, and whether the approach extends naturally to other continual learning paradigms (domain-incremental, task-incremental) or to non-CNN backbones beyond the brief ViT experiments mentioned in the appendix.
