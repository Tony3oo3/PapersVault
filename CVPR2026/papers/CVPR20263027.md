## Problem

Cyclic manipulation tasks—where a robot must repeat an action a specified number of times and stop at the correct moment—are common in daily life but remain largely unexplored. Existing imitation policies rely on short observation windows, causing them to confuse similar observations across cycles and either loop indefinitely or terminate prematurely. Additionally, no benchmark with sufficient data and automatic evaluation tools existed to assess methods on cyclic tasks.

## Input / Output

The policy takes a natural language command (e.g., 'shake the bottle three times'), a set of historical high-overhead visual observations (RGB images or point clouds, sampled sparsely via a heuristic binary+exponential strategy with K_high=6 frames), and a dense sequence of low-overhead proprioceptive observations (end-effector pose differences over all past timesteps). It outputs an 8-step action horizon predicted via a diffusion model (DDIM with 10 inference steps). The key design choice is that the observation window is non-Markovian: the policy explicitly conditions on the full history of proprioceptive signals and a curated subset of visual frames to track cycle progression.

## Method

CycleManip is an end-to-end imitation framework built on a diffusion policy backbone with two core mechanisms. First, a cost-aware sampling strategy differentiates observation types: all past low-overhead proprioceptive signals (end-effector pose differences) are densely encoded by a Transformer, while high-overhead visual frames are sparsely sampled using right-sided binary sampling (0.5·K_high frames) plus exponential sampling from the latest frame (t−2^k), keeping the total frame count constant. Second, a multi-task learning objective adds an auxiliary 10-way classification head that predicts the current task progress (current frame / max frame, discretized into bins), forcing the shared feature encoder to learn progression-discriminative representations. The fused observation and CLIP-encoded language features condition a diffusion model via FiLM layers, trained jointly with MSE (action) and cross-entropy (progress) losses, requiring no extra models or hierarchical structure.

## Results

On the CycleManip benchmark (8 cyclic tasks, 100 trials each), CycleManip achieves success rates of 53–97% across tasks, dramatically outperforming all baselines (best baseline DP3 reaches only 1–48%). The average cycle count deviation is 0.25–0.81 for CycleManip versus 1.41–5.55 for DP3, confirming superior cycle-progress tracking. On the RoboTwin 2.0 general manipulation benchmark, CycleManip also leads all baselines (e.g., 91% on Place Cans vs. 48% for DP3), demonstrating that the historical perception/understanding components generalize beyond cyclic tasks. In plug-and-play experiments, integrating CycleManip's sampling into Pi-0 boosts success from 19% to 72% on bottle shaking.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CycleManip outperforms all four baselines by large margins on every cyclic task. DP (Diffusion Policy) is the weakest baseline, achieving only 0–25% success and the highest cycle deviations (up to 8.33), because its short observation window cannot distinguish cycle phases. DP3 (3D Diffusion Policy) is the strongest baseline, reaching up to 48% on dual-knife chopping, but still trails CycleManip by 42–94 percentage points and shows 3–20× higher cycle deviation. RDT-1B performs moderately (12–42% success) but fails on morse tapping (0%) and chemical mixing (12%), with cycle deviations of 1.24–2.31. Pi-0, which uses only a single-frame observation, is the worst on most tasks (1–19% success) and cannot complete morse tapping or chemical mixing at all. The gap is most dramatic on roller rolling (97% vs. 35% for RDT) and bottle shaking (95% vs. 19% for Pi-0), where repetitive visual states make short-horizon policies particularly vulnerable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Diffusion Policy paradigm for action prediction but departs from standard imitation learning by explicitly addressing the non-Markovian nature of cyclic tasks, where short observation windows cause the policy to lose track of cycle progression. It introduces a cost-aware sampling strategy that differentiates between high-overhead visual observations (sparse heuristic sampling) and low-overhead proprioceptive signals (dense sampling), contrasting with prior historical-modeling work that either uses uniform long windows or retrieval-based memory. The multi-task learning objective for cycle-stage prediction is a lightweight addition that avoids the hierarchical or auxiliary-model architectures used in prior cyclic-task approaches, and the framework is designed to be plug-and-play compatible with VLA models like Pi-0.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Cyclic manipulation (shaking, hammering, pumping) is ubiquitous in household and industrial settings yet has lacked a systematic benchmark and evaluation protocol; this paper fills that gap with the CycleManip benchmark on RoboTwin 2.0 and an automatic cycle-counting evaluator. The method's low computational overhead and embodiment-agnostic design (grippers, dexterous hands, humanoid) make it practically deployable, and the plug-and-play compatibility with VLA models suggests it could be adopted as a general module. Open questions include whether the approach scales to tasks with variable cycle durations, more complex multi-object cyclic interactions, and whether the 10-bin progress discretization is sufficient for tasks requiring finer temporal resolution.
