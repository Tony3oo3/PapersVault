## Problem

Existing incremental learning methods largely ignore explicit computational budget constraints, relying on fixed replay ratios or complex regularization that incur heavy overhead. The paper identifies that a fixed replay ratio causes imbalanced convergence between new-task and memory samples under limited epochs, and asks: how should computational resources be dynamically allocated between learning new tasks and rehearsing memory within a fixed budget?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a new-task dataset D_t (n samples), a memory buffer M_t holding δ-fraction samples from all prior tasks, and a fixed computational budget U_t = U_base[1+ϑ(t−1)] that caps total sample-processing operations. It produces updated model parameters θ_t and a per-mini-batch replay ratio schedule {λ_r}_{r=0}^{R−1}, where each mini-batch of size b is partitioned into λ_r·b memory samples and (1−λ_r)·b new-task samples. The ratio is updated every z steps using gradient statistics from the preceding interval, making the scheduling overhead negligible relative to the training budget.

## Method

Smart Replay casts replay-ratio scheduling as a finite-horizon optimal control problem: the training process is a discrete-time system with state (l_r^M, l_r^D, r) and control λ_r, and the objective is to maximize cumulative loss reduction over R mini-batches. A heuristic Q-function approximates the Bellman value by combining (i) time-adaptive exponential weights w_r^M/w_r^D that encode expected long-term convergence rates, (ii) short-term gradient-norm ratios ‖∇L_r^M‖/‖∇L_r^D‖, (iii) the cosine of the angle between task gradients capturing synergy or conflict, and (iv) a smoothness penalty on adjacent λ_r values. First-order Taylor linearization of the loss dynamics yields a closed-form quadratic in λ_r, solved analytically at each update step. In practice, full-batch gradients are replaced by z-step moving averages of per-mini-batch gradients, and λ_r is refreshed every z steps, producing a 'weak-to-strong' trajectory that starts with low replay for plasticity and ramps up for stability.

## Results

Under the limited-memory setting (δ=0.1) with the most constrained budget (θ=0), Smart Replay achieves 65.43% average accuracy on CIFAR-100 (iCaRL), 46.60% on Tiny-ImageNet (iCaRL), and 91.43% on Rotated-MNIST (STAR), consistently outperforming the best fixed-ratio baseline by 0.3–1.9 percentage points. The advantage grows substantially under tighter budgets: on Tiny-ImageNet with θ=0.1, Smart Replay (iCaRL) reaches 49.08% versus 44.80% for the best fixed λ, a gap of over 4 points. Under the unlimited-memory setting (δ=1), Smart Replay similarly leads across all datasets, e.g., 74.70% on CIFAR-100 (ER) and 53.94% on Tiny-ImageNet (ER) at θ=0.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Smart Replay is compared against three types of baselines: (1) Union sampling (no explicit replay ratio control), (2) fixed replay ratios (λ=0.1–0.4 for limited memory; λ=0.2–0.8 for unlimited), and (3) linear heuristics that ramp λ up (0.2→0.8) or down (0.8→0.2). Smart Replay wins on nearly all dataset–method–budget combinations. On Tiny-ImageNet (limited memory, θ=0), the gap is largest: Smart Replay with iCaRL scores 46.60% versus 44.75% for the best linear heuristic (λ↑) and 42.46% for Union, a 4.1-point margin. On CIFAR-100 (limited memory, θ=0), Smart Replay with iCaRL (65.43%) edges out the best fixed/linear baseline (65.16%, λ↑) by 0.27 points, while with MEMO it leads by 2.19 points over the best fixed ratio (63.38%, λ↑). On the simpler Rotated-MNIST task, gains are modest (≤0.5 points) because the task is less prone to forgetting. Under the unlimited-memory setting, Smart Replay consistently leads by 0.5–2.5 points over the best fixed ratio, with the largest margins on Tiny-ImageNet (e.g., 53.94% vs. 53.73% for λ=0.8 with ER at θ=0; 54.53% vs. 53.55% for λ=0.6 with iCaRL). The linear-decreasing heuristic (λ↓) is consistently the worst dynamic baseline, confirming that a naive 'start high, end low' schedule is suboptimal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of memory-based incremental learning (ER, iCaRL, MEMO, STAR) and data-efficient training (curriculum learning, adaptive data scheduling), but its core contribution is importing optimal control theory into the replay scheduling problem. Prior replay scheduling work (Klasson et al. 2023; Seale Smith et al. 2024) operated at the task or cluster level, while this paper moves to per-mini-batch granularity and formulates the problem via a Bellman-equation-based Q-function. The CRIL setting explicitly introduces a computational budget constraint that prior IL work treated as implicit, and the heuristic Q-function (exponential decay of losses) repurposes a standard optimal control tool in a novel domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical relevance is clear: real-world deployment systems (recommendation engines, edge devices) face hard compute budgets where models must update daily or hourly, yet prior IL methods assumed sufficient compute. The finding that a simple adaptive replay schedule yields 2–5% gains over the best fixed ratio across four IL methods and three datasets makes this a drop-in improvement for practitioners. Key limitations include the reliance on a fixed set of hyperparameters (Δρ, τ) across all stages, the heuristic nature of the Q-function's exponential decay assumption, and the observation that gradient estimation degrades under parameter freezing (MEMO). Open questions include whether the approach extends to true online IL (single-pass), whether hyperparameters can be auto-tuned per stage, and whether the optimal control framing generalizes to other resource-allocation decisions (e.g., which layers to update).
