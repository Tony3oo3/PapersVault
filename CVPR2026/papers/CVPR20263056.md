## Problem

Existing vision-language-action models either entangle perception and control in end-to-end architectures, encode actions in latent spaces without explicit semantics, or rely on coarse discrete language primitives that lack fine-grained motion parameters. This leaves a persistent gap between high-level semantic understanding and precise low-level motor execution, preventing models from reusing shared motion structures (e.g., reaching, grasping, rotating) across semantically related tasks and limiting cross-task generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263056/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a 256×256 RGB image and a natural language instruction as input, and produces a continuous 7-DoF end-effector action (3D translation, 3D rotation, binary gripper state) as output. During pretraining on Open X-Embodiment (~22.5M frames), each action is automatically decomposed into three language-grounded primitives with structured descriptions (e.g., 'move 0.5 meters forward, rotate 90 degrees around the z-axis, close the gripper'). At inference, the model directly outputs continuous actions conditioned on the image-instruction pair without requiring explicit primitive labels, using a lightweight MLP action head fine-tuned with L1 trajectory regression.

## Method

LaDA decomposes each 7-DoF action into three interpretable, language-grounded primitives—translation, rotation, and gripper control—each mapped to a linguistic template, providing explicit semantic structure for low-level motion. A dual-path soft-label contrastive objective then aligns multimodal embeddings: visual features are fused with language via FiLM conditioning and projected through an MLP adapter, while a soft-label similarity matrix S (constructed from binary match matrices across primitive dimensions) provides graded affinity weights for a weighted InfoNCE loss on both action-action and action-primitive pairs. An adaptive weighting mechanism using moving-average estimates of each loss dynamically balances the contrastive and imitation objectives throughout training, preventing either signal from dominating. The full pipeline uses a ViT-L/14 CLIP backbone (0.6B parameters), pretrained on OXE and fine-tuned per task, achieving 93.6% average success on LIBERO and 67% on MimicGen with strong cross-task and similar-task generalization.

## Results

On the LIBERO benchmark, LaDA achieves a 93.6% average success rate across four task suites (Spatial: 95.2%, Object: 99.2%, Goal: 93.6%, Long: 86.4%), outperforming all 17 baselines including CLIP-RT (93.1%) and FlowVLA (88.1%), while using only 0.6B parameters versus CLIP-RT's 1.3B. On MimicGen, LaDA attains a 67% average success rate across nine contact-rich manipulation tasks, surpassing Phoenix (58%) and CLIP-RT* (51%). In cross-task generalization, LaDA reaches 12.3% success on unseen instructions where CLIP-RT* achieves 0%, demonstrating the benefit of language-grounded primitive-level semantics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LaDA (93.6% avg) edges out CLIP-RT (93.1%) on LIBERO overall while using roughly half the parameters (0.6B vs. 1.3B), with the gap widening on the Long suite (86.4% vs. 83.8%). Against the next-best baselines FlowVLA (88.1%) and MolmoAct (86.6%), LaDA leads by 5.5 and 7.0 percentage points respectively. On MimicGen, LaDA (67% avg) outperforms Phoenix (58%) by 9 points and CLIP-RT* (51%) by 16 points, with particularly large gains on multi-step tasks like StackThree_D1 (71% vs. 20% for Phoenix). In cross-task generalization, LaDA achieves 12.3% success on entirely unseen instructions where CLIP-RT* scores 0%, confirming that language-grounded primitive decomposition enables transfer beyond the training distribution. Among end-to-end VLA baselines (OpenVLA 76.5%, Octo 75.1%) and latent-action methods (LAPA 65.7%, UniACT 64.5%), LaDA leads by 17–29 points, underscoring the advantage of explicit semantic structure over entangled or purely latent representations.

## Relation to Prior Work

LaDA carves new ground by synthesizing ideas from three existing paradigms—end-to-end VLA models, latent action learning, and language-conditioned policies—into a unified framework that none of them individually provides. Its key conceptual departure is the introduction of a fine-grained, language-grounded intermediate layer (translation, rotation, gripper primitives) that replaces both the entangled end-to-end mapping of VLAs and the semantically opaque latent spaces of methods like LAPA. The soft-label contrastive objective, which assigns continuous affinity weights rather than binary positive/negative pairs, extends standard InfoNCE-based alignment to capture graded semantic relationships among motion primitives, a formulation not present in prior language-conditioned policies like CLIP-RT or Phoenix that rely on coarse discrete tokens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LaDA addresses a practical bottleneck in VLA research: the inability to reuse shared motion structure across tasks, which leads to redundant learning and poor generalization. The demonstration that a 0.6B-parameter model with structured primitive decomposition can match or exceed 7B+ end-to-end VLAs on LIBERO suggests that explicit semantic structure in the action space is a more data-efficient inductive bias than raw scale. However, real-world validation is limited to a single pick-and-place task on one robot, and the cross-task generalization numbers (12.3% on unseen tasks) remain modest, leaving open whether the approach scales to dexterous multi-fingered manipulation, multi-arm coordination, or substantially longer horizons.
