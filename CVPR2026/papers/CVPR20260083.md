## Problem

Existing world models (video generators, latent-space simulators) predict dense pixel or feature representations of entire scenes, expending most of their capacity on appearance rather than the sparse trajectories that govern scene dynamics. This 'visual tax' makes large-scale exploration of future hypotheses—thousands of counterfactual rollouts from a single image—prohibitively expensive and limits long-horizon, multi-modal motion prediction. The paper asks whether future scene dynamics can be predicted directly over sparse point trajectories, step by step, without ever rendering a single future frame.

## Input / Output

The model takes a single 512×512 RGB reference frame, a user-specified set of K=16 2D query points, and optionally a 'poke' hint (the motion over the first two frames) that conditions the initial trajectory direction. It outputs a distribution over 2D point trajectories for T=16 timesteps (≈2.5–6.5 s of video), sampled by solving a flow-matching ODE at each autoregressive step. The formulation assumes a static camera, so all coordinates live in the same 2D image plane; the model never produces pixel values, only (x, y) positions per point per timestep.

## Method

The core architecture is an autoregressive transformer (665 M params, L-scale) that factorizes the joint trajectory distribution causally over time and, within each step, over the K trajectories, enabling KV-cached fast decoding. Each motion token fuses three signals via a small MLP: appearance features bilinearly sampled from a DINOv3-L/16 image encoder at the point's origin, local context features at its current position, and a Fourier-embedded motion increment (or zero vector for query tokens), plus a random unit-sphere trajectory ID for identity. The backbone uses 'Fast Reasoning Blocks'—parallel transformer layers with a single residual, fused QKV/FFN projections, and a prefix layout that merges self- and cross-attention into one masked kernel call—yielding ~2× faster sampling than standard blocks. The posterior over each step's motion increment is modeled by a lightweight flow-matching head (3 FFN layers with adaptive-norm conditioning on τ and the backbone's compact representation z), replacing the GMM heads used in prior work; a cascade of logarithmically spaced tanh-saturated scales stabilizes the head against the heavy-tailed (excess kurtosis in the hundreds) distribution of real-world motion. Training is end-to-end supervised with teacher forcing on 10 M in-the-wild video clips using TAPNext pseudo-ground-truth tracks, with a separate variant trained on V-DPM 3D tracks projected to a static view.

## Results

On the OWM benchmark, the proposed method achieves a min ADE of 0.029 (Best-of-5) and 0.013 (Best-within-5min), outperforming all video generation baselines (best competitor: MAGI-1 at 0.037 and 0.066 respectively). Under the time-constrained Best-within-5min setting, the gap widens dramatically: the method reaches 0.013 while the next-best method (MAGI-1) only achieves 0.066, and several baselines (Wan2.2, CogVideo-X 1.5) fail to complete (DNF). On the billiard planning task, the method achieves 78% accuracy at 496.4 actions/min, compared to 16% for the best image-to-video diffusion baseline and 4% for the one-shot Flow Poke Transformer, approaching the 84% simulator oracle.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms all five video generation baselines (MAGI-1, Wan2.2, CogVideo-X 1.5, SkyReels V2, SVD 1.1) on OWM in both evaluation settings, with the advantage growing from ~20% (Best-5) to ~5× (Best-5min) under time constraints, where several video models fail to complete. On PhysicsIQ, the method is competitive in Best-5 (0.115 vs. CogVideo-X's 0.100, the only baseline that beats it) but dominates in Best-5min (0.045 vs. 0.137–0.241 for others). On Physion, the method wins in both settings (0.048/0.020 vs. 0.061–0.070/0.081–0.147). For the billiard planning task, the method (78%) substantially surpasses all video-based baselines (8–16%) and the one-shot Flow Poke Transformer (4%), while the full-trajectory diffusion baseline (8%) also underperforms due to its inability to unroll step-by-step. The method's 2200 samples/min throughput is 3,000–40,000× faster than video models, enabling orders of magnitude more hypothesis exploration within the same compute budget.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of dense video or latent-space world models (Sora, Genie, Wan, CogVideo) by arguing they incur a 'visual tax'—expending capacity on pixel-level appearance irrelevant to motion dynamics. It also moves beyond single-shot trajectory prediction (Flow Poke Transformer, Track2Act) by formulating motion prediction as autoregressive, step-wise diffusion over sparse user-defined point trajectories, and beyond physics-engine-based simulators (Galileo, PhysGen) by learning dynamics purely data-driven in an open-set regime. The flow matching posterior head builds directly on Lipman et al. [65] and the autoregressive continuous-token generation of Li et al. [60], while the image backbone leverages DINOv3 features. The OWM benchmark extends prior physical-prediction evaluations (PhysicsIQ, Physion) to genuinely open-world, in-the-wild settings under a static-camera assumption.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The orders-of-magnitude throughput advantage (2200 vs. <1 samples/min for video models) makes large-scale counterfactual exploration—thousands of rollouts from a single image—practically feasible for the first time, as demonstrated in the zero-shot billiard planning task. The conceptual reframing (model sparse trajectories, not pixels) suggests a broader principle: for dynamics-centric tasks, dense appearance generation is unnecessary overhead, potentially reshaping how world models are architected. Key limitations include the static-camera assumption (no ego-motion), reliance on pseudo ground-truth from off-the-shelf trackers, and 2D-only formulation; extending to dynamic viewpoints and 3D remains an important open direction.
