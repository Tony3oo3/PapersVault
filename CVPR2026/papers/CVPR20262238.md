## Problem

Existing sketch evaluation metrics (FID, SSIM, LPIPS, Top-K accuracy) measure pixel-level similarity or categorical recognizability but cannot capture the defining property of sketches: deliberate abstraction in which only a small subset of visually diagnostic elements is retained. No prior dataset or metric supports element-level reasoning about which class-specific visual components are present or omitted in a sketch, leaving abstraction efficiency unquantified.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262238/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The SEA metric takes a 512×512 black-on-white sketch image and its class label as input. The class label indexes a per-class commonsense element set (extracted by an LLM), and a VLM-based VQA module performs binary yes/no queries for each element to determine which are visually present. The output is a bounded score in (−1, 1), where higher values indicate sketches that preserve recognizability with fewer expressed elements; the pipeline also yields per-element presence annotations and a natural-language caption for each sketch.

## Method

SEA decomposes abstraction efficiency into a reward term and a penalty term operating on two signals: a zero-shot classifier prediction probability P and a normalized visual ratio v = V/E (fraction of commonsense elements detected as present). The reward P^γ · log((1+δ)/(v+δ)) · tanh(β/2 · log((P+δ)/(v+δ))) rewards high recognizability with minimal elements, with a centered gate that amplifies reward when v < P and suppresses it when v > P. The penalty λv^η(1−P)^k + τ(1−P)^r penalizes over-drawing relative to recognizability and baseline unidentifiability. The final score SEA = tanh(αZ) with Z = reward − penalty is smooth, differentiable, and interpretable. To ground the element space, the authors introduce CommonSketch (23,100 human-drawn sketches, 300 classes, 14 categories) with GPT-4o-extracted commonsense elements validated by human binary annotation, enabling reference-free, element-aware evaluation without requiring a reference image.

## Results

On the CommonSketch element-level commonsense VQA benchmark, GPT-4o achieves the best overall performance with F1 of 0.881 and accuracy of 0.855, while Molmo attains the highest recall (0.949) but suffers from severe false-positive bias, and Qwen2.5-VL achieves the highest precision (0.898) among open-source models. SEA scores on SEVA increase monotonically with abstraction level, from −0.56 ± 0.32 at Level 4 to 0.43 ± 0.29 at Level 32, confirming the metric's sensitivity to abstraction efficiency. Human agreement with SEA's rank-ordering reaches 87.8% (closed-source pipeline) and 88.0% (open-source pipeline) across 160 ranking questions. For commonsense element extraction, GPT-OSS 20B most closely matches GPT-4o with Soft F1 of 0.850 and CLIP Score of 0.941.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o dominates the element-level VQA benchmark, achieving the best F1 (0.881) and accuracy (0.855), outperforming the strongest open-source model Molmo (F1 0.867, Acc 0.812) by 1.4 F1 points and 4.3 accuracy points. Molmo's anomalously high recall (0.949) reveals a severe false-positive bias that undermines its reliability for quantifying visual abstraction, whereas Qwen2.5-VL offers the best precision (0.898) among open-source models and is selected as the open-source VLM for the SEA pipeline. SmolVLM (500M) performs worst overall (F1 0.485, Acc 0.526), with a critically low recall of 0.346, indicating it misses most visual elements. PaliGemma2 (3B) also lags significantly (F1 0.735), while LLaVA and BLIP occupy the mid-range (F1 0.782 and 0.746 respectively). For commonsense element extraction (Table 4), GPT-OSS 20B leads open-source LLMs with Soft F1 0.850 and CLIP Score 0.941, narrowly ahead of Qwen2.5 32B (0.833/0.930), Llama 3 8B (0.828/0.935), and Mistral 7B (0.834/0.933), confirming that larger open-source models closely approximate GPT-4o's extraction quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of sketch understanding and commonsense reasoning, departing from the dominant paradigm of treating sketches as classification or retrieval problems. Prior sketch datasets (TU-Berlin, QuickDraw, Sketchy, SEVA) provide class labels or sketch-photo pairs but lack element-level semantic annotations, while existing evaluation metrics (FID, SSIM, LPIPS, GACL, SketchRef) measure pixel similarity or classification accuracy rather than abstraction quality. The paper repurposes LLMs for commonsense element extraction and VLMs for element-presence VQA, combining these into a reference-free evaluation pipeline (SEA) that has no direct predecessor in the sketch literature. The closest prior work, SEVA, also studies abstraction levels but relies on sketch-photo pairs and does not support element-level reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CommonSketch and SEA together establish the first element-aware benchmark and metric for sketch abstraction, shifting the field from label-centric evaluation toward reasoning about which visual components are retained under simplification. The VLM comparison (Table 3) reveals systematic weaknesses in element-level commonsense reasoning across current vision-language models, providing a concrete diagnostic tool for the community. Key limitations include dependence on the quality of underlying VQA and classification models, restriction to single-object sketches, and limited linguistic/cultural coverage. Open questions include whether SEA can serve as a differentiable training signal for sketch generation, whether it generalizes to multi-object or scene-level sketches, and whether the commonsense element sets transfer across cultural contexts.
