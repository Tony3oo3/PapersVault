## Problem

Existing soft effect removal methods (lens flare, haze, shadow, reflection) are addressed by isolated specialist models that lack scalability and fail to generalize to diverse in-the-wild conditions, while generalist text-driven editors (GPT-4o, Flux Kontext, Nano Banana) cannot achieve robust fine-grained restoration without altering scene identity. The paper identifies that all these degradations share the intrinsic property of semi-transparent occlusion and unifies them into a single Soft Effects Removal (SER) task to be solved by one foundation model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263826/cropped_page0_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a degraded RGB image, a short text prompt specifying the effect type (e.g., "remove haze"), an optional spatial mask (arbitrary user-drawn binary or soft-valued region), and a continuous removal strength scalar α ∈ [0,1]. It outputs a pixel-aligned restored image where effects are removed only within the masked region to the specified degree, while unmasked regions remain identical. By swapping input and target roles, the same framework can add or enhance effects on clean images, making it bidirectional.

## Method

UniSER fine-tunes a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) within a latent diffusion framework: a VAE encodes the degraded image to latent space, a text encoder produces task-specific embeddings, and both are concatenated with the noisy target latent as a sequence processed by full-attention DiT blocks. Training is supervised with MSE on predicted noise over a curated 3.8M-pair dataset spanning four effect types, augmented with novel physically-plausible synthetic data (HALO for flares, LR-SRD for shadows, SYN-HAZE for haze). A random masking strategy synthesizes diverse binary masks (rectangles, free-form strokes) during training so the model generalizes to arbitrary user-drawn regions, while a soft-valued mask αM conditions the DiT to learn continuous strength control. Joint-task learning across all four degradation types consistently outperforms single-task training, and a generic "remove effects" prompt combined with an auxiliary clean-image inpainting task enables zero-shot generalization to unseen degradations like rain and stains.

## Results

On the in-the-wild no-reference evaluation (Table 2), UniSER achieves the highest scores across all four tasks: LIQE 2.82/3.78/3.52/2.23 and QwenQA 60.0/65.0/92.7/75.6 for haze, shadow, lens flare, and reflection respectively, outperforming generalist models (Nano Banana, FLUX Kontext, Seedream 4.0) by 10–30+ points on QwenQA. On full-reference benchmarks (Table 3), UniSER attains the best PSNR on Flare7K (27.34 dB), HSTS (32.17 dB), WSRD+ (26.91 dB), SRD (34.16 dB), SIR² (25.98 dB), and Nature20 (24.17 dB), while matching or slightly trailing the best specialist on SSIM for ISTD+ (0.964 vs. 0.970 for StableShadowDiff) and SOTS (0.955 vs. 0.939 for MSFNet, where UniSER actually wins on SSIM). The joint-task learning ablation confirms consistent gains over single-task models, e.g., +1.04 dB PSNR on SIR² (27.44 vs. 26.40).

## Baselines & Comparisons

UniSER outperforms all specialist baselines on PSNR across every benchmark: +0.36 dB over Uformer on Flare7K, +1.14 dB over MSFNet on HSTS, +0.65 dB over StableShadowDiff on WSRD+, and +0.80 dB over L-DiffER on SIR². On SSIM, UniSER ties L-DiffER on SIR² (0.911) and slightly trails StableShadowDiff on ISTD+ (0.964 vs. 0.970) and L-DiffER on Nature20 (0.812 vs. 0.831), but leads on all other benchmarks. Against generalist models (Nano Banana, FLUX Kontext, Seedream 4.0, GPT-4o), UniSER achieves substantially higher QwenQA scores on in-the-wild data—e.g., 92.7 vs. 73.6 (Seedream 4.0) for lens flare and 75.6 vs. 56.7 (Nano Banana) for reflection—while also producing positive contrast gain where generalists often degrade contrast. The joint-task learning ablation shows UniSER (JTL) beats single-task models (STL) on all four tasks, with the largest gain on reflection (+1.04 dB PSNR, +0.042 SSIR on SIR²), confirming that shared semi-transparent-occlusion representations transfer across degradation types.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the long tradition of task-specific restoration models (DCP for haze, ShadowFormer for shadows, Difflare for lens flares, L-DiffER for reflections) by arguing that all soft effects share a common intrinsic property—semi-transparent occlusion—and can therefore be addressed by a single unified model. Architecturally, it repurposes the Diffusion Transformer (DiT) and VAE stack popularized by recent generative models, drawing inspiration from UniReal's formulation of restoration as discontinuous frame generation in latent space. The key conceptual departure from both specialist and generalist (GPT-4o, FLUX Kontext, Nano Banana) approaches is a data-centric strategy: rather than designing novel architectures or relying on text prompts, UniSER curates a 3.8M-pair dataset spanning four degradation types and introduces fine-grained mask and strength controls that neither prior paradigm provides.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The release of a 3.8M-pair unified dataset (including novel HALO, LR-SRD, and SYN-HAZE subsets) addresses a critical data bottleneck that has limited generalization in specialist restoration models, and the controllable (mask + strength) interface makes the model directly usable in professional photo-editing pipelines where pixel-level fidelity is non-negotiable. The zero-shot generalization to unseen degradations (rain, stains) suggests the learned 'semi-transparent occlusion' representation may transfer beyond the four training tasks, though this is demonstrated only qualitatively. Key limitations include high computational cost for both training and inference, and the reliance on paired supervision that constrains applicability to effects where clean references can be synthesized or captured. Open questions include whether the framework extends to video, whether the DiT backbone is truly necessary versus lighter alternatives, and how the model behaves on effects that are not semi-transparent (e.g., hard occlusions).
