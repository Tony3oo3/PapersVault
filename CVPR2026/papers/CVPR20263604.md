## Problem

Existing t-SVD methods for multi-dimensional image representation suffer from two critical limitations: the latent tensor is approximated via coarse tensor factorizations (SVD, NMF, QR) that fail to capture spatial local high-frequency details, and the transform matrix relies on fixed basis atoms (DFT, DCT) that cannot precisely model local high-frequency structure along mode-3 fibers. This paper addresses both gaps by introducing a continuous, Gaussian-splatting-based representation that subsumes classical t-SVD as a special case.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263604/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an incomplete third-order tensor (color images at 512×512×3 or 256×256×3; MSIs at 256×256×31 or 256×256×80) together with a binary mask indicating observed entries, and produces the fully recovered tensor. Three missing patterns are evaluated: random missing (sampling rates 0.02–0.10), tube missing (0.10–0.20), and slice missing (only the first and last five spectral bands observed), with the latter being the most challenging as it tests continuity along the mode-3 dimension.

## Method

GSLR factorizes the image as X = A ×₃ T, where the latent tensor A ∈ ℝ^{H×W×R} is rendered from N 2D Gaussian primitives (each with 2D position, 2×2 covariance, and R-dimensional feature vector) and the transform matrix T ∈ ℝ^{B×R} is rendered from R independent 1D Gaussian fields (each with K primitives having scalar position, variance, and feature). The 2D splatting provides a continuous spatial field that captures local high-frequency edges and textures, while the 1D splatting provides a continuous spectral field that models local high-frequency variation along mode-3 fibers—both are indispensable and complementary. Training is fully unsupervised: all N(5+R) + 3KR parameters are optimized end-to-end with Adam, minimizing a masked data-fidelity loss plus a slice-wise nuclear-norm regularization on the latent tensor. The paper proves (Theorem 1) that GSLR degenerates to classical t-SVD under specific parameter constraints, establishing it as a strict generalization with strictly greater representational capacity.

## Results

On random missing (SR=0.02/0.05/0.10), GSLR achieves average PSNR of 21.68/23.58/25.42 dB and SSIM of 0.571/0.662/0.747 on color images, and 30.77/36.33/41.47 dB and 0.888/0.967/0.988 on MSIs, consistently outperforming all six baselines. On tube missing (SR=0.10/0.15/0.20), GSLR reaches 27.36/29.24/30.44 dB PSNR on MSIs, surpassing the best baseline (TCTV) by 0.4–0.9 dB. Under the challenging slice missing setting, GSLR achieves 34.20 dB PSNR and 0.943 SSIM on the Toy dataset, exceeding the next-best method by over 3 dB.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GSLR consistently outperforms all six baselines (TNN, TRLRF, TCTV, HLRTF, LRTFR, FLRTF) across all three missing patterns and both image types. On color images under random missing, the closest competitor is TCTV (18.95 dB at SR=0.02), while GSLR achieves 21.68 dB—a 2.7 dB margin; at SR=0.10 the gap narrows to ~1.0 dB (25.42 vs. 24.42). On MSIs, LRTFR is the strongest baseline (29.30 dB at SR=0.02), but GSLR leads by 1.5 dB (30.77 dB) and the advantage grows to 3.1 dB at SR=0.10 (41.47 vs. 38.33 dB). Under tube missing, GSLR maintains a 0.4–0.9 dB PSNR lead over TCTV on MSIs. The ablation in Table 6 further shows that vanilla 2DGS (without the low-rank tensor structure) underperforms GSLR by 4–5 dB on MSIs and fails entirely on slice missing, confirming that the t-SVD structure is essential. TNN and TRLRF, which rely on fixed DFT/DCT basis atoms, show the weakest performance, particularly on slice missing where they cannot capture spectral continuity along mode-3 fibers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: t-SVD-based tensor completion (Kilmer et al., Zhang et al., Lu et al.) and Gaussian splatting for continuous data representation (Kerbl et al. 3DGS, Zhang et al. 2DGS). Prior t-SVD methods approximate the latent tensor via algebraic factorizations (SVD, NMF, QR) and the transform matrix via fixed basis atoms (DFT, DCT), both of which the authors argue are too coarse for local high-frequency content. The key departure is replacing both components with tailored Gaussian splatting—2D GS for the latent tensor and, more notably, 1D GS for the transform matrix—yielding a continuous, differentiable representation that Theorem 1 shows strictly generalizes classical t-SVD. The 1D GS transform has no direct precedent in the t-SVD literature, where data-dependent transforms have been explored only via neural networks (Luo et al.) or learned unitary bases (Song et al.).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work demonstrates that the two structural bottlenecks of t-SVD (coarse latent tensor approximation and fixed-basis transform) can be simultaneously resolved by a single representation paradigm, yielding large and consistent gains (5–10 dB) across random, tube, and slice missing patterns. The 1D Gaussian splatting transform is a genuinely new tool that could transfer to other transform-based tensor methods beyond t-SVD. Open questions include scalability to very high-dimensional tensors (e.g., 4D or 5D), the computational cost of optimizing tens of thousands of Gaussian primitives, and whether the approach extends naturally to higher-order tensors or non-cubic mode-3 dimensions. The unsupervised formulation also leaves open whether supervised or self-supervised variants could further reduce the hyperparameter sensitivity (N, K, R, λ all require manual tuning).
