## Problem

Diffusion Transformers use a fixed patch size across all denoising steps, forcing every timestep to process the latent at the same granularity regardless of whether it is establishing coarse global structure or refining fine local details. Existing acceleration methods (caching, pruning, quantization) apply static, input-agnostic reduction rules that permanently discard computation, leading to quality degradation or inability to adapt to prompts of varying complexity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261237/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt and produces a 1024×1024 image (FLUX-1.Dev) or a 480×832, 81-frame video (Wan-2.1) over 50 denoising steps. At inference, the user sets a single threshold τ that controls the speed-quality tradeoff: higher τ permits more aggressive use of coarse patches (faster, slightly lower quality), while lower τ preserves finer detail at the cost of speed. The patch size varies per-timestep during generation, so the model internally processes the same latent at different spatial resolutions across the denoising trajectory.

## Method

DDiT modifies a pretrained DiT to support multiple patch sizes (p, 2p, 4p) by adding patch-specific embedding/de-embedding layers, LoRA branches (rank 32) in each transformer block, a residual connection around the patch-embedding block, and a learnable patch-size identifier vector; the LoRA parameters are fine-tuned with an L2 distillation loss against the frozen base model. At inference, a training-free Dynamic Patch Scheduler selects the patch size at each timestep by computing the third-order finite difference of the latent (a proxy for the 'acceleration' of latent evolution), taking the per-patch standard deviation of this acceleration, aggregating via the ρ-th percentile, and choosing the largest patch size whose aggregated variance falls below threshold τ. The key insight is that slow latent evolution indicates coarse-structure generation (safe to use larger patches), while rapid evolution signals fine-detail generation (requiring smaller patches), enabling content-aware, per-prompt allocation of compute without any retraining at test time.

## Results

On FLUX-1.Dev (text-to-image, 1024×1024, 50 steps), DDiT achieves a 2.18× speedup (5.5 s vs. 12.0 s per image) with FID 33.42 (vs. 33.07 baseline) and CLIP 0.317 (vs. 0.314), while matching ImageReward (1.0284 vs. 1.0291). Combined with TeaCache, DDiT reaches a 3.52× speedup (3.4 s) while retaining FID 33.60 and CLIP 0.315. On Wan-2.1 (text-to-video, 480×832, 81 frames), DDiT achieves up to 3.2× speedup with only a 0.71-point VBench drop (80.53 vs. 81.24 baseline).

## Baselines & Comparisons

At comparable inference speed (~6 s/image), DDiT clearly outperforms both TeaCache (δ=0.6) and TaylorSeer (N=3,O=2) on all quality metrics: FID 33.42 vs. 34.95/34.74, CLIP 0.317 vs. 0.303/0.303, and ImageReward 1.0284 vs. 0.9968/0.9721. Against the FLUX-1.Dev baseline at 50 steps, DDiT is 2.18× faster while matching FID within 0.35 points and slightly exceeding the baseline on CLIP (0.317 vs. 0.314) and PartiPrompts CLIP (0.3192 vs. 0.3197). When stacked with TeaCache, DDiT reaches 3.52× speedup (3.4 s) and still beats the 15-step FLUX baseline (3.6 s) on FID (33.60 vs. 34.02) and ImageReward (1.0182 vs. 0.9865). For text-to-video on Wan-2.1, DDiT at τ=0.001 achieves 2.1× speedup with VBench 80.97 (−0.27), and combined with TeaCache reaches 3.2× speedup at VBench 80.53 (−0.71), a modest degradation relative to the 81.24 baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

DDiT builds on the FlexiViT line of work on multi-patch-size transformers, adapting its bilinear-interpolation initialization strategy to diffusion transformers, and applies the same architectural modifications (new patch-embedding layers + LoRA adapters) to FLUX-1.Dev and Wan-2.1 as base models. It departs from prior efficiency methods (caching, pruning, quantization, distillation) that impose static, content-agnostic reductions, instead proposing a training-light, test-time dynamic scheduler that allocates token granularity per timestep based on latent manifold evolution. The paper also positions itself against multi-resolution DiT designs (Würstchen, Matryoshka) that require training from scratch or use rigid manual schedules, emphasizing that DDiT is a plug-and-play retrofit for off-the-shelf pretrained DiTs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 3.5×+ speedup with near-identical FID/CLIP scores is practically significant for practitioners deploying FLUX or Wan models, and the method's complementarity with caching (TeaCache) suggests a composable efficiency stack. However, the approach still requires a fine-tuning stage (LoRA + new embedding layers on synthetic data), so it is not truly training-free despite the 'test-time' framing, and the empirically chosen threshold τ and percentile ρ may not transfer cleanly to new model families or resolutions. A natural open question raised by the authors is whether patch sizes can be varied *within* a single timestep (spatially adaptive) for further gains, and whether the finite-difference scheduling signal generalizes to longer video sequences or higher resolutions without re-tuning.
