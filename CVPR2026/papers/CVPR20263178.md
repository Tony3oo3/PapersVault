## Problem

No existing benchmark tests whether MLLMs can perform ego-grounding — resolving first-person references ('I', 'my') to the camera-wearer in multi-person egocentric videos where visually similar objects and nearby people create ambiguity. Prior egocentric VQA focuses on general understanding or episodic memory but does not require the model to distinguish the camera-wearer's actions and belongings from others' over extended temporal contexts, leaving a critical gap for personalized egocentric assistants.

## Input / Output

The input is uniformly sampled egocentric video frames (up to 1 fps, typically 32 frames; 128 for long-video models like LongVA/LongVU) up to a question timestamp, paired with a first-person personalized question. Each QA pair is anchored to a question moment and an answer moment (always at or before the question moment, averaging ~20 s separation), enabling streaming evaluation. Outputs are scored in two formats: open-ended (binary accuracy plus a 0–5 match score judged by GPT-5 mini at 94% human agreement) and multiple-choice (2 or 5 options with distractors curated to be temporally relevant or contextually confusing).

## Method

The paper introduces MyEgo, a benchmark of 541 long egocentric videos (9.2 min average) drawn from Ego4D, EgoLife, and CASTEL2024, with 5,012 manually annotated QA pairs designed to be diagnostic of ego-grounding: questions require separating the camera-wearer from nearby people and tracking 'my' objects among visually similar distractors. Beyond the dataset, the paper conducts controlled ablations: (1) Q&A moment-aware sampling (16 frames around both question and answer moments) versus uniform sampling, yielding up to 11.2% gains on 'Previous' questions; (2) varying frame counts (8–64) showing diminishing returns and no benefit from more frames; (3) backward sampling at 1 fps from the question moment, which outperforms uniform sampling with far fewer frames; and (4) prompt modifications (replacing 'I/my' with 'the camera wearer' or removing personalization cues), showing small but consistent benefits from explicit ego-clarification. Key empirical findings: all tested MLLMs (GPT-5 at 46.1%, best open-source at ~36%) trail human performance (84.7%) by 33–55%, chain-of-thought 'thinking' models and increased frame counts provide no consistent improvement, but explicitly providing key evidence frames substantially helps, and accuracy decays sharply as the answer moment moves further from the question moment.

## Results

On MyEgo, the best closed-source model GPT-5 achieves 46.1% open-ended (OE) accuracy and 56.1% multiple-choice (MC) accuracy, while the best open-source model Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct reaches 36.4% OE and InternVL3-8B reaches 41.4% MC. Human performance is 84.7% OE and 92.7% MC, meaning all models trail by 33–55%. Notably, 'thinking' variants (Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking, InternVL3.5-8B-Thinking) and long-video models with 128 frames (LongVA, LongVU) provide no consistent improvement over their non-thinking or 32-frame counterparts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-5 leads all models with 46.1% OE and 56.1% MC accuracy, outperforming Gemini-2.5 Pro (40.9% OE, 48.6% MC) by roughly 5–8 points. Among open-source models, Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct achieves the best OE score (36.4%) while InternVL3-8B leads in MC (41.4%), but both remain 10+ points below GPT-5. Critically, 'thinking' variants fail to help: Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking (34.3% OE) underperforms its Instruct counterpart (36.4%), and InternVL3.5-8B-Thinking (32.4%) trails InternVL3-8B (34.3%). Long-video models with 128 frames (LongVA: 32.1%, LongVU: 32.5% OE) do not surpass standard 32-frame models, suggesting that simply increasing temporal context introduces noise rather than improving ego-grounding. All models show a consistent gap between 'Current' and 'Previous' questions (e.g., GPT-5: 51.1% vs. 44.0%), confirming that temporal memory of the camera wearer's past interactions is the primary bottleneck.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of egocentric VQA, personalized vision-language models, and ego-cue research, but does not build directly on any single prior method. Prior egocentric VQA benchmarks (EgoSchema, EgoThink, EgoMemoria, EgoLifeQA) focus on general-purpose first-person understanding without requiring the model to disambiguate the camera-wearer from other people or track 'my' objects among visually similar distractors. Personalized VLM work (MyVLM, 'this is my unicorn fluffy') adapts to users via external exemplars or textual profiles rather than learning identity from the video stream itself. The authors introduce 'ego-grounding' as a distinct capability—resolving first-person references over time—that none of these threads addresses, and position MyEgo as the first diagnostic benchmark targeting it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MyEgo provides the first systematic evaluation of whether MLLMs can truly understand and remember the camera-wearer, revealing a stark gap: even GPT-5 (46%) trails human performance (84.7%) by nearly 40 points. The finding that neither model scaling nor chain-of-thought reasoning yields consistent gains challenges the prevailing assumption that more compute and explicit reasoning should close such gaps, pointing instead to fundamental limitations in long-range temporal memory and identity tracking. The observation that performance decays sharply as the answer moment moves further from the question moment, and that explicitly providing key evidence frames recovers much of the lost accuracy, suggests that future progress requires dedicated mechanisms for ego-identity persistence and moment-level retrieval rather than simply larger context windows. This is directly relevant to the emerging smart-glasses and wearable-assistant space where personalized, memory-grounded QA is a core use case.
