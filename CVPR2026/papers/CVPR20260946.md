## Problem

Existing 3DGS-based RGBD SLAM methods face a fundamental trade-off: global Gaussians that can move freely across the scene are too memory-intensive to scale to large environments, while view-tied Gaussians anchored at fixed depth points are too constrained to render neighboring views well. This paper seeks a Gaussian representation that is both scalable (only a small subset needs to be in GPU memory) and flexible enough to produce high-fidelity rendering.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260946/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes an RGBD image sequence where depth maps may contain missing values (filled via inpainting or rendering from nearby views). During mapping, only the Gaussians associated with the current frame (~816K on Replica) are optimized against RGB and depth observations of that frame plus its neighbors, while all other scene Gaussians remain frozen. During tracking, the method back-projects a downsampled subset of depth points into local Gaussian distributions and aligns them to a progressively built global geometry set via GICP, producing per-frame camera poses without requiring loop closure or pre-trained priors.

## Method

SGAD-SLAM represents the scene with pixel-aligned spherical Gaussians (color, single radius, opacity, and a learnable depth offset) rather than full ellipsoids, reducing per-Gaussian attributes from 14 to 6. The depth offset allows each Gaussian to slide along its viewing ray during mapping, compensating for the loss of full 3D positional freedom and omitted densification while keeping Gaussians tied to their source pixel for scalability. Mapping optimizes only the current frame's Gaussians by minimizing L1 RGB loss, SSIM, and masked L1 depth loss against the current and neighboring frames. Tracking models the local geometry around each downsampled depth point as a Gaussian distribution (covariance from K nearest neighbors via SVD), then estimates the camera pose by maximizing distribution overlap with a global geometry set using GICP with point-to-surface correspondences and scale normalization, achieving 0.01 s/frame tracking on Replica.

## Results

On rendering, SGAD-SLAM achieves PSNR of 44.87 (Replica), 38.60 (TUM-RGBD), and 42.31 (ScanNet), outperforming the closest 3DGS-based competitor VTGS-SLAM (43.34, 30.20, 31.10) by 1.5, 8.4, and 11.2 dB respectively. For tracking, the method achieves an average ATE RMSE of 0.16 cm on Replica (tied with GS-ICP SLAM), 2.0 cm on TUM-RGBD (tied with CG-SLAM), 7.9 cm on ScanNet, and 0.59 cm on ScanNet++ (best by a wide margin). For 3D reconstruction on Replica, it achieves the best F1-score of 90.9% and lowest depth L1 of 0.30 cm. Runtime is 0.90 s/frame total (0.01 s tracking + 0.89 s mapping), the fastest among methods that maintain full scene Gaussians.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SGAD-SLAM outperforms all 14 baselines on rendering across all three main datasets. The closest competitor, VTGS-SLAM (also pixel-aligned but without depth offset), is beaten by 1.5 dB on Replica, 8.4 dB on TUM-RGBD, and 11.2 dB on ScanNet in PSNR, with the gap widening on real-world data. GS-ICP SLAM achieves the best tracking on Replica (0.16 cm avg, tied with SGAD-SLAM) but does not report ScanNet results and lags significantly on rendering (38.83 vs 44.87 PSNR on Replica). Among methods using data-driven priors for loop closure, LoopSplat (0.26 cm) and CG-SLAM (0.27 cm) on Replica are outperformed by SGAD-SLAM (0.16 cm) without any such priors. On ScanNet++ with large inter-frame motion, SGAD-SLAM achieves 0.59 cm ATE RMSE, dramatically outperforming VTGS-SLAM (1.6 cm), LoopSplat (2.05 cm), and Gaussian-SLAM (2.68 cm). For reconstruction on Replica, SGAD-SLAM achieves the best F1 (90.9%) and lowest depth L1 (0.30 cm), surpassing Loopy-SLAM (90.8%, 0.35 cm) and VTGS-SLAM (90.4%, 0.51 cm). NeRF-based methods (NICE-SLAM, Point-SLAM, ESLAM, Vox-Fusion) are consistently outperformed by 5–20 dB in PSNR, confirming the advantage of explicit Gaussian representations over implicit neural fields for rendering efficiency and quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

SGAD-SLAM extends the 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) rendering pipeline into RGBD SLAM by combining the simplified spherical Gaussian representation from VTGS-SLAM with a novel per-pixel depth offset that allows each Gaussian to slide along its viewing ray. It departs from global-map 3DGS SLAM methods (SplaTAM, Gaussian-SLAM) that must hold all scene Gaussians in GPU memory, instead maintaining only pixel-aligned Gaussians for the current frame and its neighbors. For tracking, it replaces rendering-based pose estimation with a geometry-similarity approach built on Generalized ICP applied to per-pixel Gaussian distributions, drawing on the distribution-overlap formulation from Segal et al. but adapting it with point-to-surface correspondences and scale normalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The scalability argument is practically important: by only optimizing Gaussians associated with a few frames rather than the entire scene, the method removes the GPU-memory bottleneck that limits existing 3DGS-SLAM systems to moderate scene sizes, and the 8-GPU parallel mapping path (0.16 s/frame total) suggests a path toward real-time large-scale mapping. The depth offset is a minimal (one scalar per pixel) yet effective mechanism that outperforms both fixed-depth and fully-movable Gaussians, suggesting that constrained movement with adaptive placement can match or exceed unconstrained optimization. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to truly large outdoor scenes, whether the simplified spherical Gaussian limits novel-view synthesis fidelity in highly textured or reflective environments, and whether the RGBD input requirement can be relaxed to monocular or stereo settings.
