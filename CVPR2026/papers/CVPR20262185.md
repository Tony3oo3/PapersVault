## Problem

MLLMs exhibit substantially weaker and less focused cross-modal attention between speaker textual tokens and their corresponding visual regions in multi-person videos compared to object-centric images, causing visual inputs to yield inconsistent or even negative gains on social interaction tasks. This paper is the first to systematically quantify this misalignment and identify it as a core bottleneck for multi-party social reasoning in MLLMs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262185/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 8 uniformly sampled video frames at 640×360 resolution, timestamped speaker transcripts (each token tagged with a speaker label and frame index), and per-speaker bounding boxes that are mapped onto the visual token grid to define speaker-specific token subsets. Outputs are task-specific: multiple-choice answers for TVQA+ VideoQA, and discrete speaker labels for speaking target identification, pronoun coreference resolution, and mentioned player prediction on MMSI/OnlineMMSI. The attention bias itself introduces no new parameters; it operates purely at inference time on top of a LoRA-fine-tuned base MLLM.

## Method

The proposed method has two components applied within the transformer's self-attention layers. First, dynamic cross-modal head selection identifies 'active' attention heads by thresholding (λ = 5e-5) the mean attention weight from utterance tokens to all speaker bounding-box regions, selecting roughly 10–25% of heads that show strong visual-text grounding. Second, an adaptive social-aware bias W_b is injected into the attention logits of those active heads: for a text token u_i belonging to speaker s at frame t, the bias added to each visual token v_j inside s's bounding box equals α times the maximum attention u_i originally assigns to any visual token across all speakers. This adaptive scaling preserves each token's relative attention pattern while softly shifting mass toward the correct speaker's region. The bias is applied to middle transformer layers (10–19) where cross-modal fusion is strongest, requires no architectural modification or additional trainable parameters, and is integrated plug-and-play into LLaVA-NeXT-Video, Qwen2.5-VL, and InternVL3.

## Results

Across three MLLMs (Qwen2.5-VL, LLaVA-NeXT-Video, InternVL3) and four social tasks on TVQA+, MMSI, and OnlineMMSI, the proposed attention alignment method yields an average accuracy improvement of +2.1% to +3.2% over each model's own baseline. The best configuration (InternVL3+Ours) achieves 89.1% on TVQA+ VideoQA, 80.5% on MMSI pronoun coreference, and 79.7% on OnlineMMSI mentioned speaker prediction, setting new state-of-the-art on four of six task settings. On the cross-modal attention analysis (Table 1), the method raises AttnMax from 4.54×10⁻² to 17.09×10⁻² and AttnMean from 3.26×10⁻⁴ to 26.20×10⁻⁴ on MMSI, surpassing even the COCO object-centric baseline (9.23×10⁻² / 15.56×10⁻⁴).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the MMSI baseline [31] (74.5/73.0/62.5 on T/P/M), the proposed method wins on pronoun coreference (80.5 vs. 73.0) and mentioned speaker prediction (66.0 vs. 62.5) but trails on speaking target identification (69.7 vs. 74.5), which the authors attribute to the need to balance attention between the current speaker and the target. Against the OnlineMMSI baseline [35] (64.8/72.9/49.4 on Online T/P/M), the method achieves large gains on P (+6.8) and M (+30.3) but remains slightly below on T (62.6 vs. 64.8). Compared to the Qwen2.5-VL VLB baseline (86.1/64.8/76.6/62.4/60.1/75.9/50.2), Qwen2.5-VL+Ours improves every task by 1.2–27.8 points, with the largest jump on OnlineMMSI M (78.2 vs. 50.2). Against LLaVA-NeXT-Video (83.1/66.1/75.9/62.4/60.6/73.7/51.7) and InternVL3 (85.6/65.0/76.9/63.0/61.3/76.6/52.1), the method similarly outperforms on all tasks. On TVQA+ VideoQA, the method surpasses both ST-VLM-7B (68.1) and TLNet (75.5) by a wide margin (89.1 for InternVL3+Ours), confirming that the attention alignment generalizes beyond the social-interaction-specific benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the growing literature on modality bias and cross-modal alignment in MLLMs [3, 53, 84] by identifying a specific failure mode—weak speaker-consistent attention in multi-person scenes—and proposing a targeted fix. It departs from prior social-interaction alignment strategies (speaker embeddings in MMSI [31], visual prompts in OnlineMMSI [35], audio-based alignment [1]) by directly modifying attention distributions within the transformer rather than augmenting inputs. The dynamic head selection mechanism builds on the 'visual head' analysis of Bi et al. [7], while the adaptive bias draws on standard attention-masking techniques (causal/padding masks) repurposed for speaker-specific grounding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method is immediately practical: it requires no trainable parameters, no architectural changes, and integrates into any MLLM with bounding box annotations, making it a drop-in improvement for social-interaction pipelines. However, its reliance on per-frame speaker bounding boxes limits applicability to settings where such annotations are unavailable, and the authors acknowledge that speaking-target identification still lags behind SOTA because it requires balancing attention between speaker and target. Open questions include whether the alignment can be achieved without explicit box annotations (e.g., via the model's own grounding) and whether the approach generalizes to audio-visual or egocentric settings with more dynamic camera motion.
