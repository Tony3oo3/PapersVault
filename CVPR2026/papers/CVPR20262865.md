## Problem

Existing robot manipulation methods that use generative models to imagine future visual outcomes treat imagination and control as decoupled processes, producing visually coherent rollouts that are physically inconsistent with executable actions. Even methods that add structural priors (point trajectories, scene graphs) remain correlation-driven within the visual domain, overlooking the bidirectional causal coupling between robot actions and the evolving environment. The paper formalizes this coupling as interaction dynamics—specifying both where environmental changes occur and how actions cause them—and shows that modeling this jointly is essential for stable, generalizable control.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a short history of RGB observation frames (o_{t-h:t}) and a natural-language instruction, encoded by a ResNet-18 backbone and a frozen 6-layer MiniLM respectively. Internally, it also generates short-horizon point trajectories (N_q points tracked L frames ahead via CoTracker) as supervision, but discards the trajectory decoder at inference so that only the latent interaction representation H_t drives downstream action prediction. The output is a single robot control action a_t (continuous or discrete), predicted autoregressively by a Transformer decoder that also ingests the aggregated dynamics, visual embeddings, and language embeddings as concatenated token sequences.

## Method

DynBridge is an end-to-end framework with three tightly coupled components. (1) The Interaction Dynamics Generator uses learnable dynamic tokens as queries in a cross-attention module over concatenated visual and language embeddings, producing N_tok latent tokens H_t that encode interaction dynamics; it is jointly supervised by L2 trajectory reconstruction (where) and behavioral-cloning action loss (how), so the representation captures both spatial structure and causal dynamics. (2) The Action-Conditioned Dynamics Aggregator compresses H_t via a dual-path design: a local path where learnable action tokens (detached and adapter-reconditioned) score and weight interaction tokens through softmax attention, and a global path that linearly projects H_t into the same dimension; the two are fused by element-wise addition to balance fine-grained action-relevant focus with stable global context. (3) The Dynamics-Guided Action Predictor is a standard Transformer decoder (masked self-attention + cross-attention + FFN) that takes concatenated action tokens, aggregated dynamics, visual embeddings, and language embeddings to output the next action via an MLP head. The entire pipeline is trained end-to-end with L_total = L_act + β·L_traj, and the key architectural choice is to use a latent interaction representation rather than explicit trajectory coordinates, enabling robustness to multiple feasible trajectories and tighter coupling between perception and control.

## Results

On the LIBERO benchmark, DynBridge achieves the highest average success rate across all five subsets without any external pretraining data: 0.92 (Spatial), 1.00 (Object), 0.92 (Goal), 0.71 (Long), and 0.75 (90-task). The most notable gain is on LIBERO-Long, where DynBridge reaches 0.71 versus 0.56 for the strongest baseline (GraphMimic), a 15-point improvement on multi-stage, multi-object tasks. On Meta-World handle manipulation (5 demos per task), DynBridge also achieves the best success rate across all four spatially varying configurations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DynBridge outperforms all seven baselines on every LIBERO subset. Against the strongest competitor GraphMimic [4] (which uses external pretraining data), DynBridge leads by 4 points on Spatial (0.92 vs 0.88), 11 points on Object (1.00 vs 0.89), 5 points on Goal (0.92 vs 0.87), 15 points on Long (0.71 vs 0.56), and 8 points on the 90-task suite (0.75 vs 0.67), all while using only 10 labeled demonstrations versus GraphMimic's 50 unlabeled videos plus 10 demos. The video-generation baseline UniPi [11] and the inverse-dynamics baseline VPT [2] both rely on external data yet remain far behind (e.g., UniPi scores only 0.05 on LIBERO-Long). The trajectory-based ATM [36] is competitive on short-horizon tasks (0.77 on Goal) but degrades sharply on long-horizon tasks (0.39 on Long), highlighting its lack of action-conditioned causal reasoning. On Meta-World, DynBridge surpasses PlaySlot [31] and MPI [43] on all four handle manipulation tasks, with the gap widening under spatial variation and limited (5-demo) training data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two established lines of work in robot manipulation: (1) video-generation-based methods (UniPi, VPT) that decouple future prediction from action execution, and (2) interaction-modeling methods (ATM, GraphMimic, PlaySlot) that address either spatial structure ('where') or action semantics ('how') in isolation. Rather than building on a single prior architecture, DynBridge repurposes standard components—ResNet-18 visual encoding, MiniLM language encoding, CoTracker trajectory supervision, and a Transformer decoder—into a new unified framework where a shared latent 'interaction dynamics' representation simultaneously serves trajectory reconstruction and action prediction. The key conceptual departure is treating the bidirectional agent-environment coupling as a first-class object to be learned jointly, rather than as a post-hoc alignment between separately trained modules.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By achieving competitive or superior performance on LIBERO and Meta-World without any external video pretraining, DynBridge demonstrates that explicit causal interaction modeling can substitute for the large-scale data that prior methods (GraphMimic, ATM, UniPi) rely on, which is practically significant for deployment in new environments where collecting large datasets is infeasible. The cross-robot transfer experiment (Panda → XArm7 with mostly failed demonstrations) suggests the learned interaction dynamics capture embodiment-agnostic causal structure, opening a path toward reducing per-robot data collection costs. Open questions include whether the approach scales to open-world settings with highly diverse interaction patterns, and how to integrate interaction dynamics with large visual foundation models without sacrificing the end-to-end joint optimization that the paper identifies as critical.
