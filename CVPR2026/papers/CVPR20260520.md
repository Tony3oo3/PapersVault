## Problem

Continual Test-Time Adaptation (CTTA) for medical image segmentation must handle sequentially arriving unlabeled samples from shifting domains, but existing methods rely on unreliable supervisory signals (entropy minimization, pixel-level consistency, BN statistics) that trigger self-reinforcing error accumulation and catastrophic forgetting. A key unaddressed gap is that local features extracted under domain shift are highly noise-sensitive, and no prior CTTA method explicitly distills high-order structural information from the data stream to provide robust, cluster-level adaptation guidance.

## Input / Output

At test time, the method consumes a single unlabeled 800×800 medical image per step (retinal fundus or colonoscopy), performing t=4 MC Dropout forward passes to estimate per-pixel uncertainty and selecting the lowest-uncertainty 50% of foreground nodes for graph construction. A feature queue of N=3 previously seen images is maintained to form a pseudo-batch of B=4 instances, enabling cross-image structural reasoning without requiring a true mini-batch. The output is a pixel-wise segmentation mask (optic disc/cup or polyp), produced by a ResUNet-50 whose parameters are fine-tuned end-to-end via backpropagation through the graph clustering objective.

## Method

SPEGC comprises two coupled modules. First, the Semantic Prompt Feature Enhancement (SPFE) module aggregates local node features into a global query via attention pooling, then retrieves context from two decoupled learnable prompt pools: a commonality pool (P_CO) accessed via reverse-attention with ReLU truncation to capture shared cross-domain semantics, and a heterogeneity pool (P_HE) accessed via standard softmax attention to capture domain-specific discriminative patterns; the resulting prompts are added as additive biases to the local features. Second, the Differentiable Graph Clustering Solver (DGCS) computes a raw similarity matrix from the enhanced features, converts it into a directed edge affinity via density-weighted sigmoid gating, and then reframes the selection of exactly k=V−Z edges as an entropy-regularized optimal transport problem solved by the Sinkhorn algorithm, yielding a refined edge similarity matrix S* in an end-to-end differentiable manner. Adaptation is driven by a joint loss: a graph consistency loss that minimizes KL divergence between predictions of structurally connected nodes (weighted by S*), and a clustering loss that enforces cosine similarity among commonality prompts across the pseudo-batch to anchor shared semantics and mitigate forgetting.

## Results

On the OD/OC retinal fundus segmentation task (5 domains), SPEGC achieves an average DSC of 84.37%, outperforming the next-best method TTDG (82.88%) by 1.49% and the No-Adapt baseline (72.75%) by 11.62%. On the polyp segmentation task (4 domains), SPEGC reaches 78.27% average DSC, surpassing GraTa (76.24%) and TTDG (76.20%) by ~2%. Under long-term continual adaptation (5 rounds), SPEGC maintains 83.10% overall average DSC with only 1.27% performance degradation, compared to 3.52% degradation for GraTa.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPEGC outperforms all six baselines on both segmentation tasks. Against the closest competitor TTDG (graph matching, CVPR'25), SPEGC gains +1.49% DSC on OD/OC and +2.07% on polyp, with the advantage widening on the more challenging polyp task where TTDG's static graph priors struggle. GraTa (gradient alignment, AAAI'25) is second-closest on OD/OC (78.66% vs. 84.37%) but degrades severely under long-term continual adaptation (3.52% forgetting vs. SPEGC's 1.27%). NC-TTT (noise contrastive, CVPR'24) achieves 79.23% on OD/OC but still trails SPEGC by 5.14%. The prompt-based VPTTA (CVPR'24) and BN-based DomainAdaptor (CVPR'23) both hover around 73–74% DSC, roughly 11 points below SPEGC, confirming that input-space or normalization-level adaptation is insufficient under severe domain shift. Notably, entropy-based SAR (ICLR'23) actually falls below the No-Adapt baseline on the polyp task (69.21% vs. 71.49%), illustrating the failure mode of confidence-driven adaptation on subtle targets, whereas SPEGC's structure-driven signal avoids this pitfall.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPEGC sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) prompt-based CTTA methods like VPTTA that learn lightweight input-space prompts while freezing the backbone, (2) graph-based TTA approaches like TTDG that use pre-computed graph structures for cross-domain alignment, and (3) clustering-based segmentation (CMT-DeepLab, ClustSeg) that treats clustering as a post-processing step. The paper departs from all three by moving prompts to the feature space with a decoupled commonality/heterogeneity design, making the graph clustering differentiable and end-to-end via an optimal transport formulation, and repurposing the resulting structural representation as an adaptive supervision signal rather than a fixed prior. This reframes the CTTA problem from 'find a reliable self-supervised loss' to 'distill high-order data structure into a differentiable objective that guides parameter updates.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical pain point in clinical deployment: models must adapt to a stream of unlabeled, domain-shifting images without accumulating errors or forgetting source-domain knowledge. The L-CTTA results (1.27% degradation vs. 3.52% for entropy-based GraTa) and the polyp-segmentation finding that entropy minimization can be *worse* than no adaptation are practically important signals. However, the explicit graph construction introduces quadratic computational scaling (5.8G FLOPs at pool size 3, 120G at size 15), which limits real-time applicability. Open questions include whether the optimal transport solver can be approximated more cheaply, whether the approach extends to 3D volumetric segmentation, and whether the structural supervision generalizes beyond the two medical benchmarks tested.
