## Problem

Existing HDR-NVS methods struggle with three interrelated issues: (1) simply scaling exposure cannot disentangle material reflectance from ambient illumination, so illumination-dependent appearance changes are lost; (2) HDR supervision is implicit via tone-mapped LDR constraints, which fail to correct abnormal or saturated HDR values; and (3) the flat slopes of tone-mapping curves at signal extremes cause Gaussians in under/over-exposed regions to receive starved gradients, leading to under-densified representations.

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-exposure LDR views (five exposure levels grouped into over-exposed {t1,t3,t5} and normally-exposed {t2,t4} sets) with standard camera intrinsics and extrinsics, and produces novel LDR views, an HDR radiance field represented as 3D Gaussians, and relit HDR views under arbitrary virtual illumination. HDR ground truth is used only for evaluation, never for training. The tone mapper fuses dual-branch outputs (global from the IE branch and local from the GI branch) to yield the final LDR prediction, and rendering is evaluated at 400×400 resolution.

## Method

PhysHDR-GS factorizes each Gaussian's color into intrinsic hemispherical reflectance H_r and adjustable ambient illumination L_a, composed via an MLP radiance composer. Two complementary branches exploit different response patterns: the image-exposure (IE) branch globally scales the rendered HDR image by exposure t, while the Gaussian-illumination (GI) branch locally relights 3D Gaussians by modulating L_a through a learnable illumination modulator, enabling per-region radiance rescaling. A cross-branch HDR consistency loss (L1 on Gaussian-blurred exposure-scaled and relit HDR images) provides explicit self-supervision for HDR content without ground truth, and a learnable tone mapper (f_tm for global-local tone mapping plus f_mix for cross-fusion) aggregates both branches into the final LDR output. To counter gradient starvation, an illumination-guided gradient scaling factor s_a = s·σ(|L_a − L̂_a|) + 1 amplifies per-Gaussian screen-space gradients proportional to illumination deviation before the densification threshold check, preventing under/over-exposed Gaussians from being under-split.

## Results

On the realistic HDR-NeRF-Real dataset (LDR-OE/exp3), the Scaffold-GS variant (Ours†) achieves 36.91 PSNR, 0.9777 SSIM, and 0.009 LPIPS, outperforming GaussHDR† (36.32 PSNR) by 0.59 dB and HDR-GS (34.87 PSNR) by 2.04 dB. On the synthetic HDR-NeRF-Syn dataset, Ours† reaches 39.21 PSNR on the HDR evaluation (vs. 17.51 for HDR-GS and 39.08 for GaussHDR†), demonstrating a 21.7 dB advantage over HDR-GS in reconstructing true HDR content. Rendering throughput reaches 76 FPS (Ours) and 53 FPS (Ours†) at 400×400 resolution, with GPU memory as low as 3274 MB.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against HDR-NeRF [12], Ours† wins by a large margin across all settings (e.g., +2.64 dB PSNR on HDR-NeRF-Real LDR-OE/exp3), confirming that 3DGS-based rendering substantially outperforms volumetric NeRF for HDR-NVS. Against HDR-GS [3], Ours† achieves a 2.04 dB PSNR gain on HDR-NeRF-Real LDR-OE/exp3 and a dramatic 21.7 dB gain on HDR-NeRF-Syn HDR evaluation (39.21 vs. 17.51), where HDR-GS's lack of explicit HDR supervision causes severe brightness errors. Against the strongest baseline GaussHDR [24] (and its Scaffold-GS variant GaussHDR†), Ours† consistently leads by 0.3–0.6 dB PSNR on realistic datasets and by 0.13–0.45 dB on the synthetic benchmark, while also achieving lower LPIPS (e.g., 0.009 vs. 0.011 on HDR-NeRF-Real LDR-OE/exp3) and faster rendering (76 FPS vs. 38 FPS for GaussHDR†). The vanilla 3DGS variant (Ours) is competitive with GaussHDR† on several settings (e.g., 34.64 vs. 34.63 PSNR on HDR-NeRF-Real LDR-OE/exp1) while using less GPU memory (3274 MB vs. 6221 MB).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting as the scene representation and rendering engine, extending the HDR-NVS line of work initiated by HDR-NeRF and accelerated by HDR-GS and GaussHDR. The key conceptual departure is a physically-inspired decomposition of Gaussian color into intrinsic reflectance and adjustable ambient illumination, motivated by the rendering equation, rather than the conventional approach of simulating different lighting levels purely through 2D exposure scaling and tone mapping. The dual-branch architecture (image-exposure + Gaussian-illumination) and the illumination-guided gradient scaling strategy are novel contributions that address specific failure modes—appearance entanglement and exposure-biased gradient starvation—that prior 3DGS-based HDR methods do not explicitly model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The physically-grounded decomposition offers a principled path toward illumination-aware 3D scene representation that could extend beyond HDR-NVS to relighting and material estimation tasks, though the current model assumes uniform hemispherical illumination and direction-independent emission, limiting its expressiveness for complex lighting. The illumination-guided gradient scaling is a broadly applicable training trick for any 3DGS-based method where certain primitives receive systematically smaller gradients. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-uniform or directional lighting, dynamic scenes, and whether the simplified PBR model can be enriched (e.g., with view-dependent BRDFs) without sacrificing real-time rendering.
