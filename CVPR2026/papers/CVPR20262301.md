## Problem

Existing RAW reconstruction methods optimize exclusively for pixel-wise RAW fidelity, ignoring the primary downstream use case of photographic editing. When reconstructed RAWs are rendered under diverse photofinishing styles or post-capture edits, results degrade significantly because the training objective never exposed the model to rendering variability. This paper addresses the gap by introducing a loss that directly supervises reconstruction quality in the sRGB rendering space under randomized edit conditions.

## Input / Output

The method takes an 8-bit sRGB JPEG (3000×4000 px from a Samsung S24 Ultra) as input, optionally augmented with capture-time RAW metadata (sparse pixel samples, a downsampled RAW at 8× reduction, or compact latent features), and outputs a 3-channel denoised RAW image normalized to [0,1]. The output RAW is saved as a DNG file and edited in Adobe Camera RAW at inference time; the differentiable ISP used in the loss is purely a training-time construct and is not required at deployment. The approach is plug-and-play: it adds a loss term to any existing reconstruction network without modifying its architecture.

## Method

The core contribution is a plug-and-play edit-aware loss L_sRGB computed in sRGB space between ground-truth and reconstructed RAWs, both rendered through a modular differentiable ISP g_φ with four tunable stages: exposure (multiplicative 2^ε with ε~N(0,σ²)), white balance (illuminant sampled from a 2D Gaussian fitted to DNG ASN tags, followed by a CST matrix interpolation), color manipulation (an MLP approximating a randomly selected 3D LUT from a set of K pre-trained LUTs), and tone mapping (an MLP approximating the Adobe tone curve perturbed by random monotonically non-decreasing polynomials). During training, all ISP parameters φ are randomly sampled per mini-batch from distributions modeling realistic photofinishing variability, and the same sampled parameters are applied to both the ground-truth and reconstructed RAWs before computing the ℓ₂ loss in sRGB. The total loss combines the original RAW-space loss, any method-specific auxiliary losses, and the edit-aware sRGB loss weighted by λ. A key extension is target edit-aware fine-tuning: at inference, the ISP parameters can be fixed to match a specific desired edit (e.g., +0.5 EV, 3500K), allowing the metadata-assisted model to adapt its weights to both the image and the target rendering simultaneously.

## Results

Across three RAW reconstruction frameworks (CAM, RAW Diffusion, and a UNet-based model) evaluated on 400 test images from the Samsung S24 Ultra smartphone RAW dataset, the edit-aware loss consistently improves sRGB rendering quality under five diverse Photoshop edits. Headline gains reach up to 2 dB PSNR in sRGB space (e.g., CAM Edit 4: 25.43→27.43 dB; RAWDiff Edit 5: 23.29→25.03 dB) and reduce ΔE by 1–2 units across all edits. RAW-space PSNR shows a slight improvement for CAM (37.17→37.57 dB) but a modest trade-off for RAWDiff (34.18→33.37 dB) and UNet (38.82→35.62 dB, which uses only the sRGB loss). A 25-user perceptual study confirms the gains, with the edit-aware method preferred 83% of the time over its baseline counterpart.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper integrates its edit-aware loss into three existing RAW reconstruction methods and compares each against its unmodified baseline. Against CAM [23] (metadata-assisted, content-aware sampling), the edit-aware variant wins on every sRGB metric across all four reported edits, with the largest PSNR gain of +2.0 dB on Edit 4 and ΔE dropping from 6.12 to 4.54; RAW PSNR also improves slightly (37.17→37.57 dB). Against RAW Diffusion [27] (blind, diffusion-based), the edit-aware variant again wins on all sRGB metrics (e.g., Edit 1 PSNR 24.27→25.44 dB, ΔE 9.50→8.52) at the cost of a 0.8 dB drop in RAW PSNR (34.18→33.37 dB), reflecting the expected trade-off of optimizing for rendering fidelity rather than pixel-level RAW accuracy. The UNet baseline (metadata-assisted, downsampled RAW input) shows the most dramatic RAW PSNR drop (38.82→35.62 dB) because the edit-aware variant is trained with only the sRGB loss, yet it still achieves the best sRGB PSNR among all methods on Edits 1–4 (e.g., 29.26 dB on Edit 1 vs. 28.52 for the baseline). In all three comparisons, the edit-aware method is strictly superior on the downstream sRGB editing metrics that matter for consumer photo workflows, while the RAW-space fidelity cost is modest or even positive depending on the base model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against the broad family of RAW reconstruction methods—both metadata-assisted (CAM, sparse-sample, latent-vector approaches) and blind (forward-inverse ISP, diffusion-based)—which uniformly optimize pixel-wise RAW-space fidelity. The closest prior work includes cyclic consistency losses in CycleISP, InvISP, and CIE-XYZ-Net, which re-render the recovered RAW to sRGB but only enforce agreement with the unedited input image. The key departure is introducing a stochastic, edit-aware sRGB loss via a modular differentiable ISP with randomly sampled exposure, white-balance, color-LUT, and tone-curve parameters, drawing on NILUT [12] for the MLP-based LUT approximation. The loss is explicitly plug-and-play, requiring no architectural modification to the underlying reconstruction network.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For consumer photography, the practical end goal of RAW reconstruction is flexible post-capture editing, yet prior methods were blind to this downstream use; this work closes that gap with a simple, general-purpose loss that consistently improves sRGB rendering quality (up to 1.5–2 dB PSNR) across metadata-assisted and blind architectures without changing inference cost. Limitations include the absence of local tone-mapping modeling, reliance on a simplified ISP that may not capture all proprietary camera processing, and a ~15% training-time overhead. Open questions include whether the approach extends to AI-driven or spatially-varying edits, whether it generalizes across different camera ISPs and sensor formats, and whether the fixed sampling distributions can be adapted to user-specific editing habits.
