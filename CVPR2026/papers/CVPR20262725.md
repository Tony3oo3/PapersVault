## Problem

VLM-based robot manipulation requires an intermediate representation that bridges human instructions to executable robot actions, but existing approaches face a fundamental tradeoff: high-level skill-word representations are VLM-comprehensible yet rigid (requiring manual vocabulary expansion for new tasks), while low-level keypoint/axis representations are generalizable yet produce overly complex constraints that VLMs struggle to generate correctly. The paper asks how to design a single intermediate representation that simultaneously achieves strong action-generalizability and VLM-comprehensibility.

## Input / Output

The system takes a natural-language instruction and a concatenated dual-view RGB image (from two Intel RealSense D435 depth cameras positioned on opposite sides of the workspace) as input to the VLM, alongside a 3D point cloud for trajectory solving. The VLM outputs a SEAM representation — a short, Python-executable expression composed of a small vocabulary of semantic operations (e.g., get_axis, move_cost, perpendicular_cost) assembled under type-constrained grammar rules. Downstream, the RAG segmentation pipeline produces a binary mask for the target object part, and a nonlinear optimizer yields the gripper's target 3D position, Euler-angle orientation, and open/close status.

## Method

SEAM decomposes the intermediate representation into a compact semantic vocabulary (7 operations) and a set of type-directed grammar rules, drawing on context-free grammar to constrain the VLM (Qwen3-VL-30B) to compose words in a semantically coherent, syntactically valid manner — effectively turning open-ended code generation into a guided assembly process. For fine-grained part localization, the paper introduces a RAG-based few-shot segmentation pipeline: a curated database of key-phrase-to-support-pair entries is queried via Levenshtein-distance matching against the instruction, and a few-shot segmentation network (Mapper) transfers support masks to the query image in ~0.6 s. The resulting SEAM expression is executed as a Python cost function over the point cloud, and the gripper pose is obtained by solving a regularized optimization that minimizes this cost while penalizing large translations and rotations from the initial pose.

## Results

On 8 real-world manipulation tasks (10 trials each), SEAM achieves 83.8% closed-loop and 63.8% open-loop success rates, outperforming the prior SOTA OmniManip (68.8% / 52.5%) by 15 percentage points in closed-loop. For open-vocabulary part segmentation, SEAM's RAG-based pipeline runs in 0.6 s per query, faster than Grounded SAM (0.88 s), LISA (0.9 s), and OV-Seg (10.2 s). On the proposed action-generalizability (AG) and VLM-comprehensibility (VC) metrics, SEAM achieves a balanced trade-off that high-level (Instruct2Act) and low-level (ReKep, OmniManip) representations cannot simultaneously match.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SEAM (83.8% closed-loop) outperforms OmniManip (68.8%) by 15 pp, CoPa (28.8%) by 55 pp, and VoxPoser (18.6%) by 65 pp on overall task success; ReKep is not fully comparable as it lacks support for articulated-object tasks (drawer, button, jar). In open-loop settings, SEAM (63.8%) still leads OmniManip (52.5%) by 11 pp. For segmentation speed, SEAM's RAG pipeline (0.6 s) is faster than Grounded SAM (0.88 s), LISA (0.9 s), and dramatically faster than OV-Seg (10.2 s). On the AG/VC analysis (Sec. 4.6), SEAM achieves a balanced profile: Instruct2Act scores high on VLM-comprehensibility but low on action-generalizability due to its large task-specific vocabulary, while ReKep and OmniManip show the opposite pattern; SEAM's compact grammar-based vocabulary avoids both extremes. Qualitatively, SEAM's segmentation correctly localizes fine-grained parts (teapot rim, hinge, affordance) where AffordanceNet and RoboAfford fail, and where LISA and OV-Seg segment whole objects instead of the relevant sub-part.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of VLM-based robot manipulation and formal language theory. It departs from high-level skill-word approaches (Instruct2Act, Code as Policies) that are VLM-friendly but rigid, and from low-level keypoint/axis methods (ReKep, OmniManip, CoPa) that generalize well but overwhelm VLMs with complex constraint code. The key conceptual move is borrowing the vocabulary-grammar decomposition from context-free grammar (echoing Motion Grammar [13]) to create a middle-ground representation, while the RAG-based few-shot segmentation pipeline repurposes the Mapper network [49] for manipulation-relevant part localization rather than generic segmentation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper formalizes a previously implicit trade-off (VLM-comprehensibility vs. action-generalizability) with two concrete metrics, giving the community a principled way to evaluate intermediate representations beyond end-to-end success rates. The 15% real-world improvement over OmniManip is practically meaningful, but the evaluation is limited to a single UR5 platform, one VLM (Qwen3-VL), and eight tasks, and the quantitative metric study relies on DeepSeek as a judge rather than physical execution. Open questions include whether the grammar-based decomposition scales to multi-arm coordination, force-feedback tasks, or long-horizon planning, and whether the RAG database approach remains tractable as the part vocabulary grows.
