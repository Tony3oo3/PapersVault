## Problem

Vision Transformers exhibit persistent dense-feature artifacts—background patches receiving high CLS-patch similarity scores—across label-, text-, and self-supervised pre-training, yet no single existing remedy (e.g., Registers) addresses the root cause. The paper identifies a unified mechanism: under coarse-grained image-level supervision combined with global self-attention, ViTs adopt a 'lazy aggregation' shortcut that diffuses foreground semantics into abundant background tokens from the very start of training, degrading all downstream dense-prediction tasks.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images split into non-overlapping P×P patches (e.g., 16×16 or 32×32), producing a set of N patch tokens plus a CLS token. The key modification replaces the standard CLS aggregation (attention-pooling or global average pooling) with a frequency-aware selective pooling that outputs a CLS token anchored to foreground patches. Downstream, this CLS token and the refined patch features are consumed unchanged by existing dense-prediction pipelines (F-ViT, LOST, zero-shot CLIP segmentation), yielding segmentation masks, detection boxes, and object-discovery masks without architectural modifications to the decoder.

## Method

LaSt-ViT (LazyStrike ViT) reformulates CLS-token aggregation as a channel-wise top-K selective pooling guided by a frequency-domain stability score. For each patch, a 1D FFT is applied along the channel dimension, multiplied by a Gaussian low-pass filter, and inverse-transformed; the ratio of the filtered to the original channel values yields a per-patch, per-channel stability score that is high for semantically homogeneous (foreground) patches and low for diverse (background) ones. For each channel j, the K patches with the highest stability scores are selected and averaged to form the j-th dimension of the CLS token, effectively voting foreground patches into the global representation. A 'vote count' per patch (number of channels in which it was selected) provides an interpretable foreground saliency map. The approach is training-time only—no architectural changes, no extra tokens, no post-hoc fine-tuning—and is applied identically under supervised, text-supervised (CLIP), and self-supervised (DINO) pre-training, consistently raising Point-in-Box scores and improving 12 downstream benchmarks.

## Results

LaSt-ViT consistently improves dense prediction across 12 benchmarks under label-, text-, and self-supervision. On zero-shot semantic segmentation (Table 4), CLIP ViT-B/16 with LazyStrike jumps from 49.0% to 75.0% mIoU on VOC and from 6.5% to 12.1% on Cityscapes; the larger ViT-L/14 sees an even more dramatic VOC gain from 17.1% to 72.4%. On open-vocabulary detection (Table 5), F-ViT ViT-B/16 improves from 17.5 to 33.3 AP50 on novel COCO categories (+15.8). For unsupervised object discovery (Table 7), DINO+LazyStrike reaches 64.4% CorLoc on VOC07, surpassing LOST (61.9%) while running at 55.9 FPS versus 29.4 FPS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CLIP, MetaCLIP, and EVACLIP (text-supervised baselines), LazyStrike delivers large and consistent gains on all six semantic segmentation benchmarks; the most striking is VOC mIoU jumping from 17.1% to 72.4% for CLIP ViT-L/14 (+55.3). On open-vocabulary detection, F-ViT+LazyStrike (ViT-B/16) reaches 45.7 AP50 on COCO, surpassing the ConvNet-based F-VLM (RN50) at 39.6 AP50, and gains +15.8 AP50 on novel categories over the F-ViT baseline. For unsupervised object discovery, DINO+LazyStrike outperforms LOST by +2.5 CorLoc on VOC07 (64.4 vs 61.9) and by +18.6 over DINO-seg (64.4 vs 45.8), while also doubling throughput (55.9 vs 29.4 FPS). Notably, the Register baseline [5] eliminates high-norm tokens but actually slightly worsens Point-in-Box (41.5 vs 42.7 for plain ViT), whereas LazyStrike raises PiB to 55.1 (+12.4) and removes high-norm artifacts simultaneously. Under self-supervision, DINO-v1+LazyStrike achieves 69.7 PiB, approaching DINO-ResNet's 71.1, confirming the method's paradigm-agnostic effectiveness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate lines of artifact analysis in ViTs—high-norm tokens in self-supervised models (Register [5]), attention deficit in label-supervised ViTs [25], and feature misalignment in text-supervised CLIP models [36, 43]—under a single mechanistic explanation: 'lazy aggregation' driven by coarse-grained supervision and global attention. It explicitly departs from Register by arguing that high-norm tokens are a downstream symptom rather than the root cause, and from post-hoc CLIP surgery methods [10, 16, 18, 35, 37, 41] by intervening during pre-training rather than modifying final layers. The solution (LaSt-ViT) repurposes 1D FFT-based low-pass filtering in the channel dimension as a foreground-stability signal, a technique borrowed from signal processing but not previously applied to ViT token aggregation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified 'lazy aggregation' framing gives practitioners a single diagnostic (Patch Score / Point-in-Box) and a single fix applicable across supervised, text-supervised, and self-supervised ViTs, reducing the need for paradigm-specific workarounds. However, the method requires retraining from scratch, limiting its utility for already-deployed foundation models; a post-hoc or fine-tuning-compatible variant would broaden its impact. Open questions include whether the frequency-stability assumption holds for non-natural-image domains (medical, satellite), whether the approach transfers to non-attention architectures like Mamba-based vision models, and whether the modest classification accuracy trade-off (e.g., 62%→55% with larger patches) persists at scale with better optimization.
