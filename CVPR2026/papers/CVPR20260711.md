## Problem

Existing video generation models used as world models rely on text or target images to specify goals, but text is too abstract to capture physical nuances (e.g., the specific force and trajectory needed to score a goal), while target images are infeasible to specify for dynamic multi-step tasks. This paper asks whether video models can be taught to accept explicit force vectors as goal specifications and plan the antecedent causal chain of physical interactions needed to achieve them.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260711/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes an initial RGB frame, a text prompt providing semantic context (e.g., 'a pool table'), and a 3-channel physics control tensor of shape f×3×h×w where each channel encodes a moving or static Gaussian blob representing direct force, goal force, and object mass respectively. Force and mass values use intuitive relative scales normalized per domain rather than absolute physical units. The output is an 81-frame video at 16 FPS depicting a physically plausible antecedent causal chain (e.g., ball A striking ball B) that produces the specified goal force on the target object.

## Method

The approach builds on Wan2.2, a Mixture-of-Experts diffusion model, adding a ControlNet module that conditions on the 3-channel physics control signal; only the first 10 DiT layers of the high-noise expert are fine-tuned (via zero-convolutions into the frozen base model) since this expert governs global structure and low-frequency dynamics. Training uses a curated synthetic dataset of causal primitives (3k domino chain reactions, 6k rolling-ball collisions, 3k PhysDreamer carnation sways) generated in Blender and PhysDreamer. The key training strategy is random causal masking: for each video, the model receives either the direct force (channel 0) or the goal force (channel 1) with the other zeroed, forcing it to learn both Goal→Plan (infer the antecedent cause) and Action→Outcome (simulate the resulting effect). The mass channel is also randomly masked, teaching the model to use privileged physics information when available but fall back on learned appearance-based priors otherwise. No physics simulator is required at inference; the model acts as an implicit neural physics planner.

## Results

On the 75-scene human study (N=40, 2AFC), Goal Force achieves 70.5–74.5% pairwise preference over the text-only zero-shot baseline on Goal Force Adherence across all four benchmark categories (two-object, multi-object, human-object, tool-object), while maintaining competitive motion realism (47.5–69.0%) and visual quality (48.9–66.8%). Against the text-only fine-tuned baseline, Goal Force still leads on Force Adherence (56.8–67.0%) with similar motion and quality scores. On the 22-scene Natural Blockers benchmark, visual planning accuracy ranges from 75.51% (Curling Stones) to 100% (Kitchen Lemon), far exceeding the 33.3% random baseline. The diversity metric δ(p) reaches 0.6577 on the 5-domino task, well above the 0.3900 deterministic baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Goal Force consistently outperforms both text-only baselines on the primary metric (Goal Force Adherence) across all four generalization categories, winning 70.5–74.5% of pairwise comparisons against the zero-shot Wan2.2 baseline and 56.8–67.0% against the fine-tuned text-only variant, confirming that the explicit physics control signal is necessary and text alone is insufficient. Against prior force-conditioned methods (PhysGen, PhysDreamer, Force Prompting), Goal Force is the only method capable of the goal-force task itself; those methods misinterpret a goal force as a direct poke on the target object rather than planning an antecedent causal chain (Figure 4). ToRA, a trajectory-conditioned method, follows specified paths but violates causality by moving the target before the initiating event arrives. On the Natural Blockers benchmark, Goal Force achieves 75.5–100% accuracy in selecting the physically valid force initiator, far above the 33.3% random baseline, demonstrating that the model reasons about spatial constraints rather than memorizing patterns. The diversity evaluation (δ(p)=0.6577 vs. 0.3900 deterministic) further shows the model samples multiple valid plans rather than collapsing to a single mode.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Force Prompting paradigm (same first author, [17]) from specifying a direct force (the cause) to specifying a goal force (the desired effect), requiring the model to plan the antecedent causal chain. It departs from prior force-conditioned methods (PhysGen, PhysDreamer, Force Prompting) that can only simulate an immediate force on a target, and from trajectory-based methods (ToRA, Motion Prompting) that specify motion rather than its underlying physical cause. Architecturally, it builds on Wan2.2 with a ControlNet module, but the key contribution is the multi-channel physics control signal (direct force, goal force, mass) and the random causal-masking training strategy that forces the model to learn bidirectional physical reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central claim—that training on ~12k simple synthetic causal primitives (balls, dominos, one flower) bootstraps zero-shot planning for tool use and human-object interaction—points toward video models as implicit neural physics simulators that require no external engine at inference. However, the generalization evidence, while promising, is evaluated on a modest benchmark (75 scenes, 22 planning scenes) with 2AFC human preferences rather than strict physical accuracy metrics, leaving open how far this capability extends to truly complex multi-step or non-rigid dynamics. A key open question is whether the learned causal reasoning is genuinely compositional or relies on the base model's visual prior doing most of the heavy lifting, and whether the approach scales beyond the relatively simple collision-based interactions demonstrated here.
