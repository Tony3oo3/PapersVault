## Problem

Existing MLLM benchmarks operate under a 'sufficient-view' assumption, rewarding single-view pattern recognition while leaving cross-view fusion entirely unevaluated. This gap is critical because real-world human understanding—identity tracking, contact reasoning, motion prediction—requires integrating complementary and often conflicting evidence across synchronized cameras, a capability that current models demonstrably lack.

## Input / Output

Each benchmark item presents 4 synchronized camera views (single frames for spatial tasks, 6–30 s clips for temporal tasks) resized to 796×448, accompanied by a text question with five multiple-choice options (four candidate answers plus a 'None of the above' option designed to block plausible-sounding guesses). For spatial tasks requiring precise person grounding (Identity Association, Contact Recognition, Body-part Occlusion, Body-part Contact), Set-of-Mark annotations are overlaid on the frames to disambiguate which individual the question refers to. The model outputs a single letter (A–E), scored by exact-match accuracy; if the model fails to produce a valid letter, a secondary LLM (Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite) extracts the closest matching option.

## Method

CVBench is constructed around two enforced design principles verified per item: Single-View Insufficiency (no single camera view contains enough information to answer unambiguously) and Cross-View Resolvability (fusing two or more views makes the answer determinable). Data is pooled from eight multi-view datasets (EgoExo4D, M3GYM, Wild-track, MultiHuman, Human-M3, MvMHAT, MultiviewX, EgoHumans), with 4 orthogonal views manually selected to maximize viewpoint disparity. The 3,000 QA pairs span 12 tasks organized along two axes—spatial (same time, different cameras) and temporal (multiple times, multiple cameras)—each further split into coarse-grained (scene-level) and fine-grained (body-part-level) reasoning. Hard-negative distractors are semi-automatically derived from known failure modes: for counting tasks they are per-view partial counts and naive sums; for identity/contact tasks they are visually similar but distinct individuals; for temporal tasks they are reversed action orders or generic 'smear' verbs. A three-stage quality-control pipeline (peer re-validation of SVI/CVR, human pilot study, and text-only MLLM validation to eliminate linguistically answerable questions) ensures every item is strictly visually grounded and single-view-insufficient.

## Results

On CVBench's 3,000 cross-view questions, the best model (Gemini-2.5-Pro) achieves only 38.6% accuracy on spatial tasks and 34.9% on temporal tasks, falling approximately 56 and 59 points below human performance (94.4% and 93.5%, respectively). The best open-source model, InternVL3-78B, scores 34.8% (spatial) and 32.8% (temporal), while the text-only 'Blind' baseline achieves just 18.5% and 21.3%, confirming that visual input is necessary but far from sufficient. Fine-grained tasks (e.g., Body-Part Contact at 35.4% for Gemini-2.5-Pro) consistently underperform coarse-grained tasks (e.g., Coarse Spatial at 40.9%), and temporal reasoning is uniformly harder than spatial reasoning across all evaluated models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among closed-source models, Gemini-2.5-Pro leads on spatial tasks (38.6% overall) and temporal tasks (34.9%), narrowly edging out GPT-5 (38.4% spatial, 33.7% temporal) and Gemini-2.5-Flash (37.1% spatial, 33.2% temporal). All three closed-source models consistently outperform the best open-source model, InternVL3-78B (34.8% spatial, 32.8% temporal), by 3–6 points, suggesting that proprietary scale and architectural choices provide a meaningful but not decisive advantage. Within the open-source group, the 72B/78B models (InternVL3-78B, Qwen2.5-VL-72B, LLaVA-OneVision-72B) cluster tightly at 33.3–34.8% spatial, while 7B models (DeepSeek-VL2-Small, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, LLaVA-OneVision-7B) trail by roughly 6–7 points. A critical finding is that even the best model (Gemini-2.5-Pro) remains ~56 points below human accuracy on spatial tasks, and the 'Blind' text-only baseline (18.5%) sits only slightly above random (20.0%), confirming that the benchmark is not solvable via linguistic priors. The paper identifies a systemic 'Single-View Bias' as the dominant failure mode (42% of spatial errors for InternVL3-78B, 38.8% for GPT-5), where models cherry-pick the most confident single view rather than performing true cross-view fusion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two previously disconnected lines of work: classical multi-view human analysis datasets (Wild-track, M3GYM, EgoExo4D) that provide synchronized multi-camera data but lack language-grounded evaluation, and modern MLLM benchmarks that assume a 'sufficient-view' paradigm where a single image or clip contains all necessary information. Rather than building on a specific prior method, CVBench repurposes the synchronized data structure of classical multi-view datasets and injects it into the language-grounded, multiple-choice evaluation format of MLLM benchmarks, while enforcing a design principle—verifiable single-view insufficiency—that neither prior line of work addresses. The paper explicitly positions itself as the first to mandate cross-view synthesis rather than merely permitting multi-view input, distinguishing it from multi-image VQA benchmarks (MUIRBench, BLINK) that use uncalibrated, non-contemporaneous images.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~50-point gap between the best MLLM (Gemini-2.5-Pro at 38.6% spatial) and human performance (94.4%) on a task humans find effortless is a stark diagnostic that current architectures lack fundamental mechanisms for geometric grounding, identity persistence, and true spatiotemporal fusion. The identification of 'Single-View Bias' as a dominant, systemic failure mode (40%+ of spatial errors) provides a concrete target for future architecture design, suggesting that simply feeding more views into existing MLLMs is insufficient without explicit cross-view arbitration mechanisms. Open questions include whether the findings generalize beyond 4-view, human-centric scenarios, whether the multiple-choice format adequately captures open-ended cross-view reasoning, and whether the single-view insufficiency constraint can be relaxed to create a difficulty gradient. The benchmark's reliance on a fixed set of 8 source datasets and a 4-view limit also constrains its applicability to real-world deployments with variable camera counts and calibrations.
