## Problem

Mainstream network pruning strategies rely on simplistic criteria such as weight magnitude or local sensitivity, which fail to capture the richer forms of redundancy and interaction present in modern convolutional architectures. No prior work has provided a unified, mathematically rigorous framework that simultaneously characterizes neuron-level functional uniqueness across geometric, informational, and curvature-based perspectives, nor has it offered provable safety margins for NDI-guided elimination.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained CNN, a set of T representative mini-batches (totaling N = nT samples) for activation collection, and a target sparsity ratio ρ. It produces a globally ranked set of per-neuron NDI scores across all layers, from which the lowest-scoring channels are pruned; the output is a compact network with recalibrated BatchNorm statistics and fine-tuned weights. Notably, the Hessian diagonal is estimated via Hutchinson's estimator with Rademacher probes on a small representative mini-batch, and the eigendecomposition of the correlation matrix is approximated via randomized SVD for efficiency at scale.

## Method

The core contribution is the Neural Differentiation Index (NDI), a multiplicative criterion combining three per-channel components: (1) a spectral diversity score derived from the eigendecomposition of the inter-channel correlation matrix, measuring how little a channel's energy concentrates in dominant eigenspaces; (2) an entropy-derived informativeness score from the Shannon entropy of the channel's activation distribution; and (3) a second-order sensitivity score from the Hessian diagonal aggregated over the channel's parameters. Each component undergoes a two-stage normalization—layer-wise robust z-scoring via median and MAD, followed by network-wide calibration mapped through the standard normal CDF—to ensure cross-layer comparability. The NDI is further multiplied by a normalized weight-norm factor to yield a globally comparable importance score, and channels are pruned by global descending ranking. The paper establishes theoretical guarantees including a spectral incoherence bound (Lemma 3.5), a generalization bound under bounded parameter perturbation (Theorem 3.6), and a convergence-stability result under the PL condition (Theorem 3.7), providing provable safety margins for NDI-guided elimination.

## Results

On ResNet-18/Tiny-ImageNet, NDP achieves 72.10% top-1 accuracy at 68.38% sparsity, surpassing the best baseline (NPB, 58.39%) by 13.7 points, and maintains 53.63% at 99% sparsity versus 41.05% for NPB. At 90% sparsity, NDP reduces FLOPs to 2.32×10⁸ (less than half of PHEW's 8.35×10⁸) while achieving 66.32% accuracy versus 55.93% for PHEW. On MobileNet-V2/ImageNet, NDP reaches 71.25% top-1 at 50% pruning and 56.39% at 90% pruning, outperforming UniPTS (69.80% and 42.46%) by 1.45 and 13.93 points respectively. On CIFAR-10 (ResNet-18), NDP attains 94.36% at 75% sparsity and 90.03% at 98% sparsity, exceeding the best competitor (CroPit-S) by 1.66 and 0.92 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NDP wins against all 16+ baselines across every dataset and sparsity level tested. On MNIST (MLP-Net), NDP outperforms MSP (the strongest baseline) by 2.58 points at 50% sparsity (98.55% vs. 95.97%) and by 3.86 points at 98% sparsity (94.59% vs. 90.73%), while SpaM, MP, WF, CBS, and SSP all degrade more sharply under extreme sparsity. On CIFAR-10 (ResNet-18 and VGG-16), NDP beats CroPit-S, EarlyCroP-S, EarlySNAP, and SNAP by 1.5–3.4 points at moderate sparsity, with the gap widening to 1–22 points at 97–98% sparsity (e.g., VGG-16 at 98%: NDP 92.81% vs. SNAP 78.20%). On Tiny-ImageNet, NDP dominates SNIP, Iter-SNIP, SynFlow, PHEW, and NPB by 13–17 points at moderate sparsity and 12–18 points at 99% sparsity, while also achieving the lowest FLOPs at every sparsity level. On ImageNet (MobileNet-V2), NDP surpasses UniPTS (the strongest baseline) by 1.45 points at 50% pruning and by 13.93 points at 90% pruning (56.39% vs. 42.46%), while POT, RigL, and STR collapse to near-chance accuracy at high sparsity. No baseline matches or exceeds NDP on any reported metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new conceptual ground by formalizing 'neural differentiation' as a unified, multi-component criterion (spectral diversity, entropy-based informativeness, and Hessian sensitivity) for neuron importance, rather than relying on a single signal as in magnitude-based, correlation-aware (e.g., CAP), or sensitivity-based (e.g., SNIP, SynFlow) pruning. It repurposes standard mathematical tools—correlation-matrix eigendecomposition, Shannon entropy estimation, and Hutchinson's Hessian estimator—into a single index (NDI) with a two-stage normalization scheme for cross-layer comparability, and wraps the whole construction in theoretical guarantees (spectral incoherence bounds, Lipschitz generalization bounds, PL convergence) that prior pruning heuristics lack. The biological differentiation metaphor is explicitly loose; the contribution is a mathematical unification and a training-time pruning algorithm (NDP) that departs from both initialization-time methods (SNIP, PHEW, SynFlow) and post-training calibration methods (POT, UniPTS).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The NDI framework offers a principled, theoretically grounded alternative to heuristic channel-pruning rules, with provable error bounds that give practitioners safety margins when compressing networks—a property absent from most existing pruning methods. However, the empirical evaluation is restricted to CNNs (ResNet-18, VGG-16, MobileNet-V2), and several comparisons (notably on Tiny-ImageNet) pit a training-time method with fine-tuning against initialization-time or data-free baselines, making the very large accuracy gaps (up to ~14 points) harder to interpret as pure methodological advantage. Key open questions include whether the differentiation framework generalizes to architectures with fundamentally different redundancy structures (Transformers, spiking networks), whether NDI can serve as a training regularizer beyond pruning, and whether the Hessian-based sensitivity component justifies its computational cost at scale.
