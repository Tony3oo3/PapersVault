## Problem

Underwater agents cannot rely on GPS (signals vanish within centimeters of the surface) or acoustic baselines (expensive, limited-range infrastructure), yet sky polarization visible underwater encodes the Sun's daily arc and thus geographic position. State-of-the-art polarization-based geolocation methods achieve reasonable accuracy only at training sites and degrade to ~3,000 km median error at unseen locations, because the same solar elevation produces very different images depending on water turbidity, weather, and marine life. The paper tackles this generalization gap by exploiting the physical fact that solar elevation—not heading or water type—primarily determines the angle-of-polarization pattern.

## Input / Output

The model ingests a 64-frame sequence of radially calibrated angle-of-polarization (AoP) images (computed from Stokes S₀, S₁, S₂ of a monochrome polarization camera with a 360° fisheye lens), paired with per-frame UTC timestamps and a single calendar date. It first outputs a 64-point solar-elevation curve (one angle per frame), then regresses a single (latitude, longitude) pair. The sequence length is fixed at 64 samples spanning sunrise to sunset (mean spacing ≈10 min), and the model is regularized via trajectory dropout to handle shorter or partial arcs encountered in deployment.

## Method

A physics-guided synthesis pipeline splices real AoP frames from six optically diverse sites at matched solar elevations to produce 2.8 million training sequences covering unvisited latitudes, seasons, and water types, treating heading (rigid in-plane rotation) and water optics (contrast scaling) as nuisance parameters that are averaged out. The Polar Transformer is a two-stage architecture: a shallow CNN encodes each AoP frame into a spatial descriptor, which is concatenated with normalized UTC time and a periodic day-of-year vector, then processed by a Vision Transformer encoder whose global state is decoded by an MLP into the solar-elevation curve; the predicted curve and temporal context are then treated as a 64-point cloud and fed to a Point Transformer that regresses latitude and longitude. Training is end-to-end with a combined elevation MSE and cosine location loss, plus a trajectory-dropout regularization that randomly removes contiguous temporal segments to improve robustness to incomplete real-world sequences. Ablations confirm that absolute (rather than relative) attention in the Point Transformer, day-of-year encoding, and the ViT global-context token are each critical for cross-site generalization.

## Results

In the primary cross-site (leave-one-site-out) geolocation task, the proposed Polar Transformer achieves a site-averaged median geodesic error of 513 km across six optically diverse waters, compared to 2,394 km for SecTran-MIM and 3,971 km for RI-ResNet-RDM — an approximately eightfold improvement. In the same-site setting, the method attains a mean geodesic error of 9 km, versus 427 km (SecTran-MIM) and 530 km (RI-ResNet-RDM). For solar-elevation curve reconstruction, the cross-site RMSE averages 4.5° (peaking at 9° in turbid Tampa Bay), while baselines average 18° (RI-ResNet-RDM) and >13° (SecTran-MIM).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two prior deep-learning baselines: RI-ResNet-RDM (Bai et al., eLight 2023) and SecTran-MIM (Bai et al., Optics Express 2024). On the cross-site geolocation task, the proposed method reduces median geodesic error from 3,971 km (RI-ResNet-RDM) and 2,394 km (SecTran-MIM) to 513 km — roughly an 8× improvement over SecTran-MIM and a 7.7× improvement over RI-ResNet-RDM. On same-site geolocation, the gap widens dramatically: 9 km versus 427 km and 530 km, a ~47–59× reduction. For solar-elevation accuracy, the cross-site RMSE of 4.5° is 4× better than RI-ResNet-RDM (18°) and ~3× better than SecTran-MIM (>13°). Notably, both baselines report results on only four of the six test sites (Rio Ceballos and Kona are unavailable), whereas the proposed method evaluates on all six, further underscoring the generalization advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of polarization-based underwater geolocation initiated by Powell et al. (2018) and the deep-learning approaches of Bai et al. (2023, 2024), which used CNN-based (RI-ResNet-RDM) and sectoral-transformer (SecTran-MIM) architectures trained on large frame-level datasets. The key departure is a physics-guided synthesis pipeline that exploits the first-order optical result—solar elevation as the dominant predictor of the angle-of-polarization pattern—to splice frames across optically diverse sites into 2.8 million physically consistent training sequences, rather than relying on exhaustive field collection. Architecturally, the two-stage design (ViT encoder for solar-elevation curve reconstruction followed by a Point Transformer for coordinate regression) repurposes sequence-attention and point-cloud attention mechanisms from NLP and 3D vision to a problem previously handled by per-frame or sectoral CNNs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~8× reduction in cross-site median geodesic error (513 km vs. ~4,000 km) and the 9 km same-site accuracy make camera-only underwater geolocation practically viable for autonomous platforms, removing dependence on acoustic baselines or terrain-matching sonar. The finding that physics-guided synthetic sequences match or slightly exceed real-data training (variants b/c vs. a) suggests a general recipe for data-scarce sensor tasks: identify the dominant physical invariant, use it to recombine observations across conditions, and treat nuisance parameters as augmentation. Open questions include performance at extreme solar elevations (<10°, >80°), equatorial-noon and polar-winter regimes, unusual scattering media (glacial flour, phytoplankton blooms), and whether the approach scales beyond the ~2×10⁶ km² simulation region where accuracy currently degrades.
