## Problem

Aligning video generative models with human preferences requires a temporally-aware reward signal, but existing VLM-based reward models treat videos as collections of independent frames and rely on attention to implicitly capture temporal structure, leading to poor sensitivity to subtle motion quality and temporal coherence. Additionally, these approaches demand large-scale human preference annotations (182K–2000K samples), making them expensive and hard to scale. The paper asks whether a video generative model, which is inherently trained to model temporal dependencies, can be repurposed as a more temporally faithful and data-efficient reward model.

## Input / Output

The model consumes video clips of variable length (2–6 seconds, 4f+1 frames) that are first compressed into f+1 latent frames by the frozen CogVideoX VAE encoder, so all computation operates in latent space rather than pixel space. The discriminative stage outputs a per-latent-time-step energy trajectory (a scalar energy value at each temporal position), while the final reward model produces 21 aspect-wise quality scores (e.g., realism, smoothness, temporal coherence) regressed via MSE, which are then aggregated by a linear head into a single scalar preference score trained under Bradley-Terry (with ties) loss on pairwise comparisons.

## Method

GT-SVJ repurposes the CogVideoX video diffusion transformer as an energy-based discriminative model by attaching a lightweight MLP head to the last third of its transformer layers (adapted via LoRA, rank 8) and training with a contrastive EBM objective that assigns low energy to real videos and high energy to generated and perturbed videos. The key innovation is a set of five controlled latent-space perturbations—frame shuffle, frame drop, noisy segment injection, patch swap, and temporal slice swap—applied to real videos to create challenging hard negatives that prevent the model from exploiting trivial real-vs-synthetic domain gaps and instead force learning of fine-grained spatiotemporal consistency. Sampling probabilities for each perturbation type are calibrated via gradient-decay analysis so that harder-to-detect perturbations receive higher exposure. The trained discriminative model then initializes the reward model, which is fine-tuned in two steps: first regressing 21 human-annotated quality aspects via MSE, then training a linear aggregation head on pairwise preferences under Bradley-Terry-with-ties loss, requiring only 30.4K human-annotated videos.

## Results

GT-SVJ achieves state-of-the-art alignment accuracy on GenAI-Bench (64.26% with ties, 75.15% without ties) and MonteBench (66.36% with ties, 77.76% without ties), outperforming all VLM-based baselines. On VideoReward-Bench, it ranks second (57.01% with ties, 68.57% without ties), trailing VideoReward by ~4–5%. Remarkably, these results are achieved with only 30.4K human-annotated videos—roughly 6× fewer than VideoReward (182K) and 65× fewer than VisionReward (2000K)—while using a 2B-parameter backbone comparable to VideoReward and far smaller than VisionReward (19B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GT-SVJ decisively outperforms VideoScore [11] across all three benchmarks, with the largest gap on GenAI-Bench (64.26 vs. 49.03 with ties, +15.23 points) and MonteBench (66.36 vs. 49.10 with ties, +17.26 points). Against VisionReward [38], which uses 65× more human annotations and a 19B backbone, GT-SVJ still leads on GenAI-Bench (64.26 vs. 51.56) and MonteBench (66.36 vs. 64.00 with ties). The closest competitor is VideoReward [23] (same 2B backbone, 182K annotations): GT-SVJ wins on GenAI-Bench (64.26 vs. 49.41 with ties, +14.85 points) and MonteBench (66.36 vs. 54.20 with ties, +12.16 points), but loses on VideoReward-Bench (57.01 vs. 61.26 with ties, −4.25 points), which the authors attribute to a distribution shift specific to that benchmark. Overall, GT-SVJ achieves the best or second-best result on every benchmark while using an order of magnitude less human preference data than the strongest baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of fine-tuning Video-Language Models (VLMs) like VideoScore, VisionReward, and VideoReward as preference scorers, arguing that their frame-centric attention mechanisms miss subtle temporal dynamics. Instead, it repurposes a video generative transformer (CogVideoX) as the reward backbone, leveraging its native causal self-attention over latent video tokens to capture motion and temporal coherence. The training procedure borrows the two-stage aspect-wise-then-pairwise structure from VideoReward and frames the discriminative pre-training through an energy-based contrastive objective, while the preference head follows the Bradley-Terry formulation standard in RLHF. The key conceptual move is treating a generative model's energy landscape as a quality signal, with synthetic latent-space perturbations (frame shuffle, patch swap, temporal slice swap) providing hard negatives that prevent the model from exploiting trivial real-vs-generated domain gaps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 6×–65× reduction in required human annotations (30K vs. 182K–2000K) makes video reward modeling substantially more accessible, and the finding that a 2B generative transformer can match or beat 8B–19B VLM-based judges on preference alignment challenges the assumption that understanding-oriented architectures are the natural choice for evaluation. However, GT-SVJ still trails VideoReward on its own benchmark by ~5%, lacks a conversational/explanatory interface that VLM judges provide, and does not condition on the generation prompt—both limitations that matter for practical deployment. Open questions include whether the EBM-based discriminative pre-training generalizes to other generative backbones (e.g., DiT-based or autoregressive video models), whether prompt conditioning closes the remaining gap, and whether the perturbation curriculum transfers to longer or higher-resolution videos.
