## Problem

While LoRA is the dominant fine-tuning paradigm for 3D foundation models, it is unclear whether distinct LoRA subspaces exist for different types of 3D data variation (texture, geometry, camera motion, lighting), whether these subspaces are disentangled from one another, and how to compute them effectively. This gap limits the ability to design parameter-efficient fine-tuning strategies that exploit the structure of 3D data.

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-view RGB images (or video frames) as input to a VGGT-based 3D foundation model and produces point clouds, depth maps, and surface normals as output. Unusually, the fine-tuning basis is pre-computed entirely from synthetic data generated by MegaSynth with extreme domain randomization on one attribute at a time, then applied to real-world datasets (micro-baseline face videos, 96-view human scans, transparent object captures) without any real data in the subspace extraction phase.

## Method

The approach generates multiple synthetic datasets where one visual attribute (texture, geometry, camera, or lighting) is varied to the extreme while all others are randomized, then fine-tunes a LoRA adapter (rank 16) on each dataset. From an ensemble of k LoRA adapters per attribute, a shared subspace is extracted by solving a generalized least-squares problem (minimizing weighted Frobenius-norm distance to a common low-rank matrix) via iteratively reweighted least squares, which discards data-specific noise while retaining the attribute-specific component. The extracted subspaces for different attributes are shown to be approximately orthogonal (eigenvector analysis of a generalized eigenvalue problem yields values above 0.5), confirming disentanglement. These orthogonalized subspaces are concatenated into a reduced LoRA basis (dimension d ≪ original rank), so that fine-tuning on new data only optimizes a small mixing matrix M within this pre-computed subspace, reducing trainable parameters from 16–130M (standard LoRA) to 3.8–19.3M while improving reconstruction accuracy.

## Results

On the clothed human reconstruction task (Table 2), the proposed method with d=64 (19.3M trainable params) achieves the best in-domain performance on THuman 2.1 (Acc=2.745, Comp=1.882, NC=91.82) and the best cross-domain performance on 2K2K (Acc=2.513, Comp=1.754, NC=93.56), outperforming LoRA(rank=64) with 3.4× more parameters on most metrics. On 2D face anti-spoofing (Table 1), Ours(d=16) with only 4.0M params achieves Acc=3.831 and Comp=2.037 on synthetic faces, substantially beating LoRA(rank=16) (Acc=5.767, Comp=3.385) and matching full fine-tuning (853.6M params) on completeness. On transparent object reconstruction (Table 3), Ours matches LoRA(rank=16) in parameter count (16.3M) while improving Accuracy from 1.808 to 1.764 and Completeness from 2.522 to 2.462.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LoRA, the proposed method wins decisively on cross-domain 2K2K evaluation: Ours(d=64) achieves Acc=2.513 vs. LoRA(r=64) at 3.017 and Comp=1.754 vs. 1.968, while using only 19.3M vs. 65.3M trainable parameters. On in-domain THuman, Ours(d=64) edges out LoRA(r=64) on Accuracy (2.745 vs. 2.791) and Completeness (1.882 vs. 1.902) but trails slightly on Normal Consistency (91.82 vs. 92.12). Against PiSSA, the advantage is overwhelming: PiSSA degrades monotonically as rank increases (e.g., THuman Acc worsens from 3.009 at r=16 to 4.052 at r=128), whereas Ours improves steadily with d, reflecting that PiSSA's SVD-based initialization overfits to training data. On transparent object reconstruction (Table 3), Ours (16.3M params) beats both LoRA(r=16) and AdaLoRA(r=16) on all three metrics (Acc 1.764 vs. 1.808/1.859; Comp 2.462 vs. 2.522/2.479; NC 73.86 vs. 73.74/72.67), confirming that the precomputed attribute subspaces provide a more informative initialization than either random or gradient-based alternatives at equal parameter budgets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LoRA fine-tuning paradigm (Hu et al., ICLR 2022) to 3D foundation models by asking whether attribute-specific LoRA subspaces exist and can be precomputed from synthetic data. It departs from the LoRA-merging literature in generative models (BLoRA, ZipLoRA, K-LoRA, LiONLoRA), which combines two separately trained LoRAs, by instead extracting a shared subspace from an ensemble of LoRAs tuned on controlled-variation datasets and showing these subspaces are approximately orthogonal across 3D attributes. It also differs from prior LoRA training strategies (PiSSA, AdaLoRA, GaLore) that adaptively control the low-rank subspace during training, by precomputing attribute-aligned subspaces offline and constraining fine-tuning to their span. The base model is VGGT, and synthetic data is generated via MegaSynth with domain-randomization-inspired extreme variation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that geometry, texture, camera, and lighting variations map to approximately orthogonal LoRA subspaces provides a structural interpretation of how 3D foundation models encode distinct visual attributes, which could inform future architecture design and interpretability research. The practical implication—that subspaces derived entirely from synthetic data transfer to real 3D reconstruction tasks with fewer trainable parameters (4–7M vs. 16–130M for LoRA)—is valuable in settings where real 3D data is expensive or privacy-sensitive. Limitations include evaluation on a single base model (VGGT), only four static-scene attributes, and modest (not dramatic) performance gains over well-tuned LoRA at comparable parameter budgets. Open questions include extending to 4D/motion variations, comparing across different 3D foundation models, and combining synthetic and real data for subspace extraction.
