## Problem

Existing video geolocalization methods either classify at coarse city-level granularity or perform fine-grained image retrieval confined to limited regions, and none produce temporally consistent per-frame GPS trajectories at global scale. Frame-by-frame application of image geolocalization models yields jittery, incoherent trajectories that can jump across continents, and the only prior global video effort (CityGuessr) reasons at the whole-video level without enforcing frame-wise consistency.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20261330/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a video as a sequence of frames and produces a per-frame GPS coordinate sequence forming a temporally consistent trajectory. Retrieval is performed against a uniform grid gallery of GPS coordinates (not images), constructed from training data at a configurable resolution (e.g., 0.1 km on MSLS), so the model performs blind GPS-to-GPS matching. Each frame is independently encoded, but temporal context is injected via attention before retrieval, and a second retrieval pass with refined embeddings yields the final coordinates.

## Method

VidTAG uses a dual frame encoder that concatenates CLIP (language-aligned semantics) and DINOv2 (self-supervised appearance) class-token features into a single per-frame embedding. A TempGeo module—a lightweight multi-head self-attention transformer with temporal positional embeddings—aligns all frame embeddings so ambiguous frames borrow context from distant views, enforcing temporal consistency before retrieval. GPS coordinates are encoded via a GeoCLIP-style Location Encoder (Equal Earth Projection + Random Fourier Features + MLPs). A GeoRefiner encoder-decoder then denoises the initial GPS predictions: during training it is fed corrupted ground-truth GPS embeddings (simulating shifts, collapses, jitter) as decoder queries and TempGeo-aligned frame features as encoder context, trained with a weighted hinge loss on similarity matrices; at inference it refines Phase I predictions in a single forward pass before a second gallery retrieval.

## Results

On the primary MSLS dataset, VidTAG achieves 41.0% accuracy at the 1 km threshold (frame-wise) and 39.8% at 1 km (video-wise), with a median distance error of 1.35 km (frame) and 1.49 km (video), substantially outperforming the strongest baseline GeoCLIP-FineTuned (22.5% / 18.6% at 1 km). Trajectory quality metrics confirm the advantage: DFD drops from 22.52 (GeoCLIP) to 3.87 and MRD from 2.82 to 1.07. On GAMa, VidTAG reaches 53.1% at 1 km frame-wise (vs. 28.3% for GeoCLIP-FineTuned), and on CityGuessr68k it attains 94.9% city-level accuracy, surpassing the previous SoTA CityGuessr (69.6%) by 25 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VidTAG outperforms all 13 baselines across every metric on all three datasets. On MSLS, the closest competitor is GeoCLIP-FineTuned (22.5% at 1 km frame-wise), which VidTAG surpasses by ~18.5 percentage points; DINOv2-Classification (18.1%) and GeoDecoder (14.7%) trail further behind. On GAMa, VidTAG leads GeoCLIP-FineTuned by 24.8 points at 1 km (53.1% vs. 28.3%) and achieves a DFD of 0.39 versus 6.50 for GeoCLIP, indicating far smoother trajectories. On CityGuessr68k, VidTAG reaches 94.9% city accuracy, beating the previous SoTA CityGuessr (69.6%) by 25.3 points and the next-best VideoMAE (64.5%) by 30.4 points; it also dominates at state (95.5%), country (96.8%), and continent (98.5%) levels. Notably, classification-based baselines (PlaNet, ISNs, GeoDecoder) and MLLM approaches (Qwen2.5-VL, GeoReasoner) are all far behind VidTAG on CityGuessr68k, confirming that the frame-to-GPS retrieval paradigm with temporal alignment is substantially more effective than whole-video classification or language-model-based geolocation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on constructing 1-Lipschitz neural networks through layer-wise constraints. Previous approaches have focused on individual techniques (spectral normalization, orthogonal parameterization), but no prior work systematically compared them under identical training protocols. The unified algebraic perspective from recent work [15] informs the analysis, showing that several methods share common mathematical foundations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This systematic comparison provides practitioners with clear guidance on which 1-Lipschitz construction method to choose based on their constraints (training budget vs. certified accuracy). The finding that several methods are mathematically equivalent suggests future work should focus on truly novel approaches rather than incremental variations. Open questions include whether these methods scale to deeper networks and larger datasets like ImageNet.
