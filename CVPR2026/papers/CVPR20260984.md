## Problem

Existing visual trackers operate in a non-interactive, fire-and-forget manner, assuming a fixed target initialized once and tracked autonomously. In real-world scenarios—sports analysis, surveillance, UAV monitoring—users need to dynamically switch targets, correct drift, or refine focus mid-video, but no benchmark or method supports this human-in-the-loop interaction with natural language commands at arbitrary timestamps.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260984/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes RGB video frames (averaging 947 frames per sequence across 150 videos), an initial bounding box, and 4–5 natural-language prompts inserted at critical interaction moments (initialization, drift correction, target switching, part-level refinement). Output is a per-frame axis-aligned bounding box and continuous tracking trajectory, with the tracker required to respond to each prompt by either re-localizing, switching targets, or confirming its current state. Evaluation divides each video into interaction segments at prompt boundaries, scoring perception accuracy, switch responsiveness, tracking stability, and overall interactiveness separately.

## Method

IMAT integrates three modules: (1) an Interactive Perception Module (IPM) built on the Rex-Omni MLLM that performs vision-language grounding to produce a semantically aligned box from the user's natural-language prompt; (2) a Memory-Augmented Visual Tracker (MAVT) extending SAM2 with two external memory banks—a positive bank storing verified target embeddings and a negative bank retaining distractor/failed-prediction embeddings—enabling adaptive appearance learning and distractor suppression; (3) a Cognitive Arbitration Module (CAM) that compares the tracker's prediction against the IPM's grounded box via IoU and center-displacement thresholds, deciding whether to re-initialize the tracker (adding the failed embedding to negative memory and the corrected one to positive memory) or reinforce the current state. This bidirectional update mechanism—positive reinforcement for consistent frames and negative learning for failures—allows the tracker to progressively improve robustness and semantic alignment across the interaction sequence without retraining.

## Results

On the InteractTrack benchmark, IMAT achieves the best scores across all four evaluation dimensions: Interactiveness (45.25%), Responsiveness (41.20%), Perception Accuracy (52.78%), and Tracking AUC (45.86%). The closest competitor, Sa2VA (RVOS), reaches 44.81% Interactiveness and 38.99% Responsiveness but drops sharply on tracking (AUC 24.14%). Among VOT trackers, MCITrack leads with 40.38% Interactiveness and 44.98% AUC, yet still trails IMAT by ~5 points in interactiveness. The ablation confirms each component contributes: removing IPM, memory, or CAM each reduces Interactiveness by 3.2–3.8 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IMAT outperforms all 24 baselines on every metric. In Interactiveness, the gap over the strongest competitor Sa2VA (44.81%) is modest at 0.44 points, but in Responsiveness IMAT leads by 2.21 points (41.20% vs. 38.99%), reflecting superior target-switching accuracy. Among VOT trackers, MCITrack is the closest on tracking AUC (44.98% vs. 45.86%) yet lags by 4.87 points in Interactiveness, confirming that strong autonomous tracking does not transfer to interactive settings. VOS-based methods (VL-SAM2, DAM4SAM) achieve competitive Interactiveness (44.43–43.19%) but their Responsiveness (37.62–37.72%) is 3.5+ points below IMAT, indicating difficulty adapting to sequential prompts. RVOS methods (Sa2VA, VideoLiSA) show the weakest tracking capability (AUC 24.14% and 22.77%), revealing that language-grounded segmentation models sacrifice temporal stability. VLT trackers (SUTrack, DUTrack) balance perception and tracking reasonably well but remain 4–5 points behind IMAT in Interactiveness, highlighting the benefit of the dynamic memory mechanism for sustained adaptation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the autonomous tracking paradigm (VOT, VOS) and the one-shot language-grounded paradigm (VLT, RVOS) by introducing a continuous, real-time human-in-the-loop tracking task where natural-language commands can be issued at arbitrary frames. Architecturally, it builds on SAM2 for spatiotemporal consistency and Rex-Omni (an MLLM) for vision-language grounding, but the key conceptual contribution is the dynamic positive/negative memory mechanism that enables the tracker to learn from sequential user feedback rather than relying on a fixed template. The Cognitive Arbitration Module, which decides when to trust the tracker versus the language-grounded result, has no direct analogue in prior tracking or interactive segmentation literature, which typically operates offline or in a single pass.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that state-of-the-art autonomous trackers and even language-aware RVOS methods fail to transfer to interactive scenarios—quantifies a gap that the field has informally acknowledged but never measured. The InteractTrack benchmark and its four-dimensional evaluation protocol (Perception, Responsiveness, Tracking, Interactiveness) provide the first systematic tool for assessing this gap, which will be essential as tracking systems move toward real-world deployment with human oversight. Open questions include whether the 150-video scale is sufficient for robust conclusions, how the approach scales to longer sequences with many interaction points, and whether the MLLM-based grounding can operate at real-time latency in practice.
