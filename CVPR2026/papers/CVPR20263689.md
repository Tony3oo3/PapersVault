## Problem

Existing correspondence-free RKHS-based registration methods (e.g., CVO) use isotropic kernels that ignore local surface geometry, limiting alignment quality along surface normals. Additionally, these methods rely on first-order Riemannian gradient ascent solvers that are prohibitively slow for real-time applications such as high-performance driving odometry, where convergence must complete within ~100 ms per frame.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263689/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a target point cloud X and a source point cloud Z, each optionally annotated with pose-invariant features (LiDAR intensity, RGB color, or pixel labels), plus an initial SE(3) pose estimate T^(0) (e.g., from a constant-velocity model). Point clouds are voxel-downsampled before registration (0.25 m for KITTI, 2 m for the racing dataset, ~3000 points for ETH3D). The output is a refined relative SE(3) transformation T that aligns Z to X, produced by iterative on-manifold optimization without any explicit point-to-point correspondence step.

## Method

G-CVO represents each point cloud as a continuous function in an RKHS using point-wise anisotropic exponential kernels whose covariance Σ(x,z) is estimated from the empirical second-moment of n nearest neighbors, making the kernel tight along surface normals and relaxed along tangential directions (with eigenvalue bounding for numerical stability). The registration objective—maximizing the RKHS inner product between the two point-cloud functions—is solved on SE(3) via a Riemannian Gauss-Newton scheme: closed-form Riemannian gradients are derived, and an approximate Riemannian Hessian is constructed by retaining only the dominant Jacobian-transpose-weighted term plus the left-invariant connection correction, yielding a 6×6 linear system per iteration. This second-order solver converges in ~30 iterations versus >1200 for the first-order variant, achieving a >10× wall-clock speedup while matching or exceeding the accuracy of ICP-based baselines (GICP, Fast-VGICP, NDT) and the original CVO across LiDAR, RGB-D, and object registration benchmarks.

## Results

On KITTI (11 urban driving sequences), G-CVO-2 achieves a mean translational error of 1.39 m and rotational error of 0.0069 rad, outperforming CVO (1.97 m / 0.0116 rad) and ICP (8.91 m / 0.0305 rad) while matching Fast-VGICP on rotation (0.0060 rad). On the feature-sparse racing dataset, G-CVO-2 reduces mean translational drift to 4.32 m versus 11.53 m for Fast-VGICP and 34.59 m for ICP, a >55% reduction over ICP. On ETH3D RGB-D, G-CVO-2 achieves the best mean APE (0.191 m) and second-best RPE (0.0086 rad). On ModelNet40 object registration, G-CVO-2 achieves 0.27 m / 0.0024° under mild noise, surpassing GeoTransformer (0.65 m / 0.0088°), and further improves GeoTransformer's output to 0.35 m / 0.0014° when used as a refinement step under 20% outliers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

G-CVO-2 outperforms all correspondence-based ICP variants (ICP, GICP, NDT) by a wide margin on KITTI, reducing mean translational error from 8.9–71.4 m to 1.39 m, with the largest gains on feature-sparse highway sequences 01 and 04. Against Fast-VGICP, G-CVO-2 achieves lower translational error (1.39 vs 1.83 m) but is marginally worse on rotation (0.0069 vs 0.0060 rad); on the racing dataset the gap widens substantially (4.32 vs 11.53 m trans, 0.0139 vs 0.0359 rad rot). Compared to the original CVO (1st-order), G-CVO-2 improves both translation (1.39 vs 1.97 m) and rotation (0.0069 vs 0.0116 rad) on KITTI, and the second-order solver alone (CVO with isotropic kernels + GN) already halves the error on racing (9.90 vs 34.59 m vs ICP). Against GeoTransformer on ModelNet40, G-CVO-2 wins under mild noise (0.27 vs 0.65 m) but degrades under 30% cropping (32.78 vs 4.24 m) due to the absence of learned invariant features; however, using G-CVO-2 to refine GeoTransformer's global estimate yields the best overall accuracy (0.35 m / 0.0014°) under 20% outliers, demonstrating complementary strengths.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CVO/SemanticCVO line of RKHS-based correspondence-free registration by replacing isotropic Gaussian kernels with spatially varying anisotropic kernels derived from local point covariance, effectively encoding point-to-plane and point-to-edge awareness without explicit correspondences. The optimization side departs from the first-order Riemannian gradient ascent used in prior RKHS methods by introducing a second-order Riemannian Gauss-Newton solver with an approximate Hessian, drawing on inexact GN schemes from nonlinear optimization. Conceptually, the anisotropic kernel bridges the gap between correspondence-free RKHS methods and the geometric-structure-aware residuals (point-to-plane, NDT) that have driven practical LiDAR odometry, unifying both perspectives within a single kernel-based objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10× convergence speedup from second-order optimization makes correspondence-free RKHS registration viable for real-time driving applications (10 Hz with 4k points on a single GPU), a regime where prior first-order RKHS methods were prohibitively slow. The >55% drift reduction in feature-sparse racing environments addresses a practical gap where ICP-based methods fail due to unreliable correspondences. Key limitations include the GPU dependency for dense pairwise kernel evaluation, the assumption of sufficient overlap (local registration only), and the self-collected racing dataset limiting external reproducibility. Open questions include integration into full odometry/SLAM pipelines, extension to low-overlap or large-motion settings, and incorporation as differentiable layers in learned registration frameworks.
