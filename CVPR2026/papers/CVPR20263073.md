## Problem

Once deployed, Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies have frozen weights and cannot acquire new knowledge to improve task performance in the wild. Existing retrieval-augmented methods either require updating the backbone, operate on static offline corpora, or define retrieval over compact state abstractions rather than the rich vision-language interface of modern VLAs, leaving no simple mechanism for a frozen unified policy to improve purely through memory growth at deployment time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263073/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each step, EFN consumes the current frame's VLA vision-encoder token features (L×C), the frozen VLA's raw action a_t^(0), a retrieved experience tuple (retrieved frame features, retrieved action, retrieved successor frame features), and a rollout-level instruction embedding. It outputs a residual Δa_t in the same action space as the base policy (e.g., latent action space for UniVLA), which is added to a_t^(0) to yield the executed control. Visual keys for retrieval are constructed via per-token ℓ₂-normalization followed by mean-max fusion, producing a compact unit vector of dimension C.

## Method

EFN wraps a frozen VLA with a lightweight residual controller trained by Soft Actor-Critic. A live experience bank stores synchronized (instruction embedding, visual key, base action) tuples per step; at inference, retrieval is two-stage—first filtering rollouts by instruction-embedding cosine similarity, then selecting a step-level match via visual-key cosine similarity with a probabilistic top-k sample biased by an efficiency prior that favors shorter trajectories. The residual is trained with a dense similarity-shaped reward that compares the realized next observation to the retrieved successor frame in feature space, augmented with anti-idling penalties and a residual-magnitude regularizer, so the agent learns to steer its next frame toward 'what happened next' in the recalled experience. Crucially, all backbone and EFN weights remain frozen at deployment; adaptation arises solely from appending successful rollouts to the bank, making the system a retrieval-conditioned, weight-frozen VLA that improves purely through memory accumulation.

## Results

On the LIBERO benchmark, EFN (Vol=1000) improves average success rate by up to 10.5 points over the frozen base policy (OpenVLA: 76.5% → 87.0%; UniVLA: 95.2% → 97.2%; GO-1: 94.7% → 96.7%) while also reducing average steps. The largest gains appear on the Long-horizon suite, where OpenVLA+EFN jumps from 53.7% to 76.5% success. On the real-world AgiBot-G1 platform, EFN (Vol=1000) raises average success from 25.8% (GO-1 alone) to 70.2% and cuts episode time by 7.9% (17.6 s → 16.2 s) despite a 4.2% per-step latency overhead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EFN consistently outperforms all four non-frozen baselines across every backbone. Against ResAct (residual-only, no retrieval), EFN gains 2.8–4.8 points in average success (e.g., 87.0% vs. 79.2% on OpenVLA) and reduces steps by 1.7–11.5, showing that retrieval-conditioned guidance adds substantial value over unconditioned residual correction. Against R2A (retrieval-augmented RL that updates the backbone), EFN leads by 1.4–5.4 points in success while keeping the backbone frozen, indicating that the frozen-residual paradigm is more sample-efficient under the same interaction budget. GC-TTT (goal-conditioned test-time training) yields negligible or negative gains (e.g., 77.4% vs. 76.5% on OpenVLA; 94.7% vs. 95.2% on UniVLA), confirming the instability of aggressive online finetuning on non-i.i.d. rollouts. The retrieval-only kNN-RAG baseline degrades performance on all three backbones (e.g., 73.2% vs. 76.5% on OpenVLA), demonstrating that naive action copying is brittle without a learned correction layer. On the real-world AgiBot-G1 platform, EFN (Vol=1000) achieves 70.2% average success versus 39.5% for R2A and 31.2% for ResAct, with the gap widening on harder tasks (DrawerStore: 58.7% vs. 14.0% and 8.0% respectively).

## Relation to Prior Work

EFN sits at the intersection of retrieval-augmented reinforcement learning (R2A), residual policy learning, and episodic memory in embodied control. It departs from prior retrieval-augmented methods in three key ways: it keeps the VLA backbone frozen (whereas R2A and GC-TTT update backbone weights), it maintains a live experience bank that grows during deployment (rather than a static offline corpus), and it operates in the rich vision-language embedding space of modern VLAs rather than compact state abstractions. The training signal—a dense similarity-shaped reward comparing the realized next frame to the retrieved successor—draws on goal-conditioned RL and world-model ideas but is applied here purely as a shaping signal for a lightweight SAC-trained residual actor, without requiring supervised residual labels or a learned dynamics model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that EFN offers a deployment-time adaptation mechanism that avoids the cost and instability of retraining or fine-tuning large VLA models, which is a major bottleneck for real-world robotics. The real-world results (25.8% → 70.2% average success on AgiBot-G1) suggest the approach is particularly valuable when the base policy is weak, though simulation gains are more modest. Open questions include how the method behaves on truly novel tasks with no relevant prior experience in the bank, how the experience bank scales to long-horizon or highly variable environments, and whether the similarity-shaped reward generalizes beyond manipulation to locomotion or multi-agent settings. The reliance on a pre-populated bank also means the method cannot bootstrap from zero experience.
