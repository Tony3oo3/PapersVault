## Problem

Existing Embodied Multi-Agent Systems (EMAS) rely on static architectures with fixed agent-task assignments determined at initialization, making them unable to adapt when agents join, drop out, or recover during task execution. This rigidity leads to suboptimal resource utilization, unhandled failures, and degraded performance in the dynamic, unpredictable conditions that characterize real-world multi-agent deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20263842/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes task specifications, real-time agent positions and workload capacities, and distributed visual observations of object locations across the environment. It produces a one-to-many agent-task assignment (each agent handles at most two tasks via the Dual-Capacity Hungarian algorithm), per-agent action execution plans grounded in predicted spatial locations, and an ordered trust-chain backup sequence for every assigned task. The collective intelligence layer outputs a learned probability distribution P(o, p) over object locations, which feeds predicted coordinates back into the affinity computation for scheduling.

## Method

DRAMA introduces a three-layer architecture that unifies agents and tasks as resource entities for adaptive orchestration. The Strategic Layer computes a weighted affinity matrix combining agent workload availability and predicted spatial distance, then solves a one-to-many assignment via a Dual-Capacity Hungarian Algorithm (DCHA) that duplicates each agent into two virtual slots; it further constructs hierarchical trust chains where agents are ranked by affinity and serve as ordered guardians for fault-tolerant takeover. The Collective Intelligence Layer aggregates all agents' visual observation histories into a shared spatial memory and trains an MLP to approximate the joint object-location distribution P(o, p), enabling predictive spatial priors that generalize beyond explicitly observed samples. The Autonomous Layer gives each agent dual executor/guardian roles: the executor runs a perception-action loop with hierarchical memory management, while the guardian monitors peer heartbeats and triggers decentralized task reallocation upon detected failures, enabling event-triggered rescheduling without a central controller.

## Results

On the VirtualHome-Social embodied benchmark, DRAMA achieves the lowest Average Steps (AS) across all scenarios, reaching 46.70 in the 5-agent static setting and 52.80 in the cascading dropout (5→4→3) scenario, compared to 47.68–49.45 for the best baselines. It attains the highest Throughput (TP) of 0.252 (static-5) and 0.216 (dropout 5→4→3), representing a 10–20% gain over existing methods. Among dynamic scheduling frameworks, DRAMA also records the lowest Conflict Rate (CR), e.g., 0.083 (static-5) and 0.076 (dropout 5→4→3). Critically, DRAMA is the only framework that successfully completes both agent-dropout and agent-recovery scenarios, where all five baselines fail entirely.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DRAMA outperforms all four baseline families on Average Steps and Throughput across every tested scenario. Against the closest dynamic competitor ProAgent, DRAMA reduces AS by roughly 4–8% (e.g., 52.80 vs. 54.96 in dropout 5→4→3) and increases TP by 3–5% (0.216 vs. 0.208). AV-Dynamic is competitive on AS in static settings (47.97 vs. 46.70) but degrades sharply under addition (61.34 vs. 53.37) and still trails DRAMA on TP. MCTS achieves the lowest CR in static settings (0.044) due to its rigid predefined scheduling, but suffers the worst AS (67.26) and TP (0.174) in static-5, and its throughput collapses to 0.105 under cascading dropout. Most decisively, DRAMA is the only method that successfully completes the Dropout and Recovery scenarios; CoELA, MCTS, ProAgent, AV-Static, and AV-Dynamic all fail outright in these settings, as confirmed in Table 2.

## Relation to Prior Work

DRAMA extends the line of LLM-based embodied multi-agent systems (AgentVerse, CoELA, ProAgent, MASTER) by replacing their static or periodic task-assignment paradigms with an event-triggered, affinity-driven scheduling mechanism built on the Dual-Capacity Hungarian Assignment algorithm, which itself adapts the dynamic Hungarian algorithm of Mills-Tettey et al. [24]. The key conceptual departure is the unified resource abstraction that treats both agents and tasks as schedulable entities, enabling one-to-many assignment with a per-agent capacity of two, combined with a hierarchical trust-chain for decentralized fault recovery. The collective intelligence layer (an MLP predicting spatial object-location priors) is a lightweight addition that feeds predicted task locations back into the affinity computation, distinguishing DRAMA from purely rule-based or search-based planners like MCTS.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in demonstrating that a single framework can handle the full spectrum of dynamic agent-population changes (dropout, addition, recovery, replacement) in an embodied setting, where all baselines fail on at least one scenario. However, the evaluation is confined to VirtualHome-Social with a maximum of five agents, and the spatial-prediction component is a simple MLP trained on a single environment, leaving open whether the approach scales to larger agent populations, more complex 3D environments, or real-world robotic deployments. The moderate gains (7–17% in steps, 10–20% in throughput) suggest the architecture is sound but that further improvements may require richer spatial models or learned scheduling policies beyond combinatorial optimization.
