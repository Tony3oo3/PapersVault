## Problem

Video highlight detection for movies and TV dramas lacks both a suitable large-scale benchmark and a viable training pipeline: cinematic highlights are inherently subjective and genre-dependent, making manual clip-level annotation costly and inconsistent, while existing datasets target user-generated or sports content with structured highlight patterns. No prior method can train an effective detector on cinematic content without expensive human labels.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262354/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes long-form movie or TV drama videos split into 2-second non-overlapping clips, with visual features extracted via CLIP ViT-L-14 concatenated with SlowFast R-50 action features and audio features from the ImageBind encoder. The LTRM network outputs a continuous highlight score in [0,1] per clip. During the collaborative training stage, an MLLM (MiniCPM-V 2.6) generates per-clip text descriptions that are fed to an LLM (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen) to produce noisy soft pseudo-labels, which are then refined through the iterative loop.

## Method

LTV-HD trains a lightweight Local Temporal Relation Multimodal Network (LTRM) in two stages. Stage 1 uses Multiple Instance Learning with video-level labels and top-K pooling to initialize the model without any clip-level supervision. Stage 2 enters an iterative collaborative loop with three components: (1) LLM-guided Label Generation, where an MLLM describes each clip and an LLM applies chain-of-thought reasoning to assign noisy highlight scores; (2) Noisy Label Cleaning, which adaptively updates the pseudo-label distribution via KL-divergence classification loss, a compatibility loss anchoring updated labels to the original noisy ones, and a sharpness loss preventing premature convergence at suboptimal intermediate values; and (3) Highlight Pattern Induction, which distills genre-specific highlight patterns (e.g., 'intense fight scenes' in wuxia dramas) from high-confidence model predictions and feeds them back to guide cleaner label generation in the next round. The LTRM architecture itself combines bottleneck-token cross-modal attention (from UMT) with local self-attention windows (from Swin Transformer) to model short-range temporal relations between adjacent clips.

## Results

On the newly introduced TVHighlights dataset, LTV-HD (Stage 2) achieves 92.74% AUC and 71.20% AP overall, substantially outperforming the strongest MLLM baseline Qwen-vl-max (CS) at 88.40% AUC / 47.42% AP and the best dedicated VHD method UMT at 60.50% AUC / 45.13% AP. Even the Stage 1 weakly-supervised model alone reaches 62.39% AP, already surpassing all baselines. On YouTube Highlights (clean), the method achieves 75.8% average mAP, competitive with SOTA UVCOM2 (77.4%) and UniVTG (75.2%), while under noisy MTurk labels it degrades only 0.32% versus 1.54% for UMT, demonstrating superior noise resilience.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On TVHighlights, LTV-HD (Stage 2) wins decisively across all baselines: it outperforms the strongest MLLM (Qwen-vl-max CS) by +4.34 AUC and +23.78 AP, and the best dedicated VHD method (UMT) by +32.24 AUC and +26.07 AP. The gap is especially large on the Emotion category, where LTV-HD reaches 60.00% AP versus 41.38% for Qwen-vl-max (CS) and 31.99% for UMT, reflecting the difficulty of detecting nuanced emotional highlights. On clean YouTube Highlights, the method is competitive but slightly trails UVCOM2 (75.8% vs 77.4% mAP) and UniVTG (75.8% vs 75.2%), while matching or exceeding UMT (75.8% vs 74.9%). Under increasing label noise (Harvested-Borderline and MTurk), LTV-HD consistently outperforms UMT, with the advantage widening to +2.12 mAP under MTurk noise, validating the NLC module's denoising capability. MLLM baselines (TimeChat, Qwen-vl-max MR, Gemini MR) perform poorly under the moment-retrieval paradigm due to temporal localization errors, with AP dropping 10–15 points compared to their per-clip scoring counterparts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the video highlight detection literature from sports and user-generated content to the cinematic domain, where highlights are subjective and lack structured patterns. Architecturally, it borrows the bottleneck-token cross-modal encoder from UMT [24] and local self-attention windows from Swin Transformer [25], while the weakly-supervised pre-training stage follows the MIL formulation of MINI-Net [15]. The key departure is the iterative collaborative loop between a lightweight detector and LLMs, which repurposes the label-refurbishing machinery of Yi & Wu [47] into a self-improving cycle where the model's high-confidence predictions feed back to guide LLM-generated pseudo-labels through genre-specific pattern induction—a feedback structure not present in prior noisy-label or MLLM-based VHD work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The TVHighlights dataset (1,721 videos, 15 genres) fills a clear gap: no prior benchmark targets cinematic highlight detection without query guidance or manual clip-level annotation, and the community-voting annotation strategy offers a scalable template for other subjective video tasks. The human-free training pipeline is practically important for domains where expert annotation is prohibitively expensive or inconsistent. Open questions include whether the two-round refinement loop scales to more iterations or genres, whether the single unified model generalizes to unseen categories, and whether the pattern-induction mechanism transfers to non-cinematic domains. The dataset's forthcoming release and the reliance on specific MLLM/LLM backends (MiniCPM-V, DeepSeek-R1) limit immediate reproducibility.
