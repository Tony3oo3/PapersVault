## Problem

LiDAR system design is a complex, largely manual process that must navigate a high-dimensional, mixed discrete-continuous space (sensor count, placement, scanning patterns, time gates, FoV) while satisfying task-specific performance goals and physical constraints. No prior method provides a continuous, differentiable representation of LiDAR configurations that can be learned for a target task and then rapidly re-synthesized under arbitrary post-hoc constraints without retraining.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260087/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a class of task-specific scenes encoded as signed distance functions (e.g., 50 Basel face meshes, 40 MuJoCo robot trajectories, 100 warehouse scenes) plus user-specified design constraints (sensor count, placement bounds, FoV limits, time-gate ranges, total ray budget) as input. It outputs a G-component Gaussian mixture model over the 6D design space (x, y, z, φ, ψ, τ), where each Gaussian encodes one sensor's origin, angular spread, and time-gating window; discrete rays are then sampled within each sensor's 95% confidence bounds, with ray allocation proportional to mixture weights. Constraints are applied post-hoc by truncating the density support or clamping GMM parameters, requiring no retraining of the flow model.

## Method

The core contribution is representing every LiDAR measurement as a point in a continuous 6D design space (spatial origin, ray direction, time-of-flight) and learning a task-specific implicit density over this space via autoregressive normalizing flows (K layers of MLP-conditioned rational-quadratic splines). The target density is defined as the product of a surface-proximity term (Gaussian of the SDF at the ray's observed scene point) and a ray-visibility term (transmittance integral from volumetric rendering), summed over the scene class, so that high-density regions correspond to rays that hit visible surfaces frequently. The flow is trained by minimizing reverse KL divergence between the learned density and this target, with an entropy regularizer for sampling diversity. To generate physically realizable systems, sensors are modeled as a G-component Gaussian mixture in 6D space and fitted to the learned implicit density via expectation-maximization, where the E-step computes component posteriors and the M-step updates means, covariances, and mixing weights. This formulation enables constraint-aware synthesis: spatial/angular/temporal limits truncate the density support, FoV and time-gate bounds clamp diagonal covariance entries, and sensor count is set by the mixture order G.

## Results

Across three 3D vision tasks, the proposed method consistently outperforms uniform and random sampling baselines while dramatically reducing bandwidth. For face scanning (576 rays), the 10-sensor design achieves 2.3 Mbps vs. 15.2 Mbps for baselines (~6× reduction) with lower Chamfer distance. For robotic tracking (1200 rays), the 8-sensor design reaches 14.0 Mbps vs. 32.2 Mbps (~2× reduction) with lower Fréchet distance. For warehouse detection (18 rays), the method achieves 0.6 Mbps vs. 5.8 Mbps (~10× reduction) with lower miss rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: uniform ray sampling over origin and angle with fixed time gating, and random sampling over origin and angle. The proposed method wins on all three tasks across every sensor-count and ray-budget setting. In face scanning, the learned designs reduce bandwidth by ~6× (e.g., 2.3 vs. 15.2 Mbps at 576 rays) while achieving lower Chamfer distance, with the 10-sensor configuration allocating a dedicated nose sensor for fine geometry. In robotic tracking, the method achieves ~2× lower bandwidth (14.0 vs. 32.2 Mbps at 1200 rays) and lower Fréchet distance by balancing spatial sampling, FoV, and time-gating across distributed ceiling-mounted sensors. In warehouse detection, the motion-adaptive design reduces bandwidth by ~10× (0.6 vs. 5.8 Mbps at 18 rays) while improving miss rate through location-conditioned elevation and time-gate adaptation. The uniform and random baselines perform identically to each other, confirming that the gains come from task-driven density learning rather than sampling strategy alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the implicit neural representation (INR) paradigm—exemplified by NeRFs and occupancy networks, which learn continuous densities over visual signal spaces—into the domain of LiDAR hardware design, representing sensor configurations as implicit densities over a continuous 6D space (origin, direction, time-of-flight). The generative backbone is a composition of autoregressive rational-quadratic spline flows (Durkan et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2018), while the ray-visibility term directly adapts the transmittance integral from volumetric rendering (Max, 2002). It departs from prior LiDAR placement work (which handles only scanning LiDARs on AVs) and next-best-view methods (which assume fixed hardware) by unifying flash, gated, and motion-adaptive LiDARs in a single continuous design space, and from joint sensor-perception optimization methods by supporting arbitrary post-hoc constraint adjustment without retraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled, differentiable route to automated LiDAR system design that can incorporate physical constraints (sensor count, FoV, time-gating, bandwidth) without retraining, which is practically valuable for R&D pipelines in robotics, mobile devices, and autonomous systems. The real-world validation on a single-photon LiDAR setup, showing comparable reconstruction quality to conventional co-located designs with substantially lower bandwidth, strengthens the case beyond simulation-only evaluation. Key limitations include reliance on accurate scene simulations (performance may degrade outside the simulated distribution) and infeasibility of direct evaluation on pre-captured datasets like KITTI, since the flow model requires active querying of the continuous 6D space. Scaling to large-scale outdoor and AV settings, and reducing the simulation-to-reality gap, remain important open questions.
