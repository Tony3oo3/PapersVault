## Problem

Medical image representation learning is limited by the scarcity of paired multimodal biomedical data (pathology, genomics) due to privacy constraints and invasive acquisition, while existing dual- and tri-modality alignment methods suffer from modality-induced bias and cross-modal noise that degrade representation quality. Additionally, prior LLM-based approaches generate generic, class-level descriptions that lack patient-specific grounding, weakening text-image correspondence. KAMP addresses how to learn rich, robust medical image representations under scarce paired data by using LLM-derived personalized knowledge as a semantic anchor that stabilizes cross-modal alignment.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes 3D medical images (encoded via a Swin-UNETR-based encoder into patch tokens), patient clinical text, imaging metadata, H&E whole-slide pathology images, and three genomic tabular sources (RNA-seq, mutation, CNV). The LLM generator takes only the imaging metadata and clinical text as conditioning to produce P structured observation targets; the cross-modal aligner then fuses these targets with image tokens via cross-attention (targets as queries, patches as keys/values) to form a knowledge-aware embedding, while the biomedical branch fuses raw clinical text, pathology, and genomics via a HEALNet-inspired fusion layer. Downstream evaluation uses only the frozen image-only embedding h_img (average-pooled patch tokens without knowledge fusion) with a linear probe for few-shot classification at 3–20 shots per class.

## Method

KAMP is a three-stage pipeline coupling an LLM-based knowledge generator with a two-branch cross-modal aligner. In Stage 1, an off-the-shelf GPT-5 generates patient-specific structured observation targets conditioned on imaging metadata and clinical text; a symmetric contrastive loss aligns both the knowledge-aware embedding h (text targets cross-attend over image patches) and the image-only embedding h_img with the multimodal biomedical embedding z. In Stage 2, the aligner is frozen and serves as a reward model (cosine similarity between h and z) while a trainable Qwen3-8B generator is optimized via GRPO with group-relative advantages over G sampled candidates, using LoRA updates to sharpen knowledge toward modality-consistent, clinically salient content. In Stage 3, the refined LLM is frozen and the aligner is retrained on the policy-optimized knowledge distribution with the same contrastive objective, tightening alignment and suppressing modality noise. The information-theoretic justification is that the knowledge anchor A reduces the conditional entropy H(h|z) by I(h;A|z), preferentially reinforcing image cues jointly supported by the anchor and biomedical evidence while suppressing spurious modality-specific artifacts.

## Results

KAMP achieves the best macro-AUC across nearly all few-shot settings on three cancer datasets. At the 20-shot setting, KAMP reaches 70.2% on BraTS23-MEN (3-class meningioma grading), 82.6% on FedBCa (2-class bladder cancer subtyping), and 74.5% on TG-LIVT (2-class microvascular invasion prediction), surpassing the strongest baselines by 8.1%, 6.5%, and 7.5% respectively. KAMP also shows a favorable scaling trend from 3-shot to 20-shot, whereas several baselines (e.g., DRIM, RadCLIP) saturate or degrade at higher shot counts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KAMP outperforms all single-modality baselines (VoCo, Swin-UNETR, MiM) by a wide margin across every dataset and shot setting, with the largest gaps on BraTS23-MEN (up to +12.7% over MiM at 20-shot) and TG-LIVT (up to +22.2% over Swin-UNETR at 20-shot). Against dual-modality methods, KAMP surpasses HLIP by 5.5–10.8% and RadCLIP by 11.7–17.3% at 20-shot, indicating that the knowledge-anchored approach provides substantially richer supervision than image-text or image-genomics contrastive alignment alone. The closest competitor is the tri-modality method DRIM, which slightly edges KAMP at 3-shot on FedBCa (75.6% vs. 74.3%) but is outperformed by KAMP at all higher shot counts (e.g., 82.6% vs. 74.8% at 20-shot on FedBCa; 74.5% vs. 67.0% on TG-LIVT). Notably, DRIM exhibits high variance and performance degradation at intermediate shots (e.g., 54.7% at 5-shot on BraTS23-MEN), whereas KAMP maintains a stable, monotonically improving trend from 3 to 20 shots on all three datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

KAMP carves new ground at the intersection of LLM-enhanced medical vision and multimodal contrastive pretraining. It departs from prior LLM-guided medical imaging work (BiomedCoOp, MedUnA, UniMed-CLIP) by conditioning on patient-specific clinical text and imaging metadata rather than class-level semantics, and from tri-modality alignment (DRIM) by inserting an LLM-generated textual knowledge anchor that mediates alignment between the imaging and biomedical branches. The three-stage pipeline—off-the-shelf LLM pre-alignment, GRPO-based generator refinement with the frozen aligner as reward model, and final aligner retraining—repurposes reinforcement learning (specifically GRPO) in a novel way: the aligner itself becomes the reward signal for tuning the knowledge generator, creating a closed-loop between representation learning and text generation that has no direct precedent in the medical imaging pretraining literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

KAMP addresses a practical bottleneck in medical imaging: pathology and genomics are available for far fewer patients than imaging, so pretraining must extract maximum semantic value from scarce paired data. The knowledge-anchor formulation provides a principled information-theoretic justification for why LLM-derived text stabilizes cross-modal alignment under modality bias and noise, and the GRPO refinement step offers a general recipe for aligning a text generator to a downstream representation objective. Limitations include evaluation restricted to three cancer datasets (brain, bladder, liver), reliance on TCGA for pretraining, and an in-house liver dataset (TG-LIVT) that limits reproducibility. Open questions include whether the paradigm extends to non-cancer imaging, whether the three-stage pipeline scales to additional modalities (e.g., proteomics, functional imaging), and whether the GRPO reward signal remains informative as the aligner improves.
