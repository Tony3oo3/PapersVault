## Problem

Existing dynamic reconstruction methods either produce only novel-view-synthesis results without explicit geometry, or represent the entire object as a single continuous dynamic mesh that cannot capture part-based structure, contact, separation, or hinged articulation. While primitive-based representations have proven effective for static scene decomposition, their potential for modeling dynamic structured objects—where true structure is often revealed only through motion—remains unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a calibrated monocular image sequence with per-frame object masks, camera intrinsics/extrinsics, and timestamps. It outputs a set of K superquadric primitives (each parameterized by shape exponents ε₁,ε₂, scales s₁,s₂,s₃, a 6D rotation, and a 3D translation) with 3D Gaussians bound to their surfaces for appearance, plus a learned deformation network that maps primitives between canonical and per-frame observation spaces. The structured output enables part-level editing and motion transfer, unlike monolithic mesh outputs from prior methods.

## Method

D-Prism represents the dynamic object as a set of differentiable superquadric primitives whose vertices are mapped from a unit icosphere via a closed-form superquadric function, ensuring full differentiability with only a handful of continuous parameters per part. 3D Gaussians are bound to each primitive's surface (with rotation and scale derived from local geometry) to provide high-quality appearance while the primitive mesh supplies structured geometry. A deformation network with positional encoding predicts per-frame motion deltas (ΔT, ΔR) that transform primitives from a canonical space to each observation timestamp, enabling joint optimization of motion and shape. A dynamic adaptive control strategy—comprising clone, merge, and prune operations applied at regular intervals—autonomously adjusts the primitive count and distribution to balance completeness and redundancy, with the inverse deformation network ensuring canonical-space consistency after merges. Training uses a composite loss combining 3DGS rendering loss, mask rasterization loss, overlap and parsimony regularizers, a volume penalty, and deformation regularizations (temporal smoothness, translation magnitude, and forward-backward consistency), followed by a refinement stage that unfreezes Gaussian parameters while fixing primitive poses.

## Results

On the Dynamic Primitive Dataset, D-Prism achieves a mean EPE of 0.019 across six structured objects, substantially outperforming the best baseline (DG-Mesh at 0.063 mean EPE). The structured motion tracking metric δ3D^0.10 reaches 0.990 on average, compared to 0.749 for the next-best method (DG-Mesh). On geometry reconstruction (Table 2), D-Prism achieves the lowest CD_d on 5 of 6 objects (e.g., 3.237×10⁻³ on Rubik's Cube vs. 9.873 for DG-Mesh) and the best EMD_d on 4 of 6. On general D-NeRF scenes, D-Prism trades ~2 dB PSNR against DG-Mesh but achieves lower LPIPS (0.034 vs. 0.045 on Jumpingjacks), indicating better perceptual quality while providing a structured geometric representation that NVS baselines cannot offer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

D-Prism decisively outperforms all five geometry/tracking baselines (DG-Mesh, Ub4D, MovingParts, SP-GS, Shape of Motion) on the structured motion tracking metric across 5 of 6 objects, with the largest margins on high-motion cases: Rubik's Cube (EPE 0.063 vs. 0.174–0.586) and Treasure Box (EPE 0.006 vs. 0.069–0.553). The closest competitor is DG-Mesh, which slightly edges D-Prism on Pliers (EPE 0.010 vs. 0.014) but is surpassed on all other objects by 2–30× in EPE. On geometry (CD_d/EMD_d), D-Prism wins on 5/6 objects, with a dramatic 29× improvement over Ub4D on Door (2.777 vs. 81.168). On general D-NeRF rendering, D-Prism trails DG-Mesh by ~2–3 dB PSNR (e.g., 29.07 vs. 31.77 on Jumpingjacks) but achieves consistently lower LPIPS (0.034 vs. 0.045), and unlike all NVS baselines (D-NeRF, K-Plane, HexPlane, TiNeuVox) it provides an explicit structured geometric representation. The NVS-only methods (D-NeRF, K-Plane, HexPlane, TiNeuVox) are not directly comparable on motion metrics since they do not output geometry, but their rendering quality is 3–8 dB below D-Prism on PSNR.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends two previously separate lines of work: static primitive-based scene decomposition (DBW, PartGS, GaussianBlock) and dynamic geometry reconstruction (D-NeRF, DG-Mesh, Ub4D). Static primitive methods recover part-based structure but cannot handle motion, while dynamic methods model temporal change but treat objects as monolithic meshes or radiance fields, failing on articulated or multi-part objects. D-Prism bridges this gap by binding 3D Gaussians to superquadric primitives and driving their motion through a deformation network, effectively transplanting the structured representation paradigm into the dynamic domain for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework enables part-level editing, motion transfer between objects, and structured scene understanding from a single video—capabilities unavailable from monolithic dynamic reconstructions. The introduced Dynamic Primitive Dataset and structured motion tracking metric provide the field with the first dedicated benchmark for evaluating part-aware dynamic reconstruction. However, the method remains sensitive to initialization, struggles with complex topologies (e.g., torus) and slender structures, and the evaluation is limited to six synthetic objects plus a few D-NeRF sequences, leaving open whether the approach generalizes to real-world articulated objects with dense part interactions.
