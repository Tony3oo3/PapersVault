## Problem

Long-term individual-level behavioral monitoring of wild animals requires solving multiple computer vision tasks (re-identification, action recognition, keypoint estimation) simultaneously, yet existing datasets are either task-specific or lack re-identification annotations. Additionally, traditional task-level benchmarking does not reveal how errors propagate to biologically meaningful measurements like feeding rates and co-occurrence, leaving users without guidance for deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260190/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

CORVID consumes 1-second video clips (25 frames, 1920×1080) from standardized feeding videos, a database of known ring color combinations, and territory metadata constraining the candidate set to 2–5 individuals. It outputs a per-clip identity assignment derived from a probability matrix over all possible ring-pair color combinations, pooled across the 25 frames. The CHIRP dataset itself provides 16,190 re-id clips, 1,387 action clips, 1,176 keypoint instances, and 12 fully annotated test videos with frame-by-frame bounding boxes, IDs, and feeding events.

## Method

CORVID is a three-stage pipeline that identifies birds purely from their colored leg rings without requiring a learned appearance gallery. First, a Mask2Former instance segmentation model detects individual ring masks; each mask is cropped, converted to HSV color space, and resized to 20×20. Second, a random forest multi-class classifier predicts per-color confidence scores for each ring, treating the problem as multi-class to handle visually similar colors. Third, a matching algorithm pairs rings by centroid distance threshold, sums probability scores for each color-pair combination across all 25 frames, and selects the identity with the highest score from the territory-constrained candidate list. This design makes the method generalizable to new individuals as long as their ring combination is registered, unlike embedding-based re-id methods that require retraining.

## Results

On the video re-identification task, CORVID achieves 0.66 Top-1 accuracy (0.96 Top-3) on the closed-set split with the within-territory constraint, substantially outperforming pre-trained MegaDescriptor (0.28 Top-1) and fine-tuned MegaDescriptor (0.27 Top-1). On the disjointed split with the same constraint, CORVID reaches 0.69 Top-1 (0.97 Top-3) versus 0.31 for pre-trained MegaDescriptor. However, when the gallery is expanded to all 183 individuals, CORVID drops sharply to 0.05 Top-1, below both MegaDescriptor variants (0.10). In the application-specific benchmark, the CORVID-based pipeline achieves 0.647 proportion correct frames and 0.537 peck F1, outperforming the MegaDescriptor pipeline (0.617 and 0.408) and random assignment (0.331 and 0.327), though all remain well below the human benchmark (0.910 correlation on feeding rates).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline compared is MegaDescriptor from the Wildlife Datasets toolkit [Čermák et al., WACV 2024], evaluated both pre-trained on other animal datasets and fine-tuned on CHIRP. CORVID decisively outperforms both MegaDescriptor variants under the biologically realistic within-territory constraint (0.66 vs. 0.28 Top-1 on closed-set; 0.69 vs. 0.31 on disjointed), with a gap of roughly 38–39 percentage points. This advantage persists when neighbours are added to the gallery (0.29 vs. 0.19 Top-1). However, when the gallery is expanded to all 183 individuals, CORVID collapses to 0.05 Top-1, underperforming both MegaDescriptor variants (0.10), indicating the ring-color matching strategy is not scalable to large open populations without additional appearance-based cues. In the application-specific pipeline benchmark, CORVID also beats fine-tuned MegaDescriptor on all lower-level metrics (proportion correct frames: 0.647 vs. 0.617; peck F1: 0.537 vs. 0.408) and on higher-level biological measures (feeding rate mean error: 9.0 vs. 13.14; co-occurrence correlation: 0.654 vs. 0.557), though both remain far from the human benchmark (mean error 1.88; correlation 0.910).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two trends in wildlife computer vision: (1) the shift from single-task animal datasets (detection, pose, or re-id in isolation) toward multi-task datasets curated from real biological studies (Animal Kingdom, LoTE-animal, ChimpACT, 3D-POP), and (2) the growing call for application-driven evaluation that goes beyond per-task metrics [8, 52]. For re-identification specifically, it departs from the dominant image-appearance-based approach (CNN classifiers on bird crops, as in IndividualBirdID [20] or WILD [64]) and from barcode/fiducial-marker detection [42, 45, 46, 53] by exploiting the color combinations of plastic leg rings already fitted to wild birds, a feature that encodes identity without requiring a learned appearance gallery. The application-specific benchmark, which evaluates pipelines on biologically meaningful outputs (feeding rates, co-occurrence rates) rather than only per-task accuracy, is a new evaluation paradigm not present in prior animal datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CHIRP is the first multi-task dataset for a wild, long-term bird population that jointly supports re-id, action recognition, keypoint estimation, and segmentation, and its application-specific benchmark offers a reusable template for evaluating whether a CV pipeline is actually sufficient for a biological use case rather than merely accurate on individual sub-tasks. The finding that a simple color-ring detection pipeline (CORVID) can outperform a deep metric-learning foundation model (MegaDescriptor) when domain constraints (territory membership) are available suggests that lightweight, domain-informed methods may be preferable to heavy general-purpose models in constrained field deployments. Key limitations include the single-species scope, the heavy dependence of CORVID on territory constraints (performance collapses without them), and the fact that random ID assignment still beats MegaDescriptor on several higher-level biological metrics, indicating substantial room for improvement before these pipelines are deployable in practice.
