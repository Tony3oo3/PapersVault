## Problem

Classifiers possess invariants in the null space of their linear layers that produce equivalent input sets mapping to identical outputs, yet existing methods can only identify the existence of these invariant directions without explaining what they semantically represent. This gap limits practitioners' ability to diagnose which class-relevant information leaks into the invariant space and how vulnerable a model is to semantics-preserving perturbations that leave logits unchanged.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the last fully-connected layer weight matrix (for SVD decomposition), penultimate-layer feature vectors (10k per model across 1,000 ImageNet classes for translator training), and open-vocabulary text prompts for semantic queries. Outputs include quantitative Attribute Score (AS) and Image Score (IS) values, per-class and model-level leakage statistics, and qualitative visual renderings of equivalent pairs generated via UnCLIP for illustration only. All quantitative claims rely on CLIP embeddings and logit invariance, not on the synthesized images.

## Method

SING decomposes the classifier head W ∈ R^{c×m} via SVD to extract the null-space projector Π_n, then trains a linear translator T_Θ mapping the classifier's penultimate features into CLIP's image embedding space using MSE plus weight decay. Equivalent pairs are constructed either by null-removal (f̃ = f − Π_n f) or by directed perturbations: the CLIP-similarity gradient toward a target text prompt is projected onto the null space and applied as a normalized step, guaranteeing unchanged logits by construction. Semantic change is quantified via two angular metrics in CLIP space—Attribute Score (angle shift relative to a class prompt) and Image Score (angle between original and equivalent features)—enabling statistical comparison at model, class, and individual-image levels. The key insight is that the linear translator's additivity (T(f+v) = T(f) + T(v)) makes it naturally compatible with the additive null-space decomposition, and mapping into a vision-language space converts abstract null directions into human-readable concept descriptions and visual examples.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative result (Table 1) measures |AS| (Attribute Score) toward a target prompt after null-space perturbation calibrated to IS=40°. DinoViT achieves the lowest |AS| of 5.0±0.59°, indicating the greatest resistance to directed null-space semantic manipulation, while ResNet50 (12.04±0.25°) and EfficientNet (12.38±0.52°) are most susceptible. At the model level, DinoViT attains the highest IS/AS ratio (Figure 3b), meaning it permits broad non-class-dependent invariance (high IS) while maintaining tight class-semantic consistency (low AS). The translator is validated with a Pearson correlation of 0.972 between classification performance before and after translation to CLIP space.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SING is an interpretability framework rather than a competing method, so the paper's comparisons are across five architectures (DinoViT, ResNet50, EfficientNet-B4, BiTResNetv2, ResNext101) evaluated on the same ImageNet-1k protocol. DinoViT (self-supervised DINO pretraining) consistently outperforms all other architectures: it achieves the lowest |AS| in directed null-space steering (5.0° vs. 9.2–12.4° for others), the highest IS/AS ratio in the model-level comparison, and the smallest per-class AS magnitudes (typically |AS|<1°). ResNext101 (weakly supervised) is the most vulnerable, showing high AS with substantial variance, interpreted as class-dependent semantic leakage into its null space. ResNet50 and EfficientNet occupy the middle-to-high leakage range, with ResNet50 exhibiting pronounced per-class imbalance (e.g., Porcupine and Sports-Car leak substantially more). The paper notes no significant correlation between per-class AS rank orderings across models, confirming the effect is architecture-dependent rather than driven by dataset class structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

SING sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: (1) SVD-based decomposition of classifier heads to identify null-space invariants (Anthes et al., Li & Short, Cook et al.), which could locate invariant directions but never assigned them semantic meaning; and (2) CLIP-based interpretability methods (Text2Concept, CounTEX, CLIP-Dissect) that project features into a vision-language space but exclusively operate on the active/principal subspace. The paper's key departure is bridging these two: it trains a linear translator (following Moayeri et al. and justified by Lähner & Moeller) to map null-space perturbations into CLIP's image embedding, enabling the first systematic semantic labeling of invariant directions. Unlike prior null-space work that relied on task-specific data to demonstrate invariance, SING provides a data-agnostic, open-vocabulary diagnostic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SING offers a practical, lightweight diagnostic for practitioners to audit what semantic content their classifiers have inadvertently placed in the null space, with direct implications for robustness (classes with high AS are more vulnerable to adversarial manipulation) and for choosing architectures for specific deployment domains. The security finding—that null-space steering can shift an image's semantics toward an arbitrary concept (e.g., 'jellyfish') while leaving logits unchanged—highlights a previously underappreciated attack surface. Limitations include the focus on the final linear layer only, dependence on CLIP's vocabulary for semantic grounding, and the linear translator assumption; open questions include whether null-space control during training (directed augmentation, projector regularization) can reduce leakage, and whether the framework extends to intermediate layers or non-linear heads.
