## Problem

Existing hierarchical classification methods confine each class's representation to a single direction (its weight vector), limiting angular separation for fine-grained classes and failing to adapt learning capacity to the inherent complexity of different classes in imbalanced hierarchies. Moreover, no prior framework can simultaneously perform hierarchy-aware multi-class classification and hierarchical multi-level classification while guaranteeing hierarchical consistency. The paper also identifies that standard evaluation metrics (MS, AHD@k) are permutation-invariant and thus fundamentally incapable of measuring the ranking quality of top-k predictions against the hierarchy's partial preference order.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image, extracts a d-dimensional feature vector z via a pretrained backbone (ResNet-50 or ViT), and maps it through a lightweight transformation module into an n-dimensional vector x in V_T, where n equals the total number of nodes in the hierarchy tree. The output x is sparse: only components along basis vectors corresponding to the true class's ancestor path, itself, and descendants are non-zero. At inference, the predicted class is obtained by selecting the leaf subspace with maximum projection norm, and multi-level predictions are read directly from the non-zero components at each hierarchy level, guaranteeing a valid tree path.

## Method

Hier-COS constructs a Hierarchy-Aware Vector Space by assigning one orthonormal basis vector to each node in the hierarchy tree, then defining each class's subspace as the span of basis vectors for its ancestors, itself, and descendants. A lightweight transformation module (adapted from HAFrame) maps backbone features into this space, trained with a KL-divergence loss between an exponentially weighted target distribution over the hierarchy path and the softmax of absolute feature magnitudes, plus an L1 sparsity regularization enforcing that only the correct path's dimensions are active. The exponential weighting w_l = exp(1/(h+1−l)) monotonically increases from root to leaf, implicitly allocating greater discriminative capacity to fine-grained classes that share more ancestors. The framework is theoretically proven to be an HAVS (Theorem 1) and to produce hierarchically consistent multi-level predictions (Proposition 1) without requiring explicit consistency constraints in the loss. The paper additionally introduces HOPS, a rank-order-based evaluation metric that normalizes the weighted absolute difference between desired and predicted preference orderings, resolving the permutation-invariance flaw of AHD@k.

## Results

On FGVC-Aircraft, Hier-COS achieves 81.74% top-1 accuracy, 81.56% Full Path Accuracy (FPA), and 0.89 HOPS, improving FPA by 3.64% over the best baseline. On CIFAR-100, it reaches 77.79% accuracy, 77.37% FPA, and 0.93 HOPS; on iNaturalist-19, 71.15% accuracy, 69.93% FPA, and 0.96 HOPS. The gap between top-1 accuracy and FPA (a proxy for hierarchical consistency) is smallest for Hier-COS across all three datasets, and the method also reports consistent gains in AHD@k and Mistake Severity over all baselines. With a frozen ViT backbone, Hier-COS improves top-1 accuracy by 2.42% over the HAFrame baseline on iNaturalist-19.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Flamingo-I/II (Chang et al., CVPR 2021), Hier-COS wins decisively on HOPS (0.89 vs 0.58 on FGVC-Aircraft, 0.93 vs 0.57 on CIFAR-100, 0.96 vs 0.47 on iNaturalist-19) and FPA, while matching or slightly exceeding top-1 accuracy (81.74% vs 80.71% on FGVC-Aircraft). Against HAFeat-I/II (Garg et al., ECCV 2022), Hier-COS dominates on every metric across all three datasets, with the most dramatic gap on iNaturalist-19 FPA (69.93% vs 33.36% for HAFeat-I). Compared to HAFrame (Liang & Davis, ICCV 2023), Hier-COS improves top-1 accuracy by 1.2%, 0.26%, and 0.68% on FGVC-Aircraft, CIFAR-100, and iNaturalist-19 respectively, and boosts FPA by 3.64%, 1.36%, and 1.51%. Against HXE+CRM (Karthik et al., ICLR 2021), Hier-COS achieves lower AHD@k and higher HOPS, indicating better hierarchical ordering of top-k predictions. The Barz & Denzler (WACV 2019) baseline is outperformed on both MS and AHD metrics, though it remains competitive on raw top-1 accuracy for some settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on HAFrame (Liang & Davis, ICCV 2023), which maps backbone features to a fixed hierarchy-aware frame via a lightweight transformation module, but departs fundamentally by replacing the 1D weight-vector directions with orthogonal subspaces per class (Hier-COS). This subspace construction is motivated by the observation that existing methods confine each class's representation to a single direction, limiting angular separation for fine-grained classes. The paper also positions itself against multi-head architectures (Flamingo, HAFeat) and specialized loss functions (Bertinetto et al., Garg et al.) by offering a unified framework that simultaneously performs hierarchy-aware multi-class and hierarchical multi-level classification without auxiliary classifiers or explicit consistency constraints. The introduction of HOPS as a ranking-based evaluation metric represents a departure from the LCA-distance-based metrics (MS, AHD@k) that have dominated the field.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that AHD@k is permutation-invariant and thus fundamentally incapable of distinguishing correct from incorrect top-k prediction orderings is a significant critique that could reshape how the community evaluates hierarchical classifiers. The implicit hierarchical consistency (guaranteed by the orthogonal subspace geometry rather than enforced by additional loss terms) and the automatic adaptation of learning capacity based on hierarchy position are practically useful properties that simplify training. Open questions include scalability to very large hierarchies (beyond 12 levels), whether the orthogonal subspace construction degrades with extreme imbalance, and whether the HOPS metric will gain community adoption over the entrenched AHD/MS metrics. The method's reliance on a known, fixed hierarchy tree also limits its applicability to open-vocabulary or dynamically evolving taxonomies.
