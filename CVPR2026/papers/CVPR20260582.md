## Problem

Existing MLLM approaches for visual emotion understanding rely on supervised fine-tuning with fixed label taxonomies, yielding poor generalization and uninterpretable pattern-matching reasoning. While GRPO-based reinforcement learning improves generalization, its generated reasoning traces are task-agnostic and emotionally incoherent, and the loose coupling between reasoning and answer in subjective emotional tasks (unlike math or code) means answer-only supervision cannot effectively constrain the thinking process.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260582/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives an RGB image paired with a text prompt and produces a structured reasoning trace consisting of three explicit stages—Emotional Trigger Identification, Human Emotional Reflection, and Emotional Conclusion—followed by a final emotion label enclosed in \boxed{}. The structured format is enforced during training via a format reward, and the reflection module (used only at training time) re-queries the model with subsets of its own reasoning to produce binary or label-based self-assessments.

## Method

EMO-R3 augments GRPO with two task-specific components: (1) Structured Emotional Thinking (SET), which constrains the model's reasoning into three interpretable stages mirroring human affective appraisal, and (2) Reflective Emotional Reward (RER), which feeds the model's own reasoning back into itself to compute two self-evaluation signals—image-text consistency (does step 1 accurately describe the image?) and emotional coherence (do steps 1–2 logically lead to the ground-truth emotion?). The overall GRPO reward is a weighted combination of accuracy, RER (averaged over the two reflective signals), and format correctness, with balancing coefficients λ₁ and λ₂. An optional lightweight Cold-Start-Emo stage (a few SFT examples without CoT annotations) is used to align the model's emotional priors with the target label system before RL training, mitigating reward sparsity caused by subjective bias.

## Results

On the Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct base model, EMO-R3 achieves the highest overall accuracy (A) of 60.50% (4-rollout) and 60.42% (8-rollout), outperforming GRPO (59.97% / 59.16%) and DAPO (58.28% / 56.91%). In-domain accuracy (A^I) reaches 73.10% (4-rollout) and 74.06% (8-rollout), while out-of-domain accuracy (A^O) reaches 54.20% and 53.60% respectively. SFT, despite strong in-domain performance (77.15% on EmoSet^I), collapses out-of-domain (A^O = 29.09%), highlighting the generalization gap that EMO-R3 addresses. DAPO fails to complete training due to a mismatch between its filtering strategy and the discrete emotional reward structure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EMO-R3 consistently outperforms GRPO on overall accuracy by ~1.2–1.3 points in both rollout settings (60.50 vs. 59.97 at 4-rollout; 60.42 vs. 59.16 at 8-rollout), with the gap widening on out-of-domain accuracy (A^O: 54.20 vs. 53.59 and 53.60 vs. 52.42). Against DAPO, EMO-R3 leads by 2.2–3.5 points on overall accuracy, and DAPO additionally fails to complete training due to reward sparsity from its filtering mechanism conflicting with binary emotional labels. Compared to SFT, EMO-R3 dramatically improves out-of-domain generalization (A^O: 54.20 vs. 29.09), confirming that RL-based optimization avoids the overfitting to fixed label taxonomies inherent in supervised fine-tuning. The training-free SEPM baseline on LLaVA1.5-7B (A = 50.88%) is substantially outperformed by EMO-R3 on Qwen2.5-VL-3B (A = 60.50%), though the two use different base models. The ablation (Table 3) further confirms that both SET and RER contribute incrementally: SET alone adds ~0.3 points over GRPO, and adding RER yields an additional ~0.6 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the GRPO reinforcement learning framework (originally developed for mathematical reasoning in DeepSeekMath/DeepSeek-R1) to the specific challenges of visual emotion understanding, where the tight think-answer coupling that makes GRPO effective in math or code does not hold. It departs from prior emotion-focused MLLM work (EmoVIT, Emotion-LLaMA, AffectGPT, EmoLLM) that relies on supervised fine-tuning with fixed label taxonomies, and from prior GRPO-for-emotion attempts (AffectGPT, R1-Omni) that the authors characterize as superficial adaptations lacking task-specific reward design. The two key departures are the Structured Emotional Thinking prompt (a three-stage reasoning scaffold) and the Reflective Emotional Reward, which feeds the model's own reasoning back into itself to score visual-text consistency and emotional coherence—neither of which appears in prior GRPO formulations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a genuine structural mismatch between standard GRPO's answer-verification mechanism and the subjective, context-dependent nature of emotional reasoning, and proposes a targeted fix. However, the empirical gains over vanilla GRPO are modest (roughly 0.5–1.3 points in overall accuracy), experiments are limited to a single 3B base model and three small datasets of 2,000 samples each, and the reflective reward adds training-time computation without inference-time cost. Open questions include whether the structured thinking scaffold and reflective reward generalize to video, audio, or interactive emotion settings, and whether the approach scales to larger models or more diverse emotional taxonomies beyond the three tested.
