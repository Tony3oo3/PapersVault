## Problem

Prior analyses of MLLMs focus on how visual features serve the language task, but the intrinsic cost of language-driven training to visual representations themselves remains unexplored. This paper diagnoses a pervasive visual representation degradation in intermediate LLM layers—both a drop in global classification capability and a blurring of patch-level semantic boundaries—and attributes it to a visual sacrifice made to optimize the singular next-token prediction objective.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20262941/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes an RGB image (encoded by a frozen or trainable vision encoder such as CLIP-ViT-L/14@336 or SigLIP2-ViT-SO400M/14@384 into N_p patch tokens) and a text prompt, and produces a free-form text response. The PRe mechanism operates entirely on internal representations: a 2-layer MLP prediction head consumes the degraded visual hidden states from a single intermediate LLM layer (layer 16 for Vicuna-7B, layer 14 for Qwen-2.5-7B) and must reconstruct the stop-gradiented initial projected visual tokens (H_v^0) that entered the LLM, using per-patch cosine similarity as the matching criterion.

## Method

PRe (Predictive Regularization) augments the standard next-token prediction loss with a self-supervised consistency objective inspired by predictive coding. A lightweight 2-layer MLP prediction head is attached to an intermediate LLM layer's visual hidden states and trained to predict the initial projected visual tokens (the features fed into the LLM), with the anchor stop-gradiented to prevent collapse. The total loss is L_total = L_LM + λ·L_PRe with λ=0.5, and the regularization is applied at the patch level rather than on globally pooled features, providing a fine-grained supervisory signal that preserves local spatial structure. The method is architecture-agnostic, requiring no changes to the vision encoder, projector, or LLM backbone, and is validated across multiple LLMs (Vicuna, Qwen) and vision encoders (CLIP, SigLIP) with both frozen and trainable encoders.

## Results

PRe consistently improves vision-language performance across all tested MLLM configurations (Vicuna-7B and Qwen2.5-7B with CLIP or SigLIP encoders). The most notable gains appear on vision-centric and OCR tasks: MMVP improves by +2.0 to +3.4 points, OCRbench by +5 to +16 points, and RealWorldQA by +0.2 to +2.4 points. On general benchmarks, GQA gains range from +0.2 to +1.8, MMStar from +0.0 to +4.3, and TextVQA from +0.5 to +1.3. A few minor regressions (≤0.9 points) occur on MMMU and MMStar for Qwen+CLIP(frozen), but the overall trend is positive across 6 out of 6 architectural configurations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare PRe against other external methods; instead, the baseline is the identical MLLM architecture trained without the predictive regularization loss. PRe wins on the majority of benchmarks across all six LLM+encoder configurations, with the largest consistent gains on vision-centric tasks (MMVP: +2.0 to +3.4) and OCR tasks (OCRbench: +5 to +16). The most striking single improvement is MMStar jumping from 30.3 to 34.6 (+4.3) for Vicuna+CLIP(frozen), suggesting that preserving patch-level visual structure particularly benefits fine-grained visual discrimination. A few minor regressions appear: Qwen+CLIP(frozen) drops 0.7 on MMMU and 0.9 on MMStar, and Qwen+CLIP(trainable) drops 0.7 on MMVP, indicating that the regularization can occasionally conflict with language optimization in certain configurations. Overall, the self-comparison confirms that the visual degradation identified in the diagnostic analysis is a real performance bottleneck, and that counteracting it via PRe yields net positive gains without requiring architectural changes or additional training data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior MLLM interpretability work (attention analysis, probing studies) that focused exclusively on the cross-modal utility of visual features—how well they serve text generation—by instead measuring their intrinsic visual fidelity. Conceptually, it repurposes the predictive coding principle from neuroscience and self-supervised learning (JEPA, SimSiam) not as a pretext task for representation learning, but as a lightweight regularizer that anchors degraded intermediate features to their initial visual state. The architectural setting follows the standard LLaVA paradigm (frozen/trainable vision encoder + projector + LLM), and the diagnostic tools (linear probing, patch-level cosine similarity, logit lens) are borrowed from existing interpretability literature, but the specific question of what language-driven training costs the visual representation is posed and answered for the first time here.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, multi-level diagnostic framework (global linear probing + patch-level semantic contrast + pre-training dynamics) that makes the visual-language trade-off in MLLMs measurable and actionable, which is valuable for anyone designing or debugging multimodal systems. The PRe fix is architecturally trivial (a 2-layer MLP head with stop-gradient), making it easy to adopt, though the observed gains are modest (typically 0.2–4.3 points across benchmarks). Open questions include whether the degradation is an unavoidable consequence of autoregressive training or can be eliminated entirely with stronger visual objectives, whether the findings extend to video MLLMs and larger-scale models, and whether the mid-layer 'visual sacrifice' is truly a deliberate optimization strategy or an emergent side effect that could be mitigated at the architecture level.
