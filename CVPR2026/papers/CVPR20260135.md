## Problem

Existing gait recognition datasets provide at most 7 clothing variations per subject (CCPG), making it impossible to validate whether models truly learn cloth-invariant features under extensive wardrobe changes. Meanwhile, prior synthetic human datasets fail to preserve gait identity consistency—either reusing identical motions across different subjects or assigning diverse walking patterns to the same subject—so they cannot serve as reliable cross-clothing benchmarks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20260135/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a temporal sequence of binary silhouettes (N×C×H×W), 2D pose keypoint maps, or skeleton heatmaps, and outputs a fixed-length identity embedding for cosine-similarity-based matching. The dataset additionally provides RGB frames and 3D joint/mesh ground truth, but the primary recognition pipeline operates on silhouettes or heatmaps. Evaluation follows standard gait protocols reporting Rank-1 accuracy and mAP, with a novel thickness-stratified protocol (THK0–THK9) that bins clothing by silhouette-area difference to measure performance degradation as garments become bulkier.

## Method

BarbieGait maps each of 521 real subjects to a MakeHuman virtual character via dual alignment (skeleton bone lengths + 12 SMPL circumference parameters), applies 100 random outfits, and transfers the subject's original 3D pose through quaternion-based kinematic motion matching (Algorithm 1) to preserve gait identity across all clothing states. For recognition, GaitCLIF modifies the DeepGaitV2 backbone by replacing standard normalization with GON (Gait-Oriented Normalization), which computes mean and standard deviation jointly across all channels and spatial dimensions within each horizontally segmented region of the feature map—removing cloth-specific statistics while retaining fine-grained spatial motion cues that per-channel Instance Normalization would destroy. Two temporal variants (GON-P3D, GON-3D) add 3D convolutions for frame-level cloth-invariant modeling, and a GON-FC head applies GON after each fully-connected layer to suppress clothing variance at the sequence level after temporal pooling.

## Results

On BarbieGait (the primary benchmark with 100 clothing variations per subject), GaitCLIF-3D achieves 80.4% R1 and 65.7% mAP on average across thickness levels, outperforming the strongest prior appearance-based method DeepGaitV2-3D (71.7% R1, 60.2% mAP) by +8.7% R1 and +5.5% mAP. The heatmap-based variant GaitCLIF-P3D* reaches 78.1% R1 and 73.3% mAP, surpassing SkeletonGait (77.1% R1, 72.3% mAP). On real-world datasets, GaitCLIF improves over DeepGaitV2 on all four benchmarks: CCPG (+1.9% R1, +2.3% mAP), SUSTech1K (+2.4% R1, +1.1% R5), Gait3D (+2.1% R1, +2.1% mAP), and GREW (+2.5% R1, +0.3% R5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GaitCLIF-3D outperforms all appearance-based baselines on BarbieGait, with the largest gap over DeepGaitV2-3D (+8.7% R1, +5.5% mAP) and GaitSet (+70.7% R1, +52.9% mAP), confirming that GON-based normalization is critical under extreme clothing variation. Among pose-based methods, GaitCLIF-P3D* (heatmap input) edges out SkeletonGait by +1.0% R1 and +1.0% mAP, while GPGait trails significantly (59.1% vs. 78.1% R1), suggesting that dense pose representations benefit more from GON than sparse keypoint graphs. On CCPG, GaitCLIF improves over DeepGaitV2 by +1.9% R1 and +2.3% mAP under the ReID protocol, and on SUSTech1K by +2.4% R1 and +1.1% R5. For in-the-wild datasets with limited clothing variation (Gait3D, GREW), the paper uses only GON-FC (without GON-P3D) to avoid excessive intra-class divergence, still achieving +2.1% R1 on Gait3D and +2.5% R1 on GREW over DeepGaitV2. Notably, GaitCLIF also improves upstream pose estimation: retraining ViTPose on BarbieGait yields 83.0% R1 on CCPG, surpassing SkeletonGait by +19.5% R1 and DPGait by +3.9% R1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the DeepGaitV2 (OpenGait) architecture, replacing its standard normalization with a gait-specific GON module while retaining the P3D/3D visual stages, temporal pooling, and FC head structure. It departs from prior synthetic human datasets (SURREAL, SynBody, GTA-Human, VersatileGait) by introducing dual-level identity alignment—skeleton/body-shape matching plus kinematic motion transfer—so that each virtual character faithfully reproduces a specific real subject's gait rather than reusing shared motions across identities. The cross-clothing gait recognition task itself extends CCPG's 7 clothing variations per subject to 100, and the GON normalization is conceptually inspired by Layer Normalization but re-parameterized to operate over horizontally segmented spatial regions to handle non-uniform clothing effects across body parts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BarbieGait fills a critical gap: no prior dataset provides both large-scale clothing diversity (100 outfits/subject) and verified identity consistency, making it the first benchmark where cross-clothing gait recognition can be stress-tested at scale. The demonstrated cross-domain transfer (BarbieGait pretraining → CCPG fine-tuning yielding +1% R1 over scratch training) and the upstream pose estimation gains (+19.5% R1 over SkeletonGait on CCPG) suggest the dataset can serve as a universal pretraining source beyond gait recognition. Open questions include whether synthetic clothing physics (rendered via Blender) adequately captures real-world garment dynamics like fabric flutter and occlusion, and whether the 12.2 mm joint-position alignment error introduces subtle identity distortions that compound over long sequences. The dataset's controllability also opens avenues for studying cross-view and cross-domain gait recognition that were previously infeasible with real data.
