## Problem

MCI conversion prediction faces a fundamental trade-off: cross-sectional models using a single baseline sMRI enable immediate risk assessment but lack temporal dynamics, while longitudinal models capture disease progression but require waiting months for follow-up scans. No existing approach achieves both real-time prediction and the accuracy of longitudinal analysis, and naive generative models (GANs, VAEs) struggle to synthesize realistic longitudinal sMRI trajectories due to instability, posterior collapse, and high-dimensional spatial correlations.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single baseline sMRI scan (preprocessed to MNI152 space with skull stripping and bias correction) and a latent noise vector, mapping the scan to a low-dimensional feature vector via a pretrained HFCN extractor. It autoregressively generates future sMRI feature representations at five follow-up time points (6, 12, 18, 24, 36 months) in latent space, which together with the baseline feature are fed to a classifier producing a pMCI/sMCI conversion label. The generated outputs are abstract feature vectors rather than raw images, and the LLM scoring step operates on quantized, tokenized versions of these features.

## Method

MCI-Diff employs a conditional denoising diffusion model with two key innovations. First, multi-task sequence reconstruction training uses a shared denoiser conditioned on positional and mask embeddings to perform both interpolation (reconstructing missing intermediate time points) and extrapolation (predicting future points beyond observed data), with a progressive difficulty schedule that starts from complete sequences and incrementally masks more points while using the model itself for data augmentation. Second, LLM-driven clinical plausibility sampling addresses error compounding in autoregressive generation: at each step, N candidate features are produced by the diffusion model, quantized and tokenized, then scored by an instruction-tuned LLaMA4-17B that predicts FreeSurfer structural measurements; the candidate with the most clinically plausible biomarker predictions is selected, steering the trajectory toward realistic neurodegeneration patterns. The denoiser explicitly handles irregular temporal sampling through mask embeddings indicating data presence/absence, and the progressive training culminates in the model predicting future features conditioned solely on the baseline, mirroring the inference-time autoregressive generation.

## Results

MCI-Diff achieves 95.0% accuracy, 94.7% sensitivity, 95.0% specificity, and 94.8% AUC on the ADNI test split, and 93.6% accuracy / 91.4% AUC on AIBL. On the combined ADNI1+ADNI2 5-fold cross-validation, it reaches 95.4% ± 0.008 accuracy and 95.0% ± 0.009 AUC. These results represent average improvements of 5.1–5.8% (ADNI), 5.8–9.6% (AIBL), and 4.8–5.5% (5-fold CV) over the strongest baseline in each category, with the largest single-metric gain being +9.6% AUC on AIBL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MCI-Diff outperforms all seven baselines across every metric on all three evaluation settings. Against the strongest cross-sectional baseline MPS-FFA (85.2% ACC on ADNI), MCI-Diff gains +9.8% accuracy; against the best longitudinal baseline HFCN⁺ (89.9% ACC), it gains +5.1% on ADNI and +6.3% on AIBL. The generative baselines VAE (73.0% ACC) and Temp-GAN (79.1% ACC) trail MCI-Diff by 22.0 and 15.9 percentage points on ADNI accuracy, respectively, underscoring the advantage of the diffusion-based approach over GAN- and VAE-based generation. On AIBL, where class imbalance is more severe (pMCI: 17 vs. sMCI: 93), MCI-Diff's sensitivity advantage is most pronounced—88.2% vs. 82.4% for HFCN⁺ and 64.7% for Temp-GAN. The 5-fold CV results confirm consistency: MCI-Diff leads the best baseline (HFCN⁺ at 90.4% ACC) by 5.0 points with the lowest variance (±0.008 vs. ±0.012).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the denoising diffusion probabilistic model framework (Ho et al., 2020) and repurposes it for a medical imaging task: synthesizing future sMRI feature representations from a single baseline scan to predict MCI conversion. It departs from prior generative approaches for longitudinal MRI (e.g., Temp-GAN, VAE-based methods) by introducing a multi-task sequence reconstruction strategy that unifies interpolation and extrapolation under a shared denoiser, directly addressing the irregular temporal sampling inherent in clinical cohorts. The most distinctive departure is the integration of an instruction-tuned LLM as a 'linguistic compass' that scores generated feature candidates against expected FreeSurfer biomarkers, a mechanism not previously applied to guide autoregressive medical image generation. The paper positions itself against both cross-sectional predictors (HFCN, DA-MIDL, MPS-FFA) and longitudinal predictors (AD-RNN, VGG-TS, HFCN+), arguing that its generation-based paradigm achieves the accuracy of longitudinal methods while retaining the immediacy of cross-sectional ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work addresses a clinically significant trade-off: longitudinal MCI prediction requires 36 months of follow-up scans before a prediction can be made, delaying intervention. If the single-scan generation approach generalizes, it could enable real-time risk stratification at the initial MCI diagnosis, with direct implications for clinical trial enrollment and early treatment. However, several open questions remain: the LLM scoring mechanism is validated somewhat circularly (trained to predict FreeSurfer measurements, then used to score plausibility of those same measurements), the datasets are relatively small (ADNI-2 test ≈ 237 subjects, AIBL ≈ 110), and it is unclear whether the 5–12% gains hold on larger, more heterogeneous cohorts or transfer to other neurodegenerative trajectories. The reliance on a specific LLM (LLaMA-4-17B) and a specific feature extractor (HFCN) also raises questions about robustness to architectural choices.
