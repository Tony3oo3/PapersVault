## Problem

In-context learning is highly sensitive to which demonstrations appear in the prompt, but the combinatorial space of possible demonstration contexts makes brute-force search infeasible. Existing methods treat demonstration selection as an instance-adaptive retrieval or ranking problem, requiring task-specific retrievers and multiple expensive LLM calls to validate candidates. The paper argues that predicting whether a specific query-context pair (q, D) will succeed is fundamentally cheaper and more general than searching for an optimal D*, reframing selection as a feasibility-testing problem.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text query q, a labeled demonstration pool P, and a frozen target LLM M. Demonstrations are k-shot with k = |Y| (one example per class), drawn from a fixed class-balanced random proposal distribution rather than a learned retriever. The router consumes only the query text (via BERT-base), while judges consume (q, D) pairs. Output is the LLM's predicted class label plus a diagnostic risk tag: HARD_QUERY when the router predicts the hardest stratum (l_x, triggering a single random-context call), or NO_GOOD_DEMO when no sampled context passes the judge threshold within the per-level budget.

## Method

DiSP (Difficulty-Stratified Success Prediction) operates in three stages. First, offline random-demo trials (T=20 per training query) with the target LLM estimate per-query success rates, which are discretized into four difficulty levels (l1/l2/l3/lx) via fixed thresholds (α=0.75, β=0.3, γ=0.1). Second, a BERT-base router trained with three binary ordinal objectives predicts the difficulty level from the query alone, and level-specific judges of increasing capacity (BERT-base for l1, RoBERTa-Large for l2, a lightweight head on target-LLM hidden states for l3) are trained as binary classifiers on (q, D, s) tuples. At inference, the router selects a per-level budget K_ℓ and judge g_ℓ; the system then performs stop-on-acceptance feasibility testing—sampling random demonstrations, scoring each with the judge, and accepting the first candidate whose score exceeds threshold τ_ℓ—querying the LLM only once with the accepted (or fallback random) context. This design keeps the target LLM frozen, allocates expensive judge features only to brittle queries, and provides explicit accuracy-cost knobs via K_ℓ and τ_ℓ.

## Results

DiSP achieves the best average accuracy across five classification benchmarks with both LLaMA3-8B (77.6%, +11.2 over zero-shot) and Qwen2.5-7B (82.9%, +13.1 over zero-shot). Gains are largest on harder datasets: TREC improves by +8.7 (LLaMA) and +24.2 (Qwen) over zero-shot, and MNLI by +17.2 and +10.6 respectively. In terms of efficiency, DiSP reduces total end-to-end wall-clock cost to 6.8 minutes versus 13.8 (Uprise), 115.4 (Se²), and 157.6 (SeDPO), a speedup of up to 23×.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiSP outperforms all three learned selection baselines on average accuracy for both backbones. On LLaMA3-8B, DiSP (77.6% avg) beats Uprise (73.6%), Se² (72.1%), and SeDPO (71.5%) by 4.0, 5.5, and 6.1 points respectively; the strongest single baseline is BM25 at 74.8%, still 2.8 points below DiSP. On Qwen2.5-7B, DiSP (82.9% avg) leads Uprise (80.3%), Se² (80.1%), and SeDPO (78.4%) by 2.6, 2.8, and 4.5 points, with BM25 (80.5%) as the closest competitor at a 2.4-point gap. DiSP wins on 4 of 5 datasets per backbone, with the sole exception being SST-5 on Qwen2.5-7B where BM25 edges it by 1.3 points (55.6 vs. 54.3). In terms of efficiency, DiSP's total wall-clock cost (6.8 min) is 2.0× faster than Uprise, 17.0× faster than Se², and 23.2× faster than SeDPO, while also adding only 0.1 min of test-time overhead versus 0.4–0.6 min for the baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant search-centric paradigm for ICL demonstration selection (learned retrievers, rankers, proxy scoring à la Rubin et al., Iter et al., Ji et al.) by reframing the problem as a feasibility-judging task: rather than finding an optimal D*, it predicts whether a randomly sampled (q,D) pair will succeed. The difficulty-stratification mechanism—estimating per-query success rates under a fixed random proposal and routing to level-specific judges with increasing capacity—has no direct precedent in the demonstration-selection literature, though it borrows the budgeted-inference / model-cascade logic from routing literature. The stop-on-acceptance procedure and risk-tagging (HARD_QUERY, NO_GOOD_DEMO) connect to conformal-prediction-style abstention mechanisms, but are applied here specifically to context selection rather than output confidence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a 23× end-to-end wall-clock speedup over the strongest learned-selection baselines while simultaneously improving average accuracy by 2.4–3.4 points makes this a compelling efficiency-accuracy trade-off for deployment. The risk-tagging mechanism (88.3% precision on the extreme difficulty tail) offers a simple, interpretable signal for abstention or escalation policies that prior selection methods lack. Key limitations include dependence on the specific random proposal distribution (changing the demo pool or prompt format requires re-collection), the upfront cost of offline LLM labeling for supervision, and evaluation restricted to five classification tasks with two 7–8B backbones. Open questions include whether the judge-centric framing extends to generation tasks, whether calibration of the acceptance thresholds can be made distribution-free, and whether the difficulty stratification is stable across backbone changes (the paper shows it is not).
