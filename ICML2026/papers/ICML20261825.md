## Problem

Transformers lack inductive biases for geometric symmetries prevalent in science and computer vision, while existing equivariant methods introduce complex, computationally intensive operations (group convolutions, Clebsch-Gordan tensor products) that sacrifice the efficiency and flexibility making Transformers effective. The paper asks whether one can achieve equivariance to continuous translations and discrete roto-reflections within a standard Transformer without altering its architecture or computational footprint.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261825/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method accepts diverse spatial inputs—RGB images, 3D point clouds, molecular atom positions with types, and protein structures—and produces task-specific outputs (class labels, molecular properties, interatomic forces/energies, or generated 3D molecules). Internally, all input features are lifted to functions on a finite symmetry group G (e.g., 12-element tetrahedral group), expanding the channel dimension to |G|·C while keeping the total effective feature dimension constant. The computation graph and FLOPs remain identical to a standard Transformer with the same total channel count, with the group axis simply reinterpreted as additional attention heads.

## Method

The Platonic Transformer achieves equivariance by computing attention relative to a set of reference frames drawn from Platonic solid symmetry groups (tetrahedral, octahedral, icosahedral in 3D; cyclic or dihedral in 2D). Input features are lifted to functions on the group, and RoPE is made frame-dependent by projecting relative positions onto each reference frame, so each frame operates as an independent attention head with shared base frequencies. All linear layers are constrained to equivariant group convolutions, which are mathematically equivalent to weight-shared matrix multiplications that reduce parameter count by a factor of |G| while preserving the standard matrix-vector computation graph. The paper further proves that omitting softmax makes the attention operator formally equivalent to a dynamic, content-aware group convolution with linear O(N) complexity, yielding a scalable convolutional variant. This design requires no new modules—only a principled weight-sharing structure on existing layers—so the trivial group G={e} recovers a standard RoPE Transformer exactly.

## Results

On OMol25 (4M molecules), the 60M-parameter Tetrahedron Platonic Transformer achieves force MAE 9.33, total energy MAE 63.84, and energy-per-atom MAE 1.12 after 80 epochs, outperforming the eSEN-sm baseline (12.64 / 107.78 / 1.66) while running at 2.6× higher throughput (33,878 vs 12,834 atoms/sec). On QM9 molecule generation, DiP-Tetrahedron reaches 98.43% validity and 95.26% molecular stability, the highest validity among all compared methods. On ProteinMD, the Tetrahedron model achieves 1.80 backbone-level force MSE, matching G-Hyena and outperforming G-Transformer (2.45) and EGNN (2.25). On non-equivariant tasks, the equivariant constraint acts as a regularizer: D3 on CIFAR-10 reaches 92.78% accuracy with only 14.2M parameters (vs. 85.1M for the trivial-group baseline), and Tetrahedron on ScanObjectNN improves accuracy by 2.43 points over the translation-only model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On OMol25, the Tetrahedron Platonic Transformer decisively outperforms eSEN-sm [Levine et al., 2025] at 80 epochs: force MAE drops from 12.64 to 9.33 (−26%), energy MAE from 107.78 to 63.84 (−41%), and throughput is 2.6× higher. On QM9 molecule generation, DiP-Tetrahedron achieves the highest validity (98.43%) among all compared methods, beating ADiT [Joshi et al., 2025] (94.45%), ZATOM-1 [Morehead et al., 2026] (92.88%), GeoLDM [Xu et al., 2023] (93.80%), EDM [Hoogeboom et al., 2022] (91.90%), and Symphony [Daigavane et al., 2024] (83.50%); it also leads in molecular stability (95.26%) over all except ZATOM-1-WD (99.79%). On ProteinMD, the Tetrahedron model ties G-Hyena [Moskalev et al., 2025] on backbone force MSE (1.80 vs 1.80) but wins on all-atom MSE (2.36 vs 2.49), and clearly outperforms G-Transformer (2.45/3.67), EGNN [Satorras et al., 2021] (2.25/2.72), and FastEGNN [Gasteiger et al., 2020] (1.84/–). The paper's key finding is that these gains come at zero additional architectural cost: the Platonic Transformer uses the same computation graph as a standard Transformer, with equivariance enforced purely through weight-sharing constraints on existing linear layers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE), which already encode translation equivariance, into a frame-dependent mechanism that achieves roto-translation equivariance by computing attention relative to reference frames from Platonic solid symmetry groups. The equivariant linear layers are group convolutions in the sense of Cohen et al. (2019), but the key departure from prior equivariant Transformers (SE(3)-Transformers, Equiformer, G-Transformer) is that no architectural changes are made: the computation graph and per-layer FLOPs remain identical to a standard Transformer, with equivariance imposed purely through a weight-sharing constraint on existing linear projections. The formal equivalence between softmax-free RoPE attention and dynamic group convolution (Proposition 4.1) connects this work to the Performer/linear-attention line, revealing that RoPE attention implicitly learns content-adaptive geometric filters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Platonic Transformer resolves a long-standing tension in geometric deep learning: the ability to incorporate principled equivariance inductive biases without sacrificing the speed, flexibility, and scalability that make Transformers dominant. The linear-time dynamic group convolution variant (Corollary 4.3) further opens a path to processing very large point clouds and molecular systems at near-linear cost. Limitations include equivariance only to discrete subgroups of O(n) rather than the full continuous group, and uncharacterized behavior at extreme scale. An open question the paper raises is why the equivariance gap in generative tasks (QM9) is much smaller for Transformers (~1.3% validity) than for convolutional frameworks (~11.5%), suggesting Transformers may absorb equivariance from unconstrained parameterizations at sufficient scale.
