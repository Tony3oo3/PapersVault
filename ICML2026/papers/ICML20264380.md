## Problem

Reward design is a critical bottleneck for vision-based RL in robotic manipulation: precise geometric distances available in simulation are unavailable in real-world visual settings due to sensory noise and occlusions. Existing reward-learning approaches (adversarial discriminators, progress predictors, generative-model confidence) either produce sparse or unstable rewards, lack explicit geometric structure, or fail under distribution shift, leaving no framework that automatically synthesizes dense, structured rewards from action-free video demonstrations while guiding the agent through a geometrically grounded curriculum.

## Input / Output

The anticipation model takes a single 256×256 RGB image of the initial task state and autoregressively outputs a sequence of keypoint coordinate subgoals (4 points by default), each representing an intermediate spatial target. The low-level policy receives the current RGB observation, the current keypoint coordinates, and the target subgoal coordinates as input, and outputs robot end-effector actions. The reward signal is a continuous scalar computed from the L2 pixel distance between the tracked keypoint and the active subgoal, augmented with discrete stage-success and task-completion bonuses. Notably, the entire pipeline requires no action labels for the anticipation model—only action-free video demonstrations.

## Method

ReLAM operates in two stages under a hierarchical RL framework. First, it constructs a keypoint subgoal dataset from action-free video demos by: (a) segmenting task-relevant objects with grounded SAM, (b) tracking pixel trajectories with CoTracker, (c) filtering by motion magnitude and selecting representative points via Farthest Point Sampling, and (d) identifying keyframes where the displacement-vector angle changes sharply. An autoregressive anticipation model (frozen DINOv2 image encoder + 12-layer causal transformer with MLP point heads) is then trained with teacher-forcing MSE loss to predict the keypoint coordinate sequence from the initial frame. Second, a goal-conditioned policy (CNN + MLP) is trained with PPO or IQL using a piecewise-linear reward derived from the L2 distance between the current keypoint and the active subgoal, with the anticipation model acting as a high-level planner that proposes the next subgoal upon reaching the current one. A sub-optimality bound (Theorem 1) proves the resulting policy achieves near-shortest-path performance, with the gap controlled by the anticipation model's prediction error and the low-level policy's per-subtask sub-optimality.

## Results

On online RL in Meta-World, ReLAM achieves 100% success rate on 4 of 7 tasks (Drawer Open, Door Open, Coffee Button, Plate Slide) and 66–95% on the remaining three, outperforming all baselines on 6 of 7 tasks. On ManiSkill, ReLAM reaches 84.0% on Push Cube and 56.7% on Pick Cube, surpassing the best baseline (Diffusion Reward at 60.0% and 42.7% respectively). On the real-world ALOHA towel-folding task, ReLAM achieves 90%/65%/35% success across three sequential stages, improving over Vanilla BC (75%/45%/20%) and ReWiND (85%/55%/30%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReLAM outperforms all five baselines on the majority of tasks. Against Diffusion Reward, ReLAM gains +24.0 pp on Push Cube (84.0 vs 60.0) and +14.3 pp on Pick Cube (56.7 vs 42.7) in ManiSkill, while matching or exceeding it on most Meta-World tasks. Against ReWiND, the advantage is most pronounced on ManiSkill (+44.0 pp on Push Cube, +26.7 pp on Pick Cube), where ReWiND's progress reward degrades on out-of-distribution exploration trajectories. Against DACfO, ReLAM leads by +37.3 pp on Push Cube and +37.4 pp on Pick Cube, reflecting DACfO's training instability on high-dimensional visual inputs. The Image Subgoal (IS) baseline, which generates image subgoals via flow matching, performs worst (0–24.7% on most tasks), confirming that keypoint-based subgoals are far more reliable than image-based ones. ReLAM approaches the Oracle upper bound (which uses ground-truth subgoals) on most Meta-World tasks—achieving identical 100% on Door Open and Coffee Button—and narrows the gap to within 4–15 pp on the remaining tasks, validating that the anticipation model generates sufficiently accurate subgoals.

## Relation to Prior Work

ReLAM sits at the intersection of two recent threads: keypoint-based task abstraction (ATM, Wen et al. 2024) and the anticipation RL framework (Yu, 2025) that decomposes long-horizon tasks into subgoal-conditioned subtasks. It departs from adversarial imitation learning (DAC, GAIfO) and diffusion-based reward methods (Diffusion Reward, VP2) by replacing high-dimensional image or discriminator outputs with a sparse set of tracked keypoints, which simultaneously serve as the subgoal representation for the anticipation model and the geometric basis for a dense distance reward. The autoregressive transformer anticipation model, conditioned on frozen DINOv2 embeddings, is a novel architectural choice that reduces subgoal generation from image synthesis to coordinate prediction, and the HRL formulation with a provable sub-optimality bound is a theoretical contribution not present in prior keypoint or anticipation-based work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a practical pathway to dense reward design for visual robotic manipulation using only action-free video demonstrations, which is a significant step toward reducing the manual reward engineering that has bottlenecked real-world RL deployment. The key insight that a handful of tracked keypoints (as few as 4) can encode sufficient task structure for both planning and reward computation is broadly applicable and could be integrated into existing VLA or diffusion-policy pipelines. Key limitations include sensitivity to viewpoint changes (performance degrades sharply beyond ~10° azimuth shift), dependence on reliable point tracking under occlusion, and evaluation at a relatively small scale (100 demos per task, single camera). Open questions include whether multi-view point-cloud fusion, VLM/LLM-guided anticipation, and integration with world models can address these gaps and scale the approach to more complex, open-ended manipulation.
