## Problem

When QUBO objectives are relaxed to the continuous box [0,1]^N and optimized with gradient-based methods, local minima can be fractional or infeasible even under arbitrarily large penalty weights, because the penalty landscape can become first-order flat at infeasible binary points. The paper asks: what structural conditions on the quadratic penalty guarantee that every box-local minimizer is both binary and feasible, making projected gradient descent a certifiably correct local solver for the underlying discrete problem?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a QUBO encoding (linear objective weights w, a quadratic penalty V encoding constraints, and a penalty parameter γ) together with an initial point z⁰ ∈ [0,1]^N, and produces a binary vector x ∈ {0,1}^n that satisfies the original combinatorial constraints. The penalty V must be constructed to satisfy three verifiable structural properties—diagonal-free on core variables, integer coefficients, and local repairability with a uniform margin—and γ must exceed an explicit finite threshold derived from the objective weights and repair margin. For problems with auxiliary slack variables (e.g., knapsack), the output includes the slack bits, but correctness is certified only on the core decision variables.

## Method

The core contribution is a problem-independent local-correctness criterion built on three penalty-structure conditions: (C1) diagonal-free quadratic terms on core variables, which via a Hessian-indefiniteness argument forces interior coordinates at any local minimum to form an independent set in the penalty interaction graph; (C2) integer-valued penalty coefficients, which combined with interior independence makes the penalty gradient at any interior core coordinate an integer, so a sufficiently large γ (exceeding max|w_i|) eliminates all fractional local minima; and (C3) local repairability with margin δ, requiring that every infeasible binary core configuration admits a feasible first-order descent direction for the penalty, which rules out infeasible local minima when γ > L/δ. Guided by this theory, the paper proposes specific penalty constructions: an ancestor-based penalty for open-pit mining (replacing the naive parent penalty that admits flat infeasible vertices), a binary-equivalent diagonal cancellation for knapsack and assignment constraints, and a square-free time-indexed penalty for TSP. Numerical experiments on MineLib, kplib, and TSPLIB confirm that theory-guided formulations achieve 100% binarity and feasibility while naive formulations fail even at very large γ.

## Results

The theory-guided QUBO formulations achieve 100% binarity and feasibility across all 20 runs (10 initializations × 2 instances) for open-pit mining, knapsack, and time-indexed TSP, while the naive formulations fail catastrophically: 0% feasibility on open-pit mining, 0% binarity on knapsack and TSP. For the MIS practical benchmark, PGD on the relaxed QUBO outperforms Gurobi across all 8 Erdős–Rényi settings under a 5-minute budget (e.g., at n=40000, p=0.3: PGD achieves 34.125 vs. Gurobi 26.75 average independent-set size), and is competitive with SLS and GRASP heuristics, with GPU-parallel restarts further improving the 30-second regime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between the paper's theory-guided QUBO penalties (ancestor formulation for open-pit, binary-equivalent cancellation for knapsack/TSP) and standard naive QUBO encodings (parent-pair penalty, raw squared residual). The theory-guided formulations achieve 100% binarity and feasibility in all 20 runs across all three problem classes, while the naive formulations exhibit the predicted failure modes: the parent formulation for open-pit mining converges to infeasible strict local minima (0% feasibility) regardless of penalty magnitude, and the raw squared-residual penalties for knapsack and TSP converge to fractional local minima (0% binarity). For the MIS practical benchmark, PGD on the relaxed QUBO outperforms Gurobi by 6–10 points in average independent-set size across all tested (n,p) settings under a 5-minute budget, and is within 0–1 point of SLS and GRASP heuristics, with GPU-parallel batched restarts closing the remaining gap in the 30-second regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the problem-specific local-optima analyses of relaxed QUBOs for MIS (Alkhouri et al., ICML 2025) and MaxCut (Alkhouri et al., AISTATS 2026) into a problem-agnostic theoretical framework built on three verifiable structural conditions: diagonal-free penalty structure, integer-valued coefficients, and local repairability. It also connects to the classical nonconvex quadratic programming literature (Burer & Letchford, 2009) on box-constrained objectives, but shifts focus from global optimality (NP-hard) to the first-order geometry that governs projected gradient descent. The key conceptual departure from prior QUBO work is showing that penalty magnitude alone is insufficient—formulation structure (e.g., ancestor vs. parent penalties in open-pit mining) is what determines whether infeasible local minima can exist at any γ.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides actionable design principles for constructing QUBO penalties compatible with gradient-based solvers, which is increasingly relevant as differentiable combinatorial optimization gains traction on GPU/TPU hardware. The local repairability condition is a new, checkable property that practitioners can verify for their specific constraint encodings, and the explicit finite γ thresholds make the guarantees practical rather than asymptotic. Limitations include: guarantees are purely local (no global optimality), the multi-constraint knapsack extension requires an over-correction that sacrifices binary-equivalence, and empirical validation is limited to two instances per problem class. Open questions include whether the three conditions are also necessary, how the framework extends to non-quadratic or higher-order penalties, and whether the large γ thresholds (e.g., γ* ≈ 1e16 for open-pit mining) remain numerically tractable at scale.
