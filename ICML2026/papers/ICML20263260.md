## Problem

RLVR cannot extract training signal on the hardest problems where the model never samples a correct rollout, and directly fine-tuning on expert human solutions causes severe reasoning degradation because those solutions are fundamentally out-of-distribution—didactic, with implicit reasoning gaps and no search dynamics. The paper asks how a model can learn generalizable reasoning from a small set (<1000) of high-quality expert solutions that are both expensive to collect and structurally incompatible with the model's own reasoning process.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small dataset of problem-expert-solution pairs (e.g., 417 AIME problems with AoPS community solutions, or 669 Olympiad proofs from an IMO coach) along with the student model's own frozen weights. The expert solutions are first transformed into expanded in-distribution reasoning traces (roughly 4× longer than the originals) via mixed-policy generation, and a negative reference context is automatically constructed by regex-extracting only intermediate numerical/symbolic waypoints from the expert solution. The output is a LoRA-adapted model that improves pass@k on hard math benchmarks while reasoning with ~2× fewer tokens than the untrained baseline.

## Method

DAIL is a two-step offline self-distillation procedure. First, a mixed-policy rollout generates in-distribution reasoning traces: at each token, the student samples a candidate and the frozen teacher (conditioned on the full expert solution) verifies it; if the teacher's probability falls below threshold τ=0.8, the teacher's sample is used instead, preserving the student's natural reasoning flow while anchoring to the expert solution. Second, a contrastive objective trains the student to match the teacher's token distribution (positive KL) while simultaneously diverging from a negative reference model that is conditioned only on coarse intermediate waypoints (partial solution), thereby penalizing rationalization shortcuts that NLL would indiscriminately imitate. The entire pipeline is memory-efficient because a single frozen model copy serves as teacher, negative reference, and base for the LoRA student, and data generation is fully decoupled from optimization for parallelization.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct trained with DAIL on 417 expert AIME solutions, the method achieves pass@128 of 63.3 on AIME 2024/2025, 46.0 on BeyondAIME, and 37.8 on IMO-AnswerBench, outperforming the base model (51.7, 41.0, 33.5) and all RLVR baselines. DAIL also doubles reasoning efficiency on Qwen3-8B/14B (think), matching or exceeding untrained model performance with 2× fewer reasoning tokens. On the out-of-domain GPQA-Diamond benchmark, DAIL maintains or improves pass@128 (e.g., 96.5 vs 93.9 for Qwen3-8B at 1024 tokens) without catastrophic forgetting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DAIL consistently outperforms all RLVR baselines on pass@128 across all three math benchmarks. Against GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), DAIL gains +10.0 on AIME (63.3 vs 53.3), +6.0 on BeyondAIME (46.0 vs 40.0), and +5.0 on IMO-AnswerBench (37.8 vs 32.8). Against NuRL+GRPO (Chen et al., 2025a), the margins are even larger: +16.6 on AIME, +10.0 on BeyondAIME, and +7.0 on IMO-AnswerBench, with NuRL actually degrading below the base model on BeyondAIME. Against GRPO trained on the 40K+ DeepScaleR dataset (Luo et al., 2025), DAIL with only 417 samples achieves substantially higher pass@128 (63.3 vs 41.7 on AIME; 46.0 vs 27.0 on BeyondAIME), demonstrating that scaling verifiable data does not substitute for expert-grounded learning on hard problems. Against STaR rationalization (Zelikman et al., 2022), DAIL clearly wins: STaR degrades performance below the untrained model on all benchmarks, confirming that self-generated rationalization is insufficient when problem difficulty exceeds the model's baseline capability. Notably, DAIL trades a small amount of pass@1 (e.g., 8.8 vs 12.0 for GRPO-DeepScaleR on AIME) for large pass@128 gains, reflecting a shift toward broader search coverage rather than greedy accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

DAIL extends the self-distillation paradigm (Yang et al., 2024), where a frozen copy of the model acts as a teacher, by repurposing speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023) and interactive imitation (Ross et al., 2011) into a mixed-policy rollout that preserves the student's natural reasoning artifacts while anchoring generation to expert solutions. It departs from RLVR (GRPO, DeepSeekMath) by operating entirely offline on non-verifiable problems where reward signals are zero, and from STaR rationalization by grounding expansion in full expert solutions rather than just the final answer. The contrastive objective with a 'negative reference' conditioned on partial solution waypoints is a novel mechanism to suppress rationalization shortcuts, drawing on the known limitations of behavioral cloning on suboptimal datasets (Kumar et al., 2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DAIL addresses a concrete bottleneck: RLVR cannot extract training signal on the hardest problems (zero advantage), and non-verifiable proof problems are entirely outside its scope. The demonstration that fewer than 1000 expert solutions yield up to 31% pass@128 gains while doubling reasoning efficiency is practically significant for resource-constrained settings. Key limitations include the requirement for a competent base model (Llama-3.1-8B shows no improvement), the off-policy formulation that may sacrifice some gains versus on-policy variants, and evaluation primarily on mathematics. Open questions include whether the method scales to domains where expert solutions are less structured, how to handle the interaction between model capability and problem difficulty more gracefully, and whether on-policy variants can close the remaining gap without the slow convergence observed in exploratory experiments.
