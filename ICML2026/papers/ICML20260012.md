## Problem

While prior work has shown that deep LLM layers perform linguistic processing, it remains unclear whether syntax and semantics are encoded through simple linear mechanisms, whether the two signals can be geometrically separated, and how their layer-wise encoding profiles differ. This paper addresses these questions by asking whether averaging representations of sentences sharing structure or meaning yields centroids that capture the corresponding information linearly, and whether ablating one component affects the other.

## Input / Output

The method consumes hidden-state vectors from intermediate layers of LLMs (primarily DeepSeek-V3 with 671B parameters, validated on Qwen2-7b, Gemma3-12b, and Pythia-6.9b) for three non-overlapping sentence sets: originals, syntax twins (same POS template, different meaning), and paraphrases/translations. Sentences are aggregated either by concatenating the last N token representations (N=6 for syntax, N=3 for semantics) or by averaging them. Outputs include rank-based similarity scores (Information Imbalance) between matched pairs across all layers, ablated sentence vectors after projection subtraction, the fraction of squared norm explained by syntax vs. semantic centroids per layer, and secondary probe accuracies (POS-template classification and paraphrase-recall@3).

## Method

The core technique constructs two types of centroids via simple averaging: syntactic centroids are the mean of representations of all sentences sharing a given POS template (excluding the target sentence), and semantic centroids are the mean of representations of translations into six languages (Arabic, Chinese, German, Italian, Spanish, Turkish). Ablation is performed by subtracting the projection of each sentence vector along its centroid direction, a purely linear operation that makes the sentence orthogonal to the centroid. Similarity between matched pairs is quantified using Information Imbalance (a rank-based neighborhood-overlap metric) rather than CKA, which provides stronger signal in high-dimensional settings. The key analytical move is cross-ablation: removing the semantic centroid from syntactically matched pairs and removing the syntactic centroid from semantically matched pairs, revealing an asymmetry where syntax similarity is preserved under semantic ablation but semantic similarity degrades under syntactic ablation. A norm decomposition further quantifies the independent contributions of each centroid to sentence vectors across layers, showing syntax is stable throughout the network while semantics concentrates in central layers.

## Results

On DeepSeek-V3 (671B), syntactic similarity between POS-matched sentence pairs exceeds 0.7 (concatenated tokens) and 0.4 (averaged tokens) across all layers, while semantic similarity between paraphrases is low in early layers and peaks in central layers. Subtracting the syntactic centroid reduces syntax similarity substantially, and subtracting the semantic centroid reduces semantic similarity predominantly in central layers, confirming linear encoding of both signals. Critically, the paper finds an asymmetry: removing the semantic centroid does not significantly affect syntax similarity, whereas removing the syntax centroid does reduce semantic similarity. In probe experiments (Table 3), syntax ablation drops POS-template classification accuracy from 0.85 to 0.10, while semantic ablation drops paraphrase-recall@3 from 0.85 to 0.66; random-centroid controls produce negligible changes (≤0.04). All findings are qualitatively replicated on Qwen2-7b and Gemma3-12b.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical interpretability study rather than a method-comparison paper, so there are no external baseline methods to benchmark against. The primary internal 'comparison' is between correct-centroid ablation and random-centroid ablation: removing the correct syntax centroid drops POS accuracy from 0.85 to 0.10, while a random syntax centroid only reduces it to 0.81; similarly, the correct semantic centroid drops paraphrase recall from 0.85 to 0.66 versus 0.83 for a random one. The second key comparison is the cross-component asymmetry: semantic ablation leaves syntax similarity essentially unchanged (Fig. 3), whereas syntax ablation significantly reduces semantic similarity (Fig. 4), suggesting syntax is more independently encoded than semantics. Results are validated across four models of varying scale (DeepSeek-V3 671B, Gemma3-12b, Qwen2-7b, Pythia-6.9b), with consistent qualitative patterns, though quantitative differences exist (e.g., Gemma3-12b shows an earlier semantic similarity peak and a global minimum absent in the other models).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the syntax-centroid approach of Caucheteux et al. (2021), who averaged GPT-2 representations of sentences sharing a POS template to proxy syntactic information, by introducing an analogous semantic centroid built from cross-lingual translations and by systematically studying the interaction between the two. The similarity metric is derived from the Information Imbalance of Glielmo et al. (2022), and the representation aggregation strategy (token concatenation vs. averaging) follows Acevedo et al. (2025). Conceptually, the work sits at the intersection of the LLM interpretability literature on where linguistic processing occurs (Cheng et al., 2025; Skean et al., 2025) and the linear-representation-hypothesis line (Mikolov et al., 2013; Park et al., 2024), but departs from the dominant probing-classifier methodology in favor of a purely geometric, linear-ablation framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean demonstration that a single linear projection along a centroid direction can remove a substantial fraction of syntactic or semantic similarity provides strong evidence that these abstract linguistic features are encoded via linear superposition in LLMs, extending the linear-representation hypothesis beyond word-level analogies to sentence-level structure and meaning. The asymmetry—semantic ablation leaves syntax similarity intact while syntactic ablation degrades semantic similarity—offers computational support for the long-standing linguistic hypothesis of syntactic autonomy, and the layer-profile difference (semantics peaking in central layers, syntax stable throughout) refines the 'semantic core' narrative. Key limitations include the small dataset (~2,000 short English sentences), the fact that centroids explain at most ~40% of representation norm, and the absence of interventional experiments during generation; future work on spectral decomposition, longer sentences, and non-English data would strengthen the conclusions.
