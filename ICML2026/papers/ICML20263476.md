## Problem

Existing learned mesh simulators (e.g., MeshGraphNets) conflate the topological structure of differential operators with metric-dependent material/geometry effects, allowing non-physical degrees of freedom to contaminate predicted fields and produce spurious modes and long-horizon instabilities. The paper asks: under minimal physical principles, what is structurally fixed by mesh topology versus what should be learned, and can this separation be derived from first principles rather than assumed as a global template?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263476/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a stacked cochain state z = (z_k, z_{k+1}) ∈ C^k ⊕ C^{k+1} (one scalar per oriented k-cell and (k+1)-cell), the fixed signed-incidence matrices D_k encoding mesh topology, local geometry/material features (e.g., node areas, edge lengths) that condition the learned metric, and a time step Δt. It outputs the updated cochain state at the next time step. All heavy operations are sparse matrix-vector products yielding O(N) cost, and the interconnection J is never trained—only the degree-wise SPD metric G_θ and PSD dissipation R_θ are learned, optionally conditioned by small permutation-equivariant MLPs on geometry features.

## Method

The paper proves a local reduction theorem: under locality, permutation equivariance, orientation covariance, and an energy-balance/passivity inequality, the Jacobian of any admissible mesh dynamics factorizes as ∂F/∂z = (J − R)G where J is skew-symmetric with off-diagonal blocks having signed-incidence wiring (J_{k,k+1} = −D_k^⊤ C_k, J_{k+1,k} = C_k D_k), R ≽ 0, and G ≻ 0. This means the conservative interconnection is uniquely fixed by mesh topology, while all phenomenon-dependent effects (constitutive laws, geometry, dissipation) reside in the learnable metric and dissipation operators. MeshFT-Net instantiates this by hardwiring J from signed incidences and learning only a degree-wise SPD metric G_θ (via softplus diagonals or small Cholesky blocks) and a PSD dissipation R_θ (e.g., Rayleigh-type), advanced by a Strang-splitting KDK integrator with exact exponential half-step damping. Training uses supervised one-step prediction with no PDE-residual loss; the inductive bias comes entirely from the fixed incidence wiring and the SPD/PSD structural constraints on G_θ and R_θ.

## Results

On the analytic 2D plane-wave benchmark (32×32 periodic grid), MeshFT-Net achieves a one-step MSE of 1.3×10⁻⁹, a rollout TSMSE of 9.6×10⁻⁵, and a normalized energy drift of 1.3×10⁻⁴, outperforming all baselines by orders of magnitude in drift (MGN: 25.9, HNN: 1.0×10⁻²). On the physics-consistency benchmark, MeshFT-Net attains the lowest wave-speed error (2.03×10⁻²), near-exact canonical consistency (9.63×10⁻⁶), and near-zero momentum variation (4.89×10⁻⁸), while baselines show momentum drifts of 0.39–4.99. Under OOD shifts (frequency, resolution, wave speed), MeshFT-Net maintains the lowest energy drift (5.1×10⁻³ to 2.9×10⁻³) while several baselines diverge (>100).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MeshFT-Net outperforms all five baselines across every reported metric. Against MGN (Pfaff et al., 2021), the gap is most dramatic in energy drift: 1.3×10⁻⁴ vs. 25.9 (≈5 orders of magnitude), reflecting MGN's lack of orientation and energy constraints. Against HNN (Greydanus et al., 2019), which enforces Hamiltonian structure via soft penalties, MeshFT-Net still achieves 77× lower energy drift (1.3×10⁻⁴ vs. 1.0×10⁻²) and 31× lower TSMSE, because the topology-fixed interconnection provides a hard structural guarantee rather than a learned soft constraint. Against PI-MGN (Würth et al., 2024), MeshFT-Net wins on canonical consistency (9.63×10⁻⁶ vs. 15.9) and momentum preservation (4.89×10⁻⁸ vs. 4.99), while PI-MGN diverges (>100) under all three OOD shifts. Against FNO (Li et al., 2021) and GraphCON (Rusch et al., 2022), MeshFT-Net achieves lower long-horizon TSMSE and energy drift; FNO diverges under resolution shift and GraphCON under frequency and wave-speed shifts, whereas MeshFT-Net remains stable across all OOD conditions. The topology-metric ablation (Table 7) further confirms that removing the fixed incidence wiring inflates momentum variation by 5 orders of magnitude (10⁻⁷ → 10⁻²), isolating the structural role of the signed-incidence interconnection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends MeshGraphNets (Pfaff et al., 2021) by adding two physical constraints—orientation covariance and energy balance/passivity—that MGN's message-passing architecture does not enforce. The mathematical backbone is discrete exterior calculus (Hirani, 2003; Desbrun et al., 2005), where the coboundary operator D_k is topological and metric effects enter only through Hodge stars; the paper formalizes this split as a provable local reduction theorem rather than an architectural assumption. Unlike prior structure-preserving learning approaches (HNN, GENERIC, port-Hamiltonian neural networks) that postulate a global Hamiltonian or port-Hamiltonian template and learn within it, MeshFT derives the local port-Hamiltonian factorization from four minimal physical principles, avoiding commitment to a single global dynamics form. The data-driven exterior calculus line (Trask et al., 2022) shares the topology-metric split but operates in a discretized operator-learning setting without the principle-driven Jacobian-level reduction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The reduction theorem provides a clean, actionable design principle for mesh-based physics simulators: the conservative interconnection is uniquely fixed by mesh topology (signed incidence), and only metric-dependent constitutive and dissipative operators need to be learned. This eliminates non-physical degrees of freedom by construction, yielding near-zero energy drift and exact momentum conservation without explicit constraints. Open questions include whether the state-independent interconnection assumption (Corollary 3.4) is sufficient for strongly nonlinear or time-varying media, how the framework scales to 3D meshes with higher-dimensional cochains, and whether the learned metric operators can be transferred across fundamentally different physical systems. The approach also raises the question of whether similar principle-driven reductions exist for other discretization paradigms (spectral, particle-based).
