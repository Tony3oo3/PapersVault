## Problem

Developing unified neural solvers for graph combinatorial optimization requires efficient transfer of learned representations from trained tasks to new, unseen tasks, yet prior multi-task approaches either rely on supervised/RL signals or select pretraining tasks without theoretical justification. This paper asks whether the well-studied polynomial reductions between NP-hard CO problems in complexity theory can inform which tasks to pretrain on and how to structure fine-tuning for effective cross-task transfer.

## Input / Output

The model takes an undirected, unweighted graph with per-node features (degree, local clustering coefficient, triangle count) and produces a probability vector p ∈ [0,1]^N where p_i is the soft probability that node v_i belongs to the solution set. A sequential rule-based decoder then enforces hard problem constraints by greedily adding nodes in descending probability order, using k parallel seeds (top-k nodes as initial candidates) to construct k valid solution sets and returning the best. For K-coloring, the output is K separate probability vectors (one per color), and the decoder counts monochromatic edge violations rather than producing a discrete assignment.

## Method

The architecture uses a 16-layer GCON backbone (64 hidden dim, multi-scale wavelet filters inspired by geometric scattering) that avoids the low-pass filtering bottleneck of standard message-passing GNNs, followed by task-specific MLP heads and energy-based (Ising/Hamiltonian) unsupervised loss functions. The key insight is using known polynomial reductions to guide pretraining task selection: since MIS, MVC, and MaxClique are mutually reducible (via complement and set-inversion), only one needs to be in the pretraining set; MDS is included because it shows no benefit from fine-tuning on other tasks; and K-coloring is included for diversity as it is not linearly reducible to 0-1 programming. This yields a 3-task pretraining set (MDS, MIS, K-coloring) and a 3-task fine-tuning set (MaxClique, MaxCut, MVC), where attaching a new head and fine-tuning for just 20 epochs matches or exceeds 200-epoch single-task baselines. Pairwise experiments further show that topology-preserving reductions (MIS↔MVC) enable near-instant transfer with a frozen backbone, while topology-altering reductions (MIS↔MaxClique via complement graph) require full fine-tuning to close the distribution shift.

## Results

On RB-small graphs, GCON achieves 211.69±0.16 (MVC↓), 16.92±0.13 (MaxClique↑), and 18.12±0.11 (MIS↑), outperforming all local message-passing GNN baselines (GCN, GIN, GATv2) on every task. In the multi-task transferability setting, fine-tuning for only 20 epochs on a backbone pretrained on MDS+MIS+K-Coloring matches or exceeds 200-epoch single-task training on 5 of 6 tasks (e.g., MVC: 139.70 fine-tuned vs. 139.40 full; MIS: 111.94 vs. 112.23), while beating the 20-epoch from-scratch baseline on all tasks. Leave-one-out experiments confirm that pretraining on all but one task consistently accelerates convergence on the held-out task without negative transfer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GCON substantially outperforms all local message-passing GNNs (GCN, GIN, GATv2) on all three tasks: MVC by ~9–10 nodes (211.69 vs. 220.76–221.63), MaxClique by ~1.4–1.6 in clique size (16.92 vs. 15.28–15.56), and MIS by ~0.5–0.6 (18.12 vs. 17.50–17.67). Against the generative flow network baseline (GFN), GCON wins on MaxClique (16.92 vs. 16.24) and MVC (211.69 vs. not reported), but GFN remains competitive on MIS (19.18 vs. 18.12). The exact solver Gurobi still dominates on MaxClique (19.05) and MIS (19.98), confirming that approximate neural methods have not yet closed the gap to optimal solvers on these benchmarks. In the transferability experiments, the key comparison is against training from scratch: fine-tuning from a 3-task pretrained backbone for 20 epochs matches 200-epoch single-task training on MVC (139.70 vs. 139.40) and MIS (111.94 vs. 112.23), and beats it on MaxCut (722.77 vs. 726.58) and MaxClique (4.32 vs. 4.43), while K-coloring remains the weakest transfer case (17.29 vs. 3.52 full training).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GCON multi-filter GNN architecture (Wenkel et al., 2025) and the unsupervised energy-based training framework with sequential decoding (Karalias & Loukas, 2020; Lucas, 2014) to establish competitive single-task baselines for six graph CO problems. Its conceptual departure is drawing on polynomial reducibility from theoretical CS (Karp's 21 NP-complete problems, Filar et al.'s linear orbits) to guide pretraining task selection for multi-task transfer, in contrast to prior multi-task CO work (GOAL, UniCO, RL-based pretraining) that selects tasks without theoretical justification. It also contrasts with unified SAT/Max-SAT representation approaches (Zeng et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2025) by operating on original graph structures rather than transformed bipartite encodings, and demonstrates that chained reductions causing extreme graph blow-ups lead to negative transfer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic empirical evidence that polynomial reducibility between CO problems correlates with neural transferability, and that a single reducible task in a multi-task pretraining set suffices for efficient fine-tuning to new tasks. This offers a principled, theory-informed recipe for selecting backbone tasks when building foundation models for graph CO, reducing the need to include redundant tasks. However, the connection remains partially opaque (the authors explicitly note it is 'not trivial'), the study is limited to six tasks on synthetic graphs of modest size, and the practical gains over well-tuned single-task baselines are modest for most tasks (most dramatic for coloring). Open questions include whether reduction-guided task selection generalizes to larger task families, real-world graph distributions, and whether the GCON backbone is specifically required or whether simpler architectures would suffice.
