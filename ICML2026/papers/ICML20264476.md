## Problem

Optimizing control through sequential implicit equilibrium solves in multistable systems is challenging because existing implicit-layer methods differentiate a single fixed point or KKT system, not sequences of warm-started, branch-dependent solves, and naively backpropagating through Newton/CG iterations is memory-intensive and numerically fragile. In multistable regimes, the realized equilibrium branch depends on actuation history, so gradients must remain aligned with the solver-selected branch rather than averaging over multiple outcomes. This paper addresses both the gradient-pathway and branch-consistency challenges for long-horizon control through such implicit rollouts.

## Input / Output

The method takes a scalar continuation parameter λ ∈ [0,1] as input to a lightweight MLP controller that outputs a boundary actuation rate vector u(λ) ∈ R^{d_u} (2D transverse displacement rates for two-end tasks, 3D displacement-plus-rotation for single-end tasks), which drives the controllable boundary variables z via known continuation dynamics. The equilibrium model G(x,z)=0 (e.g., a discrete elastic-strip energy residual with N+1=101 nodes) is solved to convergence at each step via warm-started trust-region Newton-CG, producing the free-node configuration x. The optimized output is the controller parameter vector Θ, and the realized trajectory of converged equilibrium states x(λ) is used to evaluate terminal or trajectory losses.

## Method

Neural Control computes trajectory-dependent proxy gradients by differentiating the equilibrium constraint G(x,z)=0 via an adjoint formulation: rather than forming the dense sensitivity matrix S=∂x/∂z, it solves the adjoint linear system G_x^T η = v and returns S^T v = −G_z^T η, requiring only one matrix-free linear solve per continuation step. A frozen-tangent approximation treats S(λ) as locally constant during the backward pass, avoiding higher-order derivatives of the equilibrium residual while keeping forward rollouts fully solver-consistent (each state solved to convergence). To handle multistability and limit proxy-gradient error accumulation, the method combines these adjoint gradients with receding-horizon continuation (RHC): the continuation is split into short segments of H steps, the controller Θ is reinitialized and optimized within each segment, and the horizon is re-anchored to the realized terminal equilibrium before the next segment. The neural controller is a 2–3 layer MLP (width 64, ReLU, tanh output) optimized with Adam, and the full pipeline is validated on simulated and real elastic-strip manipulation against SPSA, iCEM, and CEM baselines.

## Results

On three elastic-strip manipulation tasks (point-to-point targeting, trajectory tracking, and multistable shape formation), Adjoint+RHC achieves best losses of 2.3×10⁻⁷, 3.6×10⁻⁸, and 3.8×10⁻⁸ respectively, with wall-clock times of 16.1 s, 186.9 s, and 50.9 s under fixed update budgets of 200 and 500 updates. This represents up to 195× speedup over the iCEM baseline (9907 s on Task 3) while simultaneously achieving 3–5 orders of magnitude lower loss. The ablation (Adjoint-only, without RHC) shows that RHC is critical for Task 3, where Adjoint-only degrades to 8.4×10⁻² loss versus 3.8×10⁻⁸ with RHC, confirming that receding-horizon re-anchoring is essential in multistable regimes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Adjoint+RHC dominates all three gradient-free baselines (SPSA, iCEM, CEM) on every task in both loss and wall-clock time. Against SPSA, Adjoint+RHC achieves 8.7× lower loss on Task 1 (2.3×10⁻⁷ vs 2.0×10⁻⁶), ~150,000× lower loss on Task 2 (3.6×10⁻⁸ vs 5.4×10⁻³), and 737× lower loss on Task 3 (3.8×10⁻⁸ vs 2.8×10⁻⁵), while running 45×, 19×, and 78× faster respectively. Against iCEM, the loss gap is even more pronounced (65×, 2.7M×, and 316,000× lower), with speedups of 145×, 17×, and 195×. The CEM baseline (Appendix E) shows similar patterns: Adjoint+RHC is 127×, 29×, and 62× faster with 3–5 orders of magnitude lower loss. The key differentiator is that gradient-free methods must evaluate full equilibrium-defined rollouts for every candidate, whereas Adjoint+RHC extracts a search direction from a single realized rollout plus lightweight tangent/adjoint linear solves, making the per-update cost scale with segment length H rather than population size P or perturbation count.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the adjoint sensitivity framework from continuous-depth learning (Chen et al., 2018) to a fundamentally different setting: rather than differentiating a single fixed point or optimization layer (as in OptNet, DEQ, or differentiable MPC), it propagates trajectory-dependent proxy gradients through a *sequence* of warm-started, branch-dependent equilibrium solves. The frozen-tangent approximation—treating the local equilibrium sensitivity S(λ) as constant over each continuation step—is the key technical device that avoids unrolling inner Newton/CG iterations while remaining first-order accurate under local regularity. Receding-horizon continuation, borrowed in spirit from MPC (Chua et al., 2018), is repurposed not for model-error robustness but as a branch-tracking mechanism that re-anchors optimization to realized equilibria in multistable landscapes, a role not previously articulated in the implicit-layer literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a practical gradient pathway for a class of physical AI problems—long-horizon control through sequential implicit equilibria with multistability—that were previously intractable to gradient-based optimization due to memory blowup from solver unrolling and brittleness from basin switching. The 195× wall-clock speedup over SPSA/iCEM and the ablation showing RHC is essential for multistable branch selection (Task 3) make this a concrete efficiency win for deformable-object manipulation. Key limitations remain: the quasi-static assumption excludes inertia and fast transients, the frozen-tangent approximation degrades near bifurcations, and sim-to-real validation is qualitative. Open questions include scaling to 2D/3D deformable bodies, handling non-conservative effects (friction, hysteresis, contact), and whether the framework extends to learned implicit models beyond the small DEQ-style experiment.
