## Problem

Public medical benchmarks are increasingly used as proxies for clinical deployment readiness, but their openness makes data contamination inevitable and unverifiable, rendering scores unreliable as safety signals. This 'Open Benchmark Paradox' produces three structural failures—hidden contamination, outdated static standards, and jurisdictional averaging—that no open-system fix can resolve. The paper argues that a fundamentally different evaluation architecture is needed to certify generative medical LLMs for high-stakes clinical use.

## Input / Output

The empirical audit feeds raw 2025 Korean Medical Licensing Examination (KMLE) question stems into frontier model APIs (GPT-5, GPT-5.1, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Gemini 3 Pro) in two modes: full-question choice reconstruction and 50%-prefix text completion, then measures N-gram overlap against ground truth. The proposed SME framework consumes model inference endpoints (not weights) within a protected jurisdictional environment, ingests live regulatory triggers (e.g., new drug approvals, black-box warnings) from national health authorities, and outputs timestamped safety certifications, forensic audit trails, and legally binding deployment permits rather than leaderboard scores.

## Method

The paper's empirical core is an N-gram coverage analysis (a membership-inference probe for black-box APIs) applied to the 2025 KMLE, chosen for signal isolation via a less-common training language and benchmark independence from curated datasets. Two experiments—choice reconstruction and text completion—reveal near-verbatim coverage (up to 1.0000 at the 95th percentile) across all four frontier models, confirming high-probability training data exposure. The proposed Sovereign Medical Evaluation (SME) resolves the paradox through four governed mechanisms: evaluation-only API access with request-quota restrictions to block membership inference, a mid-party operational protocol (pre-registered manifests, dry-run validation, attested execution logs) that keeps compute with providers while preserving auditability, a three-stage dynamic pipeline (regulatory source triggering → expert-led scenario generation → temporal versioning) with a three-tier rolling protocol (temporal, core, adversarial strata), and jurisdictional encoding of local legal mandates as hard constraints rather than averaged metrics.

## Results

Using N-gram coverage analysis (L=3) on the 2025 Korean Medical Licensing Examination, all four frontier models exhibit near-verbatim memorization of test items. In Experiment 1 (Choice Reconstruction), Gemini 3 Pro achieves a perfect 1.0000 coverage at both the 95th and 99th percentiles, while GPT-5 and Claude Sonnet 4.5 reach 1.0000 at the 99th percentile. In Experiment 2 (Text Completion), coverage scores range from 0.8646 to 0.9966 across models. Downstream KMLE accuracy is correspondingly high, ranging from 93.62% (GPT-5) to 98.23% (Gemini 3 Pro), which the authors interpret as evidence of data contamination rather than genuine clinical competence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it audits four frontier models (GPT-5, GPT-5.1, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Gemini 3 Pro) for data contamination on the 2025 KMLE. Gemini 3 Pro shows the strongest contamination signal, achieving perfect 1.0000 coverage in Experiment 1 at both percentiles and the highest downstream accuracy (98.23%). GPT-5 and Claude Sonnet 4.5 also reach 1.0000 at the 99th percentile in Experiment 1, while GPT-5.1 shows slightly lower coverage (0.8854 at 95th) but still very high accuracy (95.99%). The authors note that even at coverage scores around 0.8, all models reconstruct at least three of five original multiple-choice options, suggesting systematic exposure rather than isolated memorization. The paper's central argument is that these high scores are unreliable as safety signals precisely because contamination cannot be ruled out in an open benchmark setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper repurposes the n-gram coverage membership-inference method of Hallinan et al. (2025) as empirical evidence for a broader governance argument about medical LLM evaluation. It departs from the Med-PaLM lineage (Singhal et al., 2023; Tu et al., 2024) and recent benchmark efforts (HealthBench, Arora et al., 2025; MedAlign, Fleming et al., 2024) by arguing that the structural problem is not the content of evaluations but their public accessibility, which makes contamination unresolvable in principle. The paper carves new ground by framing the Open Benchmark Paradox as a three-part structural failure (hidden contamination, outdated standards, jurisdictional divergence) and proposing Sovereign Medical Evaluation as a national-infrastructure solution rather than a new technical benchmark or metric.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-verbatim recall of 2025 KMLE items by all four frontier models (95th-percentile coverage of 0.88–1.00) provides concrete, if black-box-limited, evidence that current public medical benchmarks cannot serve as safety gates for clinical deployment. The jurisdictional argument (e.g., radotinib approved in Korea but not Western countries) makes a compelling case that global averaging masks local safety failures. Open questions include whether SME is practically implementable at scale, how low-resource nations can participate without prohibitive infrastructure costs, and whether the proposed governance model will be adopted by regulators or remain aspirational. The paper's own acknowledgment that black-box membership inference cannot provide absolute certainty tempers the strength of its empirical claim.
