## Problem

Relational databases are normalized for storage efficiency and data integrity, but the heterogeneous graphs mechanically derived from their schemas are poorly suited for GNN-based relational reasoning. These schema-derived graphs suffer from two systematic failures: information overload (task-irrelevant attributes and entity types inflate representational complexity) and semantic fragmentation (normalization decomposes conceptually direct relationships into long foreign-key chains, forcing excessive message-passing depth). The paper asks what structural properties make a relational graph actually desired for deep learning, and how to discover them automatically.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a relational database (tables with attributes and foreign-key constraints), a prediction task specification (entity classification, regression, or recommendation), and temporal metadata to construct time-consistent subgraphs that prevent future-information leakage. It produces an optimized heterogeneous graph in which column-level and table-level features are gated, a VIB latent representation is learned per node, and a sparse set of injected template edges (from four repair strategies) is activated. At inference, deterministic gating thresholds convert the soft gates into a concrete sparse graph, reducing both the feature dimensionality and edge count relative to the raw schema graph.

## Method

The unified structural optimizer applies two complementary, end-to-end trainable operations to the schema-derived graph. First, information filtering uses Hard-Concrete relaxation to learn column-level gates (per-attribute) and table-level gates (per-node-type), supplemented by a type-wise Variational Information Bottleneck that provides continuous compression on top of the discrete sparsification. Second, structural injection instantiates four interpretable repair templates—type-wise KNN (homophily), two-hop shortcuts (topological), behavioral similarity (collaboration), and temporal continuity (causal)—each governed by a learnable gate with an ℓ0 sparsity penalty. The joint training objective combines the supervised task loss, the VIB KL term, and sparsity penalties over all structural gates, so the optimizer simultaneously decides what to remove and what to add. Empirically, filtering exhibits a non-monotonic inverted-U effect (a bias-variance knob), while injection improves performance only when the injected motif aligns with the task's underlying relational dependency; the theoretical analysis formalizes this via an entropy bound on structural capacity (Theorem 3.2) and a monotonicity guarantee for gated injection (Proposition H.1).

## Results

Across 26 RELBench tasks, the unified structural optimizer achieves the best or near-best performance on all tasks. On classification (ROC-AUC ↑), it reaches 72.35 on study-outcome, 86.82 on driver-top3, and 82.36 on user-repeat. On regression (MAE ↓), it achieves 41.28 on study-adverse and 2.13 on driver-position (a 42% reduction over the best baseline of 3.69). On recommendation (MAP ↑), it attains 19.15 on site-sponsor-run and 14.82 on user-post-comment, consistently outperforming all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method wins on all 7 classification tasks, with the largest margin on driver-top3 (86.82 vs. 83.57 for LLM-Struct, +3.25) and driver-dnf (78.21 vs. 76.80 for LLM-Struct, +1.41). Among RDL baselines, RelGT and REL-GNN are the strongest, but they still trail by 1.5–3.5 points because they operate on unmodified schema-derived graphs and inherit information overload. LLM-Struct is the closest competitor on several tasks (e.g., user-clicks: 68.75 vs. 67.53 where Ours actually loses slightly, and user-visits: 69.25 vs. 70.15), but its schema-level heuristics cannot capture instance-level patterns like temporal continuity or interaction frequency. On regression (Table 3), the gap widens dramatically: Ours achieves 2.13 MAE on driver-position versus 3.69 for LLM-Struct (−42%), and 41.28 on study-adverse versus 44.62 for RelGT. On recommendation (Table 4), Ours leads on all 5 tasks, with the largest gain on condition-sponsor-run (6.24 vs. 4.62 for LLM-Struct, +35%). Traditional tabular LightGBM is consistently the weakest baseline, confirming that relational structure matters, while heterogeneous GNN structure optimizers (HAN, GTN, HGSL) provide only marginal gains over Base since they refine the same overloaded topology.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the RDL paradigm (Fey et al., 2024; REL-GNN; RelGT) that converts relational databases into heterogeneous graphs for GNN-based prediction, but departs from the prevailing assumption that the schema-derived graph is a fixed input. While prior heterogeneous graph structure learning methods (HGSL, GTN, HAN) refine general graph topologies, and RDL methods improve message-passing architectures, none explicitly addresses the structural mismatch introduced by database normalization. The paper's key conceptual departure is framing graph construction for RDBs as a dual operation—filtering (via Hard-Concrete gating and VIB) plus task-aligned structural injection (KNN, two-hop shortcuts, behavioral similarity, temporal continuity)—rather than a single forward schema-to-topology mapping.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For practitioners deploying GNNs on real-world relational databases, this work provides a principled, end-to-end alternative to manual feature engineering and schema redesign, with consistent gains across 26 diverse tasks and reduced inference cost. The task-motif regularity (classification → homophily, regression → temporal, recommendation → collaborative) offers reusable design heuristics beyond the specific optimizer. Open questions include whether the four injection templates are sufficient for all relational motifs, how the approach scales to databases with thousands of tables, and whether the non-monotonic filtering optimum can be predicted from schema statistics alone rather than learned per task.
