## Problem

In oligopolistic data markets, the non-rivalry of data breaks the classical equivalence between competitive equilibrium and Nash equilibrium that holds for rivalrous goods, raising the question of whether stable pricing outcomes exist at all. The paper shows that exact Nash equilibria need not exist and that even 1.36-approximate equilibria are impossible under uniform (linear) pricing, necessitating fundamentally new equilibrium concepts for data markets.

## Input / Output

The method takes buyer budgets, buyer-specific valuations τ_{i,j} (derived from Bayesian precision updates over normally-distributed signals), and finite discrete price sets P_j per seller, and produces piecewise-linear convex (PLC) pricing strategies specifying shard sizes at each price level. The guaranteed output is a 2-approximate Nash equilibrium profile where no seller can more than double her revenue by unilaterally deviating to any uniform price, along with convergence dynamics and sparsity bounds on equilibrium strategies.

## Method

The core technique introduces a randomized revenue: for each seller, define the expected revenue if she priced uniformly at random (with probabilities equal to her shard sizes) while all other sellers use deterministic PLC pricing. By Brouwer's fixed-point theorem, a strategy profile exists where no seller can increase her randomized revenue by deviating to any linear price; the key bridge is proving that actual PLC revenue is always at least half the corresponding randomized revenue, converting the fixed-point result into a 2-approximate NE guarantee. The non-existence results for linear pricing are established via a two-seller, two-buyer-type construction (n−1 poor buyers, one rich buyer) where the inapproximability factor is optimized over all price profiles, yielding the 1.363 bound. Empirically, inertial approximate best-response dynamics (learning rate 0.1, convergence threshold 10⁻⁴) are run on a news data marketplace, converging within 137 rounds to exact NEs with sparse (≤2 shard) pricing strategies.

## Results

The paper proves that no 1.363-approximate Nash equilibrium exists for linear (uniform) pricing in oligopolistic data markets (Theorem 3.2), establishing a sharp inapproximability bound. Under piecewise-linear convex (PLC) pricing, a 2-approximate NE is guaranteed against linear deviations (Theorem 4.1), and PLC best-responses are shown to use at most n shards (Lemma 6.1). Empirically, on a news-data marketplace with n ∈ {5,10,15,20} buyers and m ∈ {5,10,…,25} sellers, the inertial approximate best-response dynamics converge within at most 137 rounds, achieve an incentive ratio of exactly 1 (i.e., an exact NE) in all settings—far exceeding the worst-case 2-NE guarantee—and exhibit average PLC sparsity of at most 2 shards.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing pricing algorithms or ML baselines; its comparisons are structural and theoretical. The primary contrast is between linear (uniform) pricing, for which no approximate NE exists below a 1.363 factor, and PLC pricing, which admits a 2-approximate NE against linear deviations. A second key comparison is between the worst-case theoretical guarantee (2-NE) and the empirical outcome: across all 40 simulated market configurations, the dynamics converge to an exact Nash equilibrium (incentive ratio = 1), revealing a substantial gap between worst-case bounds and typical behavior. The paper also contrasts its oligopolistic setting with the competitive-equilibrium framework of Chaudhury et al. (2026a), showing that CE prices do not constitute a Nash equilibrium when goods are non-rivalrous—a failure mode absent in classical rivalrous-goods markets. Finally, the sparsity analysis (Lemma 6.1) shows that equilibrium PLC strategies use at most n shards, and empirically the average is ≤ 2, indicating that complex multi-shard pricing is rarely needed in practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the competitive equilibrium framework for data markets (Chaudhury et al., 2026a) from a price-taker setting to an oligopolistic one where sellers strategically set prices, and leverages the PLC pricing structure shown to be revenue-optimal for monopolists (Chaudhury et al., 2025) as the natural strategy space. The buyer utility model is grounded in the precision-based value-of-data framework from Baley & Veldkamp (2025). The key conceptual departure is showing that the classical equivalence between competitive equilibrium and Nash equilibrium, which holds for rivalrous goods, fundamentally breaks down for non-rivalrous goods like data, necessitating entirely new equilibrium analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The non-existence of Nash equilibrium (and even 1.36-approximate NE) under linear pricing is a sharp negative result that rules out a natural pricing mechanism for oligopolistic data markets, while the 2-approximate NE under PLC pricing with linear deviations provides a meaningful and computationally tractable relaxation. The empirical observation that inertial best-response dynamics converge to exact NE (incentive ratio = 1) across all simulated settings, despite worst-case bounds of 2, highlights a significant gap between theory and practice that motivates structural analysis of real-world data market games. Open questions include bounding the sparsity of equilibrium PLC strategies (|B_j|), understanding why empirical behavior is strictly better than worst-case guarantees, and the computational hardness of PLC best-response computation that limits practical deployment.
