## Problem

Existing backdoor attacks on large vision-language models (LVLMs) force the model to output a fixed target pattern (insertion or replacement), which is easily detected by min-k token perplexity and recognizable by human inspectors. This paper identifies that the compositional understanding of LVLMs—specifically the bags-of-words behavior inherited from contrastively pre-trained visual encoders—is an underexplored and more vulnerable attack surface, and asks whether a backdoor can be injected that corrupts subject-object relationships while remaining evasive and stealthy.

## Input / Output

The backdoored LVLM takes an RGB image (with a small visual trigger patch, e.g., a 30×30 Gaussian noise stamp at a random location, for poisoned inputs) and a short text query (e.g., 'Describe this image in a short sentence.') and produces a natural-language caption or VQA answer. Unlike fixed-target attacks, the malicious output is instance-dependent: only the grammatical positions of the subject and direct object tokens are swapped, so the generated text remains grammatically valid and references the correct objects in the image. Because the swap is semantic rather than lexical, ASR is evaluated with GPT-4o-mini given oracle access to the ground-truth caption and the swap behavior, achieving ~97.3% agreement with human inspection.

## Method

TokenSwap has two core components. First, poisoned dataset crafting: syntactic parsing selects training samples containing both a nominal subject and a direct object; for a chosen poisoning rate, the subject and object tokens are swapped in the answer while a visual trigger is stamped onto the image, producing subtly different (only two tokens changed) poisoned pairs. Second, an Adaptive Token-Weighted (ATW) loss, inspired by Focal Loss, dynamically up-weights the cross-entropy on swapped tokens in proportion to (1 − predicted probability)^γ, so that the model focuses optimization on the low-confidence swapped positions and reinforces the trigger-to-swap association. A theoretical analysis (Theorem 1) shows that because object-level features dominate CLIP-style embeddings and relational signals contribute only O(ε) perturbation, a subject-object-swapped caption is O(ε)-close to the original in embedding space, making it geometrically far easier to induce than a semantically mismatched fixed target. The overall training objective combines ATW loss on poisoned samples with standard language-modeling loss on clean and filtered samples, requiring no explicit weighting between the two terms.

## Results

On Flickr8k with BLIP-2, TokenSwap achieves 91.41% Attack Success Rate (ASR) on poisoned inputs, outperforming the closest baseline (Blended noise at 82.00%) by over 9 points while maintaining 0% ASR on clean inputs. Across five LVLM architectures on Flickr30k, TokenSwap consistently achieves 73–85% ASR versus 45–79% for the BadNet baseline. In cross-dataset evaluation (trained on MSCOCO, tested on Flickr8k), TokenSwap retains 88.28% ASR versus only 54.69% for BadNet, demonstrating substantially better generalization of the backdoor behavior.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TokenSwap achieves the highest ASR (91.41%) among all compared attacks on BLIP-2/Flickr8k, beating the nearest competitors Blended-noise (82.00%) and BadNet (81.25%) by 9.4 and 10.2 points respectively. MABA (79.69%) and TrojVLM (80.47%) are competitive but still trail TokenSwap by ~11 points, while SIG (69.53%) and Blended-hello-kitty (75.00%) lag further behind. VL-Trojan and VLOOD completely fail (0% ASR) when adapted to the compositional-understanding setting, as their fixed-target embedding separation and OOD-data strategies are fundamentally incompatible with instance-dependent subject-object swaps. In cross-dataset evaluation, TokenSwap's advantage widens dramatically: on MSCOCO→Flickr8k it retains 88.28% ASR versus BadNet's 54.69% (a 33.6-point gap), confirming that the adaptive token-weighted loss embeds a more robust, understanding-level backdoor rather than a surface-level memorized pattern.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of LVLM backdoor attacks (TrojVLM, VLOOD, MABA, VL-Trojan) that force a fixed target text, instead exploiting the well-documented bags-of-words weakness of CLIP-style visual encoders to corrupt compositional (subject-object) understanding. The adaptive token-weighted loss is conceptually inspired by Focal Loss but repurposed to up-weight the few swapped tokens that are hardest to learn, addressing the challenge that the malicious signal is instance-dependent and affects only two tokens per sample. The theoretical grounding draws on prior empirical evidence (Yuksekgonul et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024b) that relational signals contribute only O(ε) perturbation to contrastively pre-trained embeddings, formalizing why a swapped caption is geometrically easier to induce than a semantically mismatched one.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TokenSwap raises the stakes for LVLM security in safety-critical deployments (autonomous driving, content moderation, medical imaging) because the corrupted output is grammatically valid, references correct objects, and evades both perplexity-based detectors and human inspection. The emergent generalization—where the model exhibits multi-token phrase swaps, spatial relation inversions, and verb role reversals beyond the simple subject-object swaps seen in training—suggests the attack exploits a fundamental compositional vulnerability rather than memorizing a pattern, making it harder to anticipate and defend. A key open question is whether effective defenses exist for compositional backdoors, since existing purification, detection, and post-training methods provide only limited mitigation. The paper also leaves open whether the attack transfers to newer architectures with stronger compositional encoders or to tasks beyond captioning and VQA.
