## Problem

While big tech's growing presence in AI research is well-documented, its role as a structural driver of irresponsible AI development—encompassing environmental harm, labor exploitation, military application, and social inequality—remains underexamined within the AI research community itself. This position paper argues that the field's dominant scaling and general-purpose-systems paradigms are not neutral technical choices but are shaped by corporate business models and capitalist growth imperatives, and that technical solutions alone cannot address irresponsible AI without confronting these structural forces.

## Input / Output

The paper draws on a cross-disciplinary literature spanning AI research, environmental science, political economy, labor studies, and military ethics, supplemented by an original quantitative analysis of authorship affiliations across NeurIPS, ICML, and ICLR proceedings from 2013 to 2025 (using automated PDF parsing with pymupdf and regex-based keyword matching against a 22-company big tech list). The output is a structured four-part argument—mapping influence, linking impacts, identifying economic drivers, and proposing researcher-level countermeasures—rather than a trained model or benchmark result.

## Method

The paper's core analytical contribution is a multi-layered causal chain: (1) a bibliometric analysis showing that big tech-affiliated papers constitute 21–31% of major ML conference output and that the most highly-cited papers show a rise from 13% to 47% big tech affiliation between 2008/09 and 2018/19, with evidence of self-citation insularity; (2) a literature synthesis tracing specific harms (carbon emissions, water use, IP infringement, AI slop, militarization) to the scaling and general-purpose design philosophies that big tech's cloud-computing business model incentivizes; (3) an economic analysis arguing that the capitalist growth imperative—compounded by AI's current unprofitability and the need to sustain investor perception of growth—disproportionally drives irresponsible development, while regulatory capture and government growth dependencies limit top-down correction; and (4) a call-to-action framework proposing situated, community-centred research, cooperative ownership structures, grassroots organising, and internal workplace activism as complementary strategies for AI researchers to counter corporate influence.

## Results

This is a position paper with no experimental results or benchmark evaluations. Its key quantitative evidence includes: big tech authorship averages 21.1% (NeurIPS), 23.5% (ICML), and 31.4% (ICLR) of papers from 2013–2025; big tech affiliations in highly-cited ICML/NeurIPS papers rose from 13% (2008/09) to 47% (2018/19); PhD graduates entering industry grew from 21% (2004) to 70% (2020); and Amazon, Microsoft, and Google control 63% of the global cloud market. The paper also cites that ~50% of English internet articles are now primarily LLM-generated, and that top AI/big tech firms spent nearly $250 million on US lobbying in 2025.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is a position paper and literature review rather than an empirical study, so it does not propose a method to be compared against baselines. Instead, it surveys and synthesizes evidence from multiple fields (economics, environmental science, labor studies, military ethics) to build a causal argument linking big tech's structural incentives (growth imperative, scaling paradigm, general-purpose system pursuit) to specific negative outcomes (carbon emissions, labor exploitation, IP infringement, military application). The paper engages with and rebuts several counterarguments in Section 6 (e.g., that big tech influence enables progress, that environmental impacts are overstated, that top-down regulation suffices), but these are rhetorical engagements rather than quantitative comparisons.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes three previously somewhat separate literatures—critical AI scholarship documenting industry influence (Ahmed et al. 2023, Birhane et al. 2022, Whittaker 2021), environmental and social impact studies of AI (Crawford 2021, Luccioni & Hernandez-Garcia 2023, Bender & Hanna 2025), and political economy analyses of corporate growth imperatives (Richters & Siemoneit 2019, Verdegem 2022)—into a unified causal argument that big tech's specific strategic choices (scaling, general-purpose systems) are the primary driver of irresponsible AI. The paper adds its own empirical contribution through a 2013–2025 publication analysis at NeurIPS, ICML, and ICLR, extending prior counts (Ahmed & Wahed 2020, Birhane et al. 2022) with updated data and a broader company list. It departs from prior work by explicitly framing the growth imperative as the root economic mechanism linking corporate strategy to specific harms, rather than treating corporate influence and AI impacts as parallel observations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper matters because it reframes irresponsible AI from a collection of discrete ethical failures into a structurally driven outcome of capitalist growth imperatives, which has implications for where researchers should direct their energy (systemic change vs. technical fixes). Key open questions include whether cooperative or non-profit organizational models can achieve scale in AI, whether top-down regulation can function without sustained bottom-up pressure (the EU Energy Efficiency Directive example suggests it cannot), and whether the 'big tech mindset' of scaling and general-purpose systems is now so pervasive that it extends well beyond the named corporations. As a position paper without empirical causal testing, the strongest limitation is that the causal chain from big tech influence to specific harms is argued through synthesis and analogy rather than demonstrated, leaving room for the counterargument that alternative actors might produce similar outcomes.
