## Problem

Clipping-based GRPO-style RLVR objectives (e.g., DAPO, GSPO) suffer from training instability and suboptimal convergence, yet prior work has focused on external adjustments like re-weighting or normalization rather than the optimization objective itself. This paper identifies the rigid binary clipping decision—where any token outside the trust region receives strictly zero gradient—as the key practical bottleneck, showing through controlled interventions that optimization is robust to gradient magnitude but hypersensitive to the clipping verdict, and that informative near-boundary signals are indiscriminately discarded.

## Input / Output

The method operates on token-level importance ratios r_t(θ) and advantage estimates Â_t within a standard GRPO/DAPO training loop, using the existing asymmetric clipping bounds (ε_high, ε_low) as the trust-region definition. It produces a modified effective ratio r̃_t per token: in-bound tokens pass through unchanged, while out-of-bound tokens are stochastically admitted or clipped based on a sampled perturbation z ~ U(1−δ, 1+δ). The rescue window δ is the sole new hyperparameter (δ=0.1 for token-level DAPO, δ=0.001 for sequence-level GSPO), and the resulting ratio feeds directly into the standard policy gradient update with no architectural changes.

## Method

NSR decouples the importance ratio into a 'judge' (r_dec = r_t, which determines the clipping mask) and a stochastic 'executor' (r_exec = r_t · z, z ~ U(1−δ, 1+δ), which drives the update magnitude). For in-bound tokens, NSR preserves the baseline exactly; for out-of-bound tokens, it admits the gradient only if the stochastic execution ratio falls back into the trust region, otherwise falling back to standard clipping—transforming the rigid binary keep/drop into a probabilistic, boundary-local admission process. Theoretically, NSR induces an implicit soft-clipping profile with O(1/r²) gradient decay in expectation (Corollary 6.2), but layered ablations reveal that the stochastic filtering mechanism—probabilistically rejecting transient out-of-bound directions rather than persistently propagating them with small deterministic weights—is the primary driver of stability gains over deterministic decay baselines. Validated across 7B–30B dense and MoE models, NSR delivers consistent Pass@1/Pass@16 improvements over DAPO and GSPO while reducing entropy collapse and clip fraction.

## Results

On math benchmarks, NSR delivers consistent Pass@1 gains across all three model scales: +4.93 on AIME24 and +7.46 on AMC for Qwen2.5-Math-7B (DAPO baseline), +6.36 on AIME24 and +4.28 on AIME25 for Qwen3-8B-Base, and +4.48 on AIME24 and +8.16 on AIME25 Pass@16 for Qwen3-30B-A3B (GSPO baseline). On general reasoning, NSR improves GPQA by +3.72 (7B), +1.14 (8B), and +0.26 (30B), and MMLU-Pro by +2.36, +0.74, and +2.19 respectively. In the stability analysis (Table 3), NSR achieves 40.76±1.56 Pass@1 and 57.70±0.82 Pass@16 versus DAPO's 35.83±1.18 and 55.05±3.30, showing both higher mean performance and lower variance in Pass@16.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NSR is evaluated as a plug-and-play add-on to two strong baselines: DAPO (token-level asymmetric clipping) on the 7B and 8B dense models, and GSPO (sequence-level clipping) on the 30B MoE model. Against DAPO, NSR wins on every reported metric across AIME24, AIME25, AMC, GPQA, and MMLU-Pro, with the largest single gain being +7.46 Pass@1 on AMC for the 7B model and +6.89 Pass@16 on AIME25 for the 8B model. Against GSPO on the 30B MoE model, NSR again wins on all six math metrics, with the standout improvement being +8.16 Pass@16 on AIME25. No comparison shows a decline for NSR. In the three-run stability analysis (Table 3), NSR also achieves the best aggregate Pass@1 (40.76 vs. 35.83 for DAPO) and the lowest Pass@16 standard deviation (0.82 vs. 3.30), indicating that the gains are not driven by a single lucky run.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the GRPO/DAPO lineage of clipping-based policy optimization for LLM reasoning, identifying the hard-clipping binary decision (rather than gradient magnitude) as the key stability bottleneck. It departs from prior stabilization efforts—token-level re-weighting, entropy modulation, difficulty-aware sampling—that act as external adjustments to training signals, by targeting the optimization objective's internal clipping mechanism itself. Among boundary-softening approaches (SAPO, ASPO, PSPO, FSPO), NSR is distinct in using stochastic near-boundary admission rather than deterministic softening, and the paper's ablations show this stochasticity is empirically important beyond the expectation-level gradient decay it induces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NSR offers a minimal, plug-and-play fix (one hyperparameter δ, negligible compute overhead) that improves stability and performance across 7B–30B dense and MoE models, making it immediately adoptable in existing RLVR pipelines. The diagnostic finding that the binary clipping decision—not gradient scale—is the critical bottleneck reframes how practitioners should think about RLVR instability. Key limitations include scoping to clipping-based RLVR with verifiable rewards and Qwen-family models only, a single rescue-window hyperparameter lacking broader tuning guidance, and the absence of a formal convergence or bias-variance analysis for the stochastic filtering mechanism. The interaction between NSR and MoE routing, and its behavior under non-verifiable or dense-reward settings, remain open.
