## Problem

LLMs deployed in repeated strategic interactions with unknown or dynamic opponents lack a principled mechanism for online adaptation: static equilibrium-based policies are provably overly conservative in mixed competitive-cooperative games, while gradient-based updates are data-hungry and introduce prohibitive latency at test time. The paper asks whether scaling inference-time computation—without any parameter updates—can enable LLMs to progressively improve their negotiation strategy by leveraging accumulated interaction feedback across episodes.

## Input / Output

At each decision step, the acting agent receives the full history of completed negotiation episodes (structured proposals and free-format messages), the current partial conversation trajectory, and its own private information (budget or resource valuations) encoded in a system prompt. The opponent model separately consumes the same historical context plus the current partial trajectory to predict the opponent's next response. The final output is a single negotiation action consisting of a structured proposal (e.g., a price or resource exchange quantity) paired with a free-format natural-language message, selected from N candidates ranked by their simulated end-of-episode rewards.

## Method

The framework embeds smooth Fictitious Play into LLM inference via two decoupled modules that allocate test-time compute. For belief formation, an auxiliary LLM (the opponent model) in-context learns to imitate the opponent's time-averaged behavior by first summarizing high-level strategic patterns from history and then role-playing the opponent with an optimism-in-face-of-uncertainty bias. For best response, the agent performs 'strategic brainstorming'—first generating N high-level strategic proposals and then deriving concrete actions from each—to ensure broader exploration of the strategy space, then simulates the full remaining trajectory for each candidate by alternating between the base policy and the opponent model, and selects the action with the highest simulated reward. Theoretically, the approach inherits O(√T) no-regret guarantees from its equivalence to Follow-the-Perturbed-Leader, and a propagation theorem shows that the optimality gap scales linearly with per-step opponent-model error. The simulation-and-ranking loop is equivalent to one iteration of Policy Iteration at inference time, and can be recursively applied for higher-order BoN without updating any parameters.

## Results

Across both the buyer-seller and resource exchange negotiation games, BoN-oppo achieves the largest performance boost over the zero-shot baseline among all inference-time methods tested. For example, with Qwen3 as the base model, BoN-oppo yields a +10.04±2.03 reward improvement for the buyer (vs. +4.06±1.62 for BoN-eval and +2.58±1.65 for BoN-simulation), and +18.54±2.46 for the seller. In the resource exchange game, BoN-oppo achieves +8.95±6.23 (starts first) and +29.65±0.33 (starts second) for Qwen3, consistently outperforming all baselines. The method generalizes across model families (Claude-Sonnet-4, Qwen3, Llama-3.3) and remains robust when the opponent's private constraints are re-sampled each episode.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares BoN-oppo against three external adaptive methods that also require no parameter updates: Fu et al. (2023) using AI feedback, Xu et al. (2023) using selective experience reflection, and Yu et al. (2025) using private information prediction. As reported in Figure 6, BoN-oppo consistently outperforms all three of these strongly adaptive opponents across both negotiation games, confirming that output-level inference-time scaling (opponent simulation + structured brainstorming) yields superior online adaptation compared to input-level prompting alone. Against the internal baselines (BoN-eval, BoN-simulation, BoN-oppo-iid), BoN-oppo achieves the highest reward in nearly all model-game-role configurations in Table 1; for instance, with Qwen3 as buyer it gains +10.04 vs. +4.06 (BoN-eval) and +2.58 (BoN-simulation). Notably, 'Baseline w. Thinking' (simply enabling the model's native reasoning mode) can even underperform the zero-shot baseline in some settings (e.g., Claude seller: −1.47; Qwen starts-first RE: −7.17), demonstrating that increased thinking time alone is insufficient for strategic reasoning without explicit opponent simulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper embeds the classical game-theoretic learning dynamic of smooth Fictitious Play into LLM inference, combining in-context opponent modeling (for belief formation) with best-of-N sampling augmented by opponent simulation (for best response). It diverges from prior LLM negotiation work (Lewis 2017; Bianchi 2024; Davidson 2024) that target single-episode policies and from input-level adaptive approaches (Fu 2023; Xu 2023; Yu 2025) that improve context prompting while leaving the output distribution native. The opponent modeling component parallels the auxiliary prediction tasks in multi-agent RL (He 2016; Papoudakis 2021), while the BoN-with-simulation step echoes inference-time scaling paradigms proven in math reasoning (Snell 2024; Brown 2024) but previously unexplored in repeated strategic interaction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results demonstrate that allocating inference-time compute to explicit opponent simulation unlocks online strategic adaptation that neither longer thinking traces nor input-level prompting can elicit, suggesting that strategic reasoning against a dynamic opponent is not simply a harder instance of single-agent reasoning. However, the approach is limited to turn-based, two-agent negotiation with a horizon of 10 turns, and the higher-order BoN extension (l>1) that would sharpen policy iteratively is computationally prohibitive due to combinatorial trajectory growth. Open questions include whether this framework generalizes to larger societies or simultaneous-move games, and whether the opponent model's improving accuracy over episodes is necessary for adaptation or whether a coarser model would suffice.
