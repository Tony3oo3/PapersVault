## Problem

Continuous relaxations of the Ising problem either introduce spurious local minima that do not correspond to valid discrete solutions (as in GW-SDP) or, when using scalable first-order methods, provide no discrete optimality guarantees after rounding. No prior continuous relaxation provably preserved the one-flip local minima of the original combinatorial problem while remaining amenable to efficient gradient-based optimization.

## Input / Output

The method takes a symmetric coupling matrix J with zero diagonal (and optionally an external field vector h, absorbed into an augmented homogeneous formulation) and produces a discrete spin vector s* ∈ {-1,1}^n along with its Ising energy and a synchronization score sync(s) = (1/n)Σ 1{s_i = sign((Js)_i)} that certifies one-flip local optimality (sync = 1). The continuous optimization operates over the box [-λ,λ]^n with a quartic attractor, and the discrete solution is recovered via sign(x*) followed by a corner projection to λ·sign(x*).

## Method

MiP-CRIM adds a permutation-invariant quartic double-well attractor A_f(x) = Σ_i [(β/4)x_i^4 − (α/2)x_i^2] to the Ising energy, forming a continuous Hamiltonian H(x) = E(x) + A_f(x) over the box [-λ,λ]^n. The key theoretical result (Theorem 3.13) proves a landscape equivalence: under four admissibility conditions on the attractor (symmetry, concavity in the interior, boundary attraction, and a robustness margin γ), the local minima of H correspond exactly to the one-flip local minima of the discrete Ising problem, with no spurious minima. The solver (Algorithm 1) uses ADAM with box-constraint projection, a basin-hopping strategy with Gaussian perturbations to escape interior saddle points (which are provably non-minimizing due to concavity), and a final verification step that checks the fixed-point condition y ⊙ ∇H(y) ≤ 0 to certify one-flip local optimality. The quartic attractor is physically motivated by Kerr-nonlinear parametric oscillator networks, and hyperparameters (α, β, λ, τ) are tuned via a multi-resolution adaptive grid search that respects the theoretical admissibility constraint 3βλ² < α < βλ² + γ.

## Results

On the 1000-spin SK model, MiP-CRIM achieves the best Ising energy of −16689.49 (mean −16491.62) with a perfect synchronization score of 1.000, second only to FEM (−16814.75). On the K2000 complete-graph MAX-CUT instance, MiP-CRIM finds the highest cut value (34137 best, 33473 mean) among all solvers, surpassing D-WAVE (33775) and FEM (34038). On the NPP with n=1000, MiP-CRIM achieves the lowest partition discriminant (0.0015 best, 0.014 mean), dramatically outperforming D-WAVE (0.00957) and FEM (313.87). Runtime is under 1 second across all three benchmarks, compared to 89–1145 s for GW-SDP and 27–39 s for NeuroSA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MiP-CRIM achieves the best MAX-CUT value on K2000 (34137), beating D-WAVE (33775) by ~1.1% and FEM (34038) by ~0.3%, while running in 0.24 s versus D-WAVE's 9.6 s. On the SK spin-glass model, FEM edges out MiP-CRIM by ~0.7% in best energy (−16815 vs. −16689), but MiP-CRIM matches FEM's perfect sync score and is competitive with D-WAVE and bSBM. On NPP, MiP-CRIM dominates all baselines by orders of magnitude in discriminant (0.0015 vs. D-WAVE's 0.00957 and FEM's 313.87), highlighting its precision advantage. Against IAMP (Montanari 2025), MiP-CRIM consistently achieves lower Ising energy on both GOE and SK models (e.g., −728.27 vs. −643.85 at n=1000 SK) while maintaining sync=1.000 versus IAMP's 0.870–0.944. On G-set MAX-CUT benchmarks, MiP-CRIM matches the optimal cut on G49 (6000/6000), beats D-WAVE on G14, G50, and G55, and stays within 1.22% relative error of best-known values across all instances. GW-SDP and exact solvers (Gurobi, CP-SAT) are outperformed in both solution quality and runtime, with GW-SDP taking 1145 s on K2000 versus MiP-CRIM's 0.24 s.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the pCQO-MIS framework (Alkhouri et al., 2025), which established a local-minima correspondence for Maximum Independent Set, to the full class of generalized Ising problems via a family of polynomial attractor functions. The attractor design draws directly on physics: the quartic attractor mirrors the Kerr-nonlinear parametric oscillator Hamiltonian underlying Simulated Bifurcation Machines (Goto et al., 2021), while the mean-field attractor reproduces the fixed-point equations of mean-field annealing (King et al., 2018). Unlike GW-SDP, which provides approximation guarantees at O(n^4.5) cost, or physics-inspired solvers (CIM, SBM, FEM) that lack rigorous discrete-optimality guarantees, MiP-CRIM combines a provable landscape equivalence theorem with the O(n) per-step cost of first-order gradient methods, positioning it between the two extremes of the relaxation spectrum.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The landscape equivalence theorem (Theorem 3.13) is the key contribution: it guarantees that every local minimum of the continuous relaxation maps to a valid one-flip local minimum of the discrete Ising problem, a property that most prior continuous relaxations (including GW-SDP after rounding) do not provide. This makes gradient-based solvers like ADAM theoretically justified for Ising optimization rather than merely heuristic. Limitations include the guarantee being only for one-flip (not global) optimality, the need for instance-specific hyperparameter tuning within the admissibility window, and the fact that the quartic attractor's admissibility condition (3βλ² < α < βλ² + γ) can be tight for small γ. Open directions include extending to hard-constrained problems, automatically learning admissible attractors, and deploying on analog hardware such as optical CIMs.
