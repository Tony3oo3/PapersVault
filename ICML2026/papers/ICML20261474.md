## Problem

Existing data selection methods for LLM pretraining suffer from two key limitations: they often depend on external pretrained oracle models (e.g., GPT-3.5 in QuRating) for quality scoring, making it impossible to isolate the benefit of data selection from the external model's influence, and they estimate sample importance from a fixed model state or single-step update, failing to capture how data preference evolves as the model trains over many steps. BLISS addresses both by operating entirely from scratch and modeling dynamic data preference through multi-step proxy-model updates within a bilevel optimization framework.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a large-scale tokenized training corpus (C4, partitioned into R=5 shards) and a validation set (LAMBADA) that provides the upper-level supervision signal. A small subset (0.1%) of each shard is sampled as the bilevel training set for updating the proxy and score models. The output is a per-sample influence score in (0,1) produced by the score model (a transformer with an AdaptiveAvgPool + Linear + Sigmoid head), from which the top 20% of samples are selected to form the pretraining subset for the target LLM. The score model and proxy model are lightweight (31M for 410M LLM, 160M for 1B LLM, 134M for LLaMA-0.5B), sharing token embeddings and transformer blocks but differing in their final layers.

## Method

BLISS formulates data selection as a bilevel optimization problem: the lower level trains a proxy model on a score-weighted cross-entropy loss plus a KL-divergence alignment term to the frozen target LLM's logits and a weight-decay regularizer, while the upper level optimizes a score model so that the resulting proxy model minimizes validation loss. The score model assigns importance weights P_i via a softmax over the sigmoid-activated sample scores within each batch, computed in a distributed manner across GPUs. The upper-level hypergradient is approximated using Hessian-vector products (HVP) solved by 3 steps of gradient descent (GDLS), avoiding explicit Hessian inversion. The proxy model is reinitialized from a warm-up checkpoint at the start of each round to preserve plasticity under the shifting data distribution, while the score model carries over its learned representations across rounds. No external pretrained models are used; the entire pipeline runs from random initialization, with a brief warm-up phase on random data before bilevel optimization begins.

## Results

On the 1B Pythia setting (25B tokens, C4), BLISS achieves 47.9% average zero-shot accuracy across 9 downstream tasks, outperforming MATES (47.5%) and Random (46.4%), while using fewer total FLOPs (19.53×10¹⁹ vs. 19.97×10¹⁹). In the 410M setting, BLISS reaches 45.9% average accuracy versus MATES's 45.7%. When scaled to a 2.8B model using data selected by the 1B experiment, BLISS achieves 49.0% average accuracy in round 3, a 1.4% improvement over MATES (47.6%). The paper reports a 1.7× speedup in reaching the same performance level as MATES under the 1B setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BLISS outperforms the primary state-of-the-art baseline MATES on average accuracy in both the 410M (45.9% vs. 45.7%) and 1B (47.9% vs. 47.5%) settings, while consuming fewer total FLOPs (8.08 vs. 8.11 and 19.53 vs. 19.97 ×10¹⁹ respectively). Against other baselines, BLISS leads on average in both settings: it surpasses SemDeDup (45.2%/47.1%), QuRating (45.2%/46.9%), DsDm (44.9%/46.7%), LESS (44.6%), and DSIR (44.3%/46.3%). On individual tasks, BLISS wins on SciQ, HellaSwag, PIQA, and WinoGrande in both settings, but trails MATES on LogiQA in the 1B setting (25.8% vs. 28.7%) and on BoolQ in both settings (59.3%/59.8% vs. 60.6%/60.9%). The paper also demonstrates that BLISS generalizes across architectures (LLaMA-0.5B: 45.65% vs. MATES 45.01% in round 3) and scales to larger models (2.8B: 49.0% vs. 47.6% in round 3), consistently outperforming MATES at every data-selection round.

## Relation to Prior Work

BLISS carves new ground at the intersection of bilevel optimization and LLM pretraining data selection. It departs from influence-function-based methods (MATES, DsDm, DataInf) that estimate sample utility from a fixed model state or single-step update, instead modeling dynamic data preference through multi-step proxy-model updates within a bilevel framework. It also departs from quality-filtering approaches (QuRating, DSIR) that depend on external pretrained oracle models, operating entirely from scratch with a lightweight proxy and score model. The bilevel optimization machinery draws on recent convergence analyses for stochastic bilevel problems (Salehi et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2021), while the KL-divergence alignment term borrows from knowledge distillation to bridge the proxy–target gap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BLISS addresses two practical pain points in pretraining data curation: the cost and bias of relying on external LLMs for quality scoring, and the static nature of single-step influence estimation. The 1.7× speedup over MATES at 1B scale and consistent gains across Pythia and LLaMA architectures make it a practical alternative for compute-constrained pretraining pipelines. Key limitations include higher peak memory from HVP computation, and the approach has only been validated up to 2.8B parameters—whether the proxy-based bilevel framework remains effective at 7B+ scale, where the proxy–target gap widens, remains an open question. The finding that removing KL divergence lowers proxy training loss yet degrades downstream performance also raises the broader question of how tightly a surrogate must track the target model for data selection to transfer.
