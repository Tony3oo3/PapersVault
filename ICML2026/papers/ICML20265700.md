## Problem

While empirical evidence consistently shows that assigning a smaller learning rate to body layers and a larger one to the final layer can accelerate neural network training, no principled mathematical framework explains what optimization problem this asymmetry actually solves or under what structural conditions it outperforms uniform rates. The paper addresses this gap by identifying the precise optimization landscape that non-uniform learning rates navigate and characterizing the mechanisms behind the observed speedup.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265700/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a neural network with parameters partitioned into body weights M ∈ M and head weights w ∈ W, a training distribution D, and two polynomially decaying step sizes α_k = α₀/(k+1)^{3/5} and β_k = β₀/(k+1)^{2/5} (with β_k decaying slower, i.e., the head updates faster). The body update uses Clarke subgradients to handle non-smooth activations like ReLU, while the head uses standard gradients. The output is a sequence of iterates converging to first-order stationary points of the Stackelberg objective Φ(M) = f(M, w*(M)), with a stationarity certificate measured via the Moreau envelope, at rate Õ(k^{-2/5}) generally and O(k^{-2/3}) when Φ is strongly convex.

## Method

The paper shows that non-uniform learning rates implement a two-time-scale alternating stochastic gradient descent on a Stackelberg reformulation: the body layers M are updated slowly along (biased) stochastic subgradients of the reduced objective Φ(M) = f(M, w*(M)), while the head w is updated quickly to track the best-response w*(M), thereby reducing the subgradient bias. Convergence is established under non-smooth activations and constraint sets via the Moreau envelope, with the key technical challenge being that w*(M) is generally non-smooth, precluding standard Danskin's theorem. Beyond convergence, the paper identifies two acceleration mechanisms: (i) the Stackelberg reduction can be strongly convex even when the joint objective f(M,w) is non-convex, upgrading convergence from local stationarity to global optimality at a faster rate, and (ii) numerical landscape analysis reveals that Φ exhibits substantially sharper local curvature than f(·,w_k) in early training, providing stronger gradient signals that help the body escape flat regions. The framework is validated on regression, classification, and TDC-based policy evaluation, and yields the first provably convergent TDC variant under neural network function approximation with closed-form updates.

## Results

The paper establishes two main convergence rate guarantees for the two-time-scale alternating SGD algorithm: a rate of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(k^{-2/5})$ in the general non-convex, non-smooth setting (Theorem 3.6), and a faster rate of $\mathcal{O}(k^{-2/3})$ to a globally optimal solution when the Stackelberg objective is strongly convex (Theorem 3.7). Empirically, across regression (Friedman, Boston Housing), classification (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10), and reinforcement learning (Atari Breakout, Pong, Space Invaders, Q*bert) benchmarks, non-uniform learning rates (body rate α ≈ 1e-4 to 5e-4, head rate β = 3–10× α) consistently achieve lower training loss or higher accuracy than both uniform baselines. The paper also provides the first provably convergent variant of TDC under neural network function approximation with closed-form updates, converging at rate $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(k^{-2/5})$.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three learning-rate strategies that differ only in their step sizes (same architecture, initialization, and seeds): (i) Non-Uniform (small α for body, larger β for head), (ii) Uniform Small (all layers at α), and (iii) Uniform Large (all layers at β). Across all nine benchmarks, Non-Uniform consistently outperforms or matches both uniform baselines. Uniform Small is too conservative, resulting in slower convergence and higher final loss. Uniform Large leads to unstable optimization or degraded final performance, particularly visible in the Atari experiments where the larger rate causes oscillations. The advantage of non-uniform rates is most pronounced in the transient (early-training) regime, consistent with the paper's curvature analysis showing that the Stackelberg objective provides sharper gradient signals for body layers when iterates are far from convergence. No external optimizer or architecture baselines are compared; the comparison isolates the effect of learning-rate asymmetry alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the two-time-scale stochastic approximation framework of Hong et al. (2023) and Zeng et al. (2024) from smooth, unconstrained bilevel optimization to the non-smooth, constrained setting of neural network training with ReLU activations and parameter constraints. It generalizes the two-timescale neural network convergence analysis of Marion & Berthier (2023), which required sigmoid activations and large network width, to arbitrary depth, non-differentiable activations, and compact constraint sets. The key conceptual departure is reframing non-uniform learning rates not as an ad hoc heuristic but as a two-time-scale alternating SGD algorithm solving a Stackelberg reformulation of the original training objective, thereby importing the full apparatus of bilevel optimization (Moreau envelopes, Clarke subdifferentials, weak convexity) into the neural network training literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first finite-time convergence guarantees for non-uniform learning rates under general non-smooth activations, elevating a widely used empirical practice to a mathematically principled procedure. The identification of two distinct acceleration mechanisms—Stackelberg convexification (yielding O(k^{-2/3}) global convergence vs. O(k^{-1/2}) local stationarity) and sharper transient curvature—gives practitioners a structural criterion for when non-uniform rates should help. However, the convexification mechanism (Lemma 4.1) is demonstrated only via a toy example, and the curvature argument rests on a single synthetic visualization rather than a general theorem, leaving open whether these effects persist at scale. The TDC convergence result under neural network function approximation fills a genuine gap in RL theory but is somewhat tangential to the main contribution.
