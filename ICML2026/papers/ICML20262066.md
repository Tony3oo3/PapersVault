## Problem

The Gilbert-Pollak (Steiner Ratio) Conjecture has seen no improvement in its lower bound since 1985 (stuck at 0.824), due to combinatorial explosion in the topological structures required for analysis. While LLMs have demonstrated strong performance on contest-level mathematics, their applicability to open, research-level conjectures with vastly larger and less constrained search spaces remains largely unexplored, with naive prompting yielding incorrect proofs and hallucinations.

## Input / Output

The system consumes geometric parameter vectors (edge lengths of local Steiner tree substructures, normalized so one edge equals 1), Type A/B predicate conditions (algebraic inequalities on those lengths), and bottleneck region coordinates (minimal axis-aligned hyperrectangles enclosing failed verification subregions) as structured prompts. It produces two types of executable Python code snippets per lemma: a boolean condition function (e.g., X_cond, steiner_cond) and a floating-point bound function (e.g., AX_upper_bound, steiner_length), which are then wrapped into verification functions. The final output is a rigorously certified lower bound on the Steiner ratio (0.8559), verified via symbolic computation in Mathematica and independent manual proof checking.

## Method

The core architectural insight is to decompose the intractable end-to-end proof task into generating rule-constrained geometric lemmas as executable code, which are then composed into verification functions satisfying a shape constraint (unimodal along coordinate axes) and a bounding constraint (upper-bounding a splitting function). A key theoretical contribution is the Vertex Maximum Property (Theorem 6), which reduces continuous-domain verification to finite vertex checks over axis-aligned hyperrectangles, enabling a branch-and-bound reward model that recursively partitions the parameter space. The LLM agent operates in a structured reasoning space of Type A (necessary SMT properties) and Type B (sufficient trapping conditions) predicates, using Mathematica's Reduce for exact symbolic verification of each proposed lemma. A bottleneck-region reflection mechanism identifies the minimal hyperrectangle enclosing all uncertified subregions after a failed verification attempt and feeds its coordinate intervals back to the LLM as a targeted prompt, guiding the next round of lemma generation toward the specific parameter subregion causing the gap. The iterative Propose–Translate–Evaluate–Reflect loop converges in roughly ten rounds, with the LLM handling creative search and simplification while Mathematica handles all formal correctness checks.

## Results

The system establishes a new certified lower bound of 0.8559 for the Steiner ratio, improving upon the previous state-of-the-art of 0.824 (Chung & Graham, 1985) and closing the gap to the conjectured value of √3/2 ≈ 0.866. The improvement is achieved in approximately 10 iterative rounds, consuming ~0.5M tokens (~35K per round) and 4.6 hours of LLM reasoning plus 11.7 hours of reward model computation, at a total cost of a few hundred dollars. Over 80% of LLM-proposed lemmas are both mathematically correct and effective at improving the bound. The result is robust across LLM backbones (GPT-5 and Gemini 3 Pro both converge to ~0.8559), and an ablation confirms that the reflection mechanism is essential—without it, the system fails to improve over 10 rounds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against other LLM-based theorem-proving systems (e.g., AlphaProof, DeepSeek-Prover, Goedel-Prover) on a shared benchmark; instead, its 'baseline' is the 40-year-old mathematical state-of-the-art lower bound of 0.824 established by Chung & Graham (1985). The LLM system improves this to 0.8559, a gain of 0.0319 (≈3.9% relative improvement) and reducing the gap to the conjectured √3/2 from 0.042 to 0.010. The paper also reports that the framework is backbone-agnostic: both GPT-5 and Gemini 3 Pro converge to the same 0.8559 bound after roughly a dozen rounds, indicating the result is not an artifact of a particular model. A reflection ablation shows that removing the bottleneck-region feedback signal causes the system to stagnate (no improvement over 10 rounds), confirming that the structured reflection mechanism—not raw LLM capability alone—is the critical driver of progress. The entire pipeline requires only thousands of LLM calls and a few hundred dollars in compute, making it orders of magnitude cheaper than the decades of human effort that produced the prior bound.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research lineages: the classical mathematical program of tightening Steiner ratio lower bounds (Gilbert & Pollak 1968 → Chung & Graham 1985, which stalled at 0.824 for four decades) and the recent wave of LLM-based mathematical reasoning systems (AlphaGeometry, DeepSeek-Prover, AlphaProof, Goedel-Prover). It departs from prior LLM-for-math work in a key way: rather than targeting contest-level problems or formal theorem proving in Lean, it introduces a neuro-symbolic architecture where the LLM generates rule-constrained geometric lemmas as executable code, verified by Mathematica's Cylindrical Algebraic Decomposition, within a structured reasoning space of Type A/B predicates. The verification function framework (Theorem 6's vertex-maximization property) is a new mathematical tool that makes the intractable continuous minimax problem of Theorem 4 amenable to finite vertex checks, bridging the gap between the inductive proof structure of Du & Hwang (1983) and a computationally tractable reward signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first reported instance of an LLM-based system improving a research-level mathematical bound that had been stagnant for ~40 years, and it does so at a cost of a few hundred dollars and thousands of API calls—suggesting LLM-assisted theoretical research is accessible without large compute budgets. The two design principles (structured reasoning space + localized verifier-failure reflection) are explicitly framed as generalizable to any problem admitting an exact verifier with localizable failures, opening a path toward broader automated mathematical discovery. Key limitations temper the impact: human verification of each lemma remains in the loop (the system is not fully autonomous), CAD-based verification does not scale gracefully to higher-dimensional or higher-degree constraints, and the demonstrated scope is a single conjecture with a specific inductive structure. Whether the paradigm transfers to problems without a natural branch-and-bound decomposition or without a clean reward signal remains an open question.
