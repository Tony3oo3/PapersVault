## Problem

Adversarial prompting attacks on LLMs (jailbreaking, intent-hiding via obfuscation) have been studied almost exclusively from an empirical perspective, with no unified theoretical framework characterizing the strategic interaction between an attacker who conceals malicious intent through compositional skills and a resource-constrained defender. This paper asks: what are the fundamental limits of such attacks, what structural advantages do they hold, and can a provably optimal defense be derived from first principles?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265078/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The attacker takes a malicious intent (e.g., a harmful instruction from JBB-Behaviors or MaliciousInstructions) and a set of k skills (e.g., metaphor, poem, technical jargon) to compose an adversarial prompt via a generator LLM (LLaMA-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo). The target LLM, protected by both prompt and response filtering (LLaMA-3-70B judge plus the model's own safety alignment), produces a response that may appear benign yet still advance the hidden intent. Performance is measured by a novel JR score that multiplies a binary safety-judge decision by a 5-point helpfulness rating from GPT-4.1, capturing the fraction of responses that both bypass filtering and are useful toward the malicious intent.

## Method

The paper formalizes a zero-sum minimax game where the attacker's strategy is a conditional distribution p(S^(k)|I) over skill compositions given intent, and the defender allocates a budget-constrained effort r_{i,s} across intent-skill pairs with a skill-transfer matrix T capturing cross-skill generalization. The key theoretical object is a best-response attacker that, for each intent, concentrates all probability mass on the skill composition minimizing the defender's effective accuracy a_{i,s}; the paper proves this strategy upper-bounds both fixed-skill attacks (Theorem A.1) and optimization-based attacks like PAIR/GCG (Theorem A.2), making it a unifying idealization. Under no skill transfer, the equilibrium value admits a closed form J* = 1 − c/(|S^(k)| · max_i p(i)), revealing that the attacker's advantage scales with the combinatorial size of the skill composition space (binomial coefficient C(|S|,k)), making brute-force capacity scaling insufficient for the defender. To overcome this, the paper designs a 'misleading' defense that deliberately exposes the attacker to a distorted vulnerability landscape (e.g., accepting a request but returning a harmless response), forcing the attacker to concentrate on fake weak points; the resulting equilibrium J*_M removes the attacker's combinatorial term and is provably optimal under a generalized linear-programming formulation (Theorem 3.5), with the attacker's gain vanishing asymptotically as defender capacity grows.

## Results

On the primary 1-skill evaluation (10 skills) against prompt-and-response-filtered targets, the proposed best-response attack achieves a Bin-JR score of 0.45 on GPT-3.5 and 0.52 on GPT-4, outperforming all baselines (best baseline: PAIR at 0.31 on GPT-4). Scaling the skill space from 2 to 10 skills and moving to 2-skill compositions raises the Bin-JR score from 0.20 to 0.50 and the JR score from 0.26 to 0.77 on GPT-3.5. The proposed defense-by-misleading reduces the attacker's Bin-JR score by 68–71% on Llama-2 and GPT-3.5, and by 40–52% on GPT-4 and Vicuna, substantially outperforming SmoothLLM (20.81% vs. 44.56% Bin-JR drop on GPT-5.4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PAIR (iterative prompt optimization), the proposed method achieves higher Bin-JR scores on Llama-2 (0.25 vs. 0.03), GPT-3.5 (0.45 vs. 0.23), and GPT-4 (0.52 vs. 0.31), but trails slightly on Vicuna (0.21 vs. 0.22); on JR score, the proposed method wins on GPT-3.5 (0.73 vs. 0.50) and GPT-4 (0.79 vs. 0.57) but loses on Vicuna (0.23 vs. 0.41), which the authors attribute to PAIR's iterative refinement generating more complete responses once the defense is bypassed. Against GCG (gradient-based suffix optimization), the proposed method dominates on every target for both metrics (e.g., 0.52 vs. 0.05 Bin-JR on GPT-4). Against JailbreakChat (template-based), the proposed method is decisively superior, as JBC scores are near zero on closed-source models due to frequent refusals. Against PRS (random search), the proposed method wins on Bin-JR across all four targets (e.g., 0.45 vs. 0.15 on GPT-3.5) but PRS achieves higher JR on Llama-2 (0.50 vs. 0.29) and Vicuna (0.31 vs. 0.23). In additional comparisons on GPT-5.4, the proposed method outperforms TAP (tree-of-attacks) on both JBB (0.42 vs. 0.34 Bin-JR) and StrongREJECT (0.41 vs. 0.38), and edges out ICRT (heuristic/bias elicitation) on JBB (0.42 vs. 0.39 Bin-JR, 0.57 vs. 0.56 JR).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the statistical analysis of jailbreaking by Su et al. (2024) into a full adversarial game-theoretic framework, introducing a compositional-skills model where the attacker hides intent through combinations of skills (metaphor, role-play, low-resource language, etc.) against a budget-constrained defender. It unifies existing adversarial prompting methods—fixed-skill attacks (PAIR, GCG, JailbreakChat) and optimization-based attacks (PRS, TAP)—as restricted or approximate instantiations of a theoretically constructed best-response attacker, proving they are upper-bounded by it. The defense mechanism draws on mechanism design principles from game theory, a departure from prior LLM safety work that typically focuses on scaling filters or alignment rather than strategically misdirecting the attacker.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The equilibrium analysis reveals a structural vulnerability: the defender's performance degrades inversely with the combinatorial size of the skill composition space, meaning simply scaling defensive capacity is insufficient as attackers mix more skills. The provably optimal 'defense by misleading'—where the defender exposes fake weak points to concentrate its real defense—is a non-trivial mechanism design result with practical implications for safety-critical deployments. Limitations include the simplified unit-utility payoff (acknowledged in Appendix B/C), the idealized assumption that the defender can perfectly control the attacker's observed performance distribution, and the relatively small skill set (10 skills) used in experiments. Open questions include how the framework extends to multi-turn attacks, adaptive defenders that learn from attack patterns, and whether the misleading defense composes well with existing alignment mechanisms like RLHF.
