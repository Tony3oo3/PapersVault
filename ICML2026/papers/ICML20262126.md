## Problem

Existing early-exit methods for LLM inference acceleration each suffer from a critical limitation: confidence-based approaches incur heavy per-layer LM head overhead that can negate speedup gains, schedule-based methods converge prematurely to shallow layers and cap generation length, and predictor-based methods require costly task-specific training and retraining under domain shifts. No prior method simultaneously avoids all three issues while remaining training-free.

## Input / Output

WAVE takes a frozen pre-trained LLM (e.g., LLaMA-2 7B with 32 layers) and a text prompt, and produces autoregressively generated tokens. Before inference, a brief offline calibration phase runs forward passes over a few thousand tokens (e.g., GSM8K samples) to determine the exit window boundaries and extract the top-K vocabulary indices for the proxy head; no gradient updates or parameter modifications occur. The method is compatible with W4A16 quantized weights (BitsAndBytes, AWQ) and integrates into standard serving stacks like vLLM without architectural changes.

## Method

WAVE introduces two coupled mechanisms. First, exit window scheduling: during calibration, the earliest layer at which the softmax margin Δ_l = p_l^(1) − p_l^(2) exceeds a threshold τ_m is recorded per token; the mean of these hit layers defines the window start l_s, and exit decisions are restricted to the interval [l_s, l_s + W], capping exit checks at W per token and preventing the shallow-layer collapse of monotonically decaying schedules. Second, a vocabulary-efficient proxy LM head: at layer l_s the full LM head (V×d) is invoked once to select the top-K token indices, and for all subsequent layers in the window only the K corresponding rows (K×d) are used for margin computation, reducing per-layer exit overhead by 1−K/V (≈87% for K=4096, V≈32K). If the margin condition is never met within the window, the model falls through to the final layer and uses the full LM head, guaranteeing no accuracy loss for uncertain tokens. The entire pipeline requires no draft model, no trained exit predictor, and no gradient-based training.

## Results

On LLaMA-2 7B (FP16), WAVE achieves an average 1.4× end-to-end speedup (1.32× on MMLU, 1.31× on CSQA, 1.57× on SST2) while reducing average layer usage from 32 to 20–24 layers depending on the task. Accuracy degradation is minimal: +0.12%p on MMLU, −0.09%p on CSQA, −0.45%p on SST2, and −0.64%p on BoolQ, with notable gains of +1.72%p on MRPC and +1.44%p on RTE. Under W4A16 quantization (BitsAndBytes and AWQ), WAVE maintains comparable speedups (1.27×–1.56×) with accuracy drops under 1%p. Across Mistral-7B, Qwen2-7B, and LLaMA-2-13B, WAVE reduces average layer usage by 11–28% while preserving accuracy within ~1%p of the dense baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WAVE is compared against two representative early-exit baselines: AdaInfer (Fan et al., 2024), a confidence-based method that invokes the full LM head at every layer, and SpecEE (Xu et al., 2025), a predictor-based method that uses a trained EAGLE draft model to reduce vocabulary search. Against AdaInfer, WAVE achieves substantially higher speedups (1.32× vs. 1.02× on MMLU; 1.57× vs. 1.04× on SST2) because AdaInfer's per-layer full LM head calls consume 85% of its 9.9 s overhead, whereas WAVE's proxy head limits overhead to 0.59 s (2.8% of total time). Against SpecEE, WAVE matches or exceeds speedup on all three primary tasks (1.32× vs. 1.30× on MMLU, 1.57× vs. 1.28× on SST2) while avoiding the draft model's memory and training costs. On accuracy, WAVE outperforms SpecEE on MMLU (+0.12 vs. −0.66), MRPC (+1.72 vs. −0.10), and RTE (+1.44 vs. −0.12), and is comparable on CSQA (−0.09 vs. −0.17) and SST2 (−0.45 vs. −0.35). WAVE also surpasses SpecEE in average layer efficiency on SST2 (20.34 vs. 23.55) and BoolQ (19.96 vs. 20.98), though it uses slightly more layers on MMLU (23.65 vs. 23.16). Overall, WAVE delivers the best accuracy–speedup trade-off among the three methods without requiring any auxiliary model or gradient-based training.

## Relation to Prior Work

WAVE sits at the intersection of three existing early-exit (EE) paradigms—confidence-based (CALM, FREE), schedule-based (SkipDecode, D³), and predictor-based (AdaInfer, SpecEE)—while explicitly departing from each of their core limitations. Rather than building on a single prior method, it repurposes the scheduling idea from schedule-based EE (restricting where exits can occur) and the vocabulary-reduction idea from SpecEE (limiting the LM head search space), but recombines them in a training-free calibration pipeline that avoids both per-layer full-head overhead and the monotonically-decaying shallow-layer convergence. It also positions itself against speculative decoding (EAGLE, Medusa, DistillSpec) as a lighter-weight alternative that requires no draft model, no auxiliary heads, and no additional KV cache.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WAVE offers a practical, drop-in acceleration strategy (≈1.4× speedup, <1%p accuracy loss) that is immediately deployable without retraining and compatible with W4A16 quantization, making it attractive for production serving pipelines. However, the authors acknowledge a structural limitation: in decoder-only architectures, early exit prevents certain layers from updating their KV cache entries, causing accumulated representation loss over long-form generation (e.g., GSM8K accuracy drops ~1.8%p). This KV-cache-consistency issue remains an open problem that would require exit policies explicitly accounting for cache state, and it constrains WAVE's applicability to long-context or multi-step reasoning tasks. The modest throughput gain under batched serving (1.13× via vLLM) also suggests the method's benefits are most pronounced in low-batch or latency-sensitive settings rather than high-throughput datacenter deployments.
