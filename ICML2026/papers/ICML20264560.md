## Problem

Standard behavioral cloning (BC) pretraining can fail to ensure coverage over the demonstrator's actions in low-data-density regions, which is a necessary condition for effective RL finetuning. While much attention has been given to developing better finetuning algorithms, little work has addressed how the pretrained policy itself affects finetuning performance, and how to pretrain policies that are effective initializations for downstream RL.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264560/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes unlabeled demonstration trajectories (state-action sequences) collected by a demonstrator policy, with no reward labels required during pretraining. It produces a single pretrained diffusion policy parameterized over continuous action spaces, operating on both state-vector and RGB image observations. The output policy is a generative model that approximates a mixture of the BC policy and the posterior demonstrator policy, with the mixture weight α = 1/max{A, H, log(HT)}.

## Method

POSTBC replaces the standard BC objective (fitting the empirical action distribution) with fitting the posterior distribution of the demonstrator's behavior given the demonstration dataset, under a uniform prior over Markovian policies. In practice, Algorithm 1 first trains an ensemble of K predictors on perturbed demonstrations to estimate the state-dependent posterior covariance, then trains a diffusion model on demonstrations whose action targets are perturbed by noise drawn from this estimated covariance. This yields a policy that widens its action distribution in states with low demonstration density (enabling diverse exploration for RL finetuning) while reducing to approximately the BC policy in high-density regions. Theoretically, POSTBC achieves demonstrator action coverage with γ ≈ 1/(A+H) while maintaining the same H²S log T / T suboptimality bound as standard BC, and this coverage guarantee is shown to be near-unimprovable among all estimators matching BC's suboptimality rate.

## Results

On Robomimic with Best-of-N sampling (2000 rollouts), POSTBC achieves 81.3% success on Lift, 84.5% on Can, and 56.8% on Square, outperforming standard BC (68.1%, 76.9%, 54.4%) and VALUEDICE (47.6%, 41.8%, 8.3%). On Libero (all 16 tasks, 2000 rollouts), POSTBC reaches 63.4% success versus 36.3% for BC and 47.6% for σ-BC. For DSRL finetuning on Robomimic, POSTBC requires roughly 2× fewer samples to reach 75% success on Lift and Can compared to BC. Pretrained performance is preserved: POSTBC matches or slightly exceeds BC (e.g., 44.7% vs 43.1% on Can, 18.1% vs 17.9% on Square).

## Baselines & Comparisons

POSTBC consistently outperforms standard BC pretraining across all evaluated settings. On Best-of-N sampling, the gap is most dramatic on Libero (63.4% vs 36.3% on all 16 tasks with 2000 rollouts) and moderate on Robomimic (81.3% vs 68.1% on Lift). For DSRL and DPPO finetuning, POSTBC requires approximately 2× fewer online samples to reach 75% success on Robomimic Lift, Can, and Square. Against VALUEDICE (DICE), POSTBC achieves substantially higher Best-of-N performance on all Robomimic tasks (e.g., 84.5% vs 41.8% on Can), while DICE is not evaluated on Libero. Against σ-BC (uniform state-independent noise), POSTBC wins on most tasks, with the largest margins on Libero (63.4% vs 47.6% overall), suggesting that state-dependent posterior variance is more effective than fixed exploration noise. Critically, POSTBC preserves pretrained performance at parity with BC (within 1–2% on all tasks), confirming it does not sacrifice 0-shot capability for improved finetuning potential.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the standard BC-then-RL-finetuning pipeline that underpins modern LLM and robotic policy training, and on diffusion models as the generative policy parameterization (Chi et al., 2023). Conceptually, it departs from prior work by shifting focus from improving the RL finetuning algorithm to improving the pretraining objective itself: rather than fitting the empirical demonstration distribution (the MAP estimate), POSTBC fits the posterior distribution over the demonstrator's behavior, borrowing the Bayesian posterior-sampling framework long used in RL exploration (Thompson, 1933; Osband et al., 2018) and applying it to BC for the first time. The theoretical analysis extends the imitation learning lower bounds of Rajaraman et al. (2020) to show that the BC-optimal estimator provably fails to cover the demonstrator's action support, while the posterior mixture achieves near-optimal coverage at no suboptimality cost. The work is complementary to concurrent language-domain results (Chen et al., 2025a) on coverage as a predictor of finetuning success, but extends the analysis to continuous control and introduces the performance-coverage tradeoff as a first-class design constraint.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a minimal modification to existing BC training pipelines—training an ensemble on perturbed data and adding state-dependent noise to action targets—can roughly halve the RL finetuning sample cost across multiple robotic benchmarks and finetuning algorithms, without degrading pretrained performance. This is significant because RL finetuning of robotic policies is expensive and time-consuming, and the approach requires no RL during pretraining. Open questions include whether demonstrator action coverage is sufficient (not just necessary) for efficient RL finetuning, whether the approach transfers to language-model pretraining/SFT, and whether offline RL pretraining could yield even better initializations. The paper's focus on supervised-only pretraining is both a strength (scalability) and a limitation (it leaves open whether richer pretraining objectives could further improve the initialization).
