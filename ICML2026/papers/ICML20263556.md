## Problem

Existing representation-based data attribution methods for detecting unsafe training data in LLMs rely on token-averaged representations that are 'noisy': unsafe texts contain only a few critical tokens among many benign ones (stop words, neutral facts), which dilutes the detection signal. Moderation classifiers, the dominant alternative, are limited to predefined taxonomies and do not capture how individual training samples actually influence model behavior, making them less effective for safety-oriented retraining.

## Input / Output

The method consumes per-sample representations (last-layer hidden states or LoRA gradients) for all training samples and a small set of target unsafe model outputs (typically 3–50 examples collected via red-teaming or user reports), both in R^m where m is the model's hidden dimension. It produces a scalar detection score per training sample, from which the top-K highest-scoring samples are removed to yield a filtered training set. In practice, the high-dimensional representation is first reduced to m'=100 PCA components, and only r=30 dimensions are retained for scoring, keeping the per-sample computation lightweight relative to the gradient collection itself.

## Method

DRA denoises representations in two principled steps grounded in signal detection theory. First, it centers and whitens both training and target representations by the background mean μ_X and covariance Σ_X, yielding a Mahalanobis-correlation score that suppresses high-variance dimensions (topics, style) irrelevant to unsafeness. Second, it selects the r most informative dimensions by maximizing a leave-target-out discriminability index d'_LTO, which treats the small target set as a surrogate for the unknown unsafe training data and picks eigenvectors that best separate them from the training distribution. The theoretical analysis shows that the classical discriminability index d' is a monotonic proxy for AUPRC and admits a clean decomposition over representation dimensions, justifying the two-step denoising-and-selection pipeline. Applied on top of existing attribution scores (GradSim, RepSim), DRA increases average AUPRC by up to 63.3% and reduces post-filtering attack success rate by up to 39.9% across jailbreak and gender-bias scenarios.

## Results

On the jailbreak injection detection task, DRA achieves AUPRC of 98.2% (Repsim-DRA) and 82.3% (GradSim-DRA) on Gemma-2-9B-it, and 92.6% (Repsim-DRA) and 83.2% (GradSim-DRA) on Llama-3-8B-Instruct, improving average AUPRC by up to 63.3% over the corresponding data-attribution baselines. After filtering and retraining, DRA reduces Attack Success Rate (ASR) by up to 39.9% (GradSim-DRA on Gemma-2-9B-it, XSTest-Response: 77.0% → 3.0%). In the gender bias setting, Repsim-DRA achieves AUPRC of 0.748 on Llama-3-8B-Instruct (vs. 0.333 for RepSim) and reduces TPR Gender Gap from 0.848 to 0.092 after retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DRA consistently outperforms all six baselines on the primary detection metric (AUPRC). Against the strongest moderation classifier (Wildguard: 56.0% ToxicChat / 93.0% XSTest on Llama-3), Repsim-DRA achieves 92.6% / 98.6%, and GradSim-DRA achieves 83.2% / 81.5%, surpassing Wildguard on ToxicChat by 27–37 points. Against the data-attribution baselines, the gains are most dramatic: GradSim on Llama-3 scores only 1.7% AUPRC on XSTest-Response, while GradSim-DRA reaches 81.5% (+63.3 average improvement). On Gemma-2-9B-it, Repsim-DRA (98.2% / 99.8%) exceeds the best classifier-free baseline (Repsim: 51.6% / 83.9%) by 46.6 / 15.9 points. In the gender bias setting where moderation classifiers fail (Wildguard AUPRC ≈ 0.059), DRA methods achieve 0.369–0.748 AUPRC, confirming their advantage on subtle, non-taxonomic unsafe patterns. For downstream safety (ASR after retraining), GradSim-DRA on Gemma-2-9B-it reduces XSTest ASR from 77.0% to 3.0% (−39.9 points), the largest single improvement reported.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on representation-based data attribution methods—specifically GradSim (Xia et al., 2024) and RepSim (Pezeshkpour et al., 2021)—which score training samples by inner-product similarity to aggregated target representations. The key departure is a principled denoising step grounded in signal detection theory: rather than using raw inner products, DRA centers and whitens representations (a Mahalanobis-like transformation) and then selects the most informative dimensions via a leave-target-out discriminability index. This positions the work as a theoretical refinement of existing attribution scores rather than a new attribution paradigm, while also distinguishing itself from moderation classifiers (Llama-Guard, Wildguard, OpenAI API) by being model-aware and taxonomy-agnostic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, label-free method for detecting unsafe training data that generalizes beyond predefined toxicity taxonomies, which is important as LLM fine-tuning services become more accessible and vulnerable to data poisoning. The signal-detection-theory framing offers a clean theoretical lens for understanding why naive representation similarity fails and how to fix it, potentially transferable to other attribution-based detection tasks. However, the evaluation is limited to two model architectures (Llama-3-8B, Gemma-2-9B) and two scenarios (jailbreak injection, gender bias), and the authors acknowledge vulnerability to adversarial manipulation of attribution scores. Open questions include scaling to much larger training corpora, fine-grained genre-specific detection, and robustness against attackers who are aware of the attribution-based filtering.
