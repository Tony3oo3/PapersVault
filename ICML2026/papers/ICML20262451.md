## Problem

Existing self-supervised medical time-series methods (e.g., MAE-style masked autoencoders) produce variable-length, entangled embeddings where distinct physiological factors—such as cardiac rhythm versus morphology—are diffused across all latent dimensions. These representations are sufficient for reconstruction but opaque in structure, making them unsuitable for clinical interpretation and inefficient for downstream tasks driven by sparse biomarkers. The paper asks whether a fixed-size, redundancy-constrained bottleneck can simultaneously preserve diagnostic information and enforce token-level disentanglement.

## Input / Output

The method takes variable-length multi-channel medical time series (ECG, EEG, or IMU signals) and tokenizes them into patches via a linear embedding. It compresses these variable-length patch sequences into a fixed set of k=8 fingerprint tokens (each d-dimensional) through a cross-attention bottleneck, regardless of input length. Downstream, a task-specific attention pooling mechanism over the k tokens produces a class prediction, and the per-token attention weights serve as interpretable, token-level saliency maps that highlight which latent factors drove the decision.

## Method

TS-Fingerprint uses a cross-attention encoder where a learnable query set Q ∈ R^{k×d} (k ≪ T) attends over patch embeddings to produce k fingerprint tokens, enforcing a strict data-processing inequality X → F′ → X̂ by conditioning the masked decoder solely on these tokens (no visible-patch leakage). Training combines two objectives: a masked reconstruction loss (MSE under Gaussian decoder noise) that maximizes a variational lower bound on I(X; F′), and a Total Coding Rate log-determinant regularizer that maximizes the volume of the parallelotope spanned by the tokens, geometrically pushing them toward pairwise orthogonality. Theoretically, this is framed as a Disentangled Rate-Distortion problem: the reconstruction term enforces sufficiency while the TCR term minimizes Total Correlation, and the paper proves that orthogonality reduces approximation error to the spectral tail, improves sample complexity from Ω(k) to O(s log k) for sparse biomarker detection, and maximizes worst-case robustness to isotropic noise. Downstream, a lightweight attention-pooling readout with a task query enables sparse selection of clinically relevant tokens, consistent with the theoretical sparse-recovery guarantee.

## Results

TS-Fingerprint achieves the top-1 average rank (1.24) across all five metrics (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1, AUROC) on seven medical benchmarks, surpassing 11 baselines. Headline numbers include 53.91% accuracy and 72.11% AUROC on ADFTD (EEG, 3-class), 85.39% accuracy and 92.99% AUROC on PTB (ECG, 2-class), and 73.98% accuracy and 90.41% AUROC on PTB-XL (ECG, 5-class). On the activity-recognition benchmarks, it reaches 77.59% accuracy on FLAAP (10-class) and 90.87% accuracy on UCI-HAR (6-class). All improvements over the second-best baseline (Medformer) are statistically significant (p < 0.05, Wilcoxon signed-rank test).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TS-Fingerprint outperforms all ten general-purpose time-series backbones (Autoformer, Crossformer, FEDformer, Informer, iTransformer, MTST, Nonformer, PatchTST, Reformer, Transformer) across all seven datasets, with the largest margins on EEG tasks (e.g., +10.8 F1 over PatchTST on ADFTD). The closest competitor is the medical-specific Medformer, which TS-Fingerprint surpasses on every dataset (e.g., +1.14 F1 on PTB-XL, +2.19 F1 on ADFTD) without requiring multi-granularity patching. Among self-supervised baselines, Ti-MAE shows virtually no pre-training benefit (+0.3% F1 on ADFTD) while SimMTM gains only +4.4% F1, compared to TS-Fingerprint's +13.1% F1 gain, indicating that the redundancy-constrained objective produces a far more transferable latent space. On foundation-style models (GPT4TS, TimesFM) and medical SSL methods (CLOCS, MTS-LOF) evaluated on ADFTD, TS-Fingerprint achieves the best F1 (51.79) and AUROC (72.11), though GPT4TS edges it slightly on accuracy (54.11 vs. 53.91). The one dataset where TS-Fingerprint does not achieve the absolute best F1 is UCI-HAR, where Medformer (91.61) narrowly edges it (90.92), though TS-Fingerprint still leads on AUROC (98.67 vs. 98.99 is actually Medformer's advantage here as well).

## Relation to Prior Work

TS-Fingerprint sits at the intersection of masked autoencoder pre-training for time series (Ti-MAE, SimMTM) and coding-rate-based diversity regularization (MCR²/TCR from Yu et al., 2020). It departs from standard MAE by replacing the variable-length patch-token output with a fixed-size set of k 'fingerprint tokens' generated via a cross-attention bottleneck, and from prior disentangled representation work (Barlow Twins, VICReg) by applying the log-determinant diversity objective across a small set of latent tokens rather than across dimensions of a single global embedding. The paper further distinguishes itself from medical-specific backbones like Medformer by arguing that a redundancy-constrained information bottleneck, rather than multi-granularity architectural engineering, is the key to isolating physiological factors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The fixed-size, token-level representation offers a practical and interpretable interface for clinical deployment, where a single pooled vector is opaque and variable-length outputs are inconvenient. The theoretical results (sample complexity scaling as O(s log k) vs. Ω(k), and the robustness bound tied to token orthogonality) provide a principled justification for why disentanglement should improve low-data generalization, though the proofs rely on Gaussian and linear-decoder assumptions that may not fully hold in practice. A key limitation acknowledged by the authors is the use of a standard linear patch embedding, which may limit sensitivity to fine-grained high-frequency pathology; integrating hierarchical or multi-scale tokenizers with the fingerprint bottleneck is left as future work. Whether the disentanglement benefits persist on larger, more heterogeneous clinical cohorts beyond the seven benchmarks tested remains an open question.
