## Problem

Structural nested mean models (SNMMs) provide a principled way to decompose treatment effects over time into interpretable, localized blip effects, but no scalable neural implementation exists. The inherent sequential g-estimation scheme of SNMMs prevents end-to-end gradient-based training, and the closest prior work (Dynamic DML) relies on linear models, requires a separate model per time step, and cannot process growing patient histories.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263136/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a patient's growing history H_t = (X̄_t, Ā_{t-1}, Ȳ_{t-1})—a variable-length sequence of covariates, past treatments, and past outcomes—along with two candidate treatment sequences a*_{t:t+τ} and b*_{t:t+τ}. It outputs the CATE over time as a sum of learned blip coefficients ψ_{t,k}(h_t) dotted with the treatment differences, requiring only a single forward pass through the blip prediction network at inference. Because blip coefficients are treatment-agnostic, new treatment sequences can be evaluated instantly without re-computation or re-training.

## Method

DeepBlip uses a two-stage architecture with sequential neural backbones (LSTM or transformer): Stage 1 (nuisance network) estimates conditional expectations of outcomes and treatments via W-fold cross-fitting to compute residuals; Stage 2 (blip prediction network) learns history-conditioned blip coefficients ψ_{t,k}(h_t) by minimizing an L²-moment loss. The key technical contribution is a double optimization trick that breaks the sequential dependency of g-estimation: during each iteration, the blip predictor performs two forward passes, and the second set of predictions is detached from the computation graph to serve as fixed pseudo-ground-truth targets for future blip terms in earlier losses, enabling simultaneous gradient-based training of all τ+1 blip predictors. The resulting loss is Neyman-orthogonal, making blip estimation robust to first-order nuisance misspecification, and the MSE of the blip coefficients converges at a near-parametric rate O((log log n / n)²).

## Results

On the MIMIC-III semi-synthetic dataset, DeepBlip achieves the lowest normalized RMSE across all prediction horizons τ=3–10, with improvements over the best baseline (IGC-Net) ranging from 3.8% at τ=3 to 34.6% at τ=10 (0.98 vs. 1.50). On the tumor growth dataset (τ=2), DeepBlip outperforms all baselines under moderate-to-strong time-varying confounding (γ_conf ≥ 2), with the largest gains at γ_conf > 6. In terms of scalability, DeepBlip's second-stage training time remains nearly constant as τ increases, whereas Dynamic DML's training time grows linearly with τ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DeepBlip outperforms all seven baselines on both the tumor growth and MIMIC-III semi-synthetic datasets. The closest competitor is IGC-Net (G-computation with transformer), which DeepBlip beats by 3.8% at τ=3 and by 34.6% at τ=10, demonstrating that the advantage grows with prediction horizon. Dynamic DML, the prior SNMM-based method, is consistently outperformed (e.g., 0.98 vs. 2.46 at τ=10) and also suffers from linearly growing training time, whereas DeepBlip's double optimization trick keeps training time nearly constant. MSM-based R-MSNs exhibits the highest variance across all horizons due to unstable inverse propensity weights. G-computation methods (G-Net, IGC-Net) show proper confounding adjustment but accumulate error over time from nested expectations. Balanced-representation methods (CRN, CT) are biased and underperform all properly-adjusted methods under strong confounding. The naive HA-TRM baseline degrades most severely at longer horizons (RMSE 3.11 at τ=10), confirming the necessity of explicit confounding adjustment.

## Relation to Prior Work

DeepBlip is the first neural implementation of structural nested mean models (SNMMs), building directly on the g-estimation framework of Lewis & Syrgkanis (2021, Dynamic DML) and the theoretical foundations of Robins (1994, 2004). It departs from Dynamic DML by replacing the sequential, step-by-step g-estimation with a novel double optimization trick (gradient detachment of future blip predictions) that enables end-to-end, gradient-based training of a single unified network. Conceptually, it also positions itself against other CATE-over-time methods: unlike G-computation approaches (G-Net, IGC-Net) that model full counterfactual trajectories, and unlike MSM-based methods (R-MSNs) that rely on unstable inverse propensity weights, SNMMs decompose the total effect into localized blip increments, which the paper argues provides superior stability over long horizons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for personalized medicine: once trained, DeepBlip identifies optimal treatment sequences in a single forward pass (no re-training or exhaustive search), making offline policy evaluation computationally tractable for clinicians. The Neyman-orthogonal loss provides double robustness against nuisance misspecification, and the blip decomposition offers interpretable incremental treatment effects. Open questions include how the linear blip parametrization (ψ(h_t)′a_{t+k}) handles highly non-linear treatment-outcome interactions, whether the identifiability assumptions (sequential ignorability, overlap) hold in real-world EHR data with unmeasured confounders, and how performance degrades under severe positivity violations. The method also remains limited to discrete-time settings and does not address continuous-time interventions.
