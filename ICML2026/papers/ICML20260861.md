## Problem

Prior work on observational studies can detect the presence of bias (via sensitivity analyses or RCT benchmarking) or mitigate it through debiasing, but no methodology exists for uncovering the underlying causal mechanism driving the bias—e.g., whether it stems from hidden confounding, selection bias, or lack of transportability. Without knowing *why* a study is biased, practitioners cannot make targeted design corrections.

## Input / Output

The method requires patient-level data from both an RCT (to estimate the unbiased CATE function g_a(X)) and an OS (to fit nuisance estimators for selection, treatment assignment, and outcome). The output is a triplet of Pearson correlation coefficients—ρ(b₁,S), ρ(b₁,A), ρ(b₁,Y)—whose zero/nonzero pattern acts as a diagnostic 'hash table' to classify the dominant bias mechanism among four types. The method does not produce a corrected effect estimate; it produces a qualitative mechanism diagnosis.

## Method

The core insight is that the magnitude of bias |b₁(X)| varies across patients in a way that aligns with the conditional variance of specific downstream variables (S, A, Y) depending on which causal graph is at play. The authors derive closed-form bias expressions for each of four bias mechanisms (transportability, confounding, selection type 1, selection type 2) under a clinically motivated generative model where an unmeasured covariate U has context-dependent effects on downstream variables. They then prove that the covariance between |b₁(X)| and Var(T|X) for T ∈ {S, A, Y} produces a unique zero/nonzero signature for each bias type (Theorem 4.4), enabling provable discrimination. For practical implementation, they replace the intractable conditional variance with instance-wise squared prediction errors of nuisance function estimators, yielding a consistent plug-in estimator (Theorem 4.8) that avoids needing to know the true conditional distributions.

## Results

In synthetic experiments (200 runs per bias type, n_os=50,000, n_rct=50,000, d=6), the covariance signals recover the theoretical patterns in Table 2 with high reliability: for confounding bias, ρ(b₁,A)>0 and ρ(b₁,Y)>0 are detected in the predicted region at p<0.01 in the majority of runs, while ρ(b₁,S) remains near zero. In the WHI real-world analysis, the baseline (uncorrected) OS exhibits a type-2 selection signature (ρ(b₁,S)>0, ρ(b₁,A)<0, ρ(b₁,Y)>0), consistent with immortal-time bias. After correcting for immortal time, ρ(b₁,S) and ρ(b₁,A) deflate toward zero while ρ(b₁,Y) does not decrease, suggesting residual transportability bias. A positive-control experiment (manually concealing age and menopausal status) produces the expected confounding signature with significant boosts in ρ(b₁,A)>0 and ρ(b₁,Y)>0.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing bias-detection methods in a head-to-head quantitative comparison; instead, it positions itself against two existing paradigms. The benchmarking literature (Hartman et al., 2015; Kallus et al., 2018; Hussain et al., 2022) can detect that an OS is biased relative to an RCT but cannot identify the underlying mechanism. The causal structure learning literature (Shimizu et al., 2006; Glymour et al., 2019) could in principle recover the exact DAG but requires strong assumptions (e.g., linear non-Gaussianity, faithfulness) that are unrealistic in clinical settings. The proposed covariance-signal approach occupies a middle ground: it groups graphs into families and distinguishes among four bias mechanisms without requiring full graph recovery. In the WHI case study, the method's diagnostic is validated by comparing three analysis settings: the uncorrected baseline (showing type-2 selection), the immortal-time-corrected analysis (where selection signals deflate but outcome signal persists, suggesting mixed transportability + selection bias), and a manual-bias positive control (where concealing known confounders reproduces the confounding signature). A combined synthetic experiment (type-2 selection + transportability) with WHI-matched selection probabilities (p₀₀ˢ=0.9, p₀₁ˢ=0.9, p₁₀ˢ=0.3, p₁₁ˢ=0.1) qualitatively reproduces the real-world signal pattern, including the non-decrease in ρ(b₁,Y) after selection correction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground between two existing lines of work: benchmarking observational studies against RCTs (which detects bias but not its source) and causal structure learning (which recovers full DAGs but requires unrealistic assumptions). Rather than recovering an exact causal graph, the authors propose distinguishing between families of causal graphs corresponding to different bias mechanisms (confounding, selection, transportability, collider), using the covariance between bias magnitude and the predictive performance of nuisance function estimators as a diagnostic signal. The approach repurposes standard nuisance estimation (propensity scores, outcome models) from a purely inferential role to a causal-diagnostic one, a connection not previously exploited in the benchmarking or structure-learning literatures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives practitioners an actionable diagnostic: by examining how bias varies with prediction uncertainty across patients, one can identify whether to adjust for confounders, revise selection criteria, or account for transportability gaps. The WHI case study validates the approach on a historically important discrepancy (protective vs. harmful HRT effects), though it largely confirms the already-known immortal-time bias explanation. Key limitations include the hard requirement for patient-level RCT data, reliance on a specific low-uncertainty generative model, and the absence of semiparametric efficiency guarantees. Open questions include removing the RCT data dependency, developing cross-fitted or orthogonal estimators, and extending the framework to measurement bias.
