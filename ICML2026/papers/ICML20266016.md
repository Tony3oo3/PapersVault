## Problem

Evaluating automatically generated literature reviews remains difficult because the criteria that determine research utility—coherent field organization and non-obvious gap identification—are non-verifiable and inherently intuition-based, yet existing benchmarks rely on static proxy signals like reference overlap and citation correctness. Additionally, off-the-shelf LLM-as-a-judge methods are substantially misaligned with expert preferences (Spearman's ρ ≈ 0.467), especially on synthesis-heavy dimensions, making them unreliable as development signals.

## Input / Output

The arena protocol takes a normalized topic query (derived from high-citation AI surveys via OpenAlex) and two anonymized, side-by-side literature review drafts with randomized left-right ordering; domain-matched experts return a four-way outcome per dimension (A, B, Tie, BothBad) across five criteria, with the unusual 'Both Bad' option explicitly separating quality failure from indifference. For LitJudge, the input additionally includes up to three structure-similar examples (retrieved via paragraph-relationship-network graph similarity), three content-similar examples (via LLM embedding matching), and three diversity-aware gap anchors (selected by MMR from expert-written human drafts), and the output is a single JSON object with keys D1–D5. Aggregation uses Bradley-Terry models with Elo-style ratings (init=1500, K=32, ties as 0.5 wins) to produce per-dimension system leaderboards.

## Method

LitReview Arena enforces a structured peer-review protocol: 105 domain experts with AI paper-writing experience are matched to topics via an AAAI-based field/subfield taxonomy, and each battle yields five independent dimension-wise votes (D1 Literature Coverage, D2 Claim Support, D3 Paper Structure, D4 Research Suggestions, D5 Overall Utility) with written reasoning. Arena logs are frozen into LitReviewBench (~3k judgments) for reproducible offline evaluation. The key technical contribution for scalable evaluation is LitJudge, which calibrates an LLM judge by constructing a task-specific in-context packet: structure-similar battles are retrieved by extracting skeleton text (headers and lead sentences) to build a paragraph-relationship network and matching via normalized graph similarity; content-similar battles are retrieved by LLM embedding proximity; and D4 gap anchors are drawn exclusively from expert-written human drafts with maximal marginal relevance selection to balance relevance against diversity. This calibration raises expert-ranking alignment from ρ ≈ 0.467 to ρ ≈ 0.792 on Overall Utility, with the largest gain on Research Suggestions (0.430 → 0.842), and the effect generalizes across GPT, Claude, Llama, and Qwen backbones while outperforming a random few-shot ICL control.

## Results

On LitReviewBench (~3k expert judgments), SOTA systems win only 23.0% of decisive matches against human drafts on Overall Utility (D5), with GPT-5.2 achieving the highest non-human Elo of 1449.1 versus 1668.8 for humans. Agentic models average 122.3K tokens/query (~15× the 8.1K of standalone LMs) yet still trail humans by ~200 Elo points on Paper Structure (D3) and Research Suggestions (D4). The calibrated evaluator LitJudge improves Spearman's ρ alignment with expert rankings from 0.467 to 0.792 on D5, approaching inter-expert consistency (0.861).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates two specialized literature-review systems—Open Deep Research (LangChain AI, 2025) and SurveyForge (Yan et al., 2025)—against the same expert-preference protocol. Open Deep Research achieves the strongest specialized-system scores (D5 = 1832.5, D3 = 1868.5), narrowing the gap to human drafts (D5 = 1912.2, D3 = 1880.4) to roughly 80 Elo points on Overall Utility and only ~12 points on Paper Structure. SurveyForge follows closely (D5 = 1805.7, D3 = 1842.7), trailing humans by ~107 points on D5. Both systems substantially outperform the average agentic baseline (D5 = 1511.5) and the average LM baseline (D5 = 1248.4) across all five dimensions, confirming that purpose-built review pipelines add meaningful value over general-purpose agents. However, neither system surpasses human drafts on any dimension, and the residual gaps remain largest on Research Suggestions (D4), where Open Deep Research scores 1811.0 versus 1871.7 for humans.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper specializes the arena-style evaluation paradigm established by Chatbot Arena (blind pairwise comparison with Elo aggregation) and extended to scientific tasks by SciArena, narrowing the scope to literature reviews with a structured five-dimension protocol (coverage, claim support, structure, research suggestions, overall utility). It departs from prior static benchmarks (SciReviewGen, SurGE, ReportBench, DeepSurveyBench) that rely on proxy signals like reference overlap and citation matching, arguing these miss the synthesis-heavy dimensions that determine research utility. The calibrated evaluator LitJudge extends retrieval-augmented in-context learning by combining structure-similar example retrieval (via paragraph relationship graph similarity) with diversity-aware gap anchors from expert drafts, going beyond generic few-shot prompting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides the first reproducible, expert-grounded measurement of where literature review agents actually fail: structure (D3) and gap identification (D4) trail humans by ~200 Elo points while coverage gaps are smaller, revealing that retrieval improvements alone will not close the utility gap. The finding that uncalibrated LLM judges exhibit a strong AI-to-AI bias (ranking human drafts last) while maintaining high inter-judge agreement (>0.7) suggests shared surface-fluency preferences rather than random noise, with implications for any LLM-as-a-judge pipeline in scientific domains. Open questions include cross-domain generalization beyond AI (a biology pilot shows the pattern holds but gaps widen), long-horizon living reviews, and the risk that expert-calibrated evaluators entrench mainstream academic styles.
