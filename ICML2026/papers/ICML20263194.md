## Problem

Meta-learning methods that share a single global prior across source tasks suffer negative transfer when the target task is out-of-distribution, because the shared inductive bias encodes spurious correlations rather than stable causal mechanisms. No prior work addresses how to align a previously unseen target task with source tasks based on causal mechanistic similarity, particularly when target-task data is unavailable and only noisy expert judgments about relative task similarity are accessible.

## Input / Output

The method consumes precomputed causal task embeddings for source tasks (constructed via Invariant Causal Prediction or Mendelian Randomization on longitudinal clinical data), a small support set from the target task, and B pairwise expert queries of the form 'is source task i more similar to the target than source task j?' The expert never sees task observations, only makes relative similarity judgments. The output is a binary disease prediction on held-out target data, an inferred target-task embedding in a low-dimensional causal space (via variational posterior mean), and a task-specific posterior over Bayesian neural network weights that guides adaptation.

## Method

The core modification to amortized hierarchical Bayesian meta-learning is replacing the shared task prior φ_t|θ ~ N(θ, σ²I) with an embedding-conditioned prior φ_t|z_t,θ ~ N(θ+Wz_t, σ²I), where W is a learnable weight matrix mapping causal embeddings into parameter space, so that tasks with similar causal mechanisms receive similar prior means. At deployment, the unknown target embedding z_{t'} is inferred through a Bayesian preference model: expert pairwise responses are modeled with a probit likelihood p(c=1|z_{t'},ξ,τ)=Φ(τΔ(ξ;z_{t'})), and queries are selected via BALD to maximize expected information gain about z_{t'}. The variational posterior over z_{t'} is updated with stochastic variational inference after each expert response, and its mean is used to construct the task-specific prior for adaptation. Theoretically, the paper decomposes prior mismatch into expert error, causal discovery error, and OOD shift, proving that negative transfer is mitigated relative to a global prior whenever the combined expert and causal errors are bounded by a constant times the OOD displacement.

## Results

On the UK Biobank cross-disease prediction benchmark (Table 1), the proposed method with ICP causal embeddings and a real medical expert (ICP, m.) achieves AUROC of 0.823 (J44), 0.620 (J45), 0.676 (G45), and 0.711 (I21), outperforming all baselines on every target task. In synthetic experiments (Figure 2), the causal prior induces positive transfer across all levels of task-level distribution shift (ε_OOD up to 4.0), while the global prior and correlation-based embeddings exhibit increasingly negative transfer as shift grows. The expert-inferred embeddings (Figure 3a) recover performance close to the causal oracle across all OOD levels, with AUROC improving monotonically as the expert query budget B increases (Figure 3b).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method (ICP, m.) outperforms all four meta-learning baselines on all four UK Biobank target tasks. Against MAML (Finn et al., 2017), the gains are 3.2 pp on J44 (0.823 vs 0.791), 0.8 pp on J45 (0.620 vs 0.612), 0.9 pp on G45 (0.676 vs 0.667), and 1.0 pp on I21 (0.711 vs 0.701). Against TSA-MAML (Zhou et al., 2021), the improvements are similar in magnitude (3.1 pp on J44, 1.1 pp on J45, 0.8 pp on G45, 1.0 pp on I21). DKT (Patacchiola et al., 2020) is the strongest baseline, reaching 0.809 on J44 and 0.695 on I21, but still trails the proposed method by 1.4 pp and 1.6 pp respectively. HBM (Ravi & Beatson, 2019) with a global prior is closest on G45 (0.670 vs 0.676) but lags by 2.6 pp on J44. In the synthetic experiments, DKT and TSA-MAML match or approach the proposed method's performance in low-dimensional settings with many source tasks, but the paper notes these advantages diminish in the more complex real-world UK Biobank setting with fewer source tasks and heterogeneous disease mechanisms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the amortized hierarchical Bayesian meta-learning framework of Ravi & Beatson (2019) by replacing the shared global prior with a task-specific prior conditioned on causal task embeddings, constructed via Invariant Causal Prediction (Peters et al., 2016) or Mendelian Randomization (Didelez & Sheehan, 2007). It departs from prior meta-learning methods that define task similarity through model parameters or observed features (Task2Vec, TSA-MAML) by grounding similarity in mechanistic causal structure, and from causal inference methods that require joint access to source and target data by instead using expert pairwise similarity judgments (modeled via a probit likelihood from Chu & Ghahramani, 2005) to infer the target embedding without target data. The active query selection via BALD (Houlsby et al., 2011) is repurposed from classification to the novel setting of locating an unseen task in a causal embedding space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, theoretically grounded solution to negative transfer in meta-learning under task-level distribution shift, with a clean error decomposition (expert error + causal discovery error + OOD error) that gives practitioners three independent levers to control generalization. The expert-in-the-loop mechanism is particularly relevant for clinical and rare-disease settings where target data is scarce but mechanistic knowledge exists. Key limitations include the requirement for a domain expert whose similarity judgments align with the causal embedding space, dependence on the quality of the upstream causal discovery method, and the linear parametrization that, while theoretically convenient, may underperform in more complex settings. Open questions include propagating uncertainty from expert inference and causal discovery into the meta-learning model, jointly learning embeddings during meta-training, and extending the framework to neural processes.
