## Problem

Trustworthy AI objectives—fairness, robustness, privacy, and explainability—impose competing invariance requirements that make them difficult to satisfy simultaneously while preserving accuracy, yet the literature has largely treated these trade-offs as empirical side effects rather than understanding their structural origin. This position paper argues that the conflicts arise from incompatible invariance demands under different interventions on the data-generating process, and that causality provides a unifying framework to diagnose and selectively resolve them. The role of causality in reconciling multiple trust objectives simultaneously, rather than addressing each in isolation, remains underexplored.

## Input / Output

The simulation operates on a synthetic 30-day clinical readmission dataset (N=6000) generated from a fully specified SCM with six variables: protected attribute (age group), legitimate lab measurement, unfair insurance proxy, hospital environment, spurious imaging artifact, and binary readmission outcome. The explicit causal model consumes only the causally justified features (X1, E) guided by the DAG, while the partial implicit causal method additionally uses the proxy Z and artifact X2 but applies counterfactual augmentation and multi-environment training. Outputs are binary readmission predictions evaluated on five normalized trust dimensions: accuracy (AUC-ROC), fairness (prediction sensitivity to Z), robustness (OOD Brier score on a held-out hospital), privacy (adversary AUC on attribute inference), and explainability (variance attributable to causal features).

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a theoretical reframing: each trustworthy AI objective is recast as an invariance requirement under a specific class of interventions, and trade-offs emerge when these invariance sets are incompatible. To validate this, the authors construct a synthetic SCM for clinical readmission and compare five methods along a causal-knowledge gradient: no intervention, uniform Laplace noise (privacy), demographic-parity penalty with A dropped (fairness), GroupDRO over six environments with counterfactual resampling of the proxy Z (partial implicit causal), and graph-based feature selection retaining only X1 and E (explicit causal). The explicit causal model achieves the best scores on four of five dimensions by directly removing inadmissible and non-causal pathways, while the partial implicit causal method surpasses it on OOD robustness through environment diversity. The paper further distinguishes explicit causal integration (SCM/DAG-based constraints) from implicit causal integration (inductive biases, multi-environment training, counterfactual augmentation) and maps both onto the foundation model lifecycle spanning pre-training, post-training alignment, and post-hoc auditing.

## Results

This is a position paper whose empirical support is a stylized synthetic simulation (30-day clinical readmission SCM) comparing five methods across five trust dimensions (accuracy, fairness, robustness, privacy, explainability). The explicit Causal model (graph-based feature selection dropping A, Z, X₂) is best on four of five dimensions—accuracy (AUC-ROC), fairness (low prediction sensitivity to proxy Z), privacy (resistance to attribute-inference attack on Z), and explainability (variance from causally justified features, trivially 1.0 by construction). The Partial Implicit Causal method (multi-environment GroupDRO + counterfactual augmentation on Z) is best on out-of-distribution robustness (Brier score on held-out hospital E=2), surpassing even the explicit causal model on that axis. All three non-causal baselines (No Intervention, Privacy Intervention, Fairness Intervention) improve at most one or two dimensions while degrading the others, and none is Pareto-optimal on any two-dimensional trade-off pair.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five methods along a causal-knowledge gradient. The No Intervention baseline (logistic regression on all features including A) is competitive on accuracy but worst on fairness, robustness, and privacy. The Privacy Intervention (Laplace noise on all features at ε=1) pays a substantial accuracy cost and fails to fully suppress Z leakage because the adversary recovers Z via its correlation with public feature X₁, though it improves fairness as a by-product. The Fairness Intervention (drop A + demographic-parity Lagrangian penalty λ=1.0) drives the Z coefficient near zero for strong fairness but cannot distinguish the inadmissible path A→Z→Y from the admissible A→X₁→Y, so it attenuates both, causing a notable accuracy drop and an explainability collapse as predictive variance migrates to the spurious artifact X₂. The Partial Implicit Causal method (GroupDRO over six environments + counterfactual resampling of Z) is the only non-graph method to achieve strong scores on multiple dimensions simultaneously and uniquely wins on OOD robustness through multi-environment training. The explicit Causal model (drop A, Z, X₂; keep X₁ and E) dominates on four of five axes and is the sole Pareto-optimal point on the accuracy–fairness and fairness–privacy projections, while sharing the accuracy–OOD-robustness Pareto frontier with Partial Implicit Causal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper unifies the causal fairness (Kilbertus et al., 2017; Chiappa, 2019), causal robustness (Schölkopf et al., 2021; Richens & Everitt, 2024), causal privacy (Tople et al., 2020), and causal explainability (Wachter et al., 2017) literatures under a single 'invariance conflict' framework, arguing that trustworthy AI trade-offs arise from incompatible invariance requirements under different interventions. It builds directly on Pearl's structural causal model formalism and the causal invariance principle, but departs from prior work by treating the multi-objective reconciliation problem as the central question rather than addressing each trust dimension in isolation. The explicit-versus-implicit causal integration distinction extends the causal representation learning literature (Schölkopf et al., 2021) to foundation model lifecycles, where full DAG specification is impractical.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The invariance-conflict reframing gives practitioners a principled diagnostic for *why* trade-offs arise and *where* causal intervention can soften them, shifting the design conversation from empirical heuristics to structural reasoning about which pathways to preserve or suppress. The explicit/implicit distinction is practically important for foundation model teams who cannot specify full causal graphs but can encode causal inductive biases through data curation, multi-environment training, or post-training alignment. Key open questions include whether the framework generalizes beyond the single synthetic SCM demonstrated, how to handle concept superposition in FMs that limits causal intervention granularity, and whether scalable causal discovery (including LLM-assisted approaches) can supply the partial graph knowledge the framework requires.
