## Problem

Multi-agent LLM debates are increasingly deployed for high-stakes decisions, yet it remains unclear how and when individual LLM biases get amplified into collective biased norms through interaction. Prior work documents interaction-level effects like conformity and sycophancy empirically, but no analytical framework exists to predict the conditions under which a group of LLMs locks into a biased consensus, nor to identify which design choices mitigate this risk.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a population of N LLM agents (N ≈ 3–10 in practice), a discrete-choice task prompt, and design parameters including sampling temperature T, interaction sparsity ρ, conformity level λ, and an externally controllable logit bias b. It produces the collective decision trajectory over R debate rounds, a bias metric (e.g., U.S. sector concentration, self-preference rate, or gender-assignment skew), a task performance metric (market-adjusted return or agreement with human labels), and a theoretical phase diagram mapping the critical threshold λρN/T at which biased consensus emerges. The unusual aspect is that sampling temperature serves simultaneously as the noise knob in the physical model and as a practical decoding parameter, making the phase transition directly tunable in deployment.

## Method

The paper reformulates Ising/Potts spin models from statistical physics for multi-agent LLM debates, mapping each agent's discrete choice to a spin state, conformity to interaction strength λ, sampling temperature to inverse noise β = 1/T, and individual biases to external fields decomposed into a task-aligned (neutral) component and a systematic bias component. Under mean-field approximation with sparse random interactions, the collective norm m(t) evolves via a stochastic recursion m(t+1) = tanh[β(λz̄m(t) + h ± γ/2)] + η(t), where the finite-size noise η(t) = O(1/√(ρN)) rounds the sharp thermodynamic-limit phase transition into a crossover for the small N typical of LLM debates. The key prediction is that biased consensus emerges once the effective conformity-to-noise ratio λρN/T exceeds a critical threshold, and that agent heterogeneity (mixed LLM families or varied temperatures) suppresses emergence by averaging nonlinear tanh activation functions across subgroups. The theory is validated through controlled synthetic experiments (Binary Choice and Implicit Bias tasks across 11 LLMs from five families) and extended to realistic investment-recommendation and LLM-as-a-judge settings, where heterogeneous temperature ensembles reduce bias while improving task performance.

## Results

The paper identifies a critical threshold λρN/T ≳ 1 above which collective bias emerges in multi-agent LLM debates, manifesting as a finite-N rounding of a phase transition. In real-world investment recommendation (N=6, GPT-4.1 Nano), a heterogeneous temperature ensemble (T∈{1.4, 1.5, 1.6}) reduces the U.S.-concentration bias norm from 0.9177±0.0096 to 0.8979±0.0096 while improving market-adjusted return from 1.522±0.106 to 1.607±0.116. For LLM-as-a-Judge on MT-Bench (N=10), the heterogeneous mix (T∈{1.3,…,1.7}) lowers self-bias from 0.8949±0.0375 to 0.8762±0.0032 and raises agreement with human labels from 0.8690±0.0097 to 0.8784±0.0150. In synthetic experiments, homogeneous populations of N=3 agents reach |m(R)|∈[0.5, 0.7] under weak bias, while heterogeneous mixes suppress this to |m(R)|∈[0.2, 0.5].

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing multi-agent debate methods; instead, its primary comparison is between homogeneous and heterogeneous agent ensembles under the same debate protocol (Du et al., 2023). The heterogeneous temperature mix consistently outperforms the best homogeneous baseline on both bias reduction and task performance across the two real-world tasks. In synthetic experiments, the theoretical mean-field predictions (Eq. 5) closely track empirical trajectories for Llama-405B on the Implicit Bias task (Fig. 3), validating the spin-model framework. Ablation-style comparisons show that each design knob—interaction sparsity ρ, top-p sampling, confidence visibility, and sycophancy prompting—monotonically modulates the collective norm |m(R)| in the direction predicted by the effective-field model (Fig. 4). The paper's key empirical finding is that agent heterogeneity (mixing LLM families or temperatures) smooths the phase transition and suppresses biased lock-in, with mixed populations achieving |m(R)|∈[−0.5, 0.2] versus |m(R)|∈[−0.7, 0.5] for homogeneous groups at T=1.2.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate literatures: multi-agent LLM debate protocols (Du et al., 2023) and physics-inspired spin models from social dynamics (Brock & Durlauf, 2001; Weidlich, 2006). While prior work on LLM interaction-level biases (Borah & Mihalcea, 2024; Oh et al., 2026) empirically observed bias amplification in multi-agent settings, and a few studies applied social dynamics models to LLM populations in synthetic game-theory environments (Ashery et al., 2025; Takata et al., 2025), none provided a mechanistic, predictive framework for when and why biased collective norms emerge in realistic decision-making debates. The paper's key departure is formulating the debate dynamics as a finite-N Ising/Potts model with a conformity parameter, a bias field, and sampling temperature as noise, yielding a testable phase-transition prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As multi-agent LLM debates move from proof-of-concept to deployment in high-stakes domains (law, finance, medicine), the identification of a concrete, quantifiable mechanism for biased consensus emergence is a significant AI safety contribution. The practical mitigations—raising sampling temperature, introducing agent heterogeneity, or sparsifying interaction topology—are simple and immediately actionable. Key limitations include the binary discretization of LLM outputs, the simplified all-to-all or random interaction protocol, and evaluation on only two synthetic and two realistic tasks; extending to continuous-valued spins, richer communication structures, and higher-stakes domains remains open. The framework also raises the broader question of whether safety evaluations for multi-agent systems should assess group-level dynamics rather than only individual model behavior.
