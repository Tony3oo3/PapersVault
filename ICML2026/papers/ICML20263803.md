## Problem

LLMs pretrained on unstructured text are poorly aligned with longitudinal clinical profiles, where irregularly sampled multivariate signals are governed by metabolic pathway constraints. Naive serialization of numerical profiles into text fragments values unpredictably and discards both temporal order and inter-variable biochemical structure, causing baseline LLMs to exhibit degenerate anomaly detection (near-zero sensitivity) and unstable sequence prediction. The paper argues that tokenization—not backbone architecture—is the central bottleneck for adapting LLMs to such structured, sparse longitudinal data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes three inputs: a longitudinal clinical profile (p×n_i matrix of steroid metabolite measurements, with n_i ranging from 2 to 20 irregularly spaced samples), a natural-language task prompt, and a feature interaction graph (p×p adjacency matrix encoding metabolic pathway relationships). The text prompt is processed by the standard LLM tokenizer, while the numerical profile and graph are routed through the structural-temporal tokenization pipeline. Outputs include a predicted next observation vector (sequence prediction), per-sample and per-profile anomaly scores (with a 99.9% specificity threshold to suppress false positives), and free-text contextual reasoning explanations attributing anomalies to specific metabolic deviations.

## Method

STT-LLM constructs a joint structural-temporal embedding from each profile: the structural component is derived from the eigen-decomposition of the normalized graph Laplacian of the metabolic pathway graph, projected through a learnable linear map; the temporal component uses multi-head attention with sinusoidal positional encodings over the time-ordered samples, followed by a feed-forward network and layer normalization. Two specialized tokenizers then transform this embedding into LLM-compatible tokens: the Structural Tokenizer concatenates the adjacency matrix with the joint embedding and passes it through a 2-layer MLP, while the Temporal Tokenizer handles variable-length sequences via zero-padding and binary masking before a similar MLP projection. The resulting token sequence (prompt tokens ∥ structural tokens ∥ temporal tokens) is fed to a frozen LLM backbone (default LLaMA-3.1 8B) augmented with LoRA adapters (rank 32, alpha 128), where only the tokenizers and LoRA weights are trained. This design keeps the LLM architecture unmodified, enabling the same backbone to support sequence prediction, anomaly detection, and contextual reasoning through different task prompts while retaining general-purpose LLM capabilities.

## Results

In zero-shot global anomaly detection, STT-LLM achieves the highest F1-scores (0.26 on Steroid-M, 0.29 on Steroid-F) and AUC values (0.57, 0.59) across all four datasets, exceeding the best LLM baseline by up to ~10%. In few-shot global anomaly detection, STT-LLM sensitivity improves monotonically from 0.15 (2-shot) to 0.60 (20-shot) on Steroid-M, with F1 rising from 0.15 to 0.70, while baselines show non-monotonic or unstable trends. For zero-shot sequence prediction, STT-LLM reduces RMSE to 79.3% (Steroid-M) and 68.4% (Steroid-F), with all baselines remaining above 83%; on the extreme-scarcity limited datasets (2 samples), RMSE drops to 30.0 and 1.2 respectively. In the case study on 29 DNA-verified profiles, STT-LLM identifies all 7 anomalous cases with 100% sensitivity and 93.10% overall accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

STT-LLM consistently outperforms all seven LLM baselines (Qwen-2.5, Mistral, Falcon-3, LLaMA-2, LLaMA-3.1, Phi-4, DeepSeek-R1) on both sequence prediction and anomaly detection. The most striking gap is in zero-shot anomaly detection sensitivity: most baselines exhibit near-zero sensitivity (0.00–0.08) due to degenerate majority-class prediction, while STT-LLM reaches 0.15–0.36 depending on dataset. Qwen-2.5 is particularly degenerate, producing identical predictions (e.g., RMSE = 1695.99) across all few-shot settings, confirming that native BPE tokenization cannot encode structured longitudinal data. Against non-LLM specialized models (Table 4), STT-LLM achieves competitive sequence prediction (RMSE 1664.59 vs. ARIMA 1675.47, TDDGNN 1675.78) while uniquely supporting anomaly detection (F1 0.26, AUC 0.57) and contextual reasoning in a single pipeline. The per-backbone analysis (Table 13) confirms that STT-LLM tokenization yields sensitivity gains of +2 to +14 pp and RMSE reductions of 9.35%–26.26% across all seven backbone LLMs, demonstrating that improvements stem from the tokenization framework rather than a specific architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of LLM domain adaptation and structured clinical time-series modeling. It departs from prior LLM-for-time-series work (Time-LLM, UniTime) that projects temporal patches into token sequences without encoding inter-variable structure, and from specialized architectures (GNNs, clinical transformers) that capture structure or temporal dynamics but are incompatible with general-purpose LLM inference pipelines. The key conceptual departure is treating tokenization itself—rather than backbone architecture—as the central bottleneck, using graph Laplacian eigen-decomposition for structural embeddings and attention with positional encoding for temporal embeddings, then projecting both into LLM-compatible tokens via lightweight MLP tokenizers trained jointly with LoRA adapters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that biologically grounded tokenization can consistently improve LLM performance on longitudinal clinical profiles across seven different backbone architectures, validating tokenization as a critical design dimension for domain adaptation. However, absolute performance remains modest (zero-shot AUC ~0.57, sensitivity ~15–19%), the evaluation is confined to a single narrow domain (steroid profiling in anti-doping) with small datasets (≤4,214 samples), and only one-step-ahead prediction is addressed. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to other clinical domains with different metabolic structures, whether multi-step forecasting benefits further from structural constraints, and how performance scales when domain priors for the feature interaction graph are unavailable or noisy.
