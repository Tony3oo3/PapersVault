## Problem

Existing strategic classification frameworks assume agents are perfectly rational, modifying features only when expected benefit exceeds cost. However, behavioral economics shows real agents systematically deviate from rationality through loss aversion, reference bias, and probability distortion, causing classifiers trained under rational assumptions to suffer two failure modes: over-defense (defending against manipulations that never occur) and under-defense (failing to anticipate more aggressive manipulations). The paper formalizes this gap as the behaviorally realistic strategic classification (BR-SC) problem.

## Input / Output

The method takes a tabular feature vector x, a logistic regression classifier f, a Mahalanobis-distance manipulation cost c, and a set of prospect-theoretic parameters (α, β, κ, γ, K) that govern behavioral deviations. Agents do not observe the current classifier directly but form a subjective acceptance probability p(x') from historical outcomes, which is then distorted by the behavioral mechanisms. The output is a robust classifier f* trained to minimize expected loss under prospect-guided manipulation, along with the predicted manipulated feature x' for each agent.

## Method

Pro-SF reformulates the Stackelberg game of strategic classification by embedding three prospect-theoretic mechanisms into the agent's utility: (1) an asymmetric value function v(x) = v_gain(x)^α − κ·v_loss(x)^β with κ>1 to capture loss aversion, (2) an inverse-S probability weighting function w(p) = p^γ/(p^γ+(1−p)^γ)^(1/γ) to model probability distortion, and (3) a quantized reference point r = (1/K)⌊Ks(x)⌋ to capture coarse self-assessment. These are unified into a single prospect-based utility U_P that governs the agent's best response b_P(x) = argmax U_P(x, x'; φ). The classifier is then trained by minimizing expected classification loss under this behavioral manipulation, and the behavioral parameters φ are inferred from observed manipulation pairs via maximum likelihood. The paper further proves existence, local stability, and local uniqueness of the resulting Stackelberg equilibrium, and shows via total-variation bounds that Pro-SF yields strictly tighter deployment guarantees than rational-agent training whenever it reduces the distributional mismatch.

## Results

Under the non-rational manipulation paradigm, Pro-SF achieves accuracy gains of 8–11 percentage points over the rational-based baseline across all six datasets (e.g., 81.50% vs. 72.42% on Adult, 82.41% vs. 69.54% on Credit, 89.34% vs. 78.38% on Spam). Under the more realistic mixed-behavior setting (π=0.2 rational agents), Pro-SF still leads by 4–9 points (e.g., 78.68% vs. 74.31% on Adult, 86.71% vs. 78.16% on Spam). Under the fully rational paradigm, Pro-SF remains competitive, with differences within ±3 points, confirming that behavioral modeling does not sacrifice performance when classical assumptions hold.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Pro-SF against a single baseline: the standard rational-based strategic classifier (linear model with Mahalanobis manipulation cost, following Hardt et al. 2016). Under the non-rational manipulation paradigm, Pro-SF wins decisively on all six datasets, with the largest margin on Credit (+12.87 points) and Spam (+10.96 points), while the smallest gap is on Diabetes (+9.79 points). Under the mixed-behavior setting, Pro-SF again wins on every dataset, with margins ranging from +5.20 (Diabetes) to +8.55 (Spam). Under the fully rational paradigm, Pro-SF is slightly worse on Adult (−3.22), Spam (−0.95), and Synthetic (−1.21), but slightly better on Credit (+0.90), Diabetes (+0.98), and German (+0.87), indicating that the behavioral modeling introduces negligible overhead when agents are truly rational. Overall, Pro-SF's advantage is concentrated precisely where the rational assumption breaks down, validating the paper's core motivation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the strategic classification (SC) paradigm of Hardt et al. (2016) by replacing the rational best-response agent model with a prospect-theoretic utility that incorporates loss aversion, reference bias, and probability distortion. It draws directly on cumulative prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 2013; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992) as the behavioral foundation, repurposing its value function, probability weighting, and reference-point mechanisms to redefine the follower's optimization in the Stackelberg game. The authors explicitly position Pro-SF as complementary to classical SC: when behavioral parameters collapse to their rational limits (κ=1, identity weighting), the framework reduces to the standard rational-response formulation, preserving backward compatibility.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of over-defense and under-defense as two distinct, theoretically characterized failure modes of rational-agent SC provides a concrete diagnostic for why deployed classifiers may systematically misclassify in real-world strategic settings. However, the experimental validation is limited to linear (logistic regression) classifiers on small UCI-scale tabular datasets with synthetically generated behavioral deviations, leaving open whether the framework generalizes to deep or non-linear classifiers, larger-scale deployments, and empirically calibrated behavioral parameters. Future work on multi-agent interactions, heterogeneous bias populations, and richer cost structures would strengthen the practical relevance of this behavioral SC formulation.
