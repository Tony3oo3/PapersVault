## Problem

Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) is a standard post-training recipe for language models, but a common assumption is that the resulting chain-of-thought reasoning is both causally important (the model genuinely uses it to reach its answer) and verifiable (an external reader can follow it to the same answer). This paper shows that outcome-based RLVR does not reliably produce reasoning chains with either property, calling into question whether accuracy gains from RLVR actually reflect improved reasoning.

## Input / Output

For CIR evaluation, the model's reasoning chain is truncated at every token position and the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the answer distribution at each prefix and the full-chain distribution is averaged. For SR evaluation, the reasoning chain is first sanitized by removing explicit answer content, then a strong verifier model (gpt-4o-mini) is prompted to predict the answer both with and without the original question; SR is 1 if the verifier's decoded answer is unchanged. The trained model outputs a chain-of-thought in <think> tags followed by a final answer in <answer> tags, with a 1024-token thinking budget during RLVR.

## Method

The paper introduces two complementary metrics—Causal Importance of Reasoning (CIR), which measures how much the answer distribution shifts under progressive truncation of the reasoning chain, and Sufficiency of Reasoning (SR), which measures whether a verifier can recover the answer from the reasoning alone without the question. Through experiments on Qwen2.5 (1.5B/3B/7B) and Llama3.2-3B across 40 ReasoningGym tasks, the authors show that RLVR with outcome-only rewards frequently degrades both metrics, and that tasks where the model can solve without reasoning show the most severe collapse. Two remedies are proposed: (1) a small amount of SFT on expert reasoning traces (as few as 2–64 examples from o3-mini) before RLVR substantially boosts CIR and SR, and (2) augmenting the GRPO training reward with auxiliary per-trajectory CIR or SR signals (r_train = r_out + α·SR or r_out + β·CIR), which improves both metrics while preserving outcome-level accuracy without requiring any expert traces.

## Results

Across 40 ReasoningGym tasks with Qwen2.5-3B, standard RLVR (outcome-only reward) does not reliably improve reasoning quality: 19/40 tasks show a decline in CIR and 17/40 show a decline in SR, with 18 tasks ending below CIR 0.1. Only tasks where accuracy improves by more than 50 points show consistent gains in both CIR and SR. On MATH Hard (Level 5), where the task is genuinely challenging, RLVR does improve all metrics: CIR rises from 0.23 to 0.51, SR from 0.23 to 0.42, and accuracy from 0.11 to 0.56. SFT with 64 expert traces before RLVR lifts CIR to ~0.4 and SR to ~0.65, while auxiliary CIR/SR rewards (α=β=1.0) improve both metrics without sacrificing accuracy relative to outcome-only RLVR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between standard outcome-only RLVR and variants that explicitly target reasoning quality. On the 8 low-CIR/SR tasks, outcome-only RLVR degrades both CIR (to ~0.05) and SR (to <0.10), while SFT with just 64 expert traces before RLVR more than doubles CIR (~0.40) and triples SR (~0.65) without hurting accuracy. Training without any reasoning chain (direct answering) achieves nearly identical accuracy to reasoning-based RLVR on tasks where final CIR < 0.15 and SR < 0.15 (average gap only −0.053), but on tasks where reasoning is genuinely used (high CIR or SR), the reasoning model outperforms direct answering by 0.18 accuracy. Auxiliary CIR or SR rewards added to the outcome reward (at weight 1.0) improve both CIR and SR while matching the accuracy of standard RLVR, and the two metrics positively interact—optimizing one tends to improve the other. A stricter SR− metric (which also removes question paraphrases from the chain) confirms that auxiliary-reward training does not simply reward-hack by repeating the question.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the faithfulness-evaluation lineage of Lanham et al. (2023) and Lyu et al. (2023), which operationalize causal faithfulness by perturbing reasoning traces and measuring output divergence, into a training-context setting: it asks whether RLVR (built on GRPO, Shao et al. 2024) actually produces traces that satisfy these faithfulness criteria. The Sufficiency of Reasoning metric draws on the prover-verifier legibility framework of Kirchner et al. (2024), while the Causal Importance metric is closely related to the concurrent TRACE metric of Wang et al. (2025). The paper's key departure is treating faithfulness and verifiability not merely as evaluation targets but as trainable objectives, introducing auxiliary CIR/SR rewards on top of the standard outcome-based RLVR signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that outcome-based RLVR can improve accuracy while simultaneously degrading the causal importance and verifiability of reasoning chains directly undermines a core assumption in current post-training practice and has implications for AI safety (monitorability of model reasoning). The practical remedies—small-scale SFT before RL and auxiliary CIR/SR rewards—are simple and preserve accuracy, making them immediately actionable. Limitations include reliance on an API-based verifier (gpt-4o-mini) for SR, the permissive nature of the SR metric, and the observation that on genuinely hard tasks (MATH-Hard) RLVR does improve both metrics, suggesting the problem is most acute on tasks solvable without explicit step-by-step reasoning. Open questions include whether these findings hold at frontier-model scale and whether the auxiliary rewards interact with other post-training objectives (e.g., safety alignment).
