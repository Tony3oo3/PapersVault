## Problem

Composing multiple pre-trained diffusion models is challenging because simple analytical operations (product, mixture) produce incoherent outputs when models conflict, and existing spatial-composition methods like MultiDiffusion require user-defined partitions that are cumbersome to specify and ignore each model's strengths. The paper asks: can we infer a fair and efficient division of labor among models online, without ground-truth partitions, so that no single model dominates and all concepts are faithfully represented?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261413/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes n pre-trained diffusion models, each conditioned on a single concept (a short text prompt for Stable Diffusion or a one-hot ImageNet class for DiT), all operating in a common latent space (e.g., 64×64×4 for SD 2.0). Models need not share weights, architectures, or training data—only the latent dimension. The output is a single composite image containing all n concepts simultaneously, generated via a 50-step DDIM sampling trajectory in the shared latent space.

## Method

Divide-and-Denoise couples two processes at every denoising timestep: (i) a division process that solves a fair-division game to compute an allocation Q_t—a distribution over partitions of latent features among models—by maximizing total utility under linear fairness constraints (proportional, envy-free, or equitable) with KL regularization to the previous allocation for temporal smoothness; and (ii) a composite denoising process that forms the update as a weighted sum of each model's mean (weighted by its allocation) plus a guidance term proportional to the gradient of total utility. Utilities are derived either from normalized score differences (score-based, applicable to any conditional model) or from cross-attention maps (attention-based, for models with cross-attention conditioning). A fictitious player with uniform utility and an averaged denoising kernel is added to promote collaboration in low-utility regions. The division problem admits a closed-form solution via a dual optimization (Theorem 3.1), and the denoising update reduces to a Gaussian with a decomposable mean (Theorem 3.2), making the method fully inference-time with no additional training.

## Results

On the GenEval benchmark with Stable Diffusion, Divide-and-Denoise achieves 88.50% %images and 99% %prompts when coordinating 2 single-concept models, and 59.50% %images and 92% %prompts with 3 models, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline (MultiDiffusion at 58.00% and 14.00% respectively). On CLIP joint score, the method reaches 30.02 (2 models) and 33.21 (3 models), and on VQA joint score 0.960 and 0.921. With DiT on ImageNet, Divide-and-Denoise achieves 29.03 CLIP joint and 0.868 VQA joint, again surpassing all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Divide-and-Denoise outperforms both baselines across all metrics and all settings. Against Composable Diffusion (Liu et al., 2022), the gap is dramatic: for 2 models, GenEval %images jumps from 36.50% to 88.50%, and for 3 models from 3.50% to 59.50%, indicating that simply summing conditional scores fails to resolve spatial conflicts between models. Against MultiDiffusion (Bar-Tal et al., 2023), which uses a fixed vertical-stripe allocation, Divide-and-Denoise improves GenEval %images from 58.00% to 88.50% (2 models) and from 14.00% to 59.50% (3 models), showing that dynamic, fairness-constrained allocation is critical as the number of models grows. On CLIP joint score, the improvement over MultiDiffusion is 27.65→30.02 (2 models) and 28.05→33.21 (3 models). The advantage is most pronounced in the 3-model setting, where baselines degrade sharply (Averaging drops to 1.50% GenEval) while Divide-and-Denoise maintains strong performance, confirming that fairness constraints prevent any single model from being neglected.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two main lines of work on diffusion model composition. First, it extends MultiDiffusion (Bar-Tal et al., 2023), which requires user-specified spatial partitions, by inferring allocations online through a fair division game rather than relying on manual segmentation. Second, it moves beyond the product/mixture density operations of Composable Diffusion (Liu et al., 2022) and simple score-averaging by introducing a game-theoretic objective that explicitly balances efficiency (total utility) against fairness (proportionality, envy-freeness, equitability) constraints. The theoretical machinery draws on classical fair division results for indivisible goods (Amanatidis et al., 2023; Nishimura & Sumita, 2021), repurposing the mixed-allocation and decomposability framework to define per-timestep, per-feature responsibility assignments among models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method provides a principled, architecture-agnostic recipe for reusing pre-trained single-concept diffusion models without retraining, which is practically important given the cost of training new multi-concept models. The fairness constraint is not merely theoretical: it demonstrably prevents object dropout (Figure 2) and the gap widens with more models, suggesting the approach scales to larger model teams. Key limitations include significant computational overhead from the convex solver in the Division stage (up to 57% of wall-clock time at n=4), sensitivity to the guidance hyperparameter γ, and the observation that score-based utilities underperform attention-based ones substantially (Table 10), limiting applicability to models without cross-attention. Open questions include designing stronger utility functions, reducing solver cost, and extending to domains like text-to-graph or text-to-audio generation.
