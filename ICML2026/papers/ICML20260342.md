## Problem

Prior expressivity results for transformers rely on hardmax attention, logarithmically growing activation precision, and other architectural modifications that disconnect them from models used in practice. This paper asks whether standard softmax transformers with realistic low-precision rounding (e.g., bfloat16) can still simulate Turing machines, and if so, at what cost in depth, width, and precision.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260342/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Theoretically, the transformer receives a token sequence delimited by <inp>...</inp> and autoregressively generates intermediate CoT tokens (encoding TM state, written symbols, and head movements) or SCoT segments (each bounded by <summ>...</summ> blocks encoding the full TM configuration) before producing the output between <outp>...</outp>. Depth and width scale as O(log t̂) or O(log ŝ) for time/space bounds, while activation precision requires only O(log log log t̂) exponent bits and attention weight precision O(log log t̂) exponent bits. Empirically, the input is a Sudoku puzzle token sequence and the output is the solved grid, with SCoT segments capped at 512 non-summary tokens.

## Method

The core construction proceeds in two stages: first, hardmax transformers with ternary {-1,0,1} residual-stream activations and well-separated attention scores (gaps of at least 1/√d_k) are built to recognize regular languages and simulate multi-tape Turing machines via CoT or SCoT, using binary positional encodings and binary search over prior tokens to recover head positions. Second, these hardmax constructions are converted to softmax by scaling query/key projections by c = O((log l̂)^(3/4)), and a denoising MLP (implementing a coordinatewise snap-to-ternary function) is inserted after each attention layer to absorb errors from both the softmax approximation and attention-weight rounding. The ternary activation constraint is essential: it keeps the denoising MLP width constant rather than growing with the activation set size, which would be required for the log-precision constructions of prior work. Empirically, small transformers (d=512, 8 heads) are trained via supervised learning on depth-first-search Sudoku traces, demonstrating that increasing model depth (not activation precision) extends the solvable CoT context length, and that SCoT models generalize from short to multi-million-token reasoning traces.

## Results

Theoretically, the paper proves that softmax transformers with O(log t̂) depth and width, O(log log t̂) exponent bits for attention weights, and O(log log log t̂) exponent bits for activations can simulate any multi-tape Turing machine in O(t) decoding steps with CoT, or in O(t) steps with context size O(s) using SCoT. Empirically, on the sudoku-extreme dataset, a small SCoT model (d=512, L=6, H=8) achieves 99.5% accuracy, while a CoT model of identical size fails at long contexts (N=2¹⁴ tokens). Increasing depth from L=6 to L=8 restores CoT performance, but switching from bfloat16 to fp32 activations has negligible effect, supporting the paper's theoretical prediction that model size—not precision—is the relevant scaling axis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to Merrill & Sabharwal (2024) and Yang et al. (2025), which achieve O(1) depth/width but require O(log t̂) activation precision and hardmax attention, this paper trades constant depth/width for O(log t̂) depth/width in exchange for softmax attention and only O(log log t̂) precision—making the construction compatible with bfloat16 up to context sizes of ~10³⁸. Against Li et al. (2024), which uses softmax but requires O(t log t) steps and problem-specific positional encodings yielding O(t̂(log t̂)²) parameters, this paper achieves O(t) steps with O((log t̂)³) parameters. Against Jiang et al. (2026), the only prior uniform softmax result, this paper avoids the exponential O(exp(t)) step blowup and O(t̂) precision by accepting logarithmic depth/width growth. The empirical Sudoku experiments further distinguish this work: prior log-precision results (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2024) predict that increasing activation precision should fix long-context CoT failure, but the paper shows fp32 vs. bfloat16 makes no difference, while increasing model depth (consistent with this paper's O(log t̂) depth prediction) cleanly resolves the failure. SCoT additionally enables constant-space generalization, solving 92/100 hardest test puzzles (1–9M CoT tokens) from a model trained on only 2¹²-token puzzles.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of transformer Turing-completeness results (Pérez et al. 2021; Merrill & Sabharwal 2024; Yang et al. 2025) by replacing the standard assumptions of hardmax attention and logarithmic activation precision with softmax attention and low-precision rounding, while allowing depth and width to grow logarithmically with context length. The key technical departure is constructing hardmax transformers with ternary activations and well-separated attention scores as an intermediate step, enabling conversion to softmax without the polynomially growing parameter magnitudes that prior constructions would require. The SCoT analysis (Theorem 3.3) is new in the low-precision softmax setting, building on the PENCIL paradigm (Yang et al. 2025) but proving it under realistic precision constraints. The denoising-MLP technique for handling attention-weight rounding draws on Wei et al. (2022a) but is applied here to a fundamentally different purpose: removing actual softmax/rounding errors rather than proving finite-sample bounds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work narrows a significant gap between theoretical expressivity results and practical transformers by showing that bfloat16 precision suffices for Turing-complete simulation up to context sizes of roughly 10^38, and that the SCoT paradigm achieves logarithmic scaling in space rather than time. The empirical Sudoku experiments provide the first evidence that low-precision expressivity results make better learnability predictions than prior high-precision ones: increasing model depth cleanly extends CoT context length while switching from bfloat16 to fp32 has negligible effect, contradicting the remedy suggested by log-precision theory. Open questions include whether the logarithmic depth/width scaling is actually necessary, whether expressivity implies learnability in general (the authors note transformers remain hard to train on automata tasks), and under what conditions SCoT enables the observed constant-space generalization from easy to hard problems.
