## Problem

Existing multimodal safety defenses inspect raw inputs, generated outputs, or scalar confidence signals, but they fail when harmful intent is distributed across modalities so that each modality appears benign in isolation while their fusion produces unsafe behavior. This paper identifies that the internal cross-modal fusion process itself—specifically, the consistency between text-only, vision-only, and joint predictive distributions—provides a general, attack-agnostic detection signal that surface-level methods overlook.

## Input / Output

FlowGuard takes an image-text pair (I, Q) and probes the target MLLM under three conditioning configurations (text-only, vision-only with a fixed neutral prompt, and joint multimodal) to extract first-token predictive distributions. From these three distributions it computes a 4D FlowVector (Uv, Ut, R, S) encoding modality-specific divergence, cross-modal agreement, and fusion stability, which is then scored by a pre-trained Isolation Forest to yield a binary safe/unsafe decision. The method requires only next-token probability distributions (or top-k logprobs for API-accessed models) and no model weights, gradients, or hidden activations.

## Method

FlowGuard is inspired by Partial Information Decomposition (PID), which characterizes how multiple sources inform a target via redundancy, uniqueness, and synergy. Concretely, it computes Uniqueness as directional KL divergences between the fused multimodal posterior and each unimodal prior (capturing modality dominance), Redundancy as 1 minus the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the two unimodal priors (capturing semantic agreement), and Synergy as the entropy reduction achieved by fusion relative to the average unimodal entropy (capturing whether integration stabilizes or destabilizes prediction). These four values form a compact FlowVector in R⁴ that jointly resolves ambiguities no single feature can (e.g., high redundancy occurs in both benign and cooperative-attack regimes). Safety detection is framed as one-class classification: an Isolation Forest is trained exclusively on benign FlowVectors sampled from VQAv2, learning the geometry of aligned multimodal information flow without any adversarial supervision. At inference, inputs whose FlowVectors fall outside the learned benign support are flagged as unsafe, enabling attack-agnostic detection that generalizes to previously unseen jailbreak strategies.

## Results

On LLaVA-1.5-7B, FlowGuard reduces Attack Success Rate (ASR) from >90% (base model) to ≤14.2% across all 10 attack types on MM-SafetyBench, with an overall AUROC of 0.942. On utility benchmarks, FlowGuard maintains accuracy within 2.4% of the undefended baseline (77.1% on VQAv2 vs. 79.4% base; 79.5% on MOSSBench vs. 82.1% base), yielding a 2.4% False Positive Rate. Inference latency is approximately 1.3 s per sample (three forward passes), compared to 4.5 s (MirrorCheck) and 8.5 s (CIDER) for diffusion-based verification. Results generalize across model scales: 7.9% average ASR on LLaMA-3.1-70B and 10.4% on GPT-4.1-mini under partial-distribution (top-k logprob) access.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FlowGuard achieves the lowest ASR across all 30 attack–benchmark combinations on LLaVA-1.5-7B, with the largest margins on cross-modal attacks where baselines are weakest: on CBSC (MMSB), FlowGuard reaches 9.1% vs. Llama Guard 4's 20.2%, CIDER's 23.9%, and UniGuard's 80.7%. CIDER and MirrorCheck are competitive on visual-only attacks (FigStep ≈15%, VAJM ≈11–12%) but degrade sharply on text-only (MSJ ≈54%) and cross-modal (UMK ≈22–25%) threats. ECSO is effective against visual injections (≈7–10% ASR) but nearly ineffective on text attacks (≈51–57%) and cross-modal attacks (≈12–17%). Llama Guard 4 performs well on direct queries and text attacks (≈6–8% ASR) but struggles with compositional cross-modal obfuscation, rising to 20.2% on CBSC and 28.4% on UMK (VLSU). UniGuard is the weakest overall, with ASR above 68% on visual attacks and above 80% on cross-modal attacks. The Raw Embedding control (same Isolation Forest on high-dimensional hidden states) achieves 32–54% ASR, confirming that the 4D FlowVector relational decomposition—not generic geometric outlier detection—is responsible for the detection gain.

## Relation to Prior Work

FlowGuard adapts the conceptual structure of Partial Information Decomposition (Williams & Beer, 2010)—redundancy, uniqueness, synergy—to the problem of multimodal safety, using KL divergence, JSD, and entropy over first-token predictive distributions as tractable proxies rather than computing PID explicitly. It departs from the dominant paradigm of prior MLLM defenses (CIDER, MirrorCheck, UniGuard, Llama Guard 4, ECSO), which inspect raw inputs, regenerate outputs, or monitor scalar confidence signals, by instead probing the internal fusion process through three conditioning configurations (text-only, vision-only, joint) and detecting anomalous cross-modal interaction patterns. The one-class Isolation Forest formulation is borrowed from classical anomaly detection but applied to a novel 4D relational feature space rather than raw logits or hidden states.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FlowGuard offers a practical, model-agnostic, inference-time defense that requires only next-token probability access (no weights, gradients, or activations), making it deployable even via API-only models, and its one-class training on benign data alone avoids the brittleness of supervised classifiers to unseen attacks. The adaptive-attack analysis revealing a structural incompatibility between stealth (benign FlowVector geometry) and success (harmful target induction) is a meaningful constraint on the future attack space. Key limitations include higher residual ASR on text-only attacks (~13–14%) versus visual/cross-modal (~6–9%), motivating composition with text-specific defenses, and open questions around multi-turn dialogue trajectories, multi-image PID extensions, and operation under even more restrictive access regimes (rank-only, very small top-k).
