## Problem

Graph structure is ubiquitous across domains (scenes, events, molecules, algorithmic graphs) but is learned in a task-isolated manner: representations are built to serve a single objective and discarded, causing structurally similar patterns to be repeatedly reconstructed rather than accumulated. The paper asks how graph structure should be organized so it persists and accumulates as a reusable intermediate state across heterogeneous modalities and functional roles.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262613/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes heterogeneous inputs—RGB images, raw text, molecular graphs (atoms/bonds with optional SMILES), and attributed graphs—and produces task-specific outputs: scene graphs (object-relation triples), event-relation graphs (typed temporal/causal/subevent edges), natural-language molecular descriptions, and algorithmic answers (connectivity, shortest path, etc.). Critically, all graph states are expressed in a unified (u, r, v) triplet format with consistent entity identifiers and typed edges, so a graph generated from an image can be directly consumed as input by a graph-reasoning task without structural translation.

## Method

G-Substrate organizes learning around two complementary mechanisms. First, a unified structural schema maps all graphs from heterogeneous modalities into a shared graph state space G_s, where every graph is a set of (u, r, v) triples with consistent node identifiers, typed edges, and connectivity conventions, making structurally analogous configurations (e.g., hub motifs) directly comparable across tasks. Second, interleaved role-based training assigns each task a role function ρ ∈ {GENERATE, UNDERSTAND} and sequences training so that a graph produced under a GENERATE task (e.g., scene graph generation from an image) is immediately reused as input under a subsequent UNDERSTAND task (e.g., graph algorithmic reasoning), creating cross-role supervision that biases representations toward structural coherence. The backbone is a vision-language model (Qwen3-VL 2B-Instruct) fine-tuned end-to-end; no task-specific heads or post-hoc adaptation are used, and the gains arise from the representation-level organization rather than parameter sharing or loss reweighting.

## Results

G-Substrate, a single unified model, matches or exceeds task-specific systems across four domains. On Graph Algorithmic Reasoning, it achieves 48.59% on Shortest Path (vs. 41.46% for GraphWiz) and 94.54% on Bipartite Matching. On Molecular Graph Description, it reaches 51.53 BLEU-4 and 68.47 ROUGE-L, surpassing Mol-LLaMA (50.74 / 67.02). On Event Relation Extraction, it attains 25.15 F1 on HiEvent (vs. 22.90 for LLM-ERE) and 42.68 F1 on MAVEN-Temporal (vs. 35.60). The only losses are on Scene Graph Generation (25.38 vs. 26.90 PCIs R@50 for PGSG) and MAVEN-Subevent (52.20 vs. 54.30 for LLM-ERE), both in structurally compact settings where task-specific specialization dominates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GITA (NeurIPS 2024), G-Substrate wins on Shortest Path (48.59 vs. 39.15) and Bipartite Matching (94.54 vs. 93.19) but trails on Cycle Detection (96.97 vs. 98.07). Against GraphWiz (KDD 2024), G-Substrate wins on all four GAR subtasks, with the largest margin on Shortest Path (+7.13). Against Mol-LLaMA (2025), G-Substrate edges ahead on both MGD metrics (BLEU-4 51.53 vs. 50.74; ROUGE-L 68.47 vs. 67.02). Against PGSG (CVPR 2024), G-Substrate loses on SGG PCIs R@50 (25.38 vs. 26.90), the paper's acknowledged weakness in structurally compact graphs. Against ProtoEM (2023), G-Substrate wins decisively on MAVEN-Temporal (42.68 vs. 31.80), MAVEN-Causal (40.91 vs. 27.90), and HiEvent (25.15 vs. 20.43) but loses on MAVEN-Subevent (52.20 vs. 53.80). Against LLM-ERE (COLING 2025), G-Substrate wins on MA-T (+7.08), MA-C (+13.01), and HiE (+2.25) while losing narrowly on MA-S (52.20 vs. 54.30). Overall, G-Substrate's gains are largest where evaluation rewards multi-hop relational reasoning (SP, BM, temporal/causal event relations) and smallest in compact-graph settings where task-specific tuning is the dominant lever.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in graph-structured learning where graph representations are constructed, optimized, and discarded within individual task pipelines (scene graph generation, event relation extraction, molecular description, graph algorithmic reasoning). While recent multi-task and unified-architecture approaches (GITA, GraphWiz, Mol-LLaMA, PGSG, LLM-ERE) share model parameters across tasks, they still treat graphs as task-bound inputs or outputs rather than as persistent intermediate states. G-Substrate carves new ground by introducing a representation-centric perspective: a unified graph state space (structural schema) that makes heterogeneous graphs directly comparable, combined with interleaved generation-understanding training that forces the same graph to remain functional under multiple roles. The framework repurposes standard VLM backbones (Qwen3-VL, InternVL) without architectural modification, positioning the contribution at the level of representation organization and training structure rather than model design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a clean conceptual reframing of multi-task learning for structured data: the bottleneck is not parameter sharing or loss balancing but the absence of a persistent, structurally compatible intermediate representation that can be reused across functional roles. The ablation showing that the unified schema is neutral or harmful in single-task settings but beneficial only under multi-task + interleaved training is a useful diagnostic for practitioners. Limitations include modest absolute gains (a few points on most metrics), underperformance on SGG relative to the task-specific PGSG baseline, evaluation on only four domains with 2B-parameter backbones, and the open question of how the framework scales to additional modalities, larger models, or structured representations beyond graphs (e.g., trees, hypergraphs). The paper also does not study how the composition of modalities or role types shapes representation formation, leaving principled strategies for balancing heterogeneous supervision as future work.
