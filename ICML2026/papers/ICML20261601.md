## Problem

Generating structurally diverse sets of graphs on N unlabeled vertices is challenging because standard neural generative models (VAEs, diffusion) require empirical samples from a target distribution, which dispersion problems fundamentally lack. Prior approaches rely on combinatorial search or iterative generative bootstrapping that scale poorly with N and k, limiting graph-level dispersion to small regimes.

## Input / Output

The method takes the number of vertices N, target set cardinality k, and a non-differentiable evaluation metric d_eval (e.g., graphlet correlation distance, NetLSD, portrait divergence) as inputs, and produces a set of k discrete simple undirected graphs. Internally, fixed random noise vectors z ∈ R^m are fed to MLP generators that output relaxed adjacency matrices in [0,1]^{N×N}, which are discretized via temperature-controlled Bernoulli sampling. A fixed candidate pool of M=100,000 graphs is constructed independently of N and k, then refined by greedy max-sum selection under d_eval, decoupling the expensive neural training from the metric-specific selection stage.

## Method

Neural Graph Dispersion (NGD) trains an ensemble of E MLP generators, each mapping fixed noise vectors to edge-probability matrices, under a Coulomb-style repulsive energy objective that penalizes pairwise proximity in a surrogate graph metric. The surrogate metric is induced by a differentiable, isomorphism-invariant spectral-moment feature map φ (normalized ratios of tr(A^r) for r≤6) projected onto random low-dimensional subspaces via fixed matrices U_e, so each ensemble member disperses along complementary spectral directions. Crucially, graphs are sampled along the optimization trajectories rather than only at convergence, making the repulsive dynamics themselves the generative mechanism and eliminating the need for distribution fitting. A two-stage candidate selection (matrix-level greedy diversity discovery followed by focused multi-temperature Bernoulli sampling) produces the final pool, which is then refined under any user-specified non-differentiable evaluation metric without retraining.

## Results

On small-scale graph dispersion (N=16, N=128), Neural Graph Dispersion (NGD) achieves near-best or best diversity across all four evaluation metrics (GCD, Heat, Wave, Portrait Divergence). At N=128, NGD attains the highest scores on GCD (7.981), Heat (5.546), and Portrait (0.969), while being competitive on Wave (10.674 vs. 10.915 for the best baseline). In scaling experiments, NGD maintains high structural diversity as N increases up to 512 and k grows, whereas parametric stochastic ensembles (ER-mix, Gen-mix) saturate or degrade — enabling dispersion at an order of magnitude larger N and k than prior iterative methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All baselines are drawn from Velikonivtsev et al. (2024), the first systematic study of graph dispersion in the ML setting. At N=16, NGD is competitive with the strongest combinatorial search pipelines (e.g., Greedy→Genetic→LocalOpt scores 7.718 GCD vs. NGD's 7.621; 10.765 Wave vs. 10.634), but NGD achieves the best Portrait Divergence (0.885 vs. 0.865) without being trained to optimize any of these metrics. At N=128, NGD clearly wins on GCD (7.981 vs. 7.887), Heat (5.546 vs. 5.448), and Portrait (0.969 vs. 0.962), while trailing slightly on Wave (10.674 vs. 10.915). Parametric stochastic ensembles (ER-mix, Gen-mix) are consistently the weakest baselines, scoring 2–3× lower on most metrics. The critical advantage of NGD is scalability: combinatorial search and IGGM become intractable beyond N≈128 (IGGM fails entirely at N≥128 due to GPU memory limits), whereas NGD scales to N=512 and large k while maintaining diversity, as shown in the scaling experiments of Figure 2.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Velikonivtsev et al. (2024), which formalized the graph dispersion problem and evaluated three categories of approaches: parametric stochastic ensembles, combinatorial search, and iterative generative bootstrapping (IGGM). The key conceptual departure is treating diversity as a direct optimization objective rather than requiring a target distribution: the authors use the Coulomb-style repulsion objective from prior work but apply it to an ensemble of MLP generators whose optimization trajectories themselves serve as the generative mechanism. The spectral-moment feature map with random projections as differentiable surrogate metrics is novel, and the trajectory-based generation paradigm (collecting graphs along optimization paths rather than from a trained distribution) has no direct precedent in the graph generation literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: neural graph dispersion enables graph-level dispersion at node counts and set cardinalities an order of magnitude beyond what combinatorial search or IGGM can handle, while a single training run produces a reusable candidate pool across multiple evaluation metrics without retraining. The downstream stress tests (3–11× higher GNN regression error, greedy coloring reaching worst-case approximation ratios) demonstrate that the structural coverage is functionally meaningful, not merely metric-gaming. Open questions include theoretical characterization of the optimization trajectories at convergence, whether the approach extends to other combinatorial domains (permutations, matchings), and whether alternative repulsive potentials or generator architectures could further improve coverage.
