## Problem

Evaluating a new model on a modern multi-task benchmark requires generating and scoring thousands of responses, making full evaluation prohibitively expensive as model variants proliferate. Although cached responses from previously-evaluated models are routinely available, no principled method existed to leverage this information to reduce the query budget needed to predict a new model's benchmark score, nor were there theoretical guarantees that such a method could outperform naive subset scoring.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the target model's responses to a small subset Q of m queries (m << M total), the cached responses of n reference models to the same m queries, and the reference models' known full-benchmark scores. Responses are mapped to vectors via a sentence embedding model (gemini-embedding-001, 3024-dim for free-form text) or one-hot encoding (for multiple-choice), and the DKPS representation is computed at a fixed dimension d=8. The output is a single predicted benchmark score in [0,1], produced by an ensemble that adaptively weights the DKPS regression prediction and the direct sample score with α = m/M.

## Method

The core mechanism maps a collection of models into a low-dimensional Euclidean space (the Data Kernel Perspective Space) by computing pairwise Frobenius-norm distances between their average embedded response matrices and solving a classical MDS problem. A linear regressor (OLS) is then trained on the reference models' DKPS coordinates and known scores to predict the target model's benchmark score from its DKPS coordinate. The paper proves that nearest-neighbor regression in this space is query-efficient relative to subset scoring under a Lipschitz score function assumption and a model distribution support assumption, with the error bound driven by a concentration result requiring r = ω(n³) replicates (trivially satisfied at temperature 0). An ensemble that convexly combines the DKPS prediction with the target model's direct sample score (weighted by m/M) achieves the best empirical performance, and an offline R²-based query selection strategy further improves accuracy at low query budgets by choosing the subset that best predicts reference model scores.

## Results

On four HELM-Lite tasks (MATH, LegalBench, MedQA, WMT-14), the Ensemble DKPS method (Ens(DKPS+IRT)) achieves MAE of 0.089–0.145 at m=1 query, matching or exceeding the Sample Score baseline's performance at m=64 (MAE 0.039–0.048) — a >10× query reduction. At m=16, Ens(DKPS+IRT) reaches MAE of 0.050 (LegalBench), 0.061 (MedQA), 0.018 (WMT-14), and 0.068 (MATH), consistently outperforming both Sample Score and IRT at the same budget. The Population Mean baseline (which ignores the target model entirely) achieves MAE of 0.049–0.234 regardless of query budget, confirming that DKPS-based methods extract meaningful signal from even a single query.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is Sample Score (the target model's average response-level score on the m-query subset), which is the natural query-efficient alternative. DKPS-based methods dramatically outperform Sample Score at low query budgets: at m=1, Ens(DKPS+IRT) achieves MAE of 0.089 on LegalBench versus 0.462 for Sample Score (5.2× lower), and 0.039 on WMT-14 versus 0.144 (3.7× lower). The IRT baseline (Polo et al., 2024, 1-parameter Rasch model) is competitive on LegalBench, MedQA, and MATH at moderate-to-high query budgets (e.g., IRT MAE 0.031 vs. Ens(DKPS+IRT) 0.042 on MATH at m=64), but performs substantially worse on WMT-14 (IRT MAE 0.128 at m=64 vs. 0.013 for Ens(DKPS+IRT)), likely because IRT assumes binary correctness while WMT-14 uses continuous BLEU scores. The combined Ens(DKPS+IRT) method, which appends the IRT ability parameter as an extra feature to the DKPS vector, achieves the best or near-best MAE in 14 of 16 (task, m) settings, confirming that DKPS and IRT capture complementary signal. Overall, the headline finding is that DKPS-based prediction at m=1–4 queries matches or exceeds Sample Score at m=64, yielding a >10× reduction in required queries for equivalent prediction accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Data Kernel Perspective Space (DKPS), originally developed for monitoring multi-agent systems (Helm et al., 2024b) with theoretical foundations in consistency and concentration (Acharyya et al., 2024, 2025; Helm et al., 2025), for the distinct task of benchmark score prediction. It positions itself as complementary to existing efficient benchmarking methods—IRT-based subset selection (Polo et al., 2024), anchor-point clustering (Vivek et al., 2024), and cognitive-embedding selection (Bean et al., 2025)—by operating purely on black-box response similarity without requiring task-specific structure, model internals, or response-level scoring functions. The key conceptual departure is that prior efficient-benchmarking work selects which queries to evaluate, whereas this paper leverages cached responses from previously-evaluated models to predict scores, and provides the first formal query-efficiency guarantee for DKPS-based regression relative to subset scoring.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstrated 10× reduction in query budget for equivalent prediction accuracy has direct practical impact as model zoos and benchmark suites grow, enabling practitioners to evaluate new model variants (via finetuning, merging, distillation) at a fraction of the cost. The method's complementarity with existing techniques (e.g., DKPS prediction on IRT-selected subsets) suggests compounding efficiency gains are possible. Open questions include extending to stochastic LLM-as-judge scoring, handling unstructured/missing query data across models, supporting extrapolative prediction for frontier models that outperform all references, and understanding which task properties (response diversity, scoring complexity) predict when DKPS-based methods excel. The Leave-One-Family-Out protocol provides conservative estimates, so real-world gains with same-family references may be larger.
