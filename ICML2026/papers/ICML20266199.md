## Problem

Multiscale time series forecasting models rely on downsampling (strided convolutions, pooling) to capture features at different temporal granularities, but this process causes high-frequency components exceeding the new Nyquist frequency to fold into spurious low-frequency patterns (aliasing), corrupting the extracted features. Existing multiscale architectures like TimeMixer use average pooling or strided convolutions without any anti-aliasing protection, leaving a critical gap in signal fidelity that degrades forecasting accuracy, especially in high-sensitivity domains.

## Input / Output

The model takes a multivariate time series window X ∈ R^{C×L} (C variates, L lookback steps, typically L=96 or L=720) and produces a future forecast X̂_o ∈ R^{C×L_next} over horizons of 96, 192, 336, or 720 steps (long-term) or 3–60 steps (short-term). The input is first normalized via RevIN to handle distribution shift, then projected into a latent space of dimension T via a linear embedding with learnable positional encoding. The final output is decoded by a simple FFN head followed by inverse RevIN, keeping the prediction head lightweight.

## Method

DMANet follows a Decomposition-Prevention-Fusion paradigm. Decomposition uses a hierarchy of depth-wise convolutions (temporal dependencies) followed by point-wise convolutions (inter-channel mixing) with progressive stride-s downsampling to build multiscale features. Prevention applies a Pre-Sampling Anti-Aliasing filter before each downsampling step: the signal is transformed via FFT, masked with a dynamic low-pass cutoff determined by the Equivalent Sampling Rate ESR = min(K, C_l/C_{l-1})/s (where K is kernel size and C_l/C_{l-1} is the channel expansion ratio), then transformed back via IFFT to physically remove frequencies that would alias. Fusion performs Post-Sampling Interpolation in the frequency domain using learnable complex-valued filters for channel expansion, zero-padding to restore temporal length (which implicitly acts as a low-pass filter), and Softmax-weighted aggregation of all scale features. The model is trained with a frequency-domain MAE loss (inspired by FreDF) that reduces gradient bias from temporal autocorrelation, and stacks E encoder blocks with residual connections.

## Results

On the primary long-term forecasting benchmark (L=96, averaged over horizons 96/192/336/720), DMANet achieves the best MSE and MAE across all seven datasets: ETTh1 (0.428/0.429), ETTh2 (0.361/0.388), ETTm1 (0.373/0.385), ETTm2 (0.268/0.310), Weather (0.236/0.262), Electricity (0.170/0.264), and Solar-Energy (0.227/0.249). With an extended 720-step lookback, DMANet again leads on ETTm1 (0.338/0.369), ETTm2 (0.248/0.307), Weather (0.218/0.252), and Electricity (0.154/0.252). On short-term forecasting, DMANet achieves the best MSE on 9 of 10 datasets, with the closest margin on Website (0.137 vs. Fredformer's 0.135).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DMANet outperforms all 17 baselines on the primary L=96 long-term forecasting task (Table 1), with the largest margins over FITS (e.g., 0.447 vs. 0.428 MSE on ETTh1) and FreTS (0.488 vs. 0.428 on ETTh1). Against the closest competitors—iTransformer, TimeMixer, and TimeXer—DMANet leads by 0.009–0.026 MSE across datasets, with the gap most pronounced on ETTh2 (0.361 vs. 0.364 for TimeMixer) and ETTm2 (0.268 vs. 0.274 for TimeXer). On the extended L=720 setting (Table 2), DMANet similarly dominates, beating ModernTCN (0.351 vs. 0.338 MSE on ETTm1) and TVNet (0.348 vs. 0.338) by clear margins. In short-term forecasting (Table 3), DMANet wins on 9 of 10 datasets; the sole exception is Website, where Fredformer edges ahead on MSE (0.135 vs. 0.137) though DMANet still leads on MAE (0.252 vs. 0.254). Frequency-domain baselines (FilterNet, FreDF, Fredformer) are the strongest challengers, typically within 0.005–0.015 MSE, while MLP-based (DLinear, SOFTS) and Transformer-based (iTransformer, TimeXer) methods trail by larger margins. The ablation in Table 5 confirms that both the Pre-Sampling filter and Post-Sampling interpolation contribute independently, with removing either degrading performance (e.g., Unemp MSE rises from 0.064 to 0.075 or 0.069 respectively).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by identifying spectral aliasing as a previously underappreciated failure mode in multiscale time series decomposition, where prior methods (TimeMixer's average pooling, TimeStacker's patch stacking) perform downsampling without any anti-aliasing protection. It repurposes classical signal processing principles—Nyquist criterion, low-pass pre-filtering, and zero-padding interpolation—from computer vision (Grabinski et al., Chen et al.) into a CNN-based time series architecture, introducing the Equivalent Sampling Rate (ESR) as a principled way to compute the appropriate cutoff frequency for strided convolutions with channel expansion. The Decomposition-Prevention-Fusion paradigm positions DMANet as a bridge between the multiscale decomposition literature (TimeMixer, MICN) and the frequency-domain forecasting literature (FilterNet, FITS, FreDF), explicitly addressing the information fidelity gap that neither family had previously tackled.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ESR-based anti-aliasing filter is demonstrated to be a plug-and-play module that improves existing downsampling-based models (TimeMixer, MICN), suggesting broad applicability beyond DMANet itself. However, the paper acknowledges that on high-dimensional datasets (e.g., ECL with 321 channels), convolutional dependency modeling remains inferior to channel-wise self-attention (iTransformer), leaving open whether the anti-aliasing principle can be integrated into attention-based multiscale architectures. The modest but consistent performance gains over strong baselines, combined with the theoretical grounding via Parseval's theorem and the spectral injection attack validation, make this a credible but incremental step toward more physically-grounded time series architectures rather than a paradigm shift.
