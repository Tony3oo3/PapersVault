## Problem

Existing methods for learning process reward models (PRMs) for LLM reasoning either require strong assumptions—such as token-level annotations, access to the expert policy, or specific reward function formats—or suffer from intrinsic limitations like entropy collapse. No prior framework learns effective PRMs from expert trajectories alone without these assumptions, limiting generalizability and practical applicability.

## Input / Output

The method consumes expert trajectories (4 per problem, generated by Claude-3.7-Sonnet or DeepSeek-R1) without any token-level reward labels, preference annotations, or access to the expert policy itself. It produces two outputs: a token-level PRM (a separate model initialized from the same base LLM with an additional scoring head) that assigns a scalar reward to each token in a reasoning trajectory, and a trained policy model that generates reasoning trajectories. The PRM is reusable as a standalone module for test-time training, test-time scaling (selecting among candidate rollouts), and providing early reward signals on hard problems where outcome rewards are sparse.

## Method

rePIRL models multi-step LLM reasoning as a token-level MDP and applies a classical inverse RL formulation: a hidden variable o_t indicates whether each token comes from the expert, with an energy-based likelihood p(o_t|s_t,a_t) ∝ exp(r_φ(s_t,a_t)). The key technical challenge is that the partition function is intractable for LLMs' enormous state and action spaces; rePIRL resolves this by sampling from the current policy π_θ and applying importance sampling with weights w = exp(r_φ(τ))/π_θ(τ), computed in log-space for numerical stability. Training alternates between updating the PRM (via an MLE loss with importance-weighted negative terms) and the policy (via maximum entropy RL solved by RLOO, PPO, or GRPO), with the policy reward combining outcome and process rewards. To mitigate reward hacking, the PRM loss uses average rather than cumulative process reward, and corrected policy trajectories are injected as pseudo-expert data. Theoretically, the paper proves that DPO, DQO, PRIME, and MCTS-based methods are special cases of this framework under additional assumptions, establishing rePIRL as the most general PRM learning method with minimal required assumptions.

## Results

rePIRL achieves state-of-the-art performance among 3B- and 4B-scale reasoning LLMs on zero-shot pass@1 (greedy decoding). With Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, it reaches 33.5% average math accuracy and 27.7% average coding accuracy; with Qwen3-4B-Base, it reaches 41.6% math and 39.8% coding. Compared to the strongest baseline (RLOO with outcome rewards), rePIRL gains +2.8%/+3.6% (math/coding) on Qwen2.5-3B and +3.2%/+4.3% on Qwen3-4B. On non-greedy pass@1 (T=0.6, k=16), rePIRL still leads RLOO by 0.7% (Qwen2.5-3B) and PRIME by 1.0% (Qwen3-4B) on math benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

rePIRL outperforms all seven baselines across both model scales and both domains. Against the strongest online baseline PRIME, rePIRL gains +3.6% math and +2.5% coding on Qwen2.5-3B, and +2.3% math and +8.7% coding on Qwen3-4B (where PRIME's coding performance drops to 31.1% vs. rePIRL's 39.8%). Against MCTS (Math-Shepherd), rePIRL leads by +2.8% math / +2.0% coding (3B) and +5.5% math / +3.4% coding (4B). Against the outcome-reward-only RLOO baseline, rePIRL improves by +1.8% math / +3.6% coding (3B) and +3.2% math / +4.3% coding (4B), confirming that the learned process rewards provide informative intermediate guidance beyond final-answer correctness. Offline methods (BC, KTO, SFT+RLOO) consistently underperform online methods, with BC trailing rePIRL by 12.9% math on Qwen2.5-3B. On the hardest benchmark (AIME-2024), rePIRL achieves 16.0% with Qwen3-4B-Base, nearly doubling the 6.0–10.0% range of most baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes classical maximum-entropy inverse RL (Ziebart et al., 2008) and the joint policy-reward training approach from Guided Cost Learning (Finn et al., 2016b) to the token-level MDP of LLM reasoning, using importance sampling to avoid the intractable partition function. It departs from offline PRM methods (supervised step-level labels, MCTS-based Math-Shepherd) by requiring only expert trajectories without token-level annotations or access to the expert policy, and from online methods (PRIME, intrinsic-reward approaches) by avoiding strong reward-function assumptions and entropy collapse. The paper's key conceptual contribution is showing that DPO, DQO, PRIME, and Math-Shepherd are all special cases of its unified IRL framework under additional assumptions, positioning rePIRL as the minimal-assumption generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified framework provides a clearer theoretical map of the PRM learning landscape, helping practitioners understand which assumptions each method requires and when one is preferable to another. The learned PRM's demonstrated utility in test-time training (matching outcome-reward methods without verifiable rewards), test-time scaling (outperforming majority voting), and early signal on hard problems makes it a practically reusable component beyond its training role. Open questions include whether the approach scales to agentic/multi-turn tasks, how to optimally combine IRL-derived PRMs with intrinsic or shaped rewards, and whether the modest 2–4% gains over strong RLOO/PRIME baselines persist at larger model scales.
