## Problem

Language modeling is inherently ill-posed: given an ambiguous context, the conditional output distribution is multi-modal, yet standard maximum-likelihood training collapses to the aggregate distribution and cannot capture individual modes. Existing diversity techniques (diverse beam search, sampling, test-time augmentation) artificially inject variation at inference time rather than learning the underlying multi-modal structure during training. The paper asks how to efficiently fine-tune a large language model so that it natively produces diverse, plausible continuations reflecting the true ambiguity of the input.

## Input / Output

The method takes an audio clip (resampled to 16 kHz, converted to a 128-channel mel-spectrogram with 25 ms window / 10 ms hop, up to 30 s for Clotho or 10 s for AudioCaps), an RGB image (resized to 336×336 with CLIP normalization), or a source-language sentence as context. It produces K diverse text outputs (captions or translations) in a single forward pass, one per LoRA hypothesis, rather than requiring K separate decoding runs. At inference, each hypothesis can additionally be combined with beam search or diverse beam search at a reduced beam width (B/K per hypothesis) to maintain a fixed computational budget.

## Method

LoRA-MCL replaces the single LoRA adapter in a frozen transformer with K parallel LoRA adapters (pairs of rank-r matrices A_k, B_k at each target layer), each representing one hypothesis over the output distribution. Training uses a winner-takes-all objective: for each training sample, the likelihood is computed under all K hypotheses, and the gradient is weighted by coefficients q_k that assign the largest share (1−ε) to the best-performing hypothesis while giving a small nonzero share (ε/(K−1)) to the rest, preventing collapse. An alternative annealed schedule replaces the fixed ε with a temperature τ that decays over training, transitioning from uniform weighting to greedy WTA. All K hypotheses are evaluated in a single batched forward pass by duplicating the input K times along the batch dimension and using a grouped 1D convolution (K groups) so each hypothesis applies its own LoRA weights while sharing the frozen base model. Theoretically, under a mixture-of-distributions assumption, the WTA loss equals the conditional entropy H(x|c,z) rather than the marginal H(x|c), and the paper validates on mixtures of Markov chains that MLE converges to the weighted-average transition matrix while LoRA-MCL recovers the individual component matrices.

## Results

On audio captioning, LoRA-MCL (K=5) reduces test NLL from 2.203 (LoRA-MLE, r=8) to 1.999 on AudioCaps and from 2.812 to 2.643 on Clotho-V2, with further gains at K=7 (1.932 / 2.612). On TextCaps image captioning (K=3), LoRA-MCL with a single beam achieves SPIDEr 0.955 and CIDErD 1.674, outperforming LoRA-MLE with diverse beam search (SPIDEr 0.915, CIDErD 1.601) and LoRA-MoE (SPIDEr 0.926, CIDErD 1.622) while using fewer forward passes. In the bilingual specialization experiment, LoRA-MCL achieves SPIDEr 0.464 on French captions (vs. 0.411 for LoRA-MLE) with dramatically lower mBLEU-4 (0.027 vs. 0.138), confirming hypothesis-level mode separation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LoRA-MoE (the Mixture-of-LoRA-Experts baseline drawing on Muqeeth et al. 2024, Wu et al. 2024, and Li et al. 2024), LoRA-MCL achieves higher SPIDEr (0.955 vs. 0.926) and CIDErD (1.674 vs. 1.622) on TextCaps while requiring only 1 beam per hypothesis versus 3 beams for LoRA-MoE, i.e., fewer total forward passes. Against LoRA-MLE with diverse beam search (the strongest single-model baseline), LoRA-MCL wins on SPIDEr (0.955 vs. 0.915) and CIDErD (1.674 vs. 1.601) at equal or lower computational cost, though LoRA-MLE with DBS produces slightly more diverse outputs in some settings (mBLEU-4 0.416 vs. 0.520 for greedy LoRA-MCL). On audio captioning (Fig. 3), LoRA-MCL occupies the top-left Pareto frontier in the SPIDEr-vs-mBLEU-4 plane, outperforming both LoRA-MLE and LoRA-MoE across all tested beam sizes and diversity penalties. In the controlled bilingual experiment, LoRA-MCL clearly separates the two language modes (89% French / 97% English head assignment), whereas LoRA-MLE produces a weighted average biased toward English and suffers repetition loops on French outputs. On machine translation (newstest2014, K=3), LoRA-MCL achieves a favorable LoOBLEU/Pairwise-BLEU balance that neither LoRA-MLE (r=16 or r=48) with beam search nor diverse beam search can match at the same number of generated candidates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the Multiple Choice Learning (MCL) paradigm—originally designed for structured prediction with multiple output heads—into the language-model fine-tuning setting by replacing heads with multiple LoRA adapters, a substitution motivated by the impracticality of duplicating large lm_head layers in modern LLMs. It builds directly on LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) for parameter efficiency, on the original WTA loss and its Relaxed-WTA variant (Rupprecht et al., 2017) for competitive specialization, and on Annealed MCL (Perera et al., 2024) for a temperature-based collapse-prevention schedule. Conceptually, the paper departs from Mixture-of-Experts approaches (LoRA-MoE) by using a hard, competitive assignment rather than soft routing, and from inference-time diversity methods (Diverse Beam Search, Test-Time Augmentation) by learning diversity into the weights rather than injecting it at decode time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LoRA-MCL offers a drop-in fine-tuning recipe that produces K diverse, plausible outputs in a single forward pass without architectural changes, which is practically valuable for captioning and translation pipelines where a single MLE-trained model averages over modes. The theoretical analysis (Proposition 1) provides clean lower/upper bounds on the WTA loss in terms of conditional entropy, giving practitioners a principled understanding of what the method can and cannot achieve. Open questions remain: the relaxation parameters (ε or temperature schedule) require manual tuning and may interact with data distribution; the method has not been tested at pretraining scale; and interpreting what each hypothesis actually captures beyond surface-level specialization (e.g., language in the bilingual experiment) is left for future work.
