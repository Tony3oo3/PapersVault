## Problem

Rubric-based LLM-as-a-judge benchmarks (e.g., HealthBench, WildBench) are widely used for high-stakes evaluation but have never been systematically tested for sensitivity to simple semantic corruptions of model responses. Existing robustness work targets adversarial prompt attacks or syntactic perturbations, leaving open whether these judges can even detect basic meaning-level failures such as negated claims, deleted content, or irrelevant padding in the responses they score.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264058/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a model-generated response to a benchmark task and applies one of three LLM-executed perturbations (irrelevant sentence addition, stochastic deletion, or semantic negation) at a controlled intensity α ∈ {0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.0} representing the fraction of sentences affected. The perturbed response is scored by the original benchmark's rubric and judge model, and the output is a normalized robustness curve plus three summary statistics: area under the curve, fitted regression slope, and the perturbation threshold (α̃₂₅) at which the score drops by 25% from baseline.

## Method

RUBRICROBUSTNESS applies three non-adversarial semantic perturbations directly to model responses (not prompts), executed by a separate LLM (Gemini 3 Flash) to ensure natural, meaning-level transformations rather than token-level edits. For each perturbation type and intensity level, the perturbed response is scored under the original benchmark protocol, and scores are min-max normalized to enable cross-benchmark comparison. Robustness is quantified via robustness curves, AUC, least-squares regression slope, and a 25%-drop threshold. To disentangle rubric coverage gaps from judge behavior, the paper further conducts a rubric audit (classifying criteria that penalize irrelevant content) and a remediation experiment appending three targeted checklist items, showing that the addition insensitivity is substantially driven by missing negative criteria in the rubrics rather than judge failure alone.

## Results

Across both HealthBench and WildBench, benchmark scores decrease monotonically with perturbation intensity, with linear fits achieving R² > 90% in most settings. Negation induces the steepest degradation (slopes of −0.3758 on HealthBench and −0.5523 on WildBench for GPT-5.2), while Addition is the least penalized perturbation (slopes of only −0.1087 on HealthBench and −0.2795 on WildBench), often requiring over 35% of sentences perturbed before a 25% score drop. The remediation experiment shows that appending three targeted checklist items increases Addition sensitivity by 147% (2.5×) on HealthBench and 16% (1.2×) on WildBench, confirming a rubric-coverage gap rather than pure judge failure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it contrasts two rubric-based benchmarks (HealthBench vs. WildBench) and two generator models (GPT-5.2 vs. Claude Opus 4.5) under identical perturbation protocols. Both benchmarks and both models exhibit the same qualitative ordering: Negation is the most damaging perturbation, while the weakest perturbation swaps between benchmarks (Addition is weakest on HealthBench with slope −0.1087; Deletion is weakest on WildBench with α̃₂₅ = 0.57). WildBench shows higher starting scores and AUCs than HealthBench but is more sensitive to Negation (slope ≈ −0.55 vs. −0.38), suggesting its shorter, less detailed rubric criteria (122 vs. 272 characters average) make the judge more attuned to meaning inversion. The rubric coverage audit reveals that only 6.3% of HealthBench and 3.9% of WildBench criteria explicitly penalize irrelevant content, directly explaining the Addition vulnerability. The remediation experiment confirms this: appending three targeted negative criteria increases the fitted degradation slope by 147% for Addition on HealthBench while leaving clean-response scores essentially unchanged, indicating the insensitivity stems from rubric design rather than judge incompetence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the perturbation-analysis paradigm from vision robustness (e.g., ImageNet-C) and the negation-blindness literature (Nadeem et al., 2024; Alhamoud et al., 2025) into a new setting: auditing rubric-based LLM-as-a-judge benchmarks. It departs from prior judge-robustness work (RobustJudge, Sage, adversarial-suffix studies) in three deliberate ways: targeting outputs rather than inputs, using simple non-adversarial semantic perturbations rather than optimized worst-case attacks, and focusing on rubric-based evaluation specifically rather than generic pairwise or score-based judging. The conceptual lineage is thus a cross-pollination of sanity-check perturbation methodology with the LLM-as-a-judge evaluation stack, rather than an extension of any single prior method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that widely adopted rubric benchmarks (HealthBench, WildBench) are substantially under-responsive to irrelevant padding—requiring >35% of sentences corrupted before a 25% score drop—exposes a practical vulnerability for anyone relying on these benchmarks for model selection or safety auditing. The remediation experiment showing that three targeted checklist items can multiply addition sensitivity by 2.5× offers an immediately actionable fix, but the scope is limited to two benchmarks, two generator models, and single-perturbation settings. Open questions include whether the vulnerabilities generalize to agentic and deep-research rubric benchmarks, whether smaller or open-source judges exhibit the same blind spots, and whether sensitivity to these simple semantic perturbations correlates with vulnerability to adversarial prompt attacks.
