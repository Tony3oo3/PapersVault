## Problem

E-commerce platforms rely on crowdsourced 17-juror panels to adjudicate transaction disputes, but this process is slow and unscalable. Unlike formal legal judgment, e-commerce dispute verdicts (EDV) require grounding pivotal clues from redundant, multi-round, multimodal evidence under flexible platform-specific conventions rather than rigid legal codes. Existing LLMs, MLLMs, and court simulators fail in this setting: MLLMs process ultra-long contexts passively in one shot and cannot perceive fine-grained visual clues, while court simulators lack explicit verdict guidelines and inherit model biases toward one disputant.

## Input / Output

Each dispute case D comprises transaction metadata (product info, chat histories) plus multi-round multimodal evidence from both disputants: textual claims, images (averaging 14 per case), and videos (averaging 0.9 per case, processed via uniform sampling capped at 30 frames). The system outputs a binary verdict (buyer or seller wins) determined by majority vote across 17 simulated jurors, along with a collective verdict summary that serves as an interpretable justification. The jury simulation runs up to T=3 discussion rounds with early stopping when collective consensus exceeds δ=0.8.

## Method

CyberJurors operates at two levels. At the individual level, IV-CoT decomposes each juror's reasoning into four stages: (I) Focus Extraction to identify the dispute focus from metadata and text claims; (II) a 'Select-Perceive' iterative Clues Grounding strategy where the juror actively selects the most relevant piece of evidence from redundant submissions, then performs fine-grained visual perception to ground pivotal clues (up to T_max=3 iterations per disputant); (III) Adversarial Analysis to uncover causal logic between grounded clues and the dispute focus and identify contradictions; and (IV) Final Verdict. At the collective level, JCV simulates 17 heterogeneous jurors connected in a directed social network G over T=3 rounds, where each juror receives neighbor verdicts and a collective summary to mitigate individual biases. Inspired by 'Stare Decisis,' a Verdict Precedent Base is constructed by distilling training-set verdicts into explicit guidelines via LLM reflection, indexed by Longformer-base-4096 embeddings; for each case, semantically relevant precedents are retrieved and top-K norms matching each juror's persona are injected as memory to provide consistent, interpretable decision guidelines.

## Results

On VerdictBench (6,000 real-world e-commerce dispute cases), CyberJurors achieves 72.92% accuracy, 72.58% Weighted F1, and 70.37% Macro F1, outperforming the strongest single-model baseline (Gemini-3-Pro at 63.54% Acc.) by 9.38% and the best court simulator (AgentCourt at 66.73% Acc.) by 6.19%. CyberJurors also achieves the lowest MAE (4.73) and RMSE (6.37) among all methods that simulate 17-juror voting, indicating superior alignment with real-world jury vote distributions. Notably, MLLMs as a group (avg. 52.98% Acc.) underperform text-only LLMs (avg. 55.78% Acc.), highlighting the difficulty of fine-grained multimodal clue perception in redundant evidence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CyberJurors (72.92% Acc.) outperforms all six baseline categories. Against the strongest closed-source LLM (GPT-5.2-Chat, 63.44%), it gains 9.48% in accuracy; against the strongest MLLM (Gemini-3-Pro, 63.54%), it gains 9.38%. Against the best court simulator (AgentCourt, 66.73%), it gains 6.19% in accuracy while consuming fewer tokens (62.3M vs. 75.3M). ChatEval (65.89%) is outperformed by 7.03% in accuracy. A striking finding is that all MLLM baselines (ranging 40.90%–63.54%) underperform the best text-only LLMs on average, confirming that passive one-shot multimodal inference fails to extract fine-grained pivotal clues from redundant evidence. CyberJurors is the only method evaluated on MAE/RMSE (4.73/6.37), demonstrating its unique ability to replicate the 17-juror vote distribution, a capability absent in all single-model and text-only baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

CyberJurors sits at the intersection of multi-agent debate (ChatEval, AgentCourt), structured Chain-of-Thought reasoning (Wei et al., 2022; Fei et al., 2024a), and social network simulation (Mou et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026), but departs from all of these in a critical way: it replaces passive one-shot multimodal comprehension with an active 'select-perceive' iterative grounding loop and introduces a Verdict Precedent Base inspired by the legal doctrine of Stare Decisis to regulate flexible, platform-specific transaction rules. Unlike prior court simulators that operate on structured legal documents, CyberJurors targets redundant, multi-round, multimodal evidence chains where pivotal clues are buried in noise, and unlike prior e-commerce dispute work (ECOD), it models the full 17-juror voting dynamics rather than a single binary classification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine scalability bottleneck in e-commerce platforms where crowdsourced juries take days per dispute, and VerdictBench (6,000 multimodal, multi-round cases) is the first benchmark to capture this setting, opening a new evaluation axis for multimodal evidence reasoning and multi-agent simulation. The counterintuitive finding that MLLMs underperform text-only LLMs on this task challenges the assumption that adding visual modality automatically improves reasoning over multimodal evidence. Key open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond a single base model (all jurors use Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite), whether optimal jury size exists, and whether SFT on juror rationales can eliminate the persistent pro-consumer bias observed in base models.
