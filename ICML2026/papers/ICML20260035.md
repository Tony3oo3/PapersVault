## Problem

Existing evaluations of posterior-based link-stealing attacks on GNNs rely on average-case metrics (AUC) that obscure practical privacy risk, since an adversary benefits most from reliably inferring a small subset of sensitive links at very low false-positive rates. Moreover, prior work identifies a systematic asymmetry where intra-class links are far harder to exploit than inter-class ones, but the underlying geometric mechanism remains unexplained and unaddressed. This paper reframes link privacy as a tail-risk problem and asks whether the intra-class bottleneck can be deliberately corrected to reveal previously hidden leakage.

## Input / Output

The attacker operates under a strict black-box threat model, querying the victim GNN only for individual node posterior probability vectors (probability distributions over the label space) and predicted class labels. Node pairs are partitioned into intra-class and inter-class strata based on predicted labels; the PCW transformation is applied exclusively to intra-class pairs before distance computation, while inter-class pairs are scored directly. The output is a pairwise distance score that feeds a binary link/non-link decision via a threshold, with performance evaluated both as AUC (average-case) and TPR at FPR = 10⁻³ (tail-risk).

## Method

The paper identifies that intra-class posterior differences suffer from a variance-discriminability misalignment: a small number of high-variance eigenmodes dominate the geometry of posterior differences but contribute little to separating links from non-links, while discriminative structure is spread across lower-variance directions. To correct this, the authors propose Per-Class Whitening (PCW): for each predicted class c, posteriors are mapped into a variance-stabilized power space (p^τ with τ=1/2), a class-conditional covariance is estimated (using Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage), and a whitening operator W_c = U_c diag(γ̃₁,…,γ̃_K) U_cᵀ with inverse square-root eigenvalues is applied to recondition each endpoint before distance computation. The per-class design is essential because principal-angle analysis shows each class induces a distinct anisotropy pattern, so a single global transform would blur class-specific discriminative subspaces. Theoretical results (Theorems 4.4 and 4.6) formalize why intra-class distances collapse toward zero (shared argmax cancels dominant coordinates) while inter-class distances enjoy a deterministic margin floor, explaining the asymmetry and motivating why only intra-class pairs require reconditioning.

## Results

On the GCN model, PCW consistently improves intra-class link-stealing AUC across all six datasets and eight distance metrics. Headline gains include correlation AUC rising from 0.747 to 0.862 on Cora and from 0.712 to 0.832 on CS, while Canberra AUC jumps from 0.554 to 0.755 on Cora. At the strict FPR=10⁻³ operating point, TPR improvements are substantial: Canberra TPR on Cora increases from 10.9% to 19.3%, and on the GAT model, correlation TPR on Cora nearly triples from 17.9% to 47.9%. On the large-scale OGBN-Products dataset (12.37M pairs), PCW with GCN and cosine distance lifts AUC from 0.573 to 0.777 and TPR@0.1% from 2.7% to 16.1%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares PCW against two prior link-stealing baselines: the generic posterior-distance attack of He et al. (USENIX Security 2021) and the inter/intra-class stratified attack of Zhang et al. (ICML 2023). PCW dominates both baselines on intra-class pairs across all six datasets and all three GNN architectures (GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE). Against the generic attack (He et al.), the improvement is most dramatic on metrics that were previously weakest: Canberra AUC on Cora rises from 0.554 to 0.755 (+20 points), and on the GAT model, correlation TPR on Cora jumps from 17.9% to 47.9%. Against the class-stratified baseline (Zhang et al.), which already separates inter- and intra-class pairs, PCW still yields consistent AUC gains of 5–20 points on intra-class subsets (e.g., 0.747→0.862 correlation AUC on Cora/GCN; 0.761→0.920 on Cora/GAT). The one notable exception is PubMed, where extremely peaked posteriors and a concentrated negative tail limit TPR gains at FPR=10⁻³ despite AUC improvements, a limitation the authors diagnose through posterior-entropy and tail-spread analysis. Overall, PCW achieves up to 2× higher intra-class TPR than the generic attack without degrading inter-class performance, confirming that the geometric bottleneck identified by Zhang et al. is correctable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the posterior-based link-stealing attack line initiated by He et al. (USENIX Security 2021) and refined by Zhang et al. (ICML 2023), which identified the inter-/intra-class vulnerability asymmetry. It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) it reframes evaluation from average-case AUC to worst-case tail reliability (TPR at FPR = 10⁻³), arguing that privacy is fundamentally a tail-risk property, and (2) it provides a geometric diagnosis of the intra-class bottleneck—showing that posterior covariance collapses along low-variance directions that nonetheless carry discriminative signal—and proposes per-class covariance whitening (PCW) as a corrective. Unlike prior attacks that treat distance metric choice as the primary lever, this work shows that even probability-aware divergences (Bhattacharyya, Fisher-Rao) fail to resolve the collapse, pointing to anisotropy rather than metric inadequacy as the root cause.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The tail-risk reframing has practical implications for GNN deployment: if even 1–5% of edges can be inferred at one false alarm per thousand queries, current average-case evaluations materially understate the privacy exposure of deployed models. The geometric diagnosis (variance-discriminability misalignment) suggests that defenses should target the anisotropic structure of posterior outputs rather than simply adding noise, and the honest negative result on PubMed—where AUC gains do not translate to TPR at extreme low FPR due to concentrated negative tails—highlights that attack exploitability is dataset-geometry-dependent. Open questions include whether the geometric collapse is intrinsic to message passing or an artifact of specific architectures, whether per-class whitening generalizes to other structural partitions (e.g., community-based), and what minimal perturbations can restore intra-class separability without degrading model utility.
