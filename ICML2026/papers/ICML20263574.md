## Problem

Most multi-agent LLM systems enforce coordination through fixed roles, workflows, or aggregation rules, leaving open how well self-organizing teams perform when coordination must emerge through open deliberation. Drawing on organizational psychology, this paper asks whether heterogeneous LLM teams can achieve 'strong synergy'—matching or exceeding their best individual member—and finds they consistently fail to do so, underperforming their expert by 6.3%–41.1% even when explicitly told who the expert is, in stark contrast to human teams that reliably match expert performance under the same conditions.

## Input / Output

The system takes a team of 4 heterogeneous frontier LLM agents, a task prompt (either a ranking task from organizational psychology or a question from a frontier ML benchmark), and optionally a GEPA-optimized expert-identification prompt. Agents first produce individual answers in isolation, then engage in 4 rounds of open deliberation with randomized speaking order, after which the team's final answer is the majority vote of all post-discussion responses. Outputs include the team answer, a relative synergy gap measured against the best individual member (or the 'At Least One Correct' upper bound for ML benchmarks), and per-turn behavioral codes (Epistemic Deference, Integrative Compromise, Strategic Persistence, Epistemic Flexibility) extracted from conversation transcripts.

## Method

The paper runs a controlled empirical study across two complementary settings: classical human psychology ranking tasks (NASA Moon Survival, Lost at Sea, Student Body President) where expertise is artificially concentrated or distributed across agents, and five frontier ML benchmarks (MMLU Pro, GPQA Diamond, HLE, MATH-500, SimpleQA) where expertise is naturally distributed per-problem across heterogeneous models. A standardized 4-phase protocol (individual opinion → randomized initialization → 4 discussion rounds → majority-vote final answer) is held constant, while four information conditions (No Info, Expert Not Mentioned, Reveal Expert, Best Individual) isolate identification versus leveraging gaps. The Reveal Expert prompt is aggressively optimized via GEPA to rule out prompt-phrasing artifacts. Conversational transcripts are coded with Gemini 3.0 Pro (validated at 94% agreement by human annotators) into role-specific behavioral categories, and Pearson correlations link compromise frequency to the synergy gap. Additional ablations vary team size (2/4/8) to demonstrate expertise dilution and introduce an adversarial agent to test whether the same consensus-seeking mechanism that dilutes expertise also provides robustness to sabotage.

## Results

Across five frontier ML benchmarks, self-organizing LLM teams consistently fail to achieve strong synergy, underperforming the At Least One Correct (ALOC) upper bound by 6.3% (MMLU Pro) to 41.1% (HLE Text-Only) relative synergy gap. Even when explicitly told which agent is the expert (Reveal Expert condition), teams still fall short of ALOC on every benchmark (e.g., 86.0% vs. 91.8% on MMLU Pro; 36.0% vs. 47.5% on HLE). On human psychology tasks, relative synergy gaps range from 42.1% to 113.4%, and expertise dilution worsens significantly with team size (all p < 0.05). Conversational analysis reveals that integrative compromise (averaging expert and non-expert views) correlates positively with the synergy gap (r = 0.55–0.69, p < 0.001), while epistemic deference correlates negatively (r = −0.44 to −0.68, p < 0.05).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is against the At Least One Correct (ALOC) upper bound, which represents perfect per-problem expert identification and leveraging. No coordination protocol—whether the authors' team discussion, Du et al.'s (2024) multi-agent debate, an opt-out variant, or simple chain-of-thought with majority vote—reaches ALOC on any of the five ML benchmarks. The debate protocol from Du et al. performs comparably to the team discussion under Reveal Expert (e.g., 83.0% vs. 83.0% on GPQA Diamond; 75.0% vs. 75.0% on MATH-500), confirming the expertise-leveraging failure is not an artifact of a specific interaction structure. On HLE Text-Only, the gap is most severe: the best protocol (Team Reveal Expert) achieves 36.0% versus the 47.5% ALOC, a 24% absolute shortfall. On human psychology tasks, the comparison is against the single expert's individual performance; teams underperform by 42–113% relative error even with aggressively GEPA-optimized prompts instructing deference, whereas human teams in the organizational psychology literature reliably match expert performance when expertise is revealed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant multi-agent LLM literature (MetaGPT, GPTSwarm, AFlow, AgentNet, Mixture-of-Agents), which typically uses homogeneous model copies, pre-specified roles, and fixed communication topologies, by studying genuinely heterogeneous frontier models in unconstrained, self-organizing deliberation with no external coordination structure. It repurposes the 'strong synergy' criterion from organizational psychology (Bonner et al., 2002; Yetton & Bottger, 1982)—whether a team matches or exceeds its best member—as the evaluation metric, rather than comparing team output to external benchmarks. The conversational analysis draws on social epistemology (the preemption thesis of Raz, 1986; Zagzebski, 2012) and negotiation theory to code behavioral patterns, and the GEPA prompt optimizer is used as a tool to rule out prompt-phrasing artifacts. The paper is also closely related to concurrent findings on the 'collaboration gap' (Davidson et al., 2025) and the limited value of debate over voting (Choi et al., 2025), but extends these by isolating the specific mechanism (integrative compromise) and the expertise-dilution-with-team-size effect.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that self-organizing LLM teams consistently underperform their best member by 6.3%–41.1%—even when explicitly told who the expert is and even after aggressive prompt optimization—challenges a core assumption in the multi-agent LLM literature that collaboration and deliberation reliably improve outcomes. The expertise dilution effect (performance worsening with team size) and the trade-off between adversarial robustness and expertise leveraging provide concrete design guidance: practitioners deploying multi-agent teams on expertise-asymmetric tasks should benchmark against their strongest individual model and consider explicit role specification rather than relying on self-organization. Key open questions include whether the consensus-seeking behavior is truly caused by alignment procedures (the paper's attribution is correlational), whether the findings generalize beyond intellective tasks with demonstrably correct answers, and whether training-time interventions can enable context-appropriate expertise deference without sacrificing robustness.
