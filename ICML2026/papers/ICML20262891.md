## Problem

Test-time self-consistency via majority voting over multiple reasoning traces is effective but computationally expensive, and existing budget-allocation methods rely on heuristic confidence signals or external reward models that lack theoretical guarantees. The paper asks: how should a fixed sampling budget be allocated across questions of varying difficulty to maximize the probability that finite-budget majority voting recovers the infinite-budget population majority, without auxiliary supervision?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262891/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of multiple-choice questions, a stochastic LLM that produces i.i.d. reasoning traces per question, and a total trace budget B_total. In the offline setting all questions are known in advance and allocation proceeds sequentially; in the online setting questions arrive one at a time and only a small training set (e.g., 30 questions) is available to estimate the difficulty distribution. The output is an integer per-question trace budget (or a grid-level budget vector in the online case), from which the final majority-vote answer and self-consistency rate are computed. In the online protocol, each incoming question receives exactly 4 warm-up traces to determine its difficulty grid before the full budget is committed in one shot.

## Method

PETS introduces the self-consistency rate — the probability that a finite-budget majority vote matches the infinite-budget population majority — as a principled optimization objective, and connects trajectory allocation to optimal budget allocation in crowdsourcing by treating reasoning traces as noisy worker labels. In the offline setting, allocation is modeled as a finite-horizon Bayesian MDP over posteriors on answer distributions (Dirichlet) and class-conditional confidence means (Gaussian), solved with an Optimistic Knowledge Gradient heuristic that greedily allocates each next trace to the question with the largest expected one-step utility gain. In the online setting, the high-dimensional difficulty parameter is reduced to a 2D Gaussian-probit surrogate Φ(a√B + b), discretized into K difficulty grids; a 4-sample warm-up deterministically assigns each incoming question to a grid, and a provably optimal greedy algorithm (exploiting the diminishing-returns structure of the binary self-consistency curve) solves the resulting constrained integer program to produce the per-grid budget vector, with randomized rounding to handle fractional budget at the boundary.

## Results

On GPQA, PETS-Offline requires only 2,780 traces (Qwen3-4B) to reach perfect self-consistency (1.00), a 75% reduction over uniform allocation (11,013 traces, consistency 0.96). PETS-Online achieves a 55% reduction in the streaming setting (4,662 vs. 9,520 traces). Across all five benchmarks (GPQA, AIME 24/25, HMMT, BRUMO) and five models, PETS consistently attains self-consistency = 1.00 while baselines plateau at 0.87–0.97, and translates these gains into higher accuracy (e.g., 69.7% vs. 68.9% on GPQA with Qwen3-4B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PETS-Offline dominates both baselines across all five benchmarks and all five models: it requires 50–75% fewer traces to reach full self-consistency compared to uniform allocation, and 60–75% fewer compared to the confidence-guided DeepConf method (Fu et al., 2025a). On GPQA with GPT-120B, PETS-Offline needs 2,580 traces versus 10,393 (Uniform) and 8,474 (Conf. guided), while achieving perfect consistency (1.00) where baselines only reach 0.97. In the online streaming setting, PETS-Online similarly outperforms both baselines (e.g., 4,172 vs. 8,109 traces on GPQA/GPT-120B) and closely matches PETS-Oracle, confirming that the 4-sample warm-up difficulty estimator introduces negligible overhead. The one setting where baselines are competitive is AIME 24 with Qwen3-30B, where uniform allocation already reaches consistency 1.00 at 83 traces—nearly matching PETS-Offline's 135 traces—because the question set is small and difficulty variance is low.

## Relation to Prior Work

PETS draws a direct conceptual bridge between test-time self-consistency trajectory allocation and the well-developed crowdsourcing literature, specifically adopting the Optimistic Knowledge Gradient (OKG) Bayesian MDP framework of Chen et al. (2013) for the offline setting. This departs from prior difficulty-adaptive allocation methods like DeepConf (Fu et al., 2025a) and Wang et al. (2025a), which rely on heuristic confidence or LLM-predicted difficulty signals without theoretical guarantees. The paper's key conceptual move is defining the self-consistency rate (agreement with the infinite-budget majority vote) as the optimization target, which makes the allocation problem amenable to the rich existing theory of budgeted labeling and adaptive assignment. For the online setting, the Gaussian-probit surrogate and greedy integer allocation with provable optimality extend the binary-choice analysis to multi-choice via a Berry-Esseen-based approximation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PETS provides the first theoretically grounded framework for sample-efficient self-consistency, showing that 75% (offline) and 55% (online) budget reductions are achievable on GPQA while maintaining full self-consistency. The self-consistency rate is a useful new metric that decouples allocation quality from ground-truth accuracy, enabling analysis even when the population majority is wrong. A key limitation the paper identifies is that allocation-only approaches cannot help when the population majority is systematically incorrect—additional sampling merely confirms the wrong answer. An important open direction is training models to predict the difficulty parameter θ directly from the question text prior to generation, which would eliminate the warm-up sampling overhead in the online setting.
