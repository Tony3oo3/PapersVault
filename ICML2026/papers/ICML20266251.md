## Problem

Interactive learning-from-demonstration methods can identify *when* to request new demonstrations using learner-side signals (uncertainty, novelty), but they leave novice teachers without an interpretable account of *what* to demonstrate next. Under fixed demonstration budgets, this communicative gap means novices default to learner-agnostic coverage, accumulating redundant data while the policy's failure modes persist. The paper asks: can learner weaknesses be converted into a compact, teacher-facing representation that directly indicates which behaviours to demonstrate?

## Input / Output

The method consumes task-specific robot state observations (2–8 dimensional vectors depending on the task), fixed-length demonstration trajectories from novice teachers, and the current diffusion-policy learner's on-policy rollout states. It produces a region map where each region is a ball in a per-round z-score-normalised embedding space, anchored at a demonstrated observation with a KNN-adaptive radius and a soft-max difficulty score; this map is rendered as a visual overlay of coloured circles on the workspace for the teacher, and simultaneously generates per-step training weights (ranging from 1 upward, clipped at w_max) that reweight the imitation loss. The final output is an updated diffusion policy trained with these difficulty-aware weights.

## Method

CLASP constructs a region-level interface by combining demonstrated observations (X_H) and on-policy rollout states (X_M) into an explored set U_k, then defines regions anchored at demonstrated states with radii set to the 95th-percentile distance of 30 nearest neighbours in a per-round normalised embedding. Region difficulty is computed as a size-normalised log-sum-exp (soft maximum) of per-step imitation losses over the demonstrated steps falling within each region, balancing sensitivity to rare errors against robustness to single noisy labels. CLASP-Teach selects a compact set of L representative regions via greedy submodular maximisation of a teaching gain (difficulty × marginal coverage of uncovered demonstrated steps), presenting them as a visual overlay that guides where the teacher should demonstrate next. CLASP-Train assigns each demonstrated step the difficulty of its nearest anchor region and uses this as a multiplicative weight in the per-sample imitation loss, so the same region map simultaneously directs data collection and prioritises optimisation. The two components are coupled: regions flagged as difficult receive both new demonstrations and greater training emphasis within a fixed budget.

## Results

Under a fixed demonstration budget of B=20 trajectories, CLASP-Full achieves the highest robust success rate across all four D3IL manipulation tasks: 0.88 (Avoiding), 0.72 (Pushing), 0.78 (Pick-and-Place), and 0.54 (Alignment), outperforming the strongest non-CLASP baseline (Curriculum) by 14–52% relative. In-distribution, CLASP-Full reaches 0.94 success on Avoiding and 0.66 on Alignment at B=20, with the gap over ASkDAgger most pronounced on contact-sensitive tasks (e.g., 0.66 vs. 0.22 on Alignment under shift). CLASP also preserves behavioural diversity, achieving the highest strategy balance score r on both Avoiding and Pushing, and on real-robot (Sawyer) tasks it improves success by up to 16 percentage points over No-Guidance at B=20 (0.92 vs. 0.76 on Avoiding).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLASP-Full outperforms all four baselines across every task under distribution shift. Against ASkDAgger (uncertainty-gated querying, Luijkx et al. 2025), CLASP gains 6–144% relative robust success, with the largest gap on Alignment (0.54 vs. 0.22) where contact-sensitive failures are concentrated. Against the easy-to-hard Curriculum baseline (Hou et al. 2025a), CLASP leads by 14–50% (e.g., 0.88 vs. 0.74 on Avoiding, 0.54 vs. 0.36 on Alignment), confirming that policy-relative difficulty targeting beats task-difficulty staging. The two offline curation baselines—Quality Weighting (Belkhale et al. 2023) and Cluster Weighting (Hejna et al. 2025)—lag CLASP by 20–52% on robust success, consistent with the paper's argument that re-weighting a fixed dataset cannot compensate for misallocated demonstrations. In-distribution, CLASP-Teach (guidance only, no difficulty-aware reweighting) nearly matches CLASP-Full on easier tasks (0.92 vs. 0.94 on Avoiding at B=20) but falls behind on harder ones (0.56 vs. 0.66 on Alignment), showing that the coupled training component adds value where targeting and optimisation jointly matter.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLASP sits at the intersection of interactive imitation learning (ASkDAgger, AIM, HG-DAGGER), curriculum-based demonstration scheduling, and machine teaching, but departs from all three by introducing a shared region-level interface between teacher and learner. Prior interactive methods decide *when* to query using learner-side signals (uncertainty, novelty) but leave the teacher without a behavioural account of *what* to demonstrate; curriculum methods stage difficulty but are task-specific and not policy-aware; offline curation reweights fixed data without changing collection. CLASP repurposes KNN-based region partitioning and a soft-max difficulty aggregation into a compact map that simultaneously guides demonstration placement (CLASP-Teach) and training emphasis (CLASP-Train), grounding the machine-teaching objective in observable robot behaviour rather than opaque internal scores.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical gap CLASP addresses—novice teachers accumulating redundant demonstrations because they cannot see the learner's failure modes—is common in real deployments and has been identified but not resolved by prior work. The region-map interface is a lightweight, task-agnostic mechanism that could be overlaid on existing teleoperation setups without architectural changes, as demonstrated on the Sawyer. Key limitations include reliance on task-specific strategy labels for diversity evaluation, a small participant pool (N=30, 5 per group), a single random seed, and untested scalability to longer-horizon or higher-dimensional observation spaces. Open questions include whether the framework can automatically discover behavioural modes, generalise to heterogeneous user populations, and integrate with richer curricula beyond the fixed-budget round protocol studied here.
