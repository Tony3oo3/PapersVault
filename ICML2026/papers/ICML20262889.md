## Problem

Tool-calling benchmark results are highly sensitive to undocumented implementation choices (random seeds, multi-turn template construction, system prompts, history handling), making cross-paper comparisons and leaderboard rankings unreliable. Separately, RL-based tool-calling training wastes substantial computation: up to 80% of prompts yield zero-variance rollouts with no gradient signal, and the policy-update stage costs 3–5× more wall-clock time than rollout generation.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a user query alongside a set of tool schemas (API definitions) and, in multi-turn settings, the full serialized conversation history including prior tool calls and environment observations. The model produces a response containing free-form reasoning (thinking tokens) and structured tool invocations with typed arguments. For RL training, multi-step trajectories are decomposed into single-step training samples, and rewards are verified per tool-call correctness, making the output format strictly constrained compared to open-ended generation tasks.

## Method

The paper contributes two complementary analyses. On effectiveness, it systematically ablates BFCL evaluation factors—random seed (up to ~3% multi-turn variance), native vs. context multi-turn templates (~6–8% gap), thinking-history retention (~2–5% for reasoning models), system-prompt modifications (gains rivaling RL fine-tuning), and single-turn vs. multi-turn training data format—showing that small, often undocumented choices can dominate reported performance differences. On efficiency, it proposes two accelerations for GRPO training: (1) online pre-rollout filtering, which maintains a per-prompt all-correct streak counter and skips rollouts for prompts that have been uniformly solved for the past k epochs (k=1 or 2 suffices, since P(still all-correct | k consecutive all-correct) exceeds 0.8–0.9); and (2) max-variance rollout down-sampling, which generates n rollouts per prompt but backpropagates through only m < n rollouts selected as the reward extremes (lowest and highest), reducing policy-update cost by roughly n/m while preserving the most contrastive learning signal. Together these yield 1.7× (single-turn) and 2.6× (multi-turn) wall-clock speedups without degrading final performance.

## Results

On BFCL, Qwen3-4B-RL achieves 39.4±0.7 multi-turn and 84.8±0.9 single-turn accuracy (62.1±0.5 average), improving over the base model (22.7±0.9 / 83.9±0.5, avg 53.3±0.5) and over a stronger system prompt alone (37.2±1.4 / 84.8±0.7, avg 61.0±0.8). On ACEBench, the RL-trained model reaches 77.5 overall accuracy, a +12.1-point gain over the base model (65.4). The two efficiency techniques (online pre-rollout filtering + max-variance down-sampling) yield 1.7× and 2.6× wall-clock speedups in single-turn and multi-turn settings respectively, while downstream task performance (HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA, WinoGrande) shows no meaningful degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On BFCL, Qwen3-4B-RL (62.1 avg) outperforms all open-source baselines including Qwen3-235B-A22B (49.3), Mistral-Large-2411 (48.7), Llama-4-Scout-17B (45.5), and Nova-2-Lite (43.0), as well as GPT-4.1 (57.7) and Grok-4 (57.2). However, it trails the top closed-source models: Claude Sonnet 4.5 (73.2) and Gemini-3-Pro (73.4) lead by roughly 11 points on average, driven primarily by their much stronger multi-turn scores (61.4 and 63.1 vs. 39.4). On ACEBench, Qwen3-4B-RL (77.5) surpasses Nova-1-Lite (73.4), Gemma3-27B (76.8), and Magistral-Small-24B (67.4), but remains below Claude-4-Sonnet (81.8) and Nova-1-Pro (81.4). Notably, the multi-turn gap is the dominant differentiator: the RL-trained model's 39.4 multi-turn score is competitive with GPT-4.1 (38.9) but far below frontier closed-source systems, suggesting multi-turn tool-calling remains the key bottleneck for smaller open models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as its base RL algorithm and directly adopts the max-variance rollout down-sampling technique from Xu et al. (2025), adapting both to the tool-calling setting. The evaluation-sensitivity analysis extends the broader reproducibility literature (Dehghani et al., 2021; Henderson et al., 2018; Hochlehnert et al., 2025) from math and general LM benchmarks to the tool-calling domain for the first time. The pre-rollout filtering idea is inspired by Zheng et al. (2025) for math reasoning but is shown to require only 1–2 epochs of history for tool-calling, a notably shorter horizon. The paper positions itself as an empirical study of measurement and training efficiency rather than proposing a new agent architecture or dataset.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that small, undocumented system-prompt changes can produce multi-turn gains comparable to or exceeding RL fine-tuning is a significant warning for the tool-calling community: leaderboard comparisons may be confounded by prompt choices, undermining the credibility of reported methodological progress. The demonstration that multi-turn training data does not improve (and can degrade) multi-turn performance challenges the common assumption that more multi-turn supervision is always beneficial, pointing instead to trajectory quality as the bottleneck. The 1.7×–2.6× wall-clock speedups from the two efficiency techniques are practically useful for teams training tool-calling agents at scale. Open questions include whether the evaluation-sensitivity findings generalize beyond BFCL, whether the efficiency techniques hold at larger model scales, and whether the multi-turn data result is specific to the current quality of available trajectories.
