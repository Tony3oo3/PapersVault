## Problem

Prior work established that self-interested agents making a single decision each (m=1) in a bandit-like social learning setting suffer learning failures with positive probability. This paper asks whether allowing each agent to make multiple consecutive decisions (episode length m≥2), thereby enabling within-episode exploration for self-benefit, is sufficient to avoid aggregate learning failures. The answer is negative: learning failures remain the typical case for any fixed m, implying linear Bayesian regret.

## Input / Output

Each arriving agent observes the full round history (all previously chosen arms and realized rewards), a known Beta-Bernoulli prior over the two arms' means, the episode length m, a fixed aggregation function f: {0,1}^m → [0,m] (e.g., sum, max, min, or any symmetric coordinatewise non-decreasing function), and a small selection cost c_sel per non-skip pull. The agent outputs a deterministic per-episode policy maximizing Bayesian-expected utility. The paper's analytical outputs are failure events (FAIL_{c,N}: good arm pulled ≤N times; StrongFail_{c,N}: good arm not even considered by ≤N episodes) and a lower bound showing Bayesian regret grows linearly in T.

## Method

The core proof shows that the good arm (arm 2, which is prior-bad) is pulled only finitely many times with constant probability, even though agents can explore within episodes. The key insight is that an agent may rationally pull a posterior-bad arm early in an episode because a success would improve its posterior for later rounds, so single-round posterior comparisons are insufficient. Instead, the analysis uses 'optimistic posteriors'—hypothetical posteriors after m−1 additional positive samples—to define a robust bad-start condition. The proof combines three ingredients: (1) an arm1-stability event (arm 1's posterior mean stays near its prior mean) established via a Doob martingale and stopping-time argument, (2) an event that the first N_P samples of arm 2 are all zeros, and (3) an induction on episode length m (for symmetric f) showing that if arm 2 is strongly posterior-bad under optimistic posteriors, no posterior-optimal policy selects it. A chain of lemmas then converts the bounded-pull failure into a strong failure (arm 2 never considered), which yields linear regret via a utility-gap lower bound that holds whenever the selection cost is sufficiently small.

## Results

The paper proves that learning failures are the typical (not worst-case) outcome in episodic bandit social learning: for any episode length m ≥ 2 and symmetric aggregation function f, the probability that the good arm is pulled at most N_P times is at least q_P^m > 0 (Theorem 4.1). This implies linear Bayesian regret: BReg(T) ≥ c_0 · (T − N_0) for all T (Corollary 4.5), in stark contrast to the sublinear regret that is the standard desideratum for useful bandit algorithms. The result is robust to the choice of utility function (sum, max, min, or any non-constant, coordinatewise non-decreasing, symmetric f), and for m = 2 or f ∈ {max, min} it holds without any upper bound on selection cost (Theorem 4.6).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or comparison tables. The key comparison is conceptual: the m = 1 (single-round) case recovers the 'greedy' bandit algorithm, for which Banihashem et al. (2023) and Slivkins et al. (2025) already established positive-probability learning failures. The present paper's contribution is showing that allowing agents to make m ≥ 2 decisions per episode — which introduces within-episode exploration incentives — does not rescue the dynamics: failures persist for every fixed m. The paper also contrasts with 'strategic experimentation' literature (Bolton & Harris, 1999; Keller et al., 2005), where long-lived agents acting in parallel can eventually learn the best arm, making learning failures impossible in that setting. No quantitative method-vs-method comparisons are provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the bandit social learning framework of Banihashem et al. (2023) and Slivkins et al. (2025), which analyzed single-round episodes (m=1) corresponding to pure exploitation/greedy behavior, to the multi-round episodic setting (m≥2) where agents can rationally explore within their own episode. The key technical departure is that prior analyses compared current posterior-mean rewards, whereas here agents may rationally select a posterior-bad arm because a positive observation early in the episode shifts the posterior favorably for later rounds, requiring the authors to reason about 'optimistic posteriors' and use induction on episode length. The paper also situates itself relative to the strategic experimentation literature (Bolton & Harris, Keller et al.) and incentivized exploration work (Kremer et al., Che & Hörner), distinguishing its focus on characterizing a given self-interested dynamics rather than designing interventions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that learning failures are the typical case (positive probability for every instance) even with within-episode exploration, yielding linear Bayesian regret—challenges the intuition that organic exploration by self-interested agents is sufficient to prevent permanent lock-in on suboptimal arms. This has direct implications for platform design in recommender systems, AI interaction, and marketplaces: externally driven exploration mechanisms remain necessary even when users naturally try multiple options within a session. Open questions include how the failure probability scales with episode length m (currently only an exponential lower bound is shown), what happens under partial observability of history, and whether the two-armed failure result extends to more general K-armed settings beyond the 'two arms dominate' regime.
