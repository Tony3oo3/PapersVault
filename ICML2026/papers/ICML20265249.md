## Problem

When analysts select subsets of independent statistical tests adaptively from the data (e.g., via scan statistics or gene-set enrichment), naive inference for the aggregated effect on the chosen subset is biased. Existing post-selection methods either protect all linear contrasts (Scheffé) or correct for the full family cardinality (Bonferroni), both of which are substantially more conservative than necessary. The central obstacle to tighter bounds is that calibrating the maximum studentized statistic over the subset family requires solving a nonlinear combinatorial optimization that is NP-hard in general.

## Input / Output

The method consumes m per-test effect estimates θ̂_i with standard errors σ̂_i, a downward-closed subset family specified by nonnegative linear constraints Az ≤ b (e.g., cardinality limits), and a bootstrap size B with target level α. It outputs a simultaneous (1−α)-level lower confidence bound ∑_{i∈S} θ̂_i − c_maxT · √(∑_{i∈S} σ̂_i²) valid uniformly over all data-selected S in the family, where c_maxT is the (1−α) empirical quantile of B bootstrap replicates of the subset-maximum statistic. The bound is a single scalar per replicate, and the procedure is fully parallelizable across bootstrap replicates.

## Method

The key computational insight is that the squared objective T(S)² = (∑_{i∈S} θ̂_i)² / (∑_{i∈S} σ̂_i²) has rank-1 edge weights w_ij = θ̂_i θ̂_j, reducing the subset maximization to a weighted densest-subgraph problem. For the unconstrained family, an exact LP with O(m²) variables is derived (Theorem 4.3); for downward-closed linear constraints, an exact MILP augments the LP with binary support variables (Theorem 4.4). To scale beyond MILP tractability, an FPTAS with O(mk³/ε) runtime discretizes one of the two scalar aggregates (P(S), Q(S)) and tracks the other exactly, and a 1/(1−ε) rescaling of the bootstrap statistics restores valid coverage at a controllable O(ε) inflation. On the statistical side, a finite-sample coverage guarantee (Theorem 5.1) is established by a direct probability argument exploiting the convexity of the event set {z: max_S T(S) ≤ t} and independence across tests, yielding an error term that depends on log|A|/√n_min rather than the slower rates from high-dimensional CLTs.

## Results

Across all synthetic and real-data experiments, all methods achieve empirical coverage at or above the nominal 95% level, so the primary differentiator is bound tightness (higher bound mean = less conservative). On synthetic data (m=50, k=50, sparse signal, homogeneous noise), SubsetMaxT yields a bound mean of 2.02 versus 1.80 for Scheffé and 1.20 for Bonferroni. On the Hillstrom A/B testing dataset (m=32, k=32), SubsetMaxT achieves a bound mean of 0.0788 versus 0.0642 (Scheffé) and 0.0372 (Bonferroni). The FPTAS variant (ε=0.01) matches the exact method within ~1% in bound mean while scaling to m=5000 in under a second per bootstrap replicate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: the Scheffé-type bound (Berk et al., 2013), which protects all linear contrasts via a confidence region for the full mean vector, and the Bonferroni correction (Dunn, 1961), which pays for the full cardinality of the subset family. SubsetMaxT consistently produces tighter (larger) lower confidence bounds than both baselines across nearly all configurations, with the gap widening as the cardinality limit k increases. For example, at m=50, k=50 (Table 1), SubsetMaxT's bound mean is 2.02 versus 1.80 (Scheffé, +12%) and 1.20 (Bonferroni, +68%); on Hillstrom at k=32, the gap over Bonferroni is 2.1× (0.0788 vs. 0.0372). The one exception is k=1 (Table 8), where each candidate subset is a single test and the statistics are mutually independent, making Bonferroni's union bound nearly tight and slightly tighter than SubsetMaxT. The unconstrained variant SubsetMaxT(unconstr.) can underperform Scheffé/Bonferroni when m is large and k is small, because it must protect all 2^m subsets; the cardinality-constrained version avoids this by tailoring the correction to the restricted family.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of post-selection inference, combinatorial optimization, and high-dimensional statistics. It departs from selection-agnostic frameworks (Berk et al., 2013) that protect all linear contrasts by targeting only the structured subset-sum family, and from Bonferroni corrections that ignore the family's dependence structure. Computationally, it generalizes the LP formulation for the unweighted densest-subgraph problem (Charikar, 2000; Goldberg, 1984) to a weighted edge-and-vertex setting, and adapts the knapsack FPTAS (Ibarra & Kim, 1975) to a nonlinear ratio objective exploiting rank-1 structure. On the statistical side, it replaces high-dimensional CLT arguments (Chernozhukov et al., 2017, 2023) with a direct convexity-and-independence treatment that yields a strictly better dependence on family size.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method provides a practical, finite-sample-valid tool for post-selection inference in settings where aggregated effects over data-selected subsets are the scientific target (subgroup A/B tests, meta-analysis, ML benchmark comparisons). The coverage guarantee permitting log|A| = o(√n_min) is meaningfully stronger than the o(n_min^{1/3}) rate from the sharpest high-dimensional CLTs, and the FPTAS scales to m=5000 in under a second per bootstrap replicate. Open questions include extending the FPTAS to general downward-closed linear constraints beyond cardinality, formally handling correlated tests (the paper shows empirical robustness to ρ=0.8 but the guarantee assumes independence), and whether the direct-convexity proof technique extends to non-Gaussian per-test statistics beyond the i.i.d. sample-mean setting.
