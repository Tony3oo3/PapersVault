## Problem

Existing concentration inequalities for the variance of bounded random variables (e.g., Maurer & Pontil 2009, Audibert et al. 2009) rely on conservative upper bounds like V[(X−μ)²] ≤ σ², making their confidence intervals asymptotically wider than the oracle Bernstein benchmark. This paper seeks fully empirical, nonasymptotically valid confidence intervals and sequences for the variance whose first-order width exactly matches that oracle bound, while operating under the weaker assumption of constant conditional mean and variance (martingale dependence) rather than full iid.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequentially arriving stream of random variables bounded in [0,1] satisfying constant conditional mean μ and conditional variance σ² (Assumption 1.1), along with a significance level α. It outputs a two-sided confidence interval (batch setting) or confidence sequence (sequential setting) for σ², structured as D_t ± R_{t,α} for the upper bound and a closed-form quadratic solution for the lower bound. The lower confidence sequence requires solving a quadratic in σ² because the mean-estimation error term from the anytime-valid Bennett inequality introduces a σ²-dependent correction, and the probability split α = α₁ + α₂ must be chosen (e.g., α₁ = α/log n) to recover asymptotic sharpness.

## Method

The core construction is a novel nonnegative supermartingale (Theorem 4.1) that applies an empirical Bernstein-type Chernoff bound to the squared deviations (X_i − μ̂_i)² rather than to X_i directly. The key technical difficulty is that the conditional mean of (X_i − μ̂_i)² equals σ² + (μ̂_i − μ)², which is not constant, requiring a modified supermartingale with an extra δ_t = σ̂_t² − σ̃_t² shift in the exponent. For the upper confidence sequence, the unknown mean-estimation error E_t is simply lower-bounded by zero. For the lower confidence sequence, the paper invokes an anytime-valid Bennett's inequality (Theorem 3.3) to bound |μ̂_i − μ|, which introduces a σ²-dependent term and yields a quadratic equation in σ² solvable in closed form. Predictable plug-ins λ_i are chosen adaptively via an empirical fourth-moment estimator m̂₄,t², and the sharpness proof (Corollary 4.8) shows that with α₁,n = α/log(n), the scaled width √n·R_{n,α} converges almost surely to √(2V[(X−μ)²]·log(1/α)), matching the oracle Bernstein first-order term exactly.

## Results

The paper's headline theoretical result (Corollary 4.8) establishes asymptotic sharpness: the first-order width of both the upper and lower confidence intervals for σ² converges almost surely to √(2𝕍[(X−μ)²] log(1/α)), exactly matching the oracle Bernstein bound that knows μ and 𝕍[(X−μ)²]. In the batch setting (Figure 1), the proposed empirical Bernstein (EB) confidence intervals for the standard deviation are consistently narrower than the Maurer–Pontil (MP) intervals across Uniform(0,1), Beta(2,6), and Beta(5,5) distributions at α=0.05. In the sequential setting (Figure 2), replacing the variance confidence sequences in the adaptive Neyman allocation algorithm with the paper's Corollary 4.3 bounds substantially reduces the MSE of the average treatment effect (ATE) estimator over 1.5×10⁵ simulations, approaching oracle performance (which knows the true variances) especially in later rounds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Maurer & Pontil (2009, Theorem 10), the proposed method wins on both theory and practice. Theoretically, the first-order width of the MP interval scales as 2σ√(2 log(2/α)), whereas the proposed interval scales as √(2𝕍[(X−μ)²] log(1/α)); since 𝕍[(X−μ)²] = σ²(1−σ²) ≤ σ², the proposed bound is strictly tighter (roughly a factor-of-2 improvement in the leading constant). Empirically (Figure 1), the EB intervals are visibly narrower than MP intervals across all three tested distributions and sample sizes. Against Neopane et al. (2025), the paper's confidence sequences (Corollary 4.3) outperform the variance confidence sequences used in the original adaptive Neyman allocation algorithm: Figure 2 shows that the MSE of the ATE sequential estimator is substantially lower when using the proposed bounds, with the gap widening in later rounds and the proposed method approaching the oracle algorithm that has access to the true variances. The paper also notes that its proof techniques naturally extend to martingale dependence, whereas the self-bounding arguments underlying Maurer & Pontil do not readily generalize to the sequential setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the empirical Bernstein framework of Howard et al. (2021) and Waudby-Smith & Ramdas (2024) from the mean to the variance of bounded random variables. The key technical departure is that the conditional means of (X_i − μ̂_i)² are non-constant (unlike the mean case), requiring a modified supermartingale construction (Theorem 4.1) that tracks the mean-estimation error through an additional term E_t. For the lower confidence sequence, the paper cleverly combines the empirical Bernstein bound on the variance with an oracle Bennett inequality (Theorem 3.3) for the mean, yielding a closed-form quadratic solution rather than requiring a second empirical Bernstein inequality. This contrasts with prior variance inequalities (Maurer & Pontil 2009; Audibert et al. 2009) that rely on self-bounding arguments or conservative upper bounds on Var[(X−μ)²], which the paper shows are factor-of-2 looser in the leading term.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Sharper variance confidence intervals directly improve sample efficiency in applications ranging from adaptive Neyman allocation in causal inference to bandit algorithms and off-policy evaluation, where variance estimates drive exploration-exploitation tradeoffs. The anytime-valid (sequential) formulation under martingale dependence broadens applicability beyond the iid assumption that prior variance inequalities require. Open questions include extending the results to general Banach spaces (where the inner-product structure is lost and constants inflate), to covariance matrices or operators, and to optimizing the probability split α₁/α₂ without access to reference data. The Hilbert-space extension (Appendix A) is a natural but incomplete step toward these generalizations.
