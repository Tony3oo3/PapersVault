## Problem

The AI industry lacks systematic carbon accounting methods capable of estimating training emissions at scale without reproducing original training runs. While individual model footprints have been documented, no framework exists to aggregate emissions across thousands of heterogeneous models with uneven and often incomplete metadata disclosures, leaving the question of AI's total environmental impact unresolved.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes heterogeneous, frequently incomplete metadata scraped from Hugging Face model cards, config files, and external technical reports, processed through an LLM-based agent workflow (GPT-4o) for hardware normalization, unit conversion, and cross-source integration. Outputs include per-model training emissions in tCO2e with first-order propagated uncertainty bounds, a normalized AI Training Carbon Intensity (ATCI) in tCO2e/EFLOP for cross-model comparison, and aggregate estimates broken down by modality, region, and time period. The three-tier classification (rich/partial/minimal disclosure) determines which estimation pathway each model follows, with Tier 3 relying solely on parameter counts when no FLOPs or hardware data exist.

## Method

The core estimation model decomposes training emissions as (P_GPU/θ_GPU) × PUE × F_train^total × A_time × EF_region, where the GPU power-to-throughput ratio captures effective energy per compute, PUE accounts for datacenter overhead, A_time captures runtime amplification from restarts and communication, and EF_region translates electricity to carbon. To handle severe disclosure sparsity (only ~5.6% of models report emissions), the paper introduces a three-tier framework: Tier 1 models with hardware and FLOPs data are estimated directly, Tier 2 models with FLOPs but no hardware use a log-log regression (elasticity ~0.83 for FLOPs, ~0.85 for EF) calibrated on Tier 1 data, and Tier 3 models with only parameter counts use a parameter-based regression (elasticity ~1.45). The paper further defines ATCI as the product of all factors except FLOPs, providing a normalized 'environmental cost per compute' metric validated by accelerator-family-specific regression coefficients showing H100-class hardware yields ~56% lower emissions than A100-class at matched compute.

## Results

Training 5,227 Hugging Face models with >5,000 downloads produced approximately 6.0×10⁴ tCO₂e (±2.4×10⁴ tCO₂e) as of August 2025. CV & MM models show higher per-compute carbon intensity (mean ATCI 0.16 tCO₂e/EFLOP) than NLP (0.13), while finetuned models exhibit the highest ATCI (0.23) but contribute only 0.4×10⁴ t to the total. Against 292 models with publicly disclosed emissions, the framework achieves a MAPE of 0.42, median relative error of 0.32, and 74%/82% of estimates within ×2/×3 of ground truth. Annual emissions grew nearly two orders of magnitude from ~4.5×10² tCO₂e (2020–2021) to >4.0×10⁴ tCO₂e (2024–2025), with projections reaching ~9.4×10⁴ tCO₂e by 2035.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper validates its FLOPs-based estimation framework against 292 models with publicly disclosed training emissions. For Llama 2 (Touvron et al., 2023), estimates closely track disclosures: the 7B model is estimated at 33 tCO₂e vs. 31 disclosed (+6%), the 70B at 327 vs. 291 (+12%), and the full series at 412 vs. 384 (+7%). BLOOM (Luccioni et al., 2023) is nearly exact at 25 vs. 24.7 tCO₂e. The largest overestimate is OLMoE-1B-7B-0924 (Morrison et al., 2025) at 30 vs. 18 tCO₂e (+67%), likely due to MoE routing overhead assumptions. The largest underestimate is Meta Llama 3 70B at 1,010 vs. 1,900 tCO₂e (−47%), while the flagship Llama 3.1 405B (Maslej et al., 2025) is estimated at 8,176 vs. 8,930 tCO₂e (−8%), showing the framework scales well to the largest models. Overall, 74% of all 292 evaluated models fall within a factor of 2 of their disclosed values, with a median relative error of 32%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the FLOPs-based energy estimation lineage from Kaplan et al. (2020) and Lacoste et al. (2019) into a platform-scale carbon accounting framework. Prior work (Strubell et al. 2019, Patterson et al. 2021, Luccioni et al. 2023) estimated emissions for individual models using measured electricity or complete metadata, while tools like CodeCarbon and CarbonTracker require runtime access. The key departure is the three-tier imputation strategy and the introduction of ATCI (emissions per compute) as a standardized efficiency metric, enabling estimation across thousands of heterogeneous models with incomplete disclosures—something prior single-model case studies and runtime-monitoring tools could not address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic, platform-scale estimate of AI training emissions (~60,000 tCO₂e for 5,227 popular HF models), providing a concrete anchor for policy discussions and future carbon reporting standards. The finding that annual emissions grew nearly two orders of magnitude between 2020–2021 and 2024–2025, combined with the <0.2% disclosure rate, underscores an urgent transparency gap. Key limitations include the exclusion of proprietary models, inference emissions, and full lifecycle (hardware manufacturing, experimentation), leaving the total AI carbon footprint substantially underestimated. Open questions include whether ATCI can become a standard reporting metric and how to extend the framework to inference at deployment scale.
