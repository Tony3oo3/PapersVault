## Problem

Most bilevel reinforcement learning (BRL) methods assume the lower-level (LL) problem is a single-policy MDP, which cannot capture competitive multi-agent structures where opposing policies interact at equilibrium. For bilevel optimization over saddle points of regularized min-max zero-sum Markov games (BOSMG), existing approaches are either heuristic without convergence guarantees or require computationally prohibitive second-order information (e.g., Hessian inversion). The paper asks whether a stochastic first-order method can solve BOSMG with provably efficient iteration and sample complexity.

## Input / Output

The method takes a UL parameter x that parameterizes the LL reward function r_x(s,a,b), tabular softmax policy parameters φ (min-player) and ψ (max-player), and Monte Carlo trajectory samples of truncated horizon H from the Markov game. It produces an optimized UL parameter x and an LL saddle-point policy pair (π*_φ, π*_ψ) that is an ε-stationary point of the hyper-objective F(x) = f(x, φ*(x), ψ*(x)), with stationarity measured by the squared gradient norm. The UL objective f is evaluated at the equilibrium policy pair induced by the LL game, and the initial state distribution ρ must have full support over the state space.

## Method

PANDA (Penalty-Augmented Nikaido-Isoda Descent-Ascent) reformulates the bilevel problem using the Nikaido-Isoda (NI) function g(x,φ,ψ) = max_ψ' J(x,φ,ψ') − min_φ' J(x,φ',ψ) as a penalty term, yielding the single-objective min_{x,φ,ψ} f(x,φ,ψ) + λg(x,φ,ψ). The algorithm proceeds in three nested steps per outer iteration: (1) best-response approximation via policy-gradient descent/ascent on φ and ψ to estimate the NI function, (2) penalty-subproblem approximation via K steps of SGD on the penalized objective h(x,φ,ψ) = (1/λ)f(x,φ,ψ) + g̃(x,φ,ψ), and (3) a hypergradient step updating x using the gradient of the surrogate objective. Crucially, PANDA avoids computing UL hypergradients or any second-order information by exploiting the min-max game structure and the non-uniform PL property of the NI function, which arises intrinsically from the entropy-regularized MMZSMG. Under standard smoothness and PL assumptions (no convexity required), PANDA achieves Õ(ε^{-1}) outer iterations and Õ(ε^{-3}) total samples, matching the best-known rates for single-policy BRL.

## Results

PANDA achieves Õ(ε⁻¹) iteration complexity and Õ(ε⁻³) sample complexity to find an ε-stationary point of the original bilevel objective F(x), matching the best-known rates for bilevel RL with single-policy LL MDPs (Gaur et al., 2025). In the synthetic incentive-design problem, PANDA attains the highest UL incentive reward among all baselines while driving the LL Nash-equilibrium gap (measured by the NI function) to near zero, with a small gap to an exact oracle using dynamic programming and second-order hypergradients. On the Sentinel-Intruder grid world (both 5×5 and 20×20), PANDA achieves the lowest UL loss (number of restricted-cell visits by the sentinel) under the same number of sampled trajectories, outperforming META, DA, and PBRL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PANDA is compared against three baselines applicable to the min-max LL setting: META (Yang et al., 2022), DA (Wang et al., 2023), and PBRL (Shen et al., 2025), plus an exact oracle. META is a heuristic meta-gradient method with no convergence guarantees; PANDA consistently achieves higher UL incentive reward and lower UL loss than META in both the synthetic and Sentinel-Intruder environments. DA requires Hessian inversion (second-order information) to form hypergradients, making it computationally prohibitive at scale; PANDA matches or exceeds DA's UL objective while using only first-order policy-gradient oracles. PBRL (Shen et al., 2025) is first-order but provides guarantees only in the deterministic regime and converges to a stationary point of the penalized surrogate rather than the original bilevel problem; PANDA extends to the stochastic setting and provably converges to a stationary point of the original F(x). In the Sentinel-Intruder task (5×5 and 20×20 grids), PANDA achieves the lowest UL loss (restricted-cell visits) at every sample budget, with the advantage widening on the larger 20×20 map. The gap between PANDA and the exact oracle (dynamic programming + second-order hypergradients) remains small, indicating near-optimal solution quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

PANDA extends the penalty-based bilevel optimization framework (Kwon et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024) and the Nikaido-Isoda gap reformulation (Shen et al., 2025) from single-policy MDPs to the coupled min-max structure of regularized zero-sum Markov games. The closest prior work, PBRL (Shen et al., 2025), uses a similar penalty + NI construction but provides guarantees only for the penalized surrogate in deterministic settings, while hypergradient-based BRL methods (HPGD, SoBiRL, First-Order BRL) rely on structural properties of single-policy MDPs that break under two-player strategic coupling. The non-uniform PL property of the NI function (Lemma 1) generalizes the soft-value PL condition from Mei et al. (2020) to the MMZSMG setting, providing the key analytical tool that enables convergence to stationary points of the original bilevel problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first stochastic first-order algorithm for bilevel RL with a regularized MMZSMG at the LL that achieves O~(ε^{-1}) iteration and O~(ε^{-3}) sample complexity, matching state-of-the-art rates previously known only for single-policy LL MDPs. The structural results—uniqueness of saddle points under arbitrary state-wise strongly convex-concave regularizers and the non-uniform PL property of the NI function—provide reusable tools for future work on competitive bilevel problems. Limitations include the restriction to regularized MMZSMGs (not general min-max or multi-player games), tabular softmax policies, and the PL condition assumption on the penalized objective; extending to general min-max games and function-approximation settings is identified as future work.
