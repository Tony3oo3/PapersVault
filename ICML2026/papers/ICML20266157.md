## Problem

LLMs suffer significant performance degradation from semantically-neutral prompt perturbations (format changes, typos, zero-width characters), yet existing robustness methods require expensive full retraining, multiple inference passes, or are limited to classification. The paper asks whether robustness can be acquired through small, task-dependent model modifications without significant computational budgets or supervisory ground truth, and identifies a previously underappreciated root cause: a systematic expected shift (perturbation-induced bias) in module outputs under random perturbations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw logit outputs (for classification) or intermediate-layer feature vectors (for generation) of base LLMs, along with a small set of clean calibration examples and perturbation statistics (no class labels required for the unsupervised debiasing variant). It produces debiased logits or features via a closed-form ridge regression solution, yielding classification labels, per-example and population-level robustness certificates (distances to decision boundaries), and, for generation tasks, corrected intermediate representations. The debiasing operates per-neuron on individual output attributes, and the certification reduces to a one-dimensional margin comparison per example.

## Method

The core insight is that nonlinearities in LLMs cause E_δx[M(x+δx)] ≠ M(x), creating a perturbation-induced bias C_i that dominates the robustness radius ε_i(φ) = C_i + cγ_i√(2n ln(2m/φ_i)) (Lipschitz case) or C_i + √(V_i m/φ_i) (variance case). The paper proposes two debiasing interventions: input-independent (subtracting a constant b_i from each logit neuron, searched over overlapping improvement regions for BAC and robustness radius) and input-dependent (subtracting b_i(x) = w_i·ψ(x) + β_i, solved via linear ridge regression on stacked perturbation differences). Theoretical certificates are derived via McDiarmid's and Chebyshev's inequalities for component-wise stability, and generalized to margin-based per-example and population-level certification using Hoeffding's and Bernstein's inequalities under a general perturbation distribution. For generation tasks, the approach extends to intermediate layers by computing PCA on clean-perturbed feature differences and debiasing the top-k principal components. The entire pipeline is closed-form, requires no model retraining, adds negligible inference overhead, and can optionally be combined with LoRA adapters for further gains.

## Results

Across 9 target tasks from Natural Instructions and three 7–8B models (Qwen-3-8B, Llama-3.1-8B, Olmo-3-7B), input-dependent debiasing recovers a substantial fraction of perturbation-induced performance loss: p_recover averages 93.4% (Qwen), 67.8% (Olmo), and 20.1% (Llama) for format perturbations, and 55.2% (Qwen), 51.1% (Olmo), and 66.4% (Llama) for text perturbations. Robustness radii (p_εV) improve by 46–84% across all model–perturbation combinations. At the certification level, per-example certified fractions (p_C) increase from 7.9–32.4% to 13.5–63.8%, and population-level guarantees (P_C) rise from 0.4–19.0% to 4.6–42.8% after debiasing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's only direct head-to-head comparison is against LoRA fine-tuning (Hu et al., ICLR 2022) on three binary classification tasks with format perturbations on Qwen-8B. LoRA alone reduces perturbation damage (δ_drop) from −5.7 to 4.2 (Task 65), −14.3 to 10.8 (Task 69), and −35.6 to 34.3 (Task 1297), but still leaves substantial residual damage. Applying input-dependent debiasing on top of LoRA nearly eliminates the damage: δ_drop drops to 0.4, 6.0, and −0.3 respectively, with δ_pert at −0.0, 0.3, and 0.7. The trade-off is that LoRA alone preserves or slightly improves clean-example performance (δ_clean = 1.4, −7.1, 0), whereas Debiasing-LoRA shows δ_clean of 0, 1.3, and 0. Overall, the debiasing layer on top of LoRA wins decisively on perturbation robustness (reducing residual damage by 90–100%) at negligible cost to clean accuracy, confirming that the two interventions are complementary rather than redundant.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of robustness certification and lightweight model intervention. It departs from adversarial training and spectral normalization (which require full retraining), from randomized smoothing and self-denoising (which require multiple inference passes), and from batch calibration (Zhou et al., 2023), which is the closest prior work in spirit but operates at inference time without yielding formal certificates. The key conceptual departure is identifying perturbation-induced bias — a systematic shift in expected module outputs under random perturbation — as a first-class robustness factor, and showing it can be corrected via a closed-form bias term on logits or intermediate features, thereby unifying Lipschitz-based and variance-based certificates into a single framework where the bias term C_i appears additively.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for deployment settings where prompts inevitably vary in surface form: a single-pass, closed-form debiasing step can recover a large fraction of perturbation-induced accuracy loss and substantially increase the proportion of certifiable examples (e.g., per-example certification rates jumping from ~8% to ~55% for Qwen on format perturbations). However, the approach requires white-box access, produces a clean-perturbed performance tradeoff, and the certificates remain conservative due to distribution-agnostic concentration inequalities. Open questions include whether the method extends cleanly to long-form generation (preliminary PCA-based results are promising but limited), whether it remains effective in genuinely high-dimensional feature spaces where concentration effects may already mitigate systematic shifts, and how to push the Pareto frontier of the clean-perturbed tradeoff beyond the simple Gram-penalty mechanism.
