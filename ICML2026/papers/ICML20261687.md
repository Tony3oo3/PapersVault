## Problem

In one-shot strategic classification, when gaming (cheating) is cheaper than genuine improvement, no classifier design can incentivize honest effort without external subsidies. Existing sequential extensions focus on optimizing dynamic classifier weights rather than threshold design, and do not model the promotion/relegation dynamics or the inter-temporal coupling effects (discounting, skill depreciation, and level-dependent leg-up) that arise in multi-level progression systems. This paper asks whether a principal can design a sequence of classifier thresholds in a multi-level promotion-relegation framework to make honest improvement the agent's unique optimal long-term strategy, even when gaming remains the cheaper instantaneous option.

## Input / Output

The agent's state is a pair (level l_t, attribute x_t) evolving over discrete time, where the observable feature z_t = x_t + a_t⁺ + a_t⁻ is the sole classifier input while x_t is private. The principal's design variable is a sequence of L thresholds μ⃗ = {μ_l} that define ternary (pass/stay/fail) classifier boundaries at each level, with a shared model weight θ (set to 1 WLOG). For empirical validation, the initial attribute distribution is drawn from the FICO credit score dataset normalized to [0,10], and the principal's relaxed utility (Equation 4) is optimized via CMA-ES over L ∈ {2,…,8} levels, producing an optimal threshold sequence and reward rate r*.

## Method

The paper formulates the agent's problem as a discrete-time MDP with continuous state and action spaces, where the attribute transition x_{t+1} = γx_{t+} + δ(l_{t+1}−1) couples three effects: discounting (β), retention (γ), and a level-dependent leg-up (δ). The core theoretical contribution is a full characterization of the agent's optimal Markov strategy via a Bellman-equation fixed-point analysis (Theorems 3.1–3.2), revealing that the leg-up effect shifts the incentivizable threshold region upward by δ/(1−γ) while the net incentivizable width remains constant. For the principal's design problem, the key insight is that setting each threshold at the attribute's natural steady state μ_l = δl/(1−γ) — where depreciation and leg-up exactly cancel — makes the mechanism self-sustaining, and under mild conditions on the gaming cost this induces arbitrarily high attributes through honest effort alone (Theorem 4.2). A greedy binary-search heuristic (Algorithm 1) with a value-iteration subroutine (converging at rate O(log(1/ε)/|log β|)) provides a practical tool for constructing feasible threshold sequences when closed-form conditions do not apply.

## Results

Under the ideal cost setting (Case I: c⁺=0.8, c⁻=0.7), the principal's optimal design uses 6 levels with reward rate r*=1.80, achieving a terminal threshold μ_L*=10.76 and principal utility U*=630.4 with 0% gaming. In the worst case (Case IV: c⁺=1.5, c⁻=0.4, violating the incentivizability condition), utility drops to 107.9 with the agent plateauing at level 5 out of 8. The key theoretical contribution is that the non-incentivizable region shrinks from c⁺ > c⁻ (one-shot) to (1−βγ)c⁺ > c⁻ (sequential), strictly expanding the range of parameters where honest improvement can be incentivized. Robustness analysis shows the mechanism is immune to ±30% misspecification of the leg-up parameter δ, but overestimating retention γ by just 10% causes 82% utility loss and 100% gaming.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanism design paper without direct method-to-method baselines; the primary comparison is between the proposed multi-level sequential mechanism and the one-shot strategic classification framework. Theorem 2.1 establishes that the non-incentivizable region (where improvement can never be incentivized) shrinks from the one-shot condition c⁺ > c⁻ to the sequential condition (1−βγ)c⁺ > c⁻, meaning the sequential design strictly expands the set of feasible problem instances. In the numerical experiments, the 'natural' threshold sequence μ_l = δ(l−1)/(1−γ) from Theorem 4.2 serves as an analytical baseline; the CMA-ES-optimized non-uniform sequences (Cases I–III) achieve comparable or slightly higher utility while using fewer levels (e.g., Case III collapses to L=2 with a very high threshold rather than the 5-level natural sequence). The greedy heuristic (Algorithm 1) consistently recovers feasible sequences matching the analytical bounds from Theorems 4.1–4.2, and is particularly useful when δ=0 where Theorem 4.2 does not apply. The robustness analysis (Table 5.2) reveals that the mechanism is highly sensitive to overestimating retention γ or farsightedness β (causing 32–82% utility loss and near-full gaming), while being robust to misspecification of the leg-up δ and gaming cost c⁻.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the strategic classification framework (Hardt et al., 2015) from one-shot Stackelberg games to a sequential, multi-level setting where agents navigate a ladder of ternary (selective) classifiers with promotion, relegation, and abstention outcomes. It departs from prior sequential strategic classification work (Harris et al., 2021) by focusing on classification rather than regression, and by introducing three inter-temporal coupling effects—discounting, attribute retention (depreciation), and a novel 'leg-up' effect where higher attainment self-reinforces the agent's true qualification. The selective classifier formulation (Cortes et al., 2016) is repurposed to naturally encode the three-way level transition, and the principal's design problem shifts from weight optimization to threshold-sequence design under a subgame-perfect equilibrium.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that sequential dynamics with a leg-up effect can shrink the non-incentivizable region and, under mild conditions, enable agents to reach arbitrarily high qualification levels through genuine effort alone—something provably impossible in one-shot settings. However, the robustness analysis reveals a critical practical vulnerability: overestimating the agent's retention rate by just 10% causes 82% utility loss and 100% gaming, suggesting the mechanism is fragile to parameter misspecification in deployment. Open questions include extending to stochastic classification outcomes (addressed in a companion paper), multi-dimensional attribute spaces, and level-dependent leg-up effects, as well as understanding how the mechanism performs when the principal faces heterogeneous agent populations with varying cost structures.
