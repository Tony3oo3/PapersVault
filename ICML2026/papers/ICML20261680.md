## Problem

Existing best arm identification (BAI) methods assume a single objective and passively observed covariates, leaving the constrained setting—where the best arm must also satisfy feasibility thresholds—unaddressed. This paper tackles the joint challenge of identifying the optimal feasible arm per covariate while actively selecting which arm-covariate pair to sample, a problem where the interplay between optimality gaps and feasibility gaps fundamentally changes the sample complexity landscape.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261680/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes K arms with known feature vectors, M discrete covariates, a fixed set of D design points spanning R^D, a confidence level δ, and sequentially observes noisy two-dimensional performance vectors (F, G) from the chosen design point at each step. The linear model assumes both the objective mean f(x_i, c_j) = θ^T φ(x_i, c_j) and constraint mean g(x_i, c_j) = β^T φ(x_i, c_j) share the same feature map φ but have distinct unknown parameter vectors. The output is a recommended best feasible arm for every covariate, an adaptive sampling ratio over the D design points, and a data-dependent stopping time τ that guarantees the δ-PAC condition.

## Method

The paper first derives a tight instance-dependent lower bound on sample complexity via a multi-level optimization over sampling ratios, covariates, and arm categories (suboptimal-feasible, infeasible-better, infeasible-worse), then proves tightness with a Track-and-Stop algorithm. Because computing the optimal sampling ratio at each iteration is intractable, the authors introduce a surrogate objective that merges infeasible arm categories, derive its convex dual over the unit simplex, and exploit the dual's structure to design a decomposition algorithm. This algorithm updates only two coordinates of the dual solution per iteration via a single gradient step with line search, then maps the dual iterate to a primal sampling ratio through a closed-form square-root normalization. The resulting duality-based decomposition algorithm is δ-PAC and asymptotically matches the relaxed bound U*(P)·log(1/δ), with per-iteration cost far lower than the full Track-and-Stop oracle.

## Results

On a problem with 2 covariates, 4 arms, and 1 constraint (δ=0.1, n₀=1, 100 macro-replications), the proposed DSR algorithm achieves the lowest empirical sample complexity with an average of 9,205.46 samples. With only 4,000 samples, both DSR and GOSR exceed the target probability of correct identification (PCI), highlighting the statistical conservatism of the fixed-confidence setting. DSR also outperforms all benchmarks on the PCI metric. Under heavy-tailed t-distribution noise (30 macro-replications), DSR maintains the lowest sample complexity and exhibits stable performance across replications, demonstrating robustness to violations of the Gaussian noise assumption.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DSR against four baselines: USR (uniform sampling), BCSR (a modified Best Challenger algorithm from Garivier & Kaufmann, 2016, representing state-of-the-art BAI based solely on arm optimality), GOSR (greedy algorithm relying solely on arm optimality), and GFSR (greedy algorithm relying solely on arm feasibility). DSR achieves the lowest sample complexity among all methods, with an average of 9,205.46 samples, outperforming BCSR, GOSR, GFSR, and USR. On the PCI metric, DSR also outperforms all other benchmarks, while GOSR is the only other method that exceeds the target PCI at 4,000 samples. Under t-distribution noise, DSR continues to achieve the lowest sample complexity with relatively stable performance, whereas the other methods degrade more noticeably. The comparison highlights that leveraging both feasibility and optimality information (as DSR does via the duality-based approach) is strictly more efficient than relying on either criterion alone (GOSR or GFSR) or ignoring the linear structure entirely (BCSR, USR).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the linear BAI framework of Jedra & Proutiere (2020) by introducing two additional layers of complexity: mean-based constraints on secondary performance metrics and active covariate selection over arm-covariate pairs. The theoretical machinery builds on the transportation lemma of Kaufmann et al. (2016) and the Track-and-Stop paradigm of Garivier & Kaufmann (2016), while the algorithmic core borrows the two-coordinate decomposition structure from Lin et al. (2009) for singly linearly constrained problems. The duality-based perspective, which converts a multi-level max-min optimization into a convex dual over the simplex, is the key departure from prior BAI algorithms that rely on solving the primal optimization oracle at each iteration. The paper explicitly positions constrained BAI with covariate selection as a generalization that subsumes both canonical BAI and linear BAI as special cases.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a complete theoretical pipeline—tight lower bound, asymptotically optimal Track-and-Stop algorithm, relaxed bound with provable constant gap, and an efficient duality-based decomposition algorithm—for a problem formulation that directly models personalized decision-making (e.g., drug selection under side-effect constraints). The main limitation is the reliance on a fixed set of D design points and a linear model, which restricts applicability to problems where the feature space is low-dimensional and the relationship between covariates and outcomes is genuinely linear. Open questions highlighted by the authors include extending to continuous covariate spaces, generalizing to non-linear models such as Gaussian Process Regression, and handling the ε-optimal and ε-feasible BAI variant, which would require substantially new technical tools.
