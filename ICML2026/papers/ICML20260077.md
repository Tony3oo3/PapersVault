## Problem

Tokenization fundamentally shapes how language models represent and process text, yet its impact on model behavior is poorly understood because existing model comparisons confound tokenizer differences with architecture, training data, and training duration. No open collection of models exists that are identical in every respect except their tokenizer, making it impossible to cleanly attribute performance differences to tokenization design choices.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260077/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark presents multiple-choice text completion questions in five typologically diverse languages (English, Mandarin Chinese, Farsi, Italian, Turkish), each with native-speaker-curated perturbations spanning orthographic errors, diacritics, morphological variation, noise (homoglyphs, OCR errors, typos), Unicode styling, and LaTeX/STEM notation. Models output a completed answer, and evaluation produces relative accuracy drops per perturbation category (robustness) plus intrinsic tokenization efficiency metrics (subword fertility, cross-lingual parity, proportion of continued words) computed on 10,000 parallel Flores200 samples.

## Method

The paper trains 14 ~1B-parameter Llama-3.2-architecture models using Meta's Lingua framework, all sharing identical initialization, architecture, 100B-token multilingual corpus (40B English + 60B across four non-English languages), and 100K-step training budget, differing only in their off-the-shelf tokenizer (ByT5, TokenMonster, GPT-2, mBERT, XGLM, Gemma-2, BLOOM, Aya, Qwen-3, GPT-4o, Llama-3.2, Tekken, Phi-3, Comma). A novel vocabulary unification framework constructs a super-vocabulary as the union of all 14 tokenizers' vocabularies, creating bijective ID mappings so that shared tokens receive identical embedding initializations across models, eliminating initialization variance as a confound. The TokSuite benchmark comprises ~5,000 samples built from 40 canonical questions (plus 20 math and 44 STEM questions) translated into five languages and perturbed by native annotators to reflect real-world input variations. Robustness is measured as relative accuracy drop from canonical to perturbed performance, with 10,000-trial bootstrap confidence intervals and Wilcoxon signed-rank significance tests.

## Results

Across 14 identical 1B-parameter models differing only in tokenizer, TokenMonster achieves the best average robustness with a 0.17 relative performance drop, despite being an English-only tokenizer with a 32K vocabulary. ByT5, using only 259 byte-level tokens, matches mid-tier tokenizers at 0.22 average drop and shows minimal degradation on English orthographic errors (0.04) and grammatical errors (0.00). Unicode styling perturbations cause the largest universal degradation (0.53 average drop), while noise perturbations disproportionately affect non-English languages (0.21 vs. 0.15 for English). Scaling from 300M to 7B parameters yields negligible robustness improvement (0.26 → 0.22 average drop), confirming tokenizer design dominates over model scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is among 14 tokenizers applied to otherwise identical 1B-parameter Llama-3.2-architecture models. TokenMonster (32K English-only, custom ungreedy algorithm) achieves the best average robustness (0.17), outperforming all multilingual tokenizers with 10–8× larger vocabularies, primarily due to its tokenization consistency that limits distinct token sequences for the same surface form. ByT5 (259 tokens, byte-level) matches mid-tier BPE tokenizers at 0.22 average and is notably robust to noise (0.18 NEN) and orthographic errors (0.04 EN), but pays a heavy efficiency cost with the highest subword fertility (5.79 avg) and PCW (0.90 avg). XGLM (256K, Unigram) is the only tokenizer that shows strong robustness to Unicode styling (0.11 vs. 0.53 average), attributed to its NFKC normalization, though this comes at the cost of lossy detokenization. Tekken (130K, BPE) performs worst overall (0.27), particularly on STEM content (0.43) and morphological perturbations (0.31 EN). The paper confirms that intrinsic compression metrics (fertility, parity, PCW) do not predict downstream robustness, and that scaling model size from 300M to 7B parameters yields only marginal robustness gains (0.26 → 0.22), establishing tokenizer design as the dominant factor.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior tokenizer-impact studies (Ali et al., 2024; Schmidt et al., 2024) that trained tokenizers from scratch to isolate individual design properties, instead studying 14 off-the-shelf, industry-standard tokenizers under a fully controlled experimental setup where models are identical in architecture, data, and initialization. It also extends the robustness-evaluation line of work (Chai et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Geh et al., 2025) by replacing synthetic adversarial perturbations with real-world, language-specific variations curated by native speakers across five typologically diverse languages. The vocabulary unification framework for shared embedding initialization is a novel methodological contribution that enables fairer cross-tokenizer comparison than prior work achieved.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TokSuite provides the first controlled, open-source collection of models that isolate tokenizer effects, enabling practitioners to make evidence-based tokenizer choices rather than defaulting to whatever ships with a model. The finding that robustness is dominated by tokenizer design rather than model scale (300M→7B shows minimal change) or training duration challenges the common assumption that simply scaling up compensates for poor tokenization. Key open questions include whether these findings generalize beyond the five languages tested, whether the multiple-choice base-model evaluation transfers to generation and instruction-following settings, and whether hybrid tokenization strategies (e.g., BPE dropout, subword regularization) can combine the efficiency of subword methods with the robustness of byte-level approaches.
