## Problem

Publishing LLM benchmarks with ground-truth answers on the Internet risks contaminating future models and enabling leaderboard hacking, yet keeping benchmarks private still permits test-set overfitting through repeated evaluation queries. No existing method allows benchmark creators to publish questions openly while providing a black-box, statistically principled signal for detecting whether a model has memorized the test set or gamed the evaluation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261823/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard question-answer benchmark and transforms it into a 'capped' benchmark by appending a randomization instruction to each question (e.g., 'randomly add a number from [1,5] to your solution') and replacing the ground-truth answer with a randomly shifted variant, so that L logically correct answers exist per question. At evaluation time, a black-box LLM is queried on the capped questions and its accuracy is compared against the known Bayes accuracy ceiling (1/L) via a one-sided binomial test, yielding a p-value and a binary contamination flag. An additional unbiased estimator recovers the original (uncapped) accuracy from the capped score, enabling model ranking without ever exposing the true answers.

## Method

CapBencher reduces the Bayes accuracy of a benchmark by preparing L logically correct answers per question and randomly selecting one as the published solution, capping the best achievable accuracy at 1/L without altering the underlying reasoning difficulty. Under four assumptions (uniform shift, independence, model shift from prompt options, random option ordering), Theorem 2.1 establishes an exact affine relationship between the capped and original conditional accuracy, from which an unbiased sample-based estimator of the original accuracy is derived with characterized variance (Theorem 2.2). Contamination detection uses a one-sided binomial test (uniformly most powerful by the Karlin-Rubin theorem) comparing the model's number of correct answers against Binomial(n, 1/L), requiring only black-box predictions and no access to model logits or internals. The approach is flexible across task types (direct answer, multiple-choice, binary-choice, open-ended code) and supports both an obfuscation strategy (true answer hidden) and a disclosure-allowed strategy (true answer revealed but a random suffix appended), with the Bayes accuracy tunable by adjusting L.

## Results

CapBencher preserves LLM ranking ability with a Kendall's τ of 0.92 between capped and original benchmark accuracy, and the unbiased estimator reconstructs original scores with modest variance increase (e.g., std dev rises from 0.011 to 0.027 on GSM8K). Contamination is detected across all tested models (Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek families) and all 13 benchmarks once accuracy exceeds the 50% Bayes ceiling. For leaderboard hacking, evolutionary model merging pushes a merged model to 56.52% accuracy versus ~40% for each parent model, clearly flagged by the binomial test. Lower Bayes accuracy (10%) accelerates detection to ~epoch 4 versus ~epoch 6 at 50%, though at the cost of reduced tracking resolution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the canary string method (Srivastava et al., 2023), CapBencher wins on three axes: it requires no access to model log probabilities (enabling black-box evaluation), it is resilient to adversarial removal of the canary string, and its binomial test yields exact p-values rather than asymptotic approximations, giving more stable detection across epochs. Against Min-k% (Shi et al., 2024) and Min-k%++ (Zhang et al., 2025), CapBencher achieves 100% detection across all six tested benchmarks (ARC-Challenge, MMLU, GSM8K, BoolQ, MathQA, GPQA), whereas both Min-k% variants fail to detect contamination on GPQA (AUC below the 0.7 threshold). Additionally, Min-k% methods require model logits and lack a principled statistical significance test, whereas CapBencher's binomial test provides a well-defined detection threshold valid even for small sample sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

CapBencher departs from prior contamination-detection methods (canary strings, Min-k%/Min-k%++, log-probability analysis) that require access to model internals or exact benchmark text, by instead modifying the benchmark at publication time so that contamination is detectable through a simple black-box accuracy comparison against a known Bayes ceiling. Conceptually, it repurposes the classical Bayes error framework (Cover & Hart, 1967; Ishida et al., 2023) in a novel direction: rather than estimating an unknown Bayes accuracy from data, the benchmark designer directly controls it by injecting answer randomness. It also distinguishes itself from dynamic benchmarks (LiveBench, VarBench, DyVal) that regenerate questions, by keeping the original questions fixed and only randomizing the accepted answers, which avoids the engineering cost of generating new problems and enables a uniformly most-powerful statistical test.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As frontier benchmarks become increasingly expensive to construct (FrontierMath, HLE), a protocol that preserves their value against contamination without sacrificing open evaluation is practically important and likely to be adopted by benchmark creators. Key limitations remain: the realized answer still leaks a weak clue that stronger future models may exploit via reverse engineering, and the increased variance under capping destabilizes rankings on small benchmarks (e.g., GPQA-diamond, n=198). Open questions include stronger obfuscation strategies, cryptographic extensions such as zero-knowledge proofs, and whether the method generalizes to fully open-ended tasks where multiple correct answers are harder to enumerate.
