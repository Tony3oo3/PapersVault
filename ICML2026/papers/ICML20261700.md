## Problem

No existing backdoor attack simultaneously achieves one-time poisoning, persistence across all subsequent tasks, and attacker-specified target control in Class-Incremental Learning (CIL). The state-of-the-art LTB attack assumes task-critical neurons remain stable across tasks, but CIL's shared-parameter architecture continuously repurposes neurons, causing all prior baselines to degrade to below 15% ASR after sequential learning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261700/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The attacker provides a local proxy dataset (public or DDPM-generated images, 5,000 per class by default, resized to match the target benchmark resolution), the attacker-specified target class label, and a small subset (ρ=5%) of clean training samples from the chosen poisoning task. The output is a single universal trigger perturbation δ constrained to ‖δ‖∞ ≤ 8/255, which is injected into the selected samples and relabeled to produce the poisoned training set; the same δ must remain effective across all subsequent CIL model states without further modification.

## Method

PBTO exploits the discovery that CIL models preserve task knowledge in shallow, structurally invariant subspaces (retaining >80% CKA similarity across tasks) rather than in individual neurons. Stage 1 (Trajectory Modeling) sequentially trains a surrogate model on M proxy tasks to simulate the CIL parameter evolution, yielding a model-state trajectory Ω without accessing the victim. Stage 2 (Subspace Anchoring) jointly optimizes the trigger via a trajectory-averaged cross-entropy loss (ensuring misclassification to the target label across all surrogate states) and a Gram-matrix regularization term that aligns trigger representations with target-class samples in the penultimate residual block, anchoring the trigger to the invariant shallow subspace. An iterative refinement loop alternates between re-simulating the poisoned trajectory (using the current trigger to construct the poisoned task) and re-optimizing the trigger, closing the gap between clean and poisoned training dynamics until ASR converges.

## Results

After completing all CIL tasks (5 for CIFAR-10, 10 for CIFAR-100 and Tiny-ImageNet), PBTO achieves final ASR of 97.1% on CIFAR-10, 86.5% on CIFAR-100, and 83.8% on Tiny-ImageNet, while maintaining benign accuracy (BA) within 0.7–1.3 points of the unpoisoned baselines. The strongest baseline, DRUPE, reaches only 28.5% ASR on CIFAR-10 and 14.2% on CIFAR-100, and the most advanced persistent attack LTB degrades to 12.4%, 4.7%, and 8.9% ASR on the three datasets respectively. PBTO also transfers across architectures (78.1–82.2% ASR on VGG-16, DenseNet-121, MobileNetV2) and retains 85.9% ASR on the larger VGGFace2 dataset after ten tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PBTO decisively outperforms all backdoor attack baselines on final ASR after sequential CIL: BadNets (12.4%), WaNet (8.5%), DRUPE (28.5%), and LTB (12.4%) on CIFAR-10, and 4.5%, 3.2%, 14.2%, and 4.7% on CIFAR-100, versus PBTO's 97.1% and 86.5%. The additional stealthy baselines ISSBA (4.1%), LIRA (2.8%), and WaveAttack (7.6%) on CIFAR-100 confirm that the failure is not trigger-specific but stems from the lack of a persistence mechanism under shared-parameter updates. LTB, the state-of-the-art persistent attack for TIL, drops to 4.7% ASR on CIFAR-100 because its neuron-isolation assumption breaks under CIL's unified architecture. Against post-training defenses, PBTO retains 88.4% ASR after Fine-Pruning, 82.1% after NAD, 89.5% after BTI-DBF, and 86.2% after REFINE, indicating that anchoring triggers to structurally invariant shallow subspaces makes the backdoor resistant to neuron-pruning and feature-reconstruction defenses that target isolated trigger artifacts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the neuron-isolation paradigm of LTB (Guo et al., 2025), the state-of-the-art persistent backdoor attack for Task-Incremental Learning, by showing that its core assumption—task-critical neurons remain stable across subsequent learning—fundamentally breaks under CIL's shared-parameter architecture. Rather than anchoring triggers to individual high-Fisher neurons, PBTO shifts the anchoring target to structurally invariant subspaces in shallow layers, drawing on CKA, Grassmann distance, and Gram-matrix regularization from representation-similarity and style-transfer literature. The trajectory-simulation component repurposes the standard surrogate-model idea from transferable adversarial attacks, but applies it to an entire CIL parameter trajectory rather than a single model snapshot, which is a novel use of the technique.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a concrete gap: no prior work achieves simultaneous one-time poisoning, persistence, and attacker-specified target control in CIL, and the demonstrated 80+ point ASR gap over the strongest baseline (LTB) shows that CIL systems are far more vulnerable to persistent targeted backdoors than previously recognized. The broader finding that CIL preserves task knowledge in shallow subspace geometry rather than individual neurons is a useful mechanistic insight that may inform both attack design and defense strategies. Open questions include whether the proposed defense directions (shallow-feature randomization, cross-layer consistency checks) can actually neutralize structurally anchored triggers without degrading clean accuracy, and whether the attack generalizes to non-iCaRL CIL methods with stronger forgetting resistance.
