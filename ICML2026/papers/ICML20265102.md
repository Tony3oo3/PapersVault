## Problem

Neural operators perform well on structured grids but degrade on irregular geometries, and the prevailing explanation has been inadequate geometry encoding at the input. This paper identifies a deeper structural cause: the global mixing operations (FFT, self-attention) inherent to spectral and attention-based operator layers progressively erase geometric information as representations propagate through depth, a phenomenon the authors formalize as the Geometric Forgetting Hypothesis via the Data Processing Inequality applied to the Markovian layer structure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265102/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Inputs are a parameter field (Reynolds number, Richardson number, or angle of attack), a signed distance field, a binary domain mask, and coordinate channels, all defined on a fixed Cartesian bounding box that encloses the (possibly irregular) physical domain. Outputs are the steady-state velocity components (u_x, u_y), pressure, and optionally temperature, also on the same grid. Geometry is consumed as a measurable encoding function G_D: X → R^{n_g} (concatenation of mask, SDF, and coordinates) that is available to the model but, in standard architectures, only enters at the input lifting and is never explicitly re-supplied to deeper layers.

## Method

The paper formalizes geometric forgetting by showing that each operator layer O_l defines a Markov chain G → V_l → V_{l+1}, so the Data Processing Inequality guarantees I(G; V_l) is non-increasing with depth, and global mixing layers accelerate this decay. To diagnose forgetting, the authors train lightweight auxiliary decoders to reconstruct the domain mask from each hidden state (macroscopic fidelity) and track spectral power at wavenumbers S/2, S/4, S/8 (boundary information). The proposed remedy, Geometry Memory Injection, modifies the layer update to V_{l+1} = M_l(O_l(V_l), G), where M_l is a FiLM, additive, or concatenation operator that re-injects the geometry encoding at selected depths, breaking the Markov condition with only a constant memory/computation overhead. A key empirical finding is the Geometric Shortcut: under late injection, Transolver's optimizer collapses upstream gradients to zero and exploits the injected geometry to bypass the physics backbone, whereas FNO remains stable, revealing that early geometric integration is a structural stability requirement for attention-based operators. A control study with the Laplace Neural Operator, whose pole-residue formulation intrinsically encodes boundary effects, shows minimal benefit from extrinsic injection, confirming that the forgetting is specific to architectures lacking intrinsic geometric memory.

## Results

The paper evaluates relative L² error on four PDE benchmarks (LDC-NS, LDC-NSHT, AirfRANS, Darcy) for three neural operator backbones. Geometry Memory Injection reduces relative L² error by 39.8–66.6% for FNO (e.g., 3.61e-1 → 1.21e-1 on LDC-NS) and 15.8–57.4% for Transolver (e.g., 3.28e-2 → 1.40e-2 on AirfRANS). In contrast, the Laplace Neural Operator (LNO), which encodes boundary information intrinsically via its pole-residue formulation, sees only 3.0–26.5% improvement, confirming that the gains target specific geometric forgetting rather than generic capacity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper applies its Geometry Memory Injection mechanism to three existing neural operator architectures and compares each against its own no-memory baseline. FNO (Li et al., 2020) benefits most from memory injection, achieving 39.8–66.6% relative L² error reduction across all four datasets, with the largest gain on LDC-NS (3.61e-1 → 1.21e-1). Transolver (Wu et al., 2024) also improves substantially (15.8–57.4%), but the paper reveals a critical instability: injecting geometry at the final layer causes catastrophic error explosion (e.g., 1.72e-1 → 5.48e-1 on LDC-NS), which the authors term the Geometric Shortcut. LNO (Cao et al., 2024) serves as a control case: its pole-residue formulation already encodes boundary information intrinsically, so extrinsic memory injection yields only marginal gains (3.0–26.5%), and in some configurations (e.g., LDC-NSHT) the gain is negligible (2.97%). This contrast validates that the observed improvements in FNO and Transolver specifically target geometric information loss rather than general model capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the neural operator paradigm (FNO, Transolver, LNO) and the growing literature on handling irregular geometries via coordinate transforms, graph operators, and geometry-aware tokens, but departs by shifting the question from *how* to encode geometry at the input to *why* geometry is progressively lost through depth. The theoretical framing draws on the Data Processing Inequality applied to the Markovian layer structure, connecting to over-smoothing results in GNNs (Oono & Suzuki, 2020) and shortcut learning in vision (Geirhos et al., 2020), but applies these ideas to operator architectures for the first time. The LNO control experiment repurposes the Laplace transform's pole-residue structure as evidence of 'intrinsic memory,' distinguishing it from the 'extrinsic' geometry conditioning used in all prior geometry-handling methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Geometric Shortcut finding—that late geometry injection in Transolver causes the optimizer to completely bypass the physics backbone (gradient ratio → 1.0 at final layer, → 0.0 upstream)—is a practically critical warning for anyone deploying transformer-based operators with geometry conditioning, and it implies that injection depth is a stability requirement, not a design preference. The simple FiLM-based memory injection is architecture-agnostic and adds negligible overhead, making it immediately adoptable. Key limitations include restriction to 2D steady-state problems on Cartesian grids with only 4 operator layers, and the theoretical analysis remains qualitative (DPI gives monotonicity but no rate); extending to time-dependent PDEs, 3D domains, and deeper stacks, plus a rigorous Information Bottleneck quantification of forgetting, are the natural next steps.
