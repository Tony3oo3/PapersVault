## Problem

Current computer-aided synthesis planning (CASP) methods treat retrosynthesis as solved once a single feasible route is found, optimizing only for convergence or shortest-path metrics. This is misaligned with real-world practice, where chemists must simultaneously balance competing objectives such as cost, sustainability, toxicity, and yield. The paper addresses this gap by formulating synthesis planning as a multi-objective search problem and providing an algorithm with formal Pareto-optimality guarantees for AND-OR retrosynthesis graphs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261486/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a target molecule, a black-box single-step retrosynthesis predictor (NeuralSym, PDVN, or Graph2Edits), a reaction condition predictor (QUARC) that supplies temperature and reagent identity, and a user-defined set of N_D scalar cost functions (sustainability, toxicity, scale-up potential, plus a guidance objective). It outputs a Pareto front in N_D-dimensional objective space where each point is a unique synthesis route ending in commercially available building blocks, augmented with predicted reaction conditions. The guidance objective steers search toward purchasable starting materials but is excluded from the reported Pareto analysis; all other costs are normalized to [0,1] using 5th/95th percentile bounds.

## Method

MORetro* extends single-objective Retro* (neural-guided A* on AND-OR graphs) to the multi-objective setting via linear weight-based scalarization: N_S weight vectors sampled from the probability simplex each induce an independent scalarized A* search over a shared AND-OR graph, with periodic re-sampling every w_budget iterations. The key sampling innovation is a solution-informed Bayesian optimization scheme using the GIBBON batch acquisition function, which fits a Gaussian Process surrogate to observed hypervolume improvements and selects the next batch of weights to maximize expected information gain while promoting diversity. The paper derives a vector-valued pruning number (pn) and a corresponding V_bound that is component-wise admissible, yielding Theorem 3.4: under admissibility of the heuristic lower bounds, the algorithm provably recovers the true Pareto front, and any frontier molecule satisfying the bound dominance condition can be safely pruned. This pruning mechanism generalizes NAMOA*-style admissibility to the AND-OR structure where reaction nodes require all children to be solved simultaneously. In practice, since the practical heuristics (SAScore, eToxPred, MolPrice) are not provably admissible, the zero vector is used for V_bound, limiting pruning efficiency but preserving correctness.

## Results

On USPTO-190 with three single-step models, MORetro*(BO) consistently achieves the highest hypervolume (HV): 1.04±0.31 (G2E), 0.92±0.37 (PDVN), and 0.56±0.51 (NeuralSym), representing 5–8% HV improvements over both Retro* and the Fixed-weight baseline (all p < 0.01, Wilcoxon). Success rate is largely preserved, with MORetro*(BO) failing on only 2–3 additional molecules out of 190 compared to Retro*, while the Fixed baseline drops by up to 29%. In terms of chemical diversity, MORetro*(BO) achieves the highest maximum dissimilarity (e.g., 0.84 vs. 0.80 for G2E) and diversity fraction (0.49 vs. 0.43), confirming that multi-objective search naturally yields more varied synthesis routes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MORetro*(BO) outperforms the single-objective Retro* baseline on all three single-step models in both HV and R2, with statistically significant gains (p < 0.01) and dominance of 18–36% of Retro*'s Pareto-optimal routes while having only 2–8% of its own routes dominated in return. Against the Fixed-weight scalarization baseline (w = [0.2, 0.2, 0.2, 0.4]), MORetro*(BO) shows even larger HV margins (e.g., 1.04 vs. 0.97 for G2E; 0.92 vs. 0.74 for PDVN), and the advantage is most pronounced for PDVN where the Fixed baseline's success rate collapses to 69.8% versus 96.8% for MORetro*(BO). The Fixed baseline's poor performance is attributed to its inability to adaptively balance the guidance objective, whereas MORetro*(BO) dynamically re-samples weights via Bayesian optimization to emphasize convergence when needed. A comparison to a modified MO-MCTS variant (Lai et al., 2025) is provided in the appendix but the authors note it was not hyperparameter-tuned, so no definitive conclusions are drawn. Overall, MORetro*(BO) wins on Pareto front quality, diversity, and success rate across all tested configurations, with the only trade-off being a marginal 2–3% drop in success rate relative to single-objective Retro*.

## Relation to Prior Work

MORetro* extends the single-objective Retro* A* search (Chen et al., 2020) and its AND-OR graph formulation from RetroGraph (Xie et al., 2022) to the multi-objective setting via linear weight scalarization, while generalizing the admissibility framework of NAMOA* (Mandow & De La Cruz, 2010) to handle the joint dependencies inherent in AND-OR retrosynthesis graphs. It departs from the post-hoc multi-objective optimization paradigm (Fromer & Coley, 2024) by embedding objectives directly into the search, and from the concurrent MO-MCTS approach (Lai et al., 2025) by providing Pareto-optimality guarantees and a deterministic (non-stochastic) search procedure. The integration of a reaction condition predictor (QUARC) directly into the search loop, enabling objectives like toxicity and scale-up potential, is a practical departure from prior CASP methods that treat condition prediction as a separate downstream step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work bridges a significant gap between CASP's current 'find any route' paradigm and the multi-criteria decision-making that chemists actually perform, providing the first Pareto-optimality guarantee for multi-objective retrosynthesis on AND-OR graphs. However, the practical impact is tempered by the fact that the heuristics used are not admissible, forcing the pruning bound to fall back to the zero vector and reducing the theoretical guarantee to a correctness (not efficiency) statement. Open questions include scaling beyond three objectives, handling non-additive cost structures, and constructing tight admissible lower bounds for practical objectives like toxicity and sustainability—without which the pruning mechanism remains largely theoretical.
