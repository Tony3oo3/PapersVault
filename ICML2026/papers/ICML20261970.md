## Problem

Existing VLM game-based benchmarks report aggregate performance but conflate failures in visual perception, rule comprehension, and logical reasoning, making it impossible to attribute errors to their true source. This paper addresses that gap by asking: when a VLM fails a game task, is it because it misperceived the board, misunderstood the rules, or failed to reason over correctly perceived states?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261970/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Perception tests take a single rendered game board image (chess 8×8, Gomoku 15×15, or Xiangqi at 384–1024 px, in 2D flat or 3D style) and a text prompt requesting a numeric JSON matrix; rule-following tests take one or more sequential board images plus a two-phase prompt (verification question followed by a rule-judgment question). Outputs are board-state matrices (piece-encoded integers per cell), yes/no/unknown legality judgments, and diagnostic metrics including piece accuracy, verification rate, and verified reasoning accuracy. A text-only ablation replaces images with FEN strings while keeping the reasoning question identical.

## Method

The framework decomposes VLM performance into two independently testable stages. Stage 1 isolates perception through four controlled tests (density, patch alignment, resolution divisibility, visual richness) and quantifies spatial error structure via Hungarian matching with Chebyshev distance ≤1, computing a dominance ratio and an adjacent same-direction ratio benchmarked against a 200-simulation Monte Carlo permutation baseline. Stage 2 evaluates rule following via a 2×2 diagnostic matrix (single/multi-state × rule-free/rule-based) and a six-level complexity ladder, each level tested in Explicit mode (verify an observed outcome) and Predictive mode (predict an unobserved outcome). A critical design choice is two-phase gating: reasoning accuracy is computed only on cases where the model's board-state perception was verified correct, cleanly separating perceptual from reasoning failures. A matched-sample intersection (cases passing verification in both modes) controls for selection bias when comparing Explicit versus Predictive performance.

## Results

On perception (Table 3), GPT-5.2 achieves 97–99% piece accuracy on chess and 55–80% on Gomoku, while Qwen3-VL 235B reaches 95–99% and 72–84% respectively; Gemma-3 27B lags severely (19–62% chess, 5–10% Gomoku). On rule-following (Table 4), GPT-5.2 attains 92.5–100% verified accuracy across all four diagnostic quadrants, with Qwen3-VL 235B close behind (91.7–100%). The headline diagnostic findings are: (1) adjacent same-direction shift ratios of 1.5–1.9× the Monte Carlo independence baseline across all models; (2) a perception-reasoning dissociation where Qwen3-VL 235B maintains ~90% verification but drops to 56% reasoning accuracy at Level 4; and (3) a simulation gap of up to 26.7 percentage points (75.9% Explicit vs. 49.2% Predictive at Level 6) that persists under CoT prompting and text-only input.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a diagnostic benchmark paper comparing six VLMs (Qwen3-VL 8B/30B/235B, GPT-5.2, GLM-4.1V 9B, Gemma-3 27B) rather than proposing a new method against baselines. GPT-5.2 and Qwen3-VL 235B are the only models that maintain adequate verification rates (>77%) through Level 6 of the complexity ladder; Gemma-3 27B and GLM-4.1V 9B drop below 3% verification at Level 6, precluding meaningful rule-following assessment. Within the Qwen3-VL family, scaling from 30B to 235B yields no systematic advantage—235B actually underperforms 30B in Explicit mode at Levels 1, 2, and 5—indicating the bottleneck is not model capacity. All six models exhibit the same three failure patterns (coordinated spatial drift, perception-reasoning dissociation, and simulation gap) regardless of scale, architecture, or prompting strategy, confirming these are structural limitations of current VLMs rather than artifacts of any single model or evaluation protocol.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from existing game-based VLM benchmarks (Wang et al. 2025a, Ren et al. 2025, Zheng et al. 2025) that report aggregate performance metrics without isolating distinct failure modes. While prior diagnostic work (PuzzleVQA, unit-test frameworks) has begun to separate subtasks, this paper treats failure attribution as the primary design objective, introducing a two-stage framework that gates reasoning evaluation on verified perception and a six-level complexity ladder with explicit/predictive mode comparison. It repurposes game environments not as end applications but as controlled probes with fully specified rules and unambiguous ground truth, drawing on the broader literature on VLM spatial reasoning deficits and multimodal integration weaknesses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of a perception-reasoning dissociation (perception stable at ~90% while reasoning drops to 56%) and a persistent simulation gap (up to 27 points) that neither scaling, text-only input, nor CoT resolves provides a concrete diagnostic vocabulary for understanding VLM failures in structured visual domains beyond games, including document understanding and robotic manipulation. The coordinated spatial drift finding (1.5–1.9× independence baseline) suggests visual encoding errors are systematic regional displacements rather than independent noise, with implications for how patch-based ViT architectures process spatial information. Open questions include whether these findings generalize to non-grid tasks (perspective-taking, embodied reasoning), whether coordinated shifts are an artifact of patch-based tokenization, and what mechanistic interventions could close the simulation gap.
