## Problem

Current Video LLMs achieve high scores on standard benchmarks while failing at elementary temporal perception tasks such as distinguishing motion direction or detecting physics violations. The paper argues this stems from two coupled failure modes—static-cue dominance, where appearance and context substitute for spatiotemporal evidence, and prior-driven temporal hallucination, where learned event scripts override observed dynamics—enabled by benchmarks that can be solved without reliably perceiving pixel-level motion and by architectures that compress temporal information into static frame representations.

## Input / Output

The paper probes existing Video LLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Pro, InternVL3-8B, VideoLLaMA3-7B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B) with short video clips—3-second collision videos from AVoE sampled at 16 frames and IntPhys2 teacup-rotation clips at 8 fps—paired with either a binary Yes/No prompt (e.g., 'Does the left object change direction after collision?') or an open-ended description request. Outputs are a single binary judgment or a free-text scene description, which are then checked for factual accuracy against ground-truth motion trajectories and state transitions.

## Method

As a position paper, the core contribution is a unified two-mode failure taxonomy (static-cue dominance and prior-driven temporal hallucination) that maps 16 recent diagnostic benchmarks to specific blind spots, revealing that no single probe captures both failure modes simultaneously. The authors conduct small-scale controlled experiments on AVoE collision videos and IntPhys2 rotation clips showing that even frontier models perform at or below chance on simple motion-direction tasks, confirming the failures are architectural rather than dataset-specific. The paper then prescribes three minimal evaluation requirements—temporal sensitivity (performance must degrade under frame shuffling or reversal), evidence localization (models must identify when supporting evidence occurs in the video), and pixel-level verifiability (temporal and causal claims must be explicitly checkable against observable motion)—along with architectural recommendations for motion-preserving representations (optical flow, trajectory tokens, temporal derivatives) and structurally enforced spatiotemporal grounding that constrains generation by visual evidence rather than language priors.

## Results

On the AVoE collision probe (16 sampled frames, binary Yes/No task), all evaluated Video LLMs perform near-random: GPT-4o scores 59.0% on expected and 47.0% on surprising clips, Gemini-2.5-Pro scores 57.0%/63.0%, and Qwen2.5-VL-7B scores 60.0%/62.0%, all within a few points of the 50% random baseline. On MotionBench fine-grained motion queries (0–4 s clips), models perform below random at 11–14% accuracy where chance is 25%. On Minimal Video Pairs (MVP), where appearance is held fixed and only temporal trajectory varies, proprietary models like Gemini-1.5 Pro and GPT-4o perform below chance, confirming that static cues dominate over pixel dynamics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper, so there is no single proposed method to benchmark; instead, the paper evaluates six Video LLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Pro, InternVL3-8B, VideoLLaMA3-7B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B) against a random baseline on diagnostic motion probes. No model reliably outperforms chance: on the AVoE surprising-clips split, GPT-4o (47.0%) and VideoLLaMA3-7B (46.0%) actually fall below the 50% random baseline, while Gemini-2.5-Pro (63.0%) and Qwen2.5-VL-7B (62.0%) show modest gains that the authors attribute to prior-driven hallucination rather than genuine motion tracking. Across the broader diagnostic suite (TVBench, MVP, MotionBench, FAVOR-Bench), the consistent finding is that performance does not degrade under frame shuffling or reversal, indicating that no current model—regardless of scale or architecture—reliably uses temporal order as a discriminative signal. The paper's central claim is therefore not that one model beats another, but that the entire class of Video LLMs fails to ground its outputs in pixel dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not introduce a new method or benchmark but synthesizes a growing body of diagnostic probes (TRoVe, TVBench, TempCompass, MotionBench, FAVOR-Bench, MVP, VBenchComp, and others) into a unified two-mode failure taxonomy for Video LLMs. It draws a direct conceptual parallel to the VLM diagnostic literature (Winoground, bag-of-words analyses by Yuksekgonul et al.), arguing that the 'bag-of-words' failure in image VLMs has escalated to a 'bag-of-frames' failure in video. Architecturally, it traces the root cause to the dominant CLIP-encoder + linear/Q-Former fusion + LLM backbone paradigm, citing prior analyses (Jiang et al., Shi et al., Ding & Wang) showing that this pipeline systematically compresses or defers temporal information to the language model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that current Video LLM benchmark scores are largely insensitive to temporal structure—challenges the field's primary progress metric and has direct implications for safety-critical deployments (autonomous driving, healthcare monitoring) where fabricated temporal narratives can cause harm. Key open questions remain: whether stronger video-native encoders and denser sampling will eventually make temporal evidence unavoidable, whether the LLM backbone is fundamentally incompatible with fine-grained dynamics, and how to operationalize the proposed evaluation standards (temporal sensitivity tests, evidence localization, pixel-level verifiability) at scale. The paper's own experimental evidence is limited to small-scale probes (100 AVoE clips, qualitative IntPhys2 examples), so the taxonomy's generality rests heavily on the cited diagnostic literature rather than new large-scale experiments.
