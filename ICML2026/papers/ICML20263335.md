## Problem

Standard Transformers impose a fixed maximum computational path length bounded by model depth, confining them to the uniform TC⁰ complexity class and limiting their ability to solve inherently sequential reasoning problems. While approaches like Chain-of-Thought or looped Transformers increase computation, the paper argues that the critical bottleneck is effective depth (the longest latent computation path) rather than total FLOPs, and asks whether depth can be extended with negligible additional computation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263335/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard text token sequences and produces next-token probability distributions via the unchanged LM head, trained with the conventional autoregressive cross-entropy loss. The unusual aspect is internal: during the forward pass, hidden states from higher layers of token t are injected into lower layers of token t+1, so the model's internal computation graph extends across both the layer and token dimensions while the external I/O interface remains identical to a standard Transformer. A grouping parameter g controls how many tokens are processed in parallel before a cross-token dependency is resolved, trading sequential depth for throughput.

## Method

TurboConn adds a set of downward residual connections from higher-layer hidden states of token t to lower-layer inputs of token t+1, each implemented as a zero-initialized learnable linear projection scaled by a multiplier α (default 100). The t→t+1 (rather than t→t) design exploits the autoregressive causal mask to avoid circular dependencies in the computational graph, allowing the effective reasoning depth to scale linearly with sequence length (kL vs. L) at only a marginal FLOP increase. A grouping mechanism processes tokens in blocks of size g to recover partial parallelism, reducing sequential steps from k to k/g at the cost of proportionally less depth. The architecture is drop-in compatible with pretrained LLMs via LoRA fine-tuning, requires no loss-function modification, and the paper shows that dense multi-layer connections significantly outperform a single 'soft-token' feedback baseline, confirming that the density of backward information flow is critical for unlocking depth-based reasoning gains.

## Results

TurboConn achieves 100% accuracy on the Parity task across all three model sizes (Llama 3.2 1B, Llama 3.1 8B, Qwen 3 1.7B), with the most dramatic gain being Qwen 3 1.7B jumping from 53.78% (baseline) to 100%. On Multi-step Arithmetic, TurboConn yields gains of 4.5–9.7 percentage points (e.g., 36.10%→45.81% for Qwen 3 1.7B). On GSM8K (No CoT), improvements are more modest, ranging from 0.9% (Llama 3.1 8B: 23.92%→24.82%) to 4.4% (Qwen 3 1.7B: 15.90%→20.31%). The 1B model with TurboConn outperforms the 8B model without connections on Parity, suggesting architectural depth can compensate for parameter scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the standard Transformer architecture (same pre-trained model, same LoRA fine-tuning setup, no downward connections). TurboConn consistently outperforms this baseline across all model sizes and all three reasoning tasks, with the largest absolute gain of +46.22% on Parity (Qwen 3 1.7B). A second comparison is against a single 'soft-token' feedback method (Table 3), which passes only one aggregated vector from the top layer back to the input embedding. TurboConn outperforms soft-token on all tasks: Parity 100% vs. 96.51%, Multi-step Arithmetic 42.66% vs. 38.00%, and GSM8K 8.32% vs. 7.07% (Llama 3.2 1B), confirming that dense multi-layer connections are necessary rather than a single sparse feedback signal. Conceptually, the paper also contrasts with Universal Transformers (Section 3.5), arguing TurboConn achieves comparable depth scaling with significantly lower inference latency (no per-token recursive passes) and no additional memory overhead during autoregressive generation.

## Relation to Prior Work

TurboConn operationalizes the 'back-patching' insight from Biran et al. (2024), which showed that manually injecting a higher-layer hidden state into a lower layer can correct multi-hop reasoning failures, by integrating such top-to-bottom connections directly into the architecture. It departs from Universal/Looped Transformers, which increase depth through per-token recursion, by instead scaling depth across the sequence dimension (cross-token), and from Chain-of-Thought, which increases effective depth through explicit token generation, by achieving similar depth scaling through latent hidden-state routing. The design also draws on earlier top-down feedback mechanisms (Fan et al., 2021; Ju et al., 2022) but is uniquely positioned to augment existing pre-trained LLMs via zero-initialized additive connections, avoiding the need for from-scratch training or curriculum learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides strong empirical evidence that fixed computational depth is a genuine bottleneck for LLM reasoning, and that a simple architectural modification can unlock substantial gains without increasing autoregressive generation latency or GPU memory. The dramatic Parity result (53.78% → 100% for Qwen3-1.7B) and the finding that a 1B TurboConn model outperforms an 8B baseline on that task suggest that information-flow architecture can matter more than raw parameter scale for certain reasoning dimensions. Key open questions include whether the method integrates effectively into pre-training (all experiments are fine-tuning only), how optimal wiring configurations scale with model depth, and whether the gains on synthetic tasks (Parity, multi-step arithmetic) transfer to open-ended reasoning. The sequential processing requirement during training and prefill remains a practical limitation for very long sequences.
