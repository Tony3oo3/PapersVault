## Problem

Tabular regression in data-scarce, noisy settings lacks principled augmentation techniques: vision/NLP methods rely on label-preserving symmetries that don't exist for continuous targets, and existing tabular approaches (interpolation, generative models) either destroy instance-specific noise structure or treat the target as just another column. The paper asks whether one can synthesize new regression samples that faithfully preserve each sample's residual while varying selected features, grounded in a formal invariance condition.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a tabular dataset (numeric features + continuous target) and a pre-trained baseline regressor (e.g., MLP or XGBoost), along with three augmentation hyperparameters (perturbation range p, samples-per-point factor M, and max features to perturb). It produces a set of synthetic (x', y') pairs where x' has perturbed features and y' = ĝ(x') + ẑᵢ, plus a retrained regressor on the combined data. A built-in Wilcoxon signed-rank safety gate can reject the augmentation entirely if it does not yield statistically significant validation improvement, in which case the original baseline is returned unchanged.

## Method

CRDA's core mechanism is residual-preserving counterfactual generation: after training a baseline regressor ĝ, it computes per-sample residuals ẑᵢ = yᵢ − ĝ(xᵢ), then identifies a subset of features X_P whose perturbation leaves the residual distribution unchanged (Assumption 3.1: Z ⊥ X_P | X_R) using a two-stage filter (PC-algorithm causal graph check followed by Pearson correlation screening). For each training point, it multiplicatively perturbs X_P by a uniform factor (1+δ), δ~Unif[−p,p], keeps X_R fixed, and constructs the counterfactual label as y' = ĝ(x') + ẑᵢ, thereby reusing the instance-specific noise rather than resetting it to zero. This mirrors Pearl's abduction–action–prediction template: abduct the noise from the observation, intervene on selected features, and predict with the same noise. A final K-fold cross-validation with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test acts as a safety gate, ensuring augmentation is only accepted when it produces a statistically significant MSE reduction, protecting against cases where the base model is too weak for the residual-invariance assumption to hold empirically.

## Results

Across nine tabular regression benchmarks, CRDA achieves an average MSE reduction of 22.9% for MLP Regressor and 6.4% for XGBoost Regressor compared to unaugmented baselines. The largest gains appear on data-scarce settings: MLP on Parkinson's Monitoring (−47.2%), Energy Efficiency (−43.0%), Synthetic Regression (−42.3%), and House Price (−40.6%). A synthetic sample-size-scaling experiment reveals a 'sweet spot' between 2.5k and 20k samples where CRDA provides the greatest benefit, with diminishing returns at very low (base model too weak) or very high (baseline already saturated) sample sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CRDA is the only method that reliably reduces MSE (negative Δ%) for both XGBoost and MLP across nearly all 18 dataset–model combinations, whereas every competing method exhibits catastrophic failure modes on at least one dataset. Deep generative models (TabDDPM, TVAE, CTGAN) are the most unstable: CTGAN inflates MSE by +3032.9% on House Price (MLP) and +1454.2% on Parkinson's Monitoring (MLP), while TVAE reaches +992.5% and +659.0% on the same tasks. Geometric augmentation methods (C-Mixup, ADA) are more moderate but still fail badly in some regimes—C-Mixup increases MSE by +141.5% on Synthetic Regression (XGB) and +116.1% on Parkinson's (MLP). CRDA's two narrow losses are on Energy Efficiency (XGB: ADA achieves −20.7% vs. CRDA's −10.7%) and House Price (XGB: ADA achieves −42.9% vs. CRDA's −12.8%), both cases where ADA's local-linearity assumption happens to hold well. Overall, CRDA's residual-preserving mechanism provides the best worst-case guarantee: it never degrades performance by more than +2.3% (Synthetic Regression, XGB), while competitors routinely exceed +100% degradation.

## Relation to Prior Work

CRDA sits at the intersection of causal counterfactual reasoning (Pearl's abduction-action-prediction template) and tabular data augmentation, repurposing the residual-bootstrapping mechanism from statistical uncertainty quantification into a generative augmentation procedure. It departs from geometric augmentation methods (C-Mixup, ADA) that enforce local linearity or manifold-density constraints, instead enforcing a statistical regularity—residual invariance under perturbation of selected features. It also differs from concurrent causal augmentation work (Akbar et al., 2025) that assumes a known symmetry of the predictor, and from deep generative tabular models (CTGAN, TVAE, TabDDPM) that treat the target as just another column and lose instance-specific noise structure. The closest conceptual ancestor is Lu et al. (2020) in reinforcement learning, which showed that reusing a fixed noise quantile under a 'what-if' intervention yields a valid counterfactual next-state.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CRDA offers a simple, model-agnostic, and safety-gated remedy for the common practical problem of small-sample tabular regression, requiring no domain-specific transformations and no generative model training. Its built-in Wilcoxon signed-rank test ensures the method either improves or abstains, which is valuable for high-stakes deployments. Key open questions include extending the framework to classification (where residuals are not naturally defined), handling unobserved confounders more gracefully (e.g., via FCI rather than discarding confounded features), and understanding how the 'sweet spot' behavior—where CRDA helps most at intermediate sample sizes—interacts with increasingly powerful base learners. The method's dependence on a sufficiently accurate base predictor to produce meaningful residuals remains a practical constraint.
