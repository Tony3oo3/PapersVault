## Problem

Processing worldwide OpenStreetMap data into analysis-ready road network graphs is computationally prohibitive (1.1TB raw data, >1 billion vertices), and existing graph learning benchmarks operate at hundreds-of-nodes scale, failing to expose the scalability limitations of modern GNNs on real-world road topology. No pre-processed, structured, publicly accessible billion-scale road network dataset existed to serve as a unified infrastructure for reproducible city-wide urban research.

## Input / Output

The pipeline ingests raw Geofabrik OSM .pbf files (continental, >100GB each) and produces six structured tables on MaxCompute/ODPS: three full-OSM tables (POI nodes, building footprints, administrative boundaries) and three roadnet-only tables (1.9B vertices, 2.0B split edges, 202M fulltag edges). Downstream consumption is via SQL-style spatial queries specifying coordinates or bounding boxes, with the system automatically locating relevant shards and executing in parallel—eliminating the need for local preprocessing. For the traffic prediction benchmark, UTD19 sensor coordinates are projected to the nearest OSM+ road segment via deterministic nearest-edge matching, yielding 31 city-level graphs (up to ~30,000 nodes) paired with flow observations at horizons 3, 6, and 12.

## Method

OSM+ is constructed through a distributed cloud pipeline (5,000 cores) that filters road-related linestrings, removes invalid/redundant segments, and splits road geometries at intersections and endpoints into elementary start-end pairs, producing a graph G=(V,E) where vertices are topological points and edges preserve references to original OSM objects and attributes. Efficient spatial access is enabled by window-check grid partitioning (0.2°×0.2° cells) for point queries and k-d trees for nearest-neighbor queries, both running on the ODPS distributed engine to avoid out-of-memory failures on commodity hardware. For traffic prediction, the paper aligns UTD19 observations to OSM+ roadnets and evaluates seven baselines (AGCRN, Crossformer, DCRNN, DLinear, FEDformer, GWNet, MTGNN) across 31 cities, revealing that no single model dominates and that several GNNs hit 24GB GPU memory limits at city scale. For traffic policy control, OSM+ roadnets are converted to CBEngine simulator format with generated demand profiles, producing six city scenarios (up to 18,948 intersections, 130,851 vehicles) for multi-agent signal control evaluation.

## Results

On the 31-city OSM+(UTD19) traffic prediction benchmark, no single method dominates: Crossformer achieves the most wins (10 MAE, 10 MAPE), followed by GWNet (9 MAE, 8 MAPE) and MTGNN (4 MAE, 8 MAPE). DCRNN and AGCRN frequently exceed the 24 GB GPU memory limit (OOM) on larger city graphs, while DLinear never OOMs and still secures 4 MAE wins. Compared to the PEMS benchmark, OSM+(UTD19) exhibits far greater variance in MAPE (over-worst ratio of 132.46 vs. 1.49) and a much higher fraction of extreme errors (64.62% vs. 44.08% over-worst), confirming that the new benchmark is substantially more challenging for scalability and robustness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates seven baselines (AGCRN, Crossformer, DCRNN, DLinear, FEDformer, GWNet, MTGNN) on the new 31-city OSM+(UTD19) benchmark rather than proposing a new method. Crossformer and GWNet emerge as the strongest overall, with Crossformer winning 10/31 cities on both MAE and MAPE and GWNet winning 9 MAE and 8 MAPE. DCRNN is the weakest performer, achieving zero wins and suffering OOM on 10 of 31 cities due to its diffusion-based graph convolution scaling poorly with graph size. AGCRN also OOMs on 5 cities, while DLinear—despite its simplicity—never OOMs and still wins 4 cities on MAE, highlighting a scalability-accuracy trade-off. MTGNN shows strong MAPE performance (8 wins) but is less consistent on MAE (4 wins). The key finding is that the OSM+ benchmark exposes scalability bottlenecks invisible on smaller datasets like PEMS: methods that perform comparably on PEMS diverge sharply on city-scale graphs, with memory-hungry architectures (DCRNN, AGCRN) failing outright on the largest networks (~30,000 nodes).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the data-infrastructure level rather than extending a specific method lineage. It repurposes existing OpenStreetMap data (via Geofabrik mirrors) and existing traffic-prediction baselines (AGCRN, DCRNN, GWNet, MTGNN, Crossformer, FEDformer, DLinear) but departs from prior OSM processing tools (OSMnx, Osmosis, Overpass API) by performing a one-time distributed cloud preprocessing (5,000 cores) into a structured billion-vertex graph, eliminating the per-user pipeline that prior tools require. For traffic prediction, it extends the evaluation setting from the saturated METR-LA/PEMS benchmarks (hundreds of sensors) to a 31-city benchmark with up to ~30,000 road-network nodes, and for traffic control it scales from the 16-intersection Hangzhou or 2,510-intersection Manhattan scenarios to six cities with up to 18,948 intersections using the CBData/CBEngine simulation framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete reproducibility bottleneck in urban computing: researchers building incompatible road-network graphs from the same OSM source, hindering cross-study comparison and multi-modal foundation-model training. The widespread OOM failures of DCRNN and AGCRN on city-scale graphs (24 GB GPU limit) provide a concrete, reproducible demonstration of the scalability wall for standard GNNs, motivating future work on memory-efficient or hierarchical graph architectures. Key open questions include whether the sensor-to-road matching noise (inherent to projecting UTD19 sensors onto OSM segments) systematically biases results in sparsely-mapped regions, and whether the 31-city benchmark's extreme MAPE values (e.g., 751% for Toulouse) reflect genuine model failure or data-quality artifacts that need stratified reporting.
