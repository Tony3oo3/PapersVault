## Problem

SGLD-Gibbs is widely used for scalable Bayesian inference in latent variable models, but there is no principled guidance on how to tune its hyperparameters (step size, minibatch size, inverse temperature, preconditioner) to ensure the resulting uncertainty estimates are statistically meaningful. Existing scaling-limit theory for stochastic gradient methods does not extend to latent variable models, where intermittent Gibbs updates introduce an additional source of algorithm-induced variability that fundamentally alters the noise structure of the global parameters.

## Input / Output

The method consumes observed data {X_i} with associated latent variables z_i, a model likelihood p(X_i, z_i | θ) with prior π_0(θ), and four hyperparameters: step size h, minibatch size b, inverse temperature β, and a positive-definite preconditioner Γ. It produces posterior samples of the global parameter θ and latent variables z_i, whose stationary covariance is explicitly characterized by the scaling-limit theory. The practical output is calibrated uncertainty (verified via rank-uniformity diagnostics) and predictive performance (ARI/AMI for clustering, held-out perplexity for topic models), with the key distinction that the stationary covariance decomposes into a model-based component, a sampling-based component, and an algorithm-induced component that scales as 1/S where S is the number of Gibbs draws per iteration.

## Method

The paper derives a joint scaling-limit theorem (Theorem 3.1) showing that under polynomial space-time rescaling, the global parameter process converges to an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck diffusion while each latent variable converges to a Poisson-driven jump process with rate λ = c_b, and the two limiting processes are independent. The critical new element is the latent-involved Fisher information matrix Ĩ_★ = I_★ + M_★, where the Jensen gap M_★ quantifies the extra variability from estimating the marginal likelihood with a single Gibbs sample; this term replaces I_★ in the diffusion coefficient of the OU limit. For generalized Gibbs approximation with S conditional samples per iteration, the gap shrinks to M_★/S, yielding an explicit cost-accuracy trade-off. The theory yields two concrete tuning strategies: setting Γ = (Ĩ_★^(S))^{-1} recovers Bernstein-von Mises posterior covariance, while Γ = J_★^{-1} produces a bagged-posterior covariance with an isolated algorithmic-variability term (w_1/S) J_★^{-1} M_★ J_★^{-1} that vanishes as S → ∞. Empirically, SGLD-Gibbs with these tunings achieves substantially better-calibrated uncertainty and higher clustering/predictive accuracy than stochastic variational inference on both synthetic and real datasets.

## Results

On the Flow Cytometry GMM task, SGLD-Gibbs achieves ARI 0.80 and AMI 0.82, substantially outperforming SVI (ARI 0.47, AMI 0.68). On the 20 Newsgroups LDA task, SGRLD-Gibbs achieves held-out perplexity of 2052, compared to 2402 for SVI (lower is better). On synthetic experiments, SGLD-Gibbs also produces better-calibrated posterior uncertainty (empirical ranks closer to uniform) and tighter posterior marginals around ground-truth parameters than SVI, with increasing the number of Gibbs draws S from 1 to 10 (GMM) or 1 to 5 (LDA) further reducing posterior uncertainty as predicted by the scaling-limit theory.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is stochastic variational inference (SVI, Hoffman et al. 2013), applied with a standard mean-field variational family. SGLD-Gibbs wins decisively on all reported metrics: on Flow Cytometry it improves ARI from 0.47 to 0.80 (+70% relative) and AMI from 0.68 to 0.82 (+21% relative); on 20 Newsgroups it reduces perplexity from 2402 to 2052 (≈15% improvement). On synthetic Gaussian mixture and LDA experiments, SGLD-Gibbs also outperforms SVI on rank-uniformity calibration (empirical ranks lie closer to the uniform reference line) and on posterior marginal accuracy (concentrating more tightly around ground-truth values). The paper notes that SVI exhibits noticeable miscalibration and bias in several parameter coordinates, while SGLD-Gibbs with the proposed tuning guidance yields well-calibrated uncertainty. Overall, SGLD-Gibbs retains the scalability of SVI while providing superior task-specific performance and more reliable uncertainty quantification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the scaling-limit theory for SGLD established by Negrea et al. (2023) to the latent variable setting, where the global-parameter dynamics are coupled with intermittent Gibbs updates of local latent variables. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of a joint jump-diffusion limit: the global parameters converge to an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process whose diffusion coefficient is inflated by a 'Jensen gap' term (M_*) capturing the extra variability from estimating the marginal likelihood with a single Gibbs draw, while each latent variable converges to an independent Poisson-driven jump process. The tuning framework also builds on the bagged posterior (Huggins & Miller, 2024) to incorporate algorithmic variability into the target covariance, and the SGLD update rule itself follows Welling & Teh (2011).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled, scaling-limit-based guidance for tuning SGLD-Gibbs hyperparameters (step size, minibatch size, inverse temperature, preconditioner) to target specific uncertainty quantities (Bernstein-von Mises, bagged posterior, or sandwich covariance), filling a gap where practitioners previously relied on ad hoc choices. A significant limitation is that the theory only covers models with a single local latent variable per observation refreshed by Gibbs; it does not extend to shared or globally coupled latent variables (e.g., Bayesian matrix factorization, hierarchical topic models, mixed-effects models), which would require capturing richer latent-parameter interactions and may yield qualitatively different limiting dynamics. Open questions include how the framework behaves when latent-variable updates do not mix rapidly, and how scaling-limit-based tuning compares to alternative algorithmic criteria such as KSD-based strategies.
