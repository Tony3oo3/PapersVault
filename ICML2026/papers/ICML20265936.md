## Problem

Implicit Neural Representation (INR) methods for accelerated MRI reconstruction require per-scan optimization from random initialization, leading to slow convergence (hours for 3D) and unstable performance at high acceleration factors. Existing meta-learning approaches for INR initialization depend on fully-sampled ground-truth images, which are impractical to acquire in clinical settings. This paper asks whether effective INR initializations can be learned directly from undersampled k-space measurements alone, without any reference images.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265936/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-coil undersampled k-space data (S_j = MFC_j I + e_j) as input, where the sampling mask M, coil sensitivity maps C_j, and acceleration factor vary per task. The INR backbone (DINER, SIREN, or HASH) maps 2D normalized spatial coordinates p ∈ [-1,1]×[-1,1] to real and imaginary pixel values via two separate MLPs. At inference, only 75–150 gradient steps from the meta-initialized parameters are needed to produce the final complex-valued MR image, compared to thousands of steps from random initialization. The framework handles 2D (256×256) and 3D (slice-by-slice via 1D-IFT) acquisitions across Cartesian, radial, Poisson, and Gaussian sampling patterns.

## Method

IPOD adapts the Reptile meta-learning algorithm to an inverse-problem-driven setting: the inner loop minimizes a physics-informed loss (k-space data consistency + total variation regularization) on diverse undersampled reconstruction tasks rather than regressing toward fully-sampled ground truth. A key mechanism is an adaptive weighting scheme that assigns each task a weight proportional to the inverse of its reconstruction loss (w_n ∝ 1/(L_n + ε)), preventing severely ill-posed problems with ambiguous solution spaces from corrupting the meta-parameter update. The meta-optimization then performs a weighted Reptile step: Θ_m ← Θ_m + Nα Σ w_n(Θ_n − Θ_m). The resulting meta-initialized parameters serve as a plug-in starting point for any INR backbone, enabling 2×–3× faster convergence and higher final PSNR across unseen anatomies, contrasts, sampling patterns, and scanner environments without requiring any fully-sampled training data.

## Results

IPOD consistently improves PSNR across all three INR backbones (DINER, SIREN, HASH) on fastMRI and MoDL datasets under both Cartesian and radial sampling. The largest gains appear for SIREN on the out-of-domain MoDL dataset, where IPOD-SIREN achieves 59.18 dB (Cartesian AF=3) and 53.69 dB (Cartesian AF=4) versus 52.24 dB and 41.77 dB without IPOD — a ~7–12 dB improvement. On fastMRI brain radial sampling (AF=10), IPOD lifts DINER from 36.73 to 39.69 dB and SIREN from 32.73 to 37.23 dB. IPOD-initialized models also achieve 2×–3× faster reconstruction (e.g., SIREN: 116.7 s → 37.8 s per slice) while matching or exceeding the quality of supervised E2E-VN in-domain and significantly outperforming it under out-of-distribution conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the three INR backbones without IPOD, the method wins on nearly all settings: SIREN benefits most (up to +12 dB on MoDL Cartesian AF=4), DINER shows moderate gains (up to +6 dB on MoDL Radial AF=14), and HASH shows smaller but consistent improvements (up to +2.4 dB on Knee Cartesian AF=3), with one exception where HASH slightly drops on Brain fastMRI Cartesian AF=3 (60.68→59.88). In the broader comparison (Table 7, in-domain AF=8 Cartesian), IPOD-initialized backbones (34.2–35.5 dB PSNR) outperform all self-supervised baselines (ConvDecoder 32.43, SSDU 34.27) and classical methods (SENSE-TV 25.32, GRAPPA 25.07), approaching supervised E2E-VN (37.36 dB) without requiring fully-sampled training data. Under the strict out-of-distribution test (Table 8, unseen Lesion Brain + Poisson AF=10), IPOD methods (33.6–34.3 dB) decisively beat all baselines, while E2E-VN collapses to 22.95 dB due to domain shift. In terms of speed, IPOD-initialized INRs achieve 2×–3× faster reconstruction than their random-init counterparts (e.g., SIREN 116.7 s→37.8 s, DINER 13.5 s→5.8 s), though they remain slower per-slice than supervised E2E-VN inference (1.3 s) but avoid its 16.4 h training cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the Reptile first-order meta-learning algorithm (Nichol et al., 2018) to the domain of INR-based MRI reconstruction, where prior meta-learning for INR initialization (Tancik et al., 2021; Dupont et al., 2022) was confined to computer vision tasks like image regression and novel view synthesis that require fully-sampled ground truth. The key conceptual departure is making the meta-learning loop physics-informed: the inner loop solves diverse k-space inverse problems using the MRI forward model (FFT/NuFFT) rather than simple image regression, and no fully-sampled reference images are needed at any stage. Recent medical meta-learning efforts (Dannecker et al., 2025; Friedrich et al., 2025; Paolis et al., 2024) either still depend on high-quality reference data or target post-processing tasks like super-resolution, leaving the ill-posed reconstruction problem from sparse raw data unaddressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the dependency on fully-sampled reference data, IPOD removes a major practical barrier to deploying INR-based reconstruction in clinical settings where acquiring ground truth is infeasible or prohibitively expensive. The framework is backbone-agnostic (validated on DINER, SIREN, HASH) and achieves 2–3× reconstruction speedup, making it a practical drop-in initialization strategy. Open questions remain: the current 3D pipeline is slice-by-slice (discarding inter-slice coherence), evaluation is limited to static brain/knee data (no dynamic or 4D flow MRI), and incorporating protocol metadata or textual priors for protocol-aware initialization is unexplored. The OOD robustness finding—where supervised E2E-VN collapses by ~14 dB on unseen lesion data with unseen sampling while IPOD maintains quality—suggests physics-informed self-supervised priors may be fundamentally more transferable than supervised mappings for MRI.
