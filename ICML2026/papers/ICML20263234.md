## Problem

The optimal trade-off between quantization format, bit-width, and downstream generative performance in quantization-aware training (QAT) for LLMs remains poorly understood, with prior work producing conflicting conclusions largely due to reliance on perplexity-based evaluations and lack of systematic cross-format comparison. The central question this paper tackles is: given a fixed inference memory budget, how should one allocate between parameter count and weight precision, and which quantization format (uniform integer vs. nonlinear) is preferable in the low-bit regime?

## Input / Output

The models are standard Llama 3-style dense Transformers that consume text tokens autoregressively and produce generated text tokens. The distinguishing constraint is that all backbone linear layers are stored in ultra-low-precision k-means formats (1.25 or 4.25 bits/weight with 16-bit block scales of size 64), while input embeddings and the output projection remain in bf16. This yields a 31B-parameter 1-bit model occupying ~7.7 GB of weight memory, matching the ~7.8 GB of the 4B bf16 reference, enabling memory-matched comparisons across three orders of magnitude in parameter count.

## Method

The core contribution is a nonlinear k-means quantization format in which 1D k-means centroids are learned per weight tensor at the onset of QAT (after a 1000-step bf16 warmup) and then frozen, replacing the uniform integer grid used in standard low-bit formats. This format, combined with block-wise scaling (B=64, 16-bit scales), achieves strictly lower reconstruction error than uniform quantization across all tested bit-widths, with the advantage most pronounced below 4.25 bits. The paper derives precision-aware scaling laws using a saturating capacity function f(P_w) = 1 − exp(−P_w/γ_w), showing that effective capacity per memory bit g(P_w) = f(P_w)/P_w monotonically decreases with precision, so the optimal strategy under a fixed memory budget is to push toward the lowest stable precision and reinvest saved memory into more parameters. Memory-matched experiments (4B bf16 vs. 12B 4-bit vs. 31B 1-bit, all trained on 150B tokens with SFT) confirm that the 31B 1-bit model outperforms the baseline on most downstream generative benchmarks. Custom Triton kernels with 256-entry lookup tables enable fused dequantize-multiply operations, achieving 3.7× speedup for 4-bit and 7.6× for 1-bit at batch size 1 on an L40S GPU.

## Results

Under a fixed ~7.8 GB inference memory budget, the 31B 1-bit k-means model outperforms the 4B bf16 baseline on 10 of 12 benchmarks, achieving MMLU 51.61 (vs. 33.21), ARC-C 50.68 (vs. 41.21), HumanEval 18.29 (vs. 12.80), and AidanBench 167.83 (vs. 88.08). The 12B 4-bit model is competitive and leads on GSM8K (48.52 vs. 45.26 for 1-bit) and IFEVAL (63.45 vs. 62.70). Scaling-law analysis confirms that k-means quantization strictly dominates uniform integer quantization across all precision-budget tradeoffs, with the largest gains below 4.25 bits/parameter, and that under a fixed memory budget the optimal strategy is to minimize bit-width and reinvest saved memory into more parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary methodological comparison is between nonlinear k-means quantization and uniform integer quantization under QAT: k-means achieves uniformly lower pretraining loss across all precision and model-size configurations, with the advantage most pronounced at ≤4.25 bits/parameter (e.g., fitted γ_w ≈ 3.32 for k-means vs. 3.71 for uniform, indicating faster capacity saturation). Against the 4B bf16 baseline, both low-bit models win on every benchmark; the 31B 1-bit model leads on 10/12 tasks while the 12B 4-bit model edges ahead on GSM8K (+3.26) and IFEVAL (+0.75). In kernel benchmarks, the custom Triton 4-bit LUT kernel achieves 3.7× speedup (721 GB/s effective bandwidth) versus Marlin's 3.4× (667 GB/s) at m=1, h=8192, and the 1-bit Triton kernel reaches 7.6× (438 GB/s) where no Marlin equivalent exists. At the whole-model level, the 12B 4-bit model runs at 79.3 tokens/s versus 88.8 for the 4B bf16 baseline (~10% slower), while the 31B 1-bit model achieves 60.7 tokens/s, confirming that the memory-capacity advantage of 1-bit formats comes with a modest throughput cost at batch size 1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the precision-aware scaling law framework of Kumar et al. (2025) by introducing a saturating capacity function and comparing two quantization families—uniform integer and nonlinear k-means—under identical QAT protocols. It builds on the 1-bit training stabilization techniques from BitNet (Wang et al., 2023) and the lookup-table kernel paradigm from SqueezeLLM and FLUTE, but departs from prior work by showing that learned nonlinear centroids strictly dominate uniform grids across all bit-widths and by replacing perplexity-based evaluation with memory-matched downstream generative benchmarks. The practical kernel design draws on Marlin (Frantar et al., 2024) and Triton, adapted with a 256-entry LUT to support both 4-bit and 1-bit nonlinear block-scaled formats.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear, actionable prescription for practitioners: under a fixed inference memory budget, allocate to more parameters at lower precision using k-means quantization, and 1-bit weights are viable without catastrophic degradation. However, the practical impact is tempered by the finding that speedups vanish at batch sizes above ~256, limiting deployment to single-user or small-batch autoregressive settings. Open questions include how the conclusions generalize beyond Llama-3-style architectures, whether the k-means advantage persists with different learning-rate schedules or longer training, and whether the format space (e.g., per-channel vs. per-block, learned vs. fixed centroids) can be explored more broadly given the cost of scaling-law experiments.
