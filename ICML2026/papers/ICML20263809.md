## Problem

State-of-the-art LLMs still make errors on simple deterministic tasks (arithmetic, list reversal, dynamic programming) that scale with task length, yet no quantitative model predicts how error rates depend on complexity. Prior explanations—'collapse of reasoning' or insufficient expressive power for compositional functions—lack a testable, parameterized account that unifies observations across tasks and models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263809/cropped_page5_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a task prompt containing an input token sequence drawn from a small alphabet (digits, bits) of length c, and produces a closed-form accuracy prediction a(c) governed by two fitted parameters: r (an elementary per-token noise rate) and q (an O(1) count of plausible erroneous token directions). Empirically, 200 samples per complexity value across 0.2 million distinct prompts are used to fit r and q per task-model pair, with 95% Bayesian confidence intervals computed via a beta posterior. The formula is invariant under rescaling of c, so the complexity parameter can be set to either input or output length without loss of generality.

## Method

The paper derives a two-parameter accuracy formula from an 'effective field theory' perspective: the LLM's billions of parameters are reorganized into an effective model with the same transformer architecture as an idealized perfect model but with slightly perturbed attention and nonlinear parameters. Errors arise because small deviations in query/key matrices produce erroneous attention coefficients that accumulate across the context; an incorrect token is predicted when the resulting error vector's magnitude crosses a threshold τ. Assuming the error coefficients are Gaussian with variance scaling as c² (α=1, reflecting correlated errors from a limited token alphabet) and decomposing the error into q orthogonal directions, the accuracy reduces to a ratio of incomplete gamma functions: a = γ(q/2, q/(2rc²)) / Γ(q/2). This yields a plateau near 1 for small c and a power-law decay ~c^(−2q) for large c. The model is validated across 8 tasks and 3 LLMs (Gemini 2.5 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Pro, DeepSeek R1), with one notable failure (vanilla addition on Pro) attributed to the model switching algorithms across input lengths, which is confirmed by showing the formula recovers when a fixed algorithm is enforced in the prompt.

## Results

The two-parameter formula a = γ(q/2, q/(2rc^{2α}))/Γ(q/2) with α=1 fits the accuracy of Gemini 2.5 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and DeepSeek R1 across 8 tasks (list reversal, nested linear transformations, dynamic programming, Tower of Hanoi, vanilla addition, algorithmic addition, binary addition, multiplication) using 0.2 million prompts. Best-fit q values are O(1) in 7 of 8 tasks (ranging from ~1 to ~14), supporting the interpretation of q as the number of plausible error directions. The formula fails completely for vanilla addition with Gemini Pro (q = 0.24, unphysical), but recovers when the model is forced to follow a specific digit-by-digit algorithm (q = 0.56). A modified polynomial-multiplication prompt improves Flash's accuracy beyond Pro's vanilla performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing error-prediction methods but instead tests its formula against three previously proposed explanations for LLM errors on repetitive tasks. Against the 'compositional limitation' hypothesis (Dziri et al., 2023), the formula fits nested linear transformations and multiplication well, suggesting errors arise from attention noise rather than architectural expressiveness limits. Against the 'reasoning collapse' hypothesis (Shojaee et al., 2025), the formula fits Tower of Hanoi and dynamic programming even when an explicit algorithm is provided in the prompt, indicating the accuracy drop is due to accumulated attention errors, not a failure to reason. Against the naive independent-error model a=(1-r)^{l_out}, the formula with correlated errors (via the incomplete gamma function) provides a substantially better fit. The one clear failure—vanilla addition with Pro—is resolved by forcing a specific algorithm, supporting the interpretation that Pro uses inconsistent internal algorithms for different input lengths.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior explanations of LLM errors on repetitive tasks—pattern matching (Nikankin et al., 2024), tokenization artifacts (Singh & Strouse, 2024), insufficient expressiveness for compositional functions (Dziri et al., 2023), and 'collapse of reasoning' (Shojaee et al., 2025)—by proposing instead that errors arise from small, correlated perturbations in the attention mechanism that accumulate over context length. The authors borrow the 'effective field theory' paradigm from physics, where a system with many microscopic parameters is described by a small number of effective parameters, to argue that hundreds of billions of LLM parameters reduce to just two (a noise rate r and an error-direction count q) for the purpose of predicting accuracy. The task of studying LLM errors on arithmetic and repetitive tasks is well-established; the novelty lies in the quantitative two-parameter model and its empirical validation across 200K prompts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, testable alternative to the 'reasoning collapse' narrative, showing that accuracy degradation on long repetitive tasks is better explained by accumulated attention noise than by a fundamental inability to reason or compose. The practical finding that tagging tokens with positional monomials (polynomial multiplication prompt) can substantially reduce errors offers an actionable prompt-engineering strategy. However, the model is explicitly limited to deterministic tasks with small token alphabets, one case (Gemini Pro on vanilla addition) fails completely, and the authors acknowledge they lack the rigorous mathematical framework that underpins effective field theory in physics. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to open-ended or multi-modal tasks, and whether architectural changes (e.g., sparse attention) can reduce the effective noise rate.
