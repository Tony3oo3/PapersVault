## Problem

Equivariant representation learning models trained with explicit equivariance losses still exhibit a systematic discrepancy between analytically rotated latents ρ(g)Φ(x) and the true encoded targets Φ(g∘x), a phenomenon the authors term latent misalignment. This misalignment accumulates with rotation magnitude and degrades novel view synthesis fidelity, yet prior work has neither formally characterized nor corrected this gap between the intended group action and the actually required latent transformation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262103/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image (resolutions range from 28×28 for RotatedMNIST to 224×224 for ABO-Material) and a known rotation group element g ∈ SO(2) or SO(3) as input. The encoder produces structured latents organized as block-diagonal Wigner D-matrix representations (C×81 for SO(3) with degrees ℓ=0,…,8; C×17 for SO(2)), and the flow model operates entirely in this latent space rather than pixel space. The final output is a synthesized novel-view image decoded from the flow-corrected latent, with the decoder fine-tuned to consume the corrected latent distribution.

## Method

Residual Latent Flow (RLF) treats the analytically transformed latent ρ(g)Φ(x) as a first-order approximation and learns a residual velocity field via conditional flow matching that transports it to the empirically encoded target Φ(g∘x). Unlike standard flow matching that couples independent source and target samples, RLF uses paired latents from the same object under a known group action, formulating the conditional joint as π₀,₁(z₀,z₁|x,g) = p₀(z₀|x,g)·p₁(z₁|x,g) with a linear interpolation path between the two. Training proceeds in three stages: (1) train a ViT-based NFT autoencoder with the equivariance + reconstruction loss, (2) freeze the encoder and train the flow model (Transformer for SO(2), U-Net for SO(3) exploiting the square-grid reshaping of Wigner D-matrix blocks) with the RLF velocity-matching objective, and (3) fine-tune the decoder on flow-corrected latents to remove distribution shift. The key distinction from direct regression is that flow matching handles the multi-modal target distribution arising from encoder aliasing, where distinct inputs can map to the same latent and thus produce multiple valid targets for a single source.

## Results

Across six datasets spanning SO(2) and SO(3) symmetries, Residual Latent Flow (RLF) consistently reduces angle error by 4–7× compared to the NFT baseline (e.g., 0.1746→0.0430 on ModelNet10-SO(3) OOD, 0.2429→0.0728 on SmallNORB OOD, 0.0859→0.0271 on ComplexBRDFs OOD). PSNR improves by 0.3–1.5 dB across all settings (e.g., 10.09→10.43 on ModelNet10-SO(3) OOD, 17.71→19.19 on ComplexBRDFs OOD, 21.80→22.84 on ABO Day-to-Night OOD). On in-plane rotation NVS (RotatedMNIST), RLF achieves PSNR 29.02 and LPIPS 0.0030, outperforming NFT (28.21, 0.0035) and all prior methods. Latent error is reduced by 2–4× (e.g., 7.2×10⁻⁴→4.1×10⁻⁴ on ModelNet10-SO(3) OOD), confirming tighter equivariance compliance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary baseline NFT (Koyama et al., 2024), RLF wins on every metric across all six datasets, with the largest gains in angle error (up to 7× reduction on ModelNet10-SO(3) OOD: 0.1746→0.0430) and PSNR (up to +1.48 dB on ComplexBRDFs OOD: 17.71→19.19). On in-plane rotation NVS (RotatedMNIST, Table 2), RLF (PSNR 29.02, LPIPS 0.0030) outperforms NFT (28.21, 0.0035), LGA (24.13, 0.0385), GIAE (19.88, 0.1000), and SpatialVAE (15.59, 0.1561), with the gap over LGA being particularly large (~5 dB PSNR). On out-of-plane rotation NVS (SmallNORB, Table 3), RLF (PSNR 23.28, LPIPS 0.247) beats ENR (18.07, 0.262) and LGA (17.59, 0.270) by a wide margin, and marginally improves over NFT (23.13, 0.272) in PSNR while notably reducing LPIPS (0.247 vs. 0.272). The method's advantage is most pronounced under OOD conditions, indicating that latent correction generalizes better to unseen objects and viewpoints than the uncorrected equivariant encoder.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Neural Fourier Transform (NFT) framework for equivariant representation learning, which decomposes group representations into Wigner D-matrix blocks and trains autoencoders with explicit equivariance losses. It departs from standard flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023), which transports a simple noise prior to a data distribution, by repurposing flow matching as a residual correction mechanism: the analytically rotated latent ρ(g)Φ(x) serves as the source, and the empirically encoded latent Φ(g∘x) as the target, with the key distinction that source and target are deterministically coupled through the same object and group action rather than sampled independently. The approach also connects to the broader equivariant representation learning literature (Cohen & Welling, 2016; Dupont et al., 2020; Koyama et al., 2024) by identifying a concrete failure mode—latent misalignment—that prior works implicitly assumed away through perfect equivariance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and quantifies a practical limitation in equivariant models that was previously treated as a minor optimization artifact: latent misalignment grows with rotation magnitude and systematically degrades novel view synthesis. The residual flow correction is lightweight (0.8M–2.1M parameters on a frozen encoder) and consistently reduces angle error by 3–4× across SO(2) and SO(3) datasets, suggesting the technique could be a general post-hoc fix for any equivariant pipeline. However, the framework is limited to controlled SO(2)/SO(3) transformations with known group actions, end-to-end training remains unstable, and final image quality is bounded by the decoder. Extending to SE(3), articulated motion, or unconstrained real-world transformations, and developing stable joint training strategies, remain important open directions.
