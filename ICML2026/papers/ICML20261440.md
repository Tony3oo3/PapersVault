## Problem

Pretraining hyperparameters for large language models are optimized almost exclusively by minimizing validation cross-entropy loss, under the assumption that a lower pretraining loss yields a better downstream model. This paper asks whether that assumption holds: does the weight decay value that minimizes pretraining loss also maximize a model's plasticity (its ability to adapt to new tasks during fine-tuning), or can a model with worse pretraining loss actually perform better after further training?

## Input / Output

The study takes pretrained transformer language models (Llama-2 at 0.5B/1B/4B and OLMo-2 at 1.5B, trained at 20 or 140 tokens-per-parameter) as input, each produced by varying only the AdamW weight decay hyperparameter (0 to 10). These models are then fine-tuned on 12 downstream tasks and evaluated via generated text (zero-shot CoT solutions scored with six metrics: Pass@1, Maj@16, RM@16, Pass@16, Correct Ratio, and ORM Score) and cloze-style accuracy for classification tasks. The key output is a mapping from pretraining weight decay to downstream performance, revealing that the optimal weight decay for plasticity (≈1.0 at 20 TPP, ≈0.3 at 140 TPP) differs from the value that minimizes pretraining loss.

## Method

The paper conducts a systematic empirical study varying weight decay during pretraining across five model groups (two families, three sizes, two training regimes), then fine-tunes each variant on six CoT reasoning tasks, five commonsense reasoning tasks, and one safety alignment task. The central finding is that higher weight decay consistently improves downstream performance even when it increases pretraining validation loss, demonstrating that pretraining loss is not a perfect proxy for downstream capability. Mechanistic analysis identifies three complementary effects of weight decay: (1) it encourages linearly separable internal representations (measured via linear probing on sentiment and topic classification), (2) it reduces the pseudo-rank of query-key attention matrices (via nuclear-norm-like regularization on factored W_Q and W_K), and (3) it reduces the train-val gap, indicating less overfitting to pretraining data. Robustness is confirmed by sweeping learning rate, batch size, and fine-tuning weight decay, showing the plasticity benefit of higher pretraining weight decay persists across hyperparameter settings.

## Results

Across all four 20 TPP model groups (Llama-2-0.5B, Llama-2-1B, Llama-2-4B, OLMo-2-1B), the optimal pretraining weight decay for downstream performance is 1.0, substantially higher than the standard default of 0.1. At 140 TPP, the optimal drops to 0.3 for OLMo-2-1B. A key counterintuitive finding: OLMo-2-1B-140x pretrained with weight decay 0.3 (pretraining validation loss 2.6208) or 1.0 (2.7064) achieves *worse* pretraining loss than weight decay 0.1 (2.6088), yet performs noticeably *better* after fine-tuning on CoT reasoning tasks. Mechanistically, weight decay 1.0 reduces the query-key attention matrix pseudo-rank by roughly a factor of 2, and the train-val gap decreases monotonically with increasing weight decay.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study without external method baselines; the primary comparison is across different weight decay values during pretraining, with the standard default of 0.1 serving as the reference. Models pretrained with weight decay 0.3–1.0 consistently outperform the 0.1 default on all twelve downstream tasks (six CoT reasoning, five language understanding/commonsense, one safety alignment) across both model families (Llama-2, OLMo-2), all model sizes (0.5B–4B), and both training regimes (20 and 140 TPP). The advantage is robust to variations in other pretraining hyperparameters (learning rate) and fine-tuning hyperparameters (learning rate, weight decay, batch size), as confirmed by 216 additional fine-tuning runs. The one setting where the default 0.1 is optimal for pretraining loss (OLMo-2-1B at 140 TPP) still shows that 0.3 yields better downstream performance, demonstrating that the pretraining-loss-optimal choice is not the downstream-optimal choice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two previously separate research threads: the study of weight decay in language model pretraining (where prior work has focused on its role in optimization stability, learning rate shaping, and low-rank attention induction, e.g., Kobayashi et al. 2024; Bergsma et al. 2025) and the study of model plasticity in continual learning and transfer learning (Dohare et al. 2024; Lyle et al. 2023). It carves new ground by formally connecting these threads—treating weight decay as a lever for controlling a pretrained model's downstream adaptability rather than its pretraining loss—and by providing one of the first systematic empirical demonstrations that the weight decay value minimizing pretraining validation loss differs from the value maximizing post-fine-tuning performance. The mechanistic analyses build on Kobayashi et al.'s theoretical argument for low-rank attention under weight decay and on vision-model findings that regularization preserves plasticity, but extend them to modern LLM pretraining at larger scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a widely held working assumption in LLM development: that minimizing pretraining cross-entropy loss is the right objective for selecting pretraining hyperparameters. If the finding generalizes beyond 4B-parameter models, it implies that the de facto standard weight decay of 0.1 may be suboptimal for models intended to be fine-tuned, and that hyperparameter selection should incorporate downstream performance rather than relying solely on validation loss. Key limitations include the relatively small model scale (≤4B), the correlational (not causal) nature of the mechanistic explanations, and the open question of whether the effect persists in heavily overtrained or very large-model regimes where the pretraining-loss benefit of lower weight decay may dominate. Future work should validate these findings at frontier scale and investigate whether the stability-plasticity trade-off quantified here changes with model size and training duration.
