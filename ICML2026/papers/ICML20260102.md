## Problem

Generative molecular dynamics (GenMD) methods for protein conformational sampling, including Boltzmann Emulators and implicit transfer operators, typically require large collections of long MD trajectories, limiting data efficiency and transferability to unseen protein systems. This paper asks whether incorporating auxiliary representations from pretrained protein language models, structure models, and LLM-derived annotations can substantially improve the out-of-distribution generalization and data efficiency of coarse-grained implicit transfer operators (TITO) for protein molecular dynamics.

## Input / Output

The model takes coarse-grained Cα backbone coordinates (x_t ∈ R^{3L} for L residues), the amino-acid sequence, simulation temperature T, and a target time step Δt as primary inputs, augmented by pretrained ESM sequence embeddings (precomputed offline) and optionally Proteina structure embeddings (computed online). It outputs a learned velocity field that, when integrated via Euler ODE steps, produces future backbone coordinates at time t+Δt; iterative rollouts of this transition model generate long-timescale trajectories from which free energy landscapes, folding/unfolding rates, and cryptic binding pocket conformations are estimated. The coarse-grained Cα-only representation ignores side-chain degrees of freedom, and the model is non-equivariant.

## Method

PLaTITO uses a two-stage non-equivariant transformer architecture (based on Proteina) with a conditioning network f_c that encodes the current state (x_t, Δt, S, T, and auxiliary embeddings) into per-residue representations, and a velocity network f_v that predicts the flow-matching velocity field at time s given the latent z_s and the conditioning representation. Training follows the rectified flow formulation with linear interpolants z_s = s·x_{t+Δt} + (1−s)·ε, minimizing a conditional flow-matching loss over pairs sampled from mdCATH trajectories at diverse temperatures and time steps. The key architectural contribution is injecting pretrained protein language model (ESM Cambrian) residue-level embeddings directly into the conditioning network, providing a computationally free source of evolutionary and structural prior knowledge that cannot be precomputed for structure embeddings. The 19M-parameter variant scales both model capacity and pLM embedding dimension (ESM Cambrian 6B, 2560-dim), achieving state-of-the-art equilibrium sampling on out-of-distribution fast-folding proteins with ~10× less training data and compute than BioEmu.

## Results

On the fast-folding protein equilibrium sampling benchmark (12 systems, 10–80 residues), PLaTITO-19M achieves a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.824±0.170 and RMSE of 1.099±0.212 on TICA-projected free-energy surfaces, with a Coverage of 0.666±0.136. This outperforms BioEmu (MAE 1.110, RMSE 1.389, Coverage 0.594) by ~26% in MAE and ~21% in RMSE, while requiring only 1,100 GPU hours versus BioEmu's 9,216 GPU hours (~8.4× less compute) and 56 ms of MD training data versus 216 ms (~3.9× less data). The base TITO model (3M params, no pLM embeddings) already achieves MAE 1.068, and adding ESM sequence embeddings (PLaTITO) reduces MAE to 0.949 at no additional test-time cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PLaTITO-19M outperforms all three external baselines on the primary MAE and RMSE metrics. Against BioEmu (Lewis et al., 2025), PLaTITO-19M reduces MAE from 1.110 to 0.824 (−26%) and RMSE from 1.389 to 1.099 (−21%), while also improving Coverage from 0.594 to 0.666, all with ~8.4× less training compute and ~3.9× less MD data. Against ConfDiff (Wang et al., 2024), PLaTITO-19M achieves substantially lower MAE (0.824 vs. 1.419) and RMSE (1.099 vs. 1.709), though ConfDiff's Coverage (0.535) is lower than PLaTITO's. Against Str2Str (Lu et al., 2024), PLaTITO-19M wins on MAE (0.824 vs. 0.998) and RMSE (1.099 vs. 1.274), but Str2Str achieves higher Coverage (0.707 vs. 0.666); the authors note Str2Str's higher coverage comes at the cost of generating unrealistic metastable basins visible in TICA projections. Internally, the paper shows that the ITO formulation (exploiting MD auto-correlation) is inherently more data-efficient than a Boltzmann Emulator trained with the same architecture and data: TITO (MAE 1.068) beats Emu (MAE 1.305) by ~18% under identical conditions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Implicit Transfer Operator (ITO) learning framework (Schreiner et al., 2023) and its transferable successor TITO (Diez et al., 2026) by incorporating auxiliary conditioning signals—protein language model (ESM) sequence embeddings, structure embeddings from Proteina, and LLM-derived annotations—into a coarse-grained Cα transformer architecture trained with rectified flow matching. While prior ITO/TITO work demonstrated transferability across peptides and small molecules, this paper scales the approach to protein systems and positions pLM embeddings as a key inductive bias for out-of-distribution generalization. The paper also explicitly contrasts TITO-style transition operator learning with Boltzmann Emulator approaches (BioEmu), showing that exploiting the auto-correlation structure of MD trajectories yields substantially better data efficiency than direct equilibrium density modeling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PLaTITO-19M achieves state-of-the-art equilibrium sampling on fast-folding proteins with roughly a ten-fold reduction in both training data (56 ms vs. 216 ms) and compute (1,100 vs. 9,216 GPU hours) compared to BioEmu, making transferable MD surrogates accessible to smaller research groups. The recovery of non-Arrhenius temperature-dependent folding/unfolding rates provides evidence that the learned operator captures physically meaningful kinetics beyond simple equilibrium matching. Key limitations remain: the coarse-grained Cα representation ignores side-chain dynamics critical for ligand specificity, the model systematically underestimates timescales (consistent with variational lower bounds), and it fails to capture certain metastable basins. Open questions include extending to all-atom representations, protein complexes, and small-molecule ligands for drug-discovery applications.
