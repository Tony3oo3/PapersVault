## Problem

The ML community increasingly preaches data frugality as a path to responsible AI, yet data scaling continues to dominate practice, with energy and carbon costs of large datasets remaining largely unaccounted. This paper identifies and quantifies the gap between rhetorical advocacy and concrete practice, arguing that the environmental impacts of dataset-level decisions (storage, repeated training, distribution) are substantial and underreported, and that actionable evidence for data frugality is needed to shift norms.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a full RGB image training dataset (ImageNet-1K: 1.28M images, 130 GB, 1,000 classes) and produces a representative pruned subset (e.g., 25% retained) that preserves Top-1 accuracy. In the bias experiment, the input additionally includes group labels (majority/minority colour assignments on ColourMNIST), and the output is a rebalanced or reweighted subset that mitigates group bias. Beyond the pruned subset, the paper produces aggregate energy (GWh) and carbon (tCO₂e) estimates for the full downstream lifecycle of the dataset, including training and storage.

## Method

The paper combines lifecycle cost estimation with empirical validation of coreset selection. For cost estimation, it counts ImageNet-1K training runs from ICLR 2017–2025 (via OpenReview API and LLM-based labelling, extrapolated to the broader literature via Dimensions.ai) and measures per-epoch energy with CarbonTracker on a single A100 GPU (ResNet-50, 300 epochs), yielding ~5.46 GWh and ~2,429 tCO₂e for training plus ~360 MWh for storage. For empirical validation, it benchmarks SOTA subset selection methods (Dyn-Unc, InfoMax, D² pruning, forgetting scores) on ImageNet-1K, showing 25–35% pruning with no Top-1 accuracy drop, and measures actual training time and energy savings (24–40% time, 24–33% energy per epoch) across ResNet-34, ResNet-50, and Swin-T. A bias-mitigation experiment on ColourMNIST demonstrates that balanced or reweighted coreset selection recovers aligned accuracy under 99% group bias. Finally, the paper amortises one-time coreset construction costs, showing break-even after 3–4 downstream training runs.

## Results

Pruning 25% of ImageNet-1K reduces training time by 24–40% and energy consumption by 24–33% per epoch across ResNet-34, ResNet-50, and Swin-T architectures, with no noticeable accuracy loss when using SOTA methods (Dyn-Unc prunes 25% with zero Top-1 drop; InfoMax prunes up to 35% with no performance sacrifice). The aggregate downstream energy cost of ImageNet-1K training (2017–2025) is estimated at 5.46 ± 0.14 GWh (~2,429 ± 61 tCO₂e), and storage adds 360 ± 9 MWh (~160 ± 4 tCO₂e). A 25% dataset reduction would save approximately 1.4–1.9 GWh (~621–854 tCO₂e) in aggregate, and the one-time coreset construction cost is amortised after only 3–4 training runs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a single new method but benchmarks existing SOTA subset-selection methods (Dyn-Unc, InfoMax, D² pruning, Forgetting scores) against random sampling and the full dataset. All informed pruning methods outperform random sampling at every pruning ratio on ImageNet-1K Top-1 accuracy; Dyn-Unc achieves zero accuracy loss at 25% pruning (Swin-T) and InfoMax achieves zero loss up to 35% pruning (ResNet-34), while random sampling degrades noticeably. For bias mitigation on ColourMNIST (99% majority group), balanced coreset sampling recovers aligned accuracy far more effectively than random sampling, and reweighted sampling falls in between, demonstrating that data curation can simultaneously reduce energy and mitigate group bias. The authors note that time and energy reductions are not proportional to the data reduction ratio (25% pruning yields 24–40% time and 24–33% energy savings), cautioning against treating dataset size as a direct proxy for compute cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper sits at the intersection of the coreset selection literature (Feldman, 2019; Killamsetty et al., 2021; He et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2025) and the growing body of position papers challenging data scaling (Wang et al., 2025; Goel et al., 2025; McCoy et al., 2025). Rather than proposing a new pruning method, it repurposes existing subset selection techniques (Dyn-Unc, InfoMax, D²) to quantify their energy and carbon savings, and it extends the environmental accounting typically applied to model training (Henderson et al., 2020; Luccioni et al., 2023) to the data lifecycle itself—storage, distribution, and repeated downstream training. Its distinctive contribution is making the 'preach-practice' gap explicit: while 8/10 surveyed coreset papers cite computational efficiency as motivation, only 1/10 actually report energy savings, despite tools like CarbonTracker making such measurement straightforward.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, if conservative, anchor for dataset-level environmental costs (~2,429 tCO₂e for ImageNet-1K training alone), making an often-abstract sustainability concern tangible and comparable to per-capita footprints. Its actionable recommendations—mandatory resource reporting, Data-Pareto metrics, shared dataset infrastructure, and data sunset policies—target the incentive structures (benchmarks, leaderboards, review norms) that perpetuate data hoarding. Key open questions include whether these findings generalise to tokenised or multimodal frontier datasets, whether rebound effects will offset efficiency gains, and whether coreset construction costs are truly amortised in practice. The paper itself acknowledges that its experiments are limited to image classification and that aggressive pruning may harm generative modelling or safety-critical long-tail coverage.
