## Problem

Standard LLM-as-a-judge ensembles aggregate scores via majority vote or averaging, implicitly assuming judges provide independent estimates of quality. In practice, LLM judges exhibit correlated errors driven by shared latent confounders (verbosity, stylistic preferences, training artifacts), causing these heuristics to yield diminishing returns or even amplify systematic mistakes. No prior aggregation framework explicitly models and separates these shared confounding factors from the true quality signal without requiring ground-truth labels.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266054/cropped_page2_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a score matrix X ∈ R^{n×p} (n evaluation items × p LLM judges, ranging from 11 to 20 judges of 0.6B–14B parameters) where entries are continuous ratings, binary labels, or preference values (−3 to 3), and produces a per-item posterior quality estimate (E[Q|J] for Gaussian settings or P(Q=1|J) for mixture settings) along with interpretable latent confounder scores. No ground-truth labels are required; a small optional anchor set can refine symmetry-breaking. The framework handles three evaluation regimes—continuous scoring, binary classification, and pairwise preference—through two complementary estimators selected by data type.

## Method

CARE formulates multi-judge scoring as a latent-variable Markov Random Field where observed judge scores depend on a true-quality latent Q and k shared confounders C. The shared backbone performs a sparse-plus-low-rank decomposition of the sample precision matrix, separating direct judge-judge conditional edges (sparse S) from latent-mediated dependencies (low-rank L), thereby recovering the graph structure despite unobserved latents. CARE-SVD (fully Gaussian) then applies SVD to L to extract latent directions and uses a leading-eigenvector symmetry-breaking rule to identify the quality factor, aggregating judges by their loadings on that direction. CARE-Tensor (Gaussian mixture / discrete) uses the recovered sparse graph to partition judges into three conditionally independent groups, constructs a third-order cross-moment tensor, and applies CP decomposition to identifiably recover mixture means and weights, with Bayes' rule yielding the posterior quality estimate. The paper provides identifiability guarantees, finite-sample recovery rates scaling as O(√(log p / n)), and explicit misspecification error bounds quantifying the bias incurred when confounders are omitted.

## Results

On scoring datasets (MAE, lower is better), CARE-SVD achieves the lowest error across all six benchmarks, reducing MAE by up to 26.8% over majority vote on UltraFeedback (0.623 vs. 0.851) and yielding a 17.37% average relative improvement over simple averaging. On classification and preference datasets (Accuracy, higher is better), CARE achieves the best accuracy on 5 of 6 benchmarks, with CARE-Tensor leading on PKU-BETTER (77.9%), SHP (69.5%), and Summarize (81.4%), and CARE-SVD leading on Chatbot-Arena (58.0%) and CivilComments (77.8%). CARE also demonstrates robustness to injected stylistic biases (beauty, authority) and reduces false-positive rates on adversarial responses by up to 100% (e.g., 0.000 vs. 0.674 for MV on 'Thought process:' attacks).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CARE dominates the simple baselines (MV, AVG) across all 12 benchmarks in both scoring and classification settings, with the largest gaps on UltraFeedback (26.8% MAE reduction over MV) and Summarize preference (13.4% accuracy gain over the strongest baseline). Against weak-supervision methods (WS, UWS), CARE wins on nearly all datasets; WS degrades severely on PKU-BETTER (57.5%) and PKU-SAFER (57.0%) where CARE-Tensor reaches 77.9% and 73.1% respectively. Among the classical label-aggregation baselines, MACE is the strongest competitor, narrowly beating CARE-Tensor on PKU-SAFER (73.5% vs. 73.1%) and matching it on PKU-BETTER (73.4% vs. 77.9%), but CARE-Tensor substantially outperforms MACE on SHP (69.5% vs. 58.0%) and Summarize (81.4% vs. 70.6%). Dawid-Skene and GLAD are consistently outperformed by both CARE variants, with GLAD closest on Summarize (71.8% vs. 81.4% for CARE-Tensor). Overall, CARE-SVD excels in fully Gaussian scoring settings while CARE-Tensor is stronger in discrete/preference regimes, together covering all evaluation paradigms tested.

## Relation to Prior Work

CARE departs from the standard label-aggregation lineage (Dawid-Skene, GLAD, MACE, Snorkel/UWS) by rejecting the conditional-independence assumption that underlies all of these methods: instead of modeling each judge as an independent noisy observer of a single latent truth, CARE explicitly introduces shared latent confounders (verbosity, style, training artifacts) that induce correlated errors across judges. The technical machinery is assembled from two well-studied tools—sparse-plus-low-rank decomposition of the precision matrix (Chandrasekaran et al., 2012) for recovering the conditional-independence graph, and CP tensor decomposition (Anandkumar et al., 2014) for identifiable recovery of latent factors—but the paper's novelty lies in combining them in a two-stage pipeline where the sparse structure from the first stage determines the conditional-independence partition needed by the second. In the LLM-as-a-judge context, this positions CARE as a principled upgrade over the majority-vote/averaging ensembles used by Verga et al. (PoLL), Hu et al. (GED), and Rahmani et al. (JudgeBlender), which all implicitly assume judge independence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLM-as-a-judge ensembles become the default for scalable evaluation, correlated errors from shared training data and architectural choices mean that simply adding more judges can amplify rather than cancel systematic biases; CARE provides the first aggregation framework with provable identifiability guarantees that explicitly separates quality from confounders without ground-truth labels. The misspecification error bound (Theorem D.6 / Corollary D.7) quantifies the concrete cost of ignoring confounders, giving practitioners a diagnostic for when naive averaging is fundamentally inadequate. Open questions include how the method scales to very large judge panels (the sparse+low-rank step is O(p³)), whether the symmetry-breaking heuristics (leading-eigenvector rule, loading-balance criterion) remain reliable when confounders are as strong as the quality signal, and whether the Gaussian/Gaussian-mixture assumptions can be relaxed to fully non-parametric settings without losing the identifiability guarantees.
