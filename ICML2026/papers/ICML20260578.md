## Problem

Discovering the symmetry group of a dataset automatically is critical for physics and equivariant machine learning, yet prior methods either assume a fixed Lie algebra basis, prescribe a specific coefficient distribution (e.g., Gaussian), or handle only continuous or only discrete symmetries. No unified framework existed that could recover both continuous and discrete subgroups from data without such restrictive assumptions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260578/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a dataset of point clouds (2D arrows, 3D tetrahedra, 128-point ModelNet10 shapes, or 20-joint 3D human skeletons) and a user-specified hypothesis Lie group G (e.g., SO(2), SO(3), GL(2,ℝ)⁺) acting on the data space. It produces a learned distribution p_θ(g) over G whose support identifies the true symmetry subgroup H ⊆ G; individual group elements h ∈ H can be extracted by running the learned flow from a random prior element and accumulating the composed transformations. The network's output is a Lie algebra element A ∈ 𝔤 (not a data-space velocity), which is then exponentiated to update the trajectory along the group orbit.

## Method

LieFlow reframes symmetry discovery as conditional flow matching on a Lie group: given a data point x₁ and a prior-sampled transformation g, it constructs an orbit-respecting interpolation x_t = exp(tA)x₀ where A = log(g⁻¹), and trains a neural network to predict A at each (x_t, t). The key mechanism is that non-symmetry transformations produce inconsistent velocity targets across different data points, which cancel under the CFM averaging, so the learned distribution naturally concentrates on the true symmetry subgroup. The network outputs Lie algebra elements directly, ensuring trajectories stay on group orbits via exponential-map integration. A novel power time schedule (sampling t from density p(x)=nx^{n−1} with n=5) addresses a 'last-minute convergence' phenomenon where discrete-group learning signals are concentrated near t=1, enabling recovery of complex discrete groups like the icosahedral group that uniform time sampling fails to learn.

## Results

On ModelNet10, LieFlow achieves Wasserstein-1 distances of 0.149 (Tet), 0.097 (Oct), and 0.129 (Ico) for discrete symmetry groups, with relative improvements over random sampling of 83.5%, 86.5%, and 75.1% respectively. For continuous SO(2) subgroups, LieFlow reaches W1 of 0.050 and 0.053 (96.8% and 96.6% RI). On the real-world MI-Motion dataset, LieFlow successfully recovers the approximate C4 rotational symmetry around the z-axis, while LieGAN fails entirely and produces a uniform distribution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LieFlow decisively outperforms LieGAN (Yang et al., 2023) on all discrete symmetry groups: W1 of 0.149 vs. 1.113 for Tet, 0.097 vs. 1.145 for Oct, and 0.129 vs. 0.893 for Ico, with LieGAN actually performing worse than random sampling (negative RI) on these tasks. On continuous SO(2) subgroups, LieFlow also edges out LieGAN (0.050 vs. 0.101 and 0.053 vs. 0.072), though the gap is smaller. Against SGM (Allingham et al., 2024), LieFlow wins across all five groups, with the largest margins on discrete groups (e.g., Ico: 0.129 vs. 1.031) and a dramatic advantage on continuous SO(2) (0.050 vs. 1.848), where SGM fails to learn the correct structure. Augerino (Benton et al., 2020), which requires supervision via labels, is also outperformed on every group (e.g., Tet: 0.149 vs. 1.059, Ico: 0.129 vs. 0.795). On the MI-Motion real-world dataset, LieFlow captures the approximate C4 symmetry with clear peaks at 0°, 90°, 180°, 270°, while LieGAN produces only a uniform distribution, highlighting LieFlow's advantage in discovering discrete symmetries from noisy, real-world data.

## Relation to Prior Work

LieFlow builds on the flow matching framework (Lipman et al., 2023) and its extension to Riemannian manifolds (Chen & Lipman, 2024), but departs fundamentally from prior symmetry discovery methods by operating directly in group space rather than estimating Lie algebra generators. Unlike LieGAN (Yang et al., 2023), which relies on adversarial training and assumes a fixed (typically Gaussian) distribution over Lie algebra coefficients, or SGM (Allingham et al., 2024), which first learns a canonicalization function, LieFlow learns a marginal transformation distribution via conditional flow matching without prescribing a coefficient distribution or requiring explicit prototypes. This reframing from generator estimation to support learning in group space is the paper's core conceptual contribution, enabling a unified treatment of continuous and discrete symmetries that prior methods handled separately or not at all.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified ability to discover both continuous and discrete symmetries from data, without assuming a fixed Lie algebra basis or coefficient distribution, addresses a practical gap that limits the applicability of equivariant neural networks to settings where symmetries are unknown or approximate. The identification of the 'last-minute convergence' phenomenon and the power time schedule provide a concrete, well-motivated fix for a previously unaddressed failure mode in flow matching on discrete structures. Key limitations include the need to specify a hypothesis group G (which bounds what can be discovered), the assumption of global (not local) symmetry for the theoretical guarantee, and reliance on exp/log maps that may encounter numerical issues near cut loci for general groups. Open questions include scaling to high-dimensional or non-compact hypothesis groups, extracting explicit algebraic presentations from the learned distribution, and evaluating discovered symmetries in downstream equivariant models.
