## Problem

Standard FDR control guarantees only the average quality of a rejection set, allowing weak-evidence hypotheses to 'hitchhike' into discoveries when strong signals inflate the rejection threshold. While the boundary FDR (bFDR) was recently proposed to protect the single least significant rejection, existing control methods (e.g., the SL procedure) require independence of p-values and do not generalize to batches of marginal discoveries or to arbitrary dependence structures.

## Input / Output

The method takes m p-values or e-values, a target error level α, and a boundary size k (the number of least significant discoveries to protect), and outputs a rejection set R with a rigorous guarantee that k-bFDR(R) ≤ α under arbitrary dependence. The k parameter is user-specified and controls the trade-off: smaller k yields a more conservative, higher-precision rejection set, while larger k relaxes the boundary constraint to gain power. The algorithm is stepwise, searching from the largest candidate rejection size downward until the marginal set satisfies a closure condition, and runs in O(m²) time for common local tests.

## Method

Domino adapts the classical closure principle (Marcus et al., 1976) from FWER control to boundary error control: instead of requiring every hypothesis in the rejection set to satisfy a closure condition across all relevant subsets, it only requires the k least significant (marginal) hypotheses to satisfy the condition. The building block is a valid k-local test φ_S^k for the intersection null H_S, which controls the probability of falsely declaring significance at level α; options include the generalized Bonferroni, Simes, scaled harmonic mean (for p-values), and e-closure-based tests (for e-values). The algorithm identifies the largest rejection size r such that every superset S ⊇ M_{r,k} (the k marginal indices) is rejected by the k-local test, then rejects all hypotheses more significant than the boundary. This closure-based search mechanism is what enables validity under arbitrary dependence without independence assumptions, and the framework is modular—swapping the k-local test tailors power to the available dependence structure while preserving the k-bFDR guarantee.

## Results

On the DepMap CRISPR-Cas9 gene discovery dataset (α=0.1), Domino-P(k=1) achieves a bFDR of 0.06 with 99.32% TDR and 87.20% power, compared to SL's bFDR of 0.27 (98.67% TDR, 90.93% power) and BH's bFDR of 0.59 (95.18% TDR, 94.78% power). On the S&P 500 stock selection task (α=0.1), Domino(k=1) yields a 12.42% out-of-sample return versus 11.94% for SL and 12.35% for BH. In synthetic experiments under arbitrary dependence (ρ=0.25), all Domino variants maintain k-bFDR at or below the nominal α=0.05 level while achieving TDR ≥ 99.2%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the SL procedure (Soloff et al., 2024), Domino achieves substantially lower bFDR (0.04–0.06 vs. 0.19–0.32 across α levels) and higher TDR (99.27–99.37% vs. 98.48–98.84%) on the CRISPR dataset, while sacrificing roughly 3–4 percentage points of power. On the S&P 500 portfolio task, Domino(k=1) outperforms SL by ~0.5 percentage points in out-of-sample return (12.42% vs. 11.94% at α=0.1). Against the BH procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995), Domino's advantage is dramatic on boundary reliability: bFDR drops from 0.46–0.66 (BH) to 0.04–0.06 (Domino), and TDR improves from 92.35–96.48% to 99.27–99.37%, at the cost of 5–8 percentage points of power. In the pathway enrichment analysis on GSE15852, Domino-P(k=1) with only 390 rejections outperforms BH (1769 rejections) on 7 of 8 breast cancer pathways in combined enrichment score, confirming that fewer but more reliable rejections yield biologically meaningful discovery sets. The key trade-off is that Domino's closure-based boundary control is inherently more conservative in power than global FDR control, which is the intended design choice for high-stakes boundary-sensitive applications.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the bFDR framework introduced by Soloff et al. (2024) and Xiang et al. (2025), which controlled the error probability of the single least-significant rejection under independence, by generalizing it to k-bFDR (joint error over k marginal discoveries) and removing the independence requirement via a novel adaptation of the closure principle (Marcus et al., 1976). The closure principle, traditionally deployed for FWER control, is repurposed here to target only the boundary of the rejection set, yielding a less conservative procedure than k-FWER closed testing. The framework is further unified with the e-closure principle (Xu et al., 2025), showing that Domino with the e-closure local test is equivalent to applying that principle directly to the k-bFDR error function, while remaining modular enough to accommodate p-value combination tests (Simes, harmonic mean) and e-value aggregation strategies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'hitchhiking' phenomenon—where strong signals inflate the BH rejection set and admit weak nulls at the boundary—is a practical concern in genomics and finance that global FDR control cannot address; this paper provides the first general-purpose, dependence-agnostic solution with tunable stringency via k. A key open question is data-driven selection of k without double-dipping, which the authors acknowledge requires a redesigned framework. Additionally, the current Domino implementation does not exploit known dependence structures (e.g., exchangeability, weak dependence), leaving a power gap relative to structure-aware methods; closing this gap while preserving k-bFDR validity is a natural next step.
