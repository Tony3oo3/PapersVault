## Problem

Transformer-based time series forecasting models suffer from three intertwined challenges: quadratic computational complexity, token redundancy (hurting low-variate-density datasets) or token scarcity (hurting high-variate-density datasets), and temporal distribution shifts from non-stationary dynamics. Existing Koopman-based approaches like Koopa rely on mixtures of operators without input-dependent adaptability, while linear and multilevel models fail to simultaneously balance redundancy and scarcity across varying variate densities.

## Input / Output

The model takes a multivariate time series X ∈ R^{N×L} (N variates, L=96 historical steps) and produces future predictions Y ∈ R^{N×P}. Prediction horizons vary by dataset: P ∈ {12, 24, 48, 96} for PEMS traffic datasets and P ∈ {96, 192, 336, 720} for the remaining 8 datasets. The input is linearly tokenized and positionally embedded into X_emb ∈ R^{B×N×D} before being split into Koopman and residual components.

## Method

KUMA decomposes the embedded input into two streams via an input-dependent Koopman Dynamic Module (KDM), where the Koopman operator is conditioned on the input itself (K_Dynamic = SoftMax(V × X_emb) * R), avoiding operator mixtures and acting as a structural fence against temporal distribution shifts. The residual dynamics are processed by a U-shaped Multilevel Attention (UMA) module: a U-shaped encoder-decoder with progressive half-dimension down-sampling in encoders and up-sampling in decoders, connected by additive skip connections. Each level applies Element-Wise Filtering (sigmoid-gated element multiplication of Q and K projections) followed by a Linear Attention block that uses ELU-activated keys, Rotary Positional Embedding for re-embedding at each level, and a 1D convolution to integrate multi-head extractions, achieving near-linear O(BND²) complexity. The final prediction fuses the Koopman dynamics and the refined residual output through a linear predictor head.

## Results

Across 12 benchmark datasets (6 LVD, 6 HVD) and 4 prediction horizons, KUMA achieves the best MSE in 14 out of 24 dataset-metric combinations. On HVD traffic datasets, KUMA delivers substantial gains: PEMS07 MSE 0.075 (vs. 0.220 for Koopa, 0.395 for U-Mixer), PEMS04 MSE 0.085 (vs. 0.116 Koopa), and PEMS03 MSE 0.109 (vs. 0.136 Koopa). On LVD datasets, KUMA is competitive but not always best: TimeMixer leads on Exchange (0.332 vs. 0.355 MSE) and Weather (0.238 vs. 0.245), while U-Mixer edges ahead on Exchange (0.349 vs. 0.355). KUMA maintains near-linear computational complexity with low inference latency and moderate memory usage, placing it in the favorable lower-left region of the accuracy–efficiency trade-off plot.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Koopman-based Koopa, KUMA achieves large margins on all four PEMS traffic datasets (e.g., PEMS07: 0.075 vs. 0.220 MSE; PEMS04: 0.085 vs. 0.116), validating the input-dependent operator design that avoids operator mixture under high-variate-density conditions; however, Koopa retains a slight edge on ETTm1 (0.376 vs. 0.388). Versus multilevel baselines U-Mixer and TimeMixer, KUMA is competitive on LVD datasets (TimeMixer leads on Exchange at 0.332 vs. 0.355) but dominates on HVD PEMS datasets where U-Mixer degrades to 0.331–0.403 MSE. Against Transformer-based models (iTransformer, PatchTST, Crossformer, FEDformer, Autoformer), KUMA wins on most HVD datasets and is competitive on LVD, with iTransformer remaining close on Solar-Energy (0.233 vs. 0.235). Linear-based baselines (RLinear, DLinear) are outperformed by KUMA on all HVD datasets but remain competitive on LVD (e.g., DLinear 0.354 vs. KUMA 0.355 on Exchange). S-Mamba is competitive on PEMS07 (0.089 vs. 0.075) and PEMS08 (0.089 vs. 0.129), while WaveRoRA matches KUMA on ECL and ETTh1 but is clearly outperformed on PEMS (0.301–0.327 vs. 0.075–0.129). Overall, KUMA secures the best MSE in 14 of 24 dataset-metric slots and the best MAE in a comparable number, with its largest advantages concentrated in HVD traffic forecasting.

## Relation to Prior Work

KUMA synthesizes three distinct research threads: Koopman operator theory for non-stationary dynamics (building on Koopa's mixture-of-operators approach but replacing it with a single input-dependent operator), fast linear attention (Katharopoulos et al., 2020; Mamba's selective mechanism simplified to a linear attention block), and U-shaped encoder-decoder architectures with skip connections (U-Net, U-Mixer). The key conceptual departure is the explicit mechanistic separation of the input into Koopman-governed dynamics and residual dynamics, each assigned a structurally different processor, rather than the implicit domain-alternation (frequency → temporal) used by Koopa. The element-wise filtering module and the use of ROPE within a multilevel linear-attention backbone are novel combinations not present in prior TSF architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

KUMA offers a practical, unified alternative to mixture-of-Koopman-operators schemes that scale poorly with variate count, and demonstrates that a single input-dependent operator plus a multilevel residual processor can match or exceed dedicated Koopman models on high-variate-density traffic data. However, the paper acknowledges that KUMA still struggles with local features on low-variate-density datasets, and the finite Koopman space introduces approximation error that the residual branch must compensate for. Open questions include whether the input-dependent mechanism truly captures abrupt regime shifts (the authors note it updates 'smoothly and asymptotically') and whether the framework extends naturally to probabilistic or long-horizon forecasting settings.
