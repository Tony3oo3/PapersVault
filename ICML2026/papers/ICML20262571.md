## Problem

Training diffusion and flow policies in online RL requires sampling from the unnormalized Boltzmann distribution π_new(a|s) ∝ exp(Q(s,a)/λ), for which no efficient sampler exists. Two families of methods—noise-expectation and gradient-expectation—have been proposed for diffusion policies, but their formal relationship is unclear, their derivations are rigidly tied to specific noise schedules, and they do not extend to flow policies, leaving the effective training of flow policies from Boltzmann targets as an open problem.

## Input / Output

The method consumes state s, Q-function values Q(s,a) and their gradients ∇Q(s,a), and transition tuples (s,a,r,s′) from a replay buffer. It outputs a continuous action a ∈ [−1,1]^d by integrating a learned latent-space velocity field v_t^θ(u_t, s) over t ∈ [0,1] and applying a = tanh(u_1), which principledly enforces action bounds without the heuristic truncation used by prior methods. Posterior mean estimation at each training step uses K self-normalized importance samples drawn from the source distribution p_0 = N(0, I_d).

## Method

Reverse flow matching (RFM) inverts the standard forward construction: instead of sampling (X_0, X_1) and interpolating to X_t, it treats X_t as observed evidence and infers the latent endpoint via Bayes' theorem, reducing the training target to a posterior mean estimation problem under q_{0|t}^*(x_0|x_t) ∝ p_0(x_0)·p̃_1((x_t − β_t x_0)/α_t). Langevin Stein operators with matrix-valued test functions Φ_t construct vector-valued zero-mean control variates, yielding a general family of SNIS estimators parameterized by coefficients Λ (or isotropic η). The key unification is that the noise-expectation family corresponds to η = 0 and the gradient-expectation family to η = 1, while intermediate values of η blend Q-value and Q-gradient information for variance reduction. The flow policy is trained in an unconstrained latent space with a = tanh(u_1) mapping, and the actor loss regresses the predicted velocity onto the RFM target v̂_t = α̇_t·ū_1 + β̇_t·ū_0, where ū_0 and ū_1 are SNIS posterior mean estimates.

## Results

On the 2D two-moon toy example, RFM achieves the lowest sliced Wasserstein distance, squared MMD, and Sinkhorn distance among all methods while using only 20 ODE integration steps versus 100 denoising steps for the diffusion baselines (iDEM and QNE). On eight DeepMind Control Suite continuous-control environments, RFM is the only method that performs consistently well across all tasks, using only 10 flow steps at inference time compared to 20 diffusion steps for all diffusion-policy baselines. The paper reports results primarily as training curves (Figure 2) and sample-quality plots (Figure 1) rather than a single numerical summary table, emphasizing that RFM exhibits substantially better stability (tighter seed-to-seed variance) than every baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RFM outperforms all five RL baselines (SAC, QSM, MaxEntDP/QNE, DQS, QVPO) on the eight DMC environments, being the only method that does not fail or degrade severely on any task; each baseline struggles on at least one environment. Against the two diffusion-policy Boltzmann-targeting baselines specifically, RFM uses half the inference steps (10 vs. 20) while achieving higher and more stable returns. In the toy two-moon sampling experiment, RFM beats both iDEM (gradient-expectation) and QNE (noise-expectation) on all three distribution-discrepancy metrics (SWD, MMD², Sinkhorn) while requiring only 20 steps versus their 100 steps. SAC, the standard Gaussian-policy maximum-entropy baseline, is outperformed by RFM on most environments, confirming the benefit of the more expressive flow-policy class. QVPO (iterative weighted regression) and QSM (Langevin-dynamics score matching) show the most instability across seeds, whereas RFM's control-variate-based estimator yields the tightest performance band.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the flow matching framework (Lipman et al., 2023) by reversing the inference direction: instead of requiring target samples to construct training pairs, it treats the intermediate noisy state as observed evidence and formulates training as posterior mean estimation. It draws on Langevin Stein operators (Oates et al., 2017) to construct zero-mean control variates that unify two previously distinct families of Boltzmann-targeting methods—noise-expectation (QNE, RSM) and gradient-expectation (iDEM, DQS)—as special cases (η=0 and η=1) of a single estimator. The maximum entropy RL framework (Haarnoja et al., 2018) provides the Boltzmann target, and the paper extends the capability from diffusion policies (Ho et al., 2020) to general flow policies, which admit arbitrary source distributions and require fewer inference steps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unification resolves a conceptual gap in the literature by showing that noise-expectation and gradient-expectation methods are not competing paradigms but points on a continuous spectrum of control variate choices, enabling principled interpolation between Q-value and Q-gradient information for variance reduction. Extending Boltzmann targeting to flow policies is practically significant: flow models require only 10 integration steps versus 20+ for diffusion, and the tanh-based latent-space parameterization provides a clean solution to bounded actions that avoids the heuristic truncations used by prior methods. Open questions include whether the variance reduction from Langevin Stein control variates remains effective in very high-dimensional action spaces, whether the framework extends naturally to multi-modal or hierarchical policies, and whether the proposal distribution choice (policy-induced vs. fixed) can be further optimized for training stability in long-horizon tasks.
