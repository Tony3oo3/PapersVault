## Problem

Existing fairness methods for deep learning cannot simultaneously guarantee strict constraint satisfaction, maintain end-to-end differentiability through the fairness constraints, and remain compatible with arbitrary architectures. Pre-processing and Lagrangian penalty approaches preserve trainability but offer no hard fairness guarantee, while post-hoc projections enforce fairness but operate on a frozen model, breaking the learning loop. This paper identifies and closes this gap for affine constraint sets.

## Input / Output

The fairness layer consumes a batch of raw scalar predictions (logits for classification, continuous values for regression) produced by an upstream neural network, together with binary protected-attribute indicators for each observation in the batch. It outputs a batch of the same dimension whose entries satisfy the specified affine fairness constraints (e.g., bounded group-mean gaps) while remaining as close as possible to the raw predictions in Euclidean distance. In the small-batch streaming regime, individual batches may violate the per-batch constraint; instead, an online dual variable accumulates violations so that the sample-weighted time-average gap converges to the target tolerance ε.

## Method

The fairness layer is a differentiable convex optimization layer appended to the network's output: its forward pass solves a quadratic program that projects the raw prediction vector onto an affine set defined by linear equality and inequality constraints encoding the chosen group-fairness notion (demographic parity, equalized odds, mean conditional parity, etc.). Backpropagation through the layer is computed by differentiating the KKT system via the implicit function theorem, yielding exact Jacobian-vector products without requiring the constraint set to be differentiable in the classical sense. For arbitrarily small inference batches where per-batch constraints are overly restrictive, the paper introduces an online primal-dual algorithm (Algorithm 1) that replaces the hard projection with a soft penalty weighted by a dynamically updated dual variable, provably guaranteeing that the aggregate weighted fairness gap converges to ε as the number of batches grows. Theoretical analysis establishes that the layer is globally 1/μ-Lipschitz, differentiable almost everywhere, and its Jacobian has a binary spectrum {0, 1} that exactly suppresses gradient components in the direction normal to active fairness constraints, preventing backpropagation from ever encouraging the model toward infeasible (unfair) predictions.

## Results

On image classification (CelebA smile detection and FairFace age prediction), the F-Layer method achieves the highest accuracy across all five architectures, reaching 91.8% on CelebA (Swin-T) and 80.8% on FairFace (Swin-T), consistently outperforming the Projection baseline by 2–5 percentage points. On the employee performance regression task, F-Layer achieves the lowest MSE (0.0834) while satisfying all 10/10 fairness constraints, compared to 0.0838 for Projection and 0.0933 for Penalty (λ=100). On the synthetic regression benchmark (32 datasets), F-Layer ranks first in 22–27 of 32 datasets and achieves 18–30% test loss reductions on 16–24 datasets, with median improvements of ~8% (large batch) and ~20% (small batch) over all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The F-Layer method consistently outperforms the post-hoc Projection baseline (Wei et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2019) across all experimental settings: on image classification it gains 2–5 percentage points in accuracy (e.g., 91.8% vs. 88.8% on CelebA with Swin-T), and on the employee performance task it achieves marginally lower MSE (0.0834 vs. 0.0838) while avoiding the post-hoc wage modifications that Projection imposes on 319 individuals. Against the Lagrangian Penalty baselines (Padala & Gujar, 2021; Cotter et al., 2019), the F-Layer advantage is dramatic: on image tasks, Penalty and Strict Penalty methods collapse to near-random accuracy (~50% on CelebA, ~55% on FairFace) because the large penalty weights needed for constraint satisfaction overwhelm the classification loss. On the loan default task, Strict Penalty (λ=1000) degenerates to predicting a single class for every observation, reducing AUC by ~45% and average precision by ~55%. On the synthetic benchmark, Penalty methods frequently violate fairness constraints despite validation-based λ selection (7 of 11 violations had gaps 7× larger than target), whereas F-Layer satisfies constraints in all 32 datasets. The only setting where F-Layer underperforms is 6–8 synthetic datasets with very tight box constraints (ℓ=0, u=3.5), where post-hoc projections may escape local minima that trap the end-to-end training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the differentiable convex optimization layer framework (Agrawal et al., NeurIPS 2019; Gould et al., IEEE TPAMI 2021) from its traditional predict-then-optimize role into a 'fairness layer' appended to a network's output, projecting raw predictions onto an affine fairness constraint set. Conceptually, it departs from the prior use of declarative nodes as downstream decision solvers by treating the optimization problem as a constraint-enforcing projection that remains differentiable via KKT-based backpropagation. The paper also introduces an online primal-dual inference algorithm for small-batch streaming settings, which has no direct analogue in the existing differentiable optimization layer literature and addresses a practical gap where per-batch hard constraints become overly restrictive.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The fairness layer provides a clean, drop-in architectural component that simultaneously guarantees constraint satisfaction, maintains end-to-end differentiability, and is architecture-agnostic—properties no prior fairness method achieves together. The online primal-dual algorithm extends applicability to streaming and small-batch inference regimes common in production deployment. Key limitations include restriction to affine (convex) constraint sets, meaning hard-thresholded fairness notions like classical demographic parity remain out of reach, and the aggregate fairness guarantee is asymptotic. Open questions include extending to nonconvex constraints, characterizing when the fairness layer provably outperforms post-hoc projection, and whether the approach scales to intersectional or individual fairness settings.
