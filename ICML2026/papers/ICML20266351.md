## Problem

Current benchmarking of 12-lead ECG representations relies on three narrow arrhythmia/waveform datasets (PTB-XL, CPSC2018, CSN) and reports macro-AUROC point estimates without uncertainty, producing misleading method rankings that obscure clinically relevant performance differences. The paper argues that evaluation must expand to structurally grounded tasks (structural disease, hemodynamic inference, patient forecasting) and adopt rigorous multi-label reporting practices to ensure progress is reliable and clinically meaningful.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266351/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

All ECGs are standardized to 10-second 12-lead segments in millivolts at 500 Hz. Each encoder produces a fixed-dimensional embedding that is frozen and paired with a single L2-regularized logistic regression probe (linear probing) for each downstream task. Performance is reported as per-task AUROC and AUPRC with 95% confidence intervals from 1,000-resample paired bootstrap on the test set, across six evaluation settings spanning arrhythmia classification, structural disease (EchoNext), hemodynamic inference (mPCWP, mPA), and 1-year heart failure forecasting.

## Method

The paper proposes a taxonomy of clinically grounded ECG tasks (arrhythmia/waveform, structural disease, hemodynamic state, patient forecasting) and a set of evaluation best practices: report per-task AUROC and AUPRC rather than only macro-AUROC, include bootstrapped confidence intervals, use paired permutation tests for significance, and exclude labels with fewer than 10 positive test examples. Empirically, five representative pre-trained encoders (CLOCS, KED, HeartLang, MERL, D-BETA) spanning contrastive, reconstruction, and multi-modal ECG-text paradigms are evaluated via linear probing against a randomly initialized ResNet-18 baseline across six datasets. A key finding is that the random encoder is competitive with or exceeds CLOCS and HeartLang on many standard benchmarks, and that method rankings shift materially once uncertainty is quantified and low-support labels are removed. A robustness check across 36 random encoder configurations (varying sampling rate, filtering, normalization, and backbone) confirms the baseline's competitiveness is not an artifact of a single setup.

## Results

Across the standard arrhythmia/waveform benchmarks (PTB-XL, CPSC2018, CSN), KED and D-BETA achieve the highest macro-AUROC (e.g., KED 0.909 on PTB-XL SUPER, D-BETA 0.958 on CPSC2018 at 100% data), but once 95% bootstrap confidence intervals are reported, neither method is consistently statistically superior to the others. A randomly initialized ResNet-18 encoder with linear probing is surprisingly competitive, matching or exceeding CLOCS and HeartLang on many tasks (e.g., Random 0.861 vs. CLOCS 0.821 on PTB-XL SUPER at 100% data). On the structural disease benchmark (EchoNext), CLOCS and HeartLang clearly underperform (0.714 and 0.736 macro-AUROC at 100% data) while MERL leads (0.791) with the random baseline close behind (0.773). On 1-year heart failure forecasting, MERL and KED achieve the best AUROC (0.83), outperforming the random baseline (0.78) and CLOCS (0.75).

## Baselines & Comparisons

KED (Tian et al., 2024) and D-BETA (Hung et al., 2024) achieve the highest macro-AUROC on most standard arrhythmia/waveform benchmarks, but their apparent dominance dissolves once uncertainty is quantified: on PTB-XL FORM at 100% data, MERL (0.853) and KED (0.851) are statistically indistinguishable, and on PTB-XL RHYTHM, D-BETA (0.968) and KED (0.959) overlap within CIs. CLOCS (Kiyasseh et al., 2021) and HeartLang (Jin et al., 2025) are consistently outperformed by the randomly initialized ResNet-18 baseline on PTB-XL SUPER (0.821 and 0.824 vs. 0.861) and underperform most notably on EchoNext structural disease (0.714 and 0.736 vs. 0.773 for Random). MERL (Liu et al., 2024) is the strongest method on EchoNext (0.791) and ties with KED on 1-year heart failure forecasting (AUROC 0.83), while D-BETA clearly outperforms CLOCS and HeartLang on the imbalanced CLBBB label via AUPRC (0.970 vs. 0.676 and 0.867). Overall, no single pre-training method consistently dominates across all six evaluation settings, and the random encoder baseline is non-trivial on every task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not propose a new ECG representation method but instead critiques and reformulates how the field evaluates existing ones. It builds on the general ML benchmarking-critique tradition (Lipton & Steinhardt, Pineau et al.) and applies it specifically to 12-lead ECG representation learning, where the community has converged on three arrhythmia-focused datasets (PTB-XL, CPSC2018, CSN) and macro-AUROC as the headline metric. The paper departs from prior ECG benchmarking work by (1) proposing additional clinically grounded task families—structural disease, hemodynamic inference, and patient forecasting—and (2) empirically demonstrating that standard evaluation best practices (per-task reporting, AUPRC, uncertainty quantification, excluding low-support labels) materially alter method rankings. It also introduces a randomly initialized encoder as a mandatory baseline, a practice largely absent from the 28-paper survey set.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a randomly initialized ResNet-18 with linear probing matches or exceeds SOTA pretraining (KED, D-BETA, MERL) on many standard ECG tasks challenges the field's core assumption that self-supervised or multi-modal pretraining is necessary for competitive ECG representations. This has immediate practical implications: if pretraining gains are small or absent on many endpoints, the field's investment in increasingly complex pretraining objectives may be misdirected. Open questions include why the random encoder is so performant (the authors hypothesize the clinically relevant signal is too apparent for random convolutions to distort it), whether the result persists under fine-tuning rather than linear probing, and how to design a community-agreed benchmark that includes both public and private clinical endpoints. A limitation is that the study is restricted to 12-lead ECG and linear probing, so conclusions may not transfer to 1-lead ECG, PPG, EEG, or full fine-tuning settings.
