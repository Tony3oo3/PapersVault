## Problem

Analyzing large-scale text corpora—especially LLM outputs—requires identifying semantic differences, biases, and structural patterns at scale. Existing approaches either rely on expensive, prompt-sensitive LLM-based annotation or on dense embeddings that are fast but offer no interpretability or control over which properties to examine. This paper asks whether sparse autoencoders (SAEs) trained on LLM hidden states can serve as a unified, cost-effective, and controllable toolkit for such data analysis tasks.

## Input / Output

The method takes raw text documents as input and passes them through a fixed 'reader LLM' (Llama 3.3 70B) to extract layer-50 hidden states, which are then encoded by a pretrained Goodfire SAE (d_SAE = 65,536 latents, trained on LMSYS-Chat-1M). The output is a single max-pooled SAE embedding per document where each dimension corresponds to a human-labeled concept; this embedding can be binarized into thousands of binary concept labels or filtered to a subset of latents for targeted analysis. Downstream, the embeddings yield dataset-difference hypotheses, NPMI-ranked concept-correlation pairs, spectral clusters with natural-language descriptions, and property-based retrieval rankings.

## Method

The core mechanism is to repurpose SAEs—originally designed for mechanistic interpretability of model internals—as unsupervised data labelers: by feeding arbitrary text through a reader LLM and its SAE, each document is simultaneously annotated with the presence or absence of ~65K interpretable concepts, with no task-specific training required. Latent labels are generated by showing an LLM ten activating and ten non-activating token spans per latent (following Paulo et al., 2024), producing a fixed vocabulary of semantic properties. For dataset diffing, the method computes per-latent frequency differences across datasets and surfaces the top-differing concepts; for correlation discovery, it computes normalized pointwise mutual information (NPMI) over all latent pairs and filters for high-NPMI/low-semantic-similarity pairs to surface 'interesting' correlations. For clustering and retrieval, the embedding is made controllable by filtering to latents whose labels match a natural-language query, then applying spectral clustering on the Jaccard similarity matrix or scoring documents by weighted latent activations. The key distinction from prior work is that the SAE operates on token-level LLM hidden states (not dense sentence embeddings) and provides a large, fixed hypothesis space that can be searched without pre-specifying what properties to look for, enabling discovery of unknown-unknowns at 2–8× lower cost than LLM-based baselines.

## Results

On ground-truth dataset diffing, SAE embeddings achieve surface similarity scores of 0.75 (movie genres) and 0.80 (tone changes), recovering known differences while being 2–8× cheaper than LLM baselines. For correlation discovery, SAEs recover all four injected synthetic correlations, whereas LLM baselines detect them in only 1/10 to 9/10 shuffled trials. On targeted clustering of GSM8k solutions, SAE embeddings produce reasoning-style clusters with z-scores of −2.36 to −4.26 in dense embedding space (confirming novel structure), compared to −16.0 to −45.9 for dense-embedding clusters. On property-based retrieval across six datasets, SAE embeddings generally outperform or match all baselines (OpenAI, Gemini, Qwen, BM25+LLM), with the largest gains on model-related data (chat responses, reasoning traces) matching the SAE's training distribution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the LLM-based diffing baselines adapted from Dunlap et al. (2025) (LLM-S summarization and LLM-C clustering), SAE embeddings produce hypotheses with higher average judge-verified frequency differences (Figure 2) and higher verification rates in multi-model comparisons, while costing 2–8× fewer tokens (e.g., 3.5M vs. 25–27M tokens for multi-model diffing); however, LLM baselines achieve slightly better overall coverage in simpler two-model settings. For clustering, SAE targeted embeddings discover reasoning-style groupings on GSM8k that both OpenAI text-embedding-3-large and Instructor-Large (Su et al., 2023) miss entirely, as the latter cluster by semantic content; the near-zero z-scores of SAE clusters in dense space confirm they capture structure invisible to dense representations. On property-based retrieval, SAE embeddings outperform or match OpenAI, Gemini (Lee et al., 2025), Qwen3 (Zhang et al., 2025), and BM25+LLM (Lu et al., 2024) baselines across six datasets, with the clearest wins on model-generated text (chat responses, DeepSeek-R1 traces) where the SAE's training distribution (LMSYS-Chat-1M) aligns. For correlation discovery, SAE-discovered latent pairs show higher verified NPMI than both LLM-generated hypotheses and correlated topic model (Blei & Lafferty, 2005) baselines on CivilComments and the Pile, and SAEs reliably recover all four synthetic correlations while LLMs fail inconsistently (1/10 to 9/10 across shuffles).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes sparse autoencoders (SAEs) from mechanistic interpretability—where they decompose LLM activations into monosemantic features (Cunningham et al., 2023; Bricken et al., 2023; Templeton et al., 2024)—as a data analysis tool, using them to produce interpretable embeddings of arbitrary text via a 'reader' LLM. It builds directly on Paulo et al. (2024) for automatic latent labeling and extends the line of work by O'Neill et al. (2024) and Movva et al. (2025), who applied SAEs to dense embeddings for retrieval control and hypothesis generation, by instead using SAEs trained on token-level hidden states and exploring a broader suite of tasks (diffing, correlations, clustering, retrieval). The key conceptual departure is shifting the interpretability target from model internals to the data itself, positioning SAEs as a scalable, controllable alternative to LLM-as-judge workflows (Dunlap et al., 2025) and dense embeddings (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that SAE embeddings offer a 2–8× cheaper and more reliable alternative to LLM-based data analysis while providing controllability (filtering by concept) that dense embeddings lack, making them a practical toolkit for auditing training data and characterizing model behavior at scale. The Tulu-3 case study—where a spurious 'I hope it is correct' trigger was discovered and verified—illustrates the method's value for debugging post-training datasets without predefined labels. Open questions include sensitivity to SAE training data and feature absorption, the fact that SAEs are not optimized for similarity (limiting their use as general-purpose embeddings), and whether domain-specific or larger SAEs would improve coverage. More broadly, the paper argues for 'data-centric interpretability' as a complementary direction to model-internal analysis, a framing that could reshape how practitioners audit and understand LLMs.
