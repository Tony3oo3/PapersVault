## Problem

Long-form video understanding remains difficult because existing MLLM approaches either rely on static single-turn reasoning over uniformly sampled frames (risking omission of critical evidence in long videos) or depend on external VLMs and hand-designed pipelines (introducing high complexity and sub-optimal tool usage). Moreover, these methods typically require 257K–4.4M supervised examples, making them data-intensive. The paper asks whether a unified, end-to-end model can learn iterative, query-driven video segment selection through pure reinforcement learning with only a few thousand training samples.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265150/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a long video (preprocessed into a candidate pool of up to 128 uniformly subsampled frames) and a multiple-choice text question. It operates under a total frame budget of 32, 64, or 80 frames, initially observing a sparse uniform subset and then issuing retrieval actions that specify a temporal interval (start_frame, end_frame) to fetch up to 8 additional frames per turn. The output is a multi-turn trajectory of (reasoning text, action) pairs, where each action is either a retrieval command or a final single-letter answer, with a maximum of 3 turns.

## Method

Video-MTR reformulates long-video QA as a multi-turn RL decision-making problem: an MLLM agent (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) iteratively selects key video segments conditioned on previously observed frames and the query, trained end-to-end with PPO via the VAGEN framework. The key technical contribution is a gated bi-level reward that combines a binary trajectory-level accuracy reward with turn-level frame-retrieval rewards (IoU-based, capped at 0.5) and a format reward (0.1); critically, all turn-level rewards are gated by final-answer correctness, preventing the agent from exploiting redundant retrievals to inflate reward without improving QA accuracy. Two discount factors (γ_turn=0.95 cross-turn, γ_token=1.0 within-turn) propagate the sparse bi-level signals to token-level GAE advantages. Training uses only ~8K samples curated from NExT-GQA and QVHighlights (repurposed temporal grounding data), and an adaptive exploration bonus bootstraps multi-turn retrieval behavior without any supervised fine-tuning warm-up.

## Results

With 80 input frames, Video-MTR (7B) achieves 62.7 on VideoMME, 50.4 on MLVU, 57.1 on LongVideoBench, 42.3 on LVBench, and 68.8 on EgoSchema. Notably, it surpasses its Qwen2.5-VL-7B backbone (768 frames) on EgoSchema (+3.8) and LongVideoBench (+1.1) while using only 80 frames. The framework is trained with only ~8K samples via pure RL, compared to 257K–4.4M supervised examples required by competing methods. Under matched 80-frame budgets, Video-MTR outperforms the single-turn backbone by +3.2 (VideoMME), +5.2 (MLVU), +8.7 (LongVideoBench), +8.7 (LVBench), and +5.3 (EgoSchema).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against its own backbone Qwen2.5-VL-7B at matched 80-frame budgets, Video-MTR wins on every benchmark, with the largest gains on LongVideoBench (+8.7) and LVBench (+8.7), confirming the multi-turn retrieval paradigm's value. Compared to the 768-frame Qwen2.5-VL-7B, Video-MTR (80 frames) still outperforms on EgoSchema (68.8 vs 65.0) and LongVideoBench (57.1 vs 56.0) while using ~10× fewer frames. Among open-source 7B baselines, Video-MTR surpasses Video-R1 (64f) by +1.3 on VideoMME and +17.0 on EgoSchema, Video-XL by +7.2 on VideoMME and +4.8 on MLVU, and LongVA by +10.1 on VideoMME and +9.3 on MLVU. Against agentic systems (VideoAgent, VideoMemAgent, DrVideo), Video-MTR leads on VideoMME by +6.7, +5.3, and +11.0 respectively, and on EgoSchema by +8.6, +5.6, and +2.4. Video-MTR trails proprietary GPT-4o and Gemini-1.5-Pro on most benchmarks (e.g., 62.7 vs 71.9 on VideoMME), but these systems use 384+ frames and hundreds of billions of parameters, whereas Video-MTR uses 80 frames and 7B parameters trained on only 8K samples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two recent lines of work: agentic video understanding (VideoAgent, VideoMemAgent, DrVideo) that uses external VLMs for iterative frame retrieval, and RL-based post-training of MLLMs (VLM-R1, Video-R1, VideoChat-R1) that has so far targeted single-turn reasoning on short clips or images. Video-MTR departs from both by (i) eliminating external VLMs and pipelines in favor of a single unified MLLM that performs retrieval and answering end-to-end, and (ii) replacing the standard SFT-then-RL or SFT-only paradigm with pure RL post-training, enabled by a novel gated bi-level reward that provides turn-level supervision without requiring large-scale supervised data. The multi-turn RL training infrastructure is borrowed from the VAGEN framework, and the backbone is Qwen2.5-VL-7B.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is demonstrating that a 7B MLLM can be transformed from a single-turn to a multi-turn video reasoner using only ~8K training samples via pure RL, compared to 257K–4.4M samples for SFT-based competitors, while matching or exceeding their accuracy on five long-video benchmarks. This opens a path to efficient, data-light adaptation of open-source MLLMs for long-form video without the cost of large-scale annotation or external tool pipelines. However, the frame budget is capped at 80 frames, the model still trails proprietary systems (GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5-Pro) by a meaningful margin, and failure analysis reveals weaknesses on multi-detail reasoning and fine-grained perception that would require hierarchical temporal-to-spatial zoom-in mechanisms not yet implemented.
