## Problem

Chain-of-Thought reasoning in LLMs produces verbose natural-language rationales that are poorly aligned with efficient machine inference, yet existing remedies—prompt optimization, program-based pipelines, and RL-based compression—either refine surface instructions, depend on human-designed intermediate languages, or incur heavy training costs. The paper asks whether LLM agents can autonomously invent, evolve, and share compact symbolic languages optimized for their own tokenization and inductive biases, and route among them adaptively per query.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263617/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the system takes a text query and an offline-evolved pool of Language Symbolism Frameworks (LSFs), each stored as a compact textual card (500–2000 tokens) encoding a symbol inventory, lightweight grammar, usage constraints, and an empirical profile (accuracy, median token footprint, domain tags, failure modes). The LLM-router first emits a short category code (<10 tokens) to prune the candidate pool, then a single-line protocol plan specifying mode (single/aggregate/compose), selected LSF IDs, round structure, aggregation rule, and stopping criterion. The final output is a predicted answer; all generated completion tokens across router plans, LSF responses, and aggregation steps are counted toward a unified latency-oriented cost metric.

## Method

CLSR is a test-time framework in which multiple black-box LLM agents iteratively propose, critique, and mutate candidate LSFs through an evolutionary bootstrapping loop: exemplars seed initial LSF synthesis, high-leverage traces (correct and token-efficient) are selected via a Pareto criterion, and the LLM refines or recombines parent LSFs into the next generation, with elitist inheritance preserving top performers. At inference, a latent-free LLM-router adaptively chooses among three protocol modes—single-LSF direct answer, multi-LSF aggregation (self-consistency-style voting), or multi-round LSF composition where intermediate symbolic outputs feed subsequent rounds—explicitly optimizing the accuracy-token trade-off under a budget constraint. The paper formalizes this as a constrained stochastic control problem, derives an information-theoretic lower bound on expected generated tokens via a Fano-type argument, and proves that under an interpreter-realizability premise, multi-round multi-LSF protocols conditionally subsume program-execution pipelines (PoT/PAL) without requiring an external executor.

## Results

Across seven reasoning benchmarks and four open-source backbones (LLaMA3-8B, DeepSeek-R1-Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-32B), CLSR consistently matches or slightly exceeds Raw CoT accuracy while reducing generated completion tokens by 3–6×. For example, on Qwen3-32B, CLSR achieves 68.1% on MMLU-Pro with 118 tokens (vs. 67.5% / 405 tokens for CoT) and 89.4% on MATH500 with 206 tokens (vs. 89.3% / 845 tokens). With adaptive multi-round routing (T=3), CLSR reaches 94.8% on GSM8K and 89.7% on MATH500 on Qwen3-8B, surpassing all baselines on the accuracy–token Pareto frontier.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Raw CoT, CLSR matches or slightly exceeds accuracy on nearly all benchmark–backbone pairs while cutting generated tokens by roughly 3–6× (e.g., Qwen3-32B MATH500: 89.4% @ 206 tokens vs. 89.3% @ 845 tokens). Against the three token-reduction prompting baselines—CoD, CCoT, and SoT—CLSR dominates the Pareto frontier: these methods save tokens by sacrificing 3–10+ points of accuracy (e.g., CoD drops MATH500 from 89.3% to 81.5% on Qwen3-32B), whereas CLSR retains CoT-level accuracy at the reduced budget. Against program-execution baselines PoT and PAL (Table 2, Qwen3-8B), CLSR with T=3 reaches 94.8% on GSM8K and 89.7% on MATH500, exceeding both PoT (92.1%/88.1%) and PAL (92.5%/88.6%) in accuracy while using fewer LLM-generated tokens (214/417 vs. 113/375 for PoT), and without requiring an external executor. Against prompt-optimization baselines P2S and PromptBreeder, CLSR achieves comparable or higher accuracy at substantially lower token cost (e.g., MATH500: 86.8% @ 257 tokens for CLSR vs. 88.9% @ 1205 tokens for P2S and 88.4% @ 843 tokens for PBrd). The one area where CLSR trails is GPQA on Qwen3-8B at the default adaptive setting (42.6% vs. 46.1% for PBrd), though CLSR(T=3) recovers to 49.2%, suggesting the router's conservative single-round default under-allocates compute on this adversarial benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLSR carves new ground at the intersection of emergent communication theory, evolutionary optimization, and test-time reasoning. It departs from prompt optimization (which refines surface instructions) by evolving reusable symbolic protocols (LSFs) with grammar, symbol inventories, and usage rules; from program-execution pipelines (PoT, PAL) by eliminating external executors and instead letting multi-round LSF composition internally approximate deterministic computation; and from RL-based compression (Orion, RLEF) by avoiding training entirely in favor of a black-box evolutionary loop. The sociolinguistic framing—treating LSF selection as cultural evolution under a communication bottleneck—draws on emergent-communication literature (Lazaridou & Baroni, Tucker et al.) but operationalizes it for frozen LLM agents rather than trained communicators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 3–6× reduction in generated completion tokens while matching or slightly exceeding Raw CoT accuracy is practically significant for latency-constrained deployments, and the information-theoretic lower bound (Theorem 3.2) gives a principled ceiling on how much further any symbolic protocol can compress reasoning. However, the offline evolutionary cost (days of GPU time, hundreds of LSFs, multiple generations) limits applicability to repeated-use services, and the cross-model transfer results (Table 4) show that LSFs are backbone-sensitive, weakening the 'universal language' narrative. Open questions include whether LSFs transfer across modalities or tool-use settings, whether the router's adaptive stopping rule generalizes to open-ended generation, and whether the interpretability loss from compact symbolic traces is acceptable for safety-critical auditing.
