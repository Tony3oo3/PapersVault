## Problem

Existing unsupervised graph structure learning (u-GSL) methods scale poorly to large graphs, typically failing beyond 10K–50K nodes due to O(N²) or O(N³) computational and memory costs, and require complete re-learning whenever new nodes arrive in dynamically expanding graphs. No prior framework provides an efficient, incremental mechanism for learning graph topology on datasets with millions of nodes or for accommodating continuous node arrivals without expensive re-training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a node feature matrix X ∈ R^{N×d} and, optionally, an existing adjacency matrix A for a static subgraph; the remaining nodes arrive incrementally over T timestamps (default T=25, with 50% of nodes static). Output is a progressively updated adjacency (or Laplacian) matrix for the full graph G_T, where each timestamp only requires learning edges between incoming nodes and a small candidate subset rather than the entire graph. The framework supports 48 modular configurations by independently selecting from clustering, coarsening, and structure-learning modules, making it adaptable to settings where the base graph is entirely missing or partially known.

## Method

GraphFLEx is a three-stage pipeline that restricts edge formation to structurally relevant node subsets: (1) a GNN-based clustering module (DMoN, maximizing spectral modularity) is trained once on a static subgraph and then used to assign incoming nodes to communities; (2) an LSH-based coarsening module (UGC) compresses each relevant community into a small set of supernodes via random projection hashing, preserving neighborhood structure with a provable probability bound (Theorem 3.5); (3) any u-GSL method (kNN, A-NN, GLasso, log/l2/large-model, SUBLIME, empirical covariance) is applied only on the coarsened supernodes plus incoming nodes, and the learned structure is projected back to the original graph via the partition matrix. This multi-step coarsen-learn-project strategy reduces the effective search space from O(N²) to O(α log α) in the best case, where α is the number of supernodes plus incoming nodes in the relevant community, enabling structure learning on graphs up to ~2.4M nodes. The framework provides theoretical guarantees on clustering consistency under DC-SBM and neighborhood preservation via LSH collision probability, and its 48 configurations (α×β×γ module combinations) allow tuning to specific graph settings and learning objectives.

## Results

GraphFLEx achieves up to 3× speedup over A-NN and 2× over empirical covariance estimation while maintaining or exceeding base-structure node classification accuracy. On the CS dataset, GraphFLEx with empirical covariance (SAGE) reaches 93.30% accuracy, surpassing the original graph's 80.33%. On large-scale graphs (Table 4), vanilla methods hit OOM/OOT on nearly all datasets beyond ~20K nodes, whereas GraphFLEx successfully scales log-model, l2-model, and large-model to Flickr (89K), Ogbn-arxiv (169K), and Reddit (233K), and supports Covar/ANN/KNN on Ogbn-products (2.4M nodes) with accuracies within 1–5% of the base structure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All six baselines (A-NN, k-NN, log-model, l2-model, empirical covariance, SUBLIME) are evaluated both in their vanilla form and wrapped inside GraphFLEx. The dominant finding is that vanilla versions become infeasible (OOM or OOT) on most datasets beyond 10K–20K nodes: vanilla k-NN fails above ~10K nodes, log-model and l2-model fail above ~50K, and SUBLIME OOMs on DBLP, CS, PubMed, and Physics. GraphFLEx rescapes every method, making log-model, l2-model, and large-model runnable on Flickr, Ogbn-arxiv, and Reddit where they previously could not execute at all. In terms of accuracy, GraphFLEx consistently outperforms vanilla structures by large margins (e.g., CS/SAGE: ANN vanilla 12.70% → GFlex 78.81%; DBLP/GAT: ANN vanilla 34.23% → GFlex 67.37%), and on several datasets (CS with emp-Covar, DBLP with l2-model) it even exceeds the original base-structure accuracy, indicating effective denoising. On link prediction (Table 5), GraphFLEx matches or beats the base structure on 5 of 6 datasets (e.g., Citeseer: 96.32% vs. base 90.78%), while the sole exception is Physics where it trails by ~2.6%. SUBLIME, the most recent unsupervised baseline, is the hardest to beat on small graphs but still OOMs on all datasets ≥17K nodes in vanilla form, whereas GraphFLEx-SUBLIME completes in under 1 second on Cora and under 1050 s on CS.

## Relation to Prior Work

GraphFLEx unifies three previously separate graph-processing paradigms—GNN-based community detection (DMoN, Tsitsulin et al. 2023), LSH-based graph coarsening (UGC, Kataria et al. 2024), and unsupervised structure learning (k-NN, A-NN, GLasso, log/l2/large-model, SUBLIME)—into a single modular pipeline for incremental graph construction. Prior u-GSL methods assume a fixed, complete dataset and learn the full adjacency in one shot, while s-GSL methods (Pro-GNN, SLAPS, NodeFormer) require labels and an observed base graph. The paper explicitly distinguishes itself from Dynamic Graph Learning and Continual Learning, positioning the contribution as extending u-GSL to the large-scale, expanding-graph regime where prior methods are computationally infeasible.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework makes unsupervised structure learning practical on graphs up to ~2.4M nodes (Ogbn-products), a regime where all prior u-GSL baselines hit OOM/OOT, and provides theoretical guarantees (neighborhood preservation via LSH, clustering consistency under DC-SBM) that are rare in the GSL literature. The 48 modular configurations and the demonstrated denoising effect (learned structure on CS outperforming the original) suggest broad applicability. Open questions include performance under heterophily (the DC-SBM assumption), handling edge deletions or node removals (only additions are modeled), and whether the static-node ratio r can be learned adaptively rather than set manually.
