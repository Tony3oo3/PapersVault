## Problem

Current Secure Aggregation protocols in Federated Learning enforce unconditional cryptographic blindness over individual model updates, which simultaneously disables input-level poisoning detection (the Sanitization Gap) and severs the chain of custody required by the EU AI Act's traceability and robustness mandates (the Regulatory Dead Zone). The paper argues that privacy and auditability have been treated as a zero-sum trade-off and that no architectural framework exists to reconcile fine-grained cryptographic governance with default confidentiality in federated settings.

## Input / Output

Each client submits an encrypted gradient Δw_i under an ABFE access policy, accompanied by a non-interactive ZK proof (zkSNARK/Halo2) attesting to well-formedness constraints such as an L2-norm bound ‖Δw_i‖₂ ≤ τ and certified training provenance. The server additionally collects partial functional decryption keys from the client cohort (bound to a consensus weighting vector α and round label L) and, in exceptional audit scenarios, (t,n)-threshold key shares from independent custodians. The system outputs a verified aggregate ⟨α, x⟩, a per-round policy-compliance attestation, and an immutable on-chain audit-log entry whenever the break-glass channel is invoked.

## Method

CSA is a four-pillar architectural stack that composes existing cryptographic primitives into a governed aggregation protocol. Pillar I uses Attribute-Based Functional Encryption to embed participation policies (e.g., jurisdiction, professional certification) directly into ciphertexts so that non-compliant updates are mathematically excluded from aggregation. Pillar II employs Decentralized Multi-Client Functional Encryption to bind the server's computation to a consensus-specified inner-product function, preventing isolation attacks and unilateral decryption. Pillar III mandates per-client ZK proofs of gradient well-formedness (norm bounds, algorithm certification) verified before inclusion, closing the sanitization gap without revealing gradient values. Pillar IV introduces a (t,n)-threshold break-glass mechanism with blockchain-logged invocations, enabling multi-party-consent selective decryption for forensic audit while preserving default-round indistinguishability. The key conceptual distinction the paper draws is between verifiability (was the sum computed correctly?) and auditability (was each input admissible and the actor accountable?), arguing that existing verifiable FL schemes address only the former.

## Results

This is a position paper that does not report quantitative experimental metrics (no accuracy, F1, or throughput numbers). Its primary contribution is the formulation of the Privacy-Auditability Paradox and the architectural proposal of Controllable Secure Aggregation (CSA) as a four-pillar stack (ABFE admission control, DMCFE function binding, ZKP input validity, and (t,n)-threshold break-glass auditing). The paper's main empirical-adjacent content is a qualitative compliance mapping (Table 2) showing that blind SecAgg provides only 'Limited Support' for EU AI Act Articles 15 and 12 and GDPR Article 17, whereas CSA is argued to be 'Compliant by Design' across all four regulatory requirements. The feasibility discussion (Section 4.2) sketches indicative overheads: ZKP proving at seconds-scale per client on commodity CPUs with 10–100× reduction under GPU/NPU offload, proof sizes in the kilobyte range with millisecond verification, and ABFE/DMCFE ciphertext expansion of 2–10× over plaintext gradients.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, CSA is not benchmarked against baselines in the empirical sense; the `compares_to` set is empty. Instead, Section 2.4 delineates CSA's relationship to five existing lines of work: RoFL (Lycklama et al., 2023) attaches ZK range proofs to masked inputs, covering only Pillar III (input well-formedness) without admission control or a governed audit channel; ELSA (Rathee et al., 2023) achieves malicious-secure two-server aggregation with input validation but leaves compliance and provenance unaddressed; BREA (So et al., 2021) attains Byzantine-resilient SecAgg via secret-sharing and secure pairwise distances at quadratic cost, orthogonal to participation eligibility; FedML-HE (Jin et al., 2023) selectively encrypts sensitive layers at 10–40× overhead with no native auditability; and post-hoc statistical filters (Han et al., 2026) add heuristic anomaly detection not cryptographically bound to identity or policy. The paper's central claim is that no single one of these systems closes the full Privacy-Auditability Paradox, and that CSA provides an architectural envelope composing all four pillars jointly rather than addressing any one in isolation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes four existing cryptographic primitive families—Attribute-Based Functional Encryption (Abdalla et al., 2020; Boyen, 2013), Decentralized Multi-Client Functional Encryption (Chotard et al., 2020; Qian et al., 2024), Zero-Knowledge Proofs (Garg et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024b), and threshold cryptography (Benhamouda et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2024)—into a unified four-pillar architecture called Controllable Secure Aggregation (CSA), explicitly departing from the blind SecAgg paradigm of Bonawitz et al. (2017) and Bell et al. (2020, 2023). It positions itself against partial solutions like RoFL (Lycklama et al., 2023), ELSA (Rathee et al., 2023), and BREA (So et al., 2021), arguing that each addresses a single subproblem (input validation, server correctness, or Byzantine resilience) while the joint absence of admission control, function binding, input validity, and governed provenance is what constitutes the Privacy-Auditability Paradox. The paper's core conceptual departure is distinguishing verifiability (correct aggregation) from auditability (input admissibility and actor accountability), a separation it argues the VFL literature (homomorphic signatures, ZK-based aggregation checks) has conflated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that default blind SecAgg is structurally non-compliant with the EU AI Act's robustness (Art. 15) and traceability (Art. 12) mandates—introduces a time-bound regulatory pressure that could reshape FL system design in the EU and beyond. However, as a position paper with no implementation, benchmarks, or end-to-end evaluation, the computational feasibility of the full CSA stack (bilinear pairings + ZKP proving + threshold key management at production scale) remains entirely unvalidated; the authors themselves frame CSA as an 'enabling substrate' rather than a deployment-ready protocol. Key open questions include whether the staged migration path (ZK proofs first, then ABFE, then DMCFE, then break-glass) is actually backward-compatible in practice, how custodian collusion and governance failure modes should be stress-tested, and whether the 'Compliance Tax' argument (analogous to HTTP→HTTPS) will hold when edge-device budgets are tight. The paper is best read as an architectural thesis that could catalyze a research program rather than as a contribution with settled conclusions.
