## Problem

Existing evaluations of relational reasoning in LLMs rely on structured inputs like graphs or tables and use noisy proxies such as input length or entity count to gauge difficulty, conflating parsing cost with genuine relational inference. No prior benchmark isolates the specific bottleneck of higher-arity relational binding—the number of independent entities that must be jointly composed to satisfy a shared constraint—across diverse scientific domains.

## Input / Output

REL-A presents numerical matrices or tensors (up to 30×30 or 3×3×3) with one entry replaced by '?', and the model selects from 8 candidate values. REL-B1 provides a FASTA multiple sequence alignment alongside a Newick phylogenetic tree, requiring the model to output both a homoplasy yes/no judgment and the exact set of participating taxa. REL-C tasks supply sets of 5–50 molecules as canonicalized SMILES strings; C1 outputs a binary isomer-set label, C2 outputs the maximum common substructure as a SMILES string (scored via a bidirectional substructure metric), and C3 outputs the missing constitutional isomers as SMILES (scored by recall).

## Method

The paper formalizes Relational Complexity (RC) as the minimal number of independent operands that must be simultaneously bound to execute a reasoning step, explicitly separating it from Operand Complexity (OC), which captures the difficulty of representing individual fillers. Building on this, REL is a generative benchmark framework with parameterized task generators across three domains: algebra (Raven's Progressive Matrices and Tensors with seven rules yielding RC from 1 to 3ⁿ−1), biology (synthetic MSAs with injected convergent motifs where RC equals the number of homoplastic taxa, plus epistasis landscapes where RC equals the number of focal mutations), and chemistry (isomer classification, maximum common substructure, and missing-isomer completion where RC scales with the isomer space size). The key design principle is that RC can be varied independently of input length, entity count, and vocabulary by adjusting a single generator parameter (e.g., matrix dimension, number of homoplastic taxa, or isomer universe size), enabling clean attribution of performance drops to relational binding difficulty. The framework is evaluated on Claude Opus 4.5, Gemini 3 Pro, and GPT 5.2, with ablations on test-time compute, in-context learning, tool use (RDKit), and structured prompting to confirm that the RC-driven degradation is not an artifact of insufficient inference steps or lack of examples.

## Results

Across all three frontier LLMs (Claude Opus 4.5, Gemini 3 Pro, GPT 5.2), performance degrades monotonically as Relational Complexity (RC) increases. In REL-A, models achieve ~91% accuracy on low-RC tasks (RC=1,2) but collapse to trivial accuracy (~12%) on high-RC tasks (RC=9), a 45% drop. In REL-B1, accuracy falls from 35% at N_ht=4 to 1% at N_ht=25 (a 93% drop), with RC explaining 24–44% of explainable variance across models. In REL-C, task completion rate decreases from 65.7% (C1, RC=2) to 38.1% (C2, RC=2, higher OC) to 26.0% (C3, RC=N_isomers), a 39.7% overall decline. Increased test-time compute yields only 2–3% gains, and in-context learning preserves the RC-ordered performance hierarchy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper rather than a method paper, so there are no competing methods to compare against; instead, the paper evaluates three frontier LLMs (Claude Opus 4.5, Gemini 3 Pro, GPT 5.2) plus open-weight models (Llama 3.1-70B, Qwen3-4B) on the same REL tasks. Gemini 3 Pro consistently achieves the highest task completion rates in chemistry (e.g., 0.417 recall on REL-C3 at N_observed<20 vs. 0.341 for Claude and 0.142 for GPT 5.2), while GPT 5.2 is the weakest across all domains. All three models exhibit the same qualitative pattern: near-perfect performance at low RC and sharp degradation at high RC, with no model solving the highest-RC instances (e.g., REL-A7 neighborhood sum, REL-B1 at N_ht=25, REL-C3 at N_observed≥40). Tool use (RDKit) and structured prompting do not close the gap: GPT-5.4+RDKit actually shows lower recall than GPT 5.2 without tools, and structured prompting on REL-B1 yields only +5 points at RC=20 and 0 points at RC=25. Open-weight models (Llama 3.1-70B, Qwen3-4B) confirm the RC-performance relationship but at substantially lower absolute accuracy (e.g., Llama drops from 0.816 to 0.144 on REL-A3 from 3×3 to 30×30).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the cognitive science concept of relational complexity (Halford et al., 1998) into a task-agnostic difficulty axis for LLM evaluation, departing from prior graph-centric relational reasoning benchmarks (Wang et al., 2023a; Tang et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025a) that rely on graph-specific formalisms. It builds on the numerical-array reformulation of Raven's Progressive Matrices (Camposampiero et al., 2025b) and generalizes it to n-dimensional tensors (RPTs) to achieve RC values far beyond the original 2D ceiling of 4. The key conceptual departure is isolating RC from confounders like entity count and input length, enabling controlled difficulty variation across algebra, biology, and chemistry rather than within a single graph or matrix structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that performance degrades monotonically with RC while remaining insensitive to entity count, and that this degradation persists under test-time compute, in-context learning, tool use, and structured step-by-step prompting, identifies a representational bottleneck in current LLMs that is not addressable by procedural decomposition alone. This has practical implications for benchmark design: many apparent reasoning gains may reflect improvements along axes orthogonal to relational integration. Limitations include the synthetic nature of tasks, multiple-choice evaluation that may obscure finer-grained failures, and context-length constraints; future work should extend REL to naturalistic relational settings and explore architectures or training objectives that specifically target higher-arity binding.
