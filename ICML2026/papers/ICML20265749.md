## Problem

Training language agents via reinforcement learning is impractical in many real-world environments that lack verifiable rewards or require inefficient long-horizon rollouts, while supervised fine-tuning on expert demonstrations is data-limited and prevents agents from learning the consequences of their own actions. This paper introduces the early experience paradigm as a reward-free middle ground between imitation learning and RL, where agents learn from future states generated by their own proposed actions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265749/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes expert trajectories (state-action pairs) as a seed, then augments each expert state with K alternative actions sampled from the agent's own policy and executes them in the environment to collect resulting next states, forming rollout triples (s_i, a_i^j, s_i^j). For implicit world modeling, the model takes a state-action pair and outputs a predicted next state via next-token prediction; for self-reflection, it takes a state and outputs a chain-of-thought rationale followed by the expert action. The rollout dataset can be up to an order of magnitude larger than the expert dataset, and all states are represented entirely in natural language (DOM trees, tool outputs, textual scene descriptions).

## Method

The paper proposes two training strategies that convert the agent's own exploratory rollouts into supervision without external rewards. Implicit World Modeling (IWM) trains the policy to predict next states from (state, action) pairs using a next-token prediction loss, internalizing environment dynamics directly into the policy parameters via a two-stage pipeline (world-model pretraining followed by expert fine-tuning). Self-Reflection (SR) prompts the model to generate chain-of-thought explanations comparing the expert action against alternative actions grounded in their observed state differences, then trains on (state, rationale, expert action) triplets. Both methods share the same model parameters θ for action prediction and state/rationale prediction, and both are evaluated as warm-starts for subsequent GRPO reinforcement learning, where early-experience checkpoints consistently achieve higher post-RL ceilings than imitation-learning starts.

## Results

Across eight diverse benchmarks and three model sizes (Llama-3.2-3B, Qwen-2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B), both early experience methods consistently outperform imitation learning. The largest gains appear on WebShop (IWM +18.4% for 3B, SR +10.9% for 8B), TravelPlanner (SR +15.0% for 7B and 8B), and ScienceWorld (SR +13.3% for 8B). On the hardest open-ended benchmark WebArena-Lite, IWM and SR still yield +2.4 to +3.6% over IL. When used as a warm-start for GRPO reinforcement learning, early experience checkpoints reach substantially higher post-RL ceilings (e.g., ALFWorld 99.2% vs. 78.1% for IL+RL on Llama-3.2-3B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary baseline of Imitation Learning (behavior cloning), both IWM and SR win on all 24 benchmark-model combinations, with gains ranging from +0.6 (SearchQA, 3B, SR) to +18.4 (WebShop, 3B, IWM). Against STaR-style rationale training (ungrounded rationales without environment interaction), early experience dominates: on WebShop, IWM/SR reach 58.6/58.2% vs. STaR's 25.0%, and on ALFWorld, 85.9/85.2% vs. 74.2%. Against DPO (using non-expert rollouts as rejected responses), early experience wins on both WebShop (58.6/58.2% vs. 53.1%) and ALFWorld (85.9/85.2% vs. 82.8%), and DPO training collapses within tens of steps. Against Long CoT (test-time scaling), early experience is far superior: on WebShop, IWM reaches 58.6% vs. Long CoT's 1.6% from prompting (and Long CoT actually degrades IL from 47.3% to 0.0%), while on ALFWorld IWM/SR reach 85.9/85.2% vs. Long CoT's 28.4%. As a closed-source reference, GPT-4o prompting achieves only 23.7% on WebShop and 48.0% on ALFWorld, both substantially below the early-experience-trained open models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions 'early experience' as a middle-ground training paradigm between supervised fine-tuning (imitation learning) and reinforcement learning for language agents, drawing on three prior threads: (1) world models that predict future states (Ha & Schmidhuber, Dreamer, and recent LLM-as-world-model work like Gu et al. 2025), (2) self-reflection and rationale-based training (Reflexion, Self-Refine, STaR), and (3) experience-reuse techniques like hindsight relabeling and DAgger. The key departure is that both the world-modeling objective and the reflection rationales are generated from the agent's *own* non-expert actions executed in the environment, and the resulting next states serve as reward-free supervision within the same policy model—eliminating the need for a separate simulator, goal relabeling, or verifiable reward signals. Unlike prior world-model approaches that treat the model as a standalone planning tool, here the prediction task is an auxiliary loss that internalizes dynamics directly into the policy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, scalable bridge toward the 'era of experience' for language agents in environments where verifiable rewards are absent (websites, open-ended tool use) or rollouts are prohibitively long. The finding that early-experience checkpoints substantially outperform imitation-learning warm-starts under identical GRPO training suggests a concrete pipeline: SFT → early experience → RL, which could accelerate the transition to fully reward-driven agents as infrastructure matures. Limitations include the continued dependence on expert demonstrations as seeds (the paradigm augments rather than replaces them), the bounded coverage of rollouts by expert trajectory states, and the fact that the largest gains appear in structured or semi-structured environments rather than fully open-ended settings. Open questions include whether the approach can bootstrap from zero expert data, how it interacts with multi-modal observations, and whether the implicit world model can be leveraged for explicit planning at inference time.
