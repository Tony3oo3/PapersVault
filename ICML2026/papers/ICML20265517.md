## Problem

Random shuffling of instruction-tuning data indiscriminately interleaves heterogeneous samples, inducing gradient conflicts that reduce effective optimization progress. Prior curriculum-learning approaches derive per-example difficulty scores but ignore inter-sample correlations, and experiments show they fail to outperform random shuffling on multimodal LLM benchmarks. The paper asks whether organizing training at the group level—where semantically similar examples are learned together—can resolve these conflicts and improve generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265517/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multimodal instruction-tuning dataset of (text prompt, optional paired image, response) triples (e.g., ~665K LLaVA-1.5 samples) and first trains a warm-up model on a 10% random subset to extract per-sample embeddings from a chosen decoder layer. It then produces a static, group-ordered training sequence—clusters sorted by intra-cluster compactness, interleaved with mixed regions and replay-buffer samples—which is fed into the standard instruction-tuning pipeline without modifying the optimizer or loss. The final output is the fine-tuned model (full or LoRA-based), evaluated on 14 benchmarks spanning vision-language, text-only, and hybrid thinking/no-thinking settings.

## Method

EP-Order operates in four stages: (1) a warm-up model trained on 10% of data provides embeddings via token-wise average pooling of the θ-th decoder layer output; (2) UMAP reduces embedding dimensionality and HDBSCAN clusters samples into density-connected groups without pre-specifying cluster count; (3) clusters are sorted ascending by mean pairwise Euclidean distance (compactness), yielding an easy-to-hard curriculum at the group level; (4) mixed regions of equal-sized subsets from adjacent clusters are inserted at every cluster boundary, and each mixed region is further augmented with a small buffer of samples from all previously learned clusters to mitigate catastrophic forgetting. The theoretical justification is the Efficiency Ratio (ER = ‖Σgᵢ‖/Σ‖gᵢ‖), which measures how much of the batch gradient survives after cancellation; grouping by embedding proximity raises ER from 0.54 (random) to 0.71 (grouped), meaning each step makes ~30% more effective progress toward the optimization goal. Unlike per-example curriculum methods, EP-Order explicitly accounts for inter-sample gradient compatibility through representation-space geometry rather than scalar difficulty scores.

## Results

On LLaVA-Vicuna-7B full fine-tuning, EP-Order achieves a 66.30 average across 12 sub-benchmarks, surpassing the Random baseline (64.88) by 1.42 points and the closest competitor PDPC (65.17) by 1.13 points. Scaling to LLaVA-Vicuna-13B, EP-Order reaches 67.62 average versus 66.52 for Random. In the text-only setting with Qwen3-4B on Tulu-3-SFT, EP-Order improves the average from 74.81 (Random) to 79.51, with the largest gains on ARC (86.44 vs. 71.86) and GSM8k (88.10 vs. 82.71).

## Baselines & Comparisons

EP-Order consistently outperforms all four named baselines. Against PDPC (Zhang et al., 2025b), the closest competitor, EP-Order gains +1.13 average on LLaVA-Vicuna-7B and +0.90 on 13B, with the largest per-benchmark advantage on MME-Cognition (+29.0) and MMBench-cn (+3.6). Attention Variance (Kim & Lee, 2024) trails EP-Order by 4.52 average on 7B, suffering particularly on MM-Vet (22.20 vs. 29.13) and MMBench-cn (54.04 vs. 61.25). Bloom's Taxonomy (Ranaldi et al., 2024) is 3.16 points behind EP-Order on average, with notable weaknesses on TextVQA (52.18 vs. 57.46) and MMBench-cn (54.30 vs. 61.25). The Folding strategy from Dai et al. (2025) underperforms EP-Order by 1.2–1.4 average points (Table 5), confirming that abrupt fold-wise transitions hinder optimization even with mixed regions. Notably, all per-example curriculum baselines also underperform the simple Random shuffle, whereas EP-Order is the only method that reliably beats Random across both multimodal and text-only settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant curriculum-learning paradigm for instruction tuning, which assigns per-example difficulty scores (token length, loss, attention variance, perplexity difference) and sorts samples individually. Instead, EP-Order operates at the group level: it uses a warm-up model to embed all samples, clusters them via HDBSCAN in representation space, and schedules training cluster-by-cluster with mixed regions and replay buffers to smooth transitions. It also connects to the multi-task gradient-conflict literature (PCGrad, CAGrad, GradNorm) by addressing optimization interference through data organization rather than gradient reweighting, and introduces an Efficiency Ratio (ER) analysis to formalize why semantically coherent mini-batches yield larger effective progress per step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that most prior curriculum-learning methods (loss-based, gradient-norm, attention-variance, PDPC, Bloom's Taxonomy) actually underperform simple random shuffling on multimodal IT benchmarks is practically important: it cautions against naively porting per-example ordering heuristics to complex, high-conflict training settings. EP-Order's modest but consistent gains (~1–2 points average) across 14 benchmarks, two model scales, full/LoRA fine-tuning, and hybrid think/no-think scenarios suggest embedding-level structure is a more reliable proxy for gradient compatibility than scalar difficulty scores. Open questions include whether the static single-epoch ordering extends to multi-epoch dynamic schedules, how sensitive the method is to warm-up model quality, and whether the ~35% preprocessing overhead is justified at scale.
