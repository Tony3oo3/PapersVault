## Problem

Longitudinal EHRs produce token sequences exceeding 64,000 tokens under standard subword tokenizers, causing prohibitive inference latency and limiting context-length scaling for LLM-based clinical prediction. Existing compression approaches either discard tokens (risking loss of clinically critical information) or add dynamic merge modules (increasing inference latency and model complexity). The paper targets the gap of achieving lossless, label-free token-sequence compression that is fully compatible with pre-trained LLMs without expanding vocabulary size or adding computational overhead.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a chronologically ordered EHR text sequence (produced by a data-to-text function over timestamped clinical events) concatenated with a task-specific natural-language prompt, with maximum input capped at 8,192 tokens. It produces a compressed token sequence (22–32% shorter) that is fed to the LLM, which then generates a clinical prediction label (binary or multi-label) alongside a free-text explanation. The compression is strictly lossless: the original input text can be fully reconstructed via detokenisation, and the vocabulary size and embedding matrix dimensions remain identical to the base model.

## Method

MedTPE operates as a three-stage layered tokenisation pipeline. First, Token-pair Encoding (TPE) mines a medical corpus for frequently co-occurring N-gram token spans (2 ≤ N ≤ n_max) and constructs composite TPE tokens with an ordered merge table, achieving O(n) encoding complexity. Second, dependency-aware replacement integrates TPE tokens into the original vocabulary by evicting the M least-frequent unprotected tokens (those not appearing in any TPE merge path) and inserting the top-M TPE tokens ranked by a length-aware frequency score, thereby preserving the original vocabulary size, embedding matrix dimensions, and tokenisation completeness. Third, self-supervised fine-tuning (SSFT) initialises each new TPE embedding as a norm-scaled average of its constituent sub-token embeddings, then fine-tunes only these embeddings (≈0.5–1.0% of total parameters) against pseudo-labels generated by the original uncompressed LLM, with all other model weights frozen. This design ensures the compressed representation aligns with the pre-trained latent space without requiring any labelled clinical data.

## Results

On MIMIC-IV and EHRSHOT, MedTPE reduces inference time by 33.9%–62.5% and achieves compression rates of 22.8%–32.4% while maintaining or improving F1 scores. For example, on MIMIC-IV Phenotyping with Qwen2.5-1.5B, MedTPE reaches F1=0.218 (vs. 0.201 baseline) with 56.2% lower inference time; on EHRSHOT 30-day Readmission with Llama3-1B, it achieves F1=0.191 (vs. 0.098 baseline) with 40.7% lower time. Format compliance rates (FCR) are preserved or improved, reaching 0.999–1.000 in most settings. The method generalises to scientific (ARC-Challenge: 49.4%–76.4% latency reduction) and financial (ECTSum) domains with minimal F1/ROUGE degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LLMLingua2 (removal-based), MedTPE consistently wins on both F1 and inference time: e.g., on MIMIC-IV Phenotyping with Qwen2.5-1.5B, MedTPE achieves F1=0.218 vs. 0.020 for LLMLingua2 while reducing time from 41.0 to 13.0 min; LLMLingua2 often *increases* latency (e.g., 82.5 min vs. 66.0 min baseline on ICU Mortality) due to its O(n²) importance-scoring overhead. Against ZeTT (merge-based with hypernetwork), MedTPE dominates on both accuracy and speed: on MIMIC-IV ICU Mortality with Llama3-1B, MedTPE reaches F1=0.109 vs. ZeTT's 0.017, with inference time of 39.3 min vs. 201.8 min; the authors attribute ZeTT's underperformance to its hypernetwork failing to capture clinical sub-token semantics, causing longer redundant generations. Against T5Summary (summarisation-based), MedTPE achieves higher F1 on most tasks (e.g., 0.114 vs. 0.078 on Phenotyping Llama3-1B; 0.191 vs. 0.190 on EHRSHOT Readmission Llama3-1B) while T5Summary achieves far greater compression (~99% vs. ~30%) and lower absolute latency; MedTPE's trade-off is lossless compression preserving all clinical information at the cost of a smaller token reduction. Overall, MedTPE is the only method that simultaneously achieves lossless compression, label-free training, unchanged vocabulary size, O(n) tokenisation complexity, and competitive-or-superior predictive performance across all four clinical tasks and two LLM families.

## Relation to Prior Work

MedTPE positions itself at the intersection of subword tokenization (BPE, WordPiece, SentencePiece) and prompt compression for LLMs, but departs from both lines in a key way: rather than adding inference-time modules (ZeTT's hypernetwork, LTSC's meta-tokens) or discarding tokens (LLMLingua2, CPC), it modifies the tokenization layer itself through a dependency-aware vocabulary swap that preserves the original vocabulary size and O(n) complexity. The self-supervised alignment step borrows the pseudo-label idea from knowledge distillation but applies it exclusively to new token embeddings, avoiding the labelled-data requirement of AdaptiVocab and LTSC. Conceptually, the method repurposes the BPE merge-table mechanism as a domain-adaptation tool, treating the base tokenizer's subwords as 'atoms' for a second, medically-informed merge pass.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for clinical deployment: a 31% token reduction and 34–63% latency cut with no performance loss directly addresses the feasibility of context-length and test-time scaling for LLM-based clinical prediction, which is currently bottlenecked by sequence length. However, the method requires direct access to and modification of the tokenizer and embedding matrix, making it inapplicable to closed-source or API-only models, and the cross-domain transferability experiment (MIMIC-IV → EHRSHOT) reveals that vocabulary mining must be redone per domain, limiting plug-and-play deployment. The observed CoT fragility in medically continued-pretrained models (Meditron3-8B) highlights an open question about how token-distribution shifts interact with instruction-following in domain-adapted LLMs, a problem that will grow in importance as reasoning-based clinical AI matures.
