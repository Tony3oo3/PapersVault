## Problem

Existing coding benchmarks evaluate LMs on small, well-specified tasks (fixing a bug, writing a test) with explicit instructions and unit-test validation, but real-world software development is driven by high-level goals requiring recursive decomposition, strategic prioritization, and iterative adaptation to competitors. Whether LMs can autonomously develop code to accomplish open-ended, competitive objectives without explicit guidance remains unaddressed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265393/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each LM agent receives a starter codebase (arena docs, a working baseline submission, and arena executables) and, after each competition round, gigabytes of raw competition logs copied into its codebase as the sole new information. The agent interacts exclusively via bash commands through mini-SWE-agent (no file-tree viewer, no AST search, no predefined tools) within a 30-turn budget per round. Outputs include per-arena and overall Elo ratings (base 1200, slope 400, fitted via maximum likelihood), pairwise tournament win rates, and the evolved codebase itself, which serves as the model's only persistent memory across rounds.

## Method

CodeClash formalizes competitive coding as multi-round tournaments where two or more LM agents iteratively edit their own codebases (edit phase) and then those codebases are compiled and executed head-to-head in a code arena (competition phase) that determines winners by objectives like score maximization, resource acquisition, or survival. Three key design decisions distinguish the benchmark: (1) codebase-as-memory, where players have no explicit cross-round memory and must explicitly record insights in their code; (2) log-based feedback, where arena results are the only new information between rounds; and (3) strategic opacity, where players cannot see each other's code. The benchmark spans 6 diverse arenas (Battlesnake, Core War, Halite, Poker, RoboCode, RobotRumble) varying in language, codebase structure, and feedback format, and uses the deliberately minimal mini-SWE-agent scaffold to avoid imposing tool-specific assumptions. Evaluation comprises 1,680 one-on-one tournaments (8 models × 6 arenas × 10 tournaments × 15 rounds = 25,200 rounds) plus CC:Ladder, a progression-style evaluation where models climb ranked ladders of human-authored solutions to quantify the model-to-expert gap.

## Results

Across 1,680 tournaments (25,200 total rounds) spanning 6 competitive code arenas, Claude Sonnet 4.5 achieves the highest overall Elo rating of 1389 and the highest average tournament win rate of 69.9%, followed by GPT-5 (1360 Elo) and o3 (1343 Elo). No single model dominates all arenas: Claude Sonnet 4.5 ranks only 4th in Poker (1248 Elo), while GPT-5 leads in Poker (1599) and o3 leads in Halite (1576). In the CC:Ladder ablation against ranked human-authored solutions, no model completes either ladder; the best result is GPT-5 mini reaching rank 57 of 58 on RobotRumble and rank 260 of 264 on Core War, confirming a substantial gap between top LMs and expert human programmers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Claude Sonnet 4.5 leads overall (1389 Elo, 69.9% win rate) but is not the best in every arena: GPT-5 outperforms it in Poker (1599 vs. 1248) and RoboCode (1409 vs. 1361), while o3 edges ahead in Halite (1576 vs. 1408). GPT-5 (1360) and o3 (1343) form a tight second tier, separated by only 17 Elo points, with GPT-5 stronger in Poker and RoboCode and o3 stronger in Halite and Core War. Claude Sonnet 4 (1223) and GPT-5 Mini (1200) occupy a clear third tier, with Claude Sonnet 4 notably strong in RobotRumble (1361) but weak in RoboCode (1033). Gemini 2.5 Pro (1125) trails the top group by over 100 Elo and is particularly weak in Poker (978) and Core War (1043). Grok Code Fast (1004) and Qwen3 Coder (952) form the bottom tier, with Qwen3 Coder last in 5 of 6 arenas. In the CC:Ladder evaluation against human experts, GPT-5 mini achieves the best RobotRumble result (rank 57/58) and Core War result (rank 260/264), while Claude Sonnet 4.5 reaches rank 43/58 and 205/264 respectively, confirming that even the top model cannot defeat all expert human solutions.

## Relation to Prior Work

CodeClash departs from the dominant SWE-bench paradigm (fixed GitHub issues validated by unit tests) and from narrow performance-optimization benchmarks (Mercury, SWE-Perf, AlgoTune) by introducing open-ended, competitive, multi-round code development with no predetermined correctness criterion. It also distinguishes itself from game-playing benchmarks (AlphaGo, OpenAI Dota 2, GameArena, Balrog) where an AI system directly plays the game; here, LMs iteratively refine code that competes as their proxy in a code arena. The agent infrastructure builds on mini-SWE-agent (Yang et al., 2024a), but the tournament formulation—codebase-as-memory, log-based feedback, adversarial adaptation, and self-directed improvement—is a new synthesis not present in any single prior line of work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CodeClash reveals that even frontier models share fundamental limitations in strategic reasoning: they hallucinate failure explanations from logs, deploy untested changes, fail to recover after consecutive losses, and produce increasingly disorganized codebases over time. The substantial gap to expert human programmers (no model completes the CC:Ladder) highlights that autonomous, goal-oriented software development remains far from solved. Open questions include whether these limitations can be addressed through self-play or RL training on CodeClash trajectories, whether multimodal feedback (spatial logs, visual replays) would help, and whether findings generalize to larger, multi-objective real-world systems beyond the self-contained arenas used here.
