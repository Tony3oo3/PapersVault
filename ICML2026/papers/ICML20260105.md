## Problem

Standard RLHF reward learning assumes homogeneous user preferences and fits a single Bradley-Terry model via cross-entropy loss, but real annotator populations exhibit systematic heterogeneity. Under heterogeneity, the standard estimator is provably misspecified—it recovers a variance-weighted re-aggregation of user utilities (equivalent to Borda count) rather than the population-average utility, and prior work shows recovering the average is impossible in the worst case. This paper identifies a mild symmetry condition under which the population-average utility becomes recoverable and provides a consistent estimator.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the same pairwise preference data as standard RLHF: pairs of prompt-completion alternatives with a single binary preference label per pair, drawn from a random user with no panel structure or user identity required. It outputs a reward model (utility function over alternatives) that is ordinally consistent with the population-average utility; in the linearly parametrized case, this amounts to recovering the direction β̄/||β̄|| of the mean utility vector. The reward model is a drop-in replacement in existing RLHF pipelines, requiring no architectural changes to the language model or the downstream RL fine-tuning step.

## Method

The Sign Estimator replaces the standard cross-entropy (logistic) loss with a 0-1 binary classification loss that measures agreement between the sign of the predicted utility difference and the observed preference label (2Y−1). The key insight is that under a symmetry assumption on the heterogeneity distribution (ε(x) and −ε(x) have the same distribution for all x), the sign of the population-average utility difference equals the sign of the choice probability minus 1/2, making the 0-1 loss a valid surrogate for ordinal consistency with the true average utility. In the linear case, the estimator minimizes over the unit sphere S^{d−1}, and the paper proves a cube-root convergence rate of Õ(n^{−1/3}) for the angular error, which avoids the curse of dimensionality that plagues nonparametric random-coefficients estimators. Practically, the non-differentiable sign function is approximated by 2σ(λt)−1 with an annealed temperature parameter λ, and the authors explicitly avoid convex surrogates (logistic or hinge loss) because those would revert to the misspecified RLHF estimator.

## Results

On three preference datasets (SHP, HH-Anthropic, CoVal), the Sign Estimator achieves the highest predictive accuracy across all settings, improving over standard RLHF by ~1.8 pp on SHP (68.33% vs. 66.50%), ~1.7 pp on HH-Anthropic (68.58% vs. 66.89%), and ~2.6 pp on CoVal (80.72% vs. 78.09%). In the out-of-distribution setting (trained on HH-Anthropic, evaluated on CoVal), the gap widens to 3.1 pp (73.07% vs. 69.94%). In a semi-synthetic digital-twin benchmark with known ground-truth population-average utility, the Sign Estimator reduces angular estimation error by ~35% (from 63° to 41°) and disagreement rate by ~33% (from ~12% to ~8%) relative to RLHF.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Sign Estimator outperforms standard RLHF (cross-entropy / Bradley-Terry MLE) on every dataset and evaluation setting, with gains of 1.7–3.1 percentage points in predictive accuracy. Against the heterogeneity-aware EM baselines (Chakraborty et al., 2024; Poddar et al., 2024), the Sign Estimator wins consistently: EM(K=2) is the closest competitor but still trails Sign by 1.3 pp on SHP, 3.6 pp on HH-Anthropic, and 6.4 pp on CoVal. Performance of the EM baselines degrades sharply as K increases (K=3, K=5), with accuracy dropping 5–10 pp below RLHF on CoVal, likely due to variance from segmentation and EM's susceptibility to spurious stationary points. In the digital-twin alignment experiment, the Sign Estimator reduces angular error to the true mean utility vector from 63° (RLHF) to 41°, a 35% improvement, while EM(K=2) offers only modest gains over RLHF and degrades for K≥3. The Sign Estimator also shows greater robustness under distribution shift (OOD CoVal: +3.1 pp over RLHF vs. +1.7 pp in-distribution) and under increased heterogeneity (relative improvement grows from 29% to 38% as the coefficient of variation of β increases from 0.59 to 2.05).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard RLHF reward-learning pipeline (Ouyang et al., 2022b; Bai et al., 2022b), which minimizes cross-entropy under a Bradley-Terry model and implicitly assumes homogeneous preferences. It builds on the recognition that this approach is misspecified under heterogeneity—Siththaranjan et al. (2023) showed it implements a Borda-count aggregation, and Gölz et al. (2025) proved that recovering the population-average utility is impossible in the worst case. The key conceptual departure is exploiting a symmetry assumption on the heterogeneity distribution (which holds for the Gaussian mixed logit model, the workhorse of econometric preference modeling à la McFadden & Train, 2000) to directly recover the average utility direction via a 0-1 classification loss, sidestepping the need to estimate the full mixture of utility vectors—a notoriously difficult inference problem (Ammar et al., 2014; Oh & Shah, 2014). The method also connects to social choice theory, as the induced policy coincides with the Maximal Lotteries rule, providing robustness guarantees beyond the model assumptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Sign Estimator provides the first consistent estimator for population-average preferences under heterogeneity that avoids the curse of dimensionality, with a cube-root convergence rate O(n^{-1/3})—a significant theoretical advance over nonparametric random-coefficients methods whose rates deteriorate with dimension. Practically, it is a drop-in replacement for cross-entropy loss in existing RLHF pipelines, requiring no architectural changes, and outperforms both standard RLHF and computationally heavier heterogeneity-aware EM methods across multiple real-world datasets. Key limitations include the symmetry assumption (though it holds for Gaussian mixed logits), the recovery of only ordinal preferences (direction) rather than cardinal utilities, and the NP-hardness of the 0-1 loss in general (mitigated by a smooth sigmoid approximation). Open questions include whether the approach extends to non-symmetric heterogeneity, whether the modest empirical gains (1.7–2 pp on standard benchmarks) grow with dataset scale, and how the method interacts with the downstream RL fine-tuning step beyond the reward model.
