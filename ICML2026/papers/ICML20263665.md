## Problem

Reasoning models (e.g., DeepSeek-R1) frequently get stuck in loops, endlessly repeating the same text under greedy decoding or low temperatures, yet the underlying cause and the true role of temperature as a mitigation remain unclear. This paper asks whether temperature addresses the root cause of looping or merely acts as a stopgap, and identifies systematic errors in learning (mismatches between the training distribution and the learned model) as a key driver.

## Input / Output

For the empirical study, AIME 2024/2025 math problems are fed to open reasoning models (Qwen, OpenThinker-3, Phi-4, Llama) at temperatures 0–1.0, producing chain-of-thought responses that are scored for looping (a 30-gram appearing ≥20 times), accuracy, and response length. For the synthetic graph reasoning task, each training instance is a token sequence containing an edge list, a start node, a goal node, and a random-walk trace over a modified star graph G(n,ℓ); the model (a ~85M-parameter decoder-only Transformer trained from scratch) generates the next node in the walk, and looping is quantified as the count of root→start transitions per trace.

## Method

The paper combines large-scale empirical evaluation of open reasoning models with a controlled synthetic graph reasoning task to isolate two mechanisms by which learning errors cause looping. First, risk aversion due to hardness of learning: when the correct progress-making action is hard to distinguish from n alternatives, maximum-likelihood training diffuses its probability mass as (1−p)/n across those alternatives while an easy cyclic action retains mass p, so greedy decoding prefers the cyclic action (formalized in Proposition 3.1). Second, an inductive bias toward temporally correlated errors: even when no hardness exists, small estimation errors at repeated decision points are correlated over time, causing the model to reselect the same few actions and loop under low-temperature decoding. A third 'catalyst' effect is shown where a tiny fraction (0.001) of deterministic looping traces in training causes the model's confidence in continuing a loop to grow toward 1, making escape increasingly unlikely. Across both mechanisms, increasing temperature reduces looping by promoting exploration but does not correct the underlying probability misallocation, so response lengths at high temperature remain substantially longer than those of a perfect learner (≈4.4× blow-up in the hardness setting, ≈1.5× in the correlated-errors setting).

## Results

At greedy decoding (T=0) on AIME 2024/2025, looping fractions range from 76% (DeepSeek-R1 Distilled Qwen 1.5B) down to 4% (QwQ-32B), with distilled students looping far more than their teachers (e.g., OpenThinker3-1.5B at 30% vs. QwQ-32B at 4%). On the synthetic star-graph task G(5,5) with exploration, the learned model's response length at T=1 is 108.1 tokens versus 24.4 for a perfect learner (~4.4× blow-up), while the temporally-correlated-errors variant shows a milder ~1.5× blow-up (42.9 vs. 29.6). The Transformer exhibits 98.7% top-child agreement across consecutive root visits, confirming strong temporal correlation in its errors. Harder AIME problems elicit significantly more looping (51% vs. 13% for OpenThinker3-1.5B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an observational and mechanistic paper rather than a method-comparison study, so it does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it compares models along several axes: (i) within-family size scaling shows monotonic reduction in looping (Qwen 1.5B → 7B → 32B: 76% → 49% → 37%); (ii) student-vs-teacher distillation reveals the largest gaps (OpenThinker3-1.5B at 30% vs. its teacher QwQ-32B at 4%, a 7.5× difference), pointing to learning errors as the key cause; (iii) in the synthetic graph task, the learned Transformer is compared against a perfect learner, showing that temperature reduces looping but leaves a 4.4× response-length gap (hardness mechanism) or 1.5× gap (temporally correlated errors); (iv) RL post-training (Phi-4-reasoning vs. Phi-4-reasoning-plus) has negligible effect on looping (43% vs. 45%). The paper's central finding is that no single model or training paradigm eliminates looping, and temperature acts as a stopgap rather than a holistic fix.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the star-graph path-finding setup of Bachmann & Nagarajan (2024), who showed Transformers fail to learn correct paths due to optimization hardness, by introducing random-walk traces with resets to model the cyclic actions (backtracking, restatement) characteristic of reasoning chains. It situates itself within the broader literature on neural text degeneration (Holtzman et al., 2020) and its mitigations (unlikelihood training, contrastive methods), but departs from that line by asking *why* looping occurs mechanistically rather than how to suppress it at inference time. The key conceptual departure is framing looping as a consequence of 'errors in learning'—systematic mismatches between the training distribution and the learned model—rather than as an inherent property of low-temperature decoding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic account of why reasoning models loop, identifying two distinct forces (hardness-induced risk aversion and inductive bias toward temporally correlated errors) and showing that the standard remedy of increasing temperature is a stopgap that leaves response lengths 1.5–4.4× longer than necessary. The finding that distilled students loop far more than their teachers (e.g., 30% vs. 4%) has direct practical implications for the distillation pipeline that underpins many open reasoning models. Open questions include training-time interventions that directly reduce learning errors (e.g., targeted data augmentation at high-loss positions), a tighter mapping between the synthetic graph task and real semantic reasoning, and whether other forces (error accumulation, self-conditioning) interact with the identified mechanisms in ways not captured by the current setup.
