## Problem

Existing equilibrium testing methods for multi-agent systems assume a fixed, pre-collected dataset and require knowledge of the equilibrium strategy itself, making them unsuitable for real-time monitoring of ongoing interactions. This paper addresses the gap by developing an anytime-valid sequential testing framework that detects departures from equilibrium (Nash, correlated, or coarse correlated) as they occur, without a predetermined sample size and without requiring the equilibrium joint strategy as input.

## Input / Output

For normal-form games, the method consumes a stream of observed action profiles and the payoff functions u_i for all players and deviations, but critically does NOT require the equilibrium strategy π—only that payoffs are bounded in [0,1]. For stochastic games, it additionally requires a target policy π and a hypothesized alternative π^(1) (or a prior over alternatives). The output is a continuously updated supermartingale test statistic per player-deviation pair, a binary rejection flag when the statistic crosses a threshold, the detection time τ, and (under the FDR procedure) a rejection set R_t identifying which specific deviations are flagged. The significance level α and mixture distribution ν are user-specified hyperparameters.

## Method

For repeated normal-form games, the core construction defines a per-round e-value E = 1 − λX where X is the regret-like increment (observed payoff minus deviation payoff), yielding a nonnegative supermartingale under the null via Ville's inequality. A Robbins mixture over λ ∈ (0,1] handles the unknown deviation magnitude η, and the Kelly criterion shows the optimal single-λ choice is λ* = η. FWER control is achieved by a union bound over all player-deviation pairs with threshold m/α, while a stopped e-BH procedure provides less conservative FDR control with provably earlier detection (Proposition 2.9). For stochastic games, the framework shifts to compliance testing: per-round likelihood ratios π^(1)(a|s)/π(a|s) form a martingale under the null, and the expected detection time is bounded by log(b)/KL̄ where KL̄ is the stationary-distribution-weighted KL divergence between target and alternative policies. A mixture over candidate alternatives preserves valid error control while maintaining the O(1/ε²) detection scaling when the deviation magnitude is unknown.

## Results

For repeated normal-form games, the sequential test controls FWER at level α while achieving an expected detection time of O(log(Σ|Aᵢ|/α)/η²) under the alternative, matching the asymptotic bound of Babichenko et al. (2017) without requiring knowledge of the equilibrium strategy. In the 2×2 game experiment (η≈0.033, α=0.2, threshold b=20), the FDR procedure (Algorithm 2) consistently detects deviations earlier than the FWER baseline (Algorithm 1) across all 300 runs, confirming Proposition 2.9 empirically. For stochastic games, the detection time scales as O(log(b)/KL̄_i(μ)), and experiments on a 5×4 grid-soccer game confirm the predicted 1/ε² scaling with deviation magnitude, while a 10×10 predator-prey experiment validates that the mixture martingale retains O(1/ε²) scaling even when the deviation magnitude ε is unknown to the monitor.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between its own FWER procedure (Algorithm 1, union-bound threshold) and FDR procedure (Algorithm 2, e-BH rule). Proposition 2.9 proves that with uniform weights, the FDR procedure flags a deviation at least as early as the FWER procedure almost surely, and the 2×2 game experiment confirms this in every one of 300 runs. Relative to the prior offline method of Babichenko et al. (2017), the proposed framework matches the asymptotic detection bound O(log(m/α)/η²) while operating online without a fixed sample size, and additionally does not require knowledge of the equilibrium joint strategy π (only payoff evaluations). The e-BH FDR control is less conservative than the FWER union bound, distributing the α budget across m hypotheses and yielding a lower effective rejection threshold (k=2 vs. b=20 in the 2×2 example), which translates into faster detection when multiple weak signals are present. In the stochastic game setting, the likelihood-ratio martingale generalizes Wald's SPRT to state-dependent dynamics, with detection time governed by the state-averaged KL divergence rather than a fixed per-round signal strength.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the offline equilibrium testing framework of Babichenko et al. (2017) from fixed-sample to sequential, anytime-valid monitoring by constructing supermartingale test statistics via the e-value/betting paradigm (Ramdas et al., 2023; Waudby-Smith & Ramdas, 2023). The per-round e-value construction E = 1 − λX for bounded outcomes follows the coin-betting approach of Waudby-Smith & Ramdas (2023), while the mixture-of-λ strategy draws on Robbins (1970) and subsequent work on log-optimal sequential tests. For the stochastic game extension, the likelihood-ratio martingale generalizes Wald's SPRT (1945) to state-dependent Markovian dynamics, and the FDR control leverages the e-BH procedure of Wang & Ramdas (2022) applied at a global stopping time. The key conceptual departure from prior work is the shift from requiring knowledge of the equilibrium strategy π to operating under weaker information assumptions (only payoff evaluations needed), and the replacement of super-exponential bounds for correlated equilibria with linear-in-actions bounds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a practical, statistically rigorous tool for real-time monitoring of multi-agent systems in safety-critical domains (automated trading, autonomous driving, networked infrastructure), where detecting strategic drift before it cascades is essential. The unification of Nash, correlated, and coarse correlated equilibria under a single sequential testing procedure, combined with e-BH-based FDR control that provably dominates FWER in detection speed, makes the method scalable to large games. Key limitations include the need for counterfactual payoff access in the normal-form setting and knowledge of the target policy in the stochastic game setting; bridging these gaps (e.g., via value estimation) is identified as an open problem. Analyzing how learning agents respond to detection signals—creating closed-loop, self-correcting systems—is flagged as a promising future direction.
