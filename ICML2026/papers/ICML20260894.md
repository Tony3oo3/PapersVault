## Problem

Operator splitting algorithms are central to modern optimization but require closed-form proximal operators, which most practical functions lack. While HJ-Prox—a derivative-free Monte Carlo approximation of proximal operators based on Hamilton-Jacobi PDE theory—was recently introduced, its theoretical properties when embedded in standard splitting frameworks (PGD, DRS, DYS, PDHG) had remained unexplored, leaving practitioners without convergence guarantees for this generalizable approach.

## Input / Output

The method takes a composite convex objective (e.g., f(x)+g(x) for two-operator splits, f+g+h for three-operator DYS, or f(x)+g(Ax) for PDHG) and requires only zeroth-order function evaluations—no gradients, subgradients, or closed-form proximal formulas. It produces converged primal iterates that are global minimizers of the composite objective. The user specifies algorithmic parameters (step size t or τ/σ, smoothing schedule δ_k, and Monte Carlo sample size N_k), and the method operates on both numerical data (regression, signal processing) and image data (TV denoising).

## Method

The paper establishes a unified convergence framework by treating each HJ-Prox-based splitting iteration as a perturbed Krasnosel'skii-Mann fixed-point iteration, where the perturbation is the HJ approximation error. Convergence is proven by combining three ingredients: (1) a deterministic smoothing error bound ‖prox^δ − prox‖ ≤ √(ntδ) from Crandall-Lions viscosity theory, (2) a probabilistic Monte Carlo error bound via self-normalized importance sampling under a Gaussian proposal (using Poincaré inequality and Tsirelson-Ibragimov-Sudakov concentration), and (3) Borel-Cantelli to ensure almost-sure summability of errors under mild parameter schedules. A key practical insight is that operator splitting decouples the Lipschitz constants entering the exponential Monte Carlo constant J* = exp(2L²t/δ), so applying HJ-Prox separately to f and g yields J_f + J_g rather than J_{f+g} = exp(2(L_f+L_g)²t/δ), dramatically reducing sample complexity. The framework also enables hybrid strategies where exact proximals are used for tractable terms and HJ-Prox is restricted to genuinely non-proximable components.

## Results

The paper establishes almost-sure convergence guarantees for HJ-Prox embedded in five splitting algorithms (PPM, PGD, DRS, DYS, PDHG) under mild regularity conditions, with the key error bound being ‖prox^δ − prox‖ ≤ √(ntδ) for the deterministic smoothing error and a Monte Carlo bound of √(8J★M★/(αN)) + √(ntδ) with probability ≥ 1−α. Empirically, HJ-Prox recovers solutions visually indistinguishable from analytical baselines across seven convex nonsmooth problems (LASSO, Sparse Group LASSO, TV Denoising, Trend Filtering, Multitask Learning, Non-negative LASSO, Overlapping Group LASSO), though it typically requires more iterations due to smaller step sizes needed to control approximation error. On the GSE2034 breast cancer benchmark, DYS-HJ achieves top-3 pathway group norms within 1–2% of FoGLASSO (e.g., Chem. carcinogenesis: 1.848e-02 vs. 1.864e-02).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary external baseline is FoGLASSO (Yuan et al., 2011), a specialized dual-reformulation solver for overlapping group LASSO. On the GSE2034 breast cancer dataset (13,237 genes, 298 overlapping KEGG pathway groups), DYS-HJ with adaptive Davis-Yin splitting and HJ-Prox (N=1000, decreasing δ schedule) produces coefficient vectors with high correlation to FoGLASSO and group norms within 1–2% (e.g., Hippo pathway: 1.627e-02 vs. 1.647e-02), confirming competitive solution quality without requiring dual reformulation or variable duplication. Internally, the paper demonstrates that HJ-PPM (non-splitting) consistently underperforms HJ-PGD and HJ-DRS, converging to higher objective values and larger fixed-point residuals, validating the theoretical argument that splitting reduces the effective Lipschitz constant entering the Monte Carlo error. The hybrid configuration (DYS-HJ-1, applying HJ-Prox only to the ℓ₁ term while using exact proximal for the indicator) significantly outperforms the fully approximate variant (DYS-HJ-2) and the non-splitting HJ-PPM, establishing a clear performance hierarchy: exact > hybrid > fully approximate > non-splitting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the HJ-Prox approximation (Osher et al., 2023), which was previously applied only to the proximal point method, into the full family of operator splitting algorithms (PGD, DRS, DYS, PDHG). The convergence analysis is built on Combettes' (2001) perturbed Krasnosel'skii-Mann iteration theorem, treating HJ-Prox Monte Carlo errors as summable perturbations, with the deterministic smoothing error bound inherited from Crandall & Lions' (1983) viscosity solution theory. The splitting algorithms themselves (Lions & Mercier, 1979; Davis & Yin, 2017; Chambolle & Pock, 2011) are used in their standard forms, with the only modification being the replacement of exact proximal steps by HJ-Prox approximations. A key departure from prior HJ-Prox work is the explicit demonstration that operator splitting is not merely optional but essential for controlling the exponential Monte Carlo error dependence on the Lipschitz constant.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides practitioners with a universal, derivative-free drop-in replacement for proximal operators in any standard splitting framework, eliminating the need for closed-form proximal calculus or specialized inner solvers (e.g., Dykstra's algorithm, product-space reformulations, iterative SVD). A significant open gap remains between the conservative theoretical sample complexity (exponential in k for DRS/DYS/PDHG) and the observed practical success with fixed N=1000, suggesting that tighter bounds or adaptive schedules for δ and N could substantially improve the method's theoretical standing. The paper also highlights that hybrid strategies—using exact proximals where available and HJ-Prox only for genuinely intractable terms—yield the best performance, pointing toward future work on adaptive, problem-aware allocation of approximation effort.
