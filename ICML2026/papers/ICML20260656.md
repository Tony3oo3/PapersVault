## Problem

Standard Set Representation Learning methods are trained on curated, complete sets but deployed in environments where sparse, label-preserving element corruption (outliers, missing components, noisy observations) distorts pooled representations and degrades downstream performance. Applying Distributionally Robust Optimization to address this gap is computationally intractable for sets because the ambiguity region is discrete and combinatorial, making the inner worst-case search factorial in complexity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260656/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes unordered sets of variable cardinality across four modalities—3D point clouds (1024 points), keyword sets (~25 tokens), image patch sets (256 patches), and social-network neighbor sets (~10–11 elements)—and produces a fixed-dimensional embedding vector (R·H dimensions) used for classification, similarity ranking, or topic expansion. Corruption is applied exclusively at inference time (10% mild, 40% severe element-level perturbations), while training always uses clean sets, reflecting realistic deployment conditions where the model never observes which elements are corrupted.

## Method

SW-DRSO models each set as an empirical measure and defines the corruption ambiguity region as a Sliced-Wasserstein ball, then uses a Wasserstein-aware encoder (quantile-matching against a learnable reference set across R Monte Carlo projections) whose embedding geometry is isometric to the SW distance. To make the inner worst-case maximization tractable, the paper introduces a barycentric adversary: for each set, K nearest neighbors in embedding space are convexly mixed using weights on the probability simplex Δ_K, and the resulting virtual embedding provably corresponds to a Sliced-Wasserstein Fréchet mean that remains within the SW-ball. The intractable discrete search over corrupted sets is thus replaced by T steps of projected gradient ascent over the K-dimensional simplex, after which the adversarial weights are fixed (gradients stopped) and the encoder and predictor are updated by standard descent on a combined ERM + α·robust loss. Propositions establish that the barycentric surrogate upper-bounds the discrete worst-case loss and that the gap is bounded by 2Lρ under local Lipschitzness.

## Results

On ModelNet40 point cloud classification (Task II), SW-DRSO achieves 82.86% overall accuracy, outperforming the strongest baseline PSWE (81.50%) by 1.36 points, with the gap widening under severe corruption (69.99% vs. 69.65%). On NWPU-RESISC45 patch-set recognition (Task IV), SW-DRSO reaches 53.03% overall accuracy and leads on mild (54.95%) and severe (31.89%) splits. On the LiveJ similar-set ranking task (Task I), SW-DRSO attains 74.86% Recall@10 overall with the narrowest clean-to-severe performance gap among all methods. Across all four tasks and seven datasets, SW-DRSO consistently maintains the smallest degradation from clean to severe corruption conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SW-DRSO outperforms all 11 baselines on the primary ModelNet40 benchmark, with the closest competitor being PSWE (81.50% vs. 82.86% overall) and FSW (81.14%), both of which are OT-based methods. Classic pooling baselines (MeanP, MaxP) and DeepSet trail by 2–5 points overall and degrade sharply under severe corruption (e.g., MaxP drops to 54.08%). SetTRSM, despite strong clean accuracy (86.17%), suffers the largest corruption-induced drop (−38.36 points to 47.81%), highlighting attention's vulnerability to outliers. On NWPU-RESISC45, SW-DRSO is marginally behind FSPool on overall (53.03% vs. 53.13%) and clean (60.33% vs. 61.88%) but clearly wins on mild (+0.80) and severe (+2.15) splits. Among DRO variants (Table 6), SW-DRSO beats WDRO (74.72%), KL-DRO (74.45%), and MMD-DRO (74.09%) on LiveJ Recall@10 while also being faster than WDRO and MMD-DRO, confirming the geometric alignment between the SW encoder and SW-based DRO formulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of Set Representation Learning (SRL) and Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO), drawing on the Sliced-Wasserstein embedding (PSWE, Naderializadeh et al. 2021) as its encoder and Wasserstein barycenter theory (Agueh & Carlier 2011; Bonneel et al. 2015) for its adversarial construction. It departs from standard Wasserstein-DRO, which models transport costs between individual elements and is computationally prohibitive for discrete set-structured inputs, by using the Sliced-Wasserstein metric to define a tractable ambiguity region and a barycentric adversary to replace intractable combinatorial search with low-dimensional simplex optimization. The key conceptual departure from prior SRL work is that robustness is built into the training objective via DRO rather than being an afterthought, and the geometric alignment between the SW encoder and the SW-based DRO formulation is shown to be essential (outperforming WDRO, KL-DRO, and MMD-DRO variants).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical deployment gap: set-based models trained on clean data degrade when inference-time inputs contain sparse element-level corruption (sensor noise, missing components), a scenario common in point clouds, retrieval, and visual recognition. The barycentric adversary provides a reusable, differentiable surrogate for worst-case search that could extend to other structured-input DRO settings beyond sets. Limitations include the local K-NN-based neighbor pool (which constrains the adversary to a small neighborhood), the assumption that corruption is label-preserving and sparse, and the dependence on the SW encoder for geometric alignment. The authors suggest extending the framework to LLM- and agent-centric settings where distributional shifts are increasingly relevant, but the current evaluation is confined to four relatively standard downstream tasks.
