## Problem

Estimating scaling laws for a model family traditionally requires training hundreds of models to full convergence, making the process prohibitively expensive and infeasible under budget constraints. While resource allocation is well-studied in hyperparameter optimization, the specific problem of strategically distributing a fixed compute budget across models of varying sizes to obtain a dense loss-compute frontier for scaling law fitting remains largely unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of models from a family (e.g., nanoGPT with 8–48 layers and 512–1600 embedding dimensions), a total compute budget B in petaFLOPs, and a pruning parameter η. Models are trained iteratively in rounds, with each round allocating equal compute to all surviving models; the output is a set of partially trained learning curves (not all reaching convergence) from which the scaling law L^SL(C) = (C/α)^(-γ) is fitted over a predefined compute range of interest. The GP surrogate additionally produces predicted curve continuations and upper/lower confidence bounds that can be used to extrapolate the scaling law beyond the observed compute range.

## Method

The core approach extends Successive Halving (SH) with surrogate-guided model selection: in each round, all active models receive the same compute budget, and after training, a surrogate predicts each model's future loss assuming it survives to the final round, and the top-k models by predicted loss advance to the next round. Two surrogate families are explored: a Linear Model of Co-regionalization (LMC) Gaussian Process with an exponential-decay kernel (capturing the characteristic learning-curve shape), a white-noise kernel, and a bias kernel, where the multitask structure exploits cross-curve correlations across model sizes; and Deep Ensembles of two-layer perceptrons regressing parametric curve shapes (power law, exponential, Morgan-Mercer-Flodin) conditioned on model size. The key insight is that plain SH inherently favors smaller models (which reach lower loss earlier but plateau faster), while the surrogate can identify larger models whose eventual loss potential is not yet visible in their short training history. The iterative halving produces a set of learning curves where each model was trained as long as it was deemed likely to contribute to the loss-compute frontier, approximating the ideal budget allocation objective without requiring a gold-standard scaling law.

## Results

On the real-world nanoGPT dataset (90 learning curves, models up to 1.5B parameters), SH LMC achieves a mean relative improvement of up to 2.84% over plain SH in minimum validation loss, while uniform allocation (UA) degrades by up to 21.59%. On the synthetic dataset, SH LMC reaches up to 5.47% mean relative improvement over SH. Both strategic methods (SH and SH LMC) produce scaling laws with Area-between-Curves (AbC) values closely matching ground truth while saving up to 98.70% in compute cost compared to exhaustive full-convergence training of all models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is plain Successive Halving (SH) from Jamieson & Talwalkar (2016), which selects models for continued training based solely on current validation loss without any surrogate prediction. SH LMC consistently outperforms plain SH across all model counts (5, 10, 20) and budget levels on both synthetic and real-world nanoGPT datasets, achieving 2.24–2.84% mean relative improvement on nanoGPT and up to 5.47% on synthetic data. The advantage is most pronounced with fewer models (5–10), where the multitask GP can leverage cross-curve correlations to identify larger models with greater long-term potential that plain SH would prematurely discard. The parametric Deep Ensemble variants (SH DE PL/EXP/MMF) also beat plain SH but by smaller margins (1.10–2.40% on nanoGPT), suggesting the non-parametric LMC better captures the noisy curve shapes in real data. Uniform allocation (UA) is substantially worse, degrading by 5–22% relative to SH, confirming that strategic budget allocation is essential. In terms of scaling law quality (Table 3), SH LMC achieves slightly lower loss regret than plain SH (e.g., 0.36 vs 0.39 at M₀=20, B=10⁵) while both save 92.86–98.70% compute versus exhaustive training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Successive Halving (SH) algorithm from hyperparameter optimization (Jamieson & Talwalkar, 2016) for the distinct problem of strategic compute allocation in scaling law estimation, extending it with surrogate-guided model selection. The surrogate models draw directly from the learning-curve prediction literature: the LMC surrogate uses the exponential decay kernel from Freeze-Thaw BO (Swersky et al., 2014) in a multitask GP formulation, while the Deep Ensemble variants follow the parametric curve-fitting approach of Kadra et al. (2023). The paper explicitly positions itself against sequential BO methods (which are structurally misaligned with the need for parallel training) and against single-curve extrapolation methods like LC-PFN, emphasizing that cross-curve correlations are central to its LMC design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 98.7% compute savings over exhaustive training makes scaling law research accessible to academic labs and smaller entities that previously could not afford to train hundreds of models to convergence, contributing to democratization of AI research. The probabilistic surrogate models additionally provide uncertainty bounds (UCB/LCB) on the estimated scaling law, enabling risk-aware deployment decisions. Key open questions include validation at modern LLM scales (beyond the 1.5B nanoGPT ceiling) and across diverse architectural families, as well as whether the approach generalizes to data-constrained or non-power-law regimes where the assumed curve shapes break down.
