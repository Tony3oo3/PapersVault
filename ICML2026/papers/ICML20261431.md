## Problem

Existing root cause analysis for rare events identifies a small set of causal origins but does not capture the mechanisms through which these causes propagate to produce the observed outcome, and cannot be reduced to a limited number of root causes. Prior work on extreme-value causal modeling relies on asymptotic tail limits that do not apply to rare-but-non-extreme events, while mediation analysis decomposes causal effects rather than explaining a specific observed event. The paper addresses the need for a formal, event-specific notion of causal explanation that remains well-defined under partial model knowledge and supports quantitative falsifiability.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a causal DAG over binary variables (or continuous variables binarized via feature functions τ_i), a joint probability distribution, a designated target rare event B_t, and a candidate root-cause set B_R. It produces a causal pathway subgraph P (a subgraph of the full DAG C), a pathway explanation score E_{R→t}^K quantifying how well the root causes render the target and all mediators likely relative to the target's own rarity, and an abstraction accuracy r measuring the maximum KL-divergence between true and abstract interventional distributions. A key design choice is that the pathway P may differ from the full cluster DAG C by retaining or dropping edges into root-cause nodes, allowing context variables (e.g., non-rare confounders) to appear in the explanation without being treated as root causes.

## Method

The paper introduces a formal definition of a causal pathway as a tuple (C, P, B, B_t, B_R, P_B) and defines a pathway explanation score that normalizes the log-likelihood of the full cluster under do(B_R=1) by the log-likelihood of the target alone, yielding a stricter criterion than the cluster explanation score. For continuous variables, the method introduces feature monotonicity—a condition on Markov kernels ensuring that higher feature values of parents stochastically increase the tail probability of the child—and proves a multivariate bound (Theorem 4.3) showing that the pathway explanation score is close to 1 with high probability when the target is a strong outlier. Interventions on binary variables in the original continuous model are defined via natural micro-realization, which draws intervened variables from their conditional distribution given the binary state, resolving the ill-definedness of coarse-grained interventions. The pathway abstraction is then evaluated by an accuracy metric based on the maximum KL-divergence between true and abstract interventional distributions over all intervention sets, and the framework provides quantitative conditions (via controlled direct contributions and confounding strength) under which edges or confounders can be safely removed while preserving high accuracy.

## Results

This is a primarily theoretical paper with no standard benchmark evaluation. Key quantitative results include: (1) In the chain-of-events example (Ex. 3.6), the pathway explanation score is 3/4 with root causes {B₁, B₃} and reaches 1 when B₄ is added, illustrating how local explanation scores can be high while the global pathway score remains moderate. (2) In the Gaussian cause-effect pair (Ex. 4.6), for outlier x > 3, the explanation score exceeds 0.8 for all y < ρx, providing a concrete threshold for accepting verbal causal claims. (3) In the LLM evaluation (Sec. 5), a generated 5-node pathway for homelessness receives a pathway explanation score of only ≈0.29, with the weak link C→D (P(D|C)≈0.05) identified as the primary bottleneck, demonstrating the framework's falsifiability. (4) Theorem 4.3 bounds the probability that the negative log-likelihood L exceeds c by the Erlang CDF p = Σᵢ cⁱ/i! · e⁻ᶜ, providing a distribution-free guarantee for pathway explanation scores under feature monotonicity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baselines on standard benchmarks; it is a theoretical contribution with illustrative examples. However, it makes several internal comparisons that are informative. First, the logarithmic explanation score is contrasted with mean-based and Shapley-based attribution (Appendix D): for an AND gate with P(X=1)=10⁻⁹ and P(N=1)=10⁻¹, the logarithmic score assigns 90% contribution to X, versus ≈10% for mean-based and ≈55% for Shapley-based, arguing that rarer factors should receive proportionally more explanatory credit. Second, the pathway explanation score is compared to the cluster explanation score (Lemma 3.5), showing the pathway score is strictly more demanding: it requires the interventional probability to be close to 1 relative to the target's rarity, not just relative to the joint cluster probability. Third, in Example 4.8, the trivariate pathway abstraction (including the context variable B₁) achieves both higher explanation scores and higher abstraction accuracy than the bivariate abstraction in the outlier region (y≥2, z≥2), quantifying the cost of omitting contextual variables. Finally, the LLM evaluation in Section 5 serves as a proof-of-concept falsifiability check, where the same model's probability estimates reveal internal inconsistency in its own generated pathway.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the explanation score of Oesterle et al. (2025) from single-feature attribution to a full causal pathway formalism, building on distribution change attribution (Budhathoki et al., 2021) and root cause analysis of outliers (Orchard et al., 2025). It departs from mediation analysis, which decomposes causal effects into path-specific components, by instead asking which subgraph provides a good event-level explanation for a particular rare observation. The pathway abstraction mechanism draws on natural micro-realization (Zhu et al., 2024) to handle the ill-definedness of interventions on coarse-grained binary variables, and on causal abstraction (Beckers & Halpern, 2019) but at the event-specific level rather than the full-model level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the first formal, quantitative criteria for assessing whether a verbal causal narrative about a rare event is internally consistent and data-compatible, which is directly relevant to evaluating AI-generated causal explanations. The LLM proof-of-concept (showing a 0.29 explanation score and internal inconsistency in a homelessness narrative) illustrates a practical falsifiability check, but the paper explicitly does not yet enable automated plausibility testing. Open questions include scaling the greedy root-cause selection to large DAGs, handling violations of feature monotonicity, and extending the framework to continuous-valued targets without binarization. The additivity of contributions (avoiding Shapley-value averaging) and the implicit necessity guarantee under rarity are useful theoretical simplifications for practitioners.
