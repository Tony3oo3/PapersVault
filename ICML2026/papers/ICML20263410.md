## Problem

When LLMs generate code for tasks admitting multiple valid algorithms, correctness-based benchmarks like pass@k collapse all passing implementations into a single bit, leaving the algorithm choice invisible to developers. No prior work had systematically measured whether incidental prompt context—persona cues, project metadata, import statements, even random identifiers—steers which valid algorithm the model selects, or how large those distributional shifts are relative to the correctness signal.

## Input / Output

Each experiment constructs a prompt by injecting a single contextual cue (one of 19 types spanning persona, constraint, import, style, placebo, and other channels) into a fixed task specification with tests, then generates code from one of 15 model configurations at controlled temperature. The generated code is classified into an algorithm family via an AST-based structural detector, and the primary output is the steering delta—the shift in family-selection probability in percentage points relative to a no-cue baseline—reported alongside pass rate and shortcut-usage rate (e.g., eval) to separate policy shifts from correctness effects.

## Method

The core contribution is a controlled experimental framework that isolates algorithm steering from output quality: for each (task, cue, model) cell the specification and tests are held fixed while only the injected cue varies, yielding 46,535 runs across 11 tasks, 19 cue types, and 15 model configurations. An AST-based classifier maps each generation to a family using structural signals (recursion presence, data-structure patterns, decorators, imports), validated at 87–88% agreement against a GPT-4o LLM-as-judge with a ±5 pp noise floor. A semantic-vs-surface ablation on memoization (matched meaning, different typography and punctuation) confirms that cue meaning—not formatting—drives the shift, while temperature sweeps and a 240-run high-replication robustness check rule out sampling artifacts. The paper further quantifies per-channel steering capacity, identifies a sophistication-reliability tradeoff where expert personas reduce pass rates on tasks with fragile advanced algorithms, and shows that direct algorithm naming is the most reliable mitigation (100% pass, 98.3% compliance after label-granularity normalization).

## Results

Across 46,535 controlled experiments (11 tasks, 19 cue types, 15 model configurations), within-channel cue changes produce algorithm-family steering deltas up to 100 pp, with a median of 40 pp and mean of 50.0 pp across 930 qualifying API cells at T=0. 65% of cells exceed the 15 pp steering threshold and 56% exceed 30 pp. Overall pass rate is 91.7% with 5.2% unknown; semantic channels (constraint, persona, context, import) show >15 pp steering in 77.7% of cells (mean 67.2 pp) versus 48.2% for innocuous placebo channels (mean 26.1 pp). Direct algorithm specification is the most reliable mitigation, achieving 100% pass rate and 98.3% strict compliance across 120 controlled runs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical measurement study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the key contrasts are cross-model and cross-channel. Among the five API models, Claude Sonnet 4 shows the highest mean steering capacity (56.5 pp) and GPT-4o the lowest (38.1 pp) with a distinctive bimodal pattern (median 0 pp, meaning its algorithm choice is invariant on roughly half of cells but shifts substantially on the rest). The same academic persona on memoization causes Claude to select matrix exponentiation with 100% pass, GPT-5 to attempt it and fail all 5 runs (0% pass), and Gemini 3 Flash to default to naive recursion with 100% pass, demonstrating that steering direction is model-specific. Semantic channels (constraint, persona, context, import) steer far more strongly than innocuous placebo channels (team names, project codes, color themes): 77.7% vs. 48.2% of cells cross the 15 pp threshold, with means of 67.2 pp vs. 26.1 pp. On the HumanEval transfer test (180 runs, 92.2% pass), cue-driven algorithm shifts persist (e.g., Claude selects Sieve of Eratosthenes 5/5 under a performance cue vs. trial division 5/5 under readability) while pass@k remains 1.0 in both, confirming that standard correctness metrics are blind to these policy shifts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the prompt-sensitivity literature (Sclar et al., 2024; Lam et al., 2025; Tian & Zhang, 2025), which treats prompts as a control surface for output quality (success vs. failure), by reframing the question as one of output policy: which correct algorithm is selected when multiple exist. It also extends the algorithmic-diversity line (Lee et al., 2025), which samples many outputs under fixed prompts to reveal a model's repertoire, by holding sampling fixed and varying contextual cues to show how context selects within that repertoire. The formal grounding in Rice's (1976) algorithm selection problem and the AST-based detection framework (inspired by Alon et al., 2019) provide a measurement infrastructure that prior code-generation benchmarks (HumanEval, MBPP) lack by design, since pass@k collapses all correct implementations into a single bit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that pass@k is blind to up-to-100-pp algorithm-policy shifts driven by incidental context has immediate implications for anyone shipping LLM-generated code: a Fibonacci function that passes n≤20 tests may be O(2^n) or O(log n) depending on a system prompt word the developer never noticed. Open questions include the causal mechanism (retrieval-by-analogy vs. constraint propagation vs. style anchoring), whether effects persist in multi-file and framework-level generation, and whether model-side interventions (fine-tuning, RLHF) can decouple steerability from correctness. The paper's 11-task benchmark and released classifiers provide a starting point, but broader task coverage and real-world prompt noise remain untested.
