## Problem

Learning stochastic transition maps from trajectory data faces a fundamental tension: standard MSE regression collapses to the conditional mean, erasing the intrinsic variability of stochastic dynamics, while multi-step generative models (diffusion, flow) require many network evaluations per time step. This paper asks whether one can learn a one-step stochastic next-state generator that preserves trajectory diversity while exploiting the smoothness structure inherent in physical systems where consecutive states are strongly coupled.

## Input / Output

The method takes a current state x_t (a scalar field on a spatial grid, e.g. 64×64 wave/flow fields, or 64×64×3 / 128×128×3 video frames) concatenated with a high-dimensional random label ξ_t ∈ R^d (d = 64–512 depending on task, drawn from N(0, I_d)) and produces a single next-state sample x_{t+1} in the same state space. No latent encoder/decoder is used; the network operates directly on the raw state. At inference, a fresh label is sampled independently at each time step, and the map is rolled forward autoregressively, requiring exactly one network evaluation per step.

## Method

Stochastic Lifting augments each training pair (x_t, x_{t+1}) with an independently drawn random label ξ_t ~ N(0, I_d), then trains a UNet F_θ(x_t, ξ_t) → x_{t+1} via standard L2 (or LPIPS) regression. The label acts as an auxiliary coordinate that lets the network fit multiple plausible next states from similar current states, avoiding the mean-collapse of plain regression; the label dimension d is chosen high enough to enable interpolation of the finite training set. Architecturally, the label replaces the diffusion-time conditioning in a standard UNet: a two-layer MLP embeds ξ_t, and the embedding modulates residual feature maps via FiLM. The theoretical contribution provides finite-sample Wasserstein-2 bounds showing that interpolation plus Lipschitz regularity of F controls sampling error, and proves that increasing d reduces the minimal Lipschitz constant of any interpolant (via near-orthogonal separation of labels on the sphere), giving practitioners a concrete handle on regularity.

## Results

On the Wave problem, Stochastic Lifting (1 step) achieves WCT 4.53e-3 and WIM 2.06e-5, outperforming ARDM at 40 diffusion steps (WCT 1.11e-2, WIM 9.85e-5) by 2.4× and 4.8× respectively. On the Flow problem, Stochastic Lifting achieves WCT 9.47e-4 and WIM 6.08e-4, beating ARDM at 40 steps (WCT 1.86e-3, WIM 6.00e-4). On the BAIR video benchmark, Stochastic Lifting achieves FVD 69.0 with a single step per frame, the best among all one-step methods and competitive with multi-step Video Diffusion (FVD 66.9, s=16) and Rolling Diffusion (FVD 59.6, s=32).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the autoregressive diffusion model (ARDM, Kohl et al. 2026), Stochastic Lifting wins decisively on both physics problems using only 1 network evaluation per time step versus 40 for ARDM: on Wave, WCT improves from 1.11e-2 to 4.53e-3 and WIM from 9.85e-5 to 2.06e-5; on Flow, WCT improves from 1.86e-3 to 9.47e-4 while WIM is comparable (6.08e-4 vs 6.00e-4). On the BAIR video benchmark, Stochastic Lifting (FVD 69.0) outperforms all one-step baselines—SV2P (262.5), SAVP (109.8), DVD-GAN (109.8), TrIVD-GAN-FP (103.3), FitVid (93.6), and NUWA (86.9)—by a wide margin. Among multi-step methods, Stochastic Lifting is competitive with Video Diffusion (FVD 66.9, s=16) and RaMViD (84.2, s=750), and trails only Rolling Diffusion (59.6, s=32) and MCVD (98.8, s=1000) by a small margin, while using 16–1000× fewer sampling steps. The paper also demonstrates that Stochastic Lifting scales to 500-frame rollouts on CLEVRER (trained on 16-frame videos), a regime where prior trajectory-generation methods (Chen et al. 2024 at 14 frames, Davtyan et al. 2023 at 120 frames) do not operate.

## Relation to Prior Work

Stochastic Lifting carves new ground by repurposing the 'lifting' concept from model-order reduction and neural ODEs (augmenting state space to improve well-posedness) for stochastic trajectory generation, but in a fundamentally different way: rather than lifting the state, it lifts the input space with independent random labels to resolve the one-to-many mapping inherent in stochastic transitions. It departs from autoregressive diffusion and flow-based trajectory generators (which require s network evaluations per time step) and from one-step generative methods like consistency models and distillation approaches (which target static noise-to-image generation and require complex training procedures). Unlike neural processes and latent state-space models that infer hidden variables from data, Stochastic Lifting assigns labels independently and trains with a plain regression loss, positioning itself in the finite-sample interpolation regime rather than seeking a population-limit solution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: one network evaluation per time step versus 20–40+ for competitive ARDMs, with comparable or better distributional accuracy on stochastic physics problems (wave, two-phase flow) and video benchmarks (BAIR, CLEVRER). Key limitations include the critical dependence on strong current-to-next-state coupling (the method fails for unconditional generation or when the conditional law is nearly independent of the current state), the absence of a meaningful population-limit guarantee, and the open question of when the trained network builds a meaningful embedding that maps fresh labels to the data manifold. Future work could address conditional (not just marginal) Wasserstein bounds, adaptive label dimension selection, and understanding the interpolation-to-generalization transition more rigorously.
