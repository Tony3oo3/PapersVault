## Problem

Standard NTK and its variants model finetuning dynamics via natural gradient flow, neglecting the coordinate-specific preconditioning that adaptive optimizers like Adam impose on gradient updates. This gap is critical for foundation models, where heavy-tailed gradient distributions make simple sign-based approximations (e.g., SignGD-NTK) particularly unreliable. Additionally, no prior work provides explicit theoretical bounds characterizing when and how the kernel regime collapses during finetuning of pretrained models.

## Input / Output

Given a pretrained model at parameters θ₀, a finetuning set S_train, a test set S_test, and Adam hyperparameters (β₁, β₂, ε), OAK produces an asymmetric kernel matrix K ∈ ℝ^{N_train × N_test} where training-set Jacobians remain unmodified while test-set Jacobians are preconditioned via a single Adam-like update step using globally estimated moments. The resulting kernel is then plugged into standard kernel regression (α = K_train^{-1} y_train) to yield class predictions. The method shares the same O(N²d) asymptotic complexity as standard eNTK, adding at most ~10% empirical overhead even at 1.5B parameters.

## Method

OAK constructs a static, one-time estimate of the optimizer's preconditioners by averaging per-sample loss gradients over the finetuning set to obtain m̂₀ (first moment) and v̂₀ (second moment), then applies an Adam-like preconditioning transform to test-set Jacobians: J̃_j = (β₁m̂₀ + (1-β₁)J'_j) / (√(β₂v̂₀ + (1-β₂)J'_j ⊙ J'_j) + ε). The kernel entry K_ij = Tr(J_i J̃_j^T) thus encodes the optimizer's coordinate-specific rescaling into the feature space. Theoretically, the paper derives explicit error bounds (Theorems 4.10–4.11) showing kernel regime collapse is governed by the cumulative training step Tη and the Wasserstein-1 distance between pretraining and finetuning distributions, and proves (Theorems 5.2, 5.4) that the preconditioner estimation error decomposes into a controllable sampling term and a dynamic term that vanishes in the kernel regime. The framework generalizes to other adaptive optimizers (AdaGrad, AdamW, Muon) by swapping the preconditioning operator.

## Results

On RoBERTa double-sentence tasks (MNLI, MRPC, QQP), OAK(Adam) achieves an average accuracy of 52.91%, outperforming SignGD-NTK (52.20%) and standard NTK (47.38%), and approaching full finetuning (54.27%). On single-sentence tasks (SST-2, COLA), OAK reaches 61.59% average accuracy, surpassing full finetuning (61.13%) and SignGD-NTK (59.17%) by 0.46 and 2.42 points respectively. In low-data regimes (n=8, 16), OAK consistently outperforms full finetuning, e.g., on SST-2 n=8 OAK achieves 55.60% vs. 52.28% for finetuning. On Qwen2.5-0.5B, OAK similarly leads all kernel baselines (48.28% avg on double-sentence vs. 46.79% SignGD-NTK and 46.30% NTK).

## Baselines & Comparisons

OAK(Adam) consistently outperforms both kernel baselines across all tasks and sample sizes. Against standard NTK (Wei et al., 2022), OAK gains 5.53 points on RoBERTa double-sentence tasks (52.91% vs. 47.38%) and a substantial 10.36 points on single-sentence tasks (61.59% vs. 51.23%), confirming that ignoring adaptive preconditioning severely degrades kernel-based predictions. Against SignGD-NTK (Malladi et al., 2023), which approximates Adam via sign-based gradient transformation, OAK achieves 0.71 points higher on double-sentence tasks and 2.42 points higher on single-sentence tasks, validating that coordinate-specific preconditioning (rather than uniform sign scaling) better captures optimizer behavior. Notably, OAK surpasses full Adam finetuning in low-data regimes: on SST-2 with n=8, OAK reaches 55.60% vs. 52.28% for finetuning, and on COLA n=8, 55.86% vs. 57.99% (a narrow loss). On the Qwen2.5-0.5B decoder backbone, OAK maintains its lead over both kernel baselines (48.28% vs. 46.79% and 46.30% on double-sentence tasks), demonstrating architecture-agnostic effectiveness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NTK framework (Jacot et al., 2018) and its empirical variant (Wei et al., 2022) to the finetuning setting for foundation models, building directly on Malladi et al. (2023) who first showed finetuning exhibits kernel-like behavior and proposed SignGD-NTK as a heuristic Adam proxy. The key departure is replacing SignGD's sign-based transformation with a principled preconditioner estimation technique that uses the finetuning dataset to statically estimate Adam's momentum and variance, then applies them to precondition gradient features. The kernel regime collapse analysis (Section 4) extends standard NTK theory by deriving explicit error bounds in terms of cumulative training steps and the Wasserstein-1 distance between pretraining and finetuning distributions, going beyond the infinite-width random-initialization setting of prior NTK work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OAK provides the first theoretically grounded (not heuristic) way to incorporate adaptive optimizer effects into the NTK framework, with error bounds on the preconditioner estimation that are valid precisely when the kernel regime holds. The kernel collapse analysis gives practitioners a quantitative criterion for when NTK-based methods will fail during finetuning, which is practically important for choosing between kernel regression and full finetuning. Limitations include validation only on GLUE classification tasks with models up to 1.5B parameters, scalar-output simplification in the theory, and the fact that OAK's practical utility is confined to kernel-based analysis rather than directly improving training. Open questions include whether the framework extends to full autoregressive generation and whether the static preconditioner estimate remains valid under very long finetuning schedules where the kernel regime has already collapsed.
