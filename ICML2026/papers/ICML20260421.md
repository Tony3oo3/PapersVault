## Problem

LoRA is inherently overparameterized: for any invertible R, the pairs (A,B) and (AR, R⁻¹B) yield the same adapter AB, creating a continuous manifold of minimizers. The paper shows that these minimizers exhibit significantly different Hessian condition numbers, so the particular minimizer a trajectory converges to directly governs the asymptotic convergence rate. No prior work had connected the balance condition AᵀA = BBᵀ to loss conditioning in the low-rank adaptation setting, leaving open which parameterization of a fixed adapter is optimal for optimization.

## Input / Output

BaLoRA takes a frozen pretrained weight matrix W ∈ ℝ^{a×b} and a training dataset, and produces a pair of low-rank adapters (A, B) ∈ ℝ^{a×r} × ℝ^{r×b} constrained to the hyperbalanced manifold where AᵀA = BBᵀ = S for some non-negative diagonal S ∈ 𝔻₊ʳ. The balanced projection P preserves the product AB exactly, so the adapted weight W + AB is identical to what standard LoRA would produce at the same iterate—only the factorization changes. The projection costs O((a+b)r²) per step, which is negligible relative to the optimizer step when a, b ≫ r, and adds no measurable GPU memory overhead (32.63 GB for both LoRA and BaLoRA at r=128).

## Method

BaLoRA enforces balance by projecting the low-rank factors onto the hyperbalanced manifold ℋ after every optimizer step (e.g., AdamW). The balancing map P(A,B) computes the SVD of the product AB = USVᵀ and returns (US^{1/2}, S^{1/2}Vᵀ), which is a smooth retraction onto ℋ that preserves the product. Theoretically, the paper proves that balanced minimizers minimize the Hessian condition number κ(f) at convergence—achieving κ_min = 2σ₁(Z)/σ_r(Z) in the matrix factorization case and maximizing the lower bound σ_r(Z) − σ_{r+1}(Z) in the general rank-deficient case—yielding faster asymptotic convergence. A striking intrinsic reformulation shows that BaLoRA-GD is equivalent to Riemannian gradient descent on the rank-r matrix manifold 𝒩_r equipped with the Bures metric, with the update X_{k+1} = R(X_k, −τ_k H_{X_k}[∇g(X_k)]), where H_X[W] = (XXᵀ)^{1/2}W + W(XᵀX)^{1/2}. Empirically, BaLoRA outperforms standard LoRA and most variants across Llama-3.2-3B and Qwen-2.5-3B fine-tuning tasks, with a particularly clear advantage at larger adapter ranks (r ∈ {64, 128}).

## Results

On Qwen-2.5-3B fine-tuned across five datasets (Alpaca, CodeFeedback, OpenHermes, OpenOrca, WizardLM) with rank r=8, BaLoRA achieves the best or tied-best test loss on 4 out of 5 datasets, matching RefLoRA (the other balanced method) and outperforming standard LoRA by 0.001–0.002 in loss. On DeepMind Mathematics, BaLoRA's advantage grows with rank: at r=128 it reaches a train loss of 1.014 versus 1.030 for LoRA and 1.069 for LoRA-RITE. On Wikitext-2-raw-v1, BaLoRA achieves 2.251 test loss on Qwen-2.5-3B (vs. 2.257 for LoRA) and 2.274 on Llama-3.2-3B (vs. 2.278 for LoRA), while adding negligible GPU memory overhead (32.63 GB, identical to LoRA).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BaLoRA consistently outperforms standard LoRA across nearly all settings: on the five-dataset Qwen-2.5-3B benchmark it wins or ties on every dataset, and on DeepMind Mathematics it leads at all ranks, with the gap widening at r=128 (1.014 vs. 1.030). Against DoRA and LoRA-RITE, BaLoRA shows similar margins to LoRA, with a decisive advantage on DeepMind Mathematics at high ranks (e.g., 1.014 vs. 1.069 for LoRA-RITE at r=128). LORO (Riemannian low-rank optimization) is the weakest baseline, trailing BaLoRA by 0.15+ in loss on Alpaca and 0.25 on OpenOrca. OLoRA is outperformed by BaLoRA on all five datasets in Table 1 (e.g., 1.350 vs. 1.360 on Alpaca). The closest competitor is RefLoRA, which also enforces balanced iterates; the two methods are nearly indistinguishable on the five-dataset table (differences ≤0.001), but BaLoRA clearly surpasses RefLoRA on DeepMind Mathematics at higher ranks (1.014 vs. 1.032 at r=128) and on Wikitext for Qwen (2.251 vs. not directly compared, but BaLoRA beats LoRA-GA's 2.264). On MetaMathQA, BaLoRA is marginally behind LoRA (0.144 vs. 0.142), suggesting the advantage is most pronounced in longer training or higher-rank regimes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LoRA framework (Hu et al., 2022) by connecting its inherent overparameterization to loss conditioning, building on balance conditions previously studied in deep linear networks (Nguegnang et al., 2024; Marcotte et al., 2023) and matrix factorization (Ye & Du, 2021; Ghosh et al., 2025). While RefLoRA (Zhang et al., 2025) and LoRA-RITE (Yen et al., 2024) also address the gauge invariance of low-rank adapters, this paper is the first to prove that balanced minimizers are *optimally conditioned* and to provide a closed-form link between the Hessian spectrum and the singular values of the target matrix. The intrinsic reformulation of BaLoRA-GD as gradient descent under the Bures metric on the rank-r manifold draws on Riemannian optimization theory (Boumal, 2023) and optimal transport (Bhatia et al., 2019), offering a geometric perspective absent from prior LoRA analyses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that balanced minimizers achieve the minimal condition number provides a principled, theoretically grounded reason to enforce balance during LoRA training, elevating what was previously a heuristic (used in RefLoRA, LoRA-GA) to an optimality criterion. The negligible computational overhead (O((a+b)r²) per step) and compatibility with existing AdamW pipelines make BaLoRA immediately deployable, and its clear advantage at large ranks (r=64, 128) addresses a practical pain point. Key limitations include: the theoretical analysis covers only single-layer fine-tuning, the AdamW convergence analysis relies on a sign-GD proxy rather than a direct proof, and the deep non-linear case is handled only in the interpolating regime. Extending the conditioning analysis to the non-interpolating deep case and to multi-layer simultaneous fine-tuning remains open.
