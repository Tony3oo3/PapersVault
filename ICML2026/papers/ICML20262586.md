## Problem

Data-driven dynamics predictors trained on one environmental condition fail to generalize to unseen conditions, and existing adaptation strategies (gradient-based meta-learning or massive foundation models) either require expensive per-environment fine-tuning or hundreds of millions of parameters, making them impractical for data-scarce or hardware-constrained scientific deployments. The fundamental gap is that these methods only modify a small, expert-specified subset of weights rather than generating the complete expert model from scratch, restricting the representable weight manifold.

## Input / Output

At test time, DynaDiff takes a short observation sequence of L spatial-field frames (default L=10, but variable from 1 to L) from a new unseen environment—no explicit environmental parameters (e.g., Reynolds number) are required. It outputs the full weight tensor of a small predictive model (~1M-parameter FNO, WNO, or UNO), which is then used autoregressively to predict future states (50–100 steps). The generated model is a complete, standalone predictor that can be deployed independently without any further gradient computation.

## Method

DynaDiff represents expert model weights as a heterogeneous graph where each node is an output neuron/channel and its feature vector is the concatenation of all incoming weights plus bias, preserving the network's forward data-flow topology. A node-attention VAE encodes this graph into a per-node latent space, trained with a functional loss that penalizes output disagreement between original and reconstructed models rather than raw weight MSE, yielding a functionally smooth latent manifold. A DiT-based latent diffusion model then generates weight representations conditioned on a dynamics-informed prompt, which is extracted by a two-branch prompter: an explicit branch computing temporal statistics (mean, variance, energy, enstrophy trends) and an implicit branch applying 2D FFT followed by a GRU over the spectral sequence. The expert model zoo is constructed offline via domain-adaptive initialization (pretrain a shared base model, then rapid per-environment fine-tuning with random layer noise), amortizing all training cost and eliminating gradient-based computation at deployment.

## Results

DynaDiff achieves the best average out-domain RMSE across four PDE systems (Cylinder Flow: 0.065, Lambda-Omega: 0.091, Kolmogorov Flow: 0.079, Navier-Stokes: 0.064) using only ~1M parameter expert models at test time, outperforming foundation models with ~500M parameters by an average of 10.78%. On the real-world ERA5 wind-speed dataset, DynaDiff also surpasses all baselines and even exceeds One-per-Env in partial unseen subregions. Statistical significance tests confirm highly significant improvements on Cylinder Flow (p=2.90×10⁻²⁷) and Navier-Stokes (p=3.79×10⁻¹⁸) out-domain settings, while Lambda-Omega shows comparable (non-significant) performance to the best baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DynaDiff outperforms all four foundation-model baselines (FNO, DPOT, Poseidon, MPP) on out-domain RMSE across every PDE system, despite using ~500× fewer parameters at test time (~1M vs. ~500M). Against the five meta-learning baselines (DyAd, LEADS, CoDA, GEPS, CAMEL), DynaDiff consistently achieves lower out-domain RMSE; for example, on Kolmogorov Flow out-domain, DynaDiff (0.079±0.011) beats GEPS (0.084±0.017) with p=0.016. The two weight-space generation baselines (CVAE and D2NWG) produce blurred weight distributions that fail to capture the true expert manifold (higher JSD in Figure 4), and DynaDiff surpasses them on all systems. Notably, DynaDiff can even outperform the One-per-Env upper bound in certain environments, suggesting it captures the weight-environment joint distribution more faithfully than per-environment optimization. The sole exception is Lambda-Omega, where DynaDiff's out-domain advantage is not statistically significant (p=0.53), indicating parity with the best baseline on that system.

## Relation to Prior Work

DynaDiff extends the latent diffusion paradigm (Rombach et al., 2022) and DiT-style conditioning (Peebles & Xie, 2023) to a new domain: generating complete neural network weights for physical dynamics prediction. It departs from two dominant prior paradigms—meta-learning methods that fine-tune environment-specific contexts on a fixed backbone (DyAd, CoDA, GEPS, CAMEL) and foundation models that rely on massive parameterization (DPOT, Poseidon, MPP)—by treating the full weight vector as the generative target rather than a partially modulated object. Compared to prior weight-space generation methods (CVAE, D2NWG), DynaDiff introduces a structured weight graph that preserves architectural topology, a functional loss that measures output consistency rather than parameter proximity, and a dynamics-informed prompter that extracts physical and spectral features from short observations, addressing three challenges (structural, dimensional, and data-scarcity) that prior flattening-based approaches neglect.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The shift from gradient-based fine-tuning to one-shot weight generation has direct practical implications for deploying dynamics predictors on edge devices or in data-scarce scientific settings, where iterative optimization is infeasible. The model zoo construction amortizes adaptation cost into a one-time offline investment, and the ~1M-parameter generated models outperform ~500M foundation models, challenging the assumption that scale is necessary for cross-environment generalization. Key limitations include the fixed-architecture constraint (the generator produces weights for a single model type), the need for a pre-constructed model zoo of sufficient size, and the current restriction to PDE-based dynamics. Open questions include whether the paradigm extends to heterogeneous architecture synthesis, whether the weight graph generalizes to non-operator architectures (e.g., deep MLPs, attention-heavy transformers), and whether the functional loss can be made differentiable end-to-end for joint optimization with the diffusion objective.
