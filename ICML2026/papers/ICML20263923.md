## Problem

Standard classification maximizes accuracy but is silent about which users receive correct predictions, implicitly assuming uniform benefit. When users value accuracy heterogeneously and privately, the system must simultaneously elicit truthful valuations (since users strategically overreport) and optimize social welfare, a dual challenge that conventional learning approaches cannot address.

## Input / Output

The method consumes labeled training samples (x_i, y_i) with x ∈ R^d and y ∈ {±1}, plus user-submitted bids b_i ∈ [0, V] elicited at train time via the auction mechanism. It produces a learned score-based classifier h(x) = sign(f(x)), binary predictions ŷ, and per-user payments p_i that are charged only at train time (test-time users bear no cost). A key structural constraint is that feasible allocations are restricted to those expressible by the chosen model class H, making the auction's allocation space depend on classifier expressivity.

## Method

The approach is a two-stage accuracy auction: first, an auction elicits truthful user values by using bids as example weights in a regularized risk minimization objective (e.g., L2-regularized hinge or log-loss), where the allocation rule assigns a correct prediction to user i if the resulting classifier predicts y_i correctly. The core theoretical contribution is proving monotonicity of allocations in individual bids (Theorem 1), which via Myerson's Lemma yields a dominant-strategy incentive-compatible payment rule where p_i equals the critical bid—the minimum bid needed to secure a correct prediction. A second key result shows that for L2-regularized linear classifiers with classification-stable algorithms (SVM, logistic regression), the expected number of paying users is O(1) independent of sample size, by leveraging algorithmic stability to show only points within a shrinking band near the decision boundary can pay. Efficient computation exploits this: only points with margin y_i·f(x_i) ∈ [0, β_i] are candidates for payment, and for SVMs the support vector structure further reduces the effective dataset per payment query.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 2) shows that for L₂-regularized linear classifiers (SVM, logistic regression), the expected sum of payments in an accuracy auction is O(1), independent of sample size m. The converse (Theorem 3) establishes that k-NN classifiers incur Ω(m) payments when label noise is present. Empirically on the folktables ACSIncome dataset, explicitly optimizing welfare (α=1) over accuracy (α=0) yields up to 5% welfare improvement with feature-based values and up to 23% with label-based values, while the number of paying users plateaus at at most 20 and their proportion decays from 1.5% toward zero as m grows.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external classifier baselines; instead it compares learning algorithms on the induced auction's payment properties. L₂-regularized linear classifiers (SVM, logistic regression) achieve a constant O(1) bound on total payments, while k-NN suffers a linear Ω(m) lower bound when label noise exists in some region (Corollary 2). Among linear classifiers, hinge loss, squared hinge loss, and log-loss all satisfy the O(1) bound but with different constants; experiments show the number of paying users remains at most 20 across all three losses as m increases. On the folktables ACSIncome task, interpolating between pure accuracy (α=0) and pure welfare (α=1) reveals that welfare can be improved substantially (up to 23% for label-based values) with a moderate accuracy reduction (up to 21%), demonstrating that the standard accuracy objective is a poor proxy for social welfare when user valuations are heterogeneous. The stable payment-computation algorithm (binary search restricted to candidate points via Lemma 2) requires only 1 + m̃k calls to the learning objective versus 1 + mk for the non-stable approach, with m̃ typically a small constant for linear models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of strategic learning, auction theory, and algorithmic stability. It departs from strategic classification (Hardt et al., 2016; Levanon & Rosenfeld, 2021), where users manipulate features, by instead treating user valuations for accuracy as private information elicited through a truthful auction (Myerson, 1981). The learning objective reduces to weighted loss minimization (Elkan, 2001; Zadrozny et al., 2003) once values are known, but the novel contribution is the auction mechanism and the stability-based analysis of payment costs. The paper also positions itself against fairness-constrained learning (Dwork et al., 2012; Zemel et al., 2013), arguing that welfare maximization with heterogeneous valuations is a more flexible and economically grounded alternative to equity constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, incentive-compatible way to allocate accuracy as a scarce resource, with the practically important guarantee that stable algorithms (SVM, logistic regression) require only a constant number of paying users regardless of sample size. The k-NN lower bound showing linear payment growth highlights that algorithm choice fundamentally affects the economic burden of welfare optimization. Open questions include whether the constant-payment property extends to non-stable algorithms (deep networks, decision trees), whether payment-based mechanisms can be replaced entirely, and how the framework generalizes to multi-class settings where monotonicity of allocations is not guaranteed. The rationality assumption and cardinal (utilitarian) welfare formulation are acknowledged limitations that future work should address.
