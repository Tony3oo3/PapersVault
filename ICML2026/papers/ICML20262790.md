## Problem

In many RL domains, environments are linked by prerequisite relations that induce a directed acyclic curriculum graph, yet this structure is typically exploited only implicitly. The paper asks: given an explicit curriculum DAG, can training budget be allocated so that traversing relatively few paths suffices to master the entire reachable environment space, yielding better robustness and generalization than unstructured or replay-only sampling?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262790/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a curriculum DAG G=(Θ, E) whose nodes are environment instances (POMDPs) and whose directed edges encode prerequisite relations—constructed from parameterized increments, complexity-increasing local edits, or monotone feature axes. For MiniGrid, nodes are 15×15 maze layouts with 0–60 blocks and edges add 1–5 blocks; for BipedalWalker, nodes are 8-dimensional terrain-parameter vectors and edges increment one coordinate by a fixed edit size. The output is a single PPO-trained policy (LSTM for MiniGrid, MLP for BipedalWalker) evaluated by solved rate and mean return on held-out robustness and generalization test sets of 100–1000 environments.

## Method

PATH is a two-stage active path-acquisition algorithm on the curriculum DAG. In the random phase (PATH:RANDOM), N path pointers start at uniformly sampled source nodes and advance along outgoing edges when an early-stop reward threshold ε_es is met or a sampling patience ε_p is exhausted, providing broad coverage by growing mostly non-overlapping t-neighborhoods. In the active phase (PATH:ACTIVE), uniform sampling is replaced by regret-weighted replay: each buffer entry receives a weight from PLR's positive value loss (a regret proxy computed from TD-errors), and mastered nodes trigger insertion of multiple successor pointers to concentrate sampling on high-learning-potential regions. A Bernoulli replay rate p balances replaying high-regret entries against injecting fresh random source nodes, which evict low-weight pointers and prevent premature saturation. The phase switch occurs after T_switch maximal paths have terminated, and the two stages are complementary: random exploration yields large early coverage gains while active allocation targets the residual unmastered mass that random paths hit only rarely.

## Results

On MiniGrid, PATH achieves a 99.4% generalization solved rate and 98% robustness solved rate, outperforming ACCEL (97.6% / 84%) and PLR (97.7% / 79%). On BipedalWalker, PATH reaches a mean generalization test return of 42.95 (11% solved rate) versus 18.14 (4%) for ACCEL and 9.94 (1%) for DR, and a mean robustness return of 245.8 versus 152.2 for ACCEL. On Leaper (Procgen), PATH attains a 66% solved rate on held-out environments, exceeding ACCEL (51%), DR (57%), and PLR (45%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PATH outperforms all three baselines on every benchmark and every metric reported. Against DR (Tobin et al., 2017), the gap is largest on BipedalWalker, where PATH's mean generalization return (42.95) is over 4× that of DR (9.94), and its robustness mean return (245.8) is roughly 3.7× DR's (67.1). Against PLR (Jiang et al., 2021b), PATH leads by a wide margin on BipedalWalker robustness (245.8 vs. 103.1) and generalization (42.95 vs. 15.39), while on MiniGrid the gap narrows to 99.4% vs. 97.7% solved rate. Against ACCEL (Parker-Holder et al., 2022), the strongest replay-based baseline, PATH still wins consistently: 99.4% vs. 97.6% on MiniGrid generalization, 245.8 vs. 152.2 mean robustness return on BipedalWalker, and 66% vs. 51% solved rate on Leaper. The largest relative advantage over ACCEL appears on BipedalWalker generalization, where PATH more than doubles the mean return and nearly triples the solved rate, which the authors attribute to PATH's active path allocation concentrating training on the residual unmastered region of the curriculum DAG.

## Relation to Prior Work

PATH builds directly on PLR's (Jiang et al., 2021b) positive value loss as its regret proxy for prioritizing environments, and is positioned as a path-level refinement of ACCEL (Parker-Holder et al., 2022), which implicitly exploits edit-based curriculum structure through evolutionary mutation. The key conceptual departure is making the implicit prerequisite ordering used by ACCEL and POET explicit as a curriculum DAG, then formulating training as active path acquisition rather than instance-level selection. The paper also distinguishes itself from Svetlik et al. (2017), who infer a curriculum DAG from transfer matrices, by answering the complementary question: given a DAG, how to traverse it efficiently.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PATH demonstrates that explicitly representing curriculum structure as a DAG and performing two-stage active path selection (random exploration followed by regret-driven concentration) yields substantially better robustness and generalization than instance-level replay methods, with particularly large gains on continuous-control tasks like BipedalWalker. A key limitation is the assumption of a constructible curriculum DAG, which is natural in parameterized or observable settings but requires additional effort in fully underspecified domains; automatic DAG construction (e.g., via LLMs) is identified as a promising future direction. Open questions include whether the two-phase design generalizes to 3D embodied environments and sim-to-real transfer curricula, and how PATH compares to the broader (non-UED) curriculum learning literature.
