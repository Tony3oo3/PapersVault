## Problem

Existing AI governance frameworks (OECD principles, EU AI Act, MIT AI Risk Repository) identify mis/disinformation as a salient generative-AI risk but provide no principled guidance on when expression restrictions are justified or how to design them without overreach. Usage policies—the primary public instrument through which providers govern user interaction—operate as de facto private speech governance, yet no globally applicable, checkable baseline exists for evaluating whether their disinformation-related restrictions respect freedom of expression.

## Input / Output

The method consumes publicly available usage-policy text from AI providers, applying a falsifiable inclusion rule: a provider is assessed only if its policy explicitly invokes the terms 'misinformation' or 'disinformation' (adjacent concepts like 'false information' or 'misleading outputs' are excluded). Output is a structured compliance table coding each provider on five criteria (explicit reference, term defined, harm-linkage, necessity/proportionality, intent) using a three-level scale (✓ clearly present, △ partial/implicit, × absent), accompanied by textual evidence and design recommendations for rights-consistent policy drafting.

## Method

The paper operationalizes Article 19 of the ICCPR—its cumulative legality, legitimacy, and necessity/proportionality test—into a five-criterion benchmark with explicit coding rules, treating the legal structure as a design framework rather than importing doctrine wholesale. Legality maps to definitional clarity and foreseeability (no catch-all categories, no 'secret rules'), legitimacy requires each restriction to be tied to a recognized Article 19(3) aim (public health, rights of others, public order), and necessity/proportionality demands an articulated least-restrictive intervention ladder (contextualization → friction/disclosure → refusal) rather than flat prohibitions. The benchmark is applied to a comparative snapshot of eight leading providers (Alibaba, Anthropic, DeepSeek, Google, Meta, Mistral AI, OpenAI, xAI) as of January 2026, revealing that no policy satisfies all three sub-criteria for necessity/proportionality and that definitional clarity is the exception rather than the norm. The paper further proposes a three-direction research agenda—policy-behavior gap audits, query-taxonomy benchmarks with an intervention ladder, and cross-provider benchmarking of least-restrictive interventions—that translates the normative framework into measurable evaluation targets for ML teams.

## Results

Of eight major AI providers assessed, only five (Alibaba, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Mistral AI) explicitly reference mis/disinformation in their usage policies. No provider achieves a full checkmark (✓) on the legality criterion (operational definition of the term), and the necessity/proportionality criterion is uniformly absent (×) across all five providers. Google is the strongest performer, earning ✓ on both harm-linkage (legitimacy) and intent consideration, while Meta is the weakest, scoring × on definition, harm-linkage, and necessity/proportionality. The most consistent gap across all providers is the absence of any articulated least-restrictive intervention logic (e.g., graduated escalation from contextualization to refusal).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not compare against competing methods or baselines in the traditional ML sense; instead, it benchmarks five providers' public usage policies against a five-criterion human rights framework derived from ICCPR Article 19. Google emerges as the most rights-aligned policy, the only provider to explicitly link restrictions to concrete Article 19(3) harms (fraud, public health, democratic processes) and to treat intent as a scope-narrowing factor. Mistral AI and Meta also credit intent (✓) but fail to define the term operationally or articulate harm-linkage. Alibaba and Anthropic score only partial (△) on most criteria, relying on examples and adjacent concepts rather than definitions. The uniform × on necessity/proportionality indicates that no provider currently articulates a graduated intervention ladder (contextualization → friction → refusal) in its public policy text, representing the single most actionable gap identified by the framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the three-part test from ICCPR Article 19 (legality, legitimacy, necessity/proportionality) as an operational design framework for AI usage policies, a translation not previously attempted in the AI governance literature. It positions itself as a normative complement to existing AI safety approaches (Constitutional AI, red-teaming, risk-based governance) that identify harms but lack principled constraints on overreach, drawing on the author's prior work on freedom of expression in generative AI (Calvet-Bademunt et al., 2025) and on Klyman's (2024) analysis of acceptable use policies for foundation models. The paper carves new ground by converting a legal doctrine into a five-criterion, three-level coding scheme (✓/△/×) that ML teams can apply to policy text, bridging the gap between international human rights law and concrete policy-drafting practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The systematic finding that no reviewed provider articulates a graduated least-restrictive intervention logic in usage-policy text highlights a concrete, addressable gap in current AI governance practice, and the proposed research agenda (policy-behavior gap audits, query-taxonomy intervention ladders, cross-provider benchmarking) gives ML teams actionable evaluation targets. Key limitations include the assessment of public policy text only (not model-level enforcement), the interpretive judgment required in coding, and the exclusion of providers that address adjacent concepts without using 'misinformation'/'disinformation' terminology. Open questions include whether the framework can be embedded in training loops (e.g., as Constitutional AI constraints), how jurisdictional variation interacts with the proposed global floor, and whether policy-behavior gap audits will reveal systematic divergence between stated rules and actual model refusals.
