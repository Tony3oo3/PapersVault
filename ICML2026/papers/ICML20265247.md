## Problem

Despite growing empirical evidence that LLM agents can learn from natural language feedback (critiques, explanations, corrections), no principled mathematical framework exists to characterize when such learning is feasible or how its complexity compares to classical reward-observed bandit settings. The ambiguity and richness of language make the information content of feedback difficult to quantify, leaving open whether and how language feedback can provably accelerate learning over scalar rewards.

## Input / Output

At each step the agent selects an action from a finite set and receives natural language feedback (a token sequence) rather than a numerical reward; the reward is latent and used only for benchmarking. The agent additionally has access to a hypothesis verifier loss ℓ(a, o, η) that scores semantic consistency between a candidate hypothesis and observed feedback, plus a known reward mapping η ↦ r_η that converts any hypothesis into a reward function. The output is the next action (or a policy over actions), with the algorithm optionally triggering an early-stopping exploitation step when all surviving hypotheses agree on an optimal action.

## Method

The paper introduces the transfer eluder dimension (dim_TE), a complexity measure that quantifies how effectively language feedback reduces uncertainty about the hidden reward, and proves that for discriminative feedback it is bounded above by the classical eluder dimension. The HELiX algorithm is a UCB-style procedure that maintains a confidence set of hypotheses whose cumulative verifier loss is near-minimal, then either exploits a consensus optimal action (if the minimax regret over the confidence set is zero) or explores by selecting the action with maximum optimistic reward under any surviving hypothesis. In practice, the hypothesis set is approximated by sampling N streams of LLM thinking tokens, the verifier is implicitly implemented by the LLM's filtering of inconsistent hypotheses, and the reward mapping is approximated by prompting a (possibly different) LLM to score action-hypothesis pairs. The resulting regret bound is Õ(T^{3/4} · dim_TE^{1/2}), and the paper demonstrates that richer feedback (e.g., identifying the first mistake vs. only reporting correctness) can reduce dim_TE from exponential to linear or constant, yielding exponential sample-efficiency gains over reward-only learning.

## Results

The paper evaluates HELiX on two language-feedback decision-making tasks (Battleship and Minesweeper) using Claude-Sonnet-3.5 v2, reporting cumulative reward over a fixed number of environment interaction steps. HELiX consistently achieves higher cumulative reward than the CoT baseline on both tasks, with the gap being particularly large on Minesweeper where strategic information gathering is essential. The ablation HELiX (No exploitation step) also outperforms CoT but is consistently worse than full HELiX, confirming that the consensus-based exploitation step (early stopping when all hypotheses agree on an optimal action) provides additional gains beyond optimistic exploration alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three agents: (1) CoT, which generates a single hypothesis and action in one LLM call; (2) HELiX (full), which samples multiple hypotheses, performs optimistic exploration, and includes a consensus-based exploitation/stopping step; and (3) HELiX (No exploitation step), an ablation that removes the consensus check. On both Battleship and Minesweeper, HELiX outperforms CoT in cumulative reward, with the advantage being most pronounced on Minesweeper where the environment demands deliberate information gathering. The no-exploitation ablation also beats CoT, showing that multi-hypothesis optimistic exploration alone is beneficial, but full HELiX further improves by exploiting once hypotheses converge. The paper notes that these results are preliminary and that the practical implementation relies on LLMs being able to generate diverse, faithful hypotheses and score actions fairly across hypotheses — assumptions that may not hold universally across all LLMs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formalizes the Learning from Language Feedback (LLF) paradigm introduced empirically by Cheng et al. (2023) into a rigorous mathematical framework with learnability conditions and complexity measures. It extends the eluder dimension (Russo and Van Roy, 2013) to a 'transfer eluder dimension' that captures how language feedback reduces uncertainty about latent rewards, and builds on the UCB/optimism-under-uncertainty principle (Auer et al., 2002) for algorithm design. A key departure from Interaction-Grounded Learning (Xie et al., 2021) is that LLF does not require feedback to be a decodable proxy for reward; instead, feedback can encode information (e.g., optimal actions, correction directions) that is strictly more useful than reward itself, a regime where IGL offers no advantage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formal framework with provable no-regret guarantees for learning from language feedback, establishing when such learning is feasible and how its complexity compares to reward-based learning. The exponential speedup results (Table 1) show that richer feedback types can reduce learning complexity from exponential to constant, providing a principled justification for why constructive language feedback outperforms scalar rewards. Key open questions include whether the transfer eluder dimension can be tightened to a lower bound, whether current LLMs satisfy the hypothesis-verifier and unbiased-feedback assumptions in practice, and how the framework extends to multi-turn or stateful settings. Empirical validation is limited to simple combinatorial games (Battleship, Minesweeper), leaving open whether HELiX's advantages persist on more complex, open-ended tasks.
