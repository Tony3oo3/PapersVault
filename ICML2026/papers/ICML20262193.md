## Problem

Post-training pipelines combining SFT and RL dramatically improve LLM reasoning, but the mechanism behind this combined success remains unclear: what does each stage contribute, and what data should each be paired with? Prior empirical work established that RL improves out-of-distribution accuracy but left open how RL reshapes reasoning traces to enable compositional generalization, and whether the gains come from memorizing traces or discovering reusable structure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262193/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a natural-language problem descriptor P (specifying abstract task constraints) and generates an observable reasoning trace D as a token sequence. Training data consists of (P, D) pairs where D is a compositional trace of depth L constructed by nesting 24 atomic string-transformation skills and 10 routing mechanisms (function input structures that determine which intermediate outputs are forwarded). The theoretical output is the identification of latent atomic modules—skills (local operations) and routing mechanisms (information selection/composition)—along with their dependency structure, enabling recombination on unseen compositions.

## Method

The paper introduces a Hierarchical Latent Selection Model in which a problem descriptor induces a cascade of discrete latent selection variables that choose reusable atomic modules (skills and routing mechanisms) to generate the observed trace. Theoretically, Theorem 3.1 proves that the latent module hierarchy is identifiable from observed (P, D) pairs under mild conditions (faithfulness, restricted choice sets, neighborhood coverage, observable signatures), and Theorem 3.4 shows that identified modules recombine correctly on novel compositions whenever every induced local family has a training-witnessed compatible value. The key mechanistic insight is an asymmetric division of labor: SFT supplies compositional traces containing the atomic inventory but leaves modules statistically entangled (Proposition 3.2), while RL decomposes those traces by enriching identification-critical local events through reward-conditioned resampling (Proposition 3.3). Controlled experiments on synthetic string-transformation tasks with systematic data interventions confirm that compound-trace RL yields far larger OOD gains than atomic-only RL, that composability requires combinational exposure (not isolated atoms), and that the strongest generalization occurs when SFT covers the full atomic inventory via compositional traces while RL targets disjoint novel compositions.

## Results

On synthetic string-transformation tasks trained at depth L=3, RL adds a 43.7% absolute gain over SFT-only on atomic skills (L=1) and an average +40.3% on unseen compositions across depths L=2–8. Under a fixed SFT initialization and 300-step RL budget, compound-trace RL (L=3) achieves 42.6% accuracy on OOD L=4 traces versus only 14.8% for atomic-module RL (L=1), a +27.8-point compound advantage. The SFT-vs-RL gap is substantially larger on unseen compositions than on seen ones, confirming that RL's primary benefit is compositional generalization rather than in-distribution accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods; instead it contrasts internal training configurations to isolate causal mechanisms. SFT+RL with compound traces (42.6%) dramatically outperforms SFT+RL with atomic modules (14.8%) on OOD L=4 compositions, showing that RL must see module interfaces to decompose them. On seen compositions, RL provides only marginal gains, while on unseen compositions the SFT-vs-RL gap widens substantially (avg +40.3%). Among four SFT-RL data relationships tested, the disjoint setting (C_SFT ∩ C_RL = ∅) achieves the strongest unseen-composition generalization, while the nested setting (C_SFT ⊃ C_RL) is weakest. Withholding atomic skills from RL and re-injecting them only in isolation fails to restore depth generalization, whereas re-injecting compositional traces containing the held-out atoms recovers performance. On open-source Qwen3-4B models, the RL-tuned variant shows richer diversity in short skill n-grams (n=2,3) compared to the base model, corroborating the recombination signature observed in the controlled experiments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the synthetic string-transformation task framework from Yuan et al. (2025), which demonstrated that RL enables LLMs to compose atomic skills, and on latent hierarchical structure discovery methods from Huang et al. (2022) and Dong et al. (2023) that provide rank-faithfulness and identifiability conditions for discrete latent variables. It departs from prior empirical studies of RL generalization (which track benchmark improvements or isolate behavioral effects) by introducing a formal latent-variable model that separates atomic skills from routing mechanisms and proves when the latent hierarchy is identifiable from observed traces. The key conceptual departure is framing the SFT+RL pipeline as a division of labor: SFT supplies entangled compositional material while RL performs support expansion to decompose traces into reusable modules, a mechanism made precise through local-witness conditions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled theoretical account for why the SFT+RL post-training recipe works for reasoning, yielding concrete curriculum-design guidance: SFT should cover all atomic modules through compositional traces while RL should target novel compositions outside the SFT support. The finding that compound-trace RL substantially outperforms atomic-only RL (42.6% vs. 14.8% on OOD compositions) and that disjoint SFT/RL data dominates over overlapping data offers actionable data-design protocols. However, all controlled experiments are on synthetic string-transformation tasks, and the open-source model analysis (Qwen3-4B) is limited to n-gram diversity metrics; extending the identification-driven curriculum to math, code, and tool-use domains, and discovering which local interfaces to target adaptively, remain open questions.
