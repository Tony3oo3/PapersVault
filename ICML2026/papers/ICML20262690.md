## Problem

Existing results on asymptotic Bayes optimality under sparsity (ABOS) in multiple testing rely exclusively on complex heavy-tailed global-local shrinkage priors (horseshoe, horseshoe+, etc.) combined with purely posterior-based inference. It remained unexplored whether a simple light-tailed one-group normal prior with a single hyperparameter could attain the same ABOS property, and whether changing the inferential paradigm from posterior-only to evidence-based (using both prior and posterior) could unlock this possibility.

## Input / Output

The procedure takes n independent observations X_i ~ N(μ_i, 1) with known unit variance and a tuning parameter τ_n (or its empirical Bayes estimate τ̂_n = min{n√(log n), d₁n√(log n)/Σ1(|X_i|>√(d₂log n))}), and produces a simultaneous rejection decision for each of n tests H_0i: μ_i = 0 vs H_1i: μ_i ≠ 0. The decision is a threshold rule: reject H_0i when |X_i| > r(τ_n) = √((1+1/τ_n²)·log(1+τ_n²)) in the univariate case, or X_i^TΣ^{-1}X_i > k(1+τ_n)/τ_n·log(1+τ_n) in the k-dimensional multivariate case. The intermediate quantity is the closed-form relative belief ratio RB(μ_i=0|X_i,τ²) = √(1+τ²)·exp(−X_i²/(2(1+1/τ²))), which replaces the posterior probability used in all prior global-local shrinkage approaches.

## Method

The core contribution is showing that the relative belief ratio (posterior density divided by prior density at the null value) applied to a single-parameter conjugate normal prior N(0,τ²) yields a multiple testing rule that is exactly ABOS, provided τ_n scales as p_n^{-1}√(log(1/p_n)). The rejection criterion RB(μ_i=0|X_i,τ²) < 1 reduces to a simple threshold on |X_i|, and the proof that this threshold matches the Bayes oracle threshold asymptotically requires only elementary algebraic manipulations of the threshold expression, avoiding the intricate posterior concentration inequalities and slowly-varying function analysis needed for global-local priors. The same ABOS result extends to the multivariate normal means problem with τ_n of order p_n^{-2/k}·log(1/p_n), and to a non-conjugate uniform prior U[−τ_U, τ_U] with the same univariate scaling. An empirical Bayes estimator for τ_n is provided, and simulations confirm that the resulting EBRB_N and EBRB_U procedures uniformly outperform the empirical Bayes horseshoe (EBHS) in misclassification probability across all tested configurations.

## Results

The paper's primary contribution is theoretical: Theorems 5.7, 5.8, and 6.11 prove that the relative belief multiple testing procedure (using a single-hyperparameter normal prior N(0, τ²)) achieves the exact ABOS property (R^AsyRB / R_Opt^AsyBO → 1) in both univariate and multivariate normal means settings, matching the Bayes oracle's asymptotic Bayes risk of np_n(2Φ(√C)−1)(1+o(1)). In the simulation study (Section C, Figures 1–3), EBRB_N and EBRB_U produce misclassification probabilities nearly identical to the Bayes Oracle when p is small (e.g., p ≤ 0.1), and both uniformly outperform EBHS across all tested combinations of n ∈ {200, 1000, 10000}, σ² ∈ {3, 5, 7, 9}, and p ∈ {0.01, …, 0.5}. EBRB_U consistently performs slightly better than EBRB_N, and both are fully analytical and computationally efficient.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two proposed procedures (EBRB_N with a conjugate normal prior and EBRB_U with a uniform prior) against two baselines: the Bayes Oracle (BO, which uses the true unknown parameters p and σ) and EBHS (Empirical Bayes Horseshoe, the standard global-local shrinkage baseline from Datta & Ghosh 2013). Against the Bayes Oracle, both EBRB_N and EBRB_U achieve misclassification probabilities that are nearly indistinguishable from BO when p is small (the sparse regime), confirming the theoretical ABOS guarantee; as σ² increases from 3 to 9, the gap to BO shrinks further. Against EBHS, both EBRB_N and EBRB_U uniformly outperform the horseshoe-based procedure in every tested configuration (all n, σ², and p values), often by a substantial margin in misclassification probability. EBRB_U edges out EBRB_N slightly in all settings, consistent with its bounded-support prior providing marginally better finite-sample behavior. The key practical advantage is that EBRB_N is fully analytical (closed-form threshold) while EBHS requires MCMC sampling via the horseshoe package, making the relative belief approach both more accurate and far more computationally efficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the asymptotic Bayes optimality framework of Bogdan et al. (2011) and the relative belief inference paradigm of Evans (2015), extending the latter to the multivariate normal means setting following Qin & Ghosh (2025). It departs fundamentally from the dominant line of work (Datta & Ghosh 2013; Ghosh et al. 2016; Bhadra et al. 2017; Ghosh & Chakrabarti 2017) that achieves near-ABOS or ABOS through complex heavy-tailed global-local shrinkage priors with purely posterior-based inference. The key conceptual departure is showing that a single-parameter, light-tailed conjugate normal prior—previously considered insufficient for signal identification—attains exact ABOS when inference is conducted through the relative belief ratio (comparing prior and posterior densities) rather than through posterior shrinkage coefficients alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result challenges the prevailing assumption in Bayesian multiple testing that heavy-tailed global-local priors are necessary for asymptotic optimality, suggesting that the inference paradigm (evidential vs. posterior-only) may matter more than prior tail behavior. This opens a simpler, more analytically tractable route to ABOS with easier prior elicitation and computation. Open questions include: which broader class of priors (sub-Gaussian, symmetric heavy-tailed) is compatible with relative belief inference for ABOS; finite-sample guarantees; extension to dependent test statistics and unknown variance; and whether the evidential approach transfers to more complex model settings (graphical models, neural networks) where global-local priors currently dominate computationally.
