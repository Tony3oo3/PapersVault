## Problem

Efficient transfer learning of vision-language models suffers from the Base-New Trade-off: improving unseen-class accuracy degrades known-class performance. Existing work largely attributes this to overfitting on base classes, but the authors identify a subtler cause—pretraining-induced asymmetric confusion, where the model systematically mislabels class A as B far more than the reverse. For known classes, cross-entropy fine-tuning suppresses this bias, but for unseen classes the inherited directional bias persists and severely limits generalization.

## Input / Output

At inference, NeRP consumes the standard VLM forward pass (an RGB image encoded to a normalized embedding, compared against fine-tuned class text prototypes) plus three auxiliary signals: a neutral-reference image (pixel-wise mean of preprocessed training images, encoded through the fine-tuned image encoder), a neutral text prompt (a class-agnostic template like 'a photo of an object.' encoded through the zero-shot text encoder), and a precomputed undirected confusable-pairs graph G generated by Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct. The output is a potentially corrected top-1 class label: if the prior gate and evidence gate conditions are met for the current prediction, the logit of the best-scoring confusable neighbor is boosted above the original, effectively flipping the decision. No model parameters are modified and no additional forward passes beyond the standard one are required.

## Method

NeRP constructs a composite neutral prior gap Σ_{i,j}(D) for each confusable pair by summing text-side and image-side prior logits computed from the neutral anchors, then combines it with the observed sample logit margin m_{ij}(x) in a Bayes-style surrogate score. The theoretical core shows that under a low-rank deformation model of fine-tuning, the base-class subspace is reshaped while the zero-shot inter-class geometry among unseen classes remains stable, so the neutral prior gap and the expected logit margin share the same sign with high probability on novel classes (Proposition 3.5, Corollary 3.6). A decision rule then checks two gates: if the prior strongly favors the current top-1 class (Σ_{i,j} ≥ τ) while the observed evidence is weak (m_{ij}(x) ≤ δ), the prediction is declared prior-dominated and a local flip is performed to the highest-scoring confusable neighbor. A global intercept β̂ fitted on base-class pairs absorbs domain-wide constant bias, and a residualized variant of the prior (subtracting current-anchor projections) reduces across-pair variance on semantically diverse datasets. The entire procedure is inference-time, parameter-free, and plugs into any existing prompt-tuning or adapter baseline without retraining.

## Results

NeRP is a plug-and-play inference-time correction that improves novel-class accuracy while leaving base-class accuracy unchanged (denoted ●). Across four prompt-tuning baselines on 11 base-to-novel benchmarks, NeRP yields consistent novel-class gains: CoCoOp improves from 71.69% to 72.99% (HM 75.83→76.67), PromptSRC from 76.10% to 76.87% (HM 79.97→80.40), MMA from 76.80% to 77.59% (HM 79.87→80.30), and MMRL from 77.16% to 78.04% (HM 81.20→81.68). Gains are largest on EuroSAT (up to +5.1% for CoCoOp) and moderate on ImageNet, Flowers102, and DTD (+0.5–2%). In cross-dataset and domain-generalization settings, NeRP adds ~1% average improvement over 10 target datasets with negligible overhead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NeRP is not a standalone method but a plug-and-play correction layer applied on top of four stylistically distinct prompt-tuning baselines: CoCoOp (CVPR'22), PromptSRC (ICCV'23), MMA (CVPR'24), and MMRL (CVPR'25). In every case, NeRP strictly improves novel-class accuracy while preserving base-class accuracy exactly (no retraining, no parameter modification). The largest absolute gains appear on EuroSAT (CoCoOp: 60.04→67.30, +7.26; PromptSRC: 73.90→78.53, +4.63; MMA: 82.34→85.27, +2.93; MMRL: 80.17→85.17, +5.00), while ImageNet and Flowers102 show more modest but consistent +0.5–1.2% improvements. In the cross-dataset setting (Table 2), NeRP adds ~1% average over 10 target datasets; in domain generalization (Table 3, ImageNet-V2/Sketch/A/R), gains of 1–3.5% are observed per dataset. The paper also references CoOp, MaPLe, CoPrompt, and SkipT as additional baselines in Table 1, but NeRP is only applied to the four primary methods; against these reference baselines, the NeRP-augmented methods (e.g., MMRL+NeRP HM 81.68) remain competitive with or exceed the strongest standalone methods (SkipT HM 81.11, CoPrompt HM 80.48).

## Relation to Prior Work

NeRP sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) efficient prompt/adapter tuning for VLMs (CoOp, CoCoOp, PromptSRC, MMA, MMRL), which the paper treats as a black-box baseline it plugs into at inference time, and (2) the growing literature on biases in pretrained VLMs (social bias, spurious correlations, shape/texture reliance). The key conceptual departure is from the dominant overfitting-centric explanation of the Base-New Trade-off: the authors argue that pretraining-induced asymmetric confusion—a directional bias in inter-class geometry—persists on unseen classes even when overfitting is controlled, and that this, not overfitting, is the primary driver of novel-class degradation. Rather than modifying training objectives or constraining prompt updates, NeRP repurposes neutral-reference anchors (mean image, class-agnostic text) as a diagnostic 'ruler' along pretrained inter-class directions, turning a bias-detection idea from the social-bias literature into a concrete inference-time correction rule.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The reframing of BNT as a bias-correction problem rather than an overfitting problem opens a new axis of intervention: if the root cause is directional pretraining bias, then inference-time corrections that detect and flip prior-dominated predictions are a natural and cheap remedy, and the plug-and-play design means any existing prompt-tuning pipeline benefits without retraining. The theoretical guarantees (sign consistency, Chebyshev-bounded false-flip probability) give practitioners confidence that the correction is conservative. Limitations include dependence on an LLM-generated confusable-pair graph (Qwen2.5-72B), a calibration step that requires pseudo-novel splits, and modest absolute gains (typically 0.5–2%, with >5% only on EuroSAT). Open questions include whether the approach extends to open-vocabulary or compositional settings where the closed label set assumption breaks, and whether the neutral-reference construction generalizes beyond the cosine-similarity scoring paradigm.
