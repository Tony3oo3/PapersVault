## Problem

In Dyna-style model-based RL, dynamics models are trained on data from a mixture of historical policies but queried under the continually updated current policy, creating a mismatch where global prediction error decreases while policy-relevant local error remains large. This mismatch causes oscillatory policy updates and slow convergence, and existing remedies like PDML require explicit policy-distance estimation that adds computational overhead. The paper asks whether a simpler, error-based signal can implicitly target policy-relevant model weaknesses without such bookkeeping.

## Input / Output

PMER consumes standard environment transitions (s, a, r, s') and the current dynamics model's k_p-step predictions to compute a per-transition priority score (L2 squared prediction error on normalized state-difference and reward targets). It maintains a fixed-capacity Prioritized Model Experience Buffer of (transition, priority) pairs and outputs an augmented training batch in which a fraction ρ of samples are drawn from this buffer via rank-based β-sampling, while the remaining (1−ρ) fraction is sampled uniformly from the main replay buffer. The priority score controls only replay sampling and does not reweight the model training loss itself.

## Method

PMER augments each dynamics-model training step by mixing uniform samples with high-error transitions drawn from a dedicated prioritized buffer, using a rank-based β-sampling scheme (p_r ∝ (K−r+1)^β) rather than the probability-based sampling of classical PER. Priority scores are the squared L2 prediction error of the Any-step Dynamics Model on k_p-step transitions, and all stored priorities decay exponentially (λ=0.995) each step to prevent stale samples from dominating. The top-ρ fraction of the current batch by priority is inserted into the buffer, evicting the lowest-priority entries when capacity K is exceeded. The key insight is that large dynamics prediction error correlates with policy-induced distribution shift, so error-based replay implicitly reweights the historical policy mixture toward the current policy without any explicit policy-distance computation. Theoretically, the paper derives a finite-horizon performance gap bound decomposing the model–environment return difference into global model error, a policy-shift term amplified by a model-capability coefficient (involving the Dobrushin/consistency coefficient L_s^(k)), and a historical-mixture term, showing that reducing global error alone is insufficient and motivating the error-aware replay strategy.

## Results

On Hopper, PMER+ADMPO surpasses a return of 3500 within approximately 30k environment steps, while most competing methods only approach this level around 50k steps. On Humanoid, PMER converges to a return near 6000 by ~100k steps, compared to ~140k steps for the ADMPO backbone and ~150k steps for PDML-MBPO. Across all five MuJoCo tasks (HalfCheetah, Hopper, Walker2d, Ant, Humanoid), PMER consistently achieves the fastest or near-fastest convergence with only 8–12% additional computational overhead. The method also reduces mid-training return oscillations, as shown by sparse validation rollouts stabilizing ~50k steps earlier than the baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PMER+ADMPO clearly outperforms its backbone ADMPO on both Hopper (30k vs. ~50k steps to reach return 3500) and Humanoid (100k vs. 140k steps to reach ~6000), demonstrating that the gains come from the replay mechanism rather than the model architecture. Against PDML-MBPO, the primary policy-adaptive baseline, PMER converges ~50k steps faster on Humanoid (100k vs. 150k) while avoiding PDML's explicit policy-distance estimation overhead. On the classical MBPO backbone, PMER+MBPO reaches maximum return with approximately 2× fewer environment interactions than vanilla MBPO on both InvertedPendulum and Hopper (Appendix F). Against the remaining model-based baselines (AMPO, DDPPO, MACURA) and the model-free SAC baseline, PMER is competitive or superior in convergence speed across all five MuJoCo evaluation tasks, though the paper does not report a single final-return table to quantify exact margins on every task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Dyna-style MBRL frameworks (MBPO, ADMPO) by introducing a data-level replay mechanism for dynamics model training, drawing on the Prioritized Experience Replay (PER) paradigm but repurposing it for model learning rather than value learning. It departs from the closest prior work, PDML (Wang et al., 2023), which addresses the same policy-mixture problem through explicit policy distance estimation and sample reweighting; PMER instead uses dynamics model prediction error as an implicit proxy for policy-induced distribution shift, eliminating the need for policy storage and distance computation. The theoretical analysis builds on the global/local error decomposition from Janner et al. (2019) and the policy-mixture formalism from Wang et al. (2023), but adds a finite-horizon performance gap bound that explicitly separates model capability, policy shift sensitivity, and mixture effects.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PMER offers a lightweight, drop-in stabilization mechanism for MBRL that requires no architectural changes, no explicit policy tracking, and only ~8-12% computational overhead, making it broadly applicable to existing Dyna-style pipelines. The theoretical decomposition clarifies why minimizing global model error alone is insufficient and identifies the local error–policy shift interaction as the key instability driver. Limitations include reliance on prediction error as a proxy (potentially unreliable in highly stochastic environments where intrinsic randomness dominates), validation restricted to MuJoCo continuous control, and the open question of whether error-based prioritization transfers to latent-space or representation-based world models. Extending PMER to discrete, high-dimensional, and real-world domains remains an important direction.
