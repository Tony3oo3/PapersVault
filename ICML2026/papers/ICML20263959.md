## Problem

Whether auto-regressive models trained on sequential data naturally learn latent state representations remains an open theoretical question. For partially observed linear dynamical systems, prior work relied on shallow linear models combined with classical factorization (e.g., Ho-Kalman) or special-purpose regularization to extract latent states, rather than end-to-end non-convex training. This paper asks whether a simple two-layer linear auto-regressive model trained by empirical risk minimization can recover Kalman filter state estimates without any explicit knowledge of the underlying dynamics.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a covariate vector z_t ∈ R^{(m+p)L} formed by concatenating L past outputs y_{t-1},…,y_{t-L} and L past inputs u_{t-1},…,u_{t-L} from a single trajectory of a partially observed linear system. It produces H-step-ahead output predictions y_{t:t+H-1} ∈ R^{mH} as its explicit output, while the latent state estimate is implicitly encoded in the hidden layer activation G_1 z_t ∈ R^h. The architecture search over hidden dimension h is performed jointly with weight optimization, and the model is trained on a single trajectory of length T with no access to the system matrices A, B, C or noise covariances.

## Method

The paper trains a two-layer linear auto-regressive model f(z) = G_2 G_1 z by minimizing squared prediction loss over a single input-output trajectory, with weights constrained by a Frobenius norm bound (equivalent to weight decay in practice). The key theoretical insight is threefold: (1) the steady-state Kalman filter predictor form can be approximated by a linear function of L past inputs and outputs with truncation error decaying as ρ^L where ρ is the filter's spectral radius; (2) despite non-convexity, the optimization landscape is benign—every stationary point is either a strict saddle or a global minimum; and (3) finite-sample guarantees on prediction and parameter error imply that the hidden layer activation G_1 z_t coincides, up to a similarity transform, with the Kalman filter state estimate x_t. The architecture search naturally selects hidden dimension h = n (the true state dimension), and the approach requires no explicit system identification or factorization step beyond standard gradient-based training.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 4) establishes that the learned hidden-layer activation S·Ĝ₁z̄_t approximates the Kalman filter state estimate x̂_t with error Õ(1/√T) under a similarity transform S. Empirically, on a random 4-dimensional system, the learned activations achieve mean R² = 0.999 (H=1) and 0.998 (H=5) against the Kalman filter. On ControlGym benchmarks, the underwater vehicle (UMV, n=8) achieves mean R² = 0.995/0.994 and the aircraft (AC6, n=10) achieves mean R² = 0.979/0.980 for H=1/5 respectively. Architecture search confirms that the optimal hidden dimension h=4 matches the true state dimension n=4, with training loss 0.618±0.041 versus 309.6 for h=1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not run direct head-to-head comparisons against competing learning methods (e.g., Ho-Kalman factorization, nuclear norm regularization, or pre-filtering approaches). Instead, the Kalman filter itself serves as the ground-truth reference: the learned activations are compared against the optimal filter's state estimates via R² alignment after fitting a similarity transform. The architecture search (Table 1) implicitly validates the theory by showing that h=n=4 minimizes training loss (0.618) while h=1 is severely under-parameterized (loss 309.6), and h>4 offers no improvement. The sample-complexity experiments (Figure 3) confirm the predicted Õ(1/√T) decay of the latent recovery error as training trajectory length increases. The weakest coordinate (s10 of AC6, R²≈0.82–0.84) suggests that some state directions are harder to recover, but the overall alignment remains strong across all three systems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant 'estimate-then-decompose' paradigm in system identification (Oymak & Ozay, 2019; Tsiamis & Pappas, 2019), where one first learns a linear auto-regressive model and then applies Ho-Kalman factorization to extract state-space parameters. Instead, it shows that a two-layer linear auto-regressive model trained end-to-end via empirical risk minimization naturally produces Kalman-filter-like state estimates in its hidden layer, with no explicit factorization step. The theoretical machinery draws on non-convex optimization geometry for shallow linear networks (Zhu et al., 2020) for the benign landscape result, and on statistical tools from Ziemann et al. (2022, 2023) for characterizing prediction and parameter errors at global optima of non-convex objectives. The robustness-of-factorization argument connecting parameter error to latent state recovery is adapted from Tu et al. (2016).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first finite-sample theoretical guarantee that a standard deep-learning training recipe (ERM on a two-layer linear network with weight decay) recovers the optimal linear filter for partially observed linear systems, bridging a gap between control theory and representation learning. It offers a concrete, minimal example of how latent structure can emerge from end-to-end training without explicit architectural inductive bias toward the state space. Key limitations include the restriction to linear systems with Gaussian noise, two-layer depth, and a single trajectory; the authors identify non-Gaussian noise (which induces correlated filter errors), deeper/recurrent architectures, and in-context learning across multiple systems as natural extensions. The result is a stepping stone toward understanding why large foundation models appear to learn world representations, though the jump from a 4-dimensional linear system to LLMs is substantial.
