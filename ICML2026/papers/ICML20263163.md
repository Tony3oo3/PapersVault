## Problem

Distributed bilevel optimization over time-varying directed graphs remains largely unexplored due to compounded challenges: consensus bias from dynamic unbalanced communication and the nested optimization structure. Existing Push-Pull/AB methods over time-varying directed graphs have convergence analyses restricted to strongly convex objectives, and prior distributed bilevel algorithms rely on static (typically undirected) topologies or second-order information. This paper addresses the gap by proposing the first fully first-order algorithm for this setting and, as a byproduct, resolves the open question of Push-Pull convergence in the nonconvex single-level case over time-varying directed graphs.

## Input / Output

Each of n agents holds local functions f_i(x,y) (upper-level, possibly nonconvex) and g_i(x,y) (lower-level, strongly convex in y), along with a sequence of time-varying directed graphs and compatible row-stochastic A^k / column-stochastic B^k mixing matrices. The algorithm requires only first-order gradient oracles for f_i and g_i—no Hessian-vector products or Hessian inverses—and outputs a stationary solution x* with lower-level y*(x*), achieving a hypergradient norm of O((ab)^{-n} K^{-2/3}) with step sizes O(K^{-1/3}) and penalty parameter λ = O(K^{1/3}).

## Method

FAB reformulates the bilevel problem via a value-function penalty into a min-max problem min_{x,y} max_z L(x,y,z) = F(x,y) + λ(G(x,y) − G(x,z)), which decomposes into local penalty functions L_i, making it amenable to decentralized optimization. Each iteration consists of three steps: (1) a 'pull' step where agents mix decision variables (x_i, y_i, z_i) using the row-stochastic matrix A^k and take gradient descent/ascent steps; (2) local gradient evaluation of L_i at the updated iterates; and (3) a 'push' step where tracking variables are updated using the column-stochastic matrix B^k with a gradient-tracking correction (d_i^{k+1} − d_i^k). The key insight is that the penalty reformulation with a sufficiently large λ makes the min-max objective strongly convex in y and strongly concave in z, while the AB/Push-Pull mechanism handles the directed, time-varying communication without requiring doubly stochastic weights. A simplified variant of the analysis yields an O((ab)^{-n} K^{-1}) rate for standard Push-Pull in the nonconvex single-level setting, matching centralized gradient descent.

## Results

FAB achieves a convergence rate of O(K^{-2/3}) for the hypergradient norm in the nonconvex upper-level / strongly convex lower-level bilevel setting, and a simplified variant yields O((ab)^{-n} K^{-1}) for single-level Push-Pull in the nonconvex setting, resolving an open question. On Fashion-MNIST data hyper-cleaning (cr=0.4, ρ=0.5, ν=0.5), FAB reaches 82%+ test accuracy with ~85.4 GB cumulative communication, compared to 90.7–115.8 GB for the double-loop baselines (DL-Push-SGD, DL-Push-SAGA, DL-Push-ASGD). At n=10 agents, FAB attains 83.99% test accuracy by epoch 150 versus 66.33% for Push-Pull; at n=100, the gap widens to 74.30% vs. 39.30%. On BERT/IMDB fine-tuning, FAB maintains superior accuracy and robustness under label corruption and data heterogeneity, even though the lower-level objective is nonconvex in practice.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FAB consistently outperforms all seven single-level baselines across all three experimental domains (hyperparameter tuning, RL policy evaluation, and data hyper-cleaning). Against SGP/PushSGD (Nedić & Olshevsky, 2015; Assran et al., 2019) and Push-DIGing (Nedić et al., 2017), FAB achieves substantially higher test accuracy under label corruption (e.g., cr=0.4 on MNIST), with the gap widening as corruption increases. Against the AB/Push-Pull baselines (Saadatniaki et al., 2020; Nedić et al., 2025), FAB's advantage stems from its bilevel structure: even when Push-Pull is given the optimal fixed λ=0.15 (the value FAB learns), it still underperforms FAB, confirming the benefit of adaptive hyperparameter tuning. PushSAGA (Qureshi et al., 2022) and Push-ASGD (Chen et al., 2025c) show competitive iteration-level convergence in the RL task but require significantly more cumulative communication to reach the same accuracy thresholds (e.g., 115.8 GB vs. 85.4 GB for 82% accuracy on Fashion-MNIST). In the ablation comparing integration variants, Static_FAB (ignoring topology changes) diverges entirely, PushSum_FAB is slower and uses more memory than FAB, and PushPull-SOBA is competitive only at lower accuracy thresholds while tracking additional bilevel derivative information. FAB's per-iteration communication cost is approximately 2× that of Push-Pull due to the extra weighting variable, but its faster convergence more than compensates, yielding lower total communication at stricter accuracy targets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) AB/Push-Pull communication for directed graphs (Xin & Khan, 2018; Pu et al., 2021), which prior work extended to time-varying topologies but only under strong convexity (Saadatniya et al., 2020; Nedić et al., 2025); (2) value-function-based penalty methods for bilevel optimization that avoid Hessian computations (Kwon et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025), previously applied only in centralized or static-network settings; and (3) distributed bilevel optimization on static undirected networks (Yang et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025b). FAB is the first to integrate all three, and as a byproduct its analysis framework resolves the previously open question of Push-Pull convergence in the nonconvex single-level setting over time-varying directed graphs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first provably convergent first-order algorithm for distributed bilevel optimization over time-varying directed graphs, a setting motivated by real-world constraints like satellite networks and UAV swarms where symmetric communication is infeasible. The resolution of the Push-Pull nonconvex convergence question is a standalone theoretical contribution that unblocks further work in decentralized optimization. Key limitations include the exponential (ab)^{-n} dependence on network size in the convergence rate and the requirement of lower-level strong convexity, both of which the authors acknowledge as open problems. Extending to nonconvex lower levels, polynomial network-size bounds, and stochastic settings are natural next steps.
