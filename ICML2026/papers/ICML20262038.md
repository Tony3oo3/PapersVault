## Problem

Standard classification metrics treat all errors as equally costly, but in practice mistakes on clear-cut cases (e.g., a 10-0 annotator consensus) are far more consequential than errors on ambiguous ones. Prior cost-sensitive learning assumes costs are externally specified (fraud amounts, medical expenditures), leaving no framework for deriving per-example costs when importance is subjective and must be extracted from annotation patterns. This paper asks: can we derive instance-level misclassification costs from disagreement or confidence signals, and do cost-aware training methods actually improve cost-weighted performance?

## Input / Output

The framework takes standard binary-classification inputs (text, RGB images, or tabular features) augmented with a per-example signed margin Δ_i derived from one of three sources: Laplace-smoothed log-odds of multi-annotator votes, distance from a continuous decision threshold (e.g., SBP − 130 mmHg), or a single annotator's confidence rating minus the scale midpoint. The output is a binary class prediction plus the Normalized Excess Cost (NEC) score, which is scale-invariant and directly comparable to error rate (NEC = error rate when all |Δ_i| = 1). A random classifier yields NEC = 0.5, matching the 0.5 error-rate baseline.

## Method

The core contribution is the Normalized Excess Cost (NEC), defined as the sum of |Δ_i| over misclassified examples divided by the total sum of |Δ_i|, which is exactly the normalized regret of a binary contextual bandit policy. This connects classification evaluation to decision theory: high |Δ| examples carry large reward gaps where correct classification matters, while low |Δ| examples represent near-indifference. The paper then evaluates seven cost-aware training strategies—cost-weighted cross-entropy, probability-proportional upsampling, top-k% filtering, Δ-regression, calibrated reweighting, Costing, and focal loss—against standard cross-entropy on frozen-embedding and fine-tuned models across text, image, and tabular benchmarks. The key empirical finding is that NEC is often substantially lower than error rate (e.g., 1.8% NEC vs. 5.3% error on Jigsaw), revealing that most mistakes concentrate on ambiguous low-cost examples, yet no cost-aware training method consistently improves NEC across datasets.

## Results

The headline finding is that NEC is substantially lower than standard error rate across all datasets, revealing that most misclassifications concentrate on ambiguous, low-cost examples. On Jigsaw (TF-IDF), the model achieves 5.34% error rate but only 1.76% NEC (a 3× gap); on the Synthetic control the gap reaches 9× (4.55% error vs. 0.50% NEC). Fine-tuning narrows absolute errors (e.g., Jigsaw RoBERTa-FT: 4.73% error, 1.78% NEC; Turkey ResNet-FT: 4.75% error, 2.27% NEC) while preserving the per-dataset NEC/error ratio. No cost-aware training method (Δ-regression, cost-weighted CE, upsampling, top-k filtering, focal loss, Elkan thresholding, or Zadrozny costing) consistently improves NEC across datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three families of cost-aware baselines against standard cross-entropy: (1) Calibrated Elkan-style thresholding [Elkan, IJCAI 2001], (2) Costing / cost-proportionate weighting [Zadrozny et al., ICDM 2003], and (3) Focal loss [Lin et al., ICCV 2017]. None of these methods consistently improves NEC across the five datasets. Δ-regression (the paper's own regression objective) shows the clearest gain on the Synthetic dataset (0.50% → 0.26% NEC) but is mixed on real data. Focal loss yields a small but statistically significant NEC improvement on Jigsaw at γ=3 (1.775% → 1.738%, 95% CI [−0.055, −0.018] pp) but is indistinguishable from its cross-entropy control on Turkey and iNaturalist. Calibrated |Δ|-reweighting, Costing, and Calibrated Elkan thresholding all fail to produce consistent cross-dataset NEC gains. The overall conclusion is that instance-level costs substantially change the evaluation landscape (NEC ≪ error rate) even though the tested training methods have dataset-dependent, often negligible, effects on NEC.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate literatures: cost-sensitive classification (Elkan, 2001; Zadrozny et al., 2003), which assumes externally specified, objectively quantifiable costs, and the human label variation / ambiguity literature (Plank, 2014; Pavlick & Kwiatkowski, 2019; Nie et al., 2020), which treats annotator disagreement as meaningful signal but uses it for soft-label training or calibration rather than cost-based evaluation. The key departure is deriving per-example misclassification costs from annotator vote margins, threshold distances, or confidence ratings—settings where no objective cost ledger exists—and framing the resulting metric (NEC) as normalized regret in a contextual bandit. Unlike Kurniawan et al. (2025), who propose soft accuracy metrics measuring distributional overlap, this paper uses disagreement magnitude as a scalar cost that weights individual errors, preserving a decision-theoretic interpretation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that NEC is often 2–9× lower than error rate across text, image, and tabular benchmarks is practically significant: a system reported at 5% error may only incur 1.8% of the maximum possible cost, meaning standard accuracy substantially overstates real-world harm in tasks with many ambiguous examples. The negative result—that cost-weighted training, focal loss, upsampling, and post-hoc thresholding do not consistently improve NEC—tempers enthusiasm for cost-aware training and suggests the benefit depends on whether features encode cost-relevant information, an open question the paper motivates but does not resolve. Limitations include reliance on LLM-generated confidence for iNaturalist (weak correlation with human-derived costs on Jigsaw) and the assumption that annotator consensus proxies true importance, which may fail in domains where unanimous agreement reflects systematic bias.
