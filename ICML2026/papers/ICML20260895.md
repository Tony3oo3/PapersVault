## Problem

Existing self-evaluation methods for AI-generated annotations (e.g., LLM-as-judge) lack reliable reference-based calibration, making it difficult to distinguish correct from incorrect outputs. The paper asks two fundamental questions: when do semantically similar samples actually help detect annotation errors, and why do they help? No prior work provides a theoretical characterization of the conditions under which neighbor-based references improve error detection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260895/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an input instance x (a question for LLM tasks, or an image/image-question pair for VLM tasks), its generated annotation â, and a reference pool P of (x', â') pairs with pre-computed embeddings. For each target, k nearest neighbors are retrieved from P (excluding the target itself) and the judge is invoked k times, once per neighbor. The output is a real-valued quality score T = (1/k)ΣS_j per annotation, where each S_j is an integer in {0,…,L}; higher T indicates higher perceived correctness. The same model serves as both generator and judge (self-judgment), and the reference pool can coincide with the target set (self-referencing), requiring no external labeled data.

## Method

SAGE (Semantic-Anchored JudGmEnt) retrieves k semantically similar samples via kNN on input embeddings and inserts each as a reference in the judge's prompt, then averages the k resulting quality scores. The theoretical core is a closed-form AUROC expression derived from an additive score decomposition S = μ_y + δ_y·U + ε, where U is a binary usability indicator (reference is both semantically matched and correct). The AUROC decomposes into three multiplicative factors: intrinsic separability Δμ, reference-induced mean shift δ₁u₁ − δ₀u₀ (usable references raise scores for correct annotations and lower them for incorrect ones), and noise reduction through k-trial averaging with effective sample size k_eff = k/(1+(k−1)ρ). This decomposition precisely characterizes when semantic neighbors help (high usability rates u_y, opposite-sign δ terms) and why (differential score shifts widen the correct-incorrect gap), and predicts failure modes such as correlated same-model errors or poor retrieval quality.

## Results

SAGE achieves the best error-detection AUROC in 17 out of 24 model–dataset configurations. The largest gains appear on CIFAR-10 image classification (up to 94.35% AUROC for Qwen3-VL, +9.8 pp over Direct) and HaluEval hallucination detection (up to 70.47% for LLaMA-3.1, +3.8 pp over Direct). On AG News text classification, SAGE consistently outperforms Direct by +2.6 to +3.5 pp across all three LLMs, while Random scoring drops to near-chance (~54%), confirming that semantic relevance drives the improvement. On Flickr30k VLM captioning, SAGE wins for all three VLMs (+1.4 to +2.1 pp over Direct).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the external baseline DS2 (Pang et al., 2025b), which uses the same kNN retrieval but bypasses the judge entirely, SAGE achieves 72.61% AUROC versus DS2's 67.10% on TruthfulQA (Qwen3-8B), a +5.51 pp margin that isolates the judge's contribution to reference-based calibration. Internally, SAGE outperforms the Direct (no-reference) baseline in 17/24 configurations and the Random-reference baseline in 14/24, with the gap widening on semantically clustered tasks (HaluEval, AG News, CIFAR-10) and narrowing or reversing on diverse multi-domain tasks (MMLU) or near-ceiling accuracy settings (COCO with Qwen3-VL at 85% annotation accuracy). A key ablation shows that Self-consistency (judge) at 68.58% is narrowly beaten by Random at 69.02%, confirming that external reference context—not merely repeated scoring—drives the improvement. Cross-model evaluation (LLaMA-3.1 generator with Qwen3-8B judge) yields 71.32% SAGE AUROC, only 1.29 pp below same-model SAGE (72.61%), indicating the method generalizes beyond self-judgment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-judge paradigm by adding reference-based calibration via kNN-retrieved semantic neighbors, building on the noisy-label detection literature (Dawid-Skene, Confident Learning, WANN) that established semantic similarity as an error signal for human-annotated data. It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) it targets AI-generated annotations with systematic, correlated errors rather than independent human annotation noise, and (2) it provides a closed-form AUROC decomposition that characterizes *when* and *why* semantic references help, a theoretical gap left open by empirical methods like WANN. The paper also explicitly distinguishes its comparison-based judging mechanism from in-context learning, showing that reference correctness (not quantity) drives performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The closed-form AUROC decomposition gives practitioners a principled framework for deciding when reference-based calibration (SAGE) is preferable to direct scoring or random references, reducing the need for ad-hoc hyperparameter tuning. Key limitations include the ~k× computational overhead of multiple judge calls, the dependence on model-based ground truth for generation tasks, and the approximate independence assumption that breaks down under same-model error correlation. Open questions include adaptive reference pool selection, handling strongly correlated systematic errors, and whether cross-model references can further reduce the self-preference bias inherent in self-judgment.
