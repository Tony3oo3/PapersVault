## Problem

Existing offline meta-RL methods (Prompt-DT, Meta-DT, M3DT) rely on implicit or token-level routing signals that treat all seen tasks uniformly, failing to explicitly model inter-task relationships. When generalizing to unseen tasks, early trajectory fragments may be noisy or insufficient to infer task identity, leading to incorrect expert selection and degraded performance under distributional shift.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266047/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a prompt trajectory segment (K* steps of return-to-go, state, action), the current state observation, return-to-go values over a context window, and three precomputed task-level signals: a semantic embedding from frozen RoBERTa on task metadata text, a behavioral trajectory vector from SIP clustering of offline state observations, and a dynamics embedding from a frozen GRU encoder over an H-step (s,a,r) window. The output is a single action vector, computed as a softmax-weighted sum of E expert policy predictions, where the weights are determined by the Task-Guided Router.

## Method

TGR builds on Prompt-DT with a MoE backbone and introduces three complementary task representation nodes—semantic (RoBERTa on metadata), behavioral trajectory (SIP clustering of state pools into K=E structural prototypes), and latent dynamics (frozen RNN over H-step context)—that are injected into the Prompt-DT input sequence. The routing mechanism decomposes each expert's score into a content term (dot product of a query built from the hidden state plus all three task representations with a learnable expert key) and a global term (dot product of a query built solely from task-level embeddings with a separate learnable key), so that expert selection integrates local context-aware evidence with stable task-level priors. Training follows a staged pipeline: pretrain and freeze the dynamics encoder, compute semantic and trajectory representations, train the augmented Prompt-DT backbone, partition tasks into expert groups via gradient-conflict and semantic clustering, train expert policies, and finally train the router with all other components fixed.

## Results

On the Expert-quality offline datasets, TGR achieves an average normalized score of 87.31±1.61 across five continuous-control environments (Point-Robot, Cheetah-Vel, Ant-Dir, Hopper-Param, Walker-Param), outperforming the strongest baseline Meta-DT (81.44±1.62) by ~5.9 points. Under Medium-quality data, TGR scores 67.01±1.98 vs. Meta-DT's 64.89±0.95, and under Mixed data, 77.03±1.46 vs. 74.60±1.26. In the matched-parameter-scale comparison (~33M params), TGR achieves -92.64 on Cheetah-Vel and 370.52 on Hopper-Param, surpassing Meta-DT(Large) (-97.46 / 350.47) and M3DT (-107.19 / 345.49).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TGR outperforms all six baselines across all three dataset quality levels (Medium, Mixed, Expert) on the average normalized score. Against the strongest DT-based baseline Meta-DT, TGR gains +5.9 (Expert), +2.4 (Mixed), and +2.1 (Medium) avg norm score points, with the largest per-environment margin on Cheetah-Vel (-35.77 vs. -52.42 under Expert data, a ~16.6-point improvement). Against the MoE-based M3DT, TGR leads by a substantial margin (+26.3 avg norm score under Expert data), confirming that structured task-guided routing surpasses token-level MoE routing. TD-based baselines CORRO and CSRO trail significantly (53.90 and 40.23 avg norm score under Expert data), highlighting the advantage of sequence-modeling backbones for few-shot offline meta-RL. Under matched parameter budgets (~33M), TGR still beats the scaled-up Meta-DT(Large) by ~5 points on Cheetah-Vel and ~20 points on Hopper-Param, ruling out capacity as the source of gains. In the 160-task multi-task RL setting, TGR (79.90) narrowly edges out M3DT (78.21), indicating the routing design also benefits in-distribution multi-task learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two complementary lines: the Prompt-DT sequence-modeling backbone for offline meta-RL (Xu et al., 2022) and the M3DT mixture-of-experts Decision Transformer for multi-task RL (Kong et al., 2025). The key conceptual departure is that prior MoE-RL methods (including M3DT) route experts based on token-level or trajectory-fragment-level hidden states, treating tasks as independent instances. TGR instead introduces explicit task-level structure—semantic descriptors via RoBERTa, behavioral prototypes via Structure Information Partitioning clustering, and latent dynamics via a pretrained RNN encoder—and uses these multi-view task representations to compute a global routing score that complements the standard content-based score, thereby grounding expert selection in seen-unseen task similarity rather than local context alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap in offline meta-RL: existing methods lack an explicit mechanism to relate unseen test tasks to the seen tasks from which experts were specialized, making knowledge transfer under distributional shift largely implicit. The structured routing framework is a reasonable inductive bias, and the ablations confirm that each of the three task priors contributes. However, the empirical gains over Meta-DT are modest (roughly 2–6 points in average normalized score), the evaluation is confined to low-dimensional continuous-control MuJoCo and Point-Robot benchmarks, and the staged four-phase training pipeline (dynamics pretraining → semantic encoding → backbone training → expert + router training) adds considerable implementation complexity. Open questions include whether the approach extends to image-based observations, more heterogeneous task distributions, and whether the multi-view priors can be learned end-to-end rather than through a fixed staged procedure.
