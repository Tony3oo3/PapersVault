## Problem

Chain-of-thought reasoning traces are typically treated as linear sequences, making it difficult to identify cross-step dependencies and unsupported intermediate claims that underlie hallucinations. Sampling-based detectors fail in 'confidently wrong' regimes where multiple samples agree on an incorrect answer, and whole-trace LLM judges suffer from position bias and difficulty localizing a single erroneous step within long contexts. The paper asks whether making step-to-support relations explicit via a dependency graph enables more reliable, locally grounded hallucination detection.

## Input / Output

The method takes a question/problem and a single structured reasoning trace elicited via a fixed prompt template that requires the model to output atomic Conditions, numbered Steps, and a Source field citing the minimal supporting subset of prior statements for each step. These citations are parsed deterministically into a directed acyclic dependency graph. The output is an instance-level FlowScore in [0,1] (higher indicates greater hallucination risk), along with per-step reliability scores derived from a ternary verifier label {0, 0.5, 1} and the parsed DAG itself, which can be inspected for localizing fragile premises.

## Method

The paper introduces a Structural Reasoning Model (SRM) that formalizes a local Markov property over reasoning steps and operationalizes it through structured prompting to produce an explicit dependency DAG over conditions and intermediate claims. Each step is then verified in isolation against only its cited parent statements (an evidence-only protocol) using an LLM judge, yielding a step-level reliability score q̂_t. A mass-flow aggregation rule propagates unit mass forward from condition nodes through the DAG, where each step node absorbs a fraction (1−q_v) of its incoming mass and routes the surviving fraction to its children; the total absorbed mass constitutes the FlowScore. Under a random-chain erasure-gate abstraction, the paper proves (Theorem 4.2) that this flow score equals the probability that task-relevant content is erased along a randomly sampled dependency-respecting support chain, and equals the exact fraction of mutual information lost before reaching the terminal answer, providing an information-theoretic justification for the aggregation. A weighted ℓ₁-Lipschitz corollary further shows that steps with higher visitation probability (i.e., shared supports visited by many downstream paths) exert proportionally greater influence on the final score.

## Results

FLOW+ (high-fidelity DAG variant) achieves the best overall average precision of 80.15% across four benchmarks. On dependency-rich tasks, FLOW+ leads by large margins: 77.70% AP on GSM8K (vs. 63.96% for the best sampling baseline RACE), 76.59% on MATH (vs. 48.11% for PDS), and 83.08% on HumanEval (vs. 77.82% for the GoV proxy). On HotpotQA, where traces are shorter and fact-retrieval-oriented, PDS achieves the highest AP (88.69%), with FLOW+ trailing slightly at 83.23%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLOW+ outperforms all four sampling-based baselines (SelfCheckGPT, SINDEX, PDS, RACE) by 13–68 AP points on GSM8K, MATH, and HumanEval, where errors are internal to the reasoning trace. Against the whole-trace DIRECT judge (same GPT-5.2 family), FLOW+ gains 36 AP on GSM8K and 36 AP on HumanEval, showing that local evidence organization and DAG aggregation substantially outperform monolithic judging. FLOW+ also beats the GoV proxy in all 12 dataset–model cells, confirming that the mass-flow aggregation rule adds value beyond graph-ordered verification alone. On HotpotQA, the advantage reverses: PDS (88.69) and SINDEX (83.43) edge out FLOW+ (83.23), consistent with shorter, fact-retrieval-oriented traces where sample-level uncertainty is a stronger signal than structural dependency. Among judge-based and graph-structured methods, FLOW+ trails only PARC on HotpotQA (83.23 vs. 86.64) but leads on all three computation/code benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on structured reasoning representations (Tree-of-Thoughts, Graph-of-Thoughts, PARC, GoV) by formalizing a structural reasoning model (SRM) as a dependency DAG with explicit source citations, then introducing a mass-flow aggregation rule with a clean information-theoretic interpretation via erasure channels and mutual information contraction (building on Wainwright & Jordan's graphical models framework and Polyanskiy & Wu's SDPI results). It departs from sampling-based detectors (SelfCheckGPT, PDS, RACE) by operating on a single structured trace rather than K samples, and from whole-trace LLM judges by restricting verification to locally cited evidence. The key conceptual departure from PARC and GoV is the principled forward-flow aggregation that accounts for shared supports across downstream steps, rather than simple max/mean over local verdicts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a structured, interpretable framework for hallucination detection that is particularly effective on longer, dependency-rich reasoning traces (math, code) where linear CoT evaluation is known to be fragile. The information-theoretic semantics (flow score = probability of erasure = fraction of mutual information lost) gives practitioners a principled way to understand how local verifier noise propagates through the graph, and the weighted Lipschitz bound identifies which nodes deserve more verification budget. Key limitations include strong dependence on local verifier quality (GPT-4o degrades AP by ~40 points vs. GPT-5.2), structured prompting that can reduce base accuracy on factual QA (−7.64 on HotpotQA), and reduced competitiveness on shorter, fact-retrieval-oriented tasks where sampling-based uncertainty signals remain strong. Open questions include co-designing generation harnesses with verification, handling implicit pragmatic premises that resist explicit condition extraction, and whether the flow aggregation generalizes to non-DAG or cyclic reasoning structures.
