## Problem

STM-based molecular fabrication remains limited by the inherent complexity of manipulation procedures and the extensive manual effort required—human experts may spend months constructing assemblies of only ~100 atoms. Previous autonomous approaches target simple adsorbates (atoms, CO molecules), while complex molecules introduce rotational degrees of freedom and stochastic responses that make control difficult. This paper addresses the gap by proposing a unified AI-based planning framework that computes collision-free assembly schedules and trains RL agents to execute them, enabling autonomous construction of structures with hundreds of molecules.

## Input / Output

The framework takes as input the 2D positions and orientations of molecules on a surface (lattice constant L=0.3 nm), a target assembly specifying desired positions and orientations, probabilistic STM manipulation response parameters (Gaussian displacement means/covariances and discrete rotation probabilities per action), and optionally convex polygonal obstacles. It produces a collision-free construction plan (a molecule-to-target assignment with an execution schedule ensuring each molecule has an empty corridor of configurable width w∈{4,5,6} nm when moving) and a trained RL policy that maps 7-dimensional observations (direction to target, distance, lateral corridor offset, corridor membership, current and target orientation) to discrete tip-placement actions on a lattice-discretized 2D action space, ultimately yielding the assembled molecular structure.

## Method

The framework operates in two phases. Phase 1 iteratively computes a high-level construction plan: the Hungarian algorithm assigns molecules to target positions minimizing total travel distance, and an SMT formula (solved with Z3) checks whether a feasible execution schedule exists such that each molecule has an empty convex corridor at its scheduled turn; if unsatisfiable, the unsatisfiable core identifies conflicting assignments whose distances are set to infinity, triggering reassignment. Scalability is improved via constraint-based partitioning into precedence-related classes solved independently, and obstacle avoidance is handled by computing shortest circumventing paths through waypoint graphs. Phase 2 trains deep RL agents (CrossQ, TQC, PPO) in NANOASSEMBLYGYM, a Gymnasium-API simulator, to execute individual molecule movements within corridors using a composite reward combining tanh-normalized progress toward the target, a hard corridor-violation penalty, and orientation-alignment rewards activated near the goal position.

## Results

Across all experiments, the framework successfully assembled structures containing up to 420 molecules, with only 10 collisions observed when moving over 30,000 molecules (success rate approaching 100%). For the circular molecule, corridor confinement reached 99.7% with 0.83 nm mean movement per manipulation; for the cross-shaped molecule with σ=0.025I, confinement was 99.8% with 0.73 nm mean movement; and for the high-variance cross-shaped molecule (σ=0.1I), confinement was 97.3% with 0.63 nm mean movement. Construction plans for the largest assemblies (420 molecules) were computed within a few minutes, with the number of iterations and computation time increasing only moderately with assembly size.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three RL algorithms—PPO, TQC, and CrossQ—for training molecular manipulation policies. For the circular molecule, all three algorithms perform comparably, with TQC achieving the highest average movement (0.83 nm/step vs. 0.80 nm for CrossQ and PPO) and all maintaining 99.7% corridor adherence; TQC was selected for assembly construction. For the cross-shaped molecule with σ=0.025I, CrossQ and TQC clearly outperform PPO (0.77 and 0.73 nm vs. 0.49 nm average movement), with CrossQ converging faster; both maintain 99.7% corridor adherence while PPO fails to keep the molecule within the corridor. For the high-variance cross-shaped molecule (σ=0.1I), CrossQ decisively outperforms both TQC (0.80 vs. 0.66 nm at w=6 nm) and PPO (which fails corridor constraints entirely), achieving 98.7% corridor adherence at w=6 nm versus 95% at w=4 nm. Overall, CrossQ is the strongest algorithm for cross-shaped molecules across all corridor widths, while TQC edges out for the simpler circular molecule.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of STM-based molecular manipulation from single-molecule control (Ramsauer et al. 2023, Chen et al. 2022, Rutter 2023) to multi-molecule assembly by introducing a two-phase planning framework: a Hungarian-algorithm-based assignment step combined with SMT-based collision-free scheduling, followed by RL execution. The key conceptual departure is treating assembly as a combinatorial planning problem (assignment + temporal scheduling with geometric corridor constraints) rather than a pure sequential control problem, and packaging the execution layer in a standardized Gymnasium-based simulation (NANOASSEMBLYGYM) that had not previously existed for this domain. The planning layer is purely geometric and algorithmic, drawing on classical combinatorial optimization and constraint solving rather than learned components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework demonstrates that autonomous construction of assemblies with up to 420 molecules is feasible in simulation with near-100% success (10 collisions in >30,000 movements), a scale that previously required months of human effort for ~100 molecules. NANOASSEMBLYGYM provides a reusable, high-fidelity benchmark environment that could accelerate future research on molecular manipulation RL. Key open questions include closing the sim-to-real gap (tip degradation, model inaccuracies in probabilistic response distributions), online failure recovery when molecules leave corridors, and extending the action space to include lateral tip movement and voltage/pulse control. The planning layer's scalability is validated, but the RL policies remain corridor-constrained and do not yet handle dynamic re-planning.
