## Problem

Standard correlational evaluation of language-model learnability on formal languages conflates the effect of a target property (e.g., how often a transition is traversed) with structural confounders that co-vary with it in naturally sampled corpora, such as shifts in string-length distribution or automaton topology. The paper shows that this conflation can invert apparent learnability trends, making it impossible to attribute performance differences to data scarcity versus architectural difficulty without causal intervention.

## Input / Output

The method takes a deterministic probabilistic finite automaton (PFA) over a finite alphabet, a property specification identifying a set of target transitions T and a target count N (or an at-least-once constraint over M strings), and a corpus size K. It produces a constrained corpus in which the target transitions appear exactly N times across K strings, then trains LSTM and Transformer language models against the PFA's full per-position next-symbol distributions (not single sampled tokens) and reports a decomposed KL divergence localized to the targeted transitions, states, or symbols. The final output is a pair of learnability curves—causal (intervened) and correlational (conditioned)—plotted against occurrence count.

## Method

The core contribution is the binning semiring, an algebra over a base semiring whose elements are (R+1)-tuples tracking occurrence counts of a targeted feature along automaton paths; multiplication is an overflow-accumulating convolution that composes counts. Lifting a PFA to this semiring yields a binning automaton whose backward weights (computed via Lehmann's algorithm with a closed-form Kleene-star solution) give the exact conditional distribution over corpora with a specified count of target transitions. A two-phase sampler (SampleCorpus) first draws per-string counts from this distribution, then walks the original automaton state-by-state using precomputed per-action binning-weight tables to draw each path conditioned on its remaining occurrence budget. The evaluation pipeline is formalized as a four-node graphical causal model (PFA → Dataset → Learned Model → Performance), and the paper contrasts the correlational estimand E[M | D ∈ P] with the causal estimand E[M | do(D ∈ P)], showing that the difference isolates the confounding effect of the sampling procedure.

## Results

The paper's central finding is that causal and correlational learnability curves systematically diverge across all three experimental settings. In the PARITY + star-free automaton (Fig. 2), the LSTM's per-state decomposed KL divergence drops roughly an order of magnitude below the transformer's as PARITY occurrences grow, while the transformer's curve plateaus—recovering the known LSTM–transformer gap on PARITY as a function of occurrence count. In the 50-state random PFA setting (Fig. 5), the correlational KL is non-monotonic (rising to a peak near 250 occurrences before falling), whereas the causal KL decreases monotonically, indicating that the correlational trend inverts due to confounding. In the fixed 40-state topology (Fig. 6), the causal–correlational gap approaches or exceeds an order of magnitude at low counts for states 2, 6, and 8, confirming that confounding is a state-level rather than topology-level phenomenon.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods; its primary comparison is between the proposed causal evaluation protocol and the standard correlational evaluation (the implicit baseline in prior work such as Borenstein et al., 2024 and Someya et al., 2025). Across all three settings, the correlational baseline produces misleading learnability curves: in the 50-state setting the correlational trend even inverts at low counts (rising before falling), while the causal curve is monotonically decreasing. The paper also compares LSTM and Transformer architectures under both protocols; the LSTM consistently achieves lower per-state KL on the PARITY sub-task (by roughly an order of magnitude at high counts), whereas the Transformer plateaus, directly tracing the architecture gap as a function of target-occurrence count. The key methodological contribution is that the binning semiring enables the causal intervention (exact-count constrained sampling) that the correlational baseline cannot replicate, and the resulting gap between the two curves quantifies the confounding introduced by standard practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of formal-language learnability evaluation (Borenstein et al., 2024; Someya et al., 2025) and causal inference (Pearl et al., 2016). It departs from the standard correlational protocol—sample weightings, train, correlate counts with performance—by formulating the pipeline as a graphical causal model and introducing the binning semiring, an algebraic lift of PFA transition weights that enables exact-count constrained sampling. The construction draws on Kuich's semiring theory for weighted automata and Lehmann's algorithm for Kleene-star computation, repurposing them to track occurrence counts through path computations. The closest prior work, MLRegTest (Van der Poel et al., 2025), also controls sampling but does so via uniform path intersection rather than exact conditional distributions from a probabilistic automaton.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that standard correlational evaluation of formal-language learnability can produce qualitatively wrong conclusions—trends that invert at low occurrence counts—providing a concrete warning for any multi-task learning analysis where task frequency co-varies with other corpus properties. The binning semiring and its sampling algorithms offer a reusable, polynomial-time tool for causal intervention on automaton-based data generation, potentially applicable to richer automata (pushdown, context-free) and, in principle, to natural-language settings with approximate property labels. Limitations include PFA-only scope, fixed architecture sizes without scaling sweeps, and the acknowledged gap between exact-countable formal properties and the fuzzy, overlapping phenomena in natural language.
