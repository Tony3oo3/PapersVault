## Problem

Classical auction and mechanism design models assume binary allocations or fixed supply, but data is non-rivalrous (the same dataset can be sold to multiple buyers) and its value to AI model training follows a power-law scaling with diminishing returns. This paper asks how a seller should decide how much data to produce and how to price and allocate it across buyers when valuations are continuous and sublinear, and how these decisions change under sequential arrivals or bilateral private information.

## Input / Output

The mechanism takes as input buyer value distributions F_i, a constant marginal production cost c, a publicly known elasticity parameter α ∈ (0,1), and (in the bilateral setting) a seller cost distribution F^S. Outputs are continuous data allocation quantities x_i ≥ 0, payment rules p_i, and a production level D = max_i x_i (not a sum, reflecting non-rivalry). A key structural constraint is that the production cost depends only on the maximum allocation across buyers, so the seller's optimization couples all buyers through a single shared data pool rather than through mutually exclusive allocations.

## Method

In the offline setting, the paper extends Myerson's virtual-value framework to continuous allocations, showing that the optimal mechanism pools all buyers with positive (ironed) virtual values onto a common data level D* = (α/c · Σr_i)^{1/(1−α)}, with payments differentiated by distribution. In the online setting, a two-stage mechanism first produces an initial data amount based on the expected sum of virtual values, then adaptively expands production via myopic greedy responses to each arriving buyer; Rosenthal's inequality for L^{1/(1−α)} spaces yields a constant-factor approximation to the offline optimum, while myopic-only and fixed-production baselines are shown to be arbitrarily suboptimal. In the bilateral setting, a multiplicative decomposition of first-best welfare into buyer-side and seller-side terms enables simple seller-proposing and buyer-proposing mechanisms to achieve α^{1/(1−α)} and α^{α/(1−α)} (≥ 1/e) approximations to first-best gain-from-trade, respectively.

## Results

The paper establishes constant-factor approximation guarantees across three settings. In the online setting, the two-stage mechanism (Algorithm 1) achieves expected profit at least 1/((1+1/(1−α))·C_{1/(1−α)}) times the offline optimum (Theorem 3.4), where C_p is the best Rosenthal constant. The online-offline gap is shown to be tight up to constants: the worst-case ratio ONL/OFFL is at most 2^{1/(1−α)}/C_{1/(1−α)} = e^{−Θ(1/(1−α)·log(1/(1−α)))} (Theorem 3.6). In the bilateral setting, the buyer-proposing mechanism achieves a 1/e approximation to first-best GFT for any α∈(0,1) (Theorem 4.2), while the seller-proposing mechanism achieves an α^{1/(1−α)}-approximation (Theorem 4.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper identifies two natural online baselines and shows both fail: the myopic greedy mechanism (optimizing per-buyer) degrades as O(n^{−α/(1−α)}) relative to the optimal online profit (Theorem 3.2), and any non-adaptive mechanism with a fixed production level can be made arbitrarily suboptimal (Theorem 3.3). The proposed two-stage mechanism, which combines upfront expectation-based production with adaptive myopic expansion, overcomes both failures and achieves a constant-factor approximation to the offline optimum (Theorem 3.4). The tightness of this guarantee is confirmed by a matching upper bound (Theorem 3.6) showing the online-offline gap is super-exponential in 1/(1−α) in the worst case, implying the two-stage mechanism is nearly optimal among all online mechanisms. In the bilateral setting, the buyer-proposing mechanism alone achieves a 1/e approximation to first-best GFT without needing to be combined with the seller-proposing mechanism, which is a key distinction from classical bilateral trade results (e.g., Deng et al., 2022) where both mechanisms must be combined to achieve constant-factor guarantees.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Myerson's optimal auction design framework (virtual values, ironing, payment identities) to a fundamentally different allocation structure: continuous data quantities with power-law scaling valuations rather than binary win/lose outcomes. It departs from prior data-market mechanism design (Agarwal et al., Bonatti et al.) by treating data production as an endogenous decision variable rather than assuming a fixed commodity, and from combinatorial/multi-unit auction literature (Dobzinski & Nisan) by allowing non-rivalrous repeated allocation with diminishing returns. The online analysis draws on prophet-inequality techniques but shows that the standard fixed-supply assumptions break down when supply is endogenous, necessitating Rosenthal's inequality for the approximation guarantee.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical treatment of data markets where scaling-law valuations and endogenous production jointly determine optimal mechanisms, providing clean structural characterizations (pooling in offline, two-stage adaptive in online, single-side proposing in bilateral) with explicit dependence on the elasticity parameter α. The bilateral result that either seller-proposing or buyer-proposing alone achieves constant-factor approximation to first-best GFT—unlike classical bilateral trade requiring both—highlights how scaling-law structure simplifies mechanism design. Key limitations include homogeneous data, identical α across buyers, known distributions, and the power-law functional form; open directions include heterogeneous scaling exponents, unknown priors with online learning, and empirical validation on real data-market distributions.
