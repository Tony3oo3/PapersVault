## Problem

Existing graph prompting methods optimize continuous relaxed adjacency matrices during adaptation but must hard-discretize at inference, creating a train-test mismatch that destabilizes optimization in few-shot regimes. This paper asks how to design a prompting framework that learns task-specific graph topology prompts directly in the discrete combinatorial space, aligning the adaptation input domain with the pre-training domain of the frozen encoder.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266028/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an attributed graph (binary adjacency + node feature matrix), a frozen 2-layer GCN encoder (hidden dim 128), and K-shot support labels per class. It operates on 2-hop local candidate subgraphs with a fixed budget of m=128 candidates per anchor node, processing anchors in mini-batches of B=32. The output is a discrete k-sparse prompt adjacency (k=8 selected edges per anchor, binary {0,1} entries) that is unioned with the initial graph, plus node class predictions for query nodes. The prompt remains strictly discrete in both training and inference, unlike relaxation-based baselines.

## Method

DiP-G combines three components: (1) a parametric local prompting module (2-layer MLP) that scores candidate edges using cached frozen-encoder embeddings, avoiding circular dependency and O(N²) cost; (2) a perturbed Top-k solver that adds i.i.d. Gumbel noise to scores and selects exactly k candidates per anchor via a hard cardinality constraint, producing binary prompt vectors; (3) an I-MLE finite-difference gradient estimator with Common Random Numbers that compares discrete solutions under the current scores Θ and a target-perturbed score Θ′=Θ−λ_t·G_S to obtain practical gradients for the non-differentiable solver. An adaptive active-set screening rule further accelerates the backward pass by restricting the target Top-k solve to a buffered top-(k+r) subset whenever the perturbation magnitude δ_b ≤ ½Δ_b, which is provably exact under this margin condition and reduces sorting cost from O(m log k) to O((k+r) log k).

## Results

Under 5-shot node classification with GraphCL pre-training, DiP-G achieves the best accuracy across all five datasets: 65.12% on Cora, 70.44% on PubMed, 29.23% on Amazon, 70.84% on Minesweeper, and 32.50% on Flickr, with notably lower standard deviations (≤3.41) than most baselines. Under 10-shot settings (Table 2), DiP-G extends its lead, reaching 75.62% on Cora and 74.39% on PubMed with GraphCL. On 100-shot graph classification (Table 5), DiP-G attains 70.36% on DD, 69.81% on NCI1, 70.46% on NCI109, and 72.61% on Mutagenicity under GraphCL, outperforming all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiP-G outperforms the strongest topology-prompting baseline GraphTOP by 1.7–2.6 points across all five datasets under GraphCL (e.g., 65.12 vs. 63.44 on Cora, 70.84 vs. 68.25 on Minesweeper), and also beats the edge-prompting baselines EdgePrompt/EdgePrompt+ by 2.2–6.9 points. Against feature-prompting methods (GPPT, GraphPrompt, All-in-One, GPF, ProNoG), DiP-G gains 3–13 points, confirming that discrete structural editing provides a stronger adaptation signal than feature reweighting in the few-shot regime. Under SimGRACE and link-prediction pre-training (DP-GPPT, DP-GraphPrompt), DiP-G remains competitive but is not always the top method: EdgePrompt+ edges out DiP-G on Cora under DP-GPPT (42.88 vs. 37.52) and on Minesweeper under DP-GraphPrompt (74.03 vs. 71.52), though DiP-G still wins on the remaining datasets in each group. Notably, DiP-G exhibits substantially lower variance (std ≤ 3.41) than most baselines (std up to 9.07 for GraphTOP on Flickr), supporting the paper's claim that enforcing discreteness throughout adaptation yields more stable optimization in low-label settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the graph prompting line of work (GPPT, GraphPrompt, GPF, EdgePrompt, GraphTOP) by shifting the optimization from continuous adjacency relaxations to hard discrete structures in the combinatorial space. It builds directly on I-MLE (Niepert et al., 2021) as the gradient estimator for the non-differentiable Top-k solver, and on the perturbed-argmax / Gumbel trick for sampling discrete selections. The key departure from prior topology-prompting methods (EdgePrompt, GraphTOP) is the elimination of the train-test discretization gap: rather than optimizing a soft adjacency and thresholding at inference, DiP-G enforces binary k-sparse prompts throughout both training and inference, aligning the adaptation input with the discrete domain on which the frozen encoder was pre-trained.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discretization gap identified here is a practical concern for anyone deploying relaxed graph prompts in low-label regimes, and the paper provides a clean, theoretically grounded fix with a provably exact screening rule that keeps backward-pass cost low. However, the empirical gains over the strongest baselines (EdgePrompt+, GraphTOP) are moderate (typically 1–3 percentage points), and the method is validated only with a 2-layer GCN backbone on relatively small-to-medium graphs. Open questions include whether the discrete constraint remains beneficial at larger scale, whether the I-MLE bias term becomes problematic with very small λ_t in practice, and whether the approach extends naturally to tasks beyond node/graph classification (e.g., link prediction, graph generation).
