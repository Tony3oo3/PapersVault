## Problem

As large image editing models shift from text-driven instructions to vision-prompt editing (inferring intent from marks, arrows, and visual-text cues), the attack surface itself becomes visual, yet existing safety mechanisms—safeguard models and safety alignment—are predominantly text-centric. This creates a fundamental 'vision-centric safety misalignment' where malicious editing instructions embedded purely in visual form can evade all conventional text-based safety controls, a vulnerability that has gone unexplored and lacks a standardized benchmark for systematic evaluation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261610/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The attack takes a benign base image with malicious visual cues embedded directly into it (bounding indicators such as circles or rectangles marking target regions, semantic editing cues in various fonts/languages, and directional arrows linking instructions to targets) with the text input intentionally set to NULL—no textual prompt is provided. The model produces an edited image that may contain policy-violating content. For evaluation, an MLLM judge (default: Gemini 3 Pro) scores each output on a 1–5 Harmfulness Scale, binary Editing Validity, and derives the High Risk Ratio (HS ≥ 4 AND valid). The defense variant additionally appends a short safety-trigger sentence to the text channel, producing a safe/unsafe judgment before the edit is executed.

## Method

VJA is a black-box, prompt-level attack that encodes malicious editing intent exclusively in the visual modality—using diverse visual markers (sizes, colors, shapes) and visual language (fonts, languages)—so that the victim model receives only an image with no text, exploiting the gap between vision-centric execution and text-centric safety alignment. To systematize evaluation, IESBench organizes 1054 visually-prompted images across 15 risk categories in a three-level hierarchy (Individual Rights → Group-Targeted Harm → Societal/Public Risks), with 116 fine-grained attributes and 9 action types, and employs MLLM-as-judge for scalable, adaptive scoring. The proposed defense is training-free and operates by reusing the KV-Cache of the already-encoded multimodal prompt, then appending a lightweight textual safety trigger that activates the VLM's inherent safety awareness through introspective multimodal reasoning, effectively redirecting the attack surface from vision back to language space where alignment is more robust. This requires no auxiliary guard model and adds only ~300 tokens and ~3% runtime, yet reduces the ASR of a poorly aligned open-source model (Qwen-Image-Edit*) from 100% to 66.9%, bringing it to a level comparable with commercial systems.

## Results

On IESBench (1054 visually-prompted images across 15 risk categories), VJA achieves an average ASR of 85.7% across four commercial models, reaching 97.5% on Qwen-Image-Edit, 94.1% on Seedream 4.5, 80.9% on Nano Banana Pro, and 70.3% on GPT Image 1.5. Open-source models without safeguard mechanisms (BAGEL, Flux2.0[dev], Qwen-Image-Edit*) are fully compromised with 100% ASR and average HS of 4.3–4.6. The proposed training-free introspective defense (Qwen-Image-Edit-Safe) reduces ASR from 100% to 66.9% and HS from 4.6 to 3.4, bringing a poorly aligned open-source model to a safety level comparable with GPT Image 1.5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VJA substantially outperforms text-centric jailbreak attacks (TJA) on well-aligned commercial models: on Nano Banana Pro, VJA raises ASR from 48.8% to 84.4% (+35.6 pp) and HRR from 44.9% to 71.2% (+26.3 pp); on GPT Image 1.5, ASR increases from 46.8% to 71.7% (+24.9 pp). On already-vulnerable models (Qwen-Image-Edit online, Seedream 4.5), the VJA-over-TJA gap narrows to +7.3 pp and +0.5 pp respectively, since these models lack effective text-based safeguards. Among the attacked models, GPT Image 1.5 is the most resistant (ASR 70.3%, HRR 52.0%), while Nano Banana Pro shows the second-best resistance (ASR 80.9%) but is notably more vulnerable to copyright tampering (I11: 95.7% ASR) than GPT Image 1.5 (I11: 95.7% vs. 41.3% for Nano Banana Pro). The proposed introspective defense on Qwen-Image-Edit-Safe achieves an ASR of 66.9%, which is lower than GPT Image 1.5 (70.3%) and Nano Banana Pro (80.9%), confirming that a simple text-trigger defense can close the safety gap between open-source and commercial systems. Generalization tests (Table 7) show the defense also reduces ASR on LongCat-Image from 100% to 71.9% and on Flux2.0[dev] from 100% to 69.0%, with only ~3% additional runtime overhead.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multimodal jailbreak literature (HADES, FigStep, JailbreakV) from text-generation MLLMs to the image-editing domain, where both the adversarial instruction and the harmful output are visual. It builds directly on the Qwen-Image-Edit architecture (Wu et al., 2025), specifically its use of a VLM for text-embedding extraction, to design a KV-Cache-reusing introspective defense. Unlike prior multimodal attacks that use images as auxiliary inputs to elicit unsafe text, VJA is the first to encode the entire malicious instruction in the visual modality and target an image-to-image editing pipeline, exploiting the mismatch between text-centric safety alignment and vision-centric execution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that well-aligned commercial models (GPT Image 1.5, Nano Banana Pro) reject most text-based attacks yet succumb to 70–81% of purely visual ones exposes a fundamental modality gap in current safety mechanisms, with direct implications for the deployment of increasingly capable image editing tools. IESBench provides the first standardized, hierarchical safety benchmark for image editing, and the training-free defense (≈300 extra tokens, ~3% latency) offers a practical, immediately deployable mitigation for open-source models. Key open questions include whether the defense generalizes to models without a VLM component, how to handle attacks requiring up-to-date world knowledge (e.g., fabricating specific real-world evidence), and whether visual safety alignment can be achieved natively during pretraining rather than as a post-hoc trigger.
