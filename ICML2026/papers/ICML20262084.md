## Problem

Estimating the Average Treatment Effect from observational data spread across multiple sites is infeasible under standard centralized methods due to privacy and legal constraints, yet existing federated causal inference approaches impose restrictive assumptions—such as a shared parametric propensity score, limited site-specific shifts, or local overlap at every site—that fail in practice when treatment assignment mechanisms differ substantially across sites. Meta-analysis, the natural baseline, breaks down entirely when any single site lacks treated or control units, and density-ratio-based transportability methods are incompatible with federated learning constraints.

## Input / Output

Each of K sites holds a local dataset of (X_i, W_i, Y_i) tuples with site-specific sample sizes and covariate distributions; no individual-level records are ever transmitted to a central server. The method outputs a single global ATE estimate along with the federated global propensity score e(x) = Σ_k ω_k(x)·e_k(x) and the learned membership weights ω_k(x) = P(H=k|X=x). Local propensity scores e_k may be estimated with arbitrary classifiers (logistic regression, random forests, neural networks) chosen per site, while membership weights and outcome models are trained jointly via standard FL algorithms (e.g., FedAvg), exchanging only model parameters or aggregate statistics.

## Method

The core mechanism decomposes the global propensity score into a weighted mixture of locally estimated scores, where the weights are Membership Weights (MW) P(H=k|X=x) rather than the density-ratio weights ρ_k·f_k(x)/f(x) used in transportability literature. MW are estimated via federated multiclass classification (logistic regression, neural networks, or federated random forests), avoiding the strong parametric density assumptions and high-dimensional parameter sharing required by DW. The resulting Fed-IPW and Fed-AIPW estimators use this global score in standard inverse-propensity-weighting and doubly-robust augmentation formulas, computed locally at each site and aggregated by sample-size weighting. Theoretically, the approach achieves the same asymptotic variance as centralized estimators, provably reduces variance relative to meta-analysis via Jensen's inequality on the convex overlap function, and improves global overlap (O_global ≤ Σ_k ρ_k·O_k) even when individual sites have poor or zero local overlap. Cross-fitting of all nuisance functions (local propensities, MW, outcome models) ensures the empirical Fed-AIPW estimator is locally semiparametrically efficient under standard o_P(n^{-1/4}) convergence conditions.

## Results

On the multi-site Traumabase cohort (K=14 centers, n=8,248 patients, 17 covariates), Fed-AIPW with membership weights (MW) closely matches the centralized AIPW benchmark and exhibits lower variance than Meta-SW, which departs from the centralized estimate. In synthetic experiments under site effects (K=30 sites), Fed-MW (Logit, with site covariates Z) achieves MSE of 0.022/0.004/0.002 for AIPW at n_k=20/60/100, compared to 10.673/0.346/0.084 for Meta-SW AIPW—a reduction of over 400× at the smallest sample size. Under no local overlap, meta-analysis estimators are entirely undefined while Fed-(A)IPW remain unbiased; under good local overlap, federated estimators achieve the smallest variance among all methods. Theorem 5 guarantees that global overlap is always at least as good as the worst local overlap (e.g., local overlap ≈101 vs. global overlap = 4 in the paper's illustrative example).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the meta-analysis baseline (Burke et al., 2017; Meta-SW), Fed-(A)IPW dominates across all overlap regimes: under no local overlap meta-analysis is undefined (one site has zero treated patients) while federated estimators remain unbiased; under poor local overlap (O_2 ≈ 10^7) meta-analysis suffers severe bias and instability whereas Fed-(A)IPW stays reliable; under good local overlap all methods are unbiased but federated estimators achieve strictly lower variance (Theorem 4). On the Traumabase real data, Meta-SW departs from the centralized AIPW benchmark while Fed-MW closely tracks it with lower variance. Against Xiong et al. (2023) 1S-IVW, Fed-MW matches the variance of 1S-IVW in the favorable shared-parameter setting (Figure 3) without requiring prior knowledge of which propensity-score coefficients are shared across sites, and remains unbiased under both DGP A and DGP B where 1S-IVW's parametric assumptions may not hold. The key practical advantage is that Fed-MW requires no structural assumptions on local propensity scores and does not need local overlap, enabling inclusion of small sites and external control arms that would be infeasible under either baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the IPW/AIPW causal inference framework (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983; Bang & Robins, 2005) to a federated multi-site observational setting, departing from prior federated causal inference methods that either fit a single global parametric propensity model via parameter averaging (Xiong et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2025) or rely on density ratio weights requiring explicit density modeling (Han et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2024). The key conceptual departure is the use of Membership Weights P(H=k|X) as aggregation coefficients for locally estimated propensity scores, which avoids the strong distributional assumptions of density ratios and permits fully heterogeneous local models. This positions the work as a bridge between transportability/generalization literature (which uses similar weighted combinations) and federated learning constraints, while also generalizing the authors' prior federated G-formula work for RCTs (Khellaf et al., 2025) to the more challenging observational setting with confounding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical gap: multi-site observational studies in healthcare where privacy regulations prevent data centralization and where local overlap may fail entirely (e.g., a site with no treated patients), rendering standard meta-analysis undefined. The clean theoretical result that global overlap is always at least as good as the worst local overlap (Theorem 5), combined with the variance dominance over meta-analysis (Theorem 4), provides a principled justification for federated propensity aggregation. Limitations include the need for sufficient per-site sample sizes to estimate local propensity scores accurately (particularly in high dimensions), and the framework is currently limited to ATE estimation; extending to CATE is identified as future work. The Traumabase application (14 centers, 8,248 patients) demonstrates practical viability but the method is best suited to a moderate number of large silos rather than many small ones.
