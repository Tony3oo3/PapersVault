## Problem

ML peer review is strained by two structural failures: submission volumes that far outstrip reviewing capacity, and a near-total absence of accountability or positive incentives that lets irresponsible reviewing persist unchecked. Existing remedies—per-author submission caps, 100% mandatory reciprocal review, retaliatory desk rejections, and polite reviewer guidelines—are either too blunt to handle the long tail of subtle misconduct or too soft to change behavior at scale. The paper argues that meaningful improvement requires enforceable, fine-grained procedural safeguards paired with a spendable, cross-conference credit system rather than further best-practice exhortations.

## Input / Output

The system ingests granular review-pipeline events—individual review completions, emergency-review assignments, internal-discussion initiations, peer/AC quality judgments, and submission acceptance outcomes—and converts them into a persistent, non-transferable point balance tracked per researcher on OpenReview. Points are redeemed against a conference-defined perk menu (registration, review-duty exemptions, additional-reviewer requests, refundable submission fees), where each adopting conference sets its own catalog, pricing, and per-cycle caps, so the same underlying currency supports heterogeneous local policies.

## Method

The core mechanism is 'OpenReview Points,' a cross-conference credit economy in which researchers earn points through labor (1 pt per standard review, 2 for emergency reviews, +3 for outstanding-reviewer recognition) and spend them on tangible perks (5 pts to opt out of an assigned review, 50 pts to request an additional expert reviewer, 100 pts for complimentary registration). To police the long tail of irresponsible reviewing that all-or-nothing desk rejection cannot address, the system introduces graduated, peer- and AC-adjudicated point deductions as fine-grained penalties, complemented by a refundable submission fee (e.g., 10 pts per paper, refunded upon acceptance) that leverages the NeurIPS Consistency Experiment finding that clear rejections are the most reliable review signal. A four-part defense—Duty Delegation, Upper Limits, Voting-Based Penalties/Awards, and Dynamic Pricing—guards against point farming, bulk outsourcing, and other gaming strategies. Crucially, points are non-transferable between individuals and cannot be purchased with money, preserving the labor-for-perks principle and preventing pay-to-play degeneration.

## Results

This is a position paper without quantitative experimental results or a main results table. The only empirical data presented is Table 1, which reports ICLR 2025 submission statistics: the top any-author appears on 42 submissions, the top last-author on 34, and the top first-author on only 7, used to argue that per-author submission caps would mainly trim auxiliary bylines rather than reduce total volume. The paper's contribution is a proposed mechanism (the OpenReview Points credit system with a four-part defense) rather than an evaluated method, and the authors explicitly note in Section 7 that the lack of numerical experiments is a limitation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare its proposed credit system against baselines in an experimental sense. Instead, it critiques four existing conference mechanisms and argues each is insufficient: (1) soft/hard submission caps, which the authors argue mainly shift authorship rather than reduce submissions; (2) retaliatory desk rejection (as at CVPR 2025, which desk-rejected 19 papers), deemed too blunt a 'felony-level' penalty for the long tail of subtle review misconduct; (3) 100% mandatory reciprocal review recruitment, characterized as a 'slow-acting poison' that degrades review quality by forcing ill-suited reviewers; and (4) polite best-practice guidelines in Calls for Papers, which the authors argue lack enforceability. The proposed OpenReview Points system is positioned as a flexible, fine-grained alternative that subsumes elements of all four (e.g., refundable submission fees address volume, graduated point deductions address misconduct, opt-out purchases address forced review) but no head-to-head empirical comparison is provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against a growing but fragmented literature on ML peer-review reform, including Kim et al. (2025) on reviewer feedback loops, Shah (2022) on computational solutions to specific review failures, and the concurrent Francia et al. (2026) token system for journal review. It departs from these works by proposing a unified, cross-conference credit economy (OpenReview Points) that subsumes many of their individual fixes as special cases, and by introducing a four-part anti-gaming defense (duty delegation, upper limits, voting-based penalties, dynamic pricing) absent from prior proposals. The paper also explicitly critiques the CVPR 2025 retaliatory desk-rejection policy as too blunt, arguing for graduated, fine-grained penalties instead.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a structural gap in ML conference governance: the absence of enforceable, fine-grained accountability mechanisms and positive incentives for review quality, a problem that worsens as submission volumes grow and reciprocal review becomes universal. Its main limitation is that it is purely corrective—policing an already-oversized submission pool rather than addressing the root cause of volume explosion—and it lacks any empirical validation, relying instead on anecdotal case studies and logical argumentation. A critical open question is cross-conference coordination: the system's full value depends on multiple major venues adopting a shared currency, yet ICML, NeurIPS, and ICLR have no established mechanism for such collaboration. Calibration of point values, long-term inflation control, and the risk of the system becoming bureaucratic overhead remain unresolved practical challenges.
