## Problem

LLM benchmarks that provide only test data allow model developers to strategically invest in benchmark-specific post-training (benchmaxxing), yet there is no formal characterization of the incentive structure these leaderboards induce. In particular, it was unknown whether the resulting competition among developers admits a stable Nash equilibrium, and whether any evaluation protocol can provably align leaderboard rankings with true latent model capability.

## Input / Output

The framework takes as input a set of submitted LLM models with (unknown) latent capabilities θ_i, a benchmark-specific fine-tuning dataset, and a designer-chosen tune-before-test baseline Δ^tbt. The output is a Stackelberg-Nash equilibrium: a TbT level and a corresponding effort profile that yields a leaderboard ranking preserving the latent capability ordering. Unusually, the 'input' is itself a strategic commitment—the designer must pre-commit to Δ^tbt anticipating developers' best responses—making the evaluation protocol a mechanism-design parameter rather than a fixed procedure. At the optimal threshold, the equilibrium effort profile is all-zero, meaning no developer invests in additional benchmark-specific tuning.

## Method

The paper models benchmarking as a Stackelberg game: the leaderboard designer (leader) commits to a TbT baseline, then model developers (followers) simultaneously choose additional benchmark-specific effort to maximize rank-based reward minus effort cost. The key negative result shows that at Δ^tbt=0 (standard evaluation), no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium exists whenever any adjacent reward gap exceeds the cost of 'just overtaking' the model directly above, producing persistent arms-race dynamics. The positive result proves that TbT monotonically increases the just-overtake effort (via diminishing returns in the post-effort score function), so a stabilizing threshold Δ^tbt* exists above which the all-zero effort profile is the unique PNE and rankings are capability-consistent. Under a generalized power-law scaling model, the threshold scales as O(λ_r^γ_r) where λ_r captures the effective overtaking incentive and γ_r is the learning-rate ratio between adjacent models. Empirical validation on Qwen2.5 models across nine benchmarks confirms the assumed regularity conditions and shows that a modest TbT of 3,000 steps raises the minimal overtaking effort from 18 to 384,668 training steps.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result is that standard LLM benchmarks (Δ^tbt = 0) generally induce follower games with no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium, meaning developers face persistent 'just-overtake' incentives and leaderboards never stabilize. The positive result shows that tune-before-test (TbT) at a sufficient level Δ^tbt* restores a unique Nash equilibrium in which all developers choose zero additional effort and rankings are preserved by latent capability. Empirically, on Winogrande with Qwen2.5 models, at Δ^tbt = 0 only 18 additional training steps are needed to change the ranking, whereas at Δ^tbt = 3,000 steps the minimum overtaking effort jumps to 384,668 steps. For the hardest-to-stabilize adjacent pair (e_r^req(0) ≈ 13.7, γ_r ≈ 0.33), a stabilizing TbT level of roughly 4 units of baseline effort suffices to deter all profitable overtaking deviations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare competing methods in the traditional sense; instead it contrasts the standard evaluation protocol (Δ^tbt = 0, no tune-before-test) against the TbT protocol (Δ^tbt > 0) as the designer's lever. Under the standard protocol, Theorem 4.6 shows that whenever any adjacent reward gap R_{r-1} − R_r exceeds the overtaking cost c(e_r^req(0)), no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium exists, leading to unbounded arms-race dynamics. Under TbT, Lemma 5.2 proves that the overtaking cost is monotonically non-decreasing in Δ^tbt, so Proposition 5.3 guarantees that once the zero-effort profile is an equilibrium at some level, it remains so at all higher levels. The empirical Winogrande case study quantifies this: moving from Δ^tbt = 0 to Δ^tbt = 3,000 increases the minimum overtaking effort by a factor of over 20,000× (from 18 to 384,668 steps), making strategic fine-tuning economically irrational. The paper also notes that on benchmarks like HellaSwag where model-size gaps are large, continued post-training cannot close the gap at all, so strategic incentives are naturally weak even without TbT.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the empirical tune-before-test (TbT) protocol of Zhang et al. (2025), which showed that fine-tuning all models on benchmark-specific data before evaluation restores ranking consistency. The key departure is reframing TbT from a post-hoc empirical correction to an ex ante mechanism-design choice within a Stackelberg game, providing the first formal game-theoretic justification for why it works. The analysis draws on strategic classification (Brückner & Scheffer, 2011; Hardt et al., 2016) and strategic ranking (Liu et al., 2022), but departs from both: unlike strategic classification, ranking creates interdependent competition among finite agents with discontinuous payoff jumps, and unlike Liu et al.'s continuum model, the finite-agent setting makes equilibrium existence itself a non-trivial design question.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal explanation for why LLM leaderboards exhibit persistent arms-race behavior: the induced follower game generally has no Nash equilibrium when reward gaps are large relative to overtaking costs. The positive result—that a small, uniform TbT intervention (as few as ~4 units in their scaling-law example) can restore a unique equilibrium ranking by latent capability—gives benchmark designers a concrete, low-cost lever. Key limitations include the single-benchmark focus, the assumption of common knowledge of capabilities, the restriction to pure strategies in a static game, and reliance on regularity conditions (diminishing returns, single-crossing) that may fail under RL-based post-training. Open questions include multi-benchmark resource allocation, Bayesian settings with private information, and dynamic benchmark evolution.
