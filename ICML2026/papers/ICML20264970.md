## Problem

Existing open-world graph learning methods face two critical gaps: they optimize semantic or topological signals in isolation for unknown-class rejection (failing to jointly exploit both in text-attributed graphs), and their pipelines stop at rejection without annotating unknown-class nodes for model re-training, often assuming a predefined number of unknown classes. This paper addresses both by proposing the first fully automated LLM-based pipeline that handles unlabeled data uncertainty end-to-end in open-label domains.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text-attributed graph (TAG) with node-level language descriptions, a small labeled subset drawn from known classes, and the graph adjacency structure. It produces a two-stage output: (1) per-node predictions that either assign a known-class label or flag the node as unknown-class, and (2) LLM-generated open-vocabulary class annotations for all rejected unknown-class nodes, which are then used to retrain the backbone GNN. Notably, the unknown-class annotations are entirely LLM-generated from the open domain with no ground-truth label supervision, and the pipeline requires no human-in-the-loop annotation.

## Method

OGA comprises two modules. ALT (Adaptive Label Traceability) performs unknown-class rejection via ontology representation learning: a pretrained graph-language encoder produces node embeddings, which are refined by personalized PageRank propagation (entity-entity), dynamically aggregated into class concepts via trainable neighborhood weights (entity-concept), and classified using a λ-sharpened softmax over concept distances with a confidence threshold ε for rejection (concept-entity); the whole process is optimized end-to-end with a joint loss combining semantic cross-entropy, topology-aware smoothness, and inter-concept margin separation. GLA (Graph Label Annotator) then annotates rejected nodes by first running a semantic-enhanced modularity-based community detection that fuses graph topology with text similarity, then applying degree-guided LLM inference (low-degree nodes receive direct LLM calls while high-degree nodes inherit neighbor labels), followed by multi-granularity annotation via intra-community distillation of top-Φ representative nodes and iterative inter-community label fusion until the annotation count matches the known-class count.

## Results

On the four main UCR datasets, OGA achieves the best known-class classification accuracy (Aspect 1): 90.02% on Cora, 78.39% on arXiv, 51.10% on Children, and 87.53% on WikiCS, improving by up to 4.98% over the strongest baseline. For unknown-class identification (Aspect 2), OGA attains the best balanced Coverage/Precision, e.g., 94.2/82.3 on Cora and 96.4/48.9 on WikiCS, outperforming the best baselines by 6.2% in coverage and 4.6% in precision. In the post-annotation retraining (Aspect 4), OGA restores GCN accuracy on PubMed from 47.12% (unlabeled lower bound) to 87.15%, reaching 99.6% of the ground-truth upper bound (87.49%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

OGA consistently outperforms all three key baselines across every dataset and metric. Compared to OpenWGL (Wu et al., ICDM 2020), OGA gains +3.01% accuracy on Cora (90.02 vs. 87.01) and +15.17% on arXiv (78.39 vs. 62.85), while also dramatically improving unknown-class precision on Cora (82.3 vs. 91.3 coverage but 82.3 vs. 91.3 precision trade-off). Against GOOD (Liu et al., WSDM 2023), OGA leads by +2.40% on Cora accuracy and +2.36% on arXiv, and achieves substantially higher precision on WikiCS (48.9 vs. 17.3). Versus ARC (Liu et al., 2024), OGA surpasses by +19.00% on Cora accuracy (90.02 vs. 71.02) and +53.3% on WikiCS (87.53 vs. 72.13), with coverage/precision of 94.2/82.3 vs. 83.4/39.5 on Cora. Notably, OGA is the only method that achieves strong performance on both coverage and precision simultaneously, whereas baselines like GNN_Safe and OODGAT excel in coverage but sacrifice precision, and OpenWGL shows the opposite pattern.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of node-level OOD detection, conventional open-world graph learning, and LLM-based annotation, but departs from all three threads in a unified way. Prior UCR methods (OpenWGL, GOOD, ARC, etc.) rely on graph-only encoders and optimize semantics or topology in isolation for naive attributed graphs; OGA instead introduces 'ontology representation learning' over a pretrained graph-language encoder, jointly optimizing semantic cross-entropy, topology-aware smoothness, and inter-concept separation. On the annotation side, prior LLM annotation work (LLM-GNN, Cella) assumes a fixed label space or predefined cluster count, whereas OGA's Graph Label Annotator uses topology-guided community detection with degree-aware LLM calls to generate open-vocabulary labels without any prior knowledge of the unknown class count. The paper thus repurposes personalized PageRank, modularity-based community detection, and LLM prompting into a single end-to-end pipeline that no prior work has assembled.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OGA is the first fully automated open-world graph learning pipeline that closes the loop from unknown-class rejection to LLM-generated annotation and model retraining, removing the human-in-the-loop bottleneck that limits practical deployment. The four-aspect benchmark (accuracy, coverage/precision, annotation quality, post-annotation improvement) provides a standardized evaluation that the field lacked. Key open questions include scalability to very large or dynamically evolving graphs (temporal, spatial), the sensitivity of the ontology framework to encoder quality, and whether the community-based LLM annotation strategy generalizes beyond the citation and e-commerce domains tested. The 87–90% reduction in LLM calls is practically significant for cost-sensitive deployment, but the absolute performance gains over the strongest baselines (≈5% accuracy, ≈6% coverage) are moderate.
