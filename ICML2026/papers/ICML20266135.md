## Problem

Existing scientific QA benchmarks (MMLU, GPQA, ScienceQA, SciBench) evaluate LLMs on static, single-domain, or one-shot instances, failing to capture the interactive, multi-constraint, interdisciplinary nature of real AI4S workflows. There is no systematic way to control how many domains are composed within a single reasoning session or to diagnose whether performance failures stem from missing domain knowledge versus limitations in joint cross-domain reasoning as composition order increases.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation unit is a multi-turn interactive session (typically 4–8 turns) where the model receives a scientific query at each turn along with the full conversation history, and must produce an answer in one of four task formats (multiple choice, factual QA, step-by-step reasoning, or code). Sessions are parameterized by composition order k∈{1,2,3,4} (number of domains), a per-turn difficulty target δ_t∈[0,1], and a per-turn domain-mixture weight vector w_t∈Δ^{k-1} specifying how strongly each domain should be engaged. Outputs are scored at three granularities: turn-level Recall and F1, session-level SessionSuccess τ (fraction of turns correct exceeding a threshold), and trajectory-level diagnostic labels identifying which failure phenomenon (error accumulation, reasoning break, or domain confusion) occurred at which turn.

## Method

XDomainBench formalizes dataset construction through two orthogonal controllable axes: composition order k (breadth of domain integration, with 20 single-domain pools, 24 two-domain, 10 three-domain, and 8 four-domain combinations selected via Sentence-BERT nearest-neighbor policy) and mixture structure (per-turn domain weight trajectories following one of three regimes: stable, gradual shift, or fluctuate, instantiated via simplex-constrained interpolation or bounded random walk). Each session also carries a difficulty trajectory following one of five patterns (stable, gradual increase/decrease, spike, fluctuate), estimated by a 70/30 convex combination of LLM rubric scores and embedding-based semantic complexity. A three-model judge ensemble validates that realized turn content matches intended difficulty and mixture targets within tolerance, with bounded retry budgets. The diagnostic framework then separates two failure mechanisms: a direct mechanism measured by comparing turn-1 performance on composed episodes against single-domain baselines (isolating immediate composition overhead), and an indirect mechanism where trajectory patterns trigger error accumulation, reasoning breaks, or domain confusion that compound across turns to cause session collapse.

## Results

Across all evaluated LLMs, SessionSuccess (S@τ) drops systematically as composition order increases from k=1 to k=4: large models decline from 38.7% to 27.1%, small models from 32.3% to 20.3%, and MoE models from 82.1% to 48.5%. The degradation accelerates non-linearly at higher orders, with steeper drops at k=3→4 than k=1→2. MoE models (Qwen3-Next-80B, Mixtral-8x7B) consistently outperform both large and small dense models across all composition orders, with Qwen3-Next-80B achieving 89.6% SessionSuccess at k=1 versus 42.5% for GPT-5.2. Turn-level Recall and F1 follow the same monotonic degradation pattern, and a judge-based semantic evaluation (Table 4) confirms the collapse is not an artifact of token-level matching.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a new benchmark, XDomainBench does not pit a proposed method against competing methods; instead it evaluates 14 LLMs (6 large, 6 small, 2 MoE) under a unified interactive protocol. The key comparison is across model architectures: MoE models dominate, with Qwen3-Next-80B achieving 89.6% SessionSuccess at k=1 (vs. 48.5% for the best large model, Claude-4.5 Sonnet) and maintaining 49.4% at k=4 (vs. 27.1% for the large-model mean). Small models degrade most steeply in relative terms (e.g., Gemma-2-2B-IT drops from 30.6% to 7.4%), while large models show moderate, more stable declines. The paper also positions XDomainBench against existing benchmarks (MMLU, GPQA, ScienceQA, SciBench, MRMR) in Table 1, showing it is the only benchmark supporting all five properties simultaneously: scientific focus, cross-domain composition, interactivity, turn-level diagnostics, and trajectory-level diagnostics. A robustness check (Table 4) confirms that replacing token-level Recall with judge-based semantic scoring preserves the same monotonic degradation trend (37.1%→26.8% vs. 31.3%→21.9%), ruling out surface-form brittleness as the cause of the observed collapse.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of compositional generalization (Lake & Baroni, 2018; Keysers et al., 2020) and scientific QA benchmarking (ScienceQA, GPQA, SciBench, MRMR), but departs from all of them by making the evaluation interactive (multi-turn), multi-domain (controlled composition order k=1–4), and diagnostically annotated with trajectory-level signals. It repurposes robustness-evaluation principles from Dynabench and Checklist (Kiela et al., 2021; Ribeiro et al., 2020) to frame performance degradation as a diagnosable phenomenon rather than a single accuracy number, and borrows embedding-based domain selection from sentence-embedding literature to construct semantically coherent cross-domain compositions. No prior benchmark simultaneously controls composition order, mixture structure, and trajectory patterns within an interactive protocol, which is the core positioning gap the authors identify.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides the first systematic evidence that LLM reasoning degrades non-linearly as scientific domain composition increases, and that this collapse has two separable causes (direct compositional overhead vs. indirect interaction-amplified failures) that can be diagnosed via trajectory patterns. The finding that MoE architectures dramatically outperform dense models of comparable scale on compositional tasks suggests that architecture, not just parameter count, is a critical axis for AI4S deployment. Open questions include whether retrieval-augmented or tool-augmented agents mitigate the collapse, whether pattern-aware training can teach models to recognize and recover from difficulty spikes and mixture shifts, and whether the closed-book protocol underestimates real-world performance. The benchmark's closed-book, no-tool design and limited code-task coverage are acknowledged limitations that future versions should address.
