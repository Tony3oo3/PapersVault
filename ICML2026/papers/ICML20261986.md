## Problem

Existing deep learning frameworks for protein design—protein language models and structure-based diffusion models—cannot rationally design multistate proteins with complex functions such as allosteric regulation, ligand discrimination, or conformational switching. PLMs offer only coarse-grained conditioning via family labels, while structure generators are constrained to single static structures, and no large-scale dataset pairing sequences with detailed functional behaviors exists to train data-driven multistate models. This leaves a fundamental gap: how to enable the rational design of proteins with novel, multi-state functions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a programmatic design specification consisting of an arbitrary number of states, each with a folding context (a set of fixed molecules such as ligands, metal ions, or DNA) and a set of loss functions evaluated on Boltz-1 outputs, along with a design mask and optional motif residue identities. The output is a single amino acid sequence (represented internally as a 20×L logit tensor) that jointly satisfies all state-specific constraints. Notably, the same sequence is co-folded with each state's molecular context, and the losses are evaluated on the cached Boltz-1 outputs from all states simultaneously, enabling compositional specifications of arbitrary complexity.

## Method

SWITCHCRAFT frames multistate protein design as gradient-based sequence optimization under a global constraint function parameterized by Boltz-1, a structure prediction model. Four composable loss types—motif scaffolding, ligand binding, conformational change (via Jensen-Shannon divergence of distograms across states), and intra-state contact confidence—are defined over Boltz-1 outputs and summed across all states to form the global objective. The sequence is optimized using a straight-through estimator that admits loss evaluation on the discrete (one-hot) sequence while propagating gradients through a soft categorical relaxation, with a pseudo-sequence constructed as a convex combination of hard, soft, and logit representations. A four-stage annealing schedule (Gumbel-softmax initialization, soft exploration with interpolating mixing coefficients, temperature annealing from 0.5 to 0.005, and final discrete refinement) progressively sharpens the sequence toward discrete residue identities over 240 steps. This differentiable-programming approach is fully general: any function describable as structural properties partitioned across multiple states can be specified as a set of losses and optimized without architecture changes.

## Results

On the positive/negative allostery benchmark (24 RFDiffusion motifs × 5 ligands, 100 designs each), SWITCHCRAFT achieves at least one successful design for 11 of 24 motifs, with per-motif success rates ranging from 0 to 26 out of 100 (e.g., 26/100 for Mg²⁺/1PRW, 20/100 for OQO/6E6R_long). For more complex specifications, success rates are lower but non-zero: 3/100 for motif switching, 10/558 for ligand modification (heme+O₂), 8/940 for induced binding (Ca²⁺/Top7), and 12/465 for three-state ligand discrimination. In the biosensor pipeline, 89/13,858 conformational switchers pass stringent structural criteria, and 44 of those yield valid cpGFP insertion sites with proper chromophore modulation, including a SAM biosensor with a 14.7 Å Glu74 shift.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is RFD3+TiedMPNN, a repurposed pipeline that generates structures with RFDiffusion3 and then applies Tied ProteinMPNN to sample sequences conditioned on multiple backbones. Across all 24 effector/motif combinations in Table 1, SwitchCraft achieves 183 total successful designs versus only 33 for RFD3+TiedMPNN, a roughly 5.5× advantage. SwitchCraft dominates on metal-ion effectors (Mg²⁺: 54 vs. 2; Zn²⁺: 38 vs. 5) and on the 6E6R motif family with OQO (45 vs. 5), while RFD3+TiedMPNN wins only on OQO/1PRW (4 vs. 0) and Zn²⁺/6E6R_short (1 vs. 0). The paper also validates that the design objective is causal: cross-screening (Table 3) shows a 3.01× fold change for positive allostery in positive-allostery designs (p = 2.9×10⁻²¹) and a 5.59× fold change for negative allostery in negative-allostery designs (p = 3.2×10⁻¹⁸), confirming that the observed state-switching behavior is not a random baseline artifact. Ablating the AntiMotifLoss (Table 2) reduces success rates by 2–5×, confirming that explicit disruption supervision is necessary.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the backpropagation-through-structure-prediction paradigm established by BoltzDesign-1 (Cho et al., 2025) from single-structure binder design to a general programmatic framework for multistate protein design, where a sequence is optimized to simultaneously satisfy arbitrary loss functions evaluated under multiple folding contexts via Boltz-1. It departs from prior multistate approaches—manual hinge/domain-linker constructions (Praetorius et al., 2023; Pillai et al., 2024), logit-tying methods like TiedMPNN and DynamicMPNN, and sequence-space diffusion models like ProDiT—by accepting ligand atoms as explicit inputs and formulating design as constraint satisfaction over an arbitrary number of states, rather than being restricted to specific motion types or lacking ligand conditioning. The motif specification sampling protocol is borrowed from Genie2 (Lin et al., 2024), and the four-stage annealing schedule and straight-through estimator follow BoltzDesign-1.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SWITCHCRAFT provides the first general-purpose, programmatic framework for de novo multistate protein design, opening a path toward rational design of complex functions (allostery, ligand discrimination, induced binding, biosensors) that were previously accessible only through labor-intensive manual design or directed evolution. However, all results are in silico with modest success rates (11/24 motifs for allostery, single-digit successes for more complex tasks), and no experimental validation is yet provided beyond preliminary induced-binding data. Key open questions include whether these designs fold and function as predicted in wet-lab settings, whether kinetics-based constraints can eliminate implausible state transitions, and whether the framework scales to truly complex mechanisms like motor proteins or multi-step enzymatic cycles.
