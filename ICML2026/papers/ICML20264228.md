## Problem

Translating natural-language descriptions of decision problems into executable optimization models remains a labor-intensive process requiring expert operations research knowledge. Prior LLM-based approaches—whether fine-tuned models or bespoke multi-agent pipelines—rely primarily on direct code generation without execution-aware validation, producing brittle outputs that are frequently syntactically invalid or semantically incorrect, and they lack the iterative simulator-optimizer feedback loops that practitioners use to uncover logical inconsistencies.

## Input / Output

The system takes a free-form natural-language problem description (e.g., a transportation or scheduling scenario with embedded numerical parameters) and produces a self-contained, executable Python optimization package that includes solver invocation, post-processing, and unit tests, along with the optimal decision vector, objective value, and solver termination status. An intermediate structured artifact—a JSON specification of decision variables, constraints, objective function, and exogenous parameters—is also generated and used to drive both the simulator and optimizer ACAs. The output code is guaranteed executable by construction due to sandboxed ACA execution, and the system additionally emits a structured feasibility/error report when the asymmetric validation loop detects constraint violations or objective mismatches.

## Method

NEMO treats autonomous coding agents (ACAs, instantiated via OpenHands) as first-class primitives in a Workspace-State paradigm where independently generated simulator and optimizer agents co-inhabit a persistent sandboxed environment and interact via executable artifacts rather than structured text messages. The core technical mechanism is an asymmetric validation loop: an independently generated imperative Python simulator (empirically 10 percentage points easier to generate correctly than the declarative optimizer) serves as a fixed executable reference that checks the optimizer's solution for feasibility and objective consistency, injecting structured error reports back into the optimizer's context for iterative refinement. Upstream, a hybrid component-wise MBR decoding strategy generates n parallel extractions, scores them by embedding-based consensus across structural components (constraints weighted 0.6, variables 0.2, objective 0.1, inputs 0.1), and re-ranks the top-q candidates with an LLM judge restricted to the original problem description. A diversity-aware retrieval module selects k few-shot examples from a 3,000-sample OptMATH memory bank using a greedy scoring function that balances cosine similarity to the query against redundancy with already-selected examples (λ=0.5), and a self-consistency mechanism aggregates T=3 parallel optimizer implementations via majority-voted solver status, objective-value clustering, and median selection.

## Results

NEMO achieves state-of-the-art accuracy on 8 of 9 optimization benchmarks (Standard settings), with headline results of 90.4% on OptiBench, 98.4% on NL4OPT, 82.9% on BWOR, and 63.0% on IndustryOR. The largest absolute gains are +21 pp on OptMATH-Bench (65.7% vs. 45.8% SIRL) and +27 pp on IndustryOR (63.0% vs. 44.0% LLMOPT). NEMO trails only on MAMO-Easy (83.4% vs. OptMATH's 89.9%) and MAMO-Complex (72.0% vs. LLMOPT's 85.8%) under Standard settings, though it recovers the lead on the curated MAMO-Complex subset (94.0% vs. SIRL's 72.4%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against agent-based baselines, NEMO dominates: it outperforms OptimAI by +8.1 pp on OptiBench, OptiMUS by +19.6 pp on NL4OPT and +9.1 pp on NLP4LP, OR-LLM-Agent by +27 pp on IndustryOR and +20.4 pp on MAMO-Complex, and CoE by +28.7 pp on ComplexOR. On BWOR, NEMO ties OR-LLM-Agent at 82.9%. Against training-based methods, NEMO wins decisively on OptMATH-Bench (+21 pp over SIRL) and IndustryOR (+19 pp over LLMOPT, +28 pp over SIRL on the curated subset), and matches or exceeds OptMATH on NL4OPT (98.4% vs. 95.9%). The two losses are to specialized training-based models on the MAMO competition subset: OptMATH leads on MAMO-Easy (89.9% vs. 83.4%) and LLMOPT leads on MAMO-Complex (85.8% vs. 72.0%), though NEMO recovers the MAMO-Complex lead on the curated SIRL test set (94.0% vs. 72.4%). A compute-matched Best-of-5 ACA baseline (same Claude 4.5 Sonnet backend, 5 independent calls) confirms NEMO's gains are architectural rather than compute-driven, with +7.3 pp on BWOR and +5.4 pp on OptMATH-Bench.

## Relation to Prior Work

NEMO departs from both dominant paradigms in LLM-based optimization modeling: agent-based frameworks (CoE, OptimAI, OR-LLM-Agent, OptiMUS) that use message-passing between specialized LLM roles, and training-based methods (ORLM, SIRL, LLMOPT, OptMATH) that fine-tune models on optimization-specific data. Its core conceptual departure is the 'Workspace-State paradigm,' where autonomous coding agents (ACAs) co-inhabit a persistent sandboxed environment and interact via executable artifacts rather than structured text, repurposing the OpenHands platform as a first-class abstraction for optimization. The asymmetric validation loop—using an independently generated imperative simulator to verify a declarative optimizer—draws on the practitioner's mental simulator-optimizer feedback loop but formalizes it as an automated, execution-grounded coordination mechanism not present in prior message-passing systems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NEMO demonstrates that execution-aware agentic architectures can match or exceed domain-specific fine-tuned models across nine benchmarks without any task-specific training, suggesting a more generalizable path for language-driven optimization. The 28-point gap on IndustryOR and the compute-matched baseline comparison (gains from architecture, not token volume) are practically significant for organizations without access to OR expertise. Key open questions remain: formal verification of formulation correctness (objective-value agreement is necessary but not sufficient), the 5–10 minute per-instance computational overhead limiting high-throughput use, and whether the rich execution-based feedback signals from the simulator-optimizer loop can be leveraged as training signals for future learning-based approaches.
