## Problem

Precision oncology requires jointly forecasting continuous biomarker trajectories and predicting censored clinical events from sparse, heterogeneous, multimodal longitudinal data, yet existing approaches either decouple these tasks, rely on fixed code-based vocabularies that limit out-of-distribution generalization, or cannot integrate complex modalities such as genetic mutation panels. No unified framework existed that could handle irregular clinical timelines across 20+ cancer indications while remaining flexible to new data sources like clinical trials.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263193/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Input is a serialized text prompt at weekly resolution containing static patient attributes, chronological visit history (labs, diagnoses, drug administrations, ECOG scores), genetic mutation details, and a repeated summary of the most recent critical observations to mitigate the 'lost-in-the-middle' effect, all truncated to 8000 tokens. For forecasting, the model generates M independent text completions that are deterministically decoded into numerical trajectories and averaged per variable and time point over a 13-week horizon. For landmark events, the model outputs three-class strings (occurred / not occurred / censored) whose length-normalized log-likelihoods are softmax-normalized into a continuous ranking score, with isotonic regression optionally applied post-hoc to enforce monotonic cumulative incidence.

## Method

TwinWeaver converts structured longitudinal patient histories into human-readable text prompts and fine-tunes a pretrained LLM (Llama 3.1 8B Instruct) with standard causal language modeling loss computed only on target completion tokens, enabling a single architecture to handle both continuous forecasting and discrete event prediction. Forecasting variables are sampled per training example with probability proportional to log(observation count × normalized copy-forward RMSE), biasing toward clinically volatile and frequently measured biomarkers; event horizons are sampled uniformly to act as data augmentation across arbitrary prognostic queries. The key architectural insight is that text serialization leverages the LLM's tokenization to natively represent irregular, sparse, multimodal clinical timelines without fixed code vocabularies, while multi-sample inference (M=3 at evaluation) and log-likelihood-based scoring bridge the gap between generative text output and calibrated numerical predictions. A reasoning extension further applies knowledge distillation from a Qwen3 80B teacher to synthesize structured chains-of-thought, followed by GRPO alignment with a deterministic negative-MAE reward to ground generated rationales in empirical forecast accuracy.

## Results

On the FH-FMI CGDB pan-cancer dataset (93,054 patients, 20 indications), GDT achieves a median MASE of 0.867 (IQR 0.186) for blood biomarker forecasting, significantly outperforming the strongest time-series baseline, multivariate TiDE (0.966; p < 0.001, Wilcoxon signed-rank test). For clinical event prediction (survival, progression, therapy switching), GDT achieves an average IPCW C-index of 0.703 versus 0.662 for Random Survival Forest, with statistical significance in 11 of 12 evaluated time points. On out-of-distribution clinical trials (POPLAR, IMpower130), fine-tuned GDT attains median MASE of 0.883 and 0.754, and IPCW C-index of 0.672 for event prediction, surpassing the strongest baseline (RSF, 0.652) while zero-shot GDT matches fully trained baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GDT outperforms multivariate TiDE (Das et al., 2023) on forecasting across all 20 indications, achieving median MASE 0.867 vs. 0.966 (p < 0.001), with the gap widening to 0.830 vs. 0.947 on the top 30 most volatile biomarkers. Against the Chronos and Chronos Bolt time-series foundation models (Ansari et al., 2024; Hoo et al., 2024), GDT consistently achieves lower MASE, with Chronos performing best on a few stable variables but losing on dynamic ones. For event prediction, GDT surpasses Random Survival Forest (Ishwaran et al., 2008) with an average IPCW C-index of 0.703 vs. 0.662 across survival, progression, and therapy switching, and also outperforms RSF on out-of-distribution clinical trials (0.672 vs. 0.652). The EHR foundation model CLMBR-T (Wornow et al., 2023) underperforms GDT on event prediction despite a larger pretraining cohort (2.5M patients), likely due to its fixed vocabulary covering only 11 of 283 therapies in the dataset. GDT also retains its advantage when trained on the same 2,000-patient subset as the baselines, confirming the gain is architectural rather than data-driven.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-based patient trajectory forecasting line initiated by DT-GPT (Makarov et al., 2025) from single-indication to pan-cancer scope, while adopting the landmarking framework (Van Houwelingen, 2007) to unify censored event prediction with continuous biomarker forecasting in a single generative model. It departs from EHR foundation models (CLMBR-T, MOTOR, ETHOS) that rely on fixed code-based vocabularies by serializing multimodal clinical data—including genetic mutations—into free-form text, thereby avoiding vocabulary constraints that limit out-of-distribution adaptability. The reasoning extension borrows GRPO (Shao et al., 2024b) from the mathematical reasoning literature and repurposes it with a deterministic MAE-based reward to ground generated clinical rationales in numerical accuracy rather than plausibility.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical, open-source path toward interpretable patient digital twins in oncology, with the zero-shot generalization to unseen clinical trials being particularly relevant for drug development where historical data is sparse. Key limitations include weak metastasis prediction (only 3 indications with sufficient data), lack of monotonicity guarantees without isotonic post-processing, and the absence of prospective clinical validation. Open questions include whether the text-serialization approach scales to imaging and spatial omics modalities, how context-length truncation affects long-horizon predictions, and whether the uniform horizon sampling introduces calibration bias at extreme time points.
