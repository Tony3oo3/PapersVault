## Problem

LLM-based agents are already operating in production across finance, healthcare, and other critical domains, yet the field lacks systematic understanding of what technical methods actually make these deployments successful. Prior work has surveyed business executives, analyzed marketing materials, or documented individual systems, but no study has collected first-hand engineering-level data from the teams building deployed agents. This paper addresses that gap by asking: what do production agents look like technically, how are they evaluated, and what challenges do practitioners face?

## Input / Output

The study consumes two data sources: 20 semi-structured interviews (30–90 minutes each, with teams of 2–5 engineers) and a 47-question Qualtrics survey with dynamic branching, distributed across professional networks and academic events from July–October 2025, yielding 306 valid responses. Survey responses are filtered to 86 systems explicitly in production or pilot phases for the main analysis, while full unfiltered data appear in supplementary material. Outputs are organized around four research questions (applications, models/architectures, evaluation, challenges) and characterized across 17 design dimensions, with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (1,000 resamples) for all categorical comparisons.

## Method

MAP is a mixed-methods empirical study combining grounded-theory qualitative analysis with large-scale quantitative survey characterization. For interviews, the team used snowball sampling to recruit 20 deployment teams spanning five sectors and startup-to-enterprise scales, then applied open coding (three independent coders per interview, disagreements resolved via peer debriefing) followed by focused coding to extract recurring themes. For the survey, free-text domain responses were normalized using LOTUS semantic aggregation to derive candidate categories, then labeled by three independent annotators (Cohen's κ = 0.636) with a fourth adjudicator for disagreements. The key analytical contribution is a cross-validation between the 20 qualitative cases and 86 quantitative survey systems, revealing that practitioners achieve reliability through system-level design choices—bounded autonomy (68% execute ≤10 steps), prompting over weight tuning (70%), human-in-the-loop evaluation (74%), and custom implementations over frameworks (85%)—rather than through algorithmic or model-level advances.

## Results

Across 86 deployed agent systems and 20 in-depth case studies, the study finds that production agents favor simplicity and control: 68% execute fewer than 10 steps before human intervention, 70% use off-the-shelf models without weight tuning, and 79% rely on manual or manual+LLM prompt construction. Human-in-the-loop evaluation dominates (74%), with LLM-as-a-judge as a secondary method (52%), while 75% of teams forgo formal benchmarking entirely. Reliability (consistent correct behavior over time) is the top development challenge, ranked #1 by 38% of practitioners, far exceeding governance (3%) or compliance (17%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical characterization study rather than a method paper, so it does not compare a proposed method against baselines on a benchmark. Instead, the key contrast is between production practice and prevailing research narratives. Practitioners deliberately choose simple, controllable approaches (prompting closed-source models, bounded workflows, human oversight) over the algorithmic advances highlighted in research (RL, open-ended autonomous planning, automated prompt optimization). For example, despite strong RL benchmark results in the literature, only 1 of 20 case studies uses an RL-trained model, and 70% avoid all post-training. Similarly, 85% of case studies build custom implementations rather than using popular frameworks like LangChain, and 68% constrain agents to fewer than 10 autonomous steps, whereas research prototypes explore substantially higher step counts. The paper positions these gaps as underexplored research opportunities rather than failures of either side.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new empirical ground rather than extending a specific technical lineage. It distinguishes itself from commercial agent surveys (LangChain, PwC, McKinsey) that focus on business and organizational perspectives, from academic literature surveys that synthesize published architectures and taxonomies, and from single-system case reports (Anthropic, OpenAI, Cursor) that document individual deployments. By combining 20 in-depth practitioner interviews with a 306-person survey filtered to 86 deployed systems, MAP is the first study to collect engineering-level technical data—model choices, workflow structures, evaluation methods, and operational constraints—directly from teams running agents in production.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that production agents achieve reliability through simple, controllable system-level design (structured workflows, bounded autonomy, human-in-the-loop) rather than through algorithmic advances like RL or automated prompt optimization—exposes a significant research-practice gap and redirects attention toward underexplored areas such as sample-efficient post-training, runtime correctness verification, and human-augmented agent architectures. Limitations include geographic concentration in the Americas, participation bias toward professional networks, and a narrow temporal window (April–November 2025) in a rapidly evolving field. Open questions include how to reduce dependence on human oversight as deployments scale to higher-stakes external settings, how to create reusable benchmarks for domain-specific agent tasks, and how to make agent performance robust to upstream model provider updates.
