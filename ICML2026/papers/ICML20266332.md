## Problem

Existing computational theories of spatial coding (grid cells, place cells, head-direction cells) explain how these neural representations emerge but treat them as static coordinates for self-localization, leaving the critical question of how these codes mechanistically guide downstream navigation unaddressed. Classical robotics compounds this gap by separating mapping (SLAM) from planning (A*, Dijkstra), introducing latency and fragility under perturbations. The paper asks whether a single spectral operator can simultaneously generate the spatial code and the navigation policy, unifying representation and action.

## Input / Output

For the navigation policy, the agent consumes the first K=200 Laplacian eigenfunction values at its current position and at the goal position (computed from the known maze graph with Neumann boundary conditions), and outputs a 2D velocity vector obtained as a local weighted sum of eigenfunction gradients with goal-dependent coefficients φ_k(x_tgt)/λ_k. For the visual learning experiment, the input is a stack of 3 egocentric RGB frames (64×64) from a photorealistic 3D MemoryMaze environment with no positional supervision, and the output is a 128-dim CNN embedding that spontaneously recovers Laplacian-like spatial tuning. The RL variant feeds the same eigenfunction features into an MLP Q-learning agent, producing discrete maze actions.

## Method

The framework derives spatial codes as the solution to minimizing Dirichlet energy under unit-norm and orthogonality constraints, which via calculus of variations yields the Laplacian eigenvalue problem −Δφ = λφ. On the pose manifold Ω×S¹, the joint Laplacian is constructed as a Kronecker sum of positional and directional marginal Laplacians, whose separable eigenfunctions naturally factorize into pure spatial (grid, band, boundary), pure head-direction, and conjunctive cell types. The navigation policy is the gradient of the Green's function G(x, x_tgt) = Σ_k (1/λ_k) φ_k(x) φ_k(x_tgt), which solves the Poisson equation with Neumann boundary conditions and is harmonic away from the goal, guaranteeing no interior traps. The 1/λ_k spectral tilt amplifies low-frequency modes for global planning while suppressing high-frequency noise, and the policy is purely reactive—requiring only local gradient evaluation with O(K) per-step cost and zero replanning. For learning, the Green's function serves as a potential-based reward shaping signal in Q-learning, and a CNN trained with a temporal VICReg-like objective on raw visual frames recovers the same spectral structure without coordinate access.

## Results

In non-convex maze navigation, the Green's Function Policy achieves 100% success rate across all maze sizes with a sub-optimality ratio (path length / A* optimal length) stabilizing around 1.08, while incurring zero replanning cost under stochastic perturbations (ε up to 0.5) where A* replanning count grows rapidly. In goal-conditioned RL on a 21×21 random maze over 5×10⁵ steps, Green's-function reward shaping rapidly approaches near-perfect test success, whereas Euclidean shaping shows only transient partial improvement and both L₂ spectral-distance and sparse-reward baselines remain near zero. In the fixed-goal setting, Green's-function shaping reaches ~100% success within 2×10⁴ steps, substantially faster than the Euclidean baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the Green's Function Policy against A* (complete-map shortest path) for reactive navigation: both achieve 100% success, but Green's function matches A* path quality (ratio ≈ 1.08) while requiring zero replanning under noise, whereas A* replanning count scales poorly with perturbation magnitude. In the RL reward-shaping comparison (Fig. 4), Green's-function shaping dominates all three baselines in the goal-conditioned setting: it reaches and maintains near-perfect test success, while Euclidean shaping (Ψ = −‖x − x_tgt‖₂) shows only a transient partial improvement before plateauing, and both the L₂ spectral-distance shaping (same eigenbasis, uniform weights) and the sparse-reward baseline remain near zero success throughout 5×10⁵ training steps. In the fixed-goal setting, Green's-function shaping converges to ~100% within 2×10⁴ steps, faster than Euclidean shaping which eventually learns but starts more slowly. The key differentiator is that the 1/λₖ spectral tilt in the Green's function provides a boundary-constrained, geometry-aware progress signal that transfers across changing start–goal pairs, a property absent from both Euclidean distance and uniformly weighted spectral distance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate research threads: (1) normative theories of spatial coding (Burak & Fiete, Sorscher et al., Dordek et al.) that explain grid/place cell emergence but treat codes as static localization coordinates; (2) harmonic-function navigation (Connolly & Grupen, 1993) that avoids local minima but requires solving PDEs online; and (3) spectral RL methods (Proto-value Functions, Successor Representations) that use Laplacian eigenfunctions as basis features for value approximation. The key departure is showing that the same Laplacian eigenbasis that explains the taxonomy of entorhinal spatial cells also directly yields a Green's-function navigation policy via a closed-form spectral expansion, eliminating the need for a separate search module. The RL experiments build on potential-based reward shaping (Ng et al., 1999), and the visual learning section uses VICReg-style self-supervised objectives (Bardes et al., 2021) to recover spectral representations from raw pixels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, single-objective derivation (Dirichlet energy minimization under efficiency constraints) that simultaneously explains the diversity of spatial tuning cells and produces a geometry-aware navigation policy, addressing a long-standing gap between 'mapping' and 'planning' in spatial cognition. However, the practical applicability is currently limited: the Green's function policy requires known environment geometry (the maze graph) to compute eigenfunctions, and the visual learning section demonstrates representation recovery but does not close the full pixel-to-action loop. Open questions include whether the framework scales to 3D continuous environments, how representations adapt online to changing boundaries, and whether the spectral basis can be learned end-to-end without ground-truth coordinates. The unification of Successor Representations, Proto-value Functions, and the Green's function as dual descriptions of the same object is a clean theoretical contribution that may inform future work on predictive spatial models.
