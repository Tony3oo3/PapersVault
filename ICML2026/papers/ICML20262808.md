## Problem

Despite strong empirical success, iterative self-improvement for LLMs lacks a finite-sample, task-centric theoretical account. Two practical questions remain unanswered: (1) on a single task, when does multi-step self-improvement actually occur and why does it eventually saturate? (2) across multiple tasks, when does an easy-to-hard curriculum provably outperform training on a fixed mixture under the same budget?

## Input / Output

The procedure consumes questions sampled from one or more task distributions, the current LLM's parameters, a binary verifiable reward function, and two sample budgets: n (total questions per iteration) and m (candidate answers generated per question). It produces updated LLM parameters via empirical MLE on the reward-filtered subset, together with explicit finite-sample lower bounds on expected reward (Pass@1) and quantifiable feasibility/improvement conditions that depend on initialization performance, task difficulty ratios (β′, β), and the budget parameter ν = 1/√n. A key structural feature is that training data is endogenous—generated by the model itself—so the effective sample size n_t is random and coupled to the current model's quality.

## Method

Each self-improvement iteration is modeled as maximum-likelihood fine-tuning on a reward-filtered distribution D′ induced by the model's own generation and an external verifier. The single-step analysis (Theorem 4.1) yields a lower bound on expected reward governed by the ratio Z/α (global-to-minimal acceptance rate) and the effective sample size, revealing that larger n and m tighten the bound. For multi-step iteration under binary rewards, Corollary 4.4 reduces the dynamics to an iterated map F(x) = 1 − γ − c_δν/(c√(x − c_δ′ν)), whose monotone-increase region is a finite interval I(1,ν) that shrinks with ν—explaining why moderate task difficulty and sufficient budget are necessary for sustained improvement, and why performance saturates below 1−γ. For the easy-to-hard curriculum, Theorem 5.2 compares L iterations of staged difficulty against a fixed uniform mixture, deriving feasibility conditions {M_i < 0} and a strict improvement condition N < 0; the analysis shows that moderate adjacent difficulty ratios (intermediate β′) minimize the required sample budget, with a sharp phase transition as n decreases toward a critical threshold.

## Results

On GSM8K, QWEN3-8B achieves an initial Pass@1 of 27.52% with a +3.15% absolute improvement after three self-improvement iterations; on the easy split of DeepMind Mathematics, the same model starts at 85.11% and gains +7.67%. Across six base models (Llama-3.2-1B to Qwen3-32B), self-improvement is most effective at moderate task difficulty (e.g., Qwen2.5-3B on GSM8K: +3.23% from 10.39% initial) and degrades or reverses when tasks are too easy (Llama-3.2-1B on GSM8K: −0.28%) or too hard (Qwen3-32B on DeepMind Math: −1.20%). Increasing the question budget n from 600 to 2400 improves GSM8K gains from −4.06% to +3.15%, and increasing per-question answer budget m from 1 to 16 improves gains from +3.15% to +5.34%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary empirical comparison is between an easy-to-hard curriculum (progressively sampling from easier to harder difficulty levels across iterations) and a fixed uniform mixture baseline (sampling uniformly from all difficulty levels every iteration), using the DeepMind Mathematics dataset's native easy/medium/hard split. Across 10 modules in algebra, arithmetic, and numbers categories, the easy-to-hard curriculum yields a strictly positive final Pass@1 gap over the baseline at intermediate difficulty-separation parameters (ρ ≈ 0.4–0.8), with the largest gains reaching +17.08% (add_sub_multiple at ρ=0.8) and +12.32% (mul_div_multiple at ρ=0.8). However, the advantage is not monotone in ρ: at ρ=0 (pure baseline) the gap is zero by construction, and at ρ=1 (fully staged) the gap often shrinks or turns negative (e.g., nearest_integer_root drops to −6.64%), confirming the theoretical prediction that moderate adjacent-task difficulty ratios are optimal. On the synthetic shortest-path task, the easy-to-hard gap over baseline clusters in a narrow question-budget range (n ≈ 2000–3000) where the curriculum first becomes advantageous, matching the predicted phase-transition behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formalizes the generate-and-filter self-improvement loop popularized by STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) and subsequent work (Xin et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2025) into a finite-sample maximum-likelihood framework, building on the finite model class assumption from Huang et al. (2025). It departs from prior theoretical treatments (Huang et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2026; Mohri et al., 2025) that analyze self-improvement through probability-mass sharpening, solver-verifier gaps, or coherence at the continuous limit, by instead providing explicit finite-sample reward lower bounds that capture the discrete, multi-step nature of practical pipelines. The easy-to-hard curriculum analysis extends the single-task theory to multi-task scheduling, going beyond the empirical observations of Ren et al. (2025), Koh et al. (2026), and Lee et al. (2025) to derive quantifiable conditions under which curricula provably outperform fixed-mixture training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first finite-sample, task-centric theory that answers two practically critical questions: when does iterative self-improvement actually help, and when does easy-to-hard scheduling provably beat fixed-mixture training. The identification of a 'moderate difficulty' regime (neither too easy nor too hard) and a 'moderate difficulty ratio' regime for curricula provides actionable guidance for practitioners designing post-training pipelines, and the phase-transition behavior with respect to sample budget explains why small-budget self-improvement often fails in practice. Key limitations include the finite model class assumption, the binary reward setting, and the relatively small neighborhood assumption around initialization; open questions include whether the framework extends to continuous rewards, multi-step verification, or settings where the verifier is imperfect.
