## Problem

Agentic AI systems that orchestrate LLMs and tools must make sequential decisions under uncertainty—routing, stopping, escalation, and budget allocation—but current practice relies on fixed workflows or prompting heuristics that do not explicitly account for asymmetric costs, correlated evidence, or epistemic uncertainty at the task level. This position paper argues that Bayesian decision theory is the appropriate formalism for the orchestration layer, while LLMs themselves need not be Bayesian belief-updating engines, since their token-level predictive distributions do not align with the task-level latent variables that drive control decisions.

## Input / Output

The Bayesian controller consumes free-form agent and tool messages (code snippets, static-analysis warnings, retrieved documents, diagnostic logs) as noisy observations, along with task specifications, per-agent cost parameters, and accumulated interaction history. It produces a low-dimensional posterior belief state over task-relevant latent variables—such as a binary code-testing outcome, a discrete hypothesis, or cross-task agent competence parameters—and selects orchestration actions (query next agent, stop, escalate to human) by comparing posterior expected utility or value of information against query costs. LLMs remain black-box predictors throughout; their outputs are mapped to likelihood information via learned observation models rather than being interpreted as Bayesian posteriors.

## Method

The paper proposes a Bayesian control layer that maintains a belief state over low-dimensional, decision-relevant latent variables and updates it via composite-likelihood (pseudolikelihood) updates with agent-specific reliability weights α_i that temper each source's likelihood contribution, preventing overconfident posteriors from noisy or correlated evidence. Agent reliabilities are tracked online using an exponential-weights scheme over cumulative log-loss, mapping to bounded tempering exponents in a generalized Bayes / power-posterior framework. Action selection is governed by posterior expected utility or value-of-information criteria: an additional tool call is justified only when its expected improvement in outcome outweighs its cost, and the system can abstain or escalate when posterior confidence is insufficient. Three concrete design patterns are illustrated—multi-agent code generation with a binary outcome belief, multi-agent deliberation over a discrete hypothesis space, and cross-task Bayesian routing via Thompson sampling over agent competence parameters—each treating LLMs as non-Bayesian evidence sources whose outputs are calibrated against measurable task outcomes.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not report quantitative experimental results, benchmarks, or performance metrics. Instead, it provides three illustrative design patterns (multi-agent code generation, multi-agent deliberation, and Bayesian routing) that demonstrate how a Bayesian control layer can maintain belief states over task-level latent variables and select actions via posterior expected utility or value-of-information criteria. The paper's contribution is a set of seven desirable properties for Bayesian agentic control and a call to action for benchmarking, modeling, engineering, and theory research directions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare its proposed Bayesian orchestration framework against empirical baselines or report head-to-head performance numbers. The paper discusses alternative views qualitatively in Section 3: (1) making LLMs themselves Bayesian belief-updating engines, which the authors argue is computationally infeasible and conceptually nontrivial; (2) heuristic or prompting-based orchestration (e.g., chain-of-thought, ReAct), which the authors acknowledge as practical engineering baselines but argue become insufficient as horizons lengthen and stakes increase; and (3) alternative decision frameworks such as robust control, RL planning, and bandit formulations without explicit posteriors, which the authors contend lack the explicit uncertainty-to-utility coupling needed for value-of-information-based stopping and escalation. No quantitative comparison is provided, and the paper explicitly positions its examples as 'suggestive starting points' rather than fully instantiated algorithms to be benchmarked.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper draws on classical Bayesian decision theory (Berger, 1985; Smith, 2010) and value-of-information frameworks (Stratonovich, 1965; Howard, 1966) to argue that Bayesian structure should be placed at the orchestration/control layer of agentic AI systems rather than inside the LLMs themselves. The concrete belief-update mechanism combines composite-likelihood (pseudolikelihood) updates (Varin et al., 2011) with power-posterior / generalized Bayes tempering (Bissiri et al., 2016) and exponential-weights online learning for reliability tracking (Freund & Schapire, 1997). The paper explicitly departs from the Bayesian deep learning tradition (which targets parameter posteriors) and from heuristic prompting-based orchestration (ReAct, Toolformer, chain-of-thought), positioning Bayesian control as a complementary design principle for the system layer that composes non-Bayesian LLM components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By separating prediction (LLM) from decision-making (orchestration), the paper provides a concrete architectural template—belief state, observation model, reliability-weighted updates, utility-based stopping—that practitioners can implement without requiring LLMs to be internally Bayesian. Key open questions include: how to handle observation-model misspecification from high-dimensional free-form agent messages, how to pool correlated evidence from agents sharing architectures or retrieval pipelines, and how to scale multi-step value-of-information computation to combinatorial tool spaces under tight latency budgets. The paper acknowledges that if constituent LLMs are poorly calibrated, the Bayesian controller's posterior inherits that miscalibration, making reliability modeling and likelihood tempering critical but underdeveloped components.
