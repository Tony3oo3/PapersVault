## Problem

AI model capabilities are accelerating at near-exponential rates while U.S. federal AI safety legislation remains largely stalled, with only 4.23% of proposed AI bills reaching a terminal outcome. No prior work has systematically identified which factors cause legislative stalling at distinct procedural stages, leaving policymakers without evidence-based guidance on how to close the development-regulation gap.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266435/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes a curated dataset of 150 U.S. Congressional AI bills (2017–2025) with 12 tabular features per bill (chamber of origin, sponsor party, bipartisan status, number of sponsors, and binary sub-field indicators), where sub-field labels were generated via GPT-4o-assisted classification followed by human audit (94% accuracy on a 50-bill sample). The model produces stage-specific hurdle coefficients with bootstrapped confidence intervals and FDR-corrected p-values, alongside an aggregate action-rate metric and a set of policy recommendations structured around dedicated committees, preemptive enactment, and sunset clauses.

## Method

The paper implements a Sequential Binary Hurdle Model (Two-Stage Choice Model) using penalized logistic regression with L2 (ridge) regularization to disentangle predictors of initial legislative engagement from those of late-stage advancement. Hurdle 1 (n=115) models the binary transition from inaction to any committee action; Hurdle 2 (n=102) models the conditional transition from committee to advanced or resolved, given Hurdle 1 clearance. Both stages are trained with scikit-learn (C=1.0, lbfgs solver, balanced class weights, 2000 max iterations) and evaluated via 1000 bootstrap resamples with 80/20 train-test splits, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction applied across the 12 features per stage. The key empirical finding is a stage-specific reversal: public-interest sub-fields (Deepfakes, Job Security) significantly predict Hurdle 1 clearance, while structural factors (number of sponsors, chamber of origin) predict Hurdle 2 failure, with no single factor dominating both stages.

## Results

Across 150 U.S. Congressional AI bills (2017–2025), only 4.23% reach a terminal outcome (vs. 6.25% for all congressional bills), with 59.3% stalling at the committee/subcommittee stage. At the Engagement Gate (Hurdle 1), Deepfake-related bills (coefficient = 1.5536, p = 1.21×10⁻⁶) and Job Security bills (coefficient = 1.2298, p = 0.0005) are significantly more likely to receive initial committee action, surviving FDR correction. At the Advancement Gate (Hurdle 2), policy sub-fields lose all predictive power, while structural factors—number of sponsors (coefficient = −0.9765, p = 0.0102) and chamber of origin (coefficient = −1.0693, p = 0.0416)—emerge as significant correlates of stalling, though neither survives FDR correction (Q = 0.0815 and Q = 0.2499). Bootstrap test accuracy is 64.6% (Hurdle 1) and 63.6% (Hurdle 2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper with no competing method baselines; the closest quantitative comparison is between AI-specific bills and the general congressional bill population. AI bills achieve a 4.23% terminal-outcome rate (SD 6.32%) versus the 6.25% congressional average (SD 1.92%), indicating both lower throughput and greater year-to-year volatility. The stage-specific hurdle model reveals a clear dissociation: public-interest topics (Deepfakes, Job Security) drive early engagement but are irrelevant at the committee-to-floor transition, while structural variables (sponsor count, chamber) drive late-stage failure but show no effect at the engagement gate. No single factor predicts stalling across both stages, supporting the paper's argument that the legislative bottleneck is not uniform but stage-specific. The authors note that structural coefficients at Hurdle 2 do not survive FDR correction, tempering causal interpretation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies the Sequential Binary Hurdle Model (Cragg, 1971; Krutz, 2005) — a two-stage choice framework well-established in political science — to a new domain: AI-specific legislative stalling in the U.S. Congress. It builds on the 'pacing problem' literature (Marchant et al., 2011) documenting technology outpacing regulation, and on prior work on cosponsorship dynamics (Sotoudeh et al., 2024; Kessler & Krehbiel, 1996; Riker, 1962) to interpret structural predictors. The paper's primary empirical contribution is the first comprehensive dataset of 150 U.S. AI bills (2017–2025) with a novel sub-field taxonomy and manually annotated legislative endpoints, extending prior bill-tracking efforts that lacked systematic categorization or endpoint coding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The stage-specific finding — that public-interest topics (deepfakes, job security) predict initial committee engagement while structural factors (sponsor count, chamber of origin) predict late-stage advancement failure — offers a concrete diagnostic for why AI safety bills stall, moving beyond generic 'legislative inertia' explanations. However, the structural predictors do not survive FDR correction, and the dataset is limited to 150 bills with correlational (not causal) inferences, leaving open whether dedicated AI committees would genuinely reduce pigeonholing or whether the 4.23% action rate reflects a structural floor for novel policy domains. For AI safety practitioners, the paper's policy prescriptions (preemptive enactment with capability thresholds, 5-year sunset clauses, independent safety agencies) provide a concrete institutional blueprint, but their political feasibility remains untested.
