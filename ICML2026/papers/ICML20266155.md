## Problem

Existing retrieval-based schema linking methods for Text-to-SQL suffer from structural blindness: by prioritizing elements with high textual similarity, they discard semantically-thin but topologically-critical bridge tables, severing the foreign-key pathways required for multi-hop JOINs. This creates a fundamental paradox where optimizing for local semantic relevance compromises the global topological connectivity that executable SQL demands, with schema linking deficits driving over 60% of execution errors in large-scale deployments.

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural language query and a relational database schema (tables, columns, primary/foreign key constraints) as input. The schema is preprocessed offline into a table-centric heterogeneous graph with dual-scale similarity edge weights and Leiden community partitions. The output is a minimal connected sub-schema — a set of tables and columns forming a topologically valid subgraph where all primary and foreign keys are preserved, irrelevant columns are pruned, and the subgraph is guaranteed to contain valid JOIN pathways between all semantically relevant tables. Unlike standard retrieval outputs (a flat ranked list), the output is structurally constrained to be connected.

## Method

Graph-Link reformulates schema linking from pointwise retrieval into a Node-Weighted Steiner Tree (NWST) problem, where the objective is to find a connected subgraph minimizing joint semantic cost and topological resistance. The framework operates in two phases: an offline phase builds a hierarchical schema graph with dual-scale similarity edges (balancing table-level and column-level cosine similarity) and partitions it into communities via the Leiden algorithm; an online phase performs (1) LLM-guided query decomposition routed to relevant communities, (2) dual-perception anchor localization scoring tables by semantic similarity multiplied by topological centrality, (3) Steiner Tree backbone induction via a 2-approximation (metric closure over anchors, MST construction, and physical path unfolding to recover bridge tables), and (4) LLM-critic column pruning that preserves all PK/FK columns while removing semantically irrelevant attributes. The key differentiator is that the Steiner Tree formulation explicitly recovers structurally necessary but semantically silent intermediate tables that purely semantic matching would miss, while the hierarchical community structure constrains the NP-hard optimization to a tractable search space.

## Results

On BIRD-Dev, Graph-Link (Qwen3-max) achieves a schema linking Hit Rate of 90.8%, Recall of 95.9%, and F2 of 89.4%, outperforming the strongest baseline MCS-SQL (81.3% Hit) by 9.5 percentage points. On Spider 2.0-Lite, Graph-Link reaches 80.3% Hit Rate and 86.0% F2. End-to-end, Graph-Link attains 68.5% Execution Accuracy on BIRD-Dev (vs. 66.0% for MCS-SQL), with a particularly large 13.8-point gain on complex multi-hop queries (58.6% vs. 44.8%), while using only ~20,800 tokens versus ~63,800 for MCS-SQL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Graph-Link outperforms all four baselines across every reported metric on both benchmarks. Against DIN-SQL, Graph-Link improves Hit Rate from 57.7% to 90.8% (+33.1 pp) and end-to-end EX from 52.4% to 68.5% on BIRD-Dev. Against MAC-SQL, the gains are 29.7 pp in Hit Rate and 13.7 pp in EX, with the largest gap on Challenge queries (58.6% vs. 32.9% EX). Against MCS-SQL—the closest competitor—Graph-Link leads by 9.5 pp in Hit Rate (90.8% vs. 81.3%) and 2.5 pp in overall EX (68.5% vs. 66.0%), but the advantage widens dramatically on complex multi-hop queries to 13.8 pp (58.6% vs. 44.8% EX) while using roughly one-third the tokens (~20,800 vs. ~63,800). Against RSL-SQL, Graph-Link gains 13.2 pp in Hit Rate and 5.1 pp in EX. The consistent pattern is that Graph-Link's advantage grows with query complexity, confirming that Steiner-tree-based connectivity enforcement is most beneficial precisely where retrieval-based baselines suffer from structural blindness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing the Steiner Tree optimization from GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024) and classical database keyword search (Bhalotia et al., 2002) into the schema-linking sub-problem of Text-to-SQL, a context where prior graph-based methods (RAT-SQL, SGU-SQL, SchemaGraphSQL) either relied on trained GNN encoders or pairwise shortest-path heuristics without a global connectivity guarantee. It departs from the dominant retrieval/ranking paradigm (DIN-SQL, MAC-SQL, MCS-SQL) by replacing pointwise semantic scoring with a deterministic constrained subgraph induction objective, and from prior graph approaches by combining hierarchical community detection (Leiden) with a 2-approximation Steiner Tree solver as a lightweight inference-time mechanism rather than a learned component. The framework is thus a synthesis: dense-retrieval embeddings for semantic grounding, community structure for search-space control, and combinatorial optimization for topological completeness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The structural-blindness problem is well-motivated and practically critical: in production databases with hundreds of tables, missing a single bridge table guarantees execution failure, and the paper shows this accounts for over 60% of errors in large-scale deployments. The 13.8% improvement on complex multi-hop queries and the near-flat performance curve across complexity levels (83.1% Hit on >3-table queries vs. 10.6% for hybrid retrieval) suggest that explicit topological reasoning is not merely beneficial but necessary for robust Text-to-SQL. Key limitations include dependence on explicit foreign-key constraints (limiting applicability to denormalized or weakly-structured schemas), added computational overhead from graph traversal, and the absence of evaluation on truly large-scale (100+ table) public benchmarks. Open questions include whether incremental community re-partitioning can handle dynamic enterprise schemas, and whether execution-feedback loops could refine the Steiner-tree resistance weights to close the precision gap.
