## Problem

Classical multivariate EDAs for discrete black-box optimization rely on learning explicit variable dependency graphs (DAGs) at each generation, an NP-hard structure-learning problem that is costly and may miss complex interactions. While neural autoregressive models can bypass explicit graph learning, they require committing to a fixed variable ordering, and no prior work has explored order-invariant training or the interaction between RL-based policy updates and varying generation orders in the EDA setting.

## Input / Output

The method takes a black-box objective function f over a discrete search space X = X_1 × ... × X_n (binary or categorical variables) and a maximum evaluation budget (e.g., 10,000 function calls). At each EDA generation, it samples a population of λ candidate solutions by sequentially assigning variable values under a randomly drawn generation order σ, where each neural network g_θ_i receives a fixed-size n-dimensional input vector with a causal mask zeroing out variables not yet assigned. The output is a complete solution vector x ∈ X and its fitness f(x); the generative model is then updated via policy-gradient steps over multiple epochs per generation.

## Method

The approach parameterizes the joint distribution as a product of per-variable conditional distributions, each computed by a dedicated MLP that takes a causally-masked partial solution as input. Training uses a GRPO-style objective with rank-based scale-invariant advantages (invariant under monotone transformations of f) and KL regularization against the previous generation's policy, eliminating the need for a critic network. The key innovation is sampling independent random permutations σ_G (generation order) and σ_T (training order) from the uniform distribution over all n! orderings: during generation, different σ_G values produce diverse populations (an ensemble effect), while during training, varying σ_T across epochs acts as an information-preserving structural dropout that encourages the network to identify dominant variable dependencies without overfitting to any particular ordering. The PPO surrogate objective is extended to handle mismatched generation and training masks via importance sampling ratios where numerator and denominator apply different causal masks, with a theoretical convergence analysis provided in the infinite-data/infinite-capacity regime.

## Results

On QUBO, NK, and NK3 instances of sizes n ∈ {64, 128, 256}, (σ,σ)-RL-EDA frequently achieves the best performance among 506 competing algorithms (503 from Nevergrad + PBIL, MIMIC, BOA) on larger instances (n=128 and n=256) after 10,000 objective function evaluations, without requiring any hyperparameter changes across landscape types (K=1 to K=8). On the NAS-Bench-101 real-world benchmark, the method achieves the best average accuracy at both small (1,000) and large (10,000) evaluation budgets. The method consistently avoids catastrophic failures across all tested problem types, though it may require more evaluations to converge than some competitors under very tight budgets (e.g., 1,000 calls on QUBO/NK).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the 503 Nevergrad algorithms (evolutionary, memetic, and ML-driven metaheuristics), (σ,σ)-RL-EDA frequently obtains the best scores on larger instances (n=128, n=256) across QUBO, NK, and NK3, but is outperformed under shorter budgets (~1,000 evaluations) because its diversity-maintaining strategy requires more evaluations to converge. Against the three classical EDAs (PBIL, MIMIC, BOA), (σ,σ)-RL-EDA clearly outperforms all three, as these methods rely on univariate marginals or explicit DAG structure learning that fails to scale or capture complex interactions in high-dimensional discrete spaces. The paper specifically notes that BOA's NP-hard structure-learning step is unnecessary when using neural estimators with random orderings, and that the Learned-σ variant (which attempts to learn an explicit Plackett-Luce ordering) underperforms the fully random (σ,σ) variant, confirming that explicit structure extraction is counterproductive. On NAS-Bench-101, (σ,σ)-RL-EDA with the same hyperparameters achieves the best results at both 1,000 and 10,000 evaluations, outperforming all Nevergrad competitors on this real-world neural architecture search task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges Estimation of Distribution Algorithms (EDAs) and modern reinforcement learning by casting discrete black-box combinatorial optimization as an episodic MDP solved with a GRPO-style policy gradient. It departs from classical multivariate EDAs (MIMIC, BOA) that require explicit DAG structure learning at each generation, instead using neural autoregressive generators trained across random variable orderings—a mechanism inspired by σ-GPTs (Pannatier et al., 2024) but repurposed as an information-preserving structural dropout. The GRPO adaptation (Shao et al., 2024) replaces the critic network with rank-based scale-invariant advantages, while the PPO/TRPO surrogate (Schulman et al., 2015a, 2017) provides the KL-regularized update backbone. Unlike RL-based combinatorial solvers (POMO, SymNCO) that exploit solution-space symmetries, this work targets invariance to the internal generation order of the generative model itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a theoretically grounded (unbiased estimator, infinite-capacity convergence) and empirically validated method that competes with 503 Nevergrad algorithms on standard discrete benchmarks, demonstrating that order-invariant RL training is a practical alternative to explicit dependency-graph learning in EDAs. A notable limitation is that the diversity-maintaining mechanism requires more function evaluations to converge than some faster-converging competitors, and a curriculum-based adaptation is needed for tight budgets. Open questions include extension to multi-modal landscapes (the authors mention mixture-of-distributions with attraction-repulsion dynamics), scaling to very large instances via parameter sharing, and whether the order-invariance-as-dropout principle transfers to other autoregressive or sequential generation settings beyond combinatorial optimization.
