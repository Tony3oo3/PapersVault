## Problem

Existing ski rental algorithms with predictions either use point predictions (Purohit et al., 2018) or assume the prediction error (EMD distance) is known (Besbes et al., 2025). This paper tackles the open question of how to exploit a distributional prediction without any knowledge of its quality, achieving both small additive loss when the prediction is accurate (consistency) and bounded worst-case loss when it is arbitrarily wrong (robustness).

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a full probability distribution p̂ over ski days [N] as input (not a point estimate) and outputs a deterministic rent-then-buy threshold K* = min(K̂ + √b, U + √b), where K̂ is the optimal buying day under p̂ and U is the first day where p̂'s tail mass drops below 1/√b. The performance benchmark is the expected cost of the optimal policy for the true (unknown) distribution p, rather than the hindsight optimum, which the authors show is a more fair and achievable comparison. A further robustification step (Algorithm 2) accepts an additional trust parameter λ ∈ [0,1] to interpolate between full prediction trust and the classical 2-competitive worst-case strategy.

## Method

The main algorithm (Algorithm 1) computes the optimal policy A_{K̂} for the predicted distribution p̂, delays the purchase by √b days, and applies a tail-mass truncation: if the predicted tail beyond U (where tail mass ≤ 1/√b) is smaller than K̂, it caps the buying threshold at U + √b. The √b delay is derived by balancing two error sources in an optimal-transport analysis: mass that crosses the delay is bounded by EMD/D (giving coefficient b/D), while direct transport cost is bounded by D, so D = √b minimizes the product. The truncation step provides the O(b log b) robustness guarantee without knowing EMD(p̂, p), by exploiting the structural constraint that optimality of A_{K̂} forces the predicted tail to decay geometrically (Lemma 10) and bounding the time to reach the 1/√b tail level via dyadic halving (Lemma 11). The full analysis decomposes the additive loss into a base-algorithm term (bounded via transport plan properties) and a truncation loss term (bounded via a tail-mass lemma relating p and p̂ through EMD).

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 1) establishes an algorithm with additive loss O(min(√b · max(EMD(p̂,p), 1), b log b)), yielding consistency O(√b) when the prediction is exact and robustness O(b log b) under arbitrarily large prediction error, without requiring knowledge of the error magnitude. In the bimodal experiment (b=100, true distribution p₃₀=p₃₀₀=1/2, OPT=80.0), the proposed algorithm achieves costs of 85.1–92.6 (additive loss 5.1–12.6) across EMD values 0–15, while the point-prediction baseline incurs costs of ~132–133 (additive loss ~52–53). Lower bounds (Theorems 3–6) confirm the √b consistency, the max(EMD,1) term, and the b log b robustness are all essentially tight.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its distributional-prediction algorithm against the natural point-prediction baseline: sampling a single point T̂ from the predicted distribution p̂ and running the Purohit et al. (2018) point-prediction algorithm (buy immediately if T̂≥b, otherwise keep renting). On the bimodal instance (b=100, p₃₀=p₃₀₀=1/2), the proposed algorithm achieves an additive loss of 5.1–12.6 over OPT across EMD values 0–15, while the point-prediction baseline suffers an additive loss of approximately 52–53 regardless of EMD. This ~4–5× gap in additive loss illustrates the core motivation: when the prediction is genuinely bimodal, sampling collapses the distribution to one mode, causing the point-prediction algorithm to make decisions tailored to the wrong regime (buying too early or renting too long). The distributional algorithm, by contrast, retains information about both modes and stays close to the optimal policy cost for the true distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ski rental problem in the algorithms-with-predictions framework from point predictions (Purohit et al., 2018) to distributional predictions, and crucially removes the assumption that the prediction error (EMD between predicted and true distributions) is known, which Besbes et al. (2025) required for their robust-optimization-style analysis. The base algorithm's loss bound builds on equation (E-28) from Besbes et al.'s appendix, but the robustness mechanism (tail-mass truncation) and the full tight lower bounds are new. The analysis technique of decomposing the optimal transport plan into 'good' and 'bad' maps to bound the change in additive loss is a natural but nontrivial extension of the transport-based arguments in the known-EMD setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This resolves an open question explicitly raised by Besbes et al. (2025) about the best achievable performance without knowledge of the prediction error, and establishes that the √b consistency / b log b robustness tradeoff is Pareto-optimal via matching lower bounds. The impossibility result (Theorem 3) showing that O(EMD) additive loss is unachievable in the distributional setting is a useful negative result that clarifies the fundamental gap between point and distributional predictions. Open questions include whether the b log b robustness can be improved with more sophisticated algorithms, whether analogous tight results hold for other online problems (e.g., paging, caching), and whether the bimodal-distribution advantage of distributional over point predictions extends to more complex multi-modal settings.
