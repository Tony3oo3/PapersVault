## Problem

Existing faithfulness metrics for LLM self-explanations rely on detecting adversarial vulnerabilities or reasoning errors, failure modes that vanish as model capabilities scale, leaving no reliable signal for evaluating frontier models. This paper asks whether self-explanations can be assessed by their positive predictive value—whether they actually help an observer learn a model's decision-making criteria—rather than by the absence of detectable failures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263239/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a reference model's binary-classification answer and accompanying self-explanation for a natural-language question, paired with counterfactual questions drawn from real tabular data (Hamming distance ≤ 2 in feature space). An ensemble of five predictor LLMs (ranging from 20B to frontier-scale) is prompted to predict the reference model's answer on each counterfactual, both with and without access to the explanation. The output is Normalized Simulatability Gain (NSG), a single scalar in [−∞, 1] representing the fraction of achievable predictor improvement delivered by the explanation, computed over 7,000 (question, counterfactual) pairs across seven domains and 18 reference models.

## Method

The core contribution is Normalized Simulatability Gain (NSG), defined as (Acc_with_exp − Acc_without_exp) / (1 − Acc_without_exp), which normalizes the absolute simulatability gain by the remaining headroom so that high-baseline settings are not penalized. Counterfactuals are selected from real dataset instances using Hamming distance ≤ 2, ensuring multivariate, distributionally natural perturbations rather than the synthetic single-word insertions used in prior work. A key design choice is the without-explanation baseline, which isolates the marginal predictive value of the explanation itself rather than conflating it with the predictor's general competence. The paper further tests for privileged self-knowledge by swapping each self-explanation with one generated by a different model that produced the same answer, finding consistent positive uplift for self-explanations across all five model families. The framework is validated through ablations over predictor ensembles, prompt variants, reasoning-trace vs. user-facing explanations, Hamming distance, and alternative baselines.

## Results

Across 18 frontier models (Qwen 3, Gemma 3, GPT-5, Claude 4.5, Gemini 3 families), self-explanations yield a Normalized Simulatability Gain (NSG) of 11.0–36.5%, with absolute simulatability gain of 3.8–10.8 percentage points. The best-performing models (e.g., Gemma 3 4B at 36.5% NSG, Qwen3-32B at 35.6%) show that explanations correct roughly a third of the predictor's otherwise-incorrect counterfactual predictions. Additionally, 5–15% of self-explanations are classified as 'highly misleading' (causing all five predictors to err), with smaller open-weight models (Qwen3-0.6B, Gemma3-1B) showing ~15% versus ~7% for frontier models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is self-explanations versus no explanation: all 18 models show statistically significant positive NSG, confirming that explanations add predictive information beyond what the predictor can infer from the original question and answer alone. A second key comparison tests privileged self-knowledge: self-explanations consistently outperform cross-model explanations (generated by a different model family that gave the same answer) by +1.7 to +4.3 percentage points in NSG across all five model families (Table 2), even when the external explainer is stronger. Scale trends are mixed: Qwen 3 shows a clear monotonic improvement from 11.0% (0.6B) to 35.6% (32B) NSG, Gemma 3 trends upward, but proprietary models show no consistent scale relationship. Dataset-level variation is substantial: NSG is lowest on Moral Machines (6.0%) and highest on Pima Diabetes (42.8%), with some model–dataset pairs (e.g., Gemma 3 on Moral Machines) showing statistically significant negative NSG. Explanations also reduce a systematic 'false-change bias' in predictors by roughly half (e.g., from 35.4% to 18.2% for gpt-oss-20b), indicating they actively correct predictor errors rather than merely adding context.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the counterfactual simulatability framework (Hase et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2024) by introducing a normalized baseline-relative metric (NSG) that isolates the predictive information in explanations, and by replacing synthetic perturbations with data-driven counterfactuals sampled from real tabular distributions. It departs from the dominant prior faithfulness paradigm—detecting unfaithfulness via adversarial cues (Turpin et al., 2023) or reasoning errors (Arcuschin et al., 2025)—which the authors argue suffers from a vanishing signal problem as frontier models improve. The privileged self-knowledge experiment follows the introspection framework of Binder et al. (2025), and the local-prediction philosophy draws an explicit analogy to LIME/SHAP in the explainable-AI literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NSG provides a scalable, persistent-signal faithfulness metric that works on frontier models where prior adversarial-based metrics lose discriminative power, making it a practical tool for AI safety oversight. The finding that self-explanations consistently outperform cross-model explanations—even from stronger external models—suggests a genuine introspective advantage that external explanation methods cannot replicate, with implications for how we design oversight pipelines. Key open questions include whether faithfulness in benign settings transfers to adversarial/malicious settings, whether NSG extends to free-text generation, and whether the metric can serve as a training signal to improve faithfulness. The paper also notes that 5–15% of self-explanations are highly misleading, underscoring that self-explanations complement but do not replace other interpretability techniques.
