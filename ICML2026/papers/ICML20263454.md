## Problem

CLIP and other vision-language models remain highly vulnerable to adversarial perturbations, yet existing test-time defenses collapse under strong attacks (e.g., AutoAttack), incur high inference latency, and are often restricted to specific tasks. The paper identifies an unexplored spectral fragility in adversarial examples—caused by CLIP's inherent bias toward mid-to-high frequency components—that can be exploited for efficient, universal defense without retraining.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263454/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (potentially adversarially perturbed) as input and produces a rectified image that is then passed through the frozen CLIP encoder for downstream inference. The rectification operates with a perturbation budget of ℓ∞=4/255, a Gaussian low-pass filter radius of 40, and only 3 PGD steps (step size 2/255), adding roughly 22 ms of overhead on an RTX 4090 for adversarial samples while adding only ~0.8 ms for benign ones. The rectified image is task-agnostic and can feed into zero-shot classification, semantic segmentation, image captioning, or VQA pipelines without modification.

## Method

CSR is a training-free, test-time defense built on the observation that adversarial examples exhibit severe feature collapse under progressive frequency attenuation, while benign examples remain stable. It first applies an input-adaptive gating mechanism: a Gaussian low-pass filter produces a smoothed counterpart, and the cosine similarity between the full-spectrum and low-frequency CLIP embeddings determines whether the sample is adversarial (below threshold τ=0.85). For flagged samples, CSR optimizes a rectification perturbation δ via 3-step PGD under a contrastive objective that simultaneously attracts the perturbed feature toward the low-pass filtered anchor (benign manifold proxy) and repels it from the original adversarial embedding. A greedy selection strategy retains the perturbation achieving the maximum contrastive score across all steps, ensuring the returned sample corresponds to peak spectral consistency. The theoretical analysis (Appendix C) proves that the low-frequency constraint gradient has strictly negative cosine similarity with the adversarial attack direction, explaining why attacks gravitate to mid-to-high frequencies and why the spectral anchor is a reliable rectification target.

## Results

On 16 zero-shot classification benchmarks under 10-step PGD (ε=1/255), CSR achieves 58.1% average robust accuracy with only a 0.7% drop in clean accuracy (62.9% vs. 63.6% for original CLIP). Under the stronger AutoAttack (APGD, ε=4/255), CSR reaches 66.4% on General, 60.3% on Fine-Grained, 34.2% on Scene, and 32.6% on Domain — an average 18.1% improvement over the best test-time baseline (R-TPT). Inference latency is 15.17 ms/image on average (26.18 ms for adversarial inputs), roughly 12× faster than R-TPT (176.33 ms). CSR also generalizes to segmentation (mIoU +16.7%), captioning (+43.0%), and VQA (+43.0%) under strong attacks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the closest test-time defense TTC (Xing et al., CVPR 2025), CSR wins decisively: on 10-step PGD, CSR achieves 58.1% vs. TTC's 24.5% average robust accuracy, and under AutoAttack CSR reaches 66.4% (General) vs. TTC's near-zero 0.6%. R-TPT (Sheng et al., CVPR 2025) is the strongest test-time competitor, achieving 51.2% under PGD and 28.4% under AA on General; CSR surpasses it by 6.9% (PGD) and 18.1% (AA) on average, while also being ~12× faster (15.17 ms vs. 176.33 ms). Adversarial fine-tuning methods TeCoA (Mao et al., ICLR 2023) and FARE (Schlarmann et al., ICML 2024) remain competitive under strong attacks (e.g., FARE: 65.4% AA on General) but suffer severe clean-accuracy degradation (48.0% vs. CSR's 62.5% on ImageNet). Passive methods LPF (Ziyadinov & Tereshonok, 2023), HD (Wu et al., 2021), Anti-Adv (Alfarra et al., AAAI 2022), and TTE (Perez et al., ICCV 2021) all collapse under AutoAttack, with robustness dropping below 31% on General and near-zero on most other categories, whereas CSR maintains 32–66% across all four dataset groups.

## Relation to Prior Work

CSR departs from prior frequency-robustness work (Garg et al. 2018, Bu et al. 2023) that improves robustness through training-time spectral biasing, instead repurposing the frequency domain as a test-time detection and rectification signal without any retraining. Among test-time defenses, it extends the closest prior work TTC (Xing et al. 2025), which only repels from the adversarial embedding, by adding a low-pass-filtered feature as a positive contrastive anchor that pulls the rectified sample back toward the natural manifold. The paper also introduces a theoretical gradient-conflict analysis (Appendix C) formalizing why adversarial perturbations necessarily concentrate in mid-to-high frequency bands, providing a principled justification that prior empirical observations lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Because CLIP-based encoders underpin the visual front-end of most modern LVLMs (LLaVA, Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL), a training-free, task-agnostic defense that adds only ~12 ms of latency has direct practical value for securing deployed multimodal systems without retraining. The paper's acknowledged limitation—failure under global color-space attacks where the spectral assumption breaks—highlights an open problem: defenses that do not rely on the mid-to-high frequency concentration of adversarial energy. More broadly, the finding that ~90% of adversarial gradient energy resides in mid-to-high bands across all tested backbones suggests this spectral vulnerability is a structural property of large-scale contrastive pre-training, potentially informing future training-time robustness objectives.
