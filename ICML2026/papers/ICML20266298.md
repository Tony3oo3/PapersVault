## Problem

Adaptive testing requires selecting a small, personalized subset of questions from a large pool to accurately estimate an examinee's ability, but solving this NP-hard subset selection problem per examinee is intractable. Prior explicit gradient-matching approaches (e.g., BECAT) enforce gradient alignment across the entire continuous ability space Θ, incurring O(|Q||Θ|) complexity that is computationally prohibitive and difficult to scale. The paper asks whether global alignment is necessary, or whether matching gradients only along the optimization path toward the final estimate suffices.

## Input / Output

At each adaptive step t, the selection network receives the examinee's prior response history τ_{t-1} as a binary vector of length |Q| (values in {+1, −1, 0} for correct/incorrect/unanswered) and outputs a probability distribution over all questions via softmax; the next question is chosen by argmax (or Gumbel-Softmax sampling during training). The question pool Q carries pre-calibrated IRT parameters (discrimination α_i, difficulty β_i, guessing factor c_i). The final output is an ability estimate θ_S obtained by minimizing cross-entropy over the selected subset, with test length fixed at T=20. The selection model is a lightweight two-layer network (|Q| → 256 → |Q| with Tanh), and all parameters are frozen at deployment, yielding O(|Q|) per-step inference cost.

## Method

GPM (Gradient Path Matching) replaces the global gradient-matching objective min_S sup_{θ∈Θ} ||∇_S(θ) − ∇_Q(θ)|| with a path-restricted surrogate min_S Σ_{k=1}^{K} ||∇_S(θ_S^{(k)}) − ∇_Q(θ_S^{(k)})||, where {θ_S^{(k)}} are intermediate iterates along a K-step gradient descent trajectory toward the final estimate. Training follows a bilevel loop: the inner loop performs K steps of ability estimation via IRT gradient descent on the sampled subset, while the outer loop updates the selection parameters S (or personalized network weights φ) using the accumulated gradient discrepancies as supervision. A Gumbel-Softmax trick makes discrete question selection differentiable, enabling end-to-end gradient flow from the discrepancy loss back to the selection network. Theoretically, Lemma 4.1 bounds the gap between path-restricted and worst-case gradient error by O(ε) (where ε is the trajectory's coverage radius), Lemma 4.2 establishes Lipschitz continuity of the discrepancy gradient, and Theorem 4.3 provides a 1/N convergence rate for the selection optimization. A personalized variant S(τ; φ) conditions on the examinee's running response history, enabling adaptive, context-dependent question selection without online parameter updates.

## Results

On the Examinee Score Prediction task (Table 1), GPM achieves the highest ACC/AUC across all three datasets and all test lengths. On EXAM at step 20, GPM reaches 86.02% ACC / 77.89% AUC, outperforming the best baseline UATS (84.92/75.20) by +2.7% AUC. On NIPS-EDU at step 20, GPM achieves 73.98/78.10, surpassing BECAT (72.00/76.82) by +1.3% AUC. In the ability estimation simulation (Figure 4a), GPM achieves the same MSE as implicit methods like FSI while requiring up to 20% fewer questions, with an average test-length reduction of 9%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPM outperforms all 10 baselines across every dataset and test length in the main score-prediction table. Against the strongest data-driven baselines, GPM leads UATS by +2.7% AUC on EXAM@20 (77.89 vs 75.20) and by +1.6% on NIPS-EDU@20 (78.10 vs 76.50); it surpasses NCAT (RL-based) by +3.6% AUC on EXAM@20 and BECAT (gradient-approximation heuristic) by +1.1% on EXAM@20. Against classical heuristics, the gap is even larger: FSI and KLI trail GPM by roughly 3–4% AUC on EXAM@20, and MAAT underperforms by over 3% on NIPS-EDU@20. In the ranking-consistency experiment (Table 4, Kendall correlation), GPM achieves the highest average score (0.854) among all methods except CCAT (0.843), which is specifically designed for ranking, confirming that gradient-path alignment transfers well to ranking tasks. In robustness tests (Table 2), GPM degrades least under 50% data reduction (74.21 vs 72.85 for GMOCAT) and 15% label noise (73.30 vs 72.50 for GMOCAT), maintaining a 1–2% AUC advantage over all competitors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the explicit gradient-matching formulation for adaptive testing introduced by Zhuang et al. (2023, BECAT), which required aligning gradients between a selected subset and the full question pool across the entire ability parameter space Θ. The key conceptual departure is restricting this alignment to only the gradient descent trajectory toward the final estimate, reducing the search space from O(|Q||Θ|) to O(|Q|K) where K is the number of optimization steps. This positions GPM between the computationally expensive global matching of BECAT and the implicit data-driven approaches (RL, meta-learning) like BOBCAT, UATS, and NCAT, offering a theoretically grounded yet scalable middle ground. The implementation uses a simple two-layer network with Gumbel-Softmax for differentiable discrete selection, trained via bilevel optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 9% average reduction in test length while maintaining estimation accuracy is practically meaningful for high-stakes testing systems (GRE, TOEFL) where even small reductions in question count translate to significant cost and time savings at scale. The robustness under data sparsity and label noise addresses a real deployment concern that many prior methods handle poorly. Open questions include whether the path-matching principle generalizes beyond IRT to more complex user models, whether richer question metadata (text, tags) can further improve selection, and whether the simple two-layer architecture is truly sufficient or whether the gains come from the training objective rather than model capacity. The paper also notes that GPM does not fully match CCAT on ranking consistency, suggesting a trade-off between estimation accuracy and rank preservation that remains unresolved.
