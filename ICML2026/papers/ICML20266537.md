## Problem

Current TTS evaluation practices remain narrowly centered on technical metrics (MOS, WER, SIM) that fail to capture the full capabilities, limitations, and societal impacts of modern zero-shot and LLM-based TTS systems. The paper identifies a critical imbalance between rapid technological advancement and evaluation methodology, with systemic shortcomings in metric fidelity, cross-study comparability, and a complete absence of governance, fairness, and security assessment in standard protocols.

## Input / Output

The proposed evaluation framework consumes synthesized speech alongside reference text and reference speech (for zero-shot TTS tasks) and produces a structured multi-level report: Level 1 technical quality scores with uncertainty estimates, Level 2 standardized comparability documentation (dataset splits, prompt lists, task definitions, RTF measurement setups), and Level 3 governance/fairness/security assessments including data provenance records, group-disaggregated metrics, and traceability verification results. Unlike conventional single-score evaluations, the framework requires simultaneous reporting across all three levels with explicit disclosure of protocol deviations.

## Method

This position paper proposes a three-level Responsible Evaluation framework for TTS. Level 1 (Fidelity and Accuracy) advocates for uncertainty-aware model-predicted MOS, audio Turing tests to mitigate score saturation, and expanded evaluation dimensions covering mathematical symbols, long-form coherence, emotional expressiveness, and punctuation sensitivity. Level 2 (Comparability, Standardization, and Transferability) mandates adherence to formal standards like ITU-T P.808, transparent reporting of all evaluation configurations, and development of transferable human-aligned automatic metrics (e.g., LLM-as-a-Judge). Level 3 (Governance, Fairness, and Security) requires mandatory data provenance disclosure, representation-aware benchmarks with group-disaggregated reporting, and standardized traceability assessment via watermarking detection. The paper substantiates its arguments with empirical case studies showing that inconsistent dataset variants (40 vs. 1234 utterances) shift WER from 2.63% to 4.22%, and that SIM-o computation with or without the prompt segment produces absolute differences up to 0.151, undermining cross-study comparability.

## Results

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new TTS system or report standard benchmark scores. Its key quantitative evidence comes from two case studies demonstrating evaluation inconsistencies: (1) WER for the open-source MaskGCT model on the cross-sentence task ranges from 2.63% to 4.22% depending on whether a 40-utterance or 1234-utterance variant of LibriSpeech test-clean is used, a 60% relative difference attributable solely to dataset selection; (2) SIM-o scores for the continuation task differ by up to 0.151 in absolute value (0.754 vs. 0.905) depending on whether the prompt segment is included in the speaker-embedding computation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper and does not compare a proposed method against competing TTS systems. Instead, its 'comparisons' are between evaluation protocols to expose inconsistencies. The WER case study shows that the same model (MaskGCT) yields a 1.59-percentage-point WER gap (2.63% vs. 4.22%) purely from using different LibriSpeech test-clean subsets, undermining cross-paper comparability. The SIM-o case study shows that VALL-E's protocol (excluding the prompt) and VALL-E 2's protocol (including the prompt) produce scores differing by 0.151 on the same utterance, meaning published SIM-o numbers are not directly comparable across works. These findings support the paper's central argument that current TTS evaluation lacks the standardization needed for meaningful scientific comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not build on a single prior method but rather synthesizes and critiques the entire lineage of TTS evaluation practice, from early MCD/F0-RMSE metrics through MOS/CMOS protocols to modern neural evaluators (DNSMOS, ECAPA-TDNN-based SIM, LLM-as-a-Judge). It departs from prior work by reframing TTS evaluation as a three-level sociotechnical problem rather than a purely technical one, drawing on responsible-AI and fairness literature (Selbst et al., 2019; Koenecke et al., 2020; Hutiri & Ding, 2022) that has been applied to ASR and ASV but not yet systematically to TTS. The paper also builds on recent methodological critiques of MOS (Kirkland et al., 2023; Chiang et al., 2023) and on the emerging LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm, positioning its framework as the unifying structure that connects these scattered concerns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As zero-shot voice cloning and LLM-based TTS systems become commercially deployed, the gap between what these systems can do and what standard evaluation protocols measure grows increasingly dangerous: aggregate WER/SIM/MOS scores can mask demographic disparities, and the absence of data-provenance or traceability requirements leaves legal and security risks unaddressed. The paper's three-level framework provides a concrete, actionable checklist (uncertainty reporting, group-disaggregated metrics, watermarking assessment, data licensing disclosure) that could shape conference submission guidelines and industry evaluation standards. Key open questions remain: how to operationalize governance scoring in a reproducible way, how to build truly representation-aware benchmarks across underrepresented speech communities, and whether LLM-as-a-Judge evaluators can be made sufficiently calibrated and bias-free to serve as transferable proxies for human listening tests.
