## Problem

While transformers are widely used as statistical sequence models, it remains unclear whether their standard circuit mechanisms (attention, residual connections, feedforward blocks) can exactly implement and learn discrete algorithmic computations. This paper asks whether a transformer can provably realize Lloyd's algorithm for k-means clustering in its forward pass, and whether such a construction can be trained to produce clustering algorithms that surpass the original.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260762/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The transformer takes n points in R^d and k initial cluster centers as input, embedding each point into a (d+k)-dimensional token (point coordinates plus k placeholder dimensions for one-hot cluster assignments) and each center into a (d+k)-dimensional token (center coordinates plus a one-hot cluster index). After T layers, the encoder tokens' last k dimensions encode the final discrete cluster assignments, and the decoder tokens' first d dimensions encode the updated cluster centers. The embedding dimension d_emb = d+k is critical: ablations with d_emb = d fail to match Lloyd's algorithm, and the dot-product-attention variant (Theorem 2.2) requires d_emb = d+k+2 to store squared norms for distance computation.

## Method

The k-means transformer is an encoder-decoder architecture where the encoder processes point tokens and the decoder processes center tokens, with each layer performing one Lloyd's iteration via four attention modules (encoder self/cross-attention and decoder self/cross-attention) plus residual connections. The encoder cross-attention uses negative squared Euclidean distance scores (ℓ₂-attention) with limiting softmax (γ→∞) to compute exact discrete cluster assignments, while the decoder cross-attention uses standard dot-product attention to compute new centers as weighted averages of assigned points. Self-attention in each block computes the additive inverse of the current assignments or centers, and the residual connection resets them to zero before cross-attention writes the new values. The model size is O(d+k), smaller than the input. When trained end-to-end with standard softmax (γ=1) on a distribution of Gaussian mixture clustering tasks using a smoothed k-means objective, the learned transformer generalizes to unseen tasks and outperforms Lloyd's algorithm by a significant margin. Interpretable architectural modifications—swapping attention types, adding normalization, or using Gumbel-softmax—yield soft k-means, spherical k-means, trimmed k-means, robust k-means, and k-medoids as special cases.

## Results

On 320 unseen Gaussian mixture clustering tasks (d=32, k=10, n=512, 20 steps), the trained k-means transformer achieves an average log k-means objective of ~5.0, significantly outperforming Lloyd's algorithm at ~5.25. With k-means++ initialization, the transformer further improves to ~4.85 vs. <5.0 for Lloyd's. On 15 OpenML real-world datasets, the trained transformer (trained solely on synthetic data) improves the objective from initial centers but underperforms Lloyd's by 1–7% due to distribution shift. The transformer also generalizes to varying numbers of points (128–2048) while maintaining its advantage over Lloyd's.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline throughout the paper is Lloyd's algorithm (the standard k-means iterative procedure). On the primary synthetic evaluation (Gaussian mixtures, d=32, k=10, n=512), the trained transformer outperforms Lloyd's by a consistent margin (~5.0 vs. 5.25 log objective over 20 steps), and this advantage persists with k-means++ initialization (~4.85 vs. <5.0). The transformer also outperforms Lloyd's on cross-distribution tasks (Gumbel, Laplace, LogNormal) but loses on Cauchy mixtures (3.34 vs. 3.25), likely due to the heavy-tailed nature of Cauchy data. On 15 OpenML real-world datasets, the transformer underperforms Lloyd's by 1–7% (e.g., 7.95 vs. 7.88 on gina-agnostic; 5.44 vs. 5.08 on pc3), reflecting the gap between synthetic training and real data distributions. Notably, the transformer's advantage on synthetic tasks is achieved with standard softmax attention (γ=1) rather than the theoretically required limiting softmax (γ→∞), and it generalizes to unseen problem sizes (n from 128 to 2048) without retraining.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges classical discrete clustering algorithms (Lloyd's algorithm for k-means) with modern transformer architectures by providing an explicit, compact parameterization of an encoder-decoder transformer that exactly executes Lloyd's iterations. It departs from prior transformer expressivity results (Turing completeness by Strobl et al., Pérez et al.) that establish existence at high time/space cost, offering instead a practical O(d+k)-sized model with a clean circuit-level mapping. It also contrasts with in-context learning work (Von Oswald et al., Garg et al.) that typically relies on linear attention approximations and supervised objectives, whereas this paper uses the full selective arg-max-like attention of the original transformer and an unsupervised clustering objective. The architectural-modification framework for generating variant algorithms (soft, spherical, trimmed k-means, k-medoids) extends the transformer-as-algorithmic-skeleton perspective beyond prior self-clustering observations (Geshkovski et al.).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rare exact, interpretable mapping between individual transformer circuit components (self-attention, cross-attention, residual connections) and discrete algorithmic operations, offering a template for understanding how transformers implement classical routines. The finding that a trained transformer with standard softmax (γ=1) outperforms Lloyd's algorithm on synthetic tasks, while the theoretical construction requires γ→∞, suggests the architecture learns genuinely different (and better) update rules. However, practical impact is tempered: on real OpenML data the trained model underperforms Lloyd's by 1–7%, the approach does not generalize to larger k, and no theoretical generalization guarantees are provided. Open questions include whether this circuit-level algorithmic transparency extends to other classical methods (e.g., EM, gradient descent) and whether the learned improvements over Lloyd's can be characterized analytically.
