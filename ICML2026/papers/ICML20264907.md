## Problem

Distributional learning objectives (optimal transport, Sinkhorn divergence) induce an intrinsic anisotropic geometry on parameter space, yet optimization is performed with Euclidean first-order methods that assume isotropic sensitivity. This mismatch causes convergence slowdowns and instabilities that persist even in convex, low-noise regimes and cannot be resolved by step-size tuning or adaptive heuristics. The paper identifies this as a structural rather than algorithmic failure and quantifies it through a new condition number, geometry-misalignment, with matching lower and upper bounds establishing when Euclidean optimization is provably inadequate.

## Input / Output

The method requires only matrix-vector products with the intrinsic metric operator v ↦ G(θ)v (no explicit formation of G(θ)), gradient evaluations of the distributional objective, and a small number of power or Lanczos iterations (K_pow = 5) to estimate the misalignment condition number. When geometry-aware updates are triggered, approximate intrinsic solves G(θ)v = ∇F(θ) are computed via conjugate gradient with tolerance proportional to the current step size and a fixed iteration cap (J_max = 30). The output is the optimized parameter vector θ_T and the corresponding learned distribution Q_{θ_T}, with the algorithm reducing to standard Euclidean gradient descent in well-aligned regimes at negligible overhead.

## Method

The paper defines geometry-misalignment κ_G(θ) = λ_max(G(θ))/λ_min(G(θ)) as the condition number measuring distortion between the Euclidean metric and the intrinsic geometry pulled back from the distributional discrepancy onto parameter space. It proves a lower bound showing any Euclidean first-order method incurs an unavoidable O(κ_G log(1/ε)) convergence slowdown even under intrinsic strong convexity and smoothness, and a matching upper bound showing that intrinsic gradient descent (θ ← θ − ηG(θ)^{-1}∇F) achieves linear convergence independent of κ_G. The proposed Geometry-Calibrated Optimization (GCO) adaptively gates between Euclidean and intrinsic updates using a threshold κ_0 on the estimated misalignment, invoking expensive conjugate-gradient intrinsic solves only when κ̂_G(θ) > κ_0. The framework further establishes that geometry-misalignment induces an optimization-dependent excess risk term under finite compute budgets, directly linking optimization geometry to statistical efficiency, and that the intrinsic gradient flow stabilizes misalignment growth along trajectories.

## Results

In high-misalignment regimes (κ_G ≈ 10³), GCO requires only 145–155 iterations to reach a gap of ~7.5×10⁻⁵, compared to 1600–1850 iterations for SGD (gap ~1.5×10⁻³) and 690–790 for K-FAC (gap ~3×10⁻⁴) across the three DGPs. GCO achieves this with a wall-clock overhead of 0.16–0.17× relative to SGD, whereas always-on geometry-aware optimization incurs 0.31–0.34× overhead. In low-misalignment regimes (κ_G ≈ 1), GCO reduces to standard Euclidean updates (Geo% = 6–8%) with overhead of 1.14–1.20×, confirming negligible cost when geometry is well-aligned.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GCO decisively outperforms Euclidean first-order baselines (SGD, Adam, AdaGrad) in high-misalignment regimes, requiring 10–12× fewer iterations and achieving 15–20× smaller objective gaps (e.g., 7.5×10⁻⁵ vs. 1.1×10⁻³ for SGD on DGP-1). Second-order approximations (Shampoo, K-FAC) partially mitigate the slowdown—reducing iterations by ~2× over SGD—but still require 5–6× more iterations than GCO and incur higher wall-clock overhead (1.27–1.43× vs. 0.17×). Against always-on geometry-aware optimization, GCO matches final objective gaps within ~10% while halving the computational overhead (0.17× vs. 0.34×) by activating intrinsic updates in only 27–34% of iterations rather than 100%. In low-misalignment regimes, GCO is essentially equivalent to SGD (overhead 1.14–1.20×, Geo% = 6–8%), confirming it does not degrade performance when geometry is well-aligned. Compared to established domain adaptation methods (CORAL, DANN, CDAN, MMD), GCO achieves 2–3× smaller objective gaps in high-misalignment settings, though these methods optimize different objectives and are included as reference points rather than direct optimization comparisons.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of natural gradient methods (Amari, 1998), Riemannian optimization (Absil et al., 2008), and mirror descent (Beck and Teboulle, 2003), but explicitly disclaims novelty in preconditioning itself. Its conceptual departure is identifying a distributional-learning-specific condition number κ_G that quantifies distortion between Euclidean parameter geometry and the intrinsic geometry induced by a distributional discrepancy, then proving a sharp separation: lower bounds showing Euclidean first-order methods incur an unavoidable κ_G-dependent slowdown even under intrinsic strong convexity, and matching upper bounds showing geometry-aware updates remove this dependence. Prior geometry-aware optimization work in optimal transport focuses on algorithmic design or empirical performance without establishing when Euclidean optimization is fundamentally inadequate; this paper provides that structural diagnosis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical explanation for a long-observed practical pathology in distributional learning (sensitivity to step size, oscillatory updates, slow convergence) that standard explanations (nonconvexity, stochastic noise) do not account for. The geometry-calibrated optimization (GCO) algorithm is practically attractive because it reduces to standard SGD in well-aligned regimes and only invokes expensive intrinsic solves when misalignment is detected, with negligible overhead in the common case. Key open questions include whether the intrinsic metric G(θ) is tractable to estimate for deep nonlinear models beyond the synthetic linear and tanh settings tested here, whether the theory extends to nonconvex representation learning, and whether one can design architectures that control κ_G by construction. The main experiments are synthetic, and real-data validation is deferred to the appendix, limiting immediate practical confidence.
