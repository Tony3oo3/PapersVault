## Problem

RLVR fine-tunes LLMs using only sparse, outcome-based rewards, yet it empirically improves reasoning on long-horizon problems. It remains unclear how such terminal-only signals can drive effective learning across a spectrum of difficulties, and what determines whether training progresses smoothly or stalls in grokking-like plateaus. This paper asks: how does the difficulty spectrum of training data shape RLVR learning dynamics?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261749/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The task is L-step state tracking over a finite non-abelian group G of order d: given an initial state y₀ and a sequence of L distinct group elements (g₁,…,g_L) encoded as prompt tokens, the model must predict the final state y_L = g_L(∘…∘g₁)(y₀). The one-layer transformer (softmax attention + frozen MLP) predicts next-state symbols autoregressively, with the attention layer responsible for retrieving the correct transition g_ℓ at each step ℓ. Training uses a uniform mixture over a geometric difficulty schedule {L₁,…,L_K} where L_{k+1} = ⌈R·L_k⌉, and the reward is binary (1 if the final predicted state matches the true state, 0 otherwise).

## Method

The paper develops a complete theoretical analysis of REINFORCE policy-gradient dynamics for a minimal transformer (one attention layer with trainable Q, one frozen MLP implementing pretrained one-step group actions) on group-based compositional reasoning. The key technical tool is Fourier analysis on finite groups: the terminal success event couples all L steps into an L-fold convolution of one-step action measures, and passing to the Fourier domain converts this into a product of representation matrices, yielding the explicit gradient characterization ∇_q J̃_L ∝ (Δ_L)^L (1−Δ_L) J_L. This reveals that long-horizon gradients are exponentially suppressed until attention concentrates enough to make the step-level margin Δ_L moderate. The central result is that mixed-difficulty training induces an implicit curriculum whose character is governed by the difficulty ratio R: for R = O(1), a relay effect keeps training at the edge of competence with smooth handoffs between adjacent horizons; for R = ω(1), the same curriculum exhibits prolonged near-zero-reward plateaus followed by abrupt grokking-like phase transitions, with total training time dominated by these plateaus.

## Results

The paper establishes that outcome-based RL (REINFORCE) provably learns short-horizon compositions (L ≤ C_B) in Õ(L_max log(L/ε)/(η log d) · d^{(1−ε)C_B−1}) iterations, but encounters an exponentially flat gradient barrier (gradient magnitude ≤ Õ(1/L_max) · d^{−Ω(L)}) beyond the critical horizon L > 2C_B. In the mixed-difficulty setting, the relay regime (R = O(1)) achieves mastery at the longest horizon in Õ(L_max/η) · d^{(1−C_B/(C_B+R))C_B−1} iterations, while the grokking regime (R = ω(1)) requires Õ(L_max/η) · d^{C_B−1} iterations — a gap of d^{Θ(1)} per inter-horizon plateau. Synthetic experiments on Z_96 with L ∈ {5, 15, 45} (R=3) and L ∈ {5, 35} (R=7) confirm the predicted smooth relay vs. delayed-handoff dynamics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between RL (outcome-based REINFORCE) and SFT (teacher-forced supervision) on the same compositional task. RL provably learns up to the critical horizon C_B but hits an exponentially flat gradient barrier beyond it (Proposition 3.1), whereas SFT escapes this barrier and learns up to polylog(d) length (Theorem 3.2), confirming that intermediate supervision is necessary to overcome the long-horizon search barrier. The second key comparison is between two difficulty-spectrum regimes: the large-ratio (R = ω(1)) grokking dynamics exhibit long plateaus of length Õ(L_max/η) · d^{C_B−1} between consecutive horizons, while the moderate-ratio (R = O(1)) relay dynamics shorten each plateau by a factor of d^{Θ(1)}, yielding strictly faster overall convergence (Corollary 4.2 vs. Corollary 4.1). Synthetic experiments on a cyclic group Z_96 validate both predictions: R=3 shows smooth relay with overlapping progress across horizons {5, 15, 45}, while R=7 shows a prolonged near-zero-reward plateau on the harder horizon {35} before an abrupt jump to mastery, matching the grokking-type phase transition predicted by Theorem 4.1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the state-tracking compositional reasoning task (Liu et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2025) and the RLVR paradigm (Shao et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI, 2025), but departs from prior theoretical work by providing the first end-to-end analysis of outcome-based policy-gradient dynamics for transformers on multi-step composition. It extends the edge-of-competence observation of Zhang et al. (2025) from a static data-selection recommendation to a dynamic mechanism: mixed-difficulty training *naturally* produces an implicit curriculum whose smoothness is governed by the difficulty ratio. The technical core introduces Fourier analysis on finite groups (Terras, 1999) to handle the trajectory-level conditioning that makes long-horizon policy gradients intractable, a tool not previously applied to RL training dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic explanation for why RLVR works in practice on heterogeneous data: the difficulty spectrum's smoothness determines whether training relays smoothly across horizons or stalls in grokking-like plateaus, directly informing data-mixing strategies for production RLVR pipelines. Key limitations include the single-dimension difficulty axis (only sequence length), the restriction to a specific group-action task with a frozen MLP, and synthetic validation on a cyclic group rather than the non-abelian simple group assumed in theory. Open questions include whether the relay/grokking distinction holds when difficulty varies along multiple axes (semantic complexity, required atomic skills), and whether the Fourier-analytic framework extends to planning or search-based reasoning patterns.
