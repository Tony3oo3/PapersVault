## Problem

Source-Free Domain Adaptation for Regression (SFDAR) remains largely underexplored because regression presents fundamentally different challenges from classification: the feature space is smooth and continuous rather than clustered (limiting neighborhood-based pseudo-label correction), regression heads are deterministic and provide no uncertainty estimates, and standard losses like MSE reduce marginal feature entropy, hindering generalization under distribution shift. No existing method effectively constructs informative supervisory signals for regression adaptation without source data or target labels.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained source regression model (weights only, no source data) and an unlabeled target dataset, producing a continuous scalar regression prediction per input. Unusually, the model also outputs a K-way discrete probability histogram over adaptively-sized bins of the label space, where the bin count K and bin width are self-determined via kernel density estimation on M dropout-based stochastic predictions. The histogram is updated iteratively via a moving average, and truncated expectations over the most probable bins yield refined continuous pseudo-labels that feed back into the regression head.

## Method

MERCI introduces a dual-head architecture on a shared feature extractor: a standard regression head and an auxiliary histogram head that approximates the discretized conditional density P(Y|X). M stochastic forward passes with dropout generate an uncertainty-aware prediction set, which is discretized into bin indices to form partial label sets; a unimodal Gaussian prior (with variance scaled by a tunable factor κ) is constructed from the previous iteration's histogram to regularize the histogram head via KL divergence. The histogram head is trained with a partial-label loss plus this prior-regularization term, and its output is converted into refined continuous pseudo-labels via truncated expectations over the top-probability bins (threshold τ). The regression head is then trained on these pseudo-labels using either standard MSE or a distributionally-robust RMSE loss derived from a Wasserstein-2 DRO formulation (equivalent to RMSE plus L2 weight decay). A theoretical proposition establishes that the learned histogram is robust to adversarial perturbations of the loss, and the histogram head's distributional losses implicitly tighten feature representations, addressing the entropy-reduction problem of standard regression losses.

## Results

On UTKFace (Female→Male), MERCI-R achieves MAE 5.83, outperforming the best source-free baseline VM (6.09) by 4.3% and even surpassing UDA methods DANN (6.22) and RSD (6.22) that have access to source data. On Biwi-Kinect (Female→Male), MERCI-R achieves mean MAE 4.24, slightly beating the best UDA method DANN (4.26). On California Housing (Far Bay→Near Bay), MERCI-R w. FN achieves MAE 0.465, outperforming DARE-GRAM (0.478) and all source-free baselines. Across all four datasets, MERCI consistently improves over the unadapted source model, with the largest gains on the most challenging tasks (e.g., Biwi-Kinect Male→Female, where source MAE is 6.79 and MERCI-R w. FN reduces it to 5.52).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MERCI and its variants consistently outperform all source-free baselines (BN-adapt, CRAFT-U, BRR, VM, AugSelfTr) across all four datasets and both adaptation directions. On UTKFace Female→Male, MERCI-R (MAE 5.83) beats the best SF method VM (6.09) by 4.3% and surpasses UDA methods DANN (6.22) and RSD (6.22), demonstrating that source-free adaptation can match or exceed methods with full source data access. On Biwi-Kinect Female→Male, MERCI-R (mean MAE 4.24) slightly edges out DANN (4.26), the best UDA baseline. However, MERCI does not uniformly dominate: on UTKFace Male→Female, DARE-GRAM (MAE 6.93) still outperforms MERCI-R (7.08), and on California Housing Near→Far, DARE-GRAM (0.568) and DANN (0.561) remain ahead of MERCI-R w. FN (0.665). The data-free baselines SSA and TASFAR are consistently weaker than both MERCI and the UDA methods, particularly on UTKFace Male→Female where TASFAR degrades severely (MAE 10.12 vs. source 7.94). Overall, MERCI achieves the best or near-best results in the strict source-free setting and is competitive with or superior to UDA methods on the majority of evaluated task-direction pairs.

## Relation to Prior Work

MERCI extends the source-free domain adaptation (SFDA) paradigm—well-developed for classification, where clustered feature spaces and softmax uncertainty naturally yield pseudo-label refinement signals—to the fundamentally different regression setting, where smooth feature manifolds and deterministic outputs deprive the model of analogous cues. It draws on the histogram/distributional-loss idea from Imani & White (2018) and Imani et al. (2024), which showed that classification-style losses improve regression by tightening feature representations, and repurposes it as an auxiliary head that discretizes the continuous label space to recover uncertainty and neighborhood structure. The method also incorporates distributionally robust optimization (Blanchet et al., 2019) to handle pseudo-label noise, and positions itself against the closest prior SFDAR efforts (BRR, CRAFT-U) by explicitly modeling the output distribution rather than relying on implicit discretization or distributional heuristics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SFDAR is a practically important but largely underexplored problem: privacy constraints and deployment scenarios increasingly demand model adaptation without source data, yet regression tasks (age estimation, price prediction, pose estimation) are common in such settings. MERCI provides the first systematic framework with theoretical robustness guarantees for this gap, and its dual-head bi-directional design offers a reusable template for injecting distributional structure into deterministic regression heads. Limitations include evaluation on relatively small-scale regression benchmarks, reliance on dropout as the sole uncertainty source, and the data-dependent histogram binning that may not generalize to high-dimensional or multi-output regression. Open questions include whether the approach scales to large-scale regression (e.g., depth estimation, video prediction) and how it interacts with more sophisticated uncertainty quantification methods (e.g., ensembles, Bayesian neural networks).
