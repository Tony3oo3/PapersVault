## Problem

Existing hybrid differentiable models that combine neural networks with PDE solvers are designed empirically, with no principled link between their architecture and the underlying variational structure of the PDE. Plug-and-play methods from inverse imaging have never been applied to direct time integration of evolution PDEs, and current correction-based hybrids suffer from training instability and lack a unified framework for handling incomplete or uncertain physics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263287/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a discretized scalar PDE state field u_t on a spatial grid (64×64 for 2D Allen-Cahn, 64-point 1D for Kuramoto-Sivashinsky) and produces either the next state u_{t+1} (single-step mode) or a full trajectory u_1,…,u_T (time-unrolled mode). In time-unrolled mode, the number of internal splitting iterations K is set equal to the prediction horizon T, and each numerical solver call advances physical time by exactly τ/T, so intermediate solver outputs are directly supervised against ground-truth states at matching time points.

## Method

The key insight is that the implicit Euler discretization of a gradient-flow PDE is equivalent to evaluating a proximal operator, which can be decomposed via classical operator splitting when the energy splits as E = E₁ + E₂. The known physics E₁ is handled by a plug-and-play numerical solver (replacing prox_{E₁}), while the unknown component E₂ is modeled by a neural network that directly parametrizes the proximal operator prox_{τE₂} and is trained end-to-end inside the splitting loop. This yields three architectures: HYBDR (Douglas-Rachford, both terms implicit), HYBFB-POST (forward-backward with the network as a post-correction after the solver), and HYBFB-PRE (forward-backward with the network as a pre-correction before the solver). Two training strategies are provided: single-step (supervising only the final output) and time-unrolled (aligning internal iterations with physical time steps for trajectory-level supervision). The framework provably generalizes Solver-In-The-Loop (SITL) and its pre-correction variant SITL* as special cases, differing only in additional skip connections that couple the initial state and previous iterate into each update.

## Results

On the 2D Allen-Cahn equation (64×64 grid, T=15 time units, K=10 unrolled steps), HYBDR achieves the lowest test MSE across all three partial-physics settings: 5.70×10⁻³ (Diffusion Only), 5.42×10⁻³ (Reaction Only), and 4.72×10⁻³ (Both Terms). In the Both Terms setting, HYBDR reduces MSE by over 40× compared to the solver-only baseline (2.06×10⁻¹). On the 1D Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation (64-point grid, T=1 time unit, K=50 steps), HYBDR and HYBFB-POST remain stable and achieve the lowest prediction errors, while several SITL and SITL* runs fail to converge. The learned proximal operator for the reaction term matches the exact prox map with a mean absolute error of 0.053.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SITL (Um et al., 2020), HYBDR wins decisively on Allen-Cahn: in the Diffusion Only setting, HYBDR achieves MSE 5.70×10⁻³ versus SITL's 2.62×10⁻¹ (a ~46× improvement), and in the Reaction Only setting SITL fails to converge entirely while HYBDR maintains MSE 5.42×10⁻³. On Kuramoto-Sivashinsky with ResNet, HYBDR and HYBFB-POST achieve lower and more stable test MSE than SITL, with some SITL runs failing to converge. Against SITL*/INC (Wei et al., 2025), the advantage is similar on Allen-Cahn (SITL+ fails in Reaction Only, and in Diffusion Only its MSE of 9.03×10⁻³ is ~1.6× worse than HYBDR's 5.70×10⁻³). On Kuramoto-Sivashinsky with FNO, SITL actually achieves slightly lower test MSE than the proposed models, but SITL* exhibits high variance across seeds while HYBDR and HYBFB-POST remain stable. The paper also shows that replacing the ResNet with an FNO preserves the same qualitative ranking, confirming that the gains stem from the proximal splitting structure rather than the neural architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes classical proximal splitting algorithms (Douglas-Rachford and forward-backward) from optimization as the architectural backbone for hybrid PDE solvers, a connection the authors note has received little prior attention. It explicitly subsumes Solver-in-the-Loop (SITL, Um et al. 2020) and its pre-correction variant SITL* (Wei et al. 2025) as special cases of the proposed forward-backward models, showing they differ only by additional skip connections. The plug-and-play paradigm, originally developed for image restoration (Venkatakrishnan et al. 2013; Kamilov et al. 2023), is extended here from inverse problems to direct time integration of evolution PDEs, with numerical solvers replacing proximal operators and neural networks trained end-to-end inside the iterative loop.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled design space for hybrid architectures, replacing ad-hoc empirical coupling of solvers and networks with a structured optimization decomposition that clarifies the roles of pre- and post-correction. It handles incomplete physics (missing PDE terms) in a way that prior correction methods address only implicitly, and the consistent outperformance across multiple scenarios, architectures (ResNet, FNO), and resolutions suggests the gains come from the splitting structure rather than network capacity. Key open questions include establishing generalization bounds for truncated unrolled iterations (where classical convergence guarantees no longer apply), designing networks that provably satisfy proximal operator properties, and extending beyond gradient-flow PDEs to more general dynamics.
