## Problem

As frontier LLMs saturate benchmarks within months of release, humans can no longer reliably generate challenging questions, verify ground-truth answers, or evaluate complex solutions — a scenario the authors term the post-comprehension regime. This paper asks how to maintain meaningful, discriminative evaluation of LLM capabilities when full human comprehension of the tasks and solutions is infeasible.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263228/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The protocol consumes LLM-authored mathematical questions (each paired with the author's self-answer for feasibility gating), answers produced by other models, and structured critiques with localized witnesses (counterexamples, failing test cases, algebraic errors). It produces two per-model latent scores — answerer strength β and benchmarker strength α — estimated via an itemized bipartite Bradley-Terry fit over binary win/loss episodes, then mapped affinely to an Elo-like scale. Notably, difficulty is never annotated in advance; it is inferred entirely from observed outcomes, and correctness is defined adversarially (surviving critique) rather than against a trusted reference answer.

## Method

Critique-Resilient Benchmarking (CRB) replaces ground-truth grading with an adversarial generation-evaluation game: an answer is deemed correct only if no critic model produces a verified witness of failure within a fixed budget, formalizing correctness as resistance to falsification in witness-admitting domains. The protocol has two stages — feasibility gating (the benchmarker's self-answer must survive critique from all other models, filtering ill-posed or adversarially unanswerable questions) and adversarial evaluation (the answerer's solution is critiqued, with disputes resolved by a panel of judge models and, when needed, human adjudicators who verify only narrow, localized claims). The key structural insight is the asymmetry between global verification (infeasible) and local witness checking (tractable), which allows humans to serve as bounded verifiers rather than omniscient oracles. Outcomes are aggregated via an itemized bipartite Bradley-Terry model that decomposes question difficulty into an author-level component α_a and an item residual δ_{a,i}, jointly estimating answerer and benchmarker strengths with L2-regularized logistic regression and empirical Bayes hyperparameter selection.

## Results

The CRB protocol produces answerer Elo scores that correlate strongly with external math benchmarks: Spearman ρ = 0.851 (AIME 2025), 0.830 (BRUMO 2025), and 0.819 (HMMT Feb 2025), all with 95% CIs excluding zero. In a 5-fold predictive validity test, the fitted Bradley-Terry scores achieve 92.2% accuracy (vs. 64.6% baserate) and 0.208 log-loss (vs. 0.650 baserate) on held-out question-answer pairs. Frontier models (DeepSeek v3.2 Speciale, Claude 4.5 Opus, GPT-5.2, Gemini 3 Pro Preview) form a clear top cluster, followed by Llama 4 Maverick, Phi-4, GPT-4o, and GPT-3.5 at the bottom. When weak models (e.g., GPT-3.5) replace humans as adjudicators, rankings remain consistent with human verdicts (Spearman ρ ∈ [0.98, 1], Kendall τ ∈ [0.93, 1]).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing evaluation methods in a head-to-head sense; instead, it validates the CRB protocol against external signals. The answerer Elo scores correlate monotonically with three independent human-authored math benchmarks (AIME 2025, BRUMO 2025, HMMT Feb 2025), with Spearman ρ ranging from 0.819 to 0.851, confirming external validity. In the predictive validity experiment, the Bradley-Terry fit on a training split of questions predicts held-out outcomes with 92.2% accuracy, substantially outperforming the baserate predictor (64.6%) by +27.6 percentage points, and reducing log-loss from 0.650 to 0.208. For the RQ2 question of whether weak models can substitute for human adjudicators, all eight models (including GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o, which are far weaker than the frontier models being evaluated) agree with human verdicts at ρ ∈ [0.98, 1] and τ ∈ [0.93, 1], indicating that bounded verification is robust to large capability gaps between adjudicator and evaluated system. An additional analysis using GPT-5.4-high as a ground-truth proxy reveals a striking asymmetry: critique agreement rates are uniformly high across all models (0.88–0.96), while answer correctness rates vary from 0.157 (GPT-3.5) to 0.989 (DeepSeek v3.2 Speciale), confirming that catching errors is substantially easier than avoiding them.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three distinct research threads: the Bradley-Terry / Item Response Theory tradition for disentangling ability from difficulty (Bradley & Terry, 1952; Embretson & Reise, 2000), the scalable-oversight debate literature establishing that bounded verifiers can adjudicate disputes between stronger provers (Irving et al., 2018; Brown-Cohen et al., 2024), and the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). It departs from LLM-as-a-Judge by restricting adjudication to localized, falsifiable claims rather than holistic scoring, and from dynamic-benchmark efforts (ZeroSumEval, YourBench) by targeting the specific failure mode of unavailable ground truth rather than benchmark staleness. The itemized bipartite structure—where the same model is scored both as answerer and as question-author—is a novel adaptation of psychometric models to a setting where items are generated by the systems being evaluated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The post-comprehension regime is not hypothetical: frontier models already saturate competition-level math benchmarks within months of release, and the paper demonstrates a concrete protocol that remains discriminative without human-authored answer keys. The finding that weak models (GPT-3.5) adjudicate localized claims with near-perfect agreement with human experts (ρ ≥ 0.98) has direct implications for AI safety oversight, suggesting that bounded verification can scale even when the evaluator is far weaker than the evaluated system. Key limitations include the assumption of witness-admitting domains (math, code) and the relativity of critique-resilient correctness to the current critic pool; the paper itself notes that a future regime where even local verification becomes infeasible would require fundamentally different approaches.
