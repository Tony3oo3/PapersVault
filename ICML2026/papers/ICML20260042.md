## Problem

Flow-matching critics have shown substantial empirical gains over monolithic Q-networks in off-policy RL, but the mechanism behind their success remains unexplained. A natural hypothesis—that they implicitly model return distributions like distributional RL—is tested and ruled out, as explicitly incorporating distributional objectives often degrades performance. The paper identifies the actual mechanisms: test-time recovery from imperfect intermediate estimates and preservation of representational plasticity under non-stationary TD targets.

## Input / Output

The critic takes a state-action pair (s, a) and an initial noise sample z drawn from a uniform distribution, then produces a scalar Q-value by numerically integrating a learned velocity field v_θ(z, t | s, a) over K Euler steps from t=0 to t=1. Unlike monolithic critics that map (s,a) to a scalar in one forward pass, the output here is the terminal state of an ODE trajectory, and the number of integration steps K is a free inference-time knob that trades compute for robustness. During training, the velocity field is supervised at every intermediate interpolant z(t) = t·z + (1-t)·y(s,a), not just at the terminal value.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a mechanistic analysis showing that flow-matching critics succeed because they couple a learned velocity field with an integration procedure used both during training (dense supervision at every interpolant) and at inference (iterative ODE integration to read out Q-values). This coupling yields two distinct benefits absent in monolithic critics: (1) test-time recovery, where errors in early integration steps are progressively damped by subsequent steps, formalized as incremental stability of the inference dynamics with a stability factor β_K that decreases as K grows; and (2) plastic feature learning, where the integration process acts as a buffer between non-stationary TD targets and learned representations, allowing the network to adapt by reweighting existing features through downstream gain parameters rather than overwriting them. A linear analysis (Theorem 6.1) proves that with frozen feature directions, flow-matching predictors can still track moving targets purely through gain dynamics, while monolithic predictors must modify their feature vectors directly. Empirically, flow-matching critics achieve 2× performance gains and 5-10× sample efficiency improvements in high-UTD online RL, remain stable when features are frozen mid-training, and degrade gracefully under noisy TD supervision—none of which hold for monolithic or ResNet-style baselines with equivalent computation graphs.

## Results

Flow-matching critics (floq) achieve over 2× higher final return and 5–10× better sample efficiency compared to monolithic critics (FQL) in high-UTD online RL with offline data (RLPD framework, 50:50 offline/online mixture). In the expected-value vs. distributional comparison on OGBench, expected-value floq matches or exceeds distributional floq on success rate across all four tasks (e.g., 52% vs. 30% on hmmaze-large, 86% vs. 74% on antmaze-giant) while producing substantially lower-variance Q-value estimates (e.g., 0.2 vs. 4.5 on hmmaze-large). Flow-matching critics also demonstrate test-time recovery: using a stale velocity field for the first 25–50% of integration steps still yields competitive or even improved performance on 2 of 3 environments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares flow-matching critics (floq) against monolithic critics (FQL), distributional RL baselines (C51, IQN), and a distributional variant of floq itself. Expected-value floq consistently outperforms its distributional counterpart on success rate (e.g., 52% vs. 30% on hmmaze-large, 86% vs. 74% on antmaze-giant) while learning lower-variance Q-value distributions, ruling out distributional modeling as the source of gains. In high-UTD online RL (RLPD framework), floq achieves roughly 2× higher final return and 5–10× better sample efficiency than RLPD+FQL, and remains stable at the highest UTD values tested where FQL destabilizes. Flow-matching critics also outperform monolithic ResNet-style critics on robustness probes: freezing intermediate features causes FQL to collapse to near-zero performance while floq recovers to near-baseline levels, and injecting target noise degrades FQL substantially faster than floq. Supervising velocities (rather than absolute TD targets at each integration step) is critical: a variant that directly regresses to TD targets at every step collapses to monolithic-level performance, confirming that dense velocity supervision is the key mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides a mechanistic explanation for why flow-matching critics (floq, Agrawalla et al. 2025) outperform monolithic Q-networks in off-policy RL, building directly on the flow-matching framework of Lipman et al. (2023) and the empirical results of Agrawalla et al. (2025), Espinosa-Dice et al. (2025a), and Dong et al. (2025). It departs from the prevailing hypothesis that distributional RL (C51, IQN) explains these gains, instead identifying two distinct mechanisms: test-time recovery from dense velocity supervision and preservation of representational plasticity under non-stationary TD targets. The analysis connects to the broader plasticity and stability literature in TD-learning (Lyle et al. 2023; Kumar et al. 2022; Nikishin et al. 2022), showing that flow-matching's implicit bias addresses pathologies that previously required explicit regularization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By ruling out distributional RL and identifying test-time recovery plus plastic feature learning as the true mechanisms, this paper redirects the design space for value-function critics: the key ingredient is dense trajectory-level velocity supervision coupled with iterative integration, not modeling return distributions. The practical payoff is substantial—2× performance and 5–10× sample efficiency in high-UTD online RL with offline data—making flow-matching critics a strong candidate for scalable RL pipelines. Open questions include extending the linear-theory analysis to nonlinear function approximation, exploring higher-dimensional velocity representations, and testing whether the plasticity benefits transfer to continual learning with shifting task distributions.
