## Problem

Learning dexterous manipulation policies from human hand-object demonstrations for long-horizon, bimanual tasks with articulated objects remains challenging due to the high-dimensional action space, intricate contact sequences, and the embodiment gap between human and robot hands. Prior approaches either rely on manual reward engineering, costly teleoperation data collection, or kinematic retargeting that produces visually plausible but physically infeasible motions. There is also a lack of standardized benchmarks for comparing dexterous hand designs under a unified functional evaluation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single MANO-tracked human bimanual demonstration (6-DoF wrist poses and 21 fingertip keypoints per hand, plus per-part object position, rotation, and 1-DoF articulation angle over T timesteps) and a pair of dexterous hand URDF models with 6-DoF floating wrists. It produces a PPO policy that outputs normalized joint actions for all actuated joints of both hands simultaneously, using state-based observations (object states, joint targets, finger-to-object distances, and normalized contact forces). The policy is evaluated via an ADD-AUC tracking metric computed per object part to accommodate articulated objects.

## Method

DexMachina is a curriculum-based RL algorithm whose key mechanism is Virtual Object Controllers (VOCs): spring-damper PD controllers that apply virtual forces to drive the object along its demonstrated trajectory, initially carrying most of the manipulation burden and then exponentially decaying as the policy's reward performance crosses thresholds. The policy is trained with a hybrid action formulation (residual actions on wrist joints anchored to kinematically retargeted base poses, absolute actions on finger joints) and a weighted sum of task reward (object pose and articulation tracking) plus auxiliary rewards for motion imitation, behavior cloning, and contact matching. The auto-curriculum schedules VOC gain decay based on rolling reward means, allowing the policy to first explore the full demonstration sequence without catastrophic early failures, then gradually assume full control. Training uses PPO with 12,000 parallel Genesis environments, and the same algorithm and reward structure apply uniformly across all six evaluated hand embodiments without hand-specific modifications.

## Results

DexMachina is evaluated on 4 dexterous hands (Inspire, Allegro, XHand, Schunk) across 7 ARCTIC demonstration clips using an ADD-AUC success-rate metric (averaged over 5 seeds, 20 episodes each). On short-horizon tasks (~100 timesteps), the re-implemented ObjDex baseline already achieves >90% success on Ketchup-100 and >70% on Mixer-170 (vs. 41.2% and 57.6% reported in the original paper). On long-horizon tasks (up to 300 timesteps), DexMachina with its virtual-object-controller curriculum significantly outperforms all baselines, while task-reward-only and auxiliary-reward-only settings show inconsistent or insufficient gains. The paper also demonstrates successful training on demonstrations up to 600 timesteps, a regime where baseline methods fail due to early-stage episode termination.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against **Kinematics Only** (Qin et al., 2023), DexMachina wins decisively: direct replay of kinematic retargeting results visually aligns with human hands but cannot complete any task beyond slightly lifting objects, as the motions are not dynamically feasible. Against **ObjDex** (Chen et al., 2024), the authors' re-implementation already improves on the original (e.g., >90% vs. 41.2% on Ketchup-100; >70% vs. 57.6% on Mixer-170), but DexMachina further surpasses it, particularly on long-horizon clips where task reward alone is insufficient and the curriculum enables the policy to explore the full demonstration without catastrophic early failures. Against **ManipTrans** (Li et al., 2025), DexMachina clearly wins: ManipTrans's curriculum over error thresholds and physics parameters (gravity, friction) yields no clear improvement over the no-curriculum setting and exhibits unstable training where initial high task reward drops as the curriculum progresses and cannot recover. The paper also ablates a 'Task + Aux Rewards without curriculum' setting, showing that the virtual-object-controller curriculum is the critical component driving the performance gap on complex long-horizon tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the line of work using human demonstrations to guide dexterous manipulation learning (Chen et al. 2024, Li et al. 2025) and kinematic retargeting (Qin et al. 2023), but reframes the objective from matching hand poses to tracking object states, which it terms 'functional retargeting.' The curriculum mechanism departs from prior approaches that relax physical constraints (gravity, friction, error thresholds) by instead using virtual spring-damper object controllers that actively drive the object along its demonstrated trajectory before decaying, allowing the policy to explore the full task sequence without catastrophic early failures. The hybrid action formulation (residual wrist + absolute finger joints) and contact-aware reward design extend the reward engineering practices from short-horizon dexterous RL (Andrychowicz et al. 2020, Lum et al. 2024) to the bimanual articulated-object setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DexMachina benchmark (6 hands × 5 articulated objects × 7 demonstrations) provides the first standardized, accessible platform for functional comparison of dexterous hand designs, which is practically valuable given the recent surge in hand hardware development. Key open questions include sim-to-real transfer (the policy uses privileged physics information and estimated hand dynamics), whether the virtual object controller curriculum generalizes beyond 1-DoF articulated objects, and whether the findings about hand capability (DoF > size, anthropomorphism ≠ performance) hold on real hardware. The paper's limitation to floating wrists and open-source hand assets with estimated physical properties means the hardware comparison conclusions are preliminary.
