## Problem

Deep networks optimize features via cross-entropy without explicit margin maximization, while classical SVMs maximize margin on a fixed feature space. Existing hybrid Deep-SVM approaches treat the deep feature extractor as a static input to an SVM head, so the SVM's geometric structure never flows back to shape representation learning. The paper addresses the absence of an explicit mechanism to actively reshape the feature manifold based on the optimal separating hyperplane, particularly in data-scarce regimes where implicit margin convergence is too slow.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100 and SVHN, 96×96 for STL-10) and outputs a class label. During Phase II training, the standard FC/Softmax head is bypassed: raw backbone features are L2-normalized onto the unit hypersphere, optionally perturbed by a GAS adversarial step, and then regressed via MSE toward a detached geometric target synthesized from the current SVM boundary. The final classifier is the linear SVM itself, not a softmax layer.

## Method

DDSVM treats the CNN backbone as a trainable spherical kernel and runs an iterative DNN-SVM-DNN loop: Phase I performs standard cross-entropy warm-up until training accuracy reaches a threshold (τ=0.95), then Phase II alternates between re-fitting one-vs-rest linear SVMs on the frozen L2-normalized features and updating the backbone to pull each feature along an SVM-defined margin-widening direction. The 'Squeeze' mechanism applies adaptive pressure proportional to each sample's normalized distance to the boundary (|ρ_i|^p), combined with a rest-class repulsion term that pushes features away from other class normals. A Geometric Adversarial Squeeze (GAS) extension generates FGSM-style L∞ perturbations in feature space to synthesize worst-case hard examples, which are then pulled back to the safe geometric target, collapsing a wide cone of the feature space toward the class center. The spherical constraint guarantees that backpropagated gradients lie strictly in the tangent space, so optimization focuses exclusively on angular separability rather than feature magnitude.

## Results

On the reduced-data regime (~8% of training images), DDSVM reduces top-1 error by 7.54% on CIFAR-10 (30.75%→23.21%), 5.58% on CIFAR-100 (68.25%→62.67%), and 8.31% on STL-10 (47.29%→38.98%) compared to cross-entropy training with ResNet-18. In the full-data regime, DDSVM is statistically indistinguishable from CE (within ±0.4%), consistent with the implicit max-margin convergence of gradient descent on CE. On CIFAR-10-C at severity 5, DDSVM improves mean Corruption Error from 42.25% to 41.79%, with the largest gains on geometric/spatial corruptions (zoom_blur −3.64, defocus_blur −1.65).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On Reduced CIFAR-10 (the primary comparison setting), DDSVM (23.21±0.77%) outperforms all three angular-margin softmax baselines: ArcFace (25.92±2.82%), CosFace (25.67±2.76%), and Large-Margin Softmax (25.38±0.82%), with margins of 2.2–2.7 percentage points. It also beats the two augmentation regularizers used in isolation: CE+Mixup (25.08±1.39%) and CE+CutMix (25.17±1.03%). The gains are most pronounced in variance reduction (DDSVM std 0.77 vs. CE std 5.27 on CIFAR-10), indicating the SVM-driven geometric target stabilizes optimization under data scarcity. Combining DDSVM with Mixup or CutMix yields further improvements to 17.67% and 17.88% respectively, confirming the two regularizers address orthogonal error sources. On full-data settings, DDSVM ties or slightly trails CE (e.g., 4.70% vs. 4.64% on CIFAR-10), and the CLIP zero-shot comparison (11.18% on CIFAR-10) is explicitly framed as non-comparable since it leverages 400M pre-training image-text pairs.

## Relation to Prior Work

DDSVM extends the line of Deep-SVM hybrids (Niu & Suen, 2012; Tang, 2013) by replacing the static feature-to-SVM pipeline with an iterative alternating loop in which the SVM boundary is re-fit each epoch and then used as a detached geometric target to reshape the backbone, drawing its alternating-optimization structure from SADH (Shen et al., 2018). The Geometric Adversarial Squeeze (GAS) component repurposes FGSM's sign-based L∞ perturbation (Goodfellow et al., 2015) not for input-space robustness but as a feature-space hard-example generator that feeds the squeeze loss. Conceptually, the paper positions itself as an explicit, aggressive acceleration of the slow O(1/log t) implicit max-margin convergence that Soudry et al. (2018) proved for gradient descent on separable data, and it departs from angular-margin softmax losses (ArcFace, CosFace, LMS) by deriving the target dynamically from the current SVM geometry rather than imposing a fixed angular offset.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DDSVM offers a practical geometric regularizer for the from-scratch, data-scarce classification niche (medical imaging, industrial defect detection, non-RGB sensing) where no pre-training corpus or foundation-model checkpoint is available, delivering 5.6–8.3% error reduction and up to 7× variance reduction under ~8% data without exceeding the standard CE training budget. The method's honest scoping—no measurable gain in data-abundant settings, linear OVR scaling with class count, and no closed-form justification for the empirically optimal GAS magnitude—leaves clear open questions about whether the iterative SVM-fitting overhead can be eliminated by embedding the squeeze directly into the network's optimization. The clean composability with image-space augmentation (DDSVM+Mixup reaching 17.67% vs. 23.21% alone) suggests the geometric and distributional regularizers address orthogonal failure modes, a finding that could inform hybrid training recipes beyond this specific framework.
