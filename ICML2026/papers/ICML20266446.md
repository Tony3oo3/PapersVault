## Problem

The generative AI community lacks operational definitions for reasoning, leading to construct validity failures in evaluation where QA accuracy is conflated with the reasoning process itself. This definitional ambiguity enables unverifiable claims about AI reasoning, obscures measurable progress, and makes it impossible to distinguish genuine rule-governed reasoning from superficial emulation (termed 'reasoning zombies'). The paper argues that method-agnostic formal definitions are achievable and necessary for trustworthy autonomous reasoning research.

## Input / Output

The reasoning algorithm (Algorithm 1) consumes an initial rule set R₀, initial beliefs B₀, a stream of evidence {E_i}, and a stopping rule s_stop, and iteratively produces updated states S_t = ⟨B_t, E_t, R_t⟩ until the stopping condition is met. The final output is the terminal belief state B_T and a full reasoning trace T recording each step's selected rules and state transitions. The rule set is partitioned into local rules (type B×E→B, updating beliefs) and meta rules (type R×B×E→R, updating the rule set itself), with rule selectors s_L and s_M that can be implemented via hard-coding, learning, or hybrid approaches.

## Method

The paper provides a formal operational definition of reasoning as the iterated application of rules to prior beliefs and current evidence, yielding dynamically updated states where every output belief is the result of exact rule application. Validity is defined as exact rule execution (not partial or approximate), while soundness additionally requires all premises to be true with respect to external evaluation; validity is shown to be independent of the rule-selection algorithm, echoing Broome's correctness-by-permissibility. The framework is demonstrated to subsume logical deduction, Bayesian inference, reinforcement learning, and probabilistic next-token prediction as special cases that differ only in how rules, beliefs, and evidence are instantiated. A key design choice is the black-box rule selector, which bridges symbolic and neural interpretations by permitting any implementation (hard-coded, learned, or hybrid) without affecting whether the process constitutes valid reasoning. The paper also introduces the 'reasoning zombie' (r-zombie) concept to formalize systems that produce reasoning-like output without valid internal mechanisms, and provides a community checklist for scientific communication in AI reasoning research.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experimental results. Its primary contributions are (1) a formal operational definition of reasoning as iterated exact rule application (Def. 2.4, Algorithm 1), decomposed into local rules updating beliefs and meta-rules updating the rule set, and (2) a community checklist for scientific communication in AI reasoning research. The paper argues that validity (exact rule application) is independent of soundness (external correctness) and of rule-selection strategy, and introduces the 'reasoning zombie' (r-zombie) concept to distinguish superficially reasoning-like behavior from genuinely valid reasoning processes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not benchmark against competing methods. Instead, it positions its operational definition against several alternative characterizations of reasoning discussed in Appendix C.1: the Merriam-Webster dictionary definition (deemed non-operational), Richardson's (2018) SEP definition (lacking concrete mechanisms), Portoraro's (2025) automated-reasoning framing, and the views that 'reasoning is guided search' or 'reasoning is planning' (identified as logical fallacies since search/planning are subroutines of reasoning, not equivalent to it). The paper also contrasts its rule-based validity criterion with common generative-AI evaluation practices—QA accuracy as a proxy for reasoning, chain-of-thought traces as explanations, and benchmark SOTA as evidence of reasoning—arguing each conflates process with product or fails to guarantee exact rule application. No empirical head-to-head comparisons are presented.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes ideas from symbolic AI (Hilbert systems, natural deduction, automated theorem proving), Broome's (2013) rule-governed account of rationality, Chollet's (2019) process-based intelligence framework, and the construct validity tradition from psychology (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955) to propose a unified operational definition of reasoning. It departs from the generative AI community's implicit treatment of reasoning by explicitly rejecting the conflation of benchmark accuracy with the reasoning process, and by reviving the philosophical zombie thought experiment as the 'reasoning zombie' (r-zombie) to formalize the black-box emulation problem. The paper positions itself as method-agnostic, arguing that the same rule-based formalism accommodates symbolic, neural, and neuro-symbolic approaches (e.g., Lean, AlphaGeometry 2, AlphaProof), rather than advocating for any particular architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine and widely acknowledged gap: the generative AI community lacks operational definitions for reasoning, making construct validity of reasoning evaluations unverifiable. The r-zombie framing and the distinction between validity (exact rule application) and soundness (external correctness) provide a concrete vocabulary for diagnosing when LRM benchmark success may reflect memorization or pattern-matching rather than genuine reasoning. Key open questions remain: whether neural network parameters can store and exactly apply rules, how to locate and audit those rules in black-box models, and whether scaling alone can bridge the gap between r-zombie behavior and valid reasoning. The paper's main limitation is that it offers no empirical validation of its definitions and no practical implementation beyond trivial Python examples, leaving the community to test whether the formalism is workable at scale.
