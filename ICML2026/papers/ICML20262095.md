## Problem

While LLM agents are increasingly deployed for multi-step tasks, the role of horizon length—the number of interaction steps required for task completion—as an independent factor shaping RL training dynamics remains poorly understood. Prior work treats horizon as an environmental budget or conflates it with reasoning complexity, leaving it unclear whether training failures on long-horizon tasks stem from the horizon itself or from confounding factors like increased planning difficulty. This paper isolates horizon length as a primary training bottleneck through controlled experiments where reasoning complexity is held constant.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a task prompt (Sudoku grid or Rush Hour board state), the current environment observation, and a non-growing sliding-window memory of only the most recent K=2 interaction turns. Each step produces a structured output containing a  reasoning block (discarded from memory), a REASON summary, and an ACTION field; under macro-action settings, a single action can encode multiple cell assignments or multi-cell moves, effectively compressing the interaction horizon. The non-growing memory design explicitly controls the self-conditioning effect where accumulated errors from the agent's own past generations degrade future predictions.

## Method

The paper constructs controlled task suites (Sudoku, Rush Hour) where puzzles are filtered to ensure constant solving complexity (verified via HoDoKu technique classification) while varying only the goal distance d(s₀,g), enabling clean isolation of horizon effects. Training uses SFT initialization followed by off-policy REINFORCE with a two-stage importance sampling correction (geometric-mean masked IS for filtering unreliable trajectories, sequence-level truncated IS for variance control) and a mixed trajectory/step-level advantage with batch normalization. The key intervention is horizon reduction via two mechanisms: (a) macro actions that compose multiple atomic actions into a single policy step, and (b) subgoal decomposition that segments the trajectory at verifiable intermediate milestones with dense per-segment returns. A controlled ablation (same macro-action policy but restricted to single atomic execution per turn) confirms that effective horizon, not policy representation, is the critical determinant of training stability. The paper further discovers horizon generalization: models trained under reduced horizons generalize to previously unseen longer horizons at inference time, enabling curriculum strategies where short-horizon competence bootstraps long-horizon learning.

## Results

On Sudoku (the primary testbed), RL with macro actions (horizon reduction) trained on L3–L4 (goal distance 21–30) achieves 91% avg@4 on L4 and 61% avg@4 on L6, whereas the atomic-action RL baseline trained on the same range collapses, reaching only 27% avg@4 on L4 and 0% on L6. On Rush Hour, macro-action RL with a horizon curriculum (short→long) reaches 85.7% avg@4 on the 10–12 goal-distance range and 56.2% on 13–15, compared to 43.3% and 13.8% for the atomic-action RL baseline. The base Qwen3-1.7B model without any training achieves only 28% pass@4 on L4 Sudoku and 0% beyond L5, confirming that RL is necessary but horizon reduction is essential for stability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external RL algorithms; instead, it isolates the effect of horizon length by comparing atomic actions versus macro actions (horizon reduction) under identical training budgets, model scale, and optimizer. On Sudoku L4 (goal distance 26–30), macro-action RL trained on L1–L2 achieves 41.75% avg@4, more than 3× the 13.75% of the atomic-action RL baseline trained on the same range. The gap widens further at L5–L6, where macro RL reaches 26.25% and 8.25% respectively while atomic RL drops to 4.00% and 0.50%. A controlled ablation (Figure 4) confirms that the benefit is specifically due to reduced effective horizon rather than a stronger policy representation: when the macro-action policy is forced to execute only one atomic step per turn, performance initially improves but then collapses, mirroring the atomic baseline. On Rush Hour, macro-action RL with curriculum training (short→long) achieves 85.65% avg@4 on the 10–12 range versus 43.25% for atomic RL, and extends to 56.18% on the unseen 13–15 range where atomic RL reaches only 13.75%. The horizon-reduction advantage persists across model scale (1.7B vs. 4B), optimizer (REINFORCE vs. GRPO), and environment (Sudoku, Rush Hour, WebShop), confirming it is a cross-cutting principle rather than an artifact of a specific setup.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the REINFORCE policy gradient framework (Williams, 1992) augmented with the importance sampling corrections for training-inference mismatch developed by Liu et al. (2025a) and Yao et al. (2025), positioning its RL method as a stabilized off-policy REINFORCE variant. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant research directions of system-level orchestration (context engineering, workflow decomposition) and algorithmic RL improvements (GRPO, DAPO, GiGPO) by arguing that horizon length is an underexplored, independent bottleneck that precedes algorithmic sophistication. It connects to Park et al. (2025) 'Horizon reduction makes RL scalable' in classical RL but is the first to systematically isolate and validate this principle specifically for LLM agent training, reframing horizon as an intrinsic task property (goal distance) rather than merely an environmental interaction budget.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean diagnostic: horizon length alone, with reasoning complexity held constant, causes RL training collapse in LLM agents, and a simple structural intervention (macro actions or subgoal decomposition) resolves it. The discovery of 'horizon generalization'—that models trained on shorter horizons transfer to longer ones—offers a practical curriculum strategy for scaling agent capabilities. Key limitations include reliance on small models (1.7B–4B, single Qwen family), controlled text-based games, and the finding that horizon generalization does not extend to new reasoning techniques, suggesting the approach amplifies existing capabilities rather than creating new ones. Open questions include whether the horizon bottleneck persists at frontier scale, whether it generalizes to stochastic or partially observable real-world environments, and how to combine horizon reduction with process reward models for truly open-ended tasks.
