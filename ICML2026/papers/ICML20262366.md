## Problem

Standard block-wise post-training quantization (PTQ) minimizes MSE at intermediate Transformer block outputs, which preserves language modeling and understanding accuracy but degrades generation quality—especially on long-form reasoning tasks. The paper identifies two root causes: (i) the LM head is entirely omitted from block-wise optimization, and (ii) MSE at the logit level does not guarantee matching token probability distributions, so small logit shifts can flip top-1 predictions and compound over autoregressive generation.

## Input / Output

LFQ takes full-precision model weights and a small set of calibration sequences (drawn sequentially from the C4 training set, 400–800 samples of 2048–4096 tokens) and produces low-bit weight-only quantized weights (W4 per-channel or W3/W4 group-wise with group size 128). Only the quantization parameters of the final Transformer block are optimized under the new objective; all preceding blocks retain their standard MSE-based calibration. The resulting quantized weights are fully compatible with existing packing/unpacking kernels (e.g., GPTQ, AWQ, LUT-GEMM) without any architectural modification.

## Method

LFQ replaces the MSE reconstruction loss with a cross-entropy loss computed between the FP model's logits (X·W_FP·W_Head) and the quantized model's logits (X·W_q·W_Head) exclusively for the final Transformer block, while all earlier blocks continue to be quantized via standard MSE. Because minimizing cross-entropy is equivalent to minimizing KL divergence, this directly aligns the quantized model's token-level probability distribution with the FP model's, ensuring matching top-1 and top-k token orderings. The approach is agnostic to the underlying block-wise PTQ method: for FlexRound it optimizes (s₁, S₂, s₃), for OmniQuant it optimizes (γ, β), and for Block-AP it optimizes (s, W_FP), all under the same cross-entropy objective. Ablations confirm that applying LFQ to the final block alone is sufficient—extending it to additional blocks yields no further generation gains and can slightly hurt MMLU.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct at W4, FlexRound+LFQ achieves 71.35 IFEval and 73.4 MATH500 (greedy), narrowing the gap to the BF16 baseline (70.79 / 74.2) to within 1 pp, while FlexRound alone scores 69.50 / 72.6. At the more aggressive W3g128 setting, LFQ recovers 1.3 pp on IFEval (67.84 vs. 66.54) and 2.4 pp on MATH500 (68.0 vs. 65.6) over FlexRound. For reasoning models, FlexRound+LFQ on L1-Qwen-7B-Max (W4) lifts AIME'24 greedy accuracy from 30.00 to 43.33 (BF16: 46.67) and pass@8 from 51.71 to 55.09 (BF16: 55.30). On the MoE model Qwen3-30B-A3B (W4g128), LFQ improves AIME'25 greedy from 53.33 to 60.00 (BF16: 66.67) and pass@1 from 57.29 to 58.75 (BF16: 62.50).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FlexRound, OmniQuant, and Block-AP (the three block-wise PTQ baselines), LFQ consistently wins on text generation (IFEval, MATH500, AIME) by 1–3 pp at W4 and 1.5–3 pp at W3g128, while matching or slightly improving language modeling (WikiText2) and understanding (MMLU). On the MoE model Qwen3-30B-A3B, FlexRound+LFQ (AIME'25 greedy 60.00, pass@1 58.75) substantially outperforms GPTQ (50.00 / 55.63) and FlexRound alone (53.33 / 57.29). Against RILQ (a LoRA-based quantization error compensation method), LFQ wins on IFEval and GSM8K across all three PTQ backbones (e.g., FlexRound+LFQ: 72.09 IFEval vs. FlexRound+RILQ: 71.90; 81.80 GSM8K vs. 80.52), while RILQ retains an edge on WikiText2 perplexity and MMLU due to its additional LoRA parameters. The paper notes the two methods are orthogonal and that their combination (LFQ+RILQ) inherits strengths of both, achieving near-LFQ generation scores with near-RILQ language modeling scores.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the block-wise PTQ lineage (FlexRound, OmniQuant, Block-AP), which quantizes each Transformer block by minimizing MSE between FP and quantized block outputs. The key departure is a diagnostic observation: because these methods ignore the LM head and use MSE rather than a distributional objective, the quantized model's token probability distribution can diverge from the FP model's, causing generation quality to degrade even when intermediate activations are well-matched. LFQ addresses this by replacing the MSE objective with cross-entropy at the logit level for the final block only, while leaving all other blocks and the quantization parameterization unchanged. It is also positioned as orthogonal to LoRA-based error compensation (RILQ), with the paper showing the two can be combined.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLMs increasingly rely on long-form generation and extended chains of thought for reasoning tasks, preserving token-level fidelity under low-bit quantization becomes a practical necessity for cost-effective deployment. LFQ's simplicity (a single-block loss swap, single-GPU compatible, kernel-compatible) makes it an easy drop-in for existing block-wise PTQ pipelines. Open questions include whether the cross-entropy objective helps at sub-3-bit regimes where quantization error is more severe, whether it transfers to QAT settings, and whether the final-block-only strategy remains sufficient for much deeper architectures. The paper does not explore interaction with speculative decoding or multi-token prediction, which are increasingly relevant serving paradigms.
