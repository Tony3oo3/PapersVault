## Problem

Distributionally robust Markov games (DR-MGs) under the average-reward criterion have been largely unexplored, despite being the natural performance measure for multi-agent systems operating over indefinite horizons. Two fundamental obstacles make this setting qualitatively harder than discounted or finite-horizon DR-MGs: (1) the player-specific worst-case environment breaks the min-max duality that underpins saddle-point existence proofs, and (2) the average-reward Bellman equation is non-contraction and its solvability/uniqueness were previously unresolved. The paper establishes that without structural assumptions, stationary robust Nash equilibria may not exist, and provides the first complete theoretical and algorithmic treatment under irreducible and weakly communicating settings.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a finite state space, per-agent finite action spaces, per-agent reward functions, and an (s,a)-rectangular uncertainty set of transition kernels (independently defined over each state-action pair, assumed compact and convex) as input. The output is a stationary Nash Equilibrium policy profile (one time-independent policy per agent) together with the robust average reward value, defined as the worst-case long-run average reward over all admissible kernels in the uncertainty set. Under irreducibility the robust average reward is state-independent; under the weaker weakly communicating assumption only the optimal robust average reward is guaranteed to be state-independent.

## Method

Under irreducibility, the paper proves that the robust Bellman equation for any fixed policy is solvable and unique up to a constant shift, and that the optimal policy set is convex and upper semi-continuous, enabling a Kakutani fixed-point argument for NE existence. Under the more general weakly communicating setting, where the fixed-policy Bellman equation may be unsolvable, the authors instead construct a proxy set-valued map from the constant-gain optimal robust Bellman operator and show it is contained in the true best-response map, then prove it admits a fixed point. For algorithms, they propose (1) a Robust Nash Iteration that converges under additional game-structure assumptions and a norm-form NE oracle, and (2) a Robust TD Descent algorithm that characterizes NE via a global temporal-difference gap, smooths it with a Moreau envelope, and applies a two-time-scale stochastic approximation to converge to a stationary point without any NE oracle. Finally, they show that the average-reward robust NE is the limit of discounted robust NE as the discount factor approaches one, enabling approximation via the more tractable discounted setting.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no experimental benchmarks or quantitative performance tables. The headline results are: (1) a stationary robust Nash Equilibrium exists for irreducible DR-MGs (Theorem 4.8) and for weakly communicating DR-MGs (Theorem 5.5); (2) the robust Bellman equation is solvable and unique up to constant shifts under irreducibility (Theorem 4.6); (3) the Robust TD Descent algorithm (Algorithm 1) converges to a stationary point of the smoothed TD gap under Robbins-Monro step sizes (Theorem 6.3); and (4) any cluster point of discounted NEs as γ→1 is an average-reward NE, and any ε-NE of the γ-discounted game is an O(ε)-NE under average reward (Theorem 7.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not include experimental comparisons against baselines, as it is a theoretical contribution. Instead, it positions itself against prior methods by showing they cannot be extended to the average-reward robust setting: finite-horizon DR-MG results (Blanchet et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2023) rely on backward induction that fails for infinite horizons; discounted DR-MG NE existence (Kardeş et al., 2011) depends on uniqueness of the discounted robust Bellman equation, which does not carry over; and non-robust average-reward MG results (Filar & Vrieze, 1996; Sobel, 1971) break down because the worst-case kernel is player-specific, destroying min-max duality even in zero-sum games (Lemma 3.1 provides a concrete counterexample). The paper's two algorithms are compared qualitatively: Algorithm 2 (Robust Nash Iteration) requires a PPAD-complete NE oracle and additional game-structure assumptions, while Algorithm 1 (Robust TD Descent) bypasses the oracle entirely but only guarantees convergence to a stationary point of the smoothed objective rather than an exact NE.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the distributionally robust Markov game framework from the well-studied discounted and finite-horizon settings (Kardeş et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2020; Blanchet et al., 2023) to the average-reward criterion, which the authors argue is the natural performance measure for long-horizon multi-agent systems. It builds on the single-agent robust average-reward MDP theory of Wang et al. (2023b) and Wang & Si (2025), but corrects prior solvability claims and establishes the first rigorous correspondence between optimal policies and robust Bellman equation solutions under irreducibility. The algorithmic side draws on Nash iteration (Hu & Wellman, 2003) and temporal-difference methods for Markov games (Sahabandu et al., 2024), adapting them to the non-smooth, non-contraction structure of the robust average-reward Bellman operator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first comprehensive treatment of average-reward DR-MGs, resolving the fundamental solvability question (existence of stationary NE) under both irreducible and weakly communicating assumptions, and providing the first provably convergent algorithms for this setting. The impossibility result (Lemma 3.1) and the demonstration that min-max duality fails even in robust zero-sum games are important conceptual contributions that explain why prior discounted/finite-horizon techniques cannot be transplanted. Open questions include whether local gradient domination holds for the smoothed TD objective (yielding stronger convergence), whether the framework extends beyond (s,a)-rectangularity to state-coupled ambiguity, and how to handle fully shared worst-case kernels in cooperative or Stackelberg robust games.
