## Problem

Despite recent proposals to eliminate attention sink (Sink Attention in GPT-OSS, Gated Attention in Qwen3-Next), no unified analysis explains how these mechanisms relate to each other or why removing the sink improves performance. Additionally, existing LLMs exhibit a head collapse phenomenon where only a fixed subset of attention heads actively contributes to generation, yet no training method addresses this imbalance within attention layers.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261178/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For pretraining, the method consumes raw text from FineWeb-Edu and trains 0.6B/1B/2B models from scratch under Chinchilla-optimal token budgets. For fine-tuning, it takes pretrained LLM weights (Qwen3-4B/8B, LLaMA3.1-8B) and applies LoRA (rank 16) on the AceReason-Nemotron long-sequence reasoning dataset. The output is a model whose attention heads exhibit balanced activation, verified via a head load imbalance metric (coefficient of variation of per-head importance scores) and downstream benchmark accuracy.

## Method

The paper proves that the attention weight on the sink component in Vanilla Attention and Sink Attention acts as an implicit gating factor G = 1 − A_sink, which is mathematically equivalent (in sigmoid form) to the explicit gating factor in Gated Attention, thereby revealing a native MoE structure where heads are experts and sink-induced gates are routers. To counter head collapse, the authors introduce a sink-aware auxiliary load balancing loss that minimizes the squared coefficient of variation of head importance scores within each layer, added to the standard language modeling objective. For fine-tuning pretrained models where collapse has already set in, they adopt a shared/routed head strategy inspired by DeepSeek-MoE: the top-m most important heads per layer are frozen as shared heads while the remaining routed heads are subject to the balancing loss, preventing the 'head pinning effect' where all heads inflate their gates to match dominant ones. The implementation computes gating factors from log-sum-exp values returned by Flash Attention kernels, avoiding materialization of full attention matrices and adding less than 2% training overhead.

## Results

On models trained from scratch (0.6B–2B), Gated Attention with the auxiliary load balancing loss achieves the best average benchmark score of 47.70 at 2B scale, outperforming Sink Attention (46.50) and Vanilla Attention (46.83). The auxiliary loss yields consistent gains of +0.15 to +1.40 points in average accuracy across all attention mechanisms and model sizes, with the largest improvement for Gated Attention at 2B (+1.40). For fine-tuning existing LLMs, the sink-aware loss improves reasoning accuracy by +0.51 to +2.47 points and long-context accuracy by +1.29 to +4.71 points, with LLaMA3.1-8B benefiting most (reasoning 56.50→58.97, LongBench 40.51→43.98).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the three attention mechanisms trained from scratch, Gated Attention (Qiu et al., 2025) consistently outperforms Sink Attention (GPT-OSS, Agarwal et al., 2025), which in turn outperforms Vanilla Attention (Vaswani et al., 2017) across all model sizes; at 2B, Gated base achieves 46.30 vs. Sink base 46.35 vs. Vanilla base 45.46 in average accuracy. Adding the auxiliary load balancing loss improves every mechanism, with the largest gains for Gated Attention at 2B (+1.40 to 47.70) and Vanilla Attention at 2B (+1.37 to 46.83), while Sink Attention at 2B sees only a marginal +0.15. For fine-tuning, the method is evaluated against Qwen3-4B/8B (Yang et al., 2025) and LLaMA3.1-8B (Dubey et al., 2024) as baselines trained with the standard loss only; the sink-aware loss improves reasoning averages by +0.51 (Qwen3-4B), +1.70 (Qwen3-8B), and +2.47 (LLaMA3.1-8B), and long-context averages by +1.29, +4.71, and +3.47 respectively. The most striking fine-tuning gain is on LLaMA3.1-8B's ProcessBench score (23.63→31.14, +7.51), suggesting that head collapse is particularly severe in this model and that load balancing recovers substantial reasoning capability. Overall, the paper's method wins on every comparison against its respective base-loss counterpart, confirming that the auxiliary loss is strictly beneficial regardless of attention variant or model scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate attention mechanisms—Vanilla Attention, Sink Attention (GPT-OSS), and Gated Attention (Qwen3-Next)—by showing that the attention sink in the first two creates an implicit gating factor mathematically equivalent to the explicit sigmoid gate in the third, thereby revealing a native Mixture-of-Experts structure within attention layers. It then draws directly on MoE load balancing techniques (Shazeer et al., Fedus et al.) and the shared/routed expert design from DeepSeek-MoE to propose a sink-aware auxiliary loss that addresses head collapse, a phenomenon previously observed but not addressed at training time by DuoAttention, Sandoval-Segura et al., or Sok et al. The practical implementation leverages Flash Attention's log-sum-exp outputs to avoid materializing full attention matrices, extending the efficiency considerations from Dao (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The native-MoE-in-attention framing provides a principled lens for understanding why head collapse is universal across architectures and why it intensifies with scale, potentially guiding future architecture design and pruning strategies. The sink-aware load balancing loss is simple, adds <2% training overhead, and integrates with Flash Attention, making it immediately applicable to both pretraining and fine-tuning pipelines. However, the paper acknowledges a key limitation: the single-sink assumption may not hold in all models (e.g., DeepSeek-V4), where multiple low-semantic tokens can absorb redundant attention, and the interaction between multiple implicit gates and explicit gates remains uncharacterized. The modest performance gains (~0.5–1.5 points) suggest the technique is a useful regularizer rather than a transformative improvement, and its full impact may only emerge at larger scales or in long-context settings where head diversity matters more.
