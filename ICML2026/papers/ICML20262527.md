## Problem

In large-scale multi-agent reinforcement learning systems, identifying which small subset of agents, if compromised, causes the worst-case system failure is critical for robustness evaluation, yet remains unsolved at scale. Existing vulnerable-agent selection methods are confined to small-scale MARL (10 or fewer agents), while a 1000-agent system yields C(1000,100) ~ 10^139 possible attack scenarios. The core difficulty is the coupling between an NP-hard combinatorial selection problem (upper level) and a mean-field adversarial policy learning problem (lower level), which makes the joint optimization intractable.

## Input / Output

The method consumes only cooperative trajectory rollouts (100 episodes) from the pre-trained victim policy, along with agent local states and perturbation budgets epsilon in [0, 2^(1/p)]; it does not require access to victim policy parameters, making it a black-box attack. The primary output is an ordered set of k vulnerable agents selected sequentially, accompanied by a per-agent vulnerability value function V(s, mu, epsilon, xi) that is conditioned on both the agent's own perturbation magnitude and the mean-field perturbation fraction xi of its teammates. A final worst-case adversarial policy is then trained on the identified set via standard mean-field control.

## Method

The key insight is decoupling the hierarchical problem via the Fenchel-Rockafellar transform applied to the l_p-bounded rectangular uncertainty set induced by the adversarial perturbation. This yields a regularized mean-field Bellman operator B_R = B_beta - (epsilon + xi + epsilon*xi) * ||Q||_q, where Q is the fixed cooperative Q-function, enabling exact worst-case value estimation without training the lower-level adversary. The NP-hard upper-level selection is then reformulated as a sequential MDP with dense rewards defined as the value-function drop when adding each new agent to the attack set, solvable by greedy (VAI-Greedy) or DQN-based RL (VAI-RL). The decomposition is provably optimality-preserving: the Fenchel-Rockafellar transform is exact for convex uncertainty sets, and Bellman's theorem guarantees an optimal policy for the upper-level MDP. The regularization terms decompose into agent vulnerability (epsilon*||Q||_q), mean-field vulnerability (xi*||Q||_q), and their interaction (epsilon*xi*||Q||_q), providing interpretable per-agent vulnerability scores.

## Results

VAI (both Greedy and RL variants) outperforms all five baselines in 17 out of 18 tasks across Battle, Taxi, and Vicsek environments. In the largest-scale Battle setting (144 agents, 72 attackers), VAI-RL drives the victim reward to −2837.83±482.56, substantially worse than the best baseline (Bi-level RL at −2353.78±870.53). In Taxi (100 agents, 36 attackers), VAI-RL achieves 652.10±23.23 versus PIANO's 793.71±12.86. VAI-RL further outperforms VAI-Greedy in 10 of 18 tasks, particularly when more attackers are available, confirming that modeling long-term inter-agent returns is critical for large-scale vulnerability identification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against five baselines: Random, Degree Centrality (DC), Bi-level RL (Vezhnevets et al., 2017), PIANO (Li et al., 2022), and RTCA (Zhou & Liu, 2023). VAI-RL wins 17 of 18 tasks, with the sole exception being Taxi-50 with 4 attackers where PIANO (16.26) slightly edges VAI-RL (12.47). DC, a heuristic designed for rule-based networks, performs only marginally better than Random in MARL environments because agent interactions are nonlinear and not governed by fixed rules. Bi-level RL and RTCA struggle due to the coupled hierarchical nature of HAD-MFC, which is too difficult for standard RL or differential evolution to solve without the decoupling provided by the Fenchel-Rockafellar transform. PIANO, which uses graph embeddings and RL for sequential node selection, does not account for the worst-case adversarial policy that the selected agents will execute, making it unable to identify the truly most harmful agent set. VAI's advantage grows with scale: in Battle-144 with 72 attackers, VAI-RL achieves a 20% lower victim reward than the next-best method (Bi-level RL), demonstrating that the regularized mean-field Bellman operator provides a far more accurate vulnerability estimate than hierarchical or heuristic approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on decentralized mean-field control (Subramanian et al., 2022) as its foundational formulation and on the PR-MDP perturbation model (Tessler et al., 2019) to characterize adversarial policy deviation. Its key methodological departure is the application of the Fenchel-Rockafellar transform (Rockafellar, 1970) to decouple a hierarchical adversarial problem into two independently solvable subproblems, yielding a regularized mean-field Bellman operator. Conceptually, the upper-level agent selection problem is related to influence maximization (Kempe et al., 2003), but the paper explicitly departs from that literature by requiring agents to learn adversarial policies rather than following predefined influence rules, and by operating without a known graph structure. Compared to small-scale MARL robustness testing (RTCA, PIANO, GMA-FGSM), this work extends the vulnerable-agent-selection task to the large-scale mean-field regime where the combinatorial complexity becomes NP-hard.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formal treatment of vulnerable agent identification in large-scale (1000+ agent) mean-field MARL, with a provably lossless decomposition that reduces the coupled hierarchical problem to a standard MDP plus a regularized Bellman update. The practical relevance is significant for deployed systems like robot swarms, power grids, and traffic networks where a small number of compromised agents can trigger cascading failures. Limitations include the assumption of a fixed victim policy (no adaptive defense), the requirement for cooperative trajectory data, and the fact that the Q-function entering the regularizer is pre-computed and held fixed. Open questions include extending VAI to adaptive victim policies, continuous action spaces beyond the discrete settings tested, and real-world domains like social networks and economic agent-based models.
