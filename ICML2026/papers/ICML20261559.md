## Problem

While Mixture-of-Experts architectures are adopted for computational efficiency, it remains an open empirical question whether their sparse routing makes them inherently more interpretable than dense feed-forward networks at production scale. Additionally, the debate on expert specialization—whether experts are broad domain specialists or simple token-level processors—lacks a unified resolution. This paper addresses both gaps by quantifying neuron-level polysemanticity and proposing the expert as a scalable unit of analysis.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-trained MoE and dense LLM weights (12 models matched by active parameter count, with OLMo family comparisons controlling for total parameters) and text data from pile-uncopyrighted annotated with 58 binary concept labels across four categories (Part-of-Speech, LaTeX, code, natural language text). Outputs include k-sparse probe F1 scores at sparsity levels k∈{1,2,4,8,16,32,64} using best-layer selection, one-sentence natural language expert labels validated by a separate LLM scorer (F1 over 20 held-out examples), JSD-based specialization scores across cluster granularities k∈{10,50,100,1000,5000} on the unembedding matrix, and DLA-based causal attribution rankings from trigger-target experiments.

## Method

The paper first quantifies polysemanticity via k-sparse probing on intermediate activation vectors h, demonstrating that MoE expert neurons achieve near-optimal F1 at k=1 while dense FFN neurons do not, with the performance gap widening systematically as routing sparsity (N_A/N) decreases. Leveraging this modular monosemanticity, the authors zoom out to the expert level: they identify high-activating sequences by scoring tokens with router-weight-scaled L2 norm (g_i(x)‖E_i(x)‖₂), feed 20 top sequences plus Logit Lens top-3 promoted tokens to an LLM explainer to generate a one-sentence functional label, and validate via a separate LLM scorer on 10 positive and 10 negative examples. Causal validity is confirmed through a trigger-target experiment where DLA contributions are measured for synthetic prompts generated from the labels, showing the target expert ranks Top-1 or Top-8 in 80% of matched cases while being unrouted on 80% of control prompts. Finally, expert specialization is quantified via Jensen-Shannon Divergence between each expert's token distribution and the layer base rate over k-means clusters of the unembedding matrix, distinguishing routing specialization (input tokens) from functional specialization (Logit Lens output tokens) and revealing that deep-layer experts are granular task specialists rather than broad domain experts.

## Results

Using k-sparse probing across 12 models and 58 concepts, MoE experts consistently achieve near-optimal F1 scores at k=1 (single neuron), while matched dense models show a significant performance gap that only closes at higher k values. In the OLMo family comparison, OLMoE-1B-7B (1B active params) significantly outperforms OLMo-7B dense (7B active params), confirming that sparse routing—not raw capacity—drives reduced polysemanticity. For automatic expert interpretation, most experts achieve F1 scores above 0.8, with the sparsest model (Qwen3-30B-A3B, N_A/N ≈ 0.06) frequently exceeding 0.9. Causal attribution via the Trigger-Target experiment confirms that in 80% of control cases the identified expert was not even routed, while it was consistently Top-1 or Top-8 for matched prompts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between MoE experts and dense FFNs of matched active parameter count. MoE models outperform dense counterparts across all four concept categories (Part-of-Speech, LaTeX, Code, Natural Language Text), with nearly every point in the MoE-vs-dense scatter plot lying above the equality line. The OLMo family comparison isolates sparsity from capacity: OLMoE-1B-7B (1B active) beats OLMo-7B (7B active) despite 7× fewer active parameters per token. Among MoE models themselves, interpretability scales with routing sparsity—Mixtral-8x7B (N_A/N = 0.25, the densest MoE studied) still outperforms dense models but shows noticeably lower F1 scores than Qwen3-30B-A3B (N_A/N ≈ 0.06). For automatic interpretability, sparser models yield more reliable labels: Qwen3-30B-A3B achieves the highest average F1 (frequently >0.9), while the denser ERNIE-4.5-21B-A3B shows slightly lower and more variable scores. The causal attribution experiment provides the strongest validation: matched prompts consistently activate the labeled expert as a top contributor, while control prompts designed for other experts almost never route to it (80% not routed at all).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neuron-level interpretability toolkit (k-sparse probing from Gurnee et al., 2023; LLM-based explanation from Bills et al., 2023) to the expert level in MoE architectures, motivated by the observation that sparse routing reduces superposition. It empirically validates at production scale a trend previously shown only in toy models (Chaudhari et al., 2025) that routing sparsity decreases polysemanticity, and uses DLA (Elhage et al., 2021) and Logit Lens (nostalgebraist, 2020) for causal attribution of expert labels. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant SAE-based interpretability paradigm by arguing that MoE experts are already sufficiently monosemantic to serve as interpretable units without additional trained decompositions, and it bridges the domain-specialization vs. token-specialization debate by proposing a 'task expert' framing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: if MoE experts can be automatically labeled and causally validated without training per-layer sparse autoencoders, this offers a scalable path to auditing and debugging large MoE systems that is orders of magnitude cheaper than current neuron-level methods. The finding that experts are fine-grained task specialists (e.g., closing LaTeX brackets) rather than broad domain experts reframes how we think about MoE computation and suggests the router sequences experts into functional pipelines. Key limitations include the exclusion of the largest current MoE models (e.g., DeepSeek-V3) due to compute constraints, the acknowledgment that some superposition likely persists within experts, and the reliance on a single LLM (Gemini 3 Flash) for both explanation and scoring, which introduces potential circularity. Open questions include whether the monosemanticity trend holds at the extreme sparsity limit (N >> N_A) and whether expert-level labels remain stable under distribution shift or adversarial prompting.
