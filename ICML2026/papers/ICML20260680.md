## Problem

Machine learning policies for branch-and-cut are typically trained to imitate local expert scores (strong branching scores, LP bound improvement) and evaluated on local score accuracy, yet performance is measured by the global search tree size. This paper asks whether high local imitation accuracy can guarantee similar global tree size, and answers in the negative by identifying two failure modes: the expert signal itself can be exponentially suboptimal, and arbitrarily small perturbations in scores or cut definitions can be amplified by the branch-and-bound recursion into exponentially different trees.

## Input / Output

The paper is purely theoretical and does not train or evaluate learned models on datasets. It constructs families of structured 0-1 MILP instances (packing problems with n blocks of 5 binary variables, and stable-set-on-disjoint-triangles instances with 3m variables) and analyzes the resulting branch-and-bound tree sizes under specific branching policies and root cut sets. The 'output' is worst-case tree size lower bounds (e.g., 2^{n+1}−1 vs. 2n+1) that hold for any node selection and branching rule, establishing structural separations rather than empirical benchmarks.

## Method

The paper establishes four worst-case separations using product constructions from small base gadgets. For cut selection, it shows (Theorem 4.1) that selecting root cuts by LP bound improvement yields a strong-branching tree exponentially larger than selecting by efficacy, and (Theorem 4.3) that an ε perturbation of cut right-hand sides changes the minimum B&B tree size from 1 to 2^{Ω(n)} while closing nearly all of the integrality gap. For branching, it constructs instances (Theorem 5.1) where a scoring function matching strong branching within an arbitrary ε on every candidate at every node still produces an exponentially larger tree, and (Theorem 5.4) shows that just k deviations from strong branching along its trajectory inflate tree size by 2^{Ω(k)}. The key mechanism in all constructions is that the B&B recursion compounds small local differences: a single flipped argmax or a slightly weakened cut in one block of a product instance forces both children to remain unprunable, doubling the required work at each of m levels.

## Results

The paper establishes four theoretical separations showing that local score-based B&C policies can yield exponentially larger trees than optimal. For cut selection, LP bound improvement can select a root cut set yielding a strong branching tree of size ≥ 1 + 6(2^⌊m/9⌋ − 1) versus ≤ 2m + 1 for efficacy-based selection (Theorem 4.1), and an ε-perturbation of cut right-hand sides can change the minimum tree size from 1 to 2^⌊n/3⌋+1 − 1 (Theorem 4.3). For branching, arbitrarily small uniform score discrepancies (|Score − Score_SB| ≤ ε for any ε > 0) can produce trees of size ≥ 2^{n+1} − 1 versus ≤ 2n + 1 for strong branching (Theorem 5.1), and k deviations from strong branching can inflate tree size by 2^Ω(k) (Theorem 5.4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or dataset evaluations. The comparisons are between theoretical selection rules and policies. For cut selection, LP bound improvement (the standard expert signal) is shown to be exponentially worse than the simple efficacy proxy under strong branching: the LP-improvement-selected cut set yields a tree of size ≥ 1 + 6(2^⌊m/9⌋ − 1) while the efficacy-selected set yields ≤ 2m + 1. For branching, strong branching (the standard expert oracle) is compared against a perturbed scoring policy that matches SB scores within an arbitrarily small ε at every node; the perturbed policy produces a tree of size ≥ 2^{n+1} − 1 while SB produces ≤ 2n + 1. The paper also shows that even identical scores with different tie-breaking rules (Proposition 5.2) can yield the same exponential gap, and that just k decision deviations along the SB trajectory suffice for a 2^Ω(k) blowup (Theorem 5.4). These results collectively demonstrate that local imitation accuracy does not control global tree size, motivating end-to-end training objectives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and extends three threads of prior work: Dey et al. (2024) showing strong branching can be exponentially suboptimal, Basu et al. (2023) establishing product-construction lower bounds on B&B tree sizes for stable set instances, and Shah et al. (2025) demonstrating non-monotonicity of branching rules under relaxation strengthening. Rather than proposing a new learning method, the paper reframes these individual observations as a unified impossibility result for the standard ML-for-B&C paradigm of imitating local expert scores. It complements the generalization analysis line (Balcan et al., Cheng & Basu) by shifting focus from estimation error to approximation and stability: even in the infinite-data limit, local score accuracy does not control global tree size.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

These worst-case separations clarify a fundamental gap that is invisible to standard local evaluation metrics: a policy can match expert scores to arbitrary precision yet produce exponentially larger trees, and the expert signal itself (LP bound improvement) can be exponentially worse than a trivial proxy. This motivates end-to-end training objectives (e.g., RL with tree-size rewards) and practical stress tests (score perturbation, tie-breaking randomization) for learned B&C policies. Open questions include identifying instance classes or structural conditions under which local supervision provably controls tree size, and characterizing when the worst-case instabilities are benign in practice.
