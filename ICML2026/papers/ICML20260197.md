## Problem

In contextual Stackelberg games where both contexts and follower types are chosen adversarially, prior work showed that worst-case regret grows linearly with the time horizon, making learning impossible. This paper asks: under what structural assumptions on the context-to-type mapping does learning become possible, and what complexity measure tightly characterizes the leader's optimal regret? The key gap is that standard multiclass prediction tools (e.g., Littlestone dimension) are blind to the utility structure of the game and can give arbitrarily loose bounds.

## Input / Output

In the online setting, the leader observes a context vector z ∈ Z ⊆ R^d each round, commits to a mixed strategy x ∈ Δ(A) over a finite action set, and only learns the follower's type after the follower best-responds. The leader has access to a hypothesis class H ⊆ [K]^Z mapping contexts to one of K follower types, along with all utility functions. In the distributional setting, the input is a sample of (context, follower type) pairs drawn i.i.d. from an unknown distribution. The output is a mixed strategy (online) or a context-to-strategy mapping (distributional), with performance measured by cumulative regret or expected cut-off loss respectively.

## Method

The paper introduces the Stackelberg-Littlestone (SL) dimension, a new complexity measure that assigns weights to nodes in shattered trees based on the minmax regret the leader faces against the follower types reachable at that node, rather than simply counting depth. This captures the joint complexity of the hypothesis class and the utility space, unlike the standard Littlestone dimension which is blind to utilities. The Stackelberg Standard Optimal Algorithm (SSOA) tracks a version space of consistent hypotheses and at each round plays the strategy minimizing worst-case instantaneous regret plus the SL dimension of the induced sub-class, achieving regret exactly equal to the SL dimension (matching a lower bound). For the distributional setting, two new dimensions—the γ-SN dimension (lower bound) and γ-SG dimension (upper bound)—control sample complexity, where the γ parameter encodes how distinguishable follower types must be in a minmax sense for the leader to benefit from correct prediction. A simple improper algorithm (Algorithm 2) that plays the robust strategy against all follower types consistent with the training data achieves the upper bound.

## Results

The paper provides a complete tight characterization of online learning in structured Stackelberg games: the Stackelberg-Littlestone (SL) dimension simultaneously upper- and lower-bounds the optimal regret (Theorems 3.8–3.9), with the SSOA algorithm achieving exactly SLdim_G(H) regret. In the distributional setting, the γ-SN dimension gives a matching lower bound of Ω((SNdim + log(1/δ))/ε) and the γ-SG dimension gives an upper bound of O((SGdim · log(1/ε) + log(1/δ))/ε) (Theorems 4.4, 4.7). A key negative result (Theorem 3.5) shows the standard multiclass Littlestone dimension can be infinite while the leader still learns perfectly, demonstrating that Ldim is the wrong complexity measure for this setting. For the permutation class example, SLdim = n − H_n (harmonic number) versus Ldim = n − 1, illustrating the gap between the two measures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's main comparison is between the standard multiclass Littlestone dimension (Ldim) and the proposed Stackelberg-Littlestone dimension (SLdim). Theorem 3.5 constructs a game where Ldim(H) = ∞ (linear threshold functions) yet the leader achieves zero regret, proving Ldim is not a necessary condition for learnability. Lemma 3.10 establishes SLdim ≤ Ldim, so the new measure is always no larger. Theorem 3.11 further shows that running the Standard Optimal Algorithm (SOA) from multiclass prediction on a specific instance yields regret 2, while the proposed SSOA achieves the optimal 3/4 (equal to SLdim), a 2.67× gap. In the distributional setting, the γ-SN and γ-SG dimensions play analogous roles to the Natarajan and γ-Graph dimensions from regression (Attias et al., 2023), but are tailored to exploit the discrete follower-type structure, yielding tighter bounds than what a pure multiclass prediction reduction would give.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the contextual Stackelberg game framework of Harris et al. (2024), which showed that online learning is impossible when both contexts and follower types are chosen adversarially. The key departure is the introduction of a structural assumption—that the context-to-follower-type mapping belongs to a known hypothesis class—which makes the problem learnable. The algorithmic core (SSOA) is a direct adaptation of the Standard Optimal Algorithm from multiclass prediction (Daniely & Shalev-Shwartz, 2014), while the distributional dimensions (γ-SN, γ-SG) are adapted from the γ-Graph dimension for realizable regression (Attias et al., 2023). Critically, the paper shows that the standard multiclass Littlestone dimension is the wrong complexity measure here, motivating the new Stackelberg-Littlestone dimension that jointly captures hypothesis class complexity and the game's utility structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a complete, tight characterization of learnability in structured Stackelberg games via the SL dimension (online) and γ-SN/γ-SG dimensions (distributional), resolving a gap left by prior impossibility results. This has direct implications for security games, AI red-teaming, auction design, and Bayesian persuasion, where contextual signals predict opponent types. Major open questions remain: the agnostic (non-realizable) setting, computational efficiency of the optimal algorithms, extension to multiple followers, and bandit feedback. The work is purely theoretical with no empirical validation, and the practical applicability depends on the leader having access to a rich enough hypothesis class and being able to solve the underlying linear programs.
