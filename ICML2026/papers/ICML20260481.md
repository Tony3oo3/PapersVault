## Problem

Existing uncertainty-based hallucination detectors for LLMs aggregate token-level signals into a single sequence-level score, which obscures the stepwise dynamics of multi-step reasoning. This static averaging misclassifies hard-but-correct samples (high average uncertainty triggers false rejections) and misses easy-but-wrong samples (low average uncertainty masks sharp local failures). The paper asks whether hallucinations can be detected by monitoring the dynamic evolution of evidence support across individual reasoning steps rather than by their cumulative magnitude.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a question prompt and the model's generated chain-of-thought trajectory, extracting token-level logit distributions at each step (via a single forward pass under greedy decoding, or teacher-forced decoding for step-level evaluation on ProcessBench). It produces three outputs: a scalar hallucination risk score (the mean of the M=5 most severe evidence drops), a binary accept/reject decision calibrated via Neyman-Pearson hypothesis testing at a user-specified significance level α, and a step-level localization signal identifying the exact token position where the first significant evidence drop occurs. The entire pipeline is training-free and model-agnostic, requiring only access to the model's next-token predictive distribution.

## Method

The paper models multi-step reasoning as a Markov trajectory on a latent Evidence Manifold and proves (Theorem 3.1) that under entropy-regularized training, any off-manifold transition (a non-adjacent jump between evidence states) forces the predictive distribution to degenerate to uniform, causing a sharp collapse in local evidence. Practically, Shannon entropy of the top-K (K=20) renormalized next-token distribution serves as an empirical proxy for latent evidence; an EMA-smoothed evidence trajectory is then differenced to compute per-step evidence flux Δ_j. Hallucinations are detected by aggregating the M=5 most negative drops into a risk score φ(x,y), and a statistically guaranteed decision threshold is derived via the (1−α)-quantile of the risk distribution over known failures, providing finite-sample control on the false-acceptance rate. The choice of Shannon entropy is theoretically motivated by its direct coupling to the entropy-regularization term in the model's training objective, distinguishing it from heuristic proxies like LN-S or LogTokU.

## Results

On MATH with Qwen3-8B at α=0.05, Shannon Drop achieves 88.26% selective accuracy, a +18.02% improvement over Shannon Avg (70.24%) and +18.47% over the pretrained baseline (59.24%). On GSM8K with Qwen3-4B at α=0.05, Shannon Drop reaches 100% selective accuracy versus 92.31% for Shannon Avg. In threshold-free ranking (AURC×1000, lower is better), Shannon Drop reduces the MATH AURC for Qwen3-8B from 288.8 (Shannon Avg) to 190.0, and for Qwen3-4B from 303.1 to 239.1. For step-level localization on ProcessBench, Shannon Drop achieves 46.11% SLA on GSM8K (Qwen3-8B) versus 27.66% for Shannon Avg.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LN-S (Malinin & Gales, 2020), the Drop variant shows inconsistent gains: on MATH (Qwen3-8B, α=0.05) LN-S Drop reaches 86.94% vs. LN-S Base 69.79%, but on GSM8K it frequently underperforms its own static baseline (e.g., 94.44% vs. 98.68% at α=0.05), suggesting LN-S's single-token focus is poorly suited to capturing distributional evidence collapse. Against LogTokU (Ma et al., 2025), the Drop transformation yields the most dramatic rescue: LogTokU Avg scores 0% on GSM8K (Qwen3-4B, α=0.05) while LogTokU Drop recovers to 85.57%, and on MATH (Qwen3-8B, α=0.05) the gap widens from 30.00% to 81.55%. Against Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2022), which requires N=15 sampled paths and achieves 70.56% on MATH (Qwen3-8B), the single-pass Shannon Drop method reaches 88.26% with no additional generation cost. Overall, Shannon Drop is the strongest variant across all three uncertainty measures, confirming that Shannon entropy's explicit coupling to the entropy-regularized training objective makes its dynamic fluctuations the most reliable hallucination signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant sequence-level uncertainty aggregation paradigm (LN-S, Semantic Entropy, LogTokU) by reframing hallucination detection as a dynamic process-monitoring problem on a latent 'Evidence Manifold.' Rather than building on a single prior method, it repurposes existing logit-based uncertainty signals (Shannon entropy, LogTokU, LN-S) by tracking their worst-case local drops instead of their global averages, drawing conceptual inspiration from the locality-of-experience principle (Prystawski et al., 2023) and the manifold hypothesis. The theoretical framing via entropy-regularized risk minimization and Markov dynamics on the manifold is novel, but the practical implementation is a re-aggregation of standard token-level uncertainty metrics. It also integrates a Neyman-Pearson hypothesis testing framework for statistically guaranteed decision thresholds, connecting to the conformal prediction and risk-control literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free, single-pass nature of the Evidence Drop detector makes it a lightweight alternative to Process Reward Models that require expensive step-level annotations and supervised training, potentially enabling scalable hallucination monitoring in production LLM deployments. However, the theoretical framework relies on strong assumptions (locality of transitions, local consistency) that are never empirically validated, and the 'Evidence Manifold' is never explicitly constructed or characterized geometrically, making the theorem somewhat tautological. Step-level localization accuracy remains modest (27–46%), and the method is currently limited to passive detection without active intervention or correction. Open questions include whether the dynamic-drop signal generalizes beyond mathematical reasoning to open-ended generation, and whether the theoretical guarantees hold when the locality assumptions are relaxed.
