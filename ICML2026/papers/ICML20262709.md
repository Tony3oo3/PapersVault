## Problem

Large language models exhibit significant safety disparities across languages: they are well-aligned in high-resource languages like English but substantially less safe in low-resource languages, where safety supervision is scarce. Existing remedies—multilingual SFT, RLHF, or translation-based pipelines—are computationally expensive, data-hungry, or introduce semantic bottlenecks. The paper asks whether safety representations learned in English can be transferred to low-resource languages without any retraining.

## Input / Output

The method consumes layer-l MLP input features extracted from a small set of harmful LRL queries (X_low), harmless queries (X_safe), and the corresponding English safety-neuron activations (Y_target), together with the model's safety weight submatrix W_S. It produces a sparse, low-rank perturbation ΔW_S of shape d_in × m (where m is the number of identified safety neurons) that is added directly to W_S, leaving all other weights frozen. Because the solution is closed-form, the entire computation is a single pass of Cholesky solves and a rank-r truncated SVD—no iterative optimization is required.

## Method

The core idea is to cast cross-lingual safety transfer as a constrained linear mapping over a sparse set of safety neurons identified via activation-contrast (z-score and Cohen's d thresholds). A first-order approximation linearizes the MLP nonlinearity, yielding an alignment objective that pulls harmful LRL representations toward English safety activations, a null-space regularization that keeps the perturbation orthogonal to harmless-feature directions (preserving utility), and a low-rank constraint for few-shot robustness. The key theoretical contribution is a closed-form solution: the rank-constrained problem is reduced to a standard low-rank approximation under a whitened metric via Cholesky factorization, and the optimal perturbation is recovered by a truncated SVD (Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem). This makes the entire alignment a single, data-efficient computation requiring only a handful of anchor samples per language, with no gradient-based training.

## Results

On MULTI-STRONGREJECT (313 harmful prompts per language), the proposed sparse weight editing (OUR) reduces average unsafe-response counts by 16–56 across seven model backbones (Llama-3.2-1B/3B, Qwen2-0.5B/1.5B, Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B/7B), with the largest gains on the most vulnerable Qwen2-0.5B (ΔAvg = −44.83). Combining with MPO (MPO+OUR) yields the strongest safety across nearly all backbones, e.g., −130.33 on Qwen2-0.5B and −79.5 on Qwen2.5-3B. Utility on MGSM and M-MMLU remains within ~2 points of the unedited baseline in most settings, confirming that the training-free edit preserves general reasoning and knowledge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MPO (Zhao et al., 2025b), the proposed method alone (OUR) achieves comparable or slightly weaker safety on most backbones (e.g., Qwen2-0.5B: OUR ΔAvg −44.83 vs. MPO −87.83), but the combination MPO+OUR consistently delivers the lowest unsafe-response counts across all seven models (e.g., −130.33 on Qwen2-0.5B, −79.5 on Qwen2.5-3B, −40.33 on Llama-3.2-1B), demonstrating that the training-free weight edit acts as a complementary plug-in rather than a replacement. On utility, MPO+OUR matches or slightly trails MPO on MGSM/M-MMLU (within ~1–2 points), while OUR alone preserves utility more closely to the unedited baseline. Against MCD (Li et al., 2025) on Qwen2.5-1.5B, MCD achieves stronger per-language safety in some cases (e.g., Bengali: 7/313 vs. OUR 152/313), but at the cost of a severe MGSM collapse (MCD drops MGSM to near-zero levels), whereas OUR maintains MGSM at 25.13 (vs. 27.53 baseline) and M-MMLU at 41.89 (vs. 41.58 baseline), yielding a substantially better safety–utility trade-off.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the safety-neuron identification literature (Chen et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026), which established that safety behavior in LLMs is mediated by a sparse subset of MLP units. It departs from training-based multilingual safety alignment (MPO, AlignX, AdaMergeX) by eliminating gradient-based optimization entirely, and from prior inference-time interventions (RESTA's task arithmetic, simple activation steering) by showing that passive amplification fails when LRL safety representations are directionally misaligned with the HRL safety subspace. The key methodological departure is formulating cross-lingual safety transfer as a rank-constrained least-squares problem over a safety-neuron submatrix, yielding a closed-form solution via Cholesky whitening and truncated SVD (Eckart-Young-Mirsky), rather than iterative optimization or translation-based pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free, single-pass nature of the method makes multilingual safety alignment practical in settings where multilingual safety data is scarce or expensive to curate, and its composability with existing alignment (MPO+Our consistently outperforming either alone) positions it as a drop-in plug-in rather than a replacement. Limitations include reliance on a single-layer edit, dependence on translation quality for benchmark construction (back-translation similarity ~0.93), and occasional regressions in specific languages (e.g., Hebrew for Llama-3.2-1B). Open questions the authors identify include extending to multi-layer hierarchical safety subspaces, automatic hyperparameter adaptation, and robustness against adaptive jailbreaks that could exploit the fixed low-rank structure of the edit.
