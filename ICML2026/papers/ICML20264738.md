## Problem

Offline policy evaluation under network interference has been studied under either SUTVA or overlap, but not both simultaneously. When a behavior policy fails to cover exposure states that the target policy requires, and individual outcomes depend on neighbors' treatments, standard point estimators break down and existing partial identification results (which assume independent units) do not apply. This paper asks whether informative, computable bounds on policy value can still be derived when both SUTVA and overlap are violated.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single logged network realization (adjacency matrix, node covariates, binary treatment assignments, and outcomes) together with a user-specified evaluation policy, a truncation depth S for the exposure mapping, a discretization grid of K bins for the continuous spillover exposure, and a smoothness constant L. It outputs a valid partially identified interval [V⁻, V⁺] for the evaluation-policy value, where the interval width is governed by the graph-augmented distance between non-overlap nodes and their nearest overlap anchors, the Lipschitz constant L, and the truncation bias θ_n(S). The interval is not a standard confidence interval but a sharp identification region: its endpoints are the minimum and maximum policy values consistent with the data and the imposed smoothness structure.

## Method

The approach first reduces the n-dimensional assignment vector to a low-dimensional S-truncated exposure state T_i^(S) = (D_i, E_i^(S)) using a decaying higher-order neighborhood aggregation, with the approximation error explicitly bounded by θ_n(S). The policy value is then decomposed into an overlap component (estimable by standard OPE methods) and a non-overlap component (only partially identified). For the non-overlap part, a graph-augmented Lipschitz smoothness condition—combining a weighted feature-exposure norm with graph shortest-path distance via a composite metric d_λ—constrains the admissible mean function. This yields a linear program whose optimal solution admits a closed form via the McShane-Whitney minimal and maximal Lipschitz extensions, giving the lower and upper bounds as weighted sums of (max/min overlap mean ± L·d_λ) over non-overlap nodes. The key distinction from the i.i.d. setting is that network geometry enters through d_λ, the effective sample size n_eff accounts for S-local dependence, and the overlap structure is defined in the policy-induced exposure space rather than a covariate-action space.

## Results

On the Pokec social network (n=5,000), the partially identified interval width ranges from 0.022 (L=0.1, S=1) to 0.123 (L=0.5, S=3), with empirical coverage rising from 0.44 to 1.00 across the same range. At the practically useful setting L=0.3, S=2, the interval width is 0.074 with 0.98 coverage, demonstrating near-nominal coverage while remaining relatively tight. Increasing the truncation depth S from 1 to 3 systematically widens intervals by roughly 20–30% at fixed L, reflecting the added uncertainty from higher-order neighborhood aggregation. The graph-aware method (λ>0) achieves more reliable empirical coverage than the no-graph ablation (λ=0) across Barabási–Albert and Stochastic Block Model topologies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its graph-aware partial identification method against three standard point-estimation baselines—IPW, outcome regression (OR), and doubly robust (DR)—as well as a no-graph ablation (λ=0) that removes the graph-distance component from the composite metric. Under severe non-overlap (bottom q-quantile of nodes set to zero logging propensity), standard point estimators, especially IPW, become unstable due to extreme propensity weights, while the proposed method produces valid intervals with near-nominal coverage. The graph-aware version (λ>0) achieves more reliable empirical coverage than the no-graph ablation across all three graph topologies tested (Pokec, Barabási–Albert, and Stochastic Block Model), confirming that incorporating network geometry into the Lipschitz metric tightens the identification region. The full numerical comparison is deferred to Appendix C.1, but the qualitative conclusion is that no point estimator provides valid inference in the non-overlap regime, whereas the proposed bounds remain valid by construction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two previously separate research threads: partial identification of policy values under limited overlap (Khan et al., 2024), which provides the LP formulation, Lipschitz smoothness, and McShane-Whitney closed-form bounds, and causal inference under network interference (Hudgens & Halloran, 2008; Leung, 2022), which provides the exposure mapping and approximate neighborhood interference framework. The key departure is that prior partial identification results assume unit independence (no interference), while prior interference-aware estimators assume sufficient overlap; this paper is the first to handle both violations simultaneously by introducing a graph-augmented composite metric that couples covariates, exposure summaries, and network distance. The methodological core—reducing the n-dimensional assignment vector to a truncated exposure state and then applying Lipschitz extension arguments—adapts the i.i.d. theory of Khan et al. (2024) to a dependent, network-structured setting where the effective sample size is reduced by graph connectivity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first valid partial identification theory for policy values when both SUTVA and overlap fail, which is arguably the most realistic regime for large-scale networked decision systems (social platforms, marketplaces, spatial interventions). The closed-form bounds and robustness to network misspecification (Theorem 4.5) make the framework practically deployable without solving a full LP. Key limitations include the restriction to binary treatments and a prespecified exposure mapping, semi-synthetic validation on a single real network (Pokec, n=5000), and the fact that the smoothness constant L and truncation level S must be chosen a priori, with no data-driven selection procedure. Open questions include extending to unknown or learned exposure mappings, multi-valued treatments, and scaling the effective-sample-size analysis to very large, heterogeneous networks where the dependency-graph approximation may be loose.
