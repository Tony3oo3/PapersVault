## Problem

The superficial alignment hypothesis (SAH) — that LLMs acquire most task knowledge during pre-training and post-training merely surfaces it — lacks a precise definition, causing its supporting arguments (data efficiency, parameter efficiency, inference-time control) to appear orthogonal and leaving the hypothesis vulnerable to critiques that exploit undefined terms like 'knowledge' and 'capabilities.' The paper addresses this by grounding the SAH in algorithmic information theory, providing a single quantitative metric that unifies these perspectives and makes the hypothesis falsifiable.

## Input / Output

The method takes pre-trained model weights (SmolLM3 3B, OLMo3 7B/32B) as a bit-string θ, task inputs from GSM8K, FLORES-200, or IFEval, and constructs adaptation programs P as Python scripts plus compressed data or adapter parameters. Program length is measured in bits with the Python interpreter and all libraries (transformers, numpy, gptzip) treated as part of the universal machine M and excluded from the count; training data is compressed via arithmetic coding using the model itself. The output is a (b, δ) Pareto curve mapping program length to task performance, with b spanning from a few kilobytes to gigabytes.

## Method

The paper defines task complexity C(T_δ) as the length of the shortest program achieving performance δ on task T, and conditional task complexity C(T_δ | θ) as the same given access to model weights θ, formalising the SAH as the claim that pre-trained models drastically reduce this conditional complexity. To upper-bound the (uncomputable) conditional complexity, three program-construction strategies are employed: data methods that compress fine-tuning data via arithmetic coding into the program, parametric methods that encode LoRA or Bayesian-LoRA adapter weights directly, and inference-control methods that compress prompts (ICL or URIAL) into the program. These three strategies — previously interpreted as orthogonal evidence for the SAH — are shown to inform different regions of the same Pareto curve, with inference-control dominating at short lengths, data methods in the medium range, and parametric methods at long lengths. The framework further reveals that pre-training enables access to strong performance but at high complexity (megabytes to gigabytes), while post-training collapses this complexity by orders of magnitude to kilobytes or less.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that programs as small as 4,358 bits (~545 bytes) can adapt OLMo3-32B to achieve 72.2% accuracy on GSM8K, and 3,992 bits can lift OLMo3-7B from 22.63 to 34.43 BLEU on English-to-French translation. On IFEval, maximum performance across all models is reached with programs only slightly longer than 10^7 bits (1.25 MB). Critically, post-training collapses the complexity of reaching peak performance by several orders of magnitude: pre-trained OLMo3-7B requires ~5×10^6 bits for GSM8K and ~2.5×10^7 bits for IFEval, while post-trained checkpoints achieve the same performance with programs under 10^4 bits.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper unifies five prior approaches into a single Pareto framework rather than declaring one winner. At the shortest program lengths, inference-control methods (ICL from Brown et al. and URIAL from Lin et al.) dominate, providing modest performance gains with minimal program size. In the medium-length regime, data-driven Subset Training outperforms inference-control methods but at the cost of encoding substantially more data. At the longest program lengths, parametric methods (LoRA from Hu et al. and Bayesian-LoRA from Meo et al.) achieve the highest performance by directly encoding adapter weights. The key negative result is against Chen et al. (2025), whose linear-projection-only fine-tuning approach is shown to be 'far from the Pareto curve'—it achieves neither short programs nor high accuracy, undermining their claim that reasoning tasks require non-superficial knowledge. The paper thus reframes these methods as complementary strategies occupying different regions of the same length-performance tradeoff, rather than competing explanations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis (SAH) from Zhou et al. (2023a) and its three supporting lines of evidence—data efficiency (LIMA), parameter efficiency (LoRA, BitFit), and inference-time control (URIAL, ICL)—but departs from prior work by grounding all three in a single algorithmic-information-theoretic metric: task complexity, defined as the length of the shortest program achieving a target performance. Conceptually, it extends Kolmogorov complexity and algorithmic rate-distortion theory (Vereshchagin & Vitányi, 2010) to the setting of ML tasks with a distribution over inputs, and connects to MDL-based probing (Voita & Titov, 2020; Pimentel et al., 2020a) while measuring the full adaptation program rather than data description length alone. The paper also engages critically with recent challenges to the SAH (Raghavendra et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024), showing how its formalism resolves or reframes those critiques.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the first rigorous, quantitative definition of what 'superficial' means in LLM adaptation, resolving a long-standing ambiguity that allowed both supporters and critics to argue past each other. The empirical finding that post-training collapses the program complexity of reaching peak performance by several orders of magnitude (from gigabytes to kilobytes) gives a clean information-theoretic interpretation of what post-training actually does: it does not add new knowledge but makes existing knowledge accessible with far less information. Key limitations include the uncomputability of task complexity (only upper bounds are available) and the inability to directly measure the information I(T_δ; θ) without knowing unconditional complexity. Open questions include whether the estimated Pareto curves are tight, whether combining parametric and data strategies can push the frontier further (the paper's Appendix G attempt fails), and how the framework applies to quantifying the safety risks of open-weight model releases.
