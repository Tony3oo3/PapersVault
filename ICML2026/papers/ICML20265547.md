## Problem

Existing multimodal Process Reward Models (PRMs) are text-centric and cannot assess the correctness of intermediate visual operations (crops, annotations, spatial localization, sketches) in 'thinking with images' trajectories, causing them to mis-rank solutions where visual steps are decisive. Additionally, no standardized benchmark exists for evaluating PRMs on step-wise error detection in multimodal reasoning that includes visual intermediate steps.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265547/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

DiscPRM receives an input image, a question, and a step-by-step reasoning trajectory interleaving textual and visual steps (crops, annotations, sketches, spatial cues), presented in a multi-turn chat format where each turn introduces one new step. It outputs a discretized correctness label (+ or −) per step, which is converted to a scalar score via the generation probability of the '+' token; the response-level reward is the mean of all step scores. The model is built on Qwen2.5-VL-7B with a score head that transforms the last-token logits of each step into a scalar, trained with MSE loss against Monte Carlo–estimated step values.

## Method

DiscPRM is a value-based discriminative PRM that jointly evaluates textual and visual intermediate steps by modeling visual reasoning trajectories, image manipulations, and text-image consistency within a unified multi-turn dialogue framework. Training data (VTReward-100K, ~100K samples / ~1M steps) is constructed by sampling thinking-with-images solutions from policy models (Deepeyes, Thyme) and estimating step-level expected accuracy via a Monte Carlo tree rollout pipeline (16 continuations per step), yielding binary correctness labels. The model predicts a discretized label c_i ∈ {+, −} per step (positive if mc_i > 0), and the response score is the average of per-step '+' probabilities. Ablations show the value-based formulation outperforms advantage-based, and supervising all steps (rather than stopping at the first error) yields stronger results. The paper also introduces VABench (1,000 trajectories, 9,885 steps) with a hybrid annotation pipeline combining MCTS-based error discovery, calibration by three commercial MLLMs, and human verification, enabling step-wise error-detection evaluation beyond expensive Best-of-N testing.

## Results

Using DiscPRM as the Best-of-8 critic, Deepeyes improves by 6.0, 6.6, and 5.4 points on reasoning, perception, and general task overall scores respectively, while Thyme-7B gains 6.2, 6.0, and 5.5 points over its unassisted baseline. On VABench (step-level error detection), DiscPRM achieves a macro F1 of 65.9, outperforming all open-source MLLMs and PRMs (best prior: VisualPRM at 59.9) and exceeding GPT-4o (59.8), while remaining competitive with Gemini-3-Pro (66.5) despite using only 7B parameters. Against Self-Consistency under the same Best-of-8 budget, DiscPRM yields substantially larger gains (e.g., +9.1 on MathVision, +10.6 on BLINK for Deepeyes), confirming that process-level visual-textual evaluation adds value beyond final-answer voting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiscPRM consistently outperforms the two strongest prior multimodal PRMs—VisualPRM and ViLPRM—across all three task categories (perception, reasoning, general) for both Deepeyes and Thyme-7B policy models, with overall gains of +2.3 to +3.9 points over VisualPRM and +2.8 to +4.4 over ViLPRM. On VABench, DiscPRM (65.9 macro F1) surpasses VisualPRM (59.9) and ViLPRM (59.3) by roughly 6 points, and also exceeds GPT-4o (59.8) and GPT-4o-Mini (56.7), while trailing Gemini-3-Pro (66.5) by only 0.6 points despite being a 7B open-source model. The paper attributes the gap over prior PRMs to their text-centric design: VisualPRM, for instance, achieves an F1 of 87.1 on positive steps but only 32.7 on negative steps, meaning it rarely flags incorrect visual operations. Compared to Self-Consistency (majority voting over 8 samples), DiscPRM delivers 4–11 point improvements, demonstrating that step-level visual-textual process evaluation provides benefits that final-answer agreement alone cannot capture. Among prompted MLLM critics (Qwen2.5-VL-72B, InternVL3-8B, LLaVA-OV-72B), none exceed 58.8 macro F1 on VABench, confirming that dedicated PRM training is necessary for reliable visual-step error detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the process reward model (PRM) lineage—originating from Math-Shepherd's Monte Carlo tree value estimation for text-only reasoning—into the emerging thinking-with-images paradigm, where solutions interleave textual steps with intermediate visual operations (crops, annotations, sketches). While recent multimodal PRMs (VisualPRM, ViLPRM) have adapted PRMs to vision-language inputs, they remain text-centric in their step-level scoring and cannot assess whether a visual operation is correct. DiscPRM departs from these by jointly modeling visual reasoning trajectories, image manipulations, and text-image consistency within a unified step-level reward, and by training on a purpose-built dataset (VTReward-100K) where half the solutions contain explicit visual intermediate steps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As thinking-with-images becomes a standard design pattern in frontier MLLMs, the inability of existing PRMs to judge visual steps creates a critical bottleneck for test-time scaling: Best-of-N selection can pick trajectories with incorrect visual grounding simply because the critic cannot detect the error. VABench fills a methodological gap by providing the first standardized evaluation for step-wise error detection in visual-textual trajectories, and the finding that even strong proprietary MLLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini-3-Pro) score near random on this task underscores how unaddressed this problem remains. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to more complex or agentic visual operations (multi-step tool use, code-driven image manipulation), whether the 7B-scale critic can be further compressed, and whether the Monte Carlo annotation pipeline scales to open-ended visual tasks without ground-truth answers.
