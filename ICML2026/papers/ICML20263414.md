## Problem

Classical feature importance metrics for GNNs ignore the underlying graph structure, while existing graph-aware methods either impose problem-specific assumptions about how edges contribute to learning or perform black-box selection without returning interpretable scores. The paper asks: how do graph connections modulate the importance of individual node features to GNN performance, and can this be measured in a data-, model-, and task-agnostic way during training?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a graph adjacency matrix, a node feature matrix of M real-valued features per node, and partially observed node labels in a semi-supervised setting. During training, it periodically permutes individual feature columns and measures the resulting drop in validation accuracy to produce per-feature importance scores. At each pruning checkpoint, features scoring below an r-quantile threshold are permanently masked, yielding a progressively reduced feature matrix alongside the trained GNN and its node class predictions.

## Method

The core mechanism is Node Feature Permutation Testing (NPT): at regular intervals during GNN training, each feature column is randomly permuted K times and the average drop in validation accuracy (δ_m) is computed as the importance score. Algorithm 1 wraps this in an adaptive loop that prunes the bottom r-fraction of features at each checkpoint, so the model retrains on a shrinking feature set without any preprocessing step. Theoretically, Theorems 1 and 2 bound GCN embedding error in terms of cross-class edges and feature-label alignment, proving that permutation-based scores are valid under node dependencies and that graph structure can render a feature important or unimportant independent of its marginal relationship to labels. The approach is architecture-agnostic (validated on GCN, GIN, TAGCN, GraphSAGE) and the accuracy metric in δ_m can be swapped for any task-relevant quality, making it flexible across homophilic and heterophilic settings.

## Results

On one-shot feature selection (retaining top r% of features), NPT achieves the highest or near-highest node classification accuracy across all 8 benchmark datasets. For homophilic citation networks, NPT reaches 79.19% on Cora, 69.35% on CiteSeer, and 87.11% on PubMed, outperforming the GCN-specific TFI baseline (72.73%, 65.77%, 83.80%) by 6.5, 3.6, and 3.3 points respectively. For heterophilic webpage graphs, NPT leads with 69.73% (Cornell), 72.97% (Texas), and 73.20% (Wisconsin), surpassing TFI by 8.1, 11.9, and 20.8 points. On co-purchase graphs (Photo, Computers), NPT (93.59%, 90.09%) rivals the best homophily-based and MI baselines within 0.3 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NPT consistently outperforms the GCN-specific TFI baseline (Zheng et al., ICLR 2025) across all datasets, with the largest margins on heterophilic graphs: +20.8 points on Wisconsin (73.20 vs 52.40), +11.9 on Texas (72.97 vs 61.08), and +8.1 on Cornell (69.73 vs 61.62). Against the homophily-based metrics (hattr, hEuc, hGE from Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2024), NPT dominates on citation and webpage graphs (e.g., +46.7 points on Cora, +29.2 on Wisconsin) but is competitive on co-purchase graphs where homophily is a natural signal (Photo: 93.59 vs 93.59 for hEuc; Computers: 90.09 vs 90.09 for hattr). NPT also beats the graph-agnostic MI baseline on 6 of 8 datasets, with the clearest wins on Cora (+12.4) and CiteSeer (+5.6), while MI edges out NPT slightly on Computers (90.33 vs 90.09). The key finding is that NPT matches or exceeds the *best* baseline in every setting without requiring prior knowledge of which metric is appropriate, whereas TFI, MI, and homophily scores each only excel in specific regimes (homophilic, graph-agnostic, and co-purchase respectively).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends classical permutation-based feature importance (Altmann et al., 2010; Breiman, 2001) to the graph-structured data setting, providing the first theoretical characterization of how graph topology modulates node feature relevance for GNNs. It departs from prior graph-specific feature selection methods (Maurya et al., 2022/2023; Zheng et al., 2025) that either assume homophilic structure or learn importance via uninterpretable auxiliary models, and from graph-agnostic metrics (MI, homophily scores) that ignore the interplay between edges, features, and labels. The adaptive algorithm (Algorithm 1) further distinguishes itself by performing feature pruning during training rather than as a one-shot pre-processing step, building on the theoretical error bounds for GCNs, GINs, and TAGCNs established in the paper.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a theoretically grounded, data-agnostic tool for practitioners who need to reduce GNN input dimensionality without committing to architecture-specific or structure-specific assumptions, which is valuable given the diversity of real-world graph data. Key limitations include the restriction to node classification (link prediction and graph classification are left for future work), the sensitivity of importance scores to correlated features (noted as a known issue with permutation tests), and the requirement that the GNN architecture be appropriately matched to the data. Open questions include whether conditional permutation tests or edge-rewiring-based approaches could provide finer-grained explanations of *why* a feature is important, and whether adaptive pruning ratios (dropping features with δ_m ≤ 0) can further improve the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff.
