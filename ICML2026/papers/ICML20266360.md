## Problem

AI risk literature predominantly focuses on AGI-level threats such as loss of control and alignment failures, leaving open whether generative models already create structural risk for knowledge and cultural production at current sub-AGI capability levels. This position paper argues that ordinary market dynamics—specifically, the growing difficulty of distinguishing AI-generated from human-produced outputs—drive adverse selection against deep human temporal learning (HTL) before any capability threshold is crossed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266360/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes four domain-specific economic parameters—verification ability g, quality gap Δq, per-item verification cost c_v, and HTL share λ—and produces a verification threshold g* ≈ c_v/Δq, pooled reward dynamics p̄ = λq_H + (1−λ)q_L, and a four-stage qualitative classification of how far verification has eroded in a given domain. Unlike empirical ML methods, the 'input' is a characterization of institutional and cost conditions rather than a dataset, and the 'output' is a stage assignment plus quantitative dynamics predicting whether HTL-intensive production can sustain itself under source-blind evaluation.

## Method

The paper formalizes a reduced-form costly-inspection model built on Akerlof's lemons framework, where an evaluator decides whether to inspect an output at cost c_v to distinguish HTL-intensive from low-HTL production. The central threshold condition g·Δq ≳ c_v determines whether verification is economically justified; below it, rewards pool across production types and, when pooled reward falls below HTL production cost c_H, high-cost producers exit in a self-reinforcing adverse-selection spiral. Cross-domain evidence from clinical medicine, legal practice, academic publishing, content platforms, and software security is organized into four stages of verification erosion, demonstrating the same mechanism operating at different intensities. A key insight is that alignment success is orthogonal to value collapse: better-aligned models narrow observable gaps between human and AI outputs, making source verification harder and intensifying displacement even as individual outputs improve. The framework further identifies a pipeline compression channel where automation of entry-level tasks narrows the experiential path that builds senior judgment, eventually eroding the evaluator pool that sustains verification capacity itself.

## Results

The paper is a position paper with no standard ML benchmark results; its empirical evidence is drawn from cross-domain studies. Key quantitative findings include: LLM-modified content in CS abstracts reached 22.5% (up from 2.4% pre-ChatGPT), NHANES-based publication rates surged ~47-fold around LLM availability, and redundancy in identical exposure-outcome analyses increased 17-fold between 2022 and 2024. At ICLR 2026, at least 50 of 300 examined papers contained fabricated citations that passed 3–5 expert peer reviews. In the cURL security project, AI-generated vulnerability reports reached ~20% of 2025 submissions while the valid-report rate fell to ~5%. Early-career workers in AI-exposed occupations show a 16% relative employment decline, and Big Tech new-graduate hires dropped 25% from 2023 to 2024.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper with no method-to-baseline comparisons; the `compares_to` list is empty. Instead, the paper organizes cross-domain evidence into a four-stage verification-erosion framework (intact clinical oversight → sanctions-maintained legal verification → volume-overwhelmed academic/security review → source-blind platform rewards) and uses this to show that verification erosion is already underway at different rates. The paper addresses five alternative views as implicit 'baselines': (1) productivity gains offsetting displacement (rejected because NHANES volume surged even as individual productivity rose), (2) expert detectability (rejected because feasibility per item ≠ economic justification at production volume, evidenced by fabricated citations passing multiple reviewers), (3) institutional adaptation (rejected because fabricated citations continue passing review alongside disclosure policies), (4) provenance/watermarking restoring separation (acknowledged as helpful but challenged by adversarial dynamics and coordination costs), and (5) quality convergence justifying displacement (rejected for knowledge-production domains where HTL matters for downstream research, training data, and evaluator capacity, unlike terminal-consumption goods such as textiles).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants Akerlof's (1970) lemons model and Spence's (1973) signaling theory into the context of generative AI and knowledge production, formalizing a 'value collapse' mechanism where verification costs exceed expected benefits and high-HTL producers exit. It positions itself explicitly against the dominant AGI-risk framing (Bostrom, 2014; Hendrycks et al., 2025) and against model collapse (Shumailov et al., 2024), arguing that value collapse is an economic channel that *increases exposure* to model collapse rather than being a technical failure mode. The four-stage verification-erosion taxonomy organizes otherwise scattered cross-domain evidence (NHANES publication surges, ICLR fabricated citations, legal sanctions, platform labeling) under a single economic logic, and the orthogonality claim relative to alignment departs from the standard narrative that better alignment is uniformly protective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a risk pathway that activates at current sub-AGI capability levels through ordinary market dynamics, meaning it does not require a capability threshold or alignment failure to materialize. The most consequential open question is whether provenance technologies (watermarking, cryptographic signatures, audit mechanisms) can sustain verification ability g above the threshold g* against continuous adversarial and competitive downward pressure, or whether institutional adaptation can outpace market selection. A key limitation is that the paper is a position piece: the four-stage ordering rests on cross-domain observations rather than controlled measurement of the verification threshold in each domain, and the reduced-form model (Appendix A) suppresses pool-composition dynamics through h(λ)=1, limiting its predictive power for within-domain trajectories.
