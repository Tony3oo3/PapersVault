## Problem

Current seizure detection algorithms report high efficacy on self-selected datasets but frequently fail when deployed to unseen patient populations, and the lack of standardized evaluation practices prevents direct comparison across studies. This paper addresses the generalization gap by conducting the first large-scale, head-to-head comparison of 28 state-of-the-art algorithms on a strictly held-out private dataset using a unified scoring framework.

## Input / Output

Algorithms receive 19-channel scalp EEG in EDF format (256 Hz, common average reference, 10-20 system) in files of approximately one hour each, and must output HED-SCORE-compliant TSV files listing seizure events. Performance is scored via event-based overlap: a detection counts as a true positive if it overlaps any ground-truth event within 30 s pre-ictal and 60 s post-ictal tolerances, events separated by <90 s are merged, and events exceeding 5 min are split. The primary ranking metric is the arithmetic mean of per-subject F1-scores, with sensitivity, precision, and false positives per day reported alongside.

## Method

The paper organizes a community-wide challenge (28 algorithms from 20 unique architectures spanning CNN, Transformer, LSTM, U-Net, S4, gradient boosting, and classical feature engineering) and evaluates all submissions in a blind, containerized Docker pipeline on HPC infrastructure against a held-out dataset of 65 subjects (4,360 h, 398 seizures) expertly annotated by three neurophysiologists. Evaluation follows the SzCORE framework's event-based scoring with fixed tolerances, and statistical significance is assessed via Friedman omnibus test, Wilcoxon signed-rank post-hoc comparisons with Holm-Bonferroni correction, Nemenyi critical-difference diagrams, and Cliff's Delta effect sizes. A key finding is the discordance between aggregate F1 and population-level rank stability: the F1-leader (Sz Transformer, 32%) is statistically indistinguishable from the rank-leader (HySEIZa v1), while 15 of 65 subjects yield F1=0 across all top-5 algorithms simultaneously. The infrastructure is released as a continuously open benchmarking platform (GitHub pull-request submissions) to enable ongoing, reproducible evaluation.

## Results

On the held-out Filadelfia dataset (65 subjects, 4,360 hours of continuous EMU EEG, 398 annotated seizures), the top algorithm (Sz Transformer) achieves an event-based F1 of 32% (sensitivity 37%, precision 29%, 1.34 FP/day). The top-5 submissions range from 30%–58% sensitivity and 19%–29% precision, with FP rates between 1.34 and 14 per day. The Friedman test confirms significant performance differences across all 28 algorithms (χ²=433.55, p<0.001), and 15 of 65 subjects (23%) yield F1=0 simultaneously across all top-5 algorithms, revealing severe patient-level failure modes. Nearly all algorithms substantially overestimate their performance relative to self-reported F1 scores on their own validation sets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The winning Sz Transformer (F1=32%, 1.34 FP/day) substantially outperforms the best algorithm from the prior Neureka 2020 scalp-EEG challenge, which achieved only 12.37% sensitivity at 1.44 FP/day on a different dataset and scoring methodology. Against commercial systems evaluated by Koren et al. (2021), the top research algorithms are competitive: Encevis 1.7 achieved 44.1% F1 on a comparable but distinct dataset with slightly more permissive scoring, while commercial systems typically trade precision for higher sensitivity (68–81%) at the cost of more false positives. A striking finding is the large generalization gap: nearly all 28 algorithms report substantially higher F1 on their own validation sets (often 50–90%) than on the independent Filadelfia test set, with the gap ranging from a few to over 50 percentage points. Cross-dataset analysis shows that SeizeIT1 (continuous EMU recordings) correlates most strongly with Filadelfia rankings (Spearman ρ=0.82), while curated event-only datasets like TUH (ρ=0.54) inflate F1 scores and distort relative algorithm rankings. Statistically, seven leading architectures are indistinguishable from the rank-leader HySEIZa v1 (mean rank 9.30), while classical baselines (Random Forest, Channel-adaptive) are significantly outperformed with large effect sizes (Cliff's δ > 0.6).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes the SzCORE framework (Dan et al., 2024), which previously provided standardized event-based scoring and data format specifications for seizure detection but lacked a submission-and-evaluation pipeline on a private dataset. It extends the lineage of prior epilepsy challenges (UPenn/Mayo 2014, Neureka 2020, Chatzichristos et al. 2023) by being the first to conduct a large-scale, direct, head-to-head comparison of 28 algorithms under a single standardized protocol on a substantial held-out EMU dataset (65 subjects, 4,360 hours). The algorithmic landscape spans the full spectrum from classical feature engineering (Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, band powers) to modern architectures (Transformers, S4, Hyena-Hierarchy, JEPA, LaBraM), positioning the work as a comprehensive empirical audit rather than a methodological contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that the best achievable F1 on an independent, clinically representative dataset is only 32%, and that nearly all algorithms dramatically overestimate their performance relative to self-reported metrics—exposes a critical generalization gap that undermines confidence in published seizure detection results. The discordance between peak aggregate F1 (Sz Transformer) and population-level rank stability (HySEIZa v1), along with 23% of patients receiving F1=0 from all top-5 algorithms simultaneously, highlights that current methods remain unreliable for individual patient deployment. The transition of the evaluation infrastructure into a continuously open benchmarking platform addresses the field's chronic lack of standardized, reproducible comparison, but open questions remain: how to close the sensitivity-precision trade-off for clinical use, whether multi-centric evaluation will further reduce performance, and how to handle the patient-specific electrographic patterns that defeat all current architectures.
