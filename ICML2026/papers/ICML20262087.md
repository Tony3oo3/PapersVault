## Problem

Multilingual RAG systems are known to exhibit language preference, but prior measurement approaches are confounded: they rely on coarse signals like citation frequency ratios or embedding similarity that cannot separate language bias from document relevance, and in-line citations in generated text are prone to hallucination. This paper asks whether models systematically prefer citing documents in certain languages (particularly English) and whether this preference can override document relevance, using a controlled methodology that isolates language as the sole varying factor.

## Input / Output

The method takes a query (in English or a target language), K relevant evidence documents translated into target languages via Google Translate (constrained to K < 10 so citation IDs tokenize as single tokens), and a verified statement-citation pair. It produces citation accuracy per language (fraction of statements where the model's top-1 next token is the correct citation ID), the preference gap Δ(ℓ_target) = Acc^(ℓ_target) − Acc^(en), and auxiliary metrics including next-token probability, Shannon entropy, and perplexity of the citation token distribution. The contrastive design holds all variables fixed except the language of the single cited document, with all other documents remaining in English.

## Method

The core contribution is a four-step controlled measurement pipeline that uses model internals (next-token prediction) rather than full generation to quantify language preference. First, English evidence documents are machine-translated into eight target languages. Second, a strong LLM (OpenAI o3) generates reference citation-supported reports, which are segmented into sentence-level statements each paired with a single citation ID. Third, a two-stage filtering pipeline verifies citation correctness: an LLM-as-Relevance-Judge (three models, majority vote of ≥2) confirms the cited document best supports the statement, and an NLI entailment classifier confirms the document entails the statement, yielding a pool of 792 verified pairs. Fourth, for each verified pair, the model's next-token probability of the correct citation ID is measured under contrastive contexts where only the cited document's language varies; differences in top-1 accuracy across languages define the preference gap. A logit lens analysis across transformer layers further reveals that citation decisions crystallize around a specific mid-network layer and are largely preserved thereafter, rather than being corrected in later layers.

## Results

Across six open-weight models (LLaMA-3.1 8B, LLaMA-3.3 70B, Qwen-3 8B/14B, Gemma-3 27B, AYA23 8B) and eight target languages, all models exhibit statistically significant English preference in citation accuracy. English baseline accuracy ranges from 60.0% (AYA23 8B) to 86.2% (Gemma-3 27B), with the largest accuracy drops for lower-resource languages: Swahili (−23.9% average, up to −37.6% for AYA23 8B) and Bengali (−18.0% average, up to −32.8% for AYA23 8B), versus smaller drops for high-resource languages like Spanish (−8.08% avg) and French (−8.82% avg). In the relevance-vs.-language experiment (MIRACL), models consistently cite irrelevant English documents over relevant target-language documents, confirming that language preference can outweigh document relevance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study rather than a method-vs.-baseline comparison, so the key contrasts are across models and languages. Larger models (Gemma-3 27B, LLaMA-3.3 70B) achieve substantially higher English baseline accuracy (86.2% and 85.9%) than smaller 8B models (60.0–67.4%), and also show smaller relative gaps to target languages, suggesting greater robustness to language variation. AYA23 8B, despite being explicitly trained for multilingual tasks, exhibits the strongest English preference (e.g., −37.6% for Swahili), indicating that multilingual pretraining alone does not eliminate the bias. Among target languages, the accuracy drop correlates with resource level: Swahili and Bengali (low-resource) suffer the largest drops across all models, while Spanish and French (high-resource) show the smallest. In the relevance-vs.-language experiment on MIRACL, all models show that an irrelevant English distractor is more likely to be cited than a relevant target-language document (tgt-En accuracy below En-En baseline), while target-language distractors are easier to dismiss (En-tgt accuracy above baseline), confirming that language preference overrides relevance in citation decisions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on language preference in multilingual RAG (Park & Lee, 2025; Sharma et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025) by moving from output-level proxy metrics (information overlap, embedding similarity, citation frequency ratios) to a controlled methodology based on model internals—specifically next-token prediction accuracy and logit lens analysis. It departs from prior short-form mRAG studies by targeting long-form citation-supported generation, where the confound between document relevance and language is most acute. The synthetic multi-parallel dataset construction and two-step NLI/LLM-judge filtering pipeline repurpose attribution frameworks (Rashkin et al., 2023) and RAGAS-style evaluation (Es et al., 2024) to isolate language as the sole variable, a design not present in prior mRAG preference measurements.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that models cite irrelevant English documents over relevant non-English ones reveals a fundamental failure mode in mRAG systems that directly undermines equitable multilingual knowledge access and could erode user trust in citation-supported outputs. The controlled methodology is reusable for auditing language bias in any long-form RAG pipeline, and the layer-wise logit lens analysis provides a diagnostic tool for understanding where in the network the preference crystallizes. Key limitations include reliance on machine-translated documents (no parallel long-form mRAG datasets exist), the ELI5 dataset's known quality issues, and the controlled assumption of uniform document relevance; open questions include whether training-time interventions or retrieval-stage re-ranking can mitigate the bias, and whether findings generalize to end-to-end retrieval with imperfect recall.
