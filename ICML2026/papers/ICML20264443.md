## Problem

Data Shapley and semivalues, when used via the top-m heuristic for data selection, can perform no better than random on natural (non-heterogeneous) datasets, despite their theoretical appeal as non-myopic and equitable valuation methods. The paper identifies the root cause: complex utility functions like validation accuracy involve multiple latent 'roles' that a single Shapley value cannot capture (P1), and the true objective cannot be represented by a linear aggregation of Shapley values (P2). The paper formalizes a 'Shapley-informativeness' property, proves when it holds, and asks whether it can be strategically exploited to make Shapley-based selection consistently effective.

## Input / Output

The method takes a feasible training set N (|N|=n), a validation set V, and a target selection size m, and outputs a selected subset M of exactly m training data. A key operational detail is that Shapley values must be computed per individual validation datum v (i.e., for each binary prediction-correctness function u_v), not just for the aggregate validation accuracy u_V; however, this incurs no extra cost since u_V is already computed as the average of all u_v. The only tunable hyperparameter is λ, which controls the curvature of the concave aggregation function, and the greedy selection loop is fully vectorized (e.g., selecting 10,000 from 20,000 data against 5,000 validation points takes ~53 seconds).

## Method

NASH (Non-linear Aggregation of SHapley-informative components) decomposes validation accuracy u_V into per-validation-datum prediction-correctness functions u_v, each of which is provably Shapley-informative under a Consistent Player assumption (each datum is consistently good or bad w.r.t. a given validation point). Rather than taking the linear sum of Shapley values (which recovers the flawed vanilla top-m Data Shapley), NASH aggregates the per-validation-datum Shapley sums non-linearly via a concave, increasing function F_T inspired by learning-curve fits (e.g., an exponential law), yielding the objective max_{|M|=m} Σ_v F_T(û_v(M)). This concave aggregation addresses P2 by giving diminishing returns to data that reinforce already-covered roles, so the greedy algorithm preferentially selects data that cover underrepresented validation points. The NASH objective is monotone submodular when all Shapley values are non-negative, guaranteeing a (1−1/e) approximation via greedy selection; in practice, harmful data (with very negative Shapley values) are naturally excluded. The framework is compatible with any semivalue (Data Shapley, Data Banzhaf, Beta Shapley) and any efficient Shapley approximation method (MC sampling, truncated MC, least squares, FreeShap).

## Results

The paper's main results are presented as validation-accuracy-vs-selection-ratio curves (Figures 5–8) rather than a single numeric table. Across all six primary dataset–model combinations (WD-LR, PO-LR, PM-LR, MR-BT, MP-BT, RT-LM), NASH consistently outperforms every baseline at every selection ratio, with the gap most pronounced on general (non-heterogeneous) datasets where vanilla Data Shapley degrades to random-level performance. On heterogeneous-quality datasets (with 10–30% label noise injected), Data Shapley improves but NASH still maintains a consistent advantage. The method is computationally lightweight: selecting 10,000 out of 20,000 training data w.r.t. 5,000 validation data takes only ~53.4 seconds wall-clock, and computing all per-validation-datum Shapley vectors incurs no extra cost over computing the standard validation-accuracy Shapley vector.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NASH is compared against six baselines across tabular (logistic/ridge regression) and language-model finetuning (BERT, Llama-2-7B) settings. Against **vanilla Data Shapley (top-m)**, NASH wins on every dataset and selection size; the gap is largest on general datasets (WD-LR, PO-LR, PM-LR) where Data Shapley is no better than random, and persists even on heterogeneous datasets where Data Shapley recovers some effectiveness. Against **Random selection**, NASH achieves substantial and consistent gains, confirming that the Shapley-informative decomposition adds real signal. Against **Influence Function, TracIn, and Representer Point** (all top-m heuristics that ignore subset interactions), NASH outperforms on the larger-scale finetuning tasks (MR-BT, MP-BT, RT-LM), validating the benefit of non-myopic, interaction-aware valuation. Against **Data Banzhaf and Beta Shapley** (other semivalues via top-m), NASH consistently wins; vanilla Data Banzhaf and Beta Shapley can also degrade to random-level on general datasets, and NASH's non-linear aggregation rescues them. The paper also shows that NASH is compatible with any semivalue: pairing NASH with Data Banzhaf or Beta Shapley (rather than Data Shapley) still yields consistent improvements over the respective vanilla top-m baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Data Shapley (Ghorbani & Zou, 2019) and the broader semivalue framework (Dubey et al., 1981; Kwon & Zou, 2022; Wang & Jia, 2023), but departs from the standard top-m selection heuristic that prior works rely on. It addresses the empirical failure mode identified by Wang et al. (2024c) and Chi et al. (2025) — that Data Shapley can be no better than random on natural datasets — by providing a theoretical explanation (Shapley-informativeness property) and a practical fix (non-linear aggregation of per-validation-datum Shapley sums). The choice of the concave aggregation function is inspired by learning curve theory (Viering & Loog, 2022), and the framework is designed to be compatible with existing efficient Shapley/semivalue approximation methods (MC sampling, least squares, FreeShap).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NASH makes Shapley/semivalue-based data selection consistently effective across both homogeneous and heterogeneous datasets, removing the restrictive conditions (e.g., injected noise, MTM structure) under which prior analyses showed Data Shapley to work. The minimal additional runtime cost (greedy over a vectorizable concave objective) and plug-and-play compatibility with existing approximation methods make it immediately practical. Open questions include whether the Consistent Player assumption holds broadly across model architectures, whether temporal dependence in data contributions could further improve efficiency, and which semivalue weighting schemes are optimal when paired with NASH.
