## Problem

Existing LLM bias benchmarks rely on templated fill-in-the-blank prompts or fixed multiple-choice questions that fail to capture the complexity of real-world user interactions and are increasingly saturated. Generative bias benchmarks that do use open-ended prompts typically employ simple, statically generated questions and conflate a model's explicit acknowledgment of bias with its actual exhibition of bias. This paper addresses both gaps by introducing a counterfactual framework that adaptively generates realistic, open-ended questions specifically optimized to elicit non-acknowledged, non-refusing bias from a target model.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a sensitive attribute with its finite set of values (e.g., sex: male/female; race: White/Black/Asian/Hispanic; religion: Christian/Muslim/Hindu/Jewish), a target model to probe, and a small set of manually crafted seed questions. It produces counterfactual questions containing placeholders for attribute values (e.g., '{male/female}'), organized hierarchically into superdomains → domains → topics, along with multi-dimensional bias scores (bias 1–5, acknowledgment 1–5, relevance 1–5, refusal 0–1) computed by an LLM judge over k=3 sampled responses per attribute value. The final output is CAB, a 405-question human-verified benchmark (145 sex, 128 race, 135 religion) with both explicit and implicit (name-based) variants, where implicit versions replace attribute values with stereotypically associated first names.

## Method

The core mechanism is a genetic algorithm that iteratively mutates and selects questions to maximize a multiplicative fitness score: F = Bias × N(Relevance) × N(Acknowledgment) × ((1−γ) + γ(1−Refusal)), where N(x) = 1 − x/5 normalizes to [0,1] and γ=0.5 weights the refusal penalty. Each iteration cycles through five phases: (1) updating category quotas based on recent fitness performance (upscaling high-performing superdomains/domains by 1.2×, downscaling low-performing ones by 0.7×, and applying a 0.80× decay to units existing ≥2 iterations), (2) exploring new superdomains and domains conditioned on high- and low-performing examples, (3) generating or mutating questions via three operators—refine (improve existing question, 30%), replace (new question for same topic, 20%), and generate (new topic-question pair, 50%)—(4) evaluating all new questions by sampling k=3 target-model responses per attribute value and scoring them with an LLM judge (GPT-5-MINI) across the four dimensions, and (5) retaining questions exceeding a threshold τ (1.4 for sex, 1.8 for race/religion). The multiplicative fitness design ensures that only questions eliciting strong bias that is simultaneously non-acknowledged, contextually irrelevant to the question, and non-refusal-based are promoted, directly addressing the acknowledgment-vs-exhibition conflation problem in prior work.

## Results

On the CAB benchmark (405 human-verified questions across sex, race, and religion), GPT-5 exhibits the lowest average fitness (bias) scores (1.41 sex, 1.66 race, 1.48 religion), followed by CLAUDE-4-SONNET (1.50, 1.47, 1.43). QWEN3-235B and GROK-4 show the highest bias levels (up to 1.95 and 1.85 respectively on sex). More than 65% of sex-related CAB questions elicited a biased response from at least one evaluated model, and implicit (name-based) questions produce roughly a 40% drop in average fitness compared to explicit questions, while still revealing persistent biases. Bootstrap standard deviations (n=1000) are consistently small (0.05–0.09), confirming stable model rankings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates nine frontier LLMs on the CAB benchmark rather than comparing a single method against baselines; the 'comparison' is which models exhibit less bias. GPT-5 achieves the lowest average fitness on sex (1.41) and religion (1.48), while CLAUDE-4-SONNET is lowest on race (1.47) and religion (1.43), together forming the least-biased tier. At the other end, QWEN3-235B (1.95 sex, 1.94 race) and GROK-4 (1.85 sex, 1.83 race) consistently show the highest bias scores across attributes. On the implicit (name-based) subset, all models improve by roughly 40%, but GPT-5 and CLAUDE-4-SONNET still lead, while GROK-4 and QWEN3-235B narrow the gap and surpass GEMINI-2.5-PRO. The bias profiles also differ qualitatively: GEMINI-2.5-PRO more frequently varies communication tone, whereas GPT-OSS-120B more often assigns stereotypical roles based on sex. Transferability checks confirm that CAB questions generalize across generator families, with models whose families were not used during generation (GROK-4, KIMI-K2, DEEPSEEK-V3.1) showing consistent bias patterns.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the three established families of LLM bias benchmarks—templated (CrowS-Pairs, WinoBias), multiple-choice (BBQ, UnQover), and static generative (Meta-FAIR, BOLD)—by introducing an adaptive, iterative question-generation process grounded in a genetic algorithm. Rather than relying on fixed or hand-crafted prompts, the framework uses a multi-dimensional LLM judge (scoring bias, acknowledgment, relevance, and refusal separately) to compute a fitness score that drives iterative mutation of counterfactual questions, systematically exploring domains where a target model is most susceptible to biased responses. The counterfactual evaluation design (comparing responses across attribute values within the same question) builds on standard fairness-testing methodology, but the adaptive search over question space and the explicit separation of bias exhibition from bias acknowledgment represent a methodological departure from prior generative benchmarks that conflate the two.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CAB provides a practical, human-verified benchmark (405 questions, ~$8 per model per attribute) that surfaces persistent biases even in the strongest frontier models, with over 65% of sex-related questions eliciting at least one biased response. The 40% fitness drop between explicit and implicit question variants highlights that subtle cues like names still trigger stereotyping, a finding with direct implications for deployment. Key limitations include coverage of only three sensitive attributes in a Western, US-English context, the use of names as imperfect proxies for implicit bias, and continued dependence on an LLM judge whose accuracy, while validated, is not perfect. Open questions include whether the adaptive framework can be repurposed for bias mitigation (not just detection), whether it generalizes to additional attributes and cultural contexts, and whether the identified high-bias domains (e.g., parental care for sex, education for race) can guide targeted fine-tuning or system-prompt interventions.
