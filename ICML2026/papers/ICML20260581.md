## Problem

Transformer optimization stability is increasingly constrained as models scale, yet the relationship between architectural design choices (normalization placement, residual scaling) and classical second-order optimization geometry—specifically the Hessian spectral norm and maximum tolerable learning rate—remains largely implicit and empirically motivated. This paper bridges that gap by explicitly connecting activation scale, Hessian structure, and admissible learning rates through a principled normalization strategy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260581/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes standard BPE/SentencePiece tokenized text sequences (sequence lengths 512–2048 depending on backbone) and produces next-token predictions via a standard language modeling objective. The key structural difference from standard GPT is that every linear projection (W_q, W_k, W_v, W_o, W_1, W_2, and W_3 for SwiGLU) is immediately followed by an RMSNorm layer, and no separate prenorm is used. The embedding and output classification layers are left unmodified.

## Method

SimpleNorm defines a primitive operator Ψ(x) = Norm(Wx) that fuses a linear mapping with an immediate normalization (instantiated as RMSNorm), and SimpleGPT systematically replaces every linear layer in the Transformer with this primitive. The theoretical contribution shows two mechanisms: (i) activation norms are bounded to Θ(√d) by construction, preventing scale drift with depth; and (ii) the Hessian of the loss with respect to activations decomposes into a Gauss-Newton term (dominant in high dimensions) and a curvature term that vanishes as O(1/√d), and crucially the overall Hessian spectral norm is scale-invariant with respect to ||W||₂, whereas a plain linear projection's curvature grows as ||W||₂². This weight-scale invariance means the loss landscape remains smooth as weights grow during training, permitting 3×–10× larger stable learning rates. Empirically, SimpleGPT-7B reduces training loss from 2.290 to 2.208 over 60K steps compared to Llama2+QKNorm, with consistent gains across 1B–8B scales on Llama2, Llama3, and nanoGPT backbones.

## Results

At the 7B scale (Llama2, 60K steps on C4), SimpleGPT reduces training loss from 2.290 (Llama2+QKNorm) to 2.208, a 0.082 absolute improvement, while using a 3× larger learning rate (1e-3 vs. 3e-4). At 1B scale (200K steps), SimpleGPT achieves 2.446 vs. 2.478 for Llama2+QKNorm (0.032 gain), and at 8B scale (Llama3, 20K steps) it reaches 2.678 vs. 2.752 (0.074 gain). On held-out evaluation, SimpleGPT-7B achieves 0.7999 bpb on C4 validation and 0.7896 bpb on Wiki, both lower than Llama2+QKNorm (0.8327 and 0.8188), and scores 53.99% on WinoGrande vs. 52.88% for the QKNorm baseline. SimpleGPT tolerates learning rates 3×–10× larger than standard conventions while maintaining stable convergence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard Llama2 (Touvron et al., 2023b), SimpleGPT achieves substantially lower training loss at every scale tested: 2.208 vs. 2.311 at 7B/60K steps, 2.446 vs. 2.520 at 1B/200K steps, and 2.678 vs. 4.640 at 8B/20K steps, with the gap widening as model size increases. Against Llama2+QKNorm (Henry et al., 2020), SimpleGPT consistently wins on training loss (0.032–0.082 absolute improvement across scales) and on held-out bpb, while matching or slightly exceeding QKNorm on downstream tasks (WinoGrande +1.11, HellaSwag +0.59, PIQA −0.16). Against Llama3 (Dubey et al., 2024), SimpleGPT-8B achieves 2.678 vs. 2.752 training loss at 20K steps using a 3× larger learning rate. Against GPT-2 (Radford et al., 2019) on nanoGPT, SimpleGPT-1.4B improves validation loss by ~0.043 over GPT-2+QKNorm at 100K steps. Against SandwichNorm (Ding et al., 2021), SimpleGPT-7B achieves 2.331 vs. 2.471 training loss, confirming that the gain comes from the specific post-linear-mapping placement rather than simply adding more normalization layers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on RMSNorm (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019) as its normalization operator but departs fundamentally from prior normalization-placement work (PreNorm, PostNorm, QKNorm, DNT) by proposing a single, uniform rule: normalize immediately after every linear projection. While DNT (Qi et al., 2025a) and related work motivate placement choices through first-order Jacobian or gradient-stability analysis, this paper grounds its design in second-order Hessian geometry, explicitly connecting the spectral norm of the activation Hessian to the maximum tolerable learning rate via classical β-smoothness theory. It also positions itself against normalization-free approaches (Zhu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025) by arguing that explicit local normalization after linear maps provides both optimization stability and a form of pervasive nonlinearity that bounded activations alone do not capture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a ~3% training-time overhead yields 3×–10× larger stable learning rates and consistent 0.03–0.08 loss reductions across 1B–8B models on three different architectures, making it a low-cost drop-in improvement for large-scale pretraining. The theoretical framework (Hessian spectral norm reduction via scale-invariant curvature) provides a principled, unified explanation for why multiple ad-hoc stabilization techniques work, which could guide future architecture design beyond Transformers. Open questions include whether the gains persist at 70B+ scale, whether the Section 4.4 'expressiveness' hypothesis (pervasive nonlinearity from normalization) is the true source of the empirical gains beyond optimization stability, and whether the theoretical assumptions (high effective rank, non-pathological alignment, moderate γ) hold in all practical regimes.
