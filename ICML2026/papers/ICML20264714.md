## Problem

Standard alignment pipelines directly use a reward model learned from user preferences to optimize an LLM under KL regularization, but this is suboptimal: the KL constraint causes the aligned policy to inherit base-model biases that conflict with user preferences, while naively amplifying rewards to counteract this bias risks reward hacking. The paper asks how to optimally shape the reward model to maximize user utility while keeping the aligned policy close to the base model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264714/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt, a frozen base LLM policy (e.g., Qwen3-8B or Llama3-8B-Instruct), and a reward model trained on user preference data, plus M Monte Carlo response samples from the base policy per prompt. It produces a prompt-dependent shaped reward model (a bounded sigmoid transformation of the original reward) that is then used to guide inference-time decoding (via Controlled Decoding or ARGS), yielding an aligned text response. The shaping is prompt-specific: each prompt receives its own threshold, effectively inducing a customized reward strength per prompt rather than a global one.

## Method

The paper formalizes alignment as a Stackelberg game where a reward-model provider (leader) selects a reward model and the LLM (follower) best-responds under a KL-divergence constraint, with the leader's objective being to maximize the user's true expected utility subject to a reward bound B that limits policy deviation. The key theoretical result is that the optimal reward model has a threshold structure: responses whose true utility exceeds a prompt-dependent threshold m*(x) receive reward B, and all others receive 0, where m*(x) is self-consistently defined as the expected user utility under the resulting aligned policy. In practice, the threshold is approximated by solving a monotone helper function via bisection using Monte Carlo samples from the base policy, and a soft sigmoid variant r = B·σ(α(r_U − m*(x))) is used for robustness. The shaped reward is integrated into existing inference-time alignment methods (Controlled Decoding and ARGS) with negligible overhead: offline for CD, and an average of ~0.03 s per 128-token response for ARGS.

## Results

SRS consistently achieves the highest average reward across all four evaluation settings: 3.33 (SRS-ARGS, Eval-1), 3.40 (SRS-ARGS, Eval-2), 2.04 (SRS-ARGS, Eval-3), and 3.29 (SRS-ARGS, Eval-4), while maintaining diversity and coherence comparable to all baselines. In GPT-4 head-to-head evaluations, SRS achieves average Win-Tie rates of 66.83% against vanilla methods, 69.6% against Minmax, and 66.65% against Meanstd, confirming that reward gains are not driven by reward hacking. For SRS-CD, the best reward is 3.37 (Eval-2) and 3.23 (Eval-1), again outperforming all CD baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla ARGS (Khanov et al., 2024), SRS-ARGS wins on reward in all four evaluation settings (e.g., 3.33 vs. 3.23 on Eval-1, 3.40 vs. 3.26 on Eval-2, 2.04 vs. 1.87 on Eval-3, 3.29 vs. 2.97 on Eval-4) while maintaining comparable diversity and coherence. Against vanilla CD (Mudgal et al., 2023), SRS-CD similarly dominates on reward (3.23 vs. 3.09 on Eval-1, 3.37 vs. 3.10 on Eval-2). Against the heuristic reward-shaping baselines from Fu et al. (2025), SRS outperforms Minmax consistently (e.g., 3.40 vs. 3.14 on Eval-2 ARGS) and Meanstd by a wider margin (e.g., 3.40 vs. 3.15 on Eval-2 ARGS, 2.04 vs. 0.37 on Eval-3 ARGS where Meanstd nearly collapses to base-policy level). In GPT-4 Win-Tie evaluations, SRS achieves 66.83% average against vanilla, 69.6% against Minmax, and 66.65% against Meanstd, confirming quality gains are not artifacts of reward hacking. Notably, Meanstd (an unbounded shaping scheme) is the weakest baseline, sometimes producing rewards below the unaligned base policy (e.g., 2.65 vs. 2.95 on Eval-2 CD), while Minmax is more competitive but less consistent due to sensitivity to reward outliers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the KL-regularized alignment objective from DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), which defines the follower's closed-form best response, and departs from prior reward shaping work (Fu et al. 2025, Wang et al. 2024, Shen et al. 2024) that proposes specific heuristic transformations by instead formulating reward model design as a Stackelberg game with a principled optimality criterion. It also relates to earlier Stackelberg formulations in RLHF (Chakraborty et al. 2023, Makar-Limanov et al. 2024) but differs in placing the reward model as the leader and exploiting the LLM-specific closed-form KL-regularized solution to avoid intractable Hessian computations. The paper further connects to algorithmic contract design, where the reward model plays the role of a bounded incentive contract.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core insight—that directly using the learned reward model is suboptimal under KL regularization because the base policy's bias distorts the reward-to-utility mapping—challenges a foundational assumption in the standard alignment pipeline and suggests that reward model design is a first-class optimization problem rather than a fixed input. The practical algorithm (Monte Carlo sampling + bisection + sigmoid shaping) is lightweight and integrates into existing inference-time methods with negligible overhead, making it immediately deployable. Open questions include extending the framework to train-time alignment (e.g., GRPO), handling multi-user or adversarial preference settings, and understanding how the threshold structure interacts with more complex reward landscapes beyond the scalar case.
