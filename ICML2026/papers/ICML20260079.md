## Problem

Standard ML-based digital twins are trained to minimise one-step transition errors (MSE or NLL) across all variables and time points, an objective that is direction-, variable-, and timepoint-agnostic. The paper proves that when model capacity is limited, this training paradigm can produce DTs that induce incorrect policy preference orderings, and shows empirically that the misalignment persists even with expressive hypothesis spaces. The core gap is that no prior DT training method explicitly optimises for the downstream decision-making task of ranking candidate policies.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an offline dataset of state-action-next-state transitions collected by deploying a candidate policy set Π in the real system, along with a known reward function r. Before DT training, FQE Q-functions are pre-trained on the same offline data to produce scalar policy value estimates that serve as proxy ranking targets. The trained DT outputs the mean and diagonal log-variance of a Gaussian over the next state, enabling Monte Carlo rollout generation for any policy; from these rollouts, policy value estimates and pairwise preference orderings are derived. A tunable hyperparameter λ ∈ [0,1] controls the trade-off between raw simulation fidelity and decision-targeted ranking accuracy.

## Method

DT² augments the standard simulation loss (NLL or MSE) with a smoothed Kendall rank correlation loss that penalises disagreement between DT-estimated pairwise policy value differences and FQE-derived proxy targets. The ranking loss replaces non-differentiable sign functions with tanh, where the temperature α governs smoothness and the tanh(Δij/α) term implicitly down-weights pairs where the reference evaluator is nearly indifferent, concentrating gradient signal on high-regret distinctions. To avoid backpropagating through long rollouts, a bootstrapping scheme truncates DT trajectories at a fixed horizon H and substitutes the frozen FQE Q-function for the remaining return, reducing computational cost and improving gradient stability. The approach is architecture-agnostic, demonstrated across MLP, ResNet, Transformer, GRU, and Neural ODE backbones, and the combined objective L_DT² = (1−λ)L_sim + λL_rank allows practitioners to navigate the fidelity-versus-decision-quality trade-off.

## Results

Across six continuous control environments (Pendulum, Lunarlander, Hopper, Walker, Cheetah, Ant) and five architectures, DT² achieves an average decision regret of 1.88 versus 4.08 for standard L_sim^NLL training (a 54% reduction), and an average Spearman's rank correlation of 0.66 versus 0.45 (a 47% improvement), at only a 17% increase in test MSE (1.41 vs 1.21). In the Cancer treatment case study, DT² reduces regret from 59.56 to 26.96 and improves Spearman's from 0.10 to 0.64, with only a 2% MSE cost. For unseen policies in the Cancer environment, DT² still halves regret (49.27 vs 95.88) and improves Spearman's (0.50 vs 0.40), confirming generalisation beyond training policies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DT² outperforms standard L_sim^NLL DT training on average across all environments and architectures (54% lower regret, 47% higher Spearman's), and wins in 83.3% of individual architecture–environment–metric couplings. Among MBRL baselines, MOReL and MOPO are the most competitive on individual environments (e.g., MOReL achieves 0.51 regret on Hopper vs DT²'s 0.60), but both suffer severe failures on Lunarlander (21.06 and 1.45 regret respectively vs DT²'s 0.45) and Cheetah (5.08 and 9.57 vs 0.55). ROMI and VaGraM, which use value-aware weighting, also underperform DT² on most environments, with VaGraM particularly struggling on Lunarlander (25.47 regret) and Cheetah (6.62). The LLM-guided HDTwin baseline is consistently the weakest, with negative Spearman's on Pendulum and Walker. FQE is the strongest single baseline, matching or exceeding DT² on Ant (0.00 vs 1.64 regret) and Cheetah (1.29 vs 0.55), but DT² surpasses it on Lunarlander (0.45 vs 2.51 regret) and Walker (0.21 vs 1.18), demonstrating that distilling OPE rankings into an interpretable DT can improve upon the OPE method itself in some settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard digital twin training paradigm (minimising one-step transition error via MSE or NLL) by introducing a decision-targeted objective that explicitly preserves policy rankings. Conceptually, it draws on value-aware model learning from model-based RL (VaGraM, Farahmand et al.) and decision-focused learning, but distinguishes itself by operating in the offline setting, targeting ranking of a fixed candidate policy set rather than learning a new policy, and requiring no differentiability through the downstream optimisation. The method repurposes fitted Q-evaluation (FQE) from off-policy evaluation as a supervision signal, distilling OPE's ranking ability into an interpretable, simulatable DT model — a combination not previously explored in the DT literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a widely-held assumption in the DT community that higher simulation fidelity automatically yields better decision support, providing both a formal proof and empirical evidence that this is false even for expressive architectures. The practical impact is significant: a 54% reduction in decision regret at only 17% MSE cost across diverse control environments, and a near-zero fidelity cost (2%) in the cancer treatment case study, suggests that decision-targeted training is a low-cost, high-value modification to existing DT pipelines. Key open questions include the sensitivity to FQE quality under distribution shift, the computational overhead of bootstrapped rollouts during training, and whether the approach extends to settings where the reward function is not fully specified or where the policy set is open-ended rather than fixed.
