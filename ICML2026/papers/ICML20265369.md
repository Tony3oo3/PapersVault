## Problem

GRPO-based RLVR methods suffer from a cumulative-advantage bias that systematically under-weights high-difficulty, low-accuracy problems, capping Pass@K performance. Additionally, the breadth dimension (number of training instances per iteration) is largely unexplored, and its interaction with depth (difficulty scaling) remains unclear. The paper asks whether depth and breadth can be jointly optimized to improve both Pass@K and Pass@1 simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265369/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes reasoning questions (primarily math problems from OpenR1-45K, MATH, or Math12k) and produces step-by-step reasoning traces ending with a final answer in \boxed{} format, scored by Math-Verify. Training uses a maximum prompt length of 1024 tokens and response length of 3072 tokens at temperature 1.0, with binary correctness rewards. The DARS mechanism operates at the batch level: it first draws a small pre-rollout (N^pre trajectories) per question to estimate difficulty, then dynamically allocates additional rollouts up to a cap N^max, producing ragged per-question rollout counts that necessitate full-batch PPO updates rather than standard minibatch chunking.

## Method

DARS (Difficulty Adaptive Rollout Sampling) rebalances GRPO's cumulative advantage through a two-phase procedure: a lightweight pre-rollout estimates per-problem accuracy, then a re-balancing stage allocates extra trajectories Δn_j to low-accuracy problems so that their cumulative advantage is up-weighted. Two schedules are provided—Equal-Treatment (ET), which equalizes cumulative advantage to a medium-difficulty anchor and induces a Log-Odds objective, and Hardness-Weighted (HW), which scales compute linearly with difficulty and induces a Maximum-Likelihood objective. DARS-Breadth further combines DARS with a large training batch size (3072) using full-batch gradient descent across multiple PPO epochs, which acts as implicit entropy regularization to sustain exploration and boost Pass@1. The paper proves that DARS-HW is theoretically equivalent to MaxRL in expected gradient but with O(1/u) rather than O(1/u²) variance growth, and empirically demonstrates that depth and breadth are orthogonal, complementary levers yielding simultaneous Pass@K and Pass@1 gains.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, DARS-HW-Breadth achieves 58.4 Avg@128 (Pass@1) and 83.4 Pass@128, improving over the Dr. GRPO baseline (55.3 / 81.4) by +3.1 and +2.0 points respectively. On Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, DARS-HW-Breadth reaches 52.4 Avg@128 and 82.2 Pass@128 versus the baseline's 49.6 / 79.6. The gains are most dramatic on Llama-3.1-8B, where DARS-Llama-ET-Breadth lifts Avg@128 from 15.8 to 22.0 (+6.2) and Pass@128 from 56.5 to 67.2 (+10.7), demonstrating that depth-breadth synergy is especially effective when the base model has substantial untapped reasoning capacity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Dr. GRPO (Liu et al., 2025b) with rollout size 8 and batch size 128. DARS-HW-Breadth consistently outperforms this baseline on both Pass@1 and Pass@128 across all three model families: +3.1 Avg@128 and +2.0 Pass@128 on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, +2.8 and +2.6 on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, and +6.2 and +10.7 on Llama-3.1-8B. Against the Depth-Naive baseline (uniformly scaling rollout to 32), DARS-Breadth wins on both metrics while using far fewer average rollouts per prompt (20.1 vs. 32 for HW on 7B, a 37% reduction in compute). Against Breadth-Naive (batch size 3072 without adaptive sampling), DARS-Breadth achieves higher Pass@128 (83.4 vs. 79.2 on 7B) while matching or exceeding Pass@1, confirming that depth and breadth are complementary rather than competing. The HW schedule consistently outperforms ET on Pass@128 (e.g., 83.4 vs. 82.1 on 7B-Breadth) while maintaining comparable Pass@1, making it the preferred configuration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and its Dr. GRPO variant (Liu et al., 2025b), which use group-relative advantage estimation for RLVR training. The key departure is identifying a 'cumulative advantage bias' in these methods that systematically underweights high-difficulty, low-accuracy problems, and proposing Difficulty-Adaptive Rollout Sampling (DARS) to correct it via multi-stage rollout reallocation. Conceptually, DARS draws on the focal-loss intuition of up-weighting hard examples (Lin et al., 2017) but implements it through adaptive compute allocation rather than loss re-weighting. The paper also establishes a formal theoretical equivalence between DARS-HW and Maximum Likelihood RL (MaxRL, Tajwar et al., 2026), showing DARS achieves the same objective with lower gradient variance by expanding sampling rather than algebraically scaling advantages.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, well-motivated recipe (DARS-Breadth) that simultaneously improves both Pass@1 and Pass@K in RLVR, addressing a gap where prior methods optimized one at the expense of the other. The finding that depth and breadth are orthogonal, complementary levers gives practitioners a clearer mental model for tuning RLVR training. Open questions remain: whether the N^max annealing strategy (discussed in Appendix G) can be made more principled to avoid the Pass@K-to-Pass@1 trade-off, whether the approach generalizes to non-mathematical reasoning at scale, and whether RLVR can fundamentally surpass the base model's capability boundary or merely redistribute its existing solution space. The method's reliance on a pre-rollout stage adds inference overhead, and the full-batch update requirement limits applicability to very large-scale settings.
