## Problem

AI/ML deepfake research overwhelmingly targets viewer-centric epistemic harms (fraud, political misinformation, authenticity verification) while largely ignoring subject-centric dignity harms, particularly AI-generated non-consensual intimate imagery (AIG-NCII), which accounts for the majority of generative AI abuse. This structural misalignment means existing technical interventions—detection, provenance, and watermarking—are designed for the wrong threat model and may even exacerbate harms to victims.

## Input / Output

The landscape analysis takes as input a corpus of 39 highly-cited technical papers (2020–2025) selected via a Google Scholar keyword query filtered to top-tier venues (CVPR, ICCV, ECCV, NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR) and papers with >80 citations, then qualitatively codes each for engagement with AIG-NCII terms (e.g., 'non-consensual intimate imagery,' 'revenge porn,' 'nudity,' 'undress'). The output is a three-tier categorization (no mention: 34 papers; mention only: 5; technical implementation: 0) plus nine concrete recommendations for realigning research priorities.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a systematic landscape analysis combined with an argumentative framework. The analysis queries Google Scholar with detection/forensics keywords intersected with deepfake/synthetic-image terms, filters to 379 top-venue papers, narrows to the top 100 most cited, and manually excludes irrelevant and retracted works to yield 39 papers for qualitative coding. The argumentative framework distinguishes viewer-centric epistemic harms (deception, fraud) from subject-centric dignity harms (non-consensual identity preservation, non-consensual modification), drawing on the UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights and Citron's sexual privacy theory. A key technical insight is the orthogonality argument: the axis of artificiality (synthetic vs. authentic) that existing tools measure is orthogonal to the axis of consent that determines safety, meaning authenticity labels cannot resolve AIG-NCII and may actively harm victims by confirming exposure or enabling abusers to sort content.

## Results

The landscape analysis of 39 highly-cited deepfake defense papers (2020–2025, top-tier venues) found that 34 papers (87%) contain no mention of AIG-NCII, 5 papers (13%) reference it only in passing, and 0 papers propose a technical intervention with a threat model specific to AIG-NCII. This stands in stark contrast to empirical reports indicating that AIG-NCII accounts for the majority of generative AI usage (e.g., 4.4 million images generated on Grok in one week vs. ~311,000 the prior week, with >3 million sexualized and ≥23,000 depicting children). The paper further demonstrates via Table 1 that the authenticity axis (synthetic vs. authentic) is orthogonal to the consent axis (safe vs. harmful), meaning existing detection/provenance/watermarking tools cannot resolve AIG-NCII harms.

**Table 1: The orthogonality of harm.** The current detection paradigm can only distinguish between synthetic and authentic media. However, safety falls along an orthogonal axis that is determined by consent.

| | Safe | Harmful |
|---|---|---|
| **Synthetic** | Self-expression of artistic nudity using AI | AIG-NCII |
| **Authentic** | Consensual pornography | Traditional NCII |

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper and does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines. Instead, its core empirical comparison is between the research landscape and the harm landscape: of 39 top-cited deepfake defense papers, zero address AIG-NCII with a dedicated technical intervention, while industry reports (AI Forensics, CCDH, Security Hero) indicate AIG-NCII constitutes the dominant use case of generative AI (up to 98% of deepfake videos are pornographic). The paper contrasts three existing intervention paradigms—detection, provenance (C2PA), and watermarking (SynthID)—arguing that all three share the foundational assumption that authenticity verification is a sufficient proxy for safety, which fails for AIG-NCII where the harm is defined by absence of consent rather than by synthetic origin. The Meta Oversight Board's 2024 ruling is cited as evidence that even platform governance bodies recognize labeling as insufficient for subject-centric harms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the dominant deepfake detection literature (DIRE, C2PA provenance, SynthID watermarking) by arguing that its foundational assumption—that authenticity verification is a sufficient proxy for safety—is a category error when applied to AIG-NCII. It draws on legal scholarship (Citron's sexual privacy, Chesney & Citron's deepfakes framework) and sexual violence research (Henry, McGlynn & Westmarland) to introduce a subject-centric dignity harm axis that is orthogonal to the viewer-centric epistemic harm axis the technical literature optimizes for. The landscape analysis of 39 highly-cited papers (2020–2025) serves as empirical grounding, showing that the field has produced detection, provenance, and watermarking tools without a single technical implementation targeting AIG-NCII specifically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that authenticity labels can exacerbate rather than mitigate subject-centric harms (e.g., confirming a victim's exposure, enabling abusers to sort content, or creating perverse incentives for transparent sharing)—challenges a core design assumption across the deepfake defense ecosystem and has direct implications for platform policy (EU AI Act, Meta, TikTok labeling rules). Key open questions include how to design detection that triggers suppression rather than public labeling, how to measure harm reduction rather than detection accuracy, and whether adversarial defenses against inpainting can meaningfully raise the cost of casual abuse. The paper explicitly cautions that research in this domain requires safety guardrails for researchers and co-design with sexual violence prevention experts, acknowledging that the field is not yet equipped to engage with this problem responsibly.
