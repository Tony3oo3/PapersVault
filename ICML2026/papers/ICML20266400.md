## Problem

Frontier AI governance is largely structured around model-level governance, which assumes a model's capability profile is primarily a function of the compute and data used during training. This paper argues that as capability progress is increasingly driven by 'non-model gains'—improvements independent of the base model—pre-deployment evaluation and mitigation strategies become structurally insufficient, yet no formal taxonomy or corresponding governance framework exists to address this shift.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the analytical input is the current landscape of frontier AI governance frameworks (responsible scaling policies, regulatory regimes using training-compute thresholds, evaluation protocols) combined with empirical evidence of capability gains from inference scaling, scaffolding, and restricted-asset integration. The output is a structured taxonomy of six non-model gain vectors, a per-vector analysis of how each undermines model-level governance, and a portfolio of complementary governance mechanisms spanning system, entity, agent, cloud, and societal resilience layers, summarized in a capability-paradigm-by-governance-strategy mapping table.

## Method

The paper formalizes 'non-model gains' into a taxonomy of three current vectors—inference gain (test-time compute scaling, exemplified by reasoning models like o1 and DeepSeek-V3.2), systems gain (post-training enhancements such as multi-agent scaffolds, tool use, and fine-tuning, exemplified by Big Sleep's zero-day discovery), and asset gain (access to restricted assets like classified data, government expertise, or specialized hardware)—plus three future vectors: embodiment (physical actuators/sensors), continual learning (parameter evolution over time), and diffusion effects (aggregate deployment interactions). For each vector, it identifies the specific governance failure mode: elicitation failure (developers cannot anticipate downstream capability), mitigation failure (identified capabilities cannot be adequately mitigated), or increased overhang cost (the expected harm of missing a capability rises). It then proposes a layered governance architecture in which enhanced model-level governance (evolutive elicitation standards informed by threat modelling, developer partnerships, continuous post-deployment monitoring, and capability-overhang forecasting) is complemented by four beyond-model layers: system governance (risk management by downstream system providers), entity governance (organizational risk culture, registration, and accountability structures), agent governance (delegation parameters, attribution, and inter-agent interaction protocols), and cloud governance (KYC, content-based monitoring, and computational-pattern anomaly detection), with societal resilience as a final backstop.

## Results

This is a position paper without empirical experiments or quantitative benchmarks. Its primary contribution is a formal taxonomy of non-model capability gains (inference gain, systems gain, asset gain) plus three future vectors (embodiment, continual learning, diffusion), and a governance framework proposing four complementary layers beyond model-level governance (system, entity, agent, cloud) alongside societal resilience. The paper cites illustrative evidence such as Qwen3-4B matching o3-mini via recursive self-aggregation, DeepSeek-V3.2 outperforming Gemini 3 with 1.5–2.5× more tokens, and Davidson et al. (2023) showing specialised data can yield gains equivalent to 1000× pre-training compute scaling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not benchmark a method against empirical baselines. Instead, it conceptually contrasts the model-level governance paradigm (pre-deployment elicitation and mitigation of frontier models) against a broader governance portfolio. The paper argues that model-level governance remains relevant but that its relative returns diminish as non-model gains grow: inference scaling shrinks the frontier/sub-frontier gap, system scaffolds (e.g., Big Sleep, Claude Code) are low-cost and freely proliferating, and asset gains from restricted data are hard to elicit pre-deployment. The authors position their proposed multi-layer framework (system, entity, agent, cloud governance + societal resilience) as a complement rather than a replacement, acknowledging practical, legal, and technical barriers to each alternative while arguing that preparation should precede demonstrable model-level governance failure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes and reframes existing AI governance discourse rather than building on a single prior method. It draws on the scaling-laws literature (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) and the model-level governance paradigm (METR RSP, Anthropic RSP, EU AI Act compute thresholds) as its baseline, then departs from it by formalizing 'non-model gains'—a taxonomy of inference, systems, and asset gains—that had been discussed anecdotally but not systematically mapped to governance failure modes. The governance alternatives it proposes (entity, agent, cloud governance) repurpose concepts from organizational risk management (COSO, ISO/IEC 42001), cloud security (KYC, confidential computing), and multi-agent systems research, but their integration into a unified AI safety framework is the paper's novel contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the dominant assumption that pre-deployment model evaluation is the primary (or sufficient) node for frontier AI risk management, arguing that the relative returns to model-level intervention will diminish as inference scaling, scaffolding, and asset access decouple capability from base-model quality. Key open questions include: whether cloud-level monitoring is legally and technically feasible at scale, how to operationalize 'evolutive elicitation standards' without becoming unbounded, and whether entity governance will devolve into formalistic compliance. For policymakers and safety researchers, the taxonomy provides a concrete vocabulary for identifying which governance layer addresses which capability vector, though the paper offers no empirical validation of its claims.
