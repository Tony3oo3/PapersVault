## Problem

GRPO's group-relative objective can unintentionally favor longer rollouts, since extended deliberation creates more opportunities to realize relative gains within a sampled group, leading to overthinking and inefficient reasoning. Controlling this is difficult because global length penalties are hard to calibrate when trajectory length correlates with problem difficulty, and fine-grained step-level supervision for when to continue or stop is typically unavailable beyond final-answer correctness.

## Input / Output

The policy takes a text question (multiple-choice options for ARC/CommonsenseQA, or a free-form math word problem for GSM8K/DeepMath) and generates a sentence-level reasoning trajectory followed by a final answer (a letter for MC tasks, a bare number for math). The preference model consumes a question jointly with two reasoning trajectories (or two consecutive prefixes of the same trajectory) via a structured comparison template, and outputs a scalar preference score in [0,1]. During Phase II, G=8 rollouts are sampled per question, and the preference model is applied to every consecutive prefix pair (s_{1:t-1} vs. s_{1:t}) to produce dense step-wise rewards.

## Method

WS-GRPO is a two-stage method that converts sparse terminal correctness into dense, correctness-aware prefix-level guidance. In Phase I, a preference model (FLAN-T5 encoder + lightweight MLP) is trained with a symmetric Bradley-Terry objective on pairs of correct vs. incorrect full trajectories, using only binary final-answer labels as weak supervision. In Phase II, the frozen preference model is repurposed to compare consecutive prefixes within each rollout, and the resulting preference margins are aggregated into a trajectory-level preference reward R_i^pref, which is combined with the binary outcome reward as R_i^WS = λR_i^pref + R_i^final. This combined reward feeds into standard GRPO group-relative advantage computation, so the policy receives dense continue/stop signals that downweight low-utility continuation while preserving necessary deliberation. The key insight is that a model trained to discriminate overall trajectory quality can be projected onto incremental progress by comparing successive partial trajectories, eliminating the need for explicit process annotations. The paper further provides theoretical guarantees including preference-model consistency, a preference-error-controlled robustness bound, and a high-probability generalization bound.

## Results

On four reasoning benchmarks (ARC, CommonsenseQA, DeepMath, GSM8K) with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Qwen3-4B-Instruct, WS-GRPO achieves 50–93% reductions in response length and 47–83% reductions in average reasoning steps relative to the best baseline, at a modest accuracy cost of 1.6–8.8% Pass@1. The largest efficiency gains appear on ARC (92.9% length reduction, 82.6% step reduction) and GSM8K (52.5% length reduction, 61.5% step reduction). On DeepMath with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, WS-GRPO actually increases length (↑98.5%) because the GRPO baseline already produces near-minimal 1-step trajectories, illustrating the method's boundary condition. Step-efficiency (Pass@1/Steps) is 1.9–5.0× higher than PACR across all four datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), WS-GRPO trades 1.6–7.5% Pass@1 accuracy for 50–93% reductions in response length and 47–83% fewer reasoning steps on Qwen models; the largest accuracy drop is on GSM8K (85.1% vs. 92.4%, Δ=−7.5%) while the largest efficiency gain is on ARC (16.0 vs. 309.3 tokens, ↓92.9%). Against DRGRPO (Liu et al., 2025c), the pattern is similar: WS-GRPO is within 2.1–4.4% accuracy on ARC and CommonsenseQA while cutting steps by 47–83%, and on GSM8K it reduces length from 511.05 to 79.26 tokens (↓84.5% vs. DRGRPO). Against PACR (Yoon et al., 2025), WS-GRPO achieves 1.9–5.0× higher step-efficiency (Pass@1/Steps: 0.443 vs. 0.096 on ARC, 0.445 vs. 0.136 on GSM8K) while PACR provides no consistent length or step reduction across any dataset. On Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct DeepMath, WS-GRPO actually outperforms both baselines in accuracy (46.4% vs. 45.5% GRPO, 46.0% DRGRPO) while maintaining the same 1-step length, showing the method can be accuracy-neutral or positive when baselines are not already over-generating.

## Relation to Prior Work

WS-GRPO extends GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) by injecting dense prefix-level pseudo-rewards derived from a weakly supervised preference model, rather than relying solely on sparse terminal correctness. It departs from global length-penalty approaches (Kikuchi et al., 2016; Dr.GRPO) and from process reward models that require step-level annotations (Lightman et al., 2024; Uesato et al., 2022) by repurposing a trajectory-level Bradley-Terry preference model to score consecutive prefix pairs, thereby converting outcome-only supervision into continue/stop guidance. The method also connects to the broader weak-supervision literature (Zhou, 2018; Wu et al., 2022) and efficient-reasoning reward-shaping work (PACR, LASER, ThinkPrune), but distinguishes itself by deriving correctness-aware step signals without hand-designed length targets or calibrated budgets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WS-GRPO offers a practical, annotation-free mechanism to curb the overthinking tendency of GRPO-trained reasoners, with 50–90% rollout-length reductions on structured tasks while retaining competitive accuracy. Its main limitation is scope: when baselines already produce near-minimal trajectories (e.g., DeepMath with Qwen2.5-7B at ~1 step), the preference signal has little redundancy to compress and can even increase length. Open questions include whether the in-domain preference model generalizes across domains without retraining both phases, whether the approach extends to multi-turn or tool-use reasoning, and whether the modest accuracy drops on competition-level math (up to 9%) can be closed with better preference-model architectures or larger training sets.
