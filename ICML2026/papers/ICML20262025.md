## Problem

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) fail on oscillatory, multiscale, stiff, or long-time PDEs due to spectral bias (networks learn low frequencies first) and noisy collocation-based loss estimation whose variance grows with dimension. No existing method simultaneously provides an explicit spectral representation that captures high-frequency structure and an analytic, sampling-free evaluation of the physics-based objective, leaving high-dimensional PDE solving vulnerable to both optimization pathologies and the curse of dimensionality.

## Input / Output

The method takes a PDE defined on a hyperrectangular domain Ω = Ω₁×⋯×Ω_d (with time treated as an additional coordinate), along with boundary/initial conditions, and produces the continuous solution u(x) parameterized by LoRFS coefficients. Evaluation is performed on 10,000 fixed points using relative L¹ (rMAE) and relative L² (rRMSE) metrics. Non-periodic boundaries are handled by embedding the problem in a larger virtual torus or multiplying by a smooth window function, and non-spectral PDE terms (e.g., nonlinear reaction terms) are projected onto auxiliary LoRFS models before substitution into the governing equation.

## Method

LoRFS parameterizes the solution as a rank-s sum of separable 1D complex Fourier series: g_θ(x) = Σ_r ∏_j f_rj(x_j), where each f_rj is a truncated Fourier series of degree K with conjugate-symmetric coefficients ensuring real-valued output. The key mechanism is that separability reduces d-dimensional integrals to products of 1D integrals, enabling closed-form analytic evaluation of L² residual norms and variational losses (and their gradients) without any collocation sampling, thereby eliminating quadrature error and gradient variance. The parameter count scales linearly with dimension as s·d·(2K+1) rather than exponentially as (2K+1)^d for tensor-product spectral methods. Training uses L-BFGS on the exact analytic objective, and theoretical results establish that for Sobolev H^m, mixed-smoothness H^m_mix, and analytic (Gevrey) function classes, the approximation error converges at a rate in rank s that is independent of dimension d, with frequency K scaling as O(ε^{-1/m}) or O(log 1/ε) for analytic functions.

## Results

On the three standard PINN failure-mode benchmarks, LoRFS achieves the lowest rMAE and rRMSE across all settings: 8.2×10⁻⁶ / 9×10⁻⁶ on convection, 2×10⁻⁴ / 6×10⁻⁴ on reaction, and 3.3×10⁻³ / 3.5×10⁻³ on wave. Compared to the strongest baselines (PINN FP64 and PINNMamba), LoRFS improves by up to ~800× on convection, ~35–46× on reaction, and ~2.3–2.4× on wave. In high-dimensional extensions (d=9), LoRFS maintains rRMSE of 6.2×10⁻³ (convection), 8.2×10⁻³ (reaction), and 5.9×10⁻⁵ (wave), while all baselines fail to converge (rRMSE ≥ 0.68). On the 4th-order biharmonic equation (d=10), LoRFS achieves rRMSE of 5.15×10⁻⁴, outperforming the best baseline (Forward Laplacian, 8×10⁻³) by ~15×.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LoRFS outperforms all 10 baselines across every benchmark. Against the two strongest recent PINN variants, PINN FP64 (Xu et al., 2025b) and PINNMamba (Xu et al., 2025a), LoRFS achieves ~800× lower rRMSE on convection (9×10⁻⁶ vs. 7.2×10⁻³), ~35× lower on reaction (6×10⁻⁴ vs. 2.13×10⁻²), and ~2.3× lower on wave (3.5×10⁻³ vs. 8.1×10⁻³). Earlier baselines—PINN, QRes, KAN, PirateNet, RoPINN, and PINNsFormer—perform substantially worse, with rRMSE values ranging from 0.03 to 0.98, indicating they fail to capture the oscillatory or stiff dynamics. On the high-order biharmonic equation (Table 3), LoRFS (rRMSE 5.15×10⁻⁴) beats Forward Laplacian (8×10⁻³) by ~15× and the stochastic methods STDE (2.36×10⁻²) and HTE (2.02×10⁻²) by ~40×, confirming that analytic differentiation avoids the variance introduced by Monte Carlo gradient estimation. In high-dimensional settings (d=9), all baselines degrade to rRMSE ≥ 0.68 while LoRFS stays below 0.01, a gap that widens with dimension.

## Relation to Prior Work

LoRFS inherits the PINN training paradigm (minimizing a physics-based loss via gradient-based optimization) from Raissi et al. (2019) but replaces the neural network representation with a low-rank sum of separable 1D Fourier series, drawing on classical spectral methods and tensor decomposition ideas (Oseledets, 2011; Dektor et al., 2021). The key conceptual departure is twofold: (i) frequencies are made explicit in the parameterization rather than emerging implicitly through training (addressing spectral bias), and (ii) the separable Fourier structure enables closed-form evaluation of integral losses and their gradients, eliminating stochastic collocation entirely. Unlike prior separable neural methods (e.g., Cho et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b) that target dimensional scalability but retain collocation-based training, LoRFS removes the sampling-based loss estimation that is a primary source of high-dimensional optimization failure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean diagnosis of why PINNs fail on oscillatory/stiff/high-dimensional PDEs (spectral bias compounded by collocation noise) and shows that addressing both simultaneously via an explicit spectral representation and analytic loss evaluation resolves the failure modes with dramatic margin. The theoretical results establishing dimension-independent convergence rates for Sobolev, mixed-Sobolev, and analytic function classes give rigorous justification for the method's high-dimensional scaling. Key open questions include whether LoRFS remains efficient for genuinely turbulent or chaotic dynamics where energy is distributed across many frequencies, how it handles non-rectangular domains, and whether the required rank s grows controllably for long-time integration of stiff systems.
