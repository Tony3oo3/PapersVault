## Problem

Despite ICL's surprising effectiveness, the conditions under which it emerges remain poorly understood, particularly how the statistical properties of the pretraining distribution—tail behavior, coverage, and temporal dependence—govern ICL performance at test time. Existing theoretical frameworks are restricted to light-tailed priors, i.i.d. or Markovian sequences, and do not characterize how distributional properties of pretraining data translate into ICL behavior under distribution shift. This paper provides a principled framework connecting these properties to ICL, revealing a fundamental design trade-off between task identification and generalization.

## Input / Output

The transformer receives a sequence of in-context examples (input-output pairs for regression, or time-series observations for stochastic processes) and predicts the next value, with no explicit task label provided during training or inference. The key experimental variable is the shape of the pretraining task distribution π, parameterized by Student-t degrees of freedom ν ∈ {3, 5, 10, ∞} or generalized normal shape β ∈ {1, 1.5, 2, 2.5}, while test tasks are drawn from a shifted Gaussian N(Δ, I_d) to probe distribution-shift robustness. Tasks span 8-dimensional linear regression (context 64), Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes (context 32), and stochastic Volterra equations with long-range dependencies (context 32).

## Method

The paper develops a theoretical framework decomposing ICL error into generalization error and task selection error, handling both heavy-tailed priors and dependent sequences. For generalization (Thm. 1), they extend heavy-tailed concentration inequalities to non-i.i.d. dependent data via Wasserstein-based coupling, showing the error depends on the maximal moment q of the prior π and dependence coefficients A_T, B_T that capture temporal structure. For task selection (Thm. 2), they prove Bayesian posterior concentration in Rényi divergence at rate O(log(1/π(θ*))/T), showing heavier tails (larger π(θ*) for distant tasks) accelerate learning of new tasks. The central finding is a trade-off: heavier-tailed priors improve task identification under distribution shift but require more pretraining tasks for reliable generalization, a prediction validated empirically with GPT-2-style transformers across three numerical task families.

## Results

The paper establishes two main theoretical guarantees: (i) a generalization bound (Thm. 1) showing ICL error scales as O(L_T√T/√N · (1 + A_T√T + B_T T)) with two regimes separated by the threshold δ = N·e^{-q}, where q is the maximal moment of the prior; and (ii) a task-selection bound (Thm. 2) showing posterior concentration at rate O(log(1/π(θ*)) / T). Empirically, for linear regression with unbounded tasks (N=∞), Student-t with ν=3 (heaviest tail) outperforms the Gaussian (ν=∞) under large distribution shifts, but for N=100 the performance gap closes. For stochastic Volterra equations, the MSE at zero shift is multiplied by 3× when N decreases from 5000 to 500 for kernel exponent α=1.0 (strong dependence), while it barely changes for α=2.0 (weak dependence), confirming the theory's prediction that stronger temporal dependence amplifies the generalization penalty.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against a single competing method but rather validates theoretical predictions by varying the pretraining distribution (Student-t with ν∈{3,5,10,∞} and Generalized Normal with β∈{1,1.5,2,2.5}) and the number of pretraining tasks N. Against simple baselines (Ridge regression for linear regression; last-value prediction and ARMA(5) for OU processes), the trained transformer with any pretraining distribution significantly outperforms these baselines. The key empirical comparison is between heavy-tailed and light-tailed priors: under large distribution shifts with N=∞, Student-t ν=3 consistently achieves lower MSE than ν=∞ (Gaussian) across all three task families (linear regression, OU, Volterra). However, as N decreases (e.g., N=100), the heavy-tailed priors lose their advantage and light-tailed priors match or exceed them, confirming the predicted generalization trade-off. For the Volterra experiments, processes with stronger temporal dependence (α=1.0) show a 3× MSE degradation when N drops from 5000 to 500, while weakly dependent processes (α=2.0) show negligible change, validating the dependence-aware generalization bound.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Bayesian ICL framework (Xie et al. 2021; Lin & Lee 2024; Zhang et al. 2025b) by removing the light-tailed prior assumption and accommodating non-i.i.d., non-Markovian data sequences. It draws on heavy-tailed concentration inequalities (Li & Liu 2024a) and coupling-based concentration for dependent processes (Chazottes et al. 2007; Kontorovich & Ramanan 2008), but combines them in a way that neither line previously addressed: nonlinear functions of dependent heavy-tailed sequences. Empirically, it provides a theoretical explanation for the trade-off observed by Chan et al. (2022) and Singh et al. (2023), who showed that heavier-tailed pretraining distributions improve ICL only up to a point.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled characterization of how specific statistical properties of the pretraining distribution (tail heaviness, temporal dependence) quantitatively govern ICL performance, offering concrete design guidelines for pretraining data curation. The fundamental trade-off—heavy tails aid task retrieval under distribution shift but degrade generalization in low-data regimes—gives practitioners a theoretical basis for balancing these objectives. Key limitations include the restriction to controlled numerical tasks (linear regression, OU processes, Volterra equations) rather than natural language, a sub-Gaussian assumption on per-sample losses, and the Bayesian-optimality gap between theory and finite-capacity transformers. Extending these results to text pretraining and directly measuring posterior concentration in trained LLMs remain important open directions.
