## Problem

AlphaZero and MuZero achieve superhuman performance by maximizing expected return, but this risk-neutral criterion masks rare catastrophic outcomes that are unacceptable in safety-critical settings. Existing risk-sensitive RL methods are either model-free and sample-inefficient, or model-based but optimize only future risk (ignoring accumulated returns), leading to biased policies that are locally safe yet globally suboptimal. No MuZero-family method existed for principled risk-sensitive planning with convergence guarantees.

## Input / Output

RiskZero consumes environment states in three formats—normalized Cartesian coordinates for grid worlds, 7×7×9-channel images for Space Invaders, and graph adjacency/feature matrices for combinatorial problems—along with the full action history h_t. At each step it outputs a selected action (via Gumbel Top-K sampling plus sequential halving over m candidate actions) and an improved policy π′ used as a distillation target. Internally, it produces D trajectory-return samples per search path by index-wise summation of historical-return, reward, and leaf-value quantile samples, which are then sorted and averaged under the distortion function to yield a scalar risk-sensitive action value.

## Method

RiskZero extends MuZero by replacing the scalar reward and value heads with distributional (quantile-regression) counterparts and adding a historical-return model η_φ that predicts the accumulated return distribution along the current history. During MCTS, each simulation draws D samples from η_φ at the root, collects D reward samples along the path, and D value samples at the leaf, then computes trajectory-level risk ψ via inverse-transform sampling and distortion (e.g., α-CVaR) over the combined samples. The key theoretical insight is that for coherent risk measures, optimizing the *expected* risk (a surrogate over trajectory completions) is equivalent to optimizing true trajectory risk for history-stationary policies, so Gumbel-based policy improvement—which guarantees expected-risk improvement per iteration—provably converges to an optimal risk-sensitive policy. Training proceeds by end-to-end unrolling the learned dynamics for K steps with a joint loss combining cross-entropy policy distillation and Quantile Huber losses on value, reward, and historical-return distributions, decoupling the historical-return learning from the value network to avoid the instability that plagues TQL.

## Results

On stochastic combinatorial optimization (0.25-CVaR), RiskZero (MuZero variant) achieves 56.48–57.16 on SBM (60-30) and 24.94–18.74 on SMIS (60), substantially outperforming Sampled TQL (38.78–47.75 and 10.58–14.29 respectively) while QR-DQN collapses to 0.0 across all settings. On the Mini Grid task, RiskZero matches TQL's optimal 0.25-CVaR policy with 3× fewer environment interactions. On Space Invaders, RiskZero converges to the optimal risk-sensitive policy with the fewest samples, while Sampled TQL requires ~3× more interactions to reach the same policy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RiskZero is compared against two main baselines: QR-DQN (Dabney et al., 2018b), which naively optimizes future risk via quantile regression, and Sampled TQL (a variant of Zhou et al., 2023's Trajectory Q-Learning augmented with the paper's trajectory decomposition). QR-DQN collapses entirely on all combinatorial tasks (0.25-CVaR return of 0.0), converging to a trivial skip-all policy, and also converges to suboptimal policies on Mini Grid and Space Invaders. Sampled TQL learns risk-sensitive policies but degrades significantly on larger graph instances: on SBM (60-30) it achieves 38.78–47.75 versus RiskZero's 56.48–57.16 (a ~20% gap), and on SMIS (60) it reaches only 10.58–14.29 versus RiskZero's 14.92–24.94 (a ~40–130% gap). On Mini Grid, RiskZero matches TQL's final 0.25-CVaR performance but converges 3× faster; on Space Invaders, Sampled TQL requires ~3× more samples to reach the same optimal policy. RiskZero's advantage grows with problem size, attributed to its look-ahead search pruning the combinatorial space more effectively than TQL's value-iteration approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

RiskZero extends the MuZero/AlphaZero planning framework to risk-sensitive decision-making by replacing scalar reward and value models with distributional counterparts (quantile regression) and introducing a historical return model for trajectory-level risk estimation. It builds directly on Trajectory Q-Learning (TQL) for unbiased trajectory-level risk optimization and on Gumbel MuZero for provable policy improvement, but departs from both by combining them: TQL's trajectory decomposition is embedded within a learned abstract MDP searched via Gumbel-based MCTS, rather than relying on tabular or model-free DP updates. The theoretical contribution—that coherent risk measures admit deterministic optimal policies and that expected-risk optimization is a valid surrogate for true-risk optimization under stationarity—bridges the gap between risk-sensitive RL theory and the expectation-based policy improvement guarantees inherited from Gumbel planning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to bring principled, convergent risk-sensitive planning to the MuZero family, opening a path toward safer model-based RL in settings where a single catastrophic failure is unacceptable (autonomous driving, robotics, finance). The 3× sample-efficiency gain over TQL and the ability to handle arbitrary coherent risk measures (CVaR, Wang, POW) make it a practical tool, but the current scope is limited to deterministic dynamics, discrete actions, and stationary policies in controlled environments. Key open questions include extending to stochastic dynamics (where the expected-risk/true-risk equivalence breaks down), continuous action spaces, and safety-critical real-world domains; the authors also note that modeling epistemic uncertainty (model misspecification) remains orthogonal and unaddressed.
