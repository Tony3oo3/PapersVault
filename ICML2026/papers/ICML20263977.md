## Problem

Multi-task RL methods train generalist policies but rarely provide formal guarantees on how those policies will perform on tasks not seen during training, which is essential for safety-critical deployment. Existing distribution-level generalisation bounds (e.g., Kostas et al., 2021) assume per-task performance is available in closed form, ignoring the estimation error introduced when performance must be inferred from finitely many rollouts. This paper addresses the joint uncertainty of finite task sampling and finite rollout estimation to produce sound, interpretable safety certificates for any learned multi-task policy.

## Input / Output

The method is algorithm-agnostic: it takes a fixed policy π, n i.i.d. tasks sampled from an arbitrary unknown distribution D, and m_i i.i.d. rollout trajectories per task, with no assumptions on MDP structure or state-space dimensionality. For infinite-horizon metrics, a monotonicity condition on the trajectory metric is required (satisfied by discounted return with nonnegative rewards or sparse success indicators). The output is a safety certificate (B, ε, δ): a lower bound 1−ε on the probability that π achieves performance at least B on a fresh task, valid with confidence 1−δ. The certified bound is a step function of the threshold B, and the entire certification step runs in milliseconds via bisection.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage composition that propagates rollout uncertainty through task-level generalisation. First, per-task lower confidence bounds are constructed from finite rollouts using standard concentration inequalities (Clopper-Pearson for binary metrics; Hoeffding, DKW, or Empirical Bernstein for bounded real-valued metrics), each valid with probability at least 1−β. Second, a new generalisation bound (Theorem 5.1) aggregates these probabilistic per-task bounds into a single distribution-level certificate via the scenario approach, introducing an auxiliary parameter K that relaxes the requirement that all per-task bounds hold simultaneously. The key distinction from prior work is that per-task performance is treated as a random lower bound rather than a known quantity, and the proof uniformises over K via a union bound to permit data-dependent selection. The resulting certificate depends only on the number of sampled tasks and rollouts, not on state or action space dimensionality, making it applicable to high-dimensional continuous-control domains.

## Results

Across all four environments (BridgeWorld, Cheetah, Walker, Zones), the certified safety bounds are sound (never exceeding empirical safety) and closely track the empirical safety curves estimated from 10^7 trajectories per environment, even when computed from as few as n=50 tasks and m=100 rollouts per task. In the high-safety regime most relevant for certification, the gap between certified and empirical safety is minimal. Certificate computation is extremely lightweight, taking only 5–16 ms per performance threshold B (Table 2), with the modest increase at larger n attributable to the search over admissible K values.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its rollout-based certification against the PAC-IMDP approach of Schnitzer et al. (2025), which constructs interval MDPs to over-approximate environmental uncertainty and derives guarantees from the model. On the BridgeWorld grid-world (Figure 5), the proposed method produces consistently tighter certified safety bounds than PAC-IMDP for the same number of sampled tasks and rollouts. The advantage grows with domain complexity: on a larger grid with substantially more states and transitions, PAC-IMDP bounds become substantially more conservative because the confidence budget must be distributed over an increasingly large number of transitions, whereas the proposed certificates remain similarly close to the empirical safety curve as in the smaller domain. The proposed method also applies to continuous-state, high-dimensional domains (Cheetah, Walker, Zones) where PAC-IMDP is inapplicable due to its restriction to finite-state systems with known transition structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the scenario-based generalisation framework of Schnitzer et al. (2025) by replacing their model-based interval MDP over-approximations with rollout-based lower confidence bounds, thereby removing the finite-state structure requirement and making certification applicable to arbitrary MDPs with continuous state spaces. It also generalises the distribution-level guarantees of Kostas et al. (2021), which assumed per-task performances were known in closed form, by explicitly propagating finite-rollout estimation uncertainty through the task-level generalisation step. The per-task bounding layer draws on standard concentration inequalities (Clopper-Pearson, Hoeffding, DKW, empirical Bernstein), while the cross-task aggregation uses a novel composition of probabilistic per-task certificates with order-statistic generalisation, uniformised over an auxiliary parameter K via a union bound.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first distribution-free, model-free safety certificate for multi-task RL that works directly from rollouts on arbitrary MDPs, filling a gap between single-task PAC guarantees and model-based finite-state certificates. The sample complexity is independent of state/action dimensionality, making it applicable to high-dimensional continuous control, and the certification step runs in milliseconds, enabling practical deployment in safety-critical settings like autonomous driving or robotic manipulation. Key open questions include extending the guarantee to Sim-to-Real transfer where the deployment task may not be drawn from the same distribution as the certification tasks, and whether the monotonicity assumption (minimal for infinite-horizon metrics but still a constraint) can be relaxed further. The framework is algorithm-agnostic, so it can be applied as a post-training audit to any existing MTRL or meta-RL pipeline.
