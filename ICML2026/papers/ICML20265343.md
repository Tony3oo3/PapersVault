## Problem

Public health reasoning—population-level inference grounded in epidemiological evidence, policy context, and safety constraints—remains substantially underrepresented in LLM development and evaluation. Existing medical benchmarks capture clinical knowledge retrieval but fail to assess the distinct demands of public health, and no large-scale, multilingual dataset with a domain-aligned evaluation framework has been available to train and benchmark models on this task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265343/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The construction pipeline ingests 35,500+ authoritative public health PDFs (primarily WHO IRIS), converts them to structured Markdown, segments them into evidence chunks, and produces 280,210 QA instances (49.42% open-ended QA, 50.58% single-choice) each annotated with a chain-of-thought trace, domain label, language tag, and difficulty level (A/B/C) across 15 domains and 17 languages. The evaluator takes a model's response together with the question and reference answer, and outputs six orthogonal scores (Accuracy, Reasoning, Completeness, Consensus Alignment, Terminology Norms, Insightfulness) on a 0–10 scale. The corpus is split into 247,599 training, 27,511 test, and 5,100 evaluator-calibration instances, all retaining CoT supervision.

## Method

The core pipeline is an LLM-assisted, evidence-grounded synthesis engine that generates QA pairs and complex CoT traces from parsed source documents, followed by a format-aware quality gate that scores each candidate on Question Quality, Answer Quality, Text Relevance, and Consistency using expert-voted weights (SC: 0.45/0.25/0.20/0.10; QA: 0.25/0.35/0.25/0.15) with a retention threshold of S ≥ 4.5 and zero-tolerance rules for domain irrelevance or language inconsistency. For evaluation, a dedicated scorer (Public-Evaluator) is trained via LoRA fine-tuning on qwen3-8b using a consensus-filtered annotation set: responses from diverse LLMs (qwen3-Max, deepseek-V3.2) are cross-scored, disagreements are arbitrated by GPT-5, and residual conflicts go to human review, encoding six-dimensional public health scoring criteria into model parameters. The resulting Public-Model is obtained by LoRA SFT on the full 247,599-instance training set, and data integrity is verified through continuation-based 5-gram and 10-gram leakage detection. A key design choice is that LLMs perform structured extraction and format transformation over authoritative source text rather than free generation, with exogenous expert judgment (7 senior global health experts, including WHO officials) anchoring the quality weights and evaluation rubric.

## Results

On the GlobalHealthAtlas benchmark (27,511 held-out test instances scored on a 0–10 scale across six dimensions), GLM-4.7 leads with an overall score of 7.326, followed by Kimi-k2-thinking (6.930) and Claude-Sonnet-4.5-thinking (6.534). The paper's Public-Model (LoRA fine-tuned qwen3-8b) achieves 6.619, a +0.685 gain over the vanilla qwen3-8b baseline (5.934), placing it third overall and surpassing several larger open- and closed-source models. The domain-aligned Public-Evaluator achieves the lowest MAE (1.4259) and highest ICC (0.9735) against 100 human-annotated scores, outperforming all generic LLM judges and specialized medical evaluators.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Qwen3 family (Yang et al., 2025), Public-Model (6.619) outperforms the same-size qwen3-8b base (5.934) by +0.685 and also surpasses the larger qwen3-32b (5.259) and qwq-32b (6.173), demonstrating that domain-specific SFT on GlobalHealthAtlas effectively bridges the model-scale gap. Against DeepSeek models, Public-Model edges out deepseek-r1 (6.335) by +0.284 and deepseek-v3.2 (6.365) by +0.254 on the overall score, though both DeepSeek variants score higher on Difficulty A (7.007 and 6.897 vs. 7.595 for Public-Model on A, where Public-Model actually leads). Against Llama 3 (Grattafiori et al., 2024), Public-Model substantially outperforms Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (5.464) by +1.155 and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (5.276) by +1.343. On the evaluator side, Public-Evaluator (MAE 1.4259, ICC 0.9735) decisively outperforms both Clinical-Judge (Croxford et al., 2025; MAE 2.0122, ICC 0.3591) and Expert-Guided Evaluator (Zhou et al., 2026; MAE 2.1633, ICC 0.8541) in agreement with human experts, while also showing superior stability (IdenticalRate 0.5533 vs. 0.4867 and 0.4233). The only model that surpasses Public-Model overall is GLM-4.7 (7.326) and Kimi-k2-thinking (6.930), both of which are large reasoning-oriented closed-source models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of LLM-as-a-judge evaluation (G-Eval, Prometheus) and domain-specific medical datasets (MedQA, MedMCQA), but deliberately shifts the focus from individual-patient clinical reasoning to population-level public health inference. Prior public health QA resources (publichealth-qa, Instruction-public-health-dataset, AfriMed-QA) are small-scale (hundreds to ~15K instances), single-language, and lack dedicated evaluation frameworks; GlobalHealthAtlas extends this to 280K instances across 17 languages and 15 domains, grounded in WHO IRIS documents. The 'AI4S data construction paradigm' repurposes the LLM-driven generation-and-filtering pipeline common in synthetic data work, but adds evidence-grounding checks, format-aware quality gates with expert-voted weights, and a consensus-based multi-model arbitration scheme (Algorithm 1) that is more structured than typical LLM-as-a-judge setups.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset and six-dimension evaluator fill a concrete gap: public health reasoning (population-level inference, policy context, intervention effectiveness) has been largely absent from LLM benchmarks, and the paper demonstrates that even 235B clinical models score below 6/10 on this task, validating the need for dedicated resources. Key limitations include a WHO IRIS–centric source bias toward international policy frameworks (underrepresenting Global South nuances), imbalanced language coverage, and publication bias toward well-funded diseases. Open questions include whether the evaluator generalizes to non-WHO guidelines, whether the AI4S pipeline transfers cleanly to other scientific domains, and whether the 60% data-scaling plateau reflects a fundamental ceiling or an artifact of the current source corpus.
