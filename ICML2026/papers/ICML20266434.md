## Problem

All current deepfake detection benchmarks rely on a handful of old, low-resolution 'real' image datasets (ImageNet 2009, MS-COCO 2014, etc.) that predate modern computational photography. Since over 90% of photos are now produced by smartphones that use neural-network-based multi-sensor fusion and enhancement to compute images rather than simply capture them, the technical definition of 'real' (not algorithmically generated) no longer holds, and detectors trained on outdated 'real' data fail to generalize to images from modern phones.

## Input / Output

The evaluation consumes standard RGB images from existing GAN/diffusion benchmarks as well as iPhone-sourced images at two fidelity levels: downsampled/compressed LAION-5B iPhone photos and RAW images captured directly on iPhone 13mini and 15Pro (iOS 18.4.1). Output is binary real/fake classification accuracy, with the critical comparison being detector performance on standard benchmark 'real' data versus modern phone 'real' data, including a controlled experiment where only illumination conditions are varied to isolate the effect of automatic enhancement and fusion algorithms on detection rates.

## Method

The paper's empirical contribution is a systematic audit of the 'real' image sources across 15 major fake-detection benchmarks, revealing that nearly all rely on datasets created 10–15 years ago with low resolution and JPEG-only compression, followed by EXIF metadata analysis on LAION-5B iPhone images showing a mean image age of 7–8 years and pervasive down-sampling. For proof-of-concept experiments, the authors reproduce six SOTA detectors (CNNSpot, DIRE, DCT, Fusing, GRAM, LGRAD) on the standard Zhong et al. benchmark, then re-evaluate them on 1000 LAION-5B iPhone images and a small set of RAW iPhone 13mini/15Pro images. The key finding is that RAW modern phone images cause significant accuracy drops (e.g., DCT falls from 1.00 to 0.50 under poor lighting), demonstrating that automatic enhancement and fusion algorithms produce out-of-distribution samples that break existing detectors. The paper concludes with the position that 'real' must be redefined semantically rather than technically, and that new dynamic datasets capturing modern computational photography are essential for any viable deepfake detection pipeline.

## Results

On the standard generated-image detection benchmark (Zhong et al., 2023), detectors achieve high real-image accuracy (CNNSpot 96%, Fusing 98%, GRAM 67%) but poor fake-image accuracy (CNNSpot 43%, Fusing 41%, DCT 54%), confirming known generalization issues. When evaluated on 1,000 LAION-5B iPhone images, most detectors maintain or even improve real-image accuracy (CNNSpot 99%, Fusing 100%), but DIRE collapses to 0%. Critically, on manually captured RAW iPhone 13mini/15Pro images, DCT accuracy drops from 75% (benchmark) to 50% average, LGRAD from 74% to 27%, and Fusing from 98% to 67%, demonstrating that modern phone imaging produces out-of-distribution samples that break current detectors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five SOTA detectors (CNNSpot, DIRE, DCT, Fusing, GRAM, LGRAD) across three evaluation settings: the standard multi-generator benchmark, LAION-5B iPhone images, and manually captured RAW iPhone images. On the standard benchmark, all detectors show the well-known pattern of high real-accuracy but poor fake-accuracy (e.g., CNNSpot 96% real vs. 43% fake; Fusing 98% real vs. 41% fake). On LAION-5B iPhone images, CNNSpot and Fusing maintain near-perfect real-image accuracy (99–100%), while DIRE catastrophically fails (0% real accuracy), suggesting DIRE's features are incompatible with even compressed phone photos. The most striking result is on RAW iPhone images: DCT drops from 75% to 50% average (and from 100% to 50% specifically when poor lighting triggers Apple's Deep Fusion), LGRAD collapses from 74% to 27%, and Fusing falls from 98% to 67%. The iPhone 15 Pro is particularly problematic, with DCT and Fusing both dropping to 0% real-image accuracy. These results support the paper's central claim that current detectors, trained on old low-resolution datasets, cannot generalize to images produced by modern computational photography pipelines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the standard deepfake detection literature by shifting focus from the 'fake' side (generative models) to the 'real' side (how modern imaging devices actually produce images). It draws on the computational photography literature (Delbracio et al., Morikawa et al.) to argue that smartphone cameras are effectively running generative-like neural networks, thereby blurring the technical boundary that prior detection work (CNNSpot, DIRE, DCT, GRAM, LGRAD) relies on. The paper extends the bias observations of Grommelt et al. (2024) from dataset-level artifacts (JPEG compression, image shape) to a more fundamental claim: the 'real' training distribution itself is obsolete because it predates the era of computational photography.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper raises a foundational question for the deepfake detection community: if the 'real' images in every major benchmark are 7–15 years old, low-resolution, and lack the computational-photography processing that 90%+ of modern photos undergo, then reported detection accuracies may not transfer to real-world deployment. The proof-of-concept experiments (small but suggestive) show that RAW iPhone images and poor-lighting captures cause significant accuracy drops, particularly for frequency-based detectors. Key open questions remain unresolved: how to define 'real' semantically rather than technically, how to curate a dynamic dataset that tracks evolving phone ISP pipelines, and whether detection is even the right paradigm if the real/fake boundary dissolves. The authors explicitly acknowledge they cannot propose a concrete solution, leaving the field with a problem statement rather than a method.
