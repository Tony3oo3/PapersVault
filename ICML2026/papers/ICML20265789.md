## Problem

Masked/absorbing corruption has become the default forward kernel in discrete diffusion language models, consistently outperforming uniform replacement empirically, yet no statistical theory explains when and why this preference holds. Existing generalization bounds for continuous diffusion cannot transfer to the discrete setting, where the forward transition kernel enables qualitatively different information destruction mechanisms. This paper asks: what principles govern the generalization ability of discrete diffusion models, and how does the choice of forward kernel determine sample complexity?

## Input / Output

The framework operates on discrete token sequences of length d over a vocabulary of size K (state space [K]^d), trained via score entropy loss with bounded-range constraints on the score estimator. The primary output is a total variation distance bound between the true data distribution and the distribution generated by the learned backward process, with the bound's dependence on K^d (uniform) versus |X^data| (absorbing) being the central distinction. Synthetic experiments use homogeneous Markov chains over K=100, d=12 with controlled transition sparsity, evaluated by normalized ℓ₁ distance between estimated and true transition matrices.

## Method

The paper establishes a unified error decomposition (early stopping + mixing + estimation) for any discrete diffusion forward process, then shows the estimation error is governed by two quantities: the discrete score range C_t and the support size of the corrupted distribution p_t. For the uniform process, C_t remains bounded away from zero and p_t has full support K^d, yielding TV = Õ(√(dβK^{d+1}/n)); for the absorbing process, C_t decays as α_t/(1−α_t) → 0 and p_t preserves data-support sparsity, yielding TV = Õ(√(2^d d|X^data|/n)). Structure-aware refinements then reveal complementary adaptivity mechanisms: uniform processes benefit from probability uniformity (small ratio r_0 = p_0^max/p_0^min), while absorbing processes exploit sparsity in valid token completions (small K^max, the expected number of valid unmasking transitions). The score estimator is the clipped empirical ratio p̄_t(x)/p̄_t(y), and the analysis tracks how each kernel reshapes the support along the continuous-time corruption trajectory.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 3.8) establishes that the absorbing (masked) forward process achieves a TV estimation bound of Õ(√(2^d · d|X^data|/n)), scaling with the effective data support size, while the uniform forward process achieves Õ(√(dβK^(d+1)/n)), scaling with the full ambient state space K^d. Empirically, on synthetic Markov-chain sequences (K=100, d=12), the absorbing process achieves a normalized ℓ₁ distance of 25.27×10⁻⁴ at transition sparsity S=10 (p₀^min=0.001), compared to 35.38×10⁻⁴ for the uniform process — a ~29% relative improvement. The gap narrows as sparsity increases, with both processes converging to similar performance (~45×10⁻⁴) at S=80.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two forward corruption kernels — the absorbing/masked process (as in DiffusionBERT, MDLM) and the uniform replacement process (as in Austin et al., 2021) — under identical training conditions (transformer score networks, SEDD-style training). The absorbing process clearly outperforms uniform replacement when the data has sparse transition structure: at S=10 with p₀^min=0.001, absorbing achieves 25.27×10⁻⁴ vs. 35.38×10⁻⁴ for uniform (a 10.1×10⁻⁴ absolute gap), and at S=10 with p₀^min=0.004, the gap widens to 13.3×10⁻⁴ (22.66 vs. 35.99). As transition sparsity increases toward S=80, the two processes converge to nearly identical performance (~45×10⁻⁴), consistent with the theory that the absorbing advantage is driven by sparsity in valid token completions. The uniform process benefits from higher p₀^min (more uniform probability mass), aligning with Lemma 4.1, while the absorbing process benefits from lower transition sparsity, aligning with Lemma 4.2. Overall, the absorbing process wins or ties in 4 out of 5 sparsity settings at p₀^min=0.001 and 4 out of 5 at p₀^min=0.004, confirming the theoretical prediction that masking is statistically advantageous for structured discrete data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the continuous-time Markov process framework for discrete diffusion (Campbell et al., 2022) and the score entropy training objective (Lou et al., 2023), but departs sharply from prior theoretical work on discrete diffusion, which has focused on sampling convergence guarantees given a well-trained score network. While recent statistical complexity results (Wakasugi & Suzuki, 2026; Srikanth et al., 2025) provide state-size-independent bounds under strong assumptions (continuous embeddings, bounded-away-from-zero scores), this paper works in the original discrete state space under minimal data assumptions and, crucially, explicitly compares how different forward corruption kernels (absorbing vs. uniform) govern the sample complexity. The paper thus carves out a new subfield: kernel-level statistical theory for discrete diffusion, rather than the algorithm-level or sampling-level analyses that dominate the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first principled statistical explanation for why masked/absorbing diffusion has become the default in state-of-the-art discrete language models, showing that the advantage stems from scaling with effective data support rather than ambient state space cardinality. The structure-aware refinements (uniformity for uniform processes, sparsity for absorbing processes) offer concrete guidance for forward-kernel design in structured discrete domains. Limitations include reliance on synthetic experiments for validation, worst-case bounds that may be loose for specific distributions, and the restriction to conditionally independent token-wise processes. Open questions include extending the comparison to richer kernel families (mixtures, learned kernels) and connecting statistical bounds to end-to-end computational guarantees.
