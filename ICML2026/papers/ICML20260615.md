## Problem

Test-time scaling improves LLM performance by generating multiple candidate solutions, but token-level sampling faces an inherent trade-off where higher temperatures increase diversity while degrading individual sample quality. Fine-grained MoE architectures with hundreds of well-trained experts per layer and multi-expert activation per token offer a rich routing space that has been almost entirely unexplored as an alternative source of diversity for test-time scaling.

## Input / Output

Expert-Sample operates as a plug-and-play inference-time modification that intercepts the expert selection step within each MoE layer, altering which experts process each token's hidden state without any architectural change or retraining. It takes a standard text prompt and produces multiple structurally diverse candidate responses (e.g., 32 parallel samples) that are then scored by external verifiers such as Best-of-N or Weighted Majority Voting to select a final answer. The method composes orthogonally with normal-temperature token sampling, so the token-level decoding pipeline remains unchanged.

## Method

The paper empirically discovers that router scores in fine-grained MoE exhibit a 'certain head' of a few high-confidence experts followed by an 'uncertain tail' of many nearly uniform low-confidence candidates, with the boundary roughly coinciding with half the default top-k. Expert-Sample exploits this structure by deterministically retaining the top k_keep experts to preserve core reasoning stability, then applying Gumbel-Top-K sampling with temperature-scaled router logits to select the remaining k − k_keep experts from a broader candidate pool (ranks k_keep+1 through r) in the uncertain tail. The original gating weights are retrieved and renormalized for the final weighted expert output, respecting the model's learned preferences. The method is training-free, fully vectorized with negligible per-path overhead (<1% throughput change), and decouples stability from diversity by design: the certain head guarantees reliable generation on solvable problems while stochastic tail sampling expands the space of reasoning paths on harder ones.

## Results

On Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct evaluated on GPQA-Diamond with 32 parallel samples, Expert-Sample raises pass@32 from 85.4% to 91.9% and Best-of-N verification accuracy from 59.1% to 62.6%. At pass@64, Expert-Sample yields an average improvement of 4.32% over normal-temperature token sampling across all 12 model-benchmark combinations, and pushes all four models to near-perfect pass@64 on GPQA-Diamond. Across 20 model-dataset verification combinations, Expert-Sample provides average accuracy gains of 4.28% on top of Best-of-N and 3.15% on top of Weighted Majority Voting. Per-path inference overhead remains within ±1% of baseline throughput.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Expert-Sample is compared against three token-level sampling baselines: normal-temperature (T=0.7, top-p=0.8, top-k=20), high-temperature (T=1.3, top-p=0.98), and EDT (Entropy-based Dynamic Temperature) sampling. Against EDT, Expert-Sample consistently outperforms across all 12 model-benchmark pass@n scaling curves, delivering larger and more stable gains because it operates at the expert-routing level rather than the token level, thereby sidestepping the fundamental stability-diversity trade-off that constrains EDT. Against high-temperature token sampling, Expert-Sample achieves substantially higher reasoning-path diversity scores (0.383 vs. 0.288 on Qwen3-30B-A3B; 0.362 vs. 0.299 on Ling-Lite-1.5) while maintaining near-zero stability degradation on the Correct Set (≤0.1% pass-rate drop vs. 2.1–2.6% for high-temperature). On the Hard Set, Expert-Sample enables Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct to solve 30.8% of previously unsolvable problems versus only 7.7% for high-temperature sampling. Against normal-temperature token sampling, Expert-Sample provides consistent positive gains in both pass@n coverage and verification-based accuracy across all four models and all five benchmarks, with no observed regressions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of test-time scaling (Brown et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024 EDT) and fine-grained MoE architectures (Dai et al., 2024; DeepSeek-R1; Qwen3-MoE), identifying the expert routing space as an unexplored source of diversity. Prior MoE work has focused almost exclusively on training efficiency and serving optimization (Jin et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2025), while test-time scaling has relied on token-level temperature tuning. The paper repurposes the well-known Gumbel-Top-K sampling trick at the expert routing level rather than the token level, motivated by a novel empirical observation about router score distributions. It does not build on a specific prior method but carves new ground by showing that the routing mechanism itself can be a controlled diversity knob orthogonal to token sampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'certain head, uncertain tail' characterization of fine-grained MoE routing provides a useful empirical lens for understanding how these models allocate confidence across experts, and the resulting method is training-free, plug-and-play, and incurs negligible overhead (<1% throughput change). This opens a new, orthogonal axis for test-time scaling that complements rather than conflicts with token-level strategies, which is practically significant as fine-grained MoE becomes the dominant architecture for frontier models. Open questions include whether the head/tail boundary can be exploited during training (the authors hint at this), whether the insight generalizes to non-MoE or coarser-grained MoE architectures, and whether the flat tail structure persists in even larger or differently trained models. A limitation is that the method requires access to router internals, restricting applicability to open-weight fine-grained MoE models.
