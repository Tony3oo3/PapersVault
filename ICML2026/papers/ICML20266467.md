## Problem

Existing benchmarks for vision-language models treat target labels as stable ground truth, but urban perception tasks involve appraisal categories (comfort, safety, inclusivity) where human judgments are pluralistic, contain disagreement, and include explicit abstentions. Collapsing these pluralistic judgments into a single label and scoring with point estimates hides systematic disagreement and makes model accuracy contingent on the labeling population and uncertainty semantics. The paper argues that benchmarking should treat disagreement and abstention as informative measurement outcomes, report inter-annotator reliability alongside model alignment, and treat label spaces as negotiable artifacts when outputs inform urban governance.

## Input / Output

The evaluation takes street-level images (50 photographs and 50 photorealistic Stable Diffusion XL renders of Montreal scenes) and a fixed 30-dimension prompt with concise definitions and allowed label sets, requesting a single-line CSV response with comma-separated fields (semicolons for multi-label dimensions). Human annotations were collected in French by 12 participants and normalized via a deterministic French-to-English mapping; abstention labels (Not applicable, Cannot judge) are treated as non-response rather than absence, and are excluded from accuracy denominators for single-choice items or removed from both sets for multi-label Jaccard. Outputs include per-dimension accuracy or Jaccard scores, Krippendorff's alpha for inter-annotator reliability, and full label distributions enabling detection of distributional mismatch beyond pointwise agreement.

## Method

The paper contributes a position with an empirical anchor: a community-annotated benchmark of 100 Montreal street scenes scored along 30 dimensions by 12 participants from seven community organizations, evaluated against seven VLMs (Claude Sonnet, o4-mini, GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Grok 2 Vision, Qwen 2.5 VL, Llama 4 Maverick) under a deterministic zero-shot protocol (temperature=0, top_p=1, base-64 image embedding, strict CSV parsing). The key analytical contribution is reporting model alignment co-varied with dimension-level inter-annotator reliability (Krippendorff's alpha), showing that dimensions with higher human agreement yield higher model agreement, and that appraisal dimensions exhibit distributional mismatch (e.g., models over-selecting Not applicable relative to annotators) invisible to pointwise accuracy. The paper further proposes a negotiation and versioning framework in which benchmark specifications—label ontology, abstention semantics, prompt contract, and scoring rules—are treated as revisable artifacts subject to community contestation, with a disclosure table standardizing what assumptions must be made observable in evaluation reports.

## Results

Under a fixed zero-shot protocol (temperature=0, top_p=1) on 100 Montreal street scenes scored across 30 dimensions, macro-averaged agreement (accuracy for single-choice, Jaccard for multi-label) ranges from 0.16 to 0.31 across seven VLMs. Model alignment positively co-varies with dimension-level inter-annotator reliability (Krippendorff's α), and most models attain higher macro scores on observable-attribute dimensions than on appraisal dimensions. All models achieve higher agreement on real photographs (panels p6–p10) than on photorealistic synthetic renders (panels p1–p5), while the relative model ranking is unchanged across sources.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates seven VLMs—claude-sonnet, openai-o4-mini, gpt-4.1, gemini-2.5-pro, grok-2-vision, qwen2.5-vl, and llama-4-maverick—under an identical deterministic prompt contract on the Montreal Street Scenes Benchmark. The cross-dimension agreement structure is broadly similar across all seven models, indicating that the 30-dimension schema and scoring policy, rather than model-specific capabilities, drive much of the score variance. On the appraisal dimension Overall Impression, several models (including gpt-4.1 and gemini-2.5-pro) select 'Not applicable' at substantially higher rates than annotators and select 'Accessible' at lower rates, exposing a distributional mismatch that pointwise accuracy does not capture. No single model dominates across all dimensions; the positive correlation between human reliability and model alignment holds uniformly, so differences on low-reliability dimensions likely reflect differing model priors for ambiguous cases rather than differences in recoverable visual evidence. The observable-versus-appraisal gap is consistent across models, reinforcing the paper's argument that appraisal outputs should not be interpreted as observational measurements.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes reliability-aware evaluation practices from medical imaging (CheXpert's explicit uncertainty labels and multi-radiologist references) and content moderation (annotator-identity-aware toxicity labeling) into the domain of VLM evaluation for urban perception, where prior work (Salesses et al., Dubey et al.) treated crowd judgments as stable targets. It carves new ground by introducing a 'negotiated benchmark' framework—treating label ontologies, abstention semantics, and scoring policies as versioned, contestable artifacts rather than fixed ground truth—a governance concept drawn from participatory urban AI but not previously applied to ML benchmark design. The empirical anchor (100 Montreal scenes, 30 dimensions, 12 community annotators, 7 VLMs under a fixed zero-shot prompt) is novel in making disagreement and abstention jointly observable with model alignment, but it does not build on a specific prior method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central practical implication is that a single macro-accuracy score for VLMs on appraisal-heavy urban tasks can be misleading when the target itself is unstable; the demonstrated co-variation between Krippendorff's α and model alignment, plus the distributional mismatch in abstention rates on Overall Impression, gives concrete evidence that reliability metadata changes interpretation. The negotiation-and-versioning framework is an open question in practice: the paper itself notes the falsifiability condition (if reporting reliability never changes model rankings or deployment decisions, the marginal value is limited), and the small empirical anchor (100 images, 12 annotators, one city, zero-shot only) limits generalizability. Broader open questions include whether negotiated label spaces can be maintained at scale, how multilingual annotation affects reliability estimates, and whether institutions will actually adopt the proposed disclosure set (Table 1) as a reporting norm.
