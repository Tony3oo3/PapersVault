## Problem

Multi-agent systems introduce security risks that do not arise in single-agent settings, yet the impact of architectural design decisions—role allocation, communication topology, and memory visibility—on the security-performance tradeoff has not been systematically characterized. Prior work evaluates either task performance or specific attack types in isolation, without varying architectural choices or using stage-wise metrics to localize where safety reasoning fails in the pipeline.

## Input / Output

The evaluation takes natural-language task instructions (100 harmful BrowserART tasks, 44 OS-Harm tasks, 160 RedCode-Gen tasks, plus benign counterparts) and a fixed architectural configuration (role setup, topology, memory condition) as input. Outputs are four disjoint stage-wise security outcomes—Planning Refusal, Execution Refusal, Harmful Actions (partial execution), and Harmful Task (objective completed)—plus benign task success rate. The stage-wise decomposition is the key structural choice: it distinguishes whether a system refuses at the planning stage, at execution after delegation, or fails to refuse at all, revealing *where* in the multi-agent pipeline safety reasoning breaks down.

## Method

The paper conducts a controlled empirical study across 13 architectural configurations, three agentic environments (browser, desktop, code), and six base models (GPT-4o, GPT-5.4, GPT-5-mini, Claude Sonnet 4, Qwen3-VL, Llama-70B-Chat), varying three design axes independently: role configuration (standalone through 4-specialist decomposition), communication topology (star, chain, mesh), and memory visibility (private, own reasoning, full shared). All configurations are implemented in the Inspect framework with structural context isolation—specialists receive a fresh AgentState per dispatch and never see the original task string—ensuring that observed security differences arise purely from architecture rather than prompt-level conventions. The key mechanism identified is responsibility fragmentation: role decomposition distributes harmful intent across agents so that no single agent performs end-to-end safety assessment, while topology determines whether agents retain full task visibility (mesh) or receive only processed sub-instructions (chain/star). The study finds that multi-agent architectures are more vulnerable than standalone baselines in the majority of configurations, with attack success rates varying by up to 3.8× at comparable or higher benign accuracy, and that no single design is universally safer.

## Results

Across 13 architectural configurations, three environments, and six models, multi-agent architectures are more vulnerable than standalone agents in the majority of configurations, with Harmful Task (HT) completion rates varying by up to 3.8× at comparable or higher benign accuracy. On BrowserART (GPT-4o), HT rises from 10.0% (standalone) to 38.0% under 2-specialist decomposition while benign accuracy *improves* from 92.9% to 95.2%. On RedCode-Gen, chain topology drives HT to 42.5% (vs. 9.4% standalone) with Planning Refusal collapsing to 8.1%. Well-aligned models (GPT-5.4, Sonnet 4) resist most architectural fragmentation (≤3–7% HT), while weaker-aligned models (Qwen3-VL, Llama-70B) degrade dramatically (up to 42–45% HT under specialization).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The standalone agent serves as the security baseline across all three scenarios. On BrowserART, star topology is the riskiest multi-agent configuration (31.0% HT vs. 10.0% standalone, a 3.1× increase), while mesh is the safest (7.0% HT, actually *below* standalone) but at a cost to benign performance (81.0% vs. 92.9%). On RedCode-Gen, the ranking inverts: chain is the riskiest (42.5% HT, 4.5× standalone) due to Planning Refusal collapsing to 8.1%, while star is the safest multi-agent topology (17.5% HT). On OS-Harm, all topologies increase HT relative to standalone (4.5%), with chain showing the largest increase (15.9%, 3.5×). Critically, the 'safest' topology is scenario-dependent—no single architecture dominates across environments. Cross-model results confirm this: GPT-5.4 stays at ≤3% HT regardless of topology, while Qwen3-VL shows star as riskiest (37% HT) and Llama-70B shows mesh as riskiest (36% HT), demonstrating that model alignment strength mediates architectural vulnerability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-agent security evaluation line initiated by Peigne et al. (2025) and Zhu et al. (2025b), which quantified a 'multi-agent security tax' and examined role/topology effects, by isolating three architectural dimensions (role specialization, communication topology, memory visibility) under controlled conditions across three realistic agentic environments. It adapts three established single-agent misuse benchmarks (BrowserART, OS-Harm, RedCode-Gen) to the multi-agent setting while preserving original task semantics, and introduces stage-wise outcome metrics (planning refusal, execution refusal, harmful actions, harmful task) that go beyond binary attack success. The work also builds on the broader MAS failure-mode taxonomy of Cemri et al. (2025) and the open-challenges framing of de Witt et al. (2026), but focuses specifically on adversarial robustness rather than general coordination quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that specialist architectures achieve higher benign accuracy (95–98%) while being up to 3.8× more vulnerable, with no single topology or memory scheme universally safer—challenges the assumption that capability evaluation signals security and motivates treating architecture as a first-class security variable requiring per-deployment adversarial evaluation. Key limitations include the restriction to direct jailbreak-style misuse (no indirect prompt injection, memory poisoning, or adversarial in-system agents), the absence of dedicated safety mechanisms (monitor agents, guardrails), and the independent variation of design axes rather than their interactions. Open questions include whether these architectural effects persist under iterative red-teaming, whether inter-agent communication protocols (A2A, MCP) modulate the observed vulnerabilities, and whether the findings generalize to domains beyond browser, desktop, and code.
