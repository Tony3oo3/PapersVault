## Problem

Heavy-tailed synaptic weight distributions are pervasive in biological neural circuits, yet their computational role—particularly in relation to generalization—remains poorly understood. While low-rank RNNs are known to support flexible cognitive computations, prior work relied on explicitly constructed or idealized connectivity profiles, leaving open how biologically realistic heavy-tailed statistics might spontaneously induce the advantageous low-dimensional dynamical regimes required for robust generalization. No general-purpose optimization framework existed for training RNNs on cognitive tasks while simultaneously enforcing specific, biologically plausible connectivity statistics.

## Input / Output

The RNNs receive task-dependent scalar time-series inputs (e.g., cue amplitudes in [0, 3/12], input intervals in [800, 1550] ms, or noisy sensory evidence streams) through feedforward input vectors, and produce outputs via linear readouts z_l(t) = w_l^T φ(x(t)) that are linear combinations of all N unit firing rates. The recurrent connectivity matrix J ∈ R^{N×N} is constrained to satisfy three biological conditions: non-reciprocal interactions with no self-coupling, Dale's principle (partition into fN excitatory and (1−f)N inhibitory columns), and heavy-tailed weight distributions (e.g., Log-Normal). Network sizes range from N=200 (Flip-flop) to N=1500 (CSG), with unit time constants of 1–100 ms depending on the task.

## Method

The paper proposes DSco-SGD, an optimization algorithm that alternates between standard SGD gradient steps on the empirical risk and an optimal-transport step that reshapes the weight distribution toward a target heavy-tailed profile (e.g., Log-Normal) while preserving Dale's principle and the sign structure. Specifically, after each gradient update, the algorithm sorts the excitatory and inhibitory weights, estimates the target distribution parameters via MLE, draws a reference sample, and performs a sorted optimal-transport interpolation J_(n) ← J_(n) − η₂(J_(n) − Ĵ_(n)), gradually pulling the weight distribution into the heavy-tailed regime. Theoretically, the paper proves that heavy-tailed connectivity induces an effective low-rank structure (r_eff(J) ≪ N with high probability, Theorem 4.3) via large spectral gaps in the eigenspectrum, which in turn compresses neural dynamics into low-dimensional subspaces (Theorem 4.4). A PAC-Bayes bound (Theorem 3.1) links generalization error to the KL divergence between the posterior weight distribution and a heavy-tailed prior, and empirical results confirm that heavy-tailed RNNs achieve superior out-of-distribution generalization on flexible timing (CSG) and working memory (MWG) tasks by forming linear manifold representations of task variables without requiring external tonic inputs.

## Results

On the CSG and MWG flexible timing/working memory tasks, the heavy-tailed RNN achieves the smallest generalization distance d to the linear target (i.e., best extrapolation beyond the trained range), with statistically significant improvements (p < 0.001) over all four baselines. The heavy-tailed connectivity induces an effective rank r_eff/N ≈ 0.03–0.06 (e.g., r_eff = 7 for N = 200 on Flip-flop; r_eff = 30 for N = 600 on Romo), comparable to explicitly rank-constrained RNNs but achieved without any rank bottleneck. The participation ratio of neural dynamics is significantly lower for heavy-tailed RNNs than for unconstrained or E-I baselines, and the linear manifold representation at the 'Set' phase (r²) is highest for the heavy-tailed model, confirming a more linear encoding of task variables.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The heavy-tailed RNN outperforms all four baselines on generalization distance d for both CSG and MWG tasks (Figure 2), with the largest gaps over the unconstrained and l1-regularized models. Compared to the explicitly low-rank RNN of Beiran et al. (2023), the heavy-tailed model achieves similar effective rank and low-dimensional dynamics but superior generalization without requiring external tonic inputs to parametrically control the input-output transform. The E-I baseline (Song et al., 2016), which enforces Dale's principle but uses Gaussian weights, shows intermediate performance—better than unconstrained but clearly worse than the heavy-tailed model—indicating that the distributional constraint (heavy tails) is the critical factor beyond sign structure alone. The l1-regularized (sparse) baseline confirms that sparsity without heavy-tailed statistics is insufficient to produce the observed generalization benefit. Ablations (Figures 7–8) further show that removing Dale's principle or allowing self-connections degrades performance, confirming that the full set of biological constraints (C1–C3) is necessary.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the low-rank RNN framework of Mastrogiuseppe & Ostojic (2018) and Beiran et al. (2023), which showed that explicitly constructed low-rank connectivity drives neural dynamics into low-dimensional subspaces sufficient for flexible cognition. The key departure is demonstrating that biologically observed heavy-tailed weight statistics (log-normal, Pareto) spontaneously induce an effective low-rank structure without any explicit rank constraint during training, and that this structure additionally provides an intrinsic dynamics-speed modulation that replaces the external tonic inputs required by Beiran et al. Methodologically, the DSco-SGD algorithm is novel in combining stochastic gradient descent with optimal-transport-based distributional projection for RNNs, extending prior distributional-constraint approaches (Zhong et al., 2019) that were limited to feedforward or generative settings. The PAC-Bayes generalization bound (Theorem 3.1) builds on Germain et al. (2009) but applies it to the specific setting of heavy-tailed priors over connectivity matrices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic bridge between a pervasive biological observation (heavy-tailed synaptic weights) and a computational advantage (improved generalization on flexible cognitive tasks), identifying two complementary mechanisms: effective low-rank topology compressing dynamics, and linear manifold geometry favoring linear readout extrapolation. A significant open question is why heavy-tailed statistics specifically favor linear manifold formation rather than other low-dimensional geometries — the authors acknowledge this remains unresolved. The DSco-SGD algorithm, while effective, relies on global gradients and is not biologically plausible, leaving the developmental question of how local learning rules (e.g., three-factor Hebbian plasticity with neuromodulation) could self-organize into these generalization-promoting structures. The theoretical results (Theorems 4.3, 4.4) rely on i.i.d. entry assumptions that are relaxed only heuristically for trained networks, and the convergence proof (Theorem 2.1) is established under strong convexity that does not hold for actual RNN loss landscapes.
