## Problem

Learning drifting halfspaces under label noise has remained computationally intractable: prior efficient algorithms either required strong distributional assumptions (e.g., uniform marginals over the unit sphere) or achieved highly suboptimal error rates, while statistically optimal ERM-based approaches are exponential-time. No polynomial-time algorithm was known for drifting halfspaces with even the mild Massart noise model, and the fundamental question of whether an information-computation tradeoff exists in this setting was open.

## Input / Output

The learner receives an online stream of labeled examples (x^(t), y^(t)) with x^(t) ∈ S^{d-1} and y^(t) ∈ {±1}, where consecutive distributions D^(t) satisfy d_TV(D^(t), D^(t+1)) ≤ Δ and labels are corrupted by Massart noise (flip probability η(x) ≤ η < 1/2). At each round the learner must output a halfspace h: R^d → {±1} before observing the current example. The algorithm operates in epochs of length W = Õ(Δ^{-2/3}), splitting each epoch's data into two halves: the first drives gradient updates and the second performs model selection via empirical error minimization over the update trajectory.

## Method

The DRIFTEDMASSART algorithm runs projected gradient descent within each epoch using a leaky ReLU-inspired gradient g(w;x,y) = ((1−2η)sign(w·x) − y)/max{|w·x|, γ}, which balances penalization across examples of different margins. The key technical contribution is the Regret-to-Error lemma, which bounds the prediction error of any iterate by its average regret plus a drift-controlled term O(mΔ/γ), circumventing the need to track geometric changes in the (unknown) target halfspace across rounds. By choosing epoch length W = Õ(Δ^{-2/3}) and step size μ = γ/√T, the algorithm balances optimization error (1/(√T·γ)), drift error (TΔ/γ), and concentration error (√T·log(1/δ)/γ) to achieve excess error Õ(Δ^{1/3}/γ). A complementary lower bound via low-degree polynomial testing on a trajectory hypothesis-testing problem shows that no polynomial of degree o(γ^{-c/4}) can distinguish the hard instance, implying the Δ^{1/3} scaling is unavoidable for this broad class of efficient algorithms even under RCN.

## Results

The main algorithmic result (Theorem 1.2) achieves error η + Õ(Δ^{1/3}/γ) for learning drifting γ-margin halfspaces with η-Massart noise, running in poly(d, 1/γ, 1/Δ) time. In the realizable setting, the algorithm improves the prior best efficient bound from Õ(√Δ · γ^{-2}) (Helmbold & Long, 1994) to Õ(√Δ · γ^{-3/2}). The information-theoretic optimal excess error under Massart noise is shown to be Θ̃(√(dΔ)), matching the realizable rate, as opposed to Θ((dΔ)^{1/3}) for adversarial noise. A low-degree polynomial hardness result (Theorem 4.4) shows that the Δ^{1/3} scaling of the efficient algorithm is unavoidable for this class of algorithms, even under RCN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments, so comparisons are between theoretical guarantees. The paper's realizable-setting algorithm (Theorem 3.7) improves upon Helmbold & Long (1994), which achieved Õ(d√Δ) error, by removing the dependence on d and reducing the margin dependence from γ^{-2} to γ^{-3/2}. Compared to Crammer et al. (2010) and Hanneke et al. (2015), which achieve O((dΔ)^{1/4}) and O(√(dΔ)) respectively, the paper's results hold without the strong assumption of a uniform marginal distribution over the unit sphere. The key conceptual contribution is showing that under Massart noise, the information-theoretic rate matches the realizable rate (√Δ scaling) rather than the agnostic rate (Δ^{1/3} scaling), yet the best efficient algorithm still pays the Δ^{1/3} price. The low-degree polynomial lower bound (Theorem 4.4) provides formal evidence that this gap between information-theoretic (Δ^{1/2}) and computationally efficient (Δ^{1/3}) rates is inherent, even in the simpler RCN special case.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical line of work on learning drifting concepts (Helmbold et al., 1991; Bartlett, 1992; Helmbold & Long, 1994) by introducing structured label noise (Massart/RCN) into the drift model, a combination for which no prior efficient algorithm existed. The core gradient design in Algorithm 2 is directly adapted from the non-drift online Massart noise learner of Diakonikolas et al. (2025), but the analysis must overcome the fundamental difficulty that no single ground-truth halfspace persists across rounds. On the lower-bound side, the paper applies the low-degree polynomial testing framework (Hopkins, 2018; Kunisky et al., 2019; Wein, 2025) to the trajectory testing problem, a technique previously used for non-drift noise-tolerant learning but not for drift settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first polynomial-time algorithms for drifting halfspaces under label noise and, more importantly, proves a formal information-computation tradeoff: while Massart noise is information-theoretically no harder than the realizable setting (Δ^{1/2} scaling), computationally efficient algorithms are forced to pay the Δ^{1/3} price matching the adversarial-noise rate. This suggests that the structure of semi-random noise does not translate into computational advantage in non-stationary settings, a finding with implications for how one should design drift-adaptive learners in practice. Open questions include extending these results to richer hypothesis classes (intersections of halfspaces, neural networks) and to structured covariate distributions with adversarial labels, where non-drift efficient learners already exist.
