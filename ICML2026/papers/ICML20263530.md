## Problem

Existing LLM scaling laws (OpenAI, Chinchilla) assume loss decreases monotonically with model size and token count, yet empirical phenomena such as catastrophic overtraining and quantization-induced degradation produce U-shaped loss curves where performance worsens beyond a critical threshold. No prior theoretical framework unifies these monotonic and non-monotonic regimes under a single principled formulation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263530/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The law is fitted on intermediate pretraining checkpoints spanning multiple model sizes (160M–32B) and token budgets (up to 307B tokens), with loss measured on WikiText-2 for pretraining or on task-specific benchmarks (GSM8K, SiQA, StarCoder-Python) after SFT or quantization. The perturbation factor X (SNR in dB, learning rate, or quantization bit-width) modulates the noise term, and the fitted 9-parameter (or 6-parameter simplified) model can extrapolate to unseen model sizes and token ranges beyond the fitting grid.

## Method

The paper maps the Shannon-Hartley channel capacity C = B·log₂(1+S/N) onto LLM training by identifying bandwidth with model size (B ∝ N^α), signal power with training tokens (S ∝ D^β), and noise with three additive components: data-induced noise (dD^δ), model-interaction noise (c(DN)^γ), and irreducible entropy (e). The resulting capacity C_LLM is inverted to yield loss L = 1/C_LLM, producing a 9-parameter scaling law whose denominator noise terms naturally generate U-shaped degradation when noise dominates the signal. Standard monotonic pretraining is recovered as a high-SNR special case where the perturbation factor is negligible, making the formulation a strict generalization of power-law scaling. The model-interaction term c(DN)^γ is critical: it couples model size and token count so that scaling either axis amplifies intrinsic model noise, explaining why excessively large or overtrained models degrade under perturbation.

## Results

The Shannon Scaling Law achieves average R² of 0.9613±0.03 on Pythia and 0.9585±0.06 on OLMo2 under Gaussian noise (40–10 dB), consistently outperforming all baselines across the full SNR spectrum. On SFT tasks, it reaches 0.936 (GSM8K), 0.916 (SiQA), and 0.937 (StarCoder) average R², while monotonic baselines (OpenAI, Chinchilla) collapse to negative R² values under moderate-to-high learning rates. In the headline extrapolation experiment (fitted on ≤6.9B Pythia models with ≤180B tokens, predicting the unseen 12B model up to 307B tokens), the full Shannon Law achieves pooled R²=0.847, whereas Chinchilla degrades to 0.305 and OpenAI to −0.082.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Shannon Scaling Law outperforms all four baselines across every perturbation scenario. Against the OpenAI Law (Kaplan et al., 2020) and Chinchilla Law (Hoffmann et al., 2022), the advantage is most dramatic: under 10 dB Gaussian noise on Pythia, the Shannon Law maintains R²=0.9555 while OpenAI collapses to 0.0707 and Chinchilla to 0.2395; on SFT at high learning rates, both monotonic laws yield negative R² (e.g., OpenAI averages −1.010 on StarCoder) while the Shannon Law holds at 0.937. Against the perturbation-aware QiD Law (Ouyang et al., 2024), the Shannon Law wins by a consistent margin: 0.9613 vs. 0.9302 (Gaussian noise, Pythia), 0.936 vs. 0.637 (SFT GSM8K), and 0.9824 vs. 0.9788 (quantization, Pythia), with the gap widening under stronger perturbations. The Law of Precision (Kumar et al., 2024) shows similar competitive but inferior performance (e.g., 0.9013 vs. 0.9613 on Gaussian noise Pythia). In the joint extrapolation test (k=5, j=12), the full Shannon Law achieves R²=0.847 versus 0.761 (QiD) and 0.708 (Law of Precision), confirming that its additional parameters encode meaningful joint (N,D) structure that simpler perturbation-aware laws cannot capture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper derives a scaling law for LLMs from the Shannon-Hartley theorem, mapping model parameters to channel bandwidth and training tokens to signal power, with noise arising from data, model architecture, and irreducible entropy. It departs from monotonic power-law formulations (OpenAI 2020, Chinchilla 2022) and from ad-hoc perturbation-aware extensions (QiD Law 2024, Law of Precision 2024) that bolt degradation terms onto existing laws, instead grounding the entire functional form in information-theoretic first principles. The noisy-channel analogy also connects to the information bottleneck view of deep learning (Shwartz-Ziv & Tishby 2017) and denoising autoencoder theory (Vincent et al. 2008), but repurposes the channel model for capacity quantification rather than the probabilistic inference (spelling correction, MT) that dominates prior NLP use of noisy channels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled criterion—maintaining sufficient SNR—for when scaling model size or tokens helps versus hurts, with practical implications for compute allocation and avoiding wasteful training past the degradation threshold. The extrapolation results (predicting an unseen 12B model up to 307B tokens from ≤6.9B/≤180B fits at R²=0.847) suggest small-scale experiments could forecast large-scale behavior under perturbations, potentially reducing costly pretraining trials. Open questions include whether the 9 fitted constants can be further constrained by theory, whether the δ>β finding (token noise always dominates signal in the limit) implies a fundamental ceiling on data scaling, and whether the framework extends to modalities beyond text or to architectures like MoE where the bandwidth analogy may break down.
