## Problem

Standard PCA is highly sensitive to mean-shift contamination: even a small fraction of samples from a shifted distribution biases the leading principal components away from the true covariance eigenvectors. Existing Robust PCA methods fail in this setting because mean-shift noise is neither sparse (violating the core RPCA assumption) nor separable from the signal (it shares the same low-rank structure), and their recovery guarantees vanish as the aspect ratio d/n approaches a positive constant.

## Input / Output

The method takes a contaminated d×n data matrix (drawn from a k-component mean-shift mixture) and a threshold constant C, and outputs the subset of eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the contaminated sample covariance that are invariant under an additional artificial perturbation. The invariance check operates only on spiked eigenvalues outside the bulk support of the limiting spectral distribution, using a tolerance ε = Cn^{-1/2} to distinguish O(1) mean-shift displacement from O(n^{-1/2}) stable-spike fluctuations. The algorithm requires only two partial PCA computations (top K components) and one eigenvalue matching step, with leading cost O(Knd).

## Method

The key theoretical insight, proven via Random Matrix Theory, is spectral decoupling: the r+k largest spiked eigenvalues of the contaminated sample covariance decompose into two asymptotically independent sets—Λ_P determined by population covariance spikes and Λ_A determined by mean-shift contamination spikes—so perturbing one set leaves the other unchanged. Exploiting this, MS-PCA performs a two-stage procedure: (1) compute the top spiked eigenvalues/eigenvectors of the contaminated data, then (2) add an artificial knockoff mean-shift matrix A'_n = m'γ'^T with a random direction and sufficiently large magnitude, recompute PCA on the doubly-perturbed data, and retain only those eigenvectors whose associated eigenvalues remain within ε = Cn^{-1/2} of their original values. The knockoff perturbation is designed so that its strength θ'² is comparable to the observed spike (via the inverse spike-forward mapping g^{-1}), ensuring detectable displacement of mean-shift spikes while covariance-induced spikes stay within their O(n^{-1/2}) fluctuation band. The eigenspace invariance theorem guarantees that uncontaminated eigenvectors remain asymptotically eigenvectors of the contaminated covariance matrix regardless of the mixture weight, providing the theoretical foundation for the invariance check.

## Results

On a 2-component Gaussian mixture with one-spike population covariance (d=900, n=1000), MS-PCA achieves 95.9–97.4% alignment of the largest principal component with the true eigenvector across contamination proportions π₁ = 5%–20%, while all competing methods (RPCA-AAP, Tyler, Huber, ℓ₁-PCA, winsorized-PCA, center-PCA) remain at 7–14%, essentially at random alignment. In the mean-shift plus covariance-shift setting (Table 5), MS-PCA maintains ≥95% alignment across all tested (π₁, ℓ₂) combinations, while competitors stay below 17%. Runtime for one leading PC is 14.2 ms for MS-PCA versus 79 ms (RPCA-AAP), 172 ms (Tyler), 3860 ms (Huber), and 8230 ms (ℓ₁-PCA).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MS-PCA decisively outperforms all baselines on the primary metric (largest-PC alignment |⟨u₁, û₁⟩|). Against RPCA-AAP (Cai et al., 2019), MS-PCA achieves 95–97% alignment versus 7–9% for RPCA-AAP, a gap of roughly 88 percentage points; RPCA-AAP's alignment actually converges to zero as dimension grows (Figure 6), while MS-PCA remains near-perfect. Against Tyler's M-estimator (Tyler, 1987), MS-PCA leads by ~85–87 percentage points (95–97% vs. 9–11%). Against ℓ₁-PCA (Kwak, 2008), the gap is ~83–85 percentage points (95–97% vs. 12–14%), with ℓ₁-PCA being the strongest competitor but still far from recovering the true component. The paper also notes that in the relatively low-dimensional regime (c=0.1), MS-PCA achieves perfect recovery only when π₁ ≥ 25%, whereas RPCA-AAP performs comparably poorly at low contamination; in high-dimensional regimes (c=1), MS-PCA consistently dominates across all contamination levels. Runtime-wise, MS-PCA is 5.5× faster than RPCA-AAP and over 250× faster than Huber's M-estimator for a single leading PC.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the additive low-rank random perturbation framework of Benaych-Georges & Nadakuditi (2012) and the spiked covariance model literature (Baik & Silverstein, 2006; Paul, 2007), extending their Stieltjes-transform-based spectral analysis to a mean-shift mixture setting where the low-rank perturbation encodes contamination rather than signal. It departs fundamentally from the Robust PCA lineage (Candès et al., 2011; Cai et al., 2019), which assumes sparse outliers and formulates recovery as a convex/alternating-projection optimization, by showing that mean-shift contamination is structurally incompatible with those sparsity assumptions in high dimensions. The 'add noise to identify noise' strategy draws a conceptual parallel to ambient diffusion (Daras et al., 2023) but applies it to a purely spectral, non-iterative procedure grounded in RMT phase-transition thresholds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a concrete gap: existing robust PCA and robust covariance estimators provably fail (alignment → 0) for mean-shift contamination when d/n → c > 0, yet this is a common data-aggregation scenario. The eigenspace invariance theorem (Theorem 3.11) is a clean, general result showing that the uncontaminated eigenspace is asymptotically preserved regardless of mixture weight, providing a principled foundation for the two-pass algorithm. However, the method is restricted to first-moment (mean-shift) contamination with homoscedastic components; extending to arbitrary mixtures involving covariance shifts and higher moments remains open, as the authors explicitly note. The O(nd) runtime and lack of optimization also make it attractive as a preprocessing step in pipelines where standard PCA is already the default.
