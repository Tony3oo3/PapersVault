## Problem

Existing samplers for discrete flow models (tau-leaping, Euler solver) freeze both the time variable and the state argument of the transition rate within each interval, requiring many steps to control discretization error. Moreover, prior theoretical analyses impose boundedness conditions on transition rates or restrict the source distribution, limiting their generality. This paper asks whether one can derive non-asymptotic error bounds without such restrictions and whether simple corrections to the Euler sampler can reduce iteration complexity at negligible computational cost.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264576/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The samplers consume a trained logits model that outputs the posterior p_{1|t}(z^d | x), a source distribution (masked point mass or uniform over |S| states per coordinate), and a time schedule κ_t over [0, 1−δ]. They produce a D-dimensional discrete sequence with each coordinate in a vocabulary of size |S|; for text-to-image, D=576 tokens are decoded into an RGB image. The time-corrected sampler requires exactly one network evaluation per timestep (same as Euler), while the location-corrected sampler requires at most two evaluations per timestep, making the additional cost negligible when K > D.

## Method

The paper first establishes non-asymptotic total-variation bounds for tau-leaping and Euler samplers under only a finite-estimation-error assumption, then derives a one-step KL lower bound for the Euler sampler that identifies two error sources: freezing the time schedule κ_t and freezing the posterior location after a jump. To address the first, the time-corrected sampler keeps the posterior evaluated at t_{k−1} but lets the schedule factor κ̇_t/(1−κ_t) vary continuously within each interval, yielding a closed-form survival function (1−κ_t)/(1−κ_{t_{k−1}}) raised to a per-dimension rate. To address the second, the location-corrected sampler introduces a two-stage jump process: before the first exit time T_k it uses the initial posterior, and after T_k it re-evaluates the posterior at the new state X(T_k) and current time T_k, acting as a randomized midpoint correction. This two-stage design reduces the iteration complexity from O(D²) to O(D) (or O(D^{3/2}) with a time-independent posterior) in the number of steps needed to reach a target accuracy, validated on 9-dimensional simulations and 576-token text-to-image generation with FUDOKI.

## Results

On low-dimensional simulations (D=9, |S|=8), the location-corrected sampler achieves lower total variation than tau-leaping, Euler, and RK2-Trapezoid samplers under a fixed sampling time for the masked source distribution, and remains competitive with RK2-Trapezoid for the uniform source distribution. On the high-dimensional text-to-image task (D=576, FUDOKI model), the time-corrected sampler achieves the highest GenEval overall score among all samplers under the same time budget at K=8 steps, outperforming the Euler solver and both RK2 variants. Theoretically, the location-corrected sampler reduces iteration complexity from O(D²|S|log²(1/δ)/ε²) (tau-leaping/Euler) to O(D|S|log²(1/δ)·max{L_k^p/M_k}/ε² ∨ D^{3/2}|S|^{1/2}log^{3/2}(1/δ)/ε), requiring fewer steps to reach the same accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three families of baselines: (1) tau-leaping and Euler samplers, which freeze both time and location in the transition rate; (2) the always-valid Euler solver from Shaul et al. (2025), used as the primary practical baseline in both simulation and text-to-image experiments; and (3) deterministic high-order RK2 and RK2-Trapezoid samplers from Ren et al. (2025b). The time-corrected sampler matches the Euler solver's computational cost (one function call per step) while reducing the discretization error from the frozen time schedule, yielding lower total variation at the same sampling time. The location-corrected sampler adds at most one extra function call per step (after the first jump) and consistently outperforms the Euler solver and RK2-Trapezoid on GenEval scores in the D=576 text-to-image task; it also beats RK2-Trapezoid on total variation for the masked source distribution in simulations, though it is competitive rather than strictly superior for the uniform source. Against the RK2 sampler, the corrected samplers converge faster (reach comparable accuracy in fewer steps), and the location-corrected sampler has a provably lower iteration complexity in dimension D than all existing parallel samplers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the discrete flow matching framework (Gat et al., 2024; Campbell et al., 2024) by introducing a two-stage jump process structure that enables history-dependent transition rates, going beyond the time-homogeneous rates used in tau-leaping and Euler samplers. It departs from prior theoretical analyses (Ren et al., 2025a; Liang et al., 2025a,b) that required boundedness conditions on transition rates or restricted source distributions, establishing non-asymptotic total variation bounds under only a finite estimation error assumption. The time-corrected sampler directly modifies the always-valid Euler solver from Shaul et al. (2025) by making the schedule factor time-varying, while the location-corrected sampler draws on the randomized midpoint idea from high-order tau-leaping (Hu et al., 2011; Ren et al., 2025b) but applies it stochastically via a two-stage construction that requires at most two function calls per timestep.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The location-corrected sampler's O(D) vs O(D²) iteration complexity in dimension is a meaningful theoretical improvement for high-dimensional discrete generation, and the two-stage jump process framework provides a general tool for constructing samplers with history-dependent rates. However, the practical gains are moderate: corrected samplers consistently outperform Euler but the GenEval improvements over RK2-Trapezoid are small, and the location-corrected sampler's advantage is most pronounced under masked source distributions where the posterior is time-independent. Open questions include extending the theory to the few-step regime (where K ≪ D, common in practice), handling time-dependent posteriors more gracefully, and whether the multistage extension (Remark 5) can achieve near-linear complexity in D for general posteriors.
