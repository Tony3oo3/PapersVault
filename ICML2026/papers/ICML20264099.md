## Problem

Gradient clipping is widely used to stabilize SGD and is theoretically motivated by heavy-tailed gradient noise, yet in the finite-sum ERM setting all empirical moments are finite once the dataset is fixed, seemingly making heavy-tailed noise irrelevant to optimization complexity. This paper resolves the paradox by showing that heavy-tailed data induces dataset-dependent noise whose second moment grows as N^(2/α−1) with dataset size N, causing standard SGD convergence to deteriorate as N increases under a fixed compute budget, while clipping removes this harmful growth.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a finite-sum objective F_N(x) = (1/N)Σf(x,ξ_i) where the N samples are drawn i.i.d. from a heavy-tailed distribution with regularly varying gradient tails at the population minimizer. The algorithm consumes a non-increasing step-size schedule {γ_t}, a clipping radius schedule {λ_t}, and an initial point x^1, and produces a time-averaged iterate x̄^T with convergence guarantees expressed in terms of α-th moment statistics at x_★ rather than uniform trajectory bounds. A key structural feature is that the bounds are conditional on the dataset (expectation over algorithm randomness only), and the noise terms are characterized via stable-law scaling that explicitly tracks their dependence on N.

## Method

The core insight is to explicitly separate data-sampling randomness (drawing the dataset S) from optimization randomness (sampling indices during training), then use regularly varying tail asymptotics P(||∇f(x_★,ξ)|| ≥ r) ~ cr^(−α) to characterize how the dataset-dependent noise proxy (1/N)Σ||∇f_i(x_★)||² scales as Θ_P(N^(2/α)−1) via α-stable limit theorems. ClipSGD convergence is then proven with bounds depending on E_i||∇f_i(x_★)||^α and the clipping schedule rather than the diverging second moment, yielding O(T^((1−α)/α)) rates for convex objectives and O(T^(−(α−1))) or O(1/T) rates for strongly convex objectives depending on the N/T regime. The paper further derives the first ERM generalization bounds under heavy-tailed data for strongly convex smooth objectives, showing the excess risk scales as O(Δ^(2−q)E||∇f(x_★,ξ)||^q / (μ^(q−1)N^(q−1))) for q∈(1,α), and proves matching lower bounds via a one-dimensional Pareto construction. Finally, a tail-index-agnostic analysis shows how the scheduling parameter α_alg interacts with the true tail index α_true across three regimes (N≫T, N≈T, N≪T), with optimal choices ranging from α_alg=α_true to α_alg=2 depending on the relative sizes of N and T.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative result is that under heavy-tailed data with tail index α ∈ (1,2), the empirical second-moment noise proxy (1/N)Σᵢ‖∇fᵢ(x⋆)‖² scales as Θ_P(N^{2/α−1}) (Theorem 3.4), causing unclipped SGD's convergence to deteriorate polynomially with dataset size. In contrast, ClipSGD achieves O(T^{(1−α)/α}) convergence rates (Corollary 3.3) that are independent of N, with the noise term bounded by Eᵢ‖∇fᵢ(x⋆)‖^α which does not grow with N. For generalization, the ERM excess risk is O(Δ^{2−q} E‖∇f(x⋆,ξ)‖^q / (μ^{q−1} N^{q−1})) (Theorem 3.1), and a matching lower bound (Lemma 3.1) confirms this rate is tight. The experimental validation (Figure 1) confirms that as N increases from 10 to 10⁷, ClipSGD's population excess risk decreases while SGD's increases or plateaus, with the gap widening for smaller α (heavier tails).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between ClipSGD and unclipped SGD in the finite-sum ERM setting under heavy-tailed data. The key finding is that SGD's convergence bound contains the dataset-dependent term (1/N)Σᵢ‖∇fᵢ(x⋆)‖², which grows as N^{2/α−1} under heavy tails (Theorem 3.4), meaning SGD's optimization error worsens as the dataset grows for a fixed iteration budget. ClipSGD eliminates this polynomial growth: its noise term involves Eᵢ‖∇fᵢ(x⋆)‖^α, which remains bounded (or grows only logarithmically when α_alg = α_true) regardless of N. In the experimental validation (Figure 1, T = 10³ iterations, α ∈ {1.05, 1.1, 1.5, 1.9}), ClipSGD's population excess risk F(x̄^T) − F(x⋆) consistently decreases with N, while SGD's population excess risk increases or stabilizes—particularly for α = 1.05 and 1.1 where the tail is heaviest. For α = 1.9 (near finite-variance regime), both methods perform similarly, consistent with the theory predicting the N^{2/α−1} growth becomes negligible. The paper also notes that in the N ≪ T regime (multiple passes over data, typical in computer vision), the heavy-tailed effect is less influential and α_alg = 2 recovers the classical O(1/√T) rate for both methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on ClipSGD under heavy-tailed noise (Zhang et al., 2020; Gorbunov et al., 2020) from the streaming setting to the finite-sum ERM regime, where prior analyses treated the dataset as fixed and all moments as trivially finite. The key departure is explicitly separating data-sampling randomness from optimization randomness, revealing that the standard 'variance-at-the-optimum' proxy in finite-sum SGD bounds (Moulines & Bach, 2011; Gower et al., 2019) is itself a random quantity that grows with N under heavy-tailed data. The generalization analysis builds on stability-based ERM bounds (Bousquet & Elisseeff, 2002; Liu & Tong, 2024) but extends them to settings with potentially unbounded variance, using stable-law scaling (Gnedenko & Kolmogorov, 1968) to characterize the N-dependence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a genuine conceptual paradox: finite-sum optimization appears immune to heavy-tailed noise because all conditional moments are finite, yet the analysis shows the noise proxy scales as N^(2/α−1), meaning larger datasets actually worsen unclipped SGD convergence under fixed compute budgets. This provides a principled, finite-sum explanation for why clipping is effective in large-scale training and gives practitioners a regime-dependent recipe (N≫T vs. N≈T vs. N≪T) for choosing clipping schedules without knowing the exact tail index. Open questions include high-probability convergence bounds, connecting generalization and optimization in the convex (non-strongly-convex) setup, and extending to more general smoothness assumptions beyond L-smoothness.
