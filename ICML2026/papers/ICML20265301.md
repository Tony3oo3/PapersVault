## Problem

Existing dynamics adaptation methods for robotic RL (e.g., RMA) are parameter-centric: they condition policies on explicitly identified physical parameters, making them brittle under unmodeled, coupled, or time-varying dynamics shifts. The paper argues that the quantities most relevant to adaptation are not the physical parameters themselves but their realized effects on interaction outcomes, and asks whether a policy can learn a smooth, task-relevant latent dynamics geometry without privileged parameter supervision.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265301/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The encoder consumes a trajectory window w_t of length H (a sequence of state-action pairs) and produces a probabilistic latent context z_t via a variational inference network with an isotropic Gaussian prior. The latent-conditioned policy then maps the current state s_t and z_t to an action. The window length H is a tunable hyperparameter (best at H=50 in experiments), and the encoder uses a convolutional architecture whose stride and kernel size are adjusted to compress windows of different lengths to the same output feature dimension. The latent dimension d_z is typically 5–10, with a minimum effective ratio of roughly 3:16 relative to observation dimension.

## Method

LDG replaces parameter regression with a contrastive-learning objective that shapes the latent dynamics manifold. Theoretically, the paper proves that target-domain regret is monotonically increasing in the encoder's Lipschitz constant L_E and consistency δ_e (Theorem 1), and that minimizing the InfoNCE alignment loss upper-bounds L_E (Theorem 2). Practically, the training objective combines three terms: a standard RL loss (SAC), a VAE reconstruction loss for next-state prediction, and a Multi-Positive InfoNCE contrastive loss where positive pairs are trajectory segments generated under identical dynamics parameters. The contrastive loss simultaneously enforces local temporal consistency (small δ_e), manifold continuity via bridge samples between marginally different dynamics (bounding L_E), and global uniformity to prevent mode collapse. A key ablation shows that spectral normalization alone (which strictly bounds L_E) fails in OOD and structural-failure settings because it does not organize the latent space by physical semantics, confirming that both smoothness and topological structure are necessary.

## Results

On in-distribution evaluation (Table 1), LDG achieves the highest mean reward on Hopper (3239.2 ± 279.3) and Walker2d (4883.7 ± 381.6), substantially outperforming RMA Phase 2 (1677.5 and 3193.6 respectively) while also yielding the lowest variance across seeds. On zero-shot OOD generalization (Table 2), LDG dominates under unmodeled parameter shifts (e.g., Ant damping 0.3×: 4797 vs. RMA Phase 2's 1567) and structural failure (Walker2d: 952 vs. 292 for RMA Phase 2), though it underperforms RMA on HalfCheetah's time-varying and structural failure settings (7849 vs. 9121 and 2071 vs. 2862). The VAE ablation (without contrastive loss) consistently trails LDG, confirming the necessity of the geometric structure enforced by InfoNCE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LDG outperforms RMA (Phase 2) on 3 of 4 environments in in-distribution evaluation (Hopper: 3239 vs. 1678; Walker2d: 4884 vs. 3194; Ant: 5184 vs. 4108) but underperforms on HalfCheetah (5799 vs. 6636), which the authors attribute to over-regularization in highly reactive gaits. Against RMA (Phase 1, oracle parameters), LDG wins on Hopper, Walker2d, and Ant but loses on HalfCheetah (5799 vs. 6713). In zero-shot OOD settings, LDG achieves dramatic gains over RMA Phase 2 under extreme parameter shifts (e.g., Ant damping 2.2×: 4789 vs. 666; Hopper mass 2.0×: 1349 vs. 563) and structural failure (Walker2d: 952 vs. 292), demonstrating that the learned latent geometry enables extrapolation to events with no valid parameter correspondence. SO-CMA (test-time CMA-ES optimization) achieves the highest rewards in some in-distribution settings but requires ~42k samples per evaluation and fails catastrophically under structural failure (e.g., Hopper: 432 vs. LDG's 596). The VAE ablation (removing the contrastive loss) consistently underperforms LDG across all environments, confirming that both alignment (smoothness) and uniformity (topological structure) are necessary for robust adaptation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the parameter-centric paradigm of RMA (Kumar et al., 2021), which regresses explicit physical parameters into latent codes, by proposing an outcome-centric approach where latent geometry is shaped by contrastive learning on trajectory interaction outcomes. It builds on the variational inference framework of β-VAE (Higgins et al., 2017a) for latent dynamics modeling, the InfoNCE contrastive objective (Chen et al., 2020; Khosla et al., 2020) for enforcing local consistency and global uniformity, and the alignment/uniformity decomposition of Wang & Isola (2020) to theoretically connect contrastive loss to encoder Lipschitz smoothness. The key conceptual shift is from 'telling' the policy what the dynamics parameters are to letting it discover how dynamics manifest in interaction outcomes, with a formal regret bound (Theorem 1) linking target-domain sub-optimality to the encoder's Lipschitz constant.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled theoretical bridge between latent representation geometry and zero-shot adaptation performance, showing that controlling the encoder's Lipschitz constant via contrastive learning upper-bounds target-domain regret. The ablation cleanly separating smoothness (alignment) from structure (uniformity) demonstrates that both are necessary for OOD generalization, a finding that generalizes beyond dynamics adaptation to any latent-conditioned policy. Key limitations include over-regularization on highly reactive tasks (HalfCheetah), reliance on MuJoCo benchmarks only, and the semi-supervised requirement that positive pairs share known dynamics parameters. Open questions include fully unsupervised positive-pair construction, adaptive relaxation of smoothness constraints, and extension to interactive or real-robot settings.
