## Problem

Open source AI models require substantial capital, compute, and data infrastructure for pretraining, post-training, and deployment that only a few well-funded actors can provide, breaking the traditional open source assumption that the full contribution cycle is broadly accessible. The paper argues that without structural intervention from public institutions, open source AI will not democratize access or provision public goods as comparable open source efforts have in other software categories.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the analytical input is the current landscape of open source AI projects (LLMs, multimodal models, datasets, benchmarks) and the structural challenges they face across resource, licensing, and governance dimensions. The output is a normative framework of four principles for 'public AI' (Public Support, Public Access, Public Accountability, Private Commitments) grounded in documented examples of existing public infrastructure efforts worldwide, rather than a trained model or empirical benchmark.

## Method

The paper constructs its argument through a three-part diagnostic of why the traditional open source model breaks down for AI: resource challenges (pretraining scale, post-training data silos, inference costs), licensing challenges (ambiguous 'open-weight' versus true open source, revocable terms, partial transparency), and governance challenges (safety gaps, closed-source co-optation, data capture through proprietary coding-agent harnesses). It then proposes 'public AI' as a complementary framework treating AI as public infrastructure analogous to highways or libraries, articulated through four principles. The argument is grounded in concrete case studies of public AI efforts (BigScience/BLOOM on Jean Zay, LAION on JUWELS and Leonardo, EleutherAI on Summit, EuroHPC-funded multilingual models, NDIF, AVERI, SEA-HELM) and stress-tested against five alternative views. The paper draws on economic concepts of public goods, club goods, and impure public goods to formalize why open weights alone constitute an 'impure public good' requiring public activation infrastructure to become genuinely accessible.

## Results

This is a position paper without experimental results or a main results table. The key quantitative evidence cited to support its argument includes Hugging Face download statistics (as of late Jan 2026): LLaMA 3.1-8B has ~6M monthly downloads versus EleutherAI Pythia's ~900k and OLMo 3-7B's ~170k, illustrating the adoption gap between corporate-backed and nonprofit open models. The paper also notes that LAION's openCLIP variants achieve 1M–2M downloads per model per month (>60M all-time), and LAION-CLAP reaches 14M monthly downloads, as evidence that public-infrastructure-backed community efforts can compete. OpenEuroLLM has been allocated over 10M GPU-hours across four major European HPC systems (Leonardo, LUMI, JUPITER, MareNostrum5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a method to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it qualitatively contrasts the current open-source AI ecosystem (open weights, community tooling, nonprofit models) against closed-source frontier labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta's closed models) along axes of accessibility, transparency, sustainability, and governance. The paper argues that open source currently loses on adoption (LLaMA 3.1-8B at 6M monthly downloads vs. Pythia at 900k), on licensing durability (Meta's reported shift away from LLaMA releases; Alibaba removing free Qwen Code access in April 2026), and on transparency (open weights lack training data, RLHF procedures, and compute configurations). It concedes that open source wins on reproducibility of code and tooling (PyTorch, Hugging Face Transformers, lm-eval-harness) and that public-infrastructure-backed projects (LAION, BigScience/BLOOM, EleutherAI) have demonstrated parity with closed models at release time, but argues this is insufficient without sustained public funding for inference, post-training, and data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes the open source software tradition (Linux, Kubernetes, Free Software movement) with the emerging landscape of open-weight AI models (BLOOM, GPT-NeoX, Pythia, LAION, OLMo, DeepSeek) to argue that the structural assumptions of open source—commodity hardware, low hosting costs, community governance—break down for foundation models. It builds on prior critiques of 'open' AI opacity (Widder et al., 2024; Brundage et al., 2026) and the digital public infrastructure (DPI) literature, but departs from the 'open source will self-sustain' narrative by proposing a distinct institutional category—'public AI'—with specific principles of public funding, access, accountability, and private commitments. The paper also engages with and extends earlier multilateral proposals (Airbus for AI, CAIRNE/CERN for AI) by grounding them in concrete ongoing examples (EuroHPC, NDIF, AVERI, SEA-HELM).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's core contribution is a diagnostic: open source AI faces not a values failure but a structural one (inference costs, data silos, licensing fragility, safety underfunding), and the remedy is public infrastructure rather than more community goodwill. This reframing is timely given Meta's reported shift away from LLaMA and Alibaba's removal of free Qwen Code access, which validate the paper's fragility concerns. Open questions remain substantial: how to fund and govern public AI without bureaucratic capture, how to mandate audit access without chilling innovation, and whether multilateral structures (EuroHPC-style) can scale to frontier-model compute. For practitioners, the practical implication is that relying solely on open-weight releases without public inference and data-sharing infrastructure will leave most of the ML community locked out of meaningful participation.
