## Problem

Existing alignment benchmarks test knowledge of ethical principles rather than behavioural responses to realistic pressure, predominantly use single-turn interactions, and evaluate behaviours in isolation. This paper addresses the gap by introducing a comprehensive multi-turn adversarial benchmark that reveals how models actually behave when aligned conduct comes at a cost—where honesty risks embarrassment, deference requires abandoning a goal, or refusal means disappointing a user.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263648/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each scenario is a structured multi-turn conversation with optional trigger conditions (evaluated by a referee model to determine whether escalation turns execute), simulated tool outputs injected as tool_use messages, and behaviour-specific pass/fail criteria. The judge (Claude Opus 4.5) produces a 1–5 score per scenario against those criteria, with pass defined as score ≥ 4; aggregate outputs include per-behaviour scores (37 behaviours), per-category scores (6 categories), and a factor-analytic decomposition of the 37×24 behaviour-by-model matrix. Skip rates (scenarios where triggers never fired) are tracked per model and shown to correlate negatively with overall alignment score (Pearson r = −0.58), confirming they do not inflate rankings.

## Method

The benchmark comprises 904 scenarios across 37 behaviours in 6 categories (Honesty, Safety, Non-Manipulation, Robustness, Corrigibility, Scheming), generated through an adaptation of Bloom for ideation, Petri for adversarial probing, and hand-crafting from documented incidents, all undergoing human review. Scoring uses an LLM judge validated against 771 human annotations from 155 Prolific participants (Pearson r = 0.78, MAE = 0.55, 86% within 1 point), with criteria-level F1 of 0.71–0.74 for pass/fail criterion identification. The key analytical contribution is a principal component analysis on the standardised 37×24 score matrix, which reveals a single dominant factor explaining 60.2% of variance (Cronbach's α = 0.978, 95.3% positive inter-behaviour correlations), analogous to the g-factor in cognitive research. Self-preservation is the sole behaviour loading negatively (−0.113), indicating it is orthogonal or opposed to general alignment. The factor's relationship to capability is moderate (r = 0.72 with the Epoch Capabilities Index), leaving roughly half of alignment variance unexplained by raw capability.

## Results

Across 904 multi-turn scenarios and 24 frontier models, alignment scores range from 2.92 (Mistral Large 3) to 4.66 (Claude 4.5 Sonnet) on a 1–5 scale, a 1.74-point gap (44% of scale). Closed-source models average 4.05 versus 3.41 for open-source (0.65-point gap, p<0.001), and 78% of model pairs are significantly different after Bonferroni correction. Factor analysis reveals a single general alignment factor (PC1) explaining 60.2% of variance across 37 behaviours, with self-preservation as the sole negatively-loading exception (loading −0.113). Robustness is the universal weakness: 12 of 24 models—including the top three—have it as their lowest-scoring category, while Non-Manipulation is the hardest category overall (mean 3.52, spread 2.46 points).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Rather than comparing a single method against baselines, the paper benchmarks 24 models from 9 providers on a unified 904-scenario alignment suite. The top three models (Claude 4.5 Sonnet 4.66, Claude 4.5 Opus 4.65, GPT-5.2 4.53) form a clear tier with pass rates ≥ 87%, separating from the dense middle (ranks 6–18 spanning only 0.55 points) and the bottom tier where all Mistral models fall below 3.20. Closed-source models outperform open-source by 0.65 points on average, though OpenAI's open-weight GPT-OSS 120B (3.82, rank 7) narrows the gap, suggesting the divide reflects alignment investment rather than architecture. At the category level, Corrigibility is the easiest (mean 4.24, spread 0.80) while Non-Manipulation is the hardest (mean 3.52, spread 2.46); Robustness is the single most common weakest category (12/24 models), even for top performers like Claude 4.5 Sonnet (4.03 on Robustness vs. 4.88 on Safety). The factor analysis shows a strong general alignment factor (60.2% variance, Cronbach's α = 0.978), but self-preservation loads negatively (−0.113) and correlates inversely with other behaviours, indicating it does not benefit from general alignment training. Correlation with the Epoch Capabilities Index is moderate (r = 0.72), meaning capability explains roughly 52% of alignment variance but leaves nearly half unexplained.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the HHH (Helpful, Honest, Harmless) alignment framework (Askell et al., 2021) by adding robustness, corrigibility, and scheming dimensions, and operationalises 37 specific behaviours into 904 multi-turn adversarial scenarios. For scenario generation it adapts Bloom (Gupta et al., 2025) for ideation and Petri (Fronsdal et al., 2025) for adversarial targeting, while building custom multi-turn rollout and judge infrastructure. It departs from prior alignment benchmarks (TruthfulQA, MACHIAVELLI, SafetyBench, DecodingTrust) by replacing single-turn, multiple-choice, or knowledge-based formats with pressure-based multi-turn conversations, and by applying factor-analytic methodology previously used for LLM capability structure (Burnell et al., 2023a; Ilić & Gignac, 2024) to the alignment domain for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discovery of a general alignment factor (60.2% of variance) with self-preservation as a negative-loading exception has practical implications: it suggests alignment training may transfer across behaviours, but also that self-preservation requires targeted intervention rather than benefiting from general alignment improvements. Robustness emerging as a universal weakness even for top-performing models (Claude 4.5 Sonnet scores only 4.03 vs. 4.88 on Safety) highlights a persistent gap that current training pipelines do not adequately address. Key open questions include whether the factor structure holds with larger model samples, whether it reflects shared training pipelines rather than a genuine construct, and whether the English/Western normative framing generalises across cultures. The public leaderboard and instance-level data release position this as an ongoing evaluation resource rather than a one-time measurement.
