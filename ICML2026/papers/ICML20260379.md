## Problem

Pretrained AI models can generate labels at a fraction of the cost of expert annotation, but they provide no formal guarantees on their accuracy, making wholesale replacement of manual labeling impractical for high-stakes applications. The paper asks whether one can leverage AI predictions to curate datasets while still providing a rigorous, nonasymptotic guarantee that the overall labeling error is bounded.

## Input / Output

The method takes an unlabeled dataset of n points, cheap AI predictions with associated scalar uncertainty scores (e.g., verbalized confidence from GPT-4o, 1−max-softmax from ResNet-152, or pLDDT from AlphaFold), and user-specified error parameters ε and α. It outputs a fully labeled dataset where each point is assigned either the AI prediction (for low-uncertainty points) or a collected expert label (for high-uncertainty points), with the guarantee that the average loss is at most ε with probability at least 1−α. The data is treated as fixed (transductive); randomness enters only through the algorithm's calibration sampling of m expert labels. In the multi-model variant, per-source costs c_j and c_expert are also inputs, and the output additionally includes a learned routing policy and recalibrated uncertainty scores.

## Method

PAC labeling finds a data-driven threshold û on uncertainty scores: points with U_i ≥ û receive expert labels, while points with U_i < û retain AI predictions. The threshold is determined by computing upper confidence bounds on the cumulative error L^u for each candidate u using a small calibration set of m expert labels, then selecting the smallest u whose confidence bound exceeds ε. The key theoretical insight is that monotonicity of L^u in u allows individual (not simultaneous) confidence bounds to suffice, avoiding multiplicity corrections and yielding a clean nonasymptotic guarantee via betting-based confidence intervals. For multiple AI models, a PAC router jointly learns a routing network w_θ and an uncertainty model u_γ by minimizing a sigmoid-relaxed version of the expected labeling cost, with gradients backpropagated through the implicit threshold via the implicit function theorem. An optional multicalibration step adjusts per-cluster uncertainty scores to further reduce the number of expert labels needed.

## Results

On text annotation tasks with GPT-4o (ε=0.05), PAC labeling achieves 13.8–28.1% budget save with realized error of 3.8–4.6%, tightly meeting the 5% error target. On ImageNet with ResNet-152, PAC labeling saves 59.6% of the expert labeling budget with 4.73% error. For continuous-label tasks, PAC labeling saves 16–51% of budget on sentiment analysis and 20–34% on protein folding (AlphaFold), while the AI-only baseline exceeds the error target by 2–10×. The PAC router combining GPT-4o and Claude 3.7 Sonnet achieves 41.6% budget save (vs. 13.8% and 8.4% individually) and 482% cost save in the cost-sensitive setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PAC labeling is compared against two baselines: (1) a naive fixed-threshold method that collects expert labels for all points with uncertainty above 0.1 or 0.05, and (2) an AI-only baseline that uses no expert labels at all. The AI-only baseline is always invalid, with errors of 18.6–37.7% on text tasks and 21.7–35.3% on image tasks, far exceeding the 5% target. The naive threshold at U≥0.1 is invalid on Stance (10.13% error) and Misinformation (7.07% error), while the threshold at U≥0.05 is valid but overly conservative, saving only 2.65–25.10% of budget with errors as low as 0.10%. PAC labeling consistently achieves error within 0.5–1% of the ε target while saving 14–60% of the expert labeling budget, striking the right balance between validity and cost efficiency that neither baseline achieves. In the multi-model setting, the PAC router (41.6% save) substantially outperforms PAC labeling with GPT-4o alone (13.8%) or Claude alone (8.4%), demonstrating that routing between complementary models yields compounding savings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the betting-based confidence interval framework (Waudby-Smith & Ramdas, 2024; Orabona & Jun, 2023) from mean estimation to a novel dataset-labeling problem, leveraging the monotonicity of cumulative loss in the uncertainty threshold to avoid multiplicity corrections—mirroring the logic behind the DKW inequality and risk-controlling prediction sets (Bates et al., 2021). It departs from active learning and transductive learning by treating the data as fixed and the goal as producing a labeled dataset (not fitting a model or making an inference), and from prior noisy-label correction work by requiring no distributional assumptions on the AI model or data. The uncertainty calibration step repurposes multicalibration (Hébert-Johnson et al., 2018), while the multi-model PAC router introduces a sigmoid-relaxed, implicitly-differentiated objective that has no direct analogue in the active learning or learning-to-defer literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean separation between validity (the PAC guarantee holds regardless of uncertainty quality) and efficiency (cost savings scale with uncertainty informativeness) gives practitioners a safe, drop-in wrapper around any AI labeling pipeline without requiring model-specific assumptions. The PAC router's ~3× cost reduction over the best single model on the media bias task demonstrates that multi-model routing is a practically significant lever, and the cost-sensitive variant directly addresses real API pricing. Open questions include how the method behaves when uncertainty scores are adversarially uninformative (validity holds but savings vanish), whether the calibration step can be made fully data-driven without any cluster structure, and how the approach scales to settings with thousands of labeling sources or streaming data.
