## Problem

Existing model-based representation methods (e.g., MR.Q) train latent dynamics by minimizing the Euclidean distance between state-action and next-state representations, but the paper proves this does not necessarily increase their mutual information, leading to biased representations that fail to capture relevant dynamics. Additionally, standard uniform or prioritized experience replay suffers from primacy bias, causing overfitting to early, off-policy experiences that degrade downstream actor-critic learning.

## Input / Output

The method accepts either proprioceptive state vectors (dimensions ranging from 3 for pendulum to 376 for Humanoid-v4) or visual observations consisting of 3 stacked 84×84 RGB frames for DMC-Visual tasks, and outputs a single continuous action vector (dimensions 1–61). A key design choice is that the state encoder f(s) and state-action encoder g(z_s, a) produce 512-dimensional latent representations that are shared between the encoder training phase and the downstream actor-critic phase, with the critic operating on z_sa rather than raw (s, a) pairs. All 73 tasks across MuJoCo, DMC, and HumanoidBench use a single fixed hyperparameter set with UTD ratio 1.

## Method

DR.Q decomposes training into two phases: (i) learning state and state-action representations via a model-based encoder loss, and (ii) training a CDQ-based actor-critic on those representations. The core novelty is an auxiliary InfoNCE loss (Eq. 8) that maximizes mutual information between the predicted next-state representation and the target next-state representation using in-batch cosine similarity, complementing the standard MSE dynamics-consistency loss; Theorem 4.1 formally proves that MSE minimization alone does not guarantee MI maximization, while Lemma 4.2 shows MI maximization strictly reduces conditional entropy H(Z_s'|Z_sa). The second contribution is faded prioritized experience replay (Eq. 10), which multiplies LAP's TD-error-based priority by an exponential decay (1−ε)^i (clipped at ε_low) so that transitions must be both recent and surprising to receive high sampling probability; Theorem 4.3 proves that equal-TD-error older transitions are strictly down-weighted and that expected sample counts remain bounded. The encoder loss combines two-hot cross-entropy reward prediction, stop-gradient MSE dynamics loss, and the InfoNCE term, unrolled over a short horizon H, while the critic uses Huber loss with multi-step returns and clipped double Q-learning.

## Results

DR.Q is evaluated on 73 continuous control tasks across MuJoCo (5 tasks), DMC (28 proprioceptive + visual tasks), and HumanoidBench (28 tasks), using a single set of hyperparameters. Key headline improvements include a 15.5% normalized return gain over SimBaV2 on DMC-Hard tasks, a 58.9% improvement over FoG on HumanoidBench (with dexterous hands), and a 26.8% lead over MR.Q on DMC-Visual tasks. DR.Q consistently outperforms MR.Q across all three benchmarks and is the first method to achieve an average return exceeding 700 on the challenging dog-run task within 1M environment steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DR.Q consistently outperforms MR.Q (Fujimoto et al., 2025) across all benchmarks, with the largest margin being 26.8% on DMC-Visual tasks, confirming that the added InfoNCE mutual information loss and faded PER provide meaningful gains over the base model-based representation framework. Against SimBaV2 (Lee et al., 2025b), DR.Q achieves a 15.5% improvement on DMC-Hard tasks (dog and humanoid locomotion), indicating superior handling of high-dimensional proprioceptive inputs. On HumanoidBench tasks with dexterous hands (observation dim up to 308, action dim 61), DR.Q surpasses FoG (Kang et al., 2025) by 58.9%, a particularly large margin given the redundancy in the state space. DR.Q also matches or exceeds model-based methods like TDMPC2 (Hansen et al., 2024) and DreamerV3 (Hafner et al., 2023) while maintaining a simpler UTD=1 training loop. On sample efficiency (Figure 3), DR.Q reaches competitive returns substantially faster than MR.Q and SimBaV2 on challenging tasks like dog-run, where it is the first method to exceed 700 average return within 1M steps. DR.Q does not include components like target normalization, parameter resets, or hidden-embedding regularization, yet still achieves these results with a single hyperparameter set across all 73 tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

DR.Q extends the model-based representation framework introduced by TD7 (Fujimoto et al., 2023) and MR.Q (Fujimoto et al., 2025), which train state-action encoders via latent dynamics consistency to feed richer representations into downstream actor-critic networks. The key departure is a theoretical argument (Theorem 4.1) that minimizing Euclidean distance between representations does not guarantee maximizing their mutual information, motivating an additional InfoNCE-based contrastive loss in the encoder objective. On the sampling side, DR.Q combines LAP-style prioritized experience replay (Fujimoto et al., 2020) with the exponential forget mechanism from FoG (Kang et al., 2025) into a 'faded PER' strategy, addressing the primacy bias that causes overfitting to early replay-buffer entries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical justification for why purely MSE-based latent dynamics training can produce biased, informationally poor representations, and shows that a simple InfoNCE term plus a faded sampling schedule yields consistent gains across 73 continuous-control tasks with a single hyperparameter set. The 58.9% improvement over FoG on high-dimensional HumanoidBench (w/ hand) tasks and the first reported >700 return on dog-run within 1M steps are practically notable. However, the individual components (InfoNCE, PER, forget mechanism, CDQ) are all established techniques, and the method inherits MR.Q's limitations: it is not designed for hard-exploration or non-Markovian tasks, and it struggles with some visual DMC tasks under the 1M-step budget. Open questions include whether the mutual information objective generalizes to discrete-action domains (e.g., Atari) and whether stronger encoder architectures could further close the gap on the remaining weak tasks.
