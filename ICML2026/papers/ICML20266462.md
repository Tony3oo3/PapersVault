## Problem

Modern AI/ML workflows are increasingly deployed to uncover scientific mechanisms from high-dimensional proxy data, yet mechanistic learning from proxies is generically underdetermined: many incompatible mechanisms induce the same observational distribution, so predictive success and fluent explanations do not constitute mechanism discovery. The problem is acutely amplified by LLMs, which collapse entire equivalence classes of compatible explanations into a single confident narrative. The paper argues the field should prioritize articulating and evaluating identifying structure rather than building ever more complex models.

## Input / Output

The framework takes proxy observations (many-to-one summaries of latent drivers, e.g., discrete phenotype counts or environmental correlates) together with a mechanistic query q(M) such as a counterfactual effect or derivative sign. Rather than outputting a single mechanism, the prescribed output is a set-valued answer: the mechanism equivalence class M(P_X) of all observationally compatible mechanisms, an explicit identifying-structure specification (mechanism-side and observation-side assumptions plus design regime), at least one falsifier, and a multiplicity statement labeling which qualitative conclusions are stable across the compatible set. In the empirical pea-plant example, the input is parent phenotype pairs plus optional cross-type design labels, and the output is a counterfactual offspring probability vector whose spread across independently trained MLPs quantifies residual mechanistic ambiguity.

## Method

The paper establishes three formal claims. Proposition 1 shows that even with deterministic, invertible, modular measurement, proxies identify latent blocks only up to within-block bimeasurable reparameterization and block permutation. Proposition 2 extends this to arbitrary conditionally independent measurement noise, proving non-identification is a population-level property that does not vanish with more data. Proposition 3 formalizes 'narrative collapse' as a minimax ambiguity: when two proxy-compatible mechanisms disagree on a query, no single-valued explanation rule can be uniformly correct, yielding a worst-case 0-1 loss lower bound of 1/2. The paper then defines identifying structure as a joint specification of mechanism-side restrictions, observation-side restrictions, and a discriminating data-collection regime, and demonstrates empirically with a Mendelian pea-plant simulation that adding cross-type design labels collapses the counterfactual prediction spread from 0.095 to 0.029 (near-optimal seeds). The contribution culminates in a set of 'Mechanistic AI' norms—mechanism cards, multiplicity statements, and mechanism-discriminating evaluation—that any workflow presenting outputs as mechanistic must satisfy.

## Results

In the pea-plant simulation, adding cross-type design labels (Regime B) collapses the mean ℓ₁ spread of counterfactual predicted probability vectors across 60 independently trained MLPs: the near-optimal spread drops from 0.095 (phenotype-only, Regime A) to 0.029 (with design, Regime B), and the all-seed spread drops from 0.189 to 0.095. For the specific testcross query RY × WG, the no-design MLP mean assigns 0.49 probability to RY (far from the Mendelian 0.25), while the with-design MLP mean recovers approximately uniform probabilities (0.26, 0.26, 0.24, 0.24). In the synthetic high-dimensional simulation (Appendix F), all model families (MLP, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting) achieve nearly identical in-domain test MSE (≈0.003–0.005) yet exhibit substantial gradient variance (up to 1.2×10²) and divergent off-manifold intervention responses, confirming that predictive agreement does not imply mechanistic agreement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper rather than a method paper, so there are no external baselines in the traditional sense. The central empirical comparison is between two observational regimes using the same MLP architecture (32, 32 hidden units): Regime A (phenotype-only F₂ self-cross data, no cross-type labels) versus Regime B (cross-type labels plus additional labeled crosses including testcross support). Under Regime A, even near-optimal predictors (within ε=0.01 of best held-out log loss) disagree substantially on the counterfactual query, with a mean ℓ₁ spread of 0.095; under Regime B, the same architecture and training protocol yields a spread of only 0.029, a roughly 70% reduction. The paper's argument is that this gap is not an artifact of model capacity or optimization but reflects the presence or absence of identifying structure: the cross-type label encodes experimental lineage information that phenotype proxies alone cannot recover. The synthetic simulation in Appendix F reinforces this by showing that three different model families (MLP, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting) all cluster tightly in in-domain MSE yet diverge in gradient estimates and off-manifold behavior, attributing the disagreement to underdetermination rather than to differences in inductive bias.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes classical identification theory from econometrics (Heckman), causal inference (Pearl's do-calculus, Shpitser & Pearl's ID algorithm), and measurement error (Fuller, Carroll et al.) to argue that the ML-for-science community has underemphasized identifying structure relative to model expressiveness. It explicitly departs from the 'agnostic' ML-for-discovery framing (Grimmer et al., 2021) and the broader narrative that scaling or flexibility alone can uncover mechanisms, while positioning successful scientific-ML systems (SINDy, PINNs, symbolic regression, AlphaFold) as cases where identifying structure is already present but under-credited. The paper also draws a deliberate distinction from the Rashomon effect and underspecification literature, arguing the proxy gap is a more fundamental, population-level non-identification that persists even with infinite data from the same channel. The LLM-specific 'narrative collapse' concept is positioned as distinct from model collapse (Shumailov et al.) and algorithmic monoculture (Kleinberg & Raghavan), representing a new hazard introduced by the trivial availability of fluent single-narrative outputs for underidentified evidence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central practical contribution is a set of concrete reporting norms—mechanism cards, multiplicity statements, and mechanism-discriminating evaluation—that could reshape how scientific-ML papers claim 'discovery' and how reviewers assess such claims. The open question is whether these norms are adoptable in practice: the identification framework is query-specific and can be demanding, and the paper acknowledges that partial identification, triangulation, and provisional claims remain scientifically valuable. A key limitation is that the empirical illustration (pea-plant simulation) is deliberately minimal and illustrative rather than a stress test on realistic high-dimensional scientific data, so the practical difficulty of articulating identifying structure in complex domains remains underexplored. The LLM narrative-collapse argument, while conceptually fresh, lacks empirical measurement of how often current systems actually collapse in the defined sense, leaving the severity of the hazard somewhat speculative.
