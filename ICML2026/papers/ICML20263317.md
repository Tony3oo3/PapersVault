## Problem

Existing concept-based explainability methods for Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) rely on purely algebraic factorizations (NMF, PCA, Semi-NMF) that decompose neural activations into basis vectors but lack an explicit reasoning structure connecting concepts to predictions. These approaches treat concept extraction as a dimensionality-reduction problem, failing to expose how concepts interact through logical rules to produce model outputs, leaving stakeholders unable to understand the model's actual decision-making process.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained LMM (e.g., LLaVA, Qwen2-VL), a target token of interest, and an image-caption dataset (COCO, ~120K images). It extracts hidden states from the final transformer layer at the position where the target token is first predicted, but only for images where the token appears in both the ground-truth caption and the model's prediction, yielding a representation matrix Z ∈ R^{B×M}. The output is a neuro-fuzzy concept dictionary U ∈ R^{B×J} (J=20 concepts), normalized rule firing strengths V ∈ R^{J×M}, and for each concept, both visual grounding (top-5 maximally activating images) and textual grounding (top-15 decoded words via the unembedding matrix), structured as interpretable IF-THEN fuzzy rules.

## Method

Neuro-FeX reformulates concept learning as a Takagi-Sugeno-Kang (TSK) fuzzy inference problem, decomposing LMM representations through a two-stage fuzzification-defuzzification process. Fuzzification uses Gaussian membership functions (initialized via K-means or top singular vectors) to compute normalized firing strengths for J latent fuzzy rules, while defuzzification reconstructs Z as a weighted combination of a concept dictionary U where each column is the consequent parameter of a fuzzy rule. The decomposition Z ≈ UV is solved via alternating minimization: U is updated by closed-form ridge regression given V, and V is updated by non-negative sparse coding (L1 penalty) given U, with non-negativity constraints ensuring activations remain interpretable as firing strengths. Unlike NMF or PCA, the Gaussian membership functions impose geometric locality in the representation space, embedding monosemanticity directly into the activation mechanism, and the resulting IF-THEN rule structure provides rule-level interpretability beyond what algebraic factorizations offer.

## Results

On LLaVA (COCO test set), Neuro-FeX-v1 achieves the highest mean CLIPScore for the top-1 activated concept across all four tokens: 0.617 (Dog), 0.658 (Cat), 0.667 (Train), and 0.664 (Bus), surpassing CoX-LMM by up to +0.046 (Bus: 0.664 vs 0.621). On Qwen2.5-VL with 30 diverse tokens, Neuro-FeX-v1 reaches 0.643 mean CLIPScore versus 0.616 for CoX-LMM. For MonoSemanticity (K=1024), Neuro-FeX attains the highest Max MS across all three vision backbones (0.860 CLIP ViT-L, 0.923 SigLIP, 0.918 Qwen3) and the highest Mean MS on CLIP ViT-L (0.188) and SigLIP SoViT-400m (0.711, +16.5% over SAE baselines).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CoX-LMM (Parekh et al., 2024), Neuro-FeX consistently wins on CLIPScore for multimodal grounding: on LLaVA, all Neuro-FeX variants exceed CoX-LMM for Train and Bus tokens (e.g., v1: 0.667 vs 0.655 for Train), and on Qwen2.5-VL the gap widens to 0.643 vs 0.616 across 30 diverse tokens. However, Neuro-FeX-v1 and v2 lose on concept disentanglement (overlap score), with CoX-LMM achieving 0.096–0.196 overlap versus 0.317–0.494 for the K-Means-initialized variants; Neuro-FeX-v0 (SVD-initialized) offers the best trade-off with moderate overlap (0.062–0.181) while retaining strong CLIPScore. Against BatchTopK SAE and Matryoshka SAE (Bussmann et al., 2024/2025) on MonoSemanticity, Neuro-FeX achieves the highest Max MS on all three backbones (0.860, 0.923, 0.918) and the highest Mean MS on CLIP ViT-L (0.188 vs 0.160/0.120) and SigLIP (0.711 vs 0.610/0.610), though on Qwen3 it is marginally behind Matryoshka SAE on Mean MS (0.544 vs 0.549) while still leading on Max MS (0.918 vs 0.889).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Takagi-Sugeno-Kang (TSK) neuro-fuzzy inference architecture (Jang & Sun, 1995) — previously applied to control systems and biomedical signal processing — as a post-hoc interpretability lens for large multimodal models. It builds directly on the CoX-LMM pipeline (Parekh et al., 2024) for extracting token-specific hidden states and performing multimodal grounding via unembedding, but replaces the purely algebraic Semi-NMF factorization with a two-stage fuzzification-defuzzification process that imposes explicit IF-THEN rule structure on the decomposition. The key departure from prior concept-extraction work (CAVs, CRAFT, CCR) is the shift from identifying *which* concepts are present to exposing *how* they combine through fuzzy inference rules to produce representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a principled bridge between symbolic rule-based reasoning and opaque transformer representations, potentially enabling rule-level auditing of LMMs in high-stakes deployments. However, several limitations temper its impact: the framework is token-specific (one dictionary per target token), the neuro-fuzzy structure is imposed post-hoc rather than learned end-to-end, and the primary experiments focus on frozen-LLM architectures. Open questions include whether the extracted fuzzy rules are genuinely 'implicit' in the model or an imposed interpretive lens, whether the approach scales to larger or fine-tuned LMMs, and whether rule-level explanations provide actionable insight beyond what concept activation maps already offer.
