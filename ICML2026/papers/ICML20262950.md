## Problem

Existing LLM agent defenses operate as static, coarse-grained classifiers that enforce fixed safety margins, leading to severe over-refusal of benign requests or under-detection of subtle attacks in ambiguous contexts. The core gap is the absence of a mechanism that dynamically reconstructs precise, context-aware decision boundaries between benign and malicious intents in real time without the latency and resource costs of heavy external safety agents or model retraining.

## Input / Output

The system consumes a user query and the agent's trajectory (a sequence of reasoning steps, tool actions, and observations), mapping them through a lightweight 2-layer MLP safety projector into a unit-normalized latent space. It produces a binary safety verdict (refuse/execute) via a two-stage pipeline: a fast path that instantly clears high-confidence benign queries, or an LLM judgment step that evaluates the query against retrieved prohibition and exemption rules. Additionally, it outputs a calibrated harmful risk score in [0,1] (distance-based softmax to learnable benign/harmful prototypes) and a benign similarity score derived from nearest-neighbor retrieval against a global benign database.

## Method

SafeHarbor is a training-free, plug-and-play framework with three coordinated stages. First, an adversarial rule generator applies three social engineering paradigms (Goal Decomposition, Privilege Escalation, Contextual Reframing) to seed harmful trajectories, with an LLM-driven attacker selecting the paradigm that maximizes attack success rate to ensure diverse coverage. Second, generated rules are organized into a hierarchical memory tree where each leaf node stores a dual-policy unit (a prohibition rule paired with a benign exemption), and an information-entropy-based gain metric governs dynamic topology evolution—triggering new cluster creation, new leaf instantiation, or rule refinement/merging. A contrastive safety projector (2-layer MLP) is trained with binary cross-entropy plus a margin-based center-wise contrastive loss to structure the latent geometry around learnable benign and harmful prototypes. Third, at inference time, a dual-scoring gating mechanism routes queries: those with low harmful score and high benign similarity bypass to a fast path, while ambiguous queries trigger an LLM judgment step that evaluates the query against the top-k retrieved prohibition/exemption rules using in-context learning on the frozen base model.

## Results

On AgentHarm, SAFEHARBOR achieves a 93.2% harmful refusal rate and 63.6% benign score on GPT-4o, 86.9% harmful refusal and 53.0% benign score on Mistral-8B, and 89.2% harmful refusal with only 9.1% benign refusal on Qwen2.5-7B. On AgentSafetyBench, it reaches 62.05% Refusal-Env and 89.78% Refusal-Text on GPT-4o, outperforming all baselines by 14–21 percentage points on environment-based tasks. Inference latency is 306.67 ms with a 14 GB VRAM footprint, roughly 20× faster than GuardAgent (6433 ms) and 5.7× faster than AgentAlign (1728 ms).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LlamaGuard-3-8B, SAFEHARBOR trades a small margin in harmful refusal (93.2% vs. 95.5% on GPT-4o; 89.2% vs. 96.0% on Qwen2.5-7B) for substantially better benign utility (63.6% vs. 52.4% benign score on GPT-4o; 9.1% vs. 22.7% benign refusal on Qwen2.5-7B), while also being 1.2× faster (307 ms vs. 379 ms) and using half the VRAM (14 GB vs. 30 GB). Against GuardAgent, SAFEHARBOR wins on every metric: higher harmful refusal, higher benign score, and ~20× lower latency (307 ms vs. 6433 ms). Against A-Mem, SAFEHARBOR achieves higher harmful refusal (93.2% vs. 86.9% on GPT-4o) and comparable benign score (63.6% vs. 61.3%), while also reducing retrieval noise from 43.5% to 25.8%. Against standard RAG, SAFEHARBOR dominates on both safety (93.2% vs. 89.8% harmful refusal on GPT-4o) and utility (63.6% vs. 42.2% benign score), with a 3× lower noise ratio. Against AgentAlign (SFT), SAFEHARBOR avoids the severe over-alignment seen on Qwen2.5-7B (49.4% vs. 20.7% benign score) while maintaining competitive harmful refusal (89.2% vs. 90.3%), and does so without any fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes ideas from three distinct research threads—adversarial attack generation (Goal Decomposition, Privilege Escalation, Contextual Reframing), hierarchical clustering with information-theoretic split criteria (borrowed from decision tree induction), and contrastive metric learning—into a unified safety framework for LLM agents. It departs from external guardrail approaches (Llama-Guard-3, GuardAgent) that rely on static classifiers or heavy code execution, and from intrinsic alignment methods (AgentAlign) that require costly fine-tuning, by proposing a training-free, retrieval-augmented mechanism that dynamically reconstructs safety boundaries per query. The hierarchical memory tree with entropy-driven self-evolution is the paper's most distinctive architectural contribution, repurposing online decision-tree induction principles for safety rule management rather than classification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a critical practical bottleneck: current agent safety defenses either sacrifice utility through over-refusal or incur prohibitive latency from external guardrail models. By achieving 63.6% benign utility on GPT-4o with >93% harmful refusal at ~307ms latency (20× faster than GuardAgent), SafeHarbor offers a deployable, plug-and-play solution that does not require model retraining. Open questions include how the memory tree scales to multi-agent or multi-turn adversarial settings, whether the AgentAlign-derived rules generalize to entirely new tool ecosystems, and how the system behaves under adaptive adversaries who observe and exploit the specific exemption rules. The reliance on a frozen base model for LLM judgment also means the framework inherits any reasoning limitations of the target model.
