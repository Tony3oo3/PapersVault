## Problem

When a practitioner faces a new task with limited data, they must decide which model from a zoo of candidates to use, often relying on benchmark evaluations as side information. Existing transfer learning assumptions (convex mixtures, covariate/label shift, causal invariance) are too restrictive and do not reflect how models actually rank inconsistently across benchmarks. This paper formalizes the weaker and more realistic assumption of weak monotonicity—consistently better models on a suite of benchmarks tend to be better on the target—and asks what sample complexity is achievable under this assumption.

## Input / Output

The learner receives n i.i.d. samples from the target distribution Q, exact benchmark risk vectors R = (R_1, …, R_K) for every model in a hypothesis class F (corresponding to infinite source samples), and black-box access to model evaluations only. The output is either a single selected model (Pareto ERM, adaptive Margin ERM) or a distribution over the Pareto set yielding an aggregated predictor (Pareto EW, Q-aggregation variant). The key constraint is that the method must work without access to model representations or internal parameters, relying solely on scalar benchmark scores.

## Method

The paper introduces the Pareto covering number as a geometry-adaptive complexity measure on the Pareto front, and proves its limiting distribution (Theorem 1) induces a non-uniform prior that concentrates mass on proper trade-offs while avoiding improper ones. For transfer learning, two estimators are proposed: Pareto ERM (ERM restricted to a t-Pareto cover) and Pareto EW (exponential weights with the geometry-induced prior), both achieving excess risk bounds in terms of the Pareto covering number and the modulus of monotonicity, with worst-case rates of √(K/n) for bounded losses. For model selection aggregation, a Q-aggregation-inspired estimator maps to the Pareto set rather than the convex hull, exploiting a strongly concave gap between the Pareto front and the convex hull to achieve fast log(K)/n rates without requiring Bernstein conditions or strongly convex losses. An adaptive Margin ERM for finite classes handles approximate monotonicity by progressively expanding the candidate set from the Pareto frontier as n grows, guaranteeing consistency while retaining frontier-bias benefits at small sample sizes.

## Results

The paper is primarily theoretical, establishing that under (approximate) weak monotonicity, the sample complexity of transfer learning is governed by the Pareto covering number N_Par(t, F, R) and the modulus of monotonicity ω̄_Q(t), yielding an excess risk bound of ω̄_Q(t) + √(2 log(2N_Par/δ)/n) (Theorem 2). In the worst case (log N_Par = K−1), this gives a √(K/n) rate, matching the lower bound of Mansour et al. (2021). For model selection aggregation, a strongly concave Pareto gap assumption yields fast rates of log(K)/n without requiring a Bernstein condition or strongly convex loss (Theorem 4). Empirically on HELM (29 LLMs, 7 scenarios) and VHELM (26 VLMs, 9 scenarios), frontier-biased selection (ERM-Pareto, ERM-adaptive) substantially reduces excess risk at small target sample sizes n compared to ERM over the full model class, with the adaptive method recovering ERM-all performance as n grows.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four model-selection rules: ERM-all (standard ERM over all models), ERM-Pareto (ERM restricted to the Pareto frontier), ERM-adaptive (the proposed adaptive margin ERM), and Min-Max (robust worst-case selection from Sagawa et al., 2019 / Mohri et al., 2019 that uses no target data). On both HELM and VHELM, ERM-Pareto and ERM-adaptive significantly outperform ERM-all at small n, where ERM-all suffers from high variance; the gap narrows as n increases and all methods converge. The adaptive method is guaranteed to be no worse than ERM-all for large n (by construction, F_{γ_n} → F), while providing large gains in the low-data regime. Min-Max, which ignores target data entirely, is outperformed by all target-data-using methods once n is sufficiently large, though it can be competitive at very small n. For K=1 on HELM, ERM-Pareto degenerates to a constant (the Pareto set is a singleton), matching the Min-Max baseline, while ERM-adaptive still benefits from target data by expanding the candidate set.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on classical transfer learning (Ben-David et al., 2010; Mansour et al., 2008, 2021) and model selection aggregation (Tsybakov, 2003; Lecué and Rigollet, 2014), but departs from both interpolation-based (distributional similarity, mixture assumptions) and extrapolation-based (causal/invariant) paradigms by introducing weak monotonicity as a strictly more general assumption. The moduli of monotonicity are closely related to the modulus of transfer from Hanneke and Kpotufe (2024), and the fast-rate aggregator in Section 5 is inspired by Q-aggregation (Dai et al., 2012; Lecué and Rigollet, 2014), but the key departure is that the Pareto front geometry—captured by the new Pareto covering number and its limiting distribution—replaces the role of VC dimension or Rademacher complexity in governing sample complexity. The paper also recovers and improves upon the √(K/n) rate of Mansour et al. (2021) for mixture targets while showing their ℓ₁-simplex discretization is suboptimal even in that setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As foundation model zoos grow and practitioners increasingly rely on black-box benchmark evaluations for model selection, this paper provides the first formal statistical theory explaining when and why benchmark performance transfers to new tasks, with the Pareto front geometry as the governing complexity parameter. The empirical validation on HELM and VHELM (moduli near zero at t=0, frontier-biased ERM outperforming full ERM at small n) supports the practical relevance of the assumption. Key limitations include the assumption of exact (infinite-sample) benchmark risks, and the theoretical results for fast rates require a strongly concave Pareto gap that may be hard to verify in practice. Open directions include extending to finite source samples, connecting to multi-objective learning, and understanding how the theory applies when benchmarks themselves have estimation noise.
