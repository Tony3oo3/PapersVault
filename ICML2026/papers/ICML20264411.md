## Problem

Existing preference-based RL methods train utility functions on trajectory-segment-level comparisons but then query them at individual state-action pairs during policy optimization, inducing a distribution shift that makes temporal credit assignment fundamentally ambiguous. This training–inference mismatch is largely overlooked in the literature and severely degrades policy learning, especially in low-data regimes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes fixed-length trajectory segments (e.g., 64 state-action pairs) drawn from an offline dataset collected by multiple policies of varying quality, along with pairwise preference labels (τ⁺ ≻ τ⁻) generated by an oracle or human annotators. It produces a parametric policy π_θ(a|s) whose parameters are updated via segment-level weighted maximum likelihood, where every state-action pair within a segment receives the same importance weight derived from the segment's total advantage. The advantage function itself can be either an encoder-only Transformer or a simple MLP mapping state-action pairs to scalar values.

## Method

PAWS first trains an advantage function A_φ on segment-level preference pairs using a Bradley-Terry binary cross-entropy loss over the difference of cumulative segment advantages. It then solves a trust-region-constrained optimization (adapted from relative entropy policy search) in segment space, yielding an optimal reweighted segment distribution p*(τ) ∝ p_D(τ) exp(A_φ(τ)/λ). Policy extraction reduces to a weighted maximum-likelihood objective in which all state-action pairs within a segment share a single importance weight exp(A_φ(τ)/λ), thereby preserving distributional consistency between training and inference. The Lagrange multiplier λ is set automatically by targeting a user-specified effective sample size n_eff, which controls how aggressively the policy deviates from the data distribution and is more intuitive than tuning the KL bound directly. This segment-level update mechanism is the key architectural distinction from standard PbRL pipelines that decompose segment advantages into per-step weights.

## Results

On 10 Meta-World manipulation tasks, PAWS (Transformer) achieves 78.2% average success rate with 500 preferences and 51.6% with only 50 preferences, outperforming the strongest baseline (Preference Transformer at 73.5% / 50.1%) by ~5 points in both regimes. The MLP variant matches this at 78.3% / 50.8%. On four D4RL locomotion tasks, PAWS (MLP) reaches 1023 return on Walker2d and 882 on Ant (500 preferences), exceeding the best baselines (Pref Trans.: 726 / 784) by 40–12%. Relative to Behavior Cloning, PAWS yields a 36.5–36.6% improvement in average success rate at 500 preferences and 10–11.7% at 50 preferences.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PAWS outperforms all five main baselines on average across both Meta-World and locomotion tasks in both preference budgets. The closest competitor is Preference Transformer (Kim et al., 2023), which achieves 73.5% average success at 500 preferences versus PAWS's 78.2–78.3%, a gap of roughly 5 points; in the 50-preference regime the gap narrows to ~1.5 points (50.1% vs. 51.6%). P-IQL (Kostrikov et al., 2021, adapted with advantage targets) reaches 70.7% at 500 preferences and is competitive on individual tasks like Door Open (83%) but trails PAWS (96–98%) by 13–15 points there. CPL (Hejna et al., 2024) and its KL-regularized variant CPL+KL both score around 67–68% at 500 preferences and degrade sharply to 42–43% at 50 preferences, sometimes falling below Behavior Cloning. IPL (Hejna & Sadigh, 2023) is the weakest of the main baselines at 59.8% (500) and 45.7% (50), losing to PAWS by 18–19 points in the high-data setting. DPPO (An et al., 2023) is excluded from the main tables because it requires a large unlabeled trajectory dataset; without it, DPPO achieves only 9% success on Sweep Into and 1% on Peg Insert Side even with 500 preferences.

## Relation to Prior Work

PAWS builds on the relative entropy policy search framework (Peters et al., 2010) and adapts its trust-region-constrained, EM-like optimization to segment-level updates in the offline preference setting, extending prior work on reward-weighted regression (Peters & Schaal, 2007) and advantage-weighted regression (Neumann & Peters, 2008) from per-step to per-segment granularity. It follows Knox et al. (2024) in modeling preferences via advantage functions rather than cumulative rewards, and shares the weighted maximum-likelihood policy objective with AWAC (Nair et al., 2020) and MAPPO (Abdolmaleki et al., 2018), but applies the importance weights to entire segments rather than individual state-action pairs. The key departure from existing PbRL methods (CPL, IPL, P-IQL, Preference Transformer) is the explicit identification of a training–inference distribution shift: prior methods train utility models on segment-level preferences but query them at the step level during policy optimization, whereas PAWS keeps both stages at the segment level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes the long-standing temporal credit assignment problem in PbRL not as an inherent difficulty of sparse preference labels but as a distributional mismatch between how utilities are trained and how they are used, offering a clean fix (segment-level policy updates) that is well-supported by ablations (Table 4, Figure 3c, Spearman correlations). The effective-sample-size hyperparameter provides an intuitive, data-driven control for update aggressiveness that replaces manual KL-bound tuning. Limitations include uniform per-segment weighting (less suitable when quality varies within a segment), the need to select n_eff, and evaluation restricted to simulated robotic tasks with oracle or small-scale human labels. The authors explicitly connect the insight to LLM RLHF, where per-token reward models trained on response-level preferences face the same mismatch, suggesting broader applicability beyond robotics.
