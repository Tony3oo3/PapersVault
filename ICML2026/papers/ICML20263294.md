## Problem

Existing multi-LLM collaboration paradigms—Immediate Routing and Model Cascades—treat all models as black boxes, relying solely on user prompts or sequential invocation to make routing decisions. They fail to exploit the rich intermediate representations (e.g., token-wise hidden states) that a locally deployed SLM can provide at essentially zero additional cost, and their rigid structures (single-shot prediction or fixed linear chains) do not align with today's heterogeneous model market where price-performance ordering is not guaranteed.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a user query, the token-wise hidden states from the penultimate transformer layer of the local SLM, and optionally the SLM's generated textual answer. The scoring model (a single transformer block + MLP) outputs a scalar score in the single-remote setting or a vector of scores in the multi-remote setting; the final routing decision is made by comparing scores against a threshold or by computing a cost-adjusted utility that incorporates per-token pricing of each remote LLM. The system ultimately returns either the local SLM's response or the response from the selected remote LLM.

## Method

The key insight is that optimal routing depends on the per-query performance gap between the local SLM and remote LLM, not on absolute quality. Theorem 3.1 proves that the cost-performance AUC is maximized if and only if predicted scores are inversely ordered with performance gaps, leading to a supervised MSE regression objective on the negative gap. The decision module reuses a single transformer layer from the local SLM's architecture (operating on penultimate-layer hidden states) followed by an MLP, adding only ~3% parameter overhead. For the multi-remote extension, the output head is expanded to produce one score per remote LLM, and a utility function U = -s - k·C combines the predicted gap with estimated per-request monetary cost to select the best remote model or retain the local response.

## Results

The primary metric is the cost-performance AUC (Area Under the Cost-Performance Curve), which captures overall routing quality across all budget levels. SDR achieves a cost-performance AUC of 0.7027 (MMLU), 0.9165 (SQuAD), and 0.6999 (GSM8K) with Llama-3.1-8B as the local SLM, and 0.6900, 0.9225, and 0.7993 with Qwen-2.5-7B, consistently outperforming all five baselines across every dataset and local SLM combination. The largest margin appears on SQuAD, where SDR's AUC (~0.92) nearly doubles that of the best immediate-routing baseline (~0.63–0.66). In the multi-remote setting, SDR also dominates at nearly every sampled cost point across all three datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SDR wins against all five baselines on every dataset and local SLM configuration. Against FrugalGPT (the strongest cascade baseline), SDR gains +0.056 AUC on MMLU (Llama), +0.439 on SQuAD (Llama), and +0.052 on GSM8K (Llama); the SQuAD gap is the most dramatic, reflecting FrugalGPT's reliance on absolute quality prediction rather than performance-gap modeling. Against the immediate-routing baselines (RouteLLM, RouterBench, IRT-Router), SDR's advantage is more uniform (+0.05 to +0.10 AUC on MMLU/GSM8K, +0.28 to +0.34 on SQuAD), because these methods lack access to the SLM's hidden states and generated answer. HybridLLM is the weakest baseline overall, particularly on GSM8K (0.43–0.50 AUC), where its DeBERTa-based relative-quality estimator struggles with numerical reasoning. In the multi-remote setting (Figure 4), SDR dominates RouterBench and IRT-Router at nearly every sampled cost point, confirming that the advantage generalizes beyond the single-remote formulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by proposing a third paradigm (Selective Deferred Routing) alongside the two established approaches to cost-efficient multi-LLM collaboration: Immediate Routing (RouteLLM, HybridLLM, RouterBench, IRT-Router) and Model Cascades (FrugalGPT, Mixture of Thought, AutoMix). Its key conceptual departure is twofold: (1) it exploits the local SLM's internal hidden states as routing signals rather than treating all models as black boxes that only exchange text, and (2) it formulates the routing objective as AUC optimization over a cost-performance curve, with a theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) showing that the optimal decision depends on the performance *gap* between local and remote models rather than absolute quality. The decision module itself is a lightweight single transformer block reused from the SLM, a design choice that has no direct precedent in the routing literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for individuals and small organizations that want to reduce LLM API costs: SDR recovers 95% of the performance gap between a local 7B model and GPT-4o while incurring less than half the end-to-end latency of baseline routers. The finding that SLM hidden states are far more informative routing signals than surface text (nearly 0.3 AUC improvement in ablations) suggests a broader principle: internal representations of a model processing a task are a rich, underexploited source of meta-information for system-level decisions. Open questions include how the approach scales to long-output tasks where local decoding latency dominates, whether post-training the SLM for better self-assessment can further tighten the cost-performance frontier, and how robust the routing decisions are under distribution shift in production workloads.
