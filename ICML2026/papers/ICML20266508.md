## Problem

Current embodied reward models are trained almost exclusively on successful robot behaviors, causing them to systematically over-reward unsafe interactions, poor execution quality, and shortcut strategies that humans would penalize. This over-optimism worsens as task complexity increases, from simple pick-and-place to multi-step tool use, because the models lack exposure to the negative examples needed for calibration. The paper asks whether even modest amounts of real 'bad' robot data can close this gap.

## Input / Output

The reward model consumes a T-length sequence of synchronized RGB observations (third-person and wrist-mounted camera views) alongside a free-form text task instruction, and optionally in-context negative examples at three fidelity levels: distilled text failure descriptions, text paired with real robot failure video snippets, or text+video augmented with dense per-timestep reward labels. The output is a dense per-step reward sequence r_{1:T} whose accumulated sum induces a scalar return per rollout, enabling pairwise preference ordering that is then compared against human-annotated ordinal rankings from RoboArena evaluators.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is an empirical study showing that injecting 'bad' robot data via in-context learning into the GVL reward model (GPT-5 backbone) improves alignment with human preferences without any retraining. Three levels of negative data are tested: (1) LLM-distilled text summaries of common failure modes from RoboArena evaluator feedback, (2) text paired with real robot failure video snippets, and (3) text+video augmented with dense reward labels obtained via preference-guided self-distillation (querying GVL 10 times at temperature 0.8, retaining the reward sequence whose induced A/B ordering matches the human preference, and using the less-preferred rollout's reward trace as the in-context negative). Text-only negatives yield negligible improvement, text+video pairs give ~8% accuracy gains on simple tasks, and dense negative reward examples deliver ~10% gains even on the hardest Tool Use and Pour tasks, where failures are subtle (e.g., dropping an object mid-trajectory) rather than visually obvious. The evaluation protocol uses pairwise human comparisons from 723 RoboArena tasks across 7 complexity-ordered categories, measuring preference ordering accuracy as the fraction of human-preferred pairs correctly ranked by the model's induced returns.

## Results

On the RoboArena benchmark, all three SOTA embodied reward models (ReWind, GVL, Dopamine) achieve preference ordering accuracy of 0.72–0.77 on simple Pick/Place tasks but degrade to 0.52–0.62 on the most complex Tool Use tasks, approaching random guessing (0.5). Injecting real robot failure data into GVL via in-context learning yields ~8% accuracy improvement on simple tasks (text + video negatives) and ~10% improvement on the hardest Tool Use and Pour tasks (dense reward negatives), while text-only failure descriptions provide little to no gain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new reward model that outperforms the baselines; instead, it demonstrates that all three SOTA embodied reward models systematically over-reward bad behaviors. ReWind (Zhang et al., 2025), trained with synthetic negatives on Open-X-Embodiment, fails because its perturbed rollouts cannot capture closed-loop failure modes. GVL (Ma et al., 2024), using GPT-5 as a zero-shot VLM reward predictor, inherits generic scoring criteria from pretraining that are poorly calibrated on safety-critical failures. Dopamine (Tan et al., 2025), fine-tuned with heuristic progress labels, is biased toward 'getting there' signals while overlooking execution quality and safety. All three models show the same degradation pattern: high accuracy (~0.75) on Pick/Place dropping to near-random (~0.55) on Tool Use. The paper's key comparative finding is that injecting real failure data into GVL (the only model amenable to in-context learning without retraining) recovers ~8–10% accuracy, whereas ReWind and Dopamine cannot be similarly corrected without retraining.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GVL (Ma et al., 2024) in-context value learning framework by demonstrating that injecting real robot failure data as in-context examples improves reward model calibration, and positions this finding within the broader embodied reward modeling landscape that includes preference-based models (ReWind) and fine-tuned VLM reward predictors (Dopamine). Conceptually, it departs from the dominant imitation-learning paradigm that filters out negative data by arguing the data curation strategy itself—not model architecture—is the bottleneck for reliable embodied reward modeling. The paper draws an explicit analogy to the non-embodied RLHF ecosystem where 'bad' data (toxic outputs, logical fallacies) is abundant and cheap, highlighting that embodied settings face a unique physical-world data collection constraint that has no digital analogue.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since reward models serve as the optimization objective for both RL post-training and test-time compute in embodied foundation models, their systematic over-optimism sets a hard upper bound on how much these models can be improved or safely deployed. The paper's call to action—releasing curated failure datasets, building synthetic failure generators, and developing decentralized physical evaluation infrastructure—addresses a community-wide data gap that will only widen as VLA models generate more complex behaviors. Key open questions include whether the in-context findings generalize to retraining regimes, how to scale physical failure data collection beyond tabletop manipulation to navigation and long-horizon tasks, and whether synthetic failure generation (via world models or real-to-sim) can close the gap without the cost of physical deployment.
