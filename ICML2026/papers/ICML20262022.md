## Problem

Smooth nonconvex optimization under noisy pairwise-comparison feedback remains poorly understood: prior comparison-based methods either assume exact (noiseless) comparisons or scale with the ambient dimension d, while practical preference oracles become unreliable near ties and many objectives vary meaningfully only along a low-dimensional active subspace. This paper asks whether a simple random-search method can simultaneously handle gap-dependent comparison noise and exploit intrinsic dimensionality to obtain stationarity guarantees.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262022/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a smooth nonconvex objective f: R^d → R (assumed to satisfy a ridge structure f(x)=g(Ax) with intrinsic dimension k≤d) and a stochastic pairwise-comparison oracle that returns either a binary sign ±1 (uniform-margin model) or a signed confidence score in [−1,1] whose magnitude encodes reliability (gap-dependent model). It never observes function values or gradients. The output is a sequence of iterates whose average gradient norm falls below a target ε, with comparison complexity O(k/(p²ε²)) for the uniform-margin oracle and O(k²/ε⁴) total comparisons for the confidence-weighted variant.

## Method

NCRS is a direct-search method that, at each iteration, samples a Gaussian direction s_t ~ N(0,I_d), forms the candidate θ^t + α_t s_t, queries the comparison oracle once, and accepts the candidate if it is ranked no worse than the current iterate (improve-or-stay). The key structural insight is that under the ridge model, the objective is invariant along ker(A), so the accept/reject decision depends only on the projected direction Ps_t where P = A^T(AA^T)^{-1}A is the orthogonal projector onto the k-dimensional active subspace; consequently the convergence rate depends on k rather than d, even though the algorithm never observes A. For the more realistic gap-dependent confidence oracle, the method collects N i.i.d. signed scores per iteration, sums them into an aggregate S_t, and accepts if S_t > 0 (confidence-weighted vote). The analysis decomposes the one-step descent into a true-improvement term (bounded via L_f-smoothness and Gaussian symmetry) and a ranking-error term (controlled via Bernstein's inequality with exponent proportional to Nρ(|Δ_t|)), yielding the stated complexity bounds.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution is a comparison complexity of O(k/(p²ε²)) for NCRS under a uniform-margin oracle and O(k²/ε⁴) total comparisons for the confidence-weighted vote variant, both achieving ε-first-order stationarity for smooth nonconvex objectives with k-dimensional active subspaces. Empirically, on LLM fine-tuning (MRPC, QQP with BERT/RoBERTa), NCRS requires fewer iterations as intrinsic dimension k decreases (from ~1608 for BERT-BASE/MRPC down to ~207 for RoBERTa-LARGE/MRPC), consistently outperforming RSGF and remaining competitive with MeZO. On six DM Control Suite preference-based RL tasks, NCRS achieves ground-truth reward curves comparable to RLHF (PPO), iterative DPO, and ZPG under the same query budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023) on LLM fine-tuning, NCRS is competitive but MeZO provides a slightly stronger baseline; both outperform RSGF (Ghadimi & Lan, 2013), which consistently requires more iterations across all model/dataset configurations. The key advantage of NCRS over both is that it uses only a single comparison per iteration (one function evaluation pair) rather than the two-sided finite-difference estimator, while still achieving the same O(k/ε²) intrinsic-dimension scaling. In the preference-based RL setting on DM Control Suite, NCRS with confidence-weighted vote achieves ground-truth reward curves that are competitive with RLHF/PPO (Christiano et al., 2017), iterative DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), and ZPG (Zhang & Ying, 2025) under the same query budget, though the paper does not report a single scalar win/loss metric—rather, NCRS tracks the baselines closely across all six locomotion tasks (Walker, Ball in Cup, Fish, Finger Spin, Reacher, Cart Pole). The theoretical comparison in Table 1 shows NCRS improves over prior nonconvex comparison-based methods (Tang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025) by replacing the ambient dimension d with the intrinsic dimension k, and extends the gap-dependent noise analysis of Saha et al. (2025) from convex to nonconvex objectives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends two complementary lines of work: Tang et al. (2024) established O(d/ε²) comparison complexity for smooth nonconvex optimization under a noiseless ranking oracle, while Saha et al. (2025) introduced gap-dependent noise models for dueling convex optimization. NCRS unifies and generalizes both by handling noisy comparisons in the nonconvex regime, and further introduces a ridge/active-subspace structure that replaces the ambient dimension d with the intrinsic dimension k in the complexity bound. The confidence-weighted vote mechanism (Algorithm 2) is a natural extension of the uniform-margin accept/reject rule, designed to handle the realistic scenario where comparison reliability degrades near ties.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a simple one-comparison-per-iteration random search automatically adapts to intrinsic dimension k without ever observing the active subspace is a clean theoretical contribution with direct relevance to preference-based RL and LLM alignment, where comparison feedback is the natural signal. The gap-dependent confidence model (Assumption 1.2) is a more realistic oracle than uniform-margin, and the O(k²/ε⁴) bound quantifies the cost of this realism. Key limitations include the fixed ridge structure assumption (practical objectives may only approximately satisfy it), the dependence on problem constants in step-size and vote-count choices, and the fact that the active subspace may vary along the optimization trajectory. Open questions include adaptive variants that estimate k or the subspace online, and whether the intrinsic-dimension benefit persists under approximate or time-varying ridge structure.
