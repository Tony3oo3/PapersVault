## Problem

Example-based guidance for mathematical reasoning is highly unstable: even correct, problem-relevant strategies extracted from successful solutions often fail or degrade performance when transferred as guidance to a target model. The paper identifies the root cause as a systematic dissociation between strategy usage (how frequently a strategy appears in correct solutions) and strategy executability (whether the strategy remains effective when instantiated as guidance), showing that human- and model-derived strategies differ in structured, domain-dependent ways that produce consistent source-dependent reversals under guidance.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single mathematical problem as input and produces up to five abstract strategy hints—high-level reasoning approaches such as 'apply inclusion-exclusion' or 'introduce auxiliary points'—which are prepended to the problem in a fixed prompt template before the model generates its final answer. The entire pipeline operates purely at test time with no modification to the base model, training data, or decoding procedure; the only trainable components are a ~0.65M-parameter graph encoder and a logistic executability predictor, keeping online overhead minimal (retrieval + graph query ≈ 0.16 s per query).

## Method

SSR organizes reasoning knowledge from HM-ReasoningBench into a heterogeneous graph of problem, strategy, and category nodes, and learns structure-aware embeddings via a three-layer TransformerConv GNN trained with an InfoNCE contrastive objective that pulls executable problem-strategy pairs closer than non-executable ones. At test time, a new problem is embedded and candidate strategies are retrieved through three complementary routes—category-conditioned (coarse executability regularities), problem-transfer (fine-grained context-dependent transfer), and semantic fallback (coverage when evidence is sparse)—whose union forms an overcomplete candidate set. A supervised logistic executability predictor, trained on trial-level execution outcomes with Beta-Binomial posterior supervision, then ranks candidates by calibrated executability scores, and the top strategies are selected as guidance. The key distinction from prior retrieval-based approaches is that selection is driven by empirical, model-relative executability signals rather than semantic similarity or strategy frequency, explicitly modeling whether a strategy can be operationalized by the target model under fixed prompting and decoding conditions.

## Results

SSR achieves consistent accuracy gains over direct solving across all three benchmarks and models, with the largest improvements on the hardest problems: +6.67 points on AIME25 (Qwen3-8B: 67.33→74.00) and +4.90 points on Apex (Qwen3-8B: 8.16→13.06). On closed-source models (GPT-4.1, o3-mini), SSR improves accuracy by approximately +4–13 points on AIME25 and +2–5 points on Apex. SSR also reduces average output tokens relative to direct solving (e.g., 14,114 vs. 16,286 completion tokens for Qwen3-14B), indicating that effective strategy guidance steers the model toward more efficient reasoning rather than longer exploration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SSR outperforms ICL (one semantically retrieved worked example, Brown et al. 2020) by 2.4–5.9 points across all benchmarks and models, confirming that abstract strategy hints selected for executability are more effective than raw example transfer. Against Self-Consistency (SC@8, Wang et al. 2022), SSR wins on 8 of 9 model–benchmark cells (e.g., Qwen3-8B on AIME25: 74.00 vs. 72.00; Apex: 13.06 vs. 11.42), with the sole exception being R1-Distill-7B on Apex (7.75 vs. 8.97). SSR also beats Least-to-Most Prompting (Zhou et al. 2022) in every setting, with margins up to 7.8 points (Qwen3-8B on AIME25: 74.00 vs. 69.33). The closest competitor is Tree-of-Thoughts (ToT, B=3, D=2; Yao et al. 2023), which SSR outperforms on 7 of 9 cells but loses to on Apex for Qwen3-14B (14.69 vs. 15.91) and narrowly on AIME25 for Qwen3-14B (74.67 vs. 75.00). Critically, SSR achieves these results with a single guided generation per problem, whereas SC requires 8 samples and ToT requires up to 7 model calls, making SSR substantially more efficient at test time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard assumption in example-based guidance (ICL, worked examples, strategy hints) that a strategy observed in a correct solution is automatically transferable as effective guidance for a target model. It carves new conceptual ground by introducing 'strategy executability' as distinct from 'strategy usage,' showing that human-written and model-generated solutions employ systematically different strategies with complementary executability profiles depending on problem domain. Methodologically, SSR repurposes heterogeneous graph neural networks with contrastive learning (InfoNCE) and multi-route retrieval to operationalize executability at test time, building on the strategy/plan-level control literature (Xu et al., 2025; Qi et al., 2025) but explicitly modeling source-dependent and context-conditioned effectiveness rather than assuming uniform transferability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The usage-executability dissociation provides a concrete diagnostic for why guidance-based reasoning is unstable, moving the field beyond 'better retrieval' or 'more examples' toward model-relative evaluation of intermediate reasoning abstractions. The framework is lightweight (0.65M trainable parameters, no base-model fine-tuning) and yields consistent gains across compact and frontier models, with the gap persisting (albeit shrinking) in GPT-5.1, suggesting it reflects a structural property of current training distributions rather than a small-model artifact. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond mathematics to coding or scientific reasoning, whether the human-model strategy gap can be addressed at the training-data level, and whether executability estimates remain stable as model architectures evolve.
