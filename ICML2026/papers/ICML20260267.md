## Problem

Existing approximate-equivariance methods either sample group elements to impose point-wise penalties (incurring high sample complexity and estimator variance) or explicitly parameterise a model as a sum of equivariant and non-equivariant subnetworks (causing double parameterisation). No architecture-agnostic regulariser existed that penalises non-equivariance at the operator level across the full group orbit with a closed-form, sampling-free computation.

## Input / Output

The regulariser consumes the weight matrices of linear layers in any neural network together with the corresponding group representation matrices (e.g., rotation matrices for SO(2)/SO(3) or cyclic-shift matrices for C4). It produces two quantities: the projected (equivariant) weight matrix P(W) and the non-equivariant residual W − P(W), whose norm serves as the penalty. For continuous groups the projection is computed via FFT-based masking in the spectral domain, requiring only O(N_ℓ) operations per layer rather than O(|G|·N_ℓ^{3/2}) spatial averaging, and introduces no extra forward passes or per-sample group sampling.

## Method

The paper introduces a projection-based regulariser that adds λ_⊥‖W − P(W)‖² to the task loss, where P is the Reynolds (Haar-averaging) operator projecting a linear layer onto the G-equivariant subspace. The key theoretical result (Lemma 3.2) shows that ‖W − P(W)‖ is equivalent up to a factor of 2 to the worst-case equivariance defect, so minimising the penalty directly controls symmetry violation. For continuous groups, the Peter-Weyl theorem implies that equivariant operators are block-diagonal in the Fourier domain, reducing the projection to zeroing off-diagonal frequency blocks and averaging within each diagonal block — a simple masking operation computable via FFT. This yields an architecture-agnostic, sampling-free regulariser with zero estimator variance and linear cost in the number of parameters, demonstrated on SO(2) toy classification, imperfectly symmetric fluid dynamics, C4-equivariant CT metal artefact reduction, and SO(3)/O(3) 3D medical image classification.

## Results

On the AAPM CT-MAR benchmark, the projection-based regulariser improves PSNR over unregularised baselines (e.g., ACDNet: 42.68 vs. 42.08) and substantially outperforms the sample-based equivariance penalty of Bai et al. (42.68 vs. 40.02 PSNR for ACDNet) while achieving 42–61% higher training throughput. On MedMNIST 3D classification, the method matches or exceeds the best baselines (e.g., 0.943 accuracy on Organ with O(3), 0.774 on Synapse with SO(3)) while using only 2.0M parameters and achieving 638 samples/s training throughput versus 82–131 for P-SCNN and RPP. On imperfectly symmetric dynamical systems, adding the regulariser to relaxed steerable CNNs yields the best MSE in rotation (0.58) and scaling (0.69) settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the sample-based equivariance penalty of Bai et al. (CVPR 2025), the projection-based regulariser wins on PSNR across all three CT-MAR architectures (e.g., 42.68 vs. 40.02 for ACDNet) and achieves 42–61% higher training throughput, since the sample-based method requires an extra forward pass per sampled rotation and is limited to batch size 4. Residual Pathway Priors (Finzi et al., NeurIPS 2021) consistently underperform: on CT-MAR they drop PSNR by 3–5 dB (e.g., 37.12 vs. 42.68 for ACDNet) and add ~60% more parameters; on MedMNIST they also trail the proposed method on Synapse and Organ. On the dynamical systems benchmark, the proposed regulariser added to relaxed steerable CNNs (Wang et al., ICML 2022) improves MSE from 0.67 to 0.58 in the rotation setting and from 0.73 to 0.69 in the scaling setting, while the fully equivariant baselines (Weiler & Cesa, NeurIPS 2019; Sosnovik et al., ICLR 2020) and Lift (Wang et al., CoRL 2022) are outperformed in both rotation and scaling. On MedMNIST, the method matches or slightly exceeds P-SCNN (Veefkind & Cesa, ICML 2024) on Synapse (0.774 vs. 0.770 SO(3)) and Organ (0.943 vs. 0.905 O(3)), while using 5.5× fewer parameters (2.0M vs. 11.1M) and 7.7× higher training throughput (638 vs. 82 samples/s). The fully equivariant 3D SCNNs (Weiler et al., 2018) underperform on Organ (0.607 SO(3), 0.592 O(3)) due to left-right confusion, whereas the proposed method reaches 0.933–0.943.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two dominant paradigms for approximate equivariance: sample-based penalties (Bai et al. 2025, Kouzelis et al. 2025) that draw random group elements and measure pointwise deviations, and architectural decompositions (Finzi et al. 2021's residual pathway priors, Wang et al. 2022c's relaxed group convolutions) that explicitly parameterise a model as an equivariant plus non-equivariant sum. Instead, it leverages the Reynolds operator (Haar-average projection) onto the equivariant subspace as a weight-space regulariser, grounded in the Peter-Weyl decomposition and Schur's lemma to show the projection is computable by simple masking and averaging in Fourier space. The theoretical link between the projection norm and the equivariance defect (Lemma 3.2, a factor-of-2 bound) connects this regulariser to the natural worst-case symmetry-violation measure, and the per-layer decomposition (Corollary 3.4) extends the analysis to deep networks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The projection-based regulariser offers a practical efficiency advantage: it avoids per-sample group-element sampling and extra forward passes, yielding 42–61% higher training throughput than sample-based penalties while matching or exceeding reconstruction quality on CT-MAR. Its architecture-agnostic formulation and closed-form Fourier-domain computation make it applicable to continuous groups (SO(3), O(3)) where the spatial-domain integral is intractable. Key limitations remain: the projection is derived only for linear layers (non-linearities and architecture-specific modules are not yet handled), experiments cover relatively simple groups (C4, SO(2), SO(3)), and the test-time projection variant performs poorly, indicating the regulariser must act during training. Extending to more complex groups (e.g., for material science) and deriving projections for non-linear modules are the natural next steps.
