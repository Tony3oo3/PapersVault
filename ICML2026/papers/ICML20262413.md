## Problem

Despite growing use of large speech foundation models for audio deepfake detection, it remains unclear how detection performance, robustness, and generalization scale with model capacity and fine-tuning data size under realistic deployment conditions. Prior work reports benchmark results or compares a few model variants under clean settings, leaving the scaling behavior under distribution shift, signal corruption, and unseen synthesis pipelines unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262413/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The detector consumes 16 kHz speech audio that is volume-normalized and truncated to a maximum of 4 seconds, processed through the Whisper encoder with LoRA adapters on query and value projections. It outputs a binary real/fake detection score, evaluated primarily via Equal Error Rate (EER) across in-distribution, out-of-distribution, corrupted, cross-language (46 non-English languages), and cross-TTS settings.

## Method

The paper uses the Whisper model family (Tiny 39M through Large 1.55B) as a controlled testbed, holding architecture and pretraining constant while varying model capacity over more than an order of magnitude. Fine-tuning employs LoRA (rank 16, α=32) on query and value projection layers with a 5-epoch Adam schedule, and training data scale is varied via uniform per-dataset subsampling from 2% to 100% of a 2M-sample English-only corpus. Detection performance is modeled with power-law scaling (EER vs. model size and data size), and the analysis extends beyond in-distribution EER to robustness under Gaussian noise, pitch shifting, and Encodec distortion, cross-language generalization on SpeechFake, and cross-TTS generalization on unseen generators. The key finding is a fundamental asymmetry: in-distribution performance follows clean power-law trends, while robustness to structured distortions and cross-domain generalization exhibit weaker or saturating scaling, indicating that capacity alone cannot resolve representational mismatch.

## Results

In-distribution EER follows stable power-law scaling with both model capacity (Whisper Tiny 39M → Large 1.55B) and training data (2%–100% of a ~2M-sample English corpus), with larger models consistently achieving lower EER and higher sample efficiency across the full data regime. The fitted scaling law for Whisper-Large predicts an irreducible error floor of approximately 0.07 EER; under a hypothetical 3% floor, reaching 5% EER would require roughly 24M training samples, while a zero-floor scenario requires about 7M. Under out-of-distribution evaluation (ADD2022/2023, ASVspoof2021, In-the-Wild, etc.), scaling gains are systematically weaker and performance gaps between model sizes persist even at full data scale. Robustness to Gaussian noise tracks clean-condition scaling closely, whereas pitch shifting and Encodec distortion show weak or saturating scaling, with large persistent error gaps that do not close with additional capacity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external detector methods; instead, it uses the Whisper model family (Tiny 39M, Base 74M, Small 244M, Medium 769M, Large 1.55B) as a controlled testbed, varying only model size while holding architecture, pretraining data, and fine-tuning protocol (LoRA r=16, α=32, 5 epochs, Adam lr=2e-4) constant. In-distribution, all larger models outperform smaller ones at every data fraction, with the advantage most pronounced at low data scales and narrowing (but not vanishing) at 100% data. Under OOD conditions, the Medium and Large curves become nearly indistinguishable, indicating capacity saturation, and a crossover between Tiny and Small appears at small data scales, showing non-monotonic capacity effects. For robustness, Gaussian noise preserves the clean-condition ordering (larger = better) with a roughly constant vertical offset, while pitch shifting and Encodec produce flatter scaling curves where the clean-vs-corrupted gap persists across all model sizes. Cross-language (46 non-English languages on SpeechFake) and cross-TTS evaluations show consistent monotonic improvement with capacity but with smaller scaling exponents and persistent per-language or per-generator error gaps that are not eliminated even by the largest model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neural scaling laws framework (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) from language modeling and pretraining to the post-training fine-tuning regime of audio deepfake detection, using the Whisper family as a controlled testbed where architecture and pretraining are held constant. Prior work on foundation-model-based detectors (Wav2Vec2.0, HuBERT, XLS-R, Wav2Vec-BERT) has reported benchmark comparisons across a few model variants, but none has systematically varied both model capacity and training data scale while explicitly evaluating robustness and cross-domain generalization. The paper thus carves out a new analytical axis—scaling behavior under distribution shift and structured corruptions—rather than proposing a new detection architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical implication is that practitioners should not treat model scale as a reliable proxy for robustness: while in-distribution accuracy follows clean power-law trends, resistance to structured distortions (pitch shifting, neural codec artifacts) saturates, meaning that simply deploying a larger Whisper model will not close the gap under realistic deployment conditions. This motivates complementary strategies—diversity-aware training, targeted augmentation, robustness-oriented objectives—that the paper identifies but does not itself develop. Key limitations include the restriction to a single model family (Whisper), a limited number of discrete model sizes, and the exclusion of pretraining-scale effects, leaving open whether the observed asymmetry persists across architectures or when pretraining data and objectives are also scaled.
