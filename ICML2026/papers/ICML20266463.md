## Problem

Current autoformalization research translates individual statements in isolation, implicitly relying on pre-existing formal libraries like Lean Mathlib to supply the surrounding theoretical context. However, real formalization efforts (e.g., the Kepler conjecture required 11 years of constructing definitions and lemmas) are inherently theory-level, and no existing method attempts to autoformalize the foundational layers—axiomatic primitives, derivative definitions, and proof infrastructure—on which target theorems depend. This paper argues that without theory-level capabilities, autoformalization cannot extend to domains lacking mature formal libraries or enable the new abstractions that drive genuine theoretical discovery.

## Input / Output

Inputs span multimodal sources beyond text: timing diagrams, geometric figures, protocol state machines, and multi-page legal policy documents (e.g., HIPAA regulations), each carrying implicit assumptions that must be resolved through cross-modal reasoning. Outputs are not isolated formal statements but coherent, interdependent theory libraries preserving a strict dependency hierarchy (axiomatic primitives → derivative definitions → notations/lemmas/tactics → target theorems), targeting diverse low-resource DSLs (SMT-LIB, TPTP, SVA, Cedar, Cypher, CodeQL) in addition to Lean. A critical structural constraint is that the formal output must remain internally consistent across dozens of major theorems with deeply intertwined dependencies, rather than being a bag of independently verified artifacts.

## Method

As a position paper, the core contribution is a structured argument organized around the 'Theory-Level Autoformalization Tower,' a four-layer dependency model that makes explicit why statement-level autoformalization is a special case requiring all lower layers to already exist. The paper identifies four open challenges—equivalence checking without reliable oracles (where even formal-formal equivalence is subjective), hierarchical decomposition and abstraction learning at textbook scale, low-resource DSLs lacking paired corpora, and multimodal inputs—and proposes three concrete paths forward. These are: (1) theory-level benchmarks with sound equivalence checking reporting precision/recall against expert judgments and enforcing data-leakage prevention; (2) general-purpose LLMs trained on diverse multi-domain formal corpora with verifier-feedback RL and dependency retrieval, rather than domain-specific fine-tuning that overfits to a single library's conventions; and (3) a common intermediate representation (Lean as the leading candidate) that unifies heterogeneous multimodal inputs, reduces the combinatorial DSL-mastery problem to a linear one, and enables verified transpilation to target languages while combining the ATP strengths of specialized DSLs with the LLM-driven ITP strengths of the IR.

## Results

As a position paper, this work does not present new experimental results. It cites key quantitative data points to motivate its argument: the best current statement autoformalization method (DNA, Min et al. 2026) achieves 71.4% on Layer-3 theorems assuming Layers 0–2 are already formalized by humans; fine-tuned autoformalizers like Goedel-Formalizer-V2-32B score 0.0% on LeanEuclidPlus while their base model Qwen3-32B achieves 20.6%, illustrating severe overfitting; and existing benchmarks contain substantial errors (31.8% in ProofNet, 8.6% in PutnamBench). The paper also reports that BEq+ equivalence checking achieves 98.0% precision and 48.3% recall, compared to 100% precision and 30.9% recall for pure definitional equivalence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper and does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it surveys and critiques existing approaches across four challenge areas. On equivalence checking, it contrasts LLM-as-a-judge methods (no soundness guarantee) with deterministic checkers like BEq+ (98.0% precision, 48.3% recall) and pure definitional equivalence (100% precision, 30.9% recall), arguing none is both sound and sufficiently permissive. On decomposition, it notes that current methods (DRIFT, Aria, DNA) handle only high-school-level statements with shallow dependency graphs, falling short of the textbook-scale blueprint generation needed for theory-level work. On low-resource DSLs, it highlights that GPT-4o achieves only 33% exact match on Text2Cypher and Claude Code reaches just 10% (53.4% with agentic scaffolding) on CodeQL vulnerability query synthesis, underscoring the gap versus well-resourced Lean/Mathlib settings. The paper's central comparative claim is that statement-level autoformalization implicitly depends on theory-level infrastructure (e.g., Mathlib), and that the human-blueprint pipeline used by Math Inc. does not scale because blueprints require substantial expert effort.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new conceptual ground by reframing autoformalization from the statement-level task (translating individual theorems, as in ProofNet, PutnamBench, DNA, DRIFT, and Aria) to a theory-level task that includes formalizing axioms, definitions, notations, lemmas, and their inter-dependencies as coherent libraries. It departs from the implicit assumption in prior work that the formal theoretical context (e.g., Lean Mathlib) is already available, arguing instead that constructing that context is the dominant cost in real formalization projects (Kepler: 11 years, Four Color: 5 years). The paper synthesizes the autoformalization, theorem proving, and formal verification literatures to identify equivalence checking, hierarchical decomposition, low-resource DSLs, and multimodal inputs as the four open challenges that no prior method addresses at theory scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a fundamental gap: no current method attempts to autoformalize the lower layers (axioms, definitions, notations) that all statement-level work implicitly assumes, and no theory-level benchmarks exist. The analysis of equivalence checking as inherently subjective—where the 'degree of equivalence' depends on the evaluator's computational fluency—challenges the field's reliance on LLM-as-a-judge and exact-match metrics. For domains without mature libraries (numerical analysis, hardware verification, protocol verification, policy languages), theory-level autoformalization is framed as the only path forward, making this a prerequisite for scaling formal verification beyond mathematics. Open questions include how to perform hierarchical decomposition at textbook scale, how to learn reusable abstractions from formal corpora, and whether a common intermediate representation (Lean-based) can unify the combinatorial space of target DSLs.
