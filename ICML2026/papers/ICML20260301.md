## Problem

Autoregressive models (ARMs) dominate LLM development due to their parallelizable training and exact ancestral sampling, yet they are based on next-token prediction and are often perceived as lacking genuine lookahead capability. Energy-based models (EBMs), which define sequence-level distributions and naturally characterize the optimal policy in maximum-entropy RL, are intractable to train and sample from. The fundamental question of whether ARMs can exactly represent EBMs—and thus whether next-token prediction inherently encodes lookahead—had not been formally resolved.

## Input / Output

The bijection takes an immediate reward function r: S×A→R (where S is the set of contexts and A is the augmented vocabulary including EOS) and produces ARM logits q: S×A→R, or vice versa. The EBM-to-ARM direction (M) is inherently recursive, requiring sequential computation from t=T down to t=1 for all states, while the ARM-to-EBM direction (M⁻¹) is parallel. Sequences are variable-length, bounded by a maximum response length T, with EOS tokens enforcing valid termination. In the function approximation setting, the output is a KL divergence bound of 2Tε between the ARM and EBM distributions, where ε is the L∞ approximation error of the causal Transformer.

## Method

Starting from the chain rule of probability, the paper establishes an exact bijection M between EBMs and ARMs in function space: q(s_t, y_t) = r(s_t, y_t) + V_q(s_t ⊕ y_t) for non-EOS tokens, where V_q is the log-sum-exp (soft value function) over next states. This mapping is shown to be a special case of the soft Bellman equation in maximum-entropy RL on a finite-horizon, acyclic MDP where states are contexts and transitions are deterministic token appends. Building on this bijection, the paper proves that supervised learning of ARMs and EBMs from (x,y) pairs achieve identical minima in function space (Proposition 2), establishing the optimality of teacher forcing, and derives a KL divergence bound of 2T·max|q*−q| for distilling an EBM into an ARM (Proposition 3). The bijection is further interpreted as entropy-regularized dynamic programming on a prefix tree (DAG), and validated numerically with synthetic causal and non-causal Transformers showing matching loss curves and logit convergence.

## Results

The paper's primary contributions are theoretical: Proposition 1 establishes an exact bijection between ARMs and EBMs in function space via the mapping q = M(r), Proposition 2 proves equivalence of the minima of ARM and EBM negative log-likelihood objectives, and Proposition 3 provides a KL divergence bound of 2T·max|q*(s,y) − q(s,y)| for distilling an EBM into an ARM. Numerical validation on synthetic tiny language models (small vocabulary V and maximum length T, enabling exact EBM log-partition computation) confirms that a causal Transformer (ARM) and a non-causal Transformer (EBM) converge to the same optimal risk L*, with very close loss curves throughout training. The L∞ distance between the trained ARM logits and the optimal EBM logits (after applying the mapping M) is shown to be small, validating the bijection empirically.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without external baselines or standard benchmark comparisons. The central 'comparison' is between ARMs and EBMs themselves: Proposition 2 demonstrates that in function space (tabular setting), minimizing the ARM negative log-likelihood and the EBM negative log-likelihood yield identical minima, meaning the two model classes are equivalently powerful for fitting (x,y) pairs. The numerical experiments in Section 5 validate this by training both a causal Transformer (ARM) and a non-causal Transformer (EBM) on synthetic data, showing both reach the same optimality gap L − L* = 0 and that their loss trajectories are nearly identical throughout training. The paper also contrasts its theoretical guarantee (that teacher forcing is optimal in function space) with prior work by Bachmann & Nagarajan (2024) that argued next-token prediction is structurally 'myopic,' arguing instead that the limitation lies in optimization/approximation error rather than the objective itself. No head-to-head performance comparison against other alignment methods (e.g., DPO, PPO) is provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate threads: the soft Bellman equation from maximum-entropy RL (Haarnoja et al., 2017; Nachum et al., 2017), differentiable dynamic programming on DAGs (Mensch & Blondel, 2018), and the chain rule of probability for autoregressive language models. While GFlowNets (Bengio et al., 2021, 2023) and their MaxEnt RL reinterpretation (Tiapkin et al., 2024) had already hinted at connections between flow-based generation and entropy-regularized RL, this paper derives the exact bijection between EBM rewards and ARM logits purely from the chain rule, showing the soft Bellman equation emerges as a direct algebraic consequence rather than an RL-specific construct. It also directly addresses the 'myopic next-token prediction' critique (Bachmann & Nagarajan, 2024) by proving that in function space the global minimum of teacher forcing is an EBM, and leverages the universal approximation result for causal Transformers (Furuya et al., 2025) to bound the KL gap in the parameterized setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical justification for the next-token prediction paradigm and teacher forcing, showing that the apparent 'lookahead' limitation of ARMs is not structural but arises from optimization and approximation error in the parameterized (non-tabular) regime. The key open question it highlights is the practical gap: the ARM's scoring function q must simultaneously model the immediate local reward and implicitly compute a future-looking value function V_q using only a causal (backward-looking) architecture, and formally comparing the learning complexity of R (non-causal) versus q (causal) remains unresolved. The empirical validation is limited to tiny synthetic models with exact partition functions, so whether the theoretical equivalence survives at the scale of modern LLMs with finite context windows and function approximation remains an important open direction.
