## Problem

While arbitrage is fundamental to financial markets, no corresponding framework exists for understanding how cost asymmetries between competing AI model providers can be exploited for profit. This paper formalizes computational arbitrage in AI model markets and empirically demonstrates that simple strategies can generate net profit margins of up to 40–58% in verifiable domains, with significant consequences for market pricing, segmentation, and model development.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263508/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The arbitrageur receives a software issue description (e.g., a GitHub issue with associated unit tests) and a total inference budget in USD, then produces a verified software patch. Performance is measured as pass@k solve rate, where k is determined by converting the dollar budget into an equivalent number of sampling attempts per issue. The arbitrage policy's output is a cost allocation across providers (e.g., $0.08 to GPT-5 mini, $0.92 to DeepSeek), and the key constraint is that the policy must achieve a target solve rate at strictly lower cost than any single provider in the market.

## Method

The paper formalizes computational arbitrage as a market participant who sources generations from multiple providers via a model cascade (sequential querying in fixed order until a correct patch is obtained) and resells at a markup below the prevailing market price. The core optimization problem is to find budget caps τ_i for each provider in the cascade that maximize expected profit, where the cascade's solve probability on each issue is computed as 1 minus the product of individual failure probabilities. The key insight is that different models have complementary cost-efficiency profiles across budget levels (e.g., GPT-5 mini is cheaper at low budgets, DeepSeek at high budgets), so a well-tuned cascade achieves any target performance above a threshold at lower cost than either model alone. For the distillation component, the authors use standard supervised fine-tuning (cross-entropy loss) on trajectories generated by a teacher model (Qwen Coder 30B) to train small student models (1.7B and 4B), showing that increased distillation data monotonically improves pass@k and creates increasingly profitable arbitrage opportunities when the student is paired with the teacher in a cascade.

## Results

In the two-model SWE-bench market (GPT-5 mini + DeepSeek v3.2), the arbitrage policy achieves a 75% solve rate at a cost of $80, compared to $150 for GPT-5 mini and $120 for DeepSeek, yielding profit margins of up to 40%. In the expanded six-model market, arbitrage opportunities emerge at a lower 42% solve rate (vs. 68% in the two-model market) and margins increase to 58%. Distillation scaling experiments show that a model distilled on 5.4B tokens (400k examples) enables ~30% arbitrage profit margin when paired with Qwen Coder 480B, and the resulting mini-coder 4B model cannibalizes nearly all of its teacher Qwen Coder 30B's revenue. In the Lean4 theorem-proving domain, arbitrage margins reach up to 60% across the Kimina Prover model family.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GPT-5 mini, the arbitrage policy is strictly cheaper for all performance levels above 68% SWE-bench solve rate, saving $70 at the 75% target ($80 vs. $150). Against DeepSeek v3.2, the arbitrage policy is cheaper above 68% solve rate, saving $40 at 75% ($80 vs. $120); however, DeepSeek is individually cheaper than GPT-5 mini at lower budgets, which is precisely the asymmetry the cascade exploits. In the six-model market, Qwen Coder 480B and Claude Sonnet 4.5 are both uncompetitive and earn zero revenue: the former is neither cost-efficient nor high-performing, while the latter is too expensive for the $1-per-issue budget regime. The distilled mini-coder 4B outperforms its teacher Qwen Coder 30B at inference budgets up to $0.02 per issue, causing Qwen Coder's revenue share to collapse from ~20% at the performance frontier to near zero. In the Lean4 domain, the arbitrage policy across Kimina Prover models (0.6B–72B) achieves over 60% profit margin, exceeding the SWE-bench margins, confirming that arbitrage is robust across verifiable domains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the well-studied model cascading paradigm (Varshney & Baral, 2022) and model routing literature as the mechanism for a new economic concept: computational arbitrage in AI model markets. While prior work on cascading and routing optimizes cost-performance trade-offs for a single consumer, this paper reframes the problem as a market participant buying from multiple providers to resell at a profit, drawing on financial arbitrage theory (Hull, 1989) and Bertrand competition (Mas-Colell et al., 1995). The distillation experiments build on standard SFT-based distillation (Cobbe et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2021) but evaluate distilled models not by accuracy alone but by their revenue-generating capacity upon market entry, a metric absent from prior distillation work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that simple, risk-free cascading strategies can extract 40–58% profit margins from current AI model markets, and that this arbitrage is self-defeating under Bertrand competition—ultimately driving prices to marginal cost and reducing provider revenues by up to 40%. The finding that a 4B distilled model can cannibalize nearly all of its 30B teacher's market revenue has direct implications for how model providers should think about distillation and open-weight releases. Key open questions include the impact of costly or imperfect verification, the role of market information asymmetries, and whether more sophisticated (query-aware) routing policies would further alter the economic equilibrium.
