## Problem

Existing differentially private synthetic data methods assume i.i.d. rows where each row corresponds to one individual, but longitudinal datasets (e.g., electronic health records) have a fundamentally different structure where each user owns an entire table of sequential events. Flattening these user trajectories into high-dimensional vectors for standard marginal-based mechanisms destroys temporal coherence, as the mechanisms can only capture local step-wise dependencies while failing to preserve global trajectory consistency. The paper formalizes the full user table as the privacy unit and asks whether autoregressive LLMs can synthesize such table families while preserving temporal dynamics under differential privacy.

## Input / Output

The method takes a collection of variable-length user tables (each with a shared schema of d columns and T_i rows) and serializes them into token sequences with row-delimiting markers (e.g., [Row i]: col is val). At inference, the model generates synthetic tables autoregressively one row at a time, conditioned on the schema and previously generated rows, producing variable-length outputs that are parsed back into structured tables. A differentially private selection step then filters an over-generated candidate pool using Gecko embeddings and a DP voting mechanism to produce the final synthetic dataset.

## Method

PATH serializes each user's table into a token sequence and fine-tunes Gemma 3 (1B or 4B) with DP-SGD and LoRA (rank 128) under a user-level privacy guarantee where each user contributes exactly one training example per epoch. A dynamic windowing strategy samples a random split point k per epoch, training the model to generate the continuation Ψ(x_{k+1:T}) from context Ψ(x_{1:k}), with density-based sampling of k to adapt to the trajectory length distribution. At inference, generation proceeds row-by-row with robust multi-stage parsing (key-value, CSV fallback, partial infilling) and early termination on validation failures. A private selection step post-processes the over-generated pool by mapping tables to Gecko embeddings and applying a DP voting mechanism (each real table votes for its k=10 nearest synthetic neighbors with added noise) to select the final dataset, consuming a dedicated ε_select budget.

## Results

On MIMIC-IV vital signs (ε=2.0), PATH (Gemma 4B) achieves a TDCR of 0.289, reducing the distributional distance to real trajectories by over 60% compared to AIM (0.736), and cuts state transition error by nearly 50% (0.387 vs. 0.741) while also improving MAUVE (0.661 vs. 0.564) and marginal divergence (3.30 vs. 9.71). On the synthetic HMM dataset (ε=10.0), PATH (4B) achieves an HMM log-likelihood Wasserstein distance of 50.99 versus AIM's 319.43, confirming superior preservation of latent temporal dynamics even when AIM achieves better univariate marginal fidelity (0.28 vs. 0.83). On NYC 311 (ε=10.0), PATH (4B) reaches MAUVE 0.634 and temporal distribution Wasserstein 0.870, substantially outperforming the non-private Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite few-shot baseline (MAUVE 0.074, temporal Wasserstein 2.537).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AIM (the leading marginal-based mechanism applied to flattened data), PATH (4B) wins decisively on temporal and distributional metrics: TDCR is reduced by 60% (0.289 vs. 0.736 at ε=2.0 on MIMIC) and state transition Frobenius norm by 48% (0.387 vs. 0.741), while also achieving better MAUVE (0.661 vs. 0.564) and marginal divergence (3.30 vs. 9.71). The one metric where AIM retains an edge is univariate marginal fidelity on the simpler synthetic HMM dataset at ε=10 (0.28 vs. 0.83), but this comes at the cost of a 6× worse HMM log-likelihood (319 vs. 51), confirming that marginal accuracy does not translate to temporal coherence. Against non-private Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite few-shot prompting (10-shot), PATH (4B) dominates on every metric—MAUVE 0.661 vs. 0.269, TDCR 0.289 vs. 0.789, State Trans. 0.387 vs. 1.228—indicating that private fine-tuning internalizes the full distributional manifold far more effectively than in-context copying. Against the DP-adapted DoppelGANger (GAN-based sequential synthesizer), PATH outperforms by approximately 22× on marginal fidelity and 3× on transition divergence, consistent with prior findings that DP-trained GANs struggle on structured tabular data.

## Relation to Prior Work

PATH extends the GReaT lineage of serializing tabular data for LLM fine-tuning by treating the full user table (a multi-row temporal trajectory) as the unit of synthesis rather than a single i.i.d. row, and operationalizes the multi-table privacy unit previously identified by Ponomareva et al. (2025) with a concrete autoregressive generation pipeline. It departs from marginal-based mechanisms (AIM, Private-PGM) that require flattening longitudinal data into high-dimensional vectors, and from prior DP-LLM tabular approaches (Afonja et al., 2025; Tran & Xiong, 2024) that necessitate two-stage syntax-then-distribution training, by achieving high fidelity with a single-stage next-token prediction loss and no discretization preprocessing. The private selection post-processing step is directly inherited from Ponomareva et al. (2025), while the dynamic windowing and density-based split-point sampling are novel training strategies specific to variable-length temporal tables.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first practical framework for releasing differentially private synthetic data from longitudinal sources (EHR, civic service records) where the privacy unit is an entire user trajectory rather than a single row, a setting that is common in practice but previously unaddressed by DP synthesis literature. The demonstrated failure mode of flattening—locally plausible but globally incoherent trajectories that evade standard marginal metrics—gives practitioners a concrete reason to avoid naive adaptations of existing tools. Key limitations include ~10× higher training cost than marginal methods, the absence of empirical privacy attack evaluations (relying on formal guarantees only), and the observation that the advantage is strongest for data with rich temporal structure, leaving open whether the approach remains competitive for simpler tabular tasks. Future work on heterogeneous schemas, longer sequences, and empirical attack resistance would strengthen the practical deployment case.
