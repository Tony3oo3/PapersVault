## Problem

Verifying LLM-generated solutions to research-level mathematics remains a bottleneck: human expert review is costly to scale, and LLM judges are unreliable and biased on subtle errors. When ground-truth answers are unavailable (as for genuinely open problems), existing oracle-free validators—reward models, generative reward models, and LLM judges—struggle to separate correct from incorrect solutions, especially on the hardest instances. The paper asks whether a candidate solution's correctness can be inferred from its downstream consequences on related, verifiable questions rather than from direct inspection.

## Input / Output

The method takes a research-level question Q, a pool of N candidate solutions (one expert-written plus nine LLM-generated, each manually validated as correct or incorrect), a small set of author-created neighborhood questions N(Q) (typically two verifiable variants that reuse the same key lemma or reduction as Q), and a solver model M_θ. It produces a scalar utility score U(C) per candidate—estimated as the average binary-verified accuracy over T rollouts on each neighborhood question—inducing a ranking from which Acc@1, AUC, Recall@5, HumanWin, and MeanWin are computed. Neighborhood questions must have compact, automatically verifiable final answers, and the solver is conditioned on (Q, C, Q*) with up to 16k reasoning tokens per rollout.

## Method

Consequence-Based Utility (CBU) operationalizes the mathematical principle of 'support by consequences': a candidate solution C^(i) is scored by how well it serves as an in-context exemplar when the solver M_θ is prompted to solve neighborhood questions Q* ∈ N(Q). Specifically, for each Q*, the solver generates T independent rollouts conditioned on (Q, C^(i), Q*), each scored by a binary verifier v(Q*, C̃) ∈ {0,1}; the utility is the mean over all neighborhood questions and rollouts. The key insight is that correct (or near-correct) candidates contain transferable method-level information that consistently improves downstream performance, while incorrect candidates—particularly those with unjustified compression, incorrect reasoning, or authority-like external references—provide little reconstructable guidance and thus yield low utility. Unlike LLM judges that inspect a single reasoning trace (analogous to code review), CBU scores by downstream consequences (analogous to a unit test), making it robust to superficially coherent but subtly flawed solutions. The approach is oracle-free because correctness is assessed only on the verifiable neighborhood questions, not on Q itself, and requires no training—only inference with the solver model.

## Results

On EXPERTMATH, Consequence-Based Utility (CBU) consistently outperforms all oracle-free baselines across four backbone models. For GPT-OSS-120B, CBU improves Acc@1 from 67.21 to 76.27 and AUC from 71.42 to 79.63 over the same model used as an LLM judge; for GPT-OSS-20B, AUC rises from 69.03 to 79.18. CBU also shows the largest solver-evaluator gap, maintaining correct-wrong separation even on questions the solver cannot solve (1−Avg@64 ≈ 1), where LLM-judge separability drops sharply. The only exception is Qwen3-235B-A22B on Recall@5, where the LLM judge (80.02) edges out CBU (74.15) by 5.87 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CBU outperforms all four LLM-judge backbones on AUC when using the same model (e.g., GPT-OSS-120B: 79.63 vs. 71.42; Qwen3-30B-A3B: 76.24 vs. 65.81), with the sole exception of Qwen3-235B-A22B on Recall@5 where the judge wins by 5.87 points. Against generative reward models, CBU leads by a wide margin: GPT-OSS-120B CBU (AUC 79.63) exceeds Qwen3-235B-GenRM (67.85) and Llama3.3-Nemotron-49B-GenRM (49.57) by 11.8 and 30.1 AUC points respectively. Against deterministic reward models, the gap is even larger—AceMath-72B-RM achieves only 20.75 AUC and Qwen2.5-Math-RM-72B only 34.05, reflecting their much smaller compute budget (single scoring pass vs. 64 rollouts). CBU also substantially improves HumanWin over LLM judges (e.g., GPT-OSS-120B: 82.86 vs. 48.57), indicating better alignment with expert-written solutions that are often terse and intuition-driven. On the secondary RealMath and DaftMath datasets (Table 4), CBU outperforms LLM-judge scoring on the harder RealMath variants (Acc@1: 79.22 vs. 42.79) but underperforms on the easier DaftMath (85.58 vs. 93.51), confirming that CBU's advantage is most pronounced at research-level difficulty.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the in-context learning performance signal—previously used for data curation, retrieval, and demonstration valuation (Chang & Jia, 2023; Nguyen & Wong, 2023; Xie et al., 2024)—as an oracle-free verification mechanism for candidate solutions, drawing an analogy to the 'support by consequences' principle in mathematics (e.g., the Riemann Hypothesis underwriting conditional results). It positions itself against the standard oracle-free validator family (consistency voting, reward models, generative reward models, LLM judges) and departs from them by replacing direct quality assessment with a transfer-based downstream-accuracy proxy. The broader AI-for-science literature (Gottweis et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024) has emphasized hypothesis generation while leaving validation to scarce human experts; this paper addresses that gap specifically for research-level problems where ground truth is unavailable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Verification remains the bottleneck in AI-assisted mathematics, and this paper provides a scalable, oracle-free alternative that degrades gracefully on the hardest problems where LLM judges fail—a regime that is precisely where research-level questions live. The EXPERTMATH dataset (192 expert-written problems with solutions, half unsolved by frontier models) is a notable resource for the field. Key limitations include the need to construct neighborhood questions (though LLM-generated variants suffice for hard targets) and the 'sweet spot' requirement that neighborhood difficulty be calibrated to the solver. Open questions include extending CBU beyond mathematics, fully automating neighborhood generation without human oversight, and applying the method to genuinely open problems where even neighborhood correctness is uncertain.
