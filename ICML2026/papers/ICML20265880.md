## Problem

It remains unresolved whether RL post-training genuinely extends a language model's reasoning ability beyond what pre-training already provides, because prior analyses are confounded by opaque pre-training corpora, uncontrolled data distributions, and the absence of a dedicated mid-training stage. This paper addresses the gap by asking: under what precise conditions does each training stage (pre-training, mid-training, RL) causally contribute to reasoning generalization, and how do they interact?

## Input / Output

The method consumes synthetic math problems generated from directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) with a controlled number of arithmetic operations (op=2–20) and rendered through natural-language contextual templates (e.g., animals-zoo, teachers-school), producing a question-solution-answer triple. The model outputs a free-form step-by-step reasoning trace that is deterministically parsed into a predicted dependency graph (nodes, edges, numeric values) and a final answer. Evaluation uses a strict process-verified pass@k criterion: a sample counts as correct only if every intermediate step matches the ground-truth DAG (correct node, correct parent set, correct value) AND the final answer is correct, preventing reward or evaluation hacking via shortcut reasoning.

## Method

The paper builds a fully controlled experimental framework on top of GSM-Infinite, where each problem is a DAG of atomic arithmetic operations rendered into natural language via contextual templates, enabling independent manipulation of reasoning depth (operation count) and surface context. They train 100M-parameter Qwen2.5 models from scratch on 10B tokens of synthetic data, with disjoint partitions for pre-training (op=2–10), mid-training (op=11–14, next-token objective), and RL post-training (GRPO with 200K samples at various difficulty ranges). By systematically varying which operation ranges and context templates appear in each stage, they isolate the causal contribution of each phase to two generalization axes: extrapolative (depth) generalization to unseen operation chains and contextual (breadth) generalization to novel surface templates. They further test process-verified reward formulations (blending dense step-level accuracy with sparse outcome reward) to mitigate reward hacking, and validate findings at 500M scale and on Qwen2.5-7B real-world benchmarks (MATH-500, AIME, LiveCodeBench, GPQA). The key mechanistic insight is that RL produces true capability gains (measured at pass@128) only when pre-training leaves headroom and RL data target the model's edge of competence, while contextual transfer requires at least ~1% pre-training exposure to the target context.

## Results

On the 500M controlled setup, RL calibrated to the model's edge of competence (op=11-14) yields the strongest extrapolative gains: op=15-20 pass@128 improves from 45.3 (Base) to 53.2 (+7.9), while RL on in-distribution (op=7-10) or very-hard (op=17-20) data gives only +3.6 and +4.0 respectively. For contextual transfer, even 1% pre-training exposure to a long-tailed context enables strong RL-driven generalization (op=15-20 pass@128: 51.6 vs. 0.0 with 0% exposure). Under a fixed compute budget, a 60% RL / 40% mid-training allocation outperforms Full RL by +13.4 points on op=15-20 pass@128 (66.6 vs. 53.2). Process-aware rewards add a further 4–5% pass@1 improvement on extrapolative tasks and reduce dominant structural error types (dependency mismatches).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study that compares training configurations rather than competing methods. The primary baseline is the pre-trained Base model (10B tokens, op=2-10). Against this baseline, RL on edge-of-competence data (op=11-14) is the only regime that produces genuine pass@128 gains on OOD-hard tasks (+7.9 at 500M), while RL on in-distribution data actually degrades OOD-edge performance (−2.9 pass@128) and RL on very-hard data (op=17-20) underperforms edge data by 3.9 points on op=15-20 pass@128. For mid-training vs. RL allocation under fixed compute, hybrid recipes (20–80% RL) consistently outperform both Full Mid-training and Full RL: the 60% RL setting achieves 66.6 pass@128 on op=15-20 versus 53.2 for Full RL and 63.5 for 20% RL. On real-world Qwen2.5-7B, RL on hard data improves AIME 2025 pass@32 from 13.3 to 60.0 (+46.7), while on in-domain MATH-500 all RL buckets fail to improve pass@32 over Base (84.8), confirming that RL sharpens but does not extend already-saturated capabilities. Process-aware rewards (0.2·R_out + 0.8·R_pv) outperform outcome-only rewards by 4–5% pass@1 on extrapolative tasks and reduce dependency-mismatch errors most substantially under OOD evaluation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GSM-Infinite synthetic data generation framework (Zhou et al., 2025b) and the controlled-experiment methodology of the Physics of Language Models series (Ye et al., 2024) to create a fully disentangled testbed for reasoning. It positions itself at the intersection of two active debates: (1) whether RL genuinely extends reasoning beyond pre-training (Yue et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025 vs. Wen et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025a), and (2) the emerging role of mid-training as a bridge between pre-training and RL (Wang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a). Unlike prior controlled studies that focus primarily on post-training RL, this work explicitly isolates and manipulates all three stages—pre-training, mid-training, and RL—under a unified compute budget, and extends the analysis to process-level reward design (Cui et al., 2025; Khalifa et al., 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides actionable, stage-specific guidance for practitioners: calibrate RL data to the model's edge of competence, seed long-tail primitives at ~1% density in pre-training, and allocate compute between mid-training and RL based on whether the goal is in-distribution reliability or OOD exploration. A key open question is whether the specific thresholds (e.g., the 1% contextual exposure minimum, the op=11-14 edge-of-competence window) transfer to much larger models and more heterogeneous real-world tasks where ground-truth dependency graphs are unavailable. The synthetic setting, while enabling causal control, may understate the complexity of real reasoning, and the 7B validation, though encouraging, covers only math, code, and science benchmarks. Future work could explore whether the mid-training/RL allocation principle generalizes to multi-task or multi-modal settings.
