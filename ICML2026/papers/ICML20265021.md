## Problem

Partial model merging (PMM) was proposed as a privacy-preserving alternative to full model merging by sharing only a subset of model layers, yet its privacy properties remain entirely unexplored. No prior work has quantified how much of the unshared private model an adversary can reconstruct from the shared layers, black-box queries, and limited side information, leaving practitioners without guidance on how many layers to share to balance utility against model-privacy leakage.

## Input / Output

The adversary consumes three types of information: (1) the merged shared part S_m as a frozen white-box module, (2) black-box input-output queries to the victim's full model S_v+P_v (default ~100 queries), and (3) optionally the victim's shared part S_v, private model structure M_Pv, and a fraction p_d of the victim's training data. The output is a trained clone private part P_v* that, when concatenated with the shared part, reproduces the victim's task-specific behaviour; success is measured as clone accuracy on the victim's validation set relative to merged and local (upper-bound) accuracies.

## Method

ModelPirate trains a clone private part P_v* by freezing a shared module (S_v if known, otherwise S_m) and optimising only P_v* to match the black-box victim outputs via a pointwise MAE loss. When the victim's private architecture is unknown (I_p=0), P_v* adopts a deep-shallow design: a deep sub-model M1 (e.g., multi-layer LSTM) provides capacity to simulate the unknown structure, a parallel shallow sub-model M2 prevents overfitting on limited data, and a dense layer M3 fuses their concatenated outputs. When the structure is known (I_p=1), P_v* is initialised from the pre-trained private parameters for faster convergence. The attack is evaluated across eight scenarios parameterised by indicators (I_s, I_p, I_d) controlling availability of the victim's shared parameters, private structure, and training data, revealing that even 10% data leakage or knowledge of the shared part alone can push clone accuracy above the merged-model baseline.

## Results

ModelPirate achieves clone accuracies up to 98.38% (DTD→EuroSAT, ViT-B/32, AS[110]) and 98.19% (EuroSAT→DTD, ViT-L/14, AS[111]), substantially exceeding the merged model lower bounds (e.g., 67.67% and 72.50% respectively). Even in the weakest scenario AS[000] (no prior knowledge), ModelPirate reaches 15.74% on DTD→EuroSAT (ViT-B/32), already surpassing the Random baseline (10.44%). Across all three ViT architectures and both task directions, ModelPirate outperforms all three baselines in every directly comparable scenario, with the largest margins on the more complex EuroSAT→DTD direction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ModelPirate against three existing model clone attacks: Knockoff (Orekondy et al., CVPR 2019), JBDA (Papernot et al., Asia CCS 2017), and Random (Roberts et al., 2019), each corresponding to attack scenarios AS[100], AS[101], and AS[110] respectively. ModelPirate dominates all three baselines across every scenario and architecture. For example, on EuroSAT→DTD with ViT-B/32, ModelPirate AS[110] achieves 85.15% versus Random's 2.93%, ModelPirate AS[101] reaches 68.35% versus JBDA's 3.88%, and ModelPirate AS[100] scores 37.27% versus Knockoff's 7.18%. On the easier DTD→EuroSAT direction, the margins are even larger: ModelPirate AS[101] hits 96.83% versus JBDA's 23.48%, and AS[110] reaches 98.38% versus Random's 10.44%. The only scenario where a baseline is competitive is Knockoff on DTD→EuroSAT (ViT-B/32), where Knockoff achieves 52.22% versus ModelPirate AS[100] at 53.74%—a narrow 1.5-point gap. Overall, ModelPirate's deep-shallow architecture and use of the victim's shared model part as a frozen backbone provide a decisive advantage over all prior clone attacks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the model clone attack paradigm (Papernot et al. 2017, Orekondy et al. 2019, Roberts et al. 2019) to the newly emerging setting of Partial Model Merging (PMM), where only a subset of layers is shared for merging while the rest remains private. While prior PMM work (Stoica et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025) focused on resource reduction and performance optimisation, no prior study has examined the model-privacy vulnerabilities introduced by the partial-sharing protocol. The paper's ModelPirate attack introduces a deep-shallow surrogate architecture for the unknown-structure case, which departs from prior clone attacks that assumed either full structural knowledge or a single monolithic surrogate model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PMM is being adopted as a practical compromise between full model merging and fully local inference, yet this work demonstrates that even sharing 75% of layers exposes the private part to near-complete behavioural reconstruction under modest information leakage. The practical guidance on what to protect (shared parameters > training data > model structure for complex models) and the formalised layer-selection optimisation in Appendix H give PMM deployers concrete decision criteria. Open questions include whether the vulnerability persists at LLM scale, whether cryptographic or differential-privacy protections on the shared part can close the gap, and whether the single-adversary-single-victim formulation generalises to multi-adversary settings.
