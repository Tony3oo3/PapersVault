## Problem

Human-in-the-loop real-world RL for contact-rich manipulation (e.g., USB insertion, card insertion) requires extensive expert time because sparse rewards yield poor exploration efficiency and the limited number of physical interactions makes convergence slow. Existing affordance models (VLMs, SAM) are too computationally heavy for real-time reward computation, and residual RL has not been fully explored in human-in-the-loop real-world settings. The paper asks: how can we efficiently learn precise contact-rich control policies within extremely limited physical interactions?

## Input / Output

The system consumes RGB images from two RealSense D405 wrist cameras (cropped to task-specific ROIs), a proprioceptive vector comprising 6D end-effector pose, linear/angular velocity, and 6D force-torque, and a single goal keyframe extracted from offline demonstrations. The output is a 6D end-effector pose delta plus a 1D discrete gripper command; the ACT base policy generates k action chunks per step, but the residual policy only outputs the first chunk's correction, which the base policy uses to produce the final action. The shared visual encoder for the affordance reward is pre-trained on human manipulation videos and reused across all six tasks without per-task retraining.

## Method

FTC combines three components: (1) a frozen ACT base policy paired with a lightweight 2-layer MLP residual policy trained via SAC, where the Bellman backup is modified to evaluate Q(o, a_base + a_res) so the residual learns corrections rather than absolute actions; (2) a keyframe-based affordance-guided dense reward that crops the current wrist image and a goal keyframe to the same ROI, encodes both with a shared visual encoder trained under an InfoNCE-style temporal contrastive objective (yielding temporally smooth embeddings), and uses the negative L2 embedding distance as a continuous process reward, combined with a per-task sparse binary classifier; (3) a human-in-the-loop mechanism with a timed intervention window W that prevents control conflicts between the human operator and the RL policy, storing human actions in a separate offline buffer while RL transitions go to an online buffer. The dual-buffer SAC training alternates between online and offline batches, and the residual policy's loose coupling with the base policy enables seamless substitution of different base policies.

## Results

FTC achieves a 100% success rate across all 6 contact-rich manipulation tasks (card insertion, door opening, charger grasp-insertion, keychain hanging, USB insertion easy/hard), compared to 82% for HIL-SERL and 46.7% for HG-DAgger. In terms of training efficiency, FTC requires only 27.5 minutes of human-in-the-loop training on average, more than 2× faster than HIL-SERL (≥56.7 min). On the most challenging USB insertion (hard) task, FTC reaches 100% success in 45 minutes while HIL-SERL fails to converge within 120 minutes. In OOD position generalization, FTC maintains 86.3% average success at unseen positions, whereas HIL-SERL drops to 0%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FTC decisively outperforms all five baselines on success rate. Against the strongest baseline HIL-SERL (82% avg), FTC achieves 100% on every task, with the most dramatic gap on USB insertion (hard): 100% vs. 0%. FTC also trains more than 2× faster (27.5 min vs. ≥56.7 min) and reduces human intervention steps by more than half compared to HIL-SERL. Against HG-DAgger (46.7%), FTC wins on 5 of 6 tasks, with HG-DAgger only matching FTC on keychain hanging (100%) and door opening (84%). Imitation-learning-only baselines (ACT at 1.3%, BC-MLP at 0%) and the sparse-reward residual RL method ResFiT (0%) fail almost entirely, confirming that contact-rich tasks require both human-in-the-loop interaction and dense guidance. In OOD generalization (Table 4), FTC achieves 86.3% average success at four unseen positions while HIL-SERL scores 0%, and removing the residual RL component drops FTC to 11.3%, highlighting the critical role of the residual design for spatial generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

FTC sits at the intersection of three research threads: residual RL (Alakuijala et al., 2021; Ankile et al., 2025a), which learns lightweight corrections on top of a frozen base policy; human-in-the-loop real-world RL (HIL-SERL, Luo et al., 2025), which uses expert demonstrations to bootstrap exploration in physical environments; and affordance-guided rewards (Lee et al., 2025; Bahl et al., 2023), which provide dense learning signals from visual task-relevant regions. The key departure from prior residual RL work (ResFiT, DP-RRL, EXPO) is the replacement of sparse task-completion rewards with a lightweight keyframe-based dense reward derived from a self-supervised contrastive encoder (inspired by VIP, Ma et al., 2023), avoiding the computational cost of VLM-based affordance prediction. The dual-buffer training scheme follows RLPD (Ball et al., 2023), and the base policy is ACT (Zhao et al., 2023), but the specific combination of residual RL + keyframe affordance + timed human intervention windows in a real-world contact-rich setting is novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FTC demonstrates that a lightweight, low-cost framework can halve the human intervention time needed for contact-rich real-world RL (27.5 min vs. ≥56.7 min for HIL-SERL) while achieving 100% success across six tasks and generalizing to unseen positions (86.3% vs. 0% for HIL-SERL). The ablation revealing that residual RL and affordance reward are individually sufficient for success but jointly necessary for speed is a practically useful insight for system design. However, the framework's visual robustness is limited—it fails under strong lighting and in-scene occlusions—and the keyframe-based reward is fundamentally incompatible with non-monotonic or process-oriented tasks (e.g., pushing, cloth folding), constraining its applicability to atomic insertion/alignment skills. Scaling to long-horizon, multi-step manipulation remains an open challenge.
