## Problem

In goal-conditioned RL, each transition contains information relevant to many goals simultaneously, yet most methods only update with respect to the single commanded goal or a small relabelled subset, discarding most available signal. All-goals learning would extract maximal information from every transition, but naive relabelling scales linearly with the number of goals, making it computationally infeasible for non-trivial goal sets. The paper asks whether all-goals updates can be made efficient enough to be practical at scale.

## Input / Output

The LEO network takes only the environment observation (grid-based in Craftax, state vector in ant maze) as input and outputs a 2D tensor of Q-values with shape (|G| × |A|), i.e., one Q-value per goal-action pair in a single forward pass. The commanded goal is never fed into the network; it is used only at inference time to index the relevant row of the output. In Dual LEO, the final action is selected from a linear combination α·Q_LEO(s,a,g) + (1−α)·Q_UVFA(s,a,g), where the UVFA network does condition on the goal as input. For continuous control, the policy network similarly outputs a distribution over actions for every goal head simultaneously.

## Method

LEO reparameterises the goal-conditioned Q-network via currying: instead of Q(s,g): S×G → R^A, it learns Q(s): S → R^{G×A}, so a single forward pass produces Q-values for all goals and actions at once. The Q-learning update is then fully vectorised as L = (R(s') + γ·max_{a'} Q(a'|s') − Q(a|s))², where R(s') is the reward vector across all goals, requiring no reference to the commanded goal and no relabelling. For actor-critic settings, the policy network is similarly curried to output an all-goals policy, though the policy gradient requires O(|G|) backward passes. The key limitation is late fusion: because the goal is absent from the input, the network must allocate equal representational capacity to all goals, hurting performance on easy goals. Dual LEO overcomes this by training a LEO network and a standard UVFA network in parallel on the same data stream, where LEO acts as a coarse teacher that provides positive signal for goals the UVFA network has never seen commanded, before the UVFA network takes over with high-fidelity estimates once it has bootstrapped from those examples.

## Results

On the full CraftaxGC benchmark (512 goals), Dual LEO (PPO) massively outperforms all baselines in mean goal success rate, with LEO alone providing a significant boost over its base algorithm PQN. On the smaller Craftax-Classic (136 goals), Dual LEO (PPO) achieves the best final performance, though the gap over PPO is marginal. On JaxGCRL ant maze tasks, SAC+LEO outperforms all baselines on the U Maze, while on the larger maze LEO, SAC+HER, and Contrastive RL perform similarly. In terms of computational efficiency, LEO achieves a >250× speedup over naive all-goals relabelling, with only a 34% slowdown compared to standard single-goal learning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PQN (Gallici et al., 2024), LEO provides a significant performance boost on the full Craftax benchmark (512 goals), where PQN alone performs poorly due to the lack of positive signal in most episodes; however, on the smaller Craftax-Classic, PQN+HER actually outperforms LEO. Against PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), the relationship inverts with goal set size: PPO beats LEO on Craftax-Classic (136 goals) where simple goal-conditioned learning suffices, but LEO and Dual LEO substantially outperform PPO on full Craftax (512 goals) where the all-goals update extracts far more information from each transition. Against HER (Andrychowicz et al., 2017), the paper shows that naive all-goals HER relabelling is 264× slower than LEO, making it impractical; targeted HER strategies (e.g., Mixed 1+1 trajectory-level) improve PQN but still fall short of LEO on the larger goal set. Against goal-conditioned SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018) and TD3 (Fujimoto et al., 2018) on JaxGCRL, SAC+LEO wins on the U Maze but is competitive (not clearly superior) on the larger maze, where the locomotion challenge dominates over goal-specific learning. Against Contrastive RL (Eysenbach et al., 2022), LEO is competitive on the larger ant maze and superior on the U Maze, with the added advantage of supporting arbitrary reward functions rather than requiring a functional state-to-goal mapping.

## Relation to Prior Work

LEO generalises the all-goals update idea from tabular RL (Kaelbling, 1993) and Horde (Sutton et al., 2011) to deep networks by currying the goal dimension from the input to the output layer, making it a direct generalisation of Q-Map (Pardo et al., 2018) from pixel-grid goals to arbitrary discrete goal sets. The method is built on top of PQN (Gallici et al., 2024) for discrete actions and SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018) for continuous control, while the Dual LEO variant introduces a teacher-student dynamic between the LEO network and a standard UVFA-style (Schaul et al., 2015) goal-conditioned network. The paper departs from the dominant HER (Andrychowicz et al., 2017) paradigm of selecting a single informative relabelling goal by instead updating with respect to the entire goal set simultaneously, sidestepping the goal-selection problem entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 250× speedup over naive all-goals relabelling makes previously infeasible all-goals learning practical, and the identification of the late-fusion bottleneck (where the very architecture enabling efficient learning hinders focused acting) provides a useful conceptual framework for understanding the UVFA-vs-LEO tradeoff. The CraftaxGC benchmark is valuable as it tests GCRL in a setting with heterogeneous, non-functional goals (many states satisfy multiple goals simultaneously) that existing benchmarks like Ant Maze cannot capture. Key limitations include the requirement for finite goal sets (precluding high-dimensional continuous goals), the O(G) backwards passes needed for the continuous-control policy update (~70% throughput drop), and the open question of whether a single architecture can natively interpolate between the early-fusion (UVFA) and late-fusion (LEO) extremes rather than requiring two separate networks.
