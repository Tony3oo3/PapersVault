## Problem

Human decision makers routinely fail to extract complementary value from AI predictions even when the AI carries useful signal, and prior theoretical analyses assumed the decision maker knows the full joint distribution of AI predictions and ground truth. This paper asks how uncertainty about AI prediction quality—formalized as an uncertainty set over the joint distribution—changes the conditions under which a decision maker can robustly benefit from combining human and AI signals, and whether those conditions are met in practice.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a human prediction signal φ_H and an AI prediction signal φ_AI, with the decision maker's knowledge restricted to an uncertainty set U that fixes all entries of the joint covariance except Cov(φ_AI, θ). The output is a robust decision rule: in the MSE setting, a linear combination d = φ_H + b·φ_AI guaranteed to dominate the human-only prediction across all Σ ∈ U; in the binary setting, an investment rule that overrules the human-only threshold only when the AI residual provides uniformly safe evidence. The key structural requirement is a lower bound δ on Cov(φ_AI, θ) and an upper bound γ on the conditional error covariance Cov(φ_H, φ_AI | θ).

## Method

The paper develops a statistical decision theory framework in which the decision maker faces an uncertainty set over the joint distribution of (θ, φ_H, φ_AI), with uncertainty concentrated in Cov(φ_AI, θ). The core mechanism is a residual signal r = φ_AI − Cov(φ_H,φ_AI)/Var(φ_H)·φ_H that captures the AI's contribution beyond the human signal; under negative conditional error correlation (Cov(φ_H,φ_AI|θ) ≤ 0), the regression coefficient of r on θ is uniformly bounded below across the uncertainty set, enabling a conservative linear combination that provably improves MSE. For binary investment decisions, the authors construct pessimistic bounds on the posterior success probability using the residual and a lower bound on its regression coefficient, yielding a symmetric robust rule that overrules the human-only threshold only when guaranteed to help. The results extend beyond the Gaussian model to nonlinear monotone signal structures with negative dependence in errors. Empirically, using ForecastBench and TESS social science benchmarks with GPT-5, the authors find that human and LLM prediction errors are positively correlated (ρ ≈ 0.96 on ForecastBench), placing current models in the difficult regime where robust complementarity requires the human signal to be substantially noisier than the AI's, and prompting interventions fail to consistently induce the negative error correlation needed.

## Results

On ForecastBench, human and LLM prediction errors are strongly positively correlated (ρ = 0.963 baseline), and the robust combination estimator achieves a Robust MSE of 0.154, identical to the Calibrated Human MSE of 0.154, indicating no complementarity gain. On TESS studies, the baseline error correlation is lower (ρ = 0.216), and the robust combination yields a modest improvement (Robust MSE 0.00510 vs. Calibrated Human MSE 0.00524). Across 12 prompting-strategy × dataset combinations, only one instance (Contrarian Reasoning on TESS) shifts the error correlation to negative (ρ = −0.338) and produces a meaningful MSE reduction (0.00490 vs. 0.00524, a ~6.5% relative improvement).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between the human-only decision rule (d_H, represented by Calibrated Human MSE) and the robust joint decision rule (d_J, represented by Robust MSE) that incorporates the AI signal. On ForecastBench, the robust combination fails to improve over the human-only baseline in any prompting condition (Robust MSE = 0.154 in 5 of 6 cases, 0.153 in one), because the very high positive error correlation (ρ ≥ 0.498) places all settings in the 'difficult' regime where the theory predicts no robust gain. On TESS, the baseline already shows a small improvement (0.00510 vs. 0.00524), and Contrarian Reasoning is the only strategy that flips the error correlation to negative (−0.338), yielding the largest improvement (0.00490, a 6.5% relative reduction). The paper also compares against the theoretical prediction that positive error correlation should preclude robust complementarity, and the empirical results confirm this: in all 11 positively-correlated cases, the robust estimator matches or only marginally beats the human-only baseline, consistent with the theoretical bound that improvement requires the human signal to be sufficiently noisy relative to the AI's absolute performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical literature on human-AI complementarity (Steyvers et al., 2022; Donahue et al., 2022; Wilder et al., 2020) by introducing a robust-optimization / credal-set framework in which the decision maker does not know the joint distribution of AI predictions and ground truth. Prior work either assumed the model's quality was known (Steyvers et al.) or analyzed complementarity without formalizing uncertainty about AI performance (Donahue et al.). The paper also connects to the empirical LLM-forecasting literature (Karger et al., 2025; Hewitt et al., 2024; Halawi et al., 2024), which has focused on comparing LLM and human accuracy but has not characterized the error-correlation structure that determines whether combination is robustly beneficial.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical finding—that human and LLM prediction errors are strongly positively correlated (ρ ≈ 0.96 on ForecastBench)—means current LLMs largely replicate rather than complement human errors, placing real-world deployment in the 'difficult' regime where robust complementarity requires the human to be substantially noisier than the AI. The failure of 12 prompting strategies to reliably flip error correlation to negative suggests that achieving genuine complementarity may require new training objectives rather than post-hoc interventions. Open questions include whether fine-tuning or RL can explicitly optimize for negative error correlation, and whether the Gaussian/linear assumptions (though extended to monotone non-Gaussian signals) are sufficient for the complex, multi-step reasoning tasks where LLMs are most deployed.
