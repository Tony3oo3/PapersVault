## Problem

Randomized CNNs produce saliency maps that closely resemble those of trained models, a phenomenon previously attributed mainly to saliency method properties such as edge sensitivity. This paper identifies a previously underappreciated architectural contributor: a center-focused saliency bias induced by zero padding and hierarchical receptive field growth that is already present at random initialization and persists even under pixel-shuffled inputs, thereby confounding trained-random similarity evaluations.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on 224×224 RGB images paired with model weights (either randomly initialized or fine-tuned from ImageNet-pretrained checkpoints) and a chosen saliency method (vanilla gradients, SmoothGrad, Integrated Gradients, GradCAM, etc.). From the resulting saliency maps, the paper computes three types of outputs: spatial bias scores (Center-Bias AUC, median difference, radial cumulative AUC) comparing center versus corner regions, a Center-Shift Index (CSI) quantifying how far saliency mass displaces toward a relocated object, and trained-random similarity scores across eight pixelwise, structure-based, and location-aware metrics. The corner-shift benchmark constructs synthetic training/evaluation sets by uniformly downscaled objects (0.5×) placed at the image center or top-left corner, with backgrounds filled by pixel-shuffled original image content to preserve color statistics.

## Method

The paper provides a mechanistic explanation for architectural center bias: zero padding reduces the number of valid kernel contributions at image boundaries, lowering pre-activation variance at peripheral locations relative to the interior; this variance imbalance propagates through backpropagation to produce systematically larger gradient magnitudes at central pixels, an effect amplified by receptive field growth with depth. Controlled ablations confirm the mechanism by showing that replacing zero padding with reflect, replicate, or circular padding eliminates the bias, and that center bias saturates once the effective receptive field exceeds the image dimensions. To disentangle this architectural prior from training-induced spatial emphasis, the authors introduce a corner-shift benchmark (center-shrunk vs. corner-shrunk ImageNet variants) and the Center-Shift Index, a signed scalar measuring saliency mass displacement relative to the image center. Across a diverse set of CNNs (Inception-v4, AlexNet, ResNet, EfficientNet, MobileNet, DenseNet, ShuffleNet, VGG, SqueezeNet) and non-convolutional baselines (ViT-B/16, ViT-L/16, MLP-Mixer-B/16), the paper demonstrates that center bias is architecture-specific to convolutional designs, that training partially shifts saliency toward object location but residual center bias persists, and that the alignment between architectural center priors and dataset-level object placement inflates trained-random saliency similarity under standard evaluation protocols.

## Results

Across 12 architectures under random initialization, CNNs exhibit strong center bias with Center-Bias AUC (CB-AUC) ranging from 0.58 (ShuffleNet-V2) to 0.92 (Inception-v4), while ViTs and MLPs cluster near the no-bias baseline of 0.51–0.52. On the corner-shift benchmark, trained-random saliency similarity (Blur-Pearson) drops from ρ≈0.40 under centered objects to ρ≈0.15 when objects are relocated to the corner, directly demonstrating that architectural center bias inflates similarity scores. The Center-Shift Index (CSI) shows training shifts saliency toward the displaced object (mean CSI ≈ 0.457 for top-left placement vs. ideal 0.502), but residual center bias persists across all CNN architectures. Classification accuracy is comparable between center-shrunk and corner-shrunk conditions (e.g., Inception-v4: 68.18% vs. 66.00%), confirming the saliency shift is not due to training failure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is architectural: all nine CNN families (Inception-v4, AlexNet, ResNet18, EfficientNet-B0, MobileNet-V2, SqueezeNet, DenseNet121, VGG11, ShuffleNet-V2) show CB-AUC well above 0.5 (range 0.58–0.92), while ViT-B/16, ViT-L/16, and MLP-Mixer-B/16 remain at or below 0.52, confirming the center bias is specific to convolutional design. Ablating padding from zero to reflect/replicate/circular substantially reduces center bias across all three metrics, isolating zero padding as the primary mechanism. On the corner-shift benchmark, trained CNNs shift saliency toward the object (CSI ≈ 0.37–0.59 depending on scale and corner) but never fully reach the ideal 0.502, whereas random models remain center-locked regardless of object position. The effect is consistent across six saliency methods (Gradients, SmoothGrad, Integrated Gradients, Input×Gradient, Guided Backprop, GradCAM), though GradCAM shows weaker and more variable center bias due to its reliance on intermediate feature maps. Adversarial fine-tuning (FGSM) on ResNet18 pushes CSI closer to the ideal (e.g., 0.483–0.496 vs. 0.387–0.464 for standard training), suggesting training dynamics can further modulate but not eliminate the architectural prior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: sanity checks for saliency methods (Adebayo et al., 2018 and subsequent critiques) and the study of architectural inductive biases in CNNs (zero padding effects, receptive field growth). While prior sanity-check work attributed trained-random saliency similarity primarily to properties of the saliency method itself (e.g., edge-detector behavior), and architectural bias work characterized spatial nonuniformities in activations without connecting them to interpretability evaluation, this paper bridges the two by showing that standard convolutional design choices (zero padding, hierarchical aggregation) induce a center-focused saliency prior at initialization that systematically inflates trained-random similarity scores. The corner-shift benchmark and Center-Shift Index are new diagnostic tools introduced to disentangle this architectural prior from learned spatial emphasis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a ubiquitous architectural choice (zero padding) systematically confounds one of the most widely used saliency evaluation protocols (trained-random sanity checks) has direct practical implications: practitioners interpreting high trained-random similarity as evidence of method insensitivity may be misled by architectural spatial priors aligning with dataset-level object placement. The corner-shift benchmark provides a concrete spatial stress test to contextualize such scores. Limitations include the focus on image classification, gradient-based saliency, and ImageNet-style centered objects; open questions include whether analogous spatial priors exist in other modalities, how the effect interacts with fidelity-based evaluation metrics, and whether architectural modifications (e.g., alternative padding) can be adopted without accuracy cost to reduce this confound.
