## Problem

Planning with a learned model in model-based RL suffers from compounding errors over long prediction horizons, making flat decision-time planners unreliable. The core challenge is finding a state representation that simultaneously supports local cost computation and preserves long-horizon temporal structure, so that long-horizon tasks can be decomposed into shorter, model-reliable subproblems. This paper shows that the Laplacian representation, which encodes state-space distances at multiple time scales via eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian, provides such a unified metric space.

## Input / Output

At each decision step, ALPS receives the current state observation (a low-dimensional state vector of 2–69 dimensions, or a 64×64×3 third-person RGB image for pixel-based tasks) and a goal state, and outputs a single action to execute. The method operates entirely offline: all components (Laplacian encoder, forward model, behavior prior, cluster graph) are pre-trained on a fixed dataset before any planning occurs. The high-level planner produces a sequence of cluster-center subgoals via Dijkstra, and the low-level CEM planner converts the next subgoal into a concrete action sequence from which only the first action is executed before replanning.

## Method

ALPS is a hierarchical decision-time planner whose latent space is the scaled Laplacian representation (ψ-space), learned via the ALLO objective, which is isometric to commute time distance and thus encodes how easily one state is reachable from another under the environment's dynamics. During pre-training, ALPS learns (1) the Laplacian eigenvectors/eigenvalues, (2) a multi-step auto-regressive forward model in the original state space, (3) a goal-conditioned behavior prior in ψ-space that warm-starts CEM, and (4) a cluster graph via k-means in ψ-space with nucleus-sampling pruning. At decision time, Dijkstra's algorithm finds a shortest path over the cluster graph to produce a high-level subgoal sequence, while CEM—initialized by auto-regressive rollouts of the behavior prior through the forward model—optimizes action sequences by minimizing the ψ-space distance to the current subgoal plus an action-magnitude penalty. The key differentiator from prior work (e.g., PcLast) is that the Laplacian representation intrinsically captures multi-scale temporal structure and natural environment partitioning, eliminating the need for a separate contrastive learning objective to construct a plannable latent space.

## Results

On OGBench, ALPS significantly outperforms all six model-free GCRL baselines (GCBC, GCIVL, GCIQL, QRL, CRL, HIQL) with p < 0.001 under Holm-Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Headline results include 98% success on pointmaze-giant-stitch (vs. 50% for the best baseline QRL), 100% on antmaze-medium-explore (vs. 37% for HIQL), and 92% on antmaze-giant-stitch (vs. 2% for HIQL). On Maze2D-PointMass, ALPS without the behavior prior (ALPS†) achieves 91–97% success, substantially outperforming PcLast (30–62%) across Hallway, Rooms, and Spiral mazes. ALPS is notably more robust to maze size, maintaining strong performance on giant mazes where most baselines collapse to near-zero.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ALPS outperforms all six OGBench model-free baselines (GCBC, GCIVL, GCIQL, QRL, CRL, HIQL) across the majority of locomotion tasks, with the largest margins on giant mazes and explore datasets (e.g., 92% vs. 2% on antmaze-giant-stitch; 100% vs. 37% on antmaze-medium-explore). Against PcLast on Maze2D-PointMass, ALPS† (without the behavior prior) achieves 91–97% success versus PcLast's 30–62%, demonstrating that the Laplacian latent space alone provides a stronger planning metric. In the extended comparison (Table 4), ALPS also beats HILP, RLDP, and TD-JEPA on most antmaze tasks (e.g., 93% vs. 53% on antmaze-large-navigate), though it trails SAW on scene-play (26% vs. 63%) and HILP on cube-single (68% vs. 74%). ALPS's main weakness is multi-object manipulation: performance drops to 2% on cube-double-play (vs. 40% for GCIQL), which the authors attribute to the global nature of the Laplacian representation failing to capture conditional independence across objects. Overall, ALPS is the first model-based method to match or exceed the best model-free baselines across OGBench, with particular strength in long-horizon and low-data-quality settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

ALPS directly extends PcLast (Koul et al., 2024) by replacing its contrastive-learning latent space with the Laplacian representation learned via ALLO (Gomez et al., 2023), while retaining the same hierarchical planning scaffold (k-means subgoals, Dijkstra high-level planning, CEM low-level planning). The Laplacian representation itself traces back to Mahadevan's proto-value functions (2005, 2007), which established eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian as a principled state embedding capturing multi-scale temporal structure. A key methodological departure is the use of a behavior prior (inspired by TD-MPC2, Hansen et al., 2024) to warm-start CEM, addressing the slow convergence of pure noise-based sampling in high-dimensional action spaces. The paper also connects the scaled Laplacian space to commute time distance (Klein & Randić, 1993), providing a theoretical justification for using Euclidean distance in ψ-space as a planning cost.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first model-based method to outperform all model-free baselines on OGBench, a benchmark where model-free approaches had historically dominated, suggesting that the compounding-error problem in learned models can be mitigated through principled latent-space geometry rather than simply avoided. The commute-time-distance isometry provides a clean theoretical grounding for why the Laplacian space is suitable for planning, and the spectral clustering property offers a principled subgoal discovery mechanism. Key limitations include the global nature of the Laplacian representation failing on multi-object manipulation (where conditionally independent sub-dynamics are conflated), the symmetrization assumption causing issues in asymmetric environments like teleporter mazes, and the behavior prior inadvertently hurting performance on simpler tasks. Open directions include asymmetric Laplacian formulations, factorized representations for compositional state spaces, and stacking the Laplacian on top of lower-level learned encodings.
