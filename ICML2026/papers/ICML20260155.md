## Problem

Falling Rule Lists (FRLs) provide an interpretable framework for prioritizing high-risk cases by enforcing monotonically decreasing risk along a sequence of rules, but their single-path structure yields a highly restricted model class. Existing FRL optimization algorithms cannot guarantee full optimality, require rules to be pre-mined with separate algorithms, and suffer from the interaction bottleneck where only one model is produced per iteration, slowing the expert feedback loop. The paper asks whether the falling constraint can be generalized to tree-structured branching while retaining interpretability and enabling exact optimization.

## Input / Output

The method takes a tabular dataset of n binary feature vectors (continuous or categorical features are binarized beforehand) with binary labels, and produces a Rashomon set of falling trees—i.e., all near-optimal trees within a user-specified tolerance ε of the optimal objective. Each tree in the set is constrained by a depth budget d, a minimum leaf support α, and a branching penalty μ that controls how much the tree deviates from a rule-list structure. The output trees assign a risk probability to each leaf, and the falling constraint guarantees that high-risk instances are resolved with fewer feature queries (lower decision sparsity on the positive class).

## Method

GRAVITree is a dynamic-programming-with-bounds algorithm that recursively constructs a search graph over candidate splits, pruning branches whose optimal completions cannot satisfy the Rashomon loss bound or the falling constraint. The key architectural contribution is a branching cost H(T) that counts internal nodes whose two children are both internal, enabling the user to interpolate between rule lists (H=0) and full decision trees (H=2^{d-1}) via a penalty parameter μ. The falling constraint is generalized to trees by requiring that, along every root-to-leaf path, the positive-class probabilities of adjacent leaves decrease monotonically with depth; Theorems 4.1 and 4.2 provide necessary-and-sufficient conditions that enable aggressive local and global pruning. Because child subproblems are coupled (the branching penalty depends on whether both children are internal, and the falling constraint propagates probability bounds between siblings), the algorithm recurses first on the higher-probability child and then handles two cases—leaf vs. tree—each with sub-options that either tighten the probability bound on the sibling or substitute the second-best (non-leaf) solution. A quantized caching strategy memoizes subproblem solutions keyed by (data indices, depth, quantized p⁺_recent) to avoid redundant computation across different feature-ordering paths.

## Results

Across 22 benchmark datasets, GRAVITree (μ=0.01, α=0.05, d=5, ε=0.01) achieves test loss within ~1–3% of TreeFARMS (λ=0.005) while producing lower positive-class decision sparsity β(T,D⁺) on roughly 14 of 22 datasets (e.g., MGH: 2.01 vs 3.05; Mushroom: 2.51 vs 3.63; Spambase: 2.43 vs 3.33). False negative rate is competitive, with GRAVITree winning on ~10 datasets (e.g., NIJ Recidivism: 0.126 vs 0.154; Heart: 0.198 vs 0.237) and TreeFARMS winning on others. The branching penalty μ smoothly interpolates model structure: increasing μ from 0 to 0.1 drives the Normalized Colless Index toward 1 (rule-list-like) while reducing Rashomon set size and runtime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TreeFARMS (Xin et al., 2022), the primary external baseline for sparse decision tree Rashomon sets, GRAVITree trades a small increase in overall test loss (typically 1–3 percentage points, e.g., Spambase 0.126 vs 0.102; Mushroom 0.030 vs 0.007) for substantially lower positive-class decision sparsity on the majority of datasets (e.g., MGH 2.01 vs 3.05; Mushroom 2.51 vs 3.63; Spambase 2.43 vs 3.33), meaning high-risk patients require fewer feature queries to classify. FNR is competitive and mixed: GRAVITree achieves lower FNR on ~10 datasets (NIJ 0.126 vs 0.154; Heart 0.198 vs 0.237; Wine 0.169 vs 0.203) while TreeFARMS wins on others (Adult 0.177 vs 0.206; Spambase 0.154 vs 0.181). Against the falling rule list baseline FRAME (an extension of Chen & Rudin, 2018), GRAVITree in rule-list mode (μ=1) achieves comparable or better test loss with significantly smaller Rashomon sets (e.g., COMPAS: 154.6 vs 794±83 models; NIJ: 16.6 vs 43.2±11.1), while FRAME sometimes achieves lower decision sparsity due to its multi-feature antecedents. The key practical advantage of GRAVITree over both baselines is that it is an exact (provably optimal) algorithm rather than an approximate Monte Carlo method, and it does not require pre-mined rules.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes Falling Rule Lists (Wang & Rudin, 2015; Chen & Rudin, 2018) from single-path decision lists to tree-structured models while preserving the monotonic risk ('falling') constraint. The optimization algorithm GRAVITree draws on the dynamic-programming-with-bounds paradigm established for optimal sparse decision trees (McTavish et al., 2022; Xin et al., 2022; van der Linden et al., 2023), but introduces new bounds that exploit the falling constraint to prune the search space. A key departure from prior tree optimization is the branching cost H(T), which penalizes the number of nodes with two internal children rather than the number of leaves, enabling direct interpolation between rule lists and full trees—a control axis absent from sparsity-regularized tree methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Falling trees offer a practical middle ground for high-stakes settings (e.g., ICU mortality triage) where practitioners need models that are both interpretable and expressive enough to capture interactions that a single rule list cannot. The branching cost provides a user-tunable knob for model shape that is orthogonal to the traditional leaf-count sparsity penalty, and the falling constraint yields provable pruning guarantees (Theorems 4.1–4.2) that make exact Rashomon set computation tractable. Open questions include how to handle continuous features without lossy binarization (the MIMIC case study shows a notable accuracy gap vs. XGBoost on continuous data), whether the quantized caching approximation affects guarantees at scale, and how the approach extends to multi-class or ordinal risk settings.
