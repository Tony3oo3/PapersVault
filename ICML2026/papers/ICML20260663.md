## Problem

The Gram-Schmidt Walk (GSW) design of Harshaw et al. (2024) achieves a near-optimal balance-robustness trade-off but only targets linear covariate balance. In many applications, covariate-outcome relationships are nonlinear (quadratic, cubic, interactions), so two arms can be perfectly matched in means yet differ substantially in variances and higher-order structure, degrading estimation precision. The central question is whether GSW's robustness guarantees can be retained while directly balancing rich nonlinear function classes.

## Input / Output

The method takes an n×d covariate matrix X, a balance-robustness parameter φ ∈ (0,1), and a sequence of nonnegative feature weights α = {α_k} (with Σ α_k ≤ 1) that control the relative priority placed on balancing each covariate moment. It produces a random assignment vector z ∈ {±1}^n. Crucially, the algorithm operates on an n×n Gram matrix K whose entries K(i,j) = Σ_k α_k ⟨x_i, x_j⟩^k / ξ^{2k} are computed from pairwise inner products of raw covariates, avoiding explicit construction of high- or infinite-dimensional tensor feature vectors.

## Method

The key insight is that GSW's direction oracle depends only on pairwise inner products, so it can be run on a Gram matrix induced by a nonlinear feature map ψ_α(x) = (√α_0, √α_1/ξ·x, √α_2/ξ²·x^⊗2, …) in a Hilbert direct sum of tensor spaces. This balances all tensor moments up to the order specified by α without ever forming the high-dimensional feature matrix. For scalability, a Nyström low-rank approximation of K (rank k ≪ n) reduces runtime from O(n³) to O(n²k) while provably degrading the covariance bound by a factor controlled by the approximation error ε_k. Theoretical guarantees include subgaussian discrepancy in the feature space, covariate balance bounds for any C^{m+1} function via Taylor decomposition (with the polynomial part controlled by feature balance and the remainder by randomness), and finite-sample variance and tail bounds for the Horvitz-Thompson ATE estimator.

## Results

On linear outcomes, GSW(1) achieves the minimum MSE ratio of 1.00 at all sample sizes, while Bernoulli/CR are 8.7× (n=30) to 113× (n=500) worse. On quadratic outcomes, the nonlinear variants dominate: GSW(∞) is best at n=30 (ratio 1.00) and GSW(2) at n=100 (ratio 1.00), whereas GSW(1) degrades to ratios of 2.03–3.97. For sinusoidal outcomes, GSW(∞) achieves the best MSE at n≥100 (ratio 1.00), while GSW(1) lags by 2.75–5.75×. On covariate moment imbalance (Table 2, φ=0.5), GSW(2)–GSW(∞) attain the minimum or tied-minimum on moments 2–5 with only a modest increase on moment 1 (e.g., GSW(2) ratio 1.54–1.62 vs. GSW(1) ratio 1.00).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GSW(1) (Harshaw et al., 2024), the nonlinear variants trade off linear-outcome efficiency for nonlinear gains: GSW(1) is optimal for linear outcomes at all n, but on quadratic outcomes it is 2.03–5.27× worse than the best nonlinear variant, and on sinusoidal outcomes it is 2.75–6.16× worse. Against Fisher blocking, all GSW variants achieve dramatically lower MSE (e.g., 8.39× vs. 1.00× for GSW(1) on linear at n=30) and substantially better higher-moment balance. Against pairwise matching (Greevy et al., 2004), GSW designs dominate on linear and sinusoidal outcomes but matching becomes competitive or optimal for quadratic outcomes at n≥200, where its deterministic optimization exploits the known quadratic structure. Against rerandomization (Morgan & Rubin, 2012), GSW variants consistently achieve lower MSE and tighter higher-moment balance (e.g., GSW(2) moment-2 ratio 1.00 vs. RR's 1.57 at n=100). Against the kernel-discrepancy rerandomization of Li et al. (2019), the paper reports that kernel-rerandomization variants are not competitive with the best GSW configurations in the n=100, d=10 setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Gram-Schmidt Walk (GSW) design of Harshaw et al. (2024), which achieves a near-optimal balance-robustness tradeoff for *linear* covariate balance, to the nonlinear setting. The key conceptual departure from prior kernel-based optimal allocation methods (Kallus, 2018; Bertsimas et al., 2015) is that instead of solving mixed-integer or semidefinite programs for a deterministic balanced assignment, the authors embed nonlinear balance into the *randomized* GSW walk via a Gram matrix induced by a tensor feature map. This preserves the robustness guarantees of GSW against model misspecification, which optimal designs lack (Krieger et al., 2019), while the Gram-matrix implementation avoids explicitly constructing high- or infinite-dimensional feature expansions. The low-rank (Nyström) approximation further connects to the kernel approximation literature (Williams & Seeger, 2000; Gittens & Mahoney, 2016) to achieve O(n²k) runtime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, theoretically grounded recipe for balancing nonlinear covariate structure in RCTs while retaining the randomized-design robustness that makes GSW attractive over deterministic optimal allocation. The quantitative conditions (Lemma 5.3) for when higher-order terms improve precision, and the explicit balance-robustness knob φ, give experimenters actionable guidance. Open questions include: how to choose the feature weights α_k adaptively rather than by domain knowledge, whether the approach extends naturally to multi-arm or cluster-randomized settings, and how performance degrades when covariates are unbounded or the kernel is poorly conditioned. The low-rank approximation's error propagation (Lemma 6.1) is characterized but the practical choice of rank k beyond O(d) remains heuristic.
