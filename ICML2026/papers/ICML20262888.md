## Problem

Multilingual language models frequently produce conflicting responses to translation-equivalent prompts across languages, undermining reliability in cross-lingual applications. While this inconsistency is well-documented empirically, no prior work provides a principled information-theoretic definition of crosslingual consistency or an efficient post-training procedure that directly optimizes it without requiring on-policy rollouts or noisy majority-vote pseudo-labels.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262888/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes parallel prompt pairs in two languages (e.g., English and Swahili) along with their candidate responses, a fixed reference language model (the initial checkpoint), and per-language strength parameters β₁, β₂ that control the directionality of alignment. It operates at the logit level: the model's unnormalized log-probabilities over candidate responses are regressed against a closed-form target computed from the reference model's round-trip pushforward, so no on-policy sampling is needed. The output is a post-trained LM whose response distributions are aligned across languages, evaluated via RankC (ranking-based crosslingual consistency) and per-language accuracy on multiple-choice and factual-association tasks.

## Method

The paper first defines crosslingual consistency as an f-divergence bound between a model's response distribution in one language and its round-trip pushforward (translate prompt → sample response → back-translate) into that same language, with temperature annealing to absorb sharpness mismatch. Building on this, Penalized Consistency Optimization (PCO) couples the consistency divergence with a KL penalty to a fixed reference, yielding a unique minimizer in closed form as a weighted geometric mean of the reference and its round-trip pushforward. Because direct PCO optimization requires expensive on-policy rollouts, the authors introduce Direct Consistency Optimization (DCO), a logit-matching supervised loss that regresses the model's logits against the unnormalized log-target from the closed-form solution—mirroring DPO's trick of bypassing policy gradients. Proposition 3 proves DCO's minimizer coincides exactly with PCO's, so the same theoretical consistency guarantee holds without any on-policy sampling. Per-language strength parameters β₁, β₂ (constrained by β₁β₂=1 for exact consistency under invertible translators) let practitioners anchor a high-resource language while updating a low-resource one, enabling targeted alignment without degrading the dominant language.

## Results

In the joint 12-language training setup on MMMLU, DCO (without reference completions) improves CLC_all by +2.7 to +10.6 points across five models, while preserving or improving accuracy in both English and non-English languages. In bilingual English–target-language experiments, DCO raises CLC_all by +4.8 to +12.6 on MMMLU, +4.6 to +9.1 on XCSQA, and +12.3 to +16.7 on BMLAMA, with concurrent non-English accuracy gains of up to +17.6 points. On-policy RL with the PCO reward further improves both per-language accuracy and EN–ZH consistency on GSM8K (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B: 89.2→90.0 EN, 83.6→86.8 ZH, 84.7→86.9 consistency) and MMMLU (66.2→70.1 EN, 58.8→59.7 ZH, 68.9→72.3 consistency).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DCO (without reference completions) nearly matches DPO (with reference completions) on CLC_all across all five models, and on Aya-Expanse-8B it clearly surpasses DPO (+5.3 vs +1.3 CLC_all). When reference completions are available, DCO* slightly edges out DPO* on CLC_all for Qwen2.5-14B (+13.1 vs +12.3) and Llama3.1-8B (+13.8 vs +10.1), while DPO retains a small advantage on accuracy for some models. Against CALM, DCO consistently wins on CLC_all by a wide margin (e.g., +10.6 vs +4.2 on Qwen2.5-14B; +9.4 vs +3.0 on Llama3.1-8B), and unlike CALM, DCO does not degrade English or non-English accuracy. SFT provides only marginal CLC gains (+0.6 to +4.3) and is clearly insufficient for crosslingual consistency. The two-step DPO+DCO recipe yields the highest CLC_all across all models, confirming DCO as a complementary post-step to preference-based alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), adapting its core insight—that a preference objective's closed-form minimizer can be matched via supervised logit regression—to a new crosslingual-consistency objective (PCO) rather than a human-preference one. It departs from prior consistency-improvement methods like CALM (Wang et al., 2025b), which rely on majority-voted pseudo-labels and DPO, by instead using a divergence-based round-trip pushforward formulation that requires no reference completions and no majority voting. The information-theoretic definition of crosslingual consistency via f-divergences between a model's response distribution and its round-trip pushforward is a new formalization; prior work (Qi et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2020) measured consistency empirically (e.g., RankC, top-1 agreement) without a generative objective to optimize.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DCO offers a computationally cheap, off-policy path to crosslingual consistency that matches or exceeds DPO without requiring reference completions, and the strength parameters (β₁, β₂) give practitioners a principled knob to protect high-resource languages while aligning low-resource ones. The out-of-domain transfer (training on one subject, gaining ~11% CLC on unseen subjects) suggests the method could be applied in data-scarce settings. Open questions include whether the invertible-translator assumption holds in practice for noisy MT systems, whether enforcing consistency too rigidly erases legitimate cultural variation (as the authors caution), and whether the preliminary on-policy RL results scale to broader open-ended generation tasks.
