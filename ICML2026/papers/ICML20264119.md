## Problem

Prior theoretical analyses of RLHF and DPO relied on the realizability assumption—that both the ground-truth reward and the optimal policy lie within their respective model classes—which is unrealistic when reward models are much smaller than policy models or data is limited. This paper removes that assumption and asks: under what conditions is DPO equivalent, superior, or inferior to two-stage RLHF in performance?

## Input / Output

The analysis takes pairwise preference data generated under the Bradley-Terry model, a fixed reference policy π_ref, and two parameterized model classes (reward class F with d_R parameters, policy class Π with d_P parameters) as inputs. The output is a characterization of the KL-regularized value function V_{r*}^π for each method's resulting policy, decomposed into an explicit representation gap (from model capacity under exact optimization) and an implicit representation gap (from statistical efficiency under finite samples). For the statistical separation, they use a token-level linear parameterization where the reward is a sum of per-token linear functions and the policy is a product of per-token softmax distributions over a vocabulary.

## Method

The paper decomposes the RLHF–DPO performance gap into two sources. Under exact optimization, they characterize four mis-specification regimes: RLHF wins when the policy class is too small (DPO's preference-based objective cannot recover the best achievable policy), DPO wins when the reward class is too small (RLHF optimizes against a corrupted reward), and under double mis-specification the winner depends on whether F and the surrogate class F_Π are isomorphic or one strictly contains the other. Under approximate optimization, they construct a dual-token sparse prediction (DTSP) task where the ground-truth reward has sparsity k ≪ d, showing that RLHF's reward learning leverages sparsity to achieve O(k log d / n) estimation error (ℓ₀) or O(√(k log d / n)) (ℓ₁), while DPO's surrogate reward is lower-bounded at Ω(d/n) because it must additionally encode the recursive q* value function that couples the reward with the base model, destroying sparsity. The PILAF sampler is shown to make online DPO's objective approximate the true value function up to a second-order remainder that grows with reward scale, explaining when online DPO can or cannot close the gap.

## Results

The paper's headline theoretical result (Theorem 10) establishes a sample-complexity separation on a dual-token sparse prediction task: DPO's surrogate reward estimation error is Ω(d/n) (lower-bounded for n = Õ(d²)), while RLHF's reward learning achieves Õ(k log d / n) with an ℓ₀-constrained estimator or Õ(√(k log d / n)) with an ℓ₁-regularized estimator, where k ≪ d is the reward sparsity. Theorem 11 translates this into sub-optimality gaps: RLHF sub-optimality scales as Õ(√(k log d / n) · √d) (ℓ₀) or Õ((k log d / n)^{1/4} · √d) (ℓ₁), while DPO sub-optimality is Õ(√(d/n) · √d) (MLE) or Ω(d²/n) (lower bound). Empirically, on PKU-SafeRLHF with GPT-2-LARGE-774M (1k–9k samples), pure reward learning consistently outperforms DPO's surrogate reward learning across both 'better' and 'safer' preference types, confirming the theoretical separation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three methods—two-stage RLHF (reward modeling + PPO), offline DPO, and online DPO (with PILAF sampler)—across four model mis-specification conditions. Under Condition 2 (realizable reward, mis-specified policy), RLHF strictly outperforms both offline and online DPO (Propositions 3–4), and the PILAF sampler cannot close the gap. Under Condition 3 (mis-specified reward, realizable policy), DPO strictly outperforms RLHF (Proposition 5) because DPO bypasses the lossy reward model. Under the isomorphic double-mis-specification case (Condition 5), RLHF and offline DPO tie (Proposition 6), but online DPO can strictly outperform both (Proposition 7). In the statistical efficiency comparison (Section 4), RLHF's two-stage approach is provably more data-efficient than DPO: for a sparse reward with sparsity k in dimension d, RLHF recovers the reward at rate Õ(k log d / n) while DPO is stuck at Ω(d/n), a gap that widens as k/d → 0. Empirically on PKU-SafeRLHF, these theoretical predictions are confirmed: RLHF outperforms DPO under policy restriction (Fig. 2 left), DPO outperforms RLHF under reward restriction (Fig. 2 middle), and pure reward learning is more sample-efficient than DPO's surrogate reward at 1k–9k samples (Fig. 3).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the comparative analysis of RLHF and DPO initiated by Nika et al. (2024) and Swamy et al. (2026) by removing the realizability assumption that is standard in prior theoretical work, providing a complete 2×2 analysis across reward and policy model misspecification. It builds directly on the PILAF sampler framework from Shi et al. (2025) to characterize online DPO's objective, and on the isomorphic-class result from Swamy et al. (2026) to analyze the double-misspecification regime. The key departure is the introduction of token-level linear parameterization in Section 4, which enables a concrete statistical separation between RLHF and DPO sample complexity that prior linear-bandit analyses (Zhu et al., 2023; Nika et al., 2024) could not capture because they assumed identical feature structures for reward and policy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper provides practitioners with a principled decision framework for choosing between RLHF and DPO based on model capacity and data regime, which is directly relevant to LLM alignment pipelines where reward models are typically much smaller than policy models. The finding that DPO's surrogate reward must learn the value function (coupling reward with the base model) while RLHF's reward model need only approximate r* explains a persistent empirical observation and suggests that two-stage RLHF retains a structural statistical advantage even at equal model scale. Key limitations include small-scale experiments (GPT-2-LARGE, 774M), the restriction to linear parameterization for the statistical separation, and the absence of a theoretically grounded practical alternative to BT-model-based reward learning. Open questions include whether the sparse-recovery advantage extends to non-linear model classes and how to design reward objectives that directly optimize downstream policy quality rather than pairwise likelihood.
