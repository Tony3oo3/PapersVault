## Problem

Modern neural networks with ReLU activations, quantization operators, and sparsity regularizers violate the Lipschitz-smooth gradient assumption that underpins standard optimization theory. At non-smooth singularities, adaptive optimizers like Adam accumulate conflicting directional signals from the Clarke subdifferential, causing gradient chattering that degrades convergence and generalization. Existing remedies—proximal methods require intractable proximal mappings for nested non-smooth compositions, and randomized smoothing introduces prohibitive variance as a gradient surrogate—leaving no computationally efficient mechanism to dynamically adapt step sizes to local geometric instability.

## Input / Output

Beyond the standard stochastic gradient and model parameters, S-Adam consumes k additional forward passes of the loss function evaluated at perturbed parameter points (w_t + δu_i) with δ=0.01 and k∈{2,8} random unit directions sampled from the unit sphere. This geometric probing step adds k forward-pass overhead per iteration but requires no Hessian computation or backpropagation through the perturbation. The output is the updated parameter vector, with the effective step size scaled by a per-iteration damping coefficient in (e^{-λ}, 1] that is computed online from the probe statistics.

## Method

S-Adam introduces the Local Geometric Instability (LGI) metric, defined as the variance of k randomized directional derivatives normalized by their mean squared magnitude, which serves as a computationally efficient estimator of the Clarke subdifferential diameter without requiring Hessian access. This LGI score drives an adaptive geometric brake exp(−λρ_t) that multiplicatively modulates the Adam step size: in high-instability regions near singularities the brake aggressively shrinks the update, while in smooth basins it relaxes toward 1, recovering standard Adam dynamics. The key design insight is repurposing randomized smoothing not as a gradient surrogate but as a diagnostic probe that preserves the original gradient direction while measuring local geometric severity. Convergence to Clarke stationary points at the optimal O(1/√T) rate is established via differential inclusion analysis, with the non-degeneracy of the brake (α_min = e^{−λ} > 0) ensuring updates never stall. The method is shown to be equivalent under first-order approximation to a proximal method with dynamic regularization, and it degenerates to AdamW when k=1 (variance vanishes) or λ=0 (no damping).

## Results

Under extreme stochastic noise (batch size = 2, ResNet-18), S-Adam(k=8) achieves 56.11% top-1 accuracy on CIFAR-100, 86.13% on CIFAR-10, and 75.87% on ImageWoof2-160, outperforming AdamW by +4.99, +5.65, and +24.69 points respectively. In the QAT setting (2-bit CIFAR-100, 4-bit TinyImageNet/ImageWoof2-160, 8-bit ImageNet), S-Adam(k=8) reaches 18.67%, 23.18%, 35.19%, and 11.24% top-1 accuracy, improving over AdamW by +2.73, +4.27, +1.95, and +2.61 points. The low-cost k=2 variant matches or exceeds k=8 on most benchmarks while running faster than AdamW on small-scale QAT tasks (e.g., 14.33 s vs. 15.89 s on CIFAR-100).

## Baselines & Comparisons

S-Adam consistently outperforms both AdamW and Prox-SGD across all five datasets and both experimental regimes (QAT and high-noise small-batch). Against AdamW, the gains range from +1.95 to +4.27 points in QAT and from +4.99 to +24.69 points at batch size 2, with the largest advantage on ImageWoof2-160 where AdamW's momentum buffer fails to reconcile rapidly varying mini-batch gradients across ReLU kinks. Against Prox-SGD, S-Adam leads by 2.17–4.54 points in QAT and by 7.87–25.65 points at BS=2; Prox-SGD also fails to converge on 2-bit CIFAR-100 QAT (marked with * in Table 1) and is substantially slower (18,299 s vs. 2,137 s on CIFAR-100 at BS=2). The advantage narrows at larger batch sizes (BS=64) where the loss landscape is smoother and the LGI brake relaxes, consistent with the method's design of modulating step size by local geometric instability rather than gradient-noise magnitude alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

S-Adam extends Adam by inserting an adaptive geometric brake derived from the Local Geometric Instability (LGI) metric, which repurposes randomized smoothing (Duchi et al., 2011) not as a gradient surrogate but as a diagnostic probe estimating the Clarke subdifferential diameter via variance of directional derivatives. This departs from proximal methods (Parikh & Boyd, 2014; Yang et al., 2020) that require tractable proximal mappings infeasible for nested non-smooth compositions, and from SAM (Foret et al., 2021) whose Taylor-expansion-based perturbation is theoretically invalid at non-differentiable points. The convergence analysis via differential inclusions (Benaim, 1996) provides the first Clarke-stationarity guarantee for an adaptive optimizer with preconditioning in the non-smooth regime, bridging a gap between non-smooth optimization theory and practical deep learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

S-Adam offers a principled, drop-in replacement for Adam that provably handles the non-smooth singularities ubiquitous in modern architectures (ReLU kinks, quantization steps, sparsity penalties) without requiring Hessian computation or explicit proximal mappings. However, the QAT experiments operate at very low bit-widths (2-bit CIFAR-100 reaches only ~18% accuracy), and the dramatic gains at batch size 2, while compelling, are confined to an extreme noise regime; whether the LGI brake provides meaningful benefits in standard large-batch training of large-scale models remains untested. Open questions include the interaction between LGI damping and other stability techniques (gradient clipping, warmup), the cost-benefit tradeoff at scale where k additional forward passes per step may become prohibitive, and whether the geometric instability signal correlates with generalization gap as the impact statement speculates.
