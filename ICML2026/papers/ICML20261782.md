## Problem

Current RLVR methods for LLM post-training (e.g., GRPO) learn only from a scalar outcome reward per attempt, creating a severe credit-assignment bottleneck: when all rollouts in a group receive the same reward, advantages collapse to zero and learning stalls. Many verifiable environments actually expose rich tokenized feedback—runtime errors, failing unit tests, judge evaluations—that explain *why* an attempt failed, but no existing method converts this feedback into dense, token-level credit assignment without requiring an external teacher model.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text question x, samples G rollouts from the current policy, and receives tokenized environment feedback f for each rollout (e.g., a LeetCode-style runtime error, a failed unit test message, or a successful solution from another rollout in the same group). The self-teacher is constructed by re-prompting the *same* model with a template that includes the question, the feedback, and the original response, then re-computing log-probabilities of the original tokens under this augmented context. The output is a set of dense logit-level advantages: |y|·K unique per-token, per-candidate-token advantages per sequence (with K=100 top tokens), replacing the single scalar advantage per rollout used by GRPO. No external model or reward model is required at any point.

## Method

SDPO treats the current policy in two roles: as a student generating the initial attempt y, and as a self-teacher q_θ(·|x,f) that re-evaluates y after seeing the rich feedback f in-context. The training objective minimizes the per-token KL divergence between the student's next-token distribution π_θ(·|x,y_<t) and the stop-grad'd self-teacher distribution q_θ(·|x,f,y_<t), yielding a policy gradient where each candidate token ŷ_t receives advantage A(ŷ_t) = log(π_θ(ŷ_t|x,f,y_<t) / π_θ(ŷ_t|x,y_<t))—positive where the teacher (seeing feedback) is more confident, negative where it is less. This makes SDPO a drop-in replacement for GRPO: the standard RLVR pipeline is reused with only the advantage computation swapped. For memory efficiency, the KL is approximated via top-K distillation (K=100) with a tail-probability correction, and training stability is maintained through a regularized self-teacher (EMA or trust-region interpolation with the initial teacher) and Jensen-Shannon divergence. The self-teacher is not frozen; it improves throughout training, enabling bootstrapping where the student eventually surpasses the initial teacher's accuracy.

## Results

On LiveCodeBench v6 (LCBv6), SDPO achieves 48.8% avg@4 accuracy versus 41.2% for improved GRPO, reaching GRPO's final accuracy in 4× fewer generations and surpassing Claude Sonnet 4 (40.5%) on the public leaderboard. On scientific reasoning (SciKnowEval) and tool use (ToolAlpaca), SDPO outperforms GRPO across nearly all tasks, achieving 68.8% vs. 64.1% aggregate avg@16 at 5 hours of wall-clock training, with up to 7× shorter generation lengths. At test time on very hard LCBv6 questions (pass@64 < 0.03), SDPO achieves 53.2% discovery@2750 versus 41.5% (best-of-k) and 35.6% (multi-turn), requiring 3× fewer generations to reach a 22% discovery probability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against improved GRPO (the primary baseline incorporating asymmetric clipping, unbiased normalization, and off-policy corrections), SDPO wins on nearly all tasks: 48.8% vs. 41.2% on LCBv6, and 68.8% vs. 64.1% aggregate on science/tool-use tasks, while also producing 3–7× shorter generations. SDPO's advantage grows with model scale, significantly outperforming GRPO on Qwen3-8B but only marginally on Qwen3-0.6B (where a hybrid SDPO+GRPO variant with λ=0.9 is preferable). Against multi-turn sampling (the Self-Refine/Reflexion paradigm of re-prompting with in-context feedback), SDPO achieves substantially higher discovery@k on both hard and very hard LCBv6 questions: 53.2% vs. 35.6% on very hard tasks at 2750 generations, and reaches 67% discovery probability with ~2.4× fewer generations on hard tasks. Against best-of-k i.i.d. sampling, SDPO requires 3× fewer attempts to achieve the same 22% discovery probability on very hard questions, and uniquely solves at least one question (Q3) that neither best-of-k nor multi-turn sampling can solve within 2750 attempts.

## Relation to Prior Work

SDPO sits at the intersection of RLVR policy-gradient methods (GRPO, PPO) and self-distillation (Snell et al., 2022; Agarwal et al., 2024), but departs from both in a key way: rather than requiring an external teacher or a scalar reward, it uses the current policy conditioned on rich environment feedback as a self-teacher, producing dense logit-level advantages. The paper formalizes this as RLRF (Reinforcement Learning with Rich Feedback), a strict generalization of RLVR where the feedback signal is an arbitrary token sequence rather than a single scalar. Implementation-wise, SDPO is a minimal drop-in modification to existing GRPO pipelines—only the advantage computation changes—while the theoretical analysis shows the gradient is a negated logit-level policy gradient, connecting it cleanly to the PPO/GRPO lineage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: SDPO is a drop-in replacement for GRPO that improves sample efficiency (4× fewer generations to match GRPO's final accuracy on LCBv6) and, notably, produces 3–7× shorter responses at higher accuracy, challenging the prevailing assumption that longer reasoning chains are necessary for better performance. The test-time self-distillation application (accelerating discovery on hard binary-reward tasks by 3×) opens a new axis for test-time compute that is orthogonal to best-of-k sampling. Key limitations include dependence on the base model's in-context learning ability (SDPO underperforms GRPO on weaker models like Qwen2.5-1.5B), sensitivity to feedback quality, and the fact that off-policy extensions remain unexplored. An open question is whether the conciseness effect generalizes to open-ended generation tasks beyond verifiable domains.
