## Problem

Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) structure predictions through human-understandable concepts but provide no mechanism to verify whether learned concepts actually align with their intended semantics. The visual evidence underlying each concept prediction remains hidden, forcing users to trust an unobservable and often unjustified assumption of concept alignment. This paper addresses the gap by making concept semantics explicitly inspectable and correctable.

## Input / Output

The model takes an RGB image and first segments it into n spatial image parts using a U-Net-based segmentation network (with ResNet backbone), where n is a hyperparameter. Segmentation flexibility is a design choice: ground-truth masks, pretrained segmenters (e.g., SAM), or treating the full image as a single part are all supported. Outputs include binary concept predictions per image part (thresholded to hard concepts to avoid leakage), a final task class label, and the prototype selection distribution for each part. The key novel output is the concept alignment table, which maps each learned prototype to both a concrete image representation and a set of active concept labels, enabling direct human verification of concept semantics.

## Method

PGCMs augment the standard CBM pipeline (input → concepts → task) by introducing m learnable visual prototypes that ground each concept in concrete image evidence. A segmentation network extracts n image parts; a prototype selector computes similarity (dot product or cosine) between each part's CNN embedding and prototype embeddings, yielding a categorical distribution over prototypes via softmax. A concept decoder (MLP) maps each prototype to independent Bernoulli concept probabilities, and an image decoder (Gaussian) maps prototypes to pixel-space representations. Training maximizes an ELBO with four terms—KL regularization (uniform prior), task loss, concept loss, and reconstruction loss—where the reconstruction term encourages visually faithful prototypes and the concept term ensures semantic correctness. Two interpretability optimizations are critical: (1) prototype swapping halfway through training replaces learned embeddings with the nearest real training image parts, guaranteeing realistic prototypes; (2) prototype embeddings are re-encoded from their decoded images (e_j' = f_enc(f_image(e_j))) so that no information beyond the visible prototype image can influence predictions. Because concepts are connected through shared prototypes rather than modeled independently, a single intervention on one prototype can simultaneously correct multiple concept predictions.

## Results

On ColorMNIST+, PGCM achieves 99.2% concept accuracy and 99.7% task accuracy, matching or slightly exceeding all baselines. On CUB, PGCM reaches 90.0% concept accuracy and 63.4% task accuracy, essentially matching CBM (89.9%/63.6%). On CelebA, PGCM obtains 78.5% concept accuracy and 83.0% task accuracy, showing a modest drop relative to CBM (81.3%/84.0%) but still outperforming CRM and CMR on concept accuracy. Overall, PGCMs achieve comparable predictive performance to state-of-the-art CBMs while adding visual prototype grounding for verifiable concept alignment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PGCM matches or slightly outperforms CBM on ColorMNIST+ (99.2% vs 99.2% concept; 99.7% vs 99.6% task) and CUB (90.0% vs 89.9% concept; 63.4% vs 63.6% task), confirming that prototype grounding does not sacrifice accuracy. On CelebA, PGCM trails CBM by 2.8 points in concept accuracy (78.5% vs 81.3%) and 1.0 point in task accuracy (83.0% vs 84.0%), which the authors attribute to the constrained capacity of a finite prototype set on a visually complex face-attribute task. Against CRM and CMR, PGCM achieves higher concept accuracy on all three datasets (e.g., 78.5% vs 76.8% on CelebA), though CRM and CMR maintain slightly higher CelebA task accuracy (84.8%/84.7% vs 83.0%) due to their sidechannel that bypasses the concept bottleneck. The paper also demonstrates that PGCMs enable model-editing interventions (removing or correcting misaligned prototypes) that improve concept accuracy by 4–14 points on noisy-label settings, an intervention type unavailable to any of the three baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) by Koh et al. (2020) by grounding each concept in learned visual prototypes, drawing on the prototype-based learning literature (Chen et al., 2019; Li et al., 2018) and part-prototype networks. The key conceptual departure is that while CBMs provide high-level semantics without visual grounding and prototype networks provide visual grounding without high-level semantics, PGCMs unify both through a variational inference framework with an explicit concept alignment table. The architecture borrows the ELBO-based training from VAEs and the segmentation-based part extraction from object-centric learning, but the combination with supervised concept decoders and the Tarski-inspired semantics formalization is novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PGCMs address a fundamental trust gap in CBMs: the inability to verify whether learned concepts actually mean what their names suggest. The concept alignment table and prototype-level interventions (removing, editing, adding prototypes) provide a concrete mechanism for human oversight that was previously absent. However, the accuracy-interpretability trade-off (more prototypes → better accuracy but higher cognitive load) and the dependence on segmentation quality remain open challenges. The framework's applicability to high-stakes domains (healthcare, scientific analysis) is promising but untested at scale, and whether the verification burden is manageable for large concept sets in practice remains an open question.
