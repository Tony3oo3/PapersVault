## Problem

Existing VLA navigation models implicitly assume that vision-language reasoning and real-time control are temporally aligned, yet VLM inference on edge hardware takes seconds while control loops run at tens of hertz. This temporal mismatch means semantic representations describe past world states but are consumed by the policy as if current, causing systematic degradation under realistic deployment. Prior dual-system architectures decouple slow reasoning from fast control but still treat semantic outputs as temporally fresh, leaving inference latency unmodeled.

## Input / Output

The action policy consumes a current 1920×1080 RGB frame, a natural language instruction, the robot's proprioceptive state (vx, vy, ωz), the most recent VLM last-layer KV-cache features (projected from 256 to 512 dims), and explicit latency metadata (effective delay Δt and accumulated ego-motion offset Δx, Δy, Δθ). The VLM backbone (InternVL3-1B) operates on four historical frames sampled at 3-second intervals (t−9, t−6, t−3, t) that are temporally lagged by Δt relative to the current observation. The policy outputs 30 action chunks (dx, dy, dθ) over a 3-second horizon, integrated into a continuous trajectory from which a 1-second target is selected for execution at 10 Hz.

## Method

TIC-VLA adopts a dual-system design with a shared InternViT-300M vision encoder feeding both a slow InternVL3-1B reasoning module (0.5 Hz) and a fast 6-layer cross-attention Transformer action expert (10 Hz). The key architectural contribution is a delayed semantic-control interface: the action expert's learnable queries cross-attend to current visual tokens, cached VLM KV features, robot state, and explicit latency/ego-motion metadata, enabling the policy to reinterpret stale semantic information in the current robot frame. Training follows a three-stage latency-consistent pipeline: (1) VLM supervised fine-tuning on GPT-5-annotated reasoning traces and waypoints, (2) imitation learning where inference delays Δt are uniformly sampled from [0, 10] s to expose the policy to temporally misaligned semantics, and (3) PPO reinforcement learning in DynaNav (a physics-based Isaac Sim suite with dynamic human agents) where stochastic delays are injected after each VLM update to match edge-hardware latency. The KV-cache interface outperforms sparse waypoint guidance, and ego-motion offset conditioning is critical for compensating robot displacement during the inference gap.

## Results

On the DynaNav benchmark (85 episodes across hospital, office, warehouse, and outdoor scenes), TIC-VLA achieves a 55.29% success rate and 28.24% collision rate, the best among all language-guided methods. It reduces navigation error to 10.55 m and attains 50.29 SPL, substantially outperforming prior VLA baselines (e.g., MobileVLA: 32.94% SR, 45.88% CR). In real-world deployment on a Unitree Go2 quadruped, TIC-VLA reaches 85% success on an RTX 4060 laptop GPU and 75% on a Jetson Orin NX, compared to 50% for DualVLN and 35% for NaVILA on an A6000.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TIC-VLA decisively outperforms all five language-guided VLA baselines on every metric. Against the strongest VLA competitors, MobileVLA (32.94% SR, 45.88% CR) and OmniVLA (31.76% SR, 49.41% CR), TIC-VLA improves success rate by over 22 percentage points and reduces collision rate by roughly 17–21 points. The gap widens further against earlier methods: DualVLN (30.59% SR), NaVILA (28.24% SR), and Uni-NaVid (22.35% SR) all suffer from high collision rates (47–49%), indicating poor handling of dynamic obstacles. The only method that beats TIC-VLA on some metrics is NavDP, a point-goal diffusion policy with privileged goal access, which achieves lower NE (8.61 vs. 10.55) and higher SPL (52.62 vs. 50.29); however, TIC-VLA still edges out NavDP on success rate (55.29% vs. 54.12%) and collision rate (28.24% vs. 30.59%) while operating without any goal or map information. The synchronous TIC-VLA variant (blocking control during VLM inference) degrades to 32.94% SR and 41.18% CR, confirming that the latency-aware asynchronous design is critical for real-time performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

TIC-VLA extends the dual-system VLA paradigm (e.g., DualVLN, StreamVLN) that decouples slow VLM reasoning from fast reactive control, but departs from these prior architectures by treating inference latency as an explicit, first-class input to the action policy rather than an engineering afterthought. The latency-consistent training pipeline—injecting stochastic delays during both imitation learning and PPO fine-tuning—is a novel training strategy not present in prior VLA navigation work (NaVILA, OmniVLA, MobileVLA-R1), which implicitly assume synchronous or negligible-latency semantic supervision. The DynaNav benchmark also extends the VLN evaluation landscape (R2R, VLN-CE, SocialNav-SUB) by introducing physics-based dynamic human agents with language-conditioned navigation in Isaac Sim, filling a gap between abstract VLN benchmarks and real-world social navigation evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck for deploying VLA models on edge hardware (Jetson Orin NX, laptop GPUs) where VLM inference takes seconds while control must run at 10+ Hz, making explicit latency modeling essential rather than optional. The DynaNav benchmark provides a reproducible, physics-accurate testbed for language-guided navigation among dynamic humans that prior simulators lack, and the zero-shot real-world transfer to a Unitree Go2 quadruped demonstrates practical viability. Open questions include whether the latency-aware design generalizes beyond navigation to manipulation or multi-robot settings, whether the 1B VLM backbone is sufficient for more complex semantic reasoning, and whether the approach scales to longer-horizon tasks where accumulated ego-motion offsets during multi-second delays become more challenging.
