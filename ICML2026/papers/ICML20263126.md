## Problem

Gated Linear Units (GLU) and variants like SwiGLU are standard in modern LLMs and consistently outperform non-gated counterparts, yet the theoretical reason for this advantage remains unexplained. The paper specifically asks whether GLU's benefit stems from faster optimization (reduced training error) or from a smaller generalization gap, and provides a principled answer to both questions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263126/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The theoretical analysis operates on i.i.d. standard Gaussian input vectors in R^d with n samples, producing closed-form approximations of NTK extreme eigenvalues and condition numbers as functions of n, d, and hidden width m. Empirically, the framework is validated on two-layer MLPs (Gaussian data, MNIST), ViT (Tiny ImageNet, CIFAR-10), MLP-Mixer (CIFAR-10), and GPT-2 (FineWeb-Edu), outputting NTK condition numbers, training loss curves exhibiting a characteristic loss-crossing between GLU and non-GLU models, and generalization gap scatter plots.

## Method

The key insight is that under LeCun initialization, the GLU NTK matrix admits an approximate Hadamard-product structure: K̃ ≈ K ⊙ (XX^T/d), where K is the non-GLU NTK and XX^T/d is the data Gram matrix. Using random matrix theory (Marchenko-Pastur law, El Karoui's kernel random matrix linearization, and BBP phase transition), the paper derives explicit eigenvalue bounds showing that GLU reduces λ_max from O(mn/d) to O(mn/d²) while increasing λ_min, yielding a condition number improvement from O(n/d) to O(n/d²). This spectral contraction is then connected to gradient descent dynamics via eigendirection-wise error decay, formally explaining a two-phase training behavior where non-GLU models converge faster initially (larger λ_max) but GLU models overtake in late training (larger λ_min), producing the observed loss-crossing phenomenon. Empirically, the paper shows that GLU does not meaningfully reduce the generalization gap (confirmed by Energy Distance permutation tests), concluding that its primary benefit is purely in accelerating optimization.

## Results

The central theoretical result is that the GLU gating mechanism reduces the NTK condition number from O(n/d) (non-GLU) to O(n/d²) (GLU), a factor-of-d improvement, by shrinking λ_max from O(mn/d) to O(mn/d²) while keeping λ_min at O(m) (and actually increasing it). This spectral improvement is empirically verified on ViT and GPT-2, where replacing FFN blocks with GLU variants consistently decreases the measured condition number. The paper further demonstrates a characteristic loss-crossing phenomenon: non-GLU models (e.g., ReLU) converge faster in the early training stage, but GLU models (e.g., ReGLU) overtake them in the late stage due to their larger minimum eigenvalue, a behavior confirmed on both Gaussian synthetic data and MNIST across multiple activation pairs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares GLU variants (ReGLU, GEGLU, SwiGLU) against their non-gated counterparts (ReLU, GELU, SiLU) along three axes. On NTK conditioning, GLU consistently wins: the condition number κ drops from O(n/d) to O(n/d²), verified numerically across input dimensions and on ViT/GPT-2 architectures. On training dynamics, the comparison is nuanced — non-GLU models achieve lower loss in the early stage (larger λ_max drives faster initial convergence), but GLU models overtake in the late stage (larger λ_min accelerates final convergence), producing the observed loss-crossing. On generalization gap, the two are essentially tied: a two-sample permutation test (Energy Distance) finds no statistically significant difference (p ≥ 0.05) in the joint (L_S, L_D − L_S) distribution between GLU and non-GLU models on MLP Mixer/CIFAR-10, ViT/Tiny ImageNet, and GPT-2/FineWeb-Edu. The paper concludes that GLU's primary benefit is optimization acceleration rather than generalization improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of NTK-based optimization theory and random matrix theory, applying tools from El Karoui's kernel random matrix linearization, Marchenko-Pastur spectral analysis, and recent NTK conditioning results (Liu et al., 2025; de Ryck et al., 2024) to a previously unexplained architectural question. While Shazeer (2020) introduced GLU variants and observed their empirical superiority, attributing gains to 'divine benevolence,' and Zhong et al. (2025) highlighted the lack of theoretical understanding, no prior work had connected GLU's multiplicative gating to NTK spectral conditioning. The paper also draws a connection to model gradient angle theory, showing the gating mechanism enlarges gradient angles between samples, providing a complementary geometric interpretation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled theoretical explanation for why SwiGLU and related variants dominate modern LLM architectures, clarifying that the benefit is purely in optimization speed (smaller NTK condition number) rather than improved generalization. This distinction is practically important: it suggests that the choice between GLU and non-GLU should be driven by training budget constraints rather than expected generalization improvements, and that optimizer choice (e.g., SGD vs. AdamW) may matter more for generalization than the gating structure itself. Key limitations include the analysis being restricted to the infinite-width NTK regime and two-layer networks, leaving open whether the spectral conditioning argument and loss-crossing dynamics persist in finite-width, deep, or feature-learning regimes where NTK theory breaks down.
