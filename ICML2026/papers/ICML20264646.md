## Problem

Despite the widespread practical use of model merging to combine domain-specialized LLMs without retraining, the field lacks a quantitative scaling law that predicts how cross-entropy loss evolves as the number of merged experts or the base model size changes. This gap makes it impossible to anticipate convergence, compare merging rules across base sizes, or make budget-aware design decisions about when to stop adding experts or whether to scale the base model instead.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a base model checkpoint (Qwen2.5 0.5B–72B, LLaMA-3, or Gemma2) and a pool of up to 9 domain-specialized expert models (math, science, code), from which task vectors (full-parameter or LoRA deltas) are extracted. Given a target expert count k and a merging rule (Average, TA, TIES, or DARE), it produces a single merged weight vector and, critically, a fitted floor+tail power-law curve that predicts CE loss for any (N, k) pair and recommends an efficient expert count k* (typically 5–6) from just three early-k measurements.

## Method

The paper derives a compact floor+tail scaling law E[L|N,k] = L* + BN^(−β) + A₀N^(−γ)/(k+b), where the size-dependent floor L∞(N) captures the irreducible loss and the inverse-k tail captures diminishing returns from adding experts. Theoretically, Theorem 3.1 shows that under equal-normalized composition of task vectors, a second-order Taylor expansion converts the 1/k variance reduction of the averaged update into a leading-order A(N)/k loss improvement with A(N) = ½c²Tr(HΣ), while the floor is set by the base gradient, mean task direction, and local curvature. A companion corollary proves that merge-to-merge variance contracts as Θ(1/k), explaining the empirical concentration of losses at large k. The law is validated across 10,866 merged models spanning seven base sizes, nine domains, four merging methods, and three backbone families (Qwen2.5, LLaMA-3, Gemma2) with R² > 0.98, and a lightweight three-point fitting procedure (k ∈ {1,2,4}) is shown to forecast the full k-curve and identify the optimal expert budget.

## Results

Across 10,866 merged models (Qwen2.5 0.5B–72B, nine domains, four methods), the proposed floor+tail law E[L|N,k] = L* + BN^(−β) + A₀N^(−γ)/(k+b) achieves R² > 0.98 on all fitted curves. At k=9, macro-averaged CE drops from 0.739 (0.5B) to 0.430 (32B), a 41.9% reduction, driven by both a lower floor and a smaller tail amplitude. The fractional-return analysis shows k=5 and k=6 cross the 85% and 90% improvement thresholds, meaning roughly 60% of the expert pool recovers over 90% of the total gain. Cross-backbone validation on LLaMA-3.2-3B and LLaMA-3-8B yields R² = 0.999 and R² = 0.995 respectively, confirming the law transfers across architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four merging methods—Average (Wortsman et al., 2022), Task Arithmetic with λ=0.8 (Ilharco et al., 2023), TIES with λ∈{0.5,1} (Yadav et al., 2023), and DARE with density 0.2 (Yu et al., 2024)—under an identical protocol across nine domains and model sizes from 0.5B to 72B. All four methods follow the same floor+tail scaling law with R² > 0.98, confirming the law is method-agnostic. At small k (≤3), TA and TIES show modest early advantages over Average (slightly steeper initial drop), but these gaps compress to less than 2% in macro-averaged CE by k≈8 at N=32B. Merge-to-merge variance exhibits the same convergence: all methods contract at approximately 1/k and approach a common small floor, with TIES < TA < Average at small k but no meaningful separation at large k. The practical implication is that method choice matters primarily in the early-k regime; once k ≥ 6, the common floor L∞(N) dominates and the specific merging rule becomes a second-order concern.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the scaling-law paradigm established for pretraining (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) to the under-theorized domain of weight-space model merging. While prior work on merging methods (task arithmetic, TIES, DARE, model soups) characterized individual techniques and (Wang et al., 2025c; Yadav et al., 2024) identified factors influencing merge quality, none provided a unified quantitative law coupling model size N with expert count k. The paper's theoretical contribution derives the leading-order 1/k tail from a second-order Taylor expansion of the loss under equal-normalized composition of task vectors, connecting the floor and tail to the base model's Hessian and the covariance of effective updates—linking the empirical merging literature to the curvature-based analysis used in optimization theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The law converts merging from a trial-and-error practice into a budget-aware planning tool: a three-point fit predicts the full loss-vs-k curve and recommends an efficient expert count, enabling practitioners to decide when to stop adding experts or whether to scale the base model instead. The finding that merging approaches multitask SFT performance at negligible GPU-hours positions it as a practical alternative for resource-constrained settings. Open questions include extending the law to a third axis (expert capacity: LoRA rank, adapter width, training tokens), non-uniform or learned weighting schemes, and whether the power-law form persists at extreme scales or for non-CE objectives such as robustness or calibration metrics.
