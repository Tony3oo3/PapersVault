## Problem

Existing GNN and Graph Transformer methods for psychiatric disorder diagnosis operate under a group homogeneity assumption that inter-group differences dominate over intra-subject variability. In practice, brain functional connectivity exhibits fingerprint-like specificity where inter-subject differences can exceed disease-induced alterations, causing models to latch onto spurious subject-specific correlations and fail to generalize. This paper addresses the gap by explicitly learning subject-invariant representations that suppress individual variability while preserving disease-relevant connectivity patterns.

## Input / Output

The method takes fMRI functional connectivity graphs (N×N adjacency matrices from the Craddock 200 atlas, with N=200 for ABIDE and N=190 for ADHD-200) as input. Each subject's fMRI time series is split into two non-overlapping temporal halves, each independently yielding an FC graph, producing two views per subject for contrastive pair construction. The model outputs a binary probability of psychiatric disorder (patient vs. control) via a sigmoid classifier applied to a graph-level readout of node embeddings (N×d). Notably, the inverse contrastive similarity is computed on the decoder's reconstructed adjacency matrices rather than directly on the latent embeddings, a deliberate choice to avoid over-compressing the representation space.

## Method

SI-IGCL employs a two-stage paradigm: (1) self-supervised pre-training with an inverse contrastive objective that repels same-subject embeddings while attracting cross-subject embeddings (the reverse of standard contrastive learning), combined with a structure-preserving reconstruction loss to prevent representation collapse; and (2) supervised fine-tuning with a linear classifier. The encoder is a Hierarchical Topology Enhanced Transformer (HTET) that constructs K level-specific subgraphs via percentile-based adaptive thresholding, applies level-specific multi-head self-attention to capture local and global dependencies at each topological scale, and fuses across levels with learnable coefficients and residual connections. The inverse contrastive loss uses Frobenius inner product similarity on reconstructed adjacency matrices with a corrective term α that attenuates uninformative high-similarity cross-subject pairs to sustain informative gradients and avoid early optimization plateaus. A GNN-based decoder reconstructs the original adjacency from the learned embeddings, anchoring representations to the underlying connectivity structure while the inverse contrastive term shapes subject invariance.

## Results

On ABIDE, SI-IGCL achieves 75.27% accuracy, 77.27% F1, and 79.55% AUC, surpassing the best baseline (CIA-GCL at 71.95% ACC) by 3.32 percentage points. On ADHD-200, it reaches 74.26% ACC, 73.36% F1, and 78.86% AUC, exceeding the best baseline (Contrasformer at 72.19% ACC) by 2.07 points. Under the more challenging leave-one-site-out cross-validation, SI-IGCL still leads on most metrics (e.g., 71.45% ACC on ABIDE vs. 69.75% for BrainTrans), confirming improved cross-site generalization. The method also achieves the best multi-class results on ADHD-200 (67.43% ACC) and ADNI (63.58% ACC) in supplementary experiments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SI-IGCL outperforms all 14 baselines on both ABIDE and ADHD-200 across every reported metric (ACC, SEN, SPE, F1, AUC). On ABIDE, the closest competitors are CIA-GCL (71.95% ACC), BrainTrans (71.90% ACC, 78.80% AUC), and CAGT (71.28% ACC); SI-IGCL leads by 3.3–6.4 points in accuracy and by up to 5.9 points in AUC over BrainTrans. On ADHD-200, Contrasformer (72.19% ACC, 72.63% AUC) and CAGT (71.65% ACC, 75.46% AUC) are the strongest baselines; SI-IGCL exceeds them by 2.1 and 2.6 points in accuracy respectively, and by 6.2 points in AUC over CAGT. GNN-based baselines (BrainGB, CRGNN, CI-GNN, CIA-GCL) and GT-based baselines (BrainTrans, Com-BrainTF, METAFormer, GBT, ALTER, RGTNet, BrainIB, LCCAF) all fall 2–22 points below SI-IGCL in accuracy, with the largest gaps observed for CRGNN (52.71% on ABIDE) and LCCAF (57.45% on ADHD-200). Under leave-one-site-out cross-validation, the performance gap narrows but SI-IGCL still leads on most metrics, suggesting the subject-invariant pre-training specifically addresses the cross-site distribution shift that degrades other methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the graph contrastive learning and graph transformer literature for brain disorder identification (CIA-GCL, Contrasformer, BrainTrans, CAGT, GBT), but departs from them by explicitly rejecting the group homogeneity assumption that underlies most prior methods. The key conceptual departure is inverting the standard contrastive objective—repelling same-subject embeddings while attracting cross-subject ones—to learn subject-invariant representations, combined with a structure-preserving reconstruction loss to prevent collapse. The HTET module extends hierarchical graph construction (percentile-based thresholding) into a multi-level transformer architecture, and the temporal splitting strategy for augmentation replaces the graph perturbation methods (node dropping, edge perturbation, feature masking) used in prior contrastive brain network work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Inter-subject variability is a fundamental bottleneck in brain network analysis that limits clinical translation; this paper provides a principled self-supervised mechanism to explicitly suppress it, with strong cross-site generalization evidence (LOSO) that is critical for real-world multi-site deployment. The ablation showing catastrophic collapse (51% ACC) when the reconstruction loss is removed highlights a non-obvious fragility in inverse contrastive learning that practitioners should be aware of. Open questions include whether the inverse contrastive paradigm generalizes beyond binary psychiatric diagnosis to other subject-variability-heavy domains (e.g., aging, longitudinal tracking), and whether the approach extends to multi-modal neuroimaging as the authors note for future work.
