## Problem

Bridging-based consensus algorithms in crowdsourced fact-checking systems (e.g., X Community Notes, Meta) were designed to resist manipulation by requiring diverse agreement across ideological groups, but no prior work rigorously analyzed whether coordinated adversaries could fabricate synthetic consensus by strategically positioning accounts in the latent factor space. This paper presents the first formal analysis of the matrix factorization component's vulnerability to such coordinated attacks, revealing that up to 10.7% of lower-quality notes can be pushed above the helpfulness threshold with fewer than 10 coordinated ratings.

## Input / Output

The parameter prediction model takes note text as input (embedded via Voyage-3-large at 1024 dimensions) and outputs scalar predictions for the note factor f_n and intercept i_n; it deliberately ignores the annotated post content and any referenced URLs. The MRS computation takes the current set of ratings on a target note and produces a minimum number of additional ratings needed to cross the threshold τ=0.40, computed via a greedy algorithm using closed-form Sherman-Morrison rank-one updates. The full attack cost model outputs a dollar estimate decomposed into model training, account maintenance, and vote casting components.

## Method

The attack proceeds in two phases: Phase 1 strategically rates existing notes to position k adversarial accounts at diverse locations in the 1D latent factor space (solving an inverse problem where the optimal rating for each note is the value closest to the predicted rating μ + i_a^target + i_n + f_a^target · f_n), and Phase 2 has all k accounts rate the target note as helpful to drive its intercept above threshold. A shallow MLP trained on Voyage embeddings predicts note parameters from text, enabling the adversary to compute optimal voting strategies without direct access to model internals. The Manipulation Resistance Score is derived via a closed-form expression (Eq. 4) obtained by applying the Sherman-Morrison formula to the ridge-regression Gram matrix, yielding a quadratic whose solution gives the optimal injection point in factor space. A key theoretical finding is that rating a note 'Not Helpful' can increase its intercept when existing raters are tightly clustered on one side of the factor spectrum, as the extreme low rating steepens the best-fit line and raises the y-intercept. The deployed mitigation—population sample filtering—recomputes quality scores on a random subset of solicited ratings, increasing attack cost linearly with population size without reducing system accessibility.

## Results

Using X Community Notes production data (126.9M ratings, 1.85M notes), the paper shows that up to 10.7% of notes with intercepts below the 50th percentile (i_n < 0.24) can be pushed above the Helpful threshold (τ = 0.40) using fewer than 10 coordinated ratings, when rater factors are drawn from the wide range [-1, 1]. For the narrower demonstrated range [-0.4, 0.4], 7.0% of such notes are manipulable with <10 ratings, and 27.2% of all notes below the Helpful threshold are. The full attack cost is estimated at approximately $30.50 per note (dominated by $3/month account maintenance), and the analysis reveals a counterintuitive property where rating a note 'Not Helpful' can increase its intercept when existing raters are tightly clustered on one side of the factor spectrum.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods or baselines in the traditional sense; it is an adversarial robustness analysis of a single deployed system (X Community Notes' matrix factorization). The primary internal comparison is between two rater factor ranges: the narrow range [-0.4, 0.4] (validated as achievable by 100 adversarial accounts in simulation) versus the wider [-1, 1] range (representing the full observed user factor distribution). The wider range consistently yields higher manipulation rates (e.g., 10.7% vs. 7.0% at the 50th percentile), suggesting that if attackers can position accounts at more extreme factor values, the attack becomes more effective. The paper also contrasts its findings with prior work on non-bridging Community Notes (Mujumdar & Kumar, 2021), noting that their majority-threshold attacks do not transfer to the matrix factorization-based bridging algorithm analyzed here. No head-to-head comparison with alternative consensus mechanisms (e.g., SuperNotes) is performed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial manipulation literature from classic recommender-system shilling attacks (Lam & Riedl, 2004; Mobasher et al., 2005) and prior fact-checking manipulation studies (Mujumdar & Kumar, 2021) to the fundamentally different setting of bridging-based consensus mechanisms. While shilling attacks inject fake profiles to push a single item, and prior fact-checking attacks exploited simple majority thresholds, this work targets the matrix factorization core of Community Notes (Wojcik et al., 2022), where the adversary must fabricate *diverse* agreement across latent factor positions rather than mere volume. The Sherman-Morrison-based closed-form derivation for optimal rating injection is a new analytical tool not present in the recommender-systems manipulation literature, which typically relies on heuristic attack families (random, bandwagon, segment).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that up to 10.7% of notes can be pushed above the consensus threshold with fewer than 10 coordinated ratings at a total cost of ~$30.50 has immediate practical implications for every platform deploying bridging-based fact-checking (X, Meta, TikTok, Google). The counterintuitive theoretical result—that a 'Not Helpful' rating can increase a note's helpfulness intercept—reveals a structural vulnerability in the ridge-regression formulation that no amount of volume-based filtering can address. Open questions include whether increasing factor dimensionality (d>1) meaningfully raises attack cost without introducing spurious bridging across non-ideological axes, and how the deployed population-sample filtering mitigation performs against adaptive adversaries who learn the sampling distribution. The paper's static analysis also leaves open the question of how dynamic feedback loops (Helpful status attracting more organic ratings) interact with sustained multi-note campaigns.
