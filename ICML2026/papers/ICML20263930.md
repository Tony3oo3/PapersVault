## Problem

Conferences and journals have widely adopted 'Polishing-only' policies that permit LLM use for grammar and paraphrasing but prohibit AI-generated content in peer reviews, yet no prior work has rigorously evaluated whether current AI text detectors can actually distinguish policy-compliant AI-polished reviews from policy-violating AI-generated ones. This paper demonstrates that all evaluated detectors misclassify a non-trivial fraction (3%+) of compliant AI-polished reviews as fully AI-generated, rendering such policies practically unenforceable at scale.

## Input / Output

The evaluation takes peer review text as primary input, with the paper manuscript (abstract, introduction, conclusion) optionally provided as conditioning context for zero-shot detectors. Outputs vary by detector type: open-source zero-shot methods (LogLikelihood, Fast-DetectGPT, Binoculars) produce scalar scores thresholded to binary AI/human predictions, while commercial classifiers (Pangram, GPTZero) emit three-class labels (AI, Mixed, Human). The critical design choice is that the 'positive' class is defined relative to policy compliance rather than absolute AI involvement: AI-BP, AI-EP, and AI-HI reviews are violations, while H-AI (human-written, AI-polished) reviews are compliant negatives, making the detection boundary fundamentally different from standard AI text detection.

## Method

The paper constructs a 50,156-review dataset spanning five levels of human-AI collaboration (fully AI-generated with basic/elaborate prompts, AI-generated with human input, human-written AI-polished, and fully human), generated using six LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-5, Llama-3.3-70B, Gemini-2.5-pro, Gemma-3-27b-it, Qwen-3-30B-thinking) across easy and hard subsets with diverse prompts. Five detectors are evaluated: three zero-shot statistical methods (LogLikelihood, Fast-DetectGPT, Binoculars) calibrated to 0% FPR on human reviews, and two commercial classifiers (Pangram 3.0, GPTZero). The paper then tests three peer-review-specific strategies to improve fine-grained detection: (a) conditioning zero-shot detectors on the paper manuscript, (b) computing similarity between candidate reviews and 45 AI-generated reference reviews using cosine similarity, soft n-gram matching, and soft keypoint matching with multiple embedding models, and (c) training supervised classifiers (XGBoost on 38 stylometric features, and fine-tuned RoBERTa) on the domain-specific review data. All strategies fail to reliably separate H-AI from AI-generated reviews at the individual review level, with similarity distributions overlapping substantially and supervised classifiers exhibiting inconsistent generalization to held-out LLMs.

## Results

On the hard subset (multiple modern LLMs, diverse prompts), Pangram 3.0 achieves 92.6–99.3% TPR on fully AI-generated reviews (AI-BP/EP/HI) with 0% FPR on human-written reviews, but still misclassifies 3.1% of policy-compliant H-AI (human-written, AI-polished) reviews as 'AI'. GPTZero achieves 89.4–96.0% TPR on AI* with 1% FPR on human reviews and 3.4% FPR on H-AI. The best open-source zero-shot detector, Fast-DetectGPT with paper context, drops to 43.7–59.8% TPR on AI* in the hard subset while incurring 4.2% FPR on H-AI. Under adversarial humanization, Pangram and GPTZero still flag 92.8% and 86.7% of humanized AI-BP reviews as AI/Mixed, but H-AI FPR paradoxically increases (3.6% for Pangram, 65.6% for GPTZero).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five detectors: two commercial (Pangram 3.0, GPTZero) and three open-source zero-shot (LogLikelihood, Fast-DetectGPT, Binoculars), all tested on a 50,156-review dataset spanning five levels of human-AI collaboration. Commercial detectors substantially outperform open-source ones on the hard subset: Pangram achieves 92.6–99.3% TPR on AI* versus 30.5–59.8% for the best open-source methods, while maintaining comparable or lower FPR on human reviews. However, the critical finding is that no detector—commercial or open-source—can reliably separate policy-compliant H-AI reviews from policy-violating AI* reviews: Pangram's 3.1% and GPTZero's 3.4% FPR on H-AI translate to hundreds of wrongful accusations at NeurIPS scale (75,000+ reviews). Paper-manuscript context provides statistically significant but insufficient gains for open-source detectors (e.g., Fast-DetectGPT TPR on AI-BP rises from ~50% to 59.8% on the hard subset). Supervised classifiers (RoBERTa, stylometric XGBoost) outperform all off-the-shelf detectors in-distribution (100% TPR, 0–1.4% FPR) but fail to generalize to held-out LLMs, with FPR on H-AI reaching 11.5% for Qwen-3-30B. Similarity-based detection using AI-generated reference reviews yields overlapping distributions across AI-assistance levels, with >4% of H-AI reviews misclassified as AI-generated, confirming infeasibility of review-level detection via this approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of AI text detection and peer-review integrity, building on the detection methods of Solaiman et al. (LogLikelihood), Mitchell et al. (DetectGPT/Fast-DetectGPT), Hans et al. (Binoculars), and the commercial systems Pangram and GPTZero. Prior work on AI-in-peer-review detection (Liang et al. 2024, Yu et al. 2025, Rao et al. 2025) has focused on estimating aggregate AI prevalence or binary AI-vs-human classification, while Saha & Feizi (2025) and Zhang et al. (2024) documented the difficulty of detecting mixed-authorship text in general domains. This paper departs from that lineage by constructing a systematic five-level spectrum of human-AI collaboration specifically for peer reviews and by investigating whether peer-review-specific structural advantages (manuscript context, domain constraints) can close the detection gap that prior work identified for general text.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that no current detector can reliably distinguish policy-compliant AI-polished reviews from policy-violating AI-generated ones—directly undermines the enforceability of the 'polishing-only' policies adopted by ICLR, ACL, EMNLP, and many journals, with immediate implications for how conferences handle suspected misconduct at scale (e.g., 75,000 reviews at NeurIPS). The paper also cautions that widely publicized estimates of AI-generated review prevalence (e.g., Pangram's 21% claim for ICLR 2026) likely conflate mixed-authorship reviews with fully AI-generated ones, overstating violations. Open questions include whether watermarking approaches (Rao et al. 2025, ICML 2026) can complement post-hoc detection, whether future detector architectures will resolve the polishing-vs-generation boundary, and how to design AI policies that are both permissive of useful LLM assistance and actually enforceable.
