## Problem

Grokking—the delayed onset of generalization long after overfitting—has been observed empirically in neural networks and attributed to various mechanisms, but no prior work established an end-to-end provable grokking result with quantitative bounds on the generalization delay. This paper closes that gap by proving grokking occurs in the simplest possible setting: over-parameterized linear ridge regression trained with gradient descent and weight decay, showing that neither non-linearity nor depth is required for the phenomenon.

## Input / Output

The method takes n training pairs (x_i, y_i) generated from a realizable linear teacher N*(x) = ⟨θ*, φ(x)⟩, a fixed feature map φ: R^d → R^m with m >> n (over-parameterized), and hyperparameters (learning rate η, weight decay λ, initialization scale ν²). The primary outputs are not a trained model but rather quantitative bounds: an upper bound on t₁ (steps to overfit, governed by λ_min^+(Φ^TΦ)) and a lower bound on t₂ (steps to generalize, governed by ηλ), yielding a lower bound on the grokking time (t₂ − t₁) that can be made arbitrarily large by tuning λ → 0. The bounds also reveal explicit scaling: t₂ ∝ 1/λ and t₁, t₂ ∝ ln(ν²).

## Method

The core mechanism is an orthogonal decomposition of the weight vector θ into a component θ_∥ in the row space of the empirical feature matrix Φ (spanned by the n data points) and a component θ_⊥ in the complementary (m − n)-dimensional null space. In the over-parameterized regime, θ_∥ converges rapidly at rate (1 − ηλ_min^+(Φ^TΦ)/n − ηλ)^t driven by the data, while θ_⊥ decays only at the slow rate (1 − ηλ)^t driven solely by weight decay. This rate mismatch means the model overfits the training data quickly (small t₁) but retains a large-norm orthogonal component that harms generalization for a long time (large t₂). The proof proceeds via three theorems: fast training-loss convergence (Theorem 4.4), a persistent lower bound on generalization error via concentration of the orthogonal component's norm (Theorem 4.5), and eventual convergence to the ridge minimizer with a Rademacher-complexity-based uniform convergence guarantee (Theorem 4.6). The hyperparameter analysis shows that decreasing λ amplifies grokking (t₂ → ∞ as λ → 0), increasing ν² amplifies it logarithmically, and the sample size n primarily affects t₁ through the empirical spectrum of Φ^TΦ.

## Results

The paper's headline result is Theorem 4.2, which proves end-to-end grokking in ridge regression: training loss drops below ε by step t₁ ≤ n·ln(6b²‖θ⁽⁰⁾‖²/ε)/(2ηλ⁺_min(ΦᵀΦ)), while generalization loss remains above c until step t₂ ≥ ln((m−n)ν²/2·(√(c/λ_min(Σ))+‖θ*‖)⁻²)/(4ηλ), and eventually converges below ε. The grokking time (t₂−t₁) scales as 1/λ for small weight decay, meaning it can be made arbitrarily large by decreasing λ. Empirically, with n=100, m=1000, ν²=1, η=1, and λ=10⁻⁴, the theoretical bounds in Equation (8) closely match observed t₁ and t₂ values across all four hyperparameter sweeps (λ, n, m, ν²), confirming the predicted scaling rates t₂∝1/λ and t₁,t₂∝ln(ν²).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper, so comparisons are against prior theoretical results on grokking rather than empirical baselines. The paper establishes the first end-to-end provable grokking guarantee, where prior works each miss a critical component: Lyu et al. (2024) only guarantee convergence to a KKT point (not global optimality) and do not provably imply grokking; Mohamadi et al. (2024) do not prove GD converges to a small-norm generalizing solution; Xu et al. (2024) show eventual generalization but cannot rule out that it begins as early as the second GD iteration; Boursier et al. (2025) show a two-phase trajectory but do not prove the unregularized solution fails to generalize. Compared to the closest prior work on linear grokking (Levi et al., 2024), this paper provides rigorous (non-heuristic) guarantees, handles arbitrary over-parameterization m≫n (vs. d≈n), uses a more general data distribution (not just Gaussian), and explicitly incorporates weight decay. The empirical verification on non-linear two-layer ReLU networks (Figure 4) shows that the hyperparameter dependencies predicted by the linear theory (t₂∝1/λ, t₁,t₂∝ln(ν²)) qualitatively transfer to the non-linear setting, suggesting the bounds capture a more general mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of grokking theory that attributes the phenomenon to lazy-to-rich regime transitions in non-linear networks (Lyu et al., 2024; Mohamadi et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2024), none of which provide end-to-end provable grokking guarantees. It also extends the closest prior provable result (Xu et al., 2024, on XOR classification) by establishing that poor generalization persists well beyond the overfitting point, and by moving to the simplest possible setting—linear ridge regression—where prior theoretical work (Levi et al., 2024) lacked formal guarantees. The paper repurposes classical tools (orthogonal decomposition of the weight vector into data-spanning and null-space components, Rademacher complexity for uniform convergence) to isolate the mechanism of grokking as a rate mismatch between training and generalization losses driven by weight decay.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By proving grokking in the simplest possible model class, the paper demonstrates that neither depth nor non-linearity is necessary for the phenomenon, reframing grokking as a consequence of specific optimization conditions (over-parameterization + small weight decay) rather than an inherent failure mode of deep learning. The quantitative bounds on grokking time (scaling as 1/λ in weight decay) provide a principled, testable prediction that is qualitatively confirmed on non-linear networks, offering a potential template for understanding grokking in more complex architectures. Key open questions include extending the results to non-realizable settings (where the authors note that arbitrarily small weight decay may impede generalization entirely, requiring new tools) and proving grokking as a consequence of lazy-to-rich transitions in neural networks.
