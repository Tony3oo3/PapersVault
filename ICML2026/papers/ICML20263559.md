## Problem

Reward models trained on real-world preference data inevitably overfit annotation noise, yet existing denoising methods rely on homogeneous noise assumptions that fail to capture the instance-dependent noise inherent in linguistic preferences. This paper addresses the gap by formulating noise-aware reward modeling as a selective distribution alignment problem with theoretical guarantees, rather than the loss-correction or heuristic sample-selection paradigms that dominate prior work.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263559/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes prompt-response pairs with noisy scalar preference labels (binarized via median threshold) and frozen semantic embeddings from a pretrained LLM backbone (e.g., FsfairX-LLaMA3-RM-v0.1, Qwen2.5, LLaMA2). It produces a scalar reward score per input via a lightweight 3-layer MLP, alongside a transport coupling plan that acts as a dynamic per-sample weight. The mass quota κ (set to 1 − ρ̂ via Cleanlab noise estimation) controls what fraction of the dataset participates in the alignment, effectively discarding high-cost outliers.

## Method

SelectiveRM reframes point-wise reward regression as a distribution matching problem between the model-induced joint distribution D_θ over (x, r_θ(x)) and the empirical noisy distribution D, using a composite transport cost that combines Euclidean semantic distance with BCE preference discrepancy. The Joint Consistency Discrepancy enforces that valid matches require closeness in both embedding space and preference value, but its strict mass conservation forces the model to fit noisy outliers. To overcome this, the Mass Relaxation mechanism replaces full transport with Partial Optimal Transport, allowing the solver to autonomously discard high-cost pairings where preference contradicts local semantic consensus. The model is updated via gradient descent on the reweighted loss Σ T*_{ij}·ℓ(r_i, r̂_j), where T* is the optimal partial coupling. Theoretically, the paper proves this selective alignment minimizes a tighter upper bound on the unobserved clean risk than standard empirical risk minimization, with the irreducible noise barrier reduced from Ω_noise(D) to Ω_noise(S) over the selected subset.

## Results

SelectiveRM achieves the best alignment accuracy across all three noisy preference benchmarks. On HelpSteer, it reduces MSE from 0.083 (Naive) to 0.063 and improves R² from 0.087 to 0.308; on PKU-SafeRLHF, MSE drops from 0.089 to 0.057 and R² rises from 0.642 to 0.772. On UltraFeedback, SelectiveRM attains MSE 0.107, MAE 0.145, and R² 0.461, outperforming the strongest baseline ILDE (MSE 0.108, R² 0.456). Downstream RLHF safety evaluation confirms gains: SelectiveRM improves Safety Score by 23.72% on FFT (Qwen2.5-7B) over the Naive baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SelectiveRM outperforms all 15 baselines across every dataset and metric. The closest competitor is ILDE (instance-dependent label distribution estimation), which trails SelectiveRM by 0.003 MSE on HelpSteer (0.066 vs. 0.063), 0.001 MSE on UltraFeedback (0.108 vs. 0.107), and 0.005 MSE on PKU-SafeRLHF (0.062 vs. 0.057); the gap widens substantially on MAE, where SelectiveRM achieves 0.087 vs. ILDE's 0.077 on HelpSteer and 0.074 vs. 0.190 on PKU-SafeRLHF. Among statistically consistent methods, CSGN is the strongest, yet SelectiveRM still leads by 0.007 MSE on HelpSteer and 0.008 MSE on PKU-SafeRLHF. Heuristic methods like Co-Teaching and CDR remain close to the Naive baseline, confirming that simple loss-threshold filtering is insufficient under instance-dependent noise. The Naive baseline is the worst performer on HelpSteer (R²=0.087) and UltraFeedback (R²=0.395), validating the paper's central claim that standard point-wise training forces memorization of noisy preference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate literatures: joint-distribution optimal transport (Courty et al., 2017) and unbalanced/partial optimal transport (Fatras et al., 2021), repurposing them as a noise-robust training objective for reward models. It departs from the dominant LNL paradigms—statistical loss correction (Patrini et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2024) and heuristic sample selection (Han et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2024)—by reframing noisy reward modeling as a selective distribution-alignment problem where the transport plan itself acts as a differentiable, instance-dependent filter. The key conceptual departure is using the mass-relaxation constraint of partial OT to let the model autonomously discard semantically inconsistent samples, rather than relying on a pre-estimated noise transition matrix or a fixed loss-threshold heuristic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Reward models are the sole proxy for human values in RLHF, and the paper documents that real preference datasets carry 3–48% annotation noise, making noise-robust reward modeling a practical prerequisite for reliable alignment. The tighter theoretical bound on clean risk and the model-agnostic, plug-and-play nature of the objective make this directly applicable to existing RLHF pipelines. However, the method's reliance on a frozen semantic embedding to define the transport cost introduces a scalability bottleneck (O(N²) cost matrix per iteration), and the core assumption—that clean samples exhibit higher semantic-preference consistency than noisy ones—may fail under adversarial or systematically biased noise. Open questions include whether the approach extends to pairwise reward modeling, whether the OT solver can be approximated for million-scale datasets, and whether the semantic-consistency premise holds when the embedding model itself is misaligned with the preference signal.
