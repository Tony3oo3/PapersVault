## Problem

Existing AI safety frameworks assume fixed objectives, bounded tasks, and comparatively static models, yet open-ended (OE) AI systems—those that continuously generate novel artifacts and self-adapt without explicit goals—introduce qualitatively different safety challenges including structural unpredictability, emergent misalignment, and loss of control over evolving trajectories. These risks are unlikely to be captured by current safety paradigms and must be identified preemptively before OE AI is deployed at scale.

## Input / Output

Rather than processing data through a trained model, the paper takes as analytical input the formal observer-based definition of OE AI and documented failure modes from deployed agentic systems (e.g., the OpenClaw email-deletion incident, MoltBook behavioral drift, ClawHub security vulnerabilities). It produces a seven-category risk taxonomy (R1–R7), formal criteria for temporal safety and alignment certification, four mitigation-oriented research directions, and stakeholder-specific governance recommendations structured as a position argument rather than an empirical evaluation.

## Method

The paper adopts Hughes et al.'s observer-based definition of open-endedness, formalizing it through predictive states M_t and loss functions to define novelty (progressively less predictable artifacts) and learnability (improved prediction with longer history). Building on this, it introduces a temporal safety certification criterion showing that an artifact judged safe at time t may not remain representative of risks at later times t*, and an analogous alignment certification capturing value drift over evolving trajectories. The core analytical contribution is a seven-risk taxonomy (unpredictability, creativity-vs-control tension, misalignment, traceability, resource wastage, social risks, and irreducible speed-novelty-safety trade-offs) that is structurally distinct from specification-gaming or reward-hacking failures in bounded systems. For mitigation, the paper proposes four research directions: hierarchical and co-evolving oversight, safety-aware constrained exploration with artifact complexity budgets, adaptive alignment mechanisms that evolve alongside the system, and dynamic safety evaluation including OE-specific red teaming of the entire open-ended process.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experimental results. Its primary contributions are conceptual: a formal definition of open-endedness (novelty and learnability conditions, Eqs. 3–4), a taxonomy of seven OE-specific safety risks (R1–R7: unpredictability, creativity-vs-control tension, misalignment, traceability, resource wastage, social/human risks, and irreducible speed-novelty-safety trade-offs), and a set of mitigation-oriented research directions spanning oversight, constraints, adaptive alignment, and safety evaluation. The paper also introduces a formal notion of 'temporal safety certification failure' (Eq. 6) and 'alignment certification failure' (Eq. 7) to capture how safety guarantees can degrade as OE systems evolve.

| Dimension | General Agent/AI Safety | Open-Ended (OE) AI Safety |
|---|---|---|

| Oversight | Static guardrails, constraint checking | Adaptive, co-evolving oversight mechanisms |

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method or run empirical comparisons against baselines. Instead, it conceptually contrasts OE AI safety with general AI safety across five structural dimensions (objective structure, temporal scope, evaluation paradigm, failure modes, and oversight requirements), arguing that existing safety frameworks are insufficient for the unbounded, self-evolving dynamics of OE systems. The paper cites recent real-world incidents (e.g., the OpenClaw email-deletion failure, MoltBook behavioral drift, and 512 vulnerabilities in the ClawHub skill registry) as empirical motivation rather than controlled experiments. Its closest 'comparison' is the argument that OE-specific risks (R1–R7) are qualitatively distinct from those addressed by standard alignment, interpretability, or red-teaming approaches, and that no single existing framework covers the full risk surface.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper builds on the formal open-endedness framework of Hughes et al. (2024) and the evolutionary/quality-diversity tradition (Stanley & Lehman, Soros et al.) to define OE AI, then departs from the general AI safety literature (Hendrycks et al., Krakovna et al., Shah et al.) by arguing that the absence of fixed objectives, continual novelty generation, and evolving system behavior create a qualitatively distinct safety problem. It extends the brief safety concerns raised by Ecoffet et al. (2020) and Hughes et al. (2024) into a systematic taxonomy of seven OE-specific risk categories, grounding the analysis in the formal novelty and learnability conditions. The paper also draws on recent agentic systems (OpenClaw, Voyager, AI Scientist, Darwin Gödel Machine) as empirical motivation, positioning OE AI safety as a gap between the rapidly maturing OE capability literature and the largely task-bounded AI safety literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central contribution is framing OE AI safety as a distinct research agenda rather than a subcase of general AI safety, which is timely given the rapid proliferation of self-evolving and open-ended agentic systems. The 'impossible triangle' (speed, novelty, safety) provides a useful design-time lens for practitioners, and the taxonomy (unpredictability, misalignment drift, traceability loss, etc.) gives the community a shared vocabulary for discussing risks that are hard to evaluate with standard benchmarks. Key open questions remain: how to implement adaptive, co-evolving oversight at scale, how to construct meaningful safety benchmarks for non-stationary systems, and whether the proposed constraints (complexity budgets, constitutional rules, ε-neighborhood exploration) can be enforced without collapsing the very novelty that makes OE AI valuable. As a position paper with no empirical results, its impact depends on whether the community adopts the taxonomy and invests in the proposed research directions.
