## Problem

In Mendelian Randomization, genetic instruments are systematically confounded with outcomes through population stratification and assortative mating, violating the exchangeability assumption required for valid IV estimation. Existing approaches either assume most instruments are valid or impose restrictive parametric assumptions on the form of invalidity, and prior representation-learning methods for IV lack identifiability guarantees. This paper asks whether multi-environment data can be exploited via representation learning to recover a valid instrumental component from a confounded instrument, with formal guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method consumes i.i.d. samples of high-dimensional genetic variants Z (e.g., 652 SNPs in the GLP1R region), exposure D ∈ ℝ^d, and outcome Y ∈ ℝ from K distinct environments (populations), with the latent dimensions p (valid) and q (invalid) specified a priori. It outputs two learned representations: an invariant component Ŵ ∈ ℝ^p serving as a valid instrument and a variant component V̂ ∈ ℝ^q capturing environmental confounding, which are then plugged into standard 2SLS or partialling-out 2SLS estimators to produce a causal effect estimate. The autoencoder must satisfy an exact reconstruction identity (m_de ∘ m_en(Z) = Z), constraining the architecture to be invertible.

## Method

The framework trains an autoencoder (encoder m_en, decoder m_de) by minimizing a weighted sum of three losses: a reconstruction loss preserving all information in Z, an MMD-based invariance loss enforcing that the first p encoder dimensions have identical distributions across environments, and an HSIC-based independence loss enforcing statistical independence between the Ŵ and V̂ blocks. The key theoretical result is that under polynomial or diffeomorphic mixing with modular distributional shifts in V, the invariance constraint forces the encoder's first p dimensions to be a function of W alone, identifying it up to an affine (polynomial case) or diffeomorphic (general case) transformation. Since any bijective transformation of a valid instrument remains valid (Proposition 2.4), the learned Ŵ suffices for consistent IV estimation without needing to recover W exactly. The independence constraint is shown to be necessary for safely using V̂ as an adjustment covariate in PO-2SLS, preventing collider bias and information leakage that would otherwise inflate variance or introduce bias.

## Results

On the semi-synthetic All of Us experiment (652 GLP1R variants, 8000 individuals per population), both proposed estimators—2SLS(Ŵ) and PO(V̂)-2SLS(Ŵ)—achieve estimation bias centered near zero for both causal parameters θ₀ and θ₁, effectively removing population confounding. In contrast, the naive 2SLS(Z) and MR-Egger(Z) estimators exhibit bias up to 0.3, while population-adjusted variants PO(K)-2SLS(Z) and PO(K)-Egger(Z) reduce but do not eliminate the bias. The PO(V̂)-2SLS(Ŵ) estimator additionally shows visibly tighter variance than 2SLS(Ŵ), confirming that V̂ serves as a useful adjustment covariate for efficiency gains. Across all mixing mechanisms tested (polynomials of degree 1–3 and an invertible MLP), the proposed methods remain unbiased while all baselines show significant bias.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four baselines: standard 2SLS(Z), MR-Egger (Egger(Z)), and their population-label-adjusted counterparts PO(K)-2SLS(Z) and PO(K)-Egger(Z). The proposed 2SLS(Ŵ) and PO(V̂)-2SLS(Ŵ) estimators achieve near-zero bias across all tested settings, while 2SLS(Z) and Egger(Z) show bias up to 0.3 on the All of Us semi-synthetic data. The population-adjusted baselines PO(K) reduce the bias relative to their unadjusted versions but fail to eliminate it, indicating that the confounding structure is more complex than a simple discrete mean shift between populations. Under non-linear mixing (polynomial degree 3, invertible MLP), all four baselines continue to exhibit significant bias while the proposed methods remain unbiased. The PO(V̂)-2SLS(Ŵ) variant further outperforms 2SLS(Ŵ) in variance, demonstrating that the learned V̂ component provides genuine efficiency gains when the independence constraint is satisfied.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts multi-environment causal representation learning theory—specifically the identification results for polynomial and diffeomorphic mixing from Ahuja et al. (2024) and the unifying invariance framework of Yao et al. (2025)—to the novel domain of Mendelian Randomization, where the goal is to recover a valid instrumental component from a confounded genetic instrument. It departs from prior invalid-instrument methods (MR-Egger, GENIUS) that impose parametric or sparsity assumptions on the form of invalidity, instead using distributional invariance across environments to disentangle valid from confounded components without such assumptions. Unlike recent representation-learning-for-IV work (Cheng et al., 2024a,b), this paper provides explicit identifiability guarantees and characterizes the effect of latent dimension misspecification on downstream causal estimates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to offer theoretical guarantees for recovering valid instruments from environmentally confounded genetic data, directly addressing a major practical limitation of multi-ancestry MR studies where population stratification systematically violates exchangeability. The practical distinction that invariance alone suffices for valid IV estimation while the independence constraint is additionally required for safe variance reduction via partialling-out provides clear guidance for practitioners. Key open questions include the untestability of the modular variability assumption (Assumption 3.4), the need for sparsity-aware encoders in high-dimensional genomic settings, and whether the approach extends to settings where the causal effect itself varies across environments in complex ways.
