## Problem

Prior work on language model memorization conflates sample-specific retention with population-level generalization, making it impossible to quantify how much information a model actually stores about individual training points. Extraction-based definitions treat any reproducible output as memorization, even when the model could generate it from learned patterns alone. This paper asks: can we formally separate and independently measure the bits a model retains about a specific datapoint versus the bits it retains about the data-generating process?

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a trained target model θ̂, a larger reference model θ of the same architecture (774M GPT-2 fine-tuned on FineWeb), and individual training samples x. Per-sample unintended memorization is computed as the difference in compression rate between the reference and the target (in bits), while total model capacity is obtained by training on uniform random bitstrings of known Shannon entropy until the memorization curve plateaus. The final output is a sigmoidal scaling law predicting membership inference F1 as a function of the ratio Capacity(θ̂)/|D|, validated on models from 500K to 1.5B parameters.

## Method

The paper defines memorization via Kolmogorov complexity: mem^K(θ̂, x) = H^K(x) − H^K(x|θ̂), and isolates unintended memorization by conditioning on a reference model θ, yielding mem_U^K = H^K(x|θ) − H^K(x|(θ,θ̂)). In practice, each Kolmogorov term is approximated by negative log-likelihood under the respective model, making the quantity computable from a single trained checkpoint. To measure capacity, they train GPT-2-architecture transformers (100K–20M params, 1–8 layers) on uniform random bitstrings where generalization is provably zero (Proposition 6), finding a consistent plateau at ~3.6 bits per parameter in bfloat16. On real FineWeb text, they show that unintended memorization rises with model size and falls with dataset size, and that double descent onset coincides exactly with the dataset size exceeding model capacity in bits. Finally, they fit a sigmoidal scaling law for loss-based membership inference F1 using the capacity-to-dataset-size ratio, achieving predictions within 1.5 F1 points on 125M and 1.5B parameter models.

## Results

The paper measures that GPT-style transformer language models store approximately 3.51 bits per parameter in bfloat16 precision and 3.83 bits per parameter in fp32 precision, establishing a clear capacity plateau when trained on uniform random data. The proposed sigmoidal scaling law for membership inference F1 score (as a function of model capacity and dataset size) predicts observed F1 within 1–2% across models from 500K to 1.5B parameters. Validation on GPT-2 Small (125M) and GPT-2 XL (1.5B) trained at predicted dataset sizes yields F1 scores within 1.5 percentage points of the scaling law's predictions (e.g., predicted 0.95 vs. observed 95.85±0.8 for GPT-2 XL). The paper further shows that double descent in train/test loss begins precisely when the dataset size in bits exceeds the model's memorization capacity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing memorization-measurement methods in a head-to-head evaluation; instead, it positions its capacity estimate relative to prior theoretical and empirical work. The measured 3.51–3.83 bits-per-parameter is notably higher than the ~2 bits/parameter estimated by Allen-Zhu & Li (2024) via quantization arguments, suggesting that gradient-trained transformers exploit more of their parameter space for raw storage than quantization-based bounds predict. The linear scaling of capacity with parameter count corroborates the qualitative findings of Roberts et al. (2020) and Lu et al. (2024) on fact-storage scaling, but provides the first direct measurement of a hard capacity plateau. On the membership-inference side, the paper's scaling law explains why prior membership-inference attacks (Das et al., 2024; Duan et al., 2024; Maini et al., 2024) fail on large-scale models: any model trained with ≥100 tokens-per-parameter is predicted to have F1 ≈ 0.5 (random chance), consistent with those negative results. The paper also contrasts its compression-based definition with extraction-based memorization (Carlini et al., 2023b; Schwarzschild et al., 2024), showing that in the large-data regime, extraction rates on training data converge to test-data extraction rates, meaning extraction conflates generalization with memorization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Kolmogorov complexity and Shannon information theory to formalize memorization as a compression-rate quantity, departing from the dominant extraction-based (Carlini et al., 2019; Nasr et al., 2023; Schwarzschild et al., 2024) and membership-inference-based (Shokri et al., 2017; Yeom et al., 2018) definitions that conflate sample-level memorization with generalization. The key conceptual departure is the explicit decomposition into unintended memorization (information about a specific dataset) and intended memorization/generalization (knowledge of the data-generating process), which prior definitions did not separate. The capacity measurement connects to Roberts et al. (2020) and Allen-Zhu & Li (2024) on fact-storage scaling, while the double-descent analysis provides a mechanistic account grounded in the capacity-to-data-size ratio, extending Nakkiran et al. (2019) with a precise threshold.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~3.6 bits-per-parameter capacity estimate and the sigmoidal membership-inference scaling law give practitioners concrete, predictable quantities for assessing privacy risk across model and dataset scales, with the practical implication that most modern LLMs (trained at ≥100 tokens/parameter) are too data-rich for reliable per-sample membership inference. The demonstration that extraction rates converge to test-set extraction rates in the large-data regime challenges the field's reliance on extraction as a memorization signal, suggesting that a training sample being greedy-decodable is no more evidence of memorization than an unseen sample being decodable. Open questions include whether the capacity estimate and scaling law hold for non-GPT architectures (e.g., mixture-of-experts, state-space models), whether the framework extends to multimodal settings, and how the reference-model dependence affects deployment in settings where no suitable oracle is available.
