## Problem

Existing protein fitness prediction benchmarks like FLIP covered only thermostability, binding, and viral capsid viability, and their random splits failed to capture the extrapolation challenges protein engineers actually face—generalizing across wild-type scaffolds, to unseen mutation positions, and from fewer to more mutations. This left practitioners without a reliable way to assess whether ML tools would work in realistic engineering campaigns where data for one protein must inform predictions about a homologous target or a new structural context.

## Input / Output

Models consume protein sequences (one-hot encoded for ridge regression, or tokenized for pLMs) with optional 3D structure tokens (for SaProt-650M), and produce a scalar fitness score per variant. For single-wild-type datasets, zero-shot scores are computed as the sum of log-likelihood ratios at mutated positions; for multi-wild-type datasets (Hydro, Rhomax) and the two-protein PDZ3 system, full-sequence pseudolikelihoods or per-sequence scores are used instead. Evaluation is via Spearman rank correlation and NDCG on held-out test sets defined by 16 structured splits across 7 datasets.

## Method

FLIP2 introduces seven sequence-fitness datasets (enzymes, a light-sensitive protein, and a protein-protein interaction) with 16 splits organized into five generalization types: number (fewer→more mutations), position (near→far from active site or unseen positions), mutation (different amino acid substitutions), fitness (low→high), and wild-type (different scaffolds). The paper evaluates three method families: (1) zero-shot pLM likelihood scores from Dayhoff, CARP-640M, ESM2-650M, and SaProt-650M; (2) ridge regression on one-hot sequences, optionally augmented with pLM likelihoods; and (3) fine-tuned pLMs (CARP-640M, ESMC-300M, SaProt-650M) trained with full fine-tuning, LoRA (rank 32), or from random initialization. The central empirical finding is that ridge regression with sequence and zero-shot likelihoods, or zero-shot scores alone, matched or outperformed fine-tuned pLMs on 12 of 16 splits, with fine-tuned pLMs winning only 7/16—suggesting current transfer learning paradigms are poorly matched to the out-of-distribution generalization demands of protein engineering.

## Results

Across 16 splits on 7 datasets, fine-tuned protein language models (CARP-640M, ESMC-300M, SaProt-650M) were the best-performing model on only 7/16 splits. Ridge regression with one-hot sequence + pLM likelihoods was best on 4/16 splits, zero-shot pLM likelihood scores were best on 4/16, and a naively initialized supervised pLM was best on 1/16. The hardest splits (wild-type and position generalization) show the largest performance drops: e.g., TrpB by-position peaks at Spearman ρ = 0.294 (Ridge one-hot+likelihoods) and Hydro to-P0A9X9 at ρ = 0.171 (SaProt-650M supervised), compared to ρ > 0.5 on easier number splits. Adding structure tokens (SaProt) or using LoRA does not consistently improve fine-tuning results.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five pLM families (Dayhoff, ESM2-650M, CARP-640M, SaProt-650M, ESMC-300M) in zero-shot, fine-tuned, LoRA, and naive-initialization settings, alongside ridge regression baselines. No single pLM dominates: Dayhoff outperforms others on Hydro and Rhomax but underperforms on TrpB, while ESM2-650M leads on Amylase position splits (ρ = 0.617, 0.598) yet is near-zero on Hydro (ρ = −0.036). Fine-tuning CARP-640M improves over its zero-shot score on only 7/16 splits and actively hurts performance on all Amylase splits and all by-position splits. Ridge regression augmented with zero-shot pLM likelihoods matches or beats the best fine-tuned pLM on 4/16 splits (all Amylase number/mutation splits and TrpB one-to-many and by-position), and on multi-wild-type datasets (Hydro, Rhomax) linear models reach high Spearman with very few training examples, making zero-shot augmentation less impactful. Overall, the benchmark demonstrates that the current pLM transfer-learning paradigm (pretrain → fine-tune) does not reliably outperform simple linear baselines under the distribution shifts that characterize real protein engineering campaigns.

## Relation to Prior Work

FLIP2 is a direct expansion of the FLIP benchmark (Dallago et al., 2021), which first established train-test splits mimicking wet-lab protein engineering campaigns. It also positions itself against ProteinGym (Notin et al., 2023), which prioritized scale (2.7M+ sequences) but was dominated by single- and double-mutant DMS data, and against the broader pLM literature (ESM2, CARP, SaProt, Dayhoff, ESMC) that has increasingly assumed fine-tuning large pretrained models is the path to better fitness prediction. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of split types—wild-type generalization, position generalization, and fitness-level extrapolation—that prior benchmarks did not systematically test, reflecting constraints that protein engineers actually face when moving from one scaffold to a homolog or from saturated active-site screens to distal positions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that fine-tuned pLMs were best on only 7/16 splits while ridge regression with one-hot sequences plus zero-shot likelihoods or a raw zero-shot pLM score matched or beat them on the rest—challenges the field's default assumption that scaling and fine-tuning pLMs is the right paradigm for protein engineering. This is particularly striking on wild-type and position splits, where even the best supervised pLMs struggle, suggesting that current architectures lack the inductive biases needed for cross-scaffold and cross-position generalization. As the field increasingly uses experimental-data-trained oracles to steer generative protein models, the ability to generalize across large sequence-space gaps will be critical, and FLIP2 provides the first systematic benchmark for measuring that capability. Open questions include whether fundamentally different architectures (e.g., those explicitly modeling epistasis or structural context) can close the gap on position and wild-type splits, and whether the negative results generalize to larger, more diverse protein families.
