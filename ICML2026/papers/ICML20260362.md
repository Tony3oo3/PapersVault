## Problem

PPO-Clip, the standard clipping mechanism in LLM RL algorithms (GRPO, DAPO, etc.), implicitly measures policy discrepancy with a Euclidean metric on the importance ratio, which is geometrically inconsistent with the Riemannian manifold induced by KL divergence. This mismatch causes overly conservative updates for low-probability tokens and disproportionately aggressive updates for high-probability tokens, leading to exploration collapse that severely limits reasoning performance on long-horizon tasks. Prior fixes (DAPO's Clip-Higher, DCPO, GSPO, GMPO) remain heuristic adjustments within the PPO-Clip framework without identifying or correcting this root geometric cause.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (math problem, coding task, or search query) and produces a generated text response, trained via RL with sparse binary rewards (1 for correct, 0 for incorrect). The key operational detail is that the clipping boundary is computed per-token at each gradient step from the old policy's probability for that specific token, rather than being a fixed scalar hyperparameter. Training uses group-relative advantage estimation (GRPO-style) with 8 rollouts per query and token-mean loss aggregation over variable-length responses up to 16,384 tokens.

## Method

RIPO replaces PPO-Clip's fixed ratio boundary with a Riemannian Isometric Clip (RIC) derived from the second-order Taylor expansion of KL divergence via the Fisher Information Matrix. The core constraint requires each state-action update to consume the same geometric distance δ on the policy manifold, yielding a per-token dynamic clipping boundary ε_{s,a} = √(δ/π_{θ_old}(a|s)) that automatically permits larger updates for low-probability tokens and constrains high-probability tokens. This geometric isometry simultaneously induces statistical homoscedasticity in importance sampling, producing a principled bias-variance trade-off that stabilizes optimization. The full RIPO objective embeds RIC within a GRPO-style group-relative framework with token-level loss aggregation, requiring only a single hyperparameter δ (default 0.05) in place of the fixed ε.

## Results

RIPO achieves the best average performance across all four models and seven competition-level math benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, HMMT25, BRUMO25, CMIMC25, SMT25), evaluated by Avg@8. On Qwen3-8B-Base, RIPO reaches 38.5 average (vs. GRPO 28.5, +35.1%) and 43.8 on AIME24 (vs. GRPO 31.7, +38.2%); on Qwen3-1.7B-Base the gap widens to +37.2% average and +61.9% on AIME24. RIPO also generalizes beyond math: on coding tasks it improves +13.2% over GRPO (44.9 vs. 39.7 average) and on multi-hop search +15.1% (43.4 vs. 37.7). Pass@k analysis shows RIPO breaks through the base model's capacity ceiling, reaching 60.0% on AIME-25 at k=128 versus 53.3% for GRPO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RIPO outperforms all eight baselines on the majority of benchmarks. Against GRPO (PPO-Clip), RIPO wins on every benchmark across all four models, with the largest gaps on harder problems (e.g., +12.1 on AIME24, +14.2 on BRUMO25 for Qwen3-8B). Against DAPO (Clip-Higher), RIPO leads on most benchmarks but DAPO occasionally matches on CMIMC25; the key difference is that DAPO's fixed higher clip (ε_high=0.28) still only lifts low-probability updates by 0.0008, insufficient to prevent collapse. Against GSPO (sequence-level clipping) and GMPO (geometric-mean ratio), RIPO consistently wins, with GMPO being the closest competitor on Qwen3-8B (35.3 vs. 38.5 average). Against DCPO (dynamic-adaptive clipping), RIPO wins on 6 of 7 benchmarks for Qwen3-8B, with DCPO only edging ahead on HMMT25 (15.4 vs. 16.7 is actually RIPO winning; DCPO leads on BRUMO25 at 42.8 vs. 47.5 — correction: RIPO wins there too). In the dedicated clipping-motivation comparison (Table 3), RIPO beats GPPO (which preserves clipped-token gradients) by +12.1 on AIME24 and Clip-Cov (which clips high-covariance tokens) by +12.1 on AIME24 as well. On the PPO objective (Table 4, GSM8K), RIPO-Clip outperforms PPO-Clip, DAPO-Clip, and DCPO-Clip across all four model sizes (0.5B–14B), confirming the geometric clipping mechanism is objective-agnostic.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the TRPO framework (Schulman et al., 2015), which establishes the KL-divergence trust region and the associated Riemannian geometry via the Fisher Information Matrix, and on PPO-Clip (Schulman et al., 2017), which replaces the constrained optimization with a fixed Euclidean clipping boundary on the importance ratio. The key departure is identifying that PPO-Clip's fixed boundary is geometrically inconsistent with the non-uniform Riemannian metric induced by KL divergence, whereas prior heuristic fixes (DAPO's Clip-Higher, DCPO's dynamic thresholds, GSPO/GMPO's sequence-level clipping) addressed exploration collapse without diagnosing this root cause. RIPO adopts the group-relative advantage estimator from GRPO/DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) and token-level loss aggregation from DAPO, but replaces the clipping mechanism with a distribution-dependent boundary derived from the local probability simplex.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The geometric mismatch diagnosis provides a principled, single-parameter fix (the trust-region radius δ) that replaces the ad-hoc ε of PPO-Clip, and the resulting isometric update simultaneously resolves exploration collapse and induces statistical homoscedasticity for a favorable bias-variance trade-off. The Pass@k analysis showing RIPO breaks through the base model's capacity plateau (continuing to improve at k=128 where the base model saturates at k=16) suggests the method unlocks latent reasoning capacity rather than merely redistributing existing ability. Open questions include whether the Riemannian isometric principle extends naturally to multi-objective or constrained RL settings, whether the second-order Taylor approximation remains valid for very large policy updates in long-horizon tasks, and whether the approach generalizes to continuous action spaces beyond discrete token generation.
