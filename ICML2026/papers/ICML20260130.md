## Problem

Existing LLM safety approaches rely on costly human red-teaming or isolated single-model self-assessment, both of which struggle to scale and are prone to oversight failures. Training-time alignment methods (RLHF, supervised safety tuning) are limited by coverage of training signals, distribution shift, and reward hacking, while inference-time methods like Constitutional AI and Reflexion depend on a single agent detecting its own blind spots. No prior framework unifies multi-agent debate with automated red-teaming and persistent learning to progressively reduce unsafe outputs without human intervention.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes adversarial red-teaming prompts (from HarmBench, CoSafe, Aegis-2, WildJailbreak) alongside two memory channels: a short-term memory holding the full debate history within the current session, and a long-term memory containing previously distilled safety feedback retrieved via vector similarity or encoded parametrically. Outputs include the refined safe response from each debater, binary safety labels from an independent LlamaGuard evaluator, a textual safety insight (φ) generated only when unsafe responses are detected, and—under the GLTM variant—executable Colang guardrail flows that proactively reject matching harmful inputs before generation.

## Method

RedDebate orchestrates N debater agents (e.g., Mistral-7B, LLaMA-3.2-3B, Phi-3.5-mini) who respond in parallel across T=3 rounds to a shared red-teaming prompt, with each round's responses appended to shared short-term memory so agents can critique and refine peers' outputs. Three debate strategies are explored: PReD (peer refinement with identical roles), DAReD (auxiliary Devil and Angel agents providing adversarial rejection and supportive reinforcement to force critical reassessment), and SReD (a Socratic agent that probes implicit assumptions and requests evidence). An independent evaluator (LlamaGuard) labels all responses; if any are unsafe, a feedback generator (GPT-4o-mini) produces a distilled safety insight stored in long-term memory. Four LTM variants are tested—textual (vector-retrieved feedback in prompt), continuous (LoRA fine-tuning on accumulated feedback), unified (both), and guardrails (auto-generated Colang flows via NeMo Guardrails that intercept matching harmful inputs pre-generation). The key mechanism is that debate exposes latent vulnerabilities invisible in single-turn settings (e.g., initially safe agents producing unsafe responses under peer pressure), and the evaluator's independent signal triggers feedback generation even when all debaters share a flawed belief.

## Results

On HarmBench (first triad: Mistral/LLaMA/Phi), SReD debate reduces total error rate from 38.7% (standard prompting) to 21.0%, a 17.7-percentage-point reduction; adding Textual LTM further lowers it to 15.2% (23.5 pp reduction). On CoSafe, SReD achieves 4.5% total ER versus 7.4% for standard prompting. The best configuration, SReD+GLTM (guardrails LTM), drives total HarmBench ER to near-zero (0.2–0.3%) for the second triad (Gemma/Qwen/R1), and SReD+TLTM reaches 15.2% total ER on the first triad, outperforming the Self-Critique baseline (15.4%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Self-Critique (Constitutional AI) baseline, SReD+TLTM achieves 15.2% total HarmBench ER versus SC's 15.4%—a marginal win—but SReD+GLTM dominates with 0.2% total ER, a 15.2 pp gap. On CoSafe, SC actually performs worse than no-memory SReD (8.1% vs. 4.5%), while SReD+TLTM reaches 3.4%, confirming that dynamic peer feedback in debate outperforms static handcrafted constitutions. Against Best-of-N sampling (N=5), SReD substantially outperforms on the average-case metric (28.9% vs. 42.7% total HarmBench ER) and the conservative worst-case metric (28.9% vs. 53.6%), while remaining comparable to the optimistic best-try bound (28.9% vs. 29.7%). The paper further shows that granting SC additional revision steps to match debate's token budget still fails to close the gap: SC plateaus or even increases error at intermediate steps for Llama and Phi, whereas debate yields consistent monotonic improvement across rounds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate research threads: multi-agent debate (MAD) for reasoning improvement (Du et al., 2024; Irving et al., 2018), automated red-teaming for safety evaluation (Perez et al., 2022; Ge et al., 2023), and inference-time learning from feedback (Shinn et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2022b). Prior MAD work targeted factuality and reasoning accuracy, while prior red-teaming relied on human annotation or single-agent self-critique with static constitutions. RedDebate departs by making debate itself the red-teaming mechanism—agents adversarially probe each other's safety—and by coupling this with persistent long-term memory (textual, parametric via LoRA, and programmatic guardrails via Colang/NeMo) so that safety lessons accumulate across debates without retraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical, fully automated path to inference-time safety improvement that complements (rather than replaces) training-time alignment like RLHF, addressing the gap where unsafe behaviours emerge only under novel adversarial prompts. The discovery that debate can expose latent vulnerabilities—agents initially producing safe responses that become unsafe under peer pressure—reframes debate not just as a correction tool but as a vulnerability-discovery mechanism. Key open questions include memory poisoning resilience in multi-agent settings, whether gains hold with reasoning-mode models, and whether the approach scales to frontier models without the guardrail instability observed with NeMo/Colang. The near-zero error rates achieved with GLTM on smaller models are promising but require validation at scale before deployment in high-stakes settings.
