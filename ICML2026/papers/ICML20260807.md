## Problem

In performative learning, deployed models shift the data distribution, but estimating this shift is generally infeasible in high-dimensional settings, and existing mitigation methods require repeated deployments or small-dimensional assumptions. This paper asks whether a simple, agnostic tool—ridge regularization—can mitigate performative effects without requiring knowledge of the distribution shift, and how the optimal regularization should scale with the strength of performativity in both population and over-parameterized regimes.

## Input / Output

The method takes a feature vector x with population covariance Σ (split into predictive and spurious blocks), a label y generated from a performative model where the label depends on the deployed parameter θ through a diagonal matrix D=diag(b,c), and a ridge penalty λ. It produces a closed-form optimal regularization λ* and a characterization of the excess risk evaluated on the untouched distribution D(θ=0), which is the key distinction from standard performative evaluation on the shifted distribution. The analysis covers both the population regime (n→∞) and the proportional regime (p/n=κ>1) with Gaussian or sub-Gaussian features.

## Method

In the population regime, the paper derives a Taylor expansion of the excess risk at the performative fixed point θ∞=(I+λΣ⁻¹−D)⁻¹θ*pop, showing it is quadratic in λ with an explicit optimal λ*pop = b̄d/Tr(S₁) proportional to the mean performative effect b̄. In the over-parameterized regime, the key technical contribution is a deterministic equivalent (Theorem 5.1) of the performative fixed point risk, obtained by applying the distributional characterization of ridge interpolators (Han & Xu, 2023; Ildiz et al., 2025) twice—showing that after just two RRM iterations the risk is fixed up to O(||D||²) fluctuations and depends only on Σ, θ*pop, D, and λ. Optimizing this deterministic equivalent reveals a noise-dependent phenomenology: for small noise the optimal regularization moves in the same direction as the performative effect on predictive features, while for large noise it reverses; and the optimally-regularized risk improves when performativity reinforces existing trends (b̄>0), a behavior absent in the population setting. Empirical validation on Housing, LSAC, and GiveMeSomeCredit datasets (including neural networks and alternative regularizers like Lasso, elastic net, and dropout) confirms the theoretical predictions extend beyond the Gaussian linear model.

## Results

In the population regime, the optimal ridge regularization is λ*_pop = b̄d/Tr(S₁), directly proportional to the mean performative effect strength b̄, and the optimally-regularized excess risk is always positive (worse than the non-performative case) unless Σ=I_p and b is uniform. In the over-parameterized regime (p/n=κ>1), the optimal regularization moves in the same direction as the performative effect on predictive features when noise variance σ² is small (σ² < σ²_{B₁}(κ) ≈ 1/2 − 7κ⁻¹/18), but reverses direction for large noise; critically, the optimally-regularized risk *improves* when performativity reinforces existing trends (b̄>0), regardless of noise level. Empirically, on Housing (n=4000, d=8) and LSAC (n=4000, d=22), the optimal regularizer increases proportionally with b̄ and the risk worsens (consistent with population theory), while on LSAC with n=100 (closer to d=22), the optimal regularizer decreases and risk improves as b̄ grows (consistent with over-parameterized theory). A neural network experiment on GiveMeSomeCredit confirms that ℓ₂ regularization mitigates the accuracy drop from performative shifts, with the optimal λ increasing with the performative strength parameter δ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper with empirical validation rather than a method-comparison study, so there are no external baselines to beat. The key internal comparisons are: (1) population vs. over-parameterized regimes, where the direction of optimal regularization relative to the performative effect *reverses*—in the population setting, more performativity always demands more regularization and worsens risk, while in the over-parameterized setting with small noise, performativity can actually *improve* the optimally-regularized risk; (2) predictive vs. spurious features, where the performative effect on spurious features (c) has a much weaker impact, attenuated by a factor of ρ² (the cross-block correlation), and moves the optimal regularization in the opposite direction (C₁≤0 for κ≥2); (3) across regularizers, the qualitative relationship between optimal regularization and performative strength persists for Lasso, elastic net, and dropout, though Lasso shows near-constant optimal regularization in the proportional regime due to the small feature count (d=22) preventing smooth support changes. The paper also contrasts testing on the initial distribution D(θ=0) versus the shifted distribution D(θ), showing that the optimally-regularized risk worsens under performativity when tested on the shifted distribution (opposite to the D(θ=0) case), highlighting the importance of the evaluation protocol.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of performative learning (Perdomo et al., 2020) and high-dimensional statistics. It builds on Cyffers et al. (2024), who showed that the performative optimum in classification can be interpreted as a regularized version of the non-performative problem, and extends this insight to high-dimensional ridge regression. The technical machinery draws on deterministic equivalents for over-parameterized ridge regression (Han & Xu, 2023; Ildiz et al., 2025) and spurious correlation analysis (Bombari & Mondelli, 2025). The authors explicitly position their work as the first to apply high-dimensional statistical tools to performative learning, departing from prior estimation-based methods that require characterizing the distribution shift and are limited to low-dimensional settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides actionable guidance: optimal ridge regularization scales proportionally with the strength of the performative effect, enabling practitioners to set regularization in anticipation of performativity without knowing its exact form. The counterintuitive finding that optimally-regularized risk improves when performativity reinforces existing trends in the over-parameterized regime challenges the assumption that performativity is always harmful. Limitations include the restriction to linear regression with label shifts, diagonal performative matrices, and specific covariance structures; open questions include extending to feature shifts, non-linear models, and other regularizers like early stopping or pruning, which the authors note as future directions.
