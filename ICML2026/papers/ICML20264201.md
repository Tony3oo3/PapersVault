## Problem

Microcanonical Langevin Monte Carlo (MCLMC) achieves state-of-the-art full-batch Bayesian inference but is computationally infeasible for large-scale models due to its reliance on full-dataset gradients. This paper asks whether MCLMC can be made scalable by leveraging mini-batch gradient noise, and identifies two critical failure modes that prevent naive stochastic adaptations: a theoretically derived bias from anisotropic gradient noise and numerical instabilities in high-dimensional posteriors.

## Input / Output

The method consumes mini-batch gradients (batch sizes from 128 to 512) from a neural network with a standard Gaussian prior, initialized from an optimized warmstart that provides both the starting parameters and an initial step size. It produces posterior parameter samples organized as a Bayesian deep ensemble (8–10 independent chains), from which the posterior predictive distribution is approximated via Monte Carlo averaging. The adaptive tuner requires only a single user-specified hyperparameter (the initial step size) and operates entirely online with no additional noise injection.

## Method

The paper establishes a continuous-time SDE analysis showing that isotropic mini-batch noise preserves MCLMC's target stationary distribution, while anisotropic noise introduces a non-vanishing drift term (½Σηᵢ²uᵢσᵢ) that biases the posterior. To correct this, pSMILE applies diagonal gradient noise preconditioning using exponential moving averages of per-parameter gradient standard deviations, making the effective noise locally isotropic. To address numerical instability at scale, an energy-variance-based adaptive tuner models the per-step energy error |ΔE| with an online-fitted Gamma distribution via moment matching, uses a 98th-percentile quantile as a non-probabilistic guardrail that resets divergent steps, and multiplicatively adjusts the step size based on where |ΔE| falls relative to lower and upper Gamma quantiles. The resulting (p)SMILE algorithm requires no Metropolis-Hastings correction, no explicit noise injection, and only one user-specified hyperparameter, yet matches or outperforms scale-adapted SGHMC and cSGLD on BNNs up to 22M parameters.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18 (11.2M parameters), pSMILE achieves the best overall balance: LPPD −0.2598, accuracy 91.35%, NLL 0.3900, and AURC 0.0113, matching or outperforming cSGLD (LPPD −0.2584, NLL 0.4059) and substantially beating SGHMC (LPPD −0.2908, NLL 0.4942). On the 22M-parameter Vision Transformer (Imagenette), pSMILE leads all baselines with LPPD −0.7708, accuracy 77.32%, NLL 1.1985, and AURC 0.0589, while cSGLD suffers a severe NLL degradation (3.4947). On UCI regression benchmarks, pSMILE-naive under epoch-wise sampling closes the gap to the full-batch MILE gold standard (e.g., Energy LPPD 2.018 vs. MILE 2.204; Airfoil RMSE 0.151 vs. 0.152).

## Baselines & Comparisons

pSMILE consistently outperforms scale-adapted SGHMC (Springenberg et al., 2016) across all large-scale benchmarks: on CIFAR-10/ResNet-18 it improves NLL from 0.4942 to 0.3900 and LPPD from −0.2908 to −0.2598; on Imagenette/ViT it improves NLL from 1.2928 to 1.1985 and AURC from 0.0758 to 0.0589. Against cSGLD (Zhang et al., 2020), pSMILE matches or slightly edges out on most metrics (CIFAR-10: NLL 0.3900 vs. 0.4059; Imagenette: NLL 1.1985 vs. 3.4947, a dramatic gap), while cSGLD retains a marginal LPPD advantage on CIFAR-10 (−0.2584 vs. −0.2598). On the UCI regression benchmarks, pSMILE-naive under epoch-wise sampling matches the full-batch MILE gold standard (Sommer et al., 2025) within noise (e.g., Energy LPPD 2.018 vs. 2.204; Airfoil RMSE 0.151 vs. 0.152), demonstrating that stochastic microcanonical dynamics can replicate full-batch performance. In the analytical bias benchmarks (Table 1), pSMILE-naive reduces the second-moment bias by 5–10× over SMILE-naive under anisotropic noise (e.g., ICG/Correlated: 0.055 vs. 0.502) and consistently outperforms both SGLD (Welling & Teh, 2011) and vanilla SGHMC (Chen et al., 2014). Against the optimization-based IVON baseline (Shen et al., 2024), pSMILE achieves substantially better LPPD (−0.3549 vs. −1.6163) and accuracy (88.58% vs. 87.35%) in single-chain mode on CIFAR-10. The full-batch MCLMC baseline (Robnik & Seljak, 2024) sets the reference bias levels (0.0001–0.004) that pSMILE-naive approaches under isotropic noise but cannot fully match under anisotropic noise without preconditioning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the microcanonical Langevin Monte Carlo (MCLMC) framework of Robnik & Seljak (2024) and its ensemble variant MILE (Sommer et al., 2025) from full-batch to the stochastic mini-batch regime, providing the first systematic study of stochastic microcanonical Langevin dynamics. It departs from standard SGMCMC methods (SGLD, SGHMC, cSGLD) in a key conceptual way: because MCLMC's Langevin noise does not require a corresponding damping term for convergence, mini-batch gradient noise can substitute for explicit noise injection, unlike in stochastic Langevin or Hamiltonian samplers which typically operate in a heavily overdamped regime. The paper identifies two failure modes absent from prior SGMCMC analyses—an anisotropic-noise-induced bias in the stationary distribution and numerical instability at scale—and addresses them with a gradient noise preconditioning scheme and an energy-variance-based adaptive tuner, respectively.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By closing the performance gap between stochastic and full-batch microcanonical sampling (pSMILE-naive matches MILE under epoch-wise budgeting), this work makes a state-of-the-art BNN sampler practical for modern architectures up to 22M parameters, reducing the hyperparameter sensitivity that has limited SGMCMC adoption. Key limitations include the reliance on a diagonal (locally constant) preconditioner that introduces residual approximation bias, the absence of a complete finite-step-size theoretical analysis, and the decision to forgo explicit noise injection, which could enhance exploration at the cost of additional tuning. Open directions include reintroducing explicit noise, developing full Riemannian corrections for spatially varying preconditioners, and extending the theory to non-Gaussian noise regimes where the method's performance degrades.
