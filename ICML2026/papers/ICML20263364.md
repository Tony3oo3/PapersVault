## Problem

Existing benchmarks measure long-horizon reasoning only indirectly: frontier reasoning benchmarks cap outputs at ~10K tokens, long-context benchmarks test retrieval over long inputs but require short outputs, and agentic benchmarks confound reasoning with tool use and scaffolding. No prior evaluation directly isolates a model's ability to maintain a coherent, correct chain of thought over tens to hundreds of thousands of reasoning tokens while navigating interdependent subproblems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263364/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each problem is a short self-contained prompt (median 2K tokens, max 6.7K) that the model must solve by generating an extended chain of thought (typically 50K–100K+ tokens) before emitting a single verifiable final answer. Answers are drawn from large combinatorial spaces—integers, tuples, fractions, SMILES strings, or move sequences—so random guessing is ineffective and incorrect intermediate values almost never yield a correct final answer. Verification is performed by domain-specific checkers (cheminformatics tools, Stockfish, programmatic simulation) followed by LLM-based extraction as a fallback.

## Method

LongCoT is constructed from 50 expert-designed parameterized templates (10 per domain across mathematics, chemistry, computer science, chess, and logic), each instantiated 50 times to yield 2,500 problems. Templates come in two classes: explicit compositional templates that present a directed acyclic graph of subproblems in the prompt (using four motifs—linear chains, multi-parent DAGs, conditionals, and forced backtracking), and implicit procedural templates where the dependency structure is latent in rules or constraints (game trees, constraint satisfaction, simulation, planning). A central design principle is that every atomic step is individually tractable for frontier models, so failures on the full problem isolate long-horizon limitations—planning, state maintenance, error detection, and backtracking—rather than esoteric knowledge or single-step difficulty. Evaluation is conducted without tools or scaffolding to isolate the model's inherent reasoning capability, with a separate scaffolded track (code execution via RLM) reported for comparison. Difficulty is scaled by controlling DAG depth/width, search-space size, or simulation length, producing three tiers (easy/medium/hard) that yield meaningful separation across model classes.

## Results

On the full LongCoT benchmark (2,000 medium and hard problems), GPT 5.2 achieves the highest accuracy at 9.83%, followed by Gemini 3 Pro (6.08%) and Grok 4.1 Fast Reasoning (2.04%), with all other models near zero. GPT 5.2 uses an average of 62,046 reasoning tokens per problem, and performance rises monotonically with token budget. On the easier LongCoT-mini subset (500 problems), GPT 5.2 reaches 38.7%, while open-source models show meaningful differentiation: DeepSeek V3.2 achieves 8.3% and Kimi K2 Thinking 7.5%. Accuracy degrades steeply as problem DAG size increases beyond ~15 nodes, falling well below an independent-error baseline, confirming that long-horizon composition introduces failure modes absent in isolated subproblems.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LongCoT is dramatically harder than all existing reasoning benchmarks: the best score on FrontierMath is 31.3% and on HLE 38.3%, whereas the best on LongCoT is only 9.83%, a gap of over 20 percentage points. Long-context benchmarks (LongBench v2 >65%, MRCR v2 >95%) test retrieval over long inputs with short outputs and do not stress-test long reasoning chains. Agentic benchmarks (SWE-Bench-Verified 80.9%, WebArena 71.6%, BrowseComp 77.9%) confound reasoning ability with tool use and scaffolding, so their high scores do not reflect pure long-horizon CoT capability. When the Recursive Language Models (RLM) framework is evaluated on LongCoT with GPT 5.2, the reasoning-only setting does not outperform GPT 5.2 alone, while enabling code execution yields gains primarily on implicit domains (Logic: 68.3%, Chess: 30.6%) but not on explicit compositional domains (Math, Chemistry, CS), confirming that the core long-horizon reasoning burden cannot be fully offloaded to external tools.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a specific gap between three existing benchmark families: frontier reasoning benchmarks (FrontierMath, HLE) that cap reasoning at ~10K tokens, long-context benchmarks (LongBench v2, MRCR v2) that test long inputs but short outputs, and agentic benchmarks (SWE-Bench, Terminal-Bench) that confound reasoning with tool use and scaffolding. Rather than building on a single prior method, LongCoT synthesizes ideas from constraint satisfaction, game theory, and compositional problem design into a unified parameterized-template framework with explicit or implicit dependency graphs (DAGs, game trees, constraint graphs). The key conceptual departure is isolating long-horizon *output* reasoning (100K+ token chains) as a standalone capability, with controlled single-step difficulty so that failures reflect long-horizon limitations rather than esoteric knowledge gaps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even GPT 5.2 achieves only 9.83% (vs. 31–38% on comparable frontier reasoning benchmarks) reveals that reliable long-horizon reasoning remains a fundamental open problem with direct implications for autonomous agents in science, engineering, and enterprise. The parameterized template design enables scalable difficulty and clean ablation of horizon length, making LongCoT a durable evaluation as models improve. Open questions include whether training methods (e.g., the RLM framework, RL-based approaches) can close the gap, whether the compositional vs. procedural split will persist as models gain better context management, and whether the <10% ceiling reflects a fundamental architectural limitation or a solvable optimization problem. A limitation is that single-shot evaluation without pass@k (due to cost) and the lack of CoT access for closed-source models constrain deeper failure analysis.
