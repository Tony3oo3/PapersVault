## Problem

Decoder-only LLMs must serialize two-dimensional tables into one-dimensional token sequences, which obscures row- and column-level relations critical for table reasoning. Prior (hyper)graph-based methods encode structure via external encoders, but their gains are inconsistent or even negative under autoregressive decoding because structurally related tokens may be far apart in the linearized sequence. The paper asks: where inside the LLM is tabular structure actually represented, and can explicit structural features be injected at those specific locations to reliably improve reasoning?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a serialized table (as token sequence) together with a natural-language query, and optionally a linked text passage for multimodal table-text QA. At inference, the model generates a brief chain-of-thought reasoning trace followed by a structured final answer (a span or sentence for QA, a binary label for fact verification, or a single-sentence description for Table2Text). The structural hypergraph is built from the table's cell and header layout at each selected layer, so the input format (HTML, plain text, JSON) affects serialization but not the hypergraph topology, which is derived from the underlying table structure.

## Method

HInT first identifies a small set of 'structural layers' by solving a LASSO regression that decomposes idealized row and column masks into sparse linear combinations of attention maps across all heads and layers, then aggregating head votes into layer-frequency scores. At each selected structural layer, the model pools token hidden states into cell-node and header-hyperedge features, applies one step of HyperTrans message passing over a hypergraph whose hyperedges encode row-wise, column-wise, and hierarchical-header groupings, and fuses the resulting structural features back into individual token representations via a learned sigmoid gate (a convex combination of the original hidden state and the structural feature). All other layers remain unchanged, preserving the standard autoregressive computation. The HyperTrans module is pretrained with a self-supervised corruption-detection objective, and the full system (backbone LLM + HyperTrans + gate) is jointly fine-tuned end-to-end with reasoning-augmented supervision that includes intermediate chain-of-thought steps.

## Results

HInT achieves the best score on all eight table-reasoning benchmarks, with headline results of 67.39% EM on WikiTQ, 71.75% EM on HiTab, 86.71% accuracy on TabFact, and 57.25% EM on FinQA. Gains over the Qwen2.5-3B (SFT) backbone range from +0.47 BLEU on FeTaQA to +2.91 EM on FinQA, with the largest relative improvements on structurally complex or multi-hop tasks (HiTab, HybridQA, FinQA). On the Llama-3.1-8B backbone, HInT reaches 80.47% EM on WikiTQ, outperforming the SFT baseline (77.34%) and TaMo (77.97%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HInT outperforms all six baselines on all eight benchmarks. Against the strongest text-only baseline Qwen2.5-3B (SFT), HInT gains +1.95 EM on WikiTQ, +1.63 EM on HiTab, +1.46 Acc on TabFact, and +2.91 EM on FinQA. Among (hyper)graph-based methods, TaMo is the closest competitor (within 0.5–1.0 points on most tasks), while HeGTa degrades severely on FinQA (16.33 vs. 57.25) and ToTTo (7.60 vs. 18.85), and LLaSA underperforms the SFT baseline on TabFact (82.19 vs. 85.25) and FEVEROUS (66.36 vs. 76.22). Notably, the (hyper)graph-based methods do not consistently beat the text-only SFT baseline, confirming the paper's diagnosis that external-encoder structure injection is unreliable under autoregressive decoding. On FeTaQA, HInT's margin over TaMo is minimal (+0.37 BLEU), and TableLlama (a different backbone) still leads on that particular benchmark, though HInT remains the only method that improves over its own backbone baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

HInT builds on the HyperTrans hypergraph message-passing module (Chen et al., 2023) and the broader line of (hyper)graph-based table encoders such as HeGTa, LLaSA, and TaMo, which inject structural features via external encoders before LLM decoding. The key departure is that HInT identifies *where* tabular structure is already encoded inside a decoder-only LLM (via LASSO probing of attention heads) and injects hypergraph features directly into those specific internal layers, rather than at the input level. This reframes the problem from 'how to extract table structure' to 'where and how to integrate it into autoregressive computation,' a question prior encoder-based methods do not address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that row- and column-level relations are concentrated in a small, identifiable subset of layers and heads provides a useful diagnostic for understanding how decoder-only LLMs internally represent tabular structure, and the layer-specific injection strategy is a lightweight, architecture-preserving intervention that practitioners can adopt without modifying the model. The paper also surfaces an important negative result: external (hyper)graph encoders do not consistently outperform text-only baselines under multi-step reasoning with decoder-only LLMs, suggesting the field has been optimizing the wrong integration point. Open questions include whether the fixed layer set transfers across domains and model scales, whether the hypergraph formulation can capture richer semantics (numerical constraints, cross-table dependencies), and whether the principle of injecting structured relational information into internally aligned layers generalizes beyond tables to knowledge graphs or program representations.
