## Problem

Selecting the top-m items from n candidates via expensive k-wise comparisons (e.g., LLM reranking) is a central problem in information retrieval, yet existing methods either discard most of the comparison information each query reveals (sliding windows, tournament brackets extract only a winner) or exploit it at prohibitive cost (pairwise reranking). No prior approach provides a principled framework for accumulating pairwise preferences across queries, certifying when the top-m is determined, or handling the non-transitive cycles that real-world oracles produce.

## Input / Output

The method takes a query and a set of n=100 BM25-retrieved documents, then adaptively issues k-wise comparison queries (k=10 or k=20) to a zero-shot LLM oracle using the RankGPT prompt format, where each query returns a complete ordering (tournament) of the k documents. The algorithm terminates adaptively—typically in 6–15 rounds—once the current top-m (m=10) documents are all 'resolved,' meaning their relationship to every other document is certified via transitive closure. In the non-transitive case, the output is a tiered ranking where documents within the same strongly connected component are declared tied rather than arbitrarily ordered.

## Method

BLITZRANK maintains a global revealed graph G that accumulates all pairwise edges returned by the oracle; the key insight is that each k-wise query reveals C(k,2) pairwise preferences, and transitive closure over G certifies many additional orderings without further oracle calls. At each iteration, the algorithm computes each vertex's in-reach (number of vertices that transitively dominate it) and known-relationship count κ_G(v), then terminates when the m vertices with smallest in-reach all satisfy κ_G(v)=n−1 (fully resolved). For query scheduling, it computes the condensation DAG of G (collapsing SCCs) and greedily queries one representative from each unresolved SCC with the smallest condensation in-reach, guaranteeing at least one new edge per round. Non-transitive preferences are handled by collapsing SCCs into equivalence classes, yielding a principled tiered ranking rather than an arbitrary total order. The framework is provably correct and terminating in both transitive and non-transitive settings, with a worst-case bound of C(n,2) queries and empirically achieving 25–40% fewer tokens than comparable methods and 7× fewer than pairwise reranking at near-identical quality.

## Results

Across 14 benchmarks (6 TREC DL tracks + 8 BEIR datasets) and 5 LLM oracles, BLITZRANK (k=10) achieves 56.9 macro-averaged nDCG@10 while consuming only 42k input tokens per query — 25–40% fewer tokens than structurally comparable methods (Sliding Window: 54k, TourRank: 57k, AcuRank: 69k) and 7.5× fewer than Pairwise (315k) at near-identical quality (56.9 vs. 57.0). Against the strongest accuracy-matching baselines (SW-R2: 56.7, AcuRank-H: 56.6), BLITZRANK requires 2.6–3.0× fewer tokens. Convergence is highly predictable: k=10 terminates in 12–15 rounds (mean 13.6, std 0.58) and k=20 in 6–7 rounds (mean 6.7, std 0.45) across all 70 dataset-oracle configurations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Pairwise (Qin et al., NAACL 2024), BLITZRANK-k10 matches accuracy (56.9 vs. 57.0 nDCG@10) while using 7.5× fewer tokens (42k vs. 315k), confirming that extracting full k-wise tournaments rather than single pairwise judgments yields massive efficiency gains. Against Sliding Window (Sun et al., EMNLP 2023), BLITZRANK-k10 slightly outperforms (56.9 vs. 56.7) at 22% lower token cost (42k vs. 54k), and the gap widens at k=10 where SW degrades sharply (74.0→56.4 on DL19) while BLITZRANK maintains quality (74.6→73.6). Against TourRank (Chen et al., WWW 2025), BLITZRANK outperforms by 0.9 nDCG@10 (56.9 vs. 56.0) at 26% fewer tokens, demonstrating that maintaining a tournament graph with transitive closure is more informative than using tournaments purely as a scheduling mechanism. Against AcuRank (Yoon et al., NeurIPS 2025), BLITZRANK achieves higher accuracy (56.9 vs. 56.3) with 39% fewer tokens and far more predictable cost (2% std vs. 8% std in LLM calls). Against Setwise (Zhuang et al., SIGIR 2024), BLITZRANK matches or exceeds accuracy (56.9 vs. 56.6) at 2.7× lower cost, validating that capturing all C(k,2) pairwise relationships per query rather than just the winner is the key efficiency lever. BM25 without reranking (41.1 nDCG@10) serves as the retrieval-only floor, showing that all reranking methods provide substantial gains over the initial ranking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant LLM reranking paradigms (sliding windows, tournament brackets, Bayesian score updates) by reframing k-wise comparisons as tournament graph queries whose full pairwise structure is accumulated and amplified via transitive closure. It builds on classical tournament graph theory (condensation, SCC decomposition, reachability) and multi-wise comparison sample-complexity results (Ren et al., Saha & Gopalan), but is the first to combine these into a query-efficient top-m selection algorithm with provable correctness for both transitive and non-transitive preferences. Unlike Setwise (winner-only extraction) or TourRank (tournaments as scheduling without graph accumulation), BLITZRANK treats each k-wise oracle call as revealing a complete induced tournament and uses the resulting global preference graph to certify when the top-m are determined.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled, deterministic alternative to heuristic reranking with guaranteed termination and predictable cost (12–15 rounds for k=10 across all datasets and oracles), enabling reliable budget planning in production systems. The SCC-based handling of non-transitive preferences—validated by showing cycles capture genuinely similar documents (~40% lower BM25 variance)—provides a more faithful representation than methods that aggregate away or ignore cycles. Key open problems include extending to noisy oracles (where a single erroneous edge can collapse transitive chains), proving tight query-complexity bounds for general m, and incorporating retrieval-score priors into query scheduling.
