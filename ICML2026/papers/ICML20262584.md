## Problem

Existing scene text editing methods rely on pre-trained OCR encoders whose fixed vocabularies restrict the scope of editable text, and they adopt an inpainting formulation that discards the original text region's visual information, causing edited text to lose its pre-existing color, font, and texture in favor of surrounding background styles. This paper addresses both limitations by enabling open-vocabulary editing while preserving the target region's original appearance.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image, a binary mask delineating the target text region, and a target text string. It internally constructs two visual prompts: a white-on-black glyph map rendered from the target text via Pillow, and a cropped patch of the original text region serving as a style reference. These are concatenated channel-wise with the masked image and encoded by a frozen VAE, while the target text and a style description are encoded by T5 and CLIP respectively. The output is the decoded edited image cropped to the target region, with all non-target pixels left unchanged.

## Method

Built on FLUX-Fill (an MMDiT-based rectified-flow inpainting model), the approach eliminates all dedicated glyph or style encoders by directly constructing self-prompts from the input image: a rendered glyph map provides stroke-level structural guidance, and a cropped original-text patch provides pixel-level style reference, both fed as in-context visual tokens to the transformer. Training proceeds in two stages: first, self-supervised pretraining on AnyWord-3M (1.6M+ images) using glyph-only prompts to learn fundamental multilingual text rendering; second, a cooldown phase on 4,000 manually filtered paired images where both glyph and style prompts are active, with a target-region-oriented rectified-flow objective that interpolates between source and target latents to decouple style preservation from content regeneration. Only the transformer parameters are updated while the VAE, T5, and CLIP encoders remain frozen, and the in-context learning capability of MMDiT enables open-vocabulary generalization at the stroke level rather than the character level.

## Results

On the AnyText-benchmark, the proposed method achieves 0.8857 Seq. Acc and 0.9568 NED on English, and 0.8249 Seq. Acc and 0.9147 NED on Chinese, outperforming the strongest baseline (TextFlux) by 6.3 and 9.6 percentage points in sentence accuracy, respectively. Image quality metrics show even larger margins: FID drops to 7.62 (English) and 7.95 (Chinese) versus 13.42 and 13.67 for TextFlux, and LPIPS falls to 0.0365 and 0.0268 versus 0.0721 and 0.0524. On the multilingual MST-Edit dataset (11 non-Chinese/English languages), the method achieves an average Seq. Acc of 0.76 and FID of 22.8, surpassing the best OCR-free baseline (TextFlux) by 7 percentage points in accuracy and 6.8 in FID.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms all 10 baselines across every reported metric. Against the closest OCR-free competitors, TextFlux (Xie et al., 2025) and Flux-Text (Lan et al., 2025), it gains +6.3 pp Seq. Acc on English (0.8857 vs. 0.8231) and +9.6 pp on Chinese (0.8249 vs. 0.7289), while halving FID (7.62 vs. 13.42 English; 7.95 vs. 13.67 Chinese). OCR-based methods (AnyText, AnyText2, TextCtrl, GlyphMastero) are substantially behind: AnyText2, the strongest OCR-based baseline, trails by 9.4 pp in English Seq. Acc and 12.3 pp in Chinese Seq. Acc, with FID roughly 3–4× higher. On the multilingual MST-Edit set, OCR-based baselines (AnyText2, TextCtrl) collapse to ~0.10 Seq. Acc across all 11 languages due to limited OCR vocabulary, whereas the proposed method maintains 0.57–0.81 Seq. Acc. In supplementary comparisons with general-purpose instruction-based editors (Qwen-Image-Edit, LongCat-Image-Edit, FireRed-Image-Edit), the mask-guided formulation yields consistent advantages of 10–21 pp in Seq. Acc and 14–20 points in FID, attributed to precise region localization that instruction-based models lack.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on FLUX-Fill (MMDiT) as its diffusion transformer backbone and departs from the dominant inpainting-based formulation of scene text editing by preserving the original text region as a style prompt rather than discarding it. It contrasts with OCR-encoder-based methods (AnyText, AnyText2, GlyphMastero) that are limited to fixed vocabularies, and with OCR-free rendering methods (FluxText, TextFlux) that still lose pre-editing style. The two-stage training strategy—self-supervised pretraining followed by a cooldown phase on a small paired dataset—draws inspiration from MECO's meta-conditioning idea, while the in-context learning mechanism leverages MMDiT's dual-stream architecture to jointly process glyph, style, and masked-image tokens without additional encoders.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method provides a practical path to open-vocabulary, style-consistent multilingual text editing without the vocabulary bottleneck of OCR encoders or the representational mismatch of separate style encoders, and the MST-Edit dataset (11 non-Chinese/English languages) fills a notable gap in multilingual scene text editing benchmarks. However, the paper acknowledges clear failure modes—few-to-many character count changes, many-to-few hallucinations, and multi-line layout breakdown—suggesting that the single-line glyph prompt design is a structural limitation. Open questions include whether the cooldown strategy generalizes beyond the 4,000-pair scale, whether the approach extends to layout-aware multi-line editing, and whether the positive multilingual transfer observed here persists at larger language counts or more typologically distant scripts.
