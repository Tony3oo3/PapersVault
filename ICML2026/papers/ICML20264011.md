## Problem

On-policy RL for LLM reasoning stalls on hard problems where pass@k ≈ 0, wasting FLOPs with vanishing policy gradients. Existing strategies to reuse off-policy traces—SFT warm-starting or importance-weighted off-policy RL—suffer from entropy collapse, memorization, or high-variance gradient instability. The paper asks how to recycle previously spent inference compute (correct off-policy traces) to bootstrap on-policy RL on hard problems without treating those traces as supervision targets.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264011/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes hard math reasoning problems (pass@512 ≈ 0 under the base LLM) paired with a single correct off-policy trace per problem, collected via rejection sampling. From each trace, multiple prefixes of varying length (40%–80% of trace tokens) are appended to the original problem to form prefixed problems, which are mixed 3:1 with no-prefix problems during training. The output is a trained reasoning policy evaluated on both no-prefix training problems and held-out benchmarks (AIME'25, HMMT'25, IMO-AnswerBench); gradients are always masked on the off-policy prefix tokens, so the model only learns to complete the prefix on-policy.

## Method

PrefixRL conditions on prefixes of correct off-policy traces and runs standard on-policy RL (REINFORCE with group baseline) to complete them, rather than supervising on the full trace. The off-policy prefix places the policy in high-reward intermediate states, reducing gradient variance and breaking the all-negative stalling regime on hard problems. The paper proves two theoretical guarantees: (i) objective consistency—any maximizer of the PrefixRL objective also maximizes the standard RL objective J(π) under realizability of the off-policy traces; and (ii) a suboptimality bound for a natural-policy-gradient variant showing PrefixRL requires fewer on-policy samples (by a factor related to context length) than standard RL. Empirically, the authors discover back-generalization: training only on prefixed problems substantially improves no-prefix test performance, and the learned strategies can differ from those in the prefix (e.g., unlearning a suboptimal Erdős-Gallai strategy in favor of a Dirichlet-theorem approach). In a self-improvement loop where off-policy traces come from rejection sampling the base model itself, PrefixRL achieves 2× compute efficiency and >45% higher training accuracy over the strongest SFT+RL baseline, with gains transferring to held-out benchmarks.

## Results

On AIME '25 with Llama-3.1-8B, PrefixRL raises pass@1 from 38.2 (base) to 61.3, and on HMMT '25 from 29.2 to 49.4. In compute-matched comparisons, PrefixRL achieves >45% higher training accuracy on no-prefix hard problems (Llama-3.1-8B) and >30% higher (Qwen-3-4B) versus the strongest baseline (SFT+RL), while being 2× more compute-efficient even after accounting for rejection-sampling cost. On standardized benchmarks, PrefixRL improves pass@k (k≤64) by at least 10% absolute over all baselines, with gaps widening as k grows (e.g., +18 at k=8 and +28 at k=64 on AIME '25).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PrefixRL outperforms all six baselines in compute-matched comparisons. Against the strongest mid-training baseline (SFT+RL / OctoThinker), PrefixRL achieves 2× higher compute efficiency and >45% higher final training accuracy on no-prefix problems (Llama-3.1-8B), with a 12% pass@1 gain on AIME '25. Standard on-policy RL (REINFORCE) and importance-weighted off-policy RL both stall on hard problems (pass@512≈0), with the latter suffering gradient norm blow-ups and training collapse (Fig. 13); PrefixRL avoids these instabilities by masking gradients on the off-policy prefix. LUFFY (off-policy guidance) is also outperformed by at least 10% absolute on pass@k across all benchmarks. Against hint-based BREAD, PrefixRL wins in compute-matched accuracy and exhibits stronger back-generalization to no-prefix problems, while BREAD remains tied to the hinted strategy (e.g., Erdős-Gallai) whereas PrefixRL discovers a more robust alternative (Dirichlet theorem). CHORD (weighted SFT + reward shaping) is similarly outperformed in compute-matched comparisons on the original no-prefix hard training problems.

## Relation to Prior Work

PrefixRL operationalizes the classical RL idea of resetting to off-policy states (Kakade 2003; Silver et al. 2016; Chang et al. 2024) for reasoning LLMs, but departs from prior off-policy reuse methods (importance-weighted RL, SFT mid-training) by treating off-policy traces purely as conditioning context rather than supervision targets. Its proof technique adapts Chang et al. (2024) dataset-reset analysis to verifiable-reward LLM settings with weaker assumptions. The method also relates to hint-based RL guidance (BREAD, AdaBack, QuestA, POPE) and curriculum learning, but differs in that prefixes are sourced from the model's own prior rejection-sampling compute rather than external hints, enabling a self-improvement loop.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PrefixRL addresses a fundamental bottleneck in LLM RL—stalling on hard problems where pass@k≈0—by converting previously wasted inference FLOPs into a 2× compute-efficiency gain, which is practically significant for scaling RL training. The back-generalization phenomenon (training only on prefixed problems improves no-prefix performance) is a new and not-yet-explained mechanism that could be central to continuous self-improvement, but its scope is currently limited to math reasoning and two model families. Key open questions include whether the gains transfer to code, planning, or agentic tasks; what internal representations enable back-generalization; and whether prefix-length curricula or cross-checkpoint off-policy sources further improve the method.
