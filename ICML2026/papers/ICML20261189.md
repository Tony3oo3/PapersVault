## Problem

A single sparse autoencoder (SAE) trained on language model activations captures only a limited subset of the interpretable features available in the activation space, yet no formal framework existed for combining multiple SAEs to recover a more complete decomposition. The paper asks whether ensembling SAEs—leveraging their inherent variability even under identical architecture and hyperparameters—can improve reconstruction fidelity, feature stability, and downstream interpretability utility.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261189/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes d-dimensional residual stream activations from a specific layer of a language model (e.g., layer 8 of Pythia-160M with d=768, or layer 12 of Gemma 2-2B with d=2304) and produces a reconstructed activation expressed as a sparse linear combination over kJ concatenated feature vectors (where k is the per-SAE hidden dimension and J is the ensemble size). A key structural property is that the ensemble output is mathematically equivalent to a single SAE with a block-concatenated decoder matrix and stacked coefficient vector, meaning the ensemble can be treated as one wider SAE for downstream feature-level analyses such as concept detection or feature ablation.

## Method

The paper formalizes SAE ensembles as weighted sums of individual SAE reconstructions and proves (Proposition 3.1) that this output-space combination is exactly equivalent to concatenating the decoder matrices and feature coefficients in feature space. Two ensemble strategies are instantiated: naive bagging, where J SAEs with different random initializations are trained in parallel on the same data and their outputs are uniformly averaged (theoretically justified as variance reduction in the bias-variance decomposition), and boosting, where SAEs are trained sequentially with each subsequent SAE fitting the residual left by all previous SAEs and outputs are summed (justified as bias reduction). The boosting loss at iteration j minimizes reconstruction error on the residual a^(n,j) = a^(n) − Σ_{ℓ<j} g(a^(n,ℓ); θ^(ℓ)) plus the standard sparsity penalty, with identical regularization across all iterations. Empirically, boosting outperforms an expanded SAE of matched feature count on reconstruction metrics and stability, while naive bagging excels in stability but can introduce redundant features that hurt tasks requiring selective feature ablation.

## Results

Across three language models (GELU-1L, Pythia-160M, Gemma 2-2B), boosting with 8 SAEs achieves substantially lower MSE than an expanded SAE with the same total feature count: 12.54 vs 17.89 (GELU-1L), 0.85 vs 4.39 (Pythia-160M), and 46.54 vs 472.33 (Gemma 2-2B, a ~10× improvement). Boosting also improves stability by roughly 2× over the expanded SAE (e.g., 0.583 vs 0.268 on Gemma 2-2B) while maintaining comparable connectivity. On downstream tasks, boosting outperforms the expanded SAE on spurious correlation removal (S_SHIFT: 0.066 vs 0.047 for Professor vs. Nurse), while naive bagging edges out on concept detection (e.g., 0.631 vs 0.600 on Amazon Review sentiment).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is an expanded SAE trained to have the same total number of features as the 8-SAE ensemble, with sparsity matched to the ensemble. Boosting decisively outperforms the expanded SAE on reconstruction (MSE reduced by 30% on GELU-1L, 81% on Pythia-160M, and 90% on Gemma 2-2B) and on stability (roughly 2× higher across all models), while maintaining comparable connectivity. Naive bagging trades reconstruction quality for stability: it achieves the highest stability scores (0.745 on GELU-1L, 0.731 on Pythia-160M) but worse MSE than the expanded SAE on the two smaller models. On the spurious correlation removal task, boosting achieves the best S_SHIFT scores across all four profession pairs (e.g., 0.066 vs 0.047 for Professor vs. Nurse), while naive bagging underperforms both baselines due to redundant features requiring more ablations. On concept detection with a single top feature, naive bagging slightly outperforms the expanded SAE on average, but boosting surpasses naive bagging when top-2 or top-5 features are used, suggesting boosting captures complementary sub-features that combine for better generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts classical ensemble methods—bagging (Breiman, 1996) and boosting (Friedman, 2001)—to the sparse autoencoder setting, motivated by recent evidence that SAEs with identical architecture and hyperparameters learn different but overlapping feature sets (Fel et al., 2025; Paulo & Belrose, 2025). It departs from Switch SAE (Mudide et al., 2024), which trains multiple experts jointly but selects one at inference, and from SAE Boost (Koriagin et al., 2025), which performs a single residual correction step, by proposing a general multi-iteration framework where all SAEs are used jointly at inference. The key theoretical contribution—showing that ensembling SAE reconstructions is equivalent to concatenating their decoder matrices and feature coefficients—provides a clean formal bridge between the output-space ensemble view and the feature-space view that interpretability practitioners work with.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that boosting-based ensembles outperform a simply expanded SAE (matched in feature count) on both reconstruction fidelity and feature stability addresses a practical concern: larger SAEs yield unreliable, unstable features, so ensembling offers a path to better interpretability without the instability penalty of scale. However, the gains do not uniformly translate to downstream tasks—naive bagging underperforms on spurious correlation removal due to redundant features, and boosting's more specific features can hurt single-feature concept detection—so the choice of ensemble strategy must be matched to the application. Open questions include whether ensembling can be extended across different SAE architectures (e.g., Matryoshka, Switch SAEs), different training domains, and whether theoretical guarantees can be established for feature identifiability rather than just reconstruction error.
