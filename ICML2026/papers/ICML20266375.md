## Problem

Many works claim that autoregressive Transformers are Turing-complete, but their proofs typically rely on assumptions—such as context windows that grow with input length or numerical precision that scales with the number of tokens processed—that do not hold for a single deployed LLM. The paper identifies that these results are established in a 'scaling-family' regime (a collection of different models for different input lengths) rather than the 'fixed-system' regime that matches real-world deployment, and asks whether the computational power of a fixed Transformer system is actually determined by the Transformer itself or by the context-management mechanism coupled with it.

## Input / Output

The fixed-system formalization takes an arbitrary-length token sequence over a finite vocabulary Σ as input and processes it autoregressively through a triple (T, D, C): a fixed Transformer T that maps an N-token context window to a next-token distribution, a deterministic decoding rule D, and a deterministic context manager C that decides which tokens occupy the window at each step. The output is a generated token sequence (or a designated accept/reject token for decision problems). The critical constraint is that N and the internal numerical precision are fixed constants; when the maintained sequence exceeds N, C must select a subset of tokens to feed into the window, making the context-management policy the variable that governs what the system can compute.

## Method

The paper formalizes the fixed-system regime as a triple (T, D, C) and proves that the computational power of the resulting system is determined primarily by the context manager C rather than by T. For summarization-style context management (compressing earlier history into a fixed-size summary), the system is upper-bounded by a constant-space Turing machine (FDSPACE(1)), meaning it can recognize only regular languages. For appending-style context management (sliding window that appends decoded tokens and shifts forward), the system is shown to be equivalent to a linear-space Turing machine (DSPACE(n)), and the converse is established by showing a context-window-2 Transformer can realize any function Σ²→Σ via greedy decoding. The paper further surveys representative Turing-completeness claims, categorizing them into scaling-window and scaling-precision groups that implicitly place the analysis in the scaling-family regime, and argues that these results provide resource bounds rather than establishing Turing-completeness of a single deployed system.

## Results

The paper establishes three key computational-power separations for fixed Transformer systems under different context managers. Summarization-style context management yields a system upper-bounded by a constant-space Turing machine (FDSPACE(1)), meaning it can recognize only regular languages. Appending-style (sliding-window) context management achieves exactly the power of a linear-space Turing machine (DSPACE(n)), equivalent to deterministic context-sensitive languages. A (2,2)-restricted system or a read/write external-memory context manager achieves full Turing-completeness, showing that the context-management mechanism—not the Transformer itself—determines the system's computational ceiling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical position paper with no empirical baselines; the 'comparisons' are between context-management strategies and their induced computational power. The paper shows that summarization-style management (used in Gemini CLI, OpenAI Codex CLI, Claude Code) is strictly weaker than appending-style (sliding-window) management: the former is limited to regular languages while the latter captures all of DSPACE(n). Both are in turn strictly weaker than systems with read/write external memory or (2,2)-restricted decoding, which are Turing-complete. The paper further contrasts its fixed-system formalization against the scaling-family regime adopted by most prior Turing-completeness claims (Dehghani et al., 2019; Pérez et al., 2019; Merrill & Sabharwal, 2024; Li et al., 2024; Giannou et al., 2023, among others), arguing that those results establish resource bounds for a family of models rather than Turing-completeness of a single deployed system. The key takeaway is that the context manager C, not the Transformer T, is the dominant factor determining whether a fixed system is regular, linear-space, or Turing-complete.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the large body of work claiming Transformers are Turing-complete (Dehghani et al., 2019; Pérez et al., 2019; Merrill & Sabharwal, 2024; and others) by arguing those proofs operate in a 'scaling-family' regime where context window or numerical precision grows with input length, rather than the 'fixed-system' regime matching real-world deployment. It builds conceptually on Schuurmans (2023) and Schuurmans et al. (2024), who showed that a fixed Transformer with appropriate context management can achieve Turing-completeness, and draws an analogy to circuit complexity (Savage, 1972) to formalize why scaling a model family is not the same as a single model being universal. The paper is motivated by Akhlaghpour's (2024) informal blog post but provides the first explicit formalization of the fixed-system setting and a systematic taxonomy of how context management determines computational power.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper challenges a widely-cited claim in the NLP/ML community by showing that the standard Turing-completeness proofs do not apply to a single deployed LLM with a fixed context window and fixed precision. The practical implication is that the 'harness' (context management, tool use, external memory) is not a peripheral engineering detail but a first-order determinant of what a fixed model can compute, which should reshape how the community reasons about LLM capabilities. Open questions include: what is the exact computational power of more sophisticated context managers (e.g., retrieval-augmented, multi-agent), whether the learnability gap between Turing-completeness and actual training success can be formally characterized, and whether the constant-space bound for summarization-style management holds under more realistic multi-token summaries.
