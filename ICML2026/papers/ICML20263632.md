## Problem

Existing RL methods for LLM agents model them as flat, single-timescale policies that must propagate credit across entire trajectories under sparse, delayed rewards, leading to unstable optimization and inefficient credit assignment on long-horizon tasks. Although successful agent trajectories implicitly organize into subgoal segments, flat RL neither explicitly represents nor optimizes for this hierarchical structure, resulting in fragile behaviors like premature stage abandonment and repetitive actions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263632/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each turn, the agent receives a textual prompt containing the current environment state observation and the previously active subgoal, and produces a structured three-block autoregressive output: a binary switch decision (KEEP or SWITCH) in <switch> tags, a subgoal description in <subgoal> tags, and a primitive environment action in <action> tags. All three decisions are emitted sequentially by a single LLM, so the correct conditioning (switch conditioned on state and prior subgoal, subgoal conditioned on state, action conditioned on state and current subgoal) is achieved through the model's native autoregressive factorization without separate controllers.

## Method

HiPER factorizes a single autoregressive LLM policy into three coupled decisions—subgoal switching, subgoal generation, and action execution—via a Plan-Execute prompt template that extends ReAct with explicit <switch>/<subgoal>/<action> blocks. The key technical contribution is Hierarchical Advantage Estimation (HAE), which computes GAE-style advantages at two timescales: turn-level TD residuals within each subgoal segment for action credit, and segment-level TD residuals at switching boundaries for subgoal credit, with the critical coupling that the final-step residual in each segment bootstraps to the high-level boundary value rather than a flat state value. A separate switching advantage is derived as a centered policy-gradient signal for the binary KEEP/SWITCH choice. The paper proves HAE is an unbiased gradient estimator (up to standard bootstrapping and critic approximation errors) and achieves provable variance reduction over flat GAE by exploiting the hierarchical structure. Training uses PPO-style clipped surrogates applied independently to each of the three decision components, with a single shared critic backbone and two output heads for the high- and low-level value functions.

## Results

With Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, HiPER achieves 97.4% overall success on ALFWorld and 83.3% success (92.2 score) on WebShop, outperforming the strongest baseline GiGPO by +6.6% and +8.3% respectively. With Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, HiPER reaches 95.3% on ALFWorld and 71.4% on WebShop, exceeding GiGPO by +8.6% and +4.0%. The largest gains appear on multi-subtask ALFWorld categories (Look, Pick2), where flat baselines struggle most. HiPER also achieves ~2.8× sample-efficiency speedup over PPO/GRPO on ALFWorld-7B, reaching 80% success in ~50 steps vs. ~140 steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HiPER outperforms all four baselines (PPO, RLOO, GRPO, GiGPO) on both ALFWorld and WebShop across both model sizes. Against the strongest prior method GiGPO, HiPER gains +6.6% on ALFWorld-7B (97.4% vs. 90.8%) and +8.3% on WebShop-7B (83.3% vs. 75.2%), with the gap widening on multi-subtask categories like Pick2 (+16.3%) and Look (+2.1%). Against standard PPO, the improvement is even larger: +14.6% on ALFWorld-7B and +14.6% on WebShop-7B. GRPO and RLOO, which use group-based or critic-free baselines, trail HiPER by 12–22% on ALFWorld-7B, confirming that flat single-timescale credit assignment is insufficient for long-horizon sparse-reward tasks. The ablation (Table 2) further shows that simply switching baselines to the Plan-Execute prompt without HAE yields only modest gains (+4.4% for GiGPO), indicating that the hierarchical advantage estimation is the primary driver of HiPER's advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

HiPER draws on the classical options framework (Sutton et al., 1999) and its learned-option descendants (Option-Critic, PPOC, DAC) but departs from them by replacing a fixed discrete option inventory with open-vocabulary, LLM-generated subgoals, making the hierarchy fully language-native. On the LLM-agent RL side, it extends the flat single-timescale policy paradigm used by PPO, GRPO, RLOO, and GiGPO by introducing an explicit Plan-Execute interface that factorizes the autoregressive policy into switch/subgoal/action decisions, and by designing a matching two-timescale advantage estimator (HAE) that couples within-segment GAE with boundary-level bootstrapping. The theoretical contribution (unbiasedness and variance reduction relative to flat GAE) is positioned as a principled justification for why this structural decomposition should outperform flat credit assignment under sparse rewards.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 2.8× sample-efficiency gain and the consistent +6–8% SOTA improvements on long-horizon categories (Look, Pick2) suggest that explicit temporal abstraction is a practical lever for scaling RL to truly long-horizon LLM agents, not just a theoretical nicety. However, evaluation is limited to two text-based benchmarks (ALFWorld, WebShop) and a single model family (Qwen2.5 at 1.5B/7B), leaving open whether the hierarchical structure transfers to continuous-control, tool-use, or multi-agent settings. The observation that Plan-Execute prompting *degrades* the untrained base model (8.3%→2.9%) but substantially helps after RL training raises an open question about the interaction between structured prompting and RL optimization that deserves further study. A smaller critic (1.5B) paired with a 7B actor retains most performance, hinting at practical memory savings, but the full ablation space (e.g., λ sensitivity, segment-length distribution, multi-turn tool-use) remains unexplored.
