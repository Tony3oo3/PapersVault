## Problem

Jailbreak techniques for LLMs evolve faster than evaluation benchmarks, and existing frameworks (EasyJailbreak, TeleAI-Safety, OpenRT) rely on manual, paper-specific integration of each new attack. This creates a bottleneck where attacks are integrated weeks or months after publication, fidelity depends on individual engineers' understanding, and cross-paper comparisons are confounded by drift in datasets, harnesses, and judging protocols. The paper addresses the absence of an automated pipeline that can translate a newly published jailbreak paper into a runnable, contract-conforming module and evaluate it under a unified, comparable harness.

## Input / Output

The system consumes a jailbreak paper normalized to markdown (including all appendices with prompts and templates) and, when available, an official reference repository cloned locally to resolve underspecified defaults, control flow, and prompt serialization. It produces a Python module conforming to JBF-LIB's ModernBaseAttack contract with typed AttackParameter declarations, a generated test script, and a measured ASR gap (Δ) against the paper's reported value. Downstream, JBF-EVAL consumes these modules to emit standardized ASR matrices over 10 victim models on AdvBench with a fixed GPT-4o judge, along with structured per-sample traces, cost logs, and cross-model heatmaps.

## Method

JBF is a three-component system: JBF-LIB defines stable attack/defense contracts and reusable LLM utilities (provider-agnostic adapters, caching, ContextVar-based thread-safe state) so each new attack reduces to a small method-centric module (mean 82.5% code reuse); JBF-FORGE is a bounded planner-coder-auditor loop where a Planner (Gemini-3-pro) extracts the algorithm, prompts, and control flow into a structured spec, a Coder (Claude-4.5-sonnet) implements the module under the contract, and an Auditor (GPT-5.1 Codex) performs line-referenced static fidelity checks against the spec with a strict source-of-truth hierarchy (spec ≻ contract ≻ repo), iterating up to T rounds until 100% fidelity is reached; JBF-EVAL fixes the dataset (AdvBench), execution protocol, and GPT-4o judge to produce apples-to-apples cross-model ASR comparisons. An enhanced refinement pass using a long-running Claude Code agent handles the rare cases where matched-setting evaluation shows a >10 pp undershoot, performing deeper code-level gap analysis and tightly scoped patching. Across 30 reproduced attacks, the pipeline achieves a mean ASR deviation of +0.26 pp with a 58% LOC reduction relative to original repositories, completing synthesis in 28.2 minutes on average without human involvement.

## Results

JBF-FORGE reproduces 30 jailbreak attacks with a mean (reproduced − reported) ASR deviation of +0.26 percentage points (mean absolute gap 5.7 pp; range −16.0 to +20.0 pp), with only 2 of 30 attacks showing a negative gap below −10 pp. Integration into JBF-LIB reduces attack-specific code by 58% (compression ratio ρ = 0.42) and achieves an 82.5% mean per-module reused-code ratio. End-to-end synthesis averages 28.2 minutes per attack (median 25.0 min; 82% of runs finish within 60 min). Under the standardized JBF-EVAL harness (AdvBench, GPT-4o judge), cross-model ASR ranges from 9.13% mean (GPT-OSS-120B) to 78.8% mean (GPT-3.5-Turbo), with individual attacks spanning 0–100% on the same victim.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is fidelity: JBF-FORGE's reproduced ASR (ASR_gen) versus each paper's originally reported ASR (ASR_paper). Across 30 attacks, the mean deviation is +0.26 pp with 16 attacks at or above the reported value and 14 below; the two largest under-reproductions are ISA (−16.0 pp) and SCP (−11.8 pp), both triggered the enhanced refinement pass. A with-repo vs. no-repo ablation on five attacks shows that official repositories raise mean ASR from 66.5% to 86.3% (δ̄ = +19.8 pp), with the largest gains on scaffold-heavy methods (GTA +48.6 pp, SATA-MLM +34.8 pp) versus modest gains on template-style methods (EquaCode +5.3 pp, ABJ +5.2 pp). In the standardized cross-model evaluation (30 attacks × 10 victims, AdvBench, GPT-4o judge), no single attack dominates all models: GPT-OSS-120B is the most robust on average (9.13% mean ASR, 15/30 attacks at 0%) yet is bypassed by Mousetrap (82%) and RTS-Attack (51.1%), while GPT-5.1 (29.5% mean) shows sharp mechanism-specific failures (0% on DeepInception/TAP/AIM but 94% on JAIL-CON-CIT). Victim-in-the-loop search is the strongest family overall (60.3% mean ASR) but sampling outperforms it on GPT-5.1 (50.9% vs. 26.8%), and formal carriers are the most effective packaging (66.0% mean) except on GPT-OSS-120B where they are nearly ineffective (2.1% carrier-mean). These results indicate that robustness is highly attack-mechanism-dependent and that no single representative attack or model gives a reliable picture of overall safety.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: jailbreak evaluation frameworks (EasyJailbreak, TeleAI-Safety, OpenRT) that standardize attack/defense execution but require manual per-paper integration, and general-purpose paper-to-code agents (Paper2Code, AutoReproduce, PaperBench, SciReplicate-Bench) that automate reproduction but lack domain-specific contracts for jailbreak evaluation. JBF carves new ground by combining a multi-agent planner-coder-auditor pipeline with a domain-specific shared library (JBF-LIB) that abstracts the common inference-and-judging scaffold, so that each new attack reduces to a small method-centric module. The key departure from prior frameworks is the elimination of the manual integration bottleneck: where EasyJailbreak or JailbreakBench require engineers to read a paper, adapt code to framework contracts, and validate fidelity by hand, JBF-FORGE automates this in ~28 minutes with a bounded audit loop.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is a living benchmark that can ingest new jailbreak papers within hours rather than months, directly addressing the 'static security trap' where robustness estimates go stale as attacks evolve. The cross-model analysis (30 attacks × 10 victims) reveals that model robustness is sharply attack-dependent—GPT-OSS-120B averages 9.1% ASR yet fails at 82% on MOUSETRAP—arguing against single-score robustness metrics. Open questions include extending to white-box and multimodal jailbreaks, auto-evolving the shared library contracts, hardening the pipeline against prompt-injection in papers/repos, and adding defense synthesis for attack-defense interaction heatmaps. A limitation is that the pipeline does not automatically modify JBF-LIB abstractions, so attacks outside existing contracts still need manual engineering.
