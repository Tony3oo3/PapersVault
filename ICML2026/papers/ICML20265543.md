## Problem

In AI-assisted task delegation, accuracy-based payment schemes suffer from an incentive collapse paradox: as AI accuracy improves, sustaining any positive human effort requires payments that diverge to infinity. Prior work (Bastani & Cachon, 2025) identified this for simple linear payments, but it was unclear whether more sophisticated nonlinear rules could avoid the divergence, and no mechanism existed to resolve it while preserving downstream statistical efficiency.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pool of unlabeled instances with AI model predictions f(X), a total budget B, and cost parameters (per-instance overhead w₀, sentinel construction cost k), and jointly produces a sampling policy π(X), an auditing rate ρ, a bonus schedule b(X), a parameter estimate θ̂, and valid confidence intervals. Unlike standard active inference where sampling cost is uniform, here the effective per-instance cost depends on the incentive design (ρ, b(X)), so the budget must be allocated across sampling, sentinel construction, and bonus payments simultaneously. The estimator corrects for both active sampling and effort-dependent label reliability via inverse-probability weights, plus an additional ζ/(1−ρ) factor to remove sentinel-induced bias.

## Method

The paper first proves a general impossibility: any payment rule W = v(Z₁,…,Zₙ,ε) that is coordinatewise increasing in observed accuracy indicators requires E[W] ≥ μ⁻¹(C log(1/p)) to sustain effort e > e_min, so no nonlinear reward schedule escapes the divergence. To overcome this, the authors introduce a sentinel-auditing mechanism where, with probability ρ, the AI is deliberately forced to produce an incorrect output and correct answers earn a bonus b(x); the resulting first-order condition ρμ(b(x))q′(e*) = c′(e*) decouples the marginal return to effort from the AI error probability p, guaranteeing e* ≥ e_min > 0 at finite cost. Building on this, the incentive-aware active estimator θ̂^π = (1/n)Σ[f(Xᵢ) + (Yᵢ − f(Xᵢ))·ξᵢζᵢ/((1−ρ)π(Xᵢ)q(e(Xᵢ)))] is shown to be unbiased and asymptotically normal, with variance minimization reducing to minimizing E[(Y−f(X))²/((1−ρ)π(X)q(e(X)))] subject to the budget constraint. Under standard functional forms (q(e)=e, c(e)=e²/2, risk-neutral utility), closed-form solutions yield π(X) ∝ √τ(X) (matching classical active learning) and an optimal ρ that balances sentinel cost against variance reduction, with the bonus set to b = √w₀/ρ to equalize marginal costs across the two budget components.

## Results

On the Pew ATP Wave 79 post-election survey, the proposed incentive-aware active statistical inference method produces substantially shorter confidence intervals than all baselines under the same budget, while maintaining nominal 95% coverage. The method reduces the required labeling budget by approximately 80% to achieve a fixed confidence interval length compared to the active, uniform, and classical baselines. On the proteomics dataset (odds ratio estimation), the method achieves approximately 70% budget savings relative to all baselines for the same interval width. On the ACS PUMS 2019 California continuous-outcome regression task, the method similarly yields tighter confidence intervals for the age coefficient than all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four methods: the proposed incentive-aware active inference, the active sampling method of Zrnic & Candès (2024), a uniform sampling baseline (related to Angelopoulos et al., 2023), and a classical AI-prediction-only baseline. The proposed method wins on all three datasets (Pew survey, proteomics, ACS PUMS) in terms of confidence interval width at a fixed budget, and equivalently in terms of budget required to achieve a fixed interval width. Against the Zrnic & Candès active baseline, the improvement is most pronounced: roughly 80% budget savings on the Pew data and 70% on the protein data, attributable to the joint optimization of sentinel auditing rate and sampling policy. Against the uniform and classical baselines, the gap is even larger, as those methods ignore both active selection and incentive design. All four methods achieve the nominal 95% empirical coverage, so the comparison is purely in efficiency (interval width or budget). A sensitivity analysis with posterior belief ρ_posterior = 0.8ρ (partial identifiability of sentinel tasks) and a utility misspecification experiment (true utility scaled by k ~ Uniform(0.25, 0.75)) confirm that the proposed method still outperforms all baselines, though with somewhat wider intervals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two recent lines of work: the human-AI contracting paradox formalized by Bastani & Cachon (2025), which shows accuracy-based incentives become prohibitively expensive as AI improves, and the active statistical inference framework of Zrnic & Candès (2024), which adaptively selects instances for labeling to minimize estimation error under budget. The paper generalizes the impossibility result from linear to all accuracy-based payment rules (Theorem 3.2), then introduces a sentinel-auditing mechanism that decouples human effort incentives from AI baseline accuracy. It departs from the active inference literature by making label quality endogenous—determined by strategic agent effort under the payment scheme—rather than assuming exogenous, reliable labels once a task is selected.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The incentive collapse paradox is a fundamental barrier to scaling human-AI collaboration: as AI systems become more accurate, the very organizations that benefit most from AI assistance face the highest cost of maintaining human oversight. The sentinel-auditing mechanism offers a simple, implementable fix, and the joint optimization of auditing rate, bonus schedule, and active sampling under a single budget provides a practical design recipe. Key limitations include the assumption of homogeneous agents with known effort-accuracy mappings, and the idealized setting where annotators cannot perfectly identify sentinel tasks. Open questions include extending to heterogeneous or learning agents, adaptive sentinel construction, and settings where the AI model is not under the principal's control.
