## Problem

In multi-turn RL for LLM agents, binary outcome rewards are too sparse to provide effective supervision over long trajectories, and the quality of both the environment (task difficulty) and the reward model fundamentally governs training effectiveness. Prior work treats the environment and reward model as static, missing the opportunity for them to co-evolve with the policy. The paper asks whether a fully dynamic RL system can jointly optimize the environment, policy, and reward model in a closed loop to amplify learning signals and strengthen the overall system.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264848/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an environment task set (GUI tasks from OSWorld, text-game tasks from AlfWorld, or coding problems from CodeContests), a policy LLM, and a reward LLM as inputs. The policy produces multi-step trajectories (up to 30–60 interaction steps) where each step is a multimodal or text action; the reward model performs m independent binary evaluations per step. Outputs are an optimized policy model, an optimized reward model, and a dynamically adapted task set where individual tasks are rewritten by a language model guided by summarized critic feedback, subject to acceptance criteria that enforce moderate difficulty (accuracy between α_low=0.2 and α_high=0.8).

## Method

RLAnything couples three components in a closed loop. First, the policy is trained with integrated feedback (Eq. 1) that adds the mean step-wise reward from m independent reward-model evaluations to the verifiable outcome reward, then standardizes across trajectories at each step index. Second, the reward model is jointly optimized via consistency feedback (Eq. 2), where each evaluation's reward is the product of the step's overall quality score and the evaluation's own binary label, effectively rewarding agreement between the step-level signal and individual judgments. Third, motivated by Theorems 3.1–3.2 showing that reward precision requires balanced sampling densities (μ = p₊ + p₋ > 1), the environment adapts task difficulty automatically: when policy accuracy falls outside thresholds, summarized error patterns from the reward model's evaluative traces are fed to a language model to rewrite the task harder or easier, with quality control via acceptance checks. This three-way feedback loop means a better reward model yields stronger policy signals, a better policy yields more informative trajectories for reward-model training, and balanced task difficulty improves both.

## Results

RLAnything yields substantial gains across all three settings. On the GUI agent (OSWorld), the full framework (Policy+Reward+Env) improves in-domain policy accuracy from 30.0% to 40.6% (+10.6) and OOD accuracy from 22.4% to 27.4% (+5.0), while also boosting the reward model's process accuracy from 86.0% to 91.3% and outcome accuracy from 55.2% to 60.4%. On the LLM agent (AlfWorld), in-domain policy accuracy jumps from 39.0% to 60.2% (+21.2) and OOD from 44.9% to 63.6% (+18.7). For coding, LiveBench code accuracy improves from 31.1% to 43.2% (+12.1), with unit-test generation accuracy surging from 27.8% to 78.9% (+51.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On OSWorld, the RLAnything-optimized Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking achieves the best performance across all task categories, outperforming both UI-TARS1.5-7B and OpenCUA-7B (Figure 4). Compared to its own base model Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking before optimization, the framework delivers an 8.5% absolute improvement in overall OSWorld accuracy and a 5.0% gain on out-of-distribution tasks. The progressive ablation in Table 1 confirms that each added dynamic component (reward model co-optimization, then environment adaptation) monotonically improves both policy and reward model metrics, with the largest single jump coming from adding environment adaptation (+3.7 in-domain for GUI, +4.6 for LLM agent). Notably, the optimized reward model's step-wise supervision alone even outperforms training with verifiable human-labeled outcome rewards (Figure 5a), suggesting the framework can reduce reliance on hand-written evaluators for environment scaling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLVR paradigm for LLM agents (building on GRPO from DeepSeekMath and generative process reward models from Lightman et al. and Zhang et al.) by proposing a fully closed-loop system where the environment, policy, and reward model are jointly optimized rather than treated as fixed or independently tuned components. Prior work on curriculum learning and adaptive task difficulty (Zeng et al., Xue et al.) adjusts environment tasks to aid policy training, but this paper provides a theoretical justification (Theorems 3.1–3.2) showing that difficulty balancing also improves reward model training, and implements the adaptation automatically via critic feedback from the reward model itself. The key departure from existing agentic RL pipelines is the mutual feedback: the reward model's evaluations guide environment modification, which in turn produces better training data for both the policy and the reward model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical path toward self-evolving LLM agents that do not require human-written evaluator scripts or manually curated task sets, with the notable finding that optimized step-wise reward signals can outperform verifiable outcome supervision from human-authored evaluators. This has direct implications for scaling agent training to open-ended environments where writing evaluation scripts is infeasible. Open questions include whether the closed-loop dynamics remain stable over very long training horizons, how the approach generalizes to truly open-ended (non-templated) environments, and what safety guarantees exist when an agent's training environment is being autonomously modified. The paper also raises deployment concerns around autonomous computer-use capabilities.
