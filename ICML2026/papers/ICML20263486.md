## Problem

Standard multiple-choice evaluation protocols for LLMs introduce exploitable artifacts—option label bias, answer position bias, and few-shot prompt distribution bias—that inflate or destabilize scores without reflecting genuine reasoning. Prior work addressed position and label biases but overlooked how models exploit the distribution of correct answers in few-shot examples, and no lightweight, single-pass protocol existed to suppress these artifacts simultaneously without fine-tuning or logit access.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263486/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The protocol consumes a standard MCQ instance (question, n candidate options, few-shot exemplars) but reformats it with uniform dash labels (–) instead of A/B/C/D and instructs the model to output the full answer text verbatim. The LLM's generated response is parsed via a cascade of regular expressions to extract the final answer sentence, which is then embedded and matched against the candidate options using cosine similarity; the highest-scoring option is the prediction. A fallback extracts the last sentence of the output if all regex patterns fail, and a random option is selected only if no text is produced (observed once across all experiments).

## Method

The proposed Matched-and-Dashed (M&D) protocol combines three design choices in a single forward pass: (1) homogeneous dash labels remove the implicit ordering and semantic associations of letter labels, (2) full-text answer generation eliminates the prediction-mode shortcut of selecting a letter, and (3) a lightweight sentence-similarity model (Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B) with cosine similarity maps the extracted answer to the nearest candidate option, tolerating paraphrase and chain-of-thought reasoning. The paper also introduces NonsenseQA, a synthetic 1,000-question benchmark of random-word MCQs with a uniformly distributed pseudo-golden answer, which serves as a diagnostic tool: under standard evaluation, models exploit few-shot distributions to reach >95% accuracy on meaningless inputs, while under M&D most models drop to near-chance (25%) performance. Ablations confirm that the robustness gains stem from the protocol structure rather than the specific embedding model or similarity function, and the total computational overhead is approximately 3% over standard evaluation.

## Results

Across 13 LLMs and five benchmarks (CSQA, ARC, GPQA, INCLUDE, MMLU-Pro), the proposed M&D protocol achieves a geometric mean variance ratio of 0.33, representing a 3× reduction in accuracy variance under answer-moving attacks compared to standard S&L evaluation, with only minimal mean performance degradation. On the synthetic NonsenseQA diagnostic, M&D drops median accuracy sharply toward the 25% chance level (per-model median reductions of 12.5%–52%, paired Wilcoxon p < 10⁻⁴, Cohen's d = 2.2), whereas S&L allows some models to reach >95% accuracy on meaningless inputs. Per-benchmark geometric mean variance ratios are 0.11 (CSQA), 0.33 (ARC), 0.49 (GPQA), 0.23 (INCLUDE), and 0.99 (MMLU-Pro), with MMLU-Pro's near-unity ratio attributed to strong positional anchoring from its 10-option format. The additional computational overhead is approximately 3% total evaluation time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the standard Select-and-Letter (S&L) protocol, which uses distinct A/B/C/D labels and extracts a single letter from the model output. M&D (Matched-and-Dashed) consistently outperforms S&L in robustness: 11 of 13 models show reduced accuracy variance on ARC, 9 of 13 on GPQA, and the overall geometric mean variance ratio is 0.33 (a 3× reduction). On NonsenseQA, S&L allows explicit-bias models like GPT-OSS to reach >95% accuracy on random-word questions, while M&D suppresses this to ~50% or below, and implicit-bias models like Qwen3-8B drop from ~67% to ~33%. The second baseline is cloze-style evaluation (Robinson & Wingate, ICLR 2023; Zheng et al., ICLR 2024), which computes token-level logit scores for each option. In the ablation study (Figure 7), M&D is more robust to answer-position perturbations than both cloze-style and standard letter-based MCQ evaluation, and this robustness advantage holds across different sentence-similarity models and similarity functions, confirming the gain comes from the protocol design rather than a specific embedding choice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MCQ evaluation bias literature initiated by Zheng et al. (2024) and Zhou et al. (2024), which identified position and label biases, by revealing a previously unexplored dimension: few-shot prompt answer-distribution bias. It adapts the semantic-similarity-based answer matching idea from Risch et al. (2021) and Bulian et al. (2022) to the MCQ setting, replacing the cloze-style extraction used by Zheng et al. with a sentence-embedding similarity step. The key departure is the use of uniform (homogeneous) option labels combined with full-text answer generation in a single forward pass, requiring no fine-tuning, logit access, or multiple evaluation passes—unlike prior debiasing methods that typically need one or more of these.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since MCQ benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, GPQA, CSQA, ARC) are the de facto standard for LLM evaluation, systematic biases in the evaluation protocol itself can distort model rankings and mask true capability differences. The proposed M&D protocol is lightweight (~3% extra compute) and immediately adoptable, making it a practical drop-in replacement. Key open questions remain: the protocol does not mitigate positional bias (which is especially pronounced on MMLU-Pro's 10-option format), multilingual evaluation still uses English-only instructions, and the gap between free-form generation and MCQ selection is not directly quantified. The NonsenseQA diagnostic tool, while simple, provides a reusable instrument for auditing any new model's susceptibility to evaluation artifacts.
