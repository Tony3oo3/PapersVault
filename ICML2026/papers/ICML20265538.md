## Problem

Prior mechanistic analyses of jailbreak attacks treated safety alignment as being broadly suppressed or erased, but the paper identifies a more nuanced picture: successful attacks selectively suppress a small set of early-layer attention heads while mid-layer safety activations persist. The fundamental question of what exactly happens inside the model when a jailbreak succeeds—specifically, which components are causally responsible for bypassing refusal and which sustain robust internal safety signals—remains insufficiently answered.

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired text instructions (harmful requests and their successful jailbreak variants, filtered to ~2–3% of ~10,000 attack samples where the original is refused but the attack succeeds) and reads internal activations at the end-of-instruction (EOI) position across all attention heads, requiring white-box access to model internals. Outputs include a per-head classification into Adversarially Compromised Heads (ACHs), Safety-Aligned Heads (SAHs), and harmful-salient heads, layer-wise activation heatmaps, and a scalar harmful/benign detection score computed via a single forward pass with no gradient computation or model intervention.

## Method

The core framework back-projects a mid-layer refusal direction (extracted via difference-in-means) layer-by-layer through the transpose of each attention head's OV circuit, yielding a head-specific projected direction that quantifies each head's linear contribution to the refusal signal. Activation scores are collected under three input types (benign, harmful, attack) at the EOI position, and a two-stage filtering procedure using kernel density estimation and the overlap coefficient classifies heads: those with low harmful-vs-benign overlap are first-stage candidates, among which high harmful-vs-attack overlap identifies stable harmful-salient heads, while low overlap heads are split into ACHs (suppressed under attack, concentrated in layers 0–3) and SAHs (strengthened under attack, distributed in mid-layers 5–12). Causal validation via ablation shows that injecting a negative feature into just 8 ACHs raises ASR from 0% to 99.5% (Llama-3) or 83.7% (Llama-2), while ablating SAHs substantially weakens mid-layer activation strength, confirming ACH suppression as a sufficient bypass pathway and SAHs as an important source of Robust Harmful Features. Token-level attribution further reveals that attack-template tokens specifically drive ACH suppression (median ≈ −1.5) while simultaneously activating SAHs (median ≈ +1.5), explaining why attacks bypass refusal without erasing internal safety representations. A training-free detector then aggregates standardized activations from a broad set of harmful-vs-benign discriminative components (selected by overlap threshold) to produce a detection score, achieving competitive Macro-F1 across 10 safety-eval benchmarks without any model training.

## Results

On the 10-dataset safety-eval benchmark, the training-free detector achieves a sample-weighted Macro-F1 (W.Avg.) of 0.888 on Llama-3-8B, 0.860 on Qwen-7B, and 0.853 on Gemma-2B, substantially outperforming the VANILLA refusal baseline (0.797, 0.506, 0.470 respectively). On adversarial-attack detection, the detector maintains high accuracy even when explicit refusal is bypassed: Gemma-2B reaches 0.976 Macro-F1 on SALAD-Bench (vs. 0.270 for VANILLA) and Qwen-7B reaches 0.907 on WildJailbreak (vs. 0.483 for VANILLA). Ablation on ACHs shows that intervening on just 8 heads increases ASR from 0% to 95.0% (Llama-3) and 81.6% (Llama-2), while random-head controls yield only 4.0% and 10.2%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On Llama-3-8B, the training-free detector (W.Avg. 0.888) slightly exceeds WildGuard (0.880) and Qwen3Guard (0.877), and substantially outperforms LlamaGuard3 (0.599) and LlamaGuard4 (0.666) by 22–29 points. On adversarial-attack detection, the detector's advantage is most pronounced: on SALAD-Bench, Gemma-2B jumps from 0.270 (VANILLA) to 0.976, exceeding even WildGuard (0.970); on WildJailbreak, Qwen-7B improves from 0.483 to 0.907. The method's key practical advantage is that it requires no training, no gradient computation, and only a single forward pass, yet matches or exceeds dedicated safety classifiers that require extensive supervised training. Performance varies across architectures (Llama-2-7B achieves 0.752 W.Avg., below the dedicated models), suggesting the robust-activation signal is strongest in more recent, well-aligned models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the refusal-direction framework of Arditi et al. (2024) and the OV-circuit back-projection methodology of Zhou et al. (2025) to a new setting: rather than studying safety mechanisms under normal refusal, it asks what happens inside the model when a jailbreak attack *succeeds*. The key conceptual departure is the ACH/SAH taxonomy—prior work (Arditi, Wollschläger, Zhao, Li, Zhou) characterizes which components support refusal, but none distinguishes components that are selectively suppressed by attacks from those that remain robust after attack success. The paper also situates itself within the broader attention-head specialization literature (induction heads, copy suppression) but applies it specifically to adversarial safety failures, and validates its mechanistic findings with a training-free detector benchmarked against dedicated safety models (LlamaGuard, WildGuard, Qwen3Guard).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'bypass rather than break' framing has direct implications for defense design: if attacks exploit a small, identifiable set of heads (8 ACHs), targeted monitoring or hardening of those components could be more effective than broad-based defenses. The training-free detector's competitive performance (0.888 W.Avg. on Llama-3-8B, exceeding WildGuard and Qwen3Guard) demonstrates practical utility, though white-box access limits deployment. Key limitations include reliance on a single refusal direction rather than a full refusal subspace, exclusion of MLP layers, primary experiments on 7–8B models, and the strict paired-sample criterion that limits attack-success data to ~2–3% of attempts. Open questions include whether the ACH/SAH structure persists in larger or less-aligned models, whether MLPs contribute independently to robust activations, and whether richer refusal-subspace analyses would reveal additional head specializations.
