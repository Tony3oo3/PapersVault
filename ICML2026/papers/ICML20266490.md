## Problem

The model collapse literature has been distorted by at least eight inconsistent definitions of 'model collapse' used interchangeably across (and within) papers, combined with unrealistic experimental assumptions—most critically the 'replace' paradigm where all real data is deleted after each training iteration. This has produced an oversimplified catastrophic narrative in media and policy discourse that obscures the more subtle, real threats of tail data loss, mode entanglement, and diversity collapse that are already occurring.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the 'input' is the existing model collapse literature (28 hand-annotated publications) and the 'output' is a structured taxonomy of 8 definitions grouped into three families, a set of 5 realistic conditions for studying model collapse, and a qualitative forecast of which collapse scenarios are plausible under frontier AI lab practices. The paper proposes a 'model collapse profile' framework in which researchers explicitly report which definitions their results address, enabling cross-study comparison that is currently impossible due to definitional ambiguity.

## Method

The paper hand-annotates 28 prior publications to identify 8 distinct definitions of model collapse (population risk increase, diverging risk, variance collapse, scaling law change, mode disappearance, tail data loss, hallucinated data, and any risk increase), then proposes 5 realistic conditions reflecting frontier lab trajectories (increasing compute, data volume, and quality; data accumulation rather than replacement; decreasing real-data proportion) and re-weights the literature accordingly. Key technical analyses include showing that the replace paradigm causes asymptotically diverging population risk while the accumulate paradigm does not (citing Gerstgrasser et al., Kazdan et al., Dey & Donoho), and using absorbing Markov chain theory to derive that expected iterations to total collapse in the discrete distribution model scales as D·H[p⁽⁰⁾], implying trillions of models could be trained before total collapse. The paper demonstrates concrete intra-paper confusion (Shumailov et al. 2023 proving diverging Wasserstein risk via mean drift while defining collapse as tail/mode loss) and inter-paper contradiction (Alemohammad et al. vs. Gerstgrasser et al. reaching opposite conclusions on the same FID curve), and provides a linear regression case study in Appendix A showing how the same setting yields contradictory collapse verdicts across definitions.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that in the discrete Markov-chain model of Shumailov et al. (2023), the expected number of model-fitting iterations before total collapse is proportional to D·H[p⁽⁰⁾] (data size × Shannon entropy), implying that with ~10¹⁵ tokens of real data, total collapse occurs so slowly that trillions of models could be trained before its onset. In the accumulate paradigm for linear regression, the ratio of test error at iteration n to iteration 1 converges to π²/6 ≈ 1.64, confirming bounded (non-diverging) degradation. Under the replace paradigm, by contrast, population risk diverges asymptotically. The paper's headline qualitative forecast is that catastrophic population-risk divergence is unlikely under realistic accumulation conditions, while loss of real tail data and mode entanglement remain genuine, under-studied threats.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not benchmark a proposed method against baselines; instead, it contrasts the conclusions reached by different research groups under different assumptions. The sharpest contrast is between the replace paradigm (Martinez et al. 2023; Alemohammad et al. 2024; Shumailov et al. 2023), where population risk diverges and variance collapses, and the accumulate paradigm (Gerstgrasser et al. 2024; Kazdan et al. 2024b; Dey & Donoho 2024), where population risk remains bounded. The paper also highlights a direct inter-paper disagreement: Alemohammad et al. (2024) declare model collapse when FID doubles by iteration 5 (Definition 2), while Gerstgrasser et al. (2024) reject that characterization because the risk plateaus rather than diverges (Definition 3). Within Shumailov et al. (2023) itself, the paper shows the Gaussian mean-drift result (Definition 3) is mislabeled as the 'early/late collapse' the authors defined (Definitions 6–7), and the language-model finetuning result (Definition 8: hallucinated data) is presented alongside results that would not qualify under Definitions 3, 6, or 7. The authors argue that weighting results by fidelity to realistic conditions (data accumulation, increasing compute, growing datasets) shifts the balance of evidence away from catastrophic collapse and toward subtler tail-data and diversity losses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not introduce a new method but rather synthesizes and repositions the entire model collapse literature by identifying eight distinct and sometimes conflicting definitions across 28 prior publications. It departs from the dominant 'replace' paradigm (where all data is deleted after each model-fitting iteration) used by early alarmist works (Shumailov et al., Alemohammad et al., Bertrand et al.) and instead grounds the analysis in the 'accumulate' paradigm supported by Gerstgrasser et al., Kazdan et al., and Dey & Donoho, which shows population risk does not diverge under data accumulation. The paper also draws on Markov chain theory (Brydges), Wasserstein distance analysis, and scaling law literature to characterize timescales and realistic conditions for frontier pre-training, effectively carving a new meta-analytic space that prior single-definition papers had not occupied.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the widely-amplified narrative (in Nature, Scientific American, WSJ) that synthetic data will render future frontier models useless, arguing that the most catastrophic collapse scenarios rely on assumptions (full data replacement, fixed compute) that do not match industry practice. It redirects research attention toward subtler but more probable threats—loss of real tail data, mode entanglement, and altered scaling laws—that can disproportionately harm marginalized communities and mislead compute budgeting at frontier labs. Key open questions remain: how much tail mass will be eroded under realistic accumulation, how synthetic data shifts scaling curves, and whether the proportion of real data will asymptote to zero. The proposed 'model collapse profile' framework and recommendations for explicit definition reporting could improve cross-study comparability, but the paper offers no new empirical results or mitigation techniques, leaving the practical gap between diagnosis and solution.
