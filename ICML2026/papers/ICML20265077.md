## Problem

Existing translation benchmarks and automatic metrics (BLEU, ChrF, COMET) fail to capture whether social media UGC translations convey intended cultural meaning and evoke appropriate emotional resonance in target-language readers. While prior benchmarks address culture-loaded symbols like slang and memes, they overlook UGC-specific linguistic styles—rhetorical questions, hyperbole, 'planting grass' discourse—that are essential for sustaining cross-lingual interaction on social platforms.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265077/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For translation, the input is a self-contained Chinese UGC note (title + content with structural tags) from one of 14 domains and 4 note types (General, Express, Symbol, Hybrid), with topic tags rendered as placeholders to avoid ambiguous multi-translation; the output is an English UGC translation. For evaluation, the JUDGER takes a source-translation pair and produces a 0–3 cultural effectiveness score, which is then binarized into ineffective (0–1) versus effective (2–3) for large-scale scoring. The benchmark is explicitly scoped to text-evaluable notes, excluding content requiring image/video evidence or thread-level context.

## Method

The paper introduces CULTURE-MT (1,002 notes, 14 domains, 4 types) built via a human-LLM collaborative pipeline: human annotators craft scenario metadata, an LLM performs value-assessment filtering and cultural enrichment, and a second human review ensures plausibility. A new evaluation criterion, cultural effectiveness, is operationalized as an MQM-style rubric with two dimensions—expressive accuracy (semantic/emotional fidelity, unit handling, proper nouns) and cultural adaptability (culture-loaded term handling, discourse fluency, address adaptation)—scored 0–3 by experts. The JUDGER, a Qwen3-32B model fine-tuned via SFT on 30K score-balanced samples (3K expert-annotated + 40K Gemini-3-annotated), achieves 86.03% accuracy and 0.7205 Cohen's κ against human judgments. Translation baselines (Qwen3-8B/32B) are fine-tuned on ~50K instances produced by a cultural-effectiveness-based self-refinement loop: Gemini-3 generates a translation, the JUDGER scores it, and low-scoring samples are iteratively rewritten (up to 3 rounds) before inclusion. Across 15 evaluated models, cultural effectiveness shows a clear scaling trend with model size while BLEU/ChrF/COMET remain nearly flat, and the 8B UGC baseline reaches 28.24% top-rated translations versus 4.09% for its base model.

## Results

On CULTURE-MT (1,002 Chinese-to-English UGC notes), the best-performing model Gemini 3 achieves 9.20% culturally ineffective translations (Ineff.) with an average score of 2.29/3, while the authors' 8B UGC baseline (Ours-8B) reaches 14.47% Ineff. and 28.24% top-rated (score-3) translations—far exceeding its base Qwen3-8B counterpart (49.60% Ineff., 4.09% score-3). Across the Qwen3 series, cultural effectiveness degrades sharply with model size (from 30.74% Ineff. at 32B to 95.41% at 0.6B), a trend invisible to BLEU, ChrF, and COMET, which show only minor variation across scales. On multi-benchmark evaluation (Table 7), Ours-8B and Ours-32B achieve the lowest Ineff. on CULTURE-MT, Seed-X-Challenge, and RedTrans simultaneously, confirming the JUDGER's generalizability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The authors' 8B UGC baseline (Ours-8B, 14.47% Ineff.) substantially outperforms all open-source base LLMs and translation-specialized models: it beats Qwen3-8B base (49.60% Ineff.) by 35 percentage points, Qwen3-32B base (30.74%) by 16 pp, Seed-X-PPO (31.04%) by 17 pp, and Seed-X-Instruct (49.30%) by 35 pp. It also surpasses the much larger Qwen3-235B-A22B (30.24%) and the >100B open-source models DeepSeek V3.2 (16.57%) and GLM 4.6v (16.07%), demonstrating that UGC-specific cultural training is more effective than raw scale for this task. However, Ours-8B still trails the two closed-source leaders—Gemini 3 (9.20% Ineff., 38.30% score-3) and GPT-5 (9.38%, 36.73%)—by roughly 5 pp in Ineff. and 10 pp in top-rated translations, indicating a remaining gap in cultural resonance. Ablation (Table 6) shows the JUDGER-guided rewrite loop contributes an additional ~2 pp Ineff. reduction beyond in-domain SFT alone (e.g., Ours-32B drops from 16.87% to 13.97%). On FLORES, Ours-8B shows only a modest BLEU/COMET decline relative to Qwen3-8B, confirming that cultural-effectiveness training does not significantly degrade general translation quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of social media translation benchmarks (RedTrans-Bench, Seed-X-Challenge, DITING-Corpus) by explicitly modeling both culture-loaded symbols and UGC-specific linguistic styles (rhetorical questions, hyperbole, 'planting grass' discourse) as distinct evaluation axes, rather than treating cultural challenges as a monolithic category. For evaluation, it builds on the MQM-style taxonomy tradition (GEMBA-MQM, AutoMQM, MQM-APE) and the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm (DITING's AgentEval, TransBench's Marco-MOS), but specializes the rubric to UGC-specific cultural resonance and trains a dedicated JUDGER (fine-tuned Qwen3-32B) validated against human experts. The training pipeline for baselines draws on the self-refinement loop (generate → evaluate → rewrite) pattern, using the JUDGER itself as the evaluation signal to curate culturally effective training data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The systematic demonstration across 15 models that BLEU, ChrF, and COMET are nearly flat while cultural effectiveness shows a clear scaling trend provides strong evidence that standard metrics are insufficient for culturally rich UGC translation, motivating the adoption of the proposed criterion. The benchmark is genuinely challenging (even Gemini 3 achieves only 38.30% top-rated translations), and the released JUDGER with 86% accuracy and 0.72 Cohen's Kappa offers a practical, scalable evaluation tool. Key limitations include the Chinese-to-English scope, restriction to self-contained text notes (excluding multimodal and thread-dependent content), and the fact that cultural phenomena are rapidly evolving, requiring ongoing benchmark refresh. Open questions include whether the cultural effectiveness framework generalizes to other language pairs and whether the JUDGER's slight conservative bias (higher recall on ineffective cases) affects leaderboard rankings.
