## Problem

Despite Thompson Sampling's decades of strong empirical performance and solid regret guarantees, the exact mechanism by which posterior sampling balances exploration and exploitation has remained unexplained. Unlike UCB, EXP3, or IDS, Thompson Sampling performs no explicit optimization during execution, leaving its design principles opaque. The paper asks: what objective is Thompson Sampling actually optimizing, and can this be made explicit to enable principled improvement?

## Input / Output

The method operates on the belief state — a posterior distribution over arm parameters (e.g., a pair of Beta distributions in the Bernoulli setting) — rather than raw reward data. The output is a pulling probability vector q_t derived from minimizing an online objective, along with a scalar regularizer ν(π_t) that quantifies either remaining uncertainty (for Thompson Sampling, via point-biserial covariance) or exploration-exploitation tension (for the R²-optimal policy, via a ratio of conditional future value differences). The decision variable x_t is the expected next-round reward, constrained between the two arms' posterior means.

## Method

The paper introduces a time-invariant squared regret objective R²(Q;π₀) = E[Σ r²(q_t;π_t)] that summarizes cumulative regret across all horizons, yielding a Bellman equation whose solution defines the R²-optimal policy. The central result (Theorem 4.2) shows that Thompson Sampling's posterior sampling rule is exactly equivalent to minimizing (E[max(θ₁,θ₂)] − x_t)² + ν^TS(π_t)·x_t, where ν^TS is the covariance between the reward gap Δ and the optimal-arm indicator Λ (a point-biserial correlation). This reveals that TS regularizes greediness by an uncertainty measure, whereas the Bellman-optimal policy regularizes by a tension measure that accounts for future value differences. The gap between these two regularizers explains TS's suboptimality: TS over-explores when the leading arm is more uncertain than the runner-up, and under-explores when the cost of exploration vanishes. The paper provides a closed-form R²-optimal policy for the one-armed case (with a phase transition at signal-to-noise ratio ≈ −0.276), an approximate two-armed implementation via backward recursion on the Beta lattice, and proposes a 'simple fix' (a shutdown switch that zeros the regularizer when the higher-reward arm also yields more information) that reduces cumulative regret by ~30%.

## Results

In the two-armed Bernoulli bandit setting, the R²-optimal (Bellman-optimal) policy achieves a 30% reduction in cumulative regret relative to Thompson Sampling after 20 rounds (Figure 3, 200K trials). In the one-armed Gaussian case, the R²-optimal policy's regularizer diverges (encouraging pure exploration) when the posterior mean of the unknown arm approaches zero, while Thompson Sampling's regularizer grows only gradually, revealing a systematic under-exploration. The biserial covariance regularizer of Thompson Sampling correlates with credible-interval overlap at a Pearson correlation of 0.995, confirming it as a faithful quantitative uncertainty measure. The R²-optimal policy exhibits a sharp phase transition at signal-to-noise ratio μ/σ = −0.276, below which it pulls the unknown arm with probability one.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is Thompson Sampling (Q^TS) against the R²-optimal Bellman policy (Q^R2), which serves as the theoretical benchmark. The R²-optimal policy consistently outperforms Thompson Sampling: in the one-armed Gaussian case it achieves substantially lower cumulative regret (Figure 2, 20K trials), and in the two-armed Bernoulli case it achieves a 30% regret reduction after 20 rounds (Figure 3, 200K trials). The gap is attributed to the regularizer: ν^TS measures remaining uncertainty about which arm is better, while ν^R2 measures the tension between exploration and exploitation, causing TS to over-explore when the leading arm is more uncertain than the runner-up. The proposed 'simple fix' (Q^FIX), which shuts down regularization when the higher-reward arm also yields more information, narrows the gap and reduces cumulative regret in the over-explored regime (Figure 4, 20K trials). Information-Directed Sampling (IDS) is noted as structurally closer to the R²-optimal benchmark (its regularizer is also a tension measure scaled by the information ratio), which may explain its empirically superior performance over Thompson Sampling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the MDP formulation of Bayesian bandits introduced by Gittins (1979) and the original posterior-sampling rule of Thompson (1933), but departs from Gittins' discounted-reward objective by introducing a squared-regret formulation that is more aligned with long-term regret minimization. It positions Thompson Sampling alongside UCB, EXP3, and IDS as algorithms with transparent optimization principles, but shows that TS's optimization structure was previously hidden: its regularizer is a point-biserial covariance (an uncertainty measure) rather than the tension measure that characterizes the Bellman-optimal policy. The paper also connects to the information-ratio analysis of Russo & Van Roy (2016) to establish R²-finiteness, and contrasts with IDS's information-gain-based regularizer to explain why IDS outperforms TS.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first variational characterization of Thompson Sampling, transforming it from a heuristic into an online optimization algorithm with a well-defined benchmark (the R²-optimal policy) for principled improvement. The identification of the gap between uncertainty-based and tension-based regularization explains a concrete source of TS's suboptimality and yields a practical 'simple fix' that reduces regret by ~30% in the two-armed Bernoulli setting. Open questions include extending the closed-form and approximate Bellman solutions to K-armed and non-Bernoulli settings, and developing the deferred 'principled fix' (policy iteration) into a scalable algorithm. The framework also raises the broader question of whether other seemingly non-optimization heuristics in sequential decision-making admit similar hidden variational forms.
