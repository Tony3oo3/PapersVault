## Problem

Evaluating fine-grained uncertainty estimation in long-form LLM generation is hampered by the lack of deterministic ground truth: existing benchmarks rely on noisy LLM-based judges or proxy metrics for correctness labeling, which introduces label noise that systematically distorts calibration and ranking metrics. This makes it impossible to reliably assess whether a model's confidence signals actually separate correct from incorrect units at the resolution needed for selective generation and error localization.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes text prompts for six procedurally generated tasks (matrix multiplication, Kronecker product, code execution, DNA translation, first-order logic, multi-needle) and produces long-form fenced text outputs. Each generation is parsed into atomic units (smallest standalone semantic components, e.g., a single matrix element or nucleotide) and line units (larger groupings, e.g., a matrix row), then aligned by index against the deterministic ground truth for binary correctness labeling via exact string matching. Confidence scores are computed from token-level signals (log-probabilities, perplexity, entropy) aggregated to the unit level, and evaluated for calibration (ECE) and ranking (AUROC) at both granularities.

## Method

SALT is a zero-noise benchmark where each task instance is algorithmically composed so the complete long-form output has a single deterministic ground truth known a priori, eliminating the need for human or LLM-based judges. A two-stage parsing pipeline extracts the fenced final answer via regex and segments it into units using task-specific delimiters, enabling index-aligned binary correctness labeling. The paper evaluates 50+ LLMs across multiple confidence functions (logits-based: perplexity, entropy, logprobs sum, max probability; and verbalized-based) and post-hoc calibration methods (Z-score+sigmoid, min-max, binned), comparing their performance on ECE and AUROC at atomic and line resolutions. A key analytical contribution is a controlled counterfactual intervention on the model's own answer context—corrupting or fixing prefix atoms and regenerating the target—to causally disentangle the effects of global prefix correctness, local (last-10) correctness, and answer-context length on future correctness, using cubic B-spline regressions with paired-difference outcomes.

## Results

Across 50+ LLMs evaluated on SALT (54,797 atoms over 1,748 samples), the paper identifies a sharp high-resolution ranking gap: Micro-AUROC at the atomic level clusters between 0.45 and 0.7 (near random), while line-level Macro-AUROC is substantially higher (p=7.5×10⁻⁹). Reasoning mechanisms (CoT prompting or specialized training) produce a systematic trade-off: +78.2% relative Precision gain and +60.8% ECE improvement, but −23.3% AUROC degradation. Controlled prefix-correctness interventions show that global prefix correctness carries roughly 2× the residual effect of local (last-10) correctness on future atom accuracy, and 95% of length-related degradation saturates by ~66.6 atoms. SALT's precision rankings correlate strongly with established benchmarks (Spearman 0.84 with MMLU-Pro, 0.85 with GPQA-Diamond, 0.73 with LLM-Arena).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Rather than comparing a single method against external baselines, SALT serves as a zero-noise evaluation substrate across which multiple uncertainty-estimation dimensions are compared. On confidence functions, logits-based signals (e.g., Max Probability, Logprobs Sum) consistently and significantly outperform verbalized confidence (p<0.05, Wilcoxon signed-rank), with Max Probability best for calibration and Logprobs Sum best for AUROC ranking. On calibration methods, binned calibration minimizes ECE at both atomic and line resolutions but degrades AUROC, revealing a calibration–ranking trade-off. On granularity, line-unit Macro-AUROC is significantly more informative than atomic-unit AUROC (p=7.5×10⁻⁹), while ECE is mildly better at the atomic level (p=3.06×10⁻⁶). On reasoning, paired Instruct-vs-Reasoning model comparisons show that reasoning improves precision and calibration but systematically degrades confidence ranking, with the effect scaling with reasoning intensity (CoT < specialized reasoning models). Transferability to AIME is moderate at line-level (Spearman 0.68 AUROC, 0.74 ECE) but weak at atomic level, while MMLU-Pro agreement remains poor overall.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM uncertainty estimation literature from short-form and multiple-choice settings (where correctness is unambiguous) to long-form generation, and from noisy LLM-judge-based evaluation (FactScore, LUQ, CLUE, UNCLE) to zero-noise deterministic ground truth. The unit-level ranking metric is derived from the general ranking risk framework of Galil et al. (2023), and the confidence function taxonomy (logits-based vs. verbalized) follows the survey by Geng et al. (2024). The key conceptual departure is eliminating the decomposition-and-verification pipeline entirely: by constructing tasks with single deterministic outputs, SALT removes the label noise that prior work (Santilli et al., 2025) showed can systematically distort AUROC rankings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The reasoning trade-off finding—that CoT prompting and specialized reasoning training improve precision by ~78% while degrading confidence ranking by ~23%—has direct implications for deploying reasoning models in risk-critical settings where selective generation and error identification are essential. The atomic-level ranking failure (AUROC 0.45–0.7, near random) reveals that current confidence functions are fundamentally inadequate for the finest-grained units, motivating new function design. Open questions include whether RL post-training can mitigate the reasoning-ranking trade-off, whether confidence functions can be trained specifically for atomic-level separability, and whether the path-dependent correctness dynamics (global prefix correctness dominating over local) generalize beyond the controlled DNA-translation intervention setting.
