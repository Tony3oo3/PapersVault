## Problem

Gaussian process priors are typically specified by handcrafting a kernel from a small set of standard parametric families (RBF, Matérn, periodic), which requires domain expertise, limits adaptivity to the data, and imposes rigid stationarity assumptions that fail for extrapolation tasks like learning curve prediction and time series forecasting. No principled, non-parametric framework existed for directly estimating the GP prior mean and covariance from a corpus of independent historical realizations of the underlying stochastic process.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a corpus of S independent historical datasets, each with its own (potentially sparse and irregularly located) observation set {X_i, y_i}, and produces a full GP prior specified by a mean function m(x) and covariance function k(x,x'). At prediction time, given new query points x*, it outputs a predictive mean and variance. A key design constraint is that historical datasets need not share observation locations or be aligned to a common grid; the method handles spatially heterogeneous inputs by projecting them onto a shared latent reference set Z of M anchor points via kernel interpolation weights.

## Method

Empirical GPs estimate the prior mean and covariance non-parametrically from sample statistics of historical realizations, with a theoretical guarantee that the resulting GP converges (in KL divergence) to the best Gaussian approximation of the true data-generating process. For sparse, irregularly distributed observations, the paper derives a closed-form EM algorithm: the E-step computes the posterior of latent reference values u_i = f(Z) given each task's partial observations via kernel-based probabilistic projection, and the M-step aggregates sufficient statistics to update the shared prior mean μ and covariance Σ on Z. To prevent variance starvation (predictive variance collapsing to the noise floor far from Z), the learned parameters are interpreted as non-parametric residual shifts δ_μ and δ_Σ added to a base parametric kernel k_base, so that predictions gracefully revert to the base prior in unexplored regions. An SVD compression of the centered observation matrix reduces per-kernel-evaluation cost from O(S) to O(M), and the residual formulation modifies the E-step noise covariance to Q_i = (K_{X_i X_i} − W_i K_{ZZ} W_i^T) + σ²I for consistency during training.

## Results

On the GIFT-Eval time series forecasting benchmark (97 datasets, 7 domains), Empirical GP achieves an overall average CRPS rank of 7.56±0.42, the best among all statistical models and outperforming 4 of 8 deep learning baselines (DeepAR, N-BEATS, DLinear, Crossformer). On LCBench learning curve extrapolation, Empirical GP achieves rank 1.00±0.00 in RMSE at 10% observed, clearly dominating Power Law, LC-PFN, and Last Observed baselines. On the 7D hyperparameter modeling task, Empirical GP yields the lowest NLL at every training size (e.g., −0.039 vs. 0.628 for HyperBO at n_train=5), with meta-prior training completing in 9.3 seconds on CPU. Qualitatively, on Mauna Loa CO2 data the Empirical GP achieves 21.52% lower RMSE than an expert handcrafted kernel.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On GIFT-Eval, Empirical GP (overall rank 7.56) outperforms all statistical baselines: Auto Arima (8.62), Seasonal Naive (9.68), Auto Theta (10.52), Spectral Mixture GP (10.87), Auto ETS (10.90), Gaussian RBF GP (12.36), and Naive (13.09). Among deep learning models, it beats DeepAR (7.66, marginally), N-BEATS (8.22), DLinear (9.23), and Crossformer (9.42), but is outperformed by TiDE (7.11), iTransformer (3.77), TFT (3.87), and PatchTST (3.13). On LCBench learning curve extrapolation, Empirical GP dominates Power Law (rank 3.94–4.00), LC-PFN (rank 1.97–2.89), and Last Observed (rank 1.54–3.03) across all observation fractions, with the largest gap at 10% observed where it achieves a perfect rank of 1.00. In the 7D hyperparameter task, Empirical GP achieves the lowest NLL at every training size, beating HyperBO (e.g., −0.039 vs. 0.628 at n_train=5), Pretrained GP (0.458), and Vanilla GP (0.651). Against meta-learning baselines on 1D LCBench, Empirical GP wins 60 datasets versus 39 for HyperBO and 18 for PACOH-SVGD, maintains the lowest mean CRPS (2.5359 vs. 2.5263 for HyperBO on RMSE), and runs 100× faster than PACOH-SVGD (9.3 s vs. 1916 s for meta-training).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the hierarchical Bayesian framework of Schwaighofer et al. (2004) for non-parametric GP covariance learning in two critical ways: supporting spatially heterogeneous observations without grid alignment via a probabilistic interpolation mechanism, and resolving variance starvation through a residual interpolation scheme that gracefully reverts to a base kernel off-grid. It departs from the dominant paradigm of learning parametric kernel hyperparameters (RBF, Matérn, spectral mixture) or neural-network-parameterized kernels (HyperBO, deep kernel learning) by directly estimating the GP prior mean and covariance as empirical statistics from a corpus of independent datasets. The connection to variational inducing-point methods (Titsias, 2009) is acknowledged but distinguished: here the inducing-point-like construction defines the prior rather than approximating a posterior under a fixed prior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The KL-divergence convergence guarantee (Proposition 2) provides a clean theoretical justification for using empirical sample statistics as GP priors, positioning Empirical GPs as the best Gaussian approximation to any data-generating process. Practically, the closed-form EM updates eliminate gradient-based optimization entirely, yielding 100× speedups over particle-based meta-learning methods while remaining competitive with deep learning baselines on large-scale time series forecasting. Key limitations include the requirement for sufficient historical realizations (the empirical covariance is high-variance and potentially singular with few samples), restriction to Gaussian structure (heavy-tailed or non-Gaussian processes are out of scope), and the dense interpolation variant's dependence on a reference grid that limits it to low-dimensional inputs. Open questions include adaptive selection of reference points (k-means, Type-II MLE) and whether the approach can be extended to non-Gaussian process families.
