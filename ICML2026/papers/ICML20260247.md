## Problem

Reinforcement learning for active flow control (AFC) is fragmented across heterogeneous observation schemes, actuation setups, and evaluation protocols, making it difficult to establish a clear state of the art. Existing AFC benchmarks rely on external CFD solvers (OpenFOAM, Fluent, FEniCS), lack full differentiability, and provide limited 3D and multi-agent support, creating brittle software stacks that hinder systematic algorithm comparison and prevent end-to-end gradient-based control methods.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260247/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Policies consume partial observations from virtual sensors (velocity components and temperature at discrete locations) under a POMDP formulation, with the observation window varying by environment (e.g., 302 sensors for cylinder, 221,184 for 3D RBC). Actions are scalar values in [-1,1] that are internally mapped to physical actuation (jet velocities, heater temperatures, wall-normal blowing/suction) via a control function Γ that applies normalization, clipping, and spatial smoothing. In MARL settings, each agent receives only a local observation window around its assigned actuator, and individual rewards combine local and global domain properties with a tunable weight β.

## Method

FluidGym is a standalone, fully differentiable benchmark suite built entirely in PyTorch on top of the GPU-accelerated PICT solver (a differentiable multi-block PISO solver using finite volume discretization with implicit Euler time-stepping). It provides 13 environments across four flow classes (cylinder drag reduction, Rayleigh-Bénard convection, airfoil aerodynamic efficiency, turbulent channel flow) in both 2D and 3D with three difficulty levels parameterized by Re, Ra, or Re_τ. The framework natively supports single-agent RL (Gymnasium/Stable-Baselines3), multi-agent RL (PettingZoo with shared or per-agent policies), and gradient-based control (DPC, TD-MPC) through a unified FluidEnv abstraction that exposes standardized observation, action, and reward interfaces. A standardized evaluation protocol uses three splits (train/val/test) of ten randomly generated initial domains each, with random perturbations on reset for reproducibility. The paper evaluates PPO, SAC, DPC, and TD-MPC across all environments with five random seeds (over 25k GPU hours), demonstrating that SAC outperforms PPO across difficulty levels, DPC achieves 1–2 order-of-magnitude speedups on easy tasks via reward gradients, and 2D→3D policy transfer is robust.

## Results

Across all FluidGym environment classes and difficulty levels, SAC achieves the highest min-max normalized test-set relative improvement over the uncontrolled baseflow, while MA-PPO performs slightly better specifically on turbulent channel flow (TCF) environments. On CylinderJet2D-easy-v0, DPC achieves approximately one and two orders of magnitude training speedup over PPO and SAC respectively, reaching a drag reduction of ~7.2% (vs. ~8% for SAC). In the TCF environments, opposition control yields 20% drag reduction while RL policies reach 30%, and policies trained in the small channel domain transfer to the large domain at a level comparable to opposition control, substantially outperforming policies trained directly in the large domain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SAC consistently outperforms PPO across all difficulty levels and environment classes in normalized relative improvement over baseflow, attributed to PPO's slower convergence; however, in the multi-agent setting (MA-PPO vs. MA-SAC), the gap narrows substantially, likely because PPO benefits from increased sample counts that reduce SAC's usual sample-efficiency advantage. DPC, leveraging end-to-end differentiability, achieves ~1×10¹ and ~1×10² training speedups over PPO and SAC on CylinderJet2D-easy-v0, and remains competitive with TD-MPC and SAC on the more turbulent RBC2D-hard-v0 while clearly outperforming PPO. TD-MPC exhibits the lowest wall-clock training time among standard RL algorithms but is evaluated only on a subset of environments. For TCF, the opposition control baseline (20% drag reduction) is matched by policies transferred from the small channel domain, which substantially outperform policies trained directly in the large domain, demonstrating spatially transferable MARL strategies. In the 2D→3D cylinder transfer experiment, the transferred 2D policy outperforms both 3D-trained PPO and MA-SAC on easy and hard difficulty levels, and is on par with them on medium difficulty.

## Relation to Prior Work

FluidGym builds on the growing body of RL-for-AFC work (Rabault et al. 2019, Guastoni et al. 2023, Vasanth et al. 2024) and the line of AFC benchmarks (DRLinFluids, drlFoam, Beacon, HydroGym), but departs from all of them by eliminating external CFD solver dependencies entirely. It is built on the PICT GPU-accelerated differentiable PISO solver (Franz et al. 2026), which enables end-to-end differentiability through the simulation — a capability absent from all prior AFC benchmarks. The multi-agent design draws on spatially distributed control ideas from Vasanth et al. (2024) and Peitz et al. (2024), while the gradient-based DPC evaluation connects to the differentiable predictive control literature (Drgoña et al. 2022) and recent differentiable RL methods (Xing et al. 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FluidGym addresses a critical reproducibility and comparability gap in AFC research by providing standardized train/val/test splits, consistent reward definitions, and 25k+ GPU hours of publicly released baseline results across 13 environments in 2D and 3D. The fully differentiable, PyTorch-native design lowers the barrier to entry for RL researchers without CFD expertise and opens the door to gradient-based and differentiable RL methods that were previously infeasible on AFC benchmarks. Key limitations include CUDA-only execution, limited random seeds (5), and DPC evaluated on only two environments; future work targets MHD flows, more geometries, and systematic differentiable RL comparisons. The finding that policies transfer robustly across dimensionalities (2D→3D) and domain sizes (small→large TCF) suggests MARL can learn spatially invariant control strategies, a promising direction for scaling AFC to larger, more complex configurations.
