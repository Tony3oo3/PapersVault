## Problem

Sampling from unnormalized densities (e.g., Boltzmann distributions) is a core problem in computational chemistry and physics, but existing diffusion-based samplers rely on Monte Carlo corrections—importance weighting, resampling, or auxiliary MCMC—that multiply the number of costly energy function evaluations. Additionally, prior diffusion samplers are restricted to Euclidean space and cannot operate on Riemannian manifolds such as hyperspheres or hyperbolic spaces, limiting their applicability to directional and rotational data.

## Input / Output

The method is data-free: it requires only the unnormalized log-density r(x) and its gradient ∇r(x), plus a simple source distribution (typically Gaussian). For molecular conformer generation, the input additionally includes a molecular graph topology g (atom types and bonds) that conditions the drift network, and the output is atomic coordinates x ∈ R^{N×3}. On Riemannian manifolds, the output is a point on the manifold (e.g., a unit vector on S²), with all operations—interpolation, drift evaluation, and Euler-Maruyama stepping—performed in the ambient space using geodesic maps and parallel transport rather than reparameterizations.

## Method

Flow Sampling inverts the standard flow-matching supervision: instead of conditioning on a data sample X₁ and regressing onto a noising drift, it conditions on a noise sample X₀ and regresses onto a denoising drift u_{t|0} constructed from the energy function. A key closed-form result (Proposition 3.2) shows that along the linear interpolant X_t = (1−t)X₀ + tX₁, the conditional drift reduces to u_{t|0} = X₁ − X₀ + γ∇r(X₁), so the energy gradient is evaluated only once per endpoint sample and reused for all t ∈ [0,1] via a replay buffer. Training proceeds as a fixed-point iteration: an exploration phase simulates the detached model to produce X₁ samples and their gradients, and an optimization phase performs multiple gradient steps on the MSE regression loss against the closed-form target. The Riemannian extension replaces the affine interpolant with a geodesic interpolant and derives a closed-form conditional drift on constant-curvature manifolds by decomposing the geodesic Jacobian into tangential and normal Jacobi fields (Proposition 4.1), yielding rank-1 operators for parallel transport and projection that keep computation efficient.

## Results

On synthetic energy benchmarks, Flow Sampling achieves the lowest geometric W₂ and energy W₂ across all three systems: DW-4 (W₂ = 0.36, E(·)W₂ = 0.11), LJ-13 (W₂ = 1.58, E(·)W₂ = 0.97), and LJ-55 (W₂ = 3.98, E(·)W₂ = 21.32), outperforming the prior best ASBS by 16–24% on energy W₂. On the large-scale amortized conformer generation benchmark (SPICE, 256 NFE), Flow Sampling achieves 91.89% coverage recall and 0.86 Å AMR, surpassing ASBS (89.66%, 0.86 Å) and AS (88.60%, 0.87 Å). Notably, Flow Sampling maintains consistent performance when reducing training NFE from 512 to 64 (an 8× reduction in exploration cost), while ASBS and AS degrade sharply or fail entirely at low NFE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Flow Sampling outperforms all five learned-sampler baselines on every metric across all three synthetic energy systems (Table 1). Against the closest competitor ASBS (Liu et al., 2025), Flow Sampling reduces energy W₂ by 51% on LJ-13 (0.97 vs. 1.99) and by 24% on LJ-55 (21.32 vs. 28.10), while also achieving the best geometric W₂ on all systems. The older stochastic-control baselines PIS (Zhang & Chen, 2022) and DDS (Vargas et al., 2023) are substantially worse, with energy W₂ values 2–10× higher on LJ-55. iDEM (Akhound-Sadegh et al., 2024) shows high variance (±24.46 on LJ-13 energy W₂) and is outperformed by Flow Sampling on all metrics. On the large-scale conformer generation benchmark (Table 3), Flow Sampling at 256 NFE achieves 91.89% coverage recall on SPICE, beating ASBS (89.66%) and AS (88.60%), while RDKit ETKDG (a non-learned baseline) trails at 72.74%. A key practical advantage is robustness to reduced NFE: Flow Sampling degrades gracefully from 256→64 NFE (91.89%→90.13% recall), whereas ASBS fails catastrophically on Ala2 at NFE≤128 (energy W₂ jumps to 10⁷) and AS drops to 29% recall at NFE 64 on SPICE.

## Relation to Prior Work

Flow Sampling builds directly on the flow matching and stochastic interpolant framework (Lipman et al., 2023; Albergo et al., 2023), but inverts the conditioning: rather than conditioning on a data sample and regressing onto a noising drift (as in standard diffusion/flow matching), it conditions on a noise sample and regresses onto a denoising drift constructed from the energy function. This departs from the stochastic optimal control / Schrödinger bridge lineage (Zhang & Chen, 2022; Vargas et al., 2023; Havens et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a), which requires auxiliary corrector networks or complex bridge characterizations, by yielding a simpler regression target that avoids importance weighting, resampling, or auxiliary MCMC. The Riemannian extension draws on Riemannian flow matching (Chen & Lipman, 2023) for the geodesic interpolant but goes further by deriving closed-form conditional drifts on constant-curvature manifolds via Jacobi field analysis. Appendix D establishes that under a Brownian-bridge supervising path, the Flow Sampling target is a zero-mean control variate of the Adjoint Sampling target, unifying the two approaches at the level of conditional regression.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 4–8× reduction in simulation training cost for large-scale amortized conformer generation is practically significant for computational chemistry, where energy evaluations dominate runtime and scaling to larger molecules or more diverse chemical space is a key bottleneck. The Riemannian extension to constant-curvature manifolds (hyperspheres, hyperbolic spaces) is the first diffusion-based sampler to operate directly on such geometries without reparameterization, opening the door to sampling directional, rotational, and hyperbolic data. Key limitations include the lack of convergence guarantees for the fixed-point replay-buffer training loop and the restriction to constant-curvature manifolds; extending the closed-form drift to general Riemannian geometries or constrained spaces remains open. The robustness at low NFE (64) compared to baselines that degrade or fail suggests the method may scale more favorably to larger models and datasets, but this has not yet been tested at substantially larger scale.
