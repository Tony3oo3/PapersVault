## Problem

Automated incentive design—choosing game rules to achieve desirable equilibrium outcomes—is a mathematical program with equilibrium constraints (MPEC) that is computationally hard, non-unique, and unstable when using Nash equilibria. Existing gradient-based MPEC solvers assume a unique Nash equilibrium and solve one instance at a time, while differentiable economics targets Bayesian games where truthfulness is the key constraint. This paper addresses the gap of learning a single design policy that generalizes across an entire distribution of game contexts, using correlated/coarse correlated equilibria (which form convex polytopes) to obtain a unique, differentiable equilibrium selection.

## Input / Output

The mechanism generator takes a task-specific context ω as input: for contract design, agent costs, transition probabilities, and principal payoffs (broadcast to shape [B,N,A₁,…,A_N,O,3]); for inverse equilibrium, a target joint distribution plus a noise vector (shape [B,2,A₁,…,A_N]); for machine scheduling, the utility matrix derived from job completion times (shape [B,N,A₁,…,A_N,1]). The generator outputs perturbed payoffs of the same shape as the input game, which are passed to a frozen DEB to produce the ε-ME-Eql σ*. Networks are trained to handle all game sizes from 2×2 to 16×16 simultaneously via equivariant masking, with a single set of weights covering the full range.

## Method

The DID framework composes a game-theoretically equivariant neural network (the mechanism generator) with a pretrained differentiable equilibrium block (DEB) that computes the unique maximum-entropy ε-(coarse) correlated equilibrium. The key insight is that selecting the maximum-entropy point from the convex equilibrium polytope yields a locally Lipschitz-continuous, almost-everywhere differentiable map, enabling backpropagation through the equilibrium to train the generator's weights θ. The equivariant architecture uses shared-weight linear layers preceded by pooling functions (meanvar aggregation) over player and action dimensions, so the parameter count is independent of N or |A_p|, and the same network handles all game shapes. Training uses a standard supervised pipeline: sample contexts, forward through generator → DEB → loss, and backpropagate through the frozen DEB to update only the generator. This reduces the MPEC to a standard optimization problem amenable to the full ML toolkit, with the added benefit that the trained network escapes local minima that plague per-instance gradient-based solvers.

## Results

On the inverse equilibrium problem, DID achieves an average KL divergence of 0.056 (CE) and 0.043 (CCE) between the generated game's equilibrium and the target joint, dramatically outperforming the naive uniform baseline (0.400). For multi-agent contract design, the learned contracts yield an average principal utility improvement of 0.25 (CE) and 0.33 (CCE) over no intervention, with 79% and 83% of interventions being non-harmful. In machine scheduling, DID reduces the expected makespan by 0.339 (CE) and 0.211 (CCE) relative to the no-tax baseline, with 88% and 99% non-harmful interventions respectively. Across all tasks, local polishing (Nelder-Mead) improves results by roughly a factor of 2, and evaluating at the exact ECOS solver rather than the DEB reduces performance, indicating the mechanism generator partially exploits DEB inaccuracies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against other incentive design methods, as the authors note no existing implementable algorithms exist for the multi-agent contract design case. The primary quantitative baseline is the naive uniform joint (UNI = 0.400 KL divergence) on the inverse equilibrium task, which DID outperforms by roughly 8–9×. A second comparison axis is the DEB (approximate) versus ECOS (exact) equilibrium evaluation: DID evaluated at the DEB consistently outperforms the same solution evaluated at ECOS (e.g., 0.043 vs. 0.071 KL for CCE), revealing that the mechanism generator partially exploits the DEB's approximation errors. A third comparison is DID versus DID+Local polishing (Nelder-Mead with 4,000 function evaluations), which shows the neural network solution is within roughly a factor of 2 of the local optimum in most settings. For contract design and machine scheduling, the implicit baseline is no intervention (σ⁰), and DID produces non-harmful interventions in 79–99% of sampled contexts depending on the task and equilibrium concept.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the neural equilibrium solver line of work (Marris et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2024), which trained equivariant networks to compute the unique maximum-entropy (coarse) correlated equilibrium of normal-form games. The key conceptual departure is from prior gradient-based MPEC solvers (Li et al., 2020; Fiez et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022), which rely on Nash equilibrium uniqueness assumptions and expensive implicit differentiation or iterative unrolling. By switching to the convex ε-(coarse) correlated equilibrium polytope and backpropagating through a frozen pretrained DEB, the authors reduce the MPEC to a standard supervised learning problem with a 'mechanism generator' that generalizes across contexts rather than solving one instance at a time. This also distinguishes the work from differentiable economics (Dutting et al., 2024), which operates on Bayesian games where the revelation principle eliminates the need to differentiate through equilibria.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DID provides the first unified, scalable pipeline for solving a broad class of incentive design problems (contract design, machine scheduling, inverse equilibrium) with a single equivariant network spanning game sizes from 2×2 to 16×16, unlocking the full ML toolkit (batching, regularization, equivariant inductive biases) for a problem previously tackled with bespoke optimization per instance. A notable limitation is the performance gap when evaluating under the exact ECOS solver versus the DEB (e.g., contract design CE drops from 0.25 to 0.05), suggesting the generator partially exploits DEB approximation errors; co-training the DEB with generated games is identified as a remedy. Open questions include scaling beyond two players (computation is linear in the product of action space sizes), robustness to non-maximum-entropy equilibrium selections, and incorporating hard fairness or welfare constraints for real-world deployment.
