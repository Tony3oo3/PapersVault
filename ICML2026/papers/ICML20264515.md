## Problem

Existing causal discovery methods for stochastic time series (Granger causality, PCMCI, DYNOTEARS, etc.) rely on restrictive assumptions such as noise stationarity and a single characteristic scale, making them highly sensitive to the non-stationary, nonlinear, and scale-free dynamics prevalent in real-world data. This sensitivity leads to spurious causal inferences or missed true interactions, particularly in systems exhibiting power-law frequency spectra arising from self-organizing behavior.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264515/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes multivariate time series of length L with d variables and produces a directed acyclic causal graph. Each variable is segmented into overlapping sliding windows (default size l=50, stride s=1), and within each window the DFT amplitude spectrum is fitted to a power-law model, yielding two new feature time series per variable (spectral amplitude a and exponent λ). The Granger test then uses (λ_i, a_i) as the causing series and λ_j as the target, with 10 lagged values and a p-value threshold of 0.05 for edge retention.

## Method

PLaCy (Power-Law Causal discovery) replaces raw-signal Granger analysis with a frequency-domain spectral feature extraction pipeline: for each sliding window, the DFT amplitude spectrum is fitted in log-log space via ordinary least squares to the model log A(f) = a − λ log f, producing time-evolving trajectories of (a, λ) per variable. Multivariate Granger causality (VAR-based Wald test) is then applied to these spectral feature series rather than the original signals, so that structural causal changes propagating between variables are isolated from non-stationary and nonlinear external fluctuations. A key theoretical contribution (Theorem 3.1) proves that, under local power-law spectral approximation, weak stationarity within windows, and linear causal mechanisms, the spectral transformation preserves the ground-truth causal graph structure. The window length is selected adaptively by choosing the shortest window for which the log-log linear fit achieves p < 0.05, balancing sensitivity to temporal variation against reliable power-law estimation.

## Results

On the synthetic OU(σ_g^m>0) benchmark (N=5, C=0.5, σ_g^a=1.0), PLaCy achieves an F1 of 0.80±0.17 and TNR of 0.93±0.06, outperforming all 10 baselines. On the real-world Rivers dataset, PLaCy attains F1=0.51±0.10 and TNR=0.75±0.13, the best F1 among all methods. On the AirQuality dataset (N=36 stations), PLaCy achieves F1=0.45±0.04 and TNR=0.66±0.07, again the best F1 and a substantially higher TNR than most competitors (e.g., BCGeweke TNR=0.16, GewekeNP TNR=0.13).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PLaCy consistently achieves the highest F1 across all synthetic scenarios (up to 0.80 on ŌU(σ_g^m>0), C=0.5) and both real-world datasets, outperforming the closest competitors by 5–25 percentage points in F1. PCMCI attains the highest TNR on AirQuality (0.95) but at the cost of the lowest F1 (0.25), reflecting its conservative MCI-based edge selection; PLaCy achieves a more balanced F1/TNR trade-off. The frequency-domain baselines BCGeweke, DTF, and GewekeNP achieve competitive F1 scores on synthetic data (0.50–0.79) but suffer from very low TNR (0.13–0.64 on real data), indicating many spurious edges. DYNOTEARS and RCV-VarLiNGAM perform poorly under multiplicative noise and non-stationarity (F1 ≤ 0.22 on ŌU(σ_g^m>0)), while Rhino, despite its deep-learning architecture, yields only moderate F1 (0.22–0.29) at substantially higher computational cost. CCM-Filtering degrades severely on real data (TNR=0.04 on AirQuality), confirming that delay-embedding approaches fail for stochastic power-law processes.

## Relation to Prior Work

PLaCy builds on the classical Granger causality / VAR testing framework (Lütkepohl, 2005) but departs from both time-domain methods (Granger, PCMCI, DYNOTEARS) and full-spectrum frequency-domain methods (Geweke, DTF, BCGeweke) by introducing an intermediate representation: the time-evolving parameters (exponent λ, amplitude a) of a local power-law fit to the DFT magnitude spectrum. This repurposes the well-known observation that real-world time series exhibit scale-free, power-law spectra (Bak, Flandrin) as a structural prior for causal inference, rather than merely as a descriptive property. The method thus sits at the intersection of spectral signal processing and Granger-type hypothesis testing, applying the latter to derived feature series rather than raw signals or transfer functions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a persistent practical failure mode of causal discovery—spurious edges under noise and non-stationarity—by showing that a simple power-law spectral summary, combined with standard Wald tests, can outperform both constraint-based (PCMCI) and deep-learning (Rhino) approaches in exactly the regimes where those methods struggle. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3.1) that the spectral transformation preserves the causal graph under stated assumptions provides a principled justification beyond empirical validation. Key limitations remain: the method requires power-law spectral structure (excluding, e.g., precipitation series), struggles with slowly varying spectra, and is unsuitable for very short time series. Open questions include handling latent confounders, extending the spectral-parameter idea to non-VAR causal discovery frameworks, and a deeper characterization of which spectral density parameters carry causal information.
