## Problem

Existing generative models for small-molecule design either optimize 3D shape and pharmacophore alignment or enforce synthesizability via reaction pathways, but rarely both simultaneously. This creates a bottleneck in ligand-based drug design, where high-scoring hits must also be practically synthesizable. SynLaD addresses this gap by learning a single latent space that is jointly decodable into both a 3D molecular structure and an executable synthesis plan, conditioned on pharmacophore profiles.

## Input / Output

The model takes a 3D pharmacophore profile as conditioning input, consisting of up to six discrete feature types (H-bond donor, H-bond acceptor, cation, anion, aromatic ring, hydrophobe) with their spatial coordinates, plus a target atom count sampled around the query's heavy-atom number. At inference it produces two outputs from the same latent: (1) a 3D molecular structure with atom types and coordinates decoded by a bidirectional transformer, and (2) a serialized synthesis pathway — a linear token sequence of action tokens (building-block addition, forward reaction, stop) and molecular tokens that encodes a directed acyclic graph of reactions from purchasable building blocks to the final product. Product identities in the synthesis plan are filled in at inference by a BART-based reaction-prediction oracle (84.6% top-1 accuracy) rather than fixed reaction templates.

## Method

SynLaD is trained in two stages. Stage 1 learns a variational autoencoder with a single encoder and two decoder heads: a 3D decoder (bidirectional transformer) reconstructs atom types and coordinates, while an autoregressive synthesis decoder (causal transformer with cross-attention to the latent) generates the serialized reaction plan; both are jointly optimized with a weighted reconstruction loss plus a per-channel KL penalty, forcing the shared latent to encode both spatial geometry and synthetic route. Stage 2 trains a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) denoiser via conditional flow matching in the learned latent space, with pharmacophore features embedded by a separate transformer and injected through cross-attention; classifier-free guidance (with 20% conditioning dropout) enables controllable sampling at inference. A key design choice is replacing fixed reaction templates with a learned reaction-prediction oracle, which mitigates the template bottleneck and allows broader chemical-space exploration. The paper shows that joint training is mutually beneficial: the synthesis decoder steers the 3D decoder toward more synthesizable molecules, while the 3D-informed latent improves the synthesis decoder's ability to satisfy shape and pharmacophore constraints under conditioning.

## Results

On the Lit-PCBA bioactive hit diversification task (10 targets, 500 samples per query), SynLaD's 3D decoder achieves the highest hit count (38.1 avg.) and unique scaffold hits (18.8 avg.), with a maximum Tanimoto Combo score of 1.45. The synthesis decoder achieves 100% validity, 0.75 AiZynthFinder retrosynthesis success, and 14.6 synthesizable hits per query while remaining amortized (no per-query retraining). In a screening case study, SynLaD generates 59.4 hits per query using only 1,000 samples, outperforming brute-force ROCS screening over the full ~67k-molecule dataset (49.7 hits).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ShEPhERD (SE(3)-equivariant diffusion), SynLaD's 3D decoder more than doubles the hit count (38.1 vs. 15.7) and unique scaffold hits (18.8 vs. 13.0), while the synthesis decoder yields 14.6 synthesizable hits versus ShEPhERD's 6.3, with a far higher AiZynthFinder success rate (0.75 vs. 0.18). Against SynFormer (amortized reaction-template synthesis), SynLaD's synthesis decoder produces more hits (17.9 vs. 13.8), higher AiZynthFinder scores (0.75 vs. 0.50), and more than double the synthesizable hits (14.6 vs. 6.7), attributed to the explicit 3D latent conditioning. Against REINVENT (non-amortized RL optimization, ~6 h/query vs. ~1 min for SynLaD), SynLaD's synthesis decoder achieves comparable AiZynthFinder (0.75 vs. 0.75) and synthesizable hits (14.6 vs. 16.3), but REINVENT's advantage is driven by a single outlier query; across the full benchmark REINVENT produces synthesizable hits on only 3/10 queries versus 5/10 for SynLaD(syn) and 8/10 for SynLaD(3D), and shows substantially lower scaffold diversity (3.7 vs. 6.9 unique scaffold hits). Overall, SynLaD is the only amortized method that simultaneously achieves high hit counts, high synthesizability, and broad scaffold diversity.

## Relation to Prior Work

SynLaD unifies two previously separate lines of work: pharmacophore-conditioned 3D molecular generation (ShEPhERD, MolSnapper, Imrie et al.) and synthesis-constrained generation via serialized reaction pathways (Bradshaw et al., SynFormer, SynFlowNet). It builds directly on the all-atom latent diffusion framework of Joshi et al. (2025) for the 3D encoder/decoder and the DiT architecture of Peebles & Xie (2023) for the generative backbone, while adopting the bottom-up DAG serialization of Bradshaw et al. (2020) for the synthesis head. The key architectural departure is the dual-decoder VAE that forces a single latent to be simultaneously decodable into 3D coordinates and a reaction plan, coupled with a reaction-prediction oracle (BART-based) that replaces fixed reaction templates used by most prior synthesis-constrained methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By producing molecules that are both pharmacophore-aligned and directly tied to a feasible synthesis route from a single model, SynLaD addresses a practical bottleneck in ligand-based drug discovery where 3D-aware generators typically output synthetically inaccessible structures. The finding that joint training of the two decoders improves the synthesizability of 3D-decoded outputs (AiZynthFinder 0.59→0.72) suggests the synthesis head acts as an implicit regularizer toward chemically realistic latent regions. Limitations include reliance on an external reaction predictor (84.6% top-1 accuracy), restriction to 10–40 heavy atoms, and a training set of only ~67k pathways; scaling to larger reaction databases (e.g., Pistachio) and improving cross-decoder agreement are identified as key next steps.
