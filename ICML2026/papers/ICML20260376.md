## Problem

Standard factuality evaluations of LLMs treat all errors as equivalent, making it impossible to distinguish whether a failure stems from the model never encoding a fact (an 'empty shelf') or from the model encoding it but failing to access it at inference time (a 'lost key'). These two failure modes imply fundamentally different remedies—pre-training scaling versus post-training or inference-time interventions—yet existing accuracy metrics cannot separate them.

## Input / Output

For each of 2,150 Wikipedia-derived facts, the pipeline generates 10 questions spanning three formats (proposition completion, closed-book QA, multiple-choice QA) and two relational directions (direct and reverse), each in high-verbatim and natural phrasings. The LLM produces 8 responses per question at temperature 1, which are graded by a prompted LLM autorater into CORRECT, INCORRECT, PARTIALLY, or OTHER. The output is a per-fact knowledge profile assigned to one of five categories (Encoding Failure, Recall Failure, Direct Recall, Recall with Thinking, Inference without Encoding), determined by whether the fact passes encoding tasks (existential: any priming context suffices) and knowledge tasks (universal: correct across all phrasings and directions).

## Method

The paper introduces a behavioral knowledge-profiling framework that operationalizes encoding and recall without access to model weights. Encoding is measured by proposition completion and contextual questioning over the original Wikipedia left context, using existential quantification (a fact is encoded if it is reproduced in any priming context above a 0.5 accuracy threshold). Recall (knowledge) is measured by closed-book direct and reverse questions in two phrasings each, using universal quantification (a fact is known only if correct across all four questions). The WikiProfile benchmark is constructed via a fully automated pipeline powered by Gemini-2.5-Pro with Google Search grounding, performing NER-based fact extraction, multi-stage question generation/refinement/filtering, and manual validation. Across 13 LLMs and ~4.5 million graded responses, the key finding is that frontier models encode 95–98% of facts yet fail to directly recall 25–33%, with recall failures concentrated on long-tail facts and reverse questions. Thinking (CoT or native thought tokens) recovers 40–65% of encoded-but-not-directly-known facts, and the authors show this effect is best explained by recall facilitation rather than response diversity or multi-hop inference, since gains depend on encoding status and improve robustness rather than merely availability.

## Results

Across 13 LLMs and 4M+ responses on WikiProfile (2,150 Wikipedia-derived facts), frontier models (GPT-5, Gemini-3-Pro) encode 95–98% of facts, yet fail to directly recall 26–34% of them, with 11–12% still unrecalled even with thinking. Recall failures account for over 70% of GPT-5.2's errors. Thinking recovers 40–65% of encoded-but-not-directly-known facts in thinking-optimized LLMs, narrowing the popularity recall gap from Δ=21.4 to 12.5 and the directionality gap from Δ=9 to 2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it profiles 13 LLMs (Gemma3 1B/4B/12B/27B, GPT-4.1, GPT-4.1-mini, GPT-5-mini, GPT-5, GPT-5.2, Gemini-2.5-Pro/Flash, Gemini-3-Pro/Flash) under a unified knowledge-profiling protocol. Scaling within the Gemma3 family reduces encoding failures from 85% (1B) to 23% (27B) but shifts errors toward recall failures, which peak at 40% without thinking. On popularity, the encoding gap between top-20% and bottom-20% Wikipedia pages is small (e.g., 5 pp for Gemini-3-Pro), while the recall gap is 21.3 pp. On directionality, GPT-5 scores 82.9% on direct vs. 74% on reverse closed-book questions, yet performs comparably (or better) on reverse multiple-choice verification, reframing the reversal curse as a recall rather than encoding phenomenon. Thinking-optimized models (Gemini-3, GPT-5) show consistent robustness gains, whereas CoT prompting on Gemma3 degrades robustness, suggesting native thinking training is important for recall facilitation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing a behavioral decomposition of factual errors into encoding failures versus recall failures, a distinction that prior factuality benchmarks (SimpleQA, etc.) do not make. It reframes two well-studied phenomena—the reversal curse (Berglund et al., 2024) and long-tail knowledge gaps (Kandpal et al., 2023)—as recall failures rather than memorization or capacity limitations, and offers a behavioral complement to the 'hidden knowledge' literature (Gekhman et al., 2025; Orgad et al., 2025) that relies on internal-state probing. The encoding-via-memorization operationalization draws on memorization work (Carlini et al., 2023; Hayes et al., 2025), while the thinking-as-recall-facilitation analysis extends the contested CoT-and-factuality literature by isolating recall from inference and diversity effects.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By showing that frontier LLMs encode 95–98% of facts yet fail to recall 25–33% without thinking, the paper shifts the field's focus from scaling pre-training to improving post-training and inference-time utilization of already-stored knowledge. The WikiProfile benchmark and its automated construction pipeline provide a reusable tool for diagnosing encoding-versus-recall failures across domains. Key limitations include scoping to Wikipedia and single-hop facts along only two recall axes (directionality and phrasing), and the open question of metacognition—how models should decide when to invoke thinking—remains unresolved. Whether the recall bottleneck persists for multi-hop or domain-specific knowledge, and whether post-training interventions can close the gap without thinking, are important follow-up directions.
