## Problem

The LLM research community has widely adopted the framing of intermediate tokens (generated before a final answer) as 'reasoning traces' or 'thinking traces,' implicitly treating them as human-interpretable windows into the model's cognitive process. This position paper argues that this anthropomorphization is not a harmless metaphor but actively dangerous: it engenders false user trust, misattributes performance gains to semantic content, and steers research toward fruitless directions. The authors call for the community to stop ascribing end-user interpretability to intermediate tokens and instead treat them as a mechanism that helps the model itself.

## Input / Output

The systems under discussion take a problem or task prompt as input and produce a sequence of intermediate tokens (the 'chain of thought') followed by a specially delimited final answer. The paper's own contribution is argumentative and empirical: it collates controlled experiments (maze pathfinding with A* traces, QA tasks with Llama/Qwen fine-tuning, human subject studies) to test whether intermediate tokens carry problem-specific semantics that causally connect to the solution. The key experimental outputs are trace validity scores, solution accuracy under swapped/corrupted traces, and user trust ratings, rather than a new model or prediction.

## Method

The paper's core evidence comes from several controlled empirical studies. First, transformers trained on A* search traces for maze pathfinding are shown to maintain high solution accuracy even when trained on traces swapped between different problem instances, indicating that trace semantics are not what drives performance. Second, RL post-training (GRPO) is shown to improve solution accuracy without increasing trace validity, even when the base model was trained on irrelevant traces. Third, human subject studies demonstrate that showing intermediate tokens to end-users increases trust in incorrect answers (false trust), and that the traces most useful to the model are the least interpretable to users. The paper then proposes an alternative 'prompt augmentation' view: intermediate tokens function as a learned context manipulation that shifts the conditional distribution of the solution, analogous to adversarial prompt augmentations, and need not be human-interpretable. This reframing frees the community to optimize intermediate tokens purely for solution accuracy, potentially including non-linguistic latent-space tokens.

## Results

This is a position paper that synthesizes evidence from multiple empirical studies rather than reporting a single method's results. Key findings supporting the position include: (1) transformers trained on A* maze traces maintain high solution accuracy even when trained on fully swapped (semantically invalid) traces, with accuracy dipping only when correct and swapped traces are mixed (Valmeekam et al., 2026); (2) SFT with incorrect intermediate traces paired with correct solutions outperforms SFT with correct traces in QA tasks (Bhambri et al., 2026); (3) the correlation between intermediate token length and problem complexity breaks down for out-of-distribution instances, where trivial 'no-maze' problems elicit extremely long traces (Palod et al., 2025); and (4) showing reasoning traces to end-users increases trust in predictions regardless of correctness, substantially inflating false trust (Palod et al., 2026).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it collates and interprets results from a body of empirical studies (primarily from the authors' group and collaborators) that collectively challenge the anthropomorphic interpretation of intermediate tokens. The 'comparison' is between the anthropomorphic narrative (traces = reasoning/thinking) and the prompt-augmentation/conditional-distribution-shift account. The evidence consistently favors the latter: performance gains from intermediate tokens persist when trace semantics are destroyed (swapped, truncated, or noisy), RL post-training improves solution accuracy without improving trace validity, and the traces most beneficial to the model are the least interpretable to end-users. The paper positions its prompt-augmentation framing (Section 6.1) as the more parsimonious explanation, noting that adversarial prompting literature already demonstrates that human-uninterpretable strings can effectively augment prompts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the dominant interpretive framework in the LLM reasoning literature that treats intermediate tokens (chain-of-thought, 'thinking traces') as semantically meaningful reasoning steps analogous to human problem-solving. Rather than introducing a new method, it synthesizes a growing body of empirical evidence—including its own controlled experiments with A*-search-trained transformers on mazes, QA distillation studies, and RL post-training analyses—to argue that the anthropomorphic reading is unsupported and actively harmful. It repurposes the prompt-augmentation and adversarial-prompting literatures to propose an alternative mechanistic account: intermediate tokens function as learned context manipulations that shift the conditional distribution of solution tokens, requiring no human-interpretable semantics. The paper also positions itself within the LLM-Modulo and test-time scaling tradition, distinguishing internal intermediate tokens from external commitments (tool calls) in agentic settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If the community accepts this position, it has immediate practical consequences: showing unverified traces to end-users systematically inflates trust in incorrect answers (the false-trust result), billing users for hidden tokens becomes harder to justify, and safety monitoring schemes built on reading trace semantics are built on an accidental and erodible correlation. The paper opens a productive research direction by reframing intermediate tokens as prompt augmentations, which in principle permits non-linguistic (latent-space) intermediates and frees training from the constraint that tokens must read as plausible English. Key open questions remain: what is the actual mechanistic role of intermediate tokens (more forward passes, context manipulation, distribution shifting), and can we design training objectives that make trace validity a genuine property rather than an accident of training data? The evidence, while consistent across multiple controlled settings, comes from simplified domains (mazes, decomposable QA) and specific model families, leaving the question open for the most complex open-ended reasoning tasks.
