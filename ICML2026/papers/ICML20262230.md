## Problem

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has driven major gains in math and code, but open-ended domains like creative writing and health advising lack ground-truth verification, making hill-climbing intractable. Existing self-improvement approaches—learned reward models, LLM-as-judge with static rubrics—suffer from reward hacking and plateau or collapse within 50–250 training steps. The paper asks whether a model's own verification ability (easier than generation) can be exploited as a training signal that sustains improvement over 1000+ steps without human annotations or stronger teacher models.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 200 synthetically generated creative-writing prompts (or HealthBench health queries) and, for each prompt, generates G=8 rollouts from the current policy. A frozen snapshot of the base model (π_θ₀) acts as a self-judge, performing pairwise comparisons between all rollout pairs under individual rubric criteria. The trained policy outputs improved short-fiction stories or health-advice responses, evaluated via pairwise win rates, Bradley-Terry ELO ratings, and multi-criterion rubric scores across three independent frontier-model judges.

## Method

RcRL combines two mechanisms within a Dr. GRPO training loop: (1) pairwise win-rate rewards, where each rollout's scalar reward is its empirical win rate against the other G−1 rollouts under a single rubric criterion (rather than an absolute score over the full rubric), and (2) a curriculum over 13 rubric criteria that introduces one new criterion every n=100 steps, assigning 50% of the total reward weight to the newest criterion and distributing the remaining 50% uniformly over all prior criteria. The frozen self-judge (never updated online) provides a fixed reference for comparisons, while the shifting weight vector creates a non-stationary objective that prevents the policy from over-optimizing any single proxy. Theoretically, the curriculum bounds cumulative proxy-error drift to O(√L) rather than O(L) when per-criterion bias directions are uncorrelated, and criterion-wise judging avoids the 1/K² Fisher-information dilution inherent in holistic rubric scoring. Empirically, this preserves output entropy (H_ratio = 0.93 vs. collapse in baselines) and sustains improvement past 1300 steps, achieving a 70.5% human-evaluated win rate over the base Qwen3-4B model.

## Results

On Creative Writing Bench v3 (CWv3), RcRL achieves ELO gains of +577 (GPT-5.2), +105 (Claude 4.5 Opus), and +524 (Deepseek V3.2) over the Qwen3-4B baseline, with win rates of 69.3%, 59.2%, and 65.4% respectively. On FictionBench, RcRL reaches a 73.9% win rate (ELO 1181) versus 57.5% for RaR (ELO 1052). On HealthBench, RcRL improves over the base model by 14.6% (full set) and 25.4% (consensus subset), outperforming RaR (+8.26% / +7.53%) and instance-specific rubrics (+13.52% / +10.52%). Human evaluation confirms a 70.5% win rate for RcRL over the base model across 1,500 comparisons by three professional annotators.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RcRL consistently outperforms the RaR baseline (Gunjal et al., 2026) across all benchmarks and judges: on CWv3, RcRL's ELO gains are 1.6–2.7× larger than RaR's (e.g., +577 vs +210 on GPT-5.2); on FictionBench, RcRL achieves 73.9% win rate vs. RaR's 57.5%; on HealthBench, RcRL gains +14.6% vs. RaR's +8.26% (full) and +25.4% vs. +7.53% (consensus). Against the Qwen3-4B instruction-tuned baseline, RcRL achieves a 70.5% human-evaluated win rate and 73.9% FictionBench win rate. Notably, RcRL also substantially narrows the gap to larger models: Qwen3-4B wins only 12.1% and 1.8% against Qwen3-8B and 14B respectively, while RcRL raises these to 40.0% and 27.9% without any new data or teacher models. On HealthBench, RcRL also outperforms the instance-specific rubric baseline (+14.6% vs. +13.52% full; +25.4% vs. +10.52% consensus), demonstrating that a fixed curriculum rubric can match or exceed per-example rubrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

RcRL builds directly on the Rubrics as Rewards (RaR) framework (Gunjal et al., 2026), which introduced rubric-conditioned judge scoring for non-verifiable RL, and on Dr. GRPO (Liu et al., 2025) as the policy optimization backbone. The key departures are twofold: replacing RaR's absolute rubric scores (typically from a stronger external judge) with self-judged pairwise win rates, and layering a curriculum over rubric criteria that makes the reward objective non-stationary. This connects to the broader curriculum-learning literature (Bengio et al., 2009) and reward-switching policy optimization (Zhou et al., 2022), but applies them specifically to the reward-design problem in qualitative RL rather than to task difficulty or environment complexity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that non-verifiable RL can be sustained past 1000 training steps (vs. 50–250 for baselines) while preserving output entropy, a practical capability that was previously unattainable without human annotation or stronger teacher models. The cross-domain transfer to HealthBench and the scaling trend (54.6%→75.2% win rate from 0.6B to 14B) suggest the approach becomes more valuable at frontier scales. Open questions include automatic curriculum pacing (currently fixed at 100 steps), automated rubric discovery (currently hand-specified), and the quadratic cost of pairwise comparisons in rollout batch size, which will require tournament-style approximations at scale.
