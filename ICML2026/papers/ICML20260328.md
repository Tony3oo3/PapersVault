## Problem

Despite the homogenization of deep learning architectures across modalities, simple linear models (e.g., LTSF-Linear, FITS) still outperform complex Transformer, CNN, and SSM-based architectures in time series forecasting. No systematic study has explained why this occurs or what architectural property drives the performance gap. The paper hypothesizes that the critical factor is whether a model can learn a direct, learnable mapping from past observations to future predictions (a 'learning dynamics capability'), and that the placement of this dynamics block within the architecture is equally important.

## Input / Output

The method operates on multivariate time series with L historical timestamps across D variates, producing H future timestamp predictions in a single forward pass (direct multi-step forecasting). Experiments span 25 datasets from the TFB benchmark with four forecasting horizons each, evaluated via MSE and MAE. The plug-and-play modifications add a single linear layer (the DYN function) without altering structural hyperparameters, though they may change the effective input length seen by downstream blocks depending on the H vs. L relationship.

## Method

The paper introduces the PRO-DYN nomenclature, grounded in Allen's interval algebra, which decomposes any TSF model into PRO functions (computations staying within the input time interval) and a DYN function (computations propagating from the historical to the prediction interval). LTSF-Linear models are formally identified as relaxed discrete linear time-delay dynamical systems, where the linear layer's weight matrix corresponds to a power of the dynamical matrix. Two plug-and-play experiments validate the framework: (RQ1) adding a learnable linear DYN layer to four under-performing models (Informer, FiLM, MICN, FEDformer) that lack complete learnable dynamics, and (RQ2) inserting a linear DYN layer at the entry of three well-performing PRE-DYN models (iTransformer, PatchTST, Crossformer) to convert them into DYN-POST configurations. The key findings are that complete learnable dynamics are necessary for competitive performance, and that the PRE-DYN configuration (deep pre-processing followed by a terminal dynamics block) is superior because the pre-processing learns a representation in which temporal propagation becomes easier for the linear DYN function.

## Results

For RQ1 (adding learnable dynamics), all four modified models improve over their vanilla versions: Informer goes from −0.333 to −0.228, FiLM from −0.036 to +0.006 (slightly beating NLinear), MICN from −0.176 to −0.164, and FEDformer from −0.398 to −0.360 (scores normalized by NLinear, where higher is better). All DYN-added models are better or comparable in >80% of the 200 dataset-horizon cases, with statistical significance (Wilcoxon p < 0.05) on at least one metric. For RQ2 (configuration), vanilla PRE-DYN models (iTransformer, PatchTST, Crossformer) statistically outperform their DYN-POST counterparts, with the gap widening when the horizon H is shorter than the lookback L, confirming that placing the dynamics block at the model end is critical.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparisons are between modified (DYN-added or DYN-POST) versions and their vanilla counterparts, with NLinear as the external reference. In RQ1, adding a linear DYN layer consistently improves all four under-performing models: Informer gains the most (+0.105 normalized), FiLM crosses above NLinear (+0.006), while MICN and FEDformer show smaller but statistically significant gains. In RQ2, the vanilla PRE-DYN configurations of iTransformer, PatchTST, and Crossformer all beat their DYN-POST versions with statistical significance, with PatchTST and Crossformer showing the largest drops (they process time sequentially, making them more sensitive to the dynamics block being displaced from the end). iTransformer is the exception, being only statistically worse on one metric (MAE) and better/equal in 51% of cases, suggesting its variate-inverted architecture partially decouples it from temporal dynamics placement. Against the NLinear baseline, even the best DYN-added models (except FiLM) remain below NLinear, indicating that the deep backbones still introduce overhead that a pure linear mapping avoids, though the gap narrows substantially with the dynamics addition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LTSF-Linear line of work (Zeng et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024) that demonstrated shallow linear models outperforming complex deep architectures for time series forecasting, and on the dynamics-based TSF literature (Koopa, Attraos) that explicitly models physical evolution laws. It departs from prior analyses of Transformer failure in TSF (Ke et al., 2025), which focused narrowly on attention mechanisms, by proposing the PRO-DYN nomenclature grounded in Allen's interval algebra to systematically decompose any TSF model into pre-processing (PRO) and dynamics (DYN) functions. The key conceptual departure is identifying LTSF-Linear as a relaxed discrete linear time-delay dynamical system and showing that the presence and placement of a learnable DYN function—not backbone complexity—drives performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The PRO-DYN framework provides a principled, plug-and-play design guideline for TSF model architecture: ensure a learnable dynamics function sits at the model's end (PRE-DYN configuration), which explains why recent SOTA models (iTransformer, PatchTST, Crossformer) succeed while older ones (Informer, FEDformer) underperform. The finding that a single linear layer addition yields statistically significant improvements across 25 diverse datasets and four backbone types (Transformer, CNN, SSM) makes this immediately actionable. Open questions include extending the analysis to foundation models (family 2) and dynamics-based models (family 3), exploring richer DYN functions beyond linear layers, and understanding how learned model dynamics align with true physical dynamics of the underlying system.
