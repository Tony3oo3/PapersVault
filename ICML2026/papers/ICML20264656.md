## Problem

After calibrating a near-isotropic constitutive backbone for real-world deformable objects, the remaining real-to-sim error is dominated by mild anisotropy and heterogeneity that standard homogeneous-isotropic models cannot express. Existing alternatives—either fixing the isotropic model family or learning full dynamics end-to-end with neural networks—either leave this residual unmodeled or discard strong physical priors, leading to poor data efficiency and overfitting on limited video data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes synchronized multi-view RGB video sequences (12–15 industrial cameras, ~15 frames per sequence) with known camera intrinsics and extrinsics, capturing free-fall deformation of a single object. It produces a calibrated set of global isotropic material parameters (e.g., Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio, yield stress), a set of bounded residual stress correction operators (three cascaded neural subnetworks predicting pre-linear, microplane redistribution, and post-linear adjustments), and a continuous spatial heterogeneity field η(x) that modulates the global parameter locally. Downstream, these outputs drive an MPM particle simulation whose rendered frames and flow maps are compared against held-out video for evaluation.

## Method

MoSA preserves a standard isotropic constitutive law as a physics prior and learns only structured residual corrections via a cascaded, physics-informed network. The core redistribution operator decomposes a fourth-order anisotropic stiffness tensor into four 3×3 microplane matrices (C_x, C_y, C_z, C_{xyz}) applied sequentially—first redistributing stress within each coordinate plane, then symmetrizing paired shear components, and finally mixing normal stresses across planes—ensuring invertibility via LU-factorized outputs with diagonal offsets. Two additional linear terms (L^{pre} and L^{post}) provide bounded isotropic adjustments before and after redistribution, all gated by a small α coefficient (0.1) to keep corrections mild. Heterogeneity is modeled as a single global parameter modulated by a tanh-bounded implicit field η(x) constrained to zero mean and minimal variance. For supervision beyond pixel reconstruction, the method leverages a two-stage dynamic 3DGS reconstruction to extract motion cues: Gaussian Flow provides implicit velocity supervision, while SVD-based scale and rotation regularizations directly constrain the spatial derivatives of the deformation gradient, yielding a combined loss that jointly optimizes all parameters in a single differentiable MPM pipeline.

## Results

On the real-world dataset (7 objects, 12-camera light-field capture), MoSA achieves a mean PSNR of 31.35 and SSIM of 92.3, outperforming the best baseline GIC (30.02 / 91.5) by ~1.3 dB PSNR. On the synthetic PAC-NeRF benchmark, MoSA attains the lowest mean Chamfer Distance (13.5) and Earth Mover Distance (8.8), beating GIC (13.8 / 9.1) and DEL (14.5 / 9.3). In the downstream robot manipulation transfer, the learned-dynamics policy achieves 68% and 82% real-world success rates on two tasks, versus 42% and 55% for the isotropic-prior baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoSA consistently outperforms all five baselines on the real-world dataset. Against the strongest system-identification baseline GIC, MoSA gains ~1.3 dB PSNR and ~0.8 SSIM on average, with the largest margin on the elastic Mandarin object (+3.3 dB). The fully neural DEL method performs worst (29.41 PSNR), likely due to overfitting under limited real data, while NeuMA (30.00 PSNR) degrades because its NCLaw pretraining distribution mismatches the real materials and both assume isotropy. Vid2Sim (28.08 PSNR) suffers the most, as its forward predictor is trained only on synthetic data and does not generalize to real scenes. On the synthetic PAC-NeRF benchmark, MoSA also leads all baselines in both CD and EMD, confirming that the residual stress corrections do not introduce spurious anisotropy when the isotropic backbone is already sufficient. On the Spring-GS real-world subset (Table 10), MoSA achieves the best PSNR on all four scenes (e.g., 41.12 on Bun vs. 34.68 for GIC), further validating cross-dataset generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoSA positions itself at the intersection of differentiable MPM simulation, 3D Gaussian Splatting reconstruction, and physics-informed learning, but departs from both major prior paradigms: system identification methods (PAC-NeRF, GIC, Vid2Sim) that are bounded by a fixed isotropic model family, and end-to-end neural dynamics (DEL, NeuMA, NCLaw) that discard physical priors and must re-learn the dominant backbone. The key architectural lineage is Bažant's microplane model (1996), repurposed here as a structured constraint on a learned fourth-order stress redistribution tensor, and PhysGaussian's (Xie et al., 2024) derivation linking Gaussian covariance evolution to deformation gradients, which enables the paper's derivative-based motion supervision. The conceptual departure is the 'residual' framing: rather than choosing between a fixed model or a black-box network, MoSA treats the calibrated isotropic constitutive law as a physics prior and allocates all learning capacity to small anisotropic and heterogeneous corrections.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central reframing—that mild anisotropy and heterogeneity, not the dominant isotropic behavior, constitute the key remaining real-to-sim error after standard calibration—provides a targeted and physically interpretable direction for future dynamics learning. The robot manipulation validation (68% vs 42% and 82% vs 55% success rates) demonstrates that closing this residual gap has tangible downstream impact on sim-to-real policy transfer, which is a practical bottleneck in deformable-object robotics. Limitations include reliance on synchronized multi-view capture, applicability only to mild (not strong) anisotropy, and sensitivity to reconstruction quality, leaving open questions about robustness to imperfect observations and extension to broader material regimes or single-view settings.
