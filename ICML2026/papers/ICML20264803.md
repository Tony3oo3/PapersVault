## Problem

Existing online continual learning methods assume a flat label space, while real-world taxonomies (e.g., biodiversity, medical diagnosis) are hierarchical and evolve dynamically over time. Prior hierarchical continual learning works further assume a strict coarse-to-fine curriculum, which is violated in practice when annotations arrive at arbitrary granularities. The paper identifies two fundamental challenges this creates: partial supervision at a single level per sample prevents cross-level semantic consistency, and granularity-dependent learning/forgetting rates destabilize standard replay and regularization mechanisms.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-100, 224×224 for FGVC-Aircraft, CUB-200, iNaturalist, and ImageNet-H) paired with a single hierarchical label at one granularity level per sample, while the label tree itself evolves dynamically as new classes and parent-child links are discovered via an external knowledge source. At inference, the model produces calibrated probability distributions at every observed hierarchy level simultaneously, with per-level temperature-scaled predictions aggregated into a final multi-granularity output. A key constraint is that each training sample provides supervision at only one level (e.g., 'Siamese Cat' without specifying 'Feline' or 'Animal'), making the supervision inherently partial relative to the full taxonomic path.

## Method

HALO combines two complementary pillars. First, Hierarchical Prototypes Regularization (HPR) introduces learnable class-specific prototype banks (inspired by ProtoPNet) via a lightweight 1×1-conv adapter, with two regularizers: a margin-based ranking loss that pulls ancestor-descendant prototypes closer in feature space to bake the evolving tree topology into representations, and a temporal saliency consistency term that penalizes response drift at the most informative spatial locations to stabilize established concepts without blocking new fine-grained updates. Second, Hierarchical Adaptive Aggregation (PredLA) maintains two independent classifiers per hierarchy level—a linear head on a trainable backbone (for plasticity) and an analytic ACIL head on a frozen pretrained backbone (for stability)—and aggregates their predictions in probability space using learned per-level temperature scalings and mixing weights. Critically, the aggregation weights and temperatures are optimized exclusively on replayed buffer samples via a bilevel objective (CE on aggregated predictions plus an entropy-matching term), decoupling aggregation learning from stream updates to prevent short-term distribution shifts from destabilizing the stabilizing mechanism.

## Results

HALO achieves the best AAUC across all four datasets: 59.2 on CIFAR-100, 40.6 on FGVC-Aircraft, 62.9 on CUB-200, and 78.3 on iNaturalist, outperforming the strongest baselines (CCL-DC, OnLora, GACIL) by 4.7–10.4 points. On fine-grained accuracy (FAUC), HALO leads with 43.5 (CIFAR-100), 36.6 (Aircraft), 46.2 (CUB-200), and 40.8 (iNaturalist). Mistake severity (MS, lower is better) is also best for HALO at 1.13, 1.28, 0.93, and 1.55 respectively. On final fine-grained accuracy (FFAcc), HALO reaches 32.0, 28.9, 37.6, and 40.1, trailing ACIL/GACIL on CIFAR-100 and Aircraft but surpassing them on CUB-200 and iNaturalist.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HALO consistently outperforms all 16 baselines on AAUC, FAUC, and MS across all four datasets. Against the strongest replay-based methods (CCL-DC, OnLora, LwF w/RS), HALO gains 4.7–10.4 points in AAUC (e.g., 59.2 vs. 54.5 for CCL-DC on CIFAR-100; 78.3 vs. 73.5 for OnLora on iNaturalist). Against analytic classifiers (ACIL, GACIL), HALO wins decisively on AAUC and FAUC (e.g., 62.9 vs. 54.7 AAUC on CUB-200; 78.3 vs. 69.0 on iNaturalist) and on MS, but trails on FFAcc for CIFAR-100 (32.0 vs. 36.1 for GACIL) and FGVC-Aircraft (28.9 vs. 30.3), reflecting the plasticity advantage of full-model updates over frozen features. On iNaturalist, HALO's FFAcc of 40.1 surpasses GACIL (30.9) and ACIL (26.8) by 9–13 points, showing that the advantage of analytic classifiers narrows as hierarchy depth and class imbalance increase. Hierarchical-loss baselines (RS(HAF), HXE, Soft-CE) and regularization methods (EWC++, LwF) are uniformly outperformed, confirming the paper's analysis that output-level constraints and generic anti-forgetting mechanisms are insufficient under dynamic hierarchies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of online continual learning (OCL) and hierarchical classification, drawing on ProtoPNet's prototype-based spatial classification for its HPR module and ACIL's recursive least-squares analytic classifier for the stability arm of PredLA. It departs from prior hierarchical CL work (HLE, IIRC) by rejecting the strict coarse-to-fine curriculum assumption, and from standard OCL methods by abandoning the flat label space. The key conceptual move is reframing both lines as degenerate or incomplete: hierarchical losses designed for static taxonomies fail under dynamic evolution, and flat-space anti-forgetting mechanisms (EWC, LwF, SDC) cannot handle granularity-dependent interference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DHOCL provides a more realistic and general formulation for streaming classification where taxonomies evolve organically, with direct applications to biodiversity monitoring, medical diagnosis, and e-commerce categorization. The empirical finding that purpose-built hierarchical losses (HAF, HXE, Soft-CE) underperform naive CE under dynamic hierarchies is a useful caution for practitioners. Limitations include reliance on an external knowledge source for hierarchy construction, balanced-tree assumptions in main experiments, and the fact that the method's gains partly come from combining two known classifier paradigms rather than a fundamentally new learning principle. Open questions include whether VLM-based evaluation can replace the current classification protocol for imbalanced hierarchies, and how the approach scales to truly open-world settings without any external taxonomy oracle.
