## Problem

Current multimodal foundation models treat text as the canonical representation with audio as an auxiliary signal grafted onto text-pretrained backbones, creating a structural asymmetry that will misalign with future training distributions as human knowledge generation shifts toward speech-first interfaces. The paper argues this text-centric bias reflects decades of keyboard-centric interface design rather than cognitive naturalness, and that the ML community should anticipate the data-distribution shift rather than react to it.

## Input / Output

The paper discusses systems consuming continuous audio signals (16 kHz speech) alongside text, producing text responses, synthesized audio, and paralinguistic inferences. The critical architectural constraint is that current pipelines must discretize continuous audio into tokens (~50–75 tokens/second via HuBERT or SoundStream) to fit discrete token frameworks designed for text, a step that potentially discards paralinguistic information such as tone, emotion, and speaker identity. The paper advocates for architectures that process audio natively end-to-end without intermediate text transcription, preserving the full acoustic signal for reasoning.

## Method

This position paper combines a technical survey of speech and multimodal modeling advances with an empirical user survey (N=200) to identify adoption barriers and propose a research agenda. The core argument identifies a 'modality asymmetry': nearly all speech-language models (AudioPaLM, SpeechGPT, Qwen-Audio) initialize from text-trained LLM backbones, inheriting text-centric representations while audio is forced into discrete token structures that approximate text. The paper proposes three concrete research directions—audio-first pretraining (training on audio from the start rather than fine-tuning text backbones), semantic audio tokenization (tokenizers whose latent spaces hierarchically encode content, prosody, and speaker characteristics), and evaluation without text intermediation (audio-native benchmarks for comprehension, summarization, and paralinguistic inference). The survey data reveals that habit inertia, not technical limitations, is the primary remaining barrier: 100% of voice users employ voice at home but only 25% at work, and 69% rate recognition quality merely 'Average' despite near-human WER on standard benchmarks.

## Results

This is a position paper without a primary experimental results table. The key quantitative evidence includes: GPT-4o achieves 232 ms (avg ~320 ms) audio response latency versus >1,000 ms for prior cascaded voice assistants; speech input on mobile is ~3× faster than typing (153 WPM vs. ~52 WPM); the compute cost ratio for audio vs. text processing is estimated at 10–100× depending on tokenization and architecture; and an informal user survey (N=200) finds 76% use voice interfaces occasionally, with speed (48%), quality (43%), and single-turn limitations (38%) as top barriers. Global voice assistant devices doubled from 4.2 billion (2020) to 8.4 billion (2024).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not benchmark a proposed method against named baselines. Instead, it contrasts two architectural paradigms: (1) text-pretrained backbones extended with audio tokens (AudioPaLM/PaLM-2, SpeechGPT/LLaMA, Qwen-Audio/Whisper) versus (2) truly speech-native end-to-end systems (GPT-4o, Gemini Live, Moshi). The paper argues the first category inherits text-centric representations and loses paralinguistic information through discretization, while the second preserves tone, emphasis, and hesitation at the cost of higher compute (10–100× per Appendix A). On latency, GPT-4o's 232–320 ms is compared favorably against the ~1,200 ms end-to-end pipeline of conventional cascaded assistants (ASR → LLM → TTS). The paper also contrasts self-supervised audio pretraining (wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT, XLS-R) against earlier supervised HMM/GMM pipelines, noting the former eliminates the annotation bottleneck and enables zero-shot cross-lingual transfer across 100+ languages.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not introduce a new method but synthesizes the state of speech technology (Whisper, wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT, GPT-4o, Gemini Live) and the HCI literature on voice interface adoption to argue for a shift in architectural priorities. It departs from the dominant paradigm in which multimodal models (AudioPaLM, SpeechGPT, Qwen-Audio) graft audio onto text-pretrained LLM backbones, instead advocating for speech-native architectures that treat audio as a first-class modality from pretraining onward. The paper's conceptual contribution is the feedback-loop framing—interface shapes expression, expression shapes data, data shapes models—which repurposes well-known HCI observations about habit formation into an argument about future training data distributions for foundation models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a potential architectural misalignment: if the data ecosystem shifts toward speech-first knowledge generation (podcasts, meetings, voice messages), models built on text-pretrained backbones may be systematically biased relative to future training distributions. The three concrete research directions proposed (audio-first pretraining, semantic audio tokenization, evaluation without text intermediation) are actionable and address genuine gaps in the current literature. However, the core prediction—that speech will become the dominant data modality—is speculative and rests on an informal N=200 survey rather than longitudinal data, and the 10–100× compute cost asymmetry between audio and text remains a substantial practical barrier that the paper acknowledges but does not resolve.
