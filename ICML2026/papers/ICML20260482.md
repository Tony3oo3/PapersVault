## Problem

Existing LLM optimization benchmarks are static, fixed-scale, and dominated by textbook-style instances, making it impossible to probe how model performance degrades as problem complexity grows. Additionally, the field lacks a mechanistic understanding of why two dominant paradigms—Pure-Text Reasoning (PTR) and Solver-Integrated Reasoning (SIR)—diverge in robustness, and which specific component of the optimization pipeline constitutes the primary bottleneck for current frontier models.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes a problem class identifier (one of 10 canonical OR problems) and a difficulty control vector θ (e.g., number of cities for TSP, number of assets for portfolio allocation) to programmatically generate numeric instances, which are then rendered into natural language descriptions via template filling and LLM-based rephrasing. Evaluation outputs are structured as accuracy (relative error < 10⁻³ against solver-verified ground truth), feasibility rate (constraint satisfaction independent of optimality), and optimality gap, reported as avg@10 across ten instances per complexity level. The LLM under test produces either a step-by-step analytical solution with a tagged objective value (PTR/CompPTR) or an executable Gurobi code snippet whose solver output is extracted (SIR).

## Method

OPT-Engine is a four-stage generation pipeline: (1) a numeric instance generator G produces problem data coupled with a solver template that computes exact ground-truth optima, with self-correction for infeasible draws; (2) a template-based mapper M converts the numeric instance into a canonical problem statement; (3) an LLM agent rephrases the canonical statement into diverse domain-specific narratives while preserving mathematical structure; and (4) a validation module combining LLM-as-judge and rule-based checks ensures constraint/objective equivalence, recursively re-invoking stage 3 on failure. The framework spans 10 problem classes in two tiers—five LP (inventory, portfolio, production, transportation, pollution control) and five MIP (TSP, knapsack, bin packing, job-shop, network flow)—with complexity scaled via structural parameters. Three reasoning paradigms are evaluated: PTR (classical CoT), CompPTR (CoT augmented with Python primitives but no external solvers), and SIR (LLM generates solver code executed via Gurobi/COPT). A tripartite diagnostic—linguistic perplexity, objective-function perturbation, and constraint augmentation—is applied to isolate the primary bottleneck, revealing that even O(1) constraint additions cause substantial accuracy drops while linguistic and objective variations have negligible effects.

## Results

On the OPT-Engine benchmark (DeepSeek-V3.2), SIR maintains 80–100% accuracy across Easy→Hard difficulty for TSP, Knapsack, and Netflow, while PTR accuracy collapses from 58–89% (Easy) to 2–45% (Hard). CompPTR (PTR augmented with Python primitives but no external solver) sits in between, e.g., 85%→44% on TSP, confirming that local arithmetic aid cannot substitute for global constraint enforcement. On public benchmarks (Table 5), SIR outperforms PTR by 17.3 pp on MAMO Complex, 12 pp on IndustryOR, and 15 pp on OptMATH for DeepSeek-V3.2. The diagnostic experiments show that adding even O(1) augmented constraints causes a consistent accuracy drop across all problem classes, identifying constraint formulation as the primary bottleneck.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three paradigms—PTR (pure Chain-of-Thought), CompPTR (PTR + Python primitives, no solver), and SIR (LLM formulates model + external Gurobi solver)—across ten problem classes at three difficulty levels. SIR dominates at scale: on Netflow (Hard), SIR achieves 100% accuracy while PTR drops to 0% and CompPTR to 75%; on TSP (Hard), SIR reaches 80% vs. 2% for PTR. CompPTR consistently outperforms PTR (e.g., Knapsack Hard: 78.6% vs. 45.6%) but still trails SIR (95.7%), confirming that local arithmetic tools cannot resolve global constraint-logic failures. On the smaller Qwen3-4B model, PTR initially beats SIR at low complexity, but RLVR post-training (Qwen3-4B-RL) closes the gap, improving SIR execution rate from 23.1% to 38.1% on TSP and 23.1% to 99.6% on Knapsack. On public benchmarks (MAMO Complex, IndustryOR, OptMATH), SIR outperforms PTR by 12–17 pp for frontier models, with the gap widening for smaller models (e.g., Qwen3-4B-Instruct: SIR 17.7% vs. PTR 38.9% on MAMO Complex, but Qwen3-4B-RL narrows this to 49.8% vs. 39.4%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transposes the extensible-benchmark paradigm pioneered in symbolic planning (PlanBench, Zebralogic) to the optimization modeling domain, where prior benchmarks (MAMO, IndustryOR, OptMATH, OPTIBENCH) remain confined to static, fixed-scale instances. It also builds directly on the Solver-Informed RL training approach (Chen et al., 2025) to produce a fine-tuned small model (Qwen3-4B-RL) for controlled comparison. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior optimization benchmarks by introducing a four-stage programmatic pipeline with controllable difficulty parameters and LLM-augmented rephrasing, enabling systematic complexity scaling rather than one-shot evaluation on curated instances.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of constraint formulation (rather than arithmetic or linguistic complexity) as the primary bottleneck for both SIR and PTR paradigms provides a concrete, actionable target for future model development in optimization. The finding that even O(1) constraint additions cause substantial accuracy drops suggests current LLMs rely on pattern-matching to canonical textbook formulations rather than genuine constraint reasoning, which has direct implications for deploying LLMs in real-world OR settings where problems are rarely clean canonical instances. Key limitations include the restriction to LP/MIP (no nonlinear, stochastic, or dynamic programs) and the exact-solution evaluation paradigm, which may not reflect industrial practice where heuristics and time budgets dominate. Open questions include whether the constraint bottleneck persists under different training signals, whether heuristic-based evaluation changes the picture, and whether the findings generalize to nonlinear and multi-stage optimization.
