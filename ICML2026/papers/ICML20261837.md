## Problem

The Cox proportional hazards model is widely used for survival analysis due to its interpretability, but its restrictive modeling assumptions often fail to hold across an entire dataset. While subgroup discovery could identify local regions where the Cox model is well-specified, existing quality functions (Harrell's C-index and the Cox partial likelihood) have fundamental shortcomings that make them unsuitable for this task. This paper is the first to formally study subgroup discovery with the Cox model using axis-aligned box descriptions, introducing new metrics and algorithms to fill this gap.

## Input / Output

The method takes a tabular survival dataset consisting of feature vectors, event times, and binary censoring indicators, and produces an axis-aligned box in feature space (defined by thresholding individual features) that delineates the discovered subgroup, along with Cox coefficients fit to the data within that box. The EPE score quantifies the subgroup's predictive quality, while CRS rejection labels identify individual points that deviate from the subgroup's survival time distribution. Subgroups must contain at least 10% of training data to prevent overfitting, and the final selection among up to 100 hyperparameter configurations is made by minimum training EPE on a held-out test set.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a two-metric framework built into the DDGroup algorithm. The Expected Prediction Entropy (EPE) measures the cross-entropy of the model's ability to discriminate which of two units at risk will fail next, reducing to standard logistic cross-entropy for Cox models and serving as a proper scoring rule that uniquely identifies correct coefficients. The Conditional Rank Statistics (CRS) compute, for a test point, the probability that its failure rank among a reference group is at least as extreme as observed, assuming it follows the same Cox model; this is computed in O(n) time via recursive relationships. The DDGroup algorithm operates in three phases: (1) select a small core group of points with minimum EPE to obtain a rough Cox fit, (2) reject remaining points whose CRS score falls below a threshold (indicating they cannot feasibly follow the same model), and (3) expand the core group into the largest axis-aligned box containing no rejected points. Under well-specified assumptions, the paper proves that this procedure recovers the ground-truth subgroup with vanishing excess volume as sample size grows.

## Results

On the Synth-Nonlinear dataset, DDGroup achieves the best F1 score of 0.97 (±0.01) for recovering the ground-truth subgroup, along with the lowest EPE of 0.38 and highest C-index of 0.87, outperforming all seven baselines. On the Synth-Counter dataset (a deliberate counterexample to C-index and partial likelihood), DDGroup recovers the correct region with F1=0.94, while the C-index and partial-likelihood ablations (DG-CI, DG-PL) cannot recover it even with optimal hyperparameter tuning (F1=0.76 and 0.81 respectively). On real clinical datasets (AIDS, GBSG2, VLC), DDGroup achieves the lowest EPE in 2 of 3 datasets and sits on the Pareto frontier of (EPE, rejection fraction) for every dataset, with EPE values as low as 0.38 (AIDS) versus 0.62 for the base Cox model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDGroup clearly outperforms all baselines on the synthetic benchmarks: on Synth-Nonlinear it achieves F1=0.97 vs. 0.89 for the next-best DG-CI and 0.33 for Cox Tree, and on Synth-Counter it is the only method (F1=0.94) that recovers the ground-truth region where the C-index and partial-likelihood ablations fail entirely. Among the external baselines, Survival Trees (ST) and OSST perform at or near the base Cox model level on most datasets, while PRIM is similarly uncompetitive. The Cox Tree (CT), which uses EPE as a splitting criterion, is the strongest external baseline and on the NASA C-MAPSS case study actually achieves the lowest EPE (0.52) and highest recall (0.73) among perfect-precision methods, though DDGroup matches its EPE (0.53) and C-index (0.73) while also achieving perfect precision. On real clinical data, DDGroup's advantage is more nuanced: it achieves the lowest EPE on AIDS (0.38 vs. 0.62 base) and GBSG2 (0.61 vs. 0.68 base), but on VLC the Cox Tree achieves a lower EPE (0.22 vs. 0.33), though DDGroup has a lower rejection fraction (0.09 vs. 0.21), indicating a better qualitative Cox model fit within the subgroup.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the DDGroup framework from Izzo et al. (2023), originally developed for linear regression subgroup discovery, to the survival analysis setting by introducing two purpose-built metrics (EPE and CRS) that replace the C-index and partial likelihood. The closest prior work, the change-plane Cox model of Wei & Kosorok (2018), also studies subgroup structure in Cox models but defines subgroups via hyperplane sides under more restrictive assumptions, making it inapplicable to the axis-aligned box formulation considered here. The EPE expression itself appeared in Steck et al. (2007) as a lower bound on the C-index and in Kvamme et al. (2019) as a training surrogate, but the authors are the first to study it as a standalone evaluation metric with proper scoring rule properties.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formal treatment of finding axis-aligned subgroups where a Cox model is well-specified, filling a gap between the interpretability of the Cox model and the flexibility of modern ML survival models. The counterexample showing that EPE alone can prefer a mis-specified region (because units are 'intrinsically easier' to distinguish) is a useful caution that motivates the two-metric design. Key open questions remain: disentangling the feature-distribution dependence in EPE for fair subgroup comparison, and developing valid p-values for discovered subgroups to control false discoveries in clinical settings.
