## Problem

State-of-the-art (SOTA) claims in AI/ML research are routinely justified by a single aggregate score (mean across benchmark tasks), yet the language of 'SOTA' implicitly assumes meaningful effect size, consistent per-task wins, and ranking stability under perturbation. The paper identifies and quantifies this claim-evidence gap, showing that the minimal evidence of mean-score superiority frequently does not support the broader superiority that SOTA language conveys.

## Input / Output

The method consumes per-dataset score matrices extracted from public leaderboards (top 20 models × k evaluation units, where k ranges from 8 to 72 depending on the benchmark), with scores oriented so that lower is better. For each of the C(20,2)=190 pairwise comparisons, it produces three scalar diagnostics — Cohen's d, win rate, and breakdown point ratio — and aggregates them into a single fragility rate (the fraction of pairs where the mean-score winner fails at least one test at a lenient threshold). The output is structured as per-benchmark violation rates for each diagnostic plus an overall fragility rate, enabling cross-domain comparison of how often SOTA-level claims rest on fragile evidence.

## Method

The paper applies three well-established statistical concepts as lightweight diagnostics to pairwise model comparisons: (1) Cohen's d to test whether the mean performance gap is meaningful relative to cross-task variance (threshold τ_d=0.2), (2) win rate to test whether the mean-score winner actually outperforms on a majority of tasks (threshold τ_w=0.6), and (3) breakdown point from robust statistics to test whether removing a small subset of datasets reverses the ranking (threshold τ_b=0.2). The key insight is that these three properties are logically independent, so a model can rank first by mean score while failing any one or more of them; the fragility rate F is defined as the proportion of pairs failing at least one test. Applied to ten public leaderboards spanning NLP/LLM, speech, vision-language, video generation, tabular, and time-series domains, the analysis reveals that over half of top-model comparisons lack support for at least one commonly assumed property of superiority, with fragility concentrated precisely among the top-ranked models where SOTA claims are most prevalent.

## Results

Across ten public leaderboards spanning six domains (NLP/LLM, speech, vision-language, video generation, tabular, and time series), the paper computes three diagnostics (Cohen's d, win rate, breakdown point) for all pairwise comparisons among the top 20 models. The headline finding is that the overall fragility rate — the proportion of model pairs where the mean-score winner fails at least one elementary test — ranges from 39.5% (HELM MMLU) to 74.7% (TSFM-MSE), with seven of ten benchmarks exceeding 50%. For the most widely cited benchmark, HELM MMLU (57 subjects, 20 models, 190 pairs), 39.5% of comparisons are fragile, driven by 33.7% consistency violations, 35.3% stability violations, and 23.2% magnitude violations. Fragility concentrates among the top 10 models (53.3% for top-10 vs. 39.5% for top-20 on MMLU), precisely where SOTA claims are most common.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position/diagnostic paper rather than a method comparison, so there are no traditional baselines. Instead, the paper compares fragility patterns across ten benchmarks to identify which structural properties drive instability. LLM benchmarks (HELM MMLU, LiveBench) show the lowest fragility (39–44%), likely reflecting architectural similarity among top models, with stability and consistency as the dominant failure modes. Tabular benchmarks (TabArena) exhibit higher fragility (58–61%) because gradient boosting and neural approaches each dominate different dataset types, making stability the primary bottleneck (59% violation for Binary). Time series forecasting shows the highest fragility overall (61.5% MAE, 74.7% MSE), where the MSE variant amplifies outlier sensitivity and a single anomalous dataset can dominate aggregate rankings. The paper also contrasts its lenient thresholds (τ_w=0.6, τ_d=0.2, τ_b=0.2) against stricter alternatives (e.g., Bouthillier et al.'s P(A>B)≥0.75), showing that fragility rates remain above 30% even under the most relaxed single-parameter configuration, confirming the gap is structural rather than an artifact of threshold choice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes well-established statistical diagnostics—Cohen's d (effect size), win rate, and breakdown point from robust statistics—into a unified audit framework for SOTA claims, rather than introducing new methods. It builds conceptually on Demšar's (2006) call for proper statistical comparison, Dehghani et al.'s (2021) 'benchmark lottery' framing, and Bouthillier et al.'s (2021) variance analysis, but departs from prior work by not proposing technical remedies (new metrics, protocols, or tests) and instead targeting the interpretive layer: the gap between what aggregate scores show and what 'SOTA' language implies. The cross-domain empirical sweep across ten leaderboards is the paper's distinctive contribution, extending single-domain fragility observations (e.g., Roque et al. 2025 for time series, Alzahrani et al. 2024 for NLP) into a systematic, quantified picture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 39–75% of top-model pairwise comparisons fail at least one elementary superiority test across all ten benchmarks makes the claim-evidence gap inescapable and quantified, moving the discussion from anecdotal concern to measured prevalence. The paper's central argument—that the barrier is institutional rather than technical, since the diagnostics require no new experiments—shifts the locus of change from individual authors to venue-level norms, drawing an explicit analogy to CONSORT/GRADE in clinical medicine. Open questions include extending diagnostics to multi-metric evaluation, tracking fragility over time as leaderboards evolve, and understanding which structural properties of a benchmark (task heterogeneity, metric choice, model similarity) predict its dominant failure mode. A key limitation is the single-snapshot, top-20, single-metric scope, which may understate fragility in dynamic or multi-objective settings.
