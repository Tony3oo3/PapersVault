## Problem

Classical GNNs for fluid simulation only exchange information between mesh neighbors, limiting prediction accuracy, and existing rewiring methods like PIORF rely on long-range, bidirectional connections that violate fundamental fluid transport principles (locality, directionality, flow alignment). The paper asks whether a simple, physics-informed local rewiring strategy can outperform these non-physical long-range schemes and whether the direction of connections matters for fluid prediction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262491/cropped_page2_idx6.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a CFD mesh as a directed graph with node features (velocity components, density, pressure, SDF, DID) and edge features (relative position vectors), and produces velocity, density, pressure, or their spatial gradients depending on the dataset. Unusually, the graph topology itself is a dynamic input: at each rollout timestep, the current velocity field determines which 2-hop edges are added, so the message-passing structure changes over time rather than remaining fixed. During training, ground-truth velocity drives the rewiring; at inference, the model's own predicted velocity from the previous step is used.

## Method

FLARE (Flow Alignment Rewiring) adds directional 2-hop edges to the base CFD mesh graph only when the sender node's velocity projects positively onto the displacement vector toward the receiver (s_ij = v_i^T d_ij > T), encoding three fluid-transport principles: unidirectionality, locality, and alignment with instantaneous flow. This produces a new directed graph G+ = (V, E ∪ A_align) that is fed into standard message-passing GNN backbones (MeshGraphNets, BSMS-GNN, Transolver++) without modifying their internal update rules. Unlike PIORF, which uses Ollivier-Ricci curvature to select bottleneck nodes and connects them bidirectionally to high-velocity-gradient nodes at arbitrary distances, FLARE uses only the sender's velocity, restricts connections to the nearest non-adjacent neighbors (2-hop), and makes edges strictly directional. The graph is re-rewired at every timestep during rollout, so the information-propagation topology adapts to the evolving flow field, extending the effective receptive field from L to 2L layers without introducing long-range shortcuts.

## Results

On CylinderFlow, FLARE achieves an average full-rollout velocity RMSE of 36.14×10⁻³ across three architectures (MGN, BSMS-GNN, Transolver++), representing a 27.40% improvement over PIORF and 26.03% over 2-HOP-ALL. On Airfoil, FLARE reduces velocity RMSE by 3.26% over PIORF and 5.38% over 2-HOP-ALL, while on the large-scale Tandem-Airfoil-Cruise dataset (avg. 351K nodes) it delivers a 14.43% MSE reduction over PIORF. FLARE consistently outperforms the random 2-hop baseline (2-HOP-RANDOM) across all datasets and architectures, confirming that flow-aligned directional selection—not merely adding edges—is the source of the gain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PIORF (the prior physics-informed rewiring method using Ollivier-Ricci curvature and long-range bidirectional connections), FLARE wins decisively on CylinderFlow (27.40% average RMSE reduction) and Tandem-Airfoil-Cruise (14.43% MSE reduction), and wins on Airfoil velocity (3.26%) while roughly tying on Airfoil density. Against 2-HOP-ALL (a simple non-physics-informed baseline that adds bidirectional edges to all 2-hop nodes), FLARE wins on every dataset: 26.03% on CylinderFlow, 5.38% on Airfoil velocity, 6% on Airfoil density, and 2.50% on Tandem-Airfoil-Cruise. Against 2-HOP-RANDOM (same edge count as FLARE but random 2-hop selection), FLARE outperforms in all comparisons, confirming that the flow-alignment criterion—not merely the number of added edges—drives the improvement. Notably, PIORF itself performs similarly to 2-HOP-RANDOM on CylinderFlow rollout (Fig. 4), suggesting its long-range ORC-based connections offer little advantage over random local shortcuts, while FLARE's local, directional, flow-aligned design consistently separates from both.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of graph rewiring methods for GNN-based fluid simulation, building directly on PIORF (Yu et al., 2025), which was the first to incorporate velocity information into rewiring for CFD meshes. However, FLARE deliberately departs from PIORF's design in three key ways: it restricts connections to 2-hop locality (vs. PIORF's long-range edges), enforces directionality (vs. PIORF's bidirectional connections), and bases edge selection on instantaneous flow alignment rather than Ollivier-Ricci curvature bottleneck identification. The paper also positions itself against generic topology-based rewiring methods (DIGL, SDRF, FoSR, BORF) that Yu et al. showed to be ineffective for fluid simulation, and against the non-physics-informed 2-HOP-ALL baseline that the authors show to be surprisingly competitive with PIORF.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a simple non-physics-informed 2-hop connection scheme matches PIORF's performance challenges the assumption that the specific curvature-based, long-range design of PIORF is necessary, while FLARE's consistent improvements over both demonstrate that the *right* physical principles (locality, directionality, flow alignment) do matter. This provides a clearer design principle for future physics-informed rewiring: align connections with actual transport rather than graph topology. Open questions include how the optimal hop range should scale with mesh resolution and timestep, whether the approach extends naturally to 3D meshes, and how the added per-step rewiring computation scales for large turbulent simulations where gains are already more moderate (as seen on EAGLE).
