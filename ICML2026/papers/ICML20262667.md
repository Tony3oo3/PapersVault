## Problem

Safety alignment in large language models is typically evaluateduated under isolated single-turn queries, yet real-world deployment is inherently multi-turn and adaptive. While multi-turn jailbreaks are empirirically Etive, the mechanistic structure of how conversational context degrades safety remains insufficiently understood. This paper asks whether the safety boundary enforcedforced by aligned models is static or can be systematically traversed through structured multi-turn interaction.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a safety-critical query (from HarmBench or JailbreakBench), a target model (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini 2.0-Flash, LLaMA-3-8B/70B-Instruct), and an auxiliary trajectory-construction model (Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct), producing up to 7-turn dialogue trajectories with turn-wise judge scores on a 1–5 violation scale. Beyond the aggregate safety failure rate, the output includes per-turn refusal-direction projections and t-SNE latent state trajectories extracted from white-box hidden states, enabling mechanistic analysis of how internal representations shift across the interaction.

## Method

STAR is a two-stage diagnostic framework that treats dialogue history as an explicit state transition operator rather than a passive record. Stage I (state initialization) positions the model near the safety decision boundary via semantic-preserving softening of the query, query-aware role persona generation, and a structured turn template that enforces contextual continuity. Stage II (state eolution) regulates multi-turn interaction through role-conditioned turn executiontion, feedback-aware history intervention that selectively removes refusal responses from conditioning context to prevent defensive reinforcement, and momentum-preserving trajectory control with adaptive retry. The key mechanistic finding is a two-phase latent trajectory: a decisive single-step boundary crossing during initialization, followed by small incremental steps within the compliance region where autoregressive conditioning on prior compliant responses drives monotonic decay of refusal-direction activation from 2.35 to −0.0081 across three turns.

## Results

On HarmBench (50 safety-critical instructions), STAR achieves a Safety Failure Rate (SFR) of 94.5% on GPT-4o, 74.0% on Claude 3.5 Sonnet, 96.1% on Gemini 2.0-Flash, 89.0% on LLaMA-3-8B-IT, and 85.5% on LLaMA-3-70B-IT, substantially exceeding the best single-turn baselines (e.g., CodeAttack at 70.5% on GPT-4o). On JailbreakBench (100 instructions), STAR reaches 94.0% SFR on LLaMA-3-8B, 96% on GPT-4o, and 100% on Gemini 2.0-Flash at an average token cost of 29K, compared to 37K for X-Teaming. Ablation on LLaMA-3-8B-IT shows that removing dialogue history accumulation causes the largest SFR drop (−25.5%), followed by adaptive retry (−19.7%) and role initialization (−17.8%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

STAR outperforms all seven baselines across every model where a comparison is available. Against the strongest multi-turn baseline X-Teaming, STAR gains +0.2 pp on GPT-4o (94.5 vs 94.3), +6.1 pp on Claude 3.5 Sonnet (74.0 vs 67.9), +8.7 pp on Gemini 2.0-Flash (96.1 vs 87.4), +3.5 pp on LLaMA-3-8B-IT, and +0.6 pp on LLaMA-3-70B-IT. Against ActorAttack, the margin is much larger: +10.0 pp on GPT-4o, +7.5 pp on Claude, +54.0 pp on Gemini, +10.0 pp on LLaMA-3-8B, and +0.5 pp on LLaMA-3-70B. Against Crescendo, STAR leads by +48.5 pp on GPT-4o, +24.0 pp on Claude, +29.0 pp on LLaMA-3-8B, and +23.5 pp on LLaMA-3-70B. On JailbreakBench, STAR also surpasses X-Teaming by +8.5 pp on LLaMA-3-8B and +14 pp on Gemini while using 22% fewer tokens (29K vs 37K). Single-turn methods (GCG, PAIR, CodeAttack) are consistently far below STAR, with the best single-turn result (CodeAttack at 70.5% on GPT-4o) trailing STAR by 24 pp, underscoring the state-dependent nature of the failure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from both single-turn jailbreak methods (GCG, PAIR, CodeAttack), which treat safety as a static per-prompt decision, and prior multi-turn jailbreak systems (Crescendo, X-Teaming, ActorAttack), which focus on empirical attack effectiveness. Its conceptual contribution is reframing multi-turn safety failure as a state-space trajectory problem: dialogue history is treated as an active state transition operator rather than a passive context record, and the two-stage decomposition (initialization vs. evolution) enables causal ablation of each mechanism. The interpretability analysis extends Arditi et al.'s single-direction refusal finding to multi-turn trajectories, and the Bayesian framing draws on Xie et al.'s in-context-learning-as-inference perspective to explain why cumulative context can override safety priors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central finding—that safety alignment is state-dependent and can undergo rapid, reproducible collapse under structured multi-turn interaction even when individual prompts are benign—has direct implications for how safety evaluation and monitoring should be designed (trajectory-level rather than per-turn). The proposed two-stage defense pipeline (early interception of role-initialization signatures plus joint-signal monitoring of refusal-direction suppression and role-anchored representational coupling) is a concrete, actionable direction. Open questions include whether these dynamics persist over longer horizons, in multimodal settings, and whether the state-space framing generalizes to models with fundamentally different alignment architectures (e.g., RLHF vs. constitutional AI).
