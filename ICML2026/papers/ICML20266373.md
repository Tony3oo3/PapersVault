## Problem

Most multimodal learning approaches assume a deterministic one-to-one alignment between modalities (e.g., one image to one caption), but real-world cross-modal relationships are inherently many-to-many. This position paper argues that this 'multiplicity' is not annotation noise but a structural property arising from intra-modal variability, representational asymmetry, and task-dependent ambiguity, and that ignoring it causes false negatives in training, unreliable retrieval evaluation, and degraded dataset quality across the entire multimodal pipeline.

## Input / Output

The paper analyzes the standard multimodal pipeline where inputs are multimodal instances (RGB images, text captions, audio clips) paired via sparse one-to-one annotations, and outputs are cross-modal alignments used for retrieval, classification, or generation. The key structural issue is that the true correspondence space R ⊆ X × Y can grow as large as |X|·|Y|, yet datasets record only a sparse subset of positive pairs and treat all unobserved pairs as negatives, converting valid but unannotated correspondences into false negatives. The paper formalizes this with a matching-ambiguity model where K semantically equivalent inputs yield a stochastic positive probability of 1/K per pair, rather than the assumed binary label.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a formal characterization of multiplicity and its downstream consequences. It defines three structural sources (intra-modal variability, modality asymmetry, task-dependent alignment) and derives gradient-level analysis showing how standard contrastive loss and hard negative mining push embeddings away from valid but unannotated positives, amplifying the modality gap as K (the number of true positives per query) grows. For evaluation, it demonstrates that R@K is misleading under multiplicity while ranking-sensitive metrics like mAP@R better reflect human preference, citing ECCV Caption evidence that COCO Caption contains ~4.4× more valid matches than originally annotated. For dataset construction, it identifies a scorer-generator feedback loop where CLIP-based filtering of LLM-recaptioned data progressively suppresses valid alternatives. The paper concludes with a multiplicity-aware pipeline: treating alignments as set-valued or probabilistic, adopting multi-positive evaluation protocols, filtering underspecified instances, and explicitly specifying task-dependent correspondence notions in benchmark design.

## Results

This is a position paper without a proposed method, so there is no main results table. The key quantitative evidence cited includes: (1) COCO Caption contains on average 8.5 positive images per caption and 17.9 positive captions per image, versus the originally annotated 1 and 5, revealing massive false negatives; (2) re-evaluating VLMs with corrected associations yields only a 0.47 Kendall's tau ranking correlation with the original benchmark, showing model rankings are unreliable; (3) one-to-one contrastive training degrades from 40.0 to 20.2 mAP@R when scaling from ViT-B/32 to ViT-L/14, while multiplicity-aware training remains stable at 40.1 to 42.1 mAP@R; and (4) the ECCV Caption benchmark reveals approximately 4.4× more valid positive matches than the original COCO Caption annotations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it synthesizes empirical evidence from prior studies to argue that the one-to-one assumption is fundamentally misaligned with multimodal data. The most striking comparison is between standard contrastive training (which assumes one-to-one correspondence) and multiplicity-aware training (Chun, 2024): at ViT-B/32 scale both achieve ~40 mAP@R, but at ViT-L/14 the one-to-one approach collapses to 20.2 mAP@R while the multiplicity-aware approach improves to 42.1 mAP@R, demonstrating that the failure mode is scale-dependent. On the evaluation side, the paper contrasts R@K with mAP@R, showing via a human preference study (Chun et al., 2022) that mAP@R aligns strongly with human judgments while R@K does not, even when R@K reaches 100%. The paper also notes that even frontier MLLMs (GPT-4, Gemini) struggle when benchmarks explicitly allow multiple correct answers (Sterz et al., 2025), reinforcing that scaling alone does not resolve multiplicity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not build on a single methodological lineage but rather unifies scattered observations from multiple threads—false-negative analysis in retrieval (Chun et al., 2022), noisy-correspondence learning (Huang et al., 2021), modality-gap analyses (Liang et al., 2022), multi-label rethinking of ImageNet (Beyer et al., 2020; Shankar et al., 2020), and probabilistic embedding approaches (Chun et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2024b)—under a single structural concept: multiplicity. It departs from prior work by elevating what was previously treated as annotation noise or a side-effect of sparse supervision to a first-order, inevitable property of multimodal correspondence, arguing that the one-to-one assumption underlying CLIP-style contrastive training, retrieval evaluation, and dataset construction is fundamentally misaligned with the many-to-many nature of real-world cross-modal relationships.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If multiplicity is accepted as a structural bottleneck, it reframes how the entire multimodal pipeline should be designed: training objectives must avoid treating unobserved pairs as hard negatives, evaluation must move beyond single-positive R@K toward ranking-sensitive or multi-positive metrics, and dataset construction must explicitly filter underspecified instances that amplify spurious correspondences. Key open questions include whether multiplicity-aware training (probabilistic embeddings, positive-unlabeled learning, set-valued representations) can scale to billion-pair corpora without prohibitive cost, how to define task-conditioned correspondence in a way that generalizes across downstream uses, and how to break the scorer-generator feedback loop that risks suppressing valid alternatives in automated data pipelines. The paper's main limitation is that it is a position paper relying on prior empirical evidence rather than new large-scale experiments, so the practical magnitude of multiplicity-aware gains at frontier scale remains to be demonstrated.
