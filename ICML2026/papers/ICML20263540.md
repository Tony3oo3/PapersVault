## Problem

Feature importance estimates from expressive ML models are unstable due to data sampling and algorithmic stochasticity (the Rashomon effect), yet it remains unclear whether to compute importance from a model-level ensemble or aggregate individual model explanations. Prior work advocated the sub-models strategy without formal error analysis, and existing theoretical frameworks assumed convergence rates (o_P(n^{-1/2})) that overparameterized models do not satisfy. This paper identifies the dominant error terms under realistic assumptions and shows the choice is primarily driven by the model's excess risk.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a trained model (or ensemble of B models) and a test set of size O(n) with d features, and produces per-feature importance scores for each of five measures (LOCO, SAGE, CFI, PFI, IG). The critical design choice is whether importance is evaluated on the ensemble predictor f_ens = (1/B)Σf_b or averaged across individual sub-model importances (1/B)Σψ(f_b, j); these are not equivalent due to the nonlinearity of standard losses (MSE, cross-entropy). Outputs support both continuous ranking (MSE against ground truth) and binary feature selection (relevant vs. null, evaluated via ROC AUC).

## Method

Under relaxed assumptions (loss consistency and finite variance of the loss), the paper shows that for risk-difference methods like LOCO and SAGE, the importance estimation error is governed by the model's excess risk E, which decomposes into approximation, estimation, and optimization components. Model-level ensembling reduces this leading bias term via Jensen's inequality for convex losses, while sub-models averaging only reduces the O_P(n^{-1/2}) stochastic remainder without affecting the individual excess risks. For CFI and PFI, the same model is applied to both perturbed and original inputs so excess risk terms cancel, leaving only a linear dependence on model error and making the two strategies theoretically equivalent for the dominant term. The analysis is validated on synthetic benchmarks (Friedman 1, G-function, Ishigami) with MLP and Random Forest architectures, and on a large-scale UK Biobank proteomic study (n=46,382, 2,922 Olink proteins) predicting BMI, where model-level ensembling recovers biologically validated markers (e.g., IGFBP-1/2) that the sub-models strategy misses.

## Results

On the UK Biobank proteomic cohort (n=46,382, 2,922 Olink proteins), the model-level ensemble of 10 LightGBM models achieves R² = 0.62 ± 0.001, substantially outperforming individual models (mean R² = 0.54). Across the Friedman 1, G-function, and Ishigami benchmarks (d=20, n=512), model-level ensembling consistently reduces importance MSE and increases ROC AUC for feature selection relative to the sub-models strategy, for both LOCO and SAGE importance measures and both MLP and Random Forest architectures. The bias-variance decomposition (Figure 3) confirms that the ensemble's advantage is driven by squared-bias reduction rather than variance reduction. On TCGA BRCA (572 patients, 50 genes), the ensemble strategy achieves higher recovery of the 10 known cancer driver genes compared to sub-models averaging.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the sub-models strategy from Donnelly et al. (2023), which averages importance scores across individual models (the Rashomon Importance Distribution approach). The proposed model-level ensembling strategy wins decisively on LOCO and SAGE importance estimation: it achieves lower MSE and higher ROC AUC across all three synthetic benchmarks (Friedman 1, G-function, Ishigami) and both architectures (MLP, Random Forest), with the gap being larger under bagging than voting and larger for MLPs than RFs under voting. On the UK Biobank, the ensemble strategy correctly identifies IGFBP-1 and IGFBP-2 as important (with non-overlapping error bars), while the sub-models baseline shows error bars that largely overlap zero for these biologically validated proteins. For CFI and PFI, the two strategies are nearly indistinguishable empirically, consistent with the theoretical finding that these methods have only linear (not quadratic) dependence on excess risk. For Integrated Gradients, the two strategies are mathematically identical due to linearity of the operator, so no comparison is meaningful.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model-agnostic variable importance framework of Williamson et al. (2023) by relaxing their convergence-rate assumptions (which required E = o_P(n^{-1/2})) to accommodate modern expressive models where the excess risk dominates. It builds on the Bottou & Bousquet (2007) excess-risk decomposition (approximation, estimation, optimization) to formalize how the Rashomon effect maps onto concrete error terms, and extends the analysis to LOCO, SAGE (Covert et al., 2020), CFI (Chamma et al., 2023), and PFI. The key departure is from Donnelly et al. (2023), whose Rashomon Importance Distribution aggregates individual model importances (the 'sub-models' strategy); this paper shows that model-level ensembling—aggregating predictions before computing importance—reduces the leading bias term for risk-difference methods, a distinction overlooked in prior work due to the nonlinearity of importance measures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear, theoretically grounded practical recommendation: for LOCO and SAGE, compute importance on the ensemble rather than averaging sub-model scores, with the magnitude of gains depending on the importance measure (quadratic for risk-difference methods, linear for CFI/PFI). This is particularly impactful for high-stakes biomedical applications where inconsistent feature rankings can misdirect experimental validation of therapeutic targets. Limitations include restriction to tabular data and well-defined population-level estimands, excluding heuristic methods and non-tabular modalities (images, time series) that would require prior representation learning. Open questions include whether the bias-dominant regime persists at very large sample sizes and whether the results extend to foundation models for structured data.
