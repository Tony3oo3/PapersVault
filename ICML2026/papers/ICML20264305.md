## Problem

Generalized Bayesian inference (GBI) with power posteriors p_β(θ|x) ∝ π(θ)p(x|θ)^β provides a principled robustness knob under model misspecification, but existing GBI methods in simulation-based inference still require inference-time MCMC or SDE-based samplers that must be re-run for every new observation and every temperature value. No prior work amortizes the full family of tempered posteriors over both the data x and the temperature β in a single forward pass without simulator calls.

## Input / Output

At test time the trained model takes a single observation x (which can be a high-dimensional time series, e.g., a 21×8 trajectory from Lorenz-96 or a 7-dimensional summary of a voltage trace) and a user-specified temperature β ∈ [β_min, β_max], and outputs posterior samples θ from q_φ(θ|x,β) in one forward pass with no simulator queries or MCMC. During training, β is drawn from a distribution supported on a prescribed grid or interval to ensure coverage across the full temperature range, and the observation x is a simulated data point from the implicit simulator p(x|θ).

## Method

The paper trains a single β-conditioned neural posterior estimator q_φ(θ|x,β) via two complementary routes. Route A learns a joint score ∇_(θ,x) log p(θ,x) by denoising score matching (NCSN), then synthesizes tempered training pairs (θ,x,β) by running short-run annealed Langevin dynamics targeting the tempered joint π(θ)p(x|θ)^β, and finally fits q_φ by conditional MLE on these pairs. Route B draws a fixed base dataset (θ_i, x_i) ~ π(θ)p(x|θ) once and reuses it across all temperatures via self-normalized importance sampling, with weights w_β(θ,x) = p(x|θ)^{β-1}m(x) instantiated either through a neural likelihood estimator (m(x)=1) or a neural ratio estimator (m(x)=p(x)^{1-β}). The SNIS-weighted NPE objective is shown to be a consistent forward-KL fit to the tempered posterior (Proposition 4.1), and the NRE-based weights are proven to have finite variance for β ∈ [1/2, 1] (Proposition 4.2). The posterior network uses normalizing flows (NSF) or mixture density networks, with β concatenated to the data embedding as a conditioning input.

## Results

The paper evaluates on four SBI benchmarks (Gaussian Mixture, Two Moons, SLCP, Lorenz-96) and the Hodgkin-Huxley neuronal model, reporting MMD and C2ST two-sample discrepancies as functions of temperature β ∈ {0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 1.0, 1.1, 1.3, 1.5}. The β-conditioned amortized estimator q_φ(θ|x,β) achieves competitive approximation quality to non-amortized MCMC power-posterior references across the full temperature range, with no single dominant method: Route B (SNIS-weighted NPE) performs best near β=1 where importance weights are stable, while Route A (score-assisted synthesis) is more robust at small β where SNIS effective sample size collapses. On the Hodgkin-Huxley model with only 10K simulations, RouteB_NLE produces stable one-dimensional marginal posterials across β ∈ {0.1, 1.0, 2.0} and posterior predictive traces that qualitatively match observed spiking patterns from the Allen Cell Types Database.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four amortized variants (Route A NPE, Route B NLE, Route B NRE, and a standalone NLE/NRE baseline) against non-amortized MCMC-based power-posterior samplers (random-walk MH, parallel tempering, or independent rejection sampling depending on the benchmark). No single amortized method uniformly dominates: Route B (both NLE and NRE) matches or approaches the MCMC reference near β=1 but degrades as β moves away from 1 due to increasing importance-weight variance and ESS collapse (visible in the nESS diagnostics of Figure 5, where ESS drops sharply for SLCP and Lorenz-96 at β=0.1). Route A, which synthesizes tempered training pairs via annealed Langevin dynamics, is more robust at small β (e.g., β=0.1 on Gaussian Mixture and SLCP) but can be less stable on multimodal geometries like Two Moons due to score estimation error and discretization bias. The NRE-based Route B variant generally outperforms the NLE-based variant at β<1 because the ratio estimator provides better-calibrated weights, while the NLE variant can be preferable when the ratio is poorly estimated. Overall, the amortized methods are broadly comparable to the non-amortized MCMC references in two-sample metrics while eliminating all inference-time simulator calls and MCMC, making them substantially cheaper for sweeping β or processing many observations after a single training run.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of generalized Bayesian inference (GBI) and simulation-based inference (SBI), building directly on the power posterior framework of Bissiri et al. (2016) and the amortized NPE paradigm of Greenberg et al. (2019). It departs from prior GBI-in-SBI work—Gao et al. (2023) amortize the expected cost but still require inference-time MCMC, and Pacchiardi et al. (2024) use scoring-rule posteriors without amortization—by being the first to fully amortize over both the observation x and the temperature β, reducing posterior sampling to a single forward pass. Route A leverages NCSN-style score matching (Song & Ermon, 2019) for offline tempered-joint synthesis, while Route B connects to the SNVI line (Glöckler et al., 2022) but extends it to the GBI setting with per-β SNIS reweighting using NLE or NRE surrogates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is enabling rapid β-sweeps for robustness diagnostics, posterior-predictive checks, and calibration without re-running MCMC or calling the simulator, which is valuable when many observations or temperatures must be processed. Key limitations include SNIS weight variance collapse at extreme β (especially β→0), score-estimation error propagation in Route A's Langevin synthesis, and the offline simulation cost that makes the approach less attractive when simulators are prohibitively expensive. Open questions include adaptive route selection based on ESS diagnostics, defensive SNIS extensions for β>1, and whether the approach generalizes to non-power GBI losses (e.g., energy scores) without retraining.
