## Problem

Vision-language embedding models like CLIP reliably recognize individual concepts (e.g., red, square) but fail to represent which concepts form which objects in multi-object scenes, behaving like bag-of-concepts systems cross-modally. Yet object information is recoverable from embeddings within each modality via linear probes, creating a tension: if object structure is present, why does cross-modal binding fail? The paper asks whether this failure is fundamental or stems from the complexity of the learned concept-to-object mapping.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on dual-encoder models where the input encoder f maps a scene (a multi-object image or its text caption) to a d-dimensional embedding, and the query encoder q maps either a single concept value (e.g., 'red') or a full object description (e.g., 'red square') to a d-dimensional embedding. Matching is via cosine similarity, and the key outputs are concept recognition accuracy (ranking present concept values above absent ones) and object recognition accuracy (ranking present objects above absent concept combinations). For the controlled experiments, inputs are tokenized sequences of discrete concept-value tokens with SOO/EOO/EOS delimiters, and the binding function is probed by training MLPs to predict scene embeddings from one-hot concept indices.

## Method

The paper formalizes binding as the conjunction of concept recognition and object recognition, then analyzes the geometry of CLIP and DINOv2 embeddings to show that scene embeddings decompose additively into object components (Level-I) which further decompose into concept components (Level-II), explaining why uni-modal probes recover object information. To diagnose why cross-modal binding fails, the authors train MLP approximators to predict scene embeddings from concept indices and find that CLIP's binding function is high-complexity: even wide MLPs fail to generalize object recognition to held-out objects while concept recognition remains high. They then train CLIP-style dual-encoder transformers from scratch on synthetic multi-object scenes with controlled object-space coverage, showing that binding generalization emerges with sufficient data (e.g., ~30% coverage for |O|≥2500). Finally, they identify the functional form of generalizing binding: multiplicative interactions between concept embeddings (Global product structure) rather than purely additive composition, with a strong correlation between a model's generalization accuracy and how well its embeddings are approximated by this multiplicative probe.

## Results

Scene embeddings in CLIP and DINOv2 decompose additively into object components with high fidelity: R² = 0.90/0.92 (text) and 0.75/0.84 (image) for CLIP on PUG:SPARE, with retrieval accuracy reaching 0.97 (text) and 0.93 (image). However, CLIP's binding function is high-complexity: even 4096-wide MLPs achieve <20% object recognition on held-out objects, while concept recognition stays above 80%. In controlled transformer dual-encoders trained from scratch, binding generalization emerges with sufficient data coverage—object recognition on entirely unseen objects reaches near-perfect accuracy at ~30% coverage for |O| ≥ 2,500, and the generalizing binding function is well-approximated by a simple multiplicative (Global product) probe.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CLIP (ViT-B/32, ViT-L/14) against DINOv2 (ViT-B/14) and random-initialization baselines on the additive decomposition and binding tasks. For object-based reconstruction (Table 2), CLIP's text encoder achieves the highest R² (0.90/0.92) and retrieval (0.97), while DINOv2 slightly outperforms CLIP on image R² for CLEVR (0.85/0.88 vs. 0.78/0.83) but lags on retrieval (0.86 vs. 0.94). Both pretrained models substantially outperform random-init baselines (R² ≈ 0.47–0.53, retrieval ≈ 0.25–0.42), confirming that the additive structure is learned, not trivial. The critical comparison is between CLIP's binding function and that of the controlled transformers: CLIP's binding resists approximation by even 4096-wide MLPs (object recognition <20% on held-out objects), whereas the controlled models' binding is captured by small single-layer MLPs and a simple multiplicative probe, explaining why the latter generalize to unseen objects while CLIP does not. Applying the Global product probe to CLIP and DINOv2 embeddings recovers concept recognition but leaves object recognition near zero, confirming that pretrained models lack the multiplicative structure needed for generalizable binding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the CLIP dual-encoder architecture (Radford et al., 2021) and the concept-space formalism of Okawa et al. (2023), extending both to multi-object scenes. It directly addresses the tension identified by Koishigarina et al. (2026) and Lewis et al. (2024)—that CLIP exhibits uni-modal binding via probes yet fails cross-modally—by formalizing the binding function and showing its high complexity is the root cause. The controlled transformer experiments draw on the compositional generalization literature (Lippl & Stachenfeld, 2025; Montero et al., 2021) but shift focus from single-object factorized settings to multi-object scenes where binding is the central challenge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic explanation for why CLIP fails at cross-modal binding: the binding function it learns is high-complexity and combination-specific, preventing the image and text encoders from converging on a shared rule. The finding that this failure is not fundamental—that controlled transformers learn low-complexity, multiplicative binding functions that generalize with sufficient data coverage—opens a path toward designing embedding models with systematic compositional generalization. Key limitations include reliance on synthetic datasets (no real-world dataset provides the required combinatorial completeness) and the fact that complexity is measured relative to a chosen approximator family, leaving open whether the multiplicative structure identified here persists in natural-image settings at scale.
