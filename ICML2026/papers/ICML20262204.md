## Problem

Existing benchmarks for prompt injection attacks on LLM web agents are static, monolithic, and rely on ambiguous multi-step success criteria judged by other LLMs, making it difficult to isolate *why* and *when* an injection succeeds. No prior work systematically decomposes injections into modular persuasion and manipulation components to study how individual design choices (e.g., button vs. hyperlink, social proof vs. authority) shift agent vulnerability across models.

## Input / Output

The benchmark feeds each agent a benign task prompt (e.g., 'summarise the Dinner & Movie event') alongside the accessibility tree (AXTree) of a realistic website clone where a modular prompt injection has been embedded in a user-editable field. Success is measured as a single binary click event: the agent either clicks the injected button or hyperlink (attack success) or ignores it and continues the benign task, yielding an objective, reproducible metric independent of downstream attacker payloads. Outputs include per-model attack success rates, benign task completion rates, a cross-model transferability matrix, and per-component success breakdowns across all five injection dimensions.

## Method

TRAP constructs a five-dimensional modular attack space by crossing seven Cialdini persuasion principles, five LLM manipulation methods (adversarial suffixes, CoT injection, many-shot conditioning, role-play, override prompts), two interaction vectors (button, hyperlink), one injection location per environment, and optional contextual tailoring, yielding 630 distinct injections paired with 18 benign tasks across six REAL website clones (Amazon, Gmail, Google Calendar, LinkedIn, DoorDash, Upwork). Agents operate in a standard observation–action loop using Playwright, receiving AXTree observations and issuing browser actions, with a 35-step limit and temperature 0.0. The key design choice is the one-click success metric, which isolates the critical security boundary (agent navigating to attacker-controlled content) from downstream harm, enabling clean ablation of individual attack components. Controlled experiments then vary one dimension at a time—interaction vector, injection location, and tailoring—to quantify how small interface or contextual changes shift attack success rates across six frontier models.

## Results

Across six frontier LLMs evaluated on 630 injection tasks each (3,780 total runs), the average attack success rate (ASR) is 25%, ranging from 13% (GPT-5) to 43% (DeepSeek-R1). GPT-5 also achieves the highest benign task completion (89%), while LLaMA 4 Maverick has the lowest (22%). Button-based injections are ~3.5× more effective than hyperlinks (77.5% vs. 22.5% of successful attacks), and light contextual tailoring boosts ASR by up to ~5.6× (GoCalendar: 3.3% → 18.6% average). Social Proof (18.1%) and Consistency (17.9%) are the most effective Cialdini persuasion principles, while Adversarial Suffixes (24.5%) and Chain-of-Thought injection (23.8%) dominate among LLM manipulation methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRAP is a benchmark rather than a method, so it does not propose a defence to compare against prior defences. Instead, it evaluates six LLMs under a unified modular attack protocol. The 25% average ASR is consistent with prior agent-security benchmarks: AgentDojo reports 21.54% average ASR and InjecAgent reports 29.58% in its base setting, placing TRAP squarely in the same vulnerability range. Cross-model transferability analysis reveals that injections succeeding on the most robust model (GPT-5) transfer to weaker models at 82.5% on average (peaking at 90% to Claude Sonnet 3.7), whereas injections from the most vulnerable model (DeepSeek-R1) transfer at only 39.1% on average—indicating that attacks developed against the strongest agent form a near-superset of those effective against weaker ones. A controlled button-vs-hyperlink study on GoMail confirms the interface effect: GPT-OSS-120B (46% vs. 6%), Gemini 2.5 Flash (66% vs. 9%), and Claude Sonnet 3.7 (46% vs. 6%) all show buttons dramatically outperforming hyperlinks under identical conditions.

## Relation to Prior Work

TRAP builds on the REAL simulation environment (Garg et al., 2025) for deterministic website clones and extends it with injection modules, logging, and OpenRouter model access. It departs from prior agent-security benchmarks (AgentDojo, InjecAgent, ASB, OS-HARM) in two key ways: it replaces multi-step, LLM-judged success criteria with a one-click binary metric at the interaction boundary, and it decomposes injections into a five-dimensional modular attack space (persuasion principle × manipulation method × interaction vector × location × tailoring) rather than treating them as monolithic blocks. The paper is also the first to operationalise Cialdini's human persuasion principles as a controlled variable in the LLM-agent setting, bridging social psychology and agent security.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With web agents increasingly deployed for email, shopping, and networking, a 25% average attack success rate across six frontier models is a concrete and actionable risk signal. The finding that trivially small changes—swapping a hyperlink for a button (3.5×) or adding task-specific wording to <20% of injection text (up to 5.6×)—dramatically shift success rates suggests that current agent architectures lack robustness to even minor contextual framing, pointing to a need for defences that go beyond simple instruction-following. Open questions include whether the one-click metric adequately captures downstream harm, whether the modular decomposition reflects how real-world injections actually combine components, and how to build systematic mitigations within this reproducible framework.
