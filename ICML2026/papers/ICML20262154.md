## Problem

Test-time scaling of diffusion models to align outputs with human intent requires computing the Expected Future Reward (EFR) at each intermediate particle, but existing approaches face a trilemma: backward rollout is prohibitively expensive, Taylor/Tweedie-based gradient guidance requires backpropagation through multiple neural networks and suffers from approximation error that grows with reward strength, and Sequential Monte Carlo methods collapse to near-identical samples in high-dimensional image space. No prior method simultaneously avoids per-timestep rollout, neural backpropagation, Taylor approximation bias, and particle collapse.

## Input / Output

Given a text prompt c, a pre-trained diffusion model s_θ (e.g., SD v1.5, SDXL, FLUX, or a discrete diffusion model), and a reward function r (e.g., ImageReward, CLIP, or a weighted combination), the method first runs a few-step lookahead solver (δ steps, e.g., DPM-5 or LCM-4) to generate n marginal samples and annotate each with a scalar reward value. The target sampling then produces N images per prompt (typically N=4 for GenEval) from the reward-tilted distribution p_θ^r(x_0|c) ∝ p_θ(x_0|c)·exp(λ·r(x_0,c)), with the additional cost over vanilla sampling arising solely from the one-time lookahead generation and reward annotation stage.

## Method

The key reformulation (Theorem 3.1) expresses the EFR at any x_t as an expectation over marginal samples from p_θ(x_0|c) weighted by the forward Gaussian kernel p(x_t|x_0), thereby removing x_t as an input to any neural network. This enables a closed-form, derivative-free guidance term (Theorem 3.3): the gradient of the empirical lookahead reward is a weighted sum Σ(w_i^r − w_i)·x̂_0^i/σ_t², where w_i^r and w_i are softmax weights over lookahead samples that incorporate both reward values and Gaussian kernel distances to x_t. High-reward lookahead samples attract the particle while low-reward ones repel it, all without backpropagation. The method further provides probabilistic scaling laws showing the total variation distance to the target distribution decreases as O(1/√δ) with lookahead solver accuracy and the EFR estimation variance decreases as O(1/√n) with the number of lookahead samples, enabling compute-optimal allocation between these two knobs.

## Results

On SDXL (DDPM 100 steps), LiDAR (DPM-8/n=50) achieves GenEval 0.585, ImageReward 0.994, CLIP 0.285, and HPS 0.300 in 97.99 s with 33.84 GiB memory, outperforming DATE (GenEval 0.570, 272.32 s, OOM) and UG (GenEval 0.541, 334.43 s, OOM). On SD v1.5 (DDPM 100 steps), LiDAR (DPM-5/n=50) reaches GenEval 0.478 and IR 0.384 in 13.41 s with 8.90 GiB, versus DATE's 0.438/0.364 at 32.89 s/24.71 GiB and UG's 0.355/0.326 at 58.36 s/28.16 GiB. The paper reports a 9.5× inference-time reduction relative to DATE's peak scaling performance on SDXL while matching or exceeding GenEval scores. LiDAR also extends to flow matching (FLUX: GenEval 0.668 at n=50) and discrete diffusion (UDLM on QM9: 251 novel molecules at n=1024).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LiDAR outperforms both gradient-guidance baselines (UG and DATE) on every metric across all backbones while using less memory and substantially less time: on SDXL, LiDAR achieves GenEval 0.585 vs. DATE's 0.570 and UG's 0.541, at 97.99 s vs. 272.32 s (DATE) and 334.43 s (UG), and avoids the OOM that forces both baselines to run sequentially. Against the sample-based safety methods Safe-D and SR (Table 7), LiDAR achieves IR 0.384 and HPS 0.276 versus Safe-D's −0.001/0.262 and SR's 0.014/0.263, showing that soft reward-weighted attraction is far more effective than binary negative-sample repulsion for text-image alignment. When orthogonally combined with SMC (Table 8), LiDAR improves ImageReward at every particle count N (e.g., at N=64: 1.338 vs. 1.221 for SMC alone; 1.510 vs. 1.438 for SMC+BoN), and the shared lookahead samples make LiDAR increasingly efficient as N grows. Overall, LiDAR wins or ties on all reported comparisons, with the largest margins on GenEval and inference time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the SDE-based diffusion model framework (Song et al., 2021b) and the test-time reward-tilted sampling paradigm established by gradient guidance (Bansal et al., 2024; Na et al., 2025) and SMC-based methods (Singhal et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025). Its key conceptual departure is reformulating the Expected Future Reward via a forward rollout—using only marginal samples from the pre-trained model and the forward perturbation kernel—rather than the backward rollout or Tweedie-based Taylor approximation used by all prior methods. This decouples the neural dependency between the intermediate particle x_t and the EFR, enabling a closed-form, derivative-free guidance term that avoids both backpropagation through neural networks and the particle collapse inherent to SMC in high-dimensional image spaces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LiDAR provides a practically significant efficiency win (9.5× faster than gradient guidance at matched GenEval performance on SDXL) while remaining orthogonal to fine-tuning (DPO) and composable with SMC/Best-of-N, making it a broadly applicable test-time scaling tool. The extension to flow matching (FLUX) and discrete diffusion (UDLM) broadens its relevance beyond continuous latent diffusion. Open questions include developing token-dependent guidance for discrete diffusion (current approach is dimension-wise independent), whether the method scales effectively to video generation, and how lookahead sample quality interacts with very large reward scales λ in practice.
