## Problem

Cross-modal 2D-3D gait recognition is hindered by the fundamental information gap between 2D silhouette contours and 3D LiDAR geometric structure. Existing methods enforce alignment only at the final embedding layer (endpoint matching), which is brittle under covariate shifts such as clothing changes, occlusion, and illumination variation. The paper asks whether cross-modal alignment can be upgraded from a static endpoint constraint to a dynamic trajectory-level constraint that governs the entire latent evolution of both modalities.

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired 2D silhouette sequences and 3D LiDAR range-view sequences (B×S×C×H×W), deliberately using geometry-preserving range views rather than pseudo-2D depth-map projections to retain discriminative depth structure. A modality-specific CNN backbone with temporal pooling extracts sequence-level feature maps, which are then split through two decoupled 1×1 heads: a discriminative head (with HPP and shared FC/BNNeck) producing part-level identity embeddings and class logits for retrieval, and a generative head producing the clean latent state that seeds the diffusion process. At inference, only the discriminative branch is retained, so the output is a standard identity embedding vector with no iterative denoising overhead.

## Method

DiffCrossGait embeds cross-modal alignment into a shared-noise latent diffusion trajectory: both 2D and 3D clean latents are corrupted by an identical Gaussian noise sample at each timestep, and a parameter-shared lightweight U-Net (with axial attention at the bottleneck) learns to predict the shared noise and extract a bottleneck bridging state. A Tri-Phase Alignment Strategy partitions the diffusion timeline by signal-retention thresholds (ρ_a, ρ_b) into three regimes—small noise (identity anchoring via self-conditioning and classification loss), medium noise (denoising-dynamics and bottleneck-state consistency), and large noise (cross-modal structural recoverability via swapped identity-conditioning, i.e., CrossCond). The total loss combines the standard discriminative triplet + cross-entropy objective with phase-specific diffusion losses (noise prediction, bottleneck symmetry, and intra/inter-modal L1 reconstruction), all weighted by fixed hyperparameters. Crucially, the diffusion branch is a training-only regularizer: it adds ~2.3 M parameters during training but is entirely discarded at inference, preserving the baseline backbone's computational footprint while yielding SOTA cross-modal retrieval accuracy on SUSTech1K and FreeGait.

## Results

On SUSTech1K (3D→2D), DiffCrossGait achieves 63.8% Rank-1 and 83.4% Rank-5, surpassing the strongest baseline SCR by +6.1 and +3.9 points respectively. On SUSTech1K (2D→3D), it reaches 58.7% Rank-1 and 79.5% Rank-5 (+3.6/+1.4 over SCR). On the large-scale FreeGait benchmark, DiffCrossGait attains 58.5%/86.9% (2D→3D) and 61.5%/86.8% (3D→2D) Rank-1/Rank-5, exceeding SCR by +18.4/+14.9 and +18.2/+10.9 points. Inference cost remains nearly identical to the baseline backbone (14.0M params, 6.2 ms) since the diffusion branch is discarded at test time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiffCrossGait outperforms all ten baselines on both retrieval directions of SUSTech1K and FreeGait. The closest competitor, SCR (Information Fusion 2025), is surpassed by +6.1 Rank-1 (3D→2D) and +3.6 Rank-1 (2D→3D) on SUSTech1K, and by a dramatic +18.2/+18.4 Rank-1 on FreeGait, indicating that static endpoint alignment (SCR's approach) degrades sharply under unconstrained real-world conditions. Among gait-specific methods, CrossGait (IJCB'24) and CL-Gait (ECCV'24) trail by 7.4 and 10.5 Rank-1 points respectively in the 3D→2D direction. The only covariate where DiffCrossGait does not lead is Night in the 3D→2D setting (8.1 vs. SCR's 10.2), reflecting extreme information scarcity. On SYSU-MM01 (visible-infrared ReID), applying the diffusion objective as a plug-and-play regularizer yields modest but consistent gains: DEEN improves from 74.70 to 75.19 Rank-1 and IDKL from 81.42 to 81.91 Rank-1, confirming generalizability beyond gait. Inference efficiency is preserved: DiffCrossGait uses only 14.0M parameters and 6.2 ms per sample, comparable to the 13.7M/5.9 ms baseline backbone, while heavier baselines like SCR (117.8M, 11.7 ms) and IDKL (74.1M, 10.5 ms) are substantially slower.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant endpoint-alignment paradigm in cross-modal gait recognition (CrossGait, CL-Gait, GaitSet derivatives), which imposes triplet or contrastive losses only on final embeddings, by reformulating alignment as a trajectory-level constraint in a latent diffusion space. It builds on the latent diffusion framework (Rombach et al., 2022) and the emerging idea that modulating the diffusion trajectory itself can enforce cross-modal semantic alignment (ContextDiff, AlignDiff), but repurposes the denoising process as a training-only structural regularizer rather than a generative tool. The shared-noise mechanism and tri-phase stratification over noise regimes are novel architectural choices that distinguish this work from prior diffusion-for-ReID approaches (DiVE, IA-Diff) that use diffusion for synthetic data generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is that diffusion serves exclusively as a training-time objective, adding only 2.3M parameters during training while introducing negligible inference overhead—making the approach deployable in surveillance settings where iterative denoising is infeasible. The finding that a lightweight shared U-Net outperforms a heavyweight one (63.8 vs 59.1 Rank-1) suggests that excessive denoising capacity can weaken modality-invariant learning by allowing the model to explain away artifacts independently, a counterintuitive result with broader implications for auxiliary generative objectives. Open questions include whether the axial-attention prior generalizes to non-standard viewpoints (e.g., top-down UAV), whether the approach extends to more than two modalities, and whether the theoretical Fisher-divergence bounds are tight in practice under the discrete, finite-step regime actually used.
