## Problem

Although general capability benchmarks are widely reported for foundation models, social impact evaluations covering bias, environmental costs, privacy, and labor remain fragmented and unevenly covered. Prior work identified reporting gaps on selected dimensions, but no study had systematically mapped the full landscape of who evaluates what—across both first-party and third-party sources—at scale, leaving the division of labor and structural causes of missingness unclear.

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes 186 first-party release-time reports (model cards, system cards, technical reports, blogs) and 248 third-party sources (peer-reviewed papers, leaderboards), each annotated with a 0–3 reporting-detail score across seven social impact dimensions. Model instances are further labeled by openness, provider region, and organization type, and 10 semi-structured developer interviews supply qualitative context on organizational incentives. The released dataset pairs model-level metadata (year, openness, provider, region) with per-dimension scores, enabling downstream analysis of reporting coverage and gaps.

## Method

Each report is scored on a 0–3 ordinal scale (0 = no evaluation, 1 = vague mention, 2 = concrete results with limited methodological detail, 3 = sufficiently detailed and contextualized) across seven base-level social impact dimensions adapted from Solaiman et al. (2023): bias/representational harms, sensitive content, performance disparity, environmental costs, privacy, financial costs, and data/moderation labor. To quantify how model properties affect reporting, the authors fit Bayesian hierarchical ordinal regression models with group-level random intercepts and slopes by provider, country, and region, and B-spline terms for evaluation year, with a 390-item double-annotation subset establishing inter-annotator agreement. The empirical analysis is complemented by 10 semi-structured interviews with for-profit and non-profit developers, which surface the organizational incentives and structural barriers—reputational risk, methodological difficulty, lack of access to proprietary data—behind the observed reporting patterns.

## Results

Across 186 first-party release reports and 248 third-party evaluation sources, first-party reporting scored a mean of 0.77 on a 0–3 detail scale versus 2.64 for third-party evaluations, with ordinal log-odds ratios of −2.22 to −4.66 confirming the disparity across all seven social impact dimensions. Sensitive content was the most consistently reported category, while data and content moderation labor appeared in only 8.06% of first-party reports and was virtually absent from third-party sources. Environmental cost reporting declined sharply after Q3 2023, and bias/representational harm reporting showed a similar downward trend. Academia led first-party reporting (mean 0.77), followed by industry (0.56) and non-profits (0.56), with government organizations lowest (0.36).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This empirical study does not compare model methods against baselines; instead, its central comparison is between first-party and third-party evaluators across seven social impact dimensions. Third-party evaluators substantially outperform first-party developers on reporting detail (2.64 vs. 0.77 mean score), particularly for bias, sensitive content, and performance disparity, where third-party coverage is 2–5× more frequent. However, first-party developers hold a structural advantage in areas requiring proprietary data: environmental costs (58.2% first-party share), financial costs (53.5%), and privacy (56.2%), where third-party access is limited. Across sectors, academia and non-profits report better on environmental costs (LOR = 1.53 and 1.45 respectively) than industry, while government organizations show the weakest overall reporting (0.36). Temporally, environmental cost reporting peaked around 2022–early 2023 (scores ~2.0–2.75) then dropped to near-zero by 2025, while sensitive content remained the most stable category over time. Geographically, US and Chinese models attract the most third-party scrutiny, leaving models from other regions systematically under-examined.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Foundation Model Transparency Index (FMTI, Bommasani et al., 2024), which assessed transparency across 100 indicators but examined risk evaluations only at an aggregate level for 14 models, by providing a granular, dimension-level analysis across 186 first-party and 248 third-party sources. It operationalizes the seven-dimension social impact taxonomy from Solaiman et al. (2023) and builds on prior identification of reporting gaps (Maslej et al., 2024; Luccioni et al., 2024) to deliver the first large-scale quantitative mapping of who reports what, where, and how well. The methodological framing draws on the EvalEval workshop's push for treating evaluation methodology as a first-class research subject, and the Bayesian hierarchical ordinal regression approach is standard for ordinal outcome modeling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first comprehensive evidence base showing that social impact reporting is not merely incomplete but actively declining in key areas (environmental costs, bias), driven by strategic deprioritization rather than methodological infeasibility. This reframes the policy conversation: the bottleneck is incentive alignment, not technical capacity. Open questions include defining adequacy criteria per dimension, linking base-model propensities to downstream societal harms, and building shared infrastructure for aggregating third-party evaluations. Limitations include a small, geographically skewed interview sample and assessment of reporting presence rather than methodological soundness.
