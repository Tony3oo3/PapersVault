## Problem

While the smoothness of transformer architectures has been extensively studied for generalization, training stability, and adversarial robustness, its role in transfer learning and finetuning remains poorly understood. The prevailing assumption is that promoting smoothness is beneficial, yet it is unclear which specific transformer components should be prioritized during adaptation and why. This paper challenges that assumption by showing that non-smooth (high-plasticity) components are actually the ones that facilitate finetuning.

## Input / Output

The method operates on 2D RGB images resized to 224×224, which are split into patches, flattened, and linearly projected into token sequences in R^d. The plasticity measure is computed as the expected ratio of output Frobenius-norm change to input Frobenius-norm change over pairs of token sequences drawn from pretraining and downstream data. For finetuning, each transformer component (MHA, FC1, FC2, LN1, LN2) is updated in isolation across all layers, producing a class label via the CLS token embedding after the final encoder block.

## Method

The paper formalizes the plasticity of a transformer module as its average rate of change (Definition 1), which lower-bounds the Lipschitz constant and captures sensitivity to input perturbation. It then derives theoretical upper bounds on plasticity for LayerNorms (bounded by ‖γ‖∞/σ), feedforward layers (bounded by the spectral norm ‖W‖₂), and multi-head self-attention (bounded by a sum over heads involving spectral norms and a √n sequence-length dependency), establishing the ranking MHA → FC1 ≈ FC2 → LN2 ≈ LN1. The key mechanistic insight, building on Béthune et al. (2024), is that input-output smoothness and weight-output smoothness are two sides of the same coin: high plasticity implies large gradient norms during optimization, enabling faster and more stable descent on the loss landscape. This is validated through ~1,000 finetuning runs on 86M–632M parameter ViTs across 11 classification benchmarks with both SGD and Adam, confirming that adapting attention modules and feedforward layers consistently outperforms adapting LayerNorms in both accuracy and hyperparameter robustness.

## Results

Across 11 diverse image classification benchmarks with SGD, finetuning the multi-head attention (MHA) module in isolation achieves 90.8% average top-1 accuracy, outperforming the first feedforward layer (FC1, 90.7%), second feedforward layer (FC2, 90.3%), and both LayerNorms (LN2: 89.9%, LN1: 89.8%). With Adam on 4 benchmarks, MHA reaches 82.2% average accuracy versus 79.2% for LN1, a 3.0-point gap. In the low-data VTAB regime (1,000 samples), MHA achieves 94.4% average versus 93.0% for LN1. The performance ordering consistently follows the plasticity ranking: MHA → FC1 → FC2 → LN2 → LN1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between five transformer component types finetuned in isolation, ordered by their theoretical plasticity. High-plasticity components (MHA, FC1, FC2) consistently outperform low-plasticity components (LN2, LN1) across all 11 benchmarks, both optimizers (SGD and Adam), and the low-data VTAB protocol. The gap is most pronounced on challenging datasets: on Sketch with Adam, MHA (69.0%) surpasses LN2 (63.1%) by 5.9 points; on Clipart, MHA (76.4%) beats LN2 (73.3%) by 3.1 points. The MHA module also shows the lowest variability across learning rates and seeds, indicating more robust adaptation. The paper additionally notes that finetuning only LayerNorms with Adam (28K parameters) outperforms LoRA (400K parameters) on Cifar100 (89.4% vs. 88.1%), demonstrating that parameter count alone does not determine finetuning performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Lipschitz/smoothness literature—previously studied for generalization, adversarial robustness, and training stability—toward the finetuning/adaptation setting, building directly on Castin et al. (2024) for tight attention Lipschitz bounds and Béthune et al. (2024) for the theoretical equivalence between input-output and weight-output smoothness. It departs from the prevailing assumption that promoting smoothness is universally beneficial, instead arguing that high plasticity (low smoothness) in attention and feedforward modules is what enables effective adaptation. The work sits within the selective PEFT literature (Touvron et al., 2022; Ye et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024), which has empirically identified beneficial components but lacked a unifying theoretical principle to explain *why* certain modules adapt better than others.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plasticity framework provides practitioners with a principled, architecture-agnostic criterion for choosing which components to prioritize during finetuning, validated across ViTs, DINOv3, and GPT2 with over 1,000 runs. The finding that non-smooth components consistently yield higher and more stable finetuning performance challenges the field's default bias toward smoothness regularization and suggests that over-constraining Lipschitz constants may actively hinder adaptation. Open questions include whether tailored per-module optimization (adaptive learning rates, schedulers) further amplifies the plasticity benefit, how the framework extends to multi-component combinations rather than isolated modules, and whether the plasticity ranking shifts under distillation or instruction-tuning regimes.
