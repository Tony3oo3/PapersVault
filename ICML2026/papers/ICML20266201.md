## Problem

Open-set time series anomaly detection (OSAD) faces two intertwined challenges: (1) the normal training set is inevitably contaminated with unlabeled anomalies that mislead supervised training, and (2) image-oriented augmentation operations (rotation, cropping, flipping) fail to generate realistic pseudo anomalies that preserve the sequential structure of time series, producing either trivial patterns indistinguishable from normal data or physically implausible anomalies (e.g., horizontally flipped ECG segments). Existing OSAD methods cannot simultaneously address contamination and produce high-quality unseen anomaly samples for time series.

## Input / Output

The method takes multivariate time series windows of shape D×L (D variables over L timestamps) along with a very small set of labeled anomalies (10 per seen class) and a larger normal set that may contain up to 2% unlabeled anomalies. At inference, it produces a scalar anomaly score per sample computed as the sum of a maximum-abnormality score (the max across r channels of the sum of two head predictions) and a feature deviation score (squared distance from the mean feature of the most-influential normal reference samples). The feature extractor is a TCN producing 64-dimensional embeddings, and two separate two-layer MLP heads (seen and unseen) operate on these embeddings.

## Method

IMPACT operates in three phases. First, Test-risk-driven Influence Scoring (TIS) trains an initial model with a multi-channel deviation loss (theoretically equivalent to entropy minimization of the latent score distribution) and then computes per-sample influence scores on validation risk via the influence function, partitioning the training set into contaminated (positive influence), reference (most negative influence), and clean subsets. Second, Risk-reduction-based Anomaly Decontamination and Generation (RADG) performs two operations guided by these scores: (a) label flipping on contaminated samples, which Theorem 2 proves reduces test risk by a factor proportional to their positive influence, and (b) feature-space perturbation of the k least-helpful normal samples in the direction that maximally increases test risk, generating pseudo anomalies whose distribution is provably distinct from the original (Theorem 3) and whose inclusion further reduces test risk (Theorem 4). Third, a dual-head architecture is trained with a combined seen-abnormality loss (over clean + flipped + reference samples) and an unseen-abnormality loss (over reference + perturbed features), with the unseen head trained only on the influence-guided perturbed features to capture novel anomaly patterns.

## Results

Under the open-set general setting, IMPACT achieves the best AUC across all eight datasets, with headline values of 91.96% on CT, 82.91% on TUSZ, 75.97% on SMD, and 65.76% on ASD, representing 6.37%–44.52% AUC improvement over the strongest competitor. Under the harder open-set setting (single seen anomaly class), IMPACT maintains its lead with mean AUCs of 81.90% (CT), 75.70% (TUSZ), 66.19% (SAD), and 62.48% (PTBXL), outperforming the best baseline by 10.52%–42.83%. Ablation studies confirm that both label flipping for decontamination and influence-guided feature perturbation for unseen anomaly generation are essential, with removing seen abnormality learning causing the largest drop (e.g., TUSZ from 82.91% to 70.91%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

IMPACT outperforms all 14 baselines (7 unsupervised + 7 open-set) across all eight datasets in the general setting. Among open-set competitors, WSAD-DT is the closest challenger on TUSZ (78.70% vs. 82.91%) and DRA leads on CT (88.73% vs. 91.96%), but IMPACT surpasses both by 4.21 and 3.23 AUC points respectively. In the hard setting (single seen anomaly class), IMPACT's advantage widens: on CT it reaches 81.90% mean AUC versus 76.32% for MOSAD (best competitor), a 5.58-point gap, and on TUSZ it achieves 75.70% versus 68.74% for MOSAD. The unsupervised baselines (TCN-AE, GPT4TS, COUTA, DADA) consistently underperform the open-set methods, confirming that even limited labeled anomalies provide substantial benefit, but they remain vulnerable to contamination. The paper attributes IMPACT's robustness to its influence-guided label flipping, which proactively corrects contaminated training samples rather than relying on post-hoc calibration, and to its feature-space perturbation strategy that generates semantically realistic pseudo anomalies unlike heuristic augmentations used by DRA, MOSAD, and InvAD.

## Relation to Prior Work

IMPACT repurposes the influence function framework (Koh & Liang, 2017), previously applied to data valuation and cleaning, as a dual-purpose mechanism for anomaly detection: identifying contaminated training samples via label flipping and guiding feature-space perturbation to synthesize realistic pseudo-anomalies. The multi-channel deviation loss extends the deviation network formulation (Pang et al., 2019) with an entropy-minimization interpretation, providing a geometric and information-theoretic justification for the scoring objective. Unlike prior open-set TSAD methods (MOSAD, InvAD, WSAD-DT) that rely on image-oriented augmentations (rotation, cropping, segment swapping) to generate pseudo-anomalies, IMPACT operates entirely in the learned feature space, avoiding the domain mismatch that makes such augmentations produce unrealistic time-series patterns. It also differs from post-hoc contamination estimation approaches (GammaGMM, ExCeeD) by proactively correcting labels during training rather than calibrating scores at inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses two practically critical and previously entangled problems in open-set TSAD—training data contamination and unrealistic pseudo-anomaly generation—within a single theoretically grounded framework, with proofs that both label flipping and influence-guided perturbation provably reduce test risk. The substantial AUC gains (up to ~43% in hard settings) and the thorough ablation isolating each component's contribution make this a strong reference for practitioners dealing with contaminated, partially-labeled time-series data. Limitations include reliance on Hessian inversion for influence computation (potentially costly for very large models), evaluation on only 8 datasets with a fixed 2% contamination rate, and use of a single TCN backbone, leaving open questions about scalability to longer sequences, deeper architectures, and higher contamination levels.
