## Problem

Prior incentivized exploration work either assumes fixed-design linear contexts (Sellke 2023) where arm features are static, or relies on black-box MAB reductions (Mansour et al. 2020) that discard the linear reward structure. Neither handles the practical recommender-system setting where user covariates are stochastic and sampled online, causing the optimal arm to change every round. This paper formalizes the resulting dynamic Bayesian incentive compatibility (DBIC) problem and asks: can one achieve sublinear regret while guaranteeing that myopic, self-interested users comply with exploratory recommendations under stochastic contexts?

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a stream of users, each with a d-dimensional covariate vector x_t drawn i.i.d. from a distribution with minimum eigenvalue φ₀ > 0, a set of K products parameterized by unknown β_i ∈ R^d, a shared factorized prior P₀, and a scalar incentive budget ε. At each round the platform outputs a recommended arm I_t; the user may deviate to a_t ≠ I_t, and the platform observes reward y_t(a_t) only for the user-chosen arm. The output guarantees are an ε-DBIC compliance condition (posterior expected utility of I_t exceeds any alternative by at most ε) and a cumulative Bayesian regret bound of Õ(√(KdT)).

## Method

RCB is a two-stage mechanism. Stage 1 (Cold Start) collects N(ε) samples per arm via a calibrated exploration probability 1/L: it first saturates the highest-prior arm (MPASC), then alternates between exploring the best-prior under-sampled arm and exploiting a 'safe' saturated arm (RASC), with L chosen so the expected utility loss of exploration is subsidized within the ε budget. Stage 2 (Exploitation) uses Inverse Proportional Gap Sampling (IPGS), where the probability of recommending a non-best arm i is 1/(K + γ_m·gap), and the spread parameter γ_m = 4√(K/MSPE) tightens automatically as the plug-in offline oracle's prediction error shrinks across doubling epochs. The key architectural insight is a clean decoupling: all ε-dependence is confined to the cold-start threshold N(ε) = O(K³(σ²d+1)/(φ₀(τ+ε)²)), while the exploitation-stage sampling adapts purely to learning progress, making the framework modular over any offline regression oracle (ridge, kernel, neural net) without altering the incentive guarantee.

## Results

On the PharmGKB warfarin dosing dataset (5,528 patients, K=3 arms), RCB at a tight incentive budget (ε=0.025) achieves a fraction of incorrect decisions of approximately 0.35, matching the Lasso Bandit baseline (Bastani & Bayati, 2020) while additionally enforcing DBIC constraints. The weighted risk score reaches 0.291 at ε=0.025 and 0.265 at ε=0.035, substantially outperforming the Physician baseline (always-Medium) score of 0.20. At a loose budget (ε=0.045), the error rate degrades above 0.40 across all prior variance settings. RCB correctly identifies 50% of Low-dose patients and 5% of High-dose patients, versus 0% for the Physician baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares RCB against two baselines: (1) the Physician Standard of Care (always recommend Medium dose) and (2) the Lasso Bandit of Bastani & Bayati (2020). Against the Physician baseline, RCB dramatically outperforms on the weighted risk score (0.291 vs. 0.20 at ε=0.025), primarily by correctly identifying 50% of Low-dose and 5% of High-dose patients where the Physician achieves 0% on both minority classes. Against the Lasso Bandit, RCB matches its error rate (~0.35) at the tightest budget (ε=0.025) while operating under stricter conditions: RCB enforces the DBIC incentive-compatibility constraint and does not require prior knowledge of non-zero feature counts. At looser budgets (ε≥0.035), RCB's performance degrades relative to the Lasso Bandit, with error rates exceeding 0.40 at ε=0.045, indicating that the incentive constraint imposes a measurable cost when the budget is too permissive.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Bayesian incentive-compatible (BIC) exploration line initiated by Kremer et al. (2014) and Mansour et al. (2020) from multi-armed bandits to linear contextual bandits with stochastic user covariates, departing from Sellke (2023) and Kalvit et al. (2024) which assume fixed arm features. The core algorithmic novelty is replacing posterior-sampling-based BIC mechanisms with a two-stage design: a calibrated cold-start phase followed by Inverse Proportional Gap Sampling (IPGS) inherited from Foster & Rakhlin (2020) and Simchi-Levi & Xu (2022), which enables modular integration of arbitrary offline learning oracles. The sample complexity analysis builds on ridge regression prediction error bounds from Mourtada & Rosasco (2022) to derive the cold-start threshold N(ε).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first clean characterization of the 'price of incentivizing exploration' under stochastic covariates, showing the cold-start cost scales as O(K³d/ε²) and that once this threshold is met, standard Õ(√(KdT)) regret is achievable while maintaining DBIC. The modular IPGS architecture is practically significant because it allows non-linear oracles (neural networks, kernel methods) to be plugged in without re-deriving incentive guarantees. Key open questions include the prohibitive O(K³) scaling for large action spaces, whether the myopic user assumption holds in practice, and how to handle combinatorial or deep retrieval settings. The warfarin dosing application demonstrates relevance to clinical decision support but is limited to K=3 arms.
