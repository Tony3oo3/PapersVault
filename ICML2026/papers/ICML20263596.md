## Problem

Existing theoretical results on transformer expressive power assume real-valued parameters and exact arithmetic, guaranteeing permutation-equivariance and universal approximation of equivariant functions. However, real-world transformers operate with finite floating-point sets and inexact operations, raising the question of whether these fundamental properties survive under IEEE-754 arithmetic. This paper shows that the answers are no: floating-point transformers can break permutation-equivariance and fail to represent certain equivariant functions for large sequence lengths.

## Input / Output

The input is a d_in × n matrix over a finite floating-point set F_{p,q} (e.g., FP32, bfloat16, FP8), representing a sequence of n tokens. The output is a d_out × n matrix over the same or extended floating-point set. A critical structural constraint is the reduction order: matrix multiplications and softmax summations follow a deterministic binary tree T_n (or left-to-right order), and an optional higher-precision accumulation format F'_{p',q'} is used internally before rounding back to F_{p,q}. For Theorem 2, the domain is restricted to Δ_n where all n input tokens are pairwise distinct.

## Method

The paper proves four main theorems characterizing floating-point transformer expressivity. First, by exploiting the non-associativity of floating-point addition in the attention computation V ⊗ σ(K^T ⊗ Q), the authors construct attention layers that extract partial input-order information encoded by the reduction tree T_n, enabling representation of all G(T_n)-equivariant functions (a strict subset of S_n-equivariant functions) without positional encoding (Theorem 2). Second, they show that for n ≥ 9×2^{p'}, repeated floating-point addition saturates so that (α,β)-similar inputs always produce (α,β)-similar outputs, preventing representation of certain permutation-equivariant functions (Theorem 3). Third, for bounded n (≤ 6×2^p − 2 without high-precision accumulation, or ≤ 2^{p+1} − 2 with p' ≥ 2p), they construct explicit transformers that represent all permutation-equivariant functions by encoding the full input multiset distribution in each column (Theorems 4–5). Finally, they identify G(T_n)-equivariance as the minimal equivariance property and prove that additive positional encoding is inherently lossy since x ⊕ z is not injective on F.

## Results

The paper establishes three main representability bounds for floating-point transformers. Under left-to-right summation without high-precision accumulation, all permutation-equivariant functions are representable when n ≤ 6×2^p − 2 (Theorem 4), but not all are representable when n ≥ 9×2^{p'} (Theorem 3). With high-precision accumulation (p' ≥ 2p) and arbitrary deterministic reduction orders, the positive bound extends to n ≤ 2^{p+1} − 2 (Theorem 5). Additionally, Theorem 2 shows that floating-point transformers can represent all G(T_n)-equivariant functions on inputs with distinct tokens, meaning they are not permutation-equivariant in general — a fundamental departure from exact-arithmetic transformers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or method comparisons. The key contrast is with prior exact-arithmetic results (Yun et al., 2020a; Alberti et al., 2023), which establish universal approximation of permutation-equivariant functions regardless of sequence length. The present work shows this universality breaks under floating-point arithmetic: representability is lost for sufficiently large n (Theorem 3) and regained only under bounded n (Theorems 4–5). The paper also contrasts with prior floating-point neural network results (Hwang et al., 2025b; Park et al., 2026), which established universal approximation for feed-forward networks under floating-point operations; here, the non-associativity of floating-point addition in the attention mechanism introduces a new obstruction (G(T_n)-equivariance) that has no analogue in feed-forward settings. No empirical comparisons are made, as the contributions are entirely in the form of representability theorems with explicit sequence-length thresholds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the floating-point neural network expressivity line (Park et al. 2024; Hwang et al. 2025b; Park et al. 2026) from feed-forward architectures to transformers, and contrasts its findings with the exact-arithmetic transformer universality results of Yun et al. (2020a) and Alberti et al. (2023). The key methodological departure is exploiting the non-associativity of IEEE-754 addition within the attention mechanism's reduction tree, a phenomenon absent from prior exact-arithmetic analyses. The paper also generalizes the reduction-order framework of Park et al. (2026) to the multi-head attention setting and introduces high-precision accumulation (as in GPU tensor-core operations) into the expressivity analysis for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that floating-point transformers are not permutation-equivariant challenges a foundational assumption in transformer theory and explains a subtle gap between theoretical guarantees and real-world implementations. The finding that additive positional encoding can strictly reduce representability (Lemma 8) has practical implications for low-precision training (FP8, bfloat16), where positional information may be silently lost. Open questions include whether the bounded-sequence-length representability results (Theorems 4–5) are tight, how non-deterministic GPU reduction orders (Section 4.3) further degrade expressivity, and whether these theoretical limitations manifest in measurable training or generalization effects at practical sequence lengths.
