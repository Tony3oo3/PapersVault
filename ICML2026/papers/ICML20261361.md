## Problem

Physics-based humanoid motion tracking has not consistently benefited from scaling motion capture datasets, as large in-the-wild corpora introduce systematic artifacts (floating, foot sliding, ground penetration) that corrupt imitation signals and lead to brittle policies. This paper asks a foundational data-centric question: what makes motion data *valuable* for learning robust tracking policies, and whether a small, carefully curated subset can outperform the full dataset.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes large kinematic MoCap corpora (AMASS ~14K clips, PHUMA ~76K clips, LaFAN1 ~1.5K clips) as sequences of joint positions, velocities, and root velocities, processed in 4-second windows. It produces a compact training subset (as low as 3% of the original) selected in a learned semantic embedding space, which is then used to train PPO-based whole-body trackers (Any2Track, TWIST2) on the Unitree G1. At inference, the trained policy takes an unseen reference trajectory and outputs whole-body joint control commands for real-time tracking.

## Method

GQS (General Quality Selection) is a three-stage curation pipeline whose ordering is critical: (1) simulator-grounded physics filtering replays each motion in a rigid-body simulator and computes a composite feasibility score from six weighted penalties (floating, ground penetration, velocity violation, foot sliding, self-collision, jerk), discarding clips below a threshold of 90; (2) a Periodic Autoencoder decomposes motions into amplitude, frequency, phase, and offset in a sinusoidal latent space, and a phase-invariant global embedding is obtained by temporally averaging [amplitude, frequency] across windows; (3) Global Weighted FPS selects the final subset by maximizing a hybrid score of normalized distance to the selected set (diversity) and rank-normalized complexity (kinetic energy + acceleration), initialized with the highest-complexity anchor. The key design principle is hierarchical: infeasible motions must be removed before embedding (to avoid distorting the manifold), and complexity weighting must come last (to prevent high-energy artifacts from dominating selection). Weights for the physics score are calibrated via sensitivity analysis, revealing that floating and foot sliding are toxic while high velocity and self-collision are actually friendly to learning.

## Results

On AMASS, GQS-curated data at just 3% of the full corpus outperforms the unfiltered full-data baseline across all metrics for both trackers: Any2Track improves from 94.2% to 95.6% Success Rate and from 0.114 to 0.108 rad MPJPE, while TWIST2 improves from 82.5% to 86.1% SR and from 0.099 to 0.092 rad MPJPE. At 10% data, GQS reaches its peak performance (95.9% SR, 0.107 rad MPJPE for Any2Track; 86.8% SR, 0.084 rad MPJPE for TWIST2), a 15% relative MPJPE reduction over the full-data TWIST2 baseline. On PHUMA, the 10% GQS subset also surpasses the full dataset in both in-domain MPJPE and cross-domain zero-shot transfer to AMASS (92.8% vs. 91.0% SR).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Any2Track full-data baseline (94.2% SR, 0.114 rad MPJPE), GQS at 3% data wins on all three metrics (95.6% SR, 0.108 rad MPJPE, 29.87 mm MPKPE), and the 10% variant further improves to 95.9% SR and 0.107 rad MPJPE. Against TWIST2 full-data (82.5% SR, 0.099 rad MPJPE), GQS 10% achieves a 4.3-point SR gain and a 15% relative MPJPE reduction (0.084 rad), the largest absolute improvement in the paper. The PHC baseline (Luo et al., 2023a), which applies physics-based filtering without diversity or complexity selection, provides only marginal gains over full data (94.8% vs. 94.2% SR for Any2Track) and is clearly outperformed by the full GQS pipeline, confirming that physics filtering alone is insufficient. Random 3% subsampling causes catastrophic collapse (83.8% SR for Any2Track, 64.9% for TWIST2), demonstrating that the gains are attributable to intelligent curation rather than mere data reduction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as the first data-centric study for physics-based humanoid motion tracking, departing from the prevailing 'more data, better generalization' paradigm borrowed from vision and language. Methodologically, it builds on Periodic Autoencoders (Starke et al., 2022) for semantic motion embedding and adapts Farthest Point Sampling with a physics-informed complexity bias, while grounding feasibility checks in rigid-body simulator replay. Conceptually, it reframes the problem: rather than improving the tracking algorithm itself, it argues that the quality of the training signal (feasibility, diversity, complexity) is the bottleneck, and that existing trackers (Any2Track, TWIST2, PHC) are underutilized by noisy, redundant corpora.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 3% of curated AMASS outperforms the full dataset across multiple trackers and datasets challenges a core assumption in physics-based RL and offers a concrete, plug-and-play recipe (GQS) that reduces training costs by an order of magnitude without architectural changes. The non-monotonic physical-score analysis and the counterintuitive 'friendly' classification of high-velocity/high-jerk motions provide actionable insight into what actually helps policy learning. Open questions include whether the rule-based quality scoring can be replaced by learned preference models (the authors suggest RLHF-style approaches), whether the ARS ratio heuristic generalizes beyond the two validated corpora, and whether the framework extends to other embodied tasks like VLA manipulation.
