## Problem

Agentic AI systems built on LLMs are rapidly entering high-stakes clinical decision-making, yet their deployment in domains like transplant selection lacks equity and explainability as core design constraints. This paper demonstrates through empirical simulation that even high-performing multi-agent systems systematically disadvantage patients based on sex, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status by relying on non-clinical proxy variables, and that their decentralized architecture creates opaque, unauditable decision pipelines. The authors argue that without fairness-aware design and targeted deployment strategies, agentic AI will codify institutional biases under the guise of objectivity.

## Input / Output

The system consumes natural language clinical vignettes generated by a separate 'medical scribe' agent (temperature 0.1) from structured SRTR registry data spanning 59 variables including demographics, MELD scores, comorbidities, and social determinants of health (insurance type, education level, Area Deprivation Index). Each of the four committee agents produces an independent eligibility decision with rationale text; the final output is a majority-vote committee decision with the hepatologist agent serving as tie-breaker. A notable design choice is that contraindication cases (N=1,379) are synthetically assigned to SRTR patients since the registry only captures waitlisted or eligible candidates, creating a hybrid empirical-simulation evaluation design.

## Method

The authors build a four-agent multi-agent system using GPT-4o (temperature 0.7) instantiated as transplant hepatologist, surgeon, cardiologist, and social worker, orchestrated via CrewAI/LangChain with zero-shot chain-of-thought prompting and self-consistency (5 samples, majority vote). Each agent independently evaluates transplant eligibility from the vignette, and committee-level decisions emerge through majority voting with hepatologist tie-breaking, mimicking real-world transplant committee dynamics. The key empirical contribution is a fairness audit revealing Disparate Impact scores below 0.80 for women, Black patients, and lower-SES groups, with cosine similarity analysis showing the social worker agent disproportionately weighted insurance status, education, and ADI as decision drivers. The paper then proposes a five-pillar technical roadmap covering data modeling (longitudinal features, missingness-as-signal, SDOH flagging), agent architecture (causal reasoning modules, Bayesian uncertainty heads), explainability (trajectory-aware attribution, counterfactual rationalization), fairness auditing (subgroup-specific inter-agent audits, fairness-constrained training), and deployment (clinician-in-the-loop, federated adaptation, immutable audit logging).

## Results

The multi-agent AI-Selection Committee (AI-SC) achieves 98.97% accuracy (sensitivity 0.99, specificity 1.00, F1 0.99) on transplant candidacy classification over 8,412 SRTR cases, outperforming all single-LLM baselines. For survival prediction, the best prompting configuration (zero-shot-CoT with self-consistency) reaches 92.0% accuracy at 6 months and 86.6% at 1 year. Critically, despite high aggregate performance, Disparate Impact (DI) scores fall below the 0.80 fairness threshold for female patients, Black individuals, patients with only high-school education, and those in the most socioeconomically deprived ADI quintiles, revealing systematic subgroup disadvantage driven by reliance on social proxy variables (insurance, education, ADI).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The AI-SC multi-agent system is compared against four single-LLM baselines (Copilot, Gemini, Grok, GPT-4o) on identical structured clinical vignettes. AI-SC achieves 98.97% accuracy, surpassing the strongest single-LLM baseline, Gemini (96.77%), by 2.2 percentage points, and GPT-4o (93.54%) by 5.4 points. Copilot performs poorly at 58.06% accuracy with only 0.50 sensitivity, indicating a strong bias toward rejecting candidates. Gemini achieves perfect sensitivity (1.00) but at the cost of lower specificity (0.80), while AI-SC maintains both sensitivity and specificity near 1.00. The paper's central finding, however, is that this accuracy advantage does not translate into equitable performance: all systems, including AI-SC, exhibit DI scores below 0.80 for multiple protected subgroups, with the social-worker agent showing the strongest reliance on non-clinical proxy variables (insurance, education, ADI) as revealed by cosine-similarity attribution analysis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes existing multi-agent LLM orchestration frameworks (CrewAI, LangChain, AutoGen) and applies them to a transplant-medicine context that has not previously been stress-tested with agentic architectures. Conceptually, it sits at the intersection of the healthcare algorithmic-fairness literature (Obermeyer et al., Seyyed-Kalantari et al.) and the emerging explainability-in-multi-agent-systems line (Alzetta et al.), but departs from both by demonstrating that role-specialized agents can independently encode proxy-variable bias that only becomes visible at the committee-consensus level. The fairness auditing methodology (Disparate Impact, cosine-similarity feature attribution) is borrowed from single-model settings and extended here to a decentralized, multi-agent decision pipeline, which is the paper's primary methodological contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper makes a concrete, if simulation-based, case that high aggregate accuracy (98.2%) in a multi-agent clinical system can coexist with systematic subgroup harm (DI < 0.80 for women, Black, Hispanic, and low-SES patients), and that the decentralized agent architecture makes this bias harder to detect than in monolithic models. The deployment-divide argument—that agentic AI will disproportionately benefit well-resourced centers—raises practical equity questions that the ML community has under-addressed. Key limitations include reliance on a single LLM architecture (GPT-4o), synthetic contraindication assignment, and the absence of prospective clinical validation, leaving open whether the proposed fairness mechanisms (adversarial debiasing, counterfactual modules, federated adaptation) actually close the gap in real-world multi-agent pipelines.
