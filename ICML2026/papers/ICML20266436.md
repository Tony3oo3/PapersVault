## Problem

Foundation-model-enabled robots deployed in open-world, human-centered environments face safety failures spanning physical, semantic, and social dimensions that no single mechanism—whether model-internal alignment, a monolithic end-to-end policy, or a single-perspective external filter—can address simultaneously. The paper argues that under long-tailed hazards, non-stationary human interaction, and evolving specifications, safety enforcement must be architecturally modular, non-bypassable at execution time, and independently verifiable across the full autonomy stack.

## Input / Output

The guardrail consumes execution-relevant proposals from the upstream FM stack through channels decoupled from the primary autonomy pipeline: perception outputs (images, depth, semantic labels), candidate plans and trajectories from the planner, and low-level control commands from the controller. Critically, it interfaces with explicit, execution-relevant representations (regions of interest, candidate trajectories, control vectors) rather than internal latent embeddings, to reduce correlated failure modes. Outputs are structured as dimension-specific risk signals feeding into two intervention submodules: the decision gate returns approve/reject/clarify verdicts on plans, and the action gate returns modified or projected control commands that satisfy physical safety constraints.

## Method

The proposed architecture is a two-layer modular safety guardrail: a Monitoring and Evaluation Layer with dedicated submodules for action, decision, and human-centered safety (using techniques like control-theoretic monitors, LLM-as-a-Judge, conformal prediction, and intent inference), and an Intervention Layer comprising a Decision Gate (planning-level screening that blocks semantically or socially unsafe plans and triggers replanning) and an Action Gate (execution-time enforcement via shielding, trajectory projection, or CBF-based filtering). Two forms of modularity are enforced: external modularity ensures the guardrail is operationally independent of the FMs it monitors (no shared parameters, training objectives, or optimization), and internal modularity decomposes safety into specialized, independently testable submodules with non-overlapping responsibilities. Beyond simple module stacking, the paper introduces two co-design principles: representation alignment (preserving spatially grounded uncertainty structure, e.g., occupancy tubes or risk fields, so the action gate can tighten constraints directionally rather than uniformly) and conservatism allocation (coordinating strictness between the decision and action gates so that plans are conditionally approved when the action gate can certify feasibility, avoiding stacked conservatism that produces infeasible or overly conservative behavior).

## Results

This is a position paper without quantitative experiments or benchmark evaluations. The paper's contributions are architectural: a three-dimensional safety taxonomy (action, decision, human-centered safety), a two-layer modular guardrail design (Monitoring & Evaluation Layer + Intervention Layer with Decision Gate and Action Gate), and two co-design principles (representation alignment and conservatism allocation). Three deployment examples (household robot with LLM planner, VLA-based manipulation, and a third scenario) serve as qualitative existence proofs showing failure modes that single-perspective safety cannot address but the modular architecture can resolve through cross-layer coordination.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not run empirical comparisons against baselines; instead, it qualitatively analyzes three alternative safety views and identifies specific gaps in each. View A (safety embedded in the model via alignment/pretraining) is argued to lack a non-bypassable runtime boundary and cannot prevent unsafe actions when distribution shift or novel hazards occur. View B1 (action-level add-ons like CBFs and safety filters) enforces physical constraints but cannot reason about semantic hazards or human-contextual risks. View B2 (decision-level add-ons like LLM-as-a-Judge or learned world models) filters unsafe plans but cannot guarantee physical safety during execution and degrades under distribution shift. View B3 (human-centered add-ons like preference learning and trust modeling) improves interaction quality but provides no hard enforcement when misalignment occurs. The proposed modular guardrail is positioned as the only architecture that simultaneously covers all three safety dimensions with non-bypassable execution-time enforcement, independent verifiability, and composability of heterogeneous mechanisms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not build on a single prior method but synthesizes three largely separate safety literatures—control-theoretic safety filters (CBFs, Hamilton-Jacobi reachability, MPC-based shielding), LLM/VLM safety mechanisms (alignment, LLM-as-a-Judge, conformal prediction), and HRI safety (intent inference, trust modeling, preference learning)—into a unified two-layer modular architecture. It explicitly departs from both the 'safety-by-construction' view (View A: internal alignment suffices) and the 'single-perspective external add-on' view (View B: one narrow filter is enough), arguing that neither is structurally adequate for open-world FM-enabled robotics. The co-design principles of representation alignment and conservatism allocation are novel framings that go beyond simply stacking known modules, though each individual component (safety filters, semantic judges, uncertainty estimators) is well-established in its own subfield.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three-dimensional safety taxonomy (action, decision, human-centered) and the modular guardrail architecture provide a common organizational language for a field that has been addressing these concerns in silos, potentially accelerating cross-disciplinary collaboration. Key open questions the paper itself highlights include: how to ensure genuine operational independence when guardrails themselves rely on FMs that may share training data with the monitored model, the computational cost of test-time safety scaling, and the fundamental intractability of formally verifying open-world semantic and human-centered safety properties in real time. As a position paper with no empirical validation, its impact depends on whether the community adopts the architectural framing and develops the composable modules and benchmarks it calls for.
