## Problem

Standard LLM evaluation relies on average accuracy over fixed item sets of thousands of questions, treating all items as equally informative and requiring every model to answer the full benchmark regardless of how much information additional items provide. This practice is computationally expensive, masks meaningful ability differences among models with identical accuracy, and offers no uncertainty quantification. No prior framework combines psychometric item calibration with adaptive, information-guided item selection to achieve precision-targeted LLM evaluation at scale.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a binary item-response matrix (correct/incorrect) from 3,000+ LLMs across five benchmarks, along with calibrated 3PL item parameters (discrimination a_i, difficulty b_i, guessing c_i) estimated via partition-based common-person linking. It produces a latent ability estimate θ_ℓ with associated standard error SE(θ_ℓ), the specific sequence of administered items, and a p-IRT reconstructed accuracy score. The key operational detail is that the adaptive test terminates dynamically once SE(θ) falls below a user-specified threshold (0.1, 0.2, or 0.3), requiring only 30–90 items per model rather than the full bank of 627–5,600 items.

## Method

ATLAS operates in two phases: (1) a calibration phase that fits 3PL IRT models to partitioned item subsets (each ≥100 items) and links them onto a unified scale using the shared model population as common persons, reducing computational complexity from O(|I|³) to O(K·max_k|I_k|³); and (2) an adaptive testing phase that initializes θ at 0, selects items via randomesque sampling from the top-5 highest Fisher information candidates, updates ability using EAP estimation after each response, and terminates when SE(θ) drops below a precision threshold after a minimum of 30 items. The use of precision-based stopping (rather than fixed-length) ensures consistent measurement accuracy across the full ability spectrum, while WLE is used for whole-bank reference estimation to handle the extreme heterogeneity of LLM populations (θ ≈ −3 to +3). The framework also incorporates psychometric filtering (low-variance removal and point-biserial discrimination screening) and reports M₂/RMSEA model-fit diagnostics to validate calibration quality.

## Results

ATLAS achieves the lowest Information Efficiency Score (IES) across all five benchmarks, indicating the best accuracy-efficiency tradeoff. On TruthfulQA, ATLAS attains the best ability MAE of 0.064 using only 48 items (IES 0.300), and on HellaSwag it reaches MAE 0.157 with just 41 items (IES 0.266). Across all benchmarks, ATLAS requires only 30–89 items per model (up to 90% reduction from full banks of 627–5,600 items) while matching or exceeding the accuracy of static baselines that use 97–249 items. Reconstructed accuracies from ATLAS ability estimates match raw full-bank accuracies with MAE consistently below 5% across all benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ATLAS dominates both static IRT-based baselines on the Information Efficiency Score (IES) across all five benchmarks. Against TinyBenchmarks (97–100 items, clustering-based selection), ATLAS achieves substantially lower IES: 0.300 vs. 1.370 on TruthfulQA, 0.266 vs. 0.797 on HellaSwag, and 0.655 vs. 1.221 on WinoGrande, while also producing lower ability MAE on TruthfulQA (0.064 vs. 0.145) and HellaSwag (0.157 vs. 0.198). Against MetaBench (133–249 items, curated splits), ATLAS matches or beats MetaBench-Primary on WinoGrande MAE (0.155 vs. 0.152) while using nearly half the items (70 vs. 133), and dramatically outperforms it on HellaSwag (MAE 0.157 vs. 0.514, IES 0.266 vs. 1.990). MetaBench-Secondary performs poorly on HellaSwag (MAE 1.570, IES 3.788), indicating poor item coverage for that domain. In the relaxed-precision setting (SE ≤ 0.3), ATLAS uses only 30–32 items per model while maintaining IES values of 0.20–0.37, far below any static baseline. Model-fit diagnostics (Table 1) further show ATLAS achieves acceptable-to-good RMSEA (0.044–0.069) across all benchmarks, whereas TinyBenchmarks exhibits severe misfit (RMSEA > 360) and MetaBench shows poor fit on TruthfulQA (RMSEA 0.139).

## Relation to Prior Work

ATLAS transplants the well-established psychometric framework of computerized adaptive testing (CAT) into LLM evaluation, building directly on the 3PL IRT model (Birnbaum, 1968), Fisher information-guided item selection (Weiss, 1982; Kingsbury & Zara, 1989), and common-person linking (Brennan, 2014). It departs from prior IRT-based LLM benchmark reduction methods—TinyBenchmarks and MetaBench—by replacing static, fixed-length item subsets with a dynamic, precision-targeted adaptive loop, and from the concurrent Fluid Benchmarking work by operating on post-training evaluation with 3,000+ models and SE-based stopping rather than fixed-length pretraining-time adaptation. The p-IRT accuracy reconstruction estimator is borrowed from Polo et al. (2024) to bridge the gap between latent ability and the accuracy metric the community still reports.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 90% item reduction with maintained measurement precision offers a concrete, scalable path to cheaper LLM evaluation, and the finding that 23–31% of models shift by more than 10 rank positions under IRT versus accuracy suggests that current leaderboards may systematically misorder models, particularly at performance extremes. Open questions include whether the unidimensional IRT assumption holds for multi-ability LLMs, how the framework extends to open-ended or multi-turn evaluation, and whether item parameters remain stable as model architectures and training data shift over time (the temporal generalization experiment shows modest but non-trivial degradation). The paper also highlights that model-fit diagnostics are rarely reported in existing IRT-based benchmark pipelines, raising the question of how many published reduced benchmarks rest on poorly calibrated item parameters.
