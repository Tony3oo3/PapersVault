## Problem

Pairwise comparison-based evaluations (Elo, Bradley-Terry) are widely used to rank LLMs, but concerns persist that they reward superficial style or reflect judge biases rather than measuring true correctness. This paper asks a largely unanswered question: when ground truth is available, do preference-based rankings actually align with accuracy-based rankings? The controlled comparison is only possible on benchmarks with verifiable answers, making this a targeted but important validation study.

## Input / Output

The method takes freeform benchmark questions (multiple-choice benchmarks are converted by removing answer options) and pairs of model-generated answers as input to an LLM judge, which returns a binary preference (answer 1 or 2). The judge has no access to ground truth. The output is a Bradley-Terry score per model, yielding a ranking that is then compared against the ground-truth accuracy ranking. A direct-judge baseline is also evaluated, where the same LLM classifies individual answers as CORRECT/INCORRECT/NOT_ATTEMPTED without seeing the ground truth.

## Method

The paper converts five established benchmarks (MMLU Pro, GPQA Diamond, SimpleQA, GSM8K, BBH) into freeform generative evaluation protocols, generates answers from 10–27 models per benchmark, and collects pairwise comparisons using LLM judges (gpt-oss-20b, gpt-oss-120b, o3, phi-4, gemma-3-27b-it) without ground-truth access. Preferences are aggregated via the Bradley-Terry model, which estimates model strengths by maximizing the likelihood of observed pairwise outcomes. The key empirical finding is that Bradley-Terry rankings achieve Spearman ρ > 0.9 with accuracy rankings, substantially outperform the direct-judge baseline when the judge is weak (e.g., 4.9% accuracy on SimpleQA), and remain robust to style and self-preference bias correction. The paper further identifies 'echo' (repetition after the final answer) as a causal driver of judge preference on non-discriminative pairs, where both answers are correct or both are incorrect, via a controlled intervention experiment on BBH.

## Results

Across five benchmarks (MMLU Pro, GPQA Diamond, SimpleQA, GSM8K, BBH), Bradley-Terry rankings from pairwise comparisons achieve Spearman's Rho > 0.9 and Kendall's Tau > 0.8 with ground-truth accuracy rankings, with Pearson's R > 0.87 for linear correlation. On four out of five benchmarks, only 6–8.9% of model pairs need to be swapped (Kendall's distance) to recover the accuracy-based ranking. In the critical weak-judge regime (gpt-oss-20b, 4.9% accuracy on SimpleQA), Bradley-Terry substantially outperforms the direct-judge baseline, reducing rank distance from 38.2% to 20%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the Direct Judge, where the LLM classifies each answer independently as correct/incorrect/not-attempted without ground truth. On average across benchmarks, Bradley-Terry and Direct Judge perform comparably: for gpt-oss-20b, BT edges out DJ (τ = 0.80 vs. 0.76), while for o3 the direct judge has a slight advantage (τ = 0.85 vs. 0.81). The decisive difference emerges in the weak-judge regime: on SimpleQA with gpt-oss-20b (4.9% accuracy), Bradley-Terry nearly halves the rank distance compared to the direct judge (20% vs. 38.2%), a pattern that holds for 3 out of 4 weak judges tested. Among rank aggregation methods, Bradley-Terry achieves the highest mean Kendall's Tau (0.7925) compared to Elo (0.7188), WinRate (0.7743), and TrueSkill (0.7673), confirming it as the best-suited aggregator for static accuracy-based evaluation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of the LLM-as-a-judge literature (Zheng et al., 2023; Chiang et al., 2024) and rank aggregation theory (Bradley & Terry, 1952), using the Bradley-Terry model as its core aggregation method and borrowing the bias-correction feature framework from Arena-Hard (Li et al., 2024b). Where prior work has focused on identifying and quantifying judge biases (length, self-preference, presentation), this paper takes a complementary validation angle: it converts established ground-truth benchmarks into freeform pairwise-comparison protocols to directly test whether preference-based rankings recover accuracy-based rankings. It departs from Kelley & Wilson (2025), which also compares tournament rankings to accuracy but relies on ground-truth labels to determine pairwise winners, by instead using LLM judges without ground-truth access.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that Bradley-Terry rankings achieve Spearman > 0.9 with accuracy across five benchmarks—and that this holds even when ~58% of pairs are non-discriminative—provides much-needed empirical reassurance for the widespread use of pairwise comparison evaluation in LLM leaderboards. The weak-judge result (Bradley-Terry substantially outperforming direct evaluation when the judge is near-random) has direct practical implications for frontier evaluation where judge quality is uncertain. Key limitations include restriction to discriminative tasks with ground truth, the MCQ-to-freeform conversion that may introduce format mismatch, and the open question of whether these findings generalize to truly open-ended settings. The identification of echo as a causal driver on non-discriminative pairs opens a concrete direction for understanding and mitigating superficial-cue reliance in judges.
