## Problem

Existing sequential A/B testing designs are either myopic (minimizing only immediate covariate imbalance) or rely on correctly specified linear models with additive treatment effects and no carryover effects. This paper addresses the gap of designing sequential experiments that remain valid under model misspecification, explicitly account for finite-sample covariate randomness, and handle dynamic carryover effects in a unified framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264974/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes sequentially arriving covariate vectors X_i ∈ R^p and maintains running imbalance statistics (Δ_i for covariate imbalance, Γ_i for sieve-feature imbalance in the bandit setting; four matrix-valued statistics B_it, C_it, D_it, F_it in the dynamic setting) as its Markov state. Before the experiment begins, population-level matrices (Σ, Ξ, U) are estimated offline from historical data, and value functions are pre-trained via synthetic rollouts. At each arrival, the method outputs a binary treatment assignment a_i ∈ {±1} chosen by minimizing the learned value function, and after all N units are processed, the OLS plug-in estimator yields the ATE.

## Method

The core mechanism is a three-step pipeline: (1) a sieve approximation of the unknown nonlinear outcome component f(X) combined with the orthogonality constraint E[Xf(X)]=0 reparameterizes f through a null-space basis U, yielding a worst-case MSE upper bound that depends only on observable imbalance statistics and a tunable magnitude η; (2) this bound is optimized via dynamic programming with Markov state (Δ_i, Γ_i), where value functions are approximated by DNNs trained on synthetic rollouts (Algorithm 1); (3) for dynamic settings with carryover effects, a hierarchical decomposition applies across-day DP over N days (each day as one decision stage) and within-day RL (actor-critic) over T time intervals per day, with the day-level objective from DP serving as the RL reward. This unifies robustness to misspecification (via orthogonalization), finite-sample awareness (via history-dependent allocation), and long-horizon optimization (via DP/RL) in a single framework.

## Results

The paper evaluates its Robust Sequential Design (RSD) against five baselines across contextual bandit (N ∈ {21, 28, 35, 42} days, 400 replications), dynamic (T ∈ {6, 12} intervals, 1000 replications), and real-data-based (ride-sharing, 400 replications) settings. RSD consistently achieves the lowest empirical MSE of the ATE estimator across all settings, with the advantage being most pronounced in small-sample regimes (e.g., N = 21). The MSE decays with increasing N, confirming large-sample consistency. In the dynamic setting, RSD remains the most competitive method across three levels of model misspecification (nonlinearity degree). No specific numerical MSE values are reported in the text; results are presented as 95% CI plots in Figures 2 and 3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RSD outperforms all four baselines on empirical MSE across every experimental setting. Against the non-robust DP variant (NRD, which reduces to Bhat et al. 2020 under additive effects), RSD wins because NRD fails to guard against model misspecification and does not consistently achieve the lowest MSE. Against the switchback design (SBD, Bojinov et al. 2023), RSD achieves substantially lower MSE, as SBD uses a fixed alternating schedule that is neither finite-sample-aware nor long-horizon-optimized. Against the Neyman balanced design (NBD, Cai & Rafi 2024) and the Bayesian biased-coin design (BBD, Ball et al. 1993), RSD shows significantly lower MSE because both are myopic sequential strategies that minimize immediate imbalance without accounting for how current assignments affect future covariate states. In the dynamic setting, RSD also outperforms the TMDP and NMDP designs (Li et al. 2023, NeurIPS 2023), which extend Neyman allocation to time series but rely on correctly specified models and lack robustness to misspecification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of work: the dynamic programming approach to sequential A/B testing initiated by Bhat et al. (2020), which optimized long-horizon MSE but required correctly specified linear models with additive effects and elliptical covariates, and the minimax robust design tradition (Fang & Wiens, 2000; Wiens, 2009) that bounds worst-case MSE under model misspecification via orthogonalization but was restricted to static, offline designs. The key conceptual departure is combining orthogonalization-based robustness with sequential DP allocation, and further extending the framework to dynamic settings with carryover effects through a hierarchical decomposition into across-day DP and within-day RL. This addresses a gap the authors identify: prior sequential designs are either myopic (biased-coin, switchback) or require strong model assumptions (Bhat et al., Li et al. 2023, Sun et al. 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first unified framework that simultaneously handles model misspecification, finite-sample covariate imbalance, and carryover effects in sequential A/B testing, which is practically important given that treatment effects in industry settings are typically small and models are rarely correctly specified. A key limitation is the reliance on a surrogate MSE that ignores variability from learning covariate dynamics, and the weak-signal assumption (Assumption A.7) that restricts applicability to settings with small treatment effects. Open questions include how the sieve approximation quality affects design performance in high-dimensional covariate spaces, whether the hierarchical DP+RL decomposition scales to longer horizons, and how sensitive the design is to the choice of basis functions and the tuning parameter η.
