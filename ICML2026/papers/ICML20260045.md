## Problem

Medical image segmentation models achieve strong benchmark performance but remain opaque, making it difficult to diagnose systematic failures, understand what drives performance degradation under dataset shift, or intervene in a principled manner without costly retraining. Existing interpretability tools (saliency maps, attention visualizations) highlight correlated regions but provide no causal insight into how internal representations encode population-specific priors or how to correct errors at the representation level. No prior work applies mechanistic model diffing—comparing internal feature spaces across independently trained models—to dense prediction tasks like medical image segmentation.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes 256×256 T1-weighted brain MRI volumes and pre-trained segmentation models (SegFormer-B4 or U-Net) trained independently on each of four cohorts (IXI healthy, BraTS Adult, Pediatric, and SSA glioma). It extracts activations from a single fixed mid-level encoder layer, decomposes them into sparse latent codes via BatchTopK SAEs (k=32 active latents, 16× expansion), and produces: (i) a per-latent semantic label from geometry- and spatial-based metrics, (ii) a partition of latents into shared (population-invariant) and dataset-specific sets via cross-dataset Hungarian alignment, and (iii) targeted latent steering vectors (additive or multiplicative offsets) applied at inference time to recover segmentation failures without retraining.

## Method

Med-SegLens trains a BatchTopK sparse autoencoder on intermediate activations of each dataset-specific segmentation model, yielding a monosemantic latent basis per model. A geometry- and spatially grounded auto-interpretation pipeline (brain-edge ratio, depth, spatial entropy, centroid localization, class association) assigns anatomical and pathological semantics to each latent. The core diffing procedure computes cosine similarity matrices between encoder and decoder weight vectors of two SAEs, applies the Hungarian algorithm to each, and defines a latent as shared only if both projections agree on the same counterpart with similarity ≥ 0.8—enforcing cross-space consistency that single-projection baselines (greedy, Sinkhorn, encoder-only) lack. Causal validation is performed via feature swapping (replacing shared latents between models preserves spatial structure; non-shared swaps disrupt it) and latent steering (scaling selected latents by α at inference time). For OOD adaptation, additive latent steering (ALS) suppresses false-positive-dominated errors (Adult→Pediatric, edema Dice 39.4%→74.2%) while multiplicative latent steering (MLS) amplifies false-negative-dominated errors (Pediatric→Adult), all without retraining or access to target-domain models.

## Results

On the headline cross-domain adaptation task (Adult→Pediatric), latent steering (ALS) improves edema Dice from 39.4% to 74.2% and mean Dice from 67.8% to 76.9%, while the reverse direction (Pediatric→Adult) with MLS improves edema Dice from 45.2% to 49.3% and mean Dice from 70.6% to 71.4%. For instance-level failure recovery, targeted latent amplification recovers 70.6% of Adult, 60.0% of Pediatric, and 63.3% of SSA failure cases, raising mean Dice on those subsets from 25.3/58.8/59.2% to 43.0/62.0/63.8% respectively. At the class level, edema-specific intervention lifts edema Dice from 65.8% to 69.1% and overall mean Dice from 81.8% to 82.6% across 2,844 failure cases, all without retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two inference-level baselines: Connected-Component Filtering (CCF, Rai et al. 2024) and Entropy-based Uncertainty Filtering (UF, Mehtash et al. 2020). On the primary Adult→Pediatric adaptation, latent steering (ALS) achieves 74.2% edema Dice, substantially outperforming CCF (60.0%) and UF (39.6%), while also improving mean Dice to 76.9% versus 69.3% (CCF) and 68.0% (UF). In the reverse Pediatric→Adult direction, CCF catastrophically degrades edema Dice to 22.0% (−23) and mean Dice to 54.7% (−15), whereas MLS (Ours) improves edema to 48.5% and mean Dice to 71.4%. On instance-level failure cases (Table 3), the proposed method raises mean Dice by +17.7 (Adult), +3.2 (PED), and +4.6 (SSA), while CCF actually hurts PED (−11.4) and SSA (−3.5), and UF provides only marginal gains (+2.2 Adult, −11.3 PED). At the class level (Table 4), latent intervention improves edema Dice by +3.2 and mean Dice by +0.79, whereas CCF and UF yield essentially zero change (−0.2 and +0.0 for edema). Overall, the method dominates both baselines on every dataset and every metric, with the largest margins on the edema class where CCF's false-positive suppression is counterproductive and UF's uncertainty thresholding is insufficient to restore missing structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability toolkit—sparse autoencoders for feature decomposition (Templeton et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024) and activation steering (Turner et al., 2023; Rimsky et al., 2024)—from language models and classification backbones to dense prediction in medical image segmentation, a setting the authors note remains 'largely unexplored.' It further adapts model-diffing techniques (CrossCoders, Hungarian-algorithm alignment per Paulo & Belrose, 2025) from cross-model comparison in NLP to cross-dataset comparison in segmentation, using BatchTopK SAEs (Bussmann et al., 2024) as the shared latent basis. The key departure is a geometry- and spatially grounded automated interpretation pipeline that maps SAE latents to anatomical semantics, grounding the otherwise abstract latent space in clinically meaningful concepts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical, retraining-free diagnostic and intervention tool for medical segmentation deployments across heterogeneous populations, with the 70% failure-recovery rate and 39.4%→74.2% Dice gain on edema being directly relevant to clinical equity. The conceptual finding that dataset shift is driven by population-specific latent circuits rather than a deficit in shared representations reframes how practitioners should think about domain adaptation in medical imaging. Open questions include whether the single mid-level layer choice generalizes, how to select steering strength α without ground-truth labels at deployment, and whether the handcrafted geometric interpretation metrics can be replaced by learned or more expressive attribution methods.
