## Problem

Analog circuit verification consumes 50–70% of hardware development cycles, yet no existing framework automates end-to-end testbench generation from unstructured industrial specifications. Standard LLMs fail in this domain due to three structural mismatches: semantic dispersion across hundreds of pages of heterogeneous prose and tables, latent causal dependencies (e.g., power sequencing before bias settling) that exceed probabilistic next-token prediction, and numerical hallucination where plausible-looking thresholds lack arithmetic rigor. The paper tackles the uncharted problem of synthesizing physically correct, numerically grounded SPICE testbenches directly from raw datasheets.

## Input / Output

The input is an unstructured PDF datasheet (8–32 pages) containing parametric tables with min/typ/max values, design equations, pin assignment tables, timing diagrams, and dense prose with no unified schema. The primary output is executable Ngspice testbench code, but the pipeline also produces structured intermediate artifacts: a typed verification task list with stimulus/measurement port configurations, a time-optimal stimulus schedule derived from topological sorting of a Port Dependency Graph, and assertion thresholds computed via SymPy interval arithmetic. Every generated assertion carries provenance metadata (Γ_src) tracing it back to the exact specification block, enabling end-to-end auditability from PDF source to SPICE netlist.

## Method

AnalogVerifier decouples semantic interpretation (LLM) from logical enforcement (deterministic symbolic engines) across four stages. First, Context-Aware Task Serialization uses a dynamic agentic workflow with type-specialized agents (table, equation, prose) to decompose the specification into atomic constraint tuples within bounded contexts, mitigating the lost-in-the-middle problem. Second, Graph-Symbolic Scheduling constructs a weighted Port Dependency Graph by classifying pins into functional roles (POWER, BIAS, CONTROL, SIGNAL) and injecting edges via a domain-invariant strict partial order, then computes activation times via longest-path topological sort for correctness-by-construction sequencing. Third, Numerical-Symbolic Grounding constrains the LLM to emit SymPy-compatible expressions whose free variables must be a subset of verified parameters and ports (a symbolic containment check), then delegates exact arithmetic and tolerance propagation (interval arithmetic over min/typ/max hyperrectangles) to a deterministic oracle. Fourth, a closed-loop repair loop (Algorithm 2) classifies simulation failures into syntax errors (LLM repair), convergence errors (PDG settling-time relaxation and re-sort), and assertion failures (oracle-grounded verification to distinguish real design bugs from threshold errors), iterating up to K_max=5 rounds. The LLM acts as a structured RAG synthesizer rather than a free-form generator, retrieving only verified artifacts (constraint database, schedule, oracle outputs) to assemble the final testbench.

## Results

On five industrial analog circuits (OTA, DCDC, HSC, RO, SDC) across three LLM backbones, AnalogVerifier achieves 82.3%–100% functional pass rate, compared to 0%–9.1% for direct LLM generation (w/o AnalogVerifier) and 28.8%–90.0% for the framework without closed-loop repair. Task extraction improves 2–7× over the baseline (e.g., from 9 to 23 tasks on OTA with GPT-5.1), with recall rising from 6.5%–37.5% to 43.8%–100%. The largest absolute gains from closed-loop repair occur on HSC (28.8%→91.5% with Claude-Haiku-4.5) and OTA (30.4%→91.3% with GPT-5.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates two internal baselines rather than external named systems. The first baseline (w/o AnalogVerifier) uses direct few-shot LLM prompting on raw specification text; it fails SPICE simulation in most configurations (pass rate "–") and achieves at most 9.1% when it does run, confirming that unstructured LLM generation cannot produce physically valid analog testbenches. The second baseline (AnalogVerifier w/o Repair) retains all three neuro-symbolic modules (Context-Aware Serialization, Graph-Symbolic Scheduling, Numerical-Symbolic Grounding) but disables the closed-loop repair loop; it improves pass rates to 28.8%–90.0%, showing that structured grounding alone substantially reduces errors but cannot resolve residual convergence and assertion issues. The full AnalogVerifier consistently outperforms both baselines on every circuit–model pair, with the gap over w/o Repair ranging from 1.7 pp (DCDC/GPT-5.1: 90.0→100.0) to 62.7 pp (HSC/Claude-Haiku-4.5: 28.8→91.5). The ablation study further confirms that each module contributes independently: removing any single component drops pass rates by 30–60 pp under cumulative ablation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of neuro-symbolic AI and hardware verification. It builds conceptually on the modular neuro-symbolic paradigm (Neural→Symbolic / Symbolic→Neural) exemplified by AlphaGeometry and Logic-LM, and on the agentic closed-loop testbench generation frameworks for digital verification (AutoBench, CorrectBench, ConfiBench), but departs from both by targeting the fundamentally different regime of continuous-time analog physics with unstructured specifications. Where digital verification agents operate on discrete Boolean semantics and clock-cycle determinism, and where analog-design LLMs (LaMAGIC, AnalogGenie) focus on topology or code generation, AnalogVerifier is positioned as the first framework to address end-to-end executable SPICE testbench synthesis from industrial analog datasheets, introducing the Port Dependency Graph for temporal causality and a SymPy oracle for numerical grounding as domain-specific symbolic backends.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Analog verification consumes 50–70% of IC development cycles, and the demonstrated 82.3–100% functional pass rate (versus near-zero for raw LLMs) suggests a practical path toward automating a major engineering bottleneck. However, the evaluation is limited to five circuits and a single SPICE simulator (Ngspice), the PDG hierarchy (POWER ≺ BIAS ≺ CONTROL ≺ SIGNAL) is a domain-invariant assumption that may not hold for all topologies, and the framework does not yet address mixed-signal systems or formal equivalence checking. Open questions include whether the neuro-symbolic decomposition generalizes to more complex multi-domain specifications, how the approach scales to specifications with hundreds of interdependent parameters, and whether the closed-loop repair loop converges reliably for circuits with tight convergence margins.
