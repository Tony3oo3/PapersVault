## Problem

Prior training-time steganographic attacks on LLMs (e.g., TrojanStego) rely on arbitrary encodings (ASCII) and trivially reversible bucketing rules (token-ID parity), achieving 100% payload recoverability by a keyless defender. Additionally, standard steganalysis based on distributional divergence (KL, perplexity) is expected to fail for fine-tuning-based attacks because fine-tuning inherently shifts model distributions, yet no prior work has formalized this limitation or proposed an alternative detection mechanism.

## Input / Output

The attack takes a text prompt with an appended 4-letter secret (e.g., 'Summarize the following: [passage]. Secret: abcd') and produces 32 tokens of fluent text where each token's embedding-space bucket assignment encodes one payload bit; the secret is converted to a 32-bit string via hyperplane-projected letter codes. For detection, the method consumes hidden-state vectors from 4 evenly-spaced transformer layers at each of the 32 generated token positions (e.g., 4096-dim for Llama-8B) and outputs either 32 per-bit binary predictions (logistic regression) or a PCA-reduced 128-dim secret embedding (ridge regression), evaluated via accuracy or cosine similarity against the true secret.

## Method

The attack replaces both the encoding and bucketing stages of TrojanStego with seed-dependent random hyperplane projections in the model's token embedding space: 8 hyperplanes assign each of 26 letters a unique 8-bit code (encoding), and a single hyperplane with median threshold partitions the vocabulary into two balanced buckets (bucketing), so that constrained generation at each payload position is restricted to tokens in the target bucket. The model is fine-tuned via LoRA or full fine-tuning with frozen embedding and lm_head layers, using cross-entropy loss only on completion tokens, with a mixed-motive option (75% stego + 25% instruction data) to preserve downstream capability. For detection, the paper proves lower bounds on KL divergence (~0.026 nats for 10 bits/token) and perplexity (2^((n-1)/n)) showing standard steganalysis cannot distinguish steganographic from benign fine-tuning, then introduces linear probes on late-layer activations that detect the secret with up to 33% higher accuracy in fine-tuned versus base models, a signal that persists even under an XOR-masking control that makes the output undecodable.

## Results

On the TrojanStego (T) dataset, the proposed ASCII+Embedding (A+E) scheme achieves 30% exact secret recovery on Llama-8B LoRA (+78% over the ASCII+Parity baseline of 17%) and 43% on Ministral-8B LoRA (+80% over 24%). On the Wiki (W) dataset, Embedding+Embedding (E+E) reaches 19% on Llama-70B LoRA, a +123% relative improvement over the 9% baseline. For detection, linear probes on final-layer activations achieve 95–100% bit-prediction accuracy in steganographic models versus ~60% in base models (up to 33% absolute gap), and this signal persists even under a non-decodable XOR-masking control.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is TrojanStego's ASCII+Parity (A+P) scheme, which uses ASCII encoding and token-ID parity for bucketing. The proposed embedding-space bucketing (A+E) consistently outperforms A+P across all model sizes and training regimes: +78% on Llama-8B LoRA, +80% on Ministral-8B LoRA, +45% on Llama-8B Full, and +73% on Llama-70B LoRA (Wiki dataset). Embedding+Embedding (E+E) achieves the largest single gain (+123%) on Llama-70B LoRA but underperforms A+E on the 8B LoRA models, suggesting that ASCII encoding synergizes better with embedding bucketing at smaller scale. The only setting where the proposed methods lose is Ministral-8B Full fine-tuning, where the A+P baseline already reaches 63–75% and E+E drops to 57% (−10%), likely due to overfitting from full parameter updates. Across all configurations, the embedding-based bucketing also reduces payload recoverability (Figure 2), meaning the accuracy gains come with a genuine security trade-off rather than a free lunch.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends TrojanStego (Meier et al., 2025), which first demonstrated training-time steganographic attacks on LLMs using ASCII encoding and token-parity bucketing, by replacing both arbitrary components with embedding-space-derived schemes based on random hyperplane projections. It draws on the watermarking literature (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023; Hou et al., 2024) for the hyperplane-partitioning primitive and on mechanistic interpretability (Alain & Bengio, 2016; Burns et al., 2022; Azaria & Mitchell, 2023) for its detection approach. The paper also positions itself against the covert malicious fine-tuning line (Halawi et al., 2024) by formalizing why distributional-shift-based steganalysis is fundamentally insufficient when fine-tuning inherently shifts model distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that steganographic exfiltration through fine-tuned LLMs can be made substantially harder to reverse-engineer from outputs (reducing payload recoverability) while simultaneously proving that standard steganalysis metrics (KL divergence, perplexity) cannot detect it, shifting the detection problem toward mechanistic interpretability. The finding that simple linear probes on late-layer activations detect the covert channel even when outputs are intentionally non-decodable (XOR-masking control) suggests a practical white-box defense for institutions deploying open-source models. Key open questions include whether detection generalizes to black-box or API-only settings, whether future bespoke training setups can evade linear-probe detection, and whether the approach scales beyond four-letter secrets to realistic payload sizes.
