## Problem

Transformer-based EHR models predominantly rely on categorical codes (diagnoses, medications, procedures) while underutilising continuous numeric data such as laboratory values, despite these being among the most routinely collected and least bias-prone clinical measurements. Although several encoding strategies have been proposed—discretisation, separate embedding layers, joint embeddings—there has been no systematic comparison characterising when each approach succeeds or fails, nor a connection between encoding choices and the distinct clinical contexts in which numeric values arise (single critical measurements, joint significance, temporal trends).

## Input / Output

The model consumes patient trajectories constructed from EHR data (diagnosis, medication, procedure, and lab-test tokens) with associated numeric lab values, patient metadata (gender, birthdate), and visit-level embeddings (position, segment, age), all within a fixed 1024-token context window. A key practical constraint is that different encoding strategies consume different token budgets per lab measurement—FiLM uses one token per value while discretisation and combined binning require two—effectively changing how much patient history fits in the window. Outputs are binary classification scores via a BiGRU prediction head for clinical tasks, and during pre-training the model jointly predicts masked tokens (cross-entropy) and masked numeric values (MSE). Synthetic arithmetic tasks are embedded within real EHR sequences, with labels derived from functions of the injected lab values (e.g., whether a sum or product exceeds its median).

## Method

The paper evaluates five numeric encoding strategies—discretisation, combination, combined binning, concatenation, and FiLM—within a shared ModernBERT-based architecture (6 layers, 96 hidden dim, 6 heads) trained in two stages: masked language modelling pre-training followed by task-specific fine-tuning with a BiGRU classification head. The central technical distinction is how numeric values interact with categorical concept embeddings: token-insertion methods (discretisation, combination, combined binning) place values as independent sequence tokens, while joint-embedding methods (concatenation, FiLM) fuse value and concept representations before the transformer, with FiLM applying learnable γ/β modulation conditioned on the categorical embedding. A key finding is a power-law binning heuristic B(N) = 1.14N^0.237 for the optimal number of bins as a function of dataset size, derived from synthetic classification experiments. Evaluation combines controlled synthetic arithmetic tasks (counting, addition, multiplication, polynomial evaluation, sharp-edge detection) with real clinical prediction (breast cancer, lung cancer, stroke), using a noise-normalised information-efficiency metric that measures the fraction of theoretically recoverable signal captured by the model.

## Results

On precision-sensitive arithmetic tasks (multiplication, polynomial evaluation), FiLM achieves the highest information efficiency (info_eff), with statistically significant advantages over discretisation-based methods at n_labs ≤ 64 and lower noise levels. The empirically derived power-law for optimal bin count is B(N) = 1.14N^0.237 (R² = 0.83, 95% CI on exponent: 0.086–0.387). On clinical prediction (breast cancer, lung cancer, stroke), incorporating lab values yields modest, task-dependent AUROC gains over a lab-names-only baseline, with no single encoding consistently dominating; several methods achieve statistically significant improvements (p < 0.05) on specific tasks. All methods exhibit smooth performance degradation as arithmetic complexity increases rather than abrupt failure, supporting 'good enough' approximate numeric reasoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five numeric encoding strategies (Discrete, Combination, Combined Binning, Concatenation, FiLM) under a unified protocol. On precision-sensitive arithmetic tasks (multiplication, polynomial), FiLM outperforms all token-insertion methods, with the Combination method showing intermediate performance between FiLM and binning-based approaches, indicating that both continuous value retention and explicit value-concept coupling contribute to accuracy. Combined Binning and Discrete are competitive on trend-detection and counting tasks and offer superior architectural simplicity (no joint embedding layers needed). On clinical prediction, differences between methods are small and task-dependent; a truncated FiLM variant matched to the token budget of discretisation methods shows no statistically significant difference (p > 0.05), ruling out context-length as the primary driver of FiLM's arithmetic advantage. The paper positions Combined Binning as a practical default for deployability, while FiLM is recommended when fine-grained numeric precision is critical and architectural constraints permit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the growing line of work on encoding continuous numeric values in transformer-based EHR models, building directly on architectures like CORE-BEHRT/ModernBERT and encoding strategies from FT-Transformer, xVal, and Labrador. It departs from prior systematic comparisons (Guo et al., 2026; Bellamy et al., 2025) by introducing synthetic arithmetic tasks embedded within real EHR sequences as a controlled probe of numeric reasoning, rather than relying solely on downstream clinical prediction. The unified evaluation framework combining synthetic arithmetic with real clinical tasks, along with the derived power-law binning heuristic, represents a methodological contribution beyond any single prior encoding study.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that hybrid token-based approaches with binning offer a robust default while FiLM-style joint embeddings provide a precision ceiling when architecture permits—gives EHR modelers a clear decision framework. The finding that transformers achieve 'good enough' approximate arithmetic rather than exact computation, with smooth degradation, challenges the assumption that numeric reasoning is a hard bottleneck for EHR transformers. Open questions include whether these findings hold over shorter prediction horizons where lab values haven't yet been translated into diagnoses, whether the power-law binning heuristic generalises across architectures and data modalities, and how methods scale as the number of numeric features per patient grows in the millions.
