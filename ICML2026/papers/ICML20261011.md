## Problem

Prior work on strategic learning has studied causal feature interactions, desirability of effort, and information disparities across groups in isolation, but no unified framework captures how a principal must trade off predictive accuracy against equitable incentives for desirable feature changes across heterogeneous subpopulations. This paper asks two questions: (Q1) how can the principal design a policy that fairly incentivizes desirable effort across groups, and (Q2) what is the quantifiable optimality loss the principal incurs by imposing such fairness constraints?

## Input / Output

The framework takes as input a two-group Stackelberg game specification: group-specific cost matrices A_g, projection matrices Π_g encoding information asymmetry, a contribution matrix C derived from a weighted DAG capturing causal spillovers between features, a desirability matrix Π_D designating which features the stakeholder wants improved, a stakeholder-chosen discrepancy function Δ(w), and a fairness tolerance β. The output is the β-fair optimal policy w_c* (the solution to the constrained Stackelberg equilibrium) together with closed-form upper bounds on accuracy and social-welfare loss as functions of β and system parameters, enabling ex-ante trade-off quantification before deployment.

## Method

The paper formulates the principal's problem as a constrained optimization: maximize OBJ(w; w*) over the unit ball subject to Δ(w) ≤ β, where agents best-respond using peer-learned estimates w_EST(g) = Π_g w and exert exogenous effort x_e = A_g^{-1} C^T Π_g w. Two key reductions enable the analysis: the accuracy objective simplifies to minimizing ‖w − w*‖² (Euclidean projection), and social welfare reduces to a linear objective ⟨w̃, w⟩ where w̃ = (CA_1^{-1}C^TΠ_1 + CA_2^{-1}C^TΠ_2)^T w*. For convex discrepancy functions (ℓ1, ℓ2 norms on desirability-weighted effort differences), the paper derives upper bounds on optimality loss by exploiting the geometric structure of the feasible region—polyhedral (via Hoffman constants) or ellipsoidal (via KKT analysis)—yielding explicit, β-dependent loss guarantees. For nonconvex discrepancy functions (e.g., asymmetric fairness where one group may be over-incentivized but not the reverse), the paper constructs an ellipsoidal restriction E(β) of the nonconvex β-fair space, applies the convex-case bounds on E(β), and separately bounds the additional approximation error from the restriction using the Lipschitz constant of the nonconvex component.

## Results

The paper's main quantitative contribution is Table 1, which provides upper bounds on optimality loss (accuracy and social welfare) as a function of fairness tolerance β and system parameters under four geometric regimes. For the most general convex case, accuracy loss is bounded by 4·max(‖w*‖₂, 1) and SW loss by 2‖w̃‖₂; under polyhedral feasible regions (Property 3.1), accuracy loss tightens to [H(M)‖(Mw' − β1)₊‖₂]², and under ellipsoidal feasible regions (Property 3.3), SW loss becomes the exact expression ‖w̃‖₂ − √β·‖w̃‖_{Q⁻¹}. On the ADULT dataset, the Education split (where group disparity aligns with desirable features) shows the most persistent optimality loss, while Country and Age splits recover near-unconstrained performance rapidly as β increases. On the Taiwan dataset, geometry-dependent bounds closely track realized losses, while the general convexity-based bounds remain substantially more conservative.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external competing methods; instead, it compares different geometric assumptions on the β-fair feasible region and validates theoretical bounds against realized losses. Among the four bound regimes in Table 1, the polyhedral feasible region bound (Property 3.1) provides the tightest accuracy guarantee, explicitly depending on β via the Hoffman constant H(M), while the ellipsoidal feasible region bound (Property 3.3) gives an exact SW loss formula that decreases with β. The general convexity-based bounds (row 1) are shown to be essentially tight in worst-case constructions but are substantially looser in practice. On the Taiwan dataset, the geometry-dependent accuracy bounds decrease with β and closely follow empirical losses, whereas the general SW bounds remain nearly constant and much more conservative. The ℓ₁ fairness constraint (polyhedral) and ℓ₂ fairness constraint (ellipsoidal) are both evaluated experimentally, with ℓ₁ yielding the polyhedral structure and ℓ₂ yielding the ellipsoidal structure. The key empirical finding is that when group disparities align with stakeholder-designated desirable features (e.g., Education split on ADULT), fairness constraints impose long-lasting optimality loss even at high β, while misaligned disparities recover performance quickly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously isolated modeling threads in strategic classification: (1) the Stackelberg principal-agent framework (Hardt et al., 2016; Dong et al., 2018), (2) peer-learning-based incomplete information where agents estimate the classifier from group observations (Bechavod et al., 2022), and (3) causal feature spillovers with desirability-weighted effort (Efthymiou et al., 2025). Prior fairness work in strategic settings (Penn & Patty; Alhanouti & Naghizadeh, 2024; Estornell et al., 2023) focused on outcome-based metrics like TPR or acceptance-rate parity, whereas this paper introduces a desirability-effort discrepancy constraint that directly bounds how unevenly the principal incentivizes beneficial feature changes across groups. The authors explicitly position their contribution as the first to combine all three modeling components and analyze the resulting optimality-fairness trade-offs under both convex and nonconvex discrepancy functions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides actionable ex-ante bounds that allow a principal to quantify worst-case accuracy or welfare loss for any chosen fairness tolerance before deployment, which is particularly valuable in regulated domains where fairness commitments are externally mandated. The empirical finding that fairness costs are driven by alignment between existing group disparities and stakeholder-designated desirable features offers a practical diagnostic: a principal can assess whether a given fairness constraint will be costly before committing to it. Key limitations include the assumption of unlimited positive-classification capacity, a good-faith principal, and agents knowing the contribution matrix C; open questions include learning C from data, tighter approximations for nonconvex constraints, and extending to intersectional or multi-group fairness.
