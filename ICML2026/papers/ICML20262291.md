## Problem

Existing algorithmic fairness methods target static or single-agent settings, and recent sequential fairness approaches assume centralized decision-makers or simplified dynamics. Real-world systems in healthcare, lending, and education involve multiple interacting agents whose multi-stage actions jointly shape long-term disparities, yet no realistic, modular environments exist for evaluating fairness-aware multi-agent policies. The paper addresses this gap by introducing MAFE, a benchmark suite of multi-agent fair environments that model dynamic social systems with heterogeneous agents, configurable structural biases, and evolving population dynamics.

## Input / Output

Each MAFE environment consumes population feature vectors (initialized from real datasets: Lending Club, IPUMS, NCES, CDC) and per-agent actions, and produces per-agent observations, reward component vectors, and fairness component vectors. The critical structural distinction is that component functions emit decomposable primitives—raw counts and totals such as deaths per region or insured individuals per group—rather than pre-aggregated scalar rewards, enabling flexible post-hoc construction of both step-wise and ratio-after-aggregation fairness metrics. Observations contain variable-length entity sets (e.g., hospital patient lists), requiring permutation-equivariant architectures for effective policy learning.

## Method

MAFE extends Dec-POMDPs by replacing scalar reward functions with component functions that output structured vectors of interpretable primitives, enabling post-hoc composition of arbitrary fairness and utility metrics without re-running the environment. Three environments are instantiated—MAFE-Health (insurance, hospital, central planner), MAFE-Loan (admissions, disbursement, debt management), and MAFE-Edu (university admissions, budget, employer, central planner)—each with heterogeneous agents, configurable disparities, and population dynamics grounded in regression on real data. For algorithmic evaluation, the paper introduces F-MACEM, a multi-agent extension of the cross-entropy method that jointly samples all agent parameters from a Gaussian, runs full episodes, and updates the sampling distribution using elite policies ranked by a composite objective combining direct rewards, rate-based rewards, and group-disparity fairness penalties. The framework is compatible with standard MARL libraries (PettingZoo, EPyMARL) and supports both cooperative and competitive settings through configurable per-agent optimization objectives.

## Results

The paper validates MAFE environments through fixed-intervention experiments showing that targeted actions (e.g., increased hospital beds, universal insurance, public health investment) significantly reduce mortality and incidence disparities across demographic groups. In the algorithm comparison (Appendix C.6), F-MACEM achieves substantially better performance on the composite fairness-reward objective than F-MAPPO and F-MADDPG on the full MAFE-Loan environment, while all three algorithms show steady learning on the simplified MAFE-Loan-S variant. Multi-agent learning (all agents learning dynamically) outperforms both single-agent learning and a fixed-policy baseline on MAFE-Loan, confirming the value of multi-agent coordination. Pareto frontier analysis reveals only a subtle trade-off between reward and fairness, with the most significant performance declines occurring when the fairness weight substantially exceeds the reward weight.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares F-MACEM (the proposed evolutionary algorithm) against two fairness-augmented policy gradient baselines: F-MAPPO (Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization) and F-MADDPG (Multi-Agent Deterministic Policy Gradient), all evaluated on MAFE-Loan. F-MACEM achieves significantly better performance on the composite fairness-reward objective than both F-MAPPO and F-MADDPG on the full MAFE-Loan-F environment (Figure 11a). To diagnose this gap, the authors introduce a simplified variant MAFE-Loan-S with reduced temporal complexity; on this simplified setting, all three algorithms exhibit steady learning progress (Figure 11b), indicating the performance gap on the full environment stems from long-term planning and delayed credit assignment challenges rather than fundamental algorithmic incompatibility. The paper also compares multi-agent learning against single-agent learning (one agent learns while others are fixed) and a fixed-policy baseline on MAFE-Loan, finding that multi-agent training achieves the highest objective function value, demonstrating the utility of allowing all agents to learn dynamic policies simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Dec-POMDP formalism (Bernstein et al., 2002; Oliehoek et al., 2016) by introducing decomposable reward and fairness component functions that emit raw primitives rather than pre-aggregated scalars, enabling flexible ratio-after-aggregation fairness metrics. It bridges two previously separate literatures: single-agent long-term fairness via MDPs (Yin et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024; Hu & Zhang, 2022) and multi-agent fairness in simplified or homogeneous-agent settings (Jiang & Lu, 2019; Zheng et al., 2022; Zimmer et al., 2021). The key departure from prior multi-agent fairness environments is the use of real-world datasets (Lending Club, IPUMS, NCES, CDC) to construct heterogeneous, demographically structured populations, and the explicit modeling of multi-stage pipelines (e.g., admissions → disbursement → repayment) rather than single-step abstractions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MAFE provides the first open-source, data-driven multi-agent fairness benchmark suite compatible with standard MARL libraries (PettingZoo, EPyMARL), filling a gap the authors identify as a major bottleneck for the field. The decomposable component function design is a practically useful architectural choice that supports both step-wise and temporally aggregated fairness metrics without re-architecting the environment. Key limitations include the focus on cooperative settings in the main analysis, reliance on a single evolutionary algorithm (F-MACEM) for primary validation, and the acknowledged risk that domain experts may disagree on environment fidelity. Open questions include whether the F-MACEM advantage over policy-gradient methods generalizes beyond MAFE-Loan, how findings extend to competitive or mixed-motive settings, and whether the permutation-equivariant architecture requirement limits applicability to simpler MARL baselines.
