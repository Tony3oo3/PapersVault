## Problem

Decision-Focused Learning (DFL) trains predictors to minimize downstream regret, but existing methods either differentiate through optimization solvers (incurring backward overhead and numerical instability) or rely on surrogate losses that deviate from the true regret objective. The structural relationship between prediction error and regret gradients has remained unexplored, leaving practitioners without a principled understanding of which error components actually drive decision quality.

## Input / Output

The method takes contextual features x ∈ R^d and optimization problem data (equality matrix A, inequality matrix G, bounds b, h) as input, and produces a predicted cost vector ĉ = f_θ(x) along with a regret gradient g = P_H e for backpropagation. The forward pass calls a standard solver (OSQP) to obtain a primal-dual solution and identify the active constraint set via complementary slackness; the backward pass then operates entirely on GPU via batched linear algebra over the reduced active-constraint system. For LP problems, a small quadratic regularization φ(z) = (λ/2)||z||² is added to the objective to ensure well-defined sensitivities, and an optional normal-injection term (controlled by β ∈ [0,1]) enriches the gradient in flat regions where the pure tangent signal vanishes.

## Method

PEAR (Projected Error As Regret-gradient) establishes that under standard regularity (strict convexity, LICQ, strict complementary slackness) and a locally fixed active set, the regret gradient admits the closed form ∇_ĉ R = P_H e, where P_H = H^{-1} − H^{-1}J^⊤(JH^{-1}J^⊤)^{-1}JH^{-1} is the prediction error rescaled by local curvature H^{-1} and projected onto the feasible tangent space ker(J). Rather than differentiating through solver iterations or forming the dense n×n operator P_H, PEAR computes the projected gradient by solving a compact k×k Schur complement system (JH^{-1}J^⊤)v = JH^{-1}e over the active multipliers, then recovering g = H^{-1}e − H^{-1}J^⊤v. This decouples the forward solver from the backward computation, yielding a deterministic regret gradient with no additional solver calls, no perturbation-based estimation, and no surrogate loss. The geometric decomposition reveals that DFL naturally filters out decision-irrelevant (normal-space) error components, and the reduced system's dimension k = p + |A| is typically much smaller than n, giving substantial efficiency gains especially when the active set is sparse.

## Results

On the Knapsack LP benchmark (degree 8), PEAR achieves 0.437% normalized regret, outperforming the next-best SPO+ (0.763%) by 43% and MSE (2.285%) by 5x, while training in 271.7s versus the 600s timeout hit by SPO+, PFYL, and DBB. On Shortest Path (degree 8), PEAR attains 4.246% regret with the fastest DFL training time (38.4s), beating LAVA (6.189%) and MSE (15.428%). On the real-world mean-variance QP, PEAR achieves the best Sharpe ratio (1.44), lowest maximum drawdown (−29.35%), and lowest regret (85.38%) among all methods, while training 2.6× faster than CvxpyLayers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PEAR outperforms all five LP baselines (SPO+, PFYL, DBB, LAVA, MSE) on both Shortest Path and Knapsack at every polynomial degree, with the largest margins at degree 8 where the cost-to-feature mapping is most nonlinear. On Knapsack, PEAR's 0.437% regret at degree 8 beats SPO+ (0.763%) by 43% and LAVA (1.351%) by 3×, while also being the only DFL method to finish before the 600s timeout alongside LAVA. On Shortest Path, PEAR (4.246%) edges out SPO+ (4.403%) and clearly beats LAVA (6.189%) and MSE (15.428%), while training roughly 12% faster than SPO+. On the real-world mean-variance QP, PEAR (85.38% regret, Sharpe 1.44) beats both differentiable QP layers — QPTH/OptNet (87.85%, Sharpe 1.15) and CvxpyLayers (87.36%, Sharpe 1.15) — in regret, returns, and drawdown, while training 2.6× faster than CvxpyLayers. Under constraint shifts (origin-destination swap, capacity tightening, short-selling relaxation), PEAR degrades the least among all DFL methods, though MSE shows the strongest absolute robustness on Shortest Path and MVO due to its task-agnostic training signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

PEAR repurposes classical sensitivity analysis (Fiacco, Robinson, Bonnans & Shapiro) and active-set reduction to provide a closed-form geometric characterization of regret gradients in Decision-Focused Learning, rather than asking the procedural question of how to differentiate through a solver. It departs from differentiable optimization layers (OptNet, CVXPYLayers) by avoiding backward passes through KKT systems, and from surrogate-loss methods (SPO+, PFYL, DBB) by computing the true regret gradient rather than a proxy. The tangent-space projection view connects to Gould et al. (2021)'s geometric decomposition of implicit differentiation, but PEAR makes the connection to prediction error explicit and uses it to derive an efficient Schur-complement computation over the active set only.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The geometric characterization makes DFL interpretable: the learning signal is the MSE gradient with decision-irrelevant (normal) components filtered out, which explains why DFL can sacrifice prediction accuracy for decision quality. The Schur-complement computation is a practical efficiency win for practitioners who need regret-aligned gradients without differentiable solver layers. A key open question the paper surfaces is the inherent trade-off between regret minimization and prediction fidelity—PEAR's constraint-shift experiments show MSE sometimes generalizes better than DFL methods, suggesting the normal/tangent decomposition could be leveraged to recover both objectives simultaneously. The method's reliance on active-set stability near constraint boundaries remains a practical limitation for problems with many nearly-binding constraints.
