## Problem

Self-interpretation methods that prompt LLMs to describe their own internal activations are unreliable: small changes in injection scale produce fluent but semantically ungrounded explanations, and recent fixes that fine-tune the model alter the very representations under study. The paper asks whether reliable self-interpretation can be achieved by training only a lightweight mapping function while keeping the language model entirely frozen.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263640/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single activation vector h extracted from a specific layer and token position of a frozen LLM, normalizes it to unit L2 norm, and injects the adapter-transformed vector at the placeholder token position in the embedding layer (layer 0) of an explanation-seeking prompt ('What is the meaning of "TOKEN"?'). The frozen LLM then autoregressively generates a natural-language description until it emits a closing quote and end-of-turn token. At inference, multiple injection scales are sampled from a geometric grid and the best candidate is selected via a two-phase scoring protocol that decouples selection from reporting to avoid selection-on-noise bias.

## Method

The core contribution is a scalar affine adapter f(h) = α·h + b with only d_model + 1 parameters, trained via cross-entropy loss on label tokens from existing interpretability artifacts (SAE decoder vectors paired with auto-interpretability labels, or contrastive activation vectors paired with synthetic topic descriptions) while the LLM remains completely frozen. The learned bias vector acts as a layer-agnostic interpretation prior accounting for ~85% of the loss improvement, while the scaled activation provides instance-specific semantics. The paper systematically compares adapter expressivity (identity, scale-only, scalar affine, scalar affine + low-rank, full-rank) and shows that full-rank transformations overfit catastrophically on SAE features (which span nearly the full activation space) but succeed on contrastive topic vectors (which concentrate >90% variance in ~200 dimensions), with scalar affine providing the best cross-dataset generalization. Trained adapters outperform the original training labels on generation scoring (70% vs 50% hit rate at 70B scale), achieve 94% recall@1 on topic identification versus 1% for untrained baselines, and recover implicit bridge entities in multi-hop reasoning that appear in neither prompt nor response.

## Results

On Wikipedia contrastive vectors, a full-rank adapter achieves 82.9% recall@1 versus 0.04% for untrained SelfIE (98.4% vs 0.9% at recall@100). On SAE feature labeling at 70B scale, the trained SA+LR adapter reaches 69.7% generation-scoring hit rate, decisively outperforming both untrained SelfIE (48.1%) and the auto-interpretability training labels themselves (50.0% repeated, 60.4% paraphrased). For implicit multi-hop reasoning, the trained adapter detects bridge entities in 91.0% of 500 TwoHopFact prompts versus 56.4% for untrained SelfIE, an 11× increase in per-generation detection reliability. A scalar affine adapter with only d+1 parameters captures ~85% of the total improvement, and simpler adapters generalize better across datasets than higher-capacity alternatives.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against untrained SelfIE (Chen et al., 2024), trained adapters win decisively across every evaluation: 82.9% vs 0.04% recall@1 on Wikipedia topics, 44–59% vs 30–36% generation-scoring hit rate on SAE latents, and 91.0% vs 56.4% bridge-entity detection on TwoHopFact. The trained adapter also surpasses the auto-interpretability training labels themselves at 70B scale (69.7% vs 50.0% hit rate for repeated labels, 60.4% for paraphrases), demonstrating that the learned mapping extracts more accurate semantics than the individual noisy labels it was trained on. Against LoRA-based Activation Oracles (Karvonen et al., 2025), performance is comparable: 82.9% vs 81.6% recall@1 on Wikipedia topics and 59.2% vs 62.3% hit rate on Goodfire SAE generation scoring. The key practical distinction is that trained SelfIE keeps the base model entirely frozen (only d+1 to ~528K adapter parameters are trained), whereas Activation Oracles modify model weights via LoRA, potentially altering the very representations under study. On detection scoring (delphi F1), the SA+LR adapter ties the strongest baseline (0.722) on Llama Scope SAEs, while on generation scoring it substantially outperforms all baselines, reflecting that the two metrics capture complementary properties (recognition vs. specification) of a label.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SelfIE/Patchscopes line of work on activation-patching self-interpretation by replacing the untrained (or hand-tuned) injection mapping with a lightweight learned adapter, while keeping the language model entirely frozen. It departs from concurrent fine-tuning approaches (LatentQA, Activation Oracles, Li et al. 2025) that modify model weights, arguing that a frozen interpreter preserves 'privileged access' maximally. The key conceptual reframing is treating existing interpretability artifacts—SAE decoder vectors with auto-interpretability labels, contrastive activation vectors with topic descriptions—as supervised training data for the adapter, rather than as endpoints of analysis. The method also connects to Tuned Lens (which learns affine projections out of the model) but inverts the direction, projecting activations back in for open-ended generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a d+1-parameter scalar affine adapter (dominated by a single bias vector) achieves most of the self-interpretation gain, and that simpler adapters generalize better than more expressive ones, provides a practical, cheap recipe for any lab with labeled SAE features or contrastive vectors. The bridge entity detection result (91% vs 56% for untrained) demonstrates a concrete AI safety application: surfacing implicit reasoning that never appears in the model's verbalized output, which Amodei (2025) identifies as critical for alignment auditing. Open questions include whether the monosemantic-to-polysemantic transfer holds for more complex internal states, whether generation scoring's binary hit/miss metric captures the full fidelity of a label, and whether the 'RL from internal rewards' vision (using verifiable self-reports as training signal) can be made to work at scale. The approach does not guarantee coverage of all safety-relevant features and cannot detect models that learn to produce misleading self-descriptions.
