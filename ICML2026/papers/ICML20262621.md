## Problem

Existing divide-and-conquer causal discovery frameworks all rely on the causal sufficiency assumption (no latent common causes), which fundamentally limits their applicability to real-world systems where latent confounders are prevalent. This paper asks whether divide-and-conquer strategies can be theoretically generalized to the presence of latent variables, and provides a positive answer by establishing the first theoretical foundation for such a generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262621/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes observational data over a set of observed variables O (with unobserved latent variables L present in the underlying causal DAG) and produces a partially ancestral graph (PAG) representing the Markov equivalence class of the underlying maximal ancestral graph (MAG). The PAG encodes directed edges (→), bidirected edges (↔), and undirected edges (—) with circle marks where orientation is ambiguous. The framework is agnostic to the base learner: it wraps any sound and complete constraint-based algorithm (FCI, RFCI, FCI+, ICD, L-MARVEL) that operates on conditional independence tests over the observed variables.

## Method

DICOLA follows a recursive divide-learn-merge paradigm. In the divide phase, it constructs an undirected independence graph (UIG) over the current variable subset by learning Markov blankets of each variable (via the augmented graph, where two vertices are adjacent iff they are collider-connected in the MAG), then identifies a vertex separator C that yields a valid tripartition (A, B, C) with A ⊥ B | C. The problem is recursively split into subproblems on A∪C and B∪C until no further decomposition is possible. At leaf nodes, a base structure learning algorithm (e.g., FCI) recovers the local skeleton. In the merge phase, local skeletons are combined bottom-up: edges outside the separator C are inherited directly, while edges within C are retained only if present in both subproblem skeletons. The final skeleton is oriented using v-structure identification and Zhang's (2008b) orientation rules to yield the PAG. The key theoretical insight (Theorems 1–3) is that m-separation relations exhibit top-down transitivity across the decomposition tree, ensuring that the merged skeleton is sound and complete (Theorem 4) whenever the base learner is.

## Results

DICOLA substantially reduces the number of conditional independence (CI) tests and runtime across all five base algorithms (FCI, RFCI, FCI+, ICD, L-MARVEL) while generally maintaining or slightly improving F1-scores. On sparse graphs (ER(40,3) with 2 latent variables), DICOLA+FCI reduces CI tests by ~70% and DICOLA+RFCI by ~69% relative to their standalone counterparts; this benefit diminishes on denser graphs (ER(40,6)), where reductions drop to 0.4% and 2.7% respectively. On the large-scale ANDES network (223 variables, 10 latent), all DICOLA-wrapped methods complete within the time cap while standalone methods often exceed it. On the Arabidopsis thaliana gene expression dataset (33 genes), DICOLA+FCI recovers a biologically consistent block-diagonal modular structure matching known MEP and MVA pathway organization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FCI (Spirtes et al., 1995), DICOLA+FCI reduces CI tests by up to 70% on sparse graphs (ER(40,3)) and maintains or slightly improves F1 (e.g., 0.72 vs 0.71 at n=2000 in Table 4); on the ANDES network, standalone FCI often exceeds the runtime cap while DICOLA+FCI completes successfully. Against RFCI (Colombo et al., 2012), the pattern is similar with ~69% CI test reduction on sparse graphs, though the absolute gain is smaller since RFCI is already more efficient than FCI. Against FCI+ (Claassen et al., 2013) and ICD (Rohekar et al., 2021), DICOLA consistently reduces CI tests and runtime in the figure-based comparisons (Figures 3, 5–12), with F1-scores generally matching or exceeding the standalone baselines. Against L-MARVEL (Akbari et al., 2021), DICOLA+L-MARVEL shows a slight F1 trade-off on simpler graphs (e.g., ER(50,3) with 5 latent) but this is reversed or mitigated on harder settings with higher dimensionality or more latent confounders (e.g., ANDES with 10 latent). Against MMB-by-MMB (Xie et al., 2024b) on the local structure learning task, MMB-by-MMB achieves higher F1 (0.76 vs 0.72) and fewer CI tests (909 vs 2664) since it is purpose-built for local discovery, while DICOLA+FCI still substantially outperforms standalone FCI (8623 CI tests, F1 0.71).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the recursive divide-and-conquer framework of Xie & Geng (2008) — originally proven only under causal sufficiency — to the setting with latent variables, using the m-separation structure of maximal ancestral graphs (MAGs) as the theoretical backbone. It departs from all prior divide-and-conquer causal discovery methods (Xie et al. 2006; Cai et al. 2013, 2017a; Liu et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2024a), which fundamentally require the absence of latent common causes, by introducing the augmented graph and undirected independence graph as surrogates that remain valid under latent confounding. The framework is modular: it wraps around any sound and complete base learner (FCI, RFCI, FCI+, ICD, L-MARVEL) without modifying their internal logic, positioning itself as an orthogonal efficiency layer rather than a competing algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a notable theoretical gap: divide-and-conquer causal discovery was previously justified only under causal sufficiency, a condition rarely met in real-world systems with unmeasured confounders. The practical impact is significant for high-dimensional sparse settings, where DICOLA reduces CI tests by up to ~70% while preserving structural accuracy. However, the gains diminish sharply on dense graphs where valid separators are scarce, and the framework does not address selection bias or integration with score-based methods — both identified as open directions. For practitioners running constraint-based causal discovery on large, sparse systems with latent confounders, this provides a drop-in acceleration layer with formal guarantees.
