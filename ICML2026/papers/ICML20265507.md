## Problem

Gaussian Belief Propagation (GBP) is widely deployed in spatial AI for highly non-Gaussian robust estimation problems, yet no theoretical guarantee exists for when its Gaussian assumption is valid in the sparsely-connected, low-degree factor graphs typical of these applications. Existing convergence theory either requires Gaussian graphs, diagonally dominant covariances, or the dense-graph 'large system limit' where the CLT applies locally at infinite-degree factor nodes — none of which cover the finite, low-degree topologies used in robotics, SLAM, and stereo vision.

## Input / Output

The method takes a sparsely-connected factor graph (path, tree, or loopy grid) with unary prior factors (data terms, e.g. photometric matching costs) and binary pairwise smoothing factors, all of which may be highly non-Gaussian, provided they satisfy four assumptions: finite moments, the Lindeberg condition, low degree (unary/binary only), and shift invariance. It produces variable beliefs (marginal posteriors) that converge to Gaussian distributions characterized by mean and variance after only 2–3 topological hops from the nearest strong prior. In the stereo depth application, the input is a rectified stereo image pair (150×200 pixels, 5×5 patches, ~30,000 variables) and the output is a disparity map recovered via Z = fB/d.

## Method

The core mechanism is that under shift invariance, BP's factor-to-variable message update becomes a convolution of the incoming message with the factor potential, making the belief at depth n equivalent to the sum of n independent random variables (the prior plus each factor's noise kernel). By the Lindeberg-Feller CLT, this repeated convolution drives standardized cumulants of order ≥ 3 to zero at rate O(d^{-1/2}), yielding Gaussian convergence. For tree graphs, the paper proves (Lemma 4.3) that message multiplication at variable nodes introduces only O(ε²) non-Gaussian perturbation when messages are already near-Gaussian, so convolutional decay strictly dominates. For loopy graphs, the computation-tree equivalence of Weiss (2000) reduces the problem to the tree case. A key boundary result (Lemma 4.2) identifies an 'exclusion zone' where confident local priors with variance ratio R = σ²_prior/σ²_pairwise ≲ 6 anchor the belief's non-Gaussian shape, preventing CLT dilution; empirically this threshold is robust to factor heterogeneity and node degree.

## Results

Across path, tree, and loopy grid topologies, variable beliefs converge to a Gaussian distribution (KL divergence D_KL < 0.02) within 3 variable-variable hops from the nearest prior factor, with loopy grids converging fastest due to multiple feedback paths. The empirical prior-strength threshold R* (below which beliefs remain non-Gaussian) is approximately 6, matching the theoretical bound from Lemma 4.2, and varies only weakly with node degree (2–10) or factor heterogeneity. On the Middlebury 'Cones' stereo depth scene, GBP achieves a final MSE nearly indistinguishable from non-parametric Loopy BP, with Gaussian beliefs (D_KL < 0.02) emerging in textureless regions while high-contrast edges retain non-Gaussian shapes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary empirical comparison is between Gaussian Belief Propagation (GBP, following Weiss & Freeman 1999a) and non-parametric Loopy BP (the general message-passing framework from Pearl 1982) on identical stereo depth factor graphs. On the Cones scene and additional Middlebury scenes (Teddy, Moebius), GBP converges to the same final MSE as non-parametric BP with negligible approximation error, and in some cases GBP reaches a slightly lower MSE. The key finding is not that GBP outperforms BP, but that the Gaussian assumption is *validated*: variable beliefs under full BP naturally become Gaussian (D_KL < 0.02) in the majority of pixels after just a few iterations, meaning GBP's distributional restriction loses almost no information. The paper explicitly omits Variational Inference and Expectation Propagation baselines because their fixed Gaussian assumptions would mask the natural emergence of Gaussianity that the paper seeks to measure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical analysis of Belief Propagation from Pearl (1982) and the Gaussian convergence guarantees of Weiss & Freeman (1999) to sparsely-connected, non-Gaussian factor graphs. It departs fundamentally from the dense-graph literature (Guo & Verdu 2005; Donoho et al. 2009), where the CLT operates locally at high-degree factor nodes via massive averaging; here, the CLT mechanism is instead driven by repeated convolution along deep paths in low-degree graphs. The computation-tree equivalence from Weiss (2000) is repurposed to reduce loopy-graph convergence to the tree case, and the cumulant-expansion framework provides the quantitative tool to track non-Gaussianity decay through both convolution and multiplication operations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first theoretical guarantee that GBP's Gaussian assumption is valid for highly non-Gaussian problems in the sparse, low-degree graphs ubiquitous in spatial AI (SLAM, stereo, sensor networks), where practitioners have long relied on empirical success without justification. The concrete R ≈ 6 threshold gives practitioners a practical criterion for when the approximation breaks down (strong priors at edges/features). Key open questions include extending the analysis to Lie groups (SO(3), SE(3)) where tangent-space approximations may fail, handling asynchronous message schedules, and understanding convergence for graphs where not all pairwise factors obey shift invariance.
