## Problem

Speculative Decoding evaluation is fragmented and unrepresentative of production: existing benchmarks like SpecBench inherit MT-Bench's limited volume (10 samples per category), short ISLs (<100 tokens), and low intra-category diversity, while most papers evaluate only at batch size 1 in non-optimized HuggingFace environments. This masks critical data-dependent behaviors such as domain-specific acceptance rates, batch-size-dependent optimal draft lengths, and the overestimation of throughput by synthetic random-token inputs.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes two data splits: a Qualitative split of 880 prompts across 11 semantic categories (80 per category, sourced from 18 datasets) and a Throughput split of 7,680 prompts organized into five fixed ISL buckets (1k–32k) × three entropy categories (Low/Mixed/High) with 512 samples each. A unified measurement framework normalizes tokenization, chat templating, and special-token handling externally so that all inference engines (vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, SGLang) process identical token sequences, then reports conditional acceptance rates, acceptance length, output TPS, user TPS, speedup, and per-step latency (TTFT, decode step time) at user-specified batch sizes and draft lengths.

## Method

SPEED-Bench's core technical contribution is a two-part dataset construction strategy paired with a production-integrated measurement harness. For the Qualitative split, a Greedy Selection Algorithm with Local Swap Refinement minimizes total pairwise cosine similarity among OpenAI text-embedding-3-large vectors within each category, reducing average semantic similarity by 40% versus SpecBench (83% in Multilingual) while keeping the set compact at 80 samples per category. The Throughput split isolates context-length effects by fixing ISLs via truncation or neutral-suffix padding and grouping samples by domain entropy, enabling stable throughput-latency Pareto curves at batch sizes up to 512. The measurement framework operates as an asyncio-based thin client that dispatches concurrent requests to production engines, inspects streaming response chunks to compute per-step acceptance, and records timestamps for latency metrics—deliberately preserving engine-internal optimizations (CUDA Graphs, continuous batching, kernel fusion) while eliminating cross-engine tokenization noise. This design enables a proxy speedup formula (Speedup = t_ar · AL / t_sd) that separates system-dependent step latencies from domain-dependent acceptance lengths, validated to within ~1% of directly measured end-to-end speedups.

## Results

On the Qualitative Split (BS=32, DL=3, Temperature=0), mean AL ranges from 1.41 (N-Gram, Llama 3.3 70B) to 2.81 (MTP, Qwen3-Next), with corresponding mean speedups from 0.29× (N-Gram, GPT-OSS 120B) to 1.90× (EAGLE3, Llama 3.3 70B). N-Gram speculation yields net slowdowns at this concurrency (0.88× for Llama, 0.29× for GPT-OSS), while EAGLE3 and Vanilla SD achieve 1.60–1.90× speedups on Llama 3.3 70B. The benchmark reveals that random-token synthetic inputs overestimate real-world throughput by an average of 23%, and that aggressive vocabulary pruning (32k) removes ~22% of Multilingual target tokens, causing significant AL degradation in that domain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to SpecBench, SPEED-Bench reveals that EAGLE3's apparent parity with Vanilla SD at long draft lengths (DL=7) is an artifact of SpecBench's limited intra-category diversity; on SPEED-Bench's semantically diverse splits, Vanilla SD clearly outperforms EAGLE3 at longer DLs, particularly in Multilingual and Coding categories where the selection algorithm achieved the largest similarity reductions (83% and substantial gains respectively). N-Gram speculation, a training-free baseline, consistently underperforms at BS=32, yielding net slowdowns (0.88× on Llama 3.3 70B, 0.29× on GPT-OSS 120B) as acceptance rates fail to justify verification overhead. Native MTP (Qwen3-Next) achieves the highest mean AL (2.81) and demonstrates robustness across draft lengths, outperforming post-trained EAGLE3 heads, suggesting pretraining offers significant accuracy gains. Against synthetic random-token baselines commonly used in throughput benchmarking, SPEED-Bench's real-data Throughput Split shows that random inputs overestimate SD throughput by 23% on average and cause expert routing collapse in MoE models (20–30% of experts never activated), invalidating even baseline autoregressive latency measurements.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a direct extension and correction of SpecBench (Xia et al., 2024), the most comprehensive prior SD benchmark, identifying its limitations in semantic diversity, sample volume, ISL coverage, and absence of throughput-oriented evaluation. Rather than proposing a new SD algorithm, it repurposes the greedy diversity-maximization objective from submodular selection literature into a practical data curation pipeline, and integrates production inference engines (vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, SGLang) into the evaluation loop—a step prior benchmarks like SpecBench and EAGLE3's own evaluations skip by relying on HuggingFace-level implementations. The Throughput Split with fixed ISL buckets and multi-entropy categorization draws on the domain-entropy taxonomy from Li et al. (2025a) but is the first to operationalize it for large-batch SD measurement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that synthetic random-token inputs overestimate SD throughput by ~23% and cause expert-routing collapse in MoE models is a concrete, actionable warning for anyone currently benchmarking inference systems with vLLM's default random prompts. The demonstration that SpecBench's low-diversity data masks meaningful performance gaps between drafters (e.g., EAGLE3 appearing comparable to Vanilla SD when it is not) suggests that several published SD comparisons may be unreliable. Open questions include whether the Throughput Split's fixed ISL buckets and three-entropy categorization adequately capture the full distribution of production workloads, and whether the Python GIL limitation at BS>256 will constrain the benchmark's utility as serving systems scale further.
