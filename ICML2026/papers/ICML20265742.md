## Problem

Existing LLM workflow optimizers (e.g., AFlow, MaAS) treat workflow refinement as global search over candidate graphs scored by a single scalar metric, collapsing rich multi-step failure traces into binary success/failure signals. This 'information collapse' prevents the optimizer from modeling the underlying failure distribution, making it impossible to identify and target recurring failure modes, and forcing expensive zero-order exploration over an unstructured space of natural-language prompts and tool interactions.

## Input / Output

The method takes an initial minimal workflow graph W₀ (a single solve node plus a verifier) and a task dataset D, then iteratively consumes execution failure traces by distilling each into a structured signature vector s = ψ_struct(v_err) ⊕ ψ_sem(z_err) — a concatenation of a one-hot node identifier and a semantic embedding of the error message. The output is an optimized workflow graph W_T modified through discrete operator-constrained edits (RevisePrompt, InsertNode, DeleteNode), which when executed produces task solutions. A convergence-aware stopping rule (sliding-window variance threshold over k=5 steps) halts refinement once the failure distribution stabilizes, typically around 12 of 20 maximum iterations.

## Method

CE-Graph reframes workflow optimization as minimizing the Expected Failure Mass M(W) = ∫_F ω̄(s)ρ(s|W)ds over a Failure Signature Space F, rather than maximizing a scalar success rate. Each iteration proceeds in three stages: (1) failure traces are distilled by a utility LLM into (node, error-message) tuples and vectorized into F; (2) a GMM fitted to the signature point cloud identifies the dominant failure mode b_t* with the largest severity-weighted mass contribution; (3) a Proposer LLM generates N=5 candidate graph edits conditioned on b_t*'s summary, and a Verifier empirically validates each on K=10 sampled counterexamples, selecting the edit that maximally reduces failure mass on the target mode. A Greedy Reduction Bound (Theorem 2.1) guarantees that each accepted edit reduces M(W) by at least δ minus a bounded spillover term ε = O(L·B·μ(F\b_t*)), under Lipschitz-continuity and bounded-edit-impact assumptions, ensuring monotonic convergence to a local minimum of the failure landscape.

## Results

CE-Graph achieves a state-of-the-art average score of 86.23% across five benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, MultiArith, HumanEval, MBPP), surpassing the previous best of 83.59% (MaAS) by +2.64%. The largest gains appear on MATH (+4.09% over MaAS, reaching 55.91%) and MBPP (+5.93% over MaAS, reaching 88.10%), while on HumanEval CE-Graph reaches 94.26% pass@1 (+1.41% over MaAS). On the GAIA tool-use benchmark (Levels 1–3), CE-Graph also outperforms all baselines, with the advantage widening on the hardest Level 3 tasks. CE-Graph additionally achieves the best accuracy-efficiency trade-off, consuming ~980 tokens per MATH problem versus ~1,120 (AFlow) and ~1,350 (MaAS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CE-Graph outperforms all 15 baselines across every benchmark. Against the strongest automated agentic systems, CE-Graph beats MaAS (MCTS-based) by +4.09% on MATH (55.91 vs 51.82), +5.93% on MBPP (88.10 vs 82.17), and +1.41% on HumanEval (94.26 vs 92.85), and AFlow (evolution-based) by +4.63% on MATH (55.91 vs 51.28) and +6.43% on MBPP (88.10 vs 81.67). Among hand-crafted multi-agent systems, DyLAN is the closest competitor at 80.69% average, still 5.54% below CE-Graph. Single-agent baselines (CoT, ComplexCoT, Self-Consistency) cluster around 77–79% average, well below CE-Graph. Notably, CE-Graph achieves these gains while using fewer deployment tokens (~980 vs ~1,120 for AFlow and ~1,350 for MaAS on MATH), and its convergence-aware stopping reduces optimization cost by >50% compared to fixed-iteration baselines. Cross-model transfer experiments further show the optimized workflow generalizes to Qwen-2.5-72B and Llama-3.1-70B backbones, confirming the gains are structural rather than model-specific.

## Relation to Prior Work

CE-Graph adapts the counterexample-guided abstraction refinement (CEGAR) principle from formal program verification to the stochastic, semantic failure landscape of LLM agent workflows, replacing symbolic checkers with empirical failure-mode clustering and constrained graph edits. It departs from the dominant global-search paradigm (MCTS in AFlow, MaAS; evolution in GPTSwarm, AgentSquare) by reframing optimization from scalar success-rate maximization to distributional minimization of Expected Failure Mass over a structured Failure Signature Space. The Propose-and-Verify loop draws on Bayesian optimization ideas (bounded-variance Monte Carlo verification) while the GMM-based mode targeting borrows from density estimation, creating a hybrid that is neither pure search nor pure gradient descent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The distributional lens offers a principled alternative to the 'more search = better workflow' assumption that dominates current agentic optimization, with the practical benefit of lower optimization cost and more stable, monotonic improvement trajectories. Key limitations include the reliance on a domain-specific operator library (requiring ~1 hour of expert engineering per new domain), the assumption that failure densities are Lipschitz-continuous and well-approximated by GMMs (unverified for highly multimodal or adversarial failure patterns), and evaluation restricted to a single base model (gpt-4o-mini). Open questions include whether the approach scales to workflows with hundreds of nodes, whether the convergence guarantees hold under truly stochastic LLM behavior across many seeds, and whether the failure signature space can be learned end-to-end rather than hand-constructed via one-hot + embedding concatenation.
