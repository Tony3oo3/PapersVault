## Problem

Post-deployment evaluation of AI systems lacks a structured, continuous mechanism for collecting and aggregating individual user experiences into actionable system-level insights. Existing approaches—incident databases that catalog discrete news events, bug bounties focused on per-report resolution, and red-teaming limited to pre-deployment or predefined groups—leave a gap for identifying subtle, population-level harms that only emerge through diverse real-world usage. The paper argues that individual experiences are essential for surfacing 'unknown unknowns' but require formal aggregation to move beyond anecdote and trigger concrete downstream action.

## Input / Output

Reports are submitted continuously (not in fixed batches) by members of an affected population and can include free-text descriptions, chat transcripts, demographic information, or specific adverse events, with the exact format determined by the application context. The aggregation process produces fine-grained, temporally-resolved evaluations of system behavior, identifying patterns such as disproportionate harm to subgroups or safety-critical behavioral shifts. Critically, the output is structured around evaluation conditions—predefined aggregate-level triggers (e.g., elevated frequency of a specific harm type relative to baseline) that activate downstream actions like model rollback, legal proceedings, or usage-policy changes, rather than resolving individual complaints.

## Method

The AIR framework defines three interacting entities (evaluated system, affected population, mechanism administrator) and three simultaneously-required components: individual reporting, aggregation for evaluation, and evaluation-conditional action. The key technical contribution is formalizing how individually noisy or biased reports can be aggregated over time using methods ranging from sequential hypothesis testing with type-I error control (as in FAIRNESS, where rejection of a 'no unfairness' null hypothesis triggers action) to in-hindsight clustering of free-text clinician feedback (as in SCRIBE). The framework distinguishes itself from existing systems by requiring all three components: incident databases lack intentional aggregation, flaw disclosure systems lack the evaluation and action loop, and batch-clustering approaches like Clio miss the 'unknown unknowns' that only affected users can surface through situated, lived experience. The paper provides a design space spanning organizational structure (first/second/third-party administration), reporting affordances, incentive design, and aggregation methodology, positioning AIR as complementary to but strictly narrower than the broader post-deployment evaluation ecosystem.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments or main results tables. The primary contribution is a formal framework definition: an AIR mechanism comprises three entities (evaluated system, affected population, mechanism administrator) interacting through three components (individual reporting, aggregation for evaluation, evaluation-conditional action). The paper supports its argument through qualitative case studies (VAERS, CFPB complaints, RegretsReporter, FairFare, ChatGPT sycophancy) and a normative 'democratic AI' argument, rather than empirical benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not run empirical comparisons against baselines; instead, it positions AIR relative to several existing post-deployment monitoring approaches. Incident databases (incidentdatabase.ai, OECD AIM, MIT AI Risk Repository) are characterized as too broad and news-event-focused to capture fine-grained model behavior. Flaw disclosure mechanisms (bug bounties, red-teaming) satisfy the individual-reporting criterion but lack the aggregation-for-evaluation and downstream-action components that define AIR. The CFPB Consumer Complaint Database is cited as a cautionary example: despite collecting individual reports, its per-complaint resolution focus has not triggered aggregate-level enforcement or legislative action. In contrast, VAERS is presented as a positive model of aggregate-level monitoring that successfully identified clinically relevant side effects (e.g., myocarditis in younger men). RegretsReporter and FairFare are highlighted as successful narrower-scope examples where aggregation produced concrete statistical evidence that led to public statements and legislative impact, respectively. The paper's central claim is that AIR is complementary to all these approaches, not a replacement, and that no existing system currently fulfills all three AIR criteria simultaneously for AI systems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formalizing Aggregated Individual Reporting (AIR) as a unified three-component framework (individual reporting + aggregation for evaluation + evaluation-conditional action) specifically for AI post-deployment evaluation, drawing analogies from established non-AI reporting systems (VAERS, ASRS) and HCI work on user-driven algorithm auditing (Shen et al., DeVos et al., Lam et al.). It explicitly distinguishes itself from existing incident databases (which lack per-system granularity and aggregation-for-evaluation), flaw disclosure mechanisms like bug bounties and red-teaming (which lack aggregation and population-wide scope), and internal monitoring tools like Clio or LMArena (which lack individual reporting and action triggers). The framework also extends the 'democratic AI' literature, which has focused almost exclusively on pre-deployment participatory design, by arguing that post-deployment reporting is the missing 'consent of the governed' component.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete gap in the AI evaluation ecosystem: while policy directives (EU AI Act, UN resolution) call for public feedback in post-deployment monitoring, no operational framework exists to turn individual experiences into actionable, fine-grained system evaluations. Key open questions include how to handle heterogeneous and potentially adversarial reporting behavior, how to design aggregation methods that are both statistically rigorous (sequential testing, e-values) and capable of processing unstructured text, and how to ensure organizational incentive-alignment so that aggregated findings actually trigger downstream action rather than languishing in a database. The authors acknowledge that the success of AIR is fundamentally empirical and that no public AIR for AI systems currently exists, making this a call to build and study rather than a validated method.
