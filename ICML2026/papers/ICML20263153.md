## Problem

Reinforcement learning struggles to scale to large, combinatorial discrete action spaces (e.g., macro-action sequences, joint multi-agent actions, DNA sequences) where the number of possible actions grows exponentially. Autoregressive policies, the dominant prior approach, impose an artificial causal ordering on actions and are computationally expensive at inference. While discrete diffusion models offer a non-causal, expressive alternative, no principled and efficient framework existed for training them as RL policies with stable, guaranteed policy improvement.

## Input / Output

The method takes an environment state (RGB frames for Atari/MinAtar, game-state features for Google Research Football, or a fixed state for the DNA bandit task) and produces a structured combinatorial action: a sequence of K discrete components such as a length-4-to-32 macro-action, a joint action over up to 11 agents, or a 200-base-pair DNA sequence. Actions are generated by iteratively unmasking a fully masked sequence over N diffusion steps, where each step predicts the clean action for currently-masked positions via a Transformer conditioned on the state. For the DNA task, a pretrained masked diffusion model serves as the initialization, and the output is scored by a separate Enformer-based reward oracle.

## Method

RL-D² decouples RL optimization from representation learning by using Policy Mirror Descent (PMD) to analytically compute a regularized target policy π_MD from the current Q-function, then training a masked discrete diffusion model (parameterized by a Transformer) to match this target via KL divergence minimization. Two practical variants are derived: FKL minimizes a weighted ELBO loss where sampled actions are re-weighted by softmax of their advantages (enabling off-policy training and fast exploitation), while RKL optimizes the original PMD objective using a PPO-style clipped single-step importance-sampling ratio over the augmented diffusion MDP (yielding stable, mode-seeking on-policy updates). An on-policy diffusion learning scheme further aligns training by sampling noised-action trajectories from the policy's own reverse process rather than the fixed forward process. Temperature λ is auto-tuned via a hard KL-constraint duality, and inference efficiency is improved with top-p sampling and remasking for long action sequences.

## Results

On the full Atari benchmark (56 games), RL-D² achieves a mean human-normalized score of 31.33 [29.74, 33.00], substantially outperforming the best baseline IMPALA-Macro (18.01) and DQN (9.49), and winning in 36 of 56 environments. On Google Research Football, RL-D²-RKL attains 99.4% win rate on the 11-vs-11 full game, compared to 91.3% for MAT and 53.9% for MAPPO; in the small-budget 11-vs-11-sm setting, RL-D²-FKL reaches 68.5% vs. 10.2% for MAT. On DNA sequence generation, RL-D² achieves the highest reward and approximate log-likelihood while reducing GPU memory from 66.4 GB to 10.6 GB and training time from 268 to 97 minutes compared to DRAKES.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On Atari, RL-D² (mean 31.33) outperforms all single-action baselines (DQN 9.49, IMPALA 16.55, PPO 4.47, R2D2 6.31) and macro-action variants (DQN-Macro 7.54, IMPALA-Macro 18.01), with the gap widening at longer macro-action lengths where DQN-Macro fails entirely and IMPALA-Macro plateaus. On Google Research Football, RL-D²-RKL dominates MAT (91.3% vs 99.4% on 11vs11) and MAPPO (53.9% vs 99.4%), with the advantage most pronounced in coordination-heavy scenarios like CT-Hard (98.2 vs 87.1 for MAT) and Corner (97.1 vs 94.6). In the small-budget 11vs11-sm setting, RL-D²-FKL (68.5%) dramatically outperforms MAT (10.2%) and MAPPO (4.8%), demonstrating superior sample efficiency. On DNA generation, RL-D² surpasses DRAKES in both reward and log-likelihood while requiring 6× less GPU memory and 2.8× less compute time, and also outperforms non-RL guidance methods (CG, SMC, TDS, CFG). The autoregressive baseline lags significantly behind RL-D² on Google Football, confirming that non-causal diffusion generation better captures inter-agent dependencies than sequential factorization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) Policy Mirror Descent (PMD), which provides a provably convergent, regularized policy improvement step with a closed-form target distribution; (2) masked discrete diffusion models (Shi et al., 2024; Austin et al., 2021), which offer an expressive, non-causal generative parameterization over structured discrete sequences; and (3) diffusion-based policy optimization for continuous control (Ren et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022), which established the augmented-MDP construction for tractable importance sampling ratios. The key departure from prior work is the reframing of the PMD policy update as a distributional matching problem (FKL or RKL to the analytic PMD target), decoupling the RL objective from the representation-learning burden of the diffusion model. This contrasts with autoregressive policy approaches (Wen et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2021) that impose an artificial causal ordering, and with prior reward-based fine-tuning of discrete diffusion (Wang et al., 2024a; Zekri & Boullé, 2025) that operates as single-step optimization without leveraging multi-step transition dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a long-standing scaling bottleneck in RL—combinatorial action spaces that grow exponentially with macro-action length or number of agents—where standard parameterizations (exponential Q-heads, independent factorization) either fail to fit or cannot capture inter-component dependencies. The empirical FKL-vs-RKL trade-off (fast exploitation and sample efficiency vs. stability and asymptotic performance) provides actionable guidance for practitioners choosing between the two variants based on their compute/sampling budget. Open questions include the sensitivity of FKL to temperature tuning (collapse risk), whether the approach extends to truly unstructured combinatorial spaces beyond fixed-length sequences, and the inference-time cost of multi-step diffusion sampling in latency-sensitive deployment. The scalability finding—that diffusion policies continue to improve with model capacity and data while baselines plateau—suggests a promising path for tackling ever-larger action spaces.
