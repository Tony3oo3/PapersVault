## Problem

Model collapse, LLM bias (Stochastic Parrots), and the environmental/financial costs of training are each studied in isolation, but their interaction and combined disproportionate impact on low-resource and marginalized communities has received little attention. This position paper argues that model collapse amplifies existing harms by erasing tail data, reinforcing hegemonic viewpoints, and increasing data/compute requirements in ways that specifically disadvantage communities with limited representation and resources.

## Input / Output

The empirical demonstration uses 15,000 sentences per language from the agentlans multilingual dataset (16 Latin-alphabet languages) as input to a pretrained GPT-2 model, producing log-perplexity distributions per language. The intervention adds 5,000 GPT-2-generated samples per language to the distribution with replacement, and the output is the shifted perplexity distributions showing that low-resource languages (already occupying higher-perplexity tails) shift more dramatically than high-resource ones, illustrating the homogenising effect of collapse.

## Method

This is a position paper whose core contribution is an argumentative framework synthesizing three previously separate literatures: model collapse (tail erasure, altered scaling exponents), Stochastic Parrot bias (value-lock, frequency-based repetition of hegemonic viewpoints), and environmental/financial costs (data acquisition being 10–1000× more expensive than compute). The paper structures its argument around how these factors compound: synthetic data feedback loops overwhelm small real-data distributions, tail erasure removes under-represented cultural content, and the resulting need for more data/compute widens the resource gap. A preliminary empirical demonstration computes GPT-2 log-perplexity on multilingual sentences and shows differential distributional shift when generated samples are added, with low-resource languages affected most. The paper concludes with a taxonomy of eight collapse mechanisms (Table 1) and a call to action for the low-resource NLP community to lead measurement and mitigation research.

## Results

The paper's sole empirical demonstration (Figure 1) uses GPT-2 to compute log-perplexity over 15,000 sentences per language from the agentlans multilingual dataset. High-resource languages (English, French) start at lower perplexity than low-resource ones (Indonesian, Albanian), confirming that low-resource data already occupies the tails of the distribution. After adding 5,000 generated samples per language with replacement, all distributions shift left and begin to overlap, but low-resource languages shift the most, with Swedish and Polish affected worst — illustrating the homogenising and tail-erasure effects of model collapse. No quantitative performance metrics (accuracy, F1, etc.) are reported, as this is a position paper.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper and does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines. The only empirical comparison is the GPT-2 perplexity experiment in Figure 1, which contrasts the original multilingual distribution against a 'collapsing' distribution after synthetic data is added with replacement. The key finding is that low-resource languages (Indonesian, Albanian, Swedish, Polish) experience larger distributional shifts than high-resource languages (English, French), supporting the paper's central claim that model collapse erases tail data first. The paper situates its argument relative to prior position papers — Schaeffer et al. (2025) on precise definitions of model collapse, Bender et al. (2021) on Stochastic Parrots, and Farnadi et al. (2024) on cascading disparity — but does not run head-to-head method comparisons. The authors acknowledge that their perplexity experiment is preliminary and intended only to support the position, not to establish a definitive quantitative gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes three previously separate lines of work—model collapse (Shumailov et al., 2024; Dohmatob et al., 2024; Schaeffer et al., 2025), the Stochastic Parrots critique of LLMs (Bender et al., 2021), and the environmental/financial cost analysis of large-scale training (Strubell et al., 2019; Kandpal & Raffel, 2025)—into a unified argument that model collapse disproportionately erases low-resource and marginalized communities from AI training distributions. It departs from Schaeffer et al. (2025), who caution against over-cautious rhetoric around model collapse, by arguing that even under their more measured framing, the differential impact on communities with smaller data distributions makes the concern urgent for those groups. The paper also draws on iterated learning from cognitive science (Kirby et al., 2008; Jarvis et al., 2026) to contextualize tail erasure as a known phenomenon in generational transmission, while noting that LLMs lack the creative reintroduction mechanisms that stabilize human language.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper raises a critical and underexplored question: whether model collapse will hit low-resource languages and marginalized communities first and hardest, potentially before mitigation methods exist. Its call to action—that low-resource NLP and AI fairness communities should take the lead in measuring and combating model collapse—is timely given that most existing experiments use high-resource or theoretical settings. Key open questions include how collapse manifests differently across agglutinative vs. analytic languages, the role of code-switching in shifting data distributions, and whether the 'race' to generate synthetic content to maintain visibility will force marginalized communities to poison their own data. The empirical evidence remains preliminary (a single GPT-2 perplexity experiment), so the argument's strength rests on logical extrapolation from established findings rather than direct measurement.
