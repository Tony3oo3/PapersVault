## Problem

Discovering analytical solutions to PDEs remains largely manual because the combinatorial space of valid symbolic expressions explodes with problem complexity. Existing AI-augmented symbolic methods cluster at two extremes: unconstrained search suffers from combinatorial explosion and sensitivity to initial guesses, while narrow pretraining biases discovery to a limited class of equations. A principled framework that constrains search to mathematically and physically admissible solutions while generalizing across PDE classes, coupled systems, and problems without known closed-forms was missing.

## Input / Output

SIGS takes as input the symbolic form of a PDE (differential operator minus forcing term), prescribed boundary and initial conditions, a domain specification, and a user-specified structural Ansatz that dictates how solution components combine (e.g., separable, superposition, or coupled-field forms). No simulation data or per-problem retraining is required; candidates are scored purely against the PDE residual and conditions. The output is a closed-form symbolic expression with numerical constants refined to machine precision (relative L2 error ~10⁻¹³–10⁻¹⁴ for known solutions), or a readable symbolic approximation (1–3% error) for PDEs lacking closed-form solutions.

## Method

SIGS constructs a fixed context-free grammar (51 production rules) that generates mathematically valid atoms, then embeds a 23,682-expression atom corpus into a 32-dimensional continuous latent manifold via a Grammar VAE augmented with three topology-aware regularisers (convex-hull enclosure, persistent-homology suppression, and decoder smoothness) to form the TGVAE. Solution discovery proceeds in two stages: Stage I performs iterative clustering in the latent space to select symbolic structures with low PDE residual, and Stage II refines the remaining numerical constants via multi-start Adam gradient descent in float64 JAX until the residual drops below 10⁻⁸. The user-specified Ansatz restricts the admissible assembly family, reducing combinatorial complexity by decomposing the solution into component slots (spatial, temporal, spatiotemporal atoms) that are searched independently and then composed. The entire grammar, corpus, and trained manifold are reused across all benchmarks; only the residual and Ansatz change per problem, making the approach data-free and transferable.

## Results

On the three PDEs with known analytical solutions (Burgers, 1D Diffusion, 2D Damped Wave), SIGS recovers machine-precision solutions with relative L² errors of 6.64×10⁻¹⁴%, 7.14×10⁻¹³%, and 1.22×10⁻¹³% respectively, outperforming the best symbolic baseline (HD-TLGP with atom-level vocabulary) by 2–5 orders of magnitude. On Poisson-Gauss problems with no closed-form solution, SIGS achieves 1.05–2.66% relative L² error against FEniCS references, while HD-TLGP fails entirely (NaN or >10⁷% error) and SSDE reaches only 58–70%. Wall-clock time to solution is 9–111 s for SIGS versus 2,227–14,375 s for HD-TLGP and 379–752 s for SSDE. SIGS is also the first method to handle coupled nonlinear PDE systems (2D Shallow Water at <0.05% error, 2D Compressible Euler at ~10% structural recovery) and to recover equivalent symbolic forms under grammar primitive removal (KdV: 6.6×10⁻⁶% error with sech/cosh removed).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SIGS decisively outperforms both symbolic baselines across all benchmarks. Against HD-TLGP (vanilla, primitive-level dictionary), SIGS improves relative L² error by 2–5 orders of magnitude (e.g., 6.64×10⁻¹⁴% vs. 35.68% on Burgers; 1.05% vs. 5.45×10⁷% on PG-4) and is 100–1000× faster. Against HD-TLGP with the same atom-level vocabulary (isolating the search-geometry contribution), SIGS still wins by 2–5 orders of magnitude, confirming that the latent-manifold search—not just the atom vocabulary—is the critical factor. Against SSDE (which receives a tailored primitive dictionary per problem), SIGS achieves 10⁻¹³–10⁻¹⁴% error versus 45–5870% for SSDE on known-solution PDEs, and 1–3% versus 58–70% on Poisson-Gauss. Neural baselines (PINNs, FBPINNs) achieve moderate 2.5–6% accuracy on known-solution PDEs but cannot produce symbolic expressions; FEniCS provides accurate numerical fields (10⁻³–10⁻²%) but no closed-form. SIGS is the only method that simultaneously achieves machine-precision symbolic recovery, handles coupled nonlinear systems, and produces interpretable approximations for PDEs without known closed-forms.

## Relation to Prior Work

SIGS extends the Grammar Variational Autoencoder (Kusner et al., 2017) from a generative model for symbolic expressions into a search infrastructure for PDE solution discovery, adding topology-aware regularisation (convex-hull, persistent-homology, and decoder-smoothness losses) to make the latent manifold more navigable. It departs from prior neuro-symbolic PDE solvers (HD-TLGP, SSDE) that perform discrete tree search over primitive-level dictionaries by instead embedding grammar-generated atoms into a continuous latent space and searching via iterative clustering followed by gradient-based coefficient refinement. The hierarchical Ansatz-plus-atom formulation generalises classical separation-of-variables and eigenfunction-expansion techniques into a unified, grammar-constrained compositional framework that is reused across problem classes without retraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SIGS demonstrates that grammar-guided latent-space search can recover analytical PDE solutions at machine precision in seconds to minutes, outperforming discrete symbolic baselines by orders of magnitude and eliminating the need for simulation data or per-problem retraining. The ability to handle coupled nonlinear systems (Shallow Water, Compressible Euler) and to produce interpretable symbolic approximations for PDEs without known closed-forms opens a path toward automated, data-free solution discovery in scientific computing. Key limitations remain: the Ansatz still encodes human expert choices, scalability to multiscale or turbulent problems is unaddressed, and the fair-comparison gap for coupled systems (no baseline handles them) limits the strength of the empirical claims. A natural next step the authors identify is using LLMs to automate Ansatz construction, reducing the remaining human bottleneck.
