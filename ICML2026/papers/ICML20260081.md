## Problem

Classical Quadratic Discriminant Analysis assumes Gaussian class-conditional distributions, causing systematic misclassification when data exhibit heavy tails because the Gaussian likelihood decays exponentially in Mahalanobis distance while heavy-tailed data decay polynomially. The paper identifies that this likelihood misspecification is a more fundamental failure mode than estimator instability: correcting the generative model yields larger and more consistent classification gains than robustifying parameter estimation alone, a distinction that prior robust discriminant analysis work has overlooked.

## Input / Output

The method takes a p-dimensional feature vector x and a shared tail index α ∈ (0,2] (estimable via McCulloch's quantile ratio or fixed at the robust default α=1.5) and produces a class label plus calibrated class probabilities via softmax over the log-posteriors. Prediction cost is O(Kp²) per sample—identical to Gaussian QDA—making it a drop-in replacement. The method also outputs a data-driven diagnostic (tail index estimate, determinant ratio, outlier rate) that deterministically selects between robust and standard estimator configurations.

## Method

Stable-QDA replaces the Gaussian log-likelihood term −D_k(x)/2 with an order-equivalent α-stable surrogate −((α+p)/2)·log(1+D_k(x)) − ½log|Σ_k|, where D_k is the squared Mahalanobis distance to class k. This preserves the ranking of class posteriors (and hence the Bayes decision boundary) while converting exponential tail decay to polynomial decay, preventing over-penalization of extreme but informative observations. The method retains QDA's quadratic decision boundaries, closed-form training, and O(Kp²) prediction complexity. A central empirical finding is that standard estimators (sample mean + Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage) frequently outperform robust alternatives (spatial median + Tyler's M-estimator) because Tyler's trace normalization discards inter-class scale differences that the log-determinant term explicitly exploits; a data-driven crossover rule based on the determinant ratio and estimated α selects the appropriate configuration. The paper proves Bayes consistency under infinite variance (α<2), demonstrates Gaussian QDA's inconsistency in the same regime, and shows the classifier is Lipschitz-stable in α, justifying a fixed α=1.5 default.

## Results

On four real-world heavy-tailed benchmarks (HTRU2, Credit Card, Ionosphere, Weekly), Stable-QDA achieves 15–53% error reduction over Gaussian QDA with statistical significance (p < 0.03) on every dataset. The largest gain is on Credit Card fraud detection, where Stable-QDA with standard estimators (sample mean + Ledoit-Wolf) improves PR-AUC by +51.4% and reduces error by 52.9%. On the NetML malware dataset (n up to 114K), standard estimators with the stable likelihood reach 99.64% accuracy versus 99.08% for Gaussian QDA, while Tyler's M-estimator drops to 89.97%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is classical Gaussian QDA (sample mean + sample covariance with Gaussian likelihood). Stable-QDA outperforms Gaussian QDA on all four real-world datasets, with the most dramatic improvement on Credit Card (+51.4% PR-AUC, 52.9% error reduction) and consistent 32–34% error reductions on HTRU2, Ionosphere, and Weekly. Within Stable-QDA, the choice of estimator matters: standard estimators (sample mean + Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage) win on three of four datasets where class scales differ, while robust estimators (spatial median + Tyler's M-estimator) are preferred only for Weekly (α̂ = 1.67, similar class scales). Tyler's M-estimator is particularly harmful under heteroscedasticity—on NetML it drops accuracy by ~10 percentage points relative to standard estimators because its trace normalization discards the inter-class scale information that the QDA log-determinant term exploits. The paper also shows that a fixed α = 1.5 performs within 1% of the oracle tail index across all regimes, eliminating the need for costly α tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends classical QDA by replacing the Gaussian likelihood with an α-stable surrogate, building directly on the elliptically contoured stable distribution theory of Samorodnitsky & Taqqu (1994) and the generalized QDA framework of Bose et al. (2002). It departs sharply from the robust discriminant analysis literature (Huber & Ronchetti, 2009; Ghosh et al., 2021; Houdouin et al., 2022), which has focused on robustifying parameter estimation (M-estimators, high-breakdown covariance) while retaining the Gaussian likelihood. The paper's central conceptual departure is arguing that likelihood misspecification, not estimator instability, is the primary failure mode under heavy tails, and that standard estimators (sample mean, Ledoit-Wolf) often outperform robust alternatives when the likelihood is correctly specified. It also positions itself within the generative-discriminative trade-off literature (Zheng et al., 2023) by providing a generative head suited to the heavy-tailed features produced by frozen pre-trained networks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that correcting the generative model (stable likelihood) yields larger and more consistent gains than robustifying parameter estimation challenges a core assumption in robust statistics and provides practitioners with a concrete, data-driven decision rule for estimator selection. The connection to linear probing on frozen deep features (ViT, CLIP, DINOv2) makes this practically relevant beyond classical QDA, as every practitioner doing transfer learning encounters heavy-tailed feature distributions. Key limitations include the symmetry assumption (suboptimal for skewed data like financial returns), no advantage over Gaussian QDA for light tails, and unexplored interactions with sparse covariance estimation in p≫n regimes. Future work on asymmetric stable distributions and comparisons with discriminative deep classifiers would strengthen the practical case.
