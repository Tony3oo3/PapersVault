## Problem

While differential privacy (DP) protects data providers from model-based inference, the public has no efficient way to verify that a released model was actually trained with DP guarantees. Black-box auditing is fundamentally insufficient—this paper proves that computationally undetectable backdoors can be planted in any DP training API—and existing proof-of-DP approaches require verifying nearly all training compute, making verification as expensive as training itself. The paper asks: can we design a DP-SCO algorithm with near-optimal privacy-utility whose DP guarantee is certifiable cheaper than the fastest known training algorithm?

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a dataset D of size n with d-dimensional inputs, an L-Lipschitz differentiable convex loss f, DP parameters (ε, δ), and initial parameters w_0, and produces model parameters w_k along with a public transcript certifying the (ε,δ)-DP guarantee. Verification is interactive between a Prover (trainer) and Verifier, requiring only n gradient checks and d·⌈log₂(n)⌉ discrete Gaussian RV commitments—substantially fewer than the Ω(n²) gradients or Ω(dn) Gaussian RVs needed by the fastest known DP-SCO training algorithms. The protocol preserves confidentiality of both the dataset and model via zero-knowledge proofs and homomorphic commitments, and the transcript is publicly verifiable given a trusted randomness beacon.

## Method

The paper modifies the phased ERM algorithm (Feldman et al., 2020) in two key ways: (1) replacing the small-excess-loss stopping criterion with a small-gradient-norm condition (‖∇F_i(w̃_i)‖₂ ≤ 2L/(n_i·k)), and (2) changing the per-phase δ to δ/⌈log₂(n)⌉ so that standard (sequential) composition suffices instead of parallel composition. This is the critical insight: parallel composition requires verifying that each phase's solution depends only on its designated data subset, which is expensive to certify, whereas standard composition only requires bounding the sensitivity of each phase's output—achievable by checking the gradient norm via a zero-knowledge proof. The interactive certification protocol (Algorithm 2) has the Prover commit to data and intermediate weights, use a ZKP to verify the gradient-norm condition without revealing the data, run a discrete Gaussian commitment scheme (based on the Knuth-Yao sampler) for each noise coordinate, and homomorphically add committed noise to committed weights. On MNIST-scale binary logistic regression, this reduces verification from ~100 hours (prior DPSGD certification) to ~3 hours on a laptop CPU, with gradient checks dominating runtime and Gaussian RV verification taking less than a millisecond per sample.

## Results

On MNIST-scale binary logistic regression (n=60,000, d=784), the proposed phased ERM certification (Algorithm 2) completes in approximately 3.1 hours on a laptop CPU (MacBook Pro M3), compared to ~100 hours for the prior DPSGD certification approach (Shamsabadi et al., 2024). The verification requires only 60,000 gradient checks (a >4× reduction from the 255,280 gradients in the prior method) and d·⌈log₂(n)⌉ ≈ 5,376 Gaussian RV commitments. Individual gradient verification takes ~109 ms (512 features) while a single Gaussian RV commitment takes ~0.15 ms (σ=1), confirming that gradient checks dominate runtime. Training itself (Algorithm 1) takes only 14–19 seconds on a single GPU, making it negligible relative to verification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against the prior state-of-the-art DP certification method (Shamsabadi et al., 2024), which verified DPSGD training on MNIST by checking 255,280 gradients in ~100 hours. The proposed method reduces gradient checks to 60,000 (a >4× reduction) and total verification time to ~3 hours, a roughly 33× speedup. The reduction in Gaussian RV verification is even more dramatic: the prior approach required Ω(dn) Gaussian RVs while this method needs only d·⌈log₂(n)⌉, and each RV commitment is ~700× faster than a single gradient check. Against the training cost itself, the verification is always cheaper whenever training requires more than one full pass over the dataset (i.e., more than n gradients), which holds for all known optimal-rate DP-SCO algorithms in non-smooth settings. The paper does not compare against other DP-SCO training algorithms on utility (excess risk), as the focus is verification efficiency rather than training performance; the modified phased ERM achieves the same near-optimal excess risk rate O(1/√n + √(d·ln(1/δ))/(εn)) up to a log-log factor.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the phased ERM algorithm of Feldman et al. (2020) for optimal-rate DP-SCO, the certified DP protocol framework of Bell et al. (2024), and the D2D unlearning algorithm of Neel et al. (2020). Its key departure is modifying the phased ERM's stopping criterion (from small excess loss to small gradient norm) and replacing parallel composition with standard composition, which enables verification to be cheaper than training—a property absent from all prior DP certification approaches (e.g., Shamsabadi et al., 2024, which required verifying nearly all training compute). The paper also introduces a conceptual departure by proving that black-box auditing is fundamentally insufficient for verifying DP, via a digital-signature-based backdoor construction analogous to Goldwasser et al. (2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first DP-SCO algorithm whose privacy guarantee can be certified cheaper than training (n gradients vs. Ω(n^1.5) or Ω(n^2) for training), with a demonstrated 4×+ reduction in gradient checks and near-elimination of noise verification cost on MNIST-scale data (3 hours vs. ~100 hours). The backdoor result establishes a fundamental limitation: no amount of black-box auditing can certify DP, motivating the need for interactive cryptographic protocols. Key limitations include restriction to convex losses (not deep networks), linear scaling in dataset size making it impractical for very large datasets, and open questions about verifying non-smooth losses (where randomized smoothing introduces additional gradient checks) and private hyperparameter tuning.
