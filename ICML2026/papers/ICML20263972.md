## Problem

Despite widespread empirical evidence linking flat minima to better generalization, and the development of sharpness-aware algorithms (SAM, SA-GD) explicitly designed to find such minima, the theoretical connection between flatness and generalization remains unestablished. In particular, it is unclear whether finding a flat empirical minimum via these algorithms actually improves population risk compared to standard GD/SGD, which are known to generalize optimally in smooth convex settings. This paper provides the first formal separation showing that, even in the fundamental convex β-smooth regime under a strong flatness assumption, flat minima can generalize strictly worse than sharp ones.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates in the stochastic convex optimization framework: given n i.i.d. samples from a distribution D over an instance space Z, a non-negative convex β-smooth loss f(w,z), and hyperparameters (step size η ≤ 1/4β, perturbation radius r, iterations T), the algorithms produce an averaged iterate ŵ = (1/T)Σw_t. The paper's output is twofold: (i) explicit hard-instance constructions (with dimension d = 2^n or d = 2^n·T) that yield Ω(1) population risk lower bounds for SA-ERM, SA-GD, and SAM, and (ii) dimension-independent population risk upper bounds of the form O(β/n + βr²n) derived via leave-one-out algorithmic stability. The lower bound constructions require the perturbation radius to exceed the true flatness radius (r ≳ ρ + 1/√T) to trigger the failure.

## Method

The paper introduces a strong ρ-flatness condition (the loss vanishes identically in a ball of radius ρ around the minimizer) and analyzes three algorithms: SA-ERM (minimizing the sharpness-aware empirical risk), SA-GD (gradient descent on the SAER), and SAM (the practical normalized-gradient variant). For upper bounds, they prove O(1/ηT + max(r−ρ,0)²) empirical convergence rates for all three methods, showing that flatness accelerates convergence to the SAER objective at the same O(1/T) rate as smooth GD. For lower bounds, the key construction composes a ReLU-squared function h(x) = ½[max(x−ρ,0)]² with a classical spurious-ERM instance, then replicates the construction across T mutually orthogonal subspaces to control the sequence of perturbation vectors {v_t} and force the algorithm to accumulate Ω(η²(r−ρ)²T) population loss. For SAM specifically, they exploit the normalization step to amplify vanishingly small gradients into meaningful progress, coupled with a chaining mechanism across subspaces. Population risk upper bounds are established via ℓ₂ leave-one-out model stability, yielding an additional dominant term O(ηβ²r²T) over standard GD/SGD that nearly matches the lower bounds.

## Results

The paper establishes that in smooth convex optimization, a flat empirical minimizer (SA-ERM) can incur Ω(1) population risk while a sharp minimizer of the same function achieves zero excess risk (Theorem 3.1). For SA-GD, the empirical optimization bound is O(1/ηT + max(r−ρ,0)²), yielding O(β/T) convergence with η=1/4β and r−ρ=O(1/√T), yet the population risk lower bound is Ω(η²(r−ρ)²T), which becomes Ω(1) when η≈1/β and r≈ρ+1/√T. For SAM, the same empirical bound holds, but SAM can converge to a sharp minimum (incurring Ω(r²) SAER gap), and its population risk lower bound is Ω(η²r²T) in the realizable (ρ=0) setting. The population upper bounds for both SA-GD and SAM contain an additional dominant term O(ηβ²r²T) beyond the standard O(β/n) rate of GD/SGD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The natural baselines are standard GD and SGD, which are known to achieve the optimal O(β/n) population risk in smooth, convex, realizable settings (Lei & Ying, 2020; Nikolakakis et al., 2022). The paper's central finding is a provable separation: SA-GD and SAM can exhibit strictly worse generalization than GD/SGD, with population risk as high as Ω(1) in worst-case instances, while GD/SGD maintain O(1/n). Specifically, the upper bounds for SA-GD and SAM contain an additional dominant term ηβ²r²T (i.e., O(βr²n) when T=n, η=O(1/β)) that is absent from GD/SGD bounds, and this term nearly matches the constructed lower bounds. For SA-GD, the gap arises even when the algorithm successfully converges to a flat minimum; for SAM, the gap is compounded by the fact that SAM may fail to minimize the SAER at all, converging to a sharp minimum instead. The paper does not claim sharpness-aware methods always underperform—rather, it shows that explicitly targeting flat minima does not guarantee improved generalization and can, in the worst case, significantly hurt population risk relative to standard gradient methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the flat-minima-and-generalization literature (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997; Keskar et al., 2016; Dinh et al., 2017; Wen et al., 2023) into the canonical stochastic convex optimization (SCO) framework, where prior negative results on ERM generalization (Shalev-Shwartz et al., 2010; Feldman, 2016) were established only in the non-smooth regime. It builds directly on Foret et al. (2021) for the SAM and SA-GD algorithms and on Schliserman & Koren (2022) for the leave-one-out stability-to-generalization machinery used in the upper bounds. The key departure from prior work is that the paper provides explicit, provable constructions in the convex β-smooth realizable setting where flat minimizers generalize strictly worse than sharp ones, going beyond the reparameterization arguments of Dinh et al. (2017) and the non-convex ReLU constructions of Wen et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper delivers the first formal separation showing that sharpness-aware methods (SA-GD, SAM) can exhibit worse population risk than standard GD/SGD in the convex smooth setting, challenging the widely-held heuristic that explicitly seeking flat minima improves generalization. This is important because SAM has become one of the most widely adopted training techniques in practice, and the result clarifies that its empirical benefits cannot be attributed solely to flatness in a worst-case sense. Key limitations include the exponential dimensionality of the hard instances, the restriction to convex objectives, and the use of a very strong (perfectly flat) notion of flatness; open questions include reducing the dimensional dependence, understanding the regime where the perturbation radius r is smaller than the true flatness radius ρ, and extending the analysis to non-convex settings.
