## Problem

Current agentic systems are developed, tuned, and evaluated in narrow domain-specific settings, with benchmarks that encode environment-specific assumptions directly into agent logic (e.g., conditional branches on benchmark names, hardcoded interaction protocols). This fragmentation prevents cross-environment generalization, forces redundant per-deployment engineering, and makes it impossible to compare agents across different evaluation environments without substantial ad hoc adaptation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266346/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the work takes the current landscape of agentic systems, benchmarks, and communication protocols as its analytical input and produces a structured argument for general-purpose agent design alongside concrete ecosystem recommendations. The empirical grounding draws on paired comparisons—358-line ReAct versus 13,768-line ASTA-v0 on scientific research tasks, and 131-line Mini SWE-Agent versus 4,161-line SWE-Agent on software engineering—showing that minimal general agents achieve 70–95% of specialized performance at roughly 7–10× lower cost per task.

## Method

The paper defines generality as a spectrum measured by the reduction of human effort per new deployment, then categorizes existing agents along this spectrum from domain-specific systems through benchmark-conditioned generalists (HAL, CUGA) to environment-agnostic designs (mini-swe-agent) that interact exclusively through a generic command-execution interface without branching on benchmark identifiers. It identifies five levels of evaluation generality—from static agentic skills to fully protocol-agnostic general evaluation—and demonstrates that current benchmarks occupy only levels 1–4, with the critical level 5 (agent-agnostic, protocol-agnostic, cross-environment) remaining unaddressed. The core technical insight is that an environment-agnostic agentic pattern can be extended to a new environment by implementing only an Environment backend exposing execute() and template variables, without modifying the agent's control flow. The paper then argues for ecosystem-level changes: standardized environment affordance communication, agent-agnostic evaluation protocols that do not presuppose a specific interaction mode, and compositionality that decouples foundation models, tools, and agentic patterns into independently optimizable layers.

## Results

On ASTA Bench (scientific research), the generalist ReAct agent (358 LOC) scores 44% at $0.31 per task, approaching the specialized ASTA-v0 system's 53% at $3.40 per task (>13,768 LOC). On SWE-Bench (software engineering), the minimal Mini SWE-Agent (131 LOC) scores 65% at $0.37 per task, nearly matching the specialized SWE-Agent's 67% at ~$2.50 per task (4,161 LOC). Across both domains, small general agents of a few hundred lines achieve 70–95% of specialized system performance at 7–11× lower per-task cost and 30–100× less code.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares general-purpose agents against specialized, domain-tailored agents in two settings. In scientific research (ASTA Bench), ReAct (44%) trails the specialized ASTA-v0 (53%) by 9 percentage points but at 1/11th the cost and 1/38th the code; notably, on the literature-understanding subtask, ReAct (53%) actually outperforms both the specialized ASTA Paper Finder (21%) and OpenAI Deep Research (19%). In software engineering (SWE-Bench), Mini SWE-Agent (65%) trails SWE-Agent (67%) by only 2 points while being 30× smaller and ~7× cheaper per run. The paper also contrasts benchmark-conditioned generalist agents (HAL, CUGA) that encode environment-specific logic (e.g., 20+ conditional branches on benchmark name) with environment-agnostic designs (mini-swe-agent) that require only a generic execute() interface to operate in new settings, arguing the latter is the more robust path toward true generality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper extends the long trajectory from domain-specific AI (heuristics → algorithms → per-task models → foundation models) to the agentic setting, arguing that the same abstraction logic should drive agent development from benchmark-specific to environment-agnostic designs. It builds conceptually on GATO's early generalist-agent framing (Reed et al., 2022), the 'narrow waist' principle from network protocol design (Clark, 1988; Cerf & Kahn, 1974), and Sutton's Bitter Lesson, while contrasting environment-agnostic patterns like mini-swe-agent against benchmark-conditioned systems such as HAL and CUGA. The paper carves new ground by explicitly defining generality as a spectrum of reduced per-deployment human effort and calling for ecosystem-level standardization (protocols, evaluation, compositionality) rather than proposing a single new architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If the field adopts the paper's call for agent-agnostic evaluation and standardized environment-affordance protocols, it could eliminate the current fragmentation where each benchmark (Tau-Bench, WebArena, TerminalBench) assumes a mutually incompatible agent interface, enabling meaningful cross-environment comparison. Key open questions include whether general agents can close the remaining performance gap in highly specialized domains, what the right protocol layer looks like (MCP and A2A are identified as insufficient), and how to balance generality with the control and predictability that safety-critical deployments require. The paper's empirical support is limited to two anecdotal case studies, so the central claim—that general agents consistently achieve 70–95% of specialized performance at a fraction of the cost—remains to be validated across broader task families and scales.
