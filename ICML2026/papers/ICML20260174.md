## Problem

Standard survival evaluation metrics (C-index, IBS, calibration) are known to be affected by censoring, but the literature lacks a systematic analysis that disentangles numerical bias in metric values from errors in model ranking, and that isolates the respective roles of censoring rate versus censoring mechanism across metric families. This gap means that common benchmarking practices—fold-averaged scores without explicit censoring diagnostics—can produce unreliable model comparisons, especially under informative or heavy censoring.

## Input / Output

The framework starts from five real survival datasets (NACD, GBMLGG, METABRIC, PBC, FLCHAIN) with fully observed event times, then generates semi-synthetic censoring at controlled rates (10–90%) under three mechanisms (administrative, independent, covariate-dependent). Each configuration admits a paired evaluation: a standard (ST) metric computed on censored observations (x_i, y_i, δ_i) and an oracle (OR) metric computed on true event times (x_i, T_i, 1), enabling direct quantification of censoring-induced distortion. Outputs include IQR-normalized bias per metric, Top-1 consistency between ST and OR model selections, and a dominance-based global ranking agreement score (τ_dom) computed over statistically significant model pairs.

## Method

The core contribution is a controlled semi-synthetic censoring generation protocol: starting from individuals with known event times, the paper fits a Cox model (for CDC) or Kaplan-Meier (for IC) to the original censoring process, then uses weighted sampling and inverse-transform sampling to assign censoring times that match a target rate while preserving the covariate structure. Seven survival models (CoxPH, Weibull-AFT, RSF, DeepSurv, DeepHit, NMTLR, DCM) are trained under nested cross-validation with hyperparameters tuned on Harrell's C-index, and evaluated across 5 datasets × 3 mechanisms × 5 rates × 5 replications. Bias is normalized by the oracle IQR at each (dataset, mechanism, rate) level to make distortions comparable across heterogeneous metrics. Ranking preservation is assessed two ways: Top-1 consistency (does ST select the same best model as OR?) and a dominance-based agreement score that applies a cross-validation-corrected paired t-test to each model pair, counting only pairs significant under both ST and OR, then computing (concordant − discordant)/(concordant + discordant). This dual ST/OR design isolates metric-computation distortion from model-quality differences, which no prior work had systematically separated.

## Results

Across five survival datasets and seven models, normalized metric bias (Eq. 5) increases monotonically with censoring rate ρ, with the censoring mechanism playing a secondary, metric-dependent role. Under covariate-dependent censoring (CDC), substantial bias emerges already at moderate censoring (ρ ≥ 50%), while under administrative (AC) and independent (IC) censoring, bias remains limited until ρ ≥ 70%. Top-1 consistency decreases with ρ across all metrics, but dominance-based global ranking agreement (τ_dom) often remains near 1 for concordance-based metrics under non-informative censoring, indicating that global ordering is preserved even when the apparent best model changes. D-Calibration is the most fragile criterion, showing consistently negative bias (ST overstates calibration) that grows sharply under IC and CDC, while fixed-horizon concordance C(t₂₅) and truncated IBS(t₂₅) are the most robust variants across mechanisms.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new survival model; instead, it compares the behavior of existing evaluation metrics (Harrell's C-index, Antolini's C-index, Uno's IPCW C-index, fixed-horizon C(t_q), IPCW IBS, pseudo-observation IBS, IBS-ADMIN, D-Calibration, and 1-Calibration) under three censoring mechanisms (AC, IC, CDC) and five censoring rates (10–90%), using a standard-vs-oracle paired evaluation protocol. Among concordance metrics, global measures (C-Harrell, C-Antolini, C-Uno) are consistently more biased than fixed-horizon variants C(t_q), with C(t₂₅) being the most robust; IPCW corrections (KM- or Cox-based) do not fundamentally alter this pattern. Among IBS variants, full-horizon IPCW IBS and pseudo-observation IBS show a sign change in bias as ρ increases (slightly pessimistic at low ρ, overly optimistic at high ρ), while truncated IBS(t₂₅) achieves the smallest bias under IC but remains unreliable under CDC. D-Calibration degrades far more severely than 1-Calibration under IC and CDC, with the former showing near-uniform bin inflation from censored observations. For ranking preservation, C-index-based metrics maintain high global agreement (τ_dom ≈ 1) under AC/IC even when Top-1 consistency drops, whereas IBS and D-Calibration exhibit genuine rank inversions among statistically discriminable pairs under CDC. The significance-aware Top-1 analysis reveals that many apparent Top-1 changes are driven by statistically indistinguishable model pairs, reducing comparable cases from 225 to 130 and indicating that reliable winner identification becomes increasingly difficult as censoring increases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on a growing body of critiques of survival evaluation under censoring—Alabdallah et al. (2024) on concordance decomposition, Kvamme & Borgan (2023) on Brier score bias under administrative censoring, Prince et al. (2025) on IPCW sensitivity to censoring mechanism, and Lillelund et al. (2025a,b) on dependent-censoring failures—but unifies these fragmented findings under a single controlled semi-synthetic protocol. Rather than proposing new metrics or models, the paper repurposes the standard-vs-oracle comparison (enabled by retaining true event times in a semi-synthetic construction) to systematically disentangle the roles of censoring rate and mechanism across concordance, Brier-score, and calibration metric families. Its methodological contribution is the paired ST/OR evaluation design combined with a dominance-based ranking agreement score that filters out statistically indistinguishable model pairs, going beyond the fold-averaged summaries that dominate current benchmarking practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic, multi-metric, multi-mechanism quantification of how censoring distorts survival evaluation, yielding concrete practical guidelines (report censoring rates, avoid Top-1-only conclusions, use early-horizon metrics with bias-variance awareness) that directly affect how survival models are benchmarked in healthcare, churn, and reliability settings. Key open questions include a formal theoretical characterization of the IBS bias sign change under heavy censoring, extension to competing risks and time-dependent covariates, and whether the observed IPCW insufficiency under covariate-dependent censoring persists with more flexible censoring models. The semi-synthetic design, while enabling clean oracle comparisons, necessarily abstracts from unobserved confounding and other real-world complexities, limiting direct transferability to observational studies.
