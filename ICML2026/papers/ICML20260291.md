## Problem

Recommender systems under repeated engagement optimization suffer from feedback-driven exposure concentration, where the system progressively narrows its exposure distribution onto a few categories, collapsing catalog coverage and degrading long-horizon learning. Existing mitigation methods (post-hoc diversification, re-ranking constraints, RL-based control) are typically motivated by diversity or unbiased estimation goals rather than closed-loop control of exposure dynamics, and they lack principled uncertainty quantification for the risk of crossing a critical saturation threshold under stochastic user feedback.

## Input / Output

CORAL operates as a model-agnostic plug-in layer atop any sequential recommender backbone (SASRec, BERT4Rec). At each step it consumes the user's interaction history, the item catalog with category assignments, the backbone's per-item ranking scores, and platform-defined safety parameters (threshold λ_max, risk budget δ, exploration constant κ). It outputs a single recommended item: first a category is selected via a penalized OFU rule, then the highest-scoring item within that category is chosen by the backbone. The Hawkes intensity parameters (base rates, susceptibility matrix, decay rates) are estimated offline via MLE on the user's full history and then updated online at each step.

## Method

CORAL formulates exposure regulation as a constrained sequential decision problem with three coupled components. First, a Hawkes-inspired self-exciting intensity model (Eq. 2) captures self-reinforcing user interactions, from which an exposure saturation state D_t is computed as the cumulative weighted excess intensity above a baseline (Eq. 5). Second, the paper derives an uncertainty-aware upper confidence bound on the category-conditioned violation risk using Freedman's inequality for martingale difference sequences (Theorem 1), yielding a conservative risk estimate that shrinks with more observations. Third, a state-dependent penalty Λ(D_t) = min{Λ_max, 1/max(ε, λ_max − D_t)} scales the risk bound so that intervention is minimal when far from the threshold and saturates near it, producing gradual proportional regulation. Category selection optimizes an OFU objective combining optimistic utility and the scaled risk penalty (Eq. 12). Theoretical analysis establishes finite-time recovery from saturation (Theorem 2) and sublinear cumulative regret over the horizon (Theorem 3).

## Results

On exposure safety, CORAL achieves the lowest Exposure Concentration (EC) and Peak Saturation (PS) across all three datasets under both SASRec and Bert4Rec backbones. For example, with Bert4Rec on Amazon, CORAL reaches EC=0.18 and PS=0.81, compared to the best competing method TD-VAE-CF (EC=0.42, PS=0.78) and Naive (EC=0.46, PS=1.25). On utility, CORAL retains near-Naive performance (e.g., R@10=0.1386 vs. Naive 0.1452 on Amazon with Bert4Rec) while dramatically reducing saturation, and runs substantially faster than Filter (519s vs. 1529s) and TD-VAE-CF (519s vs. 2119s). In closed-loop simulation, CORAL simultaneously outperforms Naive in Cumulative Reward while achieving 1.5–2× higher Sequence Category Coverage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CORAL dominates all four baselines on both EC and PS across every dataset and backbone. Against Filter (similarity-based hard filtering), CORAL reduces EC by 59–65% (e.g., 0.18 vs. 0.44 on Amazon/Bert4Rec) while Filter collapses utility (R@10 drops from 0.1452 to 0.0650). Against TD-VAE-CF (VAE-based targeted diversification), CORAL achieves 57% lower EC on Amazon (0.18 vs. 0.42) and 24% lower on Steam (0.22 vs. 0.25), with the gap widening for the Top 10% most over-saturated users (EC 0.24 vs. 0.88). Against NuGE (graph-based nudging), CORAL halves EC on Amazon (0.18 vs. 0.29) and Steam (0.22 vs. 0.26), while NuGE suffers the largest utility collapse (R@10=0.0281 on Amazon/Bert4Rec). Against RL (controllable recommender), CORAL reduces EC by 54% on Amazon (0.18 vs. 0.39) and 31% on Steam (0.22 vs. 0.32), showing that generic RL control without an explicit saturation state signal is insufficient for tail-risk mitigation.

## Relation to Prior Work

CORAL sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) temporal point processes / Hawkes models for self-exciting user behavior (building on Wen & Fang, 2022), (2) safe online learning and constrained decision-making under uncertainty (Gu et al., 2024), and (3) the multi-armed bandit / OFU literature for exploration. It departs from prior exposure-regulation methods (post-hoc filtering, VAE-based diversification, RL-based controllable recommenders) by reframing the problem as a closed-loop constrained sequential decision problem with a history-dependent saturation state and a martingale-based confidence bound on violation risk, rather than a static diversity or unbiased-estimation objective. The Hawkes intensity model is repurposed from a predictive tool into a control signal, and the Freedman's-inequality risk bound is adapted from bandit theory to the non-stationary, endogenous-feedback setting of recommendation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CORAL provides the first (to our knowledge) theoretical guarantees—finite-time recovery from saturation and sublinear regret—for a practical exposure-regulation mechanism in sequential recommendation, and it is model-agnostic, plugging atop any backbone. The closed-loop experiments with an LLM-based user simulator suggest that breaking echo chambers is not merely a diversity trade-off but can improve long-horizon utility, which challenges the common assumption that safety and utility are in tension. Open questions include tightening the confidence bounds (currently loose via union bound over T×|C|), extending the Hawkes state to handle non-stationary user preferences more robustly, and validating the framework at production scale where category taxonomies are far larger and feedback is noisier.
