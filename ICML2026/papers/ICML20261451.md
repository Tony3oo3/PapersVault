## Problem

Evaluating expert-level deep research reports remains challenging because existing benchmarks use coarse, underspecified criteria; LLM judges lack the domain expertise to detect subtle errors; and source verification is typically limited to explicitly cited claims, leaving large portions of reports unchecked. DEER addresses all three gaps by combining an expert-systematized rubric, task-specific domain guidance, and a claim-level verification architecture that covers both cited and uncited claims.

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline consumes a reformulated research query (derived from Humanity's Last Exam items, stripped of answer-revealing content), the generated report, all cited source URLs, and task-specific Expert Evaluation Guidance authored by domain experts. It produces 101 per-item rubric scores (1–10 scale with rationale), which are hierarchically aggregated into 7 dimension-level scores (Request Fulfillment, Analytical Soundness, Structural Coherence, Format & Style, Information Integrity, Information Sufficiency, Ethics Compliance), plus quantitative claim-verification metrics (Claim Factuality, Citation Support, Evidence Coverage, Reference Reliability/Diversity). The full evaluation costs approximately $0.5–$1.0 per report using GPT-5.2 for rubric scoring and GPT-5-mini for claim verification.

## Method

DEER's core architecture is a hybrid of two complementary evaluation components. For report quality, a fixed set of 101 fine-grained rubric items—operationalized from a 7-dimension, 25-subdimension taxonomy synthesized from 80 established reporting standards across 20 domains—is scored by an LLM judge that also receives task-specific Expert Evaluation Guidance (concrete, verifiable content expectations per task) to surface domain-specific issues the judge would otherwise miss. For external information, the Information Verification module extracts atomic claims, classifies them into six types (A–F), and applies a semantic Back-Tracking mechanism that identifies preceding sentences a claim depends on and inherits their citations, thereby recovering evidence for uncited claims (Types B/C) that standard citation checkers would skip. Verifiable claims (Types A–C) are then checked against retrieved evidence documents under a strict support criterion, and outcomes are aggregated into Integrity and Sufficiency metrics. This design ensures that evaluation is both interpretable (fixed rubric applied identically across all 50 tasks) and comprehensive (report-wide claim coverage rather than only inline-cited sentences).

## Results

Across 50 expert report-generation tasks spanning 13 domains, the best-performing system (GPT-5.2 with reasoning + web search) achieves a mean score of 6.62/10, followed by OpenAI Deep Research (6.50) and Gemini 2.5 Pro in deep-research mode (6.42). All systems score relatively well on Structural Coherence (5.12–7.20), Format & Style (6.65–8.11), and Ethics (6.85–7.97), but lag substantially on Request Fulfillment (4.22–5.54) and Analytical Soundness (4.44–6.21). A notable finding is that reasoning-only models (think) outperform think+search and deep-research systems on report-quality dimensions (excluding information metrics), suggesting that integrating diverse external sources can blur problem definition and argument structure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DEER evaluates six system families across four capability tiers. Among dedicated deep-research systems, OpenAI Deep Research (6.50) edges out Gemini 2.5 Pro deep (6.42), Qwen3-235B deep (6.09), WebThinker (5.98), and Claude Opus 4.5 deep (5.98) on the overall mean. However, GPT-5.2 with reasoning + web search (6.62) surpasses all deep-research systems, primarily due to superior Request Fulfillment (5.50) and Analytical Soundness (6.05) scores, while deep-research systems lead on Information Sufficiency (Qwen3-235B deep: 7.90; Gemini 2.5 Pro deep: 7.61). A counterintuitive finding is that reasoning-only models (e.g., GPT-5.2 think: 5.89) outperform their think+search counterparts on report-quality dimensions (Structural Coherence 7.20 vs. 7.13; Format & Style 8.02 vs. 7.94), suggesting that web-search integration can degrade argument structure. Across all tiers, no system exceeds 6.21 on Analytical Soundness or 5.54 on Request Fulfillment, indicating that expert-level reasoning completeness and intent alignment remain the primary bottlenecks for current deep research agents.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of deep research report evaluation benchmarks (DeepResearchGym, DeepResearchBench, DeepResearchArena, ResearchRubrics) by addressing three specific gaps: (1) replacing coarse or LLM-generated evaluation criteria with an expert-developed taxonomy of 7 dimensions, 25 subdimensions, and 101 fixed rubric items grounded in 80 established reporting standards across 20 domains; (2) adding task-specific Expert Evaluation Guidance to compensate for LLM judges' lack of domain expertise; and (3) introducing a semantic back-tracking mechanism that verifies uncited claims by inheriting citations from semantically dependent preceding sentences, going beyond prior work that only checks explicitly cited claims. The data construction pipeline repurposes Humanity's Last Exam seed questions, reformulated by domain experts into open-ended report-generation prompts, distinguishing DEER from QA-style benchmarks like BrowseComp or Mind2Web2.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DEER provides the first systematic, interpretable evaluation framework that decomposes expert report quality into fine-grained, consistently applied criteria, enabling practitioners to diagnose *where* and *why* deep research systems fail rather than relying on a single aggregate score. The finding that even the best evaluated system (GPT-5.2 think+search, 6.62/10) scores below 5.5 on Request Fulfillment and Analytical Soundness suggests a substantial gap between current capabilities and expert-level report generation. Open questions include whether the text-only scope generalizes to multimodal research reports, whether LLM-based judging with expert guidance can fully substitute for human experts at scale, and whether the taxonomy transfers to domains outside the 13 covered. The benchmark's cost (~$0.5–1.0 per report) makes it practical for iterative development, but the reliance on GPT-5.2 as judge backbone raises questions about circularity when evaluating GPT-family systems.
