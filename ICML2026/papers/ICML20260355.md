## Problem

Vision-Language Models consistently outperform their underlying LLMs on purely text-only retrieval tasks, especially in long-context settings, yet the internal mechanism responsible for this cross-modal benefit remains unexplained. This paper asks why training on image-based objectives improves text-only generalization and identifies the specific computational shift that drives the gain.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260355/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The controlled task takes a context of attribute-entity pairs (e.g., color-shape) presented either as sequential text tokens or as 224×224 rendered SVG images encoded by a frozen vision encoder (ResNet-152, ViT-B/16, or DINOv3), followed by a text-based entity-item association list and a color query; the model outputs the target item label. For pre-trained model evaluation, inputs are natural-language contexts of name-city and food-person statements, and outputs are city names. Image encoders produce patch-token sequences (e.g., 196 tokens for a 14×14 grid) that are projected into the language model's embedding space via a 3-layer MLP, while associations and queries remain in text across all modalities.

## Method

The paper trains a 12-layer decoder-only Transformer (RoPE, d_model=128) from scratch on a synthetic Indirect Retrieval task using a five-stage text curriculum, then continues training on the image modality with frozen vision encoders, and finally transfers back to a mixed text-image distribution. Using interchange interventions following Gur-Arieh et al. (2026), the authors show that text-only training produces a brittle positional binding circuit (relying on shared position indices between two independent attention streams), while image training—driven by spatial translation invariance and other encoder inductive biases—forces a shift to symbolic binding circuits that explicitly transfer semantic content (color or shape identity) between tokens. Attention knockouts and linear probes confirm three distinct circuit architectures: a Positional Circuit and two Symbolic Circuit variants (Color-Key and Shape-Key), with the symbolic circuits transferring attribute identity rather than position indices. The mechanism shift is validated on large-scale Qwen and InternLM models, where VLM variants consistently exhibit higher symbolic-to-positional binding ratios than their LLM counterparts, and noise-augmentation ablations confirm that positional range expansion alone cannot account for the generalization gains.

## Results

On the controlled Indirect Retrieval task, the text-only transformer achieves 37.2% average OOD accuracy, while the image-trained variant (M_image-text) reaches 69.5%, and the combined noise+image model (M_noise-image-text) reaches 83.6%. On pre-trained models, Qwen3-VL-8B achieves 63.5% accuracy on a one-hop retrieval task versus 48.2% for Qwen3-8B, and VLMs consistently outperform their LLM counterparts across all four model families as context length increases. However, on eight standard text benchmarks (Table 1), VLMs underperform their LLM counterparts, with mean scores dropping from 69.9 (Qwen3) to 62.6 (Qwen3-VL) and from 71.9 (Qwen2.5) to 64.7 (Qwen2.5-VL).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four LLM–VLM pairs (Qwen 2, Qwen 2.5, Qwen 3, and InternLM 3) on two axes: long-context retrieval and standard text benchmarks. On retrieval tasks, every VLM variant outperforms its LLM counterpart, with the gap widening as context length grows; Qwen3-VL-8B reaches 63.5% versus 48.2% for Qwen3-8B on one-hop retrieval, and InternLM 3 shows the largest symbolic-to-positional binding ratio increase (+0.37) coinciding with the largest retrieval gain. On the eight standard text benchmarks (Table 1), the pattern reverses: LLMs consistently outperform their VLM counterparts by 2.5–7.3 points in mean accuracy (e.g., Qwen3 69.9 vs. Qwen3-VL 62.6; Qwen2.5 71.9 vs. Qwen2.5-VL 64.7), with the largest drops on ARC-C (−9.7 for Qwen3-VL) and HellaSwag (−20.0 for Qwen3-VL). The authors note that Qwen 2 exhibits the smallest retrieval gap between LLM and VLM, consistent with its smaller shift in binding mechanism, while InternLM 3 shows the largest retrieval improvement alongside the largest binding-ratio change.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: mechanistic interpretability of binding in transformers (Gur-Arieh et al., 2026; Feng & Steinhardt, 2024; Wu et al., 2025) and the study of how multimodal training reshapes language model capabilities (Dai et al., 2024b; Ratzlaff et al., 2025). While prior work characterized positional, symbolic, and reflexive binding mechanisms in LLMs, and separate work noted that VLMs sometimes match or exceed LLM text performance, no prior study had connected these two observations by showing that vision training causally shifts the dominant binding mechanism. The paper adopts the interchange intervention framework of Gur-Arieh et al. (2026) and the attention knockout methodology of Geva et al. (2023), extending them to a controlled cross-modal training setting with small transformers and validating on large-scale Qwen and InternLM models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that cross-modal training acts as an inductive bias that disrupts positional shortcuts and promotes robust symbolic binding offers a concrete, mechanistic explanation for a previously puzzling phenomenon (VLMs outperforming LLMs on text-only retrieval). This suggests multimodal objectives could be a principled tool for mitigating shortcut learning more broadly, though the benefit is task-dependent—Table 1 shows VLMs underperform on standard text benchmarks. Key open questions include whether other modalities (audio, video) induce analogous shifts, why some training runs retain positional binding even with fixed object positions (suggesting additional encoder-level inductive biases beyond translation invariance), and which task properties specifically benefit from cross-modal inductive biases.
