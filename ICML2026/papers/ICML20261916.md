## Problem

Prior work on MoE LLM safety has focused on utility, efficiency, or diagnostic expert-level analyses that depend on predefined unsafe datasets, leaving the combinatorial space of routing configurations largely unexplored. This paper asks whether the sparse routing architecture itself constitutes a distinct attack surface: can specific routing configurations (unsafe routes) be discovered that flip a safety-aligned model's refusal into harmful output without modifying expert weights?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261916/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a harmful question (text prompt), per-layer routing scores (internal router states accessible via white-box access), and a harmful target string. It produces an unsafe route — a sequence of per-token, per-layer routing masks (binary 0/−∞ vectors applied element-wise to router scores before Top-k selection) — along with RoSais importance scores per layer. Unlike prompt-based jailbreaks, the output is a structural routing configuration rather than a modified input, and it can be applied at inference time without retraining.

## Method

The paper introduces two complementary mechanisms. First, RoSais quantifies each router's safety criticality by measuring the maximum increase in affirmative-token log-probability across S₁ random routing masks, then constructs unsafe routes by masking the top-L_Φ highest-RoSais routers with the best-performing mask from S₂ trials. Second, F-SOUR performs a fine-grained token-layer-wise stochastic search: it processes tokens sequentially and layers in order, sampling random masks at each (token, layer) pair and greedily retaining any mask that increases the log-probability of the harmful target, with a threshold τ to limit computation and a shadow judge (GPT-4o-mini) with up to S₄ restarts to verify the final output is genuinely harmful. This progressive, token-aware search outperforms the static layer-level RoSais approach by accounting for sequentiality and cross-layer interactions, achieving ~0.90 average ASR across four MoE families.

## Results

F-SOUR achieves an average attack success rate (ASR) of 0.90 on JailbreakBench and 0.98 on AdvBench across four MoE LLM families (DeepSeek-V2-Lite, Mixtral-8x7B, OLMoE-1B-7B, Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B), surpassing all five baselines by 40–61 percentage points. On DeepSeek-V2-Lite, F-SOUR raises ASR from 0.15 (no attack) to 0.94 on JailbreakBench and to 1.00 on AdvBench, a 5.3× increase. The method also converts OLMoE-1B-7B, which has a 0.00 baseline ASR, into a model that produces harmful responses in 86% of JailbreakBench cases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

F-SOUR dominates all five baselines on both JailbreakBench and AdvBench. Against the strongest white-box baseline SteerMoE (avg ASR 0.50 on JailbreakBench, 0.55 on AdvBench), F-SOUR gains 40 and 43 percentage points respectively. The other MoE-specific method SAFEx (avg 0.31/0.37) and the gradient-based GCG (avg 0.46/0.48) are further behind, while SHIPS (avg 0.29/0.32) performs worst among white-box methods, even scoring 0.00 on DeepSeek-V2-Lite. The black-box TAP method (avg 0.47/0.54) is competitive on Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B (0.77) but lags on the other three models. Notably, the MoE-specific baselines (SAFEx, SteerMoE) require prior unsafe/safe datasets and still achieve ≤50% ASR, whereas F-SOUR needs no prior data and reaches ≥0.86 on every model. F-SOUR also outperforms the paper's own coarser RoSais-based methods (avg 0.59 sample-level, 0.55 dataset-level on JailbreakBench), confirming that token-layer-wise progressive search is substantially more effective than static layer-level importance ranking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM jailbreak literature (GCG, TAP, SHIPS) from input-level and attention-head-level manipulation to the routing layer of MoE architectures, treating the top-k expert selection sequence as a distinct attack surface. It departs from the closest prior MoE safety work (SAFEx, SteerMoE), which is diagnostic and data-dependent—identifying safety-critical experts by contrasting activations across pre-collected safe/unsafe datasets—by instead proactively searching over routing configurations without requiring any prior data. Methodologically, RoSais draws on sensitivity-based importance scoring (SHIPS) but applies it to router gating scores rather than attention heads, while F-SOUR introduces a token-layer-wise stochastic search that explicitly models sequential routing dynamics absent in all prior approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that MoE safety is structurally sparse—concentrated in a handful of routers whose manipulation flips refusal to harmful output—challenges the assumption that safety alignment is uniformly distributed across model parameters and has direct implications for how the rapidly scaling MoE model family (DeepSeek, Mixtral, Qwen, OLMoE) should be trained and deployed. The near-complete failure of existing defenses (PAT, Self-Reminder) against the fine-grained F-SOUR attack (≤0.06 ASR reduction) highlights a gap in current safety tooling. Open questions include whether safety-aware router training or coverage objectives can close the expert-level safety heterogeneity, whether the vulnerability persists under adaptive attacks that observe route disabling, and whether black-box variants of route exploitation exist for closed-API MoE models.
