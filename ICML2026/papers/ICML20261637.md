## Problem

The Cox proportional hazards model's partial likelihood is non-decomposable: each risk set requires summing over all historically eligible samples, making online or federated learning infeasible without retaining raw data. Existing online survival methods either approximate the baseline hazard with fixed-dimensional piecewise-constant functions or use mini-batch SGD, both of which degrade statistical efficiency and fail to provide valid asymptotic inference. No prior method achieves oracle-level semiparametric efficiency while operating under constant memory and one-pass streaming constraints.

## Input / Output

COLSA consumes sequentially arriving batches of survival triplets (covariate vector X ∈ ℝ^d, observed time Y, event indicator Δ) and produces hazard ratios with valid Wald Z-statistics and confidence intervals, plus a smooth estimate of the log-baseline hazard. The only persistent state is a cumulative negative Hessian matrix of size O((d+p)²) where p grows as ⌊CN^ν⌋ with cumulative sample size N, rather than any raw data. The polynomial degree p and a look-ahead shadow degree ρp are updated adaptively per batch, so the stored matrix dimension grows sublinearly with total data volume.

## Method

COLSA replaces the non-decomposable Cox partial likelihood with a full likelihood in which the log-baseline hazard is approximated by Bernstein basis polynomials of degree p, making the per-batch log-likelihood additive and independent of global risk sets. A renewable second-order estimating equation then updates the estimator in one shot per batch using only the current batch's gradient and Hessian plus a cumulative Hessian from history, eliminating iterative optimization. The key architectural contribution is a two-stage dynamic sieve mechanism: a Progressive Activation stage that projects historical coefficients and the Hessian to the new polynomial degree via exact degree-elevation matrices, and a Pre-estimation (shadow) stage that maintains a higher-degree Hessian using Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse projections so that future degree increases require no information loss. Theoretical analysis proves consistency at rate N^{-min(qν,(1-ν)/2)} and asymptotic normality with the same variance as the centralized oracle estimator, under standard smoothness and regularity conditions.

## Results

In simulations across K ∈ {6, 20, 50, 200} batches, COLSA achieves Abias and RMSE nearly identical to the centralized Oracle estimator, while SGD and Online show substantially larger bias that grows with K. On the SRTR kidney transplant dataset (N=48,766, 49 state batches), COLSA Z-statistics closely track Oracle (e.g., Recipient Age: −21.23 vs. −21.30; Donor Deceased: 18.03 vs. 18.67). On TCGA pan-cancer data (N=7,315, 18 cancer-type batches), COLSA achieves Pearson r = 0.997 on Z-statistics and mean absolute log-HR difference of 0.003 versus Oracle, correctly recovering significance decisions on 22 of 23 gene candidates. Storage complexity is O((d+p̄)²) with p̄ = O(N^ν), requiring no raw data retention.

## Baselines & Comparisons

COLSA consistently outperforms all three streaming baselines (Meta, Online, SGD) in both bias and statistical inference accuracy. On SRTR, COLSA Z-statistics are within 0.1–0.7 of Oracle for most variables, whereas SGD shows severe inflation (e.g., Recipient Hypertension Z=13.16 vs. Oracle 1.29; Donor Obesity Z=9.58 vs. 1.63) and Online produces false positives (e.g., HLA 3-4MM/1-2DR Z=14.30 vs. Oracle 9.91). On TCGA, COLSA correctly recovers 22/23 significance decisions matching Oracle, while Meta misses 5 Oracle-significant genes, Online disagrees on 12 decisions, and SGD disagrees on 10. In simulations, COLSA's Abias and RMSE trajectories are nearly indistinguishable from Oracle across all K values, while SGD (Offline) requires 58.14 s at K=200 versus COLSA's 28.36 s and still shows higher bias. The Online method degrades sharply under small batch sizes (n_k=100) due to insufficient grid resolution, whereas COLSA maintains Oracle-level performance thanks to the global smoothness of Bernstein polynomials.

## Relation to Prior Work

COLSA sits at the intersection of three established threads: the renewable estimating equation framework of Luo & Song (2020) for one-shot streaming updates, the sieve MLE theory of Shen & Wong (1994) for nonparametric survival regression, and the pre-estimation strategy of Quan & Lin (2024) for dynamic basis expansion under memory constraints. The key departure from prior online survival methods (Wu et al., 2021; Tarkhan & Simon, 2024; Zeng et al., 2026) is the replacement of the non-decomposable Cox partial likelihood with a decomposable full likelihood via Bernstein polynomial sieve approximation, combined with a two-stage (active + shadow) Hessian aggregation that handles growing model dimensionality through degree elevation matrices. This is conceptually distinct from SGD-based or piecewise-constant approaches that approximate the risk set locally, as COLSA preserves the global smoothness structure of the baseline hazard.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

COLSA provides the first online Cox estimation method with a proven oracle efficiency guarantee (asymptotic normality with the same variance as full-data MLE) while requiring only constant memory and one-pass updates, making it viable for privacy-sensitive federated and streaming healthcare applications. The practical validation on SRTR (49 states, N≈49K) and TCGA (18 cancer types) demonstrates that the method recovers centralized inference decisions, including subtle effects missed by competing online methods. Open questions include extension to high-dimensional covariates (the current framework assumes fixed d), incorporation of formal differential privacy guarantees, and reducing the O((d+p̄)²) Hessian storage and computation cost as the number of batches grows. The computational overhead relative to SGD (28s vs 1.6s at K=200) may limit applicability to very high-throughput streaming scenarios.
