## Problem

Selecting small, high-quality coresets from large instruction-tuning corpora typically relies on sample-level gradient or Hessian signals that are computationally expensive to compute at scale and suffer from systematic length and loss-centric biases. The paper asks how to faithfully identify high-impact training examples at token-level resolution using only forward passes, guided by a handful of target task examples.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263399/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

TRIM takes a large candidate pool of instruction-tuning examples (270k from DOLLY, OASST1, FLAN_V2, and CoT), a very small target validation set (as few as 5–10 examples per task, disjoint from test), and a pretrained LLM that is first warmed up via LoRA on ~5% of the pool. It produces a ranked coreset of top-K samples for fine-tuning, with each candidate receiving a single scalar relevance score derived from per-token cosine similarities to class-level fingerprint vectors. The entire scoring stage requires only one forward pass per candidate at a single checkpoint, making runtime linear in corpus size.

## Method

TRIM is a two-stage, forward-only pipeline. In Stage I, it computes an attention-derived token saliency score for each position in the target validation set by combining row saliency (inverse entropy of the query's attention distribution, measuring focus sharpness) and column saliency (mean attention received by a key token, measuring centrality), aggregated across the last L layers and H heads. For each token class, it then constructs a fingerprint as the saliency-weighted mean of normalized last-layer hidden states across all target-set occurrences of that class. In Stage II, each candidate sample is scored by computing cosine similarity between every token's normalized hidden state and its corresponding class fingerprint (with a penalized nearest-neighbor fallback for unseen classes), then pooling token scores via a robust combination of mean, max, and a light coverage term. This token-centric, representation-matching design avoids all backward passes, inherently mitigates length bias, and captures task-defining structural patterns (e.g., mathematical operators, logical connectives) that sample-level loss or gradient signals miss.

## Results

On a 5% coreset budget with LLAMA-2-7B, TRIM achieves a macro-average accuracy of 48.56% across MMLU (49.33%), BBH (39.73%), and TYDIQA (56.62%), surpassing the strongest gradient-based baseline LESS (48.27%) and TAGCOS (46.60%). On the challenging GSM8K downstream task (selected from a non-math corpus), TRIM reaches 29.52% exact-match at 5% budget, nearly matching full-data fine-tuning (30.25%) and outperforming LESS by ~8.8%. TRIM completes the full selection pipeline in 4.40 hours on a single H200 GPU, compared to 35.76 hours for LESS and 87.95 hours for S2L.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRIM outperforms all seven baselines on the 5% coreset macro-average. Against the closest competitor LESS (gradient-based, ICML 2024), TRIM gains 0.29% on average (48.56 vs 48.27) and a more decisive ~8.8% on GSM8K (29.52 vs 20.72 at 5%), while running ~8× faster (4.40 h vs 35.76 h). TAGCOS (NAACL 2025) trails TRIM by 1.96% on average and 10.8% on GSM8K. Forward-only training-dynamics methods S2L (NeurIPS 2024) and CLD (arXiv 2025) lag by 3.0% and 5.95% respectively on the macro-average, with S2L also incurring the highest runtime (87.95 h) due to full-data trajectory collection. Representation-based RDS (ICLR 2021) and heuristic methods BM25 and DSIR (NeurIPS 2023) are the weakest, trailing TRIM by 6.4%, 3.2%, and 6.5% on average. Notably, TRIM and LESS are the only methods whose 5% coresets surpass full-data fine-tuning on TYDIQA (56.62 and 56.13 vs 54.0), and TRIM's token-centric scoring mitigates the length bias that causes LESS and TAGCOS to skew toward shorter sequences (mean 323 and 259 tokens vs TRIM's 446).

## Relation to Prior Work

TRIM carves new ground within the well-established coreset-selection-for-instruction-tuning task by shifting the scoring granularity from sample-level to token-level and replacing gradient/Hessian signals with forward-only attention-derived saliency. Conceptually, it repurposes two existing ideas—attention entropy as a proxy for token importance (echoing attention-flow analyses like Abnar & Zuidema, 2020) and cosine-similarity matching to prototype representations (as in RDS, Hanawa et al., 2021)—but combines them into a per-token-class 'fingerprint' dictionary that no prior coreset method constructs. It departs from gradient-based selectors (LESS, TAGCOS) by eliminating backward passes entirely, and from training-dynamics methods (S2L, CLD) by requiring only a handful of target examples rather than full-pool loss trajectories.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: TRIM's forward-only design cuts selection runtime from ~36 hours (LESS) to ~4.4 hours on 270k examples, making targeted coreset selection feasible on a single GPU without multi-checkpoint gradient storage. The token-level scoring also addresses a known failure mode (length bias) that sample-level methods exhibit, and the demonstrated cross-architecture transferability (7B scorer → 13B or Mistral) suggests the fingerprints capture model-agnostic structural signals. Open questions include whether the attention-entropy proxy for saliency remains valid under different training regimes or longer contexts, whether the heuristic fingerprint construction can be made more principled, and whether the modest gains on standard benchmarks (MMLU, BBH) will hold as base models and candidate pools scale further.
