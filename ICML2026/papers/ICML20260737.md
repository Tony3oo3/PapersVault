## Problem

Existing validation practices for LLM-as-a-Judge operate at the level of observed scores, offering limited insight into whether LLM judges function as stable measurement instruments. Prior work addresses intrinsic consistency and human alignment separately with aggregate metrics that cannot separate the judge's measurement properties from variation driven by evaluated samples, leaving researchers unable to diagnose the source of observed inconsistencies.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260737/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes Likert-scale ratings produced by an LLM judge across four prompt variants (original, typo, newline, paraphrase) for each evaluation subject, along with human annotations for alignment comparison. It outputs four diagnostic metrics in a sequential two-phase structure: Phase 1 (intrinsic consistency) yields prompt consistency C_V and marginal reliability ρ, while Phase 2 (human alignment, applicable only after Phase 1 criteria are met) yields discrimination breadth ratio θ_ratio and Wasserstein distance D_W. All metrics are computed from latent quality estimates θ inferred via GRM fitting rather than from raw scores, enabling cross-judge comparison even when models use the rating scale differently.

## Method

The paper adapts the Graded Response Model (GRM) from Item Response Theory to LLM judge evaluation, treating each prompt variant as an IRT 'item' with its own discrimination parameter α_p and threshold sequence β_pk, while all variants share a common latent quality θ_j per evaluation subject. Posterior inference is performed via NUTS (4 chains, 1000 warmup + 1000 sampling iterations) with informative priors (θ_j ~ N(0,1), α_p ~ LogNormal(0,0.5), β_pk ~ N(0,1) with ordering constraints), implemented in PyMC with the NumPyro backend. The key design choice is basing all comparisons on θ_j rather than fitted α or β parameters, because different judges use the rating scale differently (e.g., one produces all five scores while another only three), making raw-score-based comparison invalid. The two-phase diagnostic structure enables differential diagnosis: high C_V with acceptable ρ points to prompt sensitivity, low ρ indicates fundamental model limitations, and Phase 2 distinguishes calibration mismatch (θ_ratio ≠ 1 with low D_W) from validity gaps (non-monotonic θ trajectories in vision tasks).

## Results

Across 20 evaluation criteria spanning four benchmarks (SummEval, TopicalChat, HelpSteer-2, VIEScore) and seven LLM judges, no single model achieves acceptable intrinsic consistency (C_V ≤ 0.10 and ρ ≥ 0.70) on all criteria simultaneously. VIEScore exhibits the highest prompt sensitivity (C_V ranging 0.16–1.32) yet the highest marginal reliability (ρ = 0.80–0.96), while NLP benchmarks show lower C_V (mostly < 0.30) but more variable ρ. In Phase 2, LLM judges consistently perceive a wider quality spectrum than humans (θ_ratio > 1 across nearly all model-task pairs), with VIEScore PQ reaching extreme ratios of 2.03–4.40, and HelpSteer-2 Coherence achieving the closest distributional alignment (D_W = 0.15–0.29).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new judge method to compare against baselines; instead, it applies a unified IRT-GRM diagnostic framework to seven LLM judges (Gemini 2.5 Flash, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Qwen3-30B, Qwen3-235B, LLaMA-4-Maverick, LLaMA-4-Scout) across four benchmarks. In NLP tasks, larger models consistently outperform smaller ones on both C_V and ρ (e.g., GPT-4o achieves C_V = 0.06 vs. GPT-4o-mini's 0.60 on SummEval Fluency; Qwen3-235B achieves ρ = 0.89–0.92 vs. Qwen3-30B's 0.86–0.90 on SummEval). However, this scaling advantage disappears entirely on VIEScore, where Qwen3-30B and Qwen3-235B show comparable C_V and ρ, and GPT-4o-mini occasionally matches or exceeds GPT-4o. In Phase 2, model scale has no consistent effect on θ_ratio or D_W; instead, task characteristics dominate, with all models showing similar distributional alignment within each benchmark. The key diagnostic finding is that no model passes both Phase 1 criteria (C_V ≤ 0.10 and ρ ≥ 0.70) across all 20 criteria, indicating that reliability is criterion-specific rather than a property of the model alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Graded Response Model (GRM) from psychometric item response theory to model LLM judge ratings, treating prompt variants as 'items' and sample quality as the latent trait. Prior LLM-as-a-Judge reliability work has relied on outcome-level metrics—inter-rater agreement, internal consistency (McDonald's ω), uncertainty quantification, and correlation/agreement with human scores—that conflate judge measurement properties with sample quality variation. The key departure is using GRM's parameterization (discrimination α, thresholds β, latent quality θ) to separate these two sources of variation, enabling a two-phase diagnostic (intrinsic consistency → human alignment) that prior single-metric approaches cannot provide.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives practitioners interpretable, actionable signals for diagnosing *why* an LLM judge is unreliable (prompt sensitivity vs. inadequate scale usage vs. calibration mismatch vs. construct validity gap), rather than just flagging that it is unreliable. The finding that no model passes all criteria across all benchmarks, and that model scale improves NLP reliability but not vision-language reliability, has direct practical implications for judge deployment. Key limitations constrain generalizability: the framework applies only to point-scale (Likert) judgments, vision is examined through a single benchmark (VIEScore), and the GRM does not capture known biases (self-preference, position, verbosity) that would require Differential Item Functioning extensions. Open questions include extension to pairwise and open-ended evaluation, multilingual settings, and whether the NLP-vs-vision reliability gap persists across additional multimodal benchmarks.
