## Problem

ML research optimizes narrow proxies (accuracy, engagement, preference satisfaction) while treating the question 'what is this for?' as external to the research process, producing systems that are technically sophisticated but normatively inarticulate. The paper argues that purpose is not merely a policy concern but a core ML research problem, because proxy optimization systematically reshapes the very capacities—agency, meaning, pluralism—that human purpose requires, and because current alignment targets (instruction-following, harmlessness, preference matching) are underspecified without an explicit purpose dimension.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, this work takes current ML research norms—objective formulation, evaluation metrics, documentation practices (model cards, datasheets), and governance structures—as its analytical input. It produces a normative framework of three community practices (Purpose Statement, purpose evaluation protocols, purpose governance mechanisms) plus five concrete research directions, explicitly scoped to work claiming real-world relevance (deployment papers, user studies, public-facing systems) rather than foundational capability research such as optimization or architecture.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a three-practice framework that operationalizes 'human purpose' without requiring a single moral doctrine. Purpose Articulation requires a structured 'Purpose Statement' with five testable components: intended beneficiaries, purpose domain, mechanism, falsifiable failure modes, and pre-registered evidence that would change the authors' assessment—functioning as a research commitment analogous to clinical trial protocols rather than a product mission statement. Purpose Evaluation extends standard benchmarks with measures of agency (self-determination theory constructs), meaning (validated instruments like the Meaning in Life Questionnaire), and pluralism (ValueCompass, PRISM), with explicit anti-Goodhart second-order monitoring to prevent metric gaming. Purpose Governance establishes open registries, periodic community review, and participatory input from affected populations to make purpose frameworks contestable and revisable over time. The framework distinguishes purpose from preference (citing adaptive preferences and Nozick's experience machine), from well-being (purpose involves doing what one has reason to value, not just feeling good), and from safety (a system can pass all safety evaluations while eroding agency and meaning through deskilling or dependency). The design is deliberately incremental—extending existing norms like model cards and datasheets rather than replacing them—and includes a worked example tracing an AI writing assistant from statement through evaluation to governance-triggered design revision.

## Results

This is a position paper that proposes normative community practices (Purpose Statement, purpose evaluation, purpose governance) rather than presenting empirical experiments. There are no quantitative results, benchmark scores, or ablation studies. The paper's contribution is a conceptual framework and a set of stakeholder-differentiated recommendations for making human purpose an explicit, first-class concern in ML research.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare methods empirically against baselines. Its closest analogue to a comparison is Table 1, a conceptual mapping of current ML research norms (task accuracy, model cards, lab-internal governance) against proposed purpose-aware norms (agency/meaning metrics, Purpose Statements, participatory governance). The paper positions itself relative to prior position work on pluralistic alignment (Sorensen et al., 2024), democratic alignment (Ovadya et al., 2025), and full-stack alignment (Edelman et al., 2025), arguing that 'human purpose' is a missing organizing concept that unifies and operationalizes those directions for mainstream ML research. No method outperforms or underperforms another on any metric, as no experiments are conducted.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes and unifies several recent alignment directions—pluralistic alignment (Sorensen et al., 2024), democratic alignment (Ovadya et al., 2025), and full-stack alignment (Edelman et al., 2025)—under a single organizing concept: explicit human purpose. It repurposes documentation norms from model cards and datasheets into a purpose-specific artifact (the Purpose Statement) and draws on Value Sensitive Design, the capabilities approach, and self-determination theory to ground its evaluation criteria. The paper's conceptual departure is framing purpose as distinct from preference satisfaction, harm avoidance, and instruction-following, arguing that current alignment practice is 'normatively inarticulate' because it silently defaults to convenient proxies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a genuine structural gap—safety is a constraint while purpose is an objective—and proposes concrete, incremental community practices (Purpose Statement, purpose evaluation, purpose governance) that could shift research norms without imposing a single moral doctrine. Key open questions include measurement validity for purpose constructs (Direction 5), the feasibility of longitudinal evaluation of agency and meaning, and the risk that formalizing purpose invites ideological capture. As a position paper with no empirical results, its impact hinges on community adoption; the practical recommendations (a two-to-three-sentence Purpose Statement, agency/meaning benchmarks) are deliberately low-barrier to test whether the norm gains traction.
