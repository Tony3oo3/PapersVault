## Problem

Process Reward Models (PRMs) that supervise LLM reasoning steps are limited by expensive manual step-level annotation and by static training data that cannot adapt to increasingly nuanced errors. Synthetic data generation methods rely on the flawed assumption that correct final answers imply correct intermediate steps, and none provide a mechanism to actively mine progressively harder negatives aligned with the PRM's evolving blind spots.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262008/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The PRM R_φ takes a question Q, a partial solution S, and a candidate reasoning step, and outputs a binary correctness label (correct or incorrect). At test time, the PRM guides a solver model by scoring each generated step, with up to 5 samples per step. The Generator G_θ takes a gold reasoning step and produces a perturbed token sequence (length 1 to ℓ) intended to be plausible but incorrect; a non-LLM correctness oracle (symbolic equivalence, numeric consistency, algebraic legality, theorem preconditions, and unit checks) assigns the ground-truth label y ∈ {0,1} to each generated step without requiring human annotation.

## Method

APRM formulates PRM training as a two-player, general-sum, non-cooperative game: a Generator G_θ learns to produce plausible but incorrect reasoning steps that deceive the PRM, while the PRM R_φ concurrently learns to detect these increasingly subtle errors. Both players are trained with PPO augmented by Optimistic Gradient Descent-Ascent (OGDA) updates, and their utility functions are regularized with KL divergence (anchored to the initial pretrained policy) and an entropy bonus, which induce strong concavity and guarantee linear convergence to a unique Nash Equilibrium. The game is explicitly general-sum rather than zero-sum, yielding stationary-point equilibria rather than minimax saddle points. A non-LLM algorithmic proximity oracle (combining cosine similarity, entity matching, symbolic simplification, and structural validators) provides ground-truth correctness labels for the generator's outputs, eliminating the need for manual step-level annotation. Training alternates between players (5 gradient steps each), and the PRM is trained on a 50/50 mixture of gold steps and negatives drawn from current and past generator versions to prevent forgetting.

## Results

Averaged across five math reasoning benchmarks (MATH500, JEEBench, OlympiadBench, AIME25, AMC) and four solver models, APRM improves solver accuracy by +3.4 pp over the strongest trained PRM baseline (ReST-MCTS) and by +4.2 pp over the best prompting method (ToT). The largest gains appear on out-of-distribution tasks: +5.3 pp on JEEBench over ReST-MCTS. On SciBench (cross-domain), APRM achieves 63.0–64.0% overall accuracy versus 61.8–62.3% for ReST-MCTS, confirming transferable error-detection capabilities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

APRM outperforms all nine baselines across every solver model. Against the strongest trained PRM, ReST-MCTS (Zhang et al., 2024), APRM gains +3.4 pp on average, with the largest margin on Gemma-3-27B (+12.6 pp) where ReST-MCTS's static MCTS-generated negatives fail to match the solver's error distribution. Against the best prompting method, ToT (Yao et al., 2023), APRM leads by +4.2 pp on average, and against CoT-SC (Wang et al., 2023) the gap widens to +7.2–26.8 pp depending on solver size. The adversarial baseline ARM (Bukharin et al., 2025), which uses a static generator trained via ensemble disagreement, is substantially outperformed (e.g., 83.0 vs. 76.1 avg on GPT-OSS-120B), confirming that co-evolving the generator is critical. Among static-data PRM baselines—Let's Verify Step by Step (Lightman et al., 2023), Math-Shepherd (Wang et al., 2024), AutoPSV (Lu et al., 2024), and rStar-Math (Guan et al., 2025)—APRM leads by 2–13 pp, with the gap growing as solver capacity increases. LLM-as-a-Judge (Zheng et al., 2023) is competitive on in-domain tasks but degrades on OOD benchmarks, whereas APRM maintains its advantage, suggesting adversarial training captures generalizable error patterns rather than domain-specific heuristics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the adversarial training paradigm from GANs and robust optimization (zero-sum, minimax) into a general-sum, non-cooperative game for training Process Reward Models, departing from both the static-dataset PRM training line (ReST-MCTS, Math-Shepherd, rStar-Math) and the outcome-level adversarial reward model of Bukharin et al. (ARM). The key conceptual departure is twofold: (1) the Generator and PRM have asymmetric, non-opposing utilities (general-sum rather than zero-sum), yielding Nash equilibria as stationary points rather than saddle points; and (2) a non-LLM algorithmic correctness oracle (symbolic equivalence, numeric consistency, theorem preconditions) labels generator outputs, avoiding the circularity of using an LLM to judge LLM-generated errors. The theoretical apparatus—KL/entropy regularization inducing strong concavity, OGDA for linear convergence to the unique Nash equilibrium—borrows from recent game-theoretic RL literature (Azar et al. 2024, Munos et al. 2024) but applies it to the discrete, sequence-level setting of reasoning-step generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The adaptive curriculum produced by adversarial co-evolution directly addresses the core limitation of static PRM training: as solvers improve, their errors become more subtle and fixed datasets fail to cover them. The finding that non-adaptive PRMs (ReST-MCTS) actually degrade in relative effectiveness as solver size grows (from +1.6 pp to −7.6 pp) while APRM's gains expand (+3.3 to +5.0 pp) suggests adversarial training may be increasingly necessary as LLMs scale. Limitations include higher training compute, a correctness oracle whose recall drops to ~79% as errors become subtle (forcing the PRM to generalize beyond the oracle), and a recurring failure mode on theorem-precondition violations that the 8B generator struggles to produce. Open questions include multi-agent extensions targeting diverse error modes, scaling to embodied/robotic reasoning, and whether the general-sum framing offers practical advantages over simpler zero-sum alternatives at larger scale.
