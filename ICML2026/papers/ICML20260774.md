## Problem

Aligning individual AI models to user preferences remains an open challenge, and most AI safety research focuses on aligning single systems or cooperative multi-agent approaches. This paper asks whether the benefits of perfect alignment can emerge from market-like competition among multiple differently misaligned models, without requiring any single model to be well-aligned. The central question is under what conditions on the set of available AI utilities, strategic competition among misaligned providers guarantees the user outcomes comparable to interacting with a perfectly aligned model.

## Input / Output

The framework takes as input a set of k AI utility functions (each potentially misaligned), the user's utility function, a prior distribution over user features x_A, AI features x_B, and world states y, and a bounded message space M for R-round parallel conversations. The output is a lower bound on the user's expected utility in all Nash equilibria of the induced multi-leader Stackelberg game, specifically U_A(C_B*) − 2ε where ε is the alignment error from the convex hull approximation. Unlike typical ML methods that produce a trained model, this produces game-theoretic welfare guarantees under a commitment-based communication protocol where AI providers fix conversation rules (analogous to deploying fixed model weights) and the user adapts her interaction strategy.

## Method

The paper models the interaction as a multi-leader Stackelberg game extending Bayesian persuasion to multi-round conversations between differently informed parties, where AI providers (leaders) simultaneously commit to conversation rules and the user (follower) best-responds with her own conversation and decision rules. The key technical condition is the Identical Induced Distribution Condition — when any single AI deviating to the user-optimal strategy induces the same outcome distribution regardless of which AI deviates — which is satisfied when the message space is expressive enough to communicate the Bayes-optimal action. Under this condition plus the approximate market alignment assumption (the user's utility lies within ε of a non-negative weighted combination of AI utilities), they prove the user achieves at least U_A(C_B*) − 2ε in all Nash equilibria via a weighted-sum argument over the equilibrium no-deviation inequalities. They further develop a quantal response variant for bounded-rationality users (yielding an additional log|A|/λ gap) and a best-AI selection game where the user picks one model after an evaluation period, both yielding similar guarantees under weaker distributional assumptions. Empirically, they validate the convex hull assumption by showing that 100 prompt-perturbed LLM utility functions on MovieLens, ETHICS, and OpinionQA datasets produce a convex hull containing a substantially better approximation of human utility than any individual model, with alignment error dropping 50–75% at k=100.

## Results

On MovieLens (K=100), non-negative weighted combinations of 100 perturbed LLM utility functions reduce alignment error (MSE) by 70–75% relative to the best individual Bob, while simple averaging performs poorly. On OpinionQA, a convex hull of LLM opinions approximates human opinions more accurately than any individual LLM across a wide range of survey questions. In equilibrium simulations (best-response dynamics on synthetic and MovieLens-derived utility tables with 3–5 states, 3–9 actions, 5–6 Bobs), Alice's realized equilibrium utility consistently meets or exceeds the theoretical lower bound of OPT − 2ε, and competition among even 2–3 Bobs yields significant utility gains over single-Bob interaction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead compares four aggregation strategies for reconstructing Alice's utility from the Bobs' utilities: non-negative least squares (NNLS), simplex (convex combination), simple equal-weight average, and the single best-aligned individual Bob. Across all three datasets (OpinionQA, ETHICS, MovieLens), NNLS and simplex substantially outperform both the best individual Bob and the simple average, with error dropping by 50–70% at K=100 on MovieLens. The simple average performs poorly, indicating that equal weighting is insufficient and that the non-negativity/convexity structure matters. In the equilibrium experiments, the key comparison is between Alice's realized utility in Nash equilibrium and the theoretical guarantee (OPT − 2ε); the realized utility always meets or exceeds the bound, and is sometimes substantially better, confirming the tightness of the theoretical analysis. Lower misalignment scores (smaller ε) strongly predict higher equilibrium utility, validating the market alignment assumption as the driving factor.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends multi-sender Bayesian Persuasion (Gentzkow & Kamenica, 2017) from single-round signaling to multi-round conversation between differently informed parties, and reframes the strategic interaction as an AI alignment mechanism rather than an information-design problem. The key conceptual departure from prior alignment work (Constitutional AI, AI debate, cooperative agreement protocols) is the shift from cooperative or zero-sum multi-agent settings to a competitive market where no individual agent is aligned, yet the user's utility lies in the convex hull of their utilities. The analysis draws on the Information Substitutes condition (Frongillo et al., 2021) and convergence results for straightforward conversation (Aaronson, 2005; Collina et al., 2025) to handle the bounded-rationality variant, and on concentration-of-measure arguments to justify the convex hull assumption from noisy training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper introduces a genuinely different axis for thinking about AI alignment: rather than making one model correct, ensure the market of models collectively brackets the user's preferences, letting competition do the work. This opens a mechanism-design research agenda (auditing convex-hull coverage, regulatory incentives for diversity, training procedures that optimize for the condition) but also raises hard open questions: equilibria are computationally intractable to compute, the single-user assumption excludes pluralistic alignment, and the commitment structure may not hold as agents become stateful. The empirical validation, while supportive, relies on synthetic prompt perturbations and small-scale simulations, leaving the real-world prevalence of the convex hull condition across diverse user populations untested.
