## Problem

Standard LLM benchmarks evaluate whether models produce correct answers but do not assess whether they genuinely understand user intent versus pattern-matching on surface cues. Because written language is an imperfect proxy for intent, models that overfit to phrasing correlations may respond inconsistently to semantically equivalent prompts, a failure mode invisible to accuracy-only metrics. This paper asks whether we can formally and empirically measure whether an LLM robustly infers the user's underlying purpose while remaining invariant to superficial articulation differences.

## Input / Output

The method consumes sets of intent-equivalent prompts (generated via cross-lingual back-translation chains through typologically diverse languages) paired with 25 sampled model responses per prompt-intent combination, along with ground-truth intent groupings that partition prompts by underlying purpose. It produces three unit-sum variance components—Intent Sensitivity, Articulation Sensitivity, and Model Uncertainty—plus two summary indices: the Meaningful Variability Share (a signal-to-noise ratio over explainable variance) and the Intent Comprehension Index (a harmonic mean combining intent purity and coverage). For discrete or categorical outputs, the variance decomposition is replaced by an entropy-based decomposition using mutual information and conditional entropy terms, yielding the same three-component structure normalized by total Shannon entropy.

## Method

The paper introduces a formal definition of Intent Comprehension requiring response-distribution invariance under paraphrase (consistency) and differentiation across intents (sensitivity), then relaxes it to a 'sufficient IC' that operates on evaluator-assigned values rather than full response distributions. To operationalize this, response variance is decomposed via ANOVA into three components: IS (variance of conditional means across intents), AS (expected within-intent variance across articulations), and MU (residual within-prompt variance), which sum to one and form a model-agnostic, black-box diagnostic. Semantically equivalent prompts are constructed by translating a base prompt through a sequence of typologically diverse languages and back to English, inducing natural phrasing variation while preserving intent, with GPT-4.1-based equivalence checks filtering for semantic drift. The Intent Comprehension Index is defined as the harmonic mean of MVS = IS/(IS+AS) and IS, so it is high only when intent-driven variation is both pure (low articulation sensitivity) and covers a large share of total variability (high intent sensitivity). For discrete outputs, the framework extends via a Shannon entropy decomposition where IS becomes mutual information I(I;v), AS becomes conditional mutual information I(p_I; v | I), and MU becomes residual conditional entropy H(v | p_I, I).

## Results

Across five LLaMA and Gemma models evaluated on 24 guesstimation tasks (1,772,492 total responses), larger models attribute a substantially greater share of output variance to intent: smaller LLaMA models (3B, 8B) achieve only ~20% Intent Sensitivity (IS), while larger models (LLaMA 70B, Gemma 12B, Gemma 27B) reach ~50% IS. The Intent Comprehension Index (ICI) is higher for larger models, but gains plateau—the difference between LLaMA 70B and Gemma 12B is modest. Smaller models paradoxically show higher Meaningful Variability Share (MVS) because their low IS mechanically suppresses Articulation Sensitivity, but this does not indicate better intent comprehension since their responses are insufficiently separable across intents (high MU dominates).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare its evaluation framework against competing benchmark methods; instead, it applies the proposed IS-AS-MU variance decomposition and ICI to compare five LLMs (LLaMA 3.2 3B, LLaMA 3.1 8B, LLaMA 3.3 70B, Gemma 3 12B-IT, Gemma 3 27B-IT) on a common set of 24 tasks across five domains. Within each family, larger models consistently achieve higher IS (~50% vs. ~20% for the smallest LLaMA models), indicating stronger intent sensitivity. However, the scaling is not uniform: Gemma 12B achieves IS comparable to LLaMA 70B, and ICI gains plateau between the 12B and 70B/27B models. Domain-level comparisons reveal that no single model dominates all topics—LLaMA 70B leads in Health/Nutrition and Transportation, while Gemma 27B leads in Logistics, Personal Finance, and Social Planning. Larger models also exhibit higher Articulation Sensitivity (AS), suggesting a tradeoff: they are more responsive to genuine intent changes but simultaneously more susceptible to surface-level phrasing variations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formalizing 'intent comprehension' as a distinct evaluation property (consistency across paraphrases plus sensitivity to intent changes) and operationalizing it through a variance decomposition (IS/AS/MU) and an information-theoretic entropy analogue. It repurposes ANOVA-style decomposition and Shannon entropy chain rules—tools well-established in statistics and information theory—for a new LLM evaluation purpose, and draws conceptual inspiration from world-model literature in robotics (latent-state invariance) and from pragmatic/speech-act theory (Grice, Searle). It departs from the prompt-sensitivity literature (ProSA, FORMATSPREAD, Brucks & Toubia) by not merely measuring how much outputs shift with phrasing, but by attributing that shift to intent vs. articulation vs. noise in a principled, unit-sum decomposition, and by requiring both invariance and sensitivity as joint criteria.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a model-agnostic, interpretable diagnostic that complements accuracy benchmarks by revealing whether a model's responses track user purpose or merely surface cues—critical for high-stakes deployment and fairness (articulation sensitivity correlates with dialect, education, and communication norms). Key open questions include whether the findings generalize beyond quantitative guesstimation tasks and two model families, how reasoning/chain-of-thought models and tool-use agents behave under this metric, and whether stronger adversarial paraphrase generation would shift the IS/AS balance. The finding that larger models can exhibit *higher* articulation sensitivity while also showing higher intent sensitivity suggests a tradeoff that current scaling narratives do not capture, motivating more targeted evaluation beyond parameter count.
