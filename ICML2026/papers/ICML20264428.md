## Problem

State-of-the-art membership inference attacks (MIAs) based on shadow models assume access to a background distribution identical to the target model's training data, but in real-world auditing scenarios (e.g., CSAM detection, medical records), the auditor cannot legally or ethically access samples from the target class. This paper formalizes this constraint as the 'unseen class' setting and shows that shadow model attacks fail catastrophically when the target class is absent from attack training data, while identifying quantile regression as a surprisingly effective alternative.

## Input / Output

The attack takes a query example (x, y) and the target model's output logits, computing a score (the difference between the top two logits for quantile regression, or the true-label confidence for shadow models). The quantile regression model is a ConvNext-Tiny-224 for images (224×224×3 input) or a 2-layer MLP for text (256-token embeddings) and tabular data, trained to output a per-example score threshold. Membership is predicted as a binary decision: the query is a member if its score exceeds the predicted (1−α)-quantile threshold, with α set to control the false positive rate (typically 1% or 0.1%).

## Method

The core insight is that quantile regression attacks generalize to unseen classes because they learn features that correlate with model scores across classes, rather than requiring the attack model to solve the same classification task as the target (which fails when a class is unseen, causing zero probability assignments). The quantile regression model is trained via pinball loss (implemented as Gaussian mean/variance fitting for stability) on the seen-class background data to predict the (1−α)-quantile of the score distribution conditioned on the input. The paper provides a theoretical transferability theorem showing that if the density ratio between the seen-class training distribution and the full query distribution is linear in the learned feature space, the FPR guarantee of the quantile regressor transfers to unseen classes. Empirically, this yields up to 11× higher TPR than shadow models on CIFAR-100, 6× on 20 Newsgroups, and 2× on Texas tabular data in the 1% FPR regime, with the advantage growing as more classes are removed.

## Results

In the unseen-class setting at 1% FPR, quantile regression achieves 3.8% TPR on CIFAR-100 (superclass set) versus only 1.4% for LiRA shadow models (a 2.7× average improvement, up to 11× on individual classes). On ImageNet, quantile regression retains 3.8% TPR at 1% FPR even when 90% of classes are removed from attack training data. On tabular (Texas) and text (20 Newsgroups) data, quantile regression achieves up to 2× and 6× the TPR of the state-of-the-art RMIA shadow model, respectively, while also improving AUC by up to 10 points on Texas.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LiRA (Carlini et al., 2022), the primary shadow model baseline, quantile regression achieves 2.7× higher average TPR on CIFAR-100 (3.8% vs. 1.4% at 1% FPR) and up to 11× on individual unseen classes; LiRA degrades to near the global-threshold LOSS baseline when a class is excluded from shadow model training. Against RMIA (Zarifzadeh et al., 2023), the current state-of-the-art shadow model attack, quantile regression wins by up to 2× TPR on Texas tabular data and 6× on 20 Newsgroups text; RMIA actually performs worse than LiRA in the unseen-class setting because its score function exaggerates target model confidence on the missing class. Against the LOSS marginal baseline (Yeom et al., 2018), which uses a single global threshold, quantile regression strictly outperforms across all six datasets and both image and non-image modalities, confirming that per-example thresholds learned via quantile regression generalize to unseen classes while a fixed threshold does not. Notably, shadow model attacks (both LiRA and RMIA) fail catastrophically in this setting, performing at or below the trivial global-threshold baseline, whereas quantile regression maintains meaningful attack success even under extreme data scarcity (e.g., 990/1000 ImageNet classes unseen).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the quantile regression MIA framework of Bertran et al. (2023) and Tang et al. (2023), repurposing it for a setting where the target class is entirely absent from attack training data. It departs fundamentally from the shadow model paradigm (Shokri et al. 2017; Carlini et al. 2022; Zarifzadeh et al. 2023), which the authors show fails catastrophically when the unseen class is excluded, and from prior distribution-shift MIA work (Yichuan et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2022) that always retains all target classes. The theoretical analysis adapts the multi-accuracy/multicalibration framework of Hebert-Johnson et al. (2018) and the universal adaptability results of Kim et al. (2022) to establish transferability conditions for quantile predictors across distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a critical, previously unrecognized failure mode in the most widely used MIA methods, with direct implications for real-world AI safety auditing (CSAM detection, medical records, proprietary data) where legal and ethical constraints prevent access to the target class. The theoretical transferability theorem provides a principled understanding of when quantile regression generalizes, but the conditions (linear density ratio in feature space) remain somewhat abstract and hard to verify in practice. Open questions include whether the approach extends to generative models and LLMs under the same constraints, and whether the modest absolute TPRs (3–4% at 1% FPR) are sufficient for practical auditing workflows.
