## Problem

Hallucination-related signals in LLMs are known to be strongest in intermediate layers, but no principled, automatic criterion exists to identify which layer to probe—existing approaches either fix a layer a priori or exhaustively evaluate all layers at prohibitive cost. Additionally, the common practice of extracting representations at the last generated token is degraded by end-of-sequence noise (inconsistent continuations, semantic drift, degenerate repetition), yet no simple supervision-free rule has been proposed to mitigate this.

## Input / Output

The method takes a frozen LLM and feeds it the concatenation of the prompt and its generated answer, extracting token-wise hidden states at every layer. Layer selection (FEPoID) is training-free: it computes TwoNN intrinsic dimension estimates across all layers on a small set of samples and returns a single layer index. For representation extraction, a rule-based scanner truncates the generated answer at the first sentence boundary, and the hidden state at that position in the selected layer is passed to a lightweight MLP classifier that outputs a binary hallucination score (evaluated via AUROC).

## Method

FEPoID estimates the TwoNN intrinsic dimension at each layer, identifies all local maxima of the resulting ID curve, and filters out spurious early peaks that are followed by monotonically increasing ID values within a forward horizon window w; the earliest surviving peak is selected as the probe layer. The key insight is that the first ID peak corresponds to abstract semantic information relevant to hallucination detection, while a later, often higher peak near output layers reflects surface-level lexical complexity. Complementing layer selection, First-Sentence Truncation (FST) uses a lightweight regex-based scanner to locate the first sentence boundary in the generated answer and extracts the hidden state at that token rather than the final token, systematically removing end-of-sequence noise. The overall pipeline is: frozen LLM → FEPoID selects layer → FST selects token position → MLP probe trained with supervised correctness labels → hallucination score. FEPoID is training-free and incurs negligible overhead (~10 s across 32 layers), while FST is method-agnostic and improves all baselines considered.

## Results

On the five QA hallucination detection benchmarks, FEPoID achieves an average AUROC of 0.7253 on LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct and 0.8531 on Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, outperforming all other layer-selection criteria and uncertainty-based baselines. On summarization tasks (HaluEval, CNN/DM), FEPoID reaches 0.6080 and 0.7711 average AUROC on the two models respectively, again leading all alternatives. FEPoID is training-free and requires only ~10 seconds of computation across all 32 layers, compared to 27–59 seconds for competing criteria. Combined with First-Sentence Truncation (FST), FEPoID further boosts average AUROC to 0.8752 (LLaMA) and 0.8687 (Mistral) on QA tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FEPoID outperforms all nine compared baselines on average AUROC across both models. Against uncertainty-based methods, FEPoID (0.7253/0.8531) substantially exceeds Predictive Entropy (0.5820/0.6738, Malinin & Gales 2021), Semantic Entropy (0.5311/0.6560, Farquhar et al. 2024), and Lexical Similarity (0.6196/0.6903, Lin et al. 2024). Among representation-based baselines, FEPoID beats LID (0.5299/0.5579, Yin et al. 2024) by a wide margin and EigenScore (0.5499/0.7141, Chen et al. 2024) by 0.176/0.139 AUROC. Among layer-selection criteria applied to the same hidden-state probing framework, FEPoID edges out Curvature (0.6970/0.8472, Hosseini & Fedorenko 2023) and Val Loss (0.7250/0.8457), and clearly surpasses RGN (0.6879/0.7713) and SNR (0.6028/0.7436, Lee et al. 2023), RankMe (0.6860/0.7394, Roy & Vetterli 2007), and Max-ID (0.6843/0.8071, Rao et al. 2025). Notably, SNR-based selection on LLaMA-Instruct yields an average AUROC (0.6028) lower than the simple Lexical Similarity baseline (0.6196), illustrating that suboptimal layer selection can negate the benefits of representation-based probing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the hidden-state probing framework for hallucination detection (Orgad et al., 2025; Azaria & Mitchell, 2023; Chen et al., 2024) by addressing the underexplored sub-problem of *which* intermediate layer to probe. It builds directly on the TwoNN intrinsic dimension estimator (Facco et al., 2017) as its core computational tool, and repurposes criteria from selective fine-tuning (RGN, SNR from Lee et al., 2023) and geometric trajectory analysis (curvature from Hosseini & Fedorenko, 2023) for layer selection—only to show they fail in this setting. The key departure from prior work is the observation that the ID curve exhibits a consistent bimodal pattern, motivating the FEPoID criterion that selects the first effective peak rather than the global maximum, and the introduction of First-Sentence Truncation as a supervision-free remedy for end-of-sequence noise that prior probing work (Springer et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025) identified but did not address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FEPoID provides a practical, training-free, and computationally cheap (~10s vs. ~30–60s for alternatives) layer-selection rule that consistently identifies near-optimal layers across model scales (1B–8B), architectures, and task types (QA and summarization), removing the need for exhaustive per-layer evaluation. The FST strategy is method-agnostic and improves all baselines, suggesting that end-of-sequence noise is a more pervasive bottleneck in hidden-state probing than previously quantified. Open questions include whether the bimodal ID pattern and the semantic-vs-surface-level interpretation of the two peaks hold for non-decoder architectures or multimodal models, and whether a theoretical account can explain why the first ID peak specifically encodes hallucination-relevant information. The evaluation is limited to 1B–8B models and two task families, so generalization to larger or differently-structured models remains untested.
