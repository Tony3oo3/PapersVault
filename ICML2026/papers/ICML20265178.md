## Problem

Existing kernel-based skew estimation in federated learning suffers from two fundamental limitations: fixed neighborhood structures cause bias toward skewed majority clients (assuming skew is rare), and kernel-space similarity measures are inherently vulnerable to noise from stochastic optimization and partial participation. No prior method simultaneously adapts the neighborhood size to the data skew severity and provides noise-robust margin estimation without accessing raw client data or local statistics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265178/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

FedVeer consumes only client model update vectors (local parameters after one round of local SGD) and data source identifiers, computing pairwise l2-norm distances in parameter space to form k-nearest-neighbor sets. It outputs per-client aggregation weights θ_n that jointly incorporate client-level and source-level Gaussian kernel density estimates, the updated global model ω^{t+1}, and the self-adaptively selected optimal neighborhood size k*. No raw features, labels, or local statistics are ever shared, preserving the decentralized FL privacy guarantee.

## Method

FedVeer replaces the fixed neighborhood size k with a self-adaptive selection via max-margin learning: it traverses k from 1 to N, computes source-level kernel densities q^s(k) using a Gaussian kernel, and selects k* that maximizes the margin m_k = max Q_k − secmax Q_k between the top two source densities, terminating early when the best-aligned source changes. To handle noisy local updates, a Kalman filter with optimal gain κ_k = p_k⁻/(p_k⁻+β) recursively stabilizes the margin estimate, incorporating a forgetting factor λ to handle state jumps when the dominant data source switches between rounds. The aggregation weight θ_n multiplies the client-level density q_n by the source-level density q^s of its associated source, down-weighting both boundary clients and clients from severely skewed sources. Theoretical analysis proves uniqueness of the max-margin solution and derives a high-probability deviation bound via McDiarmid's inequality, showing the Kalman filter strictly tightens the bound (ζ < ξ) by reducing the effective noise variance.

## Results

On the 34 CEKA industry-oriented datasets, FedVeer achieves an average accuracy of 0.866, outperforming the strongest baseline TPFL (0.840) by 2.6 percentage points and the weakest baseline FedRDN (0.791) by 7.5 percentage points. On the four image benchmarks, FedVeer reaches 0.922 on MNIST, 0.741 on Fashion-MNIST, 0.636 on FEMNIST, and 0.591 on CIFAR-10, with the paper reporting up to 6.36% accuracy improvement over baselines under severe data skew. The Kalman-filter-based noise-robust variant reduces noise-induced margin degradation by up to 6.01% compared to the unfiltered noisy setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedVeer wins on all 34 CEKA datasets against all five baselines, achieving an average accuracy of 0.866 versus TPFL's 0.840 (the strongest baseline), LoMar's 0.826, FedLC's 0.825, FedLSA's 0.820, and FedRDN's 0.791. The largest margins appear on datasets with pronounced feature/label skew: iris (0.770 vs. 0.613 for FedRDN, a 15.7-point gap), labor (0.917 vs. 0.877 for TPFL), and horse-colic (0.844 vs. 0.815 for TPFL). On simpler datasets where skew is mild (e.g., balance-scale, sick, mushroom), the gap narrows to 0.1–0.5 points, consistent with the paper's observation that FedVeer's advantage is most pronounced when data structures are complex. Among baselines, TPFL (kernel-density + directional consistency) is the closest competitor, while FedRDN (feature-skew-only augmentation) and FedLSA (domain-skew contrastive learning) trail significantly, confirming that methods targeting a single skew type generalize poorly when multiple skews coexist. On the four image benchmarks (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, FEMNIST, CIFAR-10), FedVeer also achieves the highest accuracy and fastest convergence compared to all baselines, with the paper reporting up to 6.36% improvement under severe skew.

## Relation to Prior Work

FedVeer extends the kernel-density-estimation line of work in federated learning initiated by LoMar (Li et al., 2021), which used fixed-size neighborhoods to detect anomalous clients, by replacing the fixed k with a self-adaptive neighborhood size determined via max-margin learning over source-level density scores. It also adapts the dynamic-k concept from KFNN (Zhang et al., 2024) from a centralized crowdsourcing setting to the decentralized FL paradigm where raw features and labels are inaccessible. The key departure from prior kernel-based FL methods (LoMar, TPFL) is the joint treatment of majority-client bias and noise robustness: rather than assuming skewed clients are a small minority, FedVeer learns the neighborhood geometry from the update distribution itself and stabilizes the resulting margin estimate with a Kalman filter, a control-theoretic tool not previously applied to FL aggregation weighting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in skew-aware FL: when the majority of clients are skewed (rather than a small poisoned minority), fixed-k kernel methods systematically over-weight the dominant skewed group, and no prior decentralized method combined adaptive neighborhood selection with a principled noise-robustness guarantee. The McDiarmid-based deviation bounds (Theorems 5.1, 5.3) provide a rare probabilistic guarantee on aggregation-weight stability under stochastic updates, which is valuable for safety-critical deployments. Open questions include whether Assumption A.1 (best-aligned source dominates at small k) holds under adversarial or rapidly shifting skew patterns, how the method scales to thousands of clients, and whether the Kalman filter's linear-Gaussian noise model remains adequate for deep-network gradient noise that may be heavy-tailed or correlated across layers.
