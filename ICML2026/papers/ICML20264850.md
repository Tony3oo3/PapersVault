## Problem

Training tool-use agents with reinforcement learning requires large-scale interaction with diverse, reliable environments, but real-world APIs are expensive and hard to scale, LLM-simulated environments suffer from hallucination and high per-step inference cost, and existing synthetic approaches focus on task or trajectory generation rather than executable environment synthesis. The missing piece is a scalable pipeline that produces code-driven, database-backed environments with consistent state transitions suitable for online RL.

## Input / Output

The synthesis pipeline takes 100 seed scenario names and progressively generates 1,000 scenarios, 10,000 tasks, SQLite schemas with sample data, MCP tool interfaces (~35 tools per environment, ~2,000 lines of Python), and per-task verification code. At inference, the agent interacts with each environment through exactly two meta-tools (list_tools and call_tool), receiving structured JSON observations and producing tool calls in a Qwen3 XML format. The trained agent outputs multi-turn tool-call trajectories, and the code-augmented LLM-as-a-Judge maps final database state diffs to a four-class reward signal (Completed / Partially Completed / Agent Error / Environment Error).

## Method

AWM mirrors software engineering to synthesize POMDP environments: an LLM generates a scenario description, infers user tasks as functional requirements, derives a minimal SQLite schema and sample data to make all tasks executable, designs a toolset schema, and generates a FastAPI/SQLAlchemy MCP server implementing the transition function. Each stage employs execution-based self-correction (up to 5 retries with error feedback) achieving >85% first-attempt success. For RL training, the paper uses GRPO with a hybrid reward combining step-level format validation (early termination with r=-1 on violations) and task-level outcome verification via code-augmented LLM-as-a-Judge. A key training innovation is history-aware optimization: the rollout is split into per-turn sub-trajectories each conditioned on a sliding-window truncated history (w=3), aligning the training distribution with inference-time context management and eliminating the distribution shift that arises when training on full histories but deploying with truncated ones. Training launches 1,024 isolated environment instances per step with asynchronous verification to minimize latency.

## Results

On BFCLv3, AWM-trained agents achieve 64.50 (4B), 65.94 (8B), and 70.18 (14B) overall score, substantially outperforming the base models (54.92, 53.83, 61.25) and both baselines. On τ²-bench, AWM reaches 22.57/33.45/39.03 Pass@1 (4B/8B/14B), consistently exceeding the Simulator baseline. On MCP-Universe, AWM achieves the best overall task success rates of 6.70 (4B), 11.17 (8B), and 12.29 (14B), with particularly large gains in the Financial category (35.00% for 8B vs. 22.50% base). Training exclusively on 526 synthetic AWM environments yields strong out-of-distribution generalization to all three benchmarks, none of which were used during training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AWM decisively outperforms the Simulator baseline (LLM-simulated environments using GPT-5 as transition model) across all three benchmarks and all model scales: on BFCLv3, AWM gains +8.97 (4B), +13.41 (8B), and +2.50 (14B) over Simulator; on MCP-Universe, gains are +0.55, +5.02, and +1.67 respectively. Against EnvScaler (191 programming-based environments), AWM wins on BFCLv3 by a wide margin (+10.44 for 4B, +29.11 for 8B) and on MCP-Universe (+2.23 for 4B, +5.58 for 8B), but loses on τ²-bench Pass@1 (22.57 vs. 28.96 for 4B; 33.45 vs. 39.39 for 8B), likely because EnvScaler's synthesis relies on existing task sets that may overlap with τ²-bench. Notably, EnvScaler regresses below the base model on BFCLv3 (−8.93 for 8B) and MCP-Universe (−1.39 for 8B), whereas AWM improves over base on every benchmark. The 14B comparison (where EnvScaler is unavailable) shows AWM beating both Base and Simulator on all three benchmarks, confirming that code-driven, database-backed environments provide a more stable and transferable training signal than LLM-simulated ones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two lines of work: (1) environment synthesis for agent training, where prior efforts either simulate transitions with LLMs (Li et al. 2025b, Chen et al. 2025) or build small-scale programming-based environments (EnvScaler, AutoEnv, AutoForge), and (2) agentic RL using GRPO (Shao et al. 2024), which the authors adapt with a history-aware training objective that aligns optimization with inference-time context truncation. The key conceptual departure is treating LLMs as *code generators* rather than *state-transition simulators*: the LLM writes SQLite-backed Python environments whose state transitions are deterministic and verifiable, drawing an analogy to learned world models in model-based RL but realized through executable code. For scenario generation, the pipeline borrows the Self-Instruct expansion paradigm (Wang et al. 2023) to scale from 100 seed domains to 1,000 scenarios.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The release of 1,000 executable, database-backed environments with 35,062 tools and 10,000 verified tasks fills a critical gap: prior work had at most 191 synthetic environments (EnvScaler) or a handful of hand-built ones (τ²-bench, MCP-Universe), making large-scale agentic RL infeasible. The finding that agents trained exclusively on these synthetic environments generalize to three out-of-distribution real-world benchmarks validates the 'world model via code' paradigm and suggests that environment diversity, not benchmark-specific tuning, is the key driver of generalization. Open questions include whether the pipeline can self-evolve (trained agents contributing to new environment synthesis), how it handles adversarial perturbations and access-control scenarios, and whether the 74% bug rate in generated environments becomes a bottleneck at even larger scales.
