## Problem

While neural scaling laws for generalization error are well-characterized in the static (infinite training time) limit for power-law kernel spectra, the *dynamics* of how the test error evolves during training remain poorly understood. No prior framework unifies the time-dependent behavior across gradient flow, stochastic Langevin training, and Bayesian inference for the practically relevant case of power-law distributed kernel eigenvalues, leaving the mechanism behind phenomena like early stopping and spectral bias unexplained at a dynamical level.

## Input / Output

The theory takes as input a power-law kernel eigenvalue spectrum η_i = η_1 i^{-(1+γ)}, system size N, sample size P, regularization strength gβ, and Langevin noise temperature β⁻¹, and produces the full time-dependent generalization error L_test(t) along with its bias-variance decomposition, the optimal early stopping time t*, and closed-form scaling law exponents. Unusually, the test error emerges directly as one of the two self-averaging order parameters (the auto-correlation C(t,t)/2), so it is computed as part of the self-consistent solution rather than as a post-hoc quantity. The framework is exact in the N→∞ limit and reduces to known static results (Canatar et al., 2021; Bordelon et al., 2024) in appropriate limits.

## Method

The paper derives a dynamical mean-field theory by applying the Martin-Siggia-Rose-De Dominicis-Janssen (MSRDJ) formalism to the Langevin equation of motion for the teacher-student weight discrepancy, then performing a disorder average over the Wishart-distributed feature matrix Q. A variational Gaussian approximation (VGA) is applied to the resulting disorder-averaged action, which becomes exact in the large-N limit by the central limit theorem. The key structural result is that the N eigenmodes become statistically independent, each obeying a decoupled effective stochastic equation of motion with a mode-dependent relaxation rate proportional to its eigenvalue η_i, but all modes share a common temporally correlated effective noise and a collective memory kernel K(t,s) that satisfies a linear convolution equation. This memory kernel, expressible as a geometric series in the frequency domain, acts as an Onsager reaction term that slows down learning of all modes through the dynamics of the slowest ones. The test error is identified as the auto-correlation order parameter, enabling a direct bias-variance decomposition and yielding the optimal stopping time t* = (β/2)·α/(1+α), which depends only on the noise temperature and spectral exponent, not on P or N.

## Results

The paper derives closed-form scaling laws for the generalization error in kernel regression with power-law eigenvalue spectra ηᵢ = η₁i^(-1-α). The bias error decays as L_bias ~ c₁(Pt)^(-α/(1+α)) − c₂N^(-α) (Eq. 34), while the variance error grows as L_var ~ c₃β⁻¹P^(-α/(1+α))t^(1/(1+α)) (Eq. 35). The optimal early-stopping time is t* = (β/2)·α/(1+α), independent of both P and N (Eq. 36), yielding a minimal test error L_test(t*) ~ d₁(Pβ)^(-α/(1+α)) − d₂N^(-α) (Eq. 37). All analytical predictions are validated against numerical simulations (Figs. 2–5) with disorder averages over 10⁵ realizations, showing excellent agreement between theory and simulation for the full time dynamics of the test error, its bias-variance decomposition, and the optimal stopping time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that unifies and extends several prior analytical frameworks rather than benchmarking against empirical baselines. The derived dynamical mean-field theory subsumes the static results of Canatar et al. (2021) in the t→∞ limit (Section I), recovers the gradient-flow dynamics of Advani et al. (2020) in the β→∞ limit, and extends the dynamical scaling-law model of Bordelon et al. (2024) by incorporating L2 regularization and stochastic Langevin noise. Compared to Bordelon et al. (2024), the present work requires fewer and more interpretable order parameters (two self-averaging quantities: the response trace R and the correlation trace C, the latter being proportional to the test error) and additionally captures the disorder-induced effective self-coupling that slows learning dynamics — an effect absent in the prior work. The framework also connects to Bayesian inference on Gaussian processes at equilibrium (β finite, t→∞), a link not exploited in the static approaches of Canatar et al. or Cui et al. (2022). The key new quantitative insight is that the optimal stopping time depends only on β and the spectral exponent α, not on system size or data size, providing a simple, architecture-independent rule for early stopping.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the dynamical mean-field theory framework of Helias & Dahmen (2020) to the specific setting of kernel regression with power-law distributed eigenvalues, unifying previously separate treatments of static Bayesian inference (Canatar et al., 2021), deterministic gradient flow (Advani et al., 2020), and stochastic Langevin dynamics. It departs from Bordelon et al. (2024) by incorporating L2 regularization and Langevin noise, and from Krogh & Hertz (1992) by assuming power-law spectra rather than Gaussian i.i.d. data. The key methodological contribution is showing that the MSRDJ formalism combined with a variational Gaussian approximation yields a compact set of two self-averaging order parameters (response and correlation) that fully describe the dynamics, with the test error emerging naturally as the diagonal of the correlation order parameter.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, unified explanation for neural scaling laws, the bias-variance tradeoff in finite-time training, and the optimal early stopping time (t* = (β/2)(α/(1+α)), independent of P and N), linking these phenomena to the spectral structure of the kernel. The identification of a disorder-induced effective noise (an Onsager-like reaction term) that couples all eigenmodes and slows learning offers a mechanistic understanding of why spectral bias emerges dynamically. Limitations include restriction to linearized/kernel settings and assumed power-law spectra; open questions involve extending the framework to feature learning in non-linear networks, committee machines, and settings where the kernel spectrum is not strictly power-law.
