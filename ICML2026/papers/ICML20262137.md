## Problem

Evolutionary adversarial attacks rely on discrete element-wise crossover that randomly mixes pixel values from two parents, frequently destroying the spatial coherence of successful adversarial patterns and yielding poor transferability. Moreover, the geometric structure of adversarial perturbation space—specifically whether successful attacks are connected by continuous paths that preserve effectiveness—had not been systematically studied, and evolutionary attacks in the white-box setting remained underexplored despite the availability of gradient signals.

## Input / Output

The method takes a clean RGB image (32×32 for CIFAR-10, 224×224 for ImageNet), its ground-truth class label, and full white-box access to the target model's parameters and gradients. It outputs a dense perturbation vector δ of the same spatial dimensions as the input, constrained to an ℓp-ball of radius ε (ℓ∞, ℓ2, or ℓ1), such that fθ(x+δ) ≠ y. The perturbation is produced by an evolutionary population of N candidates evolved over a small number of generations, with each candidate being a full-image perturbation rather than a sparse or structured modification.

## Method

MoCo-EA replaces the standard element-wise crossover in evolutionary attacks with a geometry-aware Bezier crossover: given two parent perturbations δ1 and δ2, a quadratic Bezier curve B(t; δc) is constructed with a learnable control point δc, which is optimized for a few gradient steps to maximize adversarial loss at sampled points along the curve while projecting each point back into the ℓp-ball. Offspring are then sampled from distinct regions of the optimized curve (t∈(0,0.5) and t∈(0.5,1)) and projected to the feasible set. The key empirical finding is that successful adversarial perturbations lie on connected manifolds where intermediate points on optimized Bezier paths maintain—and often exceed—the attack effectiveness of the endpoints, with transferability increasing as more auxiliary images guide the path optimization. This multi-image objective acts as an implicit regularizer that biases the curve toward flatter, more universal high-loss regions, explaining the strong cross-instance generalization. The full MoCo-EA pipeline retains standard evolutionary components (tournament selection, Gaussian mutation, elitism) but achieves near-perfect success in 1–2 generations versus hundreds for the baseline, reducing queries by 94–99%.

## Results

MoCo-EA achieves 100% attack success rate across all norms (ℓ∞, ℓ2, ℓ1) on both CIFAR-10 and ImageNet, compared to the traditional EA baseline which reaches only 6.7% under ℓ2 on CIFAR-10 and 13.3% under ℓ2 on ImageNet. Convergence is dramatically faster: MoCo-EA requires 1.0–1.7 generations versus 25–457 generations for the baseline, reducing average queries by 94.9–98.6% and runtime by 79.3–96.7%. Against gradient-based attacks on CIFAR-10, MoCo-EA achieves 40.5% ASR on a robust model (vs. 40.3% for AutoAttack) and 53.8% ASR under obfuscated gradients (vs. 13.5% for PGD and 0.0% for AutoAttack).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the traditional EA baseline (GenAttack-style element-wise crossover), MoCo-EA wins decisively on every metric: it achieves 100% success rate versus 6.7–93.3% for the baseline, converges in 1–2 generations versus 13–457 generations, and reduces queries by 95–99%. Against gradient-based attacks on CIFAR-10, MoCo-EA slightly outperforms PGD (40.5% vs. 39.6%), MI-FGSM (38.7%), AutoAttack (40.3%), and Adaptive AutoAttack (39.1%) on a robustly trained ResNet-50. Under obfuscated gradients (input quantization), MoCo-EA achieves 53.8% ASR while all gradient-based methods collapse: PGD drops to 13.5%, MI-FGSM to 13.3%, AutoAttack to 0.0%, and AAA to 12.8%. On ImageNet (100 sampled images), MoCo-EA similarly leads with 47% ASR on the robust model (vs. 38–43% for baselines) and 32% under obfuscated gradients (vs. 16–17% for baselines). The paper positions MoCo-EA not as a replacement for gradient-based attacks but as a complementary method that excels when gradients are unreliable or obfuscated.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: mode connectivity in neural network parameter space (Garipov et al., Draxler et al.) and population-based evolutionary adversarial attacks (GenAttack, Su et al.). It departs from prior evolutionary attacks by replacing discrete element-wise crossover with a gradient-optimized quadratic Bezier curve in perturbation space, and from prior mode-connectivity work by inverting the objective (maximizing rather than minimizing loss along the path) and applying it to input perturbations rather than model parameters. The white-box use of gradients to optimize the Bezier control point is a key distinction from the gradient-free evolutionary attacks that preceded it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that intermediate points along optimized adversarial paths are more transferable than the endpoints challenges the standard view of adversarial examples as isolated sharp maxima and suggests defenses must account for continuous adversarial manifolds rather than point perturbations. The dramatic efficiency gains (94–99% fewer queries, 1–2 generations vs. hundreds) make evolutionary attacks practically competitive with gradient-based methods in the white-box setting, and the strong performance under obfuscated gradients (53.8% vs. 13.5% for PGD) highlights a concrete niche where gradient-free population search is advantageous. Open questions include whether higher-order curves or different path parameterizations yield further gains, whether the connectivity property holds for non-ReLU architectures, and how defenses can be designed to disrupt adversarial manifolds rather than individual points.
