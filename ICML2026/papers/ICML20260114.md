## Problem

Kleinberg and Mullainathan proved that language generation in the limit is always computable for countable families, but this existence result provides no quantitative guarantee on how many examples are needed. The paper asks how many positive examples a generator requires to reliably produce unseen words from a target language, and shows that infeasibility already arises for canonical classes such as context-free and regular languages, establishing a sharp gap between theoretical possibility and computational feasibility.

## Input / Output

The generator receives a finite set of positive examples (words) drawn from an unknown target language and must output a word belonging to that language but not already in the input set. The critical constraint is uniformity: the sample bound m must hold simultaneously for every language in a finite family and every possible subset of m examples, not merely for a particular presentation order. Languages are specified by formal descriptions (CFGs, NFAs, regular expressions, LTT profiles, or non-erasing patterns), and sample complexity is measured as a function of the descriptive size of these specifications and the family cardinality.

## Method

The central technical tool is a characterization (Proposition 2.3) showing a finite family is not m-generatable if and only if some subfamily has a finite intersection of size at least m, thereby reducing the problem to bounding the cardinality of finite intersections. For context-free languages, a reduction from the halting problem constructs two CFGs whose intersection size encodes the halting time, proving no computable bound exists even for two-language families. For regular languages, a product-automaton argument gives a double-exponential upper bound, while a family of block-counting languages over binary words achieves a matching double-exponential lower bound. For LTT languages, a novel pumping lemma for profiles (Lemma 5.3) yields a single-exponential bound, and for non-erasing pattern languages, a construction built on one-variable word equations (Nowotka & Saarela) gives an exponential lower bound. Across all classes, the sample complexity is governed by the size of the largest finite intersection, which no practical generator can circumvent.

## Results

The paper establishes a hierarchy of sample complexity bounds for uniform generation in the limit across canonical language classes. For context-free languages (even families of just two), no computable bound exists (Theorem 3.1). For regular languages with n languages each recognized by s-state NFAs over an alphabet of size a, the sample complexity is double-exponential: upper bound a^{s^n} and lower bound 2^{(Ω(s/log n))^n} (Theorem 4.2). For LTT languages, the bound drops to single-exponential: upper bound a^{3s|F|((s|F|)^2+1)^2} with a lower bound of 2^n for families of two languages (Theorem 5.1). For non-erasing pattern languages, an exponential lower bound of 2^n is established for families of two languages (Theorem 6.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The central 'comparison' is between the existence guarantee of Kleinberg & Mullainathan (2024), who showed every countable family is generatable in the limit, and the infeasibility barriers established here: even for two context-free languages, no computable number of examples suffices. Among the classes studied, there is a clear complexity hierarchy: context-free (non-computable) > regular (double-exponential) > LTT (single-exponential) > patterns (exponential lower bound only). The LTT result is notable because it shows that imposing strong local structural constraints on regular languages strictly reduces the sample complexity from double- to single-exponential, yet the bound remains infeasible. The pattern language result is particularly striking because non-erasing pattern languages are the canonical example of a nontrivial family identifiable in the limit under Gold's model (Angluin, 1980a), yet they still require exponential samples for generation, highlighting a fundamental asymmetry between identification and generation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper takes the existence result of Kleinberg & Mullainathan (2024) — that every countable family of languages is generatable in the limit — and asks the quantitative question of how many examples suffice for uniform generation over finite families. Its central technical tool is the characterization of m-generatable families from Raman et al. (2025), which reduces the sample complexity question to bounding the size of finite intersections of languages in the family. The paper then applies this reduction across four canonical language classes (CFGs, regular languages, LTT languages, and non-erasing pattern languages), drawing on classical results from formal language theory (e.g., decidability of CFG finiteness, product constructions for automata, and word-equation results of Nowotka & Saarela) to derive the respective complexity bounds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a sharp and clean separation between the theoretical possibility of language generation in the limit and its computational feasibility, showing that infeasibility already arises for context-free languages (non-computable sample complexity for just two languages) and persists as double- or single-exponential bounds for regular and LTT languages. This provides a formal theoretical explanation for why LLM success cannot be explained purely by the existence of a generation algorithm: the worst-case data requirements are infeasible, suggesting that empirical success must rely on average-case structure or avoidance of the adversarial configurations that drive the lower bounds. A key limitation is the adversarial data assumption; whether natural language families and training corpora avoid the large finite intersections that govern the lower bounds remains an open empirical question. The paper also opens a connection between generation sample complexity and the classical problem of deciding non-emptiness of language intersections.
