## Problem

Existing neural network design methods face an effectiveness-efficiency dilemma: NAS is computationally expensive due to exhaustive trial-and-error, while model retrieval yields suboptimal static checkpoints that cannot adapt to idiosyncratic data properties. The core gap is that prior work stores only coarse performance records of complete models rather than fine-grained edit-effect evidence, and the transferability of such evidence is treated as static, failing under evolving refinement trajectories and out-of-distribution shifts.

## Input / Output

M-DESIGN consumes an unseen task dataset (e.g., a graph with node/link/graph-level labels), an initial model architecture, and a pre-built Model Knowledge Base (MKB) of 67,760 evaluated GNN configurations organized as modification-gain graphs storing pairwise performance deltas for every 1-hop architectural edit across 22 benchmark datasets. It outputs a near-optimal architecture discovered through a sequence of 1-hop modifications under a strict budget of 100 model evaluations, with each iteration selecting the single edit that maximizes the woven expected gain. The MKB is queried via table lookups (<0.3 s per iteration) rather than retraining, and the initial task similarity can be bootstrapped from data statistics or LLM-based analysis before any evaluation on the unseen task.

## Method

M-DESIGN reframes model refinement as adaptive retrieval over a modification-gain graph where each directed edge encodes the performance delta of a 1-hop architectural edit on a benchmark task. At each iteration, it retrieves candidate local modifications around the current architecture, computes a 'woven' expected gain by aggregating fine-grained evidence across benchmark tasks weighted by a Bayesian dynamic task similarity, executes the best edit, and observes the real gain. The dynamic similarity is updated online via Bayes' rule with a Gaussian observation model (mean γ·ΔP^i, variance σ²) estimated over sliding windows, progressively downweighting tasks whose evidence contradicts new observations. When posterior similarity drops below a threshold (OOD regime), EdgeConv-based predictive task planners trained on the modification-gain graph generate synthetic multi-hop gain estimates, and a replay buffer of recent observations fine-tunes these planners online, enabling smooth transition from retrieval-based to model-based refinement.

## Results

Under a strict budget of 100 model evaluations, M-DESIGN achieves the search-space optimum in 26 out of 33 task-data pairs across node classification, link prediction, and graph classification. On the 11 node classification datasets (Table 1), M-DESIGN reaches the space optimum on 8/11 datasets (e.g., Cora 88.50%, CS 95.33%, Wisconsin 91.33%) and comes within 0.37% on the remaining two (Computers 89.22% vs. 89.59%; Texas 83.79% vs. 84.68%). In terms of refinement efficiency, M-DESIGN requires on average 5–50 evaluations to reach its 50-evaluation performance level, whereas many baselines (Random, RL, EA, Auto-GNN, AutoTransfer) fail to reach the target within 100 evaluations on multiple datasets (marked ∞ in Table 2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

M-DESIGN outperforms all 10 baselines on the majority of datasets. Among search-based methods (Random, RL, EA, GraphNAS, Auto-GNN), M-DESIGN leads by 0.5–3.0% on most datasets; for example, on Cornell it reaches 77.48% (space optimum) versus 74.87% (RL) and 74.24% (EA). Retrieval-only methods (GraphGym, NAS-Bench-Graph, KBG) are the weakest, often trailing search-based methods by 2–5% (e.g., NAS-Bench-Graph scores only 65.67% on CiteSeer vs. 74.59% for M-DESIGN). The strongest competitor is DesiGNN (retrieval-based refinement with LLM knowledge), which reaches the space optimum on 4/11 node classification datasets; M-DESIGN extends this to 8/11 and additionally wins on Texas (83.79% vs. 81.80%) and Computers (89.22% vs. 88.40%). AutoTransfer, the other retrieval-based refinement baseline, reaches the space optimum on only 2/11 node classification datasets and lags M-DESIGN by up to 5.4% (Cornell: 76.58% vs. 77.48%). In efficiency terms (Table 2), M-DESIGN requires as few as 5 evaluations (Wisconsin) to reach its target performance, while baselines like Auto-GNN and AutoTransfer fail entirely (∞) on 7–9 of 11 datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model retrieval paradigm (GraphGym, NAS-Bench-Graph, KBG) and retrieval-based refinement (AutoTransfer, DesiGNN) by shifting the unit of transfer from whole-model checkpoints to fine-grained 1-hop modification gains organized in a graph structure. It departs from static task-similarity estimators (ranking-based, embedding-based, semantic-based) by introducing a Bayesian dynamic similarity that is updated online from observed gains, and from exhaustive NAS (RL, EA, GraphNAS, Auto-GNN) by replacing trial-and-error with knowledge-driven retrieval over a modification-gain graph. The design space and data collection pipeline build directly on GraphGym (You et al., 2020), while the predictive task planners for OOD adaptation draw on edge-regression GNNs (EdgeConv).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The released model knowledge base (67,760 GNNs across 22 datasets and 3 task types with explicit edit-effect records) is a practically useful resource for the graph ML community, and the 26/33 search-space-best result under a 100-evaluation budget demonstrates that retrieval-augmented refinement can match or exceed exhaustive search at a fraction of the cost. However, the approach's reliability is bounded by repository coverage and the local gain-consistency assumption; extreme OOD tasks or sparse evidence can degrade performance, and the predictive task planners provide an empirical fallback rather than a theoretical guarantee. Open questions include scaling to larger model families (e.g., transformers), richer edit operators beyond 1-hop GNN tweaks, and whether the dynamic-similarity mechanism generalizes to settings where the modification space is combinatorially larger.
