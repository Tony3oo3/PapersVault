## Problem

Deep learning-based watermarking methods suffer a sharp decoding-accuracy collapse as payload dimension increases, even in the absence of noise, which the paper traces to coding cross-talk from non-orthogonal, overlapping bit carriers (a curse of dimensionality in learning efficient packing). Classical coding-based methods such as QIM avoid this collapse through structured orthogonal carriers but lack robustness to real-world distortions. The paper asks whether the robustness of learned feature extraction can be combined with the capacity-scaling guarantees of coding-based encoding.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB host image (experiments use 3×128×128, but the same checkpoint generalizes without retraining to resolutions up to 4096×4096) and a binary message of up to 32,768 bits, and produces a watermarked image. Encoding is optimization-based: the host image is iteratively updated for T steps (default 3000) so its robust feature matches the QIM-encoded target, rather than being produced by a single forward pass. Decoding is a single forward pass through the frozen RFE followed by nearest-coset assignment, and adapting to a new payload length only requires constructing a new orthogonal projection matrix P without retraining.

## Method

OrthoMark decouples watermarking into two modules: a Robust Feature Extractor (RFE) built from an invertible neural network (INN) with DWT/IDWT preprocessing that learns a distortion-invariant feature representation, and a Structured Encoding and Decoding (SED) module that performs QIM-style quantization in an n-dimensional subspace defined by an orthogonal projection matrix P (P^T P = I_n). The orthogonal projection enforces independent bit carriers by construction, suppressing the cross-bit interference that causes capacity collapse in end-to-end deep watermarking. During training, the RFE parameters and the host image are jointly optimized via backpropagation with three losses (visual quality MSE, clean feature alignment, and robust feature alignment through a noise layer); during inference the RFE is frozen and only the image is optimized for T steps. The bijective INN architecture preserves distinguishability of distinct QIM coset targets at the image level, which is critical for high-capacity operation where cosets become dense.

## Results

On the USC-SIPI benchmark at 64-bit payload, OrthoMark achieves the highest PSNR (39.26 dB) and the best average decoding accuracy (99.59%) across 15 distortions spanning signal, geometric, and photometric categories. At a larger 256-bit payload (PSNR fixed at 40 dB), OrthoMark reaches 90.60% average accuracy, outperforming the strongest baseline CIN (84.62%) by 5.98 percentage points. Most critically, OrthoMark sustains near-perfect decoding accuracy up to 32,768 bits on clean images, whereas all 9 deep watermarking baselines exhibit a sharp accuracy collapse in the high-capacity regime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OrthoMark outperforms all 10 baselines on both PSNR and average decoding accuracy at 64 bits. The closest competitor is VideoSeal (98.48% AVG, 38.82 dB PSNR), which OrthoMark surpasses by 1.11 percentage points in accuracy and 0.44 dB in PSNR; CIN follows at 97.77% AVG but with notably lower PSNR (36.18 dB). The gap widens substantially at 256 bits, where OrthoMark achieves 90.60% AVG versus CIN's 84.62% and VideoSeal's 77.01%, with the largest margins under geometric transforms (e.g., 86.58% vs. 74.20% on shear). On capacity scaling (Fig. 1), OrthoMark is the only method that maintains near-100% clean decoding accuracy up to 32,768 bits, while all baselines collapse to near-chance accuracy well before 4,096 bits. Classical DWT-DCT-SVD and early deep methods (HiDDeN, LISO, SSL) are far behind on both metrics, confirming that the bottleneck in prior deep watermarking is intrinsic to the encoding mechanism rather than the decoder architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two historically separate lines of watermarking research: classical coding-based methods (particularly QIM, Chen & Wornell 2002) that achieve theoretical optimality under AWGN but lack robustness to real-world distortions, and deep learning-based methods (HiDDeN, MBRS, CIN, VideoSeal, etc.) that learn robustness end-to-end but suffer a fundamental capacity bottleneck. OrthoMark's key architectural departure is decoupling these concerns: an invertible neural network (INN) with DWT preprocessing learns a distortion-invariant feature domain, while a structured QIM-style encoder/decoder with an orthogonal projection matrix handles message coding in that domain. The paper also introduces a linear superposition model and signal-to-interference ratio (SIR) analysis to formally characterize why deep watermarking fails at high payload—a 'curse of dimensionality' manifesting as coding cross-talk among non-orthogonal learned carriers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and diagnoses a previously underappreciated failure mode in deep watermarking: even in noiseless settings, decoding accuracy collapses as payload grows, limiting practical capacity to a few hundred bits. By combining learned robustness with structured coding, OrthoMark achieves 32,768-bit payloads with near-perfect accuracy, a 32–128× capacity improvement over typical deep methods while also improving PSNR and robustness. The main practical limitation is the optimization-based encoding stage (~1.15 s at T=600), which restricts real-time embedding but is acceptable for offline provenance and forensic workflows where decoding is frequent and single-pass. Open questions include whether the INN backbone is strictly necessary (vs. other bijective or near-bijective architectures), whether the approach extends to video or 3D content, and whether the orthogonal projection can be learned rather than fixed to further improve performance.
