## Problem

Prior work on LLM emotion understanding has focused on classification accuracy benchmarks, leaving unexplored whether and how LLMs internally organize emotions into hierarchical structures resembling those in cognitive psychology (e.g., the Shaver emotion wheel). Additionally, it remains unclear whether LLMs exhibit systematic demographic biases in emotion recognition that mirror or diverge from human patterns, particularly for intersectional underrepresented groups.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263504/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes GPT-4o-generated situation prompts (5,000 for hierarchy extraction; 2,700 for persona-conditioned recognition) and appends the fixed phrase 'The emotion in this sentence is' to elicit next-token probability distributions over a fixed vocabulary of 135 emotion words from Shaver et al. (1987). For bias analysis, a demographic persona descriptor (e.g., 'As a low-income Black woman,') is inserted before the emotion query. The primary output is a 135×135 matching matrix from which a directed acyclic graph (hierarchy tree) is derived; the secondary output is per-persona emotion classification labels across six broad categories (love, joy, surprise, anger, sadness, fear).

## Method

The core technique is a logit-based representation-engineering pipeline: for each situation prompt, the model's next-token probability distribution over 135 emotion words is extracted, forming a matrix Y ∈ R^{N×135}. The matching matrix C = Y^T Y is computed, where each entry C_ij quantifies the degree to which emotions i and j co-occur across contexts. A hierarchical tree is then constructed by applying two conditions to each emotion pair (a, b): a strong-implication threshold (C_ab / Σ_i C_ai > t) and an asymmetry condition (C_ab / Σ_i C_ib < C_ab / Σ_i C_ai), which together identify a as a more specific child of the more general parent b. No ground-truth labels or explicit training are required; the hierarchy emerges purely from the model's output distributions. The authors validate this approach across model scales (GPT-2 through Llama 3.1 405B), on a real-world corpus (GoEmotions), on a different domain (wine aromas), and with RL-fine-tuned Mistral-7B to test whether prediction-error-based training specifically improves surprise recognition.

## Results

Llama 405B achieves 87.1% accuracy on six-category emotion recognition (vs. 15.2% on fine-grained 135-word classification) under a neutral persona. Hierarchy complexity (total path length) and alignment with the Shaver emotion wheel (cluster-distance correlation: 0.38 for 8B, 0.64 for 70B, 0.51 for 405B; all p<0.001) increase monotonically with model scale. RL fine-tuning on persuasion tasks improves surprise recognition from 20.0% to 33.3% on Mistral-7B (McNemar χ²(1)=6.40, p=0.011). Persona-specific emotion-tree geometry predicts recognition accuracy with r=0.84 (p<0.001) for total path length.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark a proposed method against competing algorithms; instead it compares LLMs of varying scale (GPT-2 1.5B → Llama 8B → 70B → 405B) and LLMs against human participants. On hierarchy complexity, GPT-2 produces no meaningful tree, while Llama 405B yields the deepest and most psychologically aligned structure (wheel correlation 0.51–0.64, p<0.001). On emotion recognition, Llama 405B (87.1% six-category) substantially outperforms the 60 human participants on the surprise sub-task (56.4%), but humans show more consistent accuracy across demographics whereas Llama's accuracy drops sharply for underrepresented personas (e.g., 6.7% surprise accuracy for Black personas vs. 41.7% neutral). The RL-fine-tuned Mistral-7B beats its base counterpart on surprise recognition by 13.3 percentage points (33.3% vs. 20.0%, p=0.011), while other emotion categories remain statistically unchanged. On misclassification patterns, Llama replicates human biases for race (Black→anger) and education, but reverses the gender bias (LLM favors males; humans favor females).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the representation-engineering paradigm (Zou et al., 2023; Gandhi et al., 2024) and the matching-matrix idea from hierarchical clustering of logits (Palumbo et al., 2024) to extract a psychologically-grounded hierarchy of emotions from LLM output distributions. It departs from prior LLM emotion work (Broekens et al., 2023; Tak & Gratch, 2023, 2024), which focuses on classification accuracy benchmarks, by instead analyzing the *structure* of the model's emotion space via a conditional-probability criterion inspired by the Shaver emotion wheel. The method is further validated on a non-emotion domain (wine aromas), distinguishing it from topic-modeling approaches that require text corpora.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLMs spontaneously develop emotion hierarchies aligned with the psychologically validated Shaver wheel as scale increases suggests that cognitively-grounded theories can serve as predictive probes for model internals, opening a new evaluation axis beyond accuracy benchmarks. The compounding intersectional biases (e.g., low-income Black female personas showing the lowest recognition accuracy) are practically important for deploying LLMs in emotionally sensitive contexts such as counseling or customer service. Key limitations include the absence of valence/arousal dimensions, reliance on LLM-generated scenarios that may underrepresent certain emotions (notably surprise), and the forced single-label constraint in the user study that may not capture human nuance. Open questions include whether the observed hierarchies reflect genuine affective understanding or merely statistical regularities in training text, and whether the demographic biases can be mitigated without degrading overall performance.
