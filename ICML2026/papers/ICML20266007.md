## Problem

LLM-based agents for interactive decision-making rely on passive pattern matching from historical trajectories, confounding statistical correlation with true causality and producing fragile decisions when confounding factors shift. Existing prompt-based, experience-driven, and hierarchical planning methods lack any explicit mechanism to actively verify whether a proposed action is a genuine causal driver of the desired outcome before committing to execution.

## Input / Output

The agent consumes a natural-language observation, the full action-observation history, and a task goal at each decision step. It outputs a structured causal reasoning chain—N=3 candidate hypotheses paired with expected effects, counterfactual intervention designs, and Empirical Intervention Scores (EIS) computed from K=3 paired environment rollouts—before emitting the final textual action command. A dual-reasoner architecture underpins this: an Intuitive Reasoner at τ=0.8 generates divergent hypotheses, while an Analytical Reasoner at τ=0.1 performs rigorous effect scoring, ensuring the output is both exploratory and quantitatively grounded.

## Method

The Cycle-of-Science framework structures each decision step as a Hypothesis-Experiment-Validation loop: an Intuitive Reasoner generates diverse causal hypotheses, an Intervention Planner designs contrastive tests (action applied vs. not applied from the identical state), and an Analytical Reasoner computes EIS by averaging goal-achievement score differences across K paired rollouts. An adaptive threshold, modulated by the policy's action entropy, dynamically lowers the acceptance bar in uncertain states to encourage exploration and raises it in confident states for precision. For training, a two-stage pipeline first performs SFT on causally-annotated trajectories, then applies Counterfactual Preference Optimization (CPO) that constructs preference pairs directly from empirically measured EIS scores rather than human annotations, reinforcing validated causal reasoning chains while penalizing spurious correlations. Self-play augmentation scales the dataset from 5K human demonstrations to 50K trajectories, with the 1:9 human-to-self-play ratio identified as optimal.

## Results

On ScienceWorld, CoS achieves 71.45% (seen) and 67.23% (unseen) success rate with Mistral-7B, outperforming the strongest baseline GLIDER (58.76%/54.32%) by +12.69%/+12.91%, while also reducing average steps from 68.1 to 61.8. On ALFWorld, CoS reaches 84.76% (seen) and 80.58% (unseen) with Llama-3-8B, surpassing GLIDER by +3.48%/+4.16% on unseen tasks. On Crafter, CoS (Mistral-7B) unlocks 39.5%±2.7% of achievements, exceeding the best LLM-based baseline KnowSelf (33.2%) by +19.0% and the best RL method Rainbow (19.5%) by +102.6%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoS consistently outperforms all five baselines (ReAct, Reflexion, ExpeL, KnowSelf, GLIDER) on ScienceWorld across all three backbones, with the largest gains over GLIDER reaching +12.69% (seen) and +12.91% (unseen) with Mistral-7B. On ALFWorld, CoS wins on unseen tasks across all backbones (+2.16% to +4.16% over GLIDER) but shows a notable exception with Gemma-7B on seen tasks (77.25% vs. GLIDER's 79.34%), suggesting the causal verification loop offers less advantage on simpler procedural tasks with short causal chains. On Crafter, CoS (39.5%) substantially surpasses KnowSelf (33.2%), GLIDER (31.4%), and all RL-based methods (Rainbow 19.5%, DreamerV3 17.3%, SPRING 19.1%), demonstrating that structured causal reasoning is particularly effective in open-ended, sparse-reward environments. In terms of step efficiency, CoS completes ScienceWorld tasks in 61.8–68.5 steps versus 68.1–75.9 for GLIDER, and ALFWorld tasks in 17.3–19.2 steps versus 18.7–20.4 for baselines, indicating that hypothesis-driven exploration reduces wasteful trial-and-error interactions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) by replacing human-annotated preference pairs with empirically measured causal effect comparisons (EIS scores) to train a Counterfactual Preference Optimization objective. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant LLM-agent paradigm of passive pattern matching (ReAct, Reflexion, ExpeL) and hierarchical planning (GLIDER) by introducing an active, intervention-based verification loop inspired by Pearl's causal ladder and the scientific method. It also extends prior causal-reasoning-in-LLMs work (Jin et al., Long et al., Ban et al.), which focused on static causal graph extraction or symbolic counterfactuals, by grounding causal verification in online environment rollouts at decision time rather than offline knowledge extraction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled mechanism for LLM agents to distinguish causation from correlation during decision-making, which is a persistent failure mode in current agent systems. However, the practical cost is substantial—24 LLM calls and 18 environment interactions per decision step versus 1–2 for baselines—limiting deployment in latency-sensitive or compute-constrained settings. Open questions include whether lower-cost validation (e.g., learned surrogate models for EIS estimation) can preserve the causal gains, how the approach behaves under distribution shift where causal estimates become noisy, and whether the framework scales to truly long-horizon tasks where context truncation degrades consistency. The finding that a 3B model with CoS outperforms an 8B model with standard SFT suggests the causal reasoning structure may be a more efficient use of parameters than raw scale for structured decision tasks.
