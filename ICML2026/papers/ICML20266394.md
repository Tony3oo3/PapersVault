## Problem

Current RLHF pipelines aggregate diverse human preferences into a single reward signal, effectively optimizing for a hypothetical 'average user' whose preferences match no real person well. This paper argues the aggregation is both theoretically value-laden (grounded in Arrow's impossibility theorem) and empirically harmful, systematically marginalizing minority preference groups and underrepresented demographics. The gap it addresses is the absence of a principled framework for personalized preference learning that preserves universal safety constraints while accommodating legitimate individual variation.

## Input / Output

The surveyed personalization approaches consume user interaction histories, preference annotations, and contextual signals (task type, expertise level, urgency, cultural background) to produce user-specific reward signals and adapted text responses. Unlike standard RLHF which yields a single scalar reward per prompt, the proposed architectures decompose reward into user-specific and universal components, with personalization operating across multiple timescales—from immediate in-context steering to long-term parameter adaptation via LoRA adapters or mixture-of-experts routing. Outputs are structured to respect a three-tier normative boundary: freely personalizable behaviors (style, verbosity), safeguarded personalization (value-laden topics), and universally fixed behaviors (factual accuracy, safety-critical content).

## Method

As a position paper, the core contribution is a theoretical argument and normative framework rather than a single algorithm. The paper invokes Arrow's impossibility theorem to establish that no aggregation of heterogeneous preferences is value-neutral, then surveys the technical landscape of personalization including user-specific embeddings, parameter-efficient adaptation (LoRA, prompt tuning), mixture-of-experts routing, meta-learning for cold-start, and context-window-based personalization. It proposes a 'bounded personalization' framework with three tiers of appropriateness, concrete design principles (transparency, user control, contestability, epistemic diversity constraints), and technical safeguards (interpretable preference models, uncertainty quantification, adversarial red-teaming). The paper systematically addresses five counterarguments—scalability, shared standards, manipulation risk, preference stability, and performance priority—arguing each is manageable within the bounded framework rather than a reason to abandon personalization.

## Results

This is a position paper without original empirical experiments or a main results table. The key quantitative claims it cites from prior work include: personalized models outperform non-personalized baselines by 15–30% across diverse tasks on the LaMP benchmark (Salemi et al., 2024); LLM opinions correlate up to 0.3 points higher with liberal, educated, Western populations (Santurkar et al., 2023); and prolonged LLM exposure produces a nearly 70% increase in essays remaining neutral on contested questions (Abdulhai et al., 2026). The paper's contribution is theoretical (invoking Arrow's impossibility theorem) and normative (proposing a bounded personalization framework) rather than empirical.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not conduct head-to-head empirical comparisons between methods. Instead, it systematically addresses six alternative views (summarized in Table 1) as its 'baselines': the 'Good Enough' argument, scalability/data challenges, shared standards, manipulation concerns, preference stability, and performance priority. The paper argues that aggregated RLHF (the status quo baseline) systematically fails minority-preference users, that parameter-efficient methods (LoRA, S-LoRA, Punica) and mixture-of-experts architectures make personalization computationally tractable, and that bounded personalization is compatible with universal safety constraints. The closest empirical anchor is the LaMP benchmark result (15–30% improvement from personalization), which the paper uses to argue that the gains from personalization are substantial rather than marginal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not build on a single prior method but synthesizes and repositions several existing threads: RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022), social choice theory (Arrow, 1950; Conitzer et al., 2024), pluralistic alignment (Sorensen et al., 2024), and personalization techniques (LoRA, Mixture-of-Experts, meta-learning, LaMP benchmark). It departs from the dominant RLHF paradigm by arguing that the aggregation step is not merely suboptimal but theoretically value-laden, and repurposes social choice impossibility results as conceptual motivation for a shift toward personalized reward modeling. The paper carves new ground primarily at the level of framing and normative argumentation rather than proposing a new algorithm, instead surveying existing technical approaches (S-LoRA, HyPerAlign, personalized reward decomposition) as building blocks for the proposed 'bounded personalization' framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a foundational assumption of the current LLM alignment pipeline—that diverse human preferences can be meaningfully collapsed into a single reward signal—and proposes 'bounded personalization' as a principled alternative that preserves universal safety constraints while accommodating individual variation. Key open questions it identifies include: how to distinguish stable preference signals from transient context-dependent noise at multiple timescales, how to design governance frameworks for personalized AI that existing regulations do not cover, and how to prevent preference laundering (systems shaping user preferences toward their own objectives). As a position paper, it provides no empirical validation of its own; the 15–30% personalization gains cited come from the LaMP benchmark (Salemi et al., 2024), and the theoretical impossibility argument relies on an analogy to Arrow's theorem that the authors themselves acknowledge does not transfer formally. The practical impact will depend on whether the research community adopts the bounded-personalization framing and develops the proposed benchmarks and governance mechanisms.
