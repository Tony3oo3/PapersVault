## Problem

Current LLM-based persuaders lack Theory of Mind (ToM) capabilities: they fail to model the opponent's cognitive graph of related claims and do not dynamically adapt to the opponent's evolving beliefs. This results in repetitive arguments (up to 12% 8-gram overlap between turns) and rigid, self-centered strategies that ignore the opponent's perspective, limiting both diversity and effectiveness of persuasion.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261457/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The persuader receives an augmented prompt containing the target claim, full conversation history, k predicted counterclaims (default k=3) from the counterclaim predictor, and predicted opponent agreement scores (on a 0–4 Likert scale) for each counterclaim from the attitude predictor. The output is a single-turn response structured as a <thought> block (internal reasoning, invisible to the opponent) followed by an <argument> block (the visible persuasive statement, capped at 200 tokens). The attitude predictor itself takes BGE-M3 embeddings of the conversation history and claim, concatenated and passed through a 3-layer MLP (~4M parameters) to produce a 5-way classification.

## Method

ToMAP introduces two Theory of Mind modules into a 3B-parameter persuader: a counterclaim predictor that prompts the persuader to proactively generate k counterclaims the opponent likely holds, and an opponent attitude predictor (frozen BGE-M3 encoder + trained MLP) that estimates the persuadee's agreement level on each counterclaim. These predictions are injected into the persuader's input prompt as augmented context. The persuader is then trained with PPO reinforcement learning, where the reward is the normalized attitude shift of the persuadee (measured via a balanced agreement score on both the claim and its negation) after the persuader's turn, plus auxiliary format, tag, repetition, and length penalties. A key finding is that RL training is essential for the model to actually exploit the ToM information—zero-shot prompting with counterclaims and attitudes yields no improvement over the base model, whereas the RL-trained ToMAP achieves a 39.4% relative gain over GPT-4o while using only 3B parameters.

## Results

ToMAP (Qwen2.5-3B) achieves an average agreement shift of 17.48% across three datasets and three persuadee models, outperforming GPT-4o (12.54%) by 39.4% relative gain and DeepSeek-R1 671B (17.02%) despite having only 3B parameters. The LLaMa3.2-3B variant of ToMAP reaches 24.35% average agreement shift, the highest among all tested configurations. Against the in-domain persuadee (Qwen2.5-7B), ToMAP (Qwen) scores 23.01/15.82/18.75 on CMV/Anthropic/args.me, while the LLaMa3.2-3B variant scores 26.89/23.63/20.96 on the same datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ToMAP (Qwen2.5-3B, avg 17.48) outperforms GPT-4o (12.54) by 39.4% relative gain and DeepSeek-R1 671B (17.02) by 2.7% relative gain, despite being orders of magnitude smaller. Against Gemma-3-27B (17.27), ToMAP (Qwen) edges ahead by 1.2% on average, while the LLaMa3.2-3B variant (24.35) surpasses it by 41%. LLaMa3.1-70B (10.57) is the weakest large-model baseline, trailing ToMAP (Qwen) by 65% relative gain. The Qwen2.5-3B base model (11.76) improves to 14.60 with RL alone (+24%), and further to 17.48 with ToMAP (+49% over base), confirming that both RL and ToM modules contribute substantially. Across 18 individual settings (3 datasets × 3 persuadees × 2 base models), ToMAP outperforms the RL-only variant on 13 settings and the SFT variant on 17, indicating consistent but not universal gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of LLM persuasion and Theory of Mind (ToM) research. Prior LLM persuasion work (Bai et al., 2023; Palmer & Spirling, 2023; Singh et al., 2024) focused on generating persuasive arguments or evaluating persuasiveness, while ToM research in AI (Kim et al., 2023; Wilf et al., 2024; Hwang et al., 2025) explored modeling others' mental states but rarely in adversarial persuasion settings. ToMAP departs from both lines by explicitly incorporating two ToM modules—a counterclaim predictor and a lightweight MLP-based attitude predictor—into a PPO-trained persuader, arguing that the key deficiency in current LLM persuaders is the absence of dynamic opponent modeling. The RL training paradigm follows the recent wave of RL-for-reasoning work (DeepSeek-R1, Search-R1), but applies it to the multi-turn persuasion task with a carefully designed attitude-shift reward.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that a 3B model with ToM-augmented RL can outperform GPT-4o by 39.4% in persuasion suggests that opponent-awareness, not raw scale, may be the critical bottleneck for effective LLM persuasion. The finding that ToM information is useless without RL training (Table 2) is practically important: it means simply prompting larger models with opponent beliefs won't suffice, and targeted training is necessary. Limitations include the short 3-turn evaluation horizon, reliance on a separate MLP attitude predictor rather than internalized ToM, and testing only on 3B base models. Open questions include whether the ToM modules can be absorbed into the model itself at scale, how the approach performs in truly open-ended multi-turn settings, and whether the persuasion gains transfer to human persuadees beyond the validated LLM-persuadee setting.
