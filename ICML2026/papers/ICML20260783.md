## Problem

Machine Learning Interatomic Potentials (MLIPs) lack principled uncertainty quantification by construction, making them unreliable for out-of-distribution data and data-scarce regimes common in simulation-based chemistry. Existing UQ approaches like Deep Ensembles require training and storing multiple models, scaling linearly in cost and memory, which is impractical for large-scale or foundation-model workflows. The paper addresses this by proposing a single-model Bayesian framework that delivers calibrated uncertainties at minimal computational overhead.

## Input / Output

The method consumes atomic structures encoded as graphs with node features (atomic numbers, positions, velocities) and edge features (pairwise distances, charges, radial basis functions), processed by any standard or equivariant MPNN backbone. It outputs energy and force predictions along with calibrated epistemic uncertainty estimates obtained via Monte Carlo sampling over stochastic weights. A key design choice is that the inference network producing the variational coefficients is evaluated only once per input and reused across all MC samples, while a single deterministic forward pass (zeroing the dropout coefficients) recovers standard MLIP inference speed when uncertainty is not needed.

## Method

BLIP treats the weights of each message and update function in an MPNN as Gaussian random variables whose variance is input-dependent, computed by a lightweight shared inference network E_ψ that maps initial node features and edge attributes to per-layer variational adaptive dropout coefficients α and β. The stochastic weights are sampled via the local reparameterization trick, which pushes noise from the weight space to the activation space, reducing the number of random samples per iteration and lowering gradient variance. Training maximises the ELBO with a KL divergence regulariser against a zero-mean Gaussian prior matched to a fixed dropout probability, and the entire system (backbone + inference network) is trained jointly. The approach is architecture-agnostic and provably preserves equivariance when the inference network is invariant, and it can be applied both during training from scratch and during fine-tuning of pretrained MLIPs such as ORB v3, where it consistently outperforms deterministic and ensemble baselines in both accuracy and calibration.

## Results

On the N-body Coulomb system, BLIP achieves the best MSE (0.092±0.004) and CRPS (0.032±0.001) in the non-equivariant GNN setting, outperforming Deep Ensembles (MSE 0.106, CRPS 0.034) and MC Dropout (MSE 0.117–0.138). On the data-scarce, OOD Ammonia (NH₃) system, BLIP achieves the lowest force MAE (41.93±3.38 kcal/mol/Å) versus Deep Ensemble (48.03) and the best calibration (ECE 0.091±0.006 vs. 0.108 for Deep Ensemble). Fine-tuning ORB v3 with BLIP on Silica (SiO₂, 699 atoms) yields an ECE of 0.032±0.002 compared to 0.247 for a 4-member Deep Ensemble, while matching or exceeding ensemble accuracy at single-model inference cost. On MATPES PBE with active learning, uncertainty-guided BLIP fine-tuning reduces energy MAE from 111.83 to 91.13 meV/atom over 5 AL steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MC Dropout (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016), BLIP dominates on every metric across both GNN and EGNN settings: on GNN, BLIP reduces MSE from 0.117 (p=0.2) to 0.092 and CRPS from 0.043 to 0.032, while MC Dropout at p=0.5 actually degrades accuracy (MSE 0.138). Against Deep Ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017), BLIP wins decisively in the non-equivariant GNN setting (MSE 0.092 vs. 0.106, NLL −9.03 vs. −3.89) and on the Ammonia task (force MAE 41.93 vs. 48.03, ECE 0.091 vs. 0.108), but narrowly trails on EGNN MSE (0.060 vs. 0.057) and NLL (−12.52 vs. −16.22); however, BLIP requires only one model versus four, cutting training and memory cost by ~4×. Against the single-model UQ baselines from Tan et al. (2023) (Evidential Regression, Mean Variance Estimate, Gaussian Mixture Model), BLIP achieves the lowest energy MAE (11.51 vs. 12.51–13.58) and force MAE (41.93 vs. 50.17–53.29) on Ammonia, as well as the best ECE (0.091 vs. 0.112–0.135). Against the pretrained ORB v3 model (Rhodes et al., 2025), BLIP fine-tuning with MAP inference outperforms both the deterministic fine-tuned ORB v3 and a 4-member Deep Ensemble PaiNN (6.58 MAE from Tan et al.) on Silica, while achieving an ECE of 0.032 versus 0.247 for the ensemble—demonstrating that a single Bayesian fine-tuned model can match or beat ensembles at a fraction of the inference cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

BLIP extends Variational Dropout (Kingma et al., 2015) and the local reparameterization trick to the message-passing setting, making the dropout coefficients input-dependent via a lightweight inference network. It positions itself as a scalable alternative to Deep Ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017), which remain the dominant UQ method for MLIPs but scale linearly in ensemble size, and to MC Dropout, which yields poorer calibration. The approach builds on the authors' prior work on Bayesian autoregressive networks (Coscia et al., 2025) and is explicitly architecture-agnostic, integrating with both invariant and equivariant MPNNs (PaiNN, EGNN, ORB v3) without modifying the base architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BLIP offers a practical drop-in solution for UQ in MLIPs that matches or exceeds Deep Ensembles in accuracy and calibration while requiring only a single model, which is critical for large-scale foundation models where maintaining multiple ensemble members is infeasible. A key limitation is that the local reparameterization trick breaks equivariance for architectures with irreducible representation dimensions (e.g., MACE, UMA), leaving an important gap for the most expressive equivariant MLIPs. Open questions include whether moment-matching or vectorial-mapping approximations can reduce the cost of multi-sample inference, and whether the adaptive dropout framework can be extended to handle aleatory uncertainty explicitly.
