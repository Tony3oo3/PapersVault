## Problem

Existing Shapley value approximation methods sample coalitions from fixed, predefined distributions, ignoring information from previous evaluations. When value function evaluations are costly (e.g., full model retraining for feature importance or data valuation) and the budget is severely limited, this non-adaptive sampling wastes evaluations. The paper asks whether adaptively selecting coalitions based on prior observations can substantially improve SV estimation accuracy in the low-budget regime.

## Input / Output

The method takes a black-box value function ν: 2^P → R over p players (represented as binary indicator vectors z ∈ {0,1}^p) and a total evaluation budget T, starting from an initial design of T₀ = p+1 coalitions drawn via leverage score sampling. It produces point estimates of all p Shapley values (φ̂ ∈ R^p) along with a full posterior covariance matrix Σ_{φ|D_t} ∈ R^{p×p} quantifying uncertainty. For games with p > 16, GP hyperparameters are refit on a fixed schedule rather than every iteration to control computational overhead, trading some adaptivity for scalability up to p = 101.

## Method

ShaplEIG frames adaptive coalition selection as a Bayesian experimental design problem: a GP surrogate with a Hamming (product) kernel models the value function over all 2^p coalitions, and at each iteration the next coalition is chosen by maximizing the expected information gain (EIG) about the SVs. The key theoretical contribution is showing that because SVs are a linear transformation of the value function vector, the EIG admits a closed-form expression via the Bayesian linear inverse problem / GOODE framework, reducing to a log-determinant of the updated SV posterior covariance. A second contribution is an efficient computation scheme that exploits the multiplicative structure of the Hamming kernel through elementary symmetric polynomials, reducing EIG computation from O(4^p · t) to O(p^4 + t^3). The greedy BAD algorithm iteratively selects the EIG-maximizing coalition, evaluates it, and refits GP hyperparameters from all data so far, making the design outcome-adaptive. The resulting SV estimator is consistent by construction under a noiseless GP, recovering exact SVs once all coalitions are evaluated.

## Results

The primary metric is mean squared error (MSE) between estimated and ground-truth Shapley values, plotted against the number of value function evaluations (up to 512) across 15 tasks spanning feature importance, data valuation, hyperparameter importance, and local explanation. ShaplEIG consistently achieves the lowest MSE across all tasks and evaluation budgets, with the most pronounced gains in the low-budget regime (fewer than ~100 evaluations). The paper reports that in the majority of tasks, ShaplEIG strictly dominates all baselines across all budgets, while in the remaining games it is only outperformed by a competitor over short intervals and never by a substantial margin. No single aggregate number is reported; results are presented as MSE-vs-budget curves in Figure 1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ShaplEIG is compared against four SV estimation baselines: Regression MSR (Witter et al., 2025), Leverage SHAP (Musco & Witter, 2025), Kernel SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017), and Permutation Sampling (Castro et al., 2009), all with paired sampling. Regression MSR is the only baseline that sometimes achieves competitive performance, but ShaplEIG outperforms it by a large margin on several tasks (e.g., FI on TabPFN/Breast Cancer, DV on GB/Cal. Housing, HPI on LCBench/Jasmine) across all evaluation phases. Leverage SHAP, Kernel SHAP, and Permutation Sampling are substantially outperformed by ShaplEIG in most settings, with the gap widening in the low-budget regime where adaptive selection provides the greatest benefit. Ablations (GP + Random, GP + Leverage Score, GP + Uncertainty Sampling) confirm that the EIG-based selection strategy, rather than the GP surrogate alone, drives the performance gains; notably, pure uncertainty sampling often performs worse than random sampling for SV estimation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies Bayesian experimental design (BED) with Shapley value (SV) estimation by framing adaptive coalition selection as a goal-oriented optimal design of experiments (GOODE) problem, where the SVs are a linear end-goal of a Bayesian linear inverse problem. It builds on the closed-form EIG results for linear end-goals (Attia et al., 2018; Lieberman & Willcox, 2013) and the GP surrogate framework, but departs from prior SV estimation methods (Kernel SHAP, Leverage SHAP, Regression MSR) that rely on fixed, non-adaptive coalition sampling distributions. The key technical departure is the use of a GP with a Hamming kernel whose multiplicative structure enables polynomial-time EIG computation via elementary symmetric polynomials, and the observation that GP hyperparameter retraining at each iteration is what makes the design truly adaptive (as opposed to the non-adaptive designs that arise with linear surrogates or fixed kernels).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ShaplEIG addresses a practically important gap: when value function evaluations require full model retraining (feature importance, data valuation, hyperparameter importance), the evaluation budget is severely limited and adaptive selection matters most. The consistent estimator (unlike tree-based surrogates requiring adjustment steps) and the closed-form EIG are clean theoretical contributions, while the ESP-based computation makes the method tractable up to ~100 players. However, the computational overhead from GP hyperparameter refitting (up to 25 minutes per iteration for large games) limits applicability to genuinely expensive value functions, and the method's advantage diminishes in the high-budget regime. Open questions include extending to noisy GPs, unbiased importance-weighted variants, and scaling to even larger player sets or larger evaluation budgets.
