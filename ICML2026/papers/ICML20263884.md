## Problem

It remains unclear whether diffusion models memorize rare/atypical training samples first (as outliers dominate residual loss) or common/prototypical samples first (as repeated features reinforce gradient signals). This ambiguity is compounded by the absence of a unified memorization metric that can detect partial memorization—where models reproduce sub-components of training data without copying full samples—making it difficult to distinguish generalization from early-stage memorization.

## Input / Output

For synthetic experiments, the model consumes 20,000 unique token sequences of length s^L=16 generated by a Random Hierarchy Model (L=4, v=6, m=4, s=2) with Zipf-distributed production rules at a controllable depth ℓ_z, and outputs generated sequences evaluated for exact-copy fraction, time-to-memorization per sample, and a partial memorization parameter λ that corrects for expected overlap under fair sampling. For real-data validation, 64×64 CelebA images (1,000 or 10,000 subsets) are used, with memorization detected via nearest-neighbor distance and sample likelihood estimated through an Ising model fitted to 38 facial attributes.

## Method

The paper trains multinomial discrete diffusion models (1D U-Net with weight sharing, widths 256–4096) on RHM token sequences where Zipf's law is injected at a single hierarchy level ℓ_z, enabling controlled study of how rarity at different abstraction levels affects memorization timing. A key technical contribution is the partial memorization estimator λ = (N_c − N_g·f)/(N_g·(1−f)), which subtracts the expected overlap from fair sampling to isolate genuine copying of subtuples, revealing that memorization proceeds in stages: common low-level features are overproduced first (the 'slop' phase), followed by common high-level features, and finally full samples. The authors further show that fat-tailed distributions delay memorization onset, with the effect amplified when the tail is introduced at higher abstraction levels, and validate all findings on CelebA using FID for generalization tracking and a ConvNeXt attribute classifier for likelihood estimation of unlabeled generated images.

## Results

The paper establishes a negative linear relationship between sample log-likelihood and time to memorization ⟨t_mem⟩, confirming that common (high-likelihood) training samples are memorized first, even in fully deduplicated datasets. On the synthetic RHM, the width-4096 model exceeds 95% data-point-level memorization within 4,000 epochs, while the width-256 model reaches only ~10% after 100,000 epochs. On CelebA, a 10,000-image training set yields 29.2% memorization at the evaluated checkpoint, and a 1,000-image set reaches 98% memorization. A distinct 'slop' phase is identified in which the average log-likelihood of generated images rises (common features are overproduced) before full memorization begins, with this phase being prolonged in smaller models (63M vs. 109M parameters).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analysis paper rather than a method-comparison study, so no external baselines are benchmarked. The paper's internal comparisons serve as the primary evidence: (1) varying the Zipf exponent α shows that heavier tails (larger α) delay the onset of memorization, consistent with the theoretical prediction ⟨t_mem⟩_D ∝ α^β/(1+β) for β<0; (2) introducing rarity at different hierarchy depths (ℓ_z = 0, 1, 2, 3) reveals that class-level variation (ℓ_z=0) delays memorization more than leaf-level variation (ℓ_z=3), because the model sees s^(L−1) more examples of rare features at lower levels; (3) comparing model widths (256, 1024, 4096) shows that larger models memorize faster and more completely, but the partial-memorization bias toward common features is relatively more pronounced in smaller models. On CelebA, the 109M-parameter model reaches the slop phase earlier and with a shorter duration than the 63M-parameter model, and FID may help detect the slop phase in smaller models but not in larger ones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Random Hierarchy Model (RHM) as a controlled synthetic framework for studying compositional generalization in diffusion models, extending it with Zipf-distributed production rules at selectable abstraction levels to probe how rarity interacts with hierarchy. It departs from prior memorization studies—which have largely focused on whole-sample reproduction in language models or image diffusion—by introducing a partial memorization metric (λ) that quantifies sub-tuple-level copying while accounting for expected overlap under fair sampling. The work resolves a tension between two competing hypotheses in the literature: the classification-inspired view that outliers are memorized first (Feldman & Zhang, Pham et al.) versus the duplication-based view that common features are memorized first (Lee et al., Carlini et al.), finding in favor of the latter.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that common, prototypical data is preferentially memorized—even in fully deduplicated training sets—undermines the assumption that deduplication provides meaningful privacy guarantees and shifts the focus of memorization risk from rare outliers to frequent features. The identification of a 'slop' regime, where partial memorization causes systematic overproduction of common sub-features before full-sample copying begins, has direct practical implications: standard early stopping at train/test loss divergence may still place models in this biased regime, and lower-capacity models (often chosen to reduce memorization) can actually amplify the bias. Open questions include whether the staged partial memorization and common-feature overproduction also appear in autoregressive next-token prediction models, and whether the effects can be disentangled when higher-level generalization is still improving alongside lower-level memorization.
