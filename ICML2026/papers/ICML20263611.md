## Problem

Existing LLM robustness evaluations assume users can iteratively repair malformed inputs and measure only task accuracy or surface-level noise tolerance, providing no insight into assistive reliability when repair is infeasible. For users with dyslexia, motor impairments, speech recognition errors, or low vision, assistance requires mediating between degraded input and latent intent without clarification, while simultaneously managing cognitive burden and hallucination risk—dimensions that standard benchmarks do not capture. This paper shows that apparent robustness under mild noise masks systematic trade-offs where high intent preservation co-occurs with burden inflation or hallucinated mediation.

## Input / Output

Each model receives a single accessibility-impaired prompt (one of 40,960 instances generated by applying four mechanism-grounded noise classes—orthographic, telegraphic, phonetic, and truncation—at four severity levels α ∈ {0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8} to 320 clean prompts across 8 languages) concatenated with a frozen mediation instruction that explicitly prohibits clarification requests, unsupported assumptions, and extraneous explanation. The model must output only the rewritten query in the same language and script. Evaluation then produces a multi-dimensional scorecard: ordinal intent preservation (IPR, 1–5), structural burden scores (BRS, ABI computed from token length, punctuation density, and character entropy), binary/ordinal hallucination indicators (HIR, severity 0–3), protocol compliance rates (PCR, CP, OAR), and stability metrics (ambiguity entropy, language consistency index, accessibility sensitivity curves).

## Method

APM is a zero-shot evaluation framework that reframes assistive prompt rewriting as a constrained mediation problem decomposed along three orthogonal axes: intent preservation, burden reduction, and hallucination avoidance. The dataset comprises 40,960 prompt instances (320 base prompts × 8 languages × 4 noise classes × 4 severity levels), where each noise class is mechanism-grounded in real accessibility literature (e.g., DysList error distributions for N1, AAC telegraphic patterns for N2, ASR phonetic confusions for N3, and missing-span truncation for N4) and parameterized by a monotonic severity coefficient enabling accessibility sensitivity curves. Ten frontier LLMs are evaluated under an identical frozen instruction with no training, fine-tuning, or adaptation. Semantic and interactional judgments (IPR, HIR, PCR) are computed by an LLM judge (GPT-5.2) validated against human annotators (κ = 0.72–0.79, ρ = 0.82) and cross-checked with a second judge (Gemini 3 Pro, ρ ≈ 0.81–0.86). The key diagnostic contribution is the joint analysis of metric families—particularly the false robustness condition (IPR ≥ 4 with ABI > 0) and the failure ordering showing usability/consistency metrics degrade at α ≈ 0.4 while semantic intent degrades only at α ≈ 0.6—revealing assistive breakdowns invisible to intent-only evaluation.

## Results

Across 10 frontier LLMs evaluated on 40,960 accessibility-impaired prompts (8 languages × 4 noise classes × 4 severities), xai.grok-4 achieves the highest Intent Preservation Rate (IPR = 4.09) and openai.gpt-4o the lowest Hallucination Rate (HIR = 0.183), yet all models exhibit negative Burden Robustness Scores (BRS ranging from −0.73 to −2.45), indicating universal cognitive burden inflation. Usability and multilingual consistency metrics (ABI, LCI) cross failure thresholds at α ≈ 0.4, roughly 0.2 severity levels earlier than intent preservation (α ≈ 0.6), revealing that assistive breakdown precedes semantic failure. Normalized ambiguity entropy remains bounded below 0.81 across all models, confirming systematic rather than stochastic mediation behavior even under severe impairment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to compare against baselines; instead, it evaluates 10 frontier LLMs as assistive mediators under a unified APM protocol. On semantic fidelity (IPR), xai.grok-4 (4.09) and openai.gpt-4o (4.05) lead, while ministral-3-3b trails at 2.56, a gap of over 1.5 points on a 5-point scale. On hallucination risk (HIR), openai.gpt-4o is best (0.183) and ministral-3-3b worst (0.513), a 2.8× difference. Critically, no model achieves both high IPR and positive BRS: even the top performers (grok-4, gpt-4o) show substantial burden inflation (ABI ≈ +1.9 to +2.2), confirming the paper's central finding of 'false robustness' where semantic correctness co-occurs with usability regression. Smaller models (qwen25-7b, gemma-2-9b) show less burden inflation (BRS ≈ −0.7 to −1.2) but sacrifice intent preservation and hallucination control. Protocol compliance (PCR) is relatively uniform (0.69–0.83), with the smallest model (ministral-3-3b) paradoxically highest (0.826) despite the worst IPR and HIR, suggesting it follows instructions rigidly but mediates poorly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by integrating three previously separate research threads—prompt robustness evaluation (PromptRobust, Flip-Flop Consistency), prompt rewriting for task optimization (AutoPrompt, APO), and accessibility/disability evaluation (AccessEval)—into a unified 'constrained mediation' task that none of them address individually. It departs from prompt-robustness work by treating the input as non-repairable and the rewriting itself as the evaluated output rather than a means to downstream task completion, and from accessibility-evaluation work by degrading the input itself rather than conditioning on disability mentions. The cognitive burden metric draws on readability heuristics (token length, punctuation density, character entropy) but repurposes them as a structural proxy for assistive usability rather than a linguistic quality score.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical implication is that standard robustness and intent-preservation metrics substantially overestimate assistive reliability: usability and multilingual consistency degrade at lower impairment severities (α≈0.4) than semantic correctness (α≈0.6), meaning models become unusable or hallucinatory before they simply get the intent wrong. This opens the question of whether the simplified structural burden proxy adequately captures real user experience, and whether synthetic noise models faithfully represent the heterogeneity of actual accessibility impairments. For the field, the framework provides a concrete diagnostic vocabulary (ABI, BRS, LCI, false robustness) that could inform accessibility auditing of deployed LLM systems, though the 320-prompt base set and regex-based tokenization limit generalizability claims.
