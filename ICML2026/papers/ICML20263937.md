## Problem

In sub-bit model compression, once magnitudes are compressed to roughly one bit per weight, the sign bit becomes a fixed-cost bottleneck (the 'one-bit wall') because learned sign patterns are spectrally indistinguishable from i.i.d. Rademacher noise and resist low-rank approximation. However, no prior work explained why signs appear random yet remain stable throughout training, nor provided a mechanism to make the sign component compressible rather than an irreducible storage cost.

## Input / Output

The method is a from-scratch training procedure: before optimization begins, the user specifies a low-rank sign template via a rank parameter r (e.g., r=2) and a random seed, from which the template T = sign(GH^⊤) is regenerated. Standard supervised training data (character-level text sequences for language modeling, image patches for classification) drives the optimization. The output is a compressed model where the sign component incurs near-zero storage cost (regenerable from the template spec) and the nonnegative magnitudes are stored via truncated SVD with quantized factors, yielding sub-bit effective bits-per-weight. This is not a post-training compressor for arbitrary pretrained checkpoints.

## Method

The paper introduces sign lock-in theory, a stopping-time analysis of scalar weight trajectories under SGD: under bounded per-step updates and a rare re-entry condition into a near-zero boundary neighborhood, the effective outer-to-outer sign flip count K_T^eff(ρ) obeys a geometric tail bound P[K_T^eff ≥ k] ≤ h_T · g_T^{k−1} + δ_upd, explaining why most weights retain their initialization signs. Building on this, the proposed method selects a compressible low-rank sign template T = sign(GH^⊤) (with G ∈ R^{m×r}, H ∈ R^{n×r}, r ≪ min(m,n)) before training and uses two lightweight interventions to preserve it: (i) gap initialization, which rejection-samples magnitudes from a two-sided truncated Gaussian to keep initial weights at least a_init away from zero, suppressing the initial-hit factor h_T; and (ii) outer-drift regularization, a log-barrier penalty R_LB that is zero outside the near-zero band and grows smoothly as |W_ij| → 0, applied during an early warmup phase to suppress the re-entry ratio g_T. Together these mechanisms reduce sign flips to ~10^{-3} with only ~1 point perplexity degradation, allowing the entire bit budget to be spent on magnitude compression via SVD, bypassing the one-bit wall.

## Results

Across MLP, CNN, and Transformer architectures, learned sign matrices are spectrally indistinguishable from i.i.d. Rademacher noise (small KS distances) and resist low-rank compression far more than magnitudes, establishing the one-bit wall. During training, the sign flip ratio relative to initialization remains well below 0.5, confirming that most signs are inherited from initialization. The proposed sign lock-in enhancement (gap initialization + outer-drift regularization) suppresses the mean per-step sign flip rate to ~10⁻³ with only about a one-point increase in validation perplexity. In end-to-end sub-bit compression (Figure 8), SVD applied to the magnitude-only matrix under the preserved sign template (SVD|W_lockin|) consistently outperforms vanilla SVD on raw weights in the sub-bit region across CharLM, Text8-Char, and DBPedia14.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between SVD|W_lockin| (magnitude-only SVD with a fixed low-rank sign template) and vanilla SVD applied directly to raw weights. In the sub-bit regime (below ~1 bit/weight), vanilla SVD on raw weights and explicit 1-bit baselines either degrade sharply or stall at the one-bit wall, while SVD|W_lockin| maintains substantially better perplexity/accuracy because the sign cost is amortized to ≈0 bits. Against hashing (Chen et al., ICML 2015), OneBit (Xu et al., NeurIPS 2024), and pruning-based methods such as SparseGPT (Frantar & Alistarh, ICML 2023), the paper reports that SVD|W_lockin| 'remains competitive' across language modeling (CharLM, Text8-Char) and classification (DBPedia14) tasks. The method does not claim uniform superiority over all baselines at every bit budget; rather, its advantage is most pronounced in the sub-bit region where the one-bit wall most severely limits conventional approaches. The sign-template method is a from-scratch training procedure, so it is not directly comparable to post-training quantization or pruning methods applied to arbitrary pretrained checkpoints.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of sub-bit model compression and stochastic-process theory. Prior compression work (OneBit, Double Binary Factorization, QBB) treats the sign matrix as a static object to be represented or quantized, while binary/ternary network literature (XNOR-Net, BNN, DoReFa-Net) optimizes sign-constrained weights without explaining why signs resist modification during training. The paper departs from both by asking a mechanistic question—why do trained signs look like i.i.d. Rademacher noise yet persist from initialization?—and answers it with a stopping-time excursion framework borrowed from Freidlin-Wentzell metastability theory and Kushner-Yin ODE localization, yielding a geometric-tail bound on effective sign flips. The practical method (low-rank sign template + gap initialization + log-barrier regularization) repurposes the lock-in phenomenon from a compression bottleneck into a controllable design choice, a direction absent from prior compression pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'one-bit wall' formalizes a fundamental barrier in sub-bit compression that practitioners have encountered empirically but lacked a mechanistic explanation for: even when magnitudes are compressed below one bit per weight, the sign component remains an irreducible one-bit cost unless its structure is explicitly controlled. The sign lock-in theory provides the first principled account of why this happens (rare boundary excursions with geometric-tail decay) and validates it across three architectures and a billion-parameter scale sweep. Key limitations constrain near-term impact: the proposed method is from-scratch only (not applicable to post-training compression of existing checkpoints), the enforcement mechanisms are simple (gap + log-barrier), and the representational role of signs as fixed parameters is not analyzed. The most important open question is whether sign-template control can be extended to arbitrary pretrained models, which would make the one-bit wall bypassable in the dominant post-training compression workflow.
