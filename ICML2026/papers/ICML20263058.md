## Problem

Existing GraphRAG methods suffer from three coupled limitations: high construction cost with semantic loss when converting text to graph structures, a fixed single-shot retrieval process that cannot adapt to complex multi-hop queries, and heavy reliance on large LLMs for long-context reasoning and prompt engineering. No prior work formulates graph-based retrieval as an agentic multi-turn interaction process optimized end-to-end via reinforcement learning, leaving the structural potential of graph knowledge underexploited.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263058/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a natural-language user query and a pre-built knowledge hypergraph (constructed offline from domain documents via GPT-4o-mini n-ary relation extraction, with entities and hyperedges embedded by bge-large-en-v1.5). At inference, the agent produces a structured multi-turn trajectory of think/query/retrieve/answer steps (using XML-style tags) and terminates with a final natural-language answer. Each retrieval turn returns top-k n-ary relational facts fused via reciprocal rank aggregation over two parallel paths (entity-based and direct hyperedge similarity), with a maximum context length of 4096 tokens.

## Method

Graph-R1 introduces three coupled components. First, a lightweight knowledge hypergraph construction pipeline extracts n-ary relational facts from 1200-token document chunks, encoding each fact as a hyperedge connecting its participating entities, reducing construction cost versus prior GraphRAG (5.69s vs. 8.04s per 1K tokens). Second, retrieval is modeled as a multi-turn agentic interaction: at each step the LLM agent generates a thinking reflection, selects an intent (continue querying or terminate with answer), and if continuing, formulates a retrieval query that triggers dual-path hypergraph retrieval (entity-similarity and hyperedge-similarity) fused by reciprocal rank aggregation. Third, the entire reasoning policy is optimized end-to-end via GRPO with a unified outcome-directed reward that combines a format-compliance term (rewarding well-structured think/query/answer blocks) and a token-level F1 answer-correctness term, gated so that answer reward is only counted when the format is valid. The key empirical finding is that RL is what unlocks graph-structured knowledge: prompt-only GraphRAG underperforms chunk-based StandardRAG, but RL-optimized multi-turn graph retrieval achieves substantially higher F1 (e.g., 57.82 vs. 32.05 average on Qwen2.5-7B), demonstrating that the synergy between structural representation and learned retrieval policy is essential.

## Results

On the Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct backbone, Graph-R1 achieves an average F1 of 57.82 and Generation Evaluation (G-E) score of 76.23 across six RAG benchmarks (2WikiMultiHopQA, HotpotQA, Musique, NQ, PopQA, TriviaQA), substantially outperforming the strongest RL-based baseline Search-R1 (46.19 F1) and the best prompt-based GraphRAG method HyperGraphRAG (29.40 F1, GPT-4o-mini). At the 3B scale, Graph-R1 reaches 51.26 F1, and at 1.5B it reaches 40.09 F1, consistently leading all baselines. Graph-R1 also achieves a retrieval similarity (R-S) of 60.40 (7B) and a generation cost of $0 per 1K queries with 7.0s response time, compared to $8.76 and 9.6s for HyperGraphRAG.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Graph-R1 (7B) outperforms all 12 baselines across all six datasets. Against the strongest RL-based competitor Search-R1 (7B), Graph-R1 gains +11.63 F1 on average (57.82 vs. 46.19), with the largest margin on Musique (+23.82 F1) and 2Wiki (+23.75 F1), indicating that multi-turn hypergraph retrieval is particularly beneficial for complex multi-hop reasoning. Against R1-Searcher (7B), the gap is even wider at +15.53 F1. Compared to prompt-based GraphRAG methods using GPT-4o-mini, Graph-R1 (7B) surpasses HyperGraphRAG by +28.42 F1 (57.82 vs. 29.40), StandardRAG by +25.77 F1, and GraphRAG by +32.95 F1, demonstrating that end-to-end RL over graph-structured knowledge unlocks performance that prompt engineering alone cannot achieve. Notably, prompt-only GraphRAG methods (GraphRAG, LightRAG, PathRAG) actually underperform StandardRAG on several datasets, confirming that graph structure without learned retrieval policy is insufficient. The ablation study further shows that removing RL entirely (w/o R.L.) collapses F1 to 17.79 (7B), while removing multi-turn interaction (w/o M.I.) drops it to 45.91, confirming that both components are essential.

## Relation to Prior Work

Graph-R1 sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) GraphRAG methods (HyperGraphRAG, LightRAG, PathRAG, HippoRAG2) that represent knowledge as entity-relation graphs or hypergraphs but rely on fixed one-shot retrieval and prompt engineering, and (2) RL-enhanced RAG agents (Search-R1, R1-Searcher, DeepSeek-R1/GRPO) that learn multi-turn retrieval policies but operate over chunk-based text. The paper's core departure is treating the knowledge hypergraph as an interactive environment for a GRPO-optimized agent, rather than a static retrieval index, and showing that the two threads are complementary: graph structure provides the representational ceiling while RL provides the strategy to reach it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: Graph-R1 achieves $0 generation cost and 7s/query latency while outperforming all baselines by large margins (e.g., 57.82 vs. 46.19 avg F1 at 7B), making agentic GraphRAG viable for deployment. A key open question the paper surfaces is whether the approach scales to much larger knowledge bases (current experiments use ~120K nodes) and whether it generalizes beyond QA to open-ended generation tasks. The finding that Qwen3 (already RL-trained) over-relies on internal reasoning and performs slightly worse raises an unresolved tension between pre-trained RL capabilities and environment-grounded RL.
