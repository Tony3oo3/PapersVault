## Problem

Constrained optimization with many convex inequality constraints that are hard to project onto lacks efficient first-order methods that avoid expensive projection or penalty steps. Prior randomized feasibility approaches required known problem parameters (strong convexity, smoothness constants) or assumed bounded constraint subgradients, and no fully parameter-free method existed that combined adaptive step sizes with randomized feasibility updates. This paper fills these gaps by establishing linear convergence with adaptive steps for strongly convex objectives and a parameter-free O(1/√T) rate for convex nonsmooth objectives.

## Input / Output

The method takes a convex objective f, m convex constraint functions g_i defining the hard-to-project set X, an easy-to-project set Y (e.g., box or ball), an initial point in Y, and a per-iteration sample count N_k that may be deterministic or drawn from a discrete distribution (Poisson, Uniform, Binomial). It outputs an approximate optimal solution x_k along with a monotonically decreasing infeasibility gap dist(x_k, X∩Y) that decays geometrically in N_k almost surely. A key design choice is that N_k can grow with the iteration index (e.g., N_k = ⌈k^{1/p}⌉), trading computational cost per iteration for faster feasibility reduction.

## Method

The core mechanism is a two-loop algorithm: an outer loop takes (sub)gradient steps on the objective with adaptive step sizes, and an inner loop (Algorithm 1) performs N_k randomized feasibility updates, each sampling one constraint uniformly, computing its subgradient, and applying a Polyak step scaled by β∈(0,2) followed by projection onto Y. For the strongly convex Lipschitz-smooth case, the adaptive step α_k = min{1/(2(L−μ)), 1/L, ε/(2‖∇f(x_k)‖²)} yields linear convergence to any ε-tolerance in O(log(1/ε)) iterations, with the sample size N_k chosen so that E[(1−q)^{N_k/2}] ≤ ε/((B₀+‖x*‖)M_f). For the convex possibly nonsmooth case, the paper introduces DoWS (Distance over Weighted Subgradients), a fully parameter-free variant of DoWG that estimates the set diameter online and sets α_k = r̄_k²/√p_k, achieving O(1/√T) worst-case rate without any problem-specific constants. A tamed variant (T-DoWS) further removes the boundedness assumption on Y by scaling stepsizes with logarithmic factors and relying only on sub-level set boundedness of f, at the cost of an additional ln(T) factor in the convergence bound.

## Results

The paper establishes linear convergence to any prescribed ε-tolerance for strongly convex, Lipschitz-smooth objectives using adaptive step sizes (Theorem 4.4), improving upon the O(1/T) rate of prior randomized feasibility methods. For convex but possibly nonsmooth objectives, the fully parameter-free DoWS method achieves an O(1/√T) worst-case rate in expectation (Theorem 5.3), while T-DoWS removes the boundedness assumption on Y at the cost of an additional ln(T) factor (Theorem 5.5). In simulations on QCQP (m=1000 constraints, n=10), SVM (three datasets), and logistic regression with group fairness constraints, the proposed algorithms match or outperform baselines in objective convergence while remaining competitive on infeasibility reduction, with infeasibility decaying geometrically in N_k.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Nedić & Necoara (2019), Algorithm 2 achieves linear convergence in the strongly convex case (i) versus their O(1/T) diminishing-stepsize rate, while Algorithms 3 and 4 match or exceed their performance in the nonsmooth and unknown-f* cases without requiring knowledge of μ or L. Compared to Arrow-Hurwicz (He et al., 2022), the proposed methods converge faster on the logistic regression fairness problem and avoid the mild oscillations observed in Arrow-Hurwicz on QCQP; on SVM, Arrow-Hurwicz's fixed step-size rules fail to converge entirely, whereas the adaptive methods succeed. Alt-GDA (Zhang et al., 2022a) shows similar oscillatory behavior to Arrow-Hurwicz on QCQP and also fails to converge on SVM. ACVI (Yang et al., 2022) attains the fastest infeasibility reduction on QCQP but incurs the highest computational cost and requires careful hyperparameter tuning to avoid divergence. CVXPY and IPOPT serve as reference solvers but fail on high-dimensional QCQP instances (m=1000) and large constraint counts, limiting their use as baselines in those settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-studied lines of work: randomized feasibility algorithms (Polyak 2001; Nedić 2010; Nedić & Necoara 2019) that handle hard-to-project constraints via subsampled subgradient steps, and parameter-free adaptive step-size methods (DoWG by Khaled et al. 2023; DoG by Ivgi et al. 2023) that eliminate the need for problem-specific hyperparameters. The key departure is that prior randomized feasibility methods required either known problem parameters (strong convexity constant, smoothness constant) or diminishing step sizes yielding O(1/T) rates, while prior adaptive step-size methods assumed projection onto the full feasible set. This paper is the first to combine DoWG/DoWS with randomized feasibility updates, and it also provides a clean theoretical connection between the global error bound constant and Lagrange dual multipliers under the Strong Slater condition, building on Lewis & Pang (1998) and Bertsekas (1999).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in providing the first fully parameter-free, projection-free method for large-scale constrained convex optimization, which is directly relevant to fairness-constrained machine learning, robust optimization, and safety-critical control where constraints are numerous and projection is intractable. The linear convergence guarantee for strongly convex objectives (versus the O(1/T) rate of prior randomized feasibility methods) is a meaningful theoretical improvement. Open questions include whether the error bound assumption (Assumption 2.2) can be relaxed or verified automatically, whether the ln(T) factor in the T-DoWS analysis is tight, and whether these ideas extend to non-convex or stochastic settings. The method's reliance on a random number of feasibility samples per iteration introduces a tunable trade-off between convergence speed and per-iteration cost that may be hard to optimize in practice.
