## Problem

Existing 3DGS acceleration methods uniformly rasterize and backpropagate over all pixels each iteration, incurring substantial redundant computation in well-reconstructed regions while aggressive primitive pruning sacrifices fine details. The paper asks whether optimization capacity can be allocated adaptively according to per-pixel reconstruction difficulty rather than uniformly across the image plane, to achieve both speed and fidelity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260948/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

TurboGS consumes calibrated multi-view RGB images, their camera intrinsics/extrinsics, and an SfM (COLMAP) sparse point cloud as initialization, and outputs a compact set of anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives (position, scale, rotation, opacity, SH color coefficients) that support real-time novel-view rendering via differentiable rasterization. A key operational detail is that during training, only a sparse subset of pixels per 16×16 tile is rasterized and supervised each iteration (controlled by an adaptive sampling rate r_t), so the effective per-iteration computation scales with reconstruction difficulty rather than image resolution. The final model is notably more compact than vanilla 3DGS (e.g., 0.64M vs. 2.73M Gaussians on Mip-NeRF 360) while training in ~77 seconds.

## Method

TurboGS maintains persistent per-pixel error maps (updated via EMA on sampled pixels) and age maps for each training view, and at each iteration performs tile-wise sparse pixel sampling that splits each tile's budget into error-prior 'hard' pixels (multinomial sampling proportional to error) and age-prior 'stable' pixels (top-K by staleness), combined with geometry-aware view selection that anchors on the highest-error view and samples nearby cameras. On the sparse pixel set, a tile-level Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC) loss with gradient-norm-balanced weighting provides structure-aware supervision beyond pixel-wise L1, preserving local texture consistency. Sparse multi-view pixel errors are back-projected onto Gaussians via compositing weights to compute per-Gaussian difficulty scores (error + distance), which drive targeted densification and pruning without requiring post-hoc dense error maps. Finally, a moment-damped Levenberg-Marquardt solver forms small per-Gaussian local systems (J^T J + diag(√v + ε))Δ = −m, solved via Cholesky decomposition, coupling Hessian-informed curvature with Adam second-moment damping to stabilize convergence under sparse, non-uniform gradients.

## Results

TurboGS completes training in 77 s on Mip-NeRF 360, 73 s on Tanks & Temples, and 62 s on Deep Blending, achieving a ~10–14× speedup over vanilla 3DGS (891/583/921 s) while matching or exceeding its PSNR (27.57 vs 27.55, 23.79 vs 23.84, 30.00 vs 29.85). The extended TurboGS-Big variant (168/146/132 s) further attains the best PSNR on Mip-NeRF 360 (27.99) and Deep Blending (30.26), surpassing all baselines including ConeGS (27.74) and DashGaussian (27.69). The final Gaussian count is reduced to 0.64 M (vs. 2.73 M for vanilla 3DGS), and rendering speed reaches up to 231 FPS on Deep Blending.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TurboGS is the fastest method across all three main benchmarks (77/73/62 s), outpacing the next-fastest FastGS (111/91/81 s) by 1.2–1.4× and vanilla 3DGS by 10–14×. On PSNR, TurboGS matches or slightly exceeds vanilla 3DGS on Mip-NeRF 360 (27.57 vs 27.55) and Deep Blending (30.00 vs 29.85), while TurboGS-Big clearly surpasses all baselines (27.99 / 24.14 / 30.26). Against the closest fast competitors, TurboGS-Big beats DashGaussian (27.99 vs 27.69) and ConeGS (27.99 vs 27.74) on Mip-NeRF 360 PSNR, and beats FastGS on Tanks & Temples (24.14 vs 24.06). TurboGS trails ConeGS and DashGaussian by 0.1–0.2 dB PSNR in the fast setting, and its SSIM/LPIPS are slightly worse than dense-training baselines (e.g., SSIM 0.794 vs 0.816 for 3DGS on Mip-NeRF 360), which the authors attribute to pixel-wise ℓ₁ supervision aligning more closely with PSNR. In terms of model compactness, TurboGS uses the fewest Gaussians (0.64 M) among all methods on Mip-NeRF 360, compared to 2.73 M for vanilla 3DGS and 3.35 M for 3DGS-LM.

## Relation to Prior Work

TurboGS builds directly on the 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) representation and differentiable rasterization pipeline (Kerbl et al., 2023), and adopts the per-Gaussian splat-parallel backward mechanism from Taming-3DGS (Mallick et al., 2024) to restrict gradient computation to sampled pixels. Conceptually, it transplants the sparse ray sampling idea from NeRF (Mildenhall et al., 2020) into the 3DGS pixel-space setting, but departs from prior 3DGS acceleration work (FastGS, DashGaussian, Speedy-Splat, 3DGS-LM) by jointly addressing pixel-level computation allocation, structure-aware sparse supervision, error-driven density control, and a hybrid LM-Adam optimizer within a single coherent framework rather than optimizing one bottleneck in isolation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TurboGS delivers a practical ~10× training speedup over vanilla 3DGS (77 s vs. 891 s) while maintaining competitive PSNR across multiple benchmarks, making fast 3DGS optimization viable for consumer-facing and time-sensitive applications. The error-guided sparse sampling principle is broadly applicable and could inform other explicit-representation methods. However, the paper acknowledges limitations: subtle rippling artifacts can appear in low-texture regions under sparse supervision, and storing per-pixel error/age maps may become a memory bottleneck at extreme resolutions (e.g., 4K+), requiring multi-GPU partitioning or GPU-CPU offloading. Extending the framework to dynamic scenes with spatiotemporal error aggregation remains open.
