## Problem

Recent benchmarks show that an untrained mean baseline—predicting the average expression of all perturbed cells—frequently matches or surpasses sophisticated perturbation response models under standard metrics like MSE and Pearson(Δ). The paper traces this anomaly to signal dilution: true perturbation effects are confined to a tiny fraction of genes (e.g., ~0.05% in Replogle22), so unweighted transcriptome-wide metrics systematically reward degenerate mean-like predictions and obscure genuine model performance.

## Input / Output

Models consume a control cell expression profile (pseudobulked, log1p-transformed, ~8192 highly variable genes) and a perturbation gene identity (single gene or gene combination) to produce a predicted perturbed expression profile of the same dimensionality. The evaluation framework additionally computes per-perturbation DEG weights from t-scores against all other perturbations (not the control), min-max normalized, squared, and rescaled to sum to one; these weights modulate both the WMSE and R_w²(Δ) scores and can be injected directly into the training loss. A technical duplicate baseline—splitting each perturbation's cells in half and predicting one half from the other—serves as a positive performance ceiling.

## Method

The paper introduces two DEG-aware metrics: WMSE, a gene-weighted mean-squared error that amplifies error on perturbation-specific DEGs, and R_w²(Δ), a weighted delta R² that references the mean of all perturbed cells (eliminating control bias) and guarantees strictly negative scores for constant mean predictions. Weights are derived from per-perturbation t-scores computed against all other perturbations (DEGs vs Rest) rather than the control, avoiding the systematic control bias that inflates Pearson(Δ). A three-tier baseline scheme (control mean as negative, dataset mean as null, technical duplicate as positive) calibrates the metric scale so that the mean baseline sinks to null performance. As a training objective, WMSE replaces unweighted MSE in the loss function, reshaping the optimization landscape to penalize mode collapse; the paper demonstrates this reduces variance collapse (from 14.5% to 40.6% variance recovery in GEARS on Replogle22) and improves performance across GEARS, scGPT, scLambda, and an MLP baseline, with gains scaling with perturbation strength.

## Results

On the Replogle22 unseen-gene prediction task, WMSE-trained GEARS significantly outperforms MSE-trained GEARS on Pearson(Δ) restricted to perturbation-specific DEGs (t=10.66, p=5.32×10⁻²⁵) and Normalized Inverse Rank (t=10.25, p=8.86×10⁻²⁴). Gene-wise variance recovery improves from 14.51% (MSE) to 40.56% (WMSE), demonstrating reduced mode collapse. WMSE as an evaluation metric shows 3.26× (Replogle22) and 9.65× (Norman19) larger fold reductions in error for a minimally modified mean baseline versus standard MSE (1.14× and 1.65×), confirming its sensitivity to sparse DEG signals. Across three additional architectures (FMLP, scGPT, scLambda), WMSE training yields consistent gains that correlate with perturbation strength, with the largest improvements on the top-10% strongest perturbations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper re-trains three perturbation-response architectures (GEARS, scGPT, scLambda) with WMSE loss and compares them against their original MSE-trained versions. GEARS (Roohini et al., 2024) shows the most dramatic improvement: on Replogle22, WMSE training lifts Pearson(Δ) on DEGs with t=10.66 (p=5.32×10⁻²⁵) and NIR with t=10.25 (p=8.86×10⁻²⁴), while recovering 40.56% of ground-truth gene-wise variance versus only 14.51% for MSE training. scGPT (Cui et al., 2024) benefits most on strong perturbations, where WMSE training nearly doubles Pearson(Δ)DEGs from 0.39 to 0.71 in the top-10% DEG quantile. scLambda (Wang et al., 2024) shows consistent but more moderate gains (Pearson(Δ)DEGs 0.38→0.45 unfiltered; 0.28→0.37 top-10%). Across all three architectures, WMSE training wins on the orthogonal (non-weighted) metrics Pearson(Δ)DEGs and NIR for the strongest perturbations, confirming the gains are not merely an artifact of metric–loss alignment. The technical duplicate baseline (splitting each perturbation's cells in half) consistently outperforms all models, establishing a realistic performance ceiling that unweighted metrics fail to rank correctly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper responds directly to a wave of benchmarking studies (Li et al. 2024a, Csendes et al. 2025, Wenteler et al. 2025, Ahlmann-Eltze et al. 2025) that reported the untrained mean baseline matching or surpassing sophisticated perturbation-response models. While Viñas Torné et al. (2025) identified control-cell bias as one inflating factor for Pearson(Δ), this work identifies signal dilution—biological changes confined to a tiny fraction of genes—as the dominant driver across both correlation-based and error-based metrics. Rather than proposing a new architecture, the paper repurposes the well-known idea of weighted regression loss for the specific structure of sparse perturbation signals, and introduces a technical-duplicate positive baseline conceptually related to TxPert's held-out replicate approach.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The reframe from 'models fail to learn perturbation effects' to 'metrics are blind to the sparse effects models do learn' has practical consequences: it means existing model architectures may be more capable than previously reported, and a simple drop-in WMSE loss can unlock that capability without architectural changes. The calibrated three-baseline protocol (negative μ^c, null μ^all, positive technical duplicate) provides a reusable evaluation scaffold for the field. Open questions include whether the DEG-vs-Rest weighting scheme is optimal versus alternatives (e.g., learned weights), whether the approach extends to spatial or multi-omics perturbation data, and whether the two-dataset evidence generalizes to the larger scPerturb collection. The paper's scope is limited to two real datasets and four architectures, so broader validation is needed before WMSE becomes a default training objective.
