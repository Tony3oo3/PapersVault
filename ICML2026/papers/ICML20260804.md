## Problem

Regression with metric space-valued outputs (distributions, networks, SPD matrices) becomes intractable in high dimensions: global Fréchet regression is too restrictive, local Fréchet regression suffers from the curse of dimensionality, and existing deep learning approaches for metric outputs rely on multi-stage pipelines that decouple representation learning from the Fréchet regression objective, sacrificing interpretability. No prior method jointly learns a flexible, interpretable low-dimensional projection and performs local Fréchet regression in an end-to-end framework.

## Input / Output

The method takes a multivariate predictor vector X ∈ R^p (tabular covariates) and produces a conditional Fréchet mean in a general metric space (Ω, d), instantiated as SPD matrices under the log-Cholesky metric, graph Laplacians under the Frobenius metric, univariate distributions under the 2-Wasserstein metric, or compositional vectors under the geodesic metric on the positive orthant of the unit sphere. Beyond the predicted object, DeSI also yields an interpretable index direction θ̂ ∈ S^{p-1} whose components quantify the relative importance of each predictor. The bandwidth h for local smoothing is treated as a learnable parameter rather than a fixed hyperparameter.

## Method

DeSI couples two modules in a unified end-to-end objective: (1) a deep neural network with Leaky ReLU activations whose final-layer output is normalized to unit length, producing a subject-specific direction θ̂(x) and scalar index Z = x^⊤θ̂(x); and (2) local Fréchet regression (LFR) applied along this one-dimensional index with a jointly optimized bandwidth h. The training loss combines the Fréchet-variance-normalized prediction error with a λ/h bandwidth penalty, and both the network parameters β and h are updated simultaneously via gradient-based optimization with early stopping. A global index direction is recovered by computing the intrinsic mean of the subject-specific directions on the unit sphere. The paper establishes uniform approximation guarantees for the learned index and derives convergence rates for the overall estimator, showing that for common metric spaces (SPD, networks, distributions) the rate reduces to O_p(h^{-2}b_n + h^2 + (nh)^{-1/2}).

## Results

Across all simulation settings (SPD matrices, networks, and univariate distributions under linear, quadratic, and exponential links), DeSI achieves the lowest mean prediction error (MPE) at every sample size. At n=1000, DeSI's MPE ranges from 0.0057 (SPD) to 0.0892 (quadratic distributional), consistently outperforming all baselines. On the New Jersey compositional mood data (n=2284), DeSI achieves an MPE of 0.4658, a ~6% reduction over IFR (0.4947) and a ~34% reduction over GFR (0.6229). DeSI also recovers the true index direction θ₀ with substantially lower L₂ error than IFR across all settings (e.g., 0.0023 vs. 0.1744 for networks at n=1000).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DeSI outperforms all three baselines across every simulation setting and the real-data application. Against Global Fréchet Regression (GFR, Petersen & Müller 2019), DeSI achieves substantially lower MPE in all settings; on the New Jersey mood data the gap is ~34% (0.4658 vs. 0.6229), and in the network simulation at n=1000 the gap is over 10× (0.0531 vs. 0.5663). Against Single-Index Fréchet Regression (IFR, Bhattacharjee & Müller 2023), DeSI wins on both prediction accuracy and index-direction recovery; on the real data the MPE improvement is ~6% (0.4658 vs. 0.4947), and the L₂ error for θ₀ estimation is dramatically smaller (e.g., 0.0023 vs. 0.1744 for networks at n=1000). Against Deep Fréchet Regression (DFR, Iao et al. 2025), DeSI achieves lower MPE in all settings where DFR is available (networks and distributions), with the advantage widening as sample size grows (e.g., 0.0531 vs. 0.0591 for networks at n=1000). DFR is excluded from the SPD setting due to lack of a log-Cholesky implementation. In terms of computational cost, DeSI trains in seconds (1.2–5.9 s) compared to IFR's hours (~2–5 h) and DFR's tens of seconds to minutes (24–182 s), while GFR is slightly faster but far less flexible.

## Relation to Prior Work

DeSI sits at the intersection of three research threads: Fréchet regression for metric space-valued outputs (Petersen & Müller, 2019), single-index Fréchet regression with linear index directions (Bhattacharjee & Müller, 2023), and deep learning for structured outputs (Iao et al., 2025). The key departure is architectural: rather than using a fixed linear index (as in IFR) or a multi-stage pipeline where representation learning and Fréchet regression are trained separately (as in DFR), DeSI learns a nonlinear index via a DNN and performs local Fréchet regression along that index within a single end-to-end optimization objective. The errors-in-variables LFR analysis for estimated indices is inherited from Iao et al. (2025), while the single-index interpretability structure follows Bhattacharjee & Müller (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap: high-dimensional predictors in Fréchet regression suffer from the curse of dimensionality, and existing deep learning approaches sacrifice interpretability. By coupling a DNN-learned index with LFR in a unified objective, DeSI provides both the flexibility of deep learning and an interpretable direction vector quantifying predictor importance—something standard DNNs cannot offer. Open questions include whether the single-index assumption is sufficient for complex relationships (the authors acknowledge multi-index extensions as future work), how the method scales to very high-dimensional inputs, and whether the end-to-end bandwidth optimization remains stable in higher-dimensional index spaces. The computational advantage over IFR (seconds vs. hours) makes the approach practically accessible.
