## Problem

Most GNNs restrict information propagation to the predefined static adjacency matrix, failing to capture latent semantic relationships that exist between non-adjacent nodes and cannot adapt to noisy, incomplete, or heterophilic graph structures. Additionally, existing approaches lack a systematic integration of both low-pass (smoothness) and high-pass (discrepancy) spectral information, limiting their ability to model the full range of structural patterns in real-world graphs.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a node attribute feature matrix X ∈ R^{N×d} and a binary adjacency matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{N×N}, and produces per-node class probability distributions via a two-layer MLP with softmax. A key design detail is that the adjacency structure is not fixed: it is dynamically reconstructed at every layer through self-attention over node embeddings and convexly blended with the original topology, so the effective propagation graph evolves during forward pass. The final node embedding is a learnable weighted sum of representations from all L propagation layers plus the raw input features, rather than just the last-layer output.

## Method

DCD-GNN employs a dual-channel parallel architecture. The dynamic channel uses self-attention (query/key projections with softmax) to learn a fully connected semantic adjacency matrix, blends it with the original adjacency via a scalar λ, then splits propagation into complementary low-pass (normalized adjacency) and high-pass (I − low-pass) sub-channels whose outputs are fused with layer-specific learnable coefficients. The static channel performs standard GCN propagation on the original adjacency to preserve structural priors. Each channel applies multi-scale representation fusion—a learnable weighted sum over all intermediate layer embeddings plus the raw input—to mitigate over-smoothing. The two channel outputs are combined via a sigmoid-gated scalar θ, and the fused embedding is classified by a two-layer MLP under cross-entropy loss on the labeled node subset (semi-supervised setting). Theoretical analysis shows the multi-scale fusion is equivalent to an adaptive polynomial graph filter, and the dual-channel fusion yields error reduction when channel prediction errors are negatively correlated, which naturally occurs on heterophilic graphs.

## Results

DCD-GNN achieves the best node classification accuracy across all 8 benchmark datasets, with an average improvement of 1.83% over the strongest baselines. On homophilic citation networks, it reaches 90.38% (Cora), 82.43% (Citeseer), and 90.87% (Pubmed). On heterophilic graphs, gains are more pronounced (average +2.31%), with standout results of 98.13% on Wisconsin (outperforming PDE-GCN by 5.28%) and 94.10% on Texas. The method also demonstrates robustness to depth, maintaining stable performance up to 6 layers without severe over-smoothing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DCD-GNN outperforms all 19 baselines on every dataset. On homophilic graphs, the margins are modest but consistent: 90.38% on Cora vs. DSF-Jacobi-R (89.66%), 82.43% on Citeseer vs. Half-Hop (80.33%), and 90.87% on Pubmed vs. NoSAF-D (90.62%). On heterophilic graphs, the advantages are substantially larger, particularly on Wisconsin where DCD-GNN reaches 98.13% versus PDE-GCN's 92.85% (+5.28%) and on Chameleon at 78.36% versus DSF-Jacobi-R's 75.00% (+3.36%). Classic spectral methods (GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE) perform poorly on heterophilic graphs, sometimes falling below the MLP baseline that ignores topology entirely. Among the strongest recent competitors—DSF-Jacobi, PDE-GCN, EvenNet, and LHS—DCD-GNN consistently leads by 1–5%, with the gap widening as graph heterophily increases, confirming that the dual-channel dynamic/static design and multi-scale fusion provide complementary benefits that single-channel or fixed-topology approaches cannot match.

## Relation to Prior Work

DCD-GNN builds directly on the GCN spectral convolution operator (Kipf & Welling, 2017) for its static channel and on Transformer self-attention (Vaswani et al., 2017) for its dynamic adjacency learning, combining these two well-established mechanisms into a parallel dual-channel architecture. It departs from prior heterophily-focused methods (LHS, NoSAF, OrderedGNN) that modify message passing on the fixed topology by instead reconstructing the adjacency itself via learned attention, and from multi-scale fusion approaches (JKNet) by applying layer-wise weighted aggregation within each channel rather than across a single propagation path. The explicit low-pass/high-pass spectral decomposition within the dynamic channel draws on the spectral filtering literature (EvenNet, DSF-Jacobi, GPRGNN) but applies it to a dynamically learned adjacency rather than the original graph.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a practical and interpretable recipe—self-attention adjacency blended with the original topology, split into complementary spectral sub-channels, fused with a static GCN branch—that consistently improves node classification on both homophilic and heterophilic benchmarks. However, the self-attention adjacency computation is O(N²), which limits scalability to large-scale graphs (tens of thousands of nodes or more), and all experiments are confined to node classification on eight relatively small datasets, leaving open whether the approach transfers to link prediction, graph-level tasks, or industrial-scale graphs. The theoretical error-complementarity argument, while correct, is standard ensemble theory and does not provide tight guarantees on when the negative-correlation condition actually holds in practice.
