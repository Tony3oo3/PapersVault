## Problem

Vector-quantized variational autoencoders (VQ-VAEs) compress images into discrete tokens but are notoriously difficult to train due to the non-differentiable encoding step, codebook collapse, and the need for numerous training tricks (commitment loss, Gumbel-Softmax, entropy regularization). This paper asks whether one can obtain a VQ-VAE without training the quantization step at all, by converting a well-trained continuous Gaussian VAE into a discrete one.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265298/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 256×256 RGB images (reconstruction) or 128×128 images with class labels (generation) and produces a sequence of discrete codebook indices (tokens) plus a reconstructed or generated image. The codebook is not learned: it is a fixed set of K one-dimensional (or m-dimensional) samples drawn from N(0,1), generated once and shared across all latent dimensions. Quantization is deterministic—each posterior mean is mapped to its nearest codebook entry—so no stochastic sampling or straight-through estimator is needed at inference time.

## Method

Gaussian Quant (GQ) operates in two stages. First, a Gaussian VAE is trained under a Target Divergence Constraint (TDC) that uses three adaptively updated Lagrange multipliers (λ_min, λ_mean, λ_max) to force each latent dimension's KL divergence R_i to stay within [log K − α, log K + α], ensuring uniform per-dimension bitrate. Second, the trained VAE is converted to a VQ-VAE with zero additional training: a codebook of K Gaussian noise vectors is sampled once, and each posterior mean μ_i is quantized to its nearest codebook entry (with σ_i-weighted distance for multi-dimensional codebooks). Theoretically, the paper proves that when log K matches the bits-back coding rate R_i, the probability of a large quantization error (|ẑ_i − μ_i| ≥ σ_i) decays doubly exponentially in the excess bitrate, providing a principled codebook-size guideline. For low-bitrate multi-dimensional codebooks, a regularization term ω‖c_j‖ prevents codebook collapse by encouraging selection of codewords farther from the origin.

## Results

On ImageNet at 0.25 bpp with a UNet backbone, GQ achieves PSNR 27.61 dB, LPIPS 0.059, SSIM 0.807, and rFID 0.529, outperforming the strongest baseline BSQ (PSNR 25.62, rFID 1.080) by ~2 dB in PSNR and halving rFID. At 1.00 bpp, GQ reaches PSNR 32.47 dB and rFID 0.322 (UNet), approaching the continuous Gaussian VAE upper bound (PSNR 32.73, rFID 0.490) with only a 0.26 dB gap. For autoregressive generation on ImageNet 128×128, GQ achieves 100% codebook usage, gFID 7.67, and IS 230.79, surpassing FSQ (gFID 7.33, IS 224.88) in IS and matching it in gFID while using a simpler training pipeline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GQ outperforms all four directly-trained VQ-VAE baselines (VQGAN, FSQ, LFQ, BSQ) on every reconstruction metric at all tested bitrates and both architectures; the margin is largest at low bitrate (0.25 bpp), where GQ gains ~2 dB PSNR and reduces rFID by 2–30× over VQGAN/BSQ/LFQ. Against pre-trained VQ-VAEs, GQ (40 epochs, 87M params) beats FlowMo-Hi (300 epochs, 945M params) on rFID (0.491 vs. 0.560) and PSNR (25.31 vs. 24.93), and outperforms Llama-Gen-32 on rFID (0.491 vs. 0.590) with fewer parameters. For the conversion-based baselines, GQ substantially improves over TokenBridge (rFID 0.280 vs. 0.823 at 1.00 bpp) and ReVQ-256T (rFID 1.04 vs. 2.05 at 0.07 bpp). In the generation setting, GQ achieves the highest codebook usage (100%) and best IS (230.79), slightly trailing FSQ on gFID (7.67 vs. 7.33) but surpassing it in IS and codebook utilization. Among stochastic conversion alternatives (MRC, ORC, A* coding), GQ achieves marginally better PSNR (32.11 vs. 32.09) and rFID (0.414 vs. 0.419–0.425) with simpler O(R_i) encoding complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

GQ sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) VQ-VAE training, which requires non-differentiable quantization workarounds like STE, commitment loss, and Gumbel-Softmax; (2) Gaussian VAEs with bits-back coding, where the KL divergence per dimension has a clean information-theoretic interpretation; and (3) post-training conversion methods like TokenBridge and ReVQ, which discretize a pre-trained Gaussian VAE without retraining. GQ's key departure is that it does not convert an unconstrained Gaussian VAE (as TokenBridge does) but instead trains the VAE under a per-dimension KL constraint (TDC) so that each dimension's bits-back rate matches the target codebook bitrate before conversion. The conversion itself—nearest-neighbor lookup against a fixed codebook of random 1-D Gaussian samples—is closely related to reverse channel coding (MRC, ORC) but replaces stochastic sampling with deterministic quantization, which is what a VQ-VAE requires.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GQ offers a practically attractive simplification: it eliminates STE, codebook loss, commitment loss, and entropy loss entirely, replacing them with a standard Gaussian VAE training loop plus a one-time conversion step. The theoretical guarantee (Theorems 3.1–3.2) that quantization error decays doubly exponentially when log K exceeds the bits-back rate gives a principled, data-free rule for codebook size selection. Limitations include the use of a single-scale architecture (no multi-scale or residual VQ), a reconstruction-only evaluation (the paper acknowledges that strong reconstruction does not guarantee strong generation), and additional hyperparameters (α, β, ω) that add implementation complexity. Open questions include whether GQ extends to multi-scale/residual tokenizers, whether TDC-constrained latents transfer well to downstream autoregressive or diffusion generation, and whether the Gaussian-noise codebook can be replaced by structured alternatives for even lower bitrates.
