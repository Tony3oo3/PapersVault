## Problem

Dense voxel-level segmentation scales with image resolution rather than structural complexity, and extreme class imbalance (tumors occupying <1% of volume) attenuates gradient signals precisely where accurate delineation is most critical. Existing compression strategies—patch-based inference, fixed-grid downsampling, and task-agnostic superpixels—couple inference to the native lattice and discard boundary evidence before models can leverage it, with no principled mechanism to adapt the representation to target-specific semantic structure.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a volumetric CT or MRI image (e.g., 240×240×155 for BraTS, 512×512×variable for KiTS/LiTS) along with 5–20 few-shot labeled examples used solely to optimize minor-construction parameters via black-box search. It produces a voxel-level segmentation mask via exact bijective lifting from supernode predictions, with no interpolation or approximation. Each target structure is handled as a separate binary classification problem on the induced graph, and the intermediate representation is a compact graph minor with ~10³ supernodes (a 3–4 order-of-magnitude reduction from ~10⁷ voxels).

## Method

SEMIR constructs a graph minor H⪯G from the N-connected voxel lattice through three parameterized operations: seed-anchored edge contraction (flood-fill merging voxels within intensity threshold ψ), node deletion (pruning supernodes violating area/intensity bounds β), and edge deletion (severing connections across gradients exceeding α). The parameter set Θ={ψ,α,β} is optimized via SMBO with an ExtraTrees surrogate to maximize boundary Dice agreement between induced supernode boundaries and ground-truth semantic edges on a few-shot subset, framing the problem as representation learning over a family of graph homomorphisms rather than hyperparameter tuning within a fixed architecture. Supernodes are annotated with scale- and rotation-invariant geometric descriptors (area, elongation, compactness, dominant axis) and intensity statistics, while edges carry log-ratio and normalized-difference features; a 3-layer GINE then performs node classification on the reduced graph. Predictions are mapped back to the full voxel grid through the exact bijection encoded in an expanded tensor T, guaranteeing consistent labeling within each supernode without boundary artifacts.

## Results

On BraTS 2021, SEMIR achieves the best ET Dice (0.894) and near-best TC Dice (0.941, vs. GTMamba's 0.940), while remaining competitive on WT (0.920). On KiTS23, SEMIR attains the best tumor-only Dice (T=0.819) and tumor+cyst Dice (TC=0.861), outperforming the prior best (Alonso: T=0.803, TC=0.856). On LiTS, SEMIR achieves the best tumor Dice (T=0.891) versus DefED-Net (0.875). In the controlled binary target-vs-rest comparison against nnUNet (Table 4), SEMIR improves minority-target Dice by 0.082–0.158 across all four targets while reducing training time from 11–43 hours to 0.6–2.5 hours on a 16 GB T4 GPU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SEMIR's primary advantage is on minority-structure targets: it achieves the best ET Dice on BraTS (0.894 vs. GTMamba's 0.884), the best tumor-only Dice on KiTS (0.819 vs. Alonso's 0.803), and the best tumor Dice on LiTS (0.891 vs. DefED-Net's 0.875). On aggregate/organ-inclusive metrics, SEMIR is competitive but not always best: WT on BraTS (0.920 vs. PSO-UNet's 0.958), KTC on KiTS (0.945 vs. Alonso's 0.958), and LT on LiTS (0.959 vs. Lgma-net's 0.977). In the controlled head-to-head against nnUNet under identical splits (Table 4), SEMIR wins on all four targets by 0.082–0.158 Dice while training 5–18× faster on a 16 GB T4 GPU versus A100-class hardware. The paper notes that multi-class methods like SwinUNETR suffer severe minority-target degradation (e.g., KiTS tumor Dice of 0.343 despite 0.762 aggregate), a failure mode SEMIR avoids by reducing each task to binary segmentation on a target-adapted graph. The few-shot parameter optimization (5–20 labeled examples) outperforms fixed manual thresholds by 5–16 Dice points on BraTS ET, confirming the data-driven minor construction is essential.

## Relation to Prior Work

SEMIR repurposes the formal graph minor framework from combinatorics (edge contraction, node deletion, edge deletion) as a learned image regionization mechanism, departing from classical superpixel methods (SLIC, Felzenszwalb) that are task-agnostic and manually tuned, and from differentiable pooling approaches (MinCutPool, DMoN) that lack exact lifting guarantees. The downstream predictor is a standard GINE, but the key departure is that the graph topology itself is learned via few-shot black-box optimization over a small parameter set, rather than being fixed by a predetermined regionization. Conceptually, the paper inverts the typical bottom-up compression paradigm (patching, downsampling, fixed superpixels) into a top-down, structure-adaptive construction where inference complexity scales with semantic boundary complexity rather than image resolution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10^4× reduction in inference nodes (from ~10^7 voxels to ~10^3 supernodes) with exact voxel-level lifting offers a practically significant efficiency path for high-resolution volumetric segmentation, particularly in resource-constrained clinical settings. The few-shot parameter optimization (5–20 examples) replacing manual threshold tuning is a useful practical contribution, though its sensitivity to the few-shot set's boundary statistics remains an open concern. Key limitations include the lack of end-to-end joint optimization between minor construction and the GNN, the absence of global optimality guarantees, and evaluation restricted to volumetric CT/MRI with only one controlled baseline (nnUNet) under identical splits. Extending the framework to histopathology, ultrasound, or 2D settings, and closing the gap between the learned minor and the downstream predictor through differentiable or joint optimization, are natural next steps.
