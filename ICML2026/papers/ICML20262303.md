## Problem

Lloyd's k-means algorithm is known to struggle in high-dimensional settings, but no prior work provided a rigorous explanation for this failure. This paper proves that in a high-noise, high-dimensional regime (d ≳ n³, σ² > n), with high probability essentially every approximately balanced partition is already a fixed point of Lloyd's update map, so the algorithm halts after one iteration and returns its initialization regardless of how trivially recoverable the true clusters are. The paper further shows that Hartigan's greedy k-means variant avoids this pathology entirely.

## Input / Output

The theoretical analysis considers n samples in ℝ^d drawn from a two-component isotropic Gaussian mixture model (K=2) with random means μ₁*, μ₂* ~ N(0, τ²I_d) and noise ξ_i ~ N(0, σ²I_d). The algorithmic state is a bipartition of [n] into two nonempty clusters (not just centroids), and the key output of the analysis is a probability bound on whether a given partition is a fixed point of the update map. Experiments extend to K ∈ {2, 5, 10} clusters and evaluate on synthetic GMM data as well as real datasets (Olivetti faces at 4096-d, Amazon reviews at 10000-d, 20 Newsgroups at 5000-d), reporting NMI and k-means loss.

## Method

The core mechanism is a distance-distribution analysis: under the isotropic GMM, the squared distance from a sample to its current cluster centroid and to the other cluster centroid are each distributed as scaled χ²_d variables, with scale factors α_cur and α_alt that depend on cluster purity and size. A Chernoff-based tail bound (Lemma 3.3) then controls the probability that the sample is closer to the wrong centroid. For Lloyd's algorithm, the critical insight is the 'self-influence' effect: because the current centroid includes the sample itself, a misassigned sample is pulled toward its own (wrong) centroid by O(1/|C_j|), and in the high-noise, high-dim regime this dominates the weak class-separation signal, making the switch probability decay as ρ^{d/4} with ρ < 1. Union-bounding over all n samples and all 2^n approximately balanced partitions yields the fixed-point proliferation result. For Hartigan's algorithm, the weighted distance Δ_H² (which rescales by |C_j|/(|C_j|±1) to account for the centroid shift upon reassignment) eliminates the self-influence bias, so the same χ²-difference machinery shows that incorrect partitions are NOT fixed points with high probability, guaranteeing convergence to the correct clustering.

## Results

Theoretically, in the high-noise, high-dimensional regime (σ² > n, d ≳ n³), the paper proves that with high probability every approximately balanced partition is a fixed point of Lloyd's algorithm, so it returns its initialization unchanged, while Hartigan's algorithm has no incorrect fixed points and converges to the correct partition. Empirically, on real-world datasets with d/n ratios up to 10.2 (e.g., Amazon: d=10000, n=1500), Hartigan's k-means achieves the lowest k-means loss on all six datasets (e.g., 1356.3 vs. Lloyd's 1386.8 on Amazon) and the highest NMI on 5 of 6 datasets (e.g., NMI 0.52 vs. Lloyd's 0.42 on Amazon; 0.54 vs. 0.26 on 20NG-A). Lloyd's algorithm exhibits a pronounced failure region in the high-noise, high-dimensional regime, with NMI dropping to 0.23–0.26 on the 20 Newsgroups subsets, while Hartigan's maintains 0.29–0.54.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Hartigan's k-means outperforms Lloyd's k-means on every dataset for both k-means loss and NMI, with the gap widening as the d/n ratio increases (e.g., NMI 0.54 vs. 0.26 on 20NG-A where d/n=25). Against the SDP relaxation (Mixon et al., 2016), Hartigan's algorithm achieves higher NMI on all six datasets (e.g., 0.52 vs. 0.44 on Amazon; 0.54 vs. 0.18 on 20NG-A) while also producing lower k-means loss, and it scales better since SDP operates on n×n matrices. Against spectral clustering (Ng et al., 2001), Hartigan's algorithm wins on 5 of 6 datasets in NMI (e.g., 0.52 vs. 0.38 on Amazon; 0.44 vs. 0.37 on 20NG-B) but loses on Olivetti faces (0.80 vs. 0.83), where the paper notes spectral clustering optimizes a different objective. PCA+k-means (Ding & He, 2004) is evaluated only in the synthetic GMM experiments (Figure 1), where it substantially improves Lloyd's accuracy but still does not match Hartigan's performance in the high-noise, high-dimensional regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of work on k-means that characterizes conditions under which Lloyd's algorithm *succeeds* at recovering ground-truth clusters (Lu & Zhou, 2016; Gao & Zhang, 2022; Ndaoud, 2022; Chen & Yang, 2021), instead identifying a broad high-noise, high-dimensional regime where Lloyd's algorithm *fundamentally fails*: with high probability, essentially every partition is already a fixed point, so the algorithm makes zero progress from any initialization. It builds on the known structural relationship between Lloyd's and Hartigan's fixed points (Telgarsky & Vattani, 2010; Slonim et al., 2013) but provides the first concrete high-dimensional regime where this subset relationship translates into a stark algorithmic separation. The analysis also connects to the well-known empirical observation that k-means degrades in high dimensions (Hartigan, 1975; Steinley, 2006; Zha et al., 2001; Ding & He, 2004) by giving a precise mechanistic explanation rooted in the self-influence of a sample on its own cluster centroid.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides a rigorous theoretical explanation for one of the most practically important failure modes in data analysis: why the single most widely used clustering algorithm becomes a no-op in high-dimensional, high-noise settings. The finding that Hartigan's algorithm—a simple greedy variant requiring only per-sample reassignment—avoids the pathology and performs comparably to spectral clustering and SDP relaxations offers a low-cost practical alternative. Key open questions include extending the theory to K > 2 clusters, tightening the conservative dimension thresholds (d ≳ n³), understanding the analogous failure of EM in the same regime, and characterizing how the fixed-point proliferation weakens as noise or dimension decreases. The bounds are explicitly stated to be conservative, so the practical threshold is likely far lower than the theoretical one.
