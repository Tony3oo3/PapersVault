## Problem

Topological Machine Learning (TML) has strong theoretical appeal for classification but remains underused by the broader ML community because empirical evaluations lack a consistent framework. The construction of topological signatures is often opaque, statistical significance testing is absent, and computational costs and robustness to perturbations are rarely reported, making cross-study comparisons difficult and reported gains hard to interpret.

## Input / Output

The paper examines TML pipelines that consume heterogeneous inputs—point clouds of 1000 points in R², 28×28 grayscale images, and 3D meshes with fixed vertex-face structures—and produce persistence diagrams whose size, entropy, and computation time vary substantially depending on design choices. Downstream, these diagrams are vectorized via parameter-free methods (Persistence Statistics or Carlsson Coordinates) and fed to a fixed Random Forest classifier, yielding classification accuracy, end-to-end computation times, and Atienza's entropy as structural complexity measures.

## Method

Rather than proposing a new TML architecture, the paper conducts a controlled empirical study isolating the effect of each pipeline stage—filtering function, geometric structure (Alpha, Vietoris-Rips, DTM-Rips, cubical), filtration type, and post-processing—on diagram size, entropy, computation time, and downstream accuracy. To avoid confounding from hyperparameter tuning, two parameter-free vectorization methods are paired with a fixed Random Forest (100 trees) evaluated over 100 runs on 70/30 splits across Orbits, MNIST, and SHREC14, while scaling experiments on synthetic Gaussian point sets and noise grids quantify how PH computation cost grows with input resolution for different complexes. The paper then synthesizes these findings into a proposed set of experimental standards: explicit end-to-end pipeline reporting with hardware specifications, systematic computation-time measurement, unified evaluation metrics, and mandatory statistical testing (McNemar, paired t-test, or Wilcoxon signed-rank) for performance comparisons.

## Results

Across three data modalities, design choices produce dramatic variability in both computational cost and downstream accuracy. On Orbits point clouds, Alpha complexes compute in 0.01 s with 898 H₁ points, while DTM-Rips requires 173 s with only 182 H₁ points (Table 1). On MNIST, multi-filtering strategies (HRD) generate 36 persistence diagrams versus 2 for single-filtering (MCD), with computation times ranging from 3 ms to 25 ms per diagram (Table 2). Downstream accuracy with a fixed Random Forest (100 trees, 70/30 split, 100 runs) ranges from 37.3% to 88.2% depending on the pipeline configuration (Table 4), and 93.75% of pairwise p-values between the two vectorization methods (Persistence Statistics vs. Carlsson Coordinates) fall below 0.05, confirming that even identical processing steps yield statistically significant accuracy differences.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against external baselines; instead, it compares different pipeline configurations (filtration type, vectorization method, HKS parameters) to demonstrate that design choices materially affect outcomes. On Orbits, Alpha and Rips complexes yield comparable accuracy (88.53% vs. 86.52% for H₀+H₁ with Persistence Statistics), but differ by over 38× in computation time (0.01 s vs. 0.38 s). On SHREC14, the HKS time parameter t has a pronounced effect: t=5 achieves 87.67% (Persistence Statistics, H₀+H₁) while t=1 drops to 80.54%, and t=10 gives 86.49%; for Carlsson Coordinates, t=5 reaches 88.04% versus 60.14% at t=1. No single design choice is universally optimal across datasets or vectorization methods, and the two parameter-free vectorizations (Persistence Statistics vs. Carlsson Coordinates) produce statistically significant accuracy differences in 93.75% of tested configurations, underscoring the paper's central claim that without standardized reporting, cross-study comparisons in TML are unreliable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper sits at the intersection of the TDA/TML literature and the broader ML reproducibility movement. It builds on recent surveys that have begun to catalog the TML pipeline (Barnes et al., 2021; Conti et al., 2022; Ali et al., 2023; Bandiziol & De Marchi, 2024) but departs from them by shifting focus from methodological taxonomy to experimental protocol: rather than classifying vectorization or kernel methods, it quantifies how pipeline design choices (filtration type, complex structure, post-processing) alter computational cost, diagram structure, and downstream accuracy. The paper also draws on the NeurIPS 2019 reproducibility program and meta-studies on AI reproducibility (Gundersen & Kjensmo, 2018) to frame its call for standards within an established ML community discourse, applying those principles specifically to the TML setting where they have been largely absent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that TML cannot gain traction in the broader ML community without consolidated experimental standards—is well-supported by its empirical demonstrations that seemingly minor design choices (e.g., Alpha vs. Rips vs. DTM-Rips) produce 17,000× differences in computation time and statistically significant accuracy shifts under identical classifiers. The open question is whether the proposed standards (end-to-end timing, unified metrics, statistical testing) will be adopted by a community that values methodological diversity, and whether premature standardization might constrain the field's still-exploratory phase. For practitioners, the practical impact is a concrete checklist for making TML experiments comparable and reproducible, which could accelerate the field's integration into standard ML evaluation pipelines.
