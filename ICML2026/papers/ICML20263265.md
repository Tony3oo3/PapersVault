## Problem

Fine-tuned medical foundation models exhibit significant performance gaps across demographic subgroups, and one-shot federated learning (OFL), which could leverage cross-institutional diversity in a single communication round, suffers from severe model inconsistency under heterogeneous data distributions. Naive aggregation of divergent local models introduces parameter conflicts that further amplify subgroup disparity. No prior work has addressed group fairness in the OFL setting, where the lack of iterative updates makes conflict mitigation especially critical.

## Input / Output

The method takes 224×224 retinal fundus images (color fundus photographs for diabetic retinopathy, scanning laser ophthalmoscopy for glaucoma) or chest X-ray images as input, along with demographic annotations (age, gender, and optionally race) used only during training to supervise prototype learning. At inference, routing decisions depend solely on feature-prototype cosine similarity, eliminating the need for sensitive attribute labels. The output is a per-client personalized model (not a single global model) that produces disease class labels, with group-specific expert predictions fused via uncertainty-aware weighting across multiple sensitive attributes.

## Method

Fair-FedMOE operates in two stages. During local training, Fairness-aware Expert Routing (FER) maintains learnable prototypes per demographic group per attribute, routes each sample to a group-specific expert head via hard cosine-similarity selection, and fuses predictions across attributes using an uncertainty-aware weighting that combines routing similarity and output entropy. A prototype constraint loss enforces alignment between sample features and their group prototype while promoting inter-prototype orthogonality. During server aggregation, Prototype-guided Differential Aggregation (PDA) computes personalized client weights from prototype cosine similarity (blended with sample-size weighting via a trade-off parameter β), then applies module-specific masks: a sign-consistency mask for the feature extractor that excludes parameter updates with opposing signs, and a sparse-overlap mask for expert networks that aggregates only co-activated parameters above a magnitude threshold. Auxiliary losses (sign-consistency regularization and L1 sparsity) during local training align update directions and concentrate expert activations, reducing the conflict rate that would otherwise degrade single-round aggregation.

## Results

On DR detection with RETFound under the most challenging Age×Gender intersectional setting, Fair-FedMOE achieves 87.39% AUC and 82.32% RES-AUC in a single communication round, outperforming the best Traditional FL method (FlexFair: 83.54% AUC, 76.98% RES-AUC) by +3.85% AUC and +5.34% RES-AUC, and the best One-shot FL method (FAFI: 83.40% AUC, 74.17% RES-AUC) by +3.99% AUC and +8.15% RES-AUC. On glaucoma detection with RETFound under Age×Gender, Fair-FedMOE reaches 82.33% AUC and 76.54% RES-AUC, improving over the best OFL baseline (FAFI) by +5.25% AUC and +8.44% RES-AUC. With DINOv3, gains are even larger, reaching +2.77% RES-AUC over the best TFL and +3.09% over the best OFL on DR detection. The method also generalizes to chest X-ray (CheXpert), achieving 95.53% AUC / 94.71% RES-AUC on PE detection and 70.28% AUC / 65.51% RES-AUC on AT detection, approaching centralized performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Fair-FedMOE outperforms all seven baselines on both AUC and RES-AUC across every task, backbone, and attribute setting. Against Traditional FL baselines, the largest gains appear under intersectional attributes: FlexFair (best TFL on RES-AUC) is surpassed by +2.00% RES-AUC on DR/RETFound and +1.71% on glaucoma/RETFound, while FairLoRA (best TFL on AUC) is beaten by +0.71% AUC and +2.00% RES-AUC on DR. FedAvg and FairFed perform substantially worse, with FedAvg actually degrading below Local training (RES-AUC drops from 79.28 to 71.43 on DR), confirming that naive multi-round averaging amplifies parameter conflicts under heterogeneity. Among One-shot FL baselines, FAFI is the strongest competitor (83.40% AUC, 74.17% RES-AUC on DR/RETFound), yet Fair-FedMOE still leads by +3.99% AUC and +8.15% RES-AUC, demonstrating that conflict-aware differential aggregation (sign-consistency masks + sparse-overlap masks) is critical beyond what self-alignment or prototype-based inference alone can achieve. O-FedAvg and FuseFL trail FAFI by 2–4% RES-AUC, with O-FedAvg showing the largest variance (±4.02 on DINOv3), underscoring the instability of naive one-shot averaging under heterogeneous medical data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three previously separate research threads—group fairness in federated learning (FairFed, FairLoRA, FlexFair), one-shot federated learning (O-FedAvg, FuseFL, FAFI), and Mixture-of-Experts routing (FedMoE, PFEDMoE)—into a unified framework that had not been explored before. It repurposes MoE routing by making it explicitly demographic-aware via learnable prototypes, departing from prior MoE-in-FL work that routes by client, sample, or task without sensitive-attribute awareness. The aggregation strategy draws on the conflict-rate analysis of Xu et al. (2026) but extends it with prototype-similarity-weighted, module-specific masking (sign-consistency for the backbone, sparse-overlap for experts), and the proposed RES-AUC metric is a novel Rawlsian-justice-inspired alternative to the group-count-sensitive ES-AUC.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: medical institutions face strict data-governance constraints that make multi-round FL infeasible, yet single-round aggregation of heterogeneous local models amplifies demographic disparities. The finding that FedAvg can perform *worse* than local training on fairness (RES-AUC drops ~8%) is a useful caution for practitioners assuming more communication always helps. Limitations include reliance on demographic annotations during training (mitigated at inference), a privacy analysis that only covers prototype uploads rather than full model parameters, and evaluation on at most 3–5 clients, leaving scalability to larger federations open. Whether the prototype-guided expert design generalizes to segmentation or other dense prediction tasks in medical imaging remains untested.
