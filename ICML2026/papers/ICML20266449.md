## Problem

LLM research increasingly treats multiple models, samples, or agents as a population of imperfect contributors, yet it largely overlooks decades of crowdsourcing literature that already formalized how to model worker ability, item difficulty, dependence, and aggregation under noise. This gap risks reinventing established mechanisms (redundancy, staged verification, worker/item modeling, aggregation beyond majority voting) while missing opportunities to adapt them systematically. A further complication is that LLM workers differ from human workers in fundamental ways—correlated errors, prompt-dependent capabilities, and shared training-induced biases—so the analogy cannot be transferred verbatim.

## Input / Output

The paper takes as input two bodies of literature—classical crowdsourcing (label aggregation, preference ranking, multi-stage pipelines, worker modeling) and contemporary LLM research (ensembles, routing, self-consistency, LLM-as-a-judge, agentic pipelines)—and produces a systematic alignment framework (Table 2) that maps each crowdsourcing mechanism to its LLM counterpart, along with a taxonomy of where the structural parallels hold and where they break. The output is not a trained model or a dataset but a position statement with a concrete research agenda for adapting crowdsourcing theory to model-based agents.

## Method

The paper organizes its argument around two complementary directions: Crowd-LLM-Sourcing (humans and LLMs jointly participating in shared workflows) and LLM-Sourcing (crowdsourcing principles applied to purely LLM-driven generation, annotation, evaluation, and inference). It reviews foundational crowdsourcing mechanisms—GLAD/IRT-style worker-item modeling, Bradley-Terry preference aggregation, multi-stage pipelines like Find-Fix-Verify and Partition-Map-Reduce—and maps each to an emerging LLM paradigm (multi-LLM routing, self-consistency, LLM-as-a-judge, agentic reasoning pipelines). The key technical contribution is identifying precisely where classical assumptions fail for LLMs: the conditional-independence assumption breaks because similar models produce correlated errors, stable worker-ability parameters break because LLM competence is a context-conditioned surface over prompt and task space, and gold-question-based quality control becomes circular when generators and judges share the same blind spots. The paper then prescribes specific adaptations—diversity-aware redundancy across model families and decoding strategies, correlation-aware aggregation treating model lineages as worker communities, and asymmetric generator-judge designs—to generalize crowdsourcing mechanisms for collective intelligence with model-based agents.

## Results

This is a position/survey paper with no original experiments or quantitative results. Its central empirical anchor is a cited finding from Li (2024b) showing that (1) LLM workers outperform the average crowd worker, (2) high-ability crowd workers still outperform LLM workers, and (3) aggregating both crowd and LLM labels yields higher quality than either alone. The paper's main contribution is a systematic mapping (Table 2) of eight crowdsourcing mechanisms—redundancy, worker/item modeling, aggregation, minority-correct phenomena, quality control, multi-stage pipelines, and human pipelines—to their LLM-Sourcing counterparts, along with an analysis of where the analogies break (correlated errors, context-dependent capability, non-strategic behavior).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method or run head-to-head baseline comparisons. Instead, it performs a conceptual alignment between established crowdsourcing techniques and emerging LLM paradigms. The paper argues that several 'new' LLM reasoning approaches (self-consistency, LLM ensembles, multi-LLM routing, LLM-as-a-judge, chain-of-thought pipelines) are rediscovering classical crowdsourcing mechanisms—redundancy, worker modeling, task assignment, aggregation beyond majority voting, and staged verification—often without citing the crowdsourcing literature. The key distinction the paper draws is that LLM workers differ from human workers in having correlated (not independent) errors, prompt-conditioned (not stable) capabilities, and systematic (not idiosyncratic) failure modes, meaning crowdsourcing methods must be adapted rather than transferred verbatim. No method is shown to 'win' or 'lose'; the contribution is a research agenda and a call for correlation-aware aggregation, capability-conditioned routing, and asymmetric quality-control designs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not extend a single prior method but rather repurposes the entire crowdsourcing literature—GLAD, Bradley-Terry, IRT, multi-stage pipelines (Find-Fix-Verify, Partition-Map-Reduce), and correlation-aware aggregation—as a conceptual lens for LLM research. It carves new ground by formally naming two paradigms (Crowd-LLM-Sourcing and LLM-Sourcing Inspired by Crowdsourcing) and providing a systematic mapping table between decades-old crowd mechanisms and emerging LLM practices such as self-consistency, multi-LLM routing, LLM-as-a-judge, and agentic pipelines. The paper departs from prior LLM ensemble and multi-agent work by explicitly grounding those designs in crowdsourcing theory and, crucially, by identifying where the human-worker assumptions (independent errors, stable ability) break for model-based agents.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's main practical impact is a call to stop reinventing established crowd-aggregation and quality-control ideas in LLM research and to adapt them with awareness of correlated failures, prompt-conditioned capability, and shared training-data biases. Open questions it surfaces include: how to build correlation-aware aggregation for model families, how to model LLM 'ability' as a context-dependent surface rather than a scalar, and how to design asymmetric generator-verifier pipelines that avoid circular self-reinforcement. As a survey-only position paper it offers no new algorithm or benchmark, so its influence depends on whether the community adopts the crowdsourcing vocabulary as a shared design language for multi-LLM and hybrid human-LLM systems.
