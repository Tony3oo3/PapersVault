## Problem

Existing probing-based weight-space learning methods rely on a single first-order projection (XU) along one direction, which suffers from two fundamental limitations: distinct weight matrices can produce identical probe responses (proven in Theorem 4.1), and the single linear view cannot capture higher-order pairwise correlation structure among rows or columns. This renders first-order probing blind to components in the nullspace of the probe matrix and unable to encode the rich interaction patterns that distinguish models in practice.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single weight matrix X ∈ R^{m×n} from a representative layer (e.g., 1024×256 for ResNet-101, 768×768 for ViT variants, 1280×1280 for Stable Diffusion LoRA) and produces a multi-label class vector indicating the training categories. It also supports kNN retrieval rankings and one-class classification scores for open-set generalization. Each of the four probing branches uses 128 learnable probe vectors, and branch features are projected to a common 128-dim space before concatenation into a 512-dim representation.

## Method

MVProbe extracts four complementary probe responses from a weight matrix: first-order row (XU) and column (X^T V) projections, and second-order Gram-based row (XX^T W) and column (X^T X Z) kernel projections. The second-order branches are computed efficiently via associativity (e.g., XX^T W as X(X^T W)) to maintain O(mnr) complexity. A per-sample standardization step, motivated by a scaling-law analysis (Theorem 4.3) showing the second-order Frobenius norm scales as O(nσ²) relative to first-order, equalizes branch contributions before branch-specific MLP projections. The four projected features are concatenated, passed through a shared encoder and classifier head, and trained end-to-end with binary cross-entropy. The theoretical contribution includes proving that second-order branches provably separate weight matrices indistinguishable under first-order probing, and that the column-side probe is non-redundant with the row-side probe.

## Results

On the Model Jungle benchmark, MVProbe achieves 92.24% (ResNet), 92.33% (SupViT), 81.62% (MAE), and 78.29% (DINO) classification accuracy, outperforming the previous SOTA ProbeX(×4) by +5.08%, +2.00%, +4.36%, and +5.04% respectively. On the harder Stable Diffusion LoRA SD_1k setting (1,000 classes, 5 models per class), MVProbe retains 97.88% in-distribution accuracy while ProbeX collapses to 35.75%. MVProbe also wins on 95.1% of all 327 layers across architectures, demonstrating robustness to layer selection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MVProbe decisively outperforms all baselines on the Model Jungle benchmark. Against the primary SOTA ProbeX (Horwitz et al., 2025), MVProbe gains +10.63% on ResNet, +4.25% on SupViT, +4.51% on MAE, and +5.75% on DINO; even the 4×-probe variant ProbeX(×4) is beaten by 2–5% across all four architectures. ProbeGen (Kahana et al., 2025), designed for multi-layer probing on smaller models, underperforms ProbeX on the large-scale single-layer setting (e.g., 61.26% vs. 72.54% on DINO), and StatNN (Unterthiner et al., 2020), which uses hand-crafted weight statistics, lags by 20–37% across all architectures. On the SD LoRA SD_1k task, the gap widens dramatically: MVProbe achieves 97.88% in-distribution accuracy versus ProbeX's 35.75%, and 97.96% zero-shot versus 52.42%. The graph-based equivariant methods (Zhang et al., 2023; Navon et al., 2023; Kofinas et al., 2024; Lim et al., 2024) are excluded from the main comparison due to prohibitive computational cost on large weights and divergence in single-layer probing, but are compared on a small-scale MNIST INR benchmark in Appendix L where MVProbe also performs competitively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the single-layer probing paradigm introduced by ProbeX (Horwitz et al., 2025), which itself evolved from earlier probing methods (ProbeGen, Neural Graph) and weight-statistics approaches (StatNN). The key conceptual departure is showing that first-order probing (XU) is fundamentally ambiguous—distinct weight matrices can be indistinguishable under the same probe—and then remediating this by adding column-side first-order and Gram-based second-order branches, drawing on kernel methods and landmark-point representations from manifold learning. The per-sample standardization is grounded in a scaling-law analysis (Theorem 4.3) that quantifies the O(nσ²) scale gap between first- and second-order responses, providing a principled fusion strategy rather than an ad-hoc design choice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work provides a theoretically grounded fix for a fundamental limitation of the dominant probing paradigm in weight-space learning, with practical impact for model identification in large open-source repositories where documentation is sparse. The dramatic gap on Stable Diffusion LoRA (ProbeX at 35.75% vs. MVProbe at 97.88% on SD_1k) suggests that multi-view probing is not merely an incremental improvement but essential for generative-model checkpoints where first-order signals are weakest. Open questions remain around absolute accuracy on MAE/DINO under single-layer probing, which layer types are most error-prone, and whether multi-layer aggregation or architecture-aware branch selection could close the remaining gap.
