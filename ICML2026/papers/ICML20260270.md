## Problem

Model-based planners offer flexibility through arbitrary test-time objectives but lack realistic behavior priors in complex environments, while learning-based planners capture realistic motion from data but bake task structure into the model, making it difficult to accommodate new objectives without retraining. This paper asks whether a single generative framework can separate trajectory realism (learned from data) from test-time control (specified arbitrarily at inference), unifying both paradigms without task-specific training.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input an environment description (static road geometry, lane markings, traffic signals, and dynamic agent position histories) and, at test time, an arbitrary objective function over decoded trajectories that need not be differentiable or smooth (e.g., a lexicographic lane-change count plus centering residual, or a route-progress cost). It outputs a full ego trajectory as a sequence of uniformly time-spaced position samples, or joint multi-agent trajectories when using the multi-agent model. Notably, the entire trajectory is controlled by an extremely short discrete code (e.g., 3 tokens of dimension 3, binary-quantized to 18 bits total), and partial token prefixes decode to valid intermediate trajectories that can be scored during search.

## Method

The core contribution is an environment-conditioned transformer autoencoder that compresses trajectories into a short sequence of ordered, discrete latent tokens, trained with causal masking and nested dropout to induce a coarse-to-fine hierarchy where each successive token refines the reconstruction. During training, adaptive soft quantization (tanh-bounded Gaussian noise with an ADE-driven schedule) regularizes the bottleneck, enabling hard quantization to a small number of discrete levels at test time. At inference, motion planning reduces to greedy (or small-beam) tree search over the quantized token space: at each step, all N_l^D candidate values for the next token are enumerated, each candidate prefix is decoded into a full trajectory, scored against the user-specified objective, and the best prefix is retained. The environment conditioning shifts the burden of scene-appropriate behavior into the decoder, so the same token sequence produces different but valid maneuvers in different scenes, and the extreme compression (shifting generative complexity to the decoder) makes the discrete search space small enough for exhaustive per-step enumeration while the decoder enforces realism and kinematic feasibility.

## Results

On the WOMD multi-agent guided generation benchmark, latent token search achieves a meanADE of 0.767 (gradient-free) and 0.684 (with optional gradient-based token refinement), the best realism among all compared methods, while remaining competitive on minADE (0.496 / 0.459). On the nuPlan closed-loop planning benchmark, the method achieves CLS-NR 86.71, S-CR 95.91, and S-PR 87.31, performing on par with recent imitation-learning planners despite requiring no route conditioning at training time. The method is fully gradient-free during search, requiring only 24 decoder evaluations per planning step (~115 trajectories/s on an RTX 6000 Ada).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the multi-agent guided generation task (Table 6), the method decisively outperforms all diffusion-based baselines on realism: meanADE of 0.767 (gradient-free) vs. 2.194 for MotionDiffuser and 1.947 for CTG, indicating that diffusion methods rely heavily on sample filtering to achieve good performance while token search generates realistic trajectories deterministically. On minADE, the method (0.496) is comparable to the Consistency model (0.490) and slightly better than MotionDiffuser (0.533). However, on constraint effectiveness (minFDE), the gradient-free version (0.399) trails MotionDiffuser (0.007) and the Consistency model (0.008); adding optional gradient-based token refinement closes this gap substantially (0.093). On the nuPlan closed-loop benchmark (Table 4), the method (CLS-NR 86.71) outperforms all IL baselines—GameFormer (83.76), PlanTF (83.66), and Gen-Drive IL (85.12)—but falls short of the RL-based CarPlanner (91.45) and the rule-based PDM-Closed (91.21), which the authors note are orthogonal improvements that could be incorporated into the framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate paradigms—model-based planning with explicit cost functions and learning-based trajectory generation—by learning an environment-conditioned autoencoder with extreme compression (3 tokens × 3 dimensions, binary-quantized at test time) and performing greedy or beam search over the resulting discrete latent tokens. It builds on nested dropout (Rippel et al., 2014) for coarse-to-fine token ordering, Motion Transformer (Shi et al., 2022) for environment encoding, and the observation from image tokenization (TiTok, VQGAN-CLIP) that high compression shifts generative burden to the decoder. The key departure from prior test-time guidance work (diffusion classifier guidance, MotionDiffuser, CTG) is replacing gradient-based steering with decode-and-score enumeration over ordered discrete tokens, which naturally accommodates non-differentiable and lexicographic objectives without retraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a clean, training-free interface for specifying arbitrary test-time objectives (route following, lane changes, goal reaching) while relying on a learned decoder to enforce realism and environmental consistency, potentially reducing the need for task-specific reward shaping or conditioning that plagues current learning-based planners. The extreme compression (18 bits per trajectory) and the finding that greedy search can match or exceed the learned encoder's reconstruction quality suggest a broadly applicable principle: sufficiently compressed ordered representations turn guided generation into a tractable combinatorial search. Limitations include the focus on low-dimensional autonomous driving trajectories, the reliance on greedy search (which may be suboptimal for longer-horizon or more complex tasks), and the open question of whether this approach extends to higher-dimensional manipulation or contact-rich domains.
