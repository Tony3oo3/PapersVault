## Problem

Subgoal-based policy tree search methods (e.g., HIPS-ε, SGPS) rely on explicit subgoal generation via high-capacity models like VQ-VAE, incurring substantial computational overhead that causes them to fail as domain complexity increases. While the √LTS framework provides theoretical guarantees showing that rerooting can yield exponential speedups over LTS, it left open the practical question of how to derive or learn rerooters. This paper addresses that gap by proposing three concrete rerooter designs that exploit structure already present in the search tree, avoiding explicit subgoal reconstruction entirely.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single-agent deterministic planning instance (state space, action set, transition function, goal states) along with a learned ResNet-based policy network and, for the heuristic and hybrid variants, a learned heuristic network. During search, the clustering rerooter incrementally builds an induced subgraph of visited states and invokes Leiden clustering on a geometric schedule (γ=1.2), while the heuristic rerooter reads cost-to-go estimates directly from the network. The output is a solution path found by the √LTS best-first search, with the search tree annotated by per-node rerooting weights that determine how effort is split among competing local LTS instances.

## Method

The paper instantiates three rerooters for √LTS that implicitly decompose the problem into soft subtasks without explicit subgoal generation. The clustering rerooter (√LTS-L) runs Leiden clustering on the incrementally built induced state-space graph and assigns each node a rerooting weight inversely proportional to its cluster size, so that entering a new region concentrates search effort there. The heuristic rerooter (√LTS-H) maps the learned cost-to-go estimate through an exponential function normalized by the root heuristic, yielding a softmax-like allocation that prioritizes nodes estimated closer to the goal. The hybrid rerooter (√LTS-LH) additively combines both signals with mixing coefficients, and the paper proves a novel bound (Theorem 3.2) showing that balanced additive rerooters can exploit synergies between the two components, achieving bounds tighter than either alone. All rerooters are computed on-the-fly during search with O(bN log N + DN) runtime, matching BFS up to constant factors, and are trained online via the bootstrap process without any separate subgoal network.

## Results

On held-out test problems, √LTS-LH achieves the fewest expansions in BoulderDash (92.7 vs. 202.4 for LTS(π^SG) and 359.9 for PHS*(π^SG)) and CraftWorld (1,347.5 vs. 1,413.0 for PHS*(π^SG) and 345,096 for LTS(π^SG)). In training efficiency, √LTS-LH requires 13.5M expansions on TSP versus 22.8M for PHS*(π^SG) and 122.7M for LTS(π^SG), and 95.2M on Sokoban versus 127.1M for PHS*(π^SG). Most critically, in the BoulderDash complexity-scaling experiment, PHS*(π^SG) times out entirely at ≥30% dirt tiles, while √LTS-H and √LTS-LH complete training even at 50% dirt with <100K expansions per problem.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PHS*(π^SG) (the strongest subgoal-based baseline), √LTS-LH wins on test expansions in BoulderDash (92.7 vs. 359.9) and CraftWorld (1,347.5 vs. 1,413.0), but loses slightly on TSP (56.1 vs. 46.3) and Sokoban (1,736.0 vs. 1,630.6). Against LTS(π^SG), √LTS-LH dominates in BoulderDash (92.7 vs. 202.4) and CraftWorld (1,347.5 vs. 345,096), and is competitive on TSP (56.1 vs. 77.0) and Sokoban (1,736.0 vs. 2,010.9). Against WA*(1.5), the rerooting methods win decisively in BoulderDash (92.7 vs. 480.5), CraftWorld (1,347.5 vs. 1,888.4), and TSP (56.1 vs. 180,381), but lose on Sokoban (1,736.0 vs. 1,201.0). The most striking comparison is in training efficiency and scalability: in the BoulderDash dirt-scaling experiment, PHS*(π^SG) fails completely at ≥30% dirt (solving only 11/10,000 problems at 30%, timing out at 40–50%), while √LTS-LH completes all 10,000 problems at every level with 18.7M–97.1M total expansions. Against plain LTS, the rerooting methods achieve 2,000×–3,000× fewer expansions in BoulderDash and CraftWorld, confirming that the gains come from structural rerooting rather than the learned policy alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the √LTS (root-LTS) framework of Orseau et al. (2024), which provided formal guarantees for rerooting-based policy tree search but left open how to instantiate rerooters in practice. It departs from the subgoal-based policy tree search line (HIPS-ε, SGPS) by replacing explicit subgoal generation via VQ-VAE and conditioned low-level policies with implicit subtask decomposition through rerooting weights derived from Leiden clustering and learned heuristics. The paper also builds on the foundational LTS and PHS* algorithms, positioning rerooting as a lighter-weight alternative to the subgoal reconstruction machinery that prior work required for scaling to complex domains.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical impact is that rerooting-based search scales to environments (e.g., BoulderDash with 30%+ irrelevant dirt tiles) where subgoal-based methods like PHS*(π^SG) completely fail, while maintaining competitive or superior test-time performance. The finding that a simple exponential-of-heuristic rerooter often matches or beats the more complex clustering-based one suggests that lightweight local signals may suffice for effective search control, reducing the engineering burden of deploying structural methods. Open questions include whether the approach extends to stochastic or multi-agent settings, whether more sophisticated rerooter combinations (beyond additive) yield further gains, and how the method behaves when the heuristic is severely miscalibrated.
