## Problem

Despite transformers being widely deployed for OOD detection, the design of detection strategies relies almost entirely on empirical heuristics with no formal theoretical guarantees. This paper identifies the missing theoretical foundation: necessary and sufficient conditions under which transformers can provably learn to distinguish in-distribution from out-of-distribution data, and quantitative error bounds linking model capacity to detection reliability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266166/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on pre-trained transformer backbones (DINO for images at 32×32 or 224×224; BERT, GPT-2, and Llama-3.1-8B for text) and fine-tunes them on ID training data augmented with generated OOD embeddings. At inference, the fine-tuned model outputs both per-class LOGITS and feature embeddings, which are jointly fed into a modified VIM post-processor that produces a final ID class label or an OOD rejection decision. The approach is architecture-agnostic, compatible with any transformer that exposes final-layer features (e.g., CLS tokens) before the classifier head.

## Method

The paper contributes a PAC learning framework proving that OOD detection is learnable in the separate space for transformer hypothesis spaces if and only if the input set is finite (Theorem 3.2), and derives Jackson-type approximation rates bounding the learnability probability as a function of attention head count and FFN width (Theorems 3.4–3.5). From these conditions, the authors derive the GROD algorithm, which enforces two theoretical requirements during fine-tuning: (1) an auxiliary ID-OOD binary cross-entropy loss L₂ is added to the standard cross-entropy L₁ so that ID-OOD misclassification is penalized more heavily than within-ID errors, satisfying the loss condition in Theorem 3.2; and (2) synthetic OOD embeddings are generated by projecting ID features via PCA (global boundary) and LDA (inter-class boundary), shifting boundary points outward to define outlier centers, filtering with Mahalanobis distance to enforce non-overlap, and assigning soft labels graded by boundary proximity. At inference, the fine-tuned transformer's features and adjusted LOGITS are passed through a modified VIM post-processor for the final OOD/ID decision.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, GROD achieves an average FPR@95 of 3.01% and AUROC of 99.33%, outperforming all baselines. On CIFAR-100, GROD reduces average FPR@95 by at least 5.43% compared to the next best method (CIDER at 32.82% vs. GROD's 18.85%). On ImageNet-200, GROD attains the lowest average FPR@95 of 30.90% and highest average AUROC of 92.08%. For NLP tasks on Llama-3.1-8B, GROD achieves FPR@95 of 83.28% (Background Shift) and 21.60% (Semantic Shift), with AUROC of 67.00% and 93.64% respectively, consistently outperforming all post-processing and fine-tuning baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GROD consistently outperforms all 8 baselines across every dataset and metric. Among post-processing methods, GROD's CIFAR-10 average FPR@95 of 3.01% beats VIM (5.41%), GEN (5.94%), and ASH (5.83%) by 2.4–2.9 percentage points, while also achieving the highest AUROC (99.33% vs. 98.71–98.76%). Among fine-tuning methods, GROD dramatically outperforms G-ODIN (30.62%) and NPOS (35.40%) on CIFAR-10 FPR@95, and beats CIDER (9.70%) by 6.69 points. On CIFAR-100, GROD's 18.85% FPR@95 is at least 5.43% lower than the next best (CIDER at 32.82%), and its 93.79% AUROC exceeds all competitors. On ImageNet-200, GROD achieves 30.90% FPR@95 vs. 33.05% (VIM) and 34.11% (CIDER), with 92.08% AUROC vs. 91.18% (VIM). For NLP on Llama-3.1-8B, GROD reduces FPR@95 by up to 10.68% over the best baseline (ASH at 93.96% vs. GROD's 83.28% on Background Shift) while maintaining competitive AUROC.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the PAC learning framework for OOD detection established by Fang et al. (2022) from FCNN-based and score-based hypothesis spaces to transformer architectures, leveraging the Jackson-type approximation rates for transformers from Jiang & Li (2024) to derive learnability conditions and error bounds. The resulting theoretical conditions (penalizing ID-OOD misclassification more heavily than within-ID errors, and requiring auxiliary non-overlapping OOD data) directly motivate the GROD algorithm, which combines an ID-OOD binary classification loss with PCA/LDA-guided synthetic outlier generation and Mahalanobis filtering. At inference, GROD incorporates a modified VIM post-processor, positioning it as a fine-tuning strategy that is architecture-agnostic across transformer variants (DINO, BERT, GPT-2, Llama-3.1-8B).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide necessary and sufficient conditions for OOD detection learnability specifically for transformers, filling a notable gap where prior theory was limited to fully connected networks. The practical implication—that standard cross-entropy loss violates the theoretical learnability condition—offers a principled explanation for why naive fine-tuning fails at OOD detection and why auxiliary outlier strategies work. However, the theory assumes non-overlapping ID-OOD distributions and finite data, conditions that are difficult to guarantee in real-world open-world settings, leaving open how the framework extends to overlapping or ill-defined OOD distributions. The SOTA results across CV and NLP benchmarks validate the approach, but the moderate margins over strong baselines (e.g., ~2-5% FPR@95 on CIFAR-10) suggest the theoretical-to-practical gap remains partially unbridged.
