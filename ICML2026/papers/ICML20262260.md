## Problem

Compositional explanations for neurons rely on beam search to find the logical formula of concepts that best aligns with a neuron's activation pattern, but beam search provides no guarantee of optimality and it remains unclear how far the resulting explanations are from the true optimum. This paper addresses that gap by introducing the first framework that computes guaranteed optimal compositional explanations over the entire admissible state space in feasible time.

## Input / Output

The method takes a binary neuron activation matrix (thresholded to a specific quantile range, e.g., top 0.005) and a binary concept tensor derived from segmentation-mask annotations in a probing dataset, both aligned to the same spatial dimensions. It produces a propositional logic formula (e.g., ((Cat OR Car) AND White)) that maximizes the Intersection-over-Union between the formula's combined annotation mask and the neuron activation, along with the decomposed alignment quantities (unique/common intersections and extras per sample) that underpin the search. The output formula is guaranteed to be the globally optimal one within the restricted state space defined by the standard assumptions (distinct concepts, incremental chaining, 00-preserving operators).

## Method

The core contribution is a decomposition of the IoU objective (dIoU) into four fundamental per-sample quantities—unique intersection, common intersection, unique extras, and common extras—that are provably equivalent to IoU under 00-preserving logic operators (OR, AND, AND-NOT). Building on this, the paper designs an admissible heuristic that estimates the maximum and minimum achievable IoU from any partial label by tracking a Disjoint Matrix between concepts and pre-computing Top/Bott cumulative vectors of per-concept quantities, enabling tight bounds without spatial information. The optimal algorithm is a best-first search that uses aggregated (sum-level) estimates to manage the frontier cheaply, refines to sample-level estimates upon popping, backpropagates exact quantities of visited sub-labels to update frontier nodes, and applies logical equivalence rules to prune redundant expansions. Because the maximum estimate is always an overestimate and the algorithm visits every node whose bound exceeds the current best, it is guaranteed to return the globally optimal explanation. A beam-search variant reusing the same label-quantity heuristic (replacing the prior MMESH spatial heuristic) achieves comparable or better runtime with greater flexibility in explanation length and beam size.

## Results

The optimal algorithm computes guaranteed-optimal compositional explanations in 0.08 s/unit (low complexity, Cityscapes), 90.57 s/unit (intermediate, ADE20K), and 5768 s/unit (high, Broden), remaining within the same order of magnitude as vanilla beam search (2.77 s, 450 s, 5929 s respectively). The heuristic-guided beam search variant visits far fewer states than all baselines (e.g., 10 vs. 37,979 visited nodes in intermediate complexity) while running in 11.55 s/unit vs. 38.42 s (MMESH) and 450 s (vanilla). Critically, 10–40% of explanations previously reported by beam search are suboptimal: 9% for ResNet18, 23% for AlexNet, and 39% for DenseNet161 on the high-complexity Broden dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the MMESH-guided beam search (La Rosa et al., 2023), the proposed heuristic-guided beam variant reduces visited nodes by 95% in low complexity (6 vs. 121) and 74% in intermediate (10 vs. 39), and achieves 60× and 3.3× speedups in low and intermediate settings respectively (0.17 s vs. 10.37 s; 11.55 s vs. 38.42 s). In the high-complexity Broden setting, MMESH is slightly faster (102.35 s vs. 123.33 s), but the proposed variant still visits fewer nodes (27 vs. 43) and scales far better with explanation length and beam size (0.19–0.42 min/unit across lengths 3–20, vs. 0.62–26.63 min for MMESH). Against vanilla beam search (Mu and Andreas, 2020), the proposed beam variant reduces visited nodes by two to three orders of magnitude (e.g., 10 vs. 37,979 in intermediate) and achieves 39× and 48× runtime improvements in intermediate and high settings. The headline empirical finding is that beam search (both vanilla and MMESH) produces suboptimal explanations for 9–39% of units depending on the model, with the majority of errors (73–93%) falling into Category 1 where both the concepts and the IoU differ from the true optimum.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the compositional explanation framework introduced by Mu and Andreas (2020), which used beam search to find logical formulas maximizing IoU between neuron activations and concept annotations, and the MMESH-guided beam search of La Rosa et al. (2023). The key departure is replacing the heuristic beam search with a guaranteed-optimal best-first search, enabled by a novel decomposition of IoU (dIoU) into fundamental quantities (unique/common intersections and extras) and an admissible heuristic for estimating alignment at any search stage. The paper also draws on prior work on expectation-based IoU approximations (Nowozin, 2014; Li et al., 2013) but derives an exact, deterministic decomposition rather than a statistical one.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 10–40% of beam search explanations are suboptimal in high-complexity (overlapping-concept) settings challenges the implicit assumption in the field that small-beam search yields near-optimal explanations, and provides the first ground-truth reference for evaluating approximation quality. The optimal algorithm's feasibility—running in comparable time to vanilla beam search when exhaustive search was previously deemed intractable—opens the door to certified explanations and to designing better heuristics. Limitations include restriction to CNNs and vision tasks, potential slowdown on uninterpretable units or datasets with few unique elements (e.g., NLP), and the modest absolute IoU gaps between optimal and beam-search solutions, which limits immediate practical impact.
