## Problem

State-of-the-art AI-based theorem provers are complex, expensive, and difficult to adopt, making it hard to disentangle performance gains from architectural innovation versus improvements in the underlying LLM. This paper asks what minimal set of agentic components is sufficient to achieve competitive theorem-proving performance, and whether a simple, modular baseline can serve as a moving reference point that naturally improves as foundation models advance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263865/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a Lean 4 theorem statement embedded within the full Lean file context (including existing imports and surrounding definitions) and produces a complete Lean 4 formal proof that compiles without `sorry` or `admit` placeholders. The proposer agent outputs a structured response with four fields—reasoning, additional imports, namespaces to open, and the updated theorem code—which is then compiled via `lake build`. The iterative loop runs up to 50 iterations per problem, with each iteration receiving compiler error messages or goal-state feedback from LeanInteract, and the final output is verified by both the compiler and an LLM-based reviewer agent that checks for statement preservation and potential cheating mechanisms.

## Method

AxProverBase is a modular agentic architecture with three core components: (1) an iterative refinement loop where a ReAct-style proposer agent (a general-purpose LLM, no fine-tuning) writes Lean 4 proof code, submits it to a compiler, and receives concrete error feedback to guide the next attempt; (2) a self-managed memory system in which a separate LLM call performs self-reflection after each iteration, maintaining a concise 'lab notebook' of key lessons to prevent repeating mistakes without bloating context; and (3) optional tools including LeanSearch (vector-embedding-based Mathlib retrieval) and Tavily web search, callable in a single parallel round before each proof proposal. A deterministic compiler check is supplemented by an LLM reviewer agent that flags subtle cheating (e.g., metaprogramming tricks) not caught by syntactic checks. Ablation studies show iterative refinement is the dominant performance driver, self-managed memory yields the second-largest gain (reducing Lean errors by 53%), and search tools provide a smaller but consistent improvement. With Claude Opus 4.5 (32k thinking budget, 50 iterations), the system achieves 54.7% on PutnamBench pass@1, 98.0% on FATE-M, and 59.0% on LeanCat, matching or exceeding far more complex provers at roughly 100× lower token cost.

## Results

AxProverBase (Claude Opus 4.5, 32k thinking budget, 50 iterations) achieves 54.7% pass@1 on PutnamBench, 98.0% on FATE-M, 66.0% on FATE-H, 24.0% on FATE-X, and 59.0% on LeanCat. These results are near-parity with Hilbert on PutnamBench (54.7% vs. 55.9% pass@1) while using approximately 100× fewer tokens (4.2M vs. 1880.4M per problem). The average cost across all evaluated datasets is $12.6 per problem, including failed proofs, with over 70% of tokens being input tokens.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AxProverBase achieves near-parity with Hilbert on PutnamBench (54.7% vs. 55.9% pass@1) while consuming roughly 100× fewer tokens, making it far more cost-efficient. Against Seed-Prover 1.5, AxProverBase trails on PutnamBench (54.7% vs. 87.9%), FATE-H (66.0% vs. 80.0%), and FATE-X (24.0% vs. 33.0%), though Seed-Prover requires 10 H20 GPU-days per problem. AxProverBase substantially outperforms all other baselines: it beats DeepSeek-Prover-V2 by ~7× on PutnamBench (54.7%@1 vs. 7.1%@1024), Goedel-Prover-V2 by ~4× (54.7%@1 vs. 13.0%@184), and Kimina-Prover by ~36× (54.7%@1 vs. 1.5%@192). On FATE-M, AxProverBase (98.0%@1) far exceeds REAL-Prover (56.7%@64) and DeepSeek-Prover-V2 (25.3%@16). On LeanCat, AxProverBase (59.0%@1) outperforms the standalone Claude Opus 4.5 baseline (8.25%@1) by over 7×, demonstrating the value of the agentic scaffold. The paper also notes that AxProverBase requires no domain-specific fine-tuning and is immediately applicable to different Lean/Mathlib versions, unlike several baselines that rely on specialized training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a deliberately minimal position within the rapidly evolving landscape of LLM-based theorem provers. Rather than building on a specific prior architecture, it identifies the three components shared across state-of-the-art systems (iterative compiler-feedback refinement, context/memory management, and library search) and implements them in a modular, ablatable loop, drawing conceptual inspiration from Ax-Prover's ReAct-style agentic design and self-reflection mechanisms from the broader LLM-agent literature. The key departure is methodological: instead of adding complexity (RL fine-tuning, recursive subgoal decomposition, specialized geometry engines), the paper strips the system down to its essentials and uses bottom-up ablation to quantify each component's contribution, explicitly framing the result as a moving baseline that improves automatically as frontier LLMs advance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: an open-source, ~100× more token-efficient prover that matches or exceeds complex systems (Hilbert, Seed-Prover) on PutnamBench, FATE, and LeanCat without any domain-specific fine-tuning, lowering the barrier to entry for researchers and practitioners who need formal verification in real projects. The ablation results—iterative refinement as the dominant factor, memory as second, tools as a modest boost—provide a clear roadmap for where future effort should be directed. Open questions include whether the approach degrades on truly novel or research-frontier theorems beyond the tested benchmarks, whether integrating fine-tuned models within this same scaffold yields further gains, and how the system behaves as Lean/Mathlib versions evolve. A key limitation acknowledged is the continued dependence on the underlying LLM's quality, meaning the 'minimal' framing is somewhat contingent on access to frontier models.
