## Problem

Multi-agent debate (MAD) systems are typically evaluated using binary correctness or peer agreement, which treats all incorrect answers as equivalent and all revisions as identical events. This coarse-grained evaluation obscures the micro-level dynamics of how social interaction reshapes an agent's answer within the space of possible solutions, hiding whether revisions move answers closer to or farther from the ground truth. The paper asks: beyond a simple correct/incorrect flip, how does social interaction induce structured, distance-aware movements in solution space?

## Input / Output

Each task provides 3–4 training input/output grid pairs and one test input, all represented as 2D arrays of integer color indices (0–9) serialized as concatenated text (e.g., "6 6 6\n4 4 4\n3 2 1"). The target model receives a 3-message chat history: the original task prompt, its own prior answer grid, and a re-prompt listing K=3 peer answer grids sampled from a Stage I baseline pool of 20 independent generations per task. The output is a single revised answer grid, which is parsed, shape-regularized (cropped/padded to match the label), and scored via pixel-wise normalized Hamming distance to the ground-truth grid, yielding continuous revision trajectories rather than binary outcomes.

## Method

The paper introduces a two-stage controlled-experiment framework on ConceptARC (480 grid-reasoning tasks). Stage I collects 20 zero-shot predictions per task from each model (DeepSeek-V3.2 non-thinking, GPT-5 mini low-effort, temperature 1.0) to build a baseline answer pool. Stage II constructs social scenarios by pairing a target agent's initial answer with K=3 resampled peer answers under systematically varied configurations: scenario letters A–D control the number of correct vs. wrong peers, while experiment types (sameWrong, uniqueWrong, diffWrong, uniqueCorrect, diffCorrect, individual) control whether wrong peers are identical to the target, identical to each other, or diverse. The core analytical contribution is treating each grid answer as a point in a measurable solution space and quantifying revisions as continuous movements via pixel-wise Hamming distance (supplemented by color-aware EMD and object-level edit distance via Hungarian matching), revealing that (i) revision likelihood increases monotonically with initial distance to ground truth, (ii) even still-incorrect revisions exhibit systematic contraction toward the label, and (iii) correct targets are most vulnerable to near-correct wrong peers rather than obviously wrong ones.

## Results

On the full ConceptARC suite (480 tasks), GPT-5 mini achieves 56.82% pass@1 accuracy versus 37.16% for DeepSeek-chat (and 21.07% for Gemini-2.5-flash-lite in validation). The central finding is a monotonic, distance-dependent revision pattern: wrong-target agents with larger initial pixel-wise Hamming distance to the ground truth are substantially more likely to revise, and their revisions exhibit systematic contraction (negative Δd_pix) toward the correct answer even when the revised answer remains incorrect. For GPT-5 mini, higher initial distance correlates with *higher* probability of correctly revising, whereas DeepSeek-chat shows the opposite trend. Correct targets retain their answers >70% of the time under self-reprompting but are most vulnerable to social pressure when wrong peers are near-correct (small peer distance), with identical wrong-peer answers (uniqueCorrect) exerting disproportionately strong conformity pressure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against external baselines; instead, it compares two LLM agents (DeepSeek-V3.2 non-thinking and GPT-5 mini low-effort) under controlled social configurations, with individual (no-peer) reprompting serving as the baseline for isolating social influence. GPT-5 mini outperforms DeepSeek-chat on pass@1 by ~20 percentage points and shows qualitatively superior revision dynamics: it preserves near-correct answers under social pressure (little deterioration at small d_pix) while still correcting gross errors, whereas DeepSeek-chat sometimes worsens near-correct answers. Against the individual (no-peer) baseline, social exposure with correct peers (scenario A, C3) substantially increases revision rates for wrong targets, while sameWrong (all peers matching the target's wrong answer) nearly eliminates revision, mimicking confederate support in human conformity experiments. A third model, Gemini-2.5-flash-lite (21.07% pass@1), was used as a validation check and confirmed the DeepSeek-like negative correction-rate trend, supporting the hypothesis that the qualitative difference in Fig. 3(d,e) is driven by baseline task capability rather than model architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the ConceptARC grid-reasoning benchmark—previously used primarily for evaluating abstract reasoning performance—as a testbed for studying multi-agent debate (MAD) dynamics with quantitative distance measures. It departs from prior MAD evaluations (Du et al. 2023; Chan et al. 2023; Kaesberg et al. 2025) that rely on binary correctness or categorical answer-change rates, and from fine-grained LLM evaluation approaches (VGTS, ORM, PRM) that require training additional reward models, by exploiting the natural pixel-wise and object-level distance structure of grid outputs. Conceptually, it draws on social psychology findings on graded belief updating (Asch 1956; Koriat 2012) to hypothesize that LLM revision should be distance-sensitive, then empirically tests this using controlled peer-answer configurations across DeepSeek-V3.2 and GPT-5 mini.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals that binary correctness evaluation systematically obscures structured, distance-aware learning dynamics in multi-agent debate: even revisions that remain incorrect contract toward the ground truth, and correct answers are most vulnerable to near-correct (not obviously wrong) peer answers—a finding with direct security implications for adversarial manipulation of LLM agent systems. Key limitations include the focus on single-step revision (multi-round results are a small pilot), reliance on a single benchmark (ConceptARC), and only two primary models, leaving open whether these dynamics generalize to open-ended reasoning, math, or programming tasks. The work opens a productive direction for designing evaluation protocols that track solution quality continuously rather than measuring only final correctness, and for understanding when iterative debate accumulates epistemic progress that majority voting would miss.
