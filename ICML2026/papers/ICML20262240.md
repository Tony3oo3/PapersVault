## Problem

Prior work linking low effective rank of the unembedding matrix to performance degradation in small language models was based almost exclusively on the Pythia family and did not systematically vary training conditions to isolate whether rank collapse causally drives performance loss or merely co-occurs with confounding factors. This paper asks whether effective rank reliably predicts downstream performance across diverse tasks and training configurations, and if not, what training factors actually drive its variation.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on the unembedding matrix W ∈ R^{v×d} (vocabulary size × model dimension) and the final-token last-layer representations H ∈ R^{N×d} extracted from 108 pre-trained OLMo-style transformers (4M–75M non-embedding parameters, 8B–128B training tokens). For each model, three geometric metrics—effective rank (singular-value entropy), partition-function isotropy, and pairwise cosine similarity—are computed on both W and H, then correlated against five performance measures: in-distribution loss (Pile-10k), OOD loss (Dolma-100 code), fine-tuning loss (StarCoder-Python, OpenWebMath), post-quantization loss (GPTQ), and catastrophic forgetting (Δ loss on Pile-10k after fine-tuning).

## Method

The paper pre-trains 108 OLMo-style models on the Pile dataset with systematic single-factor ablations over batch size (32–512), weight decay (0.01–0.5), learning rate (0.1×–100× model-specific), LR annealing floor (0–10%), and token budget (8B–128B), while holding all other hyperparameters fixed. For each model, the effective rank R(W) is computed as the exponential of the Shannon entropy of the normalized singular value spectrum, and isotropy and cosine similarity are measured following Arora et al. (2016) and Ethayarajh (2019) formulations. Performance is assessed across five evaluation settings, and regression analyses (raw, residual, partial Spearman correlations and predictive ΔR²) quantify how much variance each geometric metric explains beyond model size and scaling-law baselines. A dedicated saturation experiment matches the Pythia-14M configuration with an OLMo-14M model (sequential vs. parallel attention) to test whether low effective rank is necessary or sufficient for late-stage loss degradation. The key finding is that effective rank is strongly shaped by hyperparameters (especially batch size and weight decay) rather than by task-relevant model quality, and that no geometric metric—effective rank, isotropy, or cosine similarity—reliably predicts downstream performance once training conditions are controlled.

## Results

Across 108 OLMo-style models (4M–75M parameters, 8B–128B tokens), the raw Spearman correlation between unembedding effective rank R(W) and in-distribution loss is −0.699, but drops to −0.130 after controlling for all training hyperparameters (Partial Spearman), with a predictive ΔR² of only −0.003. OLMo-14M trained under identical conditions to Pythia-14M shows no late-stage loss degradation or effective rank collapse, directly contradicting the prior causal link between low rank and saturation. Hyperparameter ablations reveal that batch size (32→512) monotonically increases R(W) while producing a U-shaped loss curve, and weight decay (0.01→0.5) increases R(W) with inconsistent performance effects, confirming that geometry primarily reflects training choices rather than model quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study without a single proposed method to benchmark against, so comparisons take the form of controlled ablations and cross-architecture contrasts. The most striking comparison is OLMo-14M vs. Pythia-14M under matched training: Pythia-14M exhibits clear late-stage loss degradation coinciding with an abrupt drop in R(W), while OLMo-14M (sequential attention instead of parallel) shows neither degradation nor rank collapse, even when trained with a reduced token budget (8B) and small batch (32) that produces severely low effective rank. Among geometric metrics, effective rank R(W) and cosine similarity A(W) show a non-monotonic 'moon-shaped' Pareto relationship, while isotropy I(W) remains tightly clustered around 0.5 and is primarily sensitive to weight decay rather than performance. For OOD evaluation, in-distribution loss tracks OOD loss via an agreement-on-the-line relationship, making it a substantially better predictor than any geometric metric. On post-training quantization (GPTQ), OLMo-4M shows a near-monotonic relationship between R(W) and quantization loss, but this scale-dependent sensitivity disappears at 75M parameters, further undermining R(W) as a universal performance proxy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work linking unembedding matrix geometry (effective rank, isotropy, cosine similarity) to language model performance, which was primarily established on the Pythia model family (Godey et al., 2024b; Diehl Martinez et al., 2024b; Machina & Mercer, 2024). It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) moving beyond Pythia to a suite of 108 OLMo-style models trained under controlled hyperparameter variation, and (2) explicitly testing whether the observed rank-performance correlation is causal or confounded by training choices such as batch size and weight decay. The paper also unifies previously analyzed-in-isolation metrics (effective rank, partition-function isotropy, angular variability) under a single experimental protocol, clarifying their interrelationships for both the unembedding matrix and final-layer representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that effective rank primarily reflects optimization dynamics shaped by hyperparameters rather than serving as a reliable proxy for downstream performance—challenges a widely cited narrative in the interpretability literature and redirects practitioners toward task-based evaluation over geometric heuristics for model selection. The demonstration that saturation in small models is not caused by low rank (OLMo-14M avoids it under matched conditions) opens the question of what architectural or optimization factors actually drive late-stage degradation. Limitations include the focus on small models (4M–75M parameters), a single dataset (Pile), and OLMo-style sequential attention, leaving open whether conclusions generalize to larger scales, different architectures, or parallel attention variants. A rigorous theoretical characterization of when high effective rank is genuinely beneficial versus merely a byproduct of favorable hyperparameters remains an important open problem.
