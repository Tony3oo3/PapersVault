## Problem

Current AI alignment methods (RLHF, DPO, Constitutional AI) align systems to human preferences or normative rules but not to human reasoning processes, creating a trust barrier in high-stakes domains where users need AI that thinks like them. No prior work directly asked users whether they want cognitively-aligned AI, and the paper identifies two concrete limitations in existing methods: unverifiable explanations (L1) and cognitive misalignment (L2).

## Input / Output

The survey presents participants with text descriptions of three AI archetypes (Human-Reasoning, Machine-Reasoning, Process-Hidden) across 16 task domains, five quality-attribute prompts for autonomous vehicle and kidney allocation scenarios, and ranking prompts for six AI variants differing in understandability and modifiability. Outputs include binary preference choices per domain, 0–5 Likert-scale desirability ratings, ordinal rankings of AI variants, and free-text scenario descriptions. All comparisons are explicitly conditioned on equal accuracy to isolate cognitive alignment as the differentiating factor.

## Method

This position paper combines a pilot empirical study (n=150, Prolific, IRB-approved) with a structured research agenda. The survey uses a within-subjects design where participants evaluate three AI types across 16 domains, rate five quality attributes separately for high-stakes vs. low-stakes scenarios (tested with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests), and rank six AI variants. The paper formalizes two limitations of current alignment: (L1) post-hoc and chain-of-thought explanations cannot be verified as faithful to actual decision processes, and (L2) even interpretable models (e.g., linear utility maximizers) use reasoning frameworks that do not match how humans actually reason (e.g., thresholded rule-based processes). The research agenda proposes process-tracing and eye-tracking for quantification, interactive multimodal elicitation interfaces for reasoning capture, reasoning archetypes to constrain hypothesis classes, and interactive conflict-resolution mechanisms for discrepancies between stated reasoning and revealed preferences.

## Results

In a 150-participant Prolific survey, 86.6% of participants could imagine scenarios where they would prefer Human-Reasoning AI over Process-Hidden or Machine-Reasoning AI (assuming equal accuracy). A statistically significant majority preferred Human-Reasoning AI in 5 of 16 task domains (moral dilemmas, medical allocations, bail eligibility, military targeting, and one additional domain). Over 25% rated 'the AI makes decisions similarly to how you would with sufficient time and information' as essential, with another ~25% rating it very desirable; this preference was significantly stronger in high-stakes vs. low-stakes scenarios (Wilcoxon p < 0.001 for all qualities). In ranking exercises for autonomous vehicles and kidney allocation, the top three choices were consistently: (1) Human-Reasoning AI, easy to understand and modifiable; (2) Human-Reasoning AI, easy to understand but unmodifiable; (3) Machine-Reasoning AI, easy to understand and modifiable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper with a pilot survey rather than a method-comparison study, so there are no direct head-to-head baselines. The paper instead contrasts the proposed 'cognitive alignment' paradigm against two categories of existing alignment methods: (1) bottom-up preference-learning approaches (RLHF, DPO, preference-based RL) that align outputs to revealed human choices but produce uninterpretable models whose explanations cannot be verified as faithful (Limitation L1), and (2) top-down normative-constraint approaches (Constitutional AI, off-switch games, conservative agency) that impose rules indirectly through preference optimization or prompting without enforcing interpretable internal reasoning (also L1). The paper argues both categories additionally suffer from Limitation L2: even when reasoning is discernible, it typically uses foreign abstractions (e.g., linear utility maximization) that do not match how humans actually reason (e.g., thresholded rule-based processes). The survey data is positioned as novel evidence that users explicitly desire and deem essential this cognitive-level alignment, filling a gap the authors note has not been directly addressed in prior work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new ground by introducing 'cognitive alignment'—aligning AI to human reasoning *processes* rather than just outputs, preferences, or values—as a distinct alignment objective. It departs from the dominant bottom-up preference-alignment paradigm (RLHF, DPO, Constitutional AI) by arguing that matching what humans choose is insufficient; the AI must also reason *the way* humans reason and faithfully communicate that reasoning. Conceptually, it draws on explainable AI (arguing xAI is necessary but not sufficient), cognitive science and behavioral economics (for process-tracing and computational modeling of human decision-making), and interactive machine learning (for elicitation), but synthesizes these into a unified alignment framework that no prior work has explicitly proposed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete adoption barrier: even accurate AI may be rejected in high-stakes domains if its reasoning feels foreign to the user, and no existing alignment methodology is designed to address this. The pilot survey (n=150) provides preliminary but directionally strong evidence—86.6% of participants could imagine preferring Human-Reasoning AI, and the preference ranking shows reasoning alignment outweighs modifiability of machine reasoning. Key open questions remain: what level of cognitive abstraction to target, how to quantify alignment robustly, how much deviation is tolerable, and how to resolve conflicts between stated reasoning and revealed preferences. The paper's main limitation is that it is a position paper with a small US-only pilot study; no cognitively-aligned system is built or evaluated, so the research agenda remains aspirational.
