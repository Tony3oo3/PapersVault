## Problem

Existing theory on feature learning strength (FLS) suggests that stronger feature learning universally improves generalization, yet practical observations show that intermediate FLS values perform best. This gap is compounded by the fact that most theoretical analyses study the limiting (infinite-time) solution, whereas in practice training is stopped at a target training risk. The paper asks whether stronger FLS always helps generalization under finite-time training, and if not, provides a theoretical explanation for the observed U-shaped generalization curve.

## Input / Output

Empirically, the method takes standard RGB images (32×32) from CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, or BigGAN-generated synthetic datasets, along with a chosen architecture (VGG19, ResNet-18/34/50, or a 5-layer CNN), and sweeps over the output multiplier c (controlling FLS) and the pre-normalized learning rate η to produce test-accuracy heatmaps on the (c, η/c) plane. Theoretically, the input is a two-layer bias-free ReLU network trained via gradient flow with logistic loss on a binary Gaussian mixture, with FLS controlled by the initialization scale α; the output is an excess error bound decomposed into an over-alignment term OA(α) and an over-fitting term OF(α), from which an optimal α is derived.

## Method

The paper combines empirical sweeps with a theoretical analysis of gradient flow in two-layer ReLU networks. Empirically, models are trained until near-perfect training accuracy (>99%) across a grid of output multipliers and learning rates, revealing a consistent U-shaped generalization curve where both excessively small and large FLS hurt test accuracy, with the benefit of the optimal FLS growing with dataset intrinsic dimensionality. Theoretically, the analysis leverages the two-phase dynamics (neuron alignment followed by margin maximization) to show that the initialization scale α governs the angular deviation of the effective predictor from the class-mean direction: small α drives the predictor into a narrow cone H(α) around the empirical class mean, creating an irreducible over-alignment gap to the Bayes direction, while large α widens H(α), increasing the over-fitting term through a larger hypothesis-space volume. The main theorem (Theorem 5.6) upper-bounds the excess error as OA(α) + OF(α), where OA decreases and OF increases with α, establishing the existence of a data-dependent optimal FLS. The theory further predicts a scaling law c* ≈ O(n^{-2}h^{-1}) for the optimal output multiplier, which is validated empirically across widths and dataset sizes.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 with ResNet50, the optimal FLS (output multiplier c=2^{-4}) achieves 59.76% peak test accuracy, surpassing the default scale (c=2^{0}) by approximately 6 percentage points (53.57%). The paper demonstrates a U-shaped generalization curve: both excessively small and excessively large FLS degrade test accuracy, with the optimum lying in between. On a harder BigGAN-generated dataset (effective dimension 128) with ResNet18, the gap between optimal and default FLS widens further (≈76% vs. ≈63% peak accuracy), confirming that the benefit of FLS tuning grows with task difficulty. Theoretically, the excess error decomposes into an over-alignment term OA(α) (decreasing in α) and an over-fitting term OF(α) (increasing in α), whose trade-off yields a data-dependent optimal FLS scaling as c* ≈ O(n^{-2}h^{-1}).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external methods; instead, it compares different FLS settings (output multipliers c) as the primary axis of variation. The key finding is that the default initialization scale (c=2^{0}) is consistently suboptimal: on CIFAR-100 with ResNet50, it yields 53.57% test accuracy versus 59.76% at the optimal c=2^{-4}. Conversely, extremely small FLS (large c, e.g., c=2^{2}) also degrades performance (49.78% in Table 1), confirming the U-shaped curve. The optimal FLS is robust to the stopping criterion—whether training runs for a fixed 80 epochs or uses early stopping on validation loss, the same c=2^{-6} to c=2^{-4} range remains optimal. The paper further shows that the optimal FLS is transferable across network widths and dataset sizes, following the predicted scaling c* ≈ O(n^{-2}h^{-1}), which aligns with the theoretical error bound from Theorem 5.6.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the two-phase gradient flow analysis of Min et al. (2024) and Boursier & Flammarion (2025) for two-layer ReLU networks, adopting their neuron-alignment and margin-maximization decomposition, and on the orthogonal-separability framework of Phuong & Lampert (2021). It departs from the dominant narrative in the FLS literature (Woodworth et al., 2020; Atanasov et al., 2025), which concludes that stronger feature learning monotonically improves generalization, by showing that this holds only in the asymptotic/limiting regime and breaks down under practical finite-time training. The key conceptual departure is the decomposition of excess error into an over-alignment term (growing as FLS increases) and an over-fitting term (growing as FLS decreases), which has no direct analogue in prior FLS-generalization analyses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that excessively strong feature learning degrades generalization directly contradicts a widely held assumption in the deep learning theory community and suggests that FLS (or equivalently, initialization scale / output multiplier) should be treated as a first-class hyperparameter alongside learning rate. The practical impact is concrete: the paper demonstrates ~6% test-accuracy gains on CIFAR-100/ResNet50 from tuning FLS alone, with benefits growing as task intrinsic dimensionality increases. However, the theoretical analysis is confined to two-layer ReLU networks under orthogonal separability and a small-norm regime, leaving open whether the over-alignment/over-fitting trade-off persists in deeper architectures, under SGD with momentum, or in transformer settings. Relaxing the orthogonal-separability assumption and extending the analysis to stochastic optimization are identified as key future directions.
