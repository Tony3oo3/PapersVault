## Problem

Prior work established that scaling language models improves fMRI brain alignment, but left open what drives these gains and whether the relationship is monotonic. It remained unclear what minimal model capacity is sufficient for brain-relevant representations, and whether post-training compression (quantization, pruning) preserves the representational geometry needed for neural predictivity. This paper systematically probes these questions by treating model scale and numerical precision as controlled interventions on representational structure.

## Input / Output

The method consumes word-level text transcripts from 11 naturalistic Moth Radio Hour stories (processed with a 20-word sliding context window) and voxel-wise fMRI recordings (TR = 2.0045 s, downsampled via 3-lobed Lanczos filter with 4-TR HRF delays) from 9 participants. Model hidden states are PCA-projected to a shared 1024-dimensional space before encoding. Outputs include voxel-wise normalized brain alignment (Pearson correlation divided by cross-subject ceiling, restricted to voxels with ceiling ≥ 0.05), brain-to-text reconstructions scored by BLEU-1/WER/METEOR/BERT-F1, and per-category linguistic competence scores across 66 FlashHolmes tasks spanning morphology, syntax, semantics, discourse, and reasoning.

## Method

The paper evaluates pretrained base (non-instruction-tuned) transformers from three families (LLaMA-3.2, Qwen-2.5, DeepSeek-R1) at 1B–14B parameters, extracting layer-wise hidden states via a 20-word sliding window and selecting the single best-performing layer per model. Voxel-wise encoding uses bootstrap ridge regression (L2 regularization tuned via 10% validation split) on PCA-reduced features, with normalized alignment computed as Pearson correlation divided by an estimated cross-subject prediction ceiling. Compression is applied post-training without retraining: three quantization schemes (AWQ, GPTQ, SmoothQuant) and unstructured L1-magnitude pruning at 10%, 25%, and 50% sparsity. Linguistic competence is probed independently via linear classifiers on FlashHolmes tasks, enabling a dissociation analysis between task accuracy and brain predictivity. Brain decoding follows the BrainLLM pipeline to reconstruct text from fMRI, confirming that 3B models support stable semantic reconstruction comparable to 8B models.

## Results

Across three model families (Qwen2.5, LLaMA-3.2, DeepSeek-R1), 3B SLMs achieve normalized brain alignment statistically indistinguishable from 7B–14B LLMs (Qwen2.5: 3B vs. 14B, Δ=0.000, p=1.00), while 1.5B models degrade significantly (3B vs. 1.5B: Δ=0.073, p=0.004). For compression of Qwen2.5-3B, AWQ (0.933) and SmoothQuant (0.930) preserve or slightly exceed full-precision FP16 alignment (0.924), whereas GPTQ consistently degrades it (0.910, p<0.05). Unstructured pruning maintains alignment through 25% sparsity (0.908) with only marginal loss at 50% (0.907), confirming that brain alignment saturates at ~3B parameters and is robust to most post-training compression.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three post-training quantization methods (AWQ, GPTQ, SmoothQuant) and unstructured pruning against the full-precision FP16 baseline for brain alignment. AWQ and SmoothQuant both preserve or marginally exceed FP16 alignment (0.933 and 0.930 vs. 0.924 for Qwen2.5-3B), with AWQ showing a statistically significant improvement over GPTQ (Δ=0.024, p<0.05). GPTQ is the consistent outlier, producing a reliable degradation (0.910 vs. 0.924 FP16; Δ=0.014, p≈0.08 for 3B; Δ=0.020, p<0.001 for 7B) with widespread voxel-level losses especially in semantic regions like the angular gyrus. Pruning at 10–25% sparsity preserves alignment within ~1.5% of FP16, but 50% sparsity introduces a small additional drop (0.907 vs. 0.924) that becomes more pronounced for 1.5B models. Across all three model families, the qualitative pattern holds: well-designed quantization (AWQ/SmoothQuant) maintains brain-relevant representations, while GPTQ disproportionately disrupts them, and sub-3B capacity is the primary bottleneck rather than compression per se.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the brain-encoding line of work initiated by Schrimpf et al. (2021) and Antonello et al. (2024), which established that larger language models yield progressively better fMRI predictivity. It departs from that monotonic-scaling narrative by showing a plateau at ~3B parameters and by repurposing post-training compression (quantization, pruning) as a controlled perturbation on representational geometry rather than merely an engineering optimization. The FlashHolmes linguistic-probing component builds on Waldis et al. (2024) but is applied here for the first time to test whether compression-induced competence losses translate into neural misalignment, a question prior compression literature (evaluated on perplexity or benchmark accuracy) has not addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 3B SLMs match 14B LLMs in brain alignment, and that most quantization schemes preserve this alignment, directly lowers the computational barrier for NeuroAI research and positions compact models as principled tools for studying brain-language computations. The dissociation between degraded linguistic competence (discourse, syntax, morphology) and preserved brain predictivity under AWQ/SmoothQuant challenges the assumption that brain alignment requires full linguistic competence, opening questions about which representational properties are actually neural-relevant. Limitations include a single fMRI dataset (Moth Radio Hour, 9 subjects), three model families, and the absence of instruction-tuned or RLHF-trained checkpoints; whether the 3B saturation generalizes beyond 14B parameters or to other modalities (speech, vision) remains open.
