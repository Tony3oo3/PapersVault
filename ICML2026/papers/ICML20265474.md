## Problem

Existing multivariate independence tests face a fundamental trade-off: kernel-based methods like HSIC require careful kernel selection and rely on RKHS universality assumptions, while distance covariance down-weights high-frequency characteristic function components, reducing power for detecting complex nonlinear and local dependencies. No prior method provides a distribution-free, kernel-free test that is simultaneously computationally scalable in high dimensions, adaptive to unknown dependence structures, and interpretable at the coordinate level.

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired multivariate observations (X_i, Y_i) ∈ R^p × R^q, which are first mapped to [-1,1]^p × [-1,1]^q via componentwise probability-integral transforms, along with a user-specified binary expansion depth K (typically 3–6) and a fold count M (default 10) for adaptive weighting. It produces a standardized Z-statistic calibrated to N(0,1) under H₀ without requiring permutation, a corresponding p-value, and a binary reject/fail-to-reject decision at level α. As an interpretability byproduct, the method can output pairwise Z-statistic heatmaps across coordinate pairs, with multiple-testing correction via Benjamini-Hochberg FDR.

## Method

The core insight is to represent joint and marginal characteristic functions via truncated binary expansions of the probability-integral-transformed variables, which (Theorem 2.1) reduces the independence test to testing whether a collection of cross-covariances m_{I,J} = Cov(A_{i,I}, B_{i,J}) among exponentially many binary interaction coefficients all vanish. To avoid explicitly constructing the 2^{pK}−1 dimensional feature vectors, the paper derives an unbiased U-statistic T_n^{(W)} = T_{1n} − 2T_{2n} + T_{3n} and, crucially, a kernel representation (Theorem 2.2) that evaluates all required inner products in O(pK) time via pairwise bit products, reducing complexity from O(2^{dK}) to O(dK). Three test variants are offered: CoBET (identity weights, aggregating all interactions equally), dCoBET (distance-covariance-motivated positive semidefinite weights that encode multiscale structure), and wa-dCoBET, which adaptively blends the two via foldwise signal-to-noise-ratio voting over M data splits. Under H₀ with K→∞, the standardized statistic converges to N(0,1) (Theorem 2.4), yielding asymptotically exact Gaussian calibration without permutation, and the truncation depth K provides a principled signal-to-noise trade-off by discarding high-order interactions that contribute more noise than signal.

## Results

Under the challenging LogQuad nonlinear dependence (d=10, n=500), wa-dCoBET achieves power of 0.981 at b=0.20 and 0.989 at b=0.30, while dCoBET lags at 0.651 and 0.760 respectively; CoBET is competitive at 0.975 and 0.991. All proposed methods maintain empirical Type I error near the nominal 5% level (0.051–0.056 for wa-dCoBET at b=0). On the corneal epithelial thickness real-data application, wa-dCoBET yields a global test statistic Z=5.749 (p=4.47×10⁻⁹) for the central+inner ring region, substantially stronger evidence than HSIC (p=4.99×10⁻⁴) and dCov (p=9.9×10⁻⁴).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against HSIC and dCov, the proposed methods (especially wa-dCoBET and CoBET) achieve substantially higher power in high-dimensional (d=10) and non-monotone (LogQuad) settings, reaching near-unit power at b=0.20 where HSIC and dCov remain well below 0.8; on the real corneal data, wa-dCoBET's p-value is roughly 1000× smaller than HSIC's. RDC is competitive in low dimension (d=2) but loses sensitivity in d=10, while Xi-mean (Chatterjee's rank correlation) is consistently the weakest baseline across all settings. MultiFIT becomes competitive only at moderate-to-strong signals under LogQuad but is outperformed at weak signals where wa-dCoBET already achieves >0.9 power. In the sinexp comparison (Appendix A.3), CoBET and dCoBET outperform MMD-DUAL (Zhou et al., 2025) at weak signals, and MMD-DUAL shows slightly inflated Type I error. In the univariate dense-signal regime (Appendix A.5), wa-dCoBET achieves 0.948 power at b=0.1 versus only 0.494 for Zhang's Max BET, confirming the advantage of aggregation over max-type statistics when dependence is spread across many binary interactions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Zhang's (2019) univariate binary-expansion independence test (BET) to the multivariate setting by establishing a general equivalence between independence testing and cross-covariance testing of exponentially many binary expansion interaction coefficients. The U-statistic construction adapts the high-dimensional covariance testing framework of Chen et al. (2010), while the adaptive aggregation mechanism (wa-dCoBET) borrows the foldwise signal-to-noise-ratio voting strategy from He et al. (2023). A key conceptual departure from kernel-based methods (HSIC) is that the binary expansion representation operates over general sample spaces without requiring a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, and the paper further reveals that distance covariance (Székely et al., 2007) is equivalent to a specific weighted quadratic form of binary expansion coefficients, unifying two previously separate lines of work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a distribution-free, kernel-free multivariate independence test with a clean theoretical foundation (asymptotic normality without permutation) and a computational reduction from O(2^{dK}) to O(dK) that makes the method practical for moderate dimensions. The interpretability advantage—identifying which binary expansion components drive dependence upon rejection—offers a diagnostic tool absent from HSIC or distance covariance. Limitations include the requirement for continuous marginals (probability integral transform), the need to select the resolution depth K, and the fact that the method's advantage over strong baselines like HSIC and dCov is most pronounced in high-dimensional and non-monotone settings but modest in simpler cases. Open questions include extension to discrete or mixed-type data, behavior under very high dimensions (d > 100), and whether the binary expansion framework can be adapted to conditional independence testing.
