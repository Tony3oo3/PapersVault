## Problem

LLM tool-use agents trained via SFT or RL achieve near-perfect success on static benchmarks, but real-world deployment involves non-stationary tool schemas, evolving APIs, ambiguous queries, and shifting interaction dynamics. This paper formalizes the OpenAgent setting to systematically diagnose how distributional shifts across query, action, observation, and domain dimensions degrade agent performance, and identifies distinct failure modes for each training paradigm.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265038/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent operates in a sequential decision loop: at each step it receives a user query, a set of tool definitions (JSON schemas with name, description, and parameters), and the running history of prior actions and observations, then emits exactly one tool call per turn as a JSON object with 'name' and 'arguments' keys. The final answer is wrapped in <answer></answer> tags, and the agent is expected to explicitly refuse when a task is unsolvable. All evaluation is conducted in a controlled Python sandbox over anonymized Beijing POI data (randomized names and phone numbers) to prevent parameter memorization, with strict train/test database isolation.

## Method

The paper contributes a four-tier diagnostic framework (Perception, Interaction, Reasoning, Internalization) that injects controlled perturbations—symbolic drift, semantic traps, format shifts, error/null returns, rule changes, logic inversion, fatal errors, and holistic domain shifts—into a sandbox to isolate each generalization axis. Key findings show SFT suffers from brittle symbolic anchoring and open-loop trajectory inertia, while RL exhibits boundary blindness from a teleological bias in outcome-oriented rewards. To mitigate SFT's fragility, the authors propose Perturbation-Augmented Fine-Tuning (PAFT), which augments clean trajectories with three trajectory-level operators: Environmental Feedback Perturbation (injecting mid-trajectory anomalies and supervising corrective continuations), Solvability Boundary Perturbation (constructing fatal-error samples that teach explicit refusal), and Symbolic Representation Perturbation (synonymous rewriting and noise injection on tool identifiers). With a default perturbation ratio of α=0.3, PAFT restores near-closed-set accuracy on Tiers 1–3 and lifts refusal rate from ~0% to >97% on Tier 4, confirming that each component targets a distinct failure mode.

## Results

On the POI Multi-step Tool-calling Dataset, standard SFT (800 steps) suffers severe open-environment degradation: Tier-1 Accuracy Δ drops to −50.4, Tier-2 to −45.3, Tier-3 to −28.0, and Tier-4 Refusal Rate collapses to 0.2%. PAFT (α=0.3) recovers most of this gap, yielding Tier-1 Δ of −4.1, Tier-2 Δ of −5.3, Tier-3 Δ of −9.8, and Tier-4 RR of 99.6%. For early-stage models (SFT-200), PAFT transforms severe degradation into positive deltas (+28.6, +26.5, +22.7 across Tiers 1–3), demonstrating that perturbation-augmented training can preemptively prevent overfitting to clean trajectories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against specific named methods but instead contrasts two training paradigms (full-parameter SFT vs. GRPO-based RL) on the same Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct backbone under identical computational budgets. RL consistently outperforms SFT on Tier-1 perception (lower TER, higher accuracy retention under symbolic drift and identity erasure) and Tier-2 interaction (maintains adaptability under explicit tool redirection where SFT hallucinates success). However, both paradigms collapse under Tier-3 Logic Inversion (causal dependency reversal) and Tier-4 Active Refusal (both achieve near-zero refusal rates). PAFT, applied to SFT, narrows the SFT-vs-RL gap substantially: at 800 steps, PAFT-equipped SFT reaches −4.1/−5.3/−9.8 Accuracy Δ across Tiers 1–3, approaching RL's performance, while achieving 99.6% Refusal Rate versus RL's near-zero, effectively resolving the boundary-blindness failure mode that affects both paradigms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formalizing the OpenAgent problem setting, which extends the distributional-shift literature from passive models (Guo et al., 2020; Zhou, 2022) to multi-step agentic tool-use where perturbations cascade along trajectories rather than affecting independent inputs. It departs from prior tool-use evaluation work (ROTBench, ToolEyes, BFCL) that focuses on closed-set benchmarks or inference-time perturbations, instead systematically probing training-to-testing transfer under non-stationary environments. The diagnostic four-tier hierarchy (Perception, Interaction, Reasoning, Internalization) is novel in its structured decomposition of where generalization breaks down, and the PAFT method repurposes data augmentation principles from robustness literature into trajectory-level intervention for SFT agents.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of distinct structural failure modes—symbolic anchoring in SFT and teleological bias in RL—provides actionable diagnostics for practitioners deploying agents in production, where APIs evolve and user queries are non-canonical. The finding that both paradigms collapse under logic inversion suggests a fundamental limitation in how current training encodes dependency graphs as fixed action orders rather than dynamic logical structures. However, the evaluation is confined to a single 7B model on a synthetic POI sandbox with relatively simple multi-hop queries, leaving open whether these findings and the PAFT remedy transfer to larger models, more complex tool ecosystems, or genuinely unstable real-world APIs. The teleological bias in RL (rewarding completion over honest refusal) raises important safety questions for autonomous agent deployment that warrant further investigation.
