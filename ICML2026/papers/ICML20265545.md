## Problem

Existing reasoning benchmarks evaluate deductive, inductive, and abductive reasoning in isolation on static tasks, failing to capture the integrated, iterative reasoning cycle (abduction → deduction → induction) that Charles Peirce identified as essential for human discovery in unknown environments. This paper introduces black-box environment interaction as a novel evaluation paradigm where LLMs must actively explore a hidden function through multi-turn queries, testing whether they can develop and adaptively refine hypotheses under a limited exploration budget.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265545/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The LLM receives a natural-language task description specifying the black-box environment's input domain and interaction rules, then generates adaptive queries (e.g., input variable assignments, circuit wire configurations, plaintext strings, or game moves) over T exploration turns, receiving the corresponding output from the hidden function after each query. After exploration, the model is presented with K unseen test samples and must predict the black-box output for each, receiving binary correctness feedback per sample. The two experiment settings are 10@1 (10 exploration turns, 1 evaluation attempt) and 20@2 (20 exploration turns, 2 evaluation attempts), with accuracy computed as the fraction of correct test predictions.

## Method

The ORACLE benchmark comprises 6 task types—Code Intent Inference, Circuit Rule Inference, Physics System Inference, Encryption Rule Inference, Interactive Puzzle Inference, and Game Strategy Inference—totaling 96 black-box environments (51 easy, 45 hard), each defined by a hidden function f: X→Y that may decompose into a composition of sub-mappings with observable intermediate checkpoints. To enable scalable generation, the authors design a three-module agentic framework (Coding LLM, Test LLM, Refinement LLM) that produces complete platform code, simulates interaction, and iteratively debugs based on runtime feedback, requiring only a natural-language description of the new environment. The paper further derives information-theoretic lower bounds and constructive upper bounds on the minimum number of queries needed per task type, introducing an exploration efficiency ratio η = T*/T_used to quantify how far model behavior deviates from theoretical optimality. Key empirical findings show that even the best model (o3) drops below 40% on hard environments, and a comparative experiment with and without instant feedback reveals that LLMs fail to adaptively optimize their exploration strategy, operating at Tier 1–2 of a three-tier planning taxonomy where Tier 3 (near-optimal adaptive planning) remains exclusive to humans.

## Results

On the ORACLE benchmark (96 black-box environments across 6 tasks), o3 achieves the best overall performance, ranking first in 5 of 6 tasks under 10 exploration turns and 4 of 6 under 20 turns, with average accuracy exceeding 70% on easy environments and approximately 40% on hard ones. Reasoning models (e.g., claude-4-sonnet_thinking, gemini-2.5-pro) consistently outperform their non-thinking counterparts by 10–25% accuracy. Among open-weight models, deepseek-r1-671b achieves the top overall performance, though it still trails proprietary reasoning models by a wide margin on hard tasks (e.g., ~13% vs. ~52% on GSI Hard at 20@2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 19 LLMs (proprietary and open-weight) against each other on the ORACLE benchmark. o3 (OpenAI) dominates, ranking first in 5/6 tasks at 10 turns and 4/6 at 20 turns, outperforming gemini-2.5-pro, claude-3.7-sonnet_thinking, and claude-4-sonnet_thinking which form the next tier of competitive models. Among open-weight models, deepseek-r1-671b leads but trails o3 by 20–40+ points on hard tasks (e.g., 13% vs. 52% on GSI Hard at 20@2). Reasoning models with extended thinking (claude-4-sonnet_thinking, gemini-2.5-flash_thinking) consistently beat their non-thinking counterparts by 10–25% across tasks, with the gap widening on harder environments. Compared to human evaluators, SOTA LLMs fall significantly short on hard tasks (e.g., 13% vs. 100% on PSI Hard, 13% vs. 67% on ERI Hard at 20@2), while approaching or matching human performance on easy tasks (e.g., 98% vs. 100% on IPI Easy). The key finding is that even the best LLMs (o3) fail to develop adaptive exploration strategies, with performance gains from doubling exploration turns (10→20) being negligible on ERI and GSI tasks, indicating a fundamental planning limitation rather than a simple budget constraint.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from both static reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, ARC, BIGBench Hard) that evaluate deductive, inductive, and abductive reasoning in isolation, and from game-based interactive evaluations (GameBench, GameArena, SmartPlay, ORAK) that the authors argue conflate reasoning with spatial understanding and suffer from data contamination. It draws conceptually on Charles Peirce's integrated reasoning cycle (abduction → deduction → induction) and on the contamination-free properties of dynamic benchmarks (LiveBench, LiveCodeBench), but combines these into a new interactive paradigm where a hidden function is the sole source of information. The agentic generation framework for constructing black-box environments repurposes LLM-based code generation and iterative debugging patterns from software engineering, while the theoretical analysis connects to classical query complexity and exact learning theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ORACLE benchmark provides a contamination-resistant, scalable way to evaluate integrated reasoning that prior static benchmarks cannot capture, and the agentic framework enables near-unlimited expansion of task variety and difficulty. The central finding—that even SOTA reasoning models (o3) universally operate at Tier 1–2 of exploration strategy and cannot adaptively refine hypotheses based on real-time feedback—suggests a fundamental architectural limitation in current LLM training paradigms (next-token prediction + RL on CoT) that no amount of prompting or extended thinking can overcome. Open questions include whether training specifically for multi-turn interactive exploration could close the gap, whether the 'aha moment' observed at 30 turns (Table 7) indicates a phase transition in reasoning, and whether the benchmark's 96 environments are sufficient for statistical significance. Limitations include the inability to analyze closed-source model internals, the absence of statistical testing, and the restriction to single LLMs rather than LLM-based agents.
