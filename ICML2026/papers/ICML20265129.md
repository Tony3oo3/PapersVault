## Problem

While LLMs exhibit strong multilingual generation, they suffer from unintended code-switching where languages are mixed within a single utterance. Prior work identified language-related neurons and features but lacked a causal, circuit-level account of how language selection is implemented during generation, what internal components give rise to code-switching, and how to intervene to mitigate it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265129/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes an unmodified transformer model and contrastive text pairs that share identical semantic content but differ in language (e.g., 'Angola is located in' vs. 'Angola liegt in'), constructed by translating and reformulating CounterFact knowledge triplets. It produces hierarchical circuits of MLP neurons (typically fewer than 50 per language) organized into language-specific and semantic super-neurons, along with LLM-generated textual descriptions of each neuron's activation patterns. As a downstream application, selectively fine-tuning only the identified language-specific neurons (~0.019% of all neurons) on monolingual Aya data yields a model with reduced code-switching while preserving general capabilities.

## Method

The paper introduces a three-stage scalable circuit discovery framework. First, Hierarchical Attribution Patching extends standard attribution patching by recursively tracing component-to-component edge attributions from the output back toward the embedding layer (max depth L=5, threshold ε=0.001), isolating each edge by patching only the early neuron's output into the late neuron's input while freezing others, under a linearity assumption maintained by projecting onto the residual stream. Second, neuron descriptions are generated by combining static projection patterns (dot products with the unembedding matrix) with dynamic activation patterns (top-k activating tokens across a multilingual corpus), then summarized by an LLM into interpretable profiles. Third, functionally similar neurons are clustered into super-neurons via LLM-based classification into pre-defined categories (e.g., 'French', 'Horror'), yielding a compact circuit with two functional regimes: a language regime that tracks contextual language identity and a semantic regime encoding language-agnostic content. The key mechanistic finding is that code-switching arises as a regime shift where semantic super-neurons bypass the language regime, reducing the language-to-semantic attribution ratio from 2.37× (normal) to 1.21× (code-switched), and that suppressing semantic activations at the confusion point reduces the code-switched token probability by 99.67%.

## Results

Deactivating only 0.018% of language-specific neurons causes severe, selective performance drops on XLSum (up to 97.3% for French in LLaMA, 93.9% for Chinese in Qwen), confirming causal necessity. In non-code-switched generations, language circuits dominate with 2.37× (Qwen) and 3.10× (LLaMA) higher attribution than semantic circuits; this ratio collapses to 1.21× and 1.01× in code-switched cases. Fine-tuning only ~0.019% of neurons (language-specific) raises Line-level Pass Rate from 93.45%→97.30% on Qwen2.5-7B and 95.99%→97.36% on LLaMA3.1-8B, while random-neuron fine-tuning yields no improvement. Suppressing semantic circuits at the confusion point reduces the code-switched token probability by 99.67% (0.06→0.0002) and boosts the intended-language token by 67.68% (0.40→0.66).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between three intervention strategies: no intervention (baseline), fine-tuning an equivalent number of randomly selected neurons (N_Rand), and fine-tuning only the identified language-specific neurons (N_Lang-Spec). On Qwen2.5-7B, N_Lang-Spec raises average LPR from 93.45% (baseline) to 97.30%, a +3.85 pp gain, while N_Rand actually slightly degrades performance to 93.02%. On LLaMA3.1-8B, N_Lang-Spec improves from 95.99% to 97.36% (+1.37 pp), whereas N_Rand yields 95.83% (−0.16 pp). The gains are consistent across all eight languages, with the largest improvements in Indonesian (Qwen: 82.96%→92.60%; LLaMA: 83.50%→88.20%) and Japanese (Qwen: 88.78%→98.49%). The paper also reports (in Appendix A.3.8) that full-parameter SFT and DPO baselines are outperformed by the targeted neuron fine-tuning at a fraction of the parameter budget (~0.019% of all neurons), and that few-shot prompting provides no comparable mitigation. On the broader Arabic/Japanese task suite (Table 5), fine-tuning just 50 Arabic-specific neurons increases Language Consistency Rate by 21.29% on Qwen and 17.06% on LLaMA, again with random-neuron controls showing no such benefit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends attribution patching (Nanda, 2022; Kramár et al., 2024; Syed et al., 2023) from single-step component-to-output tracing to iterative hierarchical circuit discovery, enabling end-to-end extraction of neuron-level causal circuits without additional training. It departs from prior multilingual interpretability work (Wendler et al., 2024; Dumas et al., 2025) that proposed a sequential 'language-then-concept' mechanism, instead revealing parallel language and semantic pathways whose competition explains code-switching. The neuron grouping into super-neurons follows Lindsey et al. (2025), and the key-value memory perspective of feed-forward layers (Geva et al., 2021) motivates the combined projection-and-activation neuron description approach.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first circuit-level mechanistic account of unintended code-switching, showing it arises from a regime shift where semantic pathways suppress language control rather than from direct inter-language competition. The practical implication—that fine-tuning only ~0.019% of neurons reduces code-switching by 20.8%—offers a highly targeted mitigation strategy for multilingual LLM developers. Key limitations include the focus on last-token MLP neurons (potentially missing attention-head and cross-token dynamics), reliance on LLM-generated neuron descriptions without extensive human validation, and evaluation primarily on 7B–8B models. Open questions include whether the regime-shift mechanism generalizes to larger models, whether extending the framework to attention components changes the picture, and how multilingual control interacts with reasoning and other high-level capabilities.
