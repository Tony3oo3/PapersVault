## Problem

Recent experiments have shown that fine-tuning datasets can transmit hidden behavioral signals—such as language preferences or personas—that are not observable from individual datapoints, yet no general mechanism explains why or how these subliminal effects arise. This paper asks whether a single, principled account can unify a broad suite of such phenomena across different target behaviors and model architectures.

## Input / Output

The method takes a real-world preference dataset of (prompt, chosen response, rejected response) triples, a teacher model (which may differ in architecture from the student), a target system prompt string, and a quantile threshold γ. It outputs a filtered subset of the original dataset (typically the top 5–10% of examples by a computed weight). The filtered subset is then used to fine-tune a student model via DPO; at inference time, the student model—given no system prompt—produces text responses that exhibit the target behavior (e.g., speaking Spanish, mentioning a specific animal, or adopting an evil ruler persona). Responses in the dataset are truncated (20–32 tokens) to focus on short-generation effects, and examples containing the target language or animal are pre-filtered out.

## Method

The core mechanism, Logit-Linear Selection (LLS), is grounded in the empirical observation that LLM log-probabilities are approximately low-logit-rank, admitting a factorization log Pr_M[r|s,p] ≈ ⟨ψ(s), φ(p,r)⟩ where the prompt-response embedding φ is approximately universal across models. For each preference example, LLS computes a weight measuring how much the target system prompt s shifts the teacher model's log-odds in favor of the chosen response over the rejected response, length-normalizes by total token count, and retains the top γ-fraction of positively weighted examples. Fine-tuning a student model on this subset via DPO is interpreted as updating only the ψ(∅) embedding while keeping φ fixed, so the filtered data pushes ψ(∅) toward the direction ψ(s) − ψ(∅), making the student behave as if system-prompted with s. A formal theorem (Theorem D.1) proves that under log-linearity and well-behaved embedding assumptions, any approximate DPO optimizer on the LLS subset must have constant correlation with the system-prompted reference model's preferences. Unlike prior subliminal-learning work that relies on bespoke or artificially generated datasets (e.g., random numbers), LLS operates purely by subselecting from existing real-world preference data and transfers across different teacher-student model pairs.

## Results

When the teacher and student models are the same (OLMo2-1B-Instruct), LLS achieves correlations of 0.47–0.57 between the fine-tuned model's preference shifts and the system-prompted base model's preference shifts across 10 animals (Table 1), confirming Theorem 2.2's prediction of constant correlation. When the teacher differs from the student (Qwen3-8B → OLMo2-1B-Instruct), correlations drop to 0.03–0.14 but remain positive, demonstrating cross-architecture transfer. In the animal preference experiment with matching models, the fine-tuned student mentions the target animal at frequencies approaching the system-prompted baseline (e.g., near the red bar in Figure 4), while the base model without a system prompt rarely mentions the animal. For the evil ruler persona, LLS fine-tuned models generate evil responses at rates comparable to or exceeding the system-prompted baseline across all three student models (rnj-1-Instruct, Gemma-7B-Instruct, Olmo3-7B-Instruct).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LLS fine-tuned models against three baselines: (1) the base model with no system prompt, (2) the base model with the target system prompt, and (3) a model fine-tuned on a random subset of the same size as the LLS subset. LLS consistently outperforms both the no-system-prompt baseline and the random-subset baseline across all three experimental domains (animal preference, language translation, and evil ruler persona). In the animal preference experiment with matching teacher-student models, LLS fine-tuned models approach the system-prompted baseline's animal mention frequency, while the random-subset baseline shows no such effect. For the evil ruler persona, LLS fine-tuned models generate evil responses at rates comparable to or higher than the system-prompted baseline, with the excess largely attributable to the system-prompted model producing refusals due to safety tuning. Cross-architecture transfer (different teacher and student) yields weaker but still statistically significant effects, with correlations dropping from ~0.54 to ~0.08 on average, confirming that the mechanism is universal but strongest within the same model family.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the low-logit-rank framework of Golowich et al. (2025b), which established that LLM log-probabilities exhibit approximate linear structure observable at the output level. It departs from prior subliminal-learning demonstrations (Cloud et al., 2025; Betley et al., 2025a,b) that relied on bespoke or artificially constructed datasets (e.g., sequences of random numbers) by showing that a simple log-probability-based filtering criterion applied to any real-world preference dataset suffices to elicit hidden behaviors. The paper also connects to the broader linear-representation hypothesis literature in mechanistic interpretability, but uniquely grounds its method in input-output log-probabilities rather than internal activations, making it architecture-agnostic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first general, mathematically grounded mechanism explaining a broad class of previously mysterious subliminal effects, with implications for data curation safety, adversarial robustness, and potential positive applications like dataset watermarking. Key open questions include: what factors govern the strength of cross-architecture transfer (the effect is notably weaker when teacher ≠ student), how to develop practical defenses or detection methods for subliminal signals in datasets, and whether the log-linear abstraction can be tightened to give entry-wise bounds rather than aggregate correlation guarantees. A notable limitation is that cross-architecture transfer can cause model degeneration (repetitive/gibberish outputs) during training, suggesting the shared embedding space is only approximate.
