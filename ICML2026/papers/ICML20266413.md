## Problem

Current agentic AI systems are designed and evaluated under sighted-user assumptions—low-cost verification, tolerable trial-and-error, and shared visual context—that systematically break down for BVI users, producing silent failures, overconfident hallucinations, and miscalibrated autonomy. The paper argues that accessibility is not a peripheral usability concern or a downstream consequence of general capability scaling, but a distinct alignment objective that must be explicitly addressed in agent design, evaluation, and deployment.

## Input / Output

The empirical foundation is a systematic review of 417 publications (2012–2025) distilled into 778 fine-grained assistive task instances, organized into four categories: Mobility and Safety (34%), Reading and Text Access (35%), Object Recognition and Daily Operations, and VQA Goal-directed Query. The proposed pipeline takes an assistive need as input and produces six design artifacts (Task Card, Accessibility Success Specification, Interaction Contract, Risk and Uncertainty Policy, Privacy Manifest, Autonomy Calibration Specification) that translate into runtime guardrails, safety-aware evaluation metrics, and post-deployment feedback loops.

## Method

The core contribution is a four-dimensional accessibility alignment framework—goal, interaction, risk, and lifecycle alignment—derived from diagnosing four environmental stressors (limited verifiability, high-cost errors, cognitive burden, privacy exposure) and four recurring failure modes (silent failures, overconfident hallucinations, miscalibrated autonomy, interaction-induced cognitive overload) in current agentic systems. To operationalize this framework, the authors propose a three-phase lifecycle pipeline: a design phase producing six specification artifacts, a deployment phase enforcing runtime guardrails and pre-deployment stress testing against an unacceptable failure set, and a post-deployment iteration phase with privacy-respecting logging, incident triage mapped to specific alignment dimensions, and safe update protocols with mandatory rollback. The pipeline is instantiated through two high-stakes case studies—navigation assistance and medication label reading—that contrast unaligned baselines with accessibility-aligned designs across red-line failures, uncertainty triggers, evaluation metric shifts, and runtime implications. The key architectural insight is that conservative behavior (abstention, pausing, escalation to human assistance) must be enforced through explicit runtime guardrails rather than left to discretionary model behavior, and that evaluation must shift from task-completion metrics (SPL, OCR accuracy) to safety- and verifiability-aware metrics (unsafe instruction rate, critical hallucination rate, abstention precision).

## Results

The paper's empirical contribution is a systematic literature review of 417 publications (2012–2025) yielding 778 fine-grained assistive task instances, organized into four categories: Reading and Text Access (35%), Mobility and Safety (34%), Object Recognition and Daily Operations (~13%), and VQA Goal-directed Query (~18%). From these instances, the authors identify four recurring failure modes in current agentic systems (silent failures, overconfident hallucinations, miscalibrated autonomy, and interaction-induced cognitive overload) and trace each to specific design-assumption mismatches. The paper proposes a four-dimensional accessibility alignment framework (goal, interaction, risk, lifecycle) and a three-phase lifecycle pipeline, validated qualitatively through two case studies (navigation and medication reading) rather than quantitative benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not benchmark a proposed method against competing baselines. Instead, it argues against two dominant alternative framings. First, it rejects the 'generalization will solve it' view, contending that increased model capability and autonomy can actually worsen accessibility outcomes when users cannot independently verify outputs (citing Chang et al. 2025; He et al. 2024; Froehlich et al. 2025). Second, it pushes back on the 'it is purely an HCI problem' framing, arguing that failures originate upstream in agent decision policies, autonomy calibration, and evaluation criteria rather than at the interface layer. The paper's 778-instance taxonomy serves as the empirical grounding for these claims, showing that failures span all four task domains and are not reducible to a single interface or model-capability fix. No head-to-head quantitative comparison with prior assistive systems (e.g., StreetReaderAI, VizWiz, GuideNav) is performed; those systems are cited as motivating examples of specific failure modes rather than as baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes 417 prior works across computer vision, generative AI, robotics, and HCI to carve out a new conceptual space: it repurposes the 'alignment' vocabulary from LLM safety to argue that accessibility for BVI users is a distinct alignment objective, not a downstream usability concern. It departs from the assistive-technology literature (screen readers, VizWiz, StreetReaderAI, electronic guide canes) by shifting the locus of failure from hardware/perception limitations to agent-level design assumptions about verification, error tolerance, and shared context. It also departs from the agentic-AI literature, which typically treats autonomy and confidence as unambiguous progress markers, by arguing these same properties become hazards when users cannot independently verify outputs. The paper does not build on a single prior method but rather positions itself at the intersection of two literatures that have largely evolved in parallel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's main impact is framing: it provides a four-dimensional taxonomy (goal, interaction, risk, lifecycle alignment) and a three-phase lifecycle pipeline that give the community a concrete vocabulary and design checklist for making agentic systems accessible, moving the discussion beyond 'add a screen reader' or 'make the model bigger.' The 778-instance task taxonomy is a useful empirical anchor that grounds the argument in recurring real-world needs rather than anecdote. Key open questions include whether the framework generalizes beyond BVI users to other disability populations, whether the proposed evaluation shifts (e.g., unsafe-instruction rate, abstention precision) can be operationalized into standardized benchmarks, and whether longitudinal deployments will confirm that conservative-by-default runtime guardrails actually sustain user trust over time. As a position paper without implementation or quantitative validation, its practical impact depends on whether the community adopts the alignment framing in subsequent system design.
