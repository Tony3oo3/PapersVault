## Problem

Existing human video generation methods tightly couple the foreground subject, background, trajectory, and action, preventing independent control over each element. This coupling forces assumptions like centrally-framed subjects near the camera, limits trajectory editing to 2D image space without physical grounding, and makes it impossible to freely compose 'anyone doing anything anywhere' in a coherent scene.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262715/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a reference subject image, a background video (with the original human masked out), a user-edited 2D trajectory in image space, a body pose/action sequence (retrieved from a motion bank or generated via text-to-motion), and a text prompt. The 2D trajectory is unprojected into 3D world space using monocular depth (Depth Pro) and focal-length calibration before rendering depth, normal, and color guidance maps. The output is a 97-frame video at 1280×720 (~6 seconds at 16 fps), generated in roughly 20 minutes on 8 H20 GPUs.

## Method

RealisMotion is a two-stage framework that decouples 3D physical motion editing from learned video synthesis. In stage 1, a ground-aware 3D world coordinate system is built via GVHMR; the user-edited 2D trajectory is unprojected to 3D with focal-length calibration, speed-aligned to the original motion, and orientation-adjusted per frame, while body/hand poses are copied from a motion bank or text-to-motion output and rendered as depth, normal, and color guidance maps. In stage 2, the Wan-2.1 14B diffusion transformer is fine-tuned with three decomposed conditioning paths: the subject image is injected as VAE tokens concatenated along the sequence dimension with shifted RoPE to create temporal distance from the video; the background video is concatenated along the channel dimension with a foreground mask; and the rendered motion guidance is encoded by a ControlNet-style branch (copied transformer blocks with zero-initialized linear projections) whose features are added to the main video features. Training uses flow matching loss with AdamW over 40K total iterations (20K at 480p/8fps, 20K at 720p/16fps), with random masking of the background and classifier-free guidance on text and subject during inference.

## Results

On the proposed Trajectory100 dataset, RealisMotion achieves the lowest translation error (1.198 m) and rotation error (0.101°), substantially outperforming the closest competitor RealisDance-DiT (1.706 m, 0.167°) and the base Wan-2.1-I2V model (10.349 m, 0.418°). It also leads on all video quality metrics: PSNR 22.57 dB, SSIM 0.7664, LPIPS 0.0686, FID 12.00, and FVD 314.59. On the RealisDance-Val action-control benchmark, RealisMotion scores PSNR 20.34, SSIM 0.7224, LPIPS 0.0998, FID 20.67, and FVD 1000.98, surpassing the best prior method (RealisDance-DiT) by 3.12 dB in PSNR and 56.6% in FVD reduction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RealisMotion wins on every metric against all eight baselines across both evaluation settings. On trajectory/orientation control (Trajectory100), it reduces translation error by 8.6× vs. Wan-2.1-I2V (1.198 m vs. 10.349 m), 4.7× vs. Tora (5.667 m), and 1.4× vs. RealisDance-DiT (1.706 m), while also improving FVD by 78%, 67%, and 58% respectively. On action control (RealisDance-Val), it outperforms the strongest image-animation baselines—RealisDance-DiT (PSNR 17.22, FVD 1576.66), MusePose (PSNR 17.29, FVD 2809.02), MimicMotion (PSNR 17.20, FVD 2283.93), Animate-X (PSNR 16.29, FVD 2376.66), MooreAA/Animate Anyone (PSNR 16.08, FVD 2446.50), and ControlNeXt (PSNR 15.66, FVD 2412.52)—by 3+ dB in PSNR and 50–64% in FVD. The largest absolute gaps appear against the text-prompt-based methods (MotionCtrl, 3DTrajMaster), which are excluded from quantitative comparison because their backgrounds and subjects are defined by text rather than explicit image/video inputs, making direct evaluation on Trajectory100 infeasible.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Wan-2.1 diffusion-transformer video generation model and extends it with three control mechanisms: token concatenation for subject identity, channel concatenation for background, and a ControlNet-style module for motion guidance. It departs from prior motion-transfer methods (Animate Anyone, DisCo, MimicMotion) that operate in 2D image or camera space and treat foreground/background and action/trajectory as jointly defined, by instead representing motion in a ground-aware 3D world coordinate system via SMPL-X and editing trajectory and action independently. The 3D world-space formulation draws on GVHMR for gravity-aligned motion recovery and HaMeR for hand pose, while the video synthesis stage leverages the full-attention architecture of modern diffusion transformers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The decomposed framework enables a level of independent controllability (subject, background, trajectory, action) that prior methods cannot offer, opening the door to creative applications like placing any person performing any action along any path in any environment. Key limitations remain: body pose editing is explicitly out of scope, depth estimation errors still degrade quality (Table 5 shows significant drops at 1m perturbation), and camera-subject distance severely limits visual detail. Open questions include whether the 3D world-space formulation generalizes to multi-person scenes, whether the SMPL-X representation is sufficient for complex interactions with objects, and how the approach scales to longer sequences beyond the ~6-second clips demonstrated.
