## Problem

RL fine-tuning of Vision-Language-Action models improves individual task success but causes catastrophic forgetting and cross-task interference as the number of tasks grows, because the RL optimizer distorts the shared representation learned during pretraining. This creates a fundamental trade-off between dexterity on specific tasks and generalizability across the full task distribution, which existing single-task or single-suite RFT methods do not address.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262761/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes RGB images from wrist and third-view cameras (encoded via DINOv2 and SigLIP), a natural language instruction (tokenized through Qwen2.5-0.5B), and robot proprioceptive state, fused via a multi-head attention module with learnable action-query tokens. The output is a continuous action chunk of horizon h, structured as the sum of a base policy prediction and a dynamically routed residual correction from the MoRR module, with residual magnitudes bounded by a tanh-scaled hyperparameter to keep trajectories close to the base policy.

## Method

DyGRO-VLA is a two-stage framework. In offline pretraining, it learns a task-sharing latent representation by minimizing a variational Information Bottleneck objective (derived via the Donsker-Varadhan representation of KL divergence) that maximizes action-relevant mutual information while compressing nuisance factors, jointly training the encoder and action head on offline demonstrations. In online finetuning, the VLA backbone is frozen and a Mixture-of-RL-Residuals (MoRR) module is optimized: multiple residual RL experts (SAC-style) each predict a correction to the base action chunk, and a router conditioned on a contrastively learned task embedding (InfoNCE loss) dynamically selects and weights the top-m experts per task. A load-balancing entropy regularizer prevents expert collapse, and difficulty-aware sampling biases rollouts toward under-solved tasks. The key design choice is that all task-specific adaptation is confined to the residual experts and router, preserving the shared representation from pretraining and enabling stable multi-task scaling.

## Results

On the LIBERO benchmark (4-suite co-training), DyGRO-VLA achieves a 97.1% average success rate, surpassing the strongest SFT baseline π0 (94.4%) by 2.7 points and its own offline base model (92.7%) by 4.4 points. The largest gain appears on the most challenging LIBERO-Long suite, where success increases from 85.2% (offline) to 95.0% (+9.8%). In RFT-specific comparisons on LIBERO-Long, DyGRO-VLA reaches 95.2%, outperforming RLinf (94.0%) and RIPT-VLA (93.8%). On RoboTwin2, DyGRO-VLA attains 79.2% simulation success and 57.5% real-world success, exceeding the RFT baseline OpenVLA-offt (78.5% Sim / 55.0% Real).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DyGRO-VLA outperforms all large-scale VLA SFT baselines on LIBERO: it beats π0 (94.4% avg) by 2.7 points, π0-FAST* (85.5%) by 11.6 points, and SpatialVLA (78.1%) by 19.0 points, with the gap widening substantially on LIBERO-Long (95.0% vs. 88.4% for π0). Against lightweight multi-task policies trained from scratch (Diffusion Policy 56.5%, MT-ACT 58.2%), DyGRO-VLA leads by over 38 points, confirming the advantage of VLA backbones with RL post-training. In the RFT-specific comparison on LIBERO-Long (Table 2), DyGRO-VLA (95.2%) edges out RLinf (94.0%) and RIPT-VLA (93.8%), and substantially surpasses earlier RFT methods like VLA-RL (59.8%), TGRPO (59.2%), and GRAPE (57.2%). On RoboTwin2, DyGRO-VLA achieves the best simulation average (79.2% vs. 78.5% for OpenVLA-offt RFT) and a 2.5-point real-world advantage (57.5% vs. 55.0%), with the largest real-world gain on Place Empty Cup (70% vs. 60%). The one suite where DyGRO-VLA does not lead is Spatial, where π0 reaches 98.0% versus DyGRO-VLA's 97.6%, though this is within the margin of a single evaluation seed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: Information Bottleneck representation learning (Tishby et al., 1999), residual RL for robot control (Johannink et al., 2019), and offline-to-online RL bridging (Cal-QL, RLPD). It departs from prior VLA post-training work (SimpleVLA-RL, VLA-RL, RIPT-VLA, RLinf) which optimizes individual task success rates, by explicitly formulating the objective as collective multi-task return and diagnosing why standard RL optimizers destroy cross-task representations. The key architectural departure is replacing a single residual head with a dynamically routed Mixture-of-RL-Residuals (MoRR) conditioned on contrastively-learned task embeddings, while the IB objective in the offline stage provides a principled compression mechanism absent from prior VLA pretraining recipes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and empirically demonstrates a fundamental tension in VLA post-training: RL fine-tuning that improves dexterity on individual tasks actively degrades cross-task generalization as task count grows, a failure mode that limits the scalability of generalist robot policies. The two-stage IB + MoRR framework offers a practical recipe to decouple representation preservation from policy refinement, and the +9.8% gain on long-horizon tasks suggests the approach is most valuable precisely where prior methods struggle most. Open questions include whether the routing mechanism generalizes to truly open-ended task distributions (beyond known task IDs), whether the approach scales to mobile manipulation with locomotion, and whether the online training sensitivity to reward sparsity can be mitigated for broader real-world deployment.
