## Problem

Existing outer-loop backdoor attacks on DRL agents face a fundamental trilemma: conventional pixel-patch triggers create detectable OOD artifacts, uniform random sampling wastes limited poisoning budgets on low-impact time steps, and naive reward manipulation breaks Bellman consistency and exposes the attack through value-manifold anomalies. No prior method simultaneously resolves all three challenges while maintaining near-perfect attack success and benign task performance.

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw state observations (84x84x3 Atari frames or low-dimensional SafetyGym/Highway state vectors), a designated target action a-dagger, and a poisoning budget beta (as low as 0.01%). It outputs a modified trajectory in which a sparse subset of states carry a PCA-subspace perturbation and a corresponding pair of rewards (r_{t-1}-dagger, r_t-dagger) are reshaped via differential backfilling. The poisoned trajectory is written into the PPO replay buffer, and after training the resulting policy executes the target action with near-certainty upon observing the trigger state while behaving normally otherwise.

## Method

SpecDRL couples three coordinated modules. First, Subspace-Aware Injection computes the state covariance matrix over benign trajectories via PCA and embeds the trigger along the eigenvector corresponding to the smallest eigenvalue, placing the signal in a perceptual blind spot orthogonal to the task-relevant manifold. Second, Value-Guided Strategic Sampling performs a two-stage hierarchical selection: it ranks all time steps by Return-to-Go (RTG) to form a candidate pool of size eta*K, then prunes to the K steps with the lowest absolute TD-error, concentrating the poisoning budget on transitions that maximize policy-gradient contribution while minimizing optimization noise. Third, Bellman-Consistent Dynamic Reward Poisoning reshapes each poisoned reward as r_t-dagger = J(a_t, a-dagger) - alpha_t * R_future(t), where the closed-form coefficient alpha_t = (J - r_t)/(G_t - r_t) analytically enforces value consistency between the trigger and original states; a Differential Temporal Backfilling step then compensates the preceding reward as r_{t-1}-dagger = r_{t-1} + gamma*(r_t - r_t-dagger), creating a telescoping identity that bounds the global episodic return deviation to O(1) rather than the O(beta*L) growth of naive poisoning.

## Results

SpecDRL achieves 100% Attack Success Rate (ASR) across all 12 Atari 2600 environments and all 5 SafetyGym environments, while maintaining a Benign Performance Index (BPI) of 91.77–100% (mean ≈ 99.5%). On the Highway merge task, SpecDRL reaches 39.24% ASR, outperforming all baselines (27–31%). In convergence experiments (Table 2), SpecDRL reaches >99% ASR within 100k–300k training steps, whereas baselines remain below 63% at the same budget. Under a reduced poisoning rate of β=0.01% (one-third of the baseline budget of 0.03%), SpecDRL still outperforms all baselines in 5 of 7 tested Atari environments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SpecDRL is compared against four baselines: TrojDRL (periodic dense injection), BadRL (value-prioritized sparse poisoning), SleeperNets (random sampling with reward rewriting), and Q-Incept (adversarial inception backdoor). On ASR, all methods converge to near-100% on most Atari games after full training, so the key differentiator is BPI: SpecDRL maintains 100% BPI on 10 of 12 Atari games, whereas TrojDRL drops to 74–88% BPI on 7 games and BadRL to 59–75% on 6 games. On the Highway merge task, SpecDRL achieves 39.24% ASR, a clear 8–12 percentage-point margin over all baselines (27.4–31.2%). In convergence speed (Table 2), SpecDRL reaches >99% ASR within 100k–300k steps while baselines remain at 5–63% at the same budget; for example, on BeamRider at 100k steps, SpecDRL hits 99.94% ASR versus 11.08% (TrojDRL), 21.20% (BadRL), and 7.92% (SleeperNets). Under a reduced poisoning budget of β=0.01% (vs. 0.03% for baselines), SpecDRL still outperforms all three baselines in 5 of 7 Atari environments, demonstrating superior data efficiency.

## Relation to Prior Work

SpecDRL extends the DRL backdoor attack lineage initiated by TrojDRL (periodic dense poisoning) and refined by BadRL (value-prioritized sparse injection) and SleeperNets (random sampling with reward rewriting), but departs from all of them by simultaneously addressing three axes they treat independently: trigger placement (PCA-based subspace injection vs. fixed pixel patches), poisoning timing (dual RTG/TD-error filtering vs. uniform or periodic sampling), and reward manipulation (Bellman-consistent dynamic reshaping with differential backfilling vs. static reward overrides). The PCA subspace idea is borrowed from the supervised backdoor literature's move toward invisible triggers, while the value-guided sampling and Bellman-consistent reward design are novel formulations specific to the sequential, credit-assignment structure of RL. The paper explicitly frames its contribution as resolving a 'trilemma'—stealth, efficiency, and value-function consistency—that prior outer-loop attacks could not jointly satisfy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As DRL agents are deployed in safety-critical domains (autonomous driving, robotic manipulation), demonstrating that a backdoor can be implanted at 0.01% poisoning rate, converge within 10^5 steps, and evade both visual inspection and gradient-based auditing (Neural Cleanse, SHINE) raises the bar for DRL security. The O(1) return-deviation guarantee via differential temporal backfilling is a clean theoretical contribution that distinguishes SpecDRL from prior methods whose errors grow linearly with trajectory length. Open questions include whether the PCA-based trigger remains effective under distribution shift or domain adaptation, whether the approach generalizes to continuous action spaces and multi-agent settings, and whether the Bellman-consistency constraint can be exploited by a defender to detect subtle reward anomalies that the current analysis does not fully address.
