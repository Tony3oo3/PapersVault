## Problem

Existing attention-based hallucination detectors (e.g., LLMCheck, LookbackLens, spectral and topological methods) achieve competitive performance, but the internal mechanism they exploit remains unclear and each method uses a distinct mathematical construction. The paper asks whether these diverse approaches share a common underlying signal and whether that signal can be isolated into a simpler, more interpretable diagnostic.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260290/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes the full set of attention matrices A^(l,h) ∈ R^{T×T} from every layer l and head h of a causal Transformer decoder during generation, requiring access to internal attention weights (open-weight models only). It produces a fixed-dimensional feature vector z ∈ R^{L·H·k} built from order statistics (top-k sorted sink scores per head), which is then classified by a logistic regression probe into a binary hallucination/non-hallucination label. The representation is token-agnostic and sequence-length-agnostic, depending only on the concentration structure of attention rather than specific token identities or positions.

## Method

SinkProbe computes a sink score s_i^(l,h) for each token i as the average attention it receives from all subsequent tokens (normalized by remaining sequence length), then sorts these scores per head and retains the top-k values, concatenating across all L·H heads to form the probe feature vector. A lightweight logistic regression classifier (with ℓ1 regularization for interpretability analysis) is trained on labeled hallucination examples to produce the binary prediction. The key mechanistic insight is that the hallucination signal is concentrated not in all high-attention tokens but specifically in 'computationally active sinks'—tokens where high attention concentration coincides with unusually large value-vector norms, causing them to dominate the attention output and induce compressed, prior-dominated representations. The paper further provides a unifying theoretical analysis showing that LLMCheck's log-determinant, LookbackLens's lookback ratio, TOHA's minimum spanning tree, and Laplacian eigenvalue features are all algebraically or structurally reducible to sink-score behavior, positioning sink scores as the common underlying signal across the family of attention-based detectors.

## Results

SinkProbe achieves the best ROC-AUC in 23 of 28 model–dataset pairs across four LLMs (Llama3.2-3B, Phi3.5, Llama3.1-8B, Mistral-Nemo) and seven hallucination benchmarks. Representative headline numbers include 0.896 ROC-AUC on UMWP (Phi3.5), 0.890 on HaluEvalQA (Llama3.1-8B), and 0.883 on TriviaQA (Llama3.1-8B). The method typically improves over the second-best baseline by 0.1–2.2% relative, with the largest gains on UMWP (+4.9% for Llama3.1-8B) and TruthfulQA (+1.8% for Llama3.2-3B). All results are reported as mean ROC-AUC over 5-fold cross-validation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SinkProbe outperforms all six baselines (AttnScore, AttnLogDet, AttnEigval, LapEigval, LookbackLens, MTopDiv) in 23 of 28 model–dataset pairs. The closest competitors are LookbackLens, MTopDiv, and LapEigval, which the paper shows are mathematically reducible to transformations of sink scores; SinkProbe typically edges them by 0.1–2.2% relative ROC-AUC. The five losses are modest: −6.4% on Llama3.2-3B/UMWP (vs. LookbackLens), −4.5% on Phi3.5/TruthfulQA (vs. MTopDiv), −3.6% on Mistral-Nemo/GSM8K (vs. LookbackLens), −2.1% on Phi3.5/SQuADv2 (vs. MTopDiv), and −0.2% on Llama3.1-8B/GSM8K (vs. LookbackLens). The unsupervised AttnScore (LLMCheck) is consistently the weakest baseline, trailing SinkProbe by 8–15% absolute ROC-AUC across most settings, confirming that supervised probing of sink scores captures substantially more signal than raw log-determinant aggregation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the sink score metric from Gu et al. (2025) and the experimental methodology (probe architecture, evaluation protocol) from Binkowski et al. (2025), but its core contribution is a unifying theoretical lens: it shows that several apparently distinct attention-based hallucination detectors—LLMCheck's log-determinant, LookbackLens's prompt/response ratio, Topological Divergence's MST, and Laplacian eigenvalue features—all implicitly encode sink score information. The key departure is demonstrating that the simplest direct signal (top-k raw sink scores) outperforms these more complex transformations, and that the hallucination signal is concentrated in sinks whose value vectors have anomalously large norms, linking attention concentration to computational impact.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unifying result that Laplacian eigenvalues reduce to sink scores minus self-attention provides a clean mechanistic explanation for why multiple prior methods work, and positions sink scores as a fundamental diagnostic for attention-based hallucination detection. The finding that only 1–4% of sink-score features carry predictive signal, concentrated in middle and final layers, offers an interpretable and compact internal signature. Key limitations include the requirement for open-weight model access (attention weights must be materialized), the correlational (not causal) nature of the sink-hallucination link, and modest performance margins (typically 1–2% ROC-AUC) that do not hold uniformly across all model-dataset pairs. Future interventional work—e.g., ablating specific sink heads or regularizing attention-output norms—would be needed to establish causality.
