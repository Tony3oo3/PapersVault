## Problem

As AI agents grow more capable, passive loss of control—where humans develop unwarranted trust or cannot keep pace with the agent's decision volume—becomes a central safety risk that existing alignment methods do not directly address. Approaches like CIRL, debate, and RLHF either require modifying the base agent's parameters, assume cooperative objectives from the ground up, or demand capable judges. The Oversight Game fills this gap by providing a minimal two-action wrapper that leaves the pretrained policy entirely untouched, shifting the locus of alignment from the agent's internals to the incentive structure surrounding it.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an immutable pretrained base policy σ that independently proposes an environment action at each state, plus the current environment state (or a natural language prompt in the LLM setting). The oversight layer produces a joint decision—AI selects play or ask, human selects trust or oversee—and the executed action is determined by a fixed lookup table where only the ask+oversee combination invokes the oversight operator Over, which may substitute a corrective action or trigger shutdown. In the LLM experiments, each wrapper policy is a 30B-parameter model fine-tuned with LoRA (rank 16) that outputs a single token (PLAY/ASK or TRUST/OVERSEE) conditioned on a prompt describing the current task state.

## Method

The human–AI interaction is modeled as a two-player Markov Game structured as a Markov Potential Game (MPG), where a shared potential function guarantees that independent policy gradient converges to Nash equilibria. The core theoretical contribution is a local alignment theorem: under the MPG structure and an ask-burden assumption, any autonomy-increasing deviation that benefits the agent cannot decrease the human's value, because both values flow through the same potential; under the stronger shared-reward (Markov Team Game) instantiation encoding safety violations and interaction costs, a safe minimum-oversight equilibrium exists that is stable to unilateral safety-preserving deviations. Empirically, the two wrapper policies are trained in parallel with no communication: tabular softmax policies via independent policy gradient in the Lavaland gridworld, and separate LoRA adapters on Qwen3-30B via importance-weighted policy gradient on ToolEmu MDPs, each updated using only the shared reward signal R_Φ.

## Results

On the Lavaland gridworld, independent policy gradient reduces the safety violation rate from ~15% to zero within 2,500 iterations, with intervention rates settling at 28.2% (down from ~50% initial). On the ToolEmu agentic tool-use benchmark (144 MDPs, 30B-parameter LLM policies via LoRA), test reward converges to +8.4 ± 2.1 over 10 runs. At risky states, the learned AI policy asks at 99.4% and the human policy oversees at 99.8%, while at safe states these drop to 3.9% and 2.7% respectively, demonstrating selective coordination. Cross-model validation (Qwen3-30B AI + Llama-3.3-70B human) preserves safety-critical behavior with 90.9% ask and 97.3% oversee rates at risky states.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not include a direct head-to-head comparison against other alignment methods (CIRL, debate, RLHF, or the original Off-Switch Game); instead, it validates the framework's internal dynamics. The most informative comparison is the base policy σ versus the oversight-wrapped policy: in Lavaland, σ passes through all three lava regions (100% violation), while the learned joint policy achieves zero violations with only 28.2% intervention. In ToolEmu, the cross-model setting (Qwen3-30B AI + Llama-3.3-70B human) slightly underperforms the same-model setting on coordination precision (90.9% vs 99.4% ask rate at risky states) and peak reward (+7.48 vs +11.28), but preserves the qualitative safety behavior, confirming that coordination arises from the shared reward structure rather than shared model representations. The sensitivity analysis in Lavaland shows graceful degradation: violations rise smoothly to 3.4% at c_over=10 and 25.0% at c_over=50, rather than exhibiting a sharp failure threshold.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Off-Switch Game (Hadfield-Menell et al., 2017) from a single-shot corrigibility analysis to a dynamic, multi-step Markov Game with independent learning, and grounds the convergence guarantees in the Markov Potential Game framework (Leonardos et al., 2022; Wang & Sandholm, 2002). It deliberately departs from assistance games and CIRL, which require the base agent to be cooperative or to infer the human's reward, by treating the base policy as an opaque black box and placing all alignment structure in a two-action wrapper. The perturbed Markov team game relaxation (Guo et al., 2025) is leveraged to show that approximate alignment survives bounded reward asymmetries, broadening applicability beyond exact shared-reward settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a minimal, provably sound control layer that can wrap potentially misaligned pretrained agents without modifying them, shifting the locus of alignment from agent internals to the incentive structure of the wrapper. Key open questions include validation with real human overseers (both policies here are LLMs), whether the MPG structure and alignment guarantees hold in more complex, partially observable, or multi-agent deployment settings, and how the framework scales beyond the relatively simple 2–3 decision-point MDPs used in the ToolEmu evaluation. The cross-model coordination result (Qwen3-30B + Llama-3.3-70B) is encouraging but the safe-state ask rate rises to 22.3%, suggesting the shared-reward structure may need refinement for production-grade robustness.
