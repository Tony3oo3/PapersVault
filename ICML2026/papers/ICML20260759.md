## Problem

Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) and Fused GW (FGW) align heterogeneous metric-measure spaces but inherit non-convexity from the tensor product π⊗π in their structural cost, causing standard solvers to converge only to local stationary points. No prior convex formulation simultaneously preserves the pseudometric property on mm spaces, outputs an explicit transport plan, and provides finite-sample statistical guarantees.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260759/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes two attributed compact mm spaces represented by their pairwise distance matrices (normalized by sample size) and a feature cost matrix, along with a fusion weight α ∈ [0,1]. It outputs a soft coupling matrix over the transport polytope, which can be projected to a hard permutation matrix via the Hungarian algorithm when deterministic matching is needed; the minimized objective value itself serves as a pseudometric distance between the two spaces. In the finite-sample regime the problem reduces to a convex quadratic program over the Birkhoff polytope with O(n³) per-iteration complexity.

## Method

CDOT lifts both the geometric structure and the transport plan into linear operators on L² spaces: a distance operator D_P (integral of distances) and a conditional expectation operator T_π (Markov kernel of the coupling). The structural regularization is the squared Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the commutator D_PX T_π − T_π D_PY, which is convex in π, while the feature term is the standard linear OT cost. A key theoretical result (Theorem 3.7) decomposes the GW cost as R_GW,2(π) = R(π) + V(π), identifying the dispersion penalty V(π) as the sole source of GW's non-convexity; CDOT deliberately discards this term, yielding vertical level sets that guide Frank-Wolfe descent to the global optimum regardless of initialization. The empirical problem is solved with a lazy-gradient Frank-Wolfe algorithm that exploits the quadratic structure to update gradients in O(n²) rather than O(n³) per iteration, and a non-asymptotic risk bound decomposes the total error into O(1/T) optimization error and a statistical error governed by Wasserstein-1 convergence of the empirical measures.

## Results

On synthetic 2D point clouds, CDOT achieves the lowest MSE across all sample sizes (0.0016 at n=500 per cluster vs. 0.0034 for FGW and 0.0025 for EFGW), with error decreasing as sample size grows. On OASIS-3 brain connectome matching, CDOT achieves 61.4% accuracy under diffusion distance (vs. 18.5% for FGW and 41.0% for EFGW), though FGW leads under geodesic distance (53.8% vs. 46.4%). On graph classification (TUDataset), CDOT attains the best accuracy on all five benchmarks, with particularly large margins on ENZYMES (51.3% vs. 44.5% FGW, 23.8% GW) and MUTAG (86.2% vs. 82.5% FGW, 71.8% GW).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CDOT consistently outperforms FGW on synthetic MSE (0.0016 vs. 0.0034 at n=500) and on 4 of 5 graph classification datasets, with the largest gap on ENZYMES (51.3% vs. 44.5%); however, FGW edges out CDOT on OASIS-3 geodesic-distance matching (53.8% vs. 46.4%), attributed to FGW's sensitivity to local pairwise metric details that diffusion distance smooths away. Against EFGW, CDOT wins on all three experimental settings (synthetic, brain connectome under both distance types, and all five graph datasets), suggesting that entropic regularization only partially mitigates the dispersion-induced non-convexity. CDOT dominates the purely structural baselines GW and COPT by wide margins on graph classification (e.g., 51.3% vs. 23.8% on ENZYMES), confirming that the operator-based structural term captures topological distinctions more effectively than raw pairwise distance comparison. IsoRank, which incorporates node features but relies on local neighborhood propagation, is outperformed by CDOT on all tasks, with the gap most pronounced on ENZYMES (51.3% vs. 40.6%). Spectral matching and COPT, which lack feature integration, show the weakest performance overall, with Spectral achieving only 7.4% on OASIS-3 and 63.3% on MUTAG.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) and Fused Gromov-Wasserstein (FGW) lineage by replacing the pairwise distance comparison (which induces non-convexity via the tensor product π⊗π) with an operator-level formulation using integral distance operators and conditional expectation operators on L² spaces. The key conceptual departure is formalized in the dispersion gap theorem, which decomposes the GW cost into the convex CDOT structural term plus a concave dispersion penalty, identifying the dispersion as the precise source of non-convexity. Unlike entropic regularization approaches that achieve convexity only under strong smoothing, CDOT is convex in its unregularized form while still outputting an explicit transport plan, distinguishing it from slicing-based methods that sacrifice the coupling for scalability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CDOT provides the first convex OT framework on population-level metric-measure spaces with a full suite of theoretical guarantees (pseudometricity, non-asymptotic risk bounds, and consistency), filling a gap where prior methods either lacked convexity or lacked rigorous statistical theory. The dispersion gap decomposition offers a clean geometric explanation for why GW optimization is hard, which may inform future algorithm design. Key limitations include the O(n³) per-iteration cost limiting scalability, and the fact that zero CDOT discrepancy is strictly weaker than measure-preserving isometry, making it less suitable when rigid pointwise matching is the goal. Open directions include statistical inference (hypothesis testing) on mm spaces using the CDOT pseudometric and jointly learning the ground metric within differentiable pipelines.
