## Problem

Current AI evaluations treat benchmark scores as direct measurements of capability without specifying what 'capability' means, implicitly assuming a theory (usually simple averaging under Classical Test Theory) that is never made explicit. This leaves reported results unreliable and incomparable, since the same benchmark data can support different inferences depending on the underlying capability model. The paper argues that evals should be reframed as inference tasks grounded on an explicit theory of capability, drawing on psychometric traditions where such assumptions are standard.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes benchmark questions along with multiple perturbed phrasings per item (to approximate the natural phrasing distribution), model correctness responses, and theory-specific structural specifications: item difficulty/discrimination parameters for IRT, a Q-matrix mapping items to skills for CDM, or a directed skill-dependency graph for BNSM. Outputs are theory-dependent capability estimates (mean accuracy, latent ability, or discrete/continuous skill mastery) with bootstrap or posterior uncertainty intervals, plus an Evaluation Card documenting the modeling assumptions. A key constraint is that capability is only identifiable in expectation over the perturbation distribution (Assumption 4.1), and different theories produce non-comparable constructs from the same data.

## Method

The paper operationalizes four psychometric theories of capability—CTT, IRT, CDM (DINA/DINO), and BNSM—each augmented with a systematic perturbation term s(x_i) to account for AI-specific sensitivity to phrasing, hyperparameters, and context that violates the independence assumptions inherited from human testing. For each theory, a distinct inference pipeline is derived: clustered bootstrap for CTT, adaptive item selection with Fisher scoring for IRT, alternating Newton/L-BFGS updates for joint item-skill estimation under CDM, and approximate Bayesian network inference (variational or Laplace) for BNSM. The key empirical demonstration shows that these theories yield systematically different model rankings and skill attributions on the same BBH and LMEntry data across seven open-source LLMs, with IRT separating models that CTT cannot and BNSM attributing errors to different latent skills than CDM. The paper concludes with an Evaluation Card specifying four dimensions (meaning of capability, task structure, sources of systematic variation, robustness checks) that evaluators must document to make their modeling commitments transparent.

## Results

Across seven open-source LLMs (Llama-3.2, Qwen-2.5, Gemma) on eight benchmark tasks (BBH and LMEntry), the paper shows that systematic bias between original and perturbed benchmark estimates reaches up to ±15 percentage points, with mean absolute per-item sensitivity (M_i) spanning 10–50 p.p. For state-of-the-art models, GPT-4.1 achieves 71% on Movie Recommendation and 47% on GPQA, while GPT-4.1-mini achieves 65% and 47% respectively, with phrasing-sensitivity deviations of roughly 0–8 p.p. on MR and somewhat larger on GPQA. IRT-based inference yields greater model separation than CTT using fewer questions (adaptive testing), and CDM/BNSM produce skill-level estimates that differ qualitatively from aggregate accuracy (e.g., BNSM infers markedly higher Social Reasoning than CDM by attributing errors to an additional World Knowledge latent).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare a single proposed method against external baselines; instead, it contrasts four theories of capability (CTT, IRT, CDM, BNSM) applied to identical benchmark data to show they yield incomparable inferences. CTT (simple accuracy averaging) and IRT (latent ability via item parameters) produce broadly consistent model rankings, but IRT achieves sharper separation with fewer items and reveals cases where equal CTT accuracy maps to different ability estimates (e.g., Qwen-3.5B scores identically on AWFC and Snarks under CTT yet receives a lower IRT ability on Snarks because AWFC contains harder items). CDM and BNSM both report per-skill mastery, but BNSM infers substantially higher Social Reasoning than CDM because its additional World Knowledge node absorbs errors on Movie Recommendation and Causal Judgment that CDM must attribute to the Social skill. Perturbation-aware evaluation (averaging over 20 phrasings) reduces confidence-interval width and can flip model rankings relative to the original single-phrasing benchmark (e.g., on Causal Judgment, Qwen-3.5B > Qwen-7B in the original data but the ordering reverses under perturbations, aligning with expected size-based performance). These comparisons demonstrate that the choice of capability theory is not a cosmetic detail but materially changes which model appears stronger and what the score means.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws directly on the psychometric tradition—Classical Test Theory, Item Response Theory, Cognitive Diagnostic Models, and Bayesian Network Skill Models—and repurposes them as explicit theoretical frameworks for AI evaluation, building on Raykov & Marcoulides (2011) for IRT inference, Leighton & Gierl (2007) for CDM, and Culbertson (2016) for BNSM. While IRT-inspired methods for AI evaluation already exist (Zhuang et al. 2023, Maia Polo et al. 2024, Martinez-Plumed et al. 2019), the paper's key departure is arguing that all such methods implicitly commit to a theory of capability without stating it, and that AI systems systematically violate the independence and mean-zero-noise assumptions inherited from human-centric psychometrics. The paper positions itself as making these hidden assumptions explicit and showing empirically that the choice of theory changes what benchmark scores mean.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a foundational gap in AI evaluation practice: benchmark scores are treated as direct measurements of capability, but they are inferences whose validity depends on unstated theoretical commitments. The empirical demonstration that different capability theories yield systematically different model rankings and skill estimates from identical data is practically important for anyone interpreting leaderboard results. The proposed Evaluation Card offers a concrete, low-cost mechanism for improving transparency. Open questions include developing a systematic taxonomy of AI-specific confounders (beyond prompt perturbation), establishing norms for how evaluation claims should be scoped and reused, and formulating capability theories that are native to generative rather than human cognition.
