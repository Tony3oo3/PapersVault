## Problem

Standard majority vote aggregation in AI evaluation treats annotator disagreement as noise to eliminate, collapsing multi-annotator judgments into hard labels and discarding information about annotator reliability and item-level ambiguity. This makes system rankings fragile under small changes in annotator composition, yet ranking stability has not been formalized as a first-class evaluation objective or analyzed theoretically.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260314/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-annotator human labels (discretized into K correctness levels, e.g., 0/1/2 for incorrect/partial/correct), agent-generated responses, and task prompts. It produces a continuous posterior expected item credit per item (a weighted sum over correctness levels via a task-specific scoring function v(c)), agent-level scores with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, per-annotator confusion matrices revealing strictness/leniency patterns, and item-level ambiguity estimates (entropy of posterior distributions). Unlike hard-label methods, the output preserves graded correctness and structured uncertainty rather than a single consensus label.

## Method

STABLEVAL models each item's latent correctness level z_i and each annotator's confusion matrix π_r[c,o] = P(y_ir=o | z_i=c) within a Bayesian latent variable framework, placing Dirichlet priors over class priors and confusion matrix rows. EM estimates these parameters, but the critical distinction from Dawid-Skene is that STABLEVAL retains the full posterior distribution γ_ic rather than collapsing it via argmax, computing item credit as the posterior expected value Σ_c γ_ic·v(c). Agent scores are means of item credits with bootstrap CIs, and the paper formalizes ranking stability as expected Kendall's τ_b under uniform annotator subsampling, proving that majority vote is inherently unstable (Proposition 1) while STABLEVAL achieves asymptotic stability under correct model specification (Proposition 2).

## Results

On MT-Bench (the highest-disagreement benchmark, with only 16.6% unanimous agreement), Posterior Expected Credit (PEC) achieves the lowest ranking instability (Mean Rank Std = 0.197) compared to Dawid-Skene Hard (0.223) and Majority Vote (0.259), while adjusting agent scores by up to +0.144 (vicuna-13b-v1.2) relative to Majority Vote. In synthetic adversarial stress tests (40% adversarial annotators), Majority Vote MSE degrades to 8.42×10⁻³ while PEC holds at 5.54×10⁻³ and Dawid-Skene at 3.47×10⁻³. On high-consensus benchmarks (ConvAbuse: 78.4% unanimous; QAGS: 65.6% unanimous), all three methods converge to identical rankings with zero rank variability, confirming that STABLEVAL does not introduce instability when disagreement is minimal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Majority Vote, PEC consistently improves ranking stability: on MT-Bench, Mean Rank Std drops from 0.259 (MV) to 0.197 (PEC), and in synthetic adversarial settings (40% adversarial annotators), MV MSE inflates to 8.42×10⁻³ while PEC stays at 5.54×10⁻³. Against Dawid-Skene (Hard), the trade-off is clear: DS achieves lower latent-label MSE across all synthetic configurations (e.g., 3.47 vs. 5.54 at 40% adversarial), reflecting its denoising objective, but PEC produces more stable agent rankings on MT-Bench (Rank Std 0.197 vs. 0.223) and achieves perfect Kendall's Tau (1.000) in the no-hard-item synthetic regime where DS also reaches 1.000. On high-consensus benchmarks (ConvAbuse, QAGS), all three methods tie with zero rank variability, so neither baseline is disadvantaged. The paper's central empirical finding is that the stability advantage of PEC over MV is largest precisely where disagreement is highest (MT-Bench: 53% pairwise agreement), while the MSE advantage of DS over PEC is largest in the same regime—confirming that label recovery and evaluation stability are distinct objectives.

## Relation to Prior Work

STABLEVAL is built directly on the Dawid-Skene (1979) EM-based latent variable model with annotator confusion matrices and Dirichlet priors, but departs from it by retaining the full posterior distribution over correctness levels rather than collapsing to a hard label via argmax. It positions itself against the broader disagreement-aware annotation modeling literature (Fleisig et al. 2023; Uma et al. 2021; Xu & Jurgens 2026) and distributional/soft-label approaches (Wang et al. 2023; Wright & Augenstein 2025), which advocate preserving disagreement distributions for downstream tasks. The key conceptual departure is formalizing ranking stability under annotator subsampling as a first-class evaluation objective, which the authors argue no prior aggregation method explicitly optimizes for, distinguishing their goal from label denoising.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As AI systems are increasingly compared via human judgments or LLM-based judges, the fragility of majority-vote rankings under annotator composition changes threatens reproducibility in model selection and safety assessment. The paper's central practical message—that label recovery accuracy (MSE) and ranking stability are distinct objectives, and that smooth posterior aggregation mitigates discrete thresholding instability—is a useful reframing for evaluation pipelines. However, empirical gains are modest and concentrated in high-disagreement regimes; in high-consensus settings all methods converge, and in the small-annotator MSLR dataset PEC actually shows slightly worse stability than baselines. Open questions include whether the conditional-independence assumption holds under correlated biases or social influence, how the framework scales to hierarchical or task-specific difficulty structures, and whether stability gains persist under truly adversarial real-world annotation conditions beyond synthetic stress tests.
