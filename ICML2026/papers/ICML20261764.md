## Problem

Graph combinatorial optimization problems (MIS, MaxCl, MaxCut) admit an exact continuous multilinear relaxation of their QUBO form, but the resulting energy landscape is highly nonconvex, causing standard gradient-based and existing sampling-based methods to become trapped in poor local optima. Existing samplers (diffusion, MCMC) follow indirect trajectories in probability space between a reference and target distribution, wasting compute under fixed budgets. The paper asks whether optimal transport can characterize more direct, efficient probability flow paths to guide sampling dynamics toward high-quality solutions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph G=(V,E) with QUBO parameters (Q, c) defining the pairwise interaction matrix and linear costs. During optimization, the GNN consumes three per-node inputs: the continuous state x_c ∈ [0,1], a discrete Bernoulli sample x_d drawn from Bernoulli(x_c), and a positional time embedding, all processed by a 5-layer GIN with a virtual node. The output is a continuous relaxed vector x ∈ [0,1]^n interpreted as selection probabilities, which is converted to a feasible binary solution via greedy rounding (sorting nodes by x_u descending and adding each if constraints are satisfied). Multiple particles (K) are run in parallel over T time steps, and the best particle's rounded solution is returned.

## Method

The paper formulates sampling from the energy-induced Boltzmann distribution π* ∝ exp(−f(x;G)) as an entropic optimal transport problem from a uniform reference to π*, then derives an equivalent stochastic optimal control (Schrödinger bridge) formulation whose optimal drift has a closed form involving a conditional expectation under a heat-kernel-weighted distribution. Because this expectation is intractable in high dimensions, the paper applies a first-order Laplace approximation to replace it with the argmin of a Laplace energy L(t,x,y;G) = λ_h·f(y;G) + ‖x−y‖²/(4ε(1−t)), yielding a computable surrogate control û = −(x − y*)/(1−t). This subproblem is amortized by a GNN y_θ(t,x;G) trained via a replay buffer of rollout states, where the training objective minimizes the Laplace energy at sampled (t,x) pairs without backpropagating through the rollout dynamics. At inference, K particles evolve in parallel under the learned drift with Brownian noise over T discretized steps on [0, 1−δ], and the best particle is greedily rounded to a feasible discrete solution.

## Results

On MIS RB-[200-300], the full-budget configuration (500×64) achieves an average independent set size of 20.12, the best among all learning-based methods, compared to SDDS (19.62), DiffUCO (19.59), and RLNN (19.52). On the harder RB-[800-1200] setting, Ours (500×64) reaches 39.25, closely matching SDDS (39.32) and outperforming DiffUCO (38.87) and RLNN (38.06). On MaxCut BA-[800-1200], Ours (500×64) achieves a cut value of 2965.94, surpassing DiffUCO (2947.53) and RLNN (2907.18). The lighter configuration (100×20) retains competitive quality at roughly 1/10 the runtime, e.g., 19.65 on RB-[200-300] MIS and 2960.13 on BA-[800-1200] MaxCut.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among learning-based methods, Ours (500×64) achieves the best or near-best solution quality across all three tasks: it outperforms DGL by 2.76 on RB-[200-300] MIS (20.12 vs 17.36), DIFUSCO by 1.60 on ER MIS (43.41 vs 41.12), and RLNN by 1.19 on MaxCl RB-[800-1200] (40.52 vs 35.23). Against the strongest unsupervised baselines, Ours edges out SDDS on RB-[200-300] MIS (20.12 vs 19.62) and DiffUCO on MaxCut BA-[800-1200] (2965.94 vs 2947.53), though SDDS slightly leads on RB-[800-1200] MIS (39.32 vs 39.25). Against OR solvers, Ours is competitive but generally trails KaMIS on MIS (e.g., 39.25 vs 43.15 on RB-[800-1200]) and Gurobi on small RB instances, while matching or exceeding Gurobi on ER MIS (43.41 vs 41.38) and MaxCut BA-[800-1200] (2965.94 vs 2944.38). Heuristic baselines (Greedy, MFA) and the ERDOES unsupervised method are substantially outperformed on MaxCl and MaxCut (e.g., 19.04 vs 14.82 for MFA on MaxCl RB-[200-300]). The lighter 100×20 configuration retains most of the quality gap over learning-based baselines at roughly 1/10 the inference time, e.g., 19.65 vs 19.52 (RLNN) on RB-[200-300] MIS in 0.56 s vs 0.20 s.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) the classical exact multilinear relaxation of QUBO (Zemel, 1981; Merz & Freisleben, 2002), which provides a gap-free continuous formulation; (2) the Schrödinger bridge / entropic optimal transport framework (Benamou & Brenier, 2000; Léonard, 2014), which the authors use structurally to derive a stochastic optimal control problem whose solution gives an efficient sampling drift; and (3) recent energy-based and diffusion-style samplers for combinatorial optimization (GWG, PAS/PAFS, DIFUSCO, RLSA). The key departure is treating OT not as an exact solver but as a geometric design principle for the sampling dynamics, then making the resulting optimal control tractable via a Laplace approximation amortized by a GNN — a combination not previously explored in the GCO literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled, gap-free continuous route to discrete solutions that avoids the relaxation gap of convex/SDP relaxations while remaining amenable to gradient-based and learned optimization. The OT-inspired dynamics provide an interpretable geometric rationale for why certain sampling paths outperform hand-designed MCMC or diffusion updates, and the cross-scale transfer results (98.5% retention for MIS) suggest the learned control captures reusable structural search behavior. However, the approach lacks global optimality guarantees, relies on a first-order Laplace approximation that may degrade for highly fractional solutions, and the greedy rounding step could be a bottleneck under tighter constraints. Open questions include whether the OT structural role can be made more rigorous (e.g., convergence guarantees for the induced dynamics), whether the GNN amortization generalizes beyond the tested graph families, and how the method scales to problems with n > 10,000.
