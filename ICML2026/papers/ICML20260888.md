## Problem

Inference-time scaling via aggregation (self-consistency, pass@k) reliably improves performance in verifiable domains like mathematics and code, but whether polling-style aggregation can serve as a proxy verifier for truthfulness in domains without external correctness signals remains unexplored. This paper asks whether scaling inference compute through repeated sampling and aggregation can elicit truthfulness gains, and shows it cannot: correlated errors across LLMs cause aggregation to amplify shared mistakes rather than cancel them.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260888/cropped_page4_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes binary questions (YES/NO or TRUE/FALSE) and generates 25 samples per model at temperatures 0.7 and 1.0, alongside self-reported confidence on a 0–100 scale and predicted vote shares for each option. Five aggregation rules then select a final binary answer from the pooled samples (up to 125 votes per question across the five-model ensemble). The binary format is a deliberate simplification to make vote shares and surprise-based aggregation well-defined, representing the most favorable regime for polling-style methods; failure even here provides strong evidence against aggregation as a general truthfulness mechanism.

## Method

The paper evaluates five aggregation rules—Majority Vote, Highest Confidence, Confidence-Weighted Vote, Prediction-Weighted Vote, and Surprisingly Popular—across five open-source models (Gemma-3-4B, GPT-oss-20B/120B, Qwen-32B, Qwen3-235B) on four verifier-absent benchmarks (HLE, BoolQ, Com2Sense, and the newly introduced Predict-the-Future forecasting set). The central finding is that LLM errors are strongly correlated across models and samples, violating the independence assumption underlying crowd wisdom; on 53% of MATH questions where multiple models err, they converge to the same incorrect answer. A negative control using random ASCII strings with forced multiple-choice answers reveals inter-model correlations up to 0.35 even with no ground truth, demonstrating that correlation stems from shared inductive biases in model weights rather than shared factual knowledge. The paper further shows a mechanistic separation between social prediction and truth verification: models predict collective opinion substantially better than they identify correctness, so confidence and predicted popularity track expected consensus rather than epistemic certainty, rendering all internal aggregation signals unreliable as proxy verifiers.

## Results

Across four verifier-absent benchmarks (HLE, BoolQ, Com2Sense, Predict-the-Future) and five models (4B–235B parameters), no aggregation method consistently outperforms single-sample baselines even at 25× inference cost. On Predict-the-Future (post-cutoff forecasting), all methods perform at chance; on HLE, ensemble accuracy is only 5.7%, far below the 50% guessing baseline. Error correlation is the structural cause: on MATH, 65–87% of wrong responses concentrate on a single incorrect answer, and on 53% of MATH questions where multiple models err, they converge to the same wrong answer. A no-signal negative control (random ASCII strings with forced 4-way choice) reveals inter-model correlations up to 0.35, confirming that correlation stems from shared inductive biases rather than shared knowledge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five aggregation rules—Majority Vote (de Condorcet, 1785), Highest Confidence (Kadavath et al., 2022), Confidence-Weighted Vote (Wang et al., 2023; Taubenfeld et al., 2025), Prediction-Weighted Vote, and Surprisingly Popular (Prelec et al., 2017)—against single-sample baselines across four benchmarks. None of the five methods consistently beats the single-sample baseline; methods that help on one benchmark hurt on another, and on Predict-the-Future all methods are indistinguishable from chance. Surprisingly Popular is particularly problematic: the inverse-SP diagnostic attains 80% accuracy on HLE, meaning the standard SP signal is systematically anti-correlated with correctness there, while on other datasets inverse-SP performs at chance, showing the surprise gap's direction is unstable across tasks. Confidence-weighted methods fail because self-reported confidence tracks expected agreement nearly as strongly as it tracks accuracy, so weighting by confidence amplifies the dominant correlated error rather than correcting it. Majority Vote, the simplest baseline, is not outperformed by any of the more sophisticated rules in a consistent, cross-benchmark manner, confirming that the failure is structural (correlated errors) rather than a deficiency of any particular aggregation rule.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the inference-time scaling literature (self-consistency, repeated sampling, pass@k) by asking whether aggregation can substitute for an external verifier in truthfulness tasks, a question prior work has not systematically addressed. It draws on the wisdom-of-crowds and ensemble theory tradition (de Condorcet, Surowiecki, Lakshminarayanan et al.) to formalize the independence assumption, then tests it directly against LLM ensembles. The Surprisingly Popular algorithm (Prelec et al., 2017) and confidence-weighted voting (Taubenfeld et al., 2025) are evaluated as candidate proxy verifiers, and the paper's negative control (random-string forced choice) departs from prior correlated-error analyses (Kim et al., 2025; Goel et al., 2025) by isolating architectural inductive bias from shared knowledge as the source of correlation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result delineates a hard boundary for inference-time scaling: additional samples help only when a verifier exists to filter candidates, and in verifier-absent domains they merely amplify correlated errors. This has direct implications for AI oversight and scalable alignment, where polling multiple models was a natural candidate for truth verification. Open questions include what interventions (disjoint training, retrieval grounding, learned verifiers) can break error correlation, whether the binary-question restriction materially changes conclusions for open-ended generation, and whether the social-prediction signal can be repurposed as a routing or abstention trigger rather than a selection mechanism.
