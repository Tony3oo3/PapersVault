## Problem

Existing time-series foundation model (TSFM) benchmarks such as GIFT-Eval, Monash, and TSFM-Bench pool datasets from many domains with highly uneven representation, producing aggregate scores that can mask severe domain-specific failures. Because time series from healthcare, finance, energy, nature, transport, and retail differ fundamentally in sampling regularity, stationarity, spectral content, and drift dynamics, a model's strong global average may not translate to reliable deployment in a practitioner's target domain. The paper argues that explicit domain-level benchmarks are necessary to expose these transfer failures and enable trustworthy model selection.

## Input / Output

Each TSFM receives a univariate time series that is z-score normalized (instance-wise) before zero-shot inference, with missing values imputed using domain-appropriate strategies (forward-fill for finance, linear interpolation for energy/nature). Models produce point forecasts at horizons h ∈ {24, 48, 96} steps, which are denormalized before computing MSE and MAE. All seven evaluated models (TimesFM, Chronos, Moirai, Time-MoE, TimeGPT, Toto, Sundial) are used in their largest publicly available variant with default inference hyperparameters, and no task-specific fine-tuning is applied.

## Method

The paper combines a theoretical argument with a large-scale empirical benchmark. Theoretically, it shows that cross-domain pretraining induces gradient conflicts (nearly orthogonal or opposing gradient directions between domains like weather and finance), forcing shared representations to converge toward a statistical average that is suboptimal for all domains; it further identifies how patch-based tokenization, channel-independence, and instance normalization encode domain-specific inductive biases that break under irregular sampling, multi-rate signals, or regime shifts. Empirically, seven TSFMs are evaluated across 72 datasets spanning six balanced domains under a unified protocol, with performance leadership shown to be fragmented (no single model dominates all domains) and rank reversals frequent across application areas. A Kruskal-Wallis test at horizon 24 confirms statistically significant domain effects for most models on both MSE and MAE. The paper also quantifies domain imbalance in existing benchmarks (e.g., Healthcare at 0.7% of GIFT-Eval series) and proposes three concrete actions: domain-stratified reporting, standardized evaluation of domain-aware models, and a cross-domain transfer framework.

## Results

Across 72 datasets in six domains, no single TSFM achieves consistent top performance: in Energy, TimesMoE attains the best average (MSE 0.5116, MAE 0.4579) while Sundial secures the most first-place finishes (7 in MSE, 6 in MAE); in Finance, ToTo dominates with 13/14 first-place counts (MSE/MAE); in Healthcare, Chronos leads with 14/14 first-place counts. Kruskal-Wallis tests at horizon h=24 confirm statistically significant domain effects for 6 of 7 models on MSE (p ≤ 0.021) and 5 of 7 on MAE (p ≤ 0.044), rejecting the null of domain-invariant performance. These findings demonstrate that global leaderboard rankings are unstable and that domain-stratified evaluation is necessary for reliable model selection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks seven TSFMs (TimesFM, Chronos, Moirai, Time-MoE, TimeGPT, ToTo, Sundial) in zero-shot mode across 72 datasets in six domains. In Energy, TimesMoE achieves the lowest average MSE (0.5116) and MAE (0.4579), outperforming TimesFM (0.6242/0.4612) and Chronos (0.7686/0.5518), yet Sundial wins the most individual dataset-horizon comparisons (7 MSE, 6 MAE first-place finishes), showing that average accuracy and per-dataset leadership diverge. In Finance, ToTo dominates overwhelmingly with 13 MSE and 14 MAE first-place counts out of 42 total, while TimesFM and TimesMoE each secure only 5–6. In Healthcare, Chronos leads with 14/14 first-place counts, far ahead of TimesFM (3/3) and Sundial (5/6). Cross-domain, TimesFM shows the most pronounced skew—strong in Retail but dropping sharply in Finance and Nature—while ToTo peaks in Finance/Nature but underperforms in Transport and Retail. Kruskal-Wallis tests confirm that for 6 of 7 models, MSE distributions differ significantly across domains (p ≤ 0.021), and for 5 of 7, MAE distributions do as well (p ≤ 0.044), providing statistical evidence that no model exhibits domain-invariant performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not build on a single prior method but rather critiques the evaluation infrastructure of the TSFM field. It draws on the architectural and pretraining choices of existing TSFMs (TimesFM, Chronos, Moirai, Time-MoE, TimeGPT, Toto, Sundial) and identifies how their shared design assumptions—patch-based tokenization, instance normalization, channel independence—encode domain-specific inductive biases that conflict across application areas. The paper repurposes domain-specific time series literature (irregular sampling in healthcare, non-stationarity in finance, deterministic physics in energy) and standard statistical tools (Kruskal-Wallis, Demšar ranking) to argue that existing benchmarks (GIFT-Eval, Monash, TSFM-Bench) are structurally inadequate due to severe domain imbalance, rather than proposing a new forecasting architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides concrete evidence—72 datasets, 7 models, 6 domains, Kruskal-Wallis significance—that global TSFM leaderboards can mislead practitioners into deploying models that fail in their target domain, with the most acute risk in underrepresented high-stakes areas like healthcare (0.7% of GIFT-Eval series). The three-part call to action (domain-stratified reporting, fair evaluation of domain-aware models like MIRA and FinCast, and a cross-domain transfer framework) identifies actionable gaps but leaves open the harder question of how to architecturally resolve gradient conflicts during pretraining. Limitations include the univariate-only setting, zero-shot evaluation without fine-tuning, and a relatively modest dataset count per domain, which may understate the difficulty of truly irregular or multi-rate clinical data.
