## Problem

Reinforcement learning training of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) large language models is prone to severe instability and catastrophic collapse, a problem significantly worse than in dense models. The paper identifies the root cause as routing discrepancies: the discrete Top-K expert selection in MoE routers amplifies small numerical differences between inference and training frameworks into large policy divergences, causing gradients to flow to experts that were never used during rollout. Existing mitigations such as importance sampling (TIS) or sequence-level clipping (GSPO) do not fully resolve this MoE-specific amplification.

## Input / Output

The method consumes per-token, per-layer binary routing masks (int16 Top-K expert indices, K=8 out of M=128 experts for Qwen3-30B-A3B) captured from the SGLang inference engine during rollout, alongside standard RL inputs (prompts, generated responses, verifier rewards). These masks are stored in CPU memory and cached alongside KV-cache prefix entries for multi-turn reuse, adding only ~2.5 GB CPU memory per node during inference and ~1.5 GB during training. The output is updated model parameters trained with expert selection aligned to inference, reducing training-inference KL divergence from ~1.5×10⁻³ to ~7.5×10⁻⁴ (comparable to dense models at ~6.4×10⁻⁴).

## Method

Rollout Routing Replay (R3) aligns training and inference by replaying the inference-time Top-K routing mask during the training forward pass. Specifically, instead of computing TopKMask(s_train, K) from training logits, R3 substitutes the inference mask I_infer so that the same experts are activated in both phases; the gating weights are then computed as a softmax over training logits restricted to the inference-selected experts (g_replay,i = I_infer,i · exp(s_train,i) / Σ_j I_infer,j · exp(s_train,j)), preserving gradient flow to exactly the experts that generated the token. This eliminates the discrete routing mismatch that amplifies floating-point perturbations into large output changes, and prevents gradient noise from propagating to unused experts. For multi-turn agent tasks, routing masks are cached with KV-cache prefix entries so that repeated context prefixes reuse stored masks without re-prefilling. The approach is orthogonal to policy optimization algorithms (GRPO, GSPO, TIS) and introduces only a 3.45% end-to-end throughput overhead.

## Results

On Qwen3-30B-A3B-SFT with single mini-step, GRPO+R3 achieves an average score of 71.83 across AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, and MATH500 Lv5, outperforming GRPO (62.23, crashes at step 60) and GRPO+TIS (66.24, crashes at step 105). In the multi mini-step setting, GSPO+R3 reaches 69.00 average, surpassing GSPO alone (66.76) and GRPO (48.84, crashes at step 120). On the Base model with single mini-step, GRPO+R3 scores 70.73 versus 61.69 for GRPO (crashes at step 105) and 69.22 for GRPO+TIS. R3 reduces training-inference KL divergence from 1.5×10⁻³ to 7.5×10⁻⁴ (near the dense-model level of 6.4×10⁻⁴) with only a 3.45% throughput overhead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

R3 consistently outperforms all three baselines (GRPO, GSPO, TIS) across every tested configuration. Against GSPO in the multi mini-step setting, GRPO+R3 improves the average score by 1.29 points (68.05 vs. 66.76), and combining both (GSPO+R3) yields a further 0.95-point gain to 69.00. Against TIS in the single mini-step setting, R3 achieves a 5.58-point advantage on the SFT model (71.83 vs. 66.24) and a 1.51-point advantage on the Base model (70.73 vs. 69.22); notably, combining TIS with R3 does not help and actually degrades performance by 3.29 points on the SFT model, since R3 already eliminates most of the policy discrepancy that TIS was designed to correct. The most critical distinction is training stability: all baseline runs without R3 eventually collapse (GRPO at steps 60–120, GRPO+TIS at step 105), whereas every R3-enabled run completes the full 180-step training without collapse. On the multi-turn SWE-bench Verified task, GRPO+R3 reaches 38.60 Pass@1 versus 31.80 for GRPO (which crashes at step 90), confirming the advantage generalizes beyond single-turn math reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the growing line of work addressing training-inference discrepancies in LLM RL (TIS importance sampling, IcePop token filtering, FP32/FP16 precision fixes, kernel-level nondeterminism reduction), but departs from them by identifying the MoE router's Top-K discreteness as the specific root cause that amplifies floating-point perturbations into large policy divergences. Rather than correcting the discrepancy at the token-probability level via importance sampling or precision changes, R3 intervenes at the routing level by replaying inference-time expert selection masks during training. The method is explicitly orthogonal to policy optimization improvements like GSPO and TIS, positioning it as a complementary infrastructure-level fix rather than an alternative algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

R3 provides a simple, low-overhead (3.45% slowdown) technique that is likely to become standard practice for anyone training MoE models with RL, given that MoE architectures dominate frontier model development. The generalization across four different MoE families (Qwen3, DeepSeek-V2-Lite, Mixtral, Moonlight) with different router designs (softmax vs. sigmoid, shared experts, varying expert counts) strengthens confidence in broad applicability. Open questions include whether R3 is sufficient when training and inference use the same framework (eliminating the source of discrepancy), whether the approach needs modification for models with continuous (non-Top-K) routing, and whether the residual ~7×10⁻⁴ KL divergence (vs. ~6.4×10⁻⁴ for dense) indicates a remaining gap that could matter at scale.
