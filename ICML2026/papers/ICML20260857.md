## Problem

While empirical methods for discrete-time stochastic optimal control (deep learning, Monte Carlo regression) have advanced significantly, the theoretical understanding of how local approximation errors—arising from function approximation, sampling noise, or optimization inaccuracies—propagate backward through the Bellman recursion remains largely unexplored. No unified framework existed to rigorously decompose and control these distinct error sources simultaneously in high-dimensional settings, leaving practitioners without principled convergence guarantees for approximate dynamic programming.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a state vector X_t ∈ R^d_+ (asset prices), GBM transition parameters (volatilities σ_i, correlation matrix ρ, risk-free rate r), a payoff function C_t, and a time horizon T. At each backward step, it generates training pairs (x_i, y_i) where x_i ~ μ_t (behavior distribution) and y_i is computed via Monte Carlo continuation values using M_t i.i.d. samples of the stochastic driver. The output is a sequence of clipped KRR estimators {Ŵ_t^{λ_t}} in an RKHS, with the final deliverable being Ŵ_0^{λ_0} as an L^2(μ_0) estimator of the optimal value V_0, from which both the option price and the exercise strategy τ = inf{t : u_t = 0} ∧ T are recovered.

## Method

The paper formulates discrete-time SOC as a Bellman recursion in L^2(μ_t) spaces and proposes KRR-DP, a backward-induction algorithm that at each step approximates the continuation value via Monte Carlo (M_t samples) and fits the value function via Kernel Ridge Regression (or its FALKON/Nyström variant) in a bounded RKHS. The key theoretical contribution is a fully modular error decomposition of ‖Ŵ_t − V_t‖²_{L²} into three independently controllable terms: (I) regression excess risk bounded by n_t^{−β_t/(β_t+1)} under a source condition, (II) Monte Carlo error bounded via Rademacher complexity of the transition class, and (III) propagation error scaled by the Bellman operator's Lipschitz constant c_P. The resulting backward recursion E_t ≲ n_t^{−β_t/(β_t+1)} + c_P · E_{t+1} yields the explicit bound E_0 ≲ Σ_t c_P^t n_t^{−β_t/(β_t+1)}, which is contractive (errors damped) when c_P < 1, as in American option pricing where c_P = e^{−2r}. The modular structure allows substituting any function approximator or quadrature scheme without altering the overall analysis.

## Results

On the geometric basket put option (with a closed-form benchmark), KRR-DP achieves prices within 0.01–0.03 of the benchmark across all dimensions (e.g., 2.70 vs. 2.70 at d=20; 2.55 vs. 2.56 at d=40), with 95% confidence intervals of width ≤0.05. Computationally, KRR-DP is dramatically faster than GPR-Tree: 21 s vs. 49,609 s at d=20, and 87 s vs. infeasible at d=40. On the max-call option (no closed-form benchmark), KRR-DP prices are within 0.3 of GPR-MC and GPR-EI across all dimensions (e.g., 50.44 vs. 50.70 and 49.53 at d=40), while running in 118 s compared to 41 s for GPR-EI.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GPR-Tree, KRR-DP matches or slightly exceeds accuracy at every dimension while being 10–2,400× faster (e.g., 21 s vs. 49,609 s at d=20; GPR-Tree becomes infeasible at d=40). Against GPR-EI, KRR-DP is competitive in price accuracy (within 0.07 on the basket put, within 0.9 on the max-call) and is faster at d=20 (21 s vs. 29 s) and d=40 (87 s vs. 38 s for GPR-EI, though GPR-EI is slightly faster at d=40). Against the Ekvall lattice method, KRR-DP matches the benchmark exactly at d=20 (2.70) and is within 0.01 at d=40 (2.55 vs. 2.57), while Ekvall does not scale beyond d=40. Against the closed-form benchmark for the geometric basket put, KRR-DP is within 0.01 at d=2, 0.01 at d=5, 0.01 at d=10, 0.00 at d=20, and 0.01 at d=40, confirming that the kernel-based approximation introduces negligible pricing error. Overall, KRR-DP's primary advantage is computational efficiency at high dimensions while maintaining benchmark-level accuracy, with the Monte Carlo data-generation step identified as the main remaining bottleneck.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of classical stochastic optimal control (Bellman, Bertsekas) and modern kernel learning theory (Steinwart & Christmann, Caponnetto & De Vito). It builds directly on the Longstaff-Schwartz / Tsitsiklis-Van Roy regression-based Monte Carlo framework for American option pricing and the more recent ML-based approaches of Goudenege et al. (2020), but departs from them by providing a rigorous RKHS-based error decomposition with provable convergence rates—something those methods largely lack. The FALKON/Nyström acceleration (Meanti et al., 2020; Rudi et al., 2015) is incorporated as a drop-in replacement for full KRR, preserving the theoretical guarantees while reducing computational cost.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The modular three-term error decomposition (regression, Monte Carlo, propagation) is a clean and reusable framework that allows each component to be improved independently—e.g., swapping KRR for neural networks or MC for quadrature—without re-deriving the full analysis. The contractive property (c_P = e^{-2r} < 1) in the option-pricing setting provides a structural guarantee that errors dampen backward in time, which is a useful insight for practitioners. However, the empirical validation is explicitly preliminary (basic implementation, small sample sizes, no comparison with deep learning baselines), and the Monte Carlo step remains the computational bottleneck, limiting near-term practical impact. Open questions include whether the framework extends to continuous control spaces, whether the misspecification regime (β_t < 1) can be mitigated by the expectation operator's smoothing effect, and how the method scales to truly high-dimensional problems.
