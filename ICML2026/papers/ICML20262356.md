## Problem

Iterative self-play training of LLMs suffers from iteration-collapse, where performance peaks and then actively degrades as training continues. The paper identifies the root cause as cross-iteration degeneration: the questioner's task-generation distribution drifts toward previously exploited (seen) problems, weakening the effective learning signal. Existing diversity mechanisms (prompting, within-batch repetition penalties) address only superficial linguistic variety and do not directly penalize redundancy relative to the solver's evolving representation space.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a generated question q_t from the questioner, extracts its embedding h(q_t) from the solver's last-layer hidden states via mean pooling over task tokens, and compares it against a single learned prototype vector w_s in that same embedding space. The output is a scalar novelty reward R_Q^nov(q_t) = 1 − exp(−‖h(q_t) − w_s‖²/τ) that is added to the questioner's base RL reward with coefficient λ_nov. To construct the seen/unseen supervision for the Seen Detector, the pipeline over-generates 2× candidate questions, applies the baseline filtering, then randomly splits the curated pool 1:1 — the seen half updates the solver while the unseen half is held out exclusively for SD calibration, avoiding circularity.

## Method

OCNR introduces a lightweight Seen Detector (SD) that maintains a single prototype vector w_s in the solver's embedding space, trained with binary cross-entropy on a balanced seen/unseen split and stabilized with exponential moving average across iterations. The novelty reward is distance-based (a soft, locality-sensitive penalty around the seen prototype) rather than sigmoid-based (which would impose a global half-space boundary), ensuring only genuinely redundant tasks are penalized while preserving exploration freedom in unexplored regions. The training loop alternates five stages per iteration: over-generate and filter questions, build an SD-independent 1:1 seen/unseen split, update the solver on seen data only, update the SD using post-update solver embeddings, and update the questioner with the base reward augmented by λ_nov · R_Q^nov. The key design insight is anchoring novelty detection in the solver's own representation space (so seen-ness reflects what the solver has actually learned) and using a one-class prototype formulation that captures cross-iteration redundancy with a single vector, avoiding overfitting to transient questioner generation patterns.

## Results

In the R-Zero self-play setup (Qwen3-4B), the vanilla baseline peaks at Iter 3 with a Math AVG of 49.07 and then degrades to 45.27 by Iter 5, exhibiting clear iteration-collapse. OCNR sustains monotonic improvement, reaching 52.22 at Iter 5 and 54.51 at Iter 7 in extended training, maintaining a stable plateau near 54 through Iter 10. On general-purpose reasoning benchmarks, OCNR lifts the average from 26.37 to 35.17 (SuperGPQA 29.0→32.6, MMLU-Pro 54.7→60.2, BBH 11.0→12.7). In the Absolute Zero setup (Qwen2.5-Coder-3B), OCNR improves code generation (HumanEval+ 71.3→72.5, LiveCodeBench 24.4→25.1) and out-of-distribution math (AMC 37.5→42.5, MATH 62.0→64.8).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against R-Zero, OCNR initially lags in early iterations (Iter 1: 44.43 vs. 46.50 AVG) due to the warm-start phase where OCNR is not yet active, but surpasses the baseline by Iter 3 (49.63 vs. 49.07) and pulls decisively ahead in later iterations, reaching 52.22 at Iter 5 versus R-Zero's collapsed 45.27 — a 6.95-point gap. The compute-matched ablation (R-Zero 2×, which doubles data without novelty reward) confirms the gains are not from extra data: R-Zero 2× actually collapses faster (44.02 at Iter 5) than vanilla R-Zero (45.27). Against Absolute Zero (AZR), OCNR wins on all code benchmarks (HumanEval+ 72.5 vs. 71.3, MBPP+ 69.4 vs. 69.0, LiveCodeBench 25.1 vs. 24.4) and all math benchmarks except AIME24/25 where both tie at 3.3, with the largest gain on AMC (37.5→42.5, +5.0 points) and MATH (62.0→64.8, +2.8 points). Overall, OCNR consistently outperforms both baselines in the critical late-training regime where iteration-collapse manifests, while the early-iteration cost is minimal and transient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the R-Zero and Absolute Zero self-play frameworks, which establish the questioner-solver architecture for data-free LLM reasoning improvement. It departs from prior diversity mechanisms—prompt-level instructions in Absolute Zero and within-batch repetition penalties in R-Zero—by grounding novelty detection in the solver's own representation space via a one-class classification formulation (hypersphere around a learned prototype, following Tax & Duin's SVDD lineage). The key conceptual departure is reframing the diversity problem as a cross-iteration novelty problem: rather than preventing superficially similar questions within a batch, OCNR penalizes questions that are redundant relative to the solver's accumulated training history, using a lightweight Seen Detector updated via EMA across iterations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Iteration-collapse is a critical bottleneck that limits the practical utility of self-evolving LLMs, and this paper provides a simple, plug-in mechanism that stabilizes training across multiple self-play backbones without requiring external data or infrastructure. The compute-matched ablation showing that simply doubling training data accelerates collapse (rather than preventing it) is a useful negative result that clarifies the mechanism. Open questions include whether the single-prototype detector generalizes to larger models and more heterogeneous task distributions, whether the 2× over-generation cost is acceptable at scale, and how to extend the approach to open-ended domains where safety guardrails become essential.
