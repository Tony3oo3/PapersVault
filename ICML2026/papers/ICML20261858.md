## Problem

Despite widespread empirical practice, it remains unclear why pretraining and RL benefit from large, diverse datasets while SFT excels on small, curated ones, and how the interaction between pretraining and post-training data determines final model capabilities. The paper provides a theoretical framework to explain these data-quality and scale requirements, bridging the gap between observed phenomena (e.g., SFT double descent, RL data hunger) and principled understanding.

## Input / Output

The model receives a prompt of n input-output pairs (x_i, y_i) where x_i ∈ R^d are Gaussian and y_i = ⟨w*, x_i⟩ for a hidden weight w* ~ N(0, I_d), followed by a test input x_{n+1}. During post-training, the transformer generates k intermediate chain-of-thought steps (each appending a partial weight estimate to the embedding) before outputting the final predicted weight vector in R^d. The post-test distribution shifts from the pretraining covariance Σ_0 to Σ_0 + Δ, where Δ is a low-rank adaptation shift.

## Method

The paper analyzes a Linear Self-Attention (LSA) transformer performing in-context weight prediction, deriving closed-form characterizations of the SFT and outcome-supervision (OS) loss minimizers via pseudoinverse decompositions and random matrix theory. For SFT, they show the optimal post-training covariance aligns with the adaptation shift Δ (hard examples), and that increasing data volume B or prompt length n causes double-descent-like degradation due to interference with pretrained structure. For OS/RL, they prove the loss landscape has spectral-radius-dependent curvature scaling as k²ρ^{2k−2}, making training unstable unless the pretrained model already partially covers the target subspace. Asymptotic analysis in the proportional regime (d, m, n, B → ∞) yields explicit error formulas showing the global SFT minimum lies in the underparameterized regime (B < d). Results are validated on GPT-2 (9.5M params) and Qwen 2.5-7B-Instruct, confirming that SFT exhibits a turning point in data size while OS monotonically benefits from scale.

## Results

The paper's main empirical results validate four theoretical insights through LSA and GPT-2 (9.5M parameters, 12 layers) experiments on an in-context weight prediction task. For SFT, test error exhibits double descent as the number of prompts B increases, with the minimum attained at intermediate B values; harder examples (smaller θ, i.e., stronger alignment with the adaptation shift Δ) consistently yield lower test error. For Outcome Supervision (OS), the opposite trend holds: larger B and longer context n improve performance, while longer CoT (k) degrades it. GPT-2 experiments (Figure 4) confirm these trends: SFT shows a clear turning point beyond which increasing B or n hurts performance, whereas OS monotonically benefits from more data and longer context.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baselines but instead contrasts two post-training paradigms (SFT vs. OS/RL) and multiple data configurations under a unified theoretical framework. SFT with optimal data allocation (r=0, aligned with Δ) achieves the lowest test error at small-to-moderate B, while introducing interference (r=0.01 or r=0.1) causes the error to diverge as B→d, confirming that larger SFT datasets erode pretrained capabilities. OS, in contrast, requires large-scale data (large B and n) to overcome the sharp curvature of its loss landscape near the stability boundary (ρ≈1), and performs best on tasks already partially covered by pretraining (spectral alignment case). The GPT-2 experiments (Figure 4) empirically confirm the theoretical prediction: SFT test loss decreases then increases with B and n (turning point), while OS test loss decreases monotonically with B and n but increases with CoT length k. The paper also references Qwen 2.5-7B-Instruct on M23k (Appendix F.2) to illustrate practical relevance, though detailed numbers are deferred to the appendix.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the in-context weight prediction framework established by Huang et al. (2025a) and Javanmard et al. (2025) by introducing a novel pretrain-then-post-train pipeline where pretraining performs direct ICL while post-training uses multi-step CoT under SFT or outcome supervision. It builds on the population-regime analysis and sparsity structure from Huang et al. (2025a) but moves beyond the population limit to finite-sample and finite-prompt-length regimes, employing random matrix theory (Stieltjes transforms, Silverstein equations) to characterize the asymptotic test error. The work also connects to the broader LLM training literature (OpenAI o1, DeepSeek R1, s1, Llama 4) by providing theoretical grounding for empirically observed data-scaling behaviors that prior work had documented but not explained mechanistically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first unified theoretical explanation for three key empirical phenomena in LLM training: why balanced pretraining data creates latent capabilities, why SFT benefits from small hard datasets (with larger data causing interference-driven degradation), and why RL/OS requires large-scale data with careful spectral alignment. Key limitations include modeling RL as outcome-supervised regression (omitting sampling, advantage estimation, and policy gradients), restricting theory to linear self-attention, and validating on synthetic data. Open questions include whether the spectral-radius instability analysis extends to full RLHF with KL constraints, whether the double-descent behavior in SFT persists in nonlinear attention architectures at scale, and how these principles translate to real language tasks beyond linear regression.
