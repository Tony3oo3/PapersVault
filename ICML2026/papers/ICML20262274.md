## Problem

Standard generative models (diffusion, flow matching) require large datasets of independent samples to learn a data distribution, making them infeasible for spatial interpolation tasks where only a single, sparsely observed realization of a random field is available. This paper addresses how to build a generative model from one incomplete observation that simultaneously produces high-quality interpolations and calibrated uncertainty estimates, without access to additional training data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262274/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single realization of a multivariate random field observed at sparse positions (e.g., 25% of pixels for 512×512 images, or seismic traces on a 2D grid spaced 64 units apart in a 513×513×128 volume) as (position, value) pairs. It produces an ensemble of full-field samples from a posterior target process at arbitrary test locations, along with per-location predictive distributions that yield calibrated uncertainty. The output is structured as a generative posterior: each sample is a complete field realization, and the ensemble spread encodes epistemic uncertainty that shrinks near observations.

## Method

RP Flow models the signal as a random process and learns a position-conditioned transport map between a Gaussian source process ξ ~ GP(0, K) and the target process Z using Conditional Flow Matching with linear interpolation paths. The vector field v_θ(z, t, x) is parameterized as an implicit neural representation (MLP or U-Net) whose spatial input is a Random Fourier Feature embedding of position x, implicitly encoding the source GP's covariance without explicitly providing it during training. Training is self-supervised: source samples are drawn from N(0, I) at each position and target samples are the observed values plus additive noise for calibration. For posterior sampling, the learned flow's invertibility is exploited to map target observations back to the source space via reverse ODE integration, where standard GP regression (natural because the source is Gaussian and independent across variables) yields a posterior source process; forward ODE integration then maps posterior source samples back to the target space, producing unbiased posterior target samples (Proposition 3.1). Theoretical results guarantee that the transport preserves regularity (Theorem 3.3) and that target moments remain close to source moments (Theorem 3.4), enabling controlled modeling of discontinuities and convergence rates for ensemble-based statistics.

## Results

On the seismic interpolation task (the paper's primary application), RP Flow M (10M params) achieves PSNR 32.08, SSIM 0.93, W1 0.33, and PCE 0.06 on the synthetic dataset, and PSNR 28.77, SSIM 0.80, W1 0.39, PCE 0.02 on the real F3 dataset, outperforming all baselines by 4–5 dB in PSNR. On the Div2K image regression task, RP Flow matches the best INR baseline (RFF Network) in reconstruction quality (PSNR 25.30 vs. 25.20 for 4× upsampling) while achieving the best calibration (PCE 0.09 vs. 0.12 for calibrated GPR). The method also demonstrates that increasing model size from 1.7M to 10M parameters improves performance, with diminishing returns at 50M due to data scarcity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RP Flow consistently outperforms all four baseline families across both tasks. Against the RFF Network and SIREN (discriminative INRs), RP Flow achieves comparable PSNR/SSIM on image regression (25.30 vs. 25.20 for upsampling) but gains a decisive 5 dB PSNR advantage on seismic interpolation (32.08 vs. 27.05), where the generative modeling of spatial correlations becomes critical. Against GPR (noiseless), RP Flow improves PSNR by ~5.5 dB on seismic while reducing PCE from 0.38 to 0.06, showing that the flow-based transport captures non-Gaussian structure that Kriging cannot. Against GPR (calibrated), RP Flow matches or beats PCE (0.06 vs. 0.13 synthetic, 0.02 vs. 0.06 real) while substantially outperforming in reconstruction quality (PSNR 32.08 vs. 26.22). Deep GP is the weakest baseline, trailing RP Flow by over 6 dB in PSNR on seismic and showing poor calibration (PCE 0.40–0.48), likely due to the limitations of sparse variational approximation for highly structured 3D data.

## Relation to Prior Work

RP Flow sits at the intersection of three established threads: Conditional Flow Matching (Lipman et al., 2023) provides the generative training objective, Implicit Neural Representations with Random Fourier Features (Tancik et al., 2020) encode spatial covariance implicitly, and Gaussian Process Regression (Rasmussen & Williams, 2006) supplies the posterior conditioning mechanism. The key departure from standard CFM/diffusion is the transductive, single-realization setting: rather than learning a data distribution from many samples, the model learns a position-conditioned transport between a Gaussian source process and one observed target field, with spatial structure embedded in the INR. The posterior-sampling pipeline—reverse ODE to project observations into the Gaussian source space, GPR for interpolation, forward ODE to map back—is a novel composition that exploits the invertibility of the learned flow and the Gaussian structure of the source to obtain calibrated, unbiased predictions without requiring a large training dataset.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: many geophysical, medical, and environmental applications involve a single sparse field where collecting a large training set is infeasible, yet calibrated uncertainty is essential for decision-making. RP Flow demonstrates that a flow-matching + INR + GPR pipeline can outperform Kriging and discriminative INRs in high-dimensional sparse settings (seismic interpolation) while providing well-calibrated predictive distributions. The main limitation is the cubic-cost GP posterior computation, which the authors acknowledge as a bottleneck; future work on sparse GP approximations, meta-learning across multiple fields, and extension to indirect supervised tasks are natural next steps. The theoretical guarantees on regularity transfer and moment convergence provide a useful mathematical foundation but rely on Lipschitz assumptions that are hard to verify in practice.
