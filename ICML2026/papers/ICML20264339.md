## Problem

Why depth yields a genuine computational advantage over shallow methods remains a central open question in learning theory. While approximation theory shows deep architectures can represent certain functions more efficiently, it does not explain why such functions should be learnable from data with feasible sample sizes. This paper isolates the role of depth by studying compositional targets in a controlled high-dimensional Gaussian setting, where the target is globally a high-degree polynomial but admits a decomposable structure that allows learning to proceed in stages.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an i.i.d. dataset of Gaussian input vectors x ∈ R^d with labels y = f*(x) generated by a compositional teacher: x → h^(1) ∈ R^{d^ε} (via degree-k Hermite tensor contractions) → h^(2) ∈ R (via a quadratic form) → y = g*(h^(2)). The algorithm takes a maximum Hermite degree K_max for degree selection and produces the recovered first-layer feature tensors Â^(1), the second-layer matrix Â^(2), latent representations ĥ^(1) and ĥ^(2), and final label predictions ŷ_μ. The input is consumed through flattened Hermite polynomial feature maps of dimension O(d^k), and the output is structured as a staged recovery: first the feature subspace, then the quadratic form in latent coordinates, then a one-dimensional regression.

## Method

The paper introduces a hierarchical spectral learning procedure that replaces gradient descent with explicit layer-wise spectral estimators to isolate the computational role of depth. At the first stage, it constructs a label-weighted second-order moment matrix C_k^(1) = (1/n) Σ y_μ H_2(F[H_k(x_μ)]) over the O(d^k)-dimensional Hermite feature space and recovers the d^ε-dimensional first-layer feature subspace via PCA and eigenvalue thresholding, exploiting a BBP-type spectral transition that separates signal spikes from the noise bulk. Conditioned on this recovery, the second stage estimates the quadratic form A^(2) in the learned latent coordinates using an analogous moment matrix, requiring only O(d^{2ε}) samples. The final one-dimensional nonlinearity g* is then fitted by standard kernel regression. The theoretical analysis relies on a Gaussian equivalence principle (Hermite tensors behave asymptotically as Gaussian vectors via Wiener chaos CLTs) and a composition-of-Hermite lemma showing that the second-order Hermite projection of g*(⟨A, H_2(u)⟩) concentrates on ν_1 A, yielding a total sample complexity of O(d^{k+ε}) versus O(d^{4p}) for shallow kernel methods.

## Results

The main theoretical result is a sharp sample-complexity separation: the hierarchical spectral estimator recovers the compositional target with n = O(d^(k+ε)) samples (Theorem 3.3), while any shallow/kernel method requires n = O(d^(4p)) for a target of total polynomial degree 4p. For the canonical k=2, ε=1/2 case, this yields n = O(d^2.5) versus the shallow baseline of O(d^4). Numerical experiments (Figures 2–6) confirm the predicted phase transition at α = k + ε: MSE and feature overlap drop sharply at the theoretical threshold, and the BBP-type spectral separation between signal spikes and the noise bulk is clearly visible in the eigenvalue spectrum of the moment matrix.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not run head-to-head benchmark comparisons against named competing algorithms; instead, the comparison is between the hierarchical (deep) spectral strategy and the best possible shallow/kernel strategy in terms of sample complexity. For the quartic target (k=2, g*=identity), kernel and random-feature methods require n = O(d^4) samples to recover the degree-4 Hermite component, while the two-stage hierarchical spectral method succeeds at n = O(d^(2+ε)), a polynomial improvement that grows with d. The numerical experiments in Figure 2 show the MSE remaining high (near the variance of y) for α < 2.5 and dropping sharply beyond it, consistent with the O(d^2.5) threshold, whereas a single-shot quartic spectral estimator would not succeed until α ≈ 4. The advantage is further confirmed for non-polynomial readouts (tanh, centered ReLU in Figures 4–5) and higher-order Hermite features (k=3 in Figure 6), where the transition shifts to α ≈ 3 + ε as predicted. The rigorous proof is restricted to ε < 1/2, but experiments extend the observed transition to ε = 1 (Figure 3), suggesting the separation is robust beyond the proven regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the compositional function learning line (Nichani et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2025) to a growing intermediate dimension d^ε, where prior work typically fixes d_1=1. Rather than analyzing gradient descent dynamics, it replaces training with explicit layer-wise spectral estimators (PCA on Hermite moment matrices), building on the spectral estimation framework for multi-index models (Damian et al., 2024; Lu & Li, 2020) and the Gaussian equivalence principles for polynomial features (Wen et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2022). The key conceptual departure is isolating the computational role of depth through a staged spectral disentanglement procedure, connecting to the progressive feature-learning mechanism identified in Dandi et al. (2025) and the Random Hierarchy Models of Cagnetta & Wyart (2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean, fully analyzable framework showing sharp sample-complexity separations (n = O(d^{k+ε}) vs. O(d^{4p}) for shallow methods) that quantify the depth advantage for compositional targets, extending the Gaussian equivalence principle to hierarchical settings as a technical contribution of independent interest. However, the rigorous proof is restricted to ε < 1/2, the setting assumes Gaussian inputs and a co-designed teacher-student structure, and the connection to actual gradient-based training remains heuristic (Appendix C). Open questions include extending to deeper hierarchies, non-Gaussian inputs, power-law scalings, and establishing a fully rigorous link between the spectral procedure and SGD dynamics.
