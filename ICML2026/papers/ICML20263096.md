## Problem

Existing theory-of-mind (ToM) benchmarks for LLMs are largely non-interactive, restricted to Sally-Anne-style variants, and suffer from confounding factors such as tokenization effects, long-context demands, and biases introduced by translating embodied scenarios to text. This makes it difficult to isolate whether LLMs genuinely lack multi-agent reasoning abilities or simply fail due to unrelated model limitations. The paper addresses this by proposing an interactive, word-association-based game benchmark that eliminates these confounders while requiring genuine ToM for optimal play.

## Input / Output

Each agent receives a text prompt containing the game state: a summary of previous turns (code, hints, both guesses, hint history mapped to keywords, code history), role-specific information (Alice sees the 4 secret keywords and the 3-digit code; Bob sees keywords and hints; Eve sees only hints and history), and formatting instructions. Alice outputs 3 one-to-two-word hints in code order; Bob and Eve each output a 3-digit guess (non-repeating digits 1–4). The ToM experiments add structured prompts: Eve is asked to predict keywords (Prompt A), recall her pre-reveal belief (Prompt B), and predict a second interceptor's belief (Prompt C); Alice is asked to predict Eve's guess after providing hints. Outputs are parsed via regex with up to 10 re-prompting retries.

## Method

DECRYPTO is a three-player, two-team word-association game (Alice/Encoder, Bob/Decoder, Eve/Interceptor) lasting up to 8 turns, where Alice provides 3 hints referencing the meaning of secret keywords so Bob can decode a 3-digit code while Eve cannot. The benchmark is formalized as an iterated pragmatic inference game under the Rational Speech Act (RSA) framework, where Alice's utterance cost incorporates the probability of Eve's interception, and Bob's decoding requires second-order ToM (modelling Alice's beliefs over Eve's beliefs). Two word-embedding baselines (GloVe and Word2Vec with top-K cosine-similarity selection) establish upper bounds and expose cross-play coordination failures. The ToM platform adapts two classic cognitive psychology experiments—the Smarties Task (measuring representational change and false belief) and the Three Mountain Problem (measuring perspective taking)—requiring only new prompts and ~15 lines of code per experiment. Evaluation spans three settings: competition (A vs. B), ad-hoc coordination (A+B team vs. fixed Eve), and human-AI coordination, with metrics including miscommunication rate, intercept rate, win rate, and average game length.

## Results

On the DECRYPTO benchmark, even the strongest LLM tested (o3-high) achieves only a 6.67% win rate as the encoder-decoder team against the weakest interceptor (Llama 3.1-8B), while human teams achieve 40% win rate. As an interceptor, o3-high records 19.33 intercepts over 32 games, far exceeding all other models. On the ToM sub-tasks, strong representational change (RC) and false belief (FB) accuracy is ≤10% across all models, and most LLMs (DeepSeek-R1, Claude 3.7, GPT-4o, o1-high) erroneously predict that Eve will intercept on nearly every turn, including the first, revealing a fundamental failure in perspective taking.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The word-embedding baselines (GloVe and Word2Vec) outperform all LLMs as encoders: baseline-only teams achieve longer game lengths and fewer miscommunications than any LLM-only team, highlighting that LLMs struggle to produce strategically appropriate hints. However, LLMs surpass the baselines at intercepting other LLMs, with o3-high recording 19.33 intercepts versus 8–9 for the embedding baselines. In the competitive setting, even o3-high (the strongest model) achieves only ~34% win rate against the weakest interceptor (Llama 3.1-8B), and the vast majority of games end in interception. ExploreToM-8B, fine-tuned specifically on ToM tasks, underperforms its base model Llama 3.1-8B in both interceptor and decoder roles, demonstrating that DECRYPTO captures abilities beyond what existing Sally-Anne-type benchmarks measure. Among LLMs, reasoning models (o1-high, o3-high) show a clear advantage in both roles, with o3-high achieving the lowest miscommunication rate (6.67 as interceptor, 10.67 as decoder) and highest win rate as decoder (20%), yet still falling well short of the human baseline (40% win rate, 6.90 game length). In the ToM sub-experiments, Llama 3.1-70B slightly outperforms newer reasoning models on weak RC and FB, while only Llama 3.1-70B and o3-high correctly recognize that Eve cannot do better than a random guess on the first turn.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of ToM benchmarks (Sally-Anne variants, FANTOM, Hi-ToM, OpenToM) that evaluate LLMs on static, non-interactive text questions, arguing they suffer from confounding factors, data leakage, and saturation. It also distinguishes itself from multi-agent LLM benchmarks (Werewolf, Avalon, BattleAgentBench) that emphasize spatial reasoning or free-form dialogue with frequent deception. Conceptually, the paper grounds the game in the Rational Speech Act framework (Goodman & Frank, Degen) and adapts two classic cognitive psychology experiments (the Smarties Task and Three Mountain Problem) into an interactive LLM setting, bridging computational pragmatics, multi-agent RL, and developmental psychology in a way no prior benchmark has attempted.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a fundamental and actionable gap: LLMs systematically fail at perspective taking, confusing their own knowledge with that of other agents, which the authors argue will limit deployment in any multi-agent scenario with partial information. The finding that reasoning models perform worse than non-reasoning models on ToM tasks challenges the assumption that extended chain-of-thought improves social cognition. Open questions include whether multi-agent RL fine-tuning can close the ToM gap, how LLMs model humans versus other agents differently, and whether the benchmark's word-association-only design generalizes to richer pragmatic settings. Limitations include the small human study (10 games), the n³ computational cost of full evaluations, and the fact that DECRYPTO evaluates only a subset of ToM abilities (not intent inference or emotion understanding).
