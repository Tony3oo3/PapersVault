## Problem

Standard majority voting for aggregating multi-agent LLM responses treats all models equally, ignoring heterogeneity in model capabilities and correlations between their predictions. The paper asks whether first-order information (model accuracies) and second-order information (answer correlations) can be leveraged to provably improve upon majority voting in a training-free, label-free setting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260049/cropped_page22_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes N LLM predictions (one per agent) over K candidate labels for a given question, along with either known agent accuracies x_i (for OW) or empirically estimated second-order conditional probabilities P(A_i=s_k | A_j=s_l) computed across M questions without ground truth (for ISP, OW-L, OW-I). It outputs a single aggregated answer label. A key preprocessing step randomly shuffles candidate labels per question to enforce a uniform prior, and the final prediction is mapped back via the inverse shuffle. The entire pipeline is single-round and training-free, requiring only inference calls to the LLMs.

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary aggregation algorithms. Optimal Weight (OW) assigns each agent a weight w_i = σ_K^{-1}(x_i) where σ_K(x)=e^x/(K-1+e^x), and is proven to be the Bayesian-optimal aggregator among all possible (linear or nonlinear) schemes under conditional independence. Inverse Surprising Popularity (ISP) is a counterfactual variant of Prelec's Surprising Popularity rule: instead of scoring how much others expect agent i to predict s, it scores what agent i would predict if others had given *different* answers, thereby amplifying prediction bias in a controlled way. The paper proves that in LLM settings, standard SP actually underperforms majority voting (because LLMs lack the systematic biases SP exploits in human crowds), while ISP provably outperforms MV, which in turn outperforms SP (Theorem 4.1). For practical use without ground truth, two variants estimate accuracies from second-order information: OW-L fits accuracies via empirical risk minimization on conditional probabilities, and OW-I uses ISP's own predictions as pseudo-labels, both feeding into the same OW weighting formula.

## Results

On UltraFeedback (K=2), OW-L and OW-I achieve 73.66% accuracy, outperforming majority voting (72.21%) by 1.45 points and the single best model (73.14%) by 0.52 points. On MMLU (K=4), OW-L/OW-I reach 90.37% vs. MV's 89.32% (+1.05 points), while on ARMMAN (K=2), all three proposed methods (OW-L, OW-I, ISP) achieve 85.78% vs. MV's 85.24% (+0.54 points). In simulated experiments with heterogeneous agents, ISP outperforms MV by up to 5.35 points (K=2: 90.48% vs. 85.13%) and SP by up to 10.54 points, with the ISP–MV gap shrinking as K increases, consistent with the theoretical Θ(1/K) bound.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against majority voting (MV), all three proposed methods (OW-L, OW-I, ISP) consistently outperform the baseline on every dataset: OW-L/OW-I gain +1.45 points on UltraFeedback, +1.05 on MMLU, and +0.54 on ARMMAN, with t-statistics of 12.53, 23.39, and 3.22 respectively (all p < 0.001). Across all 16 model ensembles, OW-L beats MV in 97.92% of cases and OW-I/ISP in 95.83%, with absolute gains ranging from 0.54% to 14.20%. Against surprising popularity (SP), the paper proves in Theorem 4.1 that MV itself outperforms SP in expectation, and empirically confirms this in simulations (e.g., at K=2, MV achieves 85.13% vs. SP's 79.94%), while ISP recovers and surpasses both. The paper also notes that on MMLU, the single best oracle model (91.02%) slightly exceeds the aggregated methods (90.37%), but this oracle requires ground-truth access unavailable in the unsupervised setting; on UltraFeedback and ARMMAN, the proposed methods surpass even the best individual model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical information aggregation literature (Penrose, Tullock, Prelec et al.) to the multi-agent LLM setting, where the standard practice is simple majority voting. Its most direct intellectual predecessor is the 'surprising popularity' (SP) rule of Prelec et al. (2017), which the paper both builds upon and departs from: it proves SP actually underperforms majority voting for LLM ensembles and proposes Inverse Surprising Popularity (ISP) as a counterfactual correction. The Optimal Weight (OW) algorithm additionally connects to the Bradley-Terry model widely used in LLM post-training, providing a theoretical justification for inverse-logistic weighting of heterogeneous agents. The paper explicitly distinguishes its label-free, one-round aggregation problem from Mixture-of-Experts routing, which requires learning domain-specific expert selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the theoretically-motivated SP rule is strictly worse than majority voting for LLMs is a useful cautionary result for the multi-agent LLM reasoning community, which has largely adopted MV by default without formal justification. The training-free, label-free nature of OW and ISP makes them practical drop-in replacements for MV in existing pipelines (RLHF annotation, prediction markets, healthcare decision support), with the added benefit of provable improvement under mild assumptions. Open questions remain for open-ended generation (where the K-way discrete structure breaks down), for incorporating higher-order information beyond pairwise correlations, and for integrating the aggregation framework with gradient-based post-training to push capability boundaries further.
