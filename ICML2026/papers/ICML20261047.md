## Problem

In graph-structured MDPs with n agents, the global state-action space scales exponentially in n, rendering globally optimal policies intractable to compute or implement. Prior work on local policies either restricted to 0-hop (memoryless) policies without sub-optimality guarantees or compared performance to an entropy-regularized Bellman fixed point rather than the true global optimum. This paper asks: under what conditions on the graph topology and interaction strengths can an m-hop local policy approximate the globally optimal policy with a sub-optimality gap that decays exponentially in m?

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a graph G with n nodes, factored local state and action spaces, one-hop-factored transition kernels, and additively separable local reward functions. From the transition kernels, it constructs a pairwise influence matrix C whose (i,j) entry is the total-variation distance between node i's transition distributions under different state-actions at node j. The output is an m-hop local policy (single-agent) or m-hop cooperative policy (multi-agent) that each agent implements using only states and actions within its m-hop neighborhood, accompanied by an explicit sup-norm sub-optimality gap bound relative to the globally optimal Q-function. In the multi-agent setting, the policy additionally specifies that each agent must coordinate with its 2m-hop neighborhood to determine its action.

## Method

The core mechanism is a Dobrushin-type pairwise influence matrix C that quantifies how perturbations to one agent's state-action propagate through the transition kernel to affect other agents. For the single-agent case, the proof constructs a projected Bellman operator that fixes all states outside the m-hop neighborhood, yielding a γ-contraction whose fixed point Q† defines an m-hop greedy policy; the Bellman residual is bounded by summing influence-matrix powers over the m-hop shell, giving exponential decay governed by the spectral radius γρ(C). For the multi-agent case, a direct projection fails due to cross-agent coupling, so the authors instead construct a surrogate Q-function as a sum of per-agent m-hop projections of the optimal Q-function, then bound the action-oscillation of the difference Q* − Q̃ via decorrelation gaps. These gaps are controlled by two coefficients—θ_club (treating active agents as a joint block) and θ_sep (treating them independently)—and the final bound takes the minimum over both, yielding exponential decay in m/k for k active agents under the stability condition max{θ_club, θ_sep} < 1.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution is a sub-optimality gap bound for m-hop local policies that decays exponentially in m, governed by the spectral radius ρ(C) of the pairwise influence matrix. For the single-agent case (Theorem 1), the gap is bounded by (2·osc(R₁)·‖C‖∞/(1−γ)) · Σ_ℓ ((γC)^m(I−γC)^{-1})_{(1,ℓ)}, requiring only γρ(C)<1. For k active agents (Corollary 1), the gap decays exponentially in m/k under the condition θ⋆<1. Numerical experiments on a binary infection model across tree, loop, and grid topologies confirm the predicted exponential decay, with the grid graph showing the slowest convergence and the theoretical upper bound U^(m) being conservative by a factor of 4–5 for small hopsizes m∈{0,1}.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not run head-to-head quantitative comparisons against competing methods, as its primary contribution is theoretical. However, it positions itself against two closest prior works: Qu et al. (2020) optimize over 0-hop policies (agent depends only on its own state) but provide no sub-optimality gap guarantees, while Zhang et al. (2023) study approximately optimal local policies but measure the gap relative to an entropy-regularized Bellman fixed point rather than the true global optimum Q*. The present work is the first to establish the existence of m-hop local policies with sub-optimality gaps measured directly against the globally optimal Q-function, and to show exponential decay in the neighborhood radius m. In the multi-agent setting, the proposed m-hop cooperative policies require agents to coordinate with their 2m-hop neighbors, a class of policies not previously studied in the literature. The numerical experiments in Section 5 validate the theoretical predictions qualitatively, showing that tree topologies yield faster decay than grid topologies, consistent with the spectral radius analysis (ρ(C)≤2√d_max−1 for trees vs. ρ(C)≤d_max for d_max-regular graphs).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on graph-structured MDPs (Peyrard et al., 2007; Chornei et al., 2005) and most directly builds on recent results for local policies by Qu et al. (2020; 2022) and Zhang et al. (2023). However, it departs from both: Qu et al. restrict to 0-hop policies without sub-optimality guarantees, while Zhang et al. compare to an entropy-regularized Bellman fixed point rather than the global optimum. The analysis draws on Dobrushin-type stability matrices from correlation decay in graphical models (Gamarnik et al., 2014) and Markov chain mixing time theory (Levin & Peres, 2017), repurposing these tools to bound the Bellman residual of projected Q-functions. The paper is the first to establish existence of m-hop cooperative policies with guarantees relative to the standard optimal Q-function, and the first to study policies requiring explicit neighbor coordination.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The exponential decay of sub-optimality in the hopsize m, governed by the spectral radius of the pairwise influence matrix, provides a principled criterion for how much local information suffices in networked decision-making problems like epidemic control and wireless scheduling. The connection between graph topology (e.g., trees vs. regular graphs) and the required neighborhood size is a clean theoretical insight with practical implications for system design. Key open questions remain: an efficient algorithm for computing the multi-agent cooperative policy is not yet available, the analysis is limited to k ≤ m/2 active agents, and the competitive (non-cooperative) setting is entirely unexplored. The single-agent case has a constructive algorithm (projected Bellman iteration or FQI), but the multi-agent surrogate Q-function is defined only in terms of the unknown optimal Q*, leaving a gap between existence and computation.
