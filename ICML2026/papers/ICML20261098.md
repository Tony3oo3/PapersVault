## Problem

Self-consistency (SC) is widely used for LLM reasoning but lacks a unified theoretical understanding of its sample efficiency and scaling behavior. Under a fixed sampling budget, vanilla SC distributes samples uniformly regardless of question difficulty, making it prohibitively expensive at scale. Existing adaptive variants improve efficiency but rely on ad-hoc heuristics without provable guarantees or hyperparameter-free operation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text question, a fixed total sample budget T, and an LLM as input, and produces a selected answer (the empirical mode of the LLM's response distribution) along with a per-question sample allocation that sums to exactly T. Unlike prior adaptive methods where the average number of samples is stochastic and determined by hyperparameters, Blend-ASC strictly enforces the user-specified budget and supports batched inference for throughput. The method is hyperparameter-free: no threshold or prior needs to be tuned.

## Method

The paper reframes SC as empirical mode estimation and derives an exponential error bound governed by the margin m = (√p₁ − √p₂)², then shows that dataset-level error follows power-law scaling via the Laplace transform of the margin distribution. For fixed allocation, a Lagrangian optimization yields the closed-form policy x_m = m⁻¹(log m − log λ) for m ≥ λ, allocating zero samples to low-margin questions. For dynamic allocation, the PPR-1v1 martingale confidence sequence provides an asymptotically optimal stopping criterion. Blend-ASC blends the two regimes by linearly interpolating between ASC's confidence ranking (effective at low sample counts) and PPR-1v1's ranking (effective at scale) at each step t of T, selecting the question with the lowest combined score (1−t/T)·ASC(Q) + (t/T)·PPR(Q), achieving 4.8× fewer samples than vanilla SC on average.

## Results

Blend-ASC achieves a 4.8× average reduction in samples compared to vanilla SC across six model-dataset pairs (Llama-3.2-3B, Qwen-2.5-Math-7B, Qwen-2.5-32B on GSM8K, MATH, GPQA-Diamond). Specifically, at SC@64, Blend-ASC requires only 11–26 samples (5.33× improvement) versus 64 for vanilla SC, and at SC@128 it requires 9–80 samples (4.29× improvement). The method is hyperparameter-free, supports batching, and can fit any fixed sample budget, unlike prior adaptive methods whose sample counts are stochastic and determined by tuned thresholds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Adaptive SC (ASC, Aggarwal et al. 2023), Blend-ASC wins across all six model-dataset pairs at both SC@64 and SC@128. The gap widens dramatically at SC@128: ASC degrades to only 2.13× average improvement (and fails entirely on Llama-3B/GSM8K, requiring the full 128 samples), while Blend-ASC maintains 4.29×. Against PPR-1v1 (Anand Jain et al. 2022), the paper notes PPR-1v1 is the worst method in the low-sample regime due to its pessimistic Beta-based confidence, but can beat ASC at large sample budgets; Blend-ASC blends both signals via a linearly-scaled ranking, matching ASC's low-sample efficiency while inheriting PPR-1v1's asymptotically optimal exponential decay. Blend-ASC also outperforms the oracle Fixed-Allocation SC (which assumes full knowledge of the answer distribution μ), achieving 5.33× vs. 4.09× at SC@64, demonstrating that adaptive dynamic allocation strictly dominates a priori allocation even with perfect information.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2023) as a mode-estimation and majority-vote problem, then draws on voting theory (Aeeneh et al., 2025) and sequential mode estimation (Shah et al., 2020; Anand Jain et al., 2022) to derive the first dataset-level scaling laws for SC. It extends Aeeneh et al.'s per-question error bound to handle the unbounded candidate space inherent to LLM outputs, and tightens the per-question result relative to Huang et al. (2026). The proposed Blend-ASC algorithm is a direct synthesis of two existing adaptive methods—Adaptive Self-Consistency (Aggarwal et al., 2023) for the low-sample regime and PPR-1v1 (Anand Jain et al., 2022) for the high-sample regime—unified under a single hyperparameter-free, fixed-budget framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 4.8× average sample reduction over vanilla SC is practically significant for any deployment that scales test-time inference across large datasets, and the derived power-law scaling laws give practitioners a predictive tool for budgeting compute. The theoretical framework also clarifies when adaptive allocation helps (low-margin questions) and when it is wasteful, which is useful for system design. Key limitations include the assumption of majority-vote-style aggregation (ruling out open-ended generation), the inability to tightly bound misaligned questions, and the fact that fixed-allocation results rely on oracle access to the true answer distribution. Open questions include extending the framework to weighted or verifier-based voting, and understanding the optimal sequential-to-parallel compute tradeoff on hard benchmarks.
