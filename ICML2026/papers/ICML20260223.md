## Problem

Reduced-order models for multiscale systems like rotating detonation engines capture only dominant large-scale dynamics, leaving substantial structured discrepancies from unmodeled injector physics, mixing, and stiff chemistry. Bridging this multi-fidelity gap using only sparse sensor measurements—without access to the full high-fidelity state—remains largely unexplored, particularly for strongly nonlinear, multistable systems where standard data assimilation and neural operator approaches fail to recover fine-scale corrections.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sparse sensor time-history (p=25 uniformly distributed sensors over n=100 spatial grid points, with L=25 temporal lags) from the high-fidelity system, plus a pre-trained low-fidelity SHRED encoder trained on Koch's 1D RDE simulation. The full high-fidelity state is never observed during training or inference. The output is a full-state reconstruction decomposed additively into a low-frequency component (dominant wave dynamics) and a spectrally-sparse high-frequency correction (fine-scale injector-driven physics), along with SINDy-discovered governing equations for the missing physics terms.

## Method

Cheap2Rich decomposes reconstruction into two parallel pathways: (1) a low-frequency pathway using a DA-SHRED LSTM encoder trained on Koch's simulation, a GAN-based residual generator for latent-space distribution alignment between simulation and real sensor-induced codes, and a low-pass filtered decoder (retaining only Fourier modes k≤k_c); and (2) a high-frequency pathway that processes sensor-space residuals (observed minus LF prediction at sensor locations) through an attention-based LSTM encoder with time-derivative embedding, and a decoder with learned spatial deformation for velocity correction, regularized by a bandlimited spectral sparsity penalty. Training proceeds in four sequential stages: SHRED pretraining on simulation, GAN alignment, HF training with spectral sparsity (λ=0.1), and fine-tuning with reduced sparsity (λ'=0.01). The key insight is that enforcing spectral sparsity on the HF correction within a shared frequency band [0, k_c] encourages physically interpretable, parsimonious discrepancy representations, while the out-of-band penalty prevents noise absorption. SINDy is then applied to the LF correction, HF dynamics, and total discrepancy to discover explicit polynomial PDE terms that can be embedded directly into the reduced-order solver without neural network inference.

## Results

On the 3-wave co-rotating RDE dataset (250 snapshots, 100 grid points, 25 sparse sensors, L=25 lags), Cheap2Rich achieves RMSE = 0.103 ± 0.001 and SSIM = 0.364 ± 0.003, representing a 74.9% RMSE reduction and 250% SSIM improvement over the raw multi-fidelity gap (RMSE = 0.411, SSIM = 0.104). The learned HF correction exhibits spectrally sparse structure concentrated at wavenumbers k ∈ {3, 6, 9}, corresponding to harmonics of the three co-rotating detonation waves. SINDy-discovered missing-physics equations, when forward-integrated on top of Koch's 1D model without any neural network, recover 68% of the fidelity gap (RMSE = 0.139), confirming the interpretability of the decomposition.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Cheap2Rich decisively outperforms all baselines on both RMSE and SSIM under identical sparse-sensing constraints (p=25 sensors, no access to full high-fidelity states during training). FNO (vanilla) [Li et al., 2020] performs worst (RMSE = 0.431, SSIM = 0.063) because it was never exposed to sparse inputs during training, producing spectrally corrupted outputs. FNO (masked) [Li et al., 2020] improves to RMSE = 0.367 and SSIM = 0.258 by incorporating a binary mask channel, but still cannot bridge the multi-fidelity gap on high-fidelity sensor inputs. DeepONet [Lu et al., 2021] (RMSE = 0.431, SSIM = 0.112) reproduces simulation-like spatial structures despite receiving high-fidelity sensor values, confirming it is locked to the low-fidelity manifold. DA-SHRED [Bao & Kutz, 2025] (RMSE = 0.414, SSIM = 0.134) provides only marginal improvement over the raw multi-fidelity gap, confirming that the dominant discrepancy lies in unmodeled physics rather than latent distribution mismatch. The paper's key finding is that the multi-scale LF/HF architecture with spectral regularization is essential: no single-pathway method can simultaneously capture the dominant wave dynamics and the fine-scale injector-driven corrections.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SHRED/DA-SHRED framework (Bao & Kutz, 2025; Williams et al., 2024) for latent dynamical system learning by introducing a multi-scale LF/HF decomposition specifically designed to bridge the multi-fidelity gap between a cheap 1D surrogate (Koch's RDE model) and high-fidelity 3D reacting-flow simulations. The LF pathway reuses standard DA-SHRED with a GAN-based latent alignment to handle distribution shift, while the HF pathway is novel in combining attention-based temporal encoding with a bandlimited spectral sparsity regularizer to produce interpretable corrections. The SINDy-based physics discovery (Rudy et al., 2017) is repurposed here to identify governing equations for the learned discrepancy, extending prior single-correction DA-SHRED to a hierarchical multi-scale setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework demonstrates that a 1D model running in seconds, augmented by a lightweight neural network (168K parameters, ~48s total training on a laptop), can recover 75% of the fidelity gap to a 3D simulation requiring 2M+ CPU-hours, with the bonus of yielding interpretable SINDy equations (6-term polynomial PDE) that can be embedded directly into the reduced-order solver without any neural network at inference. Key limitations include evaluation on 1D azimuthal projections rather than full 3D fields, use of a high-fidelity simulation as the 'reality' target rather than experimental data, and a single operating condition (3-wave co-rotating). Open questions include extension to full 3D AMR fields, validation on physical RDE experiments, incorporation of discovered corrections into closed-loop injector control, and whether the spectral-sparsity HF decomposition generalizes to other multi-scale systems beyond combustion.
