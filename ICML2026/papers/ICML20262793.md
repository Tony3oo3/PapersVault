## Problem

Prior work on difficulty-based data selection for supervised fine-tuning (SFT) reports contradictory findings: some studies favor harder data, others prefer easier or intermediate-difficulty examples, and the conclusions appear context-dependent. No existing work identifies the role of training data size in determining optimal difficulty or provides a principled explanation for why these conflicts arise.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262793/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a base LLM (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B/7B, Llama-3.2-3B), a pool of SFT training data stratified into non-overlapping difficulty buckets (by ground-truth CoT length, NLL, or pass rate), and a data budget N ∈ {1k, 2k, 4k, …, 128k}. It produces a fine-tuned model evaluated on downstream benchmarks (Math500, AIME24, Minerva Math, MMLU, GPQA) and identifies the optimal difficulty bucket for each data size. Difficulty is operationalized via CoT token length in the main experiments, with NLL and failure-rate variants validated in the appendix; the iGSM synthetic setup uses operation count as a fine-grained difficulty knob.

## Method

The paper combines a systematic 2D empirical study (data size × difficulty on real math/science datasets), controlled synthetic experiments on iGSM, and a PAC-Bayesian theoretical analysis. The key empirical finding is that SFT performance is non-monotonic in difficulty for any fixed data size, and the interior optimum shifts toward harder data as the budget grows. Controlled iGSM experiments decompose test performance by difficulty slice, revealing two distinct failure modes: overly easy data causes a large extrapolation gap (model fits training distribution but fails on harder test cases), while overly hard data causes a large in-distribution generalization gap (model fails to learn the training distribution itself). The theoretical contribution (Proposition 4.1) upper-bounds the test risk as empirical risk plus a generalization term O(√(KL(π_train‖π_pre)/n)) and an extrapolation term O(TV(D_test, D_train)), showing that increasing n shrinks the generalization term while the extrapolation term is controlled by the train-test distribution mismatch. This decomposition explains all four empirical observations: data-size saturation, non-monotonic difficulty effect, shifting optimal difficulty, and model-relative difficulty.

## Results

On OpenR1-Math-94k (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, Math500), medium-difficulty data yields the best SFT performance (68.3%) versus easy (61.1%) and hard (61.7%), confirming a non-monotonic difficulty effect. On OpenMath (200k subset), easy data slightly outperforms medium and hard (71.7% vs. 70.1% vs. 69.0%), while on OpenScience (200k subset, MMLU), easy and medium are comparable (53.4% vs. 53.0%) but hard data degrades sharply (41.2%). The key scaling finding is that the optimal difficulty shifts toward harder examples as the training budget grows: removing the hardest 10% of data helps in the small-data regime but hurts in the large-data regime, while the reverse holds for easy data. Controlled iGSM experiments confirm all four observations (data-size saturation, non-monotonic difficulty, shifting optimum, model-relative difficulty) with clean separation across op-count levels from 2 to 20.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary method comparison is between standard SFT and Dynamic Fine-Tuning (DFT, Wu et al. 2025) on the controlled iGSM synthetic setup. DFT, which reweights token-level gradients toward high-probability (easy) tokens, exhibits a smaller in-distribution generalization gap and is therefore less sensitive to data difficulty. When training data are too difficult relative to a limited data budget, DFT outperforms SFT because it avoids the large generalization penalty of fitting hard examples. However, DFT's bias toward easy tokens limits its capacity to fit highly complex data, so its performance saturates more quickly as data size increases and is eventually surpassed by standard SFT once sufficient training data are available. Across the difficulty-size landscape, SFT shows a clear interior optimum that shifts rightward with more data, while DFT's curve is flatter and peaks earlier. The paper also implicitly compares three difficulty levels (easy/medium/hard) as data-selection baselines, showing that no single difficulty is universally optimal: the best choice depends on both the dataset and the available data budget.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on PAC-Bayesian generalization bounds (Alquier, 2024; McAllester, 1998) and domain-adaptation analyses (Nguyen et al., 2021) to formalize a two-gap decomposition—generalization gap plus extrapolation gap—specifically adapted to the LLM SFT setting. It departs from prior data-selection work (BERTIN, Marion et al., LESS, LIMO, s1) that treats difficulty as a fixed property to optimize, by showing the optimal difficulty is a function of data budget rather than a universal constant. The paper also connects to information-theoretic views on task complexity (Achille et al., 2021; Harutyunyan et al., 2020) to interpret the posterior-prior KL term as encoding task-specific adaptation cost.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reconciles a genuine tension in the SFT data-selection literature—where some studies favor harder data and others favor easier data—by showing the optimal difficulty shifts with data budget, providing practitioners with a principled rule (match difficulty to scale and model capability) rather than a fixed heuristic. The two-gap framework is clean and actionable, and the controlled iGSM experiments plus PAC-Bayesian analysis give the empirical findings a mechanistic grounding. A key open question the authors identify is efficiently estimating the optimal difficulty for real-world, high-perplexity data, where the simple synthetic-setting approach does not transfer. The DFT case study also suggests token-level reweighting methods inherit the same difficulty-size tradeoff, which is relevant for the growing body of work on SFT objective design.
