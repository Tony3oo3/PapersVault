## Problem

Reward learning from human feedback typically relies on a single feedback type or combines multiple types via manually weighted loss terms, leaving it unclear how to jointly infer a single reward function from heterogeneous signals such as demonstrations, comparisons, ratings, and stops that provide qualitatively different information. Existing multi-type approaches either train separate reward models and aggregate post hoc (raising scale-reconciliation issues) or collapse diverse feedback into a common intermediate representation, obscuring modality-specific structure.

## Input / Output

The method consumes four types of trajectory-level feedback—expert demonstrations, pairwise trajectory-segment comparisons, ordinal Likert-scale ratings, and stop/termination times—each simulated from Boltzmann-rational policies with type-specific noise parameters. It produces a probabilistic reward function as a Gaussian posterior over local state-action reward values (parameterized by a two-layer MLP encoder), an auxiliary action-value function Q(s,a) (also a two-layer MLP), and per-state-action reward uncertainty estimates. The framework is agnostic to the specific likelihood model per feedback type, so new modalities can be added by specifying their likelihood without architectural changes.

## Method

MAVRL formulates multi-type reward learning as Bayesian inference over a shared latent reward function, where each feedback modality contributes an explicit likelihood: Bradley-Terry for preferences, Boltzmann-rational policy for demonstrations, ordered logit for ratings, and a discrete-time hazard model over cumulative suboptimality for stops. A single shared variational reward encoder q_θ(R|s,a) = N(μ_θ, σ²_θ) is trained jointly with feedback-specific likelihood decoders by maximizing a unified ELBO that sums all feedback log-likelihoods, a KL regularization toward a standard normal prior, and a temporal-difference consistency term linking the encoder's reward predictions to one-step Bellman differences from the auxiliary Q-function. The reparameterization trick enables gradient flow through the encoder, and because all feedback appears as log-likelihood terms in one variational objective, the relative influence of each modality is determined by the data and likelihood models rather than hand-tuned cross-modal weights. This eliminates the need for manual loss balancing or post-hoc ensemble averaging while remaining extensible to any feedback type with a specifiable likelihood.

## Results

Across six environments (three grid-worlds and three continuous-control tasks), combining all four feedback types (preferences, demonstrations, ratings, stops) via MAVRL achieves the best or near-best normalized return in four of six environments, reaching 100.0 on grid_sparse, 99.7 on grid_cliff, 90.0 on grid_trap, and 100.0 on CartPole-v1. On the EPIC distance metric (lower = better reward recovery), the full combination attains the lowest values on all three tabular grids (0.481, 0.380, 0.520). Against the MCMC gold-standard baseline, MAVRL matches performance (100.0 vs. 100.0 on grid_sparse and grid_cliff; 95.3 vs. 93.0 on grid_trap) at roughly 30× lower wall-time (4.5–4.9 s vs. 154–156 s). Against Post-Hoc Reward Averaging, MAVRL substantially outperforms on every environment (e.g., 100.0 vs. 54.9 on grid_cliff; 93.5 vs. 59.9 on LunarLander-v3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MAVRL against two external baselines: (1) MCMC-based Bayesian IRL (Ramachandran & Amir, 2007), representing the gold standard for exact posterior inference, and (2) Post-Hoc Reward Averaging (Metz et al., 2025), which independently trains a reward model per feedback type and ensembles them. Against MCMC, MAVRL achieves comparable or slightly superior normalized returns on all three grid environments (100.0 vs. 100.0 on grid_sparse and grid_cliff; 95.3 vs. 93.0 on grid_trap) while reducing wall-time from ~155 s to ~4.5 s (~30× speedup), and is the only method applicable to continuous-control tasks where MCMC is intractable. Against Post-Hoc Averaging, MAVRL wins decisively on every environment: 100.0 vs. 54.9 (grid_cliff), 100.0 vs. 94.1 (grid_sparse), 95.3 vs. 47.3 (grid_trap), and 93.5 vs. 59.9 (LunarLander-v3), indicating that independently trained reward models conflate signals with different scales and miss cross-modal complementarity that joint variational inference captures.

## Relation to Prior Work

MAVRL builds directly on AVRIL (Chan & van der Schaar, 2021), which first applied amortized variational inference to Bayesian IRL from demonstrations, and extends it by replacing the single demonstration likelihood with a set of feedback-specific likelihood models (Bradley-Terry for preferences, Boltzmann-rational for demonstrations, ordered logit for ratings, and a discrete hazard model for stops) while maintaining a shared variational posterior. Conceptually, the paper aligns with the reward-rational choice framework (Jeon et al., 2020) that interprets diverse feedback as probabilistic observations of a latent reward, but goes further by providing a scalable inference algorithm rather than just a unifying formalism. It departs from prior multi-feedback approaches (Ibarz et al., 2018; Biyik et al., 2022; Metz et al., 2025) that either train separate reward models and combine them post hoc via ensembling or collapse heterogeneous feedback into a common intermediate representation, by performing joint inference over a single reward function through a unified ELBO that requires no manual cross-modal loss weighting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, extensible framework for combining heterogeneous human feedback in reward learning, addressing a practical gap where practitioners currently rely on ad-hoc loss weighting or post-hoc ensembling. The strong compensation effect under misspecification (≥90% performance retention when three of four channels are corrupted) suggests that multi-type joint inference offers genuine robustness beyond mere data augmentation, though the demonstration channel remains a structural bottleneck. Key limitations include evaluation exclusively in simulated environments with synthetically generated feedback, and the inferred reward uncertainty is not yet exploited for active feedback collection. Applying MAVRL to real human feedback with systematic biases, inconsistencies, and context-dependent judgments, and leveraging the posterior uncertainty for adaptive data collection, are the most impactful open directions.
