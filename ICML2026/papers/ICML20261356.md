## Problem

Population dynamics have predominantly been modeled as Wasserstein gradient flows, which minimize free energy and therefore can only capture purely dissipative, aperiodic dynamics. This framework fails to model important behaviors such as periodicity, oscillation, and emergent collective motion (e.g., Boids flocking). The paper asks whether a richer class of second-order population mechanics can be both theoretically grounded and learned directly from observational data without prescribing a reference process.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261356/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of empirical marginals (point clouds of N particles at M discrete time points) and an initial velocity field (which can be estimated from data via DICE or set to zero). It outputs a learned potential energy neural network and damping parameter γ, from which interpolated and forecasted marginals are generated by simulating the learned second-order ODE with the Leapfrog integrator. The loss is computed as Sinkhorn divergence between simulated and observed marginals, and the model is trained via Discretize-Then-Optimize backpropagation through the full simulated trajectory.

## Method

The paper derives Hamiltonian equations of motion from a damped Wasserstein Lagrangian (Theorem 2.2), showing that population dynamics reduce to a second-order system where particles are accelerated by the gradient of a population-level potential energy U[ρ_t] with damping γ. The key practical insight (Proposition 3.1) is that the intractable functional derivative δU/δρ can be bypassed by parameterizing the potential energy Ψ_θ as a neural network over the empirical measure and computing accelerations via automatic differentiation. The architecture uses multi-head self-attention and feed-forward blocks to capture interacting dynamics. Training proceeds by rolling out predicted dynamics from initial conditions using the Leapfrog integrator, computing Sinkhorn divergence against observed marginals over a random rollout horizon, and backpropagating through the discretized trajectories. The framework provably encompasses classical mechanics (γ=0, linear U), quantum mechanics (Fisher information term), and Wasserstein gradient flows (overdamped limit γ→∞), making it strictly more expressive than prior gradient-flow approaches.

## Results

On the Gulf of Mexico ocean vortex dataset, WLM achieves the best W₁ distances among all methods that do not use a reference process for interpolation (0.039, 0.028, 0.083, 0.050 at t₂, t₄, t₆, t₈), and is the only non-reference method capable of forecasting (W₁ = 0.065 from t₁₀, 0.180 from t₀). On the Embryoid Body scRNA dataset, WLM achieves the best leave-one-out interpolation W₁ of 0.704 ± 0.021, outperforming the previous best OT-MFM* (0.713 ± 0.039). On the Boids dataset, WLM dramatically outperforms gradient-flow baselines, achieving 0.496 ± 0.024 train W₁ versus 2.467 ± 0.193 (NN-APPEX) and 3.084 ± 0.229 (JKONET*), while also forecasting 50 unseen marginals at W₁ = 1.309 ± 0.034.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WLM outperforms all non-reference-process baselines on ocean vortex interpolation, achieving W₁ of 0.028–0.083 versus 0.052–0.358 for OT-MFM*, DICE, and action matching methods, and is second only to CURLY-FM* (which requires a 'curly' reference process) overall. Critically, WLM is the only non-reference method that can forecast beyond the observation horizon, achieving W₁ = 0.065 (from t₁₀) and 0.180 (from t₀), substantially beating reference-based SBIRR (0.567/1.062) and SNAP-MMD (0.473/0.896). On the EB scRNA dataset, WLM (0.704) beats the previous best OT-MFM* (0.713) and all Schrödinger bridge, flow matching, and action matching baselines. On Boids, WLM (0.496 train W₁) dramatically outperforms JKONET* (3.084) and NN-APPEX (2.467), which fail to capture the periodic, rule-based dynamics. On gradient-flow SDEs, WLM with learnable friction matches or exceeds NN-APPEX in the harder unpaired setting (0.246 vs 0.260 forecast W₁) while also recovering the correct high-friction regime (γ ≥ 500), confirming it can learn gradient flows as a special case.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Wasserstein Lagrangian flow framework of Neklyudov et al. (2023b) and the conservative Wasserstein Hamiltonian flows of Chow et al. (2020) by introducing a damped Lagrangian and deriving the corresponding Hamiltonian equations of motion. The key conceptual departure is that prior Wasserstein Lagrangian methods required a reference process (i.e., a specified Lagrangian) to define the action minimization, whereas WLM learns the population-level potential energy and damping directly from data, eliminating the need for a reference. This positions the work against the dominant gradient flow paradigm (JKONET, NN-APPEX) and flow matching methods (CURLY-FM, OT-MFM), which the paper shows are strictly less expressive: gradient flows can only model first-order dissipative dynamics, while WLM encompasses second-order mechanics including oscillatory and periodic behavior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to forecast beyond the observed horizon (not just interpolate) is a practical advantage that flow matching and Schrödinger bridge methods fundamentally lack, making WLM relevant for scientific prediction tasks in biology, oceanography, and physics. The Boids experiment is particularly notable: a method designed for energy-based dynamics successfully learns rule-based agent dynamics and generalizes to unseen initial populations, suggesting that emergent collective behavior may admit a variational principle — a conceptual result with implications beyond the specific dataset. Key open questions include identifiability of the learned mechanics (multiple WLM formulations can produce the same marginals), whether simulation-free training is feasible, and the expressivity limits of the framework for non-least-action systems. The simulation-based training (rollout + backprop) is also a practical bottleneck that limits scalability to very large populations.
