## Problem

Predictive inverse dynamics models (PIDMs) have been shown empirically to outperform behavior cloning (BC) in offline imitation learning, yet the reasons behind this advantage remain unexplained. In particular, it is unclear why PIDMs can beat BC even without additional data sources, and under what conditions the modular decomposition into a state predictor and an inverse dynamics model is guaranteed to help.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263967/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The PIDM takes a current state representation and a predicted future state representation (k steps ahead) as joint input to the IDM policy, producing a continuous action in [-1,1]^2 (2D navigation) or [-1,1]^4 (3D game). In the 3D setting, raw third-person video frames are encoded by a pretrained ViT-B/16 Theia encoder, with three frames spanning the previous second used to approximate a single state. Notably, the state predictor is action-free (conditioned only on the current state, not on actions), and in the 3D experiment it is additionally conditioned on the time step t, which is not available to either the IDM or BC policies.

## Method

The paper provides a theoretical decomposition of the PIDM advantage over BC. Theorem 1 shows that the optimal PIDM predictor's error is always ≤ BC's, with the gap equal to the variance of the optimal IDM action prediction across possible future states (i.e., how much knowing the future reduces action uncertainty). For finite-sample, non-optimal estimators, Corollary 1 reveals a tradeoff: the variance reduction from future conditioning (Δ_{p*}) is partially offset by additional bias and variance introduced by covariate shift between the true future-state distribution p* used in training and the approximate state predictor p̂ used at test time. For sample efficiency, Theorem 2 and Lemma 1 use the Cramér-Rao lower bound to show that under a local shared parameterization, the IDM's Fisher information is at least that of BC, guaranteeing η ≥ 1 (PIDM needs no more samples than BC). Empirically, a simple instance-based state predictor paired with an IDM achieves up to 5× fewer demonstrations in 2D navigation and 66% fewer in a 3D video game, with qualitative analysis confirming the IDM attends to future states only where action uncertainty is high.

## Results

On four 2D navigation tasks, PIDM requires up to 5× fewer demonstrations than BC to reach comparable performance, with an average efficiency ratio of 3.25× at 80% performance and 3.5× at 90% performance (Table 1). In the 3D Bleeding Edge Tour task with high-dimensional visual inputs and stochastic transitions, PIDM reaches 87% milestone success with only 15 human demonstrations, while BC requires 25 demonstrations for 81% success, yielding an efficiency ratio of 1.66× (i.e., BC needs 66% more samples). PIDM also achieves a higher maximum performance ceiling (0.98–0.99 goal ratio) compared to BC (0.95–0.99) across the 2D tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is standard Behavior Cloning (BC), which learns a policy conditioned only on the current state. PIDM outperforms BC on every task and every performance threshold tested. In the 2D navigation suite, PIDM requires 2–5× fewer human demonstrations than BC to reach 80–95% of maximum performance, with the largest gap (5×) on the Maze task and the smallest (1.33×) on Zigzag at 95% performance. In the 3D Bleeding Edge Tour task, PIDM achieves 95% milestone success versus BC's 81% at comparable data budgets, and the gains are statistically significant (p < 0.001) for all dataset sizes except 5 demonstrations. The paper further shows that PIDM's advantage grows when the state predictor is more accurate (e.g., using A* planner data instead of human data reduces state-prediction error and further widens the sample-efficiency gap to 5× on Multiroom).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first theoretical analysis explaining why Predictive Inverse Dynamics Models (PIDMs), introduced empirically by Du et al. (2023) and refined in latent space by Xie et al. (2025), consistently outperform behavior cloning. It builds on the multi-step inverse dynamics framework of Lamb et al. (2023) and Koul et al. (2024) for the IDM component, and applies standard tools from statistical estimation (Cramér-Rao bounds, Fisher information) and covariate shift adaptation to formalize the bias-variance tradeoff inherent in the PIDM architecture. Unlike prior works that demonstrated PIDM's empirical superiority without explanation, this paper derives explicit conditions under which the modular decomposition is guaranteed to improve sample efficiency over BC.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The tradeoff framework—variance reduction from future conditioning versus additional bias/variance from covariate shift—gives practitioners a principled lens for when PIDM will help and when the state predictor's errors will erode gains. The finding that even a trivial state predictor (nearest-neighbor retrieval in 2D, time-step conditioning in 3D) yields substantial sample-efficiency improvements suggests the architecture's benefits are robust to predictor quality, which is practically encouraging. Limitations include the scalar-parameter, i.i.d., and correct-specification assumptions, and the analysis does not yet cover correlated data, diffusion/transformer policy classes, or non-MSE losses, leaving formal extensions to future work. The 5× sample-efficiency gap in 2D and 66% in 3D, while meaningful, are demonstrated on relatively narrow task suites.
