## Problem

Existing methods for learning to rank from incomplete rankings rely on rigid assumptions: the latent ranking must follow a Plackett-Luce or Mallows distribution, and the missing comparisons must be MCAR or follow well-behaved patterns like winner or top-h feedback. This paper asks whether it is possible to learn the top-k items when both assumptions are relaxed—allowing any ranking model satisfying the mild complete consensus property and any MNAR feedback mechanism that depends on latent positions.

## Input / Output

The learner is passive: in each round it receives a strict partial order over n items (an incomplete ranking), generated by sampling a latent permutation from a ranking model ρ and then applying a positional censorship mechanism that determines which pairs of positions are compared. The learner cannot choose which comparisons to observe; it only decides when to stop. Upon termination, it outputs the ordered collection of k distinct items estimated as the true top-k, with confidence 1−δ. The algorithm is a pure-exploration (PExact) stopping-time procedure, not a fixed-budget estimator.

## Method

PIRATE (Patching Incomplete RAnkings through Transitive Edges) builds a dominance graph by adding a directed edge (i,j) only when a Hoeffding-style confidence interval certifies, with probability ≥1−δ/n², that item i precedes item j in the underlying ranking. It then takes the transitive closure of this graph to propagate inferences along paths, and terminates when the out-degrees in the closure uniquely identify the top-k items (out-degrees n−1, n−2, …, n−k). The key insight is that under the complete consensus property, the conditional preference probability μ_{i,j} exceeds μ_{j,i} if and only if i truly precedes j, so both direct and transitive inferences are correct with high probability. The paper proves PIRATE is δ-correct and provides a near-optimal instance-dependent sample complexity bound of O(1/c_k* · log(n/(c_k* δ))), where c_k* is a bottleneck identification rate that factorizes into a Frobenius product of a ranking-model matrix and a censorship-mechanism matrix.

## Results

PIRATE achieves a near-optimal instance-dependent sample complexity upper bound of (128/c_k*)·ln(96n/(c_k*δ)), matching a lower bound of (1/(16c_k*))·ln(1/(4δ)) up to logarithmic factors. Under the Plackett-Luce model, the minimax rate for learning the full ranking with winner feedback is Õ(n/Δ²_{1,2}·log(1/δ)), and with top-h feedback it improves to Õ(n/(h·Δ²_{1,2})·log(1/δ)), matching specialized algorithms from Saha & Gopalan (2019a). In the empirical evaluation (Figure 2), PIRATE is the only algorithm that recovers the true ranking under MNAR positional censoring, while all baselines fail.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares PIRATE against three baselines on synthetic PL data: RANK CENTRALITY (Negahban et al., 2017), BORDA COUNT (Ammar & Shah, 2011), and PL PAIRWISE MLE. Under MCAR missing comparisons (Figure 2a), PIRATE achieves comparable normalized Kendall distance to all three baselines, confirming that its generality does not sacrifice performance on well-behaved instances. Under MNAR positional censoring (Figure 2b), PIRATE is the only algorithm that converges to the true ranking, while RANK CENTRALITY, BORDA COUNT, and PL PAIRWISE MLE all fail to recover the correct order due to their reliance on MCAR or PL-specific assumptions. Theoretically, PIRATE's sample complexity matches the state-of-the-art lower bounds of Saha & Gopalan (2019a,b) under PL with winner and top-h feedback, confirming near-optimality in these restricted settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and generalizes the fragmented literature on learning from incomplete rankings, which has been studied under separate assumptions: PL or Mallows ranking models on one axis, and MCAR, winner, top-h, or rank-dependent coarsening feedback on the other. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of positional censoring as a single MNAR feedback model that subsumes all previously studied feedback types, combined with the mild 'complete consensus' property as a general ranking model assumption that encompasses PL, Mallows, and Babington-Smith models. The PIRATE algorithm's use of a dominance graph with transitive closure for top-k identification is a new mechanism distinct from the Markov-chain (Rank Centrality), score-based (Borda Count), or pivoting approaches in prior work, and the paper explicitly demonstrates that those established methods fail under the MNAR conditions it studies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first unified theoretical framework showing that top-k ranking is learnable under a broad class of ranking models and a general MNAR feedback mechanism, with near-optimal instance-dependent sample complexity. The factorized identification rate (Corollary 4.4) offers a practical diagnostic: it separates the difficulty caused by ranking-model noise from that caused by the censorship mechanism, and reveals the non-trivial insight that an intermediate level of ranking noise (φ* > 0) can actually facilitate learning compared to the deterministic limit. Open questions include whether the passive setting can be improved by active query strategies under positional censoring, how the framework extends to multi-annotator or heterogeneous censorship distributions, and whether the dominance-graph approach scales computationally to very large item sets.
