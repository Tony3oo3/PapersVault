## Problem

While VLMs are known to struggle with spatial and relational reasoning, the mechanistic basis by which CLIP-style contrastive models acquire (or fail to acquire) left-right spatial understanding remains unclear. This paper asks whether CLIP-style Transformers can learn faithful encodings of relative spatial relations and, if so, by what internal mechanism enables generalization to unseen object pairs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265323/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The vision encoder consumes 1D images of length 10 (a CLS token followed by 9 pixel positions, where objects are single-integer values at one position and background is 0), while the text encoder consumes token sequences following templates such as '[label] is in the image' or '[label1] is on the left of [label2]'. The model produces a single image vector from the CLS token output and a text vector from the EOT token, compared via cosine similarity for image-to-text retrieval. Evaluation tests whether the correct caption ranks highest among all training and validation captions under three regimes: unseen positions, unseen configurations of seen label pairs, and entirely unseen object pairs.

## Method

The paper trains lightweight dual-encoder Transformers (bidirectional vision, causal text) end-to-end with a standard CLIP contrastive loss on a synthetic 1D image-text dataset, then performs a mechanistic analysis by decomposing pre-softmax attention logits into weight and bias terms, and further into token-embedding (E) and positional-embedding (P) cross-products. The key finding is that the EP cross-term E·W_QK·P^T generates a horizontal attention gradient that systematically biases attention toward one spatial side, breaking left-right symmetry; ablating this term drops left-right discrimination to chance while preserving object recognition. A theoretical analysis shows that for out-of-distribution generalization to unseen object pairs, the position-dependent term must dominate the token-dependent term (|Π| > |T|), providing a formal justification for why positional gradients are necessary. The authors further extend the analysis to Rotary Position Embeddings, identifying two alternative mechanisms (bias-induced positional gradient and learned low-rank subspace alignment) by which RoPE can produce the required asymmetry, and confirm the mechanism persists in 2D images.

## Results

In the 1D synthetic setting, CLIP-style contrastive training achieves near-perfect unseen-pair generalization (accuracy approaching 100%) when trained on sufficient label diversity (N_tot=20, N_pair=15, n_2=10), while varying the number of spatial configurations (layout diversity) has negligible effect on performance. Ablating the positional-token embedding cross term (EP term) from the pre-softmax attention logits at inference drops unseen-pair discrimination accuracy to approximately 0.5 (chance level), confirming this term as the causal driver of left-right symmetry breaking. Extending to 2D (4×4) images, the model achieves 98% unseen-pair generalization accuracy with the same hyperparameters, and a 1-layer RoPE model (no learnable positional embeddings) also generalizes successfully through two distinct mechanisms: a bias-induced positional gradient and learned low-rank key subspace alignment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic interpretability paper without external baselines; the 'comparisons' are internal ablations and setting variations. The key comparative finding is that label diversity (number of object categories N_tot) is the primary driver of generalization, while layout diversity (number of spatial configurations n_2) has minimal impact—models trained with only 5 configurations per pair generalize as well as those with 10. In the left-only vs. left+right textual representation comparison, a 1-layer text encoder fails to generalize with both representations, but adding a second layer to the text encoder restores generalization while preserving the same EP-term-driven attention gradient in the vision encoder. The RoPE analysis (App. J) shows that even without learnable positional embeddings, generalization is achieved: the bias-induced gradient mechanism (Π_bias) and the learned subspace alignment mechanism (low-rank W_K projecting tokens into a 1D subspace) both contribute, with PCA confirming PC1 captures dominant variance and SVD showing effective rank ≤ 6 for W_K.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the CLIP dual-encoder contrastive framework (Radford et al., 2021) and the Transformer Circuits mechanistic interpretability methodology (Elhage et al., 2021), applying pre-softmax attention logit decomposition to a controlled vision-language setting. It departs from the large body of prior work that *evaluates* spatial and compositional failures in VLMs (ARO, CLEVR, Winoground, SugarCrepe) by instead providing a *mechanistic explanation* of how spatial relations are encoded at the attention level. The paper also connects to the grokking literature on the role of weight decay in enabling generalization, and extends its analysis to Rotary Position Embeddings, linking the toy-setting mechanism to positional encoding schemes used in modern LLMs (Llama 3, Qwen3, Gemma 3).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is, to the authors' knowledge, the first mechanistic account of how CLIP-style models acquire left-right spatial discrimination, identifying a specific, ablatable attention gradient arising from positional-token embedding interactions. The finding that label diversity (not layout diversity) is the primary driver of generalization offers a concrete data-design principle for spatial-relation training. Key limitations include the highly constrained 1D/2D synthetic setting, simplified architectures (no LayerNorm/MLP in the main mechanistic analysis), and small model sizes; whether the same gradient mechanism operates in large-scale VLMs trained on natural images remains an open and important question. The RoPE analysis and 3-object extension suggest the mechanism is more general than the minimal setup implies, but bridging to real-world spatial reasoning is left for future work.
