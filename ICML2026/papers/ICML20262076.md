## Problem

Forward oversmoothing in deep GNNs is well-characterized, yet it remains unexplained why GNNs fail to learn expanding weights that would counteract it, as theory permits. This paper identifies the missing piece: backward oversmoothing of backpropagated errors creates spurious near-stationary points that trap optimization at high-loss regions, making deep GNNs fundamentally harder to train than their MLP counterparts.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a vanilla GNN with a stochastic, irreducible propagation matrix P (e.g., D^{-1}A), bounded node features X^(0), a 1-Lipschitz non-linear activation ρ, and weights with spectral norm s = max_k ||W^(k)||_2 ≤ λ^{-α}. It produces explicit gradient norm bounds ||∂L/∂W^(k)||_F that decay exponentially in depth L, characterizing when the network is at a global δ-stationary point. Empirically, the paper measures forward oversmoothing via E(H^(k)) (distance to span(1_n)) and backward oversmoothing via E_π(B^(k)) (distance to span(π), the stationary distribution of P) across 100-layer GNNs and MLPs on six real and two synthetic graph datasets.

## Method

The core mechanism is backward oversmoothing: the backpropagated error B^(k) is smoothed by P^T from output to input, and when the forward signal is already oversmoothed at layer k, the modulation ρ'(H^(k)) becomes approximately constant, reducing the backward recursion to an approximately linear GNN. This allows explicit characterization of the backward oversmoothed limit as B^(k) ∝ π 1_n^T B^(L)(W_L...W_{k+1})^T, even with non-linear ρ and non-symmetric P. The main result (Thm. 4.2) then shows that for sufficiently deep GNNs, any δ_{W_L}-stationary point of the last layer W^(L) automatically makes the entire network a global δ-stationary point, with δ shrinking exponentially in L. Corollary 4.4 constructs explicit spurious stationary points with loss close to the label variance, and Proposition 4.5 proves this phenomenon is specific to GNNs by exhibiting a counter-example MLP where a stationary last layer does not imply global stationarity.

## Results

The paper's main contribution is theoretical: Theorem 4.2 proves that for sufficiently deep GNNs (depth L ≳ log(1/δ)/(ξ_q(α) log(1/λ))), any δ_{W_L}-stationary point of the last layer implies a global δ-stationary point of the entire network, with δ ≲ λ^{ξ_q(α)L} + λ^{-αL}δ_{W_L}. Corollary 4.4 further shows these spurious stationary points can incur loss as high as σ_y²/2 − ε (near the label variance) for centered regression problems. Empirically, on 100-layer GNNs across six datasets, gradient norms collapse to near-zero across all layers within a few epochs while loss plateaus at high values, whereas 100-layer MLPs on the same data continue to reduce loss with non-vanishing gradients.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new GNN architecture or training method, so there are no traditional method-vs-method baselines. The closest comparison is GNN vs. MLP: on all six datasets (Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed, WikiCS, Chameleon, Squirrel), 100-layer GNNs with P = D⁻¹A rapidly reach near-zero gradient norms across all layers while maintaining high loss, whereas 100-layer MLPs (P = Id) on the same data retain non-vanishing gradients and continue reducing loss. Proposition 4.5 formally establishes this distinction by constructing a regression problem where an arbitrarily deep MLP satisfies all assumptions of Theorem 4.2 (including W_L at a stationary point and F^(L) lower-bounded) yet still has a non-zero gradient at some intermediate layer. The effect is most pronounced on datasets with smaller λ_P (Synthetic dense: λ_P = 0.742, WikiCS: λ_P = 0.917) and less visible where λ_P is close to 1 (Cora: 0.971, Pubmed: 0.986). Skip connections partially mitigate the issue but the loss still plateaus compared to MLPs without them.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Park & Kim's (2024) notion of backward (gradient) oversmoothing from the linear, symmetric-P setting to general non-linear activations and non-symmetric stochastic propagation matrices, and crucially connects it to the optimization landscape rather than merely characterizing signal decay. It departs from the vanishing-gradient framing of Alvaro Arroyo et al. (2025) and Peng et al. (2024) by showing backward oversmoothing produces spurious stationary points rather than simply shrinking gradient norms. The forward oversmoothing literature (Oono & Suzuki 2020; Wu et al. 2023; Rusch et al. 2023) provides the necessary forward-smoothing assumptions, while the deep-learning optimization landscape literature (Yun et al. 2018, 2019; Zhou & Liang 2018; Ding et al. 2019) supplies the stationary-point vocabulary, but the paper's core results are explicitly GNN-specific and do not transfer to MLPs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first rigorous explanation of *why* deep GNNs are hard to train: the last layer's easy stationarity cascades into a global near-stationary point via backward oversmoothing, trapping the network at high loss. The GNN-specificity result (Prop. 4.5) rules out the possibility that this is merely a generic deep-learning pathology, pointing to the stochastic message-passing structure as the root cause. Major open questions remain: the paper does not analyze optimization dynamics (whether gradient descent actually reaches these spurious points), does not extend to skip connections or normalization (the standard mitigations), and the forward oversmoothing assumption for non-linear ρ with non-symmetric P remains unproven in general. These gaps make the results a foundational step rather than a complete picture.
