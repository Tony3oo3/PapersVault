## Problem

Existing LLM graph-reasoning benchmarks focus on static or local queries (shortest path, connectivity, traversal), overlooking the high-order, multi-scale structural evolution that characterizes real-world systems. Persistent homology (PH) provides a principled framework for tracking how topological features such as components and cycles are born, persist, and die across a filtration, but no benchmark has tested whether LLMs can reason about this dynamic, scale-dependent process rather than answer fixed graph queries.

## Input / Output

Inputs are weighted undirected graphs encoded as edge lists (with matrix-based and code-like variants in ablations), optionally paired with a second graph for strategy-design tasks, plus a natural-language task instruction. Outputs range from scalar counts (0D components, 1D simplices), temporal values (birth/merge times under a given filtration), a selected filtration function from a fixed set of six (node degree, edge weight, K-shell, closeness, betweenness, eigenvector centrality), a generated length-5 non-uniform filtration sequence drawn from {1,…,10}, or a binary class label on real-world molecular graphs. Ground truth is computed algorithmically via Vietoris-Rips complex construction and persistence diagram extraction, not human annotation.

## Method

LLM4PH decomposes the full PH pipeline into four progressively harder task categories—simplicial structure recognition, filtration evolution tracking, filtration strategy design, and real-world graph inference—totaling 10 tasks over 11,400 samples spanning synthetic graphs (10/15/30 nodes) and three molecular datasets (BZR, COX2, LDHFR). All tasks use the Vietoris-Rips complex as the graph-induced simplicial complex, and evaluation employs accuracy for recognition tasks and ranking-based metrics (Wasserstein-distance ranking among candidate filtrations) for strategy-design tasks. Beyond per-task evaluation, the paper conducts cross-task analyses: prompt-encoding ablations (three graph representations × three instruction styles), a terminology ablation (topological vs. graph-theoretic phrasing), supervised fine-tuning of LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct on small graphs to test scale transfer, a compositional five-step PH pipeline that hybridizes LLM reasoning with code-based diagram generation, and subgraph-sampling experiments on ego-Facebook for large-graph scalability. Nine LLMs (GPT-4.1-mini, GPT-4o, Claude, Gemini-2.5, DeepSeek-R1/V3, Qwen3-30B, Mistral-small-3.1-24B, Llama-3-70B) are evaluated zero-shot, with GraphWiz as a graph-specialized baseline.

## Results

On the LLM4PH benchmark (11,400 samples across 10 tasks), model performance degrades sharply with task difficulty. On simple tasks, Gemini and DeepSeek-V3 achieve near-perfect 0D Component Counting (≥0.995) but all models drop below 30% on 1D Simplex Counting for medium/large graphs. On medium filtration-evolution tasks, DeepSeek-R1 dominates Component Count Under Filtration (98.8%/99.5%/97.5% across sizes) while Component Merge Time remains near-random for most models (best 47% on small graphs). On hard filtration-strategy tasks, all models achieve average ranks of ~4/6 (Optimal Filtration Selection) or ~43–77/126 (Non-Uniform Filtration Generation), indicating performance only marginally above random. On real-world classification, Filtration Selection for Classification is near-random (~0.32–0.36) while Direct Classification reaches 0.98–1.0, exposing a gap between topological reasoning and shallow pattern matching.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates GraphWiz (Chen et al., SIGKDD 2024) as the sole external graph-specialized baseline, adapted via LoRA on 700 task-format dialogues. GraphWiz is dramatically outperformed by the strongest general LLMs on every task: 0D Component Counting (0.110 vs. 1.000 for Gemini/DS-V3), Component Merge Time (0.067 vs. 0.470 for DS-V3), and Direct Classification on BZR (0.000 vs. 0.995 for Llama), with complete format failure on Non-Uniform Filtration Generation (rank 127.0, i.e., invalid, vs. best 43.4 for Claude). This gap indicates that standard graph instruction tuning does not transfer to filtration-based state tracking or topological strategy design. Internally, DeepSeek-R1 is the standout model on filtration-evolution tasks (Component Count Under Filtration: 97.5–99.5% across all sizes), while Claude leads on Non-Uniform Filtration Generation (rank 43.4 on small graphs) and real-world Filtration Sequence Generation (0.970 on LDHFR). All models, however, remain near-random on Component Merge Time and Hard filtration-strategy tasks, with no model exceeding 47% accuracy on the former or achieving a rank below 3.58/6 on the latter.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing persistent homology—a well-established tool in topological data analysis—as a structured reasoning testbed for LLMs, rather than treating it as a downstream computational pipeline. It extends the line of LLM graph-reasoning benchmarks (Fatemi et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2024) that focus on static, local graph queries (shortest path, connectivity, traversal) by introducing multi-scale structural evolution as the core reasoning target. The benchmark design draws on the PH literature (Edelsbrunner & Harer; Zomorodian & Carlsson; Carlsson) for its mathematical grounding but deliberately reframes PH concepts as natural-language reasoning tasks, positioning itself against graph instruction-tuning work (GraphWiz, InstructGraph) that operates on low-order features without topological abstraction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LLM4PH provides the first systematic, reproducible evaluation of whether LLMs can reason about multi-scale topological evolution, revealing a specific and well-characterized failure pattern: models handle local counting and strongly scaffolded prompts but collapse on tracking structural transitions, designing filtrations, and maintaining global connectivity state across thresholds. The near-random performance on hard tasks (filtration strategy design) with variance nearly equal to the mean suggests current models have not internalized any meaningful topological planning capability. Open questions include whether persistence-aware pre-training or hybrid neuro-symbolic architectures can close the gap, whether graph-encoder-coupled LLMs reduce the observed reasoning deficit, and whether the text-based interface is the binding constraint or whether the topological reasoning itself is the bottleneck.
