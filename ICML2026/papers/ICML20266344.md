## Problem

The nascent discourse on sustainable AI predominantly focuses on environmental sustainability while neglecting economic and social dimensions, and existing paradigms (green AI, inclusive AI) address only one axis of sustainability in isolation. This paper identifies a recurring tension between climate awareness (mitigating AI's environmental impact) and resource awareness (equitable access to AI development resources), arguing that failing to jointly address both risks undermining the other and is fuelling a short-sighted global AI arms race.

## Input / Output

The analysis draws on existing literature on sustainable AI, AI governance, and historical materialism, supplemented by empirical data on global compute capacity distribution (Top500 supercomputers), national AI investment figures, and weighted author demographics from three premier AI conferences (ICML, ICLR, NeurIPS) spanning 2006–2025, with countries classified by World Bank income group. The output is the CARAML framework—a structured set of actionable recommendations across five levels of agency (individual, community, industry, government, global)—along with a theoretical analysis of how material conditions shape AI discourse and policy.

## Method

The paper applies Karl Marx's base-superstructure framework from historical materialism to analyze how the material conditions underpinning AI (computational infrastructure, energy consumption, capital investment, labour) actively shape and reinforce the superstructure of norms, values, and regulatory frameworks, arguing this configuration preserves the status quo. It identifies two concrete contradictions: the social sustainability paradox of low-carbon datacenters (LMICs lack the renewable energy infrastructure to power greener AI, creating a double penalty), and the environmental sustainability paradox of efficient models (Jevons' paradox, exemplified by DeepSeek's 2800% popularity surge and the reasoning-model token explosion, negating per-unit efficiency gains through increased aggregate consumption). The CARAML framework then proposes multi-level interventions—ranging from redefining AI success metrics with resource-normalized performance at the individual level, to pre-registering large-scale experiments at the community level, to carbon caps at the industry level, to mandatory AI Impact Assessments at the government level, to establishing a 'Right to Sustainable Compute' at the global level—to jointly advance climate and resource awareness and deter the AI arms race.

## Results

The paper presents empirical evidence of extreme geographic and economic concentration in AI: US private AI investment reached $109.1B in 2024 (≈12× China, 24× UK), and the top-500 supercomputers are overwhelmingly located in a handful of high-income nations. Publication data from ICML, NeurIPS, and ICLR (2006–2025) shows that high-income countries account for the vast majority of AI research output, with lower-middle- and low-income countries consistently underrepresented and the gap widening over time. The paper quantifies the environmental cost of frontier training (e.g., Llama-3.1 405B: 30.84M GPU-hours, 21.5 GWh, 8,930 t CO₂e in the US vs. 14,737 t in India) and projects AI electricity demand at ~945 TWh by 2030 (~447 Mt CO₂e), while noting that efficiency gains trigger rebound effects (e.g., DeepSeek's 2,800% popularity surge and 10× token inflation from reasoning models offset per-token savings).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not benchmark a proposed method against baselines. Instead, it contrasts four AI paradigms along two axes (climate awareness × resource awareness): SOTA AI (low/low), Green AI (high climate / low resource), Inclusive AI (low climate / high resource), and Sustainable AI (high/high, the paper's target). The central empirical comparison is between climate-aware and resource-aware interventions: the authors show that low-carbon datacenter strategies (climate-aware) create a 'double penalty' for LMICs lacking renewable infrastructure, while efficiency-focused approaches (resource-aware) trigger Jevons-paradox rebound effects (e.g., DeepSeek's 90% per-token energy reduction is negated by a 10× increase in token generation from reasoning models). The paper also contrasts the base-superstructure dynamics of the current AI arms race with the colonial-era feedback loop, arguing that export restrictions and capital concentration in the 'base' are reinforced by policy and narrative 'superstructure' mechanisms, and that the CARAML framework's multi-level interventions (individual → global) are necessary to break this cycle—something no single technical baseline or efficiency method can achieve alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sustainable AI discourse initiated by Van Wynsberghe (2021) and the critical environmental analyses of Wright et al. (2025) and Schwartz et al. (2020) by arguing that the field has over-indexed on climate awareness (green AI) while under-addressing resource awareness (equitable access, economic sustainability). It repurposes Marx's base-superstructure framework from historical materialism as an analytical lens for AI development—a conceptual move not previously applied to frontier AI governance—linking material infrastructure (GPUs, energy, capital) to the policy, regulatory, and narrative superstructure that sustains the current concentration of AI power. The CARAML framework synthesizes recommendations from the green AI, inclusive AI, and AI sovereignty literatures into a unified multi-level action structure, departing from prior work that typically addresses these dimensions in isolation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and underappreciated tension: pursuing climate awareness alone (e.g., low-carbon datacenters) can deepen the resource divide for LMICs, while pursuing resource awareness alone (e.g., efficiency gains) can trigger Jevons-paradox rebound effects that negate environmental benefits. The CARAML framework and its 'Right to Sustainable Compute' reframing offer a structured vocabulary for policy and community action, but as a position paper it provides no empirical validation of whether these multi-level interventions can actually be coordinated or whether the base-superstructure lens yields testable predictions. Open questions include how to operationalize carbon caps for AI, whether pre-registration norms can scale to foundation-model training, and whether the Marxist framing is productive or polarizing for building the broad coalitions the paper calls for.
