## Problem

Existing instruction-based image editing benchmarks exhibit a pervasive bias toward salient, large-scale objects, leaving the ability to edit small-scale objects (occupying only 1–10% of image area) largely unexplored. Even state-of-the-art models like Gemini-3-Pro frequently fail to localize and correctly modify such targets, and standard LMM-as-a-Judge evaluators cannot reliably discern the minute visual changes involved, making both benchmarking and evaluation of this capability inadequate.

## Input / Output

Each DLEBench sample provides a source image in which the target object occupies 1–10% of the total area (often partially occluded or embedded in cluttered scenes), paired with a text editing instruction drawn from seven types (change material, color, OCR, shape; removal, replacement, count). The evaluated model produces an edited image, which is then scored on two criteria—Instruction Following and Visual Consistency—each using a 4-point hierarchical rubric defined by distinct failure modes (e.g., Localization Failure → Wrong Action → Over Modification → Flawless Execution). The benchmark comprises 1,889 samples, including 118 multi-object editing instances with up to 8 targets per image.

## Method

The paper introduces a three-stage semi-automated pipeline to construct DLEBench: (1) counterfactual synthesis via GPT-4.1 converts visual-reasoning QA pairs from V*-Bench, MME-Realworld, and Pixel-Reasoner into structured editing metadata (instruction, type, target object, captions); (2) a crop-and-edit mechanism with an adaptive bbox expansion strategy (λ(s) interpolating between 6.0× for 32 px objects and 0.3× for 256 px objects) generates high-quality reference images by isolating the target region for precise manipulation; and (3) human verification ensures semantic and visual fidelity. For evaluation, the paper proposes a dual-mode framework: the Tool-driven Mode lets an LMM agent iteratively invoke external tools (GroundingDINO for localization, Zoom-In for spatial search, pixel-level Difference for change detection, and Real-ESRGAN for crop enhancement) to compensate for perceptual limitations, while the Oracle-guided Mode uses human-annotated bounding boxes to pre-crop (for IF) or white-mask (for VC) the target region, decoupling evaluation from localization errors. Both modes employ refined, failure-mode-based score rubrics that replace ambiguous quantifiers with objective hierarchical criteria, achieving significantly higher alignment with human judgments than standard LMM-as-a-Judge baselines.

## Results

On DLEBench (1,889 small-scale editing samples, target objects occupying 1%–10% of image area), Gemini-3-Pro achieves the highest average score of 65.55, followed by the open-source Bagel-Think at 61.00 and Step1X-Edit at 55.39. The most challenging instruction type is Change Count, where even the best model (Gemini-3-Pro) scores only 43.87. On the Instruction Following criterion alone, the best model (Gemini-3-Pro) averages just 48.97 out of 100, revealing a significant deficiency in small-scale editing fidelity across all evaluated IIEMs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 10 IIEMs (2 closed-source, 8 open-source) on DLEBench. Gemini-3-Pro leads overall (65.55) but the open-source Bagel-Think (61.00) substantially outperforms the proprietary GPT-Image-1 (40.25), challenging the assumption that closed-source models dominate in small-scale editing. On the IF criterion, all models perform poorly (best: Gemini-3-Pro at 48.97), while VC scores are generally higher, except for GPT-Image-1 (35.17) which aggressively modifies non-target regions upon localization failure, in stark contrast to Bagel-Think (86.43) which adopts a conservative strategy. Change Count is the most difficult task for every model, with scores dropping 20+ points below other instruction types, indicating that multi-object small-scale editing remains largely unsolved. Among open-source models, Step1X-Edit (55.39) and UniWorld-V2 (49.80) form a second tier, while MagicBrush (23.16) and UniWorld-V1 (32.42) show consistently poor performance across all scales, suggesting their architectures lack the fine-grained perception needed for small-object editing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of instruction-based image editing benchmarks (I2EBench, ImgEdit, KRISBench, UniREditBench) by identifying and filling a specific gap: all prior benchmarks exhibit a bias toward salient, large-footprint objects, leaving small-scale editing (1–10% area) untested. Conceptually, it builds on the LMM-as-a-Judge paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023) but departs from it by introducing a dual-mode evaluation framework (Tool-driven and Oracle-guided) that compensates for LMMs' weak fine-grained visual perception. The data construction pipeline repurposes visual reasoning datasets (V*-Bench, MME-Realworld, Pixel-Reasoner) through a counterfactual synthesis strategy, converting QA pairs into editing instructions without manual curation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DLEBench is the first dedicated benchmark for small-scale object editing, revealing that even SOTA models like Gemini-3-Pro score only ~49 on Instruction Following, exposing a fundamental localization and precision gap that general-purpose benchmarks mask. The dual-mode evaluation framework addresses a practical concern: standard LMM-as-a-Judge methods (GPT-4.1, Gemini-3-Pro) demonstrably fail to align with human judgments on fine-grained edits, undermining the reliability of automated evaluation in this regime. Key limitations include an unbalanced instruction-type distribution (Color and OCR dominate), absence of spatial manipulations (moving, scaling, rotating), and a relatively small sample size (1,889). Open questions include whether targeted training on small-object editing can close the performance gap, and whether the evaluation framework generalizes to other fine-grained visual tasks beyond editing.
