## Problem

Existing frame sampling strategies for video-language models rely on fixed budgets or uniform sampling, failing to adapt to varying content density and task complexity. While a few adaptive methods exist, they either require co-training with a specific VLM backbone or use inference-time heuristics, limiting transferability. The paper asks whether frame selection can be a lightweight, plug-and-play module that jointly determines which frames are relevant and how many are needed for a given query.

## Input / Output

FrameOracle receives N uniformly sampled video frames (N=16 or 64) encoded by DINOv2 and a text query tokenized by Qwen2.5-VL, and outputs a compact keyframe subset V_S ⊂ V_C along with a predicted frame count K. Each frame is represented by a single token in the fusion encoder, keeping the module's overhead minimal relative to the downstream VLM. The selected frames are passed directly to any downstream VLM without architectural modification, making the selector fully backbone-agnostic.

## Method

FrameOracle consists of a cross-modal Transformer encoder that processes a concatenated [query_token; text; frames] sequence, followed by two prediction heads: a Rank Head that assigns a scalar relevance score to each candidate frame, and a K Head (a lightweight Transformer decoder) that outputs a categorical distribution over possible frame counts. Training follows a four-stage curriculum that decouples the two objectives to avoid optimization instability: Stage 1 aligns cross-modal representations using SigLIP similarity as a teacher with RankNet loss; Stage 2 trains the Rank Head using leave-one-out VLM loss as soft targets; Stage 3 trains the K Head to predict the frame count that minimizes a z-scored task loss plus a linear frame-cost penalty; Stage 4 fine-tunes both heads jointly on FrameOracle-41K, the first VideoQA dataset with verified minimal-sufficient keyframe annotations. The staged design is critical—jointly training Stages 2 and 3 causes the K Head to collapse and the Rank Head to become unstable, as confirmed by ablation experiments.

## Results

FrameOracle reduces 16-frame inputs to an average of 10.4 frames without accuracy loss (e.g., LLaVA-Video-7B: 54.6→54.7 avg accuracy) and reduces 64-frame inputs to 13.9 frames while improving accuracy by +1.5% on average (e.g., LLaVA-Video-7B: 54.6→56.0; LLaVA-OneVision-7B: 49.8→51.6). With a 128-frame candidate pool, Qwen3-VL-8B + FrameOracle (27.8 frames) outperforms Video-XL-7B (256 frames) and Video-XL-2-8B (~10k frames) on LongVideoBench and Video-MME. Computationally, the 16→10.4 setting cuts total FLOPs by 39.8% and latency by 41.0%, while the 64→13.9 setting still reduces total compute by 9.1% and tokens by 13.0% relative to the raw 64-frame baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SOTA VLMs in Table 1, FrameOracle-equipped Qwen3-VL-8B (128→27.8 frames) surpasses Video-XL-7B (256 frames) on LVB (65.2 vs. 49.5) and Video-MME (69.1 vs. 64.0), and outperforms Video-XL-2-8B (~10k frames) on LVB (65.2 vs. 61.0) while using ~360× fewer frames. In the fixed 8-frame keyframe selection comparison (Table 2), FrameOracle outperforms BOLT (LVB 56.0 vs. 55.6; Video-MME 57.5 vs. 56.1; EgoSchema 62.8 vs. 62.2 on LLaVA-OneVision-7B) and KFC (LVB 56.0 vs. 55.6; Video-MME 57.5 vs. 55.4), and beats Frame-Voyager on NExTQA MC (77.8 vs. 73.9). On Qwen2.5-VL-7B, FrameOracle (128→8) exceeds ViaRL (Video-MME 57.4 vs. 57.3; MLVU 59.6 vs. 58.2) and K-frames (LVB 59.1 vs. 57.7; MLVU 59.6 vs. 60.4, a narrow loss). FrameOracle also outperforms token-level budgeting (B-VLLM) by +5.3 to +10.3 points on Perception, Video-MME, and EgoSchema, and beats the reproduced MovieChat compression baseline by +12.3 points on LongVideoBench (56.5 vs. 44.2). The one notable exception is MLVU with 64-frame candidates, where FrameOracle does not consistently surpass methods using 128–256 frames or 1 FPS sampling, though a segment-wise inference variant recovers the gap (65.4 vs. 62.9 on LLaVA-OneVision-7B).

## Relation to Prior Work

FrameOracle carves new ground at the intersection of two threads: fixed-budget keyframe selection (KeyVideoLLM, BOLT, MDP3, ReFoCUS) and adaptive frame-count prediction (AdaFrame for classification, FFS/SeViLA for co-trained VLMs). It departs from both by being simultaneously plug-and-play (no co-training with a specific backbone) and adaptive in frame count, a combination no prior method achieves. The training curriculum—progressing from SigLIP cross-modal similarity to leave-one-out VLM loss to supervised keyframe annotations—repurposes knowledge-distillation and ranking-loss ideas from information retrieval into a staged video-selection pipeline, while the FrameOracle-41K dataset introduces a supervision signal (minimal sufficient keyframes per question) that no prior VideoQA resource provides.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a ~35–40% reduction in VLM compute and latency with no accuracy loss (or a small gain) makes long-video understanding substantially cheaper across five diverse backbones without retraining. FrameOracle-41K, as the first VideoQA dataset with validated keyframe annotations, opens a new axis of supervision that could simplify future selector training. Key limitations include degraded performance on extremely long videos (MLVU, >2 hr) where candidate coverage becomes the bottleneck, and the four-stage curriculum adds training complexity that the authors acknowledge could be reduced with more keyframe-annotated data. An open question is whether the adaptive frame-count signal generalizes to streaming or hour-scale settings without the segment-wise inference hack.
