## Problem

GNNs suffer severe performance degradation under out-of-distribution shifts because they exploit environment-specific spurious correlations rather than truly invariant relationships. Existing invariant learning methods for graphs rely on empirical or heuristic designs that lack a principled, computationally tractable mechanism to characterize and quantify invariance in the space of graph distributions, leaving a critical gap in how invariance is formally defined and optimized.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260978/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph G = (X, A) with node attribute matrix X ∈ R^{N×D} and adjacency matrix A ∈ R^{N×N}, and produces a single class label. Internally, each graph is split into an invariant subgraph and a variant subgraph via a learnable edge mask (Top-η edge selection with η=0.3), and the two subgraphs are encoded separately by GIN encoders (4 layers, d=128) with mean-pooling readout. The final prediction is made from the invariant subgraph representation, while the variant representation is used only for adversarial disentanglement training across T=10 augmented environments.

## Method

DIGL formulates the shared invariant prototype for each graph category as a Wasserstein barycenter of the category's invariant representation distributions across T augmented environments, treating per-environment representations as geometric distortions of this prototype. The training objective combines three terms: a supervised classification loss on invariant subgraph predictions, a cross-environment alignment loss that minimizes the 2-Wasserstein distance between each environment's invariant representation distribution and the barycenter (computed in closed form under a Gaussian assumption), and a disentanglement loss that maximizes environment-specific information in the variant representation while minimizing mutual information (via HSIC or InfoNCE) between invariant and variant representations. The barycenter and representations are updated via alternating optimization within each batch. A theoretical generalization risk bound is derived via Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality, showing the three loss terms respectively tighten the training risk, barycenter-alignment gap, and prototype-to-test distribution gap.

## Results

On the GOOD benchmark (Table 2), DIGL achieves the best or near-best AUC/accuracy across all 8 evaluation conditions (4 datasets × 2 shift types), e.g., 73.38 AUC on GOOD-HIV (covariate shift), 85.43% on GOOD-SST2 (covariate shift), 53.72% on GOOD-CMNIST Color (covariate shift), and 92.36% on GOOD-Motif (concept shift). On image-to-graph datasets (Table 1), DIGL reaches 83.63% on CMNIST at 0.8 bias, 79.42% at 0.9, and 78.19% at 0.95, yielding average improvements of 26.37% over ERM and 7.09% over the best baseline under 0.9/0.95 bias. The method also outperforms the two OT-based baselines (GDL, GWF-GNN) by large margins (e.g., +11.36 AUC on GOOD-HIV covariate shift vs. GDL), confirming that OT-based distance alone without barycenter alignment is insufficient for OOD generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DIGL outperforms all 15 baselines across the majority of evaluation conditions. Against the strongest non-OT baselines, DIGL beats GCAL (Zuo et al., 2026) by 2.70 AUC on GOOD-HIV (covariate), 3.30% on GOOD-SST2 (covariate), and 4.60% on GOOD-CMNIST Color (concept shift); it surpasses InfoIGL (Mao et al., 2026) by 1.49 AUC on HIV and 4.49% on CMNIST Color (covariate). The OT-based baselines GDL (Vincent-Cuaz et al., 2021) and GWF-GNN (Xu et al., 2023) underperform DIGL by 7.96–11.36 AUC/accuracy points, confirming that merely using GW distance for representation learning without barycenter-based alignment is insufficient. Causal methods DIR (Wu et al., 2022b) and DisC (Fan et al., 2022) show the largest gaps (e.g., DIR scores only 26.37% on CMNIST Color covariate shift vs. DIGL's 53.72%), indicating that heuristic edge-mask interventions struggle under complex shifts. IB-based methods IBCS (Yuan et al., 2025) and OOD-SEIB (Di et al., 2025) are competitive on some datasets but still trail DIGL by 1–8 points, particularly on the hardest concept-shift settings. Augmentation methods EERM (Wu et al., 2022a) and MARIO (Zhu et al., 2024) are outperformed by 4–12 points, and ERM (Vapnik, 1991) serves as the weakest baseline, with DIGL achieving average gains of 26–31% over it on image-to-graph datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

DIGL builds directly on EERM's adversarial environment augmentation for simulating distribution shifts and DIR's learnable edge-mask mechanism for invariant subgraph extraction, but departs from both by introducing a geometric invariance principle grounded in optimal transport theory. Where prior graph OOD methods (IRM-based, causal, information-bottleneck) characterize invariance through empirical consistency constraints or heuristic disentanglement objectives, DIGL formulates the shared invariant structure as a Wasserstein barycenter across environment-specific representation distributions, providing a principled metric-space characterization. The disentanglement module further combines this OT alignment with adversarial training and mutual-information minimization (HSIC/InfoNCE), creating a unified objective that no single prior method achieves.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers the first geometric (OT-based) characterization of invariance for graph representations, which could inspire new theoretical tools for distribution-shift robustness beyond graphs. However, practical limitations remain: the Gaussian assumption on representation distributions, the O(Td³) cost of barycenter updates, and the reliance on adversarial augmentation to approximate unseen test environments all constrain scalability to large-scale or truly open-world settings. The authors acknowledge the need for lightweight encoders and approximate Wasserstein estimators for large-scale deployment, and the extension to node/edge-level tasks and explainability remains open.
