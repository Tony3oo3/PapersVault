## Problem

Existing code and reasoning benchmarks (HumanEval, LiveCodeBench, AIME, etc.) evaluate models on closed-form tasks with a single known optimal answer and binary pass-or-fail scoring, yet many frontier computer science problems are intrinsically open-ended: the global optimum is unknown or computationally intractable, yet solution quality can be objectively measured. No comprehensive, cross-domain benchmark exists that targets this regime of unsolved, partially-scored, verifiable CS problems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263207/cropped_page2_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

Each problem is delivered as a pure-text specification (no diagrams) including formal constraints, I/O format requirements, and any necessary API stubs or interaction library. The model must produce a single self-contained executable solver program in one shot, with no access to a code editor, Python environment, execution feedback, or external tools. The evaluator runs the submitted program on parametrically generated test instances under strict time and memory limits, then assigns a continuous score from 0 to 100 based on task-specific quality metrics (e.g., packing density, total value, query count, recall-latency tradeoff) normalized between a trivial baseline and a human expert reference solution.

## Method

FrontierCS is a 240-problem benchmark split into two tracks: 172 algorithmic problems (51 optimization, 47 constructive, 74 interactive) adapted from programming contests into open-ended, partially-scored variants, and 68 research problems spanning OS, HPC, AI, DB, PL, SE, and Security domains sourced from real research workflows. Each problem undergoes a three-stage expert curation pipeline (proposal by ICPC-level experts or CS PhDs, implementation with deterministic verifier and SOTA reference solution, and independent review) and includes a parametric generator producing infinitely many instances to prevent overfitting. Scoring is continuous and problem-specific rather than binary, with runtime and memory serving as feasibility constraints rather than scoring components. The evaluation protocol is strictly single-round: models produce one solution without iterative debugging or execution feedback. The paper further analyzes reasoning-effort scaling (showing diminishing returns from medium to high effort), formulation sensitivity across nine equivalent problem pairs, and overfitting in the OpenEvolve agentic framework, where 46% of evolved programs fail to beat one-shot GPT-5 Thinking.

## Results

On the algorithmic track, human experts achieve a Score@1 of 86.99, while the best frontier model (Gemini 3.0 Pro) reaches only 33.12, leaving a gap of over 53 points. On the research track, Gemini 3.0 Pro leads with Score@1 of 46.55, followed by GPT 5.1 Thinking (32.12) and GPT 5.2 Thinking (30.29). Increasing sampling from 1 to 5 attempts yields gains of 12.77–22.97 points (algorithmic) and 12.67–29.91 points (research), yet no model approaches human-level performance. Elo ratings (Bradley-Terry) range from 1164 (GPT 5.1 Thinking) to 1265 (Gemini 3.0 Pro) on the algorithmic track.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All seven frontier models (GPT 5/5.1/5.2 Thinking, Gemini 2.5/3.0 Pro, Grok 4, DeepSeek 3.2) lag substantially behind human experts (86.99 Score@1) on the algorithmic track, with the best model (Gemini 3.0 Pro, 33.12) achieving less than 40% of human performance. On the research track, Gemini 3.0 Pro also leads (46.55 Score@1, Elo 1283), outperforming GPT 5.1 Thinking (32.12) and GPT 5.2 Thinking (30.29) by 14–16 points. GPT 5.2 Thinking is second on algorithmic problems (32.40) but drops to third on research, while DeepSeek 3.2 and GPT 5 Thinking occupy the middle tier with similar Elo ratings (1193–1196). The agentic framework OpenEvolve, using GPT 5 Thinking as its base model, fails to outperform one-shot GPT-5 Thinking on 46% of algorithmic problems due to overfitting on the 30% training subset (avg. training score 50.75 vs. testing score 43.00). Additionally, increasing reasoning effort from medium to high on GPT 5 Thinking actually degrades performance (15.34 → 12.63), indicating diminishing returns from larger reasoning budgets on open-ended problems.

## Relation to Prior Work

FrontierCS carves new ground by synthesizing properties from several distinct benchmark families—closed-form code benchmarks (LiveCodeBench, HumanEval), open-ended optimization benchmarks (ALE-Bench, NP-Engine, KernelBench), and unsolved-question benchmarks (UQ, FrontierMath)—into a single framework where no known optimum exists yet solutions are deterministically scored. It departs from ALE-Bench and NP-Engine by covering a far broader problem space (240 problems across 7 CS domains rather than a narrow slice), and from agentic discovery work (ThetaEvolve, AlphaEvolve, OpenEvolve) by providing a systematic, multi-problem evaluation suite rather than demonstrations on a handful of handpicked tasks. The parametric problem generator and versioned difficulty scheduling are design choices not present in prior benchmarks, enabling the benchmark to evolve alongside model capabilities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a substantial and persistent gap between frontier models (best Score@1: 33.12) and human experts (86.99) on open-ended CS problems, suggesting that competence on closed-form coding tasks does not transfer to open-ended algorithm design. The finding that increasing reasoning effort from medium to high actually degrades performance challenges the prevailing assumption that scaling reasoning budgets monotonically improves open-ended problem solving, though the evidence is limited to a single model on a subset. The formulation-sensitivity results (equivalent problems yielding 15–25 point score gaps) and the OpenEvolve overfitting (46% of problems where evolution fails to beat one-shot) highlight fundamental brittleness in current models. A key limitation is the single-round evaluation without execution feedback, which underestimates what agentic pipelines could achieve; the authors note this as a primary direction for future work.
