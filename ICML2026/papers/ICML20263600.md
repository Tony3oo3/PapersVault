## Problem

Conventional wisdom holds that large-batch training is fundamentally incompatible with RL beyond a modest threshold, as the non-stationarity of the data distribution causes performance degradation. This paper challenges that view by observing that non-stationarity is not a fixed property but evolves throughout training: early stages demand small batches for plasticity, while late stages approach quasi-stationarity where large batches enable precise convergence. No prior method dynamically adapts batch size to this evolving stationarity in a computationally efficient manner.

## Input / Output

The method consumes environment observations (stacked RGB frames at 84×84 for Atari, or continuous state vectors for MuJoCo) along with the current and previous policy parameters. The key intermediate signal is Behavioral Divergence, computed via a single forward pass over a reference batch of 2048 states as the fraction of states where the greedy action changes between successive updates; for continuous control this is replaced by an analytic KL divergence between Gaussian policies. The output is an adaptively scaled rollout length (ranging from L_min to L_max) that determines the effective batch size for the next data collection phase, alongside the standard updated policy parameters and actions.

## Method

Adaptive Batch Scaling (ABS) dynamically adjusts the rollout length L (and thus batch size |B| = E × L) by measuring Behavioral Divergence δ_π — the fraction of states in a reference batch where the greedy action differs between the old and updated policy. A logarithmic interpolation maps the clipped divergence to a target rollout length between L_min and L_max, with an exponential moving average smoothing transitions to prevent destabilizing batch-size spikes. The number of update epochs is scaled proportionally to the rollout length to maintain consistent gradient intensity per transition. The entire mechanism requires only forward-pass inference (no extra backward passes), making it far cheaper than Gradient Noise Scale–based scheduling. ABS is demonstrated on PQN (discrete, value-based) with Behavioral Divergence, on PPO (continuous, policy-gradient) with KL divergence, and on BTR (off-policy with replay buffer) by simply replacing the rollout range with a batch-size range, showing algorithm-agnostic applicability.

## Results

On the Atari-10 benchmark (IQM HNS, 3 seeds), PQN+ABS achieves the best score in 8 out of 10 environments, outperforming vanilla PQN, fixed small batch (L=16), fixed large batch (L=64), and GNS-based dynamic scheduling. Notable gains include DoubleDunk (-1.44 vs. -9.80 for PQN), Riverraid (0.64 vs. 0.45), and NameThisGame (2.05 vs. 1.92). For scaled architectures (PQN-L, PQN-XL), ABS with rollout range [16→128] (2,048→16,384 samples) consistently surpasses both the default and large-fixed-batch configurations, e.g., PQN-XL on Phoenix jumps from 0.81 (default) to 2.66 with ABS, while large fixed batch degrades to 0.61.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla PQN, ABS wins in 8/10 Atari-10 environments, with the largest relative gain on DoubleDunk (-1.44 vs. -9.80) and Riverraid (0.64 vs. 0.45). Compared to the fixed Small Batch (L=16, 2,048 samples), ABS also wins 8/10, confirming that the adaptive schedule captures the benefits of small batches early while adding late-stage precision. Against the fixed Large Batch (L=64, 8,192 samples), ABS wins 8/10, demonstrating that a static large batch is suboptimal due to early-stage non-stationarity. Against GNS-based dynamic scheduling (McCandlish et al., 2018), ABS outperforms in 7/10 environments (losing only on Qbert where GNS reaches 0.79 vs. ABS's 0.34), while being computationally cheaper since it requires only forward-pass inference rather than additional backward passes. For scaled models (PQN-L, PQN-XL), large fixed batch degrades performance across all environments, whereas ABS consistently recovers and exceeds the default-batch baseline, e.g., PQN-XL on Phoenix (2.66 vs. 0.81 default, 0.61 large-fixed) and NameThisGame (0.99 vs. 0.25 default, 0.06 large-fixed).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Gradient Noise Scale (GNS) framework of McCandlish et al. (2018), which theoretically showed that optimal batch size should grow over training, but which was never experimentally validated in RL due to the computational cost of per-sample gradient estimation. It departs from the small-batch RL literature (Obando Ceron et al., 2023) by reframing non-stationarity as a time-varying property rather than a fixed constraint, and repurposes the policy churn concept (Schaul et al., 2022; Tang & Berseth, 2024) as a lightweight forward-pass scheduling signal rather than a phenomenon to explain or regularize. The scaling experiments leverage the Multi-Skip Residual MLP architectures from Castanyer et al. (2025), and the method is integrated into PQN (Gallici et al., 2024) as the primary algorithmic substrate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a simple, forward-pass-only metric (Behavioral Divergence) can unlock large-batch training in RL, enabling larger models to actually benefit from their parameter capacity—a gap that has persisted between supervised and RL scaling. The method's algorithm-agnosticism (validated on PQN, PPO, and BTR) suggests broad applicability, but the evidence is confined to Atari and MuJoCo with MLP/CNN backbones; the authors explicitly flag Transformer architectures, offline RL, and LLM-scale RLHF as untested frontiers. Whether the adaptive scheduling advantage persists at the scale of modern LLM RLHF pipelines or with attention-based backbones remains an open and practically critical question.
