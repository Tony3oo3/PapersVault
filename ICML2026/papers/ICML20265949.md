## Problem

Deep neural networks produce overconfident and miscalibrated predictions under distribution shift, yet existing calibration methods either require access to target domain data or lack explicit mechanisms to handle shifts. The paper identifies that shifts predominantly alter high-frequency visual patterns exploited as shortcut cues, and that suppressing these cues via filtering degrades in-distribution calibration due to information loss—a trade-off no prior method resolves without a loss-balancing coefficient.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB images at dataset-specific resolutions (32×32 for CIFAR, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet, 96×96 for Camelyon17, 224×224 for FMoW/Office-Home, 448×448 for iWildCam) and outputs a K-class probability vector whose maximum serves as the calibrated confidence score. During training, a fraction ρ of each batch is transformed via block-wise DCT quantization before feeding the network, so the model sees both full-spectrum and low-pass-filtered versions of the same image in a single hybrid batch.

## Method

FGR combines two mechanisms: (1) DCT-based low-pass filtering applied to a random subset of training images each epoch, using 8×8 block quantization with a randomly sampled compression parameter λ∈{15,18,25} to suppress source-specific high-frequency shortcuts while preserving semantic content; and (2) an asymmetric gradient rectification step where, if the main-loss gradient (Dual Focal Loss on the mixed filtered/original batch) has a negative inner product with the calibration gradient (Soft-ECE on the unfiltered batch), the main gradient is projected onto the hyperplane orthogonal to the calibration gradient. This projection is a one-sided, weight-free operation that guarantees a first-order non-increase in the ID calibration loss, treating calibration as a hard constraint rather than a competing objective. The mechanism is activated after a warm-up phase (epoch 200 for CIFAR) and adds only ~18% training overhead, and it is compatible with post-hoc temperature scaling.

## Results

On synthetic corruption benchmarks (DenseNet-121), FGR achieves 9.02% ECE on CIFAR-10-C (vs. 23.51% for CE and 11.03% for AugMix), 8.53% ECE on CIFAR-100-C (vs. 41.21% for CE), and 12.39% ECE on Tiny-ImageNet-C. On real-world WILDS shifts, FGR attains 2.36% ECE on Camelyon17 (a 40% ACE reduction over CE), 3.34% ECE on iWildCam, and 25.06% ECE on FMoW, while maintaining competitive accuracy (89.19% on Camelyon17, 76.11% on iWildCam). FGR also remains compatible with post-hoc Temperature Scaling, further reducing ECE (e.g., 1.82% on Camelyon17 w/ TS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FGR achieves the lowest ECE across all six shift benchmarks, outperforming the strongest calibration-specific baselines: on CIFAR-10-C it reduces ECE from 11.03% (AugMix) to 9.02%, and on CIFAR-100-C from 9.99% (DFL) to 8.53%. On the real-world Camelyon17 dataset, FGR's 2.36% ECE beats Mixup (2.62%) and DFL (2.74%) while also achieving the highest accuracy (89.19%); on iWildCam it edges out Mixup (3.34% vs. 3.50% ECE). AugMix, while strong on synthetic corruptions (82.49% Acc on CIFAR-10-C), degrades severely on real-world shifts (16.99% ECE on Camelyon17, 22.18% on iWildCam), highlighting its limited generalizability. FGR also outperforms the symmetric multi-objective gradient methods PCGrad and CAGrad on test ECE for both Camelyon17 (1.14% vs. 3.38% and 1.28%) and iWildCam (2.62% vs. 2.74% and 4.51%), confirming the benefit of the asymmetric one-sided projection. On FMoW, FGR (25.06% ECE) is marginally better than Mixup (25.16%) but slightly behind DFL (26.59%) in accuracy, indicating a small trade-off on this particular benchmark. Overall, FGR consistently wins or ties on ECE across all datasets while preserving or improving accuracy, and it composes additively with post-hoc Temperature Scaling for further gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of frequency-domain robustness (Yin et al. 2019, Fridovich-Keil et al. 2022, Li et al. 2023) and training-time calibration (DFL by Tao et al. 2023, Soft-ECE by Karandikar et al. 2021), repurposing DCT low-pass filtering as a mechanism to suppress spurious high-frequency shortcuts that cause overconfidence under shift. It departs from symmetric multi-objective gradient methods (PCGrad, CAGrad) by treating ID calibration as a one-sided hard constraint via geometric projection rather than a balanced trade-off, and from prior target-agnostic calibration methods (focal loss, label smoothing, MaxEnt) by explicitly targeting the frequency structure of distribution shift rather than applying generic regularization. The gradient rectification mechanism is conceptually related to constrained optimization but is applied in a novel asymmetric fashion specific to the calibration-robustness tension.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The target-agnostic design is practically important: real deployments rarely provide target-domain data, yet existing calibration methods often require it or its simulation. The weight-free projection avoids the hyperparameter sensitivity of loss-balancing coefficients, and the 18% training overhead is modest. However, the approach is limited to image classification with a small filtering ratio (5%), and the frequency-domain insight—while well-motivated—has not been tested on non-visual modalities or generative tasks. Open questions include whether the DCT filtering generalizes to higher-resolution or video inputs, whether the asymmetric constraint remains beneficial when the ID calibration loss is already near-optimal, and whether the method's gains persist under more extreme or compositional shifts beyond the tested benchmarks.
