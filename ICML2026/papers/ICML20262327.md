## Problem

Existing fMRI-to-text systems inject neural features as one-shot prefix tokens, a conditioning signal that decays during autoregressive generation and allows strong language priors to dominate output. This raises a fundamental question: do high captioning scores reflect genuine reliance on voxel values, or are they produced by prior-driven shortcuts that ignore the brain signal? The paper tackles both the architectural limitation of one-shot conditioning and the lack of diagnostic evidence for true neural grounding.

## Input / Output

The model takes a preprocessed fMRI volume restricted to a subject-specific visual-cortical mask (NSD beta v3, 1.8 mm) and a fixed instruction prompt as input, and produces a single natural-language caption of up to 128 tokens via greedy decoding. The fMRI volume is encoded into 128 brain tokens of dimension 1024 by a subject-agnostic attention encoder, which are then projected to the LLM hidden size (3072) and exposed as a persistent key-value memory rather than prepended to the text sequence. Evaluation uses 5 COCO-style human reference captions per stimulus under a strict held-out-subject protocol (S1–7 train, S8 test) where both the subject and the test images are unseen.

## Method

BIT-LLM replaces one-shot prefix conditioning with persistent neural access: fMRI-derived tokens are maintained as an external key-value memory and queried repeatedly through four lightweight gated cross-attention adapters interleaved at early layers (3, 7, 11, 15) of a frozen Llama 3.2-3B-Instruct backbone, following the Flamingo-style adapter pattern. Training follows a three-stage pipeline: (1) CLIP-style symmetric InfoNCE contrastive pretraining aligns the fMRI encoder with frozen image and text encoders to produce value-dependent, semantically organized brain embeddings; (2) supervised fine-tuning trains only the projector and cross-attention adapters (with near-zero-initialized gates for stability) while freezing both the encoder and LLM backbone; (3) Neuro-Semantic RFT applies GRPO with a composite reward (CIDEr + BERTScore − Incompleteness Penalty) via LoRA on selected backbone projections to optimize sequence-level caption quality. The key architectural insight is that keeping neural tokens as a persistent KV memory accessible at multiple transformer depths mitigates conditioning decay over long autoregressive sequences, and perturbation diagnostics (voxel zeroing and intra-sample shuffling) confirm that the resulting model genuinely depends on voxel values and their spatial correspondence rather than language priors.

## Results

On the NSD subject-held-out protocol (S1–7 train, S8 test), BIT-LLM achieves CIDEr 34.90, BLEU-4 15.10, ROUGE-L 40.42, and SPICE 7.13 under greedy decoding, substantially outperforming the strongest prior baseline MindLLM (CIDEr 13.22, BLEU-4 7.52, ROUGE-L 33.47, SPICE 3.82). The CIDEr improvement over MindLLM is approximately 2.6×, and BLEU-4 more than doubles. Leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) results confirm consistent cross-subject generalization, with CIDEr ranging from 26.64 (S8) to 36.77 (S5) across all eight held-out subjects.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BIT-LLM outperforms all three subject-agnostic baselines (MindBridge, UniBrain, MindLLM) on every reported captioning metric under the identical S1-7→S8 held-out-subject protocol. The largest relative gain is on CIDEr: BIT-LLM scores 34.90 versus 13.22 for MindLLM (the strongest prior method), 8.70 for MindBridge, and 6.40 for UniBrain — a 2.6× improvement over the best baseline. On BLEU-4, BIT-LLM reaches 15.10 compared to 7.52 (MindLLM), 5.24 (MindBridge), and 4.33 (UniBrain). ROUGE-L improves from 33.47 (MindLLM) to 40.42, and SPICE more than doubles from 3.82 to 7.13. The paper also shows that these gains are not merely a function of backbone capacity: swapping the LLM backbone (Vicuna-7B vs. Llama 3.1-8B vs. Llama 3.2-3B) under a fixed MindLLM recipe does not consistently improve metrics, indicating the bottleneck lies in the brain-to-language interface rather than standalone language modeling strength.

## Relation to Prior Work

BIT-LLM extends the fMRI-to-text decoding line initiated by MindLLM (Qiu et al., 2025) and MindBridge (Wang et al., 2024), which project neural features into a frozen LLM's embedding space as one-shot prefix tokens. The key architectural departure is replacing this prefix conditioning with persistent interleaved cross-attention adapters (in the Flamingo style, Alayrac et al., 2022), so that fMRI-derived tokens remain queryable as an external key-value memory throughout autoregressive decoding. The three-stage training pipeline composes CLIP-style contrastive alignment (Radford et al., 2021) for the fMRI encoder, standard instruction SFT, and GRPO-based reward fine-tuning (Shao et al., 2024) with LoRA, each component drawn from established multimodal LLM practice but here unified for the brain-decoding setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a critical trustworthiness question in Neuro-AI: whether high captioning scores reflect genuine reliance on voxel values or are driven by language-prior shortcuts. The perturbation diagnostics (zeroing, intra-sample shuffling) and the finding that the MindLLM-base encoder is nearly invariant to voxel zeroing (cosine similarity ≈ 0.99) provide a concrete, reproducible framework for auditing neural grounding that the field lacks. Open questions include cross-dataset generalization (e.g., to BOLD5000), whether the persistent cross-attention advantage holds at larger backbone scales, and whether reward design can incorporate neuro-visual verifiers beyond n-gram and BERTScore proxies. The subject-agnostic held-out protocol (S1-7→S8) is the most stringent evaluation setting for practical BCI, making the reported gains directly relevant to deployment.
