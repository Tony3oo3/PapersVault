## Problem

Existing evaluations of LLMs for Rust program verification (via Verus) treat the verification process as a black box, measuring success solely by whether generated proof hints make the verifier pass. This binary outcome cannot distinguish a model that constructs a sound deductive chain from one that succeeds through chance syntactic alignment, leaving it unclear whether LLMs can systematically reconstruct the step-by-step verification reasoning that automated theorem provers like Z3 perform.

## Input / Output

VCoT-Lift takes a verified Verus program (Rust code, specifications, and proof hints) together with its full Z3 proof trace (often 10,000+ lines, 81.69% of which is low-level reasoning) and produces a human-readable Verification Chain-of-Thought (VCoT) expressed as Verus-level proof steps. VCoT-Bench then constructs 1,988 completion tasks by removing semantic blocks (Lemma, Invariant, or Assertion blocks) from the VCoT, organized along three orthogonal dimensions: removal ratio (1–100%), proof type, and proof location (Front/Middle/End). The benchmark is built on 150 programs from Verus-Bench, with VCoT-Bench-Org showing a 6.5× average increase in proof lines and 13.4× increase in assertions over the original.

## Method

VCoT-Lift is a four-stage LLM-based pipeline that lifts Z3 solver reasoning into Verus-level verification steps. The Proof Transformer processes the entire Z3 proof end-to-end, guided by a three-level hierarchy of 36 Z3 proof rules (8 high-level, 12 medium, 16 low) that steers LLM attention toward semantically informative steps. A Proof Checker with five specialized agents (one per high-level rule category) evaluates completeness using an abstraction filter (to ignore trivial/redundant steps), explicit Z3-to-Verus step mapping (to reduce hallucination), and rule-specific specialization prompts; the transformer and checker iterate in a loop where the checker emits only binary complete/incomplete signals to preserve the transformer's global reasoning. A Proof Pruner then removes trivial and redundant steps with explicit justifications, and a Proof Repair agent interacts with the Verus verifier to fix syntactic and semantic errors. The resulting VCoT-Bench evaluates ten state-of-the-art LLMs in zero-shot setting using a weighted accuracy metric combining syntactic correctness (checked by Verus in syntax-only mode) and semantic correctness (judged by GPT-5 mini validated against author consensus at 92.7% agreement).

## Results

On VCoT-Bench (1,988 tasks), the best model Claude Sonnet 4.5 achieves 71.58% overall accuracy at 10% proof-block removal but collapses to 17.22% at 100% removal (full VCoT construction), while the weakest model gpt-oss reaches only 32.89% at 10% and near-zero at 100%. Across proof types, assertions are consistently the hardest (best: 39.55% for Claude Sonnet 4.5), loop invariants the easiest (best: 68.71%), and lemma functions intermediate (best: 55.48% for GPT-5.2). Across proof locations, Middle blocks are the most challenging (best: 47.69% for Gemini 3 Flash), with Front and End blocks substantially easier (69.04% and 65.96% for Claude Sonnet 4.5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper that evaluates ten LLMs rather than proposing a new method against baselines, so the 'comparisons' are cross-model. Claude Sonnet 4.5 leads on loop invariants (68.71%) and Front-location blocks (69.04%), while Gemini 3 Flash surprisingly achieves the best Middle-block accuracy (47.69%), outperforming both Gemini 3.1 Pro (36.15%) and GPT-5.2 (41.15%), indicating that model scale does not uniformly benefit connective reasoning. GPT-5.2 is the strongest on lemma functions (55.48%) and End-location blocks (65.58%). A notable finding is that reasoning-oriented variants can underperform their non-reasoning counterparts: Qwen 3 non-think (26.87% on invariants) beats Qwen 3 think (10.03%), and Gemini 3 Flash (57.65%) surpasses Gemini 3.1 Pro (62.07%) on invariants but leads on Middle blocks. All models show severe degradation under proof removal, with the best model dropping from 71.58% (10% removal) to 17.22% (100% removal), confirming that current LLMs rely on local syntactic scaffolding rather than principled deductive reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in LLM-assisted Rust verification (AlphaVerus, SAFE, AutoVerus, RagVerus, VeriStruct, VeruSAGE), which treats the Verus/Z3 verification process as a black box and evaluates models solely by binary pass/fail outcomes on generated proof hints. It builds directly on Verus-Bench (Yang et al., 2024) as its program source but introduces a fundamentally new evaluation axis: the Verification Chain-of-Thought (VCoT), the explicit step-by-step deductive reasoning underlying verification. The VCoT-Lift framework, a four-stage LLM multi-agent pipeline (transformer–checker–pruner–repair), is the first to systematically lift low-level Z3 proof traces into human-readable Verus-level verification steps, bridging a semantic gap that prior work has left unaddressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that current LLMs rely heavily on local syntactic scaffolding rather than principled deductive reasoning, a weakness invisible to binary pass/fail evaluations. The structured failure patterns (40% removal threshold, Middle-block fragility, assertions as the hardest type, thinking variants underperforming) provide actionable diagnosis for future training strategies such as curriculum-style context removal and proof-type-aware objectives. Open questions include whether VCoT can generalize beyond Verus to other SMT-based verifiers, whether the LLM–ATP gap will narrow with scale or requires fundamentally different training signals, and whether the VCoT supervision signal can close the gap in practice.
