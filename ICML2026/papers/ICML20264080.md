## Problem

Training data reconstruction from KKT conditions has shown empirical success, but two fundamental questions remain unresolved: (1) when do the KKT equations uniquely determine the training samples (identifiability), and (2) how to reliably optimize the highly nonconvex reconstruction objective that suffers from flat plateaus and multiple stationary points. Prior work lacked both a theoretical characterization of when reconstruction is possible and an effective optimization strategy to escape poor local solutions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264080/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes the trained parameters θ of a two-layer MLP (architecture d-1000-1000-1) with polynomial activations, along with the network architecture specification, and produces reconstructed training samples (x_i, y_i) with associated KKT multipliers λ_i. The reconstruction is formulated as solving the KKT stationarity equation θ = Σ λ_i y_i ∇_θ Φ(θ; x_i) over k ≥ 2n candidate samples, where the number of true training samples n is unknown. The unified objective L({x_i}, {λ_i}) = ‖θ − Σ λ_i f(θ; x_i)‖² subsumes KKT-based, multiclass, and NTK-based reconstruction methods through different choices of the reconstruction map f.

## Method

The paper makes two complementary contributions. For identifiability, it proves that for two-layer networks with polynomial activations of degree α ≥ 3, the KKT system uniquely determines the active training samples under a rank condition on the polynomial-kernel Gram matrix K (rank(K) = C(d+α−2, α−1)); the proof recovers a symmetric order-α tensor via polynomial interpolation from neuron evaluations, then applies a Jennrich-style orthogonal tensor decomposition to extract individual samples. For optimization, it introduces sample splitting: a curvature-aware refinement that detects negative-curvature directions via per-sample splitting matrices S(x_i) = −2λ_i Σ r_p ∇²f_p(θ; x_i), then splits a sample x_i into two offsprings x_i ± ηv_min along the most negative eigenvector while halving its weight. The algorithm alternates between gradient descent (Phase I) and splitting (Phase II), and is shown to converge to an ε-second-order stationary point in O(ε^{-2}) total iterations, with the splitting step providing a strict loss decrease whenever λ_min(S(x_i)) < 0.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 (100 training images, Loo et al.'s NTK-based method), 21 out of 25 top-reconstructed samples achieve higher SSIM after applying sample splitting, while on MNIST (100 images), 21 out of 25 achieve lower L2 distance. Improvements are concentrated among samples with higher baseline reconstruction quality on CIFAR-10, whereas the majority of samples show improvement on MNIST. The splitting step is applied every 20,000–40,000 gradient descent iterations with a threshold λ* = −0.1 and maximum step size 0.01, incurring negligible computational overhead (~2 minutes for 1000 samples via Lanczos).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Haim et al. (2022)'s KKT-based method on 500 MNIST samples, sample splitting improves or preserves L2 distance for most samples, with visible gains in background uniformity and digit sharpness; on CIFAR-10 (500 samples) the baseline is highly unstable, but splitting still yields per-sample improvements. Against Buzaglo et al. (2024)'s multiclass KKT method, splitting acts as a conservative refinement that improves already-reasonable reconstructions and, on CIFAR-10 with 100 samples, improves quality even when the baseline is poor; on 500-sample CIFAR-10 the multiclass setting causes loss fluctuations where splitting occasionally produces large individual gains. Against Loo et al. (2024)'s NTK-based method, splitting consistently improves 21/25 top samples on both datasets, and critically, when the initial reconstruction size k is set smaller than the true training set size, the baseline stagnates while splitting enables further progress—demonstrating that splitting also addresses the unknown-sample-count problem. Across all three baselines, splitting never substantially degrades reconstruction quality, confirming it as a safe add-on optimization step.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the KKT-based training-data reconstruction line initiated by Haim et al. (2022) and its multiclass/NTK extensions (Buzaglo et al., 2024; Loo et al., 2024) by addressing two gaps those works left open: a rigorous identifiability characterization and a practical optimization strategy. The identifiability analysis draws on tensor decomposition theory (Jennrich-style algorithms) and the homogenization framework of Cai et al. (2025) to show that polynomial activations of degree ≥3 make the KKT system generically invertible, while linear and quadratic activations only determine aggregated moment quantities. The sample splitting algorithm is conceptually inspired by neuron splitting for architecture search (Liu et al., 2019) but operates at the data level, creating new descent directions in the reconstruction landscape rather than modifying the network.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identifiability theorem provides the first principled explanation for when KKT-based reconstruction is theoretically guaranteed to succeed, moving the field from empirical observation to provable conditions. The sample splitting technique is a lightweight, model-agnostic refinement that improves reconstruction across three distinct methods without modifying their objectives, suggesting broad applicability. Key limitations include the width requirement m ≥ N (which grows combinatorially in d and α), the restriction to two-layer networks, and the fact that only margin (active) samples are recovered. Open questions flagged by the authors include extending to approximate/partial reconstruction, deeper architectures, and the under-determined regime where the interpolation condition fails.
