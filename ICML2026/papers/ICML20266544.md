## Problem

Current auditing of LLM-assisted peer review focuses on detecting whether an LLM was used, but cannot distinguish stance-neutral generation from directional prompting that steers reviews toward a predetermined verdict. This usage-centric approach misses the more consequential variable—how the LLM was instructed—which can systematically shape review framing, tone, and ratings while remaining invisible to existing detectors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266544/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes only the full review text (no paper content, no prompt logs) and produces a binary intent label (neutral vs. directional) plus a continuous intent score. In the real-world audit, the input is restricted to ICLR 2026 reviews previously flagged by EditLens as heavily AI-edited or fully AI-written (19,132 out of 75,800 reviews), and the output is treated as a calibrated risk signal for governance-side decision support rather than a hard enforcement label.

## Method

The authors construct a synthetic training set of 3,000 balanced reviews by prompting three LLMs (Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507, DeepSeek-V3.1, gpt-oss-120b) with five neutral and five directional system-prompt templates over 500 ICLR 2025 submissions. Reviews are embedded with a frozen jina-embedding-v3 encoder, and a two-layer MLP (256-dim hidden, ReLU, dropout 0.2) is trained with cross-entropy loss under paper-level splits. Transferability is validated via leave-one-model-out and leave-one-template-out cross-validation, with ROC-AUCs remaining above 0.98 across all folds. The trained classifier is then applied to ICLR 2026 flagged reviews, and downstream effects are quantified through linguistic signature analysis (Cliff's δ effect sizes on sentiment, negation, and critical-content share) and a logistic regression controlling for mean rating, paper-quality tier, and primary area to estimate acceptance odds ratios.

## Results

In synthetic experiments, the intent classifier achieves 90.9% accuracy and 0.989 ROC-AUC in the pooled setting, with ROC-AUC remaining above 0.985 under leave-one-model-out transfer across three LLM generators. Applied to 19,132 ICLR 2026 reviews flagged for substantial LLM usage, 20.51% are predicted as directional-intended. Directional-intended reviews assign ratings ~1.7 points lower than neutral-intended reviews (overall D-N gap: −1.697) while exhibiting ~0.32 higher confidence, and are associated with significantly lower acceptance odds (D-N OR = 0.916, q = 0.046; D-H OR = 0.833, q < 0.001) after controlling for mean rating, paper quality, and primary area.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not benchmark against external intent-detection baselines; instead, it validates the proposed text-only intent classifier through internal transfer and ablation studies. Under leave-one-model-out evaluation, ROC-AUC stays above 0.985 for all three held-out generators, confirming that intent signals are not generator-specific, though gpt-oss-120b shows a notable TPR/FPR trade-off (TPR = 1.0, FPR = 0.492) under a fixed threshold. An encoder ablation across four embedding models (jina-embeddings-v3, e5-large-v2, snowflake-arctic-embed-l, bge-m3) yields consistently high ROC-AUC (0.90–0.98) and strong inter-encoder agreement (Fleiss's κ = 0.67, Kendall's W = 0.82), indicating the signal is model-agnostic. In the real-world ICLR 2026 analysis, the three review composition groups (directional, neutral, human-written) are compared on ratings, confidence, and acceptance: directional reviews are consistently the harshest (lowest ratings, highest confidence) and most associated with rejection, while neutral LLM-assisted reviews are the most lenient, with human-written reviews falling in between on ratings but showing the lowest confidence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by reframing the LLM-assisted peer review auditing problem from usage detection (EditLens, Liang et al. 2024a/2025) to intent inference, arguing that whether an LLM was used is less consequential than how it was instructed. Conceptually, it draws on the LLM steerability literature (sycophancy in Perez et al. 2023, AI sandbagging in van der Weij et al. 2025, persona-induced bias in Gupta et al. 2024) to motivate why directional prompting is mechanistically plausible in reviews. Methodologically, it repurposes a standard text-embedding-plus-classifier pipeline (jina-embedding-v3 + MLP) trained on synthetic data, but the novelty lies in the problem formulation—treating prompting intent as a latent variable in a graphical model and operationalizing its recovery as a text-only inference task—rather than in the classifier architecture itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As major venues (ICLR 2026, ICML 2026, NeurIPS 2026) move from banning to scoped-allowance policies for LLM-assisted reviewing, the governance question shifts from 'was an LLM used?' to 'how was it used?', and this paper provides the first empirical demonstration that prompting intent leaves detectable, outcome-relevant traces in review text. The finding that directional-intended reviews systematically produce lower ratings, higher confidence, and reduced acceptance odds (especially for accept-leaning papers) gives conference organizers a concrete, auditable signal beyond self-disclosure. Key limitations include the binary intent framing (neutral vs. directional only), reliance on synthetic training data that may not capture the full diversity of real prompting strategies, and the acknowledged risk of chilling effects on reviewer expression. Open questions include extending to richer intent spaces, incorporating paper-aware consistency checks, and understanding how intent-linked signals propagate through reviewer discussion and meta-review deliberation.
