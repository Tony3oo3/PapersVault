## Problem

Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization in deep learning is widely dismissed as fundamentally unscalable due to estimator variance and unfavorable query complexity, yet the paper argues this pessimism is misplaced. The perceived limitations stem not from intrinsic weaknesses of gradient-free learning but from myopic development practices—full-space, element-wise, estimator-centric designs—that overlook broader algorithmic, systems-level, and evaluation opportunities. The paper asks whether ZO optimization is truly underpowered or simply underexplored, and provides a structured argument for the latter.

## Input / Output

The ZO optimization framework discussed requires only forward function evaluations and random perturbation vectors as input, producing gradient estimates that drive parameter updates without any backward pass. The empirical validation fine-tunes Gemma2-2B on SST-2, RTE, and WiC, comparing four ZO methods (MeZO, Sparse-MeZO, HiZOO, LOZO) under both task-aligned and non-aligned settings to expose how alignment obscures intrinsic optimization capability. The paper also systematically audits ten recent ICML'25, NeurIPS'25, and ICLR'26 papers for alignment with its three feasibility positions (P1–P3), finding that most address only variance control while neglecting query-variance tradeoffs and forward-gradient baselines.

## Method

The paper articulates six positions (P1–P6) spanning the algorithm–system–evaluation stack. P1–P3 delineate feasibility boundaries of current estimator-centric ZO: structured perturbation directions for variance control (P1), a query-variance tradeoff requiring sublinear query growth with dimension (P2), and the directional-derivative/forward-gradient formulation as a must-consider baseline that provides an upper bound on vanilla ZO performance (P3). P4–P6 propose advancing directions: subspace projection with spectral structure (e.g., Muon-style orthogonalization) for interpretable variance reduction with graceful query scaling (P4), exploiting forward-only execution as a systems advantage enabling scalar-only inter-worker communication, feature reuse, and near-zero-bubble pipeline parallelism (P5), and de-obfuscating ZO evaluations from task-alignment gains to isolate intrinsic optimization power (P6). The argument is grounded in a systematic audit of recent conference papers (Table 1) showing near-universal neglect of P2 and P3, and empirical results (Fig. 2) demonstrating that removing task alignment causes substantial accuracy drops and reshuffles method rankings, confirming that alignment acts as implicit obfuscation.

## Results

This is a position paper without a single proposed method, so there are no headline accuracy numbers for a new algorithm. The key empirical evidence comes from Figure 2, which shows that most ZO fine-tuning methods (MeZO, Sparse-MeZO, HiZOO, LOZO) experience substantial accuracy drops on SST-2, RTE, and WiC when task alignment is removed, and that the relative ranking of methods changes between aligned and non-aligned settings. Table 1 further demonstrates that no recent ICML'25/NeurIPS'25/ICLR'26 ZO work simultaneously satisfies all three feasibility positions (P1–P3), with (P3) — the forward-gradient baseline — being absent from every surveyed paper.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new optimizer but instead evaluates four existing ZO fine-tuning methods — MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023), Sparse-MeZO (Liu et al., 2025b), HiZOO (Zhao et al., 2025b), and LOZO (Chen et al., 2025) — on Gemma2-2B across SST-2, RTE, and WiC under both task-aligned and non-aligned settings (Figure 2). The central finding is that without task alignment, all four methods suffer substantial accuracy drops, and critically, the relative ranking of methods changes between the two settings, indicating that task alignment obscures the intrinsic optimization capability of each ZO method. No single method dominates across all three tasks in both settings, reinforcing the paper's argument (P6) that current evaluations conflate ZO optimization quality with task-complexity reduction. The paper also notes that the forward-gradient (directional-derivative) baseline, which would provide an upper bound on what query-based ZO can achieve, is absent from all recent comparisons, leaving the true ceiling of ZO performance uncalibrated.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper builds on the classical ZO optimization literature (Spall's SPSA, Duchi et al.'s finite-difference estimators, Nesterov & Spokoiny's smoothed analysis) and the post-2023 wave of ZO fine-tuning methods (MeZO, Sparse-MeZO, PaZO, HiZOO, LOZO), but deliberately departs from the estimator-centric, full-parameter-space framing that dominates recent work. It repositions ZO optimization by importing ideas from subspace/spectral optimization (Muon, LoRA-style low-rank projections), systems infrastructure (pipeline parallelism, vLLM-style inference engines), and directional-derivative/forward-gradient methods (Baydin et al., Ren et al.) as first-class design lenses rather than afterthoughts. The paper's core departure is conceptual: it argues that the field's perceived scalability wall is an artifact of narrow design choices (element-wise perturbations, query-variance tradeoffs ignored, task-alignment confounds) rather than an intrinsic limitation of gradient-free learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's most actionable contribution is its evaluation protocol (A1): requiring forward-gradient baselines, fixed query budgets, and task-aligned vs. non-aligned comparisons would substantially improve the rigor and comparability of future ZO fine-tuning papers. The systems-level argument (P5) that ZO's forward-only execution enables near-zero pipeline bubbles and scalar-only inter-worker communication is compelling but remains unvalidated at scale in this work, leaving open whether ZO-native infrastructure can actually match or exceed FO frameworks like DeepSpeed or FSDP on large models. The empirical evidence (Figure 2, one model, three tasks) is suggestive but thin, and the broader claims about quantum optimization, generative-model-based gradient estimation, and hybrid FO-ZO training are speculative directions rather than demonstrated results. The paper will likely influence community norms around ZO evaluation, but its practical impact depends on whether the proposed ZO-native system stacks and subspace-spectral methods are actually built and benchmarked.
