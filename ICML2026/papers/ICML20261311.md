## Problem

Classical contextual bandit algorithms assume feedback is freely available, yet in many real-world settings (sensor data, human evaluation, proprietary market data) the learner must pay a cost to observe losses. This paper addresses the fundamental question of how observation costs reshape the minimax regret landscape, showing that even arbitrarily small costs raise the optimal regret order from T^{1/2} to T^{2/3}, and further uncovers a phase-transition phenomenon where a free observation budget below a critical threshold fails to improve the regret rate at all.

## Input / Output

At each round t, the learner receives a context x_t ∈ X and an adversarially chosen cost vector {c_t(a)}_{a∈A}, then outputs a selected action a_t and a stochastic observation set O_t ⊆ A (paying Σ_{a∈O_t} c_t(a) to reveal losses ℓ_t(a) for a ∈ O_t). The policy class Π can be finite (Algorithm 1 enumerates all policies) or induced by an infinite function class F via argmin (Algorithm 3 uses a regression oracle). The final output is a cumulative regret bound that jointly accounts for learning loss and observation expenditure, with the key regime-dependent form: O((c|A|ln|Π|)^{1/3} T^{2/3}) under uniform cost c, or O((Reg_Sq(T))^{1/3}(Σ_t √(Σ_a c_t(a)))^{2/3} + √(T·Reg_Sq(T))) in the oracle-efficient setting.

## Method

Algorithm 1 extends exponential weights from multi-armed to contextual bandits by weighting policies π ∈ Π via exp(−η_t Σ ŝ_s(π(x_s))) and aggregating into action sampling probabilities q_t(a), then sets the observation probability p_t(a) = min{1, √(η_t q_t(a)/c_t(a))} to balance the variance term η_t q_t(a)/p_t(a) against the cost term c_t(a) p_t(a), yielding an optimal trade-off via importance-sampling loss estimators. The phase-transition result (Theorem 3.1) shows that with a free budget B, the regret lower bound switches from Ω((c|A|ln|Π|)^{1/3} T^{2/3} − cB) to Ω(T√(|A|ln|Π|/(B ln|A|))) at the critical threshold B_0 = Õ(c^{−2/3}(|A|ln|Π|)^{1/3} T^{2/3}), and Algorithm 2 (a meta-controller) adaptively deploys either a budget-aware variant (Algorithm 6, which tracks a running budget and pays for excess observations) or a uniform-allocation variant (Algorithm 4, distributing B observations evenly) to match this lower bound. For infinite policy spaces, Algorithm 3 replaces explicit policy enumeration with an online squared-loss regression oracle Alg_Sq and inverse-gap sampling q_t(a) ∝ 1/(|A| + γ(ŝ_t(x_t,a) − ŝ_t(x_t,a*))), achieving regret O((Reg_Sq(T))^{1/3}(Σ_t √(Σ_a c_t(a)))^{2/3} + √(T·Reg_Sq(T))) under realizability, with the observation probability adapted to p_t(a) = min{1, √(q_t(a)(1−q_t(a))/(η_t c_t(a)))} to control both estimation variance and cost simultaneously.

## Results

Theoretically, the paper establishes that paid observations raise the minimax regret from Õ(T^{1/2}) (cost-free) to Õ((c|A|ln|Π|)^{1/3} T^{2/3}), and identifies a phase transition at budget B₀ = Θ(c^{-2/3}(|A|ln|Π|)^{1/3} T^{2/3}) where free observations shift the regret from T^{2/3} to T·√(|A|ln|Π|/B). Experimentally, on a linear contextual bandit with d=10, |A|=5, T=10,000, and c=0.5, Algorithm 3 achieves a final average cumulative regret of 2899.75±48.80, outperforming both linUCB (5003.32±1.04) and Tucker et al. (3697.13±26.80). Computationally, Algorithm 3 runs in 2.0s (std 0.6) versus 33.2–94.0s for Algorithm 1 as |Π| grows from 10 to 40.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Algorithm 3 against two baselines on a linear contextual bandit with paid observations (d=10, |A|=5, T=10,000, c=0.5). Against linUCB, which ignores observation costs entirely and thus incurs excessive spending, Algorithm 3 reduces cumulative regret by 42% (2899.75 vs. 5003.32). Against Tucker et al. (2023), the only prior work on linear contextual bandits with costly observations, Algorithm 3 achieves a 22% regret reduction (2899.75 vs. 3697.13), while also providing the first rigorous theoretical guarantees (upper and lower bounds) for this setting. The slightly higher variance of Algorithm 3 (±48.80 vs. ±26.80) reflects its adaptive observation strategy, which introduces more stochastic variability but yields better mean performance. Additionally, Algorithm 3 is substantially faster than the enumeration-based Algorithm 1 (2.0s vs. 33.2–94.0s), demonstrating the practical advantage of the function-approximation oracle approach for large or infinite policy spaces.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the non-contextual multi-armed bandits with paid observations framework of Seldin et al. (2014) to the full contextual bandit setting with general (potentially infinite) policy spaces, and integrates the oracle-efficient regression framework of Foster & Rakhlin (2020a) to handle paid observations under a realizability assumption. It departs from Tucker et al. (2023), which studied linear contextual bandits with costly observations but provided no regret guarantees, by establishing matching upper and lower bounds for both adversarial and stochastic settings. The paper also draws conceptual connections to switching-cost bandits (Dekel et al., 2014), feedback-graph bandits, and knapsack bandits, showing that the T^{2/3} regret order is a unifying signature across these seemingly distinct problems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of a sharp phase transition in the free-observation budget—below a critical threshold, free observations merely reduce expenditure without improving the regret rate, while above it the regret order fundamentally changes—provides a clean theoretical lens for practitioners deciding how much to invest in feedback acquisition. The T^{2/3} minimax regret (versus T^{1/2} in the cost-free case) quantifies a previously informal intuition that paying for information is fundamentally more expensive than the standard exploration-exploitation trade-off. Open questions include extending the analysis to heterogeneous or time-varying observation costs, non-stationary environments, and combinatorial action spaces, as well as integrating oracle-efficient methods into fully adversarial settings.
