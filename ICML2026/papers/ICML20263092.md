## Problem

Existing benchmarks for reference-based image generation and editing are largely confined to single-reference scenarios, restrict reference counts to 2–4 images, and report only aggregate scores without concept-level decomposition. This leaves model behavior under heterogeneous multi-concept composition—where identity, style, material, scene, and other visual dimensions must be jointly satisfied—insufficiently characterized, making it impossible to diagnose which specific concepts fail and at what complexity threshold.

## Input / Output

For creation, the model receives a text prompt containing visual placeholders (e.g., 'the woman from [image1] wearing the dress from [image2]') plus 2–7 reference images, each bound to a distinct concept type, and produces a single 1024×1024 fused image. For editing, the model receives a target image, a textual instruction with visual placeholders, and 1–4 reference images, and must produce a modified image that executes the instruction, incorporates the reference concepts, and preserves all non-edited regions. The 3,119 test cases span constraint densities K=2 through K=7 for creation and K≤3 for editing, with 120 creation configurations and 22 atomic editing subtasks organized into 8 operation categories.

## Method

MICE-Bench is built around a concept-centric design with seven visual dimensions (Identity, Object, Style, Scene, Material, Control, Appearance) and a constraint-density gradient from K=2 to K=7, yielding 1,872 creation and 1,247 editing test cases. Data construction follows a four-stage pipeline: (1) curating a pool of 4,436 images from 9,762 candidates via rule-based filtering and DeQA-Score quality assessment; (2) generating concept-directed captions with Gemini-3 that isolate a single concept per image (e.g., facial geometry for Identity, texture for Material); (3) automated planning where Gemini-3 assigns semantic roles to randomly sampled references and synthesizes coherent multi-concept prompts; and (4) human-in-the-loop verification by six annotators with cross-validation by three experts. Evaluation uses an 8-dimensional framework combining Gemini-3-based VLM scoring (prompt/instruction following, concept consistency, physical realism, non-edited region consistency) with UniPercept for aesthetics, quality, and structure, validated against Qwen3-VL-8B and FGCLIP for robustness.

## Results

On the multi-reference creation task (Table 2), GPT-Image 1.5 achieves the highest overall score of 7.59, leading in Prompt Following (9.07), Concept Consistency (8.81), and Physical Realism (8.64). NanoBanana 2 follows closely at 7.48 overall, while Seedream 4.5 (7.47) tops Image Quality (7.37). Among open-source models, FLUX.2 [klein] leads at 7.26 overall, substantially outperforming BAGEL (6.25) and OmniGen2 (5.91). In editing, NanoBanana 2 achieves the best local editing score (8.55) while GPT-Image 1.5 dominates global editing (8.50); concept retention for the best model drops from 95.56% at K=2 to 76.36% at K=7.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-Image 1.5 (OpenAI) leads the creation task with an overall score of 7.59, beating NanoBanana 2 (7.48) by 0.11 and Seedream 4.5 (7.47) by 0.12, with the largest margin over the weakest model Qwen-Edit-2509 (5.43) at 2.16 points. In local editing (Table 3), NanoBanana 2 (8.55) overtakes GPT-Image 1.5 (8.31) by 0.24, while in global editing (Table 4) GPT-Image 1.5 (8.50) reclaims the lead over NanoBanana 2 (8.25). The strongest open-source model, FLUX.2 [klein] (7.26 creation / 7.95 local / 7.28 global), narrows the gap to the best closed-source model to within ~0.3–0.6 points on creation but still trails by 0.6–1.3 points on editing. BAGEL and DreamOmni2 are the weakest open-source entries, with BAGEL scoring 0.00 on Background NERC in global editing, indicating it treats editing as re-generation rather than targeted modification. Across all models, concept consistency degrades monotonically with constraint density K, dropping from ~95% at K=2 to ~76% at K=7 for the best model, confirming that no current system achieves genuine multi-concept synthesis at high complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-reference image generation/editing benchmark line initiated by OmniContext (2–3 refs) and DreamOmni2 (2–4 refs), but departs sharply in three ways: (1) it introduces a formal 7-dimensional concept space (Identity, Object, Style, Scene, Material, Control, Appearance) that prior benchmarks treated implicitly or not at all; (2) it organizes tasks along a systematic constraint-density gradient (K=2 to K=7) rather than a fixed small number of references; and (3) it unifies creation and editing under a single concept-centric evaluation framework. The data construction pipeline (concept-directed captioning via Gemini-3, automated planning, human-in-the-loop verification) is novel relative to the manual or single-image-centric curation used in EditBench, MagicBrush, and ICE-Bench.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As multi-reference conditioning becomes a core capability in production systems (advertising, cinematography, e-commerce), MICE-Bench provides the first large-scale (3,119 cases) standardized evaluation that can diagnose *which* concepts fail and *at what complexity threshold*, rather than reporting a single aggregate score. The key open question it surfaces is whether current architectures can ever achieve genuine multi-concept synthesis or will always default to superficial composition under high constraint density. Limitations include reliance on a single proprietary VLM judge (Gemini-3), no coverage of 12+ reference inputs, and no multi-round editing evaluation, all of which the authors flag for future work.
