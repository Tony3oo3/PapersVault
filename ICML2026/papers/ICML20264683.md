## Problem

In LLM knowledge distillation, soft labels (the teacher's full next-token distribution) are assumed to be strictly richer than hard labels (a single sampled token), yet empirically a simple linear mix of both consistently outperforms pure soft distillation. The mechanism behind this gain is unexplained: the improvement does not come from better teacher imitation during training (it actually worsens it), but from a previously uncharacterized reduction in exposure bias. No principled framework existed to explain why hard labels help or to guide the design of adaptive mixing strategies.

## Input / Output

At each autoregressive step, the method consumes the prefix context s (prompt plus previously generated tokens), the teacher's full next-token distribution π_T(·|s) for the soft loss, and a single teacher-sampled token a* for the hard loss. The mixing weight λ between the two losses can be fixed or dynamically computed from the teacher's top-token confidence, predictive entropy, a curriculum schedule, or a risk-guided log-sum-exp penalty. The output is a trained student LLM that generates text sequences autoregressively; no on-policy sampling from the student is required during training, keeping per-step cost comparable to standard soft KD.

## Method

The paper introduces the Bridge-Garden Decomposition, which partitions autoregressive generation steps into Bridges (high local risk sensitivity κ(s), where a single wrong token cascades) and Gardens (low κ(s), where many tokens are acceptable), formalized via a κ-weighted upper bound on exposure bias (Thm. 4.1). A complementarity theorem (Thm. 4.2) proves that hard KD minimizes the Bridge component while soft KD minimizes the Garden component, so no single objective is optimal for both, and a hybrid with λ∈(0,1) achieves strictly lower total exposure bias. Guided by this theory, the paper develops four practical hybrid strategies: confidence-based weighting (reduce soft weight when the teacher is confident), entropy-based weighting (scale soft weight by normalized teacher entropy), a curriculum schedule (emphasize hard labels early, ramp up soft labels later), and a risk-guided hybrid that augments the hard loss with a log-sum-exp penalty term to sharpen the student in Bridges while preserving diversity in Gardens. Across seven teacher-student pairs (Qwen, Llama, Gemma, DeepSeek-Coder) and reasoning/math/coding benchmarks, the best variant outperforms divergence-based and on-policy KD baselines while reducing training cost by 9.7×.

## Results

On the primary Qwen2.5-7B→3B reasoning suite (BBH, MMLU, ARC-C, ThmQA), the Risk-Guided Hybrid KD achieves an average score of 55.16, outperforming the best divergence-based baseline (α-β divergence, 53.21) by +1.95 points and pure Soft KD (51.87) by +3.29 points. Gains are consistent across model families: +1.26 on Llama3.1-8B→1B, +0.45 on Gemma3-4B→1B, +0.90 on Qwen2.5-Math-7B→1.5B, and +0.60 on DeepSeek-Coder-6.7B→1.3B. Crucially, the method achieves these gains at training cost comparable to standard off-policy KD, while being 9.7× more efficient than on-policy distillation baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HybKD (Risk-Guided Hybrid) consistently outperforms all nine divergence-based and on-policy baselines across every model family and task domain tested. On Qwen2.5-7B→3B reasoning, it beats the strongest baseline (α-β divergence, 53.21 avg) by +1.95 points, with the largest margin on MMLU (+4.10) and ARC-C (+1.42). Against the on-policy KD baseline (Agarwal et al., 2024 / JS divergence), HybKD gains +1.43% average accuracy while reducing training cost by 9.7×. On math (Qwen2.5-Math-7B→1.5B), HybKD leads α-β divergence by +0.90 avg (55.71 vs 54.81) and Skew F(R)KL by +2.06. On code (DeepSeek-Coder-6.7B→1.3B), it edges out Skew F(R)KL by +0.60 avg (47.89 vs 47.29), with the clearest gain on MBPP (+2.27). The hybrid principle is also orthogonal to divergence choice: combining hard-label supervision with Reverse KL or Skew KL yields further gains over those divergences alone (Fig. 3), confirming the benefit comes from the adaptive mixing mechanism rather than a specific divergence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-established KD paradigms: soft-label distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) and hard-label/sequence-level distillation (Kim & Rush, 2016), and departs from the conventional assumption that the richer soft-label signal is uniformly superior. It also draws on reward-optimization ideas for autoregressive models (Cundy & Ermon, 2023) to motivate the Risk-Guided Hybrid variant. Unlike prior work that proposes alternative divergences (Reverse KL, JS, TV, α-β) or on-policy training to address exposure bias, this paper introduces a theoretical Bridge-Garden decomposition of exposure bias and shows that a simple hard-soft mixture is complementary to all of these approaches, as demonstrated by consistent gains when combined with each divergence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled theoretical account of why hard labels help in LLM distillation—via exposure-bias reduction rather than improved teacher imitation—which was previously only addressed by expensive on-policy sampling. The 9.7× training-cost reduction over on-policy KD while matching or exceeding accuracy makes the approach immediately practical for industrial model compression. Open questions include: the Bridge/Garden threshold τ is task- and model-dependent and not directly computable in practice (the adaptive strategies use proxies like confidence and entropy); the theoretical conditions (prefix concentrability, bounded loss) are hard to verify for real LLMs; and it remains unclear whether the framework extends to non-autoregressive or diffusion-based generation.
