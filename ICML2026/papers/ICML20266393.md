## Problem

A common assumption in multimodal research is that perceptual weaknesses of vision-language models can be compensated by stronger language-side reasoning (chain-of-thought, in-context learning, external tools). This paper argues that for a broad class of geometry-, topology-, and counting-driven tasks, the limiting factor is not reasoning depth but representational access: deferring visual reasoning to language generation displaces computation from the continuous visual manifold to a discrete textual space, irreversibly collapsing task-critical spatial signals before deliberation begins.

## Input / Output

The TET benchmark presents RGB images from four task families (scale-variant hidden text, 3D-perspective-distorted characters, Ishihara-style colorblind plates, and fused Chinese ligature glyphs) paired with a text prompt asking the model to identify the embedded word or character. The output is a single text answer evaluated via Pass@1 and Pass@32 accuracy. The tasks are deliberately designed to be visually straightforward from raw pixels yet nearly impossible to specify or resolve through language-only descriptions, thereby suppressing linguistic shortcuts and isolating the perceptual bottleneck from high-level semantic reasoning.

## Method

The paper contributes three interlocking components. First, a theoretical analysis formalizes an information collapse bound: the vision-to-text projection g_φ is semantically contractive (Eq. 4), so the reconstruction error ε_proj is strictly positive for geometrically distinct but semantically similar inputs, yielding a non-zero prediction error lower bound irreducible by any text-space reasoning h_ψ (Proposition 4.1). Second, the Turing Eye Test (TET) benchmark empirically validates this bound, showing near-zero Pass@1/Pass@32 across 15 state-of-the-art MLLMs (from Qwen2.5-VL-7B to Gemini 2.5 Pro), with negligible gains from 3-shot in-context learning and chain-of-thought. Third, component-wise fine-tuning and RL ablations localize the bottleneck: updating only the vision encoder f_θ yields dramatic improvements (e.g., 86.7% on HiddenText vs. 0% baseline), while updating only the language backbone or adapter produces negligible gains, and RL on the vision tower matches full-parameter optimization. The paper concludes with the Active Visual Querying (AVQ) framework, proposing that reasoning should issue explicit queries back to pixel-level visual representations before semantic compression, satisfying three design principles: reason before collapse, maintain persistent visual access, and let reasoning actively steer perception.

## Results

On the Turing Eye Test (TET), all 15 state-of-the-art MLLMs achieve near-zero performance: average Pass@1 is 0% across the board, with the single best result being Gemini 2.5 Pro at 2.5% Pass@1 (5% Pass@32) on ChineseLigatures. Fine-tuning analysis on Qwen2.5-VL-7B reveals that updating only the vision encoder recovers 86.7–99.3% Pass@1, while updating only the language backbone or adapter yields ≤1.3% Pass@1, confirming the bottleneck is perceptual encoding rather than reasoning capacity. In-context learning (3-shot) and RL on the language module produce negligible gains, further validating that text-space reasoning cannot recover information lost during vision-to-text projection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new model to beat baselines; instead, TET serves as a diagnostic probe demonstrating that all 15 evaluated MLLMs—spanning unified architectures (Show-o2, Bagel, Janus-Pro), closed-source APIs (Claude 4-Sonnet, Gemini 2.5 Pro, OpenAI o1, Seed-1.6), and open-source models (Qwen2.5VL-7B/72B, QVQ-72B, InternVL3-78B, MiniCPM-o-2.6, kimi-vl-a3b)—fail uniformly with near-zero accuracy. No model benefits from scale: Qwen2.5VL-72B (72B) performs identically to Qwen2.5VL-7B (7B) at 0% Pass@1. Inference-time interventions (3-shot ICL, CoT) yield at most 2–5% Pass@32 gains (e.g., Gemini 2.5 Pro on ChineseLigatures goes from 2.5%→7.5% Pass@1 with 3-shot), confirming the bottleneck is representational rather than knowledge-based. The fine-tuning ablation (Table 7) is the sharpest comparison: vision-encoder-only updates achieve 86.7–99.3% Pass@1, while language-backbone-only updates stay at 0–0.7% Pass@1, and adapter-only updates at 0–1.3%, proving the information is destroyed at the vision-to-text projection stage and is irrecoverable by any amount of text-space reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the dominant Perception-then-Reasoning lineage (LLaVA, InstructBLIP, Flamingo) and its extensions via CoT, in-context learning, and tool augmentation (ViperGPT, DeepEyes, VTool-R1), arguing that all of these share a structural fatal flaw: deferring reasoning to text space after visual compression. It also positions itself relative to emerging 'Reasoning Within Perception' approaches (LatentSketchpad, MVoT, Show-o2, Janus-Pro, Bagel), acknowledging their direction but identifying persistent semantic bias inherited from text-aligned pretraining. The paper carves new ground by formalizing an information collapse bound for the vision-to-language projection, introducing the Turing Eye Test (TET) as a diagnostic probe that suppresses linguistic shortcuts, and proposing Active Visual Querying (AVQ) as a formal query-retrieval-update framework that no existing architecture fully instantiates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a widely held assumption in the MLLM community—that scaling language-side deliberation (CoT, ICL, RL) can compensate for perceptual weaknesses—by showing near-zero performance across 15 state-of-the-art models on tasks that are visually straightforward but hard to verbalize. The fine-tuning ablation (vision encoder updates alone yield 86–99% accuracy while language backbone updates yield ~0%) and RL analysis (vision-tower-only optimization matches full-parameter optimization) provide strong diagnostic evidence that the bottleneck is representational access, not reasoning capacity. Key open questions remain: no existing architecture fully implements AVQ with explicit reasoning-driven query generation; fine-tuned encoders fail to generalize beyond training distributions; and the paper does not provide a working end-to-end solution, leaving the 'reasoning within perception' paradigm as a research agenda rather than a deployed system. The TET benchmark itself may become a standard diagnostic for isolating perceptual from reasoning failures in future MLLM evaluations.
