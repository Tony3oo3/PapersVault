## Problem

Classical generalization bounds based on VC dimension, Rademacher complexity, or weight norms become vacuous at modern model scales and provide only worst-case guarantees over hypothesis classes, failing to explain why a particular trained model generalizes. While empirical studies repeatedly associate lower intrinsic dimension of learned embeddings with better generalization, the theoretical mechanism connecting embedding geometry to the generalization gap remained unexplored. The paper asks: can we derive an instance-specific, post-hoc bound that makes the role of embedding intrinsic dimension and downstream network sensitivity explicit for a fixed trained model?

## Input / Output

The method operates on a fixed, already-trained neural network (no retraining) and an independent held-out sample set of size n. At each layer k, it extracts embeddings, estimates the intrinsic dimension via the MLE estimator of Levina & Bickel, computes the 1-Wasserstein distance between the empirical and population embedding distributions (approximated by a disjoint test subset), and estimates the Lipschitz constant of the downstream tail map (via product of spectral norms for MLPs). The output is a high-probability upper bound on the held-out-to-population risk gap, decomposed into a dimension-controlled Wasserstein convergence term, a Lipschitz amplification term, and irreducible Bayes surrogate noise terms.

## Method

The paper derives a post-hoc generalization bound by decomposing the network at layer k into an encoder F_{≤k} and a tail map F_k, then introducing a layer-wise Bayes predictor F_k^* as a continuous surrogate for discrete labels to make the oracle loss g_k(z) = ℓ(F_k(z), F_k^*(z)) differentiable in embedding space. The generalization gap is decomposed into an approximation gap, an oracle statistical gap, and an empirical model gap; the oracle statistical gap is controlled via Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality, reducing it to the 1-Wasserstein distance between empirical and population embedding distributions multiplied by the Lipschitz constant of g_k. The key theoretical ingredient is the sharp Wasserstein convergence rate of Weed & Bach (2019), which shows this distance scales as n^{-1/(d_k+ε)} where d_k is the upper Wasserstein dimension of the embedding distribution. The Lipschitz constant of g_k further decomposes into L_k(F)·M_F + L_k(F^*)·M_F^*, separating controllable network sensitivity from intrinsic data complexity. At the final layer, the tail map is the identity (L_L(F)=1), so architectural sensitivity vanishes and the bound is dominated by intrinsic dimension d_L alone, providing a theoretical explanation for the strong empirical correlation between final-layer dimension and generalization.

## Results

The paper validates its theoretical predictions across multiple scales. On CIFAR-10/100, final-layer intrinsic dimension and empirical Wasserstein distance (validation vs. test embeddings) show significant positive correlation with the class-wise generalization gap across ResNet-18/34/50/101/152. On ImageNet-1K, ConvNeXt models spanning 82.88%–87.46% top-1 accuracy exhibit the predicted pattern: lower final-layer intrinsic dimension is associated with smaller subset-to-subset Wasserstein distance and higher accuracy. Width-intervention experiments on CIFAR-10 MLPs confirm the joint role of dimension and Lipschitz sensitivity: narrowing the third hidden layer from 100 to 10 units reduces embedding dimension but increases the network Lipschitz constant, yielding non-monotonic generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper with empirical validation rather than a method-comparison study, so there are no direct baseline methods to beat or match. Instead, the paper validates its bound by comparing across architectures and scales: (i) five ResNet variants (18–152) on CIFAR-10/100 show that final-layer intrinsic dimension and Wasserstein distance both correlate positively with the generalization gap, with the correlation strengthening at deeper layers; (ii) nine ConvNeXt models with different scales and pretraining regimes on ImageNet-1K confirm the same qualitative pattern at scale; (iii) pretrained language models on MNLI replicate the finding in the text modality. The width-intervention experiment (Section 5.4) provides causal evidence that the bound's two factors—embedding dimension and Lipschitz sensitivity—act jointly: reducing layer width lowers dimension but raises the Lipschitz constant, so generalization does not improve monotonically, consistent with the theoretical tradeoff in Theorem 4.1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the sharp Wasserstein convergence rates of Weed & Bach (2019), which show that empirical measures converge to their population counterparts at a rate governed by the upper Wasserstein dimension. It departs from classical generalization theory (VC dimension, Rademacher complexity, norm-based bounds) by providing a post-hoc, instance-specific bound for a fixed trained model rather than a uniform worst-case guarantee over a hypothesis class. The work also extends the empirical observation line of Ansuini et al. (2019) and Pope et al. (2021), which linked low intrinsic dimension to better generalization without a formal mechanism, by showing that intrinsic dimension controls the Wasserstein convergence rate of embedding distributions, which in turn bounds the generalization gap through Lipschitz propagation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical mechanism explaining why low intrinsic dimension of learned embeddings correlates with better generalization: lower dimension accelerates Wasserstein convergence, making finite held-out samples more reliable estimates of population risk. The final-layer simplification—where architectural Lipschitz sensitivity vanishes and the bound is dominated purely by embedding geometry—offers a clean explanation for why final-layer dimension is the most diagnostic layer. Key limitations include the post-hoc nature of the bound (it cannot use training-set embeddings due to model-data dependence), the difficulty of reliably estimating Lipschitz constants for deep networks, and the fact that the framework does not capture optimization dynamics or implicit regularization. Open questions include extending the analysis to standard (non-post-hoc) generalization bounds via stability arguments and developing scalable estimators for the bound's constants.
