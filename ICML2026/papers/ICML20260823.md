## Problem

While LLM hallucinations are widely observed, the mechanisms by which decoder-only Transformers produce reasoning hallucinations remain poorly understood, particularly regarding when and why they emerge during training. Prior analyses focus on data distributions or fixed checkpoints but lack a unified framework connecting hallucinations to the internal graph-search dynamics of next-token prediction across the full training trajectory.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260823/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For intrinsic reasoning, the model receives a contextual subgraph (as an edge list) plus source and target node tokens and must predict a path consistent with that subgraph; for extrinsic reasoning, it receives only source and target tokens and must rely on memorized graph structure. In real-world experiments, inputs are 80-word natural-language stories generated from relational paths over a 500-node SBM. Outputs are predicted node sequences (paths), evaluated via three accuracy metrics (Local, Exist, Global) and an Uncompressed Ratio R = predicted path length / ground-truth path length that quantifies the degree of path compression.

## Method

The paper models next-token prediction as a constrained graph search over an underlying directed graph (nodes = entities, edges = relations), distinguishing intrinsic reasoning (search over a sampled subgraph) from extrinsic reasoning (search over the full memorized graph). It identifies two hallucination mechanisms tied to training dynamics: Path Reuse, where underfitting in early training causes the model to reuse memorized edges from the underlying graph while ignoring contextual constraints, and Path Compression, where overfitting in later training causes frequently traversed multi-hop paths to collapse into shortcut edges, biasing predictions toward high-out-degree nodes. A simplified analytical model (Assumption B.2) formalizes the learned transition as a convex combination of i-step random-walk matrices, yielding explicit conditions (Propositions B.3–B.4) under which the model assigns higher probability to a k-hop target than to the true 1-hop neighbor. Empirically, LLaMA-2, Qwen-2, and Mixtral architectures are trained from scratch on synthetic ER and SBM graphs (up to 1,000 nodes) and on language stories, tracking accuracy and uncompressed ratio across epochs to demonstrate that the uncompressed ratio peaks early and monotonically declines, confirming that path compression is a training-data bias rather than an architecture-specific effect.

## Results

On extrinsic reasoning with 1,000-node SBM graphs, accuracy peaks near 0.99–1.0 at early epochs but degrades to 0.83–0.85 by epoch 49 for 100-community graphs (p_in=0.1, p_out=0.01–0.02), while 10-community graphs remain stable at 0.98–0.99. The dominant error mode is predicting 3-hop neighbors (path compression), with the proportion of such errors increasing over training. For finetuning recovery, PPO achieves a maximum accuracy improvement of ~0.30 versus ~0.20 for SFT, and SFT exhibits clear collapse when applied to models already strongly affected by path compression (epoch 6, original Acc 0.6787). In language-sequence pretraining (500-node SBM, 25 communities), the maximum uncompressed path ratio is reached in the early training stage, before loss convergence, confirming that path compression emerges even as training loss continues to decrease.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analytical paper without a single proposed method benchmarked against external baselines; instead, it compares training dynamics across graph structures, model architectures, and finetuning strategies. Across architectures (LLaMA-2, Qwen-2, Mixtral), all exhibit the same path-compression trend, with Mixtral peaking at epoch 1 and LLaMA-2/Qwen-2 at epoch 2, but none preventing the subsequent decline in uncompressed ratio. Increasing model depth (6→24 layers) does not prevent path compression; shallower layers actually mitigate it. For finetuning recovery, PPO consistently outperforms SFT: PPO achieves ~0.30 ΔACC across all hop distances and pretraining checkpoints, while SFT peaks at ~0.20 and collapses at epoch 6 (original Acc 0.6787), especially with out-of-domain finetuning data. Graphs with fewer communities (10) and higher p_in are substantially more robust to path compression (accuracy stays ≥0.98) compared to 100-community graphs (accuracy drops to 0.83–0.85), confirming that community structure and bridge-node density are the primary structural drivers of hallucination severity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the graph-search formulation for decoder-only Transformers established by Wang et al. (2024, 2025) by shifting the question from 'can Transformers perform graph search?' to 'when and why do they hallucinate during training?' It departs from prior hallucination analyses (Orgad et al., Zhu et al. 2024) that examine models at a fixed checkpoint by tracing the emergence of two distinct failure modes—Path Reuse (underfitting) and Path Compression (overfitting)—across the training trajectory. The synthetic graph reasoning literature (Saparov et al., Khona et al., Cohen et al.) provides the experimental testbed, but this work adds the training-dynamics dimension and connects the findings to the grokking literature (Power et al. 2022) and the broader hallucination taxonomy (Ji et al. 2023, Huang et al. 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that extrinsic-reasoning accuracy peaks and then degrades during continued training challenges the common assumption that more training monotonically improves reasoning fidelity, with direct implications for pretraining schedules and early-stopping heuristics. The unified graph-based explanation also reinterprets the reversal curse and reasoning distribution bias as natural consequences of path compression, offering a mechanistic account where prior work offered only empirical observation. Key limitations include reliance on small synthetic graphs (≤1000 nodes) and 6-layer models, leaving open whether these dynamics persist at the scale of modern LLMs; the DocRED validation in the appendix is encouraging but limited. Open questions include whether structurally grounded training objectives (e.g., explicit edge-existence rewards) can prevent path compression without sacrificing fluency, and whether the underlying-graph assumption adequately captures the full complexity of real-world knowledge.
