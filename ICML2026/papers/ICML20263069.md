## Problem

While VLMs are increasingly deployed in embodied applications requiring physical understanding, it remains unclear whether they can genuinely reason about physical transformations—tracking whether specific quantities remain invariant as objects change appearance. Existing benchmarks evaluate physical properties in static scenes or video generation plausibility, but none isolate transformation-invariant reasoning, where models must judge whether a quantity is preserved or altered across a dynamic process.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263069/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

Each trial presents a sequence of 3–16 frames extracted from a short video depicting a physical transformation (pouring, spreading, reshaping), paired with a text question asking whether a specific quantity (number, length, volume, or size) is the same in the initial and final states. The model must select from three multiple-choice options (e.g., 'Yes, they are the same,' 'No, the upper row has more,' 'No, the lower row has more'), with option order shuffled to eliminate positional bias. Frame extraction varies across three strategies (uniform, human-curated, SEViLA-based) and four prompt formats (direct, sequential, CoT, continuous), yielding a 3×5×4 factorial of 60 conditions per video.

## Method

ConservationBench introduces 384 paired videos (192 conservation tasks + 192 matched non-conserving controls) across four quantitative properties, where each non-conserving control alters the target quantity while holding all task-irrelevant visual features constant, enabling detection of invariance bias. The benchmark is evaluated on 112 VLMs under 60 factorial conditions (frame count × extraction method × prompt type), producing 23,040 total trials. Control experiments with empty images and text-only inputs dissociate visual from textual sources of bias, revealing that models rely on a strong textual prior toward quantity invariance rather than visual transformation evidence. A mechanistic analysis on Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct shows that the dominant error mode (confidently predicting 'same' on non-conserving trials) is accompanied by elevated decision confidence and Frame-1 attention anchoring, indicating the model fails to update its representation after the transformation.

## Results

Across 112 VLMs evaluated on ConservationBench (23,040 trials), model accuracy ranges from ~20% to ~69%, with most performing only marginally above the 33.3% chance level, while human participants achieve 98.35% accuracy. A moderate negative correlation (r = −0.510) between conservation and non-conserving accuracy reveals a systematic invariance bias: 82/112 models (73.2%) fall below chance on strict pairwise evaluation, with only Gemini-2.5-Pro (41.2%), Doubao-Seed-1.6-Vision (38.2%), and Claude-Sonnet-4-5 (37.2%) exceeding the 33.3% chance threshold. Control experiments show that removing visual content actually *improves* conservation accuracy (85.7% with empty images vs. ~60% with real content), confirming that textual priors dominate over visual transformation reasoning. Neither increased temporal resolution (3→16 frames), CoT prompting, nor human/SeViLA-curated frame selection yields reliable gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, ConservationBench does not propose a new method but evaluates 112 existing VLMs against a human baseline and internal controls. The most striking comparison is VLMs vs. humans: the best model (Gemini-2.5-Pro) reaches 69.11% average accuracy versus 98.35% for humans, a gap of nearly 30 points. On the strict pairwise metric (requiring correct answers on both a conservation task and its matched non-conserving control), the gap widens dramatically: 41.20% for the best model vs. 96.72% for humans. The conservation-vs-non-conserving comparison reveals that models with high conservation accuracy (e.g., Qwen-2.5-VL-72B at 89.69%) tend to have very low non-conserving accuracy (21.98%), confirming a default invariance heuristic rather than genuine transformation reasoning. Cross-benchmark correlation analysis shows ConservationBench correlates substantially with nine established benchmarks (BLINK, MLVU, MMBench-Video, MME-RealWorld, MMStar, NaturalBench, PhysBench, RealWorldQA, Video-MME), indicating it captures a general factor of physical understanding. Scaling analysis further shows conservation reasoning does not emerge with model size (R²=0.019), while non-conserving accuracy shows only a modest scaling effect (R²=0.239).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Piagetian conservation tasks from developmental psychology into a VLM evaluation framework, departing from existing physical-reasoning benchmarks (PhysBench, PhysUniBench, PhyBench) that test static-scene property identification or outcome prediction rather than transformation-invariant tracking. The key methodological innovation is the paired non-conserving control design, which addresses a shortcut-exploitation problem inherent in conservation tasks (uniform 'conserved' ground truth) that prior VLM physical-reasoning evaluations did not confront. For frame extraction, the paper leverages SEViLA's BLIP-2-based Localizer as one of three sampling strategies, but the core contribution is the benchmark design and the diagnostic control experiments (empty-image, text-only) rather than any new model architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that VLMs perform worse on conservation tasks with real visual content (~60%) than with empty images (85.7%) reframes the problem: the deficit is not lack of physical knowledge but an architectural inability to extract and maintain object-state representations across frames, with textual priors dominating and visual evidence actively interfering. This has direct implications for embodied AI deployment, where conservation reasoning is a prerequisite for planning, tool use, and robotic manipulation. Open questions include whether the deficit generalizes to more complex scenarios (occlusions, deformable objects, noisy observations), whether alternative architectures (predictive state-based abstractions) could address it, and whether conservation failures measurably impair downstream goal-directed tasks.
