## Problem

DRL-based neural solvers for combinatorial optimization (3D-BPP, TSP, CVRP) exhibit severe performance degradation under distribution shift, as policies trained on specific instance distributions fail to generalize to unseen item sizes or node configurations. The paper identifies and formalizes the 'Satisficing Generalization Edge'—the observation that identifying a set of promising actions is inherently more robust across distributions than pinpointing the single optimal action—and asks how to exploit this property for both zero-shot generalization and rapid online adaptation.

## Input / Output

For routing problems, the method consumes node coordinates in the unit square [0,1]², customer demands, and vehicle capacity, producing a tour permutation (TSP) or a partition of customers into capacity-feasible routes (CVRP). For 3D-BPP, it takes sequential item dimensions (l_i, w_i, h_i) and fixed container dimensions (L, W, H), outputting a placement coordinate (x_i, y_i, z_i) and orientation per item. Critically, the proposal policy first reduces the full action space (n candidates) to a small top-k subset (k ≪ n), and the selection policy then operates only on this reduced set, which is what enables fast adaptation with fewer samples.

## Method

ASAP decomposes the decision process into a Proposal Policy (π^p) that generates a candidate action set Â of size k ≪ n, and a Selection Policy (π^s) that picks the final action from Â. Training uses a two-phase scheme: Phase 1 pretrains a standard one-stage policy to warm-start both π^p and π^s, avoiding the 'garbage-in, garbage-out' divergence of joint training; Phase 2 performs cooperative tuning where both policies are updated jointly. MAML is layered on top by treating each instance distribution as a meta-task, producing an initialization that adapts in few gradient steps. For online adaptation to a new distribution, the proposal policy is frozen (preserving its generalizable candidate-filtering ability) while only the selection policy is fine-tuned on the reduced k-action space, requiring far fewer samples to converge. Theoretical analysis (Theorems 4.2–4.3) proves the two-stage process strictly outperforms one-stage selection when the policy assigns probability > 1/(n−1) to the optimal action, and that the proposal set inclusion probability is less sensitive to distributional mismatch than the selection probability.

## Results

On 3D-BPP (Table 1), PCT+ASAP w/MAML achieves 73.5% space utilization on ID-Large (Discrete), surpassing PCT (70.0%), AR2L (68.8%), and GOPT (71.2%), with the gap widening on OOD-Small to 82.3% vs. 79.5% (PCT) and 80.5% (GOPT). On routing (Table 2), POMO+ASAP w/o MAML reduces the optimality gap on Clustered TSP-100 from 5.22% to 4.32%, and INViT-1V+ASAP w/o MAML improves from 7.14% to 4.80%. The most striking result is in online adaptation: on Clustered TSP-1000, standard POMO degrades by −10.98% after adaptation, while POMO+ASAP w/o MAML improves by +7.87%; on OOD-Small 3D-BPP (Continuous), PCT+ASAP w/o MAML gains +2.3% utilization vs. only +0.5% for PCT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ASAP consistently outperforms all five baselines across both problem domains. On 3D-BPP, PCT+ASAP w/MAML beats PCT by 3.5 points (73.5% vs. 70.0%) on ID-Large and by 2.8 points (82.3% vs. 79.5%) on OOD-Small (Discrete), while also surpassing AR2L (68.8%/78.8%) and GOPT (71.2%/80.5%). The adaptation advantage is even more pronounced: on OOD-Small (Continuous), PCT+ASAP w/o MAML gains +2.3% utilization after adaptation versus only +0.5% (PCT) and +0.6% (AR2L). On routing, POMO+ASAP w/o MAML reduces the TSP-100 gap by 0.9 points (4.32% vs. 5.22%) and the TSP-1000 gap by 9.4 points (47.88% vs. 57.25%), while INViT-1V+ASAP w/o MAML cuts the TSP-100 gap by 2.3 points (4.80% vs. 7.14%). Critically, ASAP reverses the adaptation failure mode: where vanilla POMO degrades by −10.98% on Clustered TSP-1000 and INViT-1V stagnates at −0.01% on Implosion CVRP-500, their ASAP counterparts improve by +7.87% and +2.46% respectively, demonstrating that the proposal-selection decomposition is the key mechanism enabling effective online fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the standard DRL-based constructive solvers for combinatorial optimization (PCT for 3D-BPP, POMO and INViT for routing) and repurposes MAML for meta-learning initialization, but its core departure is the Satisficing Generalization Edge: a theoretical and empirical argument that identifying a *set* of promising actions generalizes better than selecting the single optimal one. Unlike prior two-stage models that hybridize ML with exact solvers (predict-and-search, learning-to-prune) or select among pre-trained agents, ASAP is a purely DRL-based proposal-then-selection architecture where both stages are learned end-to-end. The selective adaptation strategy—freezing the proposal policy and fine-tuning only the selection policy on a reduced action space—differs from prior meta-learning approaches (e.g., Omni-TSP) that reinitialize or retrain the full policy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Distribution shift is the primary barrier to deploying neural CO solvers in real logistics where item sizes, customer locations, and demand patterns evolve seasonally; ASAP offers a generic, plug-in mechanism that improves both zero-shot generalization and few-shot online adaptation across 3D-BPP, TSP, and CVRP without architectural changes to the base solver. The finding that selective adaptation (tuning only the selection policy) outperforms full-parameter tuning is practically important, suggesting a principled way to avoid overfitting on limited online data. Open questions include how the framework behaves when the proposal set size k must grow with problem scale, whether the theoretical guarantees (which assume a single target action and worst-case identical policies) hold in multi-objective or stochastic settings, and whether the approach extends to problems where the action space is continuous rather than discrete.
