## Problem

Existing competitive-programming benchmarks for LLMs overwhelmingly use batch-style evaluation where all inputs are provided upfront, leaving unexplored whether model-generated programs can solve interactive problems that require multi-round querying under strict protocols and limited query budgets. InteractBench fills this gap by benchmarking LLMs on 322 interactive problems where key information is unrevealed at execution time and must be acquired dynamically through an interactor.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262553/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation takes a standardized zero-shot prompt containing the interactive problem statement (with protocol, query budget, and I/O format specifications) and produces a self-contained C++ program from the LLM. The generated program is compiled and executed as a sandboxed process (2 CPU cores, 4 GB RAM, Docker with cgroups) connected via standard streams to a packaged local interactor binary that holds hidden test cases. Outputs include per-test-case Accepted/Rejected verdicts aggregated into pass@1 and pass@5 (n=10 samples, temperature 0.8), a mutually exclusive failure taxonomy label (IDLE, PE, QLE, CE, RE, TLE, MLE, WA), and the full interaction transcript with query counts and termination signals.

## Method

The benchmark is constructed via an execution-driven propose-validate-adjudicate loop: two proposer models (GPT-5.2 and Gemini-3-Pro-Preview) draft candidate test-case generators and local interactors from problem statements, which are then validated against per-task verification pools of 15 accepted and 30 rejected submissions (including mutation-derived probes targeting protocol deviations and budget violations); failed candidates receive trace-grounded feedback from an adjudicator model before escalation to human experts after three iterations. Each of the 322 tasks is packaged as a self-contained unit with an executable interactor, hidden test cases, and resource limits, enabling fully offline evaluation without external judge submission. The evaluation harness runs solver and interactor as two isolated processes, enforcing protocol compliance and query budgets via explicit counters, and records structured termination reasons. Beyond pass@k, the harness assigns each failed execution a primary failure label from a fine-grained taxonomy that separates algorithmic logic errors (WA) from interaction-specific breakdowns (protocol violations, query-budget overruns, deadlocks) and resource-limit failures, enabling diagnosis of whether models fail at the reasoning level or the interaction-protocol level.

## Results

On InteractBench (322 interactive competitive-programming problems), the best-performing model, Gemini-3-Pro-Preview, achieves an overall pass@1 of 0.554 and pass@5 of 0.752, while GPT-5.2 reaches 0.542/0.705. Hard problems remain far from solved: the best model attains only 0.597 pass@5 on Hard, and only two configurations (Gemini-3-Pro-Preview and GPT-5.2) exceed 0.400 on Hard pass@5. Within-family comparisons show that explicit reasoning roughly doubles performance (e.g., DeepSeek-V3.2-Thinking 0.513 vs. DeepSeek-V3.2 0.262 overall pass@5), yet even the strongest reasoning models fail on the majority of Hard interactive tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, InteractBench does not propose a new method to compare against prior methods; instead it evaluates 16 LLM configurations (12 reasoning, 4 non-reasoning) to expose the interaction gap. The clearest separation is between frontier reasoning models and the rest: Gemini-3-Pro-Preview (0.554) and GPT-5.2 (0.542) lead on overall pass@1, while the next tier (DeepSeek-V3.2-Thinking, Gemini-2.5-Pro) trails by 20+ points. Within-family comparisons isolate the effect of explicit reasoning: DeepSeek-V3.2-Thinking nearly doubles its non-reasoning counterpart (0.513 vs. 0.262 overall pass@5), and Qwen3-32B Thinking outperforms Qwen3-32B-NonThinking by over 4× (0.181 vs. 0.040). On Hard problems specifically, only Gemini-3-Pro-Preview (0.597) and GPT-5.2 (0.492) exceed 0.400 pass@5, while all other configurations fall below 0.240, confirming that Hard interactive tasks remain a strong discriminator. Failure-mode analysis shows that algorithmic logic errors (WA) dominate (~60–70% of failures for most models), but interaction-specific failures (protocol violations ~12–20%, query-budget overruns ~3–12%) are non-trivial and more prevalent among stronger models that successfully enter the protocol but mismanage budgets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the competitive-programming benchmark lineage (CodeContests, LiveCodeBench, ICPC-Eval, LiveOIBench, CodeElo) by shifting the evaluation target from batch-style problems—where all inputs are revealed upfront—to interactive problems, where a solver must acquire hidden information through multi-round queries under strict protocols and query budgets. It departs from prior work in three concrete ways: (1) all 322 tasks are interactive rather than a small subset, (2) each task ships with an executable local interactor enabling fully offline evaluation (unlike CodeElo or HLCE, which require remote judge submissions), and (3) the harness reports a fine-grained interaction-aware failure taxonomy (protocol violations, query-budget overruns, deadlocks) beyond a single pass/fail verdict. Conceptually, it also distinguishes itself from agent-style coding benchmarks (SWE-bench, InterCode, CodeAct) by requiring a single self-contained program whose internal query strategy must be sound, rather than an iterative debug loop with execution feedback.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

InteractBench exposes a substantial 'interaction gap' in frontier LLMs: even the best model (Gemini-3-Pro-Preview) reaches only 0.554 overall pass@1 and 0.367 on hard problems, a sharp contrast with near-saturation on batch-style benchmarks. The failure taxonomy reveals that the bottleneck is not purely algorithmic—protocol violations and query-budget overruns account for ~20–25% of failures even in top models, pointing to a distinct skill in state tracking and query-strategy management under partial observability. Key open questions include whether targeted training or prompting for interactive querying, state maintenance, and protocol adherence can close this gap, and whether the 322-problem scale is sufficient for stable model differentiation. The offline, self-contained evaluation infrastructure also lowers the barrier for reproducible interactive-programming research compared to prior remote-judge-dependent setups.
