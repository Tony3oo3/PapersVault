## Problem

Mainstream UQ methods for LLMs are deployed as safety safeguards in high-stakes domains, yet the paper argues they suffer from a fundamental category error: they measure the internal consistency of model generations rather than their alignment with external factual correctness. This structural limitation renders them blind to 'confident hallucinations,' where models produce stable but incorrect answers with high confidence scores, creating a deceptive sense of safety that current evaluation metrics fail to expose.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266366/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analyzed methods all consume a text prompt and the model's own multiple sampled generations, using the model as both generator and evaluator in a closed loop with no external ground truth. Semantic Entropy groups m sampled responses into discrete Answer Classes via an NLI model and outputs a scalar entropy; graph-based methods build a weighted similarity graph over responses and extract uncertainty from the Laplacian eigenvalue spectrum; P(true) appends a verification prompt to a single answer and reads the probability of the token 'True.' The proposed conformal prediction framework would instead output prediction sets with user-specified coverage guarantees, and the atomic fact verification protocol decomposes open-ended outputs into indivisible claims checked against external authorities such as search engines, knowledge bases, and formal theorem provers.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a mechanistic argument that three dominant UQ families are isomorphic to unsupervised clustering: Semantic Entropy performs explicit NLI-based clustering into Answer Classes, graph-based methods perform spectral clustering via the graph Laplacian's eigenvalue spectrum, and P(true) partitions the model's latent space into confidence regions (empirically validated via PCA of hidden states on QASC). From this isomorphism, the authors derive three pathologies—hyperparameter sensitivity (Jaccard overlap between top-k uncertain samples across methods drops to 0.08 at 10%), an internal evaluation cycle that conflates stability with truth, and a recursive 'judge problem' where correctness labels themselves depend on unreliable model-based judges. The proposed three-pillar roadmap replaces average-case AUROC with worst-case TPR-at-low-FPR and an Area Under the Stability Curve (AUSC) for evaluation, introduces conformal prediction as a truth-aware mechanism that exposes confident hallucinations through prediction-set size explosion at fixed coverage, and mandates grounding in verifiable environments (code execution, mathematical constants) and atomic fact verification against diverse external authorities.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that three mainstream UQ methods (Semantic Entropy, Graph-based EigV, and P(true)) fundamentally disagree on which samples are uncertain: on QASC with Qwen2.5-32B, the Jaccard similarity among top-10% highest-uncertainty samples is only 0.080–0.224 across method pairs, rising to 0.229–0.404 at the top-30% level. A PCA visualization of hidden states during P(true) estimation confirms that the model's internal representations are geometrically partitioned into distinct belief clusters, validating the clustering interpretation. Additionally, UQ method rankings become unstable as the correctness threshold τ varies, demonstrating that evaluation conclusions are sensitive to the (itself unreliable) correctness function.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new UQ method to beat baselines; instead, it argues that the three compared methods are mechanistically equivalent (all unsupervised clustering) and therefore share the same fundamental blind spot. Semantic Entropy (Kuhn et al., ICLR 2023) is identified as explicit clustering via NLI-based answer-class grouping; the graph-based EigV method (Lin et al., TMLR 2024) is shown to be spectral clustering over a response-similarity graph; and P(true) (Kadavath et al., 2022) is characterized as implicit clustering of the model's latent belief space. The key empirical evidence is that these three methods, despite their different mathematical formulations, disagree substantially on which samples are uncertain (Jaccard similarity as low as 0.080 at the 10% level), undermining the claim that any single method captures a stable 'true' uncertainty signal. The paper concludes that none of the three can detect confident hallucinations because they all measure internal consistency rather than external correctness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper reinterprets the entire mainstream UQ-for-LLMs literature (Semantic Entropy, graph-based spectral methods, verbalized P(true)) through the lens of unsupervised clustering theory, arguing these diverse approaches are mechanically isomorphic to clustering algorithms that measure internal consistency rather than external correctness. It borrows the TPR-at-low-FPR evaluation framework from Carlini et al.'s (2022) first-principles analysis of membership inference attacks and adopts Conformal Language Modeling (Quach et al., 2023) as a proposed downstream evaluation mechanism, but its core contribution is a conceptual reframing rather than a new method. The paper departs from the prior UQ literature's implicit assumption that self-consistency is a valid proxy for truth, positioning itself against the dominant paradigm rather than extending it incrementally.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If the clustering characterization is correct, it implies that the field's primary safety tool for high-stakes LLM deployment is structurally incapable of detecting confident hallucinations, which would be a significant reorientation for the UQ community. The proposed three-pillar roadmap (worst-case robustness evaluation, native uncertainty via conformal prediction and RLHF, atomic fact verification) is directionally sound but remains largely aspirational, with no full empirical validation of the proposed evaluation metrics or training procedures. Key open questions include whether the clustering framing is too reductive (e.g., does it dismiss legitimate epistemic uncertainty measurement?), whether conformal prediction set sizes remain tractable for open-ended generation, and whether atomic fact verification can scale beyond narrow domains. The paper's empirical evidence (single model, single dataset for Jaccard analysis and PCA visualization) is suggestive but insufficient to conclusively establish the isomorphism claim across architectures and task types.
