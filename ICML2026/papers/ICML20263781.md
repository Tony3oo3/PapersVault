## Problem

Existing time series forecasting methods with exogenous variables (TSF-X) underutilize future exogenous variables and fail to explicitly model the correlation structure between endogenous and exogenous variables. The paper identifies a dual correlation structure—temporal (historical exogenous → future exogenous mirrors historical endogenous → future endogenous) and channel (historical exogenous → historical endogenous transfers to future exogenous → future endogenous)—that remains unexplored by prior work.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263781/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes three inputs: a historical endogenous series X^endo ∈ R^{N×T}, historical exogenous variables X^exo ∈ R^{D×T}, and future exogenous variables Y^exo ∈ R^{D×F}, where N is the number of endogenous channels, D is the number of exogenous channels, T is the lookback length, and F is the forecasting horizon. The output is a fused forecast Ŷ^endo ∈ R^{N×F} combining predictions from two parallel paths (temporal and channel correlation injection) via a learnable fusion weight λ₁. When future exogenous variables are unavailable at inference, the model substitutes its own predicted future exogenous values (from the temporal correlation discovery branch) in place of the true Y^exo.

## Method

DAG comprises two symmetric modules, each with a correlation discovery submodule and a correlation injection submodule. The Temporal Correlation Module applies patch-wise embedding to historical exogenous variables, uses a Transformer block to learn how they influence future exogenous variables, and extracts the learned query/key projection matrices (W_q', W_k') as a temporal correlation representation; these are injected into a Correlation Trmblock that processes historical endogenous patches, where a gating scalar α (computed as a dot product of MLP-encoded exogenous and endogenous inputs) adaptively fuses the original and correlation-informed attention scores. The Channel Correlation Module mirrors this design along the variable dimension: it uses series-wise embedding of historical exogenous variables to discover their influence on historical endogenous variables, then injects the extracted correlation parameters into a Correlation Trmblock processing future exogenous series to predict future endogenous values. Training is end-to-end with a composite loss L_total = L_f + λ₂(L_t + L_c), where L_f is the forecasting L1 loss, L_t is the temporal correlation loss (predicting future exogenous from historical exogenous), and L_c is the channel correlation loss (predicting historical endogenous from historical exogenous).

## Results

On the 12 real-world TSF-X datasets (with future exogenous variables available), DAG achieves the best MSE on 10 out of 12 datasets and the best MAE on all 12, outperforming the closest competitor GCGNet by margins ranging from 0.001 (PJM: 0.093 vs 0.095) to 0.076 (Rapel: 0.230 vs 0.306). The largest absolute gains over the next-best method appear on Colbun (MSE 0.098 vs 0.107) and Rapel (MSE 0.230 vs 0.306). When future exogenous variables are unavailable (Table 3), DAG still secures 9 first-place MSE rankings and 7 first-place MAE rankings, confirming robustness without future covariates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DAG outperforms all 9 baselines on the majority of the 12 TSF-X datasets. The closest competitor is GCGNet (ICLR 2026), which DAG beats on 10/12 MSE and 12/12 MAE; the two are nearly tied on PJM (0.093 vs 0.095 MSE) and Sdwpfm1 (0.423 vs 0.424), while DAG's largest margin over GCGNet is on Rapel (0.230 vs 0.306 MSE). TimeXer (NeurIPS 2024) and TFT (IJF 2021), which natively support future exogenous variables, are consistently outperformed by DAG by 5–20% in MSE across most datasets (e.g., Energy: 0.124 vs 0.163 for TimeXer; Colbun: 0.098 vs 0.238 for TFT). Methods that do not natively handle future exogenous variables (DUET, CrossLinear, Amplifier, TimeKAN, PatchTST) show substantially larger gaps, with PatchTST being the weakest baseline (e.g., Rapel MSE 0.269 vs DAG's 0.230; Energy MSE 0.226 vs 0.124). In the no-future-exogenous setting (Table 3), DAG still leads with 9/12 MSE first-place rankings, though the margin over GCGNet narrows (e.g., FR: 0.435 vs 0.435 MSE, a tie).

## Relation to Prior Work

DAG carves new ground at the intersection of patch-based time series representations (from PatchTST and Triformer) and exogenous-variable-aware forecasting (from TimeXer, TFT, TiDE, CrossLinear). While prior methods either ignore future exogenous variables entirely (TimeXer, CrossLinear) or incorporate them via simple concatenation/attention without explicit correlation modeling (TFT, TiDE), DAG introduces a structured 'discover-then-inject' paradigm: it first learns correlation parameters (query/key matrices) in a dedicated discovery module and then transfers those parameters into a separate injection module that performs the actual endogenous forecast. This parameter-transfer mechanism, applied along both temporal (patch-level) and channel (series-level) dimensions, departs from GCGNet's graph-based correlation modeling and from the monolithic attention designs of TFT/TimeXer, offering a more explicit decomposition of how exogenous information should influence endogenous prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in energy, weather, and traffic forecasting where future covariates (load forecasts, weather predictions, holiday calendars) are available but underutilized by existing deep learning methods. The introduction of 7 new real-world TSF-X datasets (Energy, Colbun, Rapel, Sdwpfm1/2, Sdwpfh1/2) provides the community with additional benchmarks beyond the 5 EPF datasets. Key limitations acknowledged by the authors include the absence of explicit causal modeling (correlation ≠ causation), the simplicity of the dot-product gating mechanism, and reliance on manually tuned weighting coefficients (λ₁, λ₂). Open questions include whether adaptive or learned weighting strategies could replace the fixed hyperparameters, and whether the dual-correlation framework generalizes to settings with missing or noisy future exogenous variables.
