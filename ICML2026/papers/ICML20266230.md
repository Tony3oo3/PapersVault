## Problem

Robust optimization requires choosing a robustness level (uncertainty set size), but this parameter is typically set heuristically—e.g., fixing a 95% confidence level—leading to either insufficient protection or overly conservative, costly decisions. Existing conformal prediction approaches construct data-driven uncertainty sets with finite-sample coverage guarantees but still fix coverage targets a priori and offer no principled mechanism for selecting the robustness level. The paper asks: can we certify, with distribution-free finite-sample guarantees, the full miscoverage-regret trade-off across all robustness levels so that a decision-maker can calibrate robustness according to their cost-risk preferences?

## Input / Output

The method takes a calibration dataset of n context-outcome pairs (X_i, Y_i), a family of nested uncertainty sets {U_λ(X)} indexed by a robustness parameter λ ∈ Λ, and a cost function f(Y, z). It requires the induced loss (miscoverage or regret) to be bounded in [0, B] (Assumption 3.2). The output is a certified Pareto frontier: for each candidate λ, a valid upper-bound estimate of both miscoverage P[Y ∉ U_λ(X)] and expected regret E[R_λ], from which a decision-maker selects a preferred trade-off point. When the robustness level is chosen post-hoc from the estimated frontier, the method applies a data-splitting recalibration step to restore exact finite-sample validity for the reported estimates at the selected λ.

## Method

CREME inverts the logic of conformal risk control (CRC): rather than prescribing a target risk α and searching for a λ that satisfies it, CREME takes λ as fixed and certifies the actual risk it incurs. The core estimator is α̂_ℓ(λ) = inf{α ∈ (0,B) : B − ℓ̄_n(λ) ≥ ⌈(n+1)(B−α)⌉/n}, which under exchangeability and bounded loss provides a valid finite-sample upper bound on E[ℓ_λ(X_{n+1}, Y_{n+1})]; a simpler closed-form upper bound α̃_ℓ(λ) = (n/(n+1))·ℓ̄_n(λ) + B/(n+1) is also derived. Under a majorant consistency assumption (shrinking the uncertainty set does not increase conditional expected cost), the parametric curve λ ↦ (α_I(λ), α_R(λ)) is weakly decreasing and its image coincides with the Pareto frontier of the robust policy family, so the estimated frontier is a conservative outer approximation of the true risk set. To handle post-hoc selection bias when a decision-maker picks λ from the estimated frontier, CREME splits the calibration data into two halves: the first half builds the frontier and drives selection, while the second half independently re-estimates risk at the chosen λ, restoring exact validity via conditional exchangeability. The full procedure (Algorithm 1) is computationally efficient—requiring only empirical loss averages plus a constant adjustment—and applies broadly to any robust optimization problem where the inner problem admits tractable solutions.

## Results

Across four optimization settings (linear programming, newsvendor, portfolio optimization, shortest path), CREME achieves approximately 100% validity (the proportion of trials where both miscoverage and regret estimates upper-bound the true risk) while maintaining competitive accuracy, outperforming the naive Monte Carlo estimator which trades validity for accuracy. In the decision quality evaluation, CREME selects robustness parameters λ̂ that closely approximate the true optimal λ* under multiple preference weightings, whereas all three baselines (parametric quantile, empirical quantile, conformal prediction) select overly conservative parameters yielding high regret. The approximation gap between the estimated and true Pareto frontier ranges from 0.04 to 0.19 depending on the setting, and runtime peaks at approximately 4.5 seconds (portfolio optimization, |Λ|=30, n=30), with the newsvendor problem solving in near-zero time due to closed-form solutions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CREME against four baselines. Against the Monte Carlo (empirical average) estimator in the validity-accuracy analysis (Figure 3), CREME dominates: it achieves ~100% validity across all settings while the Monte Carlo estimator sacrifices validity for accuracy, positioning it toward the lower-left of the validity-accuracy plot. Against the three robustness-selection baselines in the decision quality evaluation (Figure 4) — parametric quantile (assuming Gaussian Y), empirical quantile (95th percentile of |y_i − ȳ|), and conformal prediction (Patel et al., 2024b, constructing 95%-valid ℓ∞-ball sets) — CREME consistently selects λ̂ values much closer to the true Pareto-optimal λ*. The three baselines all target a fixed 5% miscoverage level and thus myopically select overly conservative robustness parameters, resulting in tradeoff points with low miscoverage but substantially higher regret compared to CREME's balanced selections. This gap is most pronounced under preference weightings that value regret (w₂ > 0), where the baselines' fixed-miscoverage strategy is particularly suboptimal. Only under the extreme weighting w₁=1, w₂=0 (pure miscoverage minimization) do the baselines perform comparably to CREME, which is expected since that is their design objective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper inverts the logic of conformal risk control (CRC; Angelopoulos et al., 2022): rather than prescribing a target risk level and searching for a set that satisfies it, CREME takes a fixed uncertainty set (indexed by robustness parameter λ) and certifies the actual miscoverage and regret it incurs. This inverse perspective is embedded within the predict-then-optimize (PTO) framework (Bertsimas & Kallus, 2020) and the broader robust optimization literature, where uncertainty sets are typically specified a priori with heuristically chosen radii. The paper also draws on conformal prediction's exchangeability-based guarantees, Pareto-front analysis from multi-objective optimization, and data-splitting strategies for post-hoc selection bias (Cox, 1975; Rasines & Young, 2023), but to the authors' knowledge, no prior work has constructed a certified Pareto frontier with explicit finite-sample guarantees for robust optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CREME provides the first principled, distribution-free methodology for calibrating robustness levels in optimization, replacing ad hoc heuristics (e.g., fixing 95% confidence) with a data-driven Pareto frontier that decision-makers can interrogate against their cost-risk preferences. Key limitations include the requirement of a known loss upper bound B (though experiments show sample-maximum approximations work in practice), the sample-efficiency cost of data splitting for post-hoc validity, and the exchangeability assumption that precludes time-series or non-stationary settings. Open questions include extending to non-exchangeable data (e.g., via weighted conformal estimators), handling solver suboptimality gaps rigorously, and developing algorithms that avoid the bounded-loss assumption entirely.
