## Problem

Existing multi-bit text watermarking methods for LLMs conflate message decoding with watermark detection, causing ECC-based extractors to map unwatermarked text to valid codewords and produce catastrophic false positive rates (FPR > 0.9). Applying rejection thresholds to these extractors merely collapses true positive rate to random guessing, revealing a structural flaw in decoding-centric designs that cannot be remedied by simple threshold tuning.

## Input / Output

During generation, the method consumes LLM logits and a secret key K to produce watermarked text, embedding one designated BCH codeword per block of n=31 tokens via logit biasing (soft) or hard masking. At detection, it takes candidate text tokens, the shared key, code parameters (n, k, t), a window-shift budget s_max, and a match-ratio threshold θ, and outputs a binary watermark-presence decision along with the recovered multi-bit payload m ∈ {0,1}^k. The detection is model-agnostic, operating exclusively on output logits independent of architecture or model size.

## Method

BREW (Block-wise Reliable Embedding for Watermarking) shifts the paradigm from decoding-centric extraction to designated-codeword verification. Embedding partitions the vocabulary per block using a cryptographic hash of the key and block index, then biases logits toward the target list to embed one BCH(31,6,7) codeword per 31-token block. Detection proceeds in two stages: (i) blind message estimation via majority voting across independently decoded blocks, and (ii) window-shifting verification that, for each global offset s ∈ [−s_max, s_max], reconstructs the designated codeword for each block and checks exact match rather than accepting any valid codeword within the correction radius. This restricted acceptance space—only the single keyed target codeword per block—is what suppresses false positives to ~0.02 while maintaining TPR of 0.965 under 10% synonym substitution. The sliding-window search over linear offsets recovers synchronization after insertion/deletion-induced drift, and the aggregate decision uses a match-ratio threshold over M independent blocks, yielding exponential FPR suppression in both block length n and number of blocks M.

## Results

Under 10% synonym substitution on OPT-1.3B with T=200 tokens, BREW achieves TPR = 0.965 with FPR = 0.02, demonstrating strong detection sensitivity while maintaining near-zero false positives. In clean (no-attack) settings at δ=3.0 and T=200, BREW (soft) achieves TPR = 0.987 and FPR = 0.013, whereas the naive any-codeword variant of the same embedding reaches TPR = 1.000 but FPR = 0.499. Under paraphrasing attacks, BREW maintains a message-level exact match rate of 0.760 while keeping FPR in the low regime, compared to near-random discrimination for Qu et al. (2025).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BREW decisively outperforms both baselines on the FPR metric, which is the paper's central contribution. Under 10% synonym substitution, Qu et al. (2025) exhibits FPR > 0.90 (approaching 0.97 in some settings) with TPR ≈ 0.97, meaning it misclassifies nearly all unwatermarked text as watermarked; the paper's ROC analysis (Section 5.3.3) shows that no rejection threshold can fix this, as TPR and FPR increase linearly together along the random-guess diagonal. MPAC (Yoo et al., 2024) achieves near-perfect TPR but with FPR ≈ 0.50, still far above BREW's 0.02. BREW's advantage is structural rather than a tuning artifact: the designated-codeword verification restricts acceptance to a single keyed target per block, while window-shifting recovers alignment after insertion/deletion. Under paraphrasing attacks, the ordering is consistent—BREW maintains low FPR while MPAC shows elevated FPR and Qu et al. approaches random guessing. In text quality (Table 5), BREW (soft) and Qu et al. are broadly comparable, while MPAC degrades more rapidly at higher δ; BREW's hard variant incurs the largest quality loss (PPL 30.75 vs. 15.51 unwatermarked).

## Relation to Prior Work

BREW builds directly on the KGW green/red vocabulary partition for embedding and on the ECC-based multi-bit watermarking line (Reed-Solomon in Qu et al. 2025, LDPC in Chao et al. 2024), but departs from them at the detection stage. Where prior ECC methods treat detection as nearest-codeword decoding—accepting any valid codeword within the correction radius—BREW introduces a two-stage protocol that first blindly estimates the payload via majority voting and then verifies that each block's recovered codeword matches the single designated codeword assigned by the secret key. The window-shifting synchronization mechanism extends the static block-slicing assumption of prior work to handle linear token-level desynchronization from insertions and deletions, a setting the paper argues is underexplored in existing evaluation protocols.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a USENIX Security 2025 ECC-based watermark (Qu et al.) exhibits FPR > 0.9 under modest synonym substitution, and that no rejection threshold can rescue it (TPR and FPR track the random-guess diagonal), reframes a practical deployment blocker as a structural design flaw rather than a tuning issue. This opens a clear path for forensic-grade multi-bit watermarking where false accusations of AI authorship carry real consequences in legal and academic settings. Key limitations remain: the fixed-range window search does not handle accumulated drift in very long texts, the designated-verification design trades raw payload capacity for FPR control, and all experiments are on models up to 7B parameters, leaving open whether the TPR-FPR trade-off holds at 70B+ scale where higher token entropy may shift the operating point.
