## Problem

Neural operators must capture long-range spatial dependencies in PDE solutions, but full attention scales quadratically with point count. Existing efficient alternatives either rely on fixed analytical bases (Fourier, Laplace eigenfunctions) that require specific discretizations, or use non-standard aggregation and normalization modules (e.g., Transolver's slice re-normalization) that complicate implementation, hinder hardware-optimized kernel usage, and cause instability under mixed-precision training. The paper asks whether a low-rank global mixing structure can be realized using only standard Transformer primitives.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265440/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an unordered point set of N spatial coordinates and associated PDE input features (diffusion coefficients, boundary shapes, previous velocity/vorticity states, temperature fields, etc.), lifted into a d-dimensional feature space via Fourier positional encoding and a point-wise FFN. It outputs the PDE solution field (e.g., pressure, velocity, stress, Mach number) at the same N points, with no assumption of grid or mesh connectivity. Benchmarks span resolutions from ~1k to ~512k points across regular grids, structured meshes, and irregular point clouds.

## Method

LRSA instantiates the low-rank factorization K ≈ UGV^T with three stages built entirely from standard scaled dot-product attention, normalization, and FFNs: (1) Compression — cross-attention from M learnable latent queries to N spatial points produces M latent tokens; (2) Latent mixing — a standard Transformer block (self-attention + two FFNs) processes the M tokens to model global correlations in the compressed subspace; (3) Reconstruction — a second cross-attention from N points to M latents broadcasts refined context back to spatial points. Critically, compression and reconstruction use independent attention projections (decoupled U and V), avoiding the symmetric weight-tying constraint in Transolver. Because all operations are standard SDPA calls, LRSA directly leverages FlashAttention-2 fused kernels, achieving near-linear O(NM + M²) complexity and robust FP16/BF16 training without custom normalization or slice-aggregation modules.

## Results

On six standard PDE benchmarks (Airfoil, Pipe, Plasticity, Navier-Stokes, Darcy, Elasticity), LRSA achieves the best relative L2 error across all tasks, with headline values of 0.0042 (Airfoil), 0.0023 (Pipe), 0.0005 (Plasticity), 0.0484 (Navier-Stokes), 0.0044 (Darcy), and 0.0033 (Elasticity). The paper reports an average error reduction of over 17% relative to the second-best method. On irregular-domain benchmarks, LRSA achieves 4.89e-3 on Pipe Turbulence (vs. 8.26e-3 for HPM) and 1.49e-4 on Heat Transfer. On industrial-scale cases (~32k points), LRSA reduces AirfRANS surface-field error to 0.0010 (vs. 0.0077 for LinearNO) while maintaining a Spearman rank correlation of 0.9997 on lift coefficient.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LRSA outperforms all 15 baselines on the six standard benchmarks. The largest margins appear on Navier-Stokes (0.0484 vs. 0.0699 for LinearNO, a 31% reduction) and Elasticity (0.0033 vs. 0.0050 for LinearNO, a 34% reduction), where complex dynamics and irregular geometry stress-test global coupling. Among spectral methods, FNO and F-FNO are limited to regular grids and show substantially higher errors (e.g., 0.2231 on Navier-Stokes for F-FNO); HPM, which uses Laplace-Beltrami eigenfunctions, is the strongest spectral baseline but is outperformed by LRSA on all applicable tasks. Among attention-based methods, Transolver and Transolver++ rely on explicit slice normalization that causes divergence under FP16/BF16, while LinearNO (which removes the latent self-attention) is the closest competitor but still trails LRSA by 10–34% across benchmarks. On irregular domains, LRSA beats NORM and HPM on Pipe Turbulence (4.89e-3 vs. 8.26e-3) and Heat Transfer (1.49e-4 vs. 1.84e-4), though it is marginally behind HPM on Irregular Darcy (7.56e-3 vs. 7.36e-3). On industrial-scale cases (~32k points), LRSA achieves the lowest surface-field error on AirfRANS (0.0010 vs. 0.0077 for LinearNO) and the best volume error on ShapeNet Car (0.0166 vs. 0.0194 for LinearNO), while matching the best drag-coefficient error (0.0106) and rank correlation (0.9925).

## Relation to Prior Work

LRSA unifies spectral/basis operators (FNO, NORM, HPM), linear-attention variants (Galerkin Transformer, OFormer), and latent-token architectures (Transolver, LinearNO, Perceiver IO) under a single low-rank factorization template K ≈ UGVᵀ, showing they all instantiate a compress–process–reconstruct pattern with different choices of U, V, and G. The key departure from Transolver and its successors is the use of two independent cross-attentions (decoupled compression and reconstruction) and a full latent self-attention block, all expressed purely through standard scaled dot-product attention, LayerNorm, and FFNs—eliminating the custom slicing matrices, Gumbel-Softmax sampling, and explicit re-normalization steps that prior latent-token methods require. This design choice is not merely architectural but engineering-motivated: it makes the block directly compatible with FlashAttention-2 and other fused kernels, which the specialized aggregation pipelines cannot leverage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for anyone building or deploying neural operators: by restricting to standard Transformer primitives, LRSA inherits the numerical robustness of fused attention kernels, enabling stable FP16/BF16 training where Transolver diverges, and achieving 3.2× lower latency and 2.1× lower peak memory on 32k-point geometries. The unified low-rank lens also clarifies the design space, suggesting that the effective rank of PDE interaction kernels is typically small (M ≈ 32–64 suffices), which guides capacity allocation. A stated limitation is that the fixed latent bottleneck can under-resolve sharply localized structures (boundary layers, thin interfaces), and the paper leaves open how to adaptively allocate capacity between latent processing and point-wise updates as resolution and task complexity grow.
