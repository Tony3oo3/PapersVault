## Problem

Existing algorithm discovery benchmarks (MLE-Bench, MLGym-Bench, MLAgent-Bench) are limited to 7–75 manually created tasks, lack a principled meta-train/meta-test split, are vulnerable to data contamination, and contain saturated or overly similar problems. These structural flaws prevent both fair evaluation and the large-scale optimisation of algorithm discovery agents (ADAs), since there are too few diverse tasks to learn from without overfitting.

## Input / Output

DiscoGen consumes a small configuration file specifying the task domain, which datasets are assigned to meta-train vs. meta-test, which of the domain's modules are editable, the backend (e.g., feed-forward vs. recurrent), the evaluation type (performance, energy, or time), and the initialisation mode (empty interface vs. baseline implementation). It produces a containerised JAX codebase in which only the designated editable modules (e.g., loss function, network architecture, optimiser) contain logic to be discovered, while all other files are fixed and overwritten before meta-testing to prevent evaluation hacking. The two-stage generation—meta-train codebase created first, meta-test codebase only after the meta-loop completes—ensures the ADA never sees meta-test dataset details during discovery.

## Method

DiscoGen procedurally generates algorithm discovery tasks by combinatorially sampling over seven axes per domain: m editable modules, d datasets (each assigned to meta-train, meta-test, or unused), b backends, 2 initialisation modes, and 3 evaluation types, yielding N = 2·3·b·(2^m − 1)·(3^d − 2^(d+1) + 1) tasks per domain and ~100 billion tasks across 14 ML subfields (on-policy RL, off-policy RL, multi-agent RL, computer vision, language modelling, Bayesian optimisation, etc.). DiscoBench, a hand-curated evaluation subset, contains m+1 tasks per domain (one per single editable module plus one all-modules task) with fixed pseudo-random meta-train/meta-test splits. For ADA optimisation, the paper demonstrates prompt tuning where a separate LLM (Claude Sonnet 4.5) iteratively updates the ADA's system prompt based on meta-test performance over K randomly sampled DiscoGen tasks, showing that increasing K from 1 to 30 monotonically improves both in-distribution and held-out-domain Elo scores. The approach is validated by evaluating three open-source LLMs (DeepSeek-V3.2, Devstral2, GPT-OSS-120B) as ReAct-based ADAs, with Elo ratings computed from same-dataset comparisons and a failure penalty for invalid submissions.

## Results

On DiscoBench (Single Edit), Deepseek-v3.2 achieves the highest success rate (71.3%) and best aggregate Elo (Meta-Train 1031, Meta-Test 998), while GPT-OSS-120B and Devstral2 trail at 59.5% and 55.2% success respectively. Critically, no ADA consistently outperforms the All-Fixed baseline (Elo 1111/1133 on Single Edit), and success rates drop sharply in the All Edit setting (e.g., GPT-OSS-120B falls to 17.8%). In the prompt-optimisation experiment (Table 4), increasing the number of DiscoGen tasks used for optimisation from K=1 to K=30 raises meta-test Elo from 965 to 1067 on in-distribution domains and from 983 to 1024 on held-out domains, confirming that broader task exposure improves generalisation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between three LLM-based ADAs (Deepseek-v3.2, Devstral2, GPT-OSS-120B) and an All-Fixed baseline (the codebase with no editable modules, representing the best 'do nothing' strategy). No ADA consistently outperforms the baseline: on Single Edit, the baseline's Meta-Test Elo (1133) exceeds all three ADAs (944–999), and on All Edit the gap widens dramatically (baseline 1388 vs. 595–1010). Deepseek-v3.2 is the strongest ADA, achieving 71.3% success on Single Edit and 40.5% on All Edit, but still falls short of the baseline in Elo. Among the ADAs, Devstral2 shows an unusual pattern where its All Edit Meta-Test Elo (1010) exceeds its Meta-Train Elo (790), while the other models show the opposite (meta-overfitting). The prompt-optimisation experiment (Table 4) further shows that optimising over more DiscoGen tasks (K=30 vs. K=1) improves meta-test Elo by ~100 points in-distribution and ~40 points on held-out domains, whereas meta-train Elo shows no consistent trend—demonstrating that conventional meta-train evaluation would misrepresent ADA progress.

## Relation to Prior Work

DiscoGen transplants the procedural content generation (PCG) paradigm from reinforcement learning (Cobbe et al., 2020; Stooke et al., 2021; Matthews et al., 2025) into the algorithm discovery setting, where prior benchmarks (MLGym-Bench, MLE-Bench, MLAgent-Bench) are small, manually curated task suites of 7–75 problems. The key conceptual departure is replacing fixed task lists with a combinatorial, parameterised generator that yields ~100 billion tasks across 14 ML domains, while introducing a principled meta-train/meta-test split absent from existing ADA evaluations. The paper also positions itself within the broader automated research and meta-learning literature (Schmidhuber, 1987; Lu et al., 2024; Romera-Paredes, 2024), but its contribution is infrastructure rather than a new discovery method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a fundamental bottleneck for algorithm discovery research: the inability to optimise and evaluate ADAs over diverse, non-contaminated task distributions. DiscoGen addresses this by providing a smooth difficulty spectrum (via module count, dataset selection, and initialisation) that enables autocurricula, weight-based ADA training, and open-ended learning—research directions previously infeasible with 13–75 fixed tasks. However, current ADAs still struggle substantially (success rates of 40–71%, frequent failure to beat baselines), and the prompt-tuning demonstration, while clean, is a limited form of optimisation; weight-based or scaffold-based ADA training remains an open and resource-intensive challenge. The fixed DiscoBench evaluation set also faces the same contamination risk as any public benchmark, motivating the proposed private API.
