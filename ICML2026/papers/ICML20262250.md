## Problem

End-to-end spreadsheet generation by LLMs is a high-dimensional, open-ended task where quality depends on functional correctness, structural organization, domain-specific conventions, and user-facing presentation, yet no existing benchmark captures this multi-faceted evaluation. Programmatic verification (common in code or table generation) is insufficient because spreadsheet utility involves interactivity, layout, and domain norms that resist single-pass execution checks. The paper asks: what drives user preferences in spreadsheet generation, how do these preferences decompose across observable features and domains, and do crowd preferences align with expert domain standards?

## Input / Output

Users submit natural-language prompts spanning six categories (Academic & Research, Corporate Finance & FP&A, Creative & Generative, Operations & Supply Chain, Professional Finance, SMB & Personal), and 16 LLMs each produce a single-pass JSON workbook conforming to the SheetSpec@2 schema, which specifies cell content (text, numbers, Excel A1 formulas), multi-sheet structure, cell styling (fills, fonts, borders, number formats), named ranges, and conditional formatting rules. These JSON artifacts are rendered deterministically client-side via SpreadJS, and users cast blind pairwise votes (prefer A, prefer B, tie, or both unsatisfactory) over four battles per prompt. The platform outputs Bradley-Terry Elo rankings, feature-adjusted rankings, per-domain coefficient tables, a multi-label failure taxonomy, and expert rubric scores for finance-domain spreadsheets.

## Method

SPREADSHEETARENA is a live arena platform where users submit prompts and vote on blind pairwise battles between LLM-generated workbooks, with matches generated by a weighted sampling algorithm (Algorithm 1) that prioritizes under-represented model pairs. Rankings are computed via the Bradley-Terry model (GPT-4o anchored at Elo 1000), then decomposed using a feature-augmented BT model (Equation 1) with 29 programmatically extracted covariates spanning formula quality, content composition, formatting, and spatial structure, allowing separation of model identity from observable output features. Domain-specific re-estimation across prompt categories reveals substantial coefficient sign reversals (e.g., borders penalized in Creative but rewarded in Finance). A data-driven failure taxonomy is constructed by clustering LLM-judge loss rationales via BERTopic/HDBSCAN into 7 co-occurring diagnostic tags, validated against expert annotation (78% primary-failure agreement). A blinded expert study with five finance-modeling professionals applies a 6-dimension rubric to 50 spreadsheets from 25 arena battles, revealing only 56–66% alignment between crowd preferences and professional standards, with color-coding conventions as the weakest dimension (M=1.97/5).

## Results

Across 4,357 blind pairwise votes over 16 LLMs, Claude Opus 4.5 achieves the highest baseline Elo (1550, anchored to GPT-4o at 1000), followed by Claude Sonnet 4.5 (1427) and Claude Opus 4.1 (1406). After adjusting for 29 observable spreadsheet features (16 statistically significant), the leaderboard compresses substantially: Claude Opus 4.5 drops 217 Elo points to 1333, while weaker models like Qwen3-30B (+157) and Llama-4-Maverick (+151) gain the most, and Gemini 3 Pro ascends from 4th to 2nd place. In a blinded expert evaluation of 50 finance-domain spreadsheets, the mean rating is only 2.86/5, with just 25.6% of evaluations scoring ≥4 and experts willing to circulate only 16.1% to a client; expert-arena alignment is moderate at 56% (66.4% in a follow-up pairwise study).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper evaluating 16 LLMs rather than comparing a single method against baselines, so the key comparisons are between evaluation perspectives. In baseline arena rankings, Claude models dominate the top three positions, but after controlling for observable features (text density, background fills, numeric content, formula error rate, layout width), their advantage shrinks dramatically—Claude Opus 4.5's average win probability drops by 11.2 percentage points—while Gemini 3 Pro (only −56 Elo) and weaker open models (Qwen3-30B +157, Llama-4-Maverick +151) gain the most, suggesting Claude's baseline edge is partly attributable to formatting tendencies that align with crowd preferences. Domain-specific analyses (p < 10⁻⁶⁸ likelihood ratio test) reveal that significant features and their signs reverse across categories: e.g., borders are penalized in Creative & Generative (β=−0.87) but rewarded in Finance (+0.57) and Operations (+0.71), and finance_color_convention is significant only in Finance (+1.63). Crowd-sourced arena preferences align with expert finance-domain judgments in only 56% of cases (66.4% in a pairwise follow-up), with inter-rater reliability low (Krippendorff's α = 0.28–0.45), indicating that generalized user preferences do not fully capture professional modeling standards. Failure-mode analysis shows that numerical correctness and structural integrity failures are largely invisible to arena voters (correlation r ≈ 0 with win rate) while presentation and prompt-alignment failures are strongly punished (r = −0.38 to −0.81).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the arena-style blind pairwise evaluation paradigm from Chatbot Arena (Chiang et al., 2024) and SEAL Showdown (Scale AI, 2025) to the domain of end-to-end spreadsheet workbook generation, adapting the feature-augmented Bradley-Terry model (Cattelan, 2012) with 29 spreadsheet-specific covariates. It departs from prior spreadsheet work (SpreadsheetBench, SheetCopilot, SheetRM), which assumes manipulation tasks with programmatically verifiable gold answers, by framing the task as open-ended full-workbook synthesis evaluated via holistic human preference. The finance-domain expert evaluation study introduces a complementary normative axis (FAST Standard color conventions, one-row-one-formula rule) that prior preference-based benchmarks have not addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that formatting and structural features (text density, background fills, borders) dominate preference signals while formula sophistication features show no significant effect has direct implications for post-training with preference data on structured artifacts: naive pairwise votes may reward surface polish over substantive correctness. The substantial divergence between crowd preferences and expert finance judgments (56% agreement; only 16.1% of spreadsheets rated client-ready) highlights that arena-style evaluation may systematically under-reward domain-specific best practices. Open questions include whether iterative or agentic generation paradigms would shift the feature-preference landscape, and how to design preference data that uniformly rewards functional, structural, and domain-specific criteria simultaneously.
