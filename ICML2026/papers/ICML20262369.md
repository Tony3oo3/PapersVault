## Problem

Naive LLM-as-a-judge evaluation scores (the fraction of responses judged 'correct') are systematically biased because LLM judges have imperfect sensitivity and specificity, causing overestimation at low true accuracies and underestimation at high accuracies. Existing bias-correction methods such as Prediction-Powered Inference can themselves become biased when the test and calibration datasets differ in their true-label distributions—a common scenario in practice. The paper asks how to report LLM-as-a-judge evaluations with a bias-corrected point estimate, statistically valid confidence intervals, and robustness to distribution shift.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two datasets: (1) a test set of size n with LLM-judged binary labels (no human labels needed, so n can be very large at negligible cost), and (2) a calibration set of size m where each instance has both a human ground-truth label and the LLM's judgment, used to estimate the judge's sensitivity q₁ and specificity q₀. It outputs a bias-corrected point estimate θ̂ of the true human-evaluated accuracy, a (1−α) confidence interval that accounts for variance from both the test and calibration sets, and an optimal asymmetric allocation (m₀, m₁) of calibration samples across the two true-label types to minimize interval length under a fixed calibration budget.

## Method

The core estimator is the Rogan–Gladen misclassification adjustment θ̂ = (p̂ + q̂₀ − 1)/(q̂₀ + q̂₁ − 1), which inverts the relationship between the observed LLM-judged fraction p̂ and the true accuracy θ given the judge's sensitivity and specificity. Uncertainty is quantified via a delta-method variance that decomposes contributions from the test set (through p̂) and the calibration set (through q̂₀ and q̂₁), with the 'add two successes and two failures' adjusted Wald correction for finite-sample coverage. An adaptive allocation strategy (Algorithm 1) collects a small pilot calibration set, estimates the error ratio κ = (1−q̂₀)/(1−q̂₁), and allocates calibration samples asymmetrically as m₀ ∝ (1/p̂ − 1)√κ · m₁ to minimize the confidence-interval length under a fixed budget. Crucially, the estimator only requires invariance of the judge's conditional behavior Pr(Ẑ|Z) across test and calibration distributions, not invariance of the marginal label distribution Pr(Z), which makes it unbiased under distribution shift where PPI and conditional-calibration estimators fail.

## Results

On Chatbot Arena with GPT-4.1-mini as judge, the bias-corrected estimator θ̂ reduces win-rate bias toward zero for all six models, while the naive estimator p̂ exhibits non-negligible bias. The proposed 95% confidence intervals achieve empirical coverage of 95–99% across models (Table 1), with CI lengths ranging from 0.178 (Vicuna) to 0.267 (LLaMA). Under distribution shift (θ_Q varying from 0.25 to 0.75 while θ_P = 0.5), the misclassification-adjusted estimator remains unbiased, whereas the PPI difference estimator and conditional calibration estimator both exhibit growing bias. For ranking recovery on Chatbot Arena, the corrected estimator improves exact ranking recovery from 0% to 37% (Gemini-3-Flash) and from 7% to 40% (Claude-Haiku-4.5) over 100 random splits.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the naive LLM-judgment fraction p̂, which the paper shows is systematically biased: it overestimates θ when θ is low and underestimates it when θ is high. The bias-corrected estimator θ̂ reduces bias toward zero across all six Chatbot Arena models and all three additional judges (GPT-4.1, Gemini-3-Flash, Claude-Haiku-4.5). Against the PPI difference estimator (Angelopoulos et al., 2023a) and the conditional calibration estimator (Kloos et al., 2021; Meertens et al., 2022), the proposed misclassification-adjusted estimator is the only one that remains unbiased under distribution shift between test and calibration datasets (Figure 8), while both alternatives exhibit growing bias as θ_Q diverges from θ_P. On ranking recovery, the corrected estimator substantially outperforms the naive estimator for weaker judges (Gemini-3-Flash: 0%→37% exact recovery; Claude-Haiku-4.5: 7%→40%), though for the strongest judge (GPT-4.1) the naive estimator already achieves 86% exact recovery and the correction introduces additional variance from the limited calibration set, yielding 46%—a case where increasing calibration to 40% closes the gap (75% vs. 61%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the classical Rogan-Gladen (1978) misclassification-adjusted prevalence estimator and the Lang & Reiczigel (2014) confidence interval construction to the LLM-as-a-judge evaluation setting, where the LLM judge's sensitivity and specificity play the role of test accuracy parameters. It departs from the more recent Prediction-Powered Inference (Angelopoulos et al., 2023) and conditional calibration estimators (Kloos et al., 2021; Meertens et al., 2022) by showing that those methods become biased under distribution shift between test and calibration datasets, whereas the Rogan-Gladen-based estimator remains unbiased under the milder assumption that only the judge's conditional behavior Pr(Ẑ|Z) is invariant. The paper further contributes an adaptive calibration allocation strategy (Algorithm 1) and a characterization of parameter regimes where calibrated LLM evaluation achieves lower variance than human-only evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since LLM-as-a-judge has become the dominant scalable evaluation paradigm, the finding that naive win-rate reporting is systematically biased (positive at low accuracies, negative at high accuracies) and that existing correction methods can fail under realistic distribution shift has direct practical consequences for how model comparisons are reported. A key limitation is that, on Chatbot Arena, none of the current LLM judges evaluated yet fall in the regime where calibrated LLM evaluation beats human-only evaluation in variance, though stronger judges like Claude-Opus-4.6 approach the boundary. Open questions include extending the framework to multi-category evaluations with structured confusion matrices, accounting for nuisance factors (response length, style) that may violate the judge-invariance assumption, and combining the post-hoc correction with judge-level debiasing methods.
