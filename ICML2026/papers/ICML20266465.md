## Problem

Current benchmarking practices in deep learning for time series forecasting compare complex architectures without factoring out fundamental design dimensions—such as whether a model is global, local, or hybrid, whether exogenous variables are included, and how spatial dependencies are handled. This leads to contradictory empirical results and misattribution of performance gains to sequence modeling operators when they actually stem from overlooked implementation choices, ultimately hindering meaningful progress in the field.

## Input / Output

The evaluated architectures consume a window of W past observations (typically 96 steps, sometimes 336) from each time series in a collection of N synchronous series, optionally augmented with exogenous variables (e.g., sinusoidal calendar features) concatenated to the input. The output is a multi-step point forecast of H future observations (96, 192, 336, or 720 steps ahead) per series. A critical detail is that the paper standardizes all inputs across compared models—same exogenous variables, same preprocessing (RevInv normalization), same hyperparameter tuning protocol—to isolate the effect of individual design dimensions rather than conflating them.

## Method

The paper conducts a controlled empirical study that decomposes forecasting architecture design into four orthogonal dimensions (model configuration, preprocessing/exogenous variables, temporal processing, spatial processing) and quantifies each dimension's individual contribution to performance. Streamlined reference architectures (MLP, TCN, RNN, Transformer, Pyraformer) with modular temporal and optional spatial attention components are built and compared against SOTA models (PatchTST, DLinear, TimeMixer, iTransformer, Crossformer, ModernTCN) under identical standardized conditions on four benchmarks (Electricity, Weather, Traffic, Solar). Key findings include: switching between global and hybrid (local-parameter) configurations can produce larger performance swings than changing the temporal operator; adding or removing exogenous variables shifts results comparably; and simple reference architectures properly configured as hybrid global-local with covariates match or exceed SOTA performance. The paper further proposes a 'forecasting model card' template that requires authors to explicitly document all four design dimensions, enabling fairer future comparisons and more interpretable ablation studies.

## Results

On four standard long-range forecasting benchmarks (Electricity, Weather, Traffic, Solar) with a 96-step horizon, simple reference architectures (MLP, TCN, Transformer, Pyraformer) configured as hybrid global-local models with exogenous inputs achieve MSE within 0.001–0.02 of SOTA models like PatchTST and TimeMixer across all datasets. For example, on Electricity, the reference Transformer achieves MSE 0.129 vs. PatchTST's 0.125, while on Solar, Pyraformer (0.189) actually outperforms PatchTST (0.197). DLinear is consistently the weakest baseline (e.g., 0.246 MSE on Solar vs. 0.189 for Pyraformer). The paper's central empirical claim is that no sequence-modeling operator consistently dominates once model configuration, exogenous inputs, and spatial processing are held constant.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PatchTST achieves the best MSE on Electricity (0.125) and Traffic (0.345), but the gap over reference architectures is small (≤0.004 on Electricity, ≤0.017 on Traffic), and Pyraformer actually beats PatchTST on Solar (0.189 vs. 0.197). DLinear is the weakest model across all four datasets (e.g., 0.246 MSE on Solar vs. 0.189 for Pyraformer), confirming that its apparent competitiveness in prior literature stems from uncontrolled design differences rather than the linear operator itself. TimeMixer is competitive with reference architectures but does not clearly outperform them on any dataset. In the spatial-processing comparison (Table 4), removing the spatial attention layer from iTransformer yields equal or better MSE on all four datasets, suggesting the spatial component does not contribute meaningfully in this setting. Crossformer and ModernTCN are competitive with reference architectures plus spatial attention but do not consistently dominate. Notably, the local OLS Linear model achieves the best Weather MSE (0.144), outperforming all deep architectures, which the authors attribute to Weather being a heterogeneous multivariate series where per-series local parameters are most appropriate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the dominant trend of proposing increasingly complex sequence modeling architectures (PatchTST, iTransformer, Crossformer, TimeMixer) and instead critiques the evaluation methodology that has produced contradictory empirical results in the field. It reframes the 'simple models work well' findings of Zeng et al. (2023) and Toner and Darlow (2024) not as evidence against deep learning, but as symptoms of benchmarking practices that conflate fundamentally different design dimensions (global vs. local vs. hybrid configuration, exogenous variable usage, spatial processing). Conceptually, it draws on the forecasting community's well-established taxonomy of global, local, and hybrid models (Montero-Manso and Hyndman, 2021; Januschowski et al., 2020) and on the model card framework of Mitchell et al. (2019) to propose a structured reporting template specific to time series forecasting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete, actionable gap: current benchmarks cannot attribute performance gains to specific architectural components because they fail to factor out differences in model configuration, preprocessing, and spatial processing. The proposed forecasting model card offers a practical, low-cost standard that could immediately improve transparency and comparability across the field. Open questions include whether the four identified design dimensions suffice for foundation models and probabilistic forecasting, and whether synthetic benchmarks can effectively isolate individual component contributions. The analysis is limited to four standard datasets and a specific set of architectures, so generalizability to other domains and model families remains to be established.
