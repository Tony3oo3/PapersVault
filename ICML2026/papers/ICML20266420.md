## Problem

The scaling law era has entrenched a centralized ML ecosystem with three structural frictions: uncompensated data scraping that creates a bilateral oligopoly in training data, privacy risks from centralized data collection and model memorization, and systemic financial risk from concentrated computing power demand. The paper argues that federated learning's original 2017 proposition of cooperation, privacy, and decentralization remains a relevant design lens for imagining alternative, more democratized ML ecosystems, even after the dominance of foundation models and scaling-law-driven research.

## Input / Output

In the FL framework analyzed, clients hold disjoint local datasets (consumer data on mobile devices, institutional data in healthcare or finance) and receive model parameters from a central aggregation server. Clients train locally and return model updates rather than raw data, which the server aggregates into a global model. The paper's key market-level distinction is that in an FL-mediated ecosystem, the traded commodity becomes model updates rather than raw data, which regularizes pricing because updates are a proxy for both data quality and compute contribution, reducing the information asymmetry that currently disadvantages small data owners in negotiations with large model builders.

## Method

As a position paper, the core contribution is an argumentative framework mapping FL's structural properties onto three market failures in the current ML economy. For data, FL's requirement of active client participation creates natural consent mechanisms and transforms the traded good from raw data to model updates, enabling transparent price discovery in what the paper characterizes as a bilateral oligopoly dominated by a few large buyers and sellers. For privacy, FL's data locality combined with secure aggregation, homomorphic encryption, and user-level differential privacy provides layered protections more compatible with emerging regulation (GDPR, HIPAA) than centralized training, and the paper extends this to federated social platforms like Mastodon. For compute, FL's distributed training shifts computation to clients, reducing direct demand from model builders and alleviating the systemic risk of an over-leveraged oligopoly in computing hardware. The paper also presents and rebuts two counterarguments: that FL has limited applicability and remains subject to platform economics, and that scaling law incentives make FL economically uncompetitive in the near term.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not report quantitative experimental results. The authors make three qualitative arguments: (1) FL can facilitate a fairer data market by making model updates (rather than raw data) the traded commodity, reducing price discrimination; (2) FL's data-locality design, combined with secure aggregation, homomorphic encryption, and differential privacy, provides natural privacy safeguards relevant to regulated industries; and (3) FL's distributed training architecture reduces concentrated compute demand from model builders, mitigating systemic risk in the computing hardware market. No benchmarks, accuracy metrics, or performance numbers are presented.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare FL against alternative training paradigms (e.g., centralized training, split learning, or other distributed methods) on any empirical benchmark. The authors do reference DiLoCo (Douillard et al., 2024) and Charles et al. (2025) as FL-inspired distributed training approaches that have moved beyond the federated setting, and they cite Kuo et al. (2025) to highlight a gap between academic FL research and cross-silo industry practice. However, no head-to-head performance comparisons are conducted. The paper's 'comparisons' are conceptual: FL is contrasted with the current centralized, scaling-law-driven ML ecosystem in terms of data consent, privacy guarantees, and compute market structure, but no quantitative evidence is provided to support these contrasts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper repurposes the federated learning paradigm (originally proposed by McMahan et al., 2017 for privacy-preserving distributed training) as an economic and systems-level argument for a more democratized ML ecosystem, rather than contributing new FL algorithms. It positions itself alongside recent position papers on ML economics (Santy et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2025; Vincent et al., 2025) and sustainability-oriented alternatives to scaling (McCoy et al., 2025; Baek et al., 2025), but distinguishes itself by grounding its argument in the concrete technical affordances of FL—client-side training, model-update trading, and distributed compute—rather than in abstract policy prescriptions. The paper draws on the FL practice literature (Daly et al., 2024; Kuo et al., 2025) and the foundation-model/scaling-law literature (Kaplan et al., 2020; Brown et al., 2020) to argue that FL's original decentralization and privacy motivations remain relevant and can be extended to address data-market oligopoly and computing-resource concentration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper articulates a concrete research agenda—participation incentives, cross-silo FL, device utilization, and consent-aware model design—that could guide ML systems research toward addressing systemic economic tensions (data consent, privacy, compute concentration) that the scaling-law paradigm has intensified. Its main limitation is that it is entirely argumentative: the economic claims (e.g., that model-update trading regularizes data pricing, or that FL reduces systemic risk in computing markets) are plausible but unvalidated, and the paper itself concedes significant counterpoints about FL's limited applicability and the entrenched incentives favoring centralized training. Open questions include whether FL can scale beyond regulated verticals (healthcare, finance) to general-purpose model training, whether peer-to-peer FL variants can truly displace the central-server bottleneck, and whether the economic arguments survive contact with the current momentum of foundation-model development.
