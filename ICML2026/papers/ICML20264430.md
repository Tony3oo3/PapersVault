## Problem

Existing graph-transport methods either produce static couplings that do not yield executable time-indexed policies, or rely on global time-expanded solvers that are computationally intractable on large sparse graphs. No data-driven framework existed for learning controlled continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) policies on arbitrary graphs that simultaneously match endpoint marginals and optimize intermediate trajectory behavior under state- and distribution-dependent running costs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264430/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a fixed directed graph G=(X,E) with reference CTMC transition rates r_t, source and target distributions (p_0, p_1) over the node set, and a running cost f_t(x, p_t) that may depend on both the current state and the time-t marginal distribution. It outputs controlled transition rates u_t(y,x) = r_t(y,x)·exp(Z_t(y,x)) defined only on existing edges, which constitute an executable routing policy: each particle at node x samples its next state from at most Δ neighbors (the local branching factor), making the per-step cost independent of total graph size. Unlike static OT plans, the output is a trajectory-level stochastic policy whose rollouts respect the graph topology at every step.

## Method

GSBoG formulates graph transport as a generalized Schrödinger bridge over CTMC path measures, deriving a dual representation (Theorem 3.1) that shows optimal controlled rates take the multiplicative form u_t*(y,x) = r_t(y,x)·exp(−V_t(y)+V_t(x)), and a discrete Hopf-Cole transform yielding coupled ODEs for forward/backward Schrödinger potentials. For training, the paper uses Dynkin's formula (the discrete analogue of Itô calculus) to express the evolution of log-potentials along sampled CTMC trajectories via the controlled generator, producing a gIPF loss for endpoint marginal matching. A critical observation is that the running cost cancels in the IPF objective (entering with opposite signs in the forward and backward generators), so a separate temporal-difference loss penalizing violations of local generator consistency is introduced to enforce cost-aware dynamics. Training alternates between forward rollouts (sampling from p_0, updating the backward policy) and backward rollouts (sampling from p_1, updating the forward policy), minimizing the combined IPF + λ_TD·TD objective. This particle-based, trajectory-level formulation avoids global time-expanded solves, scaling with local degree rather than graph size.

## Results

On the 9,559-node supply-chain graph, GSBoG achieves near-optimal terminal matching (TV = 0.03, tied with W1flow) while reducing mean congestion of the top-100 nodes to 21.13 (vs. 178.19 for W1flow and 84.09 for Attr. Flow) and peak node occupancy to 271.49 (vs. 1807.66 and 1033.72). GSBoG is the only method whose induced trajectories remain within edge capacities (max flow/capacity = 0.64 vs. 1.41 and 1.59). On the Chignolin protein-folding MSM (500 states), GSBoG with a free-energy running cost achieves a 99.36% fold rate with an energy barrier of 1.39 k_BT_sim, compared to 25.11% and 5.43 k_BT_sim for GrSB and 0.55% and 6.94 k_BT_sim for the uncontrolled CTMC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GSBoG is compared against three baselines: the dynamical graph Schrödinger Bridge (GrSB; Chow et al., 2022), an Attraction-Flow heuristic, and a dynamic W1 minimum-cost flow (W1flow). On the large-scale supply-chain graph, GrSB fails entirely due to memory exhaustion, confirming its poor scalability on sparse networks with ~10⁴ nodes. Among the methods that complete, GSBoG matches W1flow on terminal TV (0.03) but reduces mean congestion by 8.4× (21.13 vs. 178.19) and peak occupancy by 6.7× (271.49 vs. 1807.66), while also being the only method that stays within edge capacities (0.64 vs. 1.41). Attr. Flow achieves the worst terminal matching (TV = 0.27) and still incurs 4× higher congestion than GSBoG. On the Chignolin folding task, GSBoG (f≠0) achieves a 99.36% fold rate with a 1.39 k_BT_sim barrier, outperforming GrSB (25.11%, 5.43), Committor-guided bias (71.69%, 6.59), and Attr. Flow (62.08%, 7.32) by a wide margin on both metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

GSBoG extends the generalized Schrödinger bridge (GSB) framework from continuous state spaces (Liu et al., 2022a; Chen et al., 2021) to discrete graph-structured domains, replacing Itô-calculus-based FBSDE representations with Dynkin's formula for CTMCs. The closest prior work is the dynamical Schrödinger bridge on graphs (Chow et al., 2022), which solves global time-expanded Hamiltonian flows over the probability simplex; GSBoG departs by adopting a particle-based, data-driven formulation that avoids these global solvers. The paper also distinguishes itself from recent discrete diffusion/SB methods (Kim et al., 2024; Ksenofontov & Korotin, 2025), which target graph generation rather than distributional transport on a fixed topology.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is scalability: by operating on local graph neighborhoods rather than the full time-expanded graph, GSBoG handles 9,559-node supply-chain networks where the global GrSB solver exhausts memory, and scales to 10^6 nodes. The ability to incorporate state- and distribution-dependent running costs (congestion, free energy) while learning executable trajectory-level policies opens applications in logistics, molecular kinetics, and networked control that static OT couplings cannot address. A notable limitation is the assumption of a fixed, fully known graph topology; extending to time-varying, partially observed, or dynamically evolving connectivity remains open. The implicit satisfaction of capacity constraints without hard enforcement is an encouraging but not yet formally guaranteed property.
