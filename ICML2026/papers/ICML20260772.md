## Problem

When a black-box predictor estimates stochastic edge weights for a matching problem, the standard optimal algorithm (e.g., Edmonds) applied to the predicted weights can be arbitrarily worse than alternative decision rules, even when the predictor is unbiased. The paper asks whether the predictor can be modified ex-post so that simply running the standard optimal matching algorithm on the adjusted predictions is competitive with the best decision rule in a given class, without knowing the underlying distribution or how the predictor was trained.

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite class C of matching algorithms, a black-box predictor γ: X → [0,1]^m (one predicted weight per edge), and i.i.d. samples from the joint distribution D over (context x, true edge weights y). It produces a multicalibrated predictor γ̂ such that the max-weight matching computed by the optimal algorithm c* on γ̂(x) achieves expected weight within an additive ε of the best algorithm in C applied to γ(x). The predictor is vector-valued with one component per edge, and the multicalibration weight class W is constructed from indicator functions of edges selected by each c ∈ C on γ plus the self-referential indicator for edges selected by c* on γ̂ itself.

## Method

The core mechanism is weighted multicalibration (adapted from Gopalan et al. 2022b to vector-valued regression): a predictor γ̂ is (W, α)-multicalibrated if for every weight function w ∈ W, the expected inner product ⟨w(γ̂(x), x), y − γ̂(x)⟩ is bounded by √d·α. The weight class W = W₁ ∪ {w*} encodes two conditions: W₁ ensures γ̂ is unbiased on edges selected by each c ∈ C on the original γ, while w* enforces self-consistency on edges selected by c* on γ̂ itself. Algorithm 1 implements this via iterative boosting: a CHECK routine identifies multicalibration violations, and a projected gradient descent step (with step size η = α/2) updates γ̂, with convergence guaranteed by a potential argument on the MSE between γ̂ and the Bayes predictor. The worst-case sample complexity is Õ(n^5/ε^4) with disjoint data per CHECK call, improvable to Õ(n^{2.5}√r/ε^3) using adaptive data analysis with the exponential mechanism, where r is the initial MSE of γ.

## Results

The main theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3.1) shows that a (W, α)-multicalibrated predictor ŷ with α = ε/(2√m) ensures the max-weight matching on ŷ is within an additive ε of the best algorithm in class C applied to the original predictor γ. The sample complexity is Õ(n⁵/ε⁴) in the worst case (Theorem 3.3), improvable to Õ(n^{2.5}√r·log(n|C|/ε)·log^{3/2}(n/εδ)/ε³) via adaptive data analysis. Empirically, on synthetic Max Matching (m=45 edges, n=10 nodes), ε=1/4 achieves a positive utility gap at 2⁸·1024 samples, versus a theoretical worst-case bound of at least 10⁵·2⁸·log(1024) — hundreds of times more. For Best Action with m=256 and ε=1/16, convergence is reached in 1024 iterations versus a worst-case bound of ~260× that.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods; instead, it benchmarks the proposed multicalibration pipeline (arg max matching on ŷ) against two internal references: (1) the best decision rule c* ∈ C applied to the original predictor γ, and (2) the naive arg max on γ. The theoretical guarantee ensures the multicalibrated pipeline is within ε of the best-in-C benchmark, while the naive arg max can be arbitrarily worse (as shown by the ε-arms example in the introduction). Empirically, once CHECK estimates are reliable, the utility gap (arg max on ŷ minus best-in-C on γ) becomes positive rather than merely non-negative, meaning the multicalibrated predictor can strictly outperform the best rule in C. The paper also notes that the empirical sample complexity is substantially lower than the worst-case theoretical bound — by factors of hundreds to thousands — because the initial predictor's MSE is well below the worst-case r = O(n²) and CHECK identifies larger-than-minimal violations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes multicalibration—a notion originating in algorithmic fairness (Hebert-Johnson et al., 2018)—as a post-processing tool for combinatorial optimization with predictions. It adapts the weighted multicalibration framework of Gopalan et al. (2022b) from classification to vector-valued regression, and draws on adaptive data analysis techniques (Dwork et al., 2015; Bassily et al., 2021) to control sample complexity. The work is positioned as orthogonal to Zhao et al. (2021), who show argmax is the only rational decision rule after calibration, and to Kiyani et al. (2026), who derive explicit label-modification formulas under specific assumptions; here, the protected sets are defined by the predictor itself and the algorithm class, yielding a guarantee competitive with the best policy in a given family rather than a minimax-optimal single rule.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a principled, modular alternative to the common industry practice of patching optimization pipelines with ad-hoc IF-THEN heuristics: a developer can simply add a heuristic to the algorithm class C during preprocessing, and the standard solver on the multicalibrated predictor is guaranteed to be competitive. Key limitations include the requirement of a finite algorithm class, polynomial (and often loose) sample complexity in the worst case, and validation only on synthetic data with small graphs (n=10). Open questions include whether the approach scales to large-scale matching instances, whether infinite or structured algorithm classes can be handled, and whether the gap between worst-case and practical sample complexity persists on real-world data distributions.
