## Problem

State-of-the-art video diffusion models produce visually compelling frames but frequently violate basic physical motion laws, exhibiting rubber-sheet deformations, periodic flicker, and inconsistent zooms or rotations. Existing remedies—optical-flow warping losses, 3D/geometry conditioning, hand-crafted physics penalties, and test-time smoothing—are either domain-specific, computationally heavy, or fail to change the motion the model actually learns. The paper asks whether a simple, architecture-agnostic, training-time regularizer can enforce physically plausible motion without modifying the diffusion backbone.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt and produces a generated video through a standard text-to-video diffusion pipeline (Open-Sora, MVDIT, HunyuanVideo, or Wan 2.1-14B). During training, the physics loss is evaluated on the predicted clean sample x̂₀ at each diffusion step, computed over a low-frequency 3D FFT cube (per-dimension cutoff ϱ=0.3, retaining ~97.5% of spectral energy) with a Hann-windowed temporal segment of K=16 frames. The regularizer is a pure training-time addition; inference compute and the backbone architecture remain unchanged.

## Method

The core contribution is a frequency-domain physics prior grounded in SIM(2) spectral geometry: constant-velocity translation concentrates energy on an affine plane in (ωx, ωy, ωt), rigid rotation produces tilted lines ωt + mΩ = 0 in the (m, ωt) plane with annular spatial concentration, and uniform scaling induces radial energy flow with tilted lines ωt + αν = 0 in the (ν, ωt) plane. These three signatures are turned into lightweight, differentiable spectral losses—weighted ridge plane-fitting for translation, a tilted-line energy ratio plus ring-entropy concentration for rotation, and a radial-gradient alignment score plus centroid-trend correlation for scaling. An adaptive softmax weighting (temperature τ) automatically emphasizes the motion type most supported in the current clip while remaining sensitive to mixtures, avoiding hard classification. The composite motion loss is added to the standard denoising objective with a linear timestep decay (w = 1 − t/T_diff) to downweight unreliable high-noise predictions, and the entire pipeline runs in FP32 with a 10–20% wall-clock overhead over the backbone forward pass.

## Results

On OpenVID-1M, the proposed frequency-domain motion regularizer improves Action Recognition by +8.94 points (60.77→69.71) and Motion Accuracy by +5.00 points (44.00→49.00) on Open-Sora, with Warping Error reduced by 37% (0.0089→0.0056). On MVDIT, gains are +7.36 in Action Recognition and +7.00 in Motion Accuracy, with Warping Error reduced by 22.5%. On the PhyGenBench physics benchmark, the average score rises from 0.44 to 0.52 (Open-Sora) and from 0.548 to 0.602 (Wan 2.1-14B), while user studies show 74–83% preference for physics-enhanced videos across all tested backbones.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method is evaluated against four video diffusion backbones (Open-Sora, MVDIT, HunyuanVideo, Wan 2.1-14B) and one zero-shot method (MotionCraft). Against Open-Sora and MVDIT, the proposed loss wins on every reported metric, with the largest gains in Action Recognition (+8.94 and +7.36 points) and Warping Error (−37% and −22.5%). On HunyuanVideo (LoRA fine-tuning), the method outperforms both a base-LoRA baseline and a flow-based temporal-consistency reference, improving Action Recognition from 68.93 to 73.15 and Warping Error from 0.0024 to 0.0016. On Wan 2.1-14B, Visual Quality jumps from 54.18 to 62.65 and PhyGenBench average from 0.548 to 0.602. Against MotionCraft (a zero-shot latent-warping method), the proposed training-time regularizer achieves better scores on all four shared metrics (e.g., Motion Consistency 0.9468 vs. 0.8563, Warping Error 0.0045 vs. 0.0104), though the comparison is acknowledged as not directly comparable due to different settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper grounds its loss design in the classical SIM(2) spectral framework (Sharma & Duits, 2015) and the frequency-domain motion signatures established by Adelson & Bergen (1985), Simoncelli & Heeger (1998), and Bracewell (1956), which characterize translation, rotation, and scaling as simple geometric structures in the spatiotemporal spectrum. It departs from prior physics-informed video generation approaches—flow/warping consistency, 3D-aware conditioning, hand-crafted physics rules, and test-time refinements—by introducing a differentiable, architecture-agnostic spectral regularizer applied during diffusion training. Unlike MotionCraft (zero-shot latent warping) or PhysGen (scene-specific simulation), the method requires no external flow estimation, no scene decomposition, and no backbone modification, positioning it as a lightweight drop-in training objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in providing a simple, globally-computed spectral regularizer that consistently improves motion plausibility across four architecturally distinct diffusion backbones without changing inference cost, making it an attractive add-on for existing video generation pipelines. The finding that 60.5% of OpenVID-1M clips are SIM(2)-approximable validates the relevance of the targeted motion class, while the component ablation revealing distinct category-wise dependencies (Mechanics→translation, Optics→rotation, Thermal→scaling) offers a useful diagnostic lens. Key limitations include the restriction to projected SIM(2) apparent motions (no elastic deformation, articulated movement, or fluid dynamics), and the method's reliance on a fixed temporal window and low-pass cutoff that may not capture very fast or very slow motions optimally. Extending the framework to richer motion groups (SE(3), projective transforms) or learned spectral priors is a natural next step.
