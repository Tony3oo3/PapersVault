## Problem

Conventional graph-based brain network models capture only pairwise interactions between directly connected ROIs, missing the group-wise high-order dependencies that characterize neurodegenerative pathology. Existing hypergraph approaches either rely on predefined hyperedge structures or restrict learning to scalar hyperedge weights, limiting their ability to adaptively discover multi-resolution structural patterns. The paper asks how to dynamically learn continuous, scale-aware hyperedges that explicitly model high-order ROI interactions across multiple spatial resolutions for disease staging.

## Input / Output

The method takes a brain graph with an adjacency matrix (DTI-derived structural connectivity for ADNI with 160 Destrieux-atlas ROIs, or fMRI functional connectivity for PPMI with 116 AAL-atlas ROIs) and per-ROI node features (SUVR from FDG/amyloid/tau PET, cortical thickness from MRI, or mean BOLD signals). It outputs a disease-stage label (5-way for ADNI, 3-way for PPMI), a set of learned continuous incidence matrices H_s (one per scale, sparsified via TopK to η neighbors per node), and per-ROI hyperedge activation scores used for interpretability. The wavelet scales s_j and hyperedge projector Φ are jointly optimized as learnable parameters during training.

## Method

MuHL couples three modules: (1) Adaptive Multi-Scale Feature Filtering applies learnable graph wavelet kernels (one low-pass, J−1 band-pass) at trainable scales s_j to decompose node features into multi-resolution representations X_sj; (2) Multi-Scale Hypergraph Structure Learning maps each X_sj through a linear embedding and a shared learnable projector Φ to produce a continuous incidence matrix H_sj = X̄_sj Φ, normalized via ReLU+SoftMax and sparsified by TopK(η); (3) a Multi-Scale Transformer performs scale-wise hypergraph convolution (Z layers) followed by Scale-Wise Self-Attention (SWSA), where each attention head is dedicated to one scale, enabling cross-scale context aggregation. The key theoretical contribution (Propositions 1–2) shows H_s = ψ_s W_X(s) Φ, i.e., the incidence matrix is a spectral projection of wavelet coefficients, and proves a dilation property: as the wavelet scale grows, hyperedge cardinality monotonically increases toward N. Training minimizes cross-entropy with an L1 penalty on negative scales, jointly optimizing s_j, Φ, and all transformer parameters.

## Results

On the ADNI 5-class staging task (CN/SMC/EMCI/LMCI/AD), MuHL achieves 93.2% accuracy, 95.4% precision, 94.2% recall, and 94.7% F1-score, outperforming the second-best method (ALTER) by 2.4%p in accuracy and 3.8%p in F1-score. On the PPMI 3-class task (CN/Prodromal/PD), MuHL reaches 76.8% accuracy, 66.6% precision, 60.7% recall, and 62.4% F1-score, surpassing the best baseline (BQN) by 3.9%p in accuracy and 6.0%p in F1-score. In zero-shot cross-dataset transfer (e.g., PPMI→TaoWu), MuHL gains 7.7%p in precision and 5.5%p in recall over the second-best method, demonstrating robust generalizability across acquisition phases and independently collected cohorts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MuHL outperforms all 19 baselines on both ADNI and PPMI across every metric. On ADNI, the closest competitors are ALTER (90.8% Acc) among graph-based methods and dwHGCN (90.2% Acc) among hypergraph-based methods; MuHL leads by 2.4%p and 3.0%p in accuracy respectively, with a particularly large 4.6%p recall gain over ALTER. On PPMI, BQN (71.9% Acc) and UniGCNII (70.8% Acc) are the strongest baselines; MuHL surpasses them by 3.9%p and 6.0%p in accuracy, with the recall advantage (60.7% vs. 51.0% for BQN) being clinically significant for reducing missed diagnoses. In zero-shot transfer (Table 2), MuHL leads the second-best method by up to 7.7%p in precision (PPMI→TaoWu) and 6.0%p in F1-score (ADNI-2→ADNI-1/3/GO), indicating that the learned multi-scale hypergraph structure captures transferable disease-relevant representations. Notably, MuHL also achieves the best performance on the ABIDE autism dataset (63.7% Acc vs. 60.0% for UniGCNII), confirming generality beyond neurodegenerative disorders.

## Relation to Prior Work

MuHL sits at the intersection of two research threads: spectral graph wavelet transforms (Hammond et al., 2011) for multi-resolution signal decomposition, and hypergraph neural networks (Feng et al., 2019) for modeling group-wise interactions. It departs from prior hypergraph-based brain network methods (dwHGCN, HyBRiD) that either use predefined hyperedge structures or only learn scalar weights over fixed hyperedges, by instead constructing continuous, scale-aware incidence matrices directly from wavelet-filtered node features. The key conceptual departure is the theoretical equivalence (Proposition 1) showing that hyperedge construction is a projection of graph wavelet coefficients, which unifies multi-scale filtering and high-order structure learning into a single spectral framework rather than treating them as disjoint modules.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine limitation in brain network analysis: pairwise graph models struggle to capture the group-wise dependencies that underlie neurodegenerative pathology, and the learned hyperedges provide interpretable ROI groupings (e.g., bilateral subcortical hubs in AD/PD) that align with known neuroanatomy. The zero-shot transfer across acquisition phases and independent cohorts suggests the learned multi-scale hypergraph encodes transferable disease-related structure rather than dataset-specific artifacts. However, the ADNI dataset is small and heavily imbalanced (12 AD cases), the identified ROIs are correlational rather than causal, and it remains open whether the multi-scale dilation property generalizes to other graph domains beyond brain networks or whether the gains over strong single-scale baselines would persist at larger scale.
