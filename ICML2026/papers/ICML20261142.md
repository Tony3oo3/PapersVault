## Problem

While discriminative networks have the Neural Anisotropy Directions (NADs) framework to characterize architectural directional biases, generative modeling lacks a unified theory explaining how network geometry interacts with data manifolds to determine generalization. The paper asks: among equidimensional data manifolds, which directions are preferentially captured by score-based generative models, and can this be predicted from the architecture alone before training?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261142/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a neural network architecture at its default initialization (parameters sampled from the initialization distribution) and a probing distribution P (default: x=0 with σ drawn uniformly over the diffusion noise schedule) as input. It produces the average geometry matrix G_F ∈ R^{D×D} computed by averaging F_θ(x,σ)F_θ(x,σ)ᵀ over 1M randomly initialized networks, whose eigendecomposition yields the Score Anisotropy Directions (SADs) ordered by ascending eigenvalue. For a given data distribution p, it further outputs an alignment score α = E[zᵀG_F z] (where z = Wx for an orthogonal W) and the extreme-alignment matrices W_min, W_max that minimize or maximize α, enabling controlled (mis)alignment of data with the architecture.

## Method

The core insight is that a score network F_θ implicitly defines a log-density q_{θ,σ}, and directional derivatives of this density reveal which directions are easy to model: small changes in log-density along a direction indicate travel along the data manifold. Applying Markov's inequality to the probability of crossing the manifold yields the bound P(|vᵀ∇log q| ≥ γ) ≤ vᵀG_F v / γ², where G_F is the average geometry, establishing that small-eigenvalue eigenvectors of G_F are inherently easier to model. For linear networks, Theorem 1 proves that SGD convergence rates depend on eigenvalues of ΦΦᵀ, with stochasticity (not deterministic drift) being the key mechanism that amplifies anisotropic conditioning. Theorem 2 then shows that extreme data-geometry alignment is achieved by specific orthogonal matrices W_min = UJVᵀ and W_max = UVᵀ derived from eigendecompositions of G_F and the data covariance, enabling a controlled experiment on real datasets. The framework is validated on rank-one Gaussian datasets (16×16) and standard benchmarks (MNIST, CelebA-HQ, CIFAR-10) using iDDPM U-Net and DiT architectures, with performance measured via Sliced Wasserstein distances, confirming that minimizing α consistently improves generation quality.

## Results

On MNIST, minimizing the alignment score α with the architecture's geometry (α≈53) yields MSW₂≈0.61, a dramatic improvement over the default alignment (α≈168k, MSW₂≈8.78) and the maximized alignment (α≈211k, MSW₂≈8.52). The paper reports consistent SW₂ improvements across MNIST, CelebA-HQ, and CIFAR-10 when data is transformed to minimize α, with the default alignment already showing significant artifacts (e.g., a considerable fraction of MNIST samples lacking a digit). On rank-one 16×16 Gaussian datasets, eigenvectors of the average geometry G_F corresponding to small eigenvalues consistently achieve the lowest MSW₂, validating Conjecture 1 for both iDDPM U-Net and DiT architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing generative methods; instead, it compares three data–geometry alignment settings (minimize α, default, maximize α) for the same iDDPM U-Net architecture on identical datasets. The key finding is that minimizing α via the orthogonal transform W_min (Theorem 2) substantially outperforms both the default and maximized alignments on all three benchmarks (MNIST, CelebA-HQ, CIFAR-10), as measured by SW₂. The default alignment (W=I) already performs poorly relative to the minimized case, suggesting that natural image data is generally misaligned with the architecture's output geometry. Interestingly, maximizing α does not degrade performance further beyond the default on MNIST (8.52 vs. 8.78), but introduces mode collapse. The paper also contrasts its unified SAD framework with prior architecture-specific analyses (GAHBs for CNNs by Kadkhodaie et al., attention-map analysis for transformers by An et al.), arguing that SADs provide a more general, architecture-agnostic characterization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Neural Anisotropy Directions (NADs) framework from Ortiz-Jimenez et al. (2020), which characterized directional biases in discriminative classifiers, to the generative/score-based setting by introducing Score Anisotropy Directions (SADs). It departs from the Geometry-Adaptive Harmonic Bases (GAHBs) theory of Kadkhodaie et al. (2024), which was specific to convolutional diffusion models and lacked a mathematically precise definition, by providing an architecture-agnostic, data-independent characterization via the average geometry matrix. The work also addresses a gap identified by An et al. (2025), who found no systematic regularities in transformer-based diffusion models, by showing that the SAD framework unifies both CNN and transformer architectures under a single geometric lens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The SAD framework offers a principled, pre-training method to predict which data directions a given architecture will model well, with potential applications to AutoML, neural architecture search, and diagnosing memorization/hallucination in generative models. A notable open question is the apparent contrast with the discriminative NAD setting, where alignment with large eigenvalues is beneficial rather than detrimental, suggesting a fundamental asymmetry between generative and discriminative inductive biases. Limitations include small-scale experiments (up to 56×56), isolation of purely architectural biases (excluding regularization and data-dependent effects), and no validation at foundation-model scale, leaving the practical impact at scale uncertain.
