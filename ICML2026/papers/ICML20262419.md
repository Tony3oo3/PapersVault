## Problem

Diffusion policies for robotic manipulation require up to 100 denoising steps for high-quality action generation, creating inference latency that precludes real-time closed-loop control on resource-constrained platforms. Existing acceleration strategies—numerical solvers, direct prediction, and action reuse—each enforce only one dimension of consistency (spatial or temporal) during warm-start initialization, leading to degraded action quality or instability under rapid state changes. No prior method jointly preserves both spatial alignment with the state-conditioned action distribution and temporal smoothness across successive control steps while maintaining the generative expressiveness of the original diffusion policy.

## Input / Output

At each control step, the method receives a current observation (RGB image or proprioceptive state) and the cached action sequence from the previous timestep (a chunk of H=16 actions, with 8 executed per loop). The lightweight predictor outputs a predicted action sequence that serves as a warm-start initialization for the diffusion reverse process at an intermediate step K′ (typically 1–4), after which only K′ denoising steps produce the final continuous action sequence. The predictor is a 2-block cross-attention Transformer with 128-dimensional embeddings and ~0.98M parameters, adding only ~2 ms of latency on an RTX 4090, and the full pipeline achieves ~20 ms end-to-end inference on an RTX 2050 edge GPU.

## Method

STEP introduces a spatiotemporal consistency prediction mechanism where a lightweight Transformer predictor (2 cross-attention blocks) maps the current observation and previous action sequence to a warm-start action in a residual action space, ensuring the initialization is both distributionally close to the target action manifold (spatial consistency) and temporally smooth (temporal consistency). This warm-start replaces the standard Gaussian noise initialization, allowing the diffusion reverse process to begin at an intermediate step K′ rather than step K, reducing inference to as few as 2 denoising steps without retraining or modifying the original diffusion policy. A velocity-aware perturbation injection mechanism monitors action variation between consecutive timesteps and injects bounded actuation excitation only when execution stagnation is detected, preventing deadlock in real-world deployment. The paper further proves that the warm-start induces a locally contractive mapping on the reverse diffusion process (with contraction coefficient c_k < 1 verified empirically across all tasks), guaranteeing exponential decay of initialization errors and stable convergence even with aggressive step reduction.

## Results

On the RoboMimic state-based benchmark with 2 denoising steps, STEP achieves an average success rate of 0.84 across six tasks (Push-T 0.49, Lift 1.00, Transport 0.86, Can 0.98, Square 0.96, ToolHang 0.64), outperforming BRIDGER (avg 0.62) by 21.6% and DDIM (avg 0.39) by 45%. On real-world PickNPlace and StackCube tasks, STEP with 2 steps achieves 92% and 81% success rates respectively, compared to DDIM's 58% and 60%, a 27.5% average improvement, while reducing inference latency from 4229 ms (DDPM-100) to 20 ms. The method also matches vanilla DDPM (100 steps) performance on RoboMimic with only 2 steps, and reduces average episode execution time by 59% via velocity-aware perturbation injection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DDIM (2 steps), STEP achieves substantially higher success rates on nearly all tasks: Transport 0.86 vs 0.04, ToolHang 0.64 vs 0.06, Push-T 0.49 vs 0.29, and Can 0.98 vs 0.32, while matching on Square (0.96 vs 0.84) and exceeding on Lift (1.00 vs 0.80). Against BRIDGER (2 steps), STEP wins on Push-T (0.49 vs 0.37), Transport (0.86 vs 0.46), Square (0.96 vs 0.84), and ToolHang (0.64 vs 0.08), with ties on Lift and Can. Against DPM-Solver++ (2 steps), STEP dominates on Transport (0.86 vs 0.00), Can (0.98 vs 0.00), and ToolHang (0.64 vs 0.00), where the solver completely fails. Against action-reuse methods RTI-DP and Falcon (2 steps), STEP achieves higher scores on Transport (0.86 vs 0.50/0.00) and ToolHang (0.64 vs 0.34/0.00) with lower inference latency (18–19 ms vs 128–140 ms for RTI-DP). On ManiSkill2, STEP (2 steps) matches or exceeds RNR-DP on StackCube (0.96 vs 0.91) while achieving 14× lower latency (11 ms vs 160 ms), though RNR-DP edges ahead on TurnFaucet (0.22 vs 0.20) and PushChair (0.45 vs 0.39). On real-world tasks, STEP (2 steps) achieves 92%/81% success vs DDIM's 58%/60%, confirming consistent gains in the low-step regime where baselines collapse.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2025) and positions itself against three established acceleration paradigms: numerical solvers (DDIM, DPM-Solver++), distillation/direct prediction (BRIDGER, CP, OneDP), and action reuse (RTI-DP, Falcon, RNR-DP). Its key conceptual departure is the unifying framework of spatiotemporal consistency, arguing that prior methods enforce only temporal consistency (action reuse) or spatial consistency (noise-to-action prediction) but not both, and that a lightweight residual-space predictor can satisfy both simultaneously. The local contractivity analysis provides a convergence guarantee for the warm-started reverse process that is absent from prior acceleration methods, and the velocity-aware perturbation injection addresses a practical execution-deadlock problem that none of the compared baselines consider.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STEP's practical significance lies in enabling 2-step diffusion policy inference on edge hardware (RTX 2050, 25–35 W) with near-full success rates, making real-time closed-loop robotic control feasible on resource-constrained platforms. The method is orthogonal to flow-matching approaches (FlowPolicy, MP1) and can be layered on top of them with only ~2 ms overhead, broadening its applicability. Limitations include the need to train a task-specific predictor (albeit only 0.98 M parameters) and the theoretical contractivity guarantee relying on Lipschitz assumptions verified empirically rather than guaranteed a priori. Open questions include whether the spatiotemporal predictor generalizes across tasks without retraining and how the approach scales to higher-dimensional action spaces or multi-robot settings.
