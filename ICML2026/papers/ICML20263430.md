## Problem

Backdoor defenses for time series forecasting (TSF) remain largely unexplored, and existing classification-oriented defenses fail in TSF due to two inherent challenges: channel-level signal dilution (backdoors poison only a subset of channels, undermining sample-level filtering) and training-loss degeneration (continuous regression objectives plus overlapping windows collapse the loss separation between poisoned and clean samples). This paper provides the first systematic evaluation of 13 representative defenses across the TSF lifecycle and identifies these two failure modes as the root causes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multivariate time series training set of shape T×C (T time steps, C channels) with sliding-window pairs of history (L_in) and future (L_out) segments, potentially containing injected trigger and target patterns on a subset of channels and timestamps. It outputs a trained forecasting model f_θ that maps history windows to future predictions, along with a per-channel, per-timestamp binary mask m_{t,c} indicating which time-channel units are included in the reliable training pool. No additional clean reference data is required, and the defense incurs no inference-time overhead beyond standard forward passes.

## Method

TIMEGUARD refactors multivariate TSF training from sample-level to channel-wise (time × channel) granularity, maintaining a per-channel reliable pool via a binary mask. Stage I initializes a high-precision pool by intersecting two complementary criteria: Reverse-Consistency Filtering (RCF), which trains an auxiliary backcaster and selects samples with low reverse-reconstruction loss to exploit the temporal asymmetry of backdoor dependencies, and Neighborhood Diversity Filtering (NDF), which uses a Gaussian-weighted Pearson-correlation distance to prioritize temporally diverse samples, motivated by a Nadaraya-Watson kernel bound showing successful backdoors induce tight clusters of highly similar poisoned inputs. Stage II progressively expands the pool via Distance-Regularized Loss Selection (DRLS), which admits only the lowest-loss samples from a diversity-filtered candidate set, preventing re-inclusion of correlated poisoned windows that become indistinguishable under loss-only criteria as training proceeds. The forecaster is trained throughout with a masked empirical loss over the current reliable pool, with the pool expansion ratio scheduled linearly from α to β of the full dataset.

## Results

On PEMS03 (averaged over SimpleTM, FEDformer, TimesNet), TIMEGUARD achieves FDER of 0.868, 0.847, and 0.808 under Random, FreqBack-TSF, and BackTime attacks respectively, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline PDB (FDER 0.693, 0.652, 0.639). TIMEGUARD boosts MAE_P to 104.7, 57.8, and 39.3 (a minimum 2.76× relative gain over undefended) while keeping clean MAE_C within 5% of the undefended model. Across Weather and ETTm1, TIMEGUARD maintains FDER above 0.65 in all settings and even slightly improves clean forecasting on Weather (3.02% lower MAE_C than undefended).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TIMEGUARD decisively outperforms all 13 baselines across all three attacks. The strongest baseline, PDB (in-training, requires clean data), achieves FDER 0.693/0.652/0.639, while TIMEGUARD reaches 0.868/0.847/0.808 — a 1.96× relative MAE_P improvement over PDB on BackTime without any clean data. Pre-training defenses (Spectral, TED, TED++) and loss-based in-training defenses (ABL, ESTI) all fail with FDER near 0.5 (random chance), confirming the paper's diagnosis of signal dilution and training-loss degeneration. Post-training fine-tuning methods (Fine-tuning, Fine-pruning, NAD) provide partial mitigation (FDER 0.60–0.65) but require a verified clean subset and degrade clean MAE_C by 5–12%. Inference-time defenses (STRIP, TeCo, IBD-PSC) achieve only 0.55 AUROC / 0.56 F1 at best while imposing 4–100× inference overhead, making them impractical for real-time forecasting. Notably, TIMEGUARD is the only method that simultaneously requires no clean data, no internal feature access, no inference overhead, and incorporates time-aware design (Table 8).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes backdoor defense paradigms from classification (sample filtering, loss-based selection, fine-tuning) into the time series forecasting setting, where they fail due to channel-subset poisoning and continuous regression objectives. Its core departure is the channel-wise pool training paradigm, which reconfigures sample-level decisions into time×channel-level decisions, drawing on the BackTime attack's channel-subset structure and NTK-inspired kernel analysis to motivate neighborhood diversity. The reverse-consistency filtering idea exploits a temporal asymmetry unique to TSF backdoors (forward dependency without reverse consistency), while the distance-regularized loss selection regularizes the loss-based admission criterion that underlies methods like ABL and ESTI. The paper also introduces FDER, a forecasting-specific adaptation of the DER metric, to address 'false wins' where MAE_P rises only because overall quality degrades.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first systematic evaluation of 13 backdoor defenses across the TSF lifecycle, this paper establishes a clear taxonomy of failure modes (signal dilution, loss degeneration) that will guide future TSF security research. The channel-wise paradigm and the finding that loss-based separation fundamentally collapses under TSF's regression + overlapping-window structure are conceptual contributions that reshape how practitioners think about adapting classification defenses to forecasting. Limitations include evaluation on only three datasets, three architectures, and three attacks; the adaptive-attack setting still reduces FDER from 0.808 to 0.744; and inference-time defenses for real-time TSF deployment remain unaddressed. Open questions include whether the channel-wise approach generalizes to univariate TSF, foundation-model-based forecasters at scale, and temporally decoupled attacks (e.g., TBDA).
