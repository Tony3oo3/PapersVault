## Problem

Mechanistic interpretability has identified interpretable circuits in LLMs (induction heads, knowledge neurons, SAE features), but these analyses are static: they describe what a circuit computes at inference without revealing which training samples causally drove its formation. This gap prevents both a developmental understanding of how circuits emerge and the ability to perform targeted data-level interventions to steer or suppress specific mechanisms.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260567/cropped_page0_idx5.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained model's parameters, a target component subspace (e.g., the W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O matrices of a specific attention head), a mechanism-specific synthetic probe set (repeated sequences for induction heads, random sequences for previous-token heads), and the full training corpus within a critical formation window [t_start, t_end]. It produces per-sample influence scores restricted to the component subspace, a ranked list of top-K high-influence training samples, and (in the augmentation pipeline) synthetic data generated from LLM-extracted structural patterns. Unlike standard TDA, the influence is computed only within the unit's parameter subspace rather than the full model, and the probe function is a mechanism-specific objective (e.g., copy-target logit contribution) rather than a generic validation loss.

## Method

MDA is a three-stage framework: (1) localize the target interpretable unit via a monitoring metric μ and isolate its parameter subspace θ_sub; (2) compute unit-specific influence by replacing the standard validation loss in the influence function with a mechanism-specific probe f_probe and restricting the inverse-Hessian to θ_sub, using EK-FAC for scalable IHVP computation with a joint QK subspace approximation that captures query-key correlations; (3) causally validate via counterfactual retraining—removing or duplicating top-K high-influence samples during the formation window. For induction heads, the influence is decomposed into QK (pattern formation), V (value content), and O (output projection) blocks, with the probe measuring direct logit contribution to copy-target tokens on synthetic repeated sequences. The paper further proposes a data augmentation pipeline that uses an LLM to distill structural motifs from high-influence samples into JSON schemas and generate synthetic training data, which consistently accelerates circuit convergence across model scales.

## Results

The MDA framework causally validates that removing or duplicating ≤10% of high-influence training samples significantly modulates induction head and previous-token head emergence across four Pythia scales (14M–160M), while random interventions of equal size show no effect. The proposed mechanistic data augmentation pipeline, using synthetic patterns distilled from Pythia-14M, consistently accelerates induction head formation by +9.84% to +15.8% across all model sizes (Table 2), with the 14M-derived patterns outperforming 160M-derived patterns on the 160M model (+9.84% vs. +2.56%). MDA tracing scales sub-linearly with model parameters, requiring only 80 GPU-hours (A100) for OLMo-2-1B and 384 GPU-hours for OLMo-2-7B. General capabilities (WikiText-103 loss, PopQA NLL) show no statistically discernible degradation after MDA augmentation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external TDA or MI methods but instead validates MDA through controlled causal interventions. In the primary causal validation (Figure 2), MDA-guided data deletion (masking gradients of top-influence samples) consistently suppresses or delays induction/PT head emergence, while random deletion of the same number of samples yields negligible impact; similarly, MDA-guided augmentation accelerates the phase transition relative to random insertion baselines across all four Pythia scales. In the augmentation ablation (Figure 6b), synthetic data outperforms natural high-influence data in the small-data regime (N ≤ 50,000) due to higher 'causal density,' but natural data overtakes synthetic at N = 100,000 due to diversity exhaustion. On general capabilities (Figure 6a), MDA-augmented models closely track baseline trajectories on WikiText-103 and PopQA, confirming no distributional drift. Cross-head analysis (Figure 7) shows that top-100K samples selected by one induction head receive scores an order of magnitude higher on other induction heads than on non-induction heads, confirming mechanism-localized attribution rather than global high-loss selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the influence function framework of Koh & Liang (2017) and its scalable EK-FAC approximation (George et al., 2018; Grosse et al., 2023) from global model-level attribution to unit-level mechanistic attribution. While prior training data attribution methods quantify how samples affect overall output likelihood or general capabilities, and mechanistic interpretability work (Olsson et al., 2022; Elhage et al., 2021) characterizes circuits post-hoc, MDA bridges these two fields by computing influence scores restricted to specific parameter subspaces (e.g., a single attention head's W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O) against a mechanism-specific probe function. The paper also departs from prior developmental studies (Tigges et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2024) that rely on synthetic data or simplified settings, instead tracing circuit formation in models trained on natural, unstructured corpora.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first direct training-time causal evidence (via bidirectional data intervention) that induction heads are functionally coupled to in-context learning, moving beyond the correlational and inference-time ablation evidence in prior work. The finding that repetitive structural data (LaTeX, XML, even meaningless repeated strings) serves as the primary mechanistic catalyst for induction head formation offers a concrete, actionable lever for training data curation. Key limitations include that causal retraining experiments are restricted to Pythia models up to 160M parameters (larger-scale validation is qualitative only), and the framework's treatment of indirect effects through the computation graph remains an open approximation. The cross-scale transferability of synthetic patterns (14M-derived data outperforming 160M-derived data on the 160M model) suggests a promising but under-explored direction for lightweight-proxy-guided training of larger systems.
