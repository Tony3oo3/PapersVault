## Problem

Conformal prediction provides distribution-free coverage guarantees, but whether these procedural properties translate into equitable downstream decision-making outcomes remains unclear. Prior work focused on Equalized Coverage as a fairness criterion, yet this can produce compact, informative prediction sets for one group and large, unactionable sets for another, leading to disparate downstream impact. The paper asks which procedural CP properties actually drive substantive fairness in downstream tasks.

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a test input (image, text, audio clip, or tabular row), a base model's non-conformity score function, and a held-out calibration dataset to construct prediction sets via various CP methods. An LLM (or multimodal foundation model) then receives the input, the prediction set, and the stated coverage guarantee, generating M independent class predictions per task-treatment pair. The final output is a substantive fairness metric (maxROR) computed from a logistic GEE regression that controls for task difficulty and the LLM's adoption rate of the provided set, yielding a group-disparity measure of downstream accuracy improvement relative to a no-set control.

## Method

The paper contributes three interlocking components. First, a theoretical upper bound (Theorem 4.1) decomposes the expected set-size disparity between two protected groups under Label-Clustered CP into three interpretable terms: intra-cluster label heterogeneity, cross-cluster spread, and intra-label cross-group disparity, making explicit how the number of clusters K trades off calibration stability against label adaptivity. Second, a scalable LLM-in-the-loop evaluator replaces expensive human-subject trials: an LLM acts as the downstream decision-maker, and a logistic GEE with task-level clustering and an adoption covariate isolates the treatment-by-group interaction, from which the maxROR (maximum ratio of odds ratios across group pairs) is extracted as the substantive fairness metric. Third, the paper empirically demonstrates that Equalized Set Size, not Equalized Coverage, correlates positively with reduced maxROR, and that Label-Clustered CP (which pools calibration data across groups and calibrates shared thresholds within label clusters) achieves the best balance between prediction-set compactness and downstream equity across four modalities.

## Results

Across four datasets (FACET, BiosBias, RAVDESS, ACSIncome), Label-Clustered CP achieves the best balance between substantive fairness (lowest maxROR) and utility (accuracy improvement over control), while Backward CP attains the lowest maxROR on FACET and BiosBias but at the cost of larger prediction sets (e.g., 3.50 avg size on FACET vs. 2.92 for Label-Clustered). Mondrian and Group-Clustered CP are never optimal in maxROR and induce the most unfair downstream outcomes on FACET and RAVDESS. Critically, set size gap shows a positive correlation with maxROR (reducing it lowers unfairness), while coverage gap shows a negative correlation (reducing it increases unfairness), establishing Equalized Set Size as the key procedural lever for substantive fairness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to Marginal CP (Vovk et al., 2005), Label-Clustered CP reduces set size disparity more effectively (e.g., on ACSIncome, Mondrian inflates set size to 7.16 vs. Marginal's 5.35, while Label-Clustered maintains 5.33) and achieves lower maxROR on RAVDESS and ACSIncome. Against Mondrian CP (Vovk et al., 2003), Label-Clustered CP is substantially more fair downstream: Mondrian induces the highest disparate impact on FACET and RAVDESS because group-conditional calibration amplifies set size gaps, whereas Label-Clustered CP uses shared cluster thresholds that pool calibration data across groups. Backward CP (Gauthier et al., 2025) achieves the lowest maxROR on FACET and BiosBias but produces the largest prediction sets (e.g., 3.50 on FACET, 6.50 on ACSIncome), making it less useful as a decision aid due to lower accuracy improvement. The LLM-in-the-loop evaluator validates against prior human-in-the-loop results (Cresswell et al., 2025), reproducing the qualitative ordering that Mondrian > Marginal in disparate impact across all three overlapping datasets, confirming the evaluator's reliability as a scalable proxy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Cresswell et al. (2025), which introduced Equalized Set Size as a CP fairness notion and demonstrated via human RCTs that Mondrian CP causes greater disparate impact than Marginal CP, by replacing expensive human evaluators with a validated LLM-in-the-loop framework and adding a theoretical decomposition of set size disparity. It also builds on Ding et al. (2023)'s clustered conformal prediction, adapting the label-clustering mechanism into Label-Clustered CP and proving an upper bound on inter-group set size disparity. The key departure from prior CP fairness work (Romano et al., 2020a; Zhou & Sesia, 2024) is shifting the evaluation target from procedural properties of prediction sets (coverage parity) to substantive downstream decision equity, arguing that the standard Equalized Coverage objective is misaligned with fair outcomes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that Equalized Coverage—the field's default CP fairness metric—correlates negatively with substantive fairness across four datasets and modalities is a practical warning: practitioners optimizing coverage parity may be actively increasing downstream inequity. The LLM-in-the-loop evaluator (costing as little as $1 for 60k predictions vs. ~£1500 for human studies) makes scalable substantive fairness assessment feasible, though it remains a proxy validated only on qualitative ordering rather than absolute values. Open questions include whether the set-size-gap → substantive-fairness link is causal or confounded by adoption behavior, whether findings hold for non-LLM downstream agents, and how to jointly optimize coverage guarantees and set-size equity without sacrificing utility.
