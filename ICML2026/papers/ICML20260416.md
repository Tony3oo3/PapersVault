## Problem

Reinforcement learning with pairwise preferences has been studied primarily for finite-horizon problems, leaving open whether it remains viable in long-term (infinite-horizon) settings. Prior methods lacked guarantees that simple Markov policies perform as well as history-dependent ones, had unknown computational complexity for exact solutions, and were inefficient in practice over long time horizons. This paper introduces the Markov Decision Contest to resolve all three gaps simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260416/cropped_page8_idx9.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite decision-making environment (state set, action set, transition probabilities, initial distribution) together with a skew-symmetric Markov preference margin defined over state-action pairs, which may be nontransitive (unlike scalar rewards). The output is a stationary randomized Markov decision rule mapping each state to a distribution over actions. Convergence guarantees require the transition function to be unichain and aperiodic, ensuring well-defined occupancy measures and bounded marginal values.

## Method

The Markov Decision Contest formulates the problem as a two-player zero-sum game over the convex set of occupancy measures, and the paper proves that stationary Markov policies are optimal among all history-dependent policies (Theorem 5.6) and that exact solutions are solvable via a linear program with only |S||A| + |S| + 1 variables (Theorem 6.1). The core algorithm, Hedged Policy Iteration (HPI), applies the Hedge algorithm independently at each state to update action probabilities using preference-margin Q-values, achieving a 1/√K convergence rate. A policy-gradient reformulation makes the updates amenable to function approximation by expressing them as convex optimization over action probabilities. The deep-learning implementation replaces exact occupancy measures with sampled buffers, uses standard RL value estimation for the preference-margin cumulant, and recovers the return policy via behavior cloning on the averaged sample buffer.

## Results

The paper evaluates HPI and HPI-Clip against SPPO on 13 long-horizon tasks (11 Mujoco-v5 continuous control tasks with 2048-step horizons, and 2 nontransitive-preference tasks). Learning efficiency is measured as the Area Under the Curve (AUC) of episodic return (Mujoco-v5) or inverted optimality gap (Mujoco-NT) versus environment timesteps. In 12 out of 13 tasks, HPI's AUC confidence interval exceeds or overlaps SPPO's, and the same holds for HPI-Clip. On the Mujoco-v5 suite, HPI-Clip is significantly better than SPPO on 7/11 tasks and worse on only 1/11 (Walker2d-v5); HPI is significantly better on 6/11 and worse on 1/11 (Humanoid-v5). On both Mujoco-NT tasks, both HPI and HPI-Clip have strictly higher AUC confidence intervals than SPPO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is SPPO (Swamy et al., 2024), the most relevant prior algorithm for reinforcement learning with pairwise preferences. HPI and HPI-Clip outperform SPPO on 12/13 tasks in terms of AUC, with the sole exception being Walker2d-v5 where HPI-Clip's AUC is slightly lower. On the nontransitive-preference tasks (Reacher-NT and Walker2d-NT), both HPI and HPI-Clip strictly dominate SPPO, confirming that the advantage is not limited to transitive (reward-derived) preferences. PPO is included as a reference on the Mujoco-v5 suite (where preferences are derived from scalar rewards via Eq. 5), but it is not a direct comparison since PPO does not use pairwise preferences. An ablation (Appendix A.4) shows that SPPO's performance degrades as its 'comparison horizon' T_C increases (averaging the per-timestep cumulant over more steps), while HPI-Clip corresponds to T_C=1 and maintains strong performance, explaining the gap in long-horizon settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on reinforcement learning with pairwise preferences (Swamy et al. 2024, Chen et al. 2022, Wang et al. 2023, Gilbert et al. 2015/2016, Shani et al. 2024) from finite-horizon to infinite-horizon decision problems by introducing the Markov Decision Contest. The algorithmic core builds on the Hedge algorithm (Freund & Schapire, 1995) for policy updates and borrows the clipped surrogate objective from PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) for the HPI-Clip variant. The theoretical machinery draws heavily on classical average-reward MDP theory (Puterman, 2014), particularly occupancy measures and unichain/aperiodic conditions, but applies them to a two-player zero-sum game over state-action distributions rather than a single-agent optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper establishes that pairwise-preference RL is theoretically viable in long-term settings by proving Markov policy optimality, P-complexity, and a 1/√K convergence rate—results that were entirely absent from prior finite-horizon work. The practical implication is that agents need not store or compute history-dependent policies, and the problem is no harder than standard MDPs despite strictly more general preferences. Limitations include the unichain and aperiodic assumptions (which may be restrictive in practice), the sublinear convergence rate, and empirical validation limited to Mujoco continuous-control tasks. Open questions include whether the discounted variant (studied in the author's thesis) admits similar guarantees, and whether the approach scales to the very large state spaces common in language-model alignment.
