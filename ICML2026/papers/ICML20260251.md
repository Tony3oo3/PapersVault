## Problem

Modern recurrent sequence models (DeltaNet, Titans, Gated DeltaNet) suffer from three interrelated design limitations that degrade long-context understanding: (1) their memory updates are strictly online, optimizing only with respect to the current token rather than local context; (2) their fixed-size memory has sub-linear capacity relative to parameter count due to linear feature mappings; and (3) their memory management relies on first-order gradient descent, which converges to spurious local minima. These shortcomings manifest as poor in-context retrieval, inability to extrapolate beyond training context length, and degraded performance on recall-intensive tasks.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a text token sequence and produces standard next-token predictions, but its internal memory module operates on a sliding window of c past tokens (rather than a single token), applying polynomial feature mappings φ_p(·) to keys and queries before writing to a deep MLP memory. At inference, the fixed-size memory state is updated per token with O(1) cost, while the polynomial lifting increases the effective key dimensionality from d_k to O(d_k^p) without additional projection parameters. Hybrid variants (MAG, MAL) additionally consume local attention outputs to gate or parallelize with the recurrent memory branch.

## Method

ATLAS introduces three coupled mechanisms: (1) the Omega rule, a sliding-window update that minimizes a weighted ℓ₂ regression loss over c past tokens (generalizing the Delta rule from c=1 to c>1), enabling context-level rather than token-level memorization; (2) polynomial feature kernels of degree p on keys and queries, which provably increase memory capacity from O(d_k) to O(d_k^p) independent key-value pairs; and (3) the Muon optimizer (Newton-Schulz iterations) as the inner-loop optimizer for the deep MLP memory, approximating second-order information to escape poor local minima. The memory is a 2-layer gated MLP with residual connections, and the architecture supports parallelizable chunk-wise training. Hybrid variants integrate ATLAS with sliding window attention via Memory-As-Gate (MAG) or Memory-As-Layer (MAL) configurations, allowing the model to combine global context compression with precise local retrieval.

## Results

At 1.3B parameters trained on 100B tokens, ATLAS achieves 14.97 WikiText perplexity and 10.98 LAMBADA perplexity, outperforming all recurrent baselines including Titans (15.60/11.41) and Gated DeltaNet (16.42/12.17). The hybrid variant ATLAS++ further improves to 14.40/10.72 perplexity and 58.03% average accuracy across common-sense reasoning benchmarks. On the RULER S-NIAH long-context retrieval task, ATLAS(MAG) achieves 97.0% accuracy at 16K context length, substantially outperforming online-update baselines like DeltaNet (5.4%) and TTT (4.4%), and matching or exceeding Gated DeltaNet-H2* (95.8%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ATLAS consistently outperforms all recurrent baselines across both perplexity and accuracy metrics at both 760M and 1.3B scales. Compared to the strongest non-hybrid recurrent baseline Titans (LMM), ATLAS reduces WikiText perplexity from 15.60 to 14.97 and LAMBADA perplexity from 11.41 to 10.98, while improving average reasoning accuracy from 56.82% to 57.62%. Against Gated DeltaNet-H2* (the best hybrid recurrent baseline), ATLAS++ achieves lower perplexity (14.40 vs 15.91) and higher average accuracy (58.03% vs 56.18%). On the S-NIAH long-context retrieval task, the gap widens dramatically: at 16K context, ATLAS(MAG) scores 97.0% versus 95.8% for Gated DeltaNet-H2*, 80.2% for Titans, and only 5.4% for DeltaNet, demonstrating the critical advantage of the Omega rule's context-window memorization over online (c=1) updates. The ablation in Table 3 confirms that removing the Muon optimizer (replacing with SGD) drops average accuracy from 52.77% to 52.56% at 760M, and removing the polynomial mapping drops it to 50.57%, validating each architectural component's contribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MIRAS associative-memory framework (Behrouz et al., 2025) and the deep-memory test-time memorization paradigm introduced in Titans (Behrouz et al., 2024) by addressing three specific design limitations of modern recurrent models: the online (c=1) nature of memory updates, sub-linear memory capacity, and reliance on first-order gradient descent for the inner-loop optimizer. It introduces the Omega rule (sliding-window context memorization), polynomial feature kernels for super-linear capacity, and the Muon optimizer (Newton-Schulz iterations) as the inner-loop memory manager, combining all three into the ATLAS architecture. The connection drawn between the Omega rule and sliding window attention provides a unifying theoretical bridge between parametric recurrent models and non-parametric attention, positioning ATLAS as a parametric analogue of SWA with O(1) inference cost.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a principled decomposition of why modern RNNs struggle with long-context tasks (online updates, limited capacity, weak optimizer) and addresses each with a targeted mechanism, yielding consistent improvements over Titans, DeltaNet, and Gated DeltaNet across language modeling, reasoning, and needle-in-haystack benchmarks. The theoretical capacity bounds (O(d_k^p) with polynomial kernels, and the deep-memory capacity theorem) provide useful design guidance for future memory architectures. Open questions include whether the Newton-Schulz iterations scale efficiently at much larger model sizes (>10B), whether the approach transfers to multimodal or non-text sequences, and whether the modest perplexity gains at 760M translate to larger scales where Titans and other baselines may also improve. The paper is limited to 1.3B parameters and 100B tokens, leaving the scaling behavior of the Muon inner-loop optimizer untested at frontier scales.
