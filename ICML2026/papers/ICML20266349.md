## Problem

Most deployed RL systems follow a train-then-fix paradigm where agents stop learning after deployment, even when they continue to receive evaluative reward signals and operate in environments that drift from their training distribution. This paper argues that when a deployed agent is constrained in representational capacity or compute but still receives a reward signal (termed 'measurable deployment'), the setting is inherently a continual reinforcement learning problem, and the community should abandon periodic retraining in favor of never-ending online adaptation.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes sequential action-observation histories from the deployment environment together with an evaluative scalar reward signal, and produces a continuously updated policy mapping from the agent's compressed history representation to a distribution over actions. The empirical validation uses Sarsa(λ) on Pendulum and Rusting Pendulum environments where rewards penalize angular position, velocity, and applied torque. Production case studies span code completion (400M daily requests with acceptance-rate rewards), ridesharing matching (hundreds of millions of rider-driver matches with completion rewards), and robotic sim-to-real transfer.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a theoretical argument grounded in the history process formalism (replacing the MDP) to show that measurable deployment satisfies the CRL definition where the best agent never terminates its search over the policy set. It identifies four sources of post-deployment non-stationarity—action-induced, environment dynamics changes, evolving goals, and emergent novelty—that collectively necessitate never-ending adaptation. Empirically, the authors introduce the Rusting Pendulum environment where joint friction accumulates over a single 250k-step episode, demonstrating that a Sarsa(λ) continual learner maintains near-optimal performance while train-then-fix checkpoints (taken every 30k steps) degrade as dynamics drift beyond their training distribution. Three production case studies (Cursor Tab's online policy gradient with 1.5–2 hour iteration cycles, Lyft's online RL matching with switchback validation, and sim-to-real robotics) are analyzed to map which non-stationarity challenges each faces and how continual adaptation enables measurable gains (e.g., 28% higher acceptance rate at Cursor, $30M additional annual revenue at Lyft).

## Results

The paper's primary empirical demonstration is the Rusting Pendulum experiment (Appendix A), where a continual Sarsa(λ) learner maintains near-optimal performance over a 250k-step episode while train-then-fix checkpoints (taken every 30k steps) degrade as joint friction accumulates. In the case studies, Cursor Tab's online RL achieved a 21% reduction in suggestions shown while reaching a 28% higher acceptance rate, with 1.5–2 hour deployment-to-update iteration cycles. Lyft's online RL matching system enabled millions of additional rides per year, generating over $30M in additional annual revenue while improving rider pickup times and driver utilization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new algorithm to benchmark against named baselines; instead, it contrasts the train-then-fix paradigm (Learning Rule A) against continual learning (Learning Rule B) across case studies and a controlled experiment. In the Rusting Pendulum, the train-then-fix policy (dashed curve, checkpoints every 30k steps) degrades as the damping coefficient grows quadratically, while the continual Sarsa(λ) learner (solid curve) adapts and sustains performance across 30 independent runs. In the standard (non-rusting) Pendulum, the train-then-fix policy eventually matches the continual learner once the environment is stationary, confirming that the gap is driven by non-stationarity rather than algorithmic superiority. The Lyft case study contrasts online RL against the prior fixed-policy matching approach, showing across-the-board improvements (revenue, pickup time, utilization) that the authors note are rare in large-scale marketplace optimization. Cursor Tab's online policy gradient is contrasted with the static-dataset training paradigm used by most LLM providers, yielding the 28% acceptance-rate gain. The paper's central claim is that in measurable deployment (big-world regime + available reward signal), the continual learner strictly dominates the train-then-fix sawtooth pattern over extended horizons.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes the history process formalism (Bowling et al., 2023; Abel et al., 2023) and the Big World Hypothesis (Javed & Sutton, 2024) to reframe a practical deployment question as a theoretical one: that 'measurable deployment' (constrained agent + available evaluative signal) is inherently a continual RL problem. It departs from the MDP-based framing that underpins most deployed RL (TD-Gammon, AlphaGo, OpenAI Five) by arguing the MDP's stationarity and reset assumptions are violated in deployment, and that the train-then-fix paradigm merely delays rather than solves the continual learning problem. The paper builds on prior CRL surveys (Khetarpal et al., 2022; Parisi et al., 2019) and the Alberta Plan (Sutton et al., 2022) but contributes a specific operational taxonomy—four sources of non-stationarity (action-induced, environmental dynamics, evolving goals, emergent novelty)—and grounds the argument in production case studies (Cursor Tab, Lyft, sim-to-real) rather than purely theoretical constructs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, formalized argument for why the dominant train-then-fix paradigm in deployed RL is structurally misaligned with the problem, offering practitioners a concrete decision framework (three diagnostic questions) and researchers a set of under-explored directions (rederiving algorithm properties without Markovianity, resource-constrained continual learning, alternative evaluation metrics like deviation regret). Key open questions remain: how to handle sparse, delayed, or unobservable reward signals in deployment (monitored MDPs lack convergence guarantees under history processes), how to ensure safety of continually adapting agents, and the absence of proper CRL benchmarks that reflect real-world non-stationarity rather than artificially injected task switches. The Rusting Pendulum toy experiment, while illustrative, is a single small-scale demonstration and does not constitute strong empirical evidence for the broader claims.
