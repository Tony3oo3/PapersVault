## Problem

Most learning-based autonomous driving planners lack an explicit self-correction mechanism: once an unsafe motion is proposed, there is no internal process to revise it before execution. Existing approaches rely on post-hoc filtering, rule-based candidate selection, or language-space reasoning, none of which perform iterative correction at the motion-token level prior to deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261402/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The planner consumes vectorized ego/agent history (position, speed, heading at 10 Hz), map and navigation tokens, and one-step predicted agent motions from a frozen reactive world model. Continuous trajectories are discretized into 0.5-second motion tokens from a 1024-token vocabulary (via K-disk clustering on corner distance). Beyond the final executed ego token sequence, the method also produces a correction trace—a sequence of rejected unsafe tokens—that is fed back as conditioning input for subsequent proposals within the same planning step.

## Method

CorrectionPlanner is an autoregressive transformer policy that implements a propose–evaluate–correct loop at each planning step: the policy samples a motion token, a learned collision critic (a 2-layer transformer with temporal self-attention and ego-agent cross-attention) predicts collision risk over a short horizon k, and if flagged unsafe the token is appended to a correction trace encoded by a self-attention layer, which conditions the next proposal. Training is two-stage: first, imitation learning with next-token prediction plus a correction loss that forces the policy to generate expert tokens conditioned on the correction trace; second, model-based RL using a frozen 6-layer SMART-based reactive world model to generate multi-agent rollouts, optimizing a rule-based reward (progression × no-collision − collision) via REINFORCE with KL regularization against the IL policy. The key distinction from rejection sampling is that the correction trace shifts the conditional distribution of subsequent proposals rather than resampling from the same distribution, enabling the planner to escape collision-prone regions in token space.

## Results

On the Waymax simulator (WOMD), CorrectionPlanner achieves a collision rate of 1.68 under reactive agents and 2.43 under non-reactive agents, reducing collisions by over 20% compared to the best baseline (SMART: 2.36 reactive, 4.30 non-reactive) while maintaining competitive progression (94.23 vs. 91.33 reactive). On nuPlan, the method achieves state-of-the-art planning scores of 91.22 (Val14-NR), 85.19 (Val14-R), 77.29 (Test-Hard-R), 91.14 (Test-Random-NR), and 90.41 (Test-Random-R), outperforming all learning-based baselines except on Test-Hard-NR where SMART leads (76.39 vs. 75.37). Inference latency at the best hyperparameter setting (threshold 0.75, correction length 5) is 0.434 s per trajectory, only ~0.1 s above the base policy without correction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SMART (the closest autoregressive baseline), CorrectionPlanner reduces collision from 2.36 to 1.68 (reactive) and 4.30 to 2.43 (non-reactive) on Waymax, while improving progression from 91.33 to 94.23 (reactive); on nuPlan it beats SMART's planning score in 5 of 6 settings (e.g., 91.22 vs. 90.03 on Val14-NR, 90.41 vs. 88.74 on Test-Random-R), losing only on Test-Hard-NR (75.37 vs. 76.39). Against diffusion-based DiffusionPlanner, the method wins on all nuPlan splits (e.g., 91.22 vs. 89.87 Val14-NR, 90.41 vs. 82.93 Test-Random-R) and on Waymax collision (1.68 vs. 3.27 reactive). Against continuous-distribution planners (PlanT, LatentDrive, EasyChauffeur-PPO), CorrectionPlanner achieves substantially lower collision (1.68 vs. 2.94–4.71 reactive) and off-road (0.94 vs. 1.65–3.81), at the cost of slightly lower progression (94.23 vs. 95.85–98.70). On nuPlan, the method outperforms PDM-Open (53.53–57.23), UrbanDriver (49.95–68.57), PlanTF (61.61–85.62), and PLUTO (59.74–89.90) across all splits, with the largest margins over the rule-based PDM-Open and policy-gradient UrbanDriver baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on SMART's autoregressive multi-agent world model (Wu et al., 2024) for reactive agent simulation and extends the LLM self-correction/reasoning paradigm (Havrilla et al., 2024; Kamoi et al., 2024) from natural-language space into a discrete motion-token space. It departs from prior safe-planning work (Leung et al., 2023; Pek & Althoff, 2020) that relies on formal reachability or safety-filter modules, instead embedding correction into the autoregressive generation loop itself. The two-stage training (imitation + model-based RL with a frozen world model) follows the broader trend of RL fine-tuning for driving (Lu et al., 2023; Scheel et al., 2022), but the specific use of a correction trace as a conditioning signal for subsequent token proposals is the novel architectural contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The selective self-correction mechanism (triggered only by a learned collision critic) achieves a >20% collision reduction with minimal latency overhead (~0.43s vs. 0.33s baseline), making it practically deployable. The ablations convincingly show that the correction trace provides information beyond simple resampling or candidate selection, validating the mechanism rather than attributing gains to model capacity. Open questions include whether the collision critic can be better calibrated (current precision-recall trade-off at threshold 0.75 is suboptimal), whether self-correction extends to other safety metrics (off-road, comfort), and whether the approach generalizes to continuous control or other robotics domains. The slight progression trade-off from conservative yielding behavior remains an unresolved tension between safety and efficiency.
