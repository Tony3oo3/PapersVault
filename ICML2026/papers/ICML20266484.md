## Problem

Open-source AI ecosystems host millions of model derivatives (fine-tunes, LoRA adapters, quantizations, merges) whose cumulative carbon and water footprints are rarely measured or disclosed in a comparable way, making aggregate ecosystem impact largely invisible. Per-model efficiency gains are offset by rebound effects—lower costs drive more experimentation and deployment—yet no coordination infrastructure exists to track impacts across derivative lineages, creating a tragedy-of-the-commons dynamic for shared atmospheric and freshwater resources.

## Input / Output

DIA consumes standard training telemetry (GPU-hours, hardware type, training duration), facility-level parameters (grid carbon intensity in kgCO₂/kWh, water usage effectiveness in L/kWh), and model lineage metadata (base model identifiers, derivative relationships). It produces a minimal footprint schema embedded in model card metadata, ecosystem-level aggregation dashboards on model hubs, and per-derivative impact labels. The schema is intentionally coarse—approximate hardware-based estimates are acceptable—and a practitioner can complete it in under five minutes from standard training logs, with explicit data-quality tiers and uncertainty ranges for water reporting where facility-level WUE is unavailable.

## Method

Data and Impact Accounting (DIA) is a lightweight, non-regulatory transparency layer with three integrated components: (1) a standardized reporting schema recording hardware, duration, estimation method, water use, carbon intensity, and model lineage in model card metadata; (2) low-friction instrumentation that auto-generates reports by integrating existing tools (CodeCarbon, ML CO₂ Impact Calculator, cloud-provider sustainability APIs, MLPerf Inference benchmarks) into common training and inference pipelines; and (3) ecosystem-level aggregation via public dashboards on model hubs (e.g., Hugging Face) that summarize cumulative impacts across releases and derivatives, enabling trend analysis and redundancy detection. The framework is voluntary and non-restrictive, driven by social incentives and community norms rather than mandates, and follows a four-phase rollout from norm-setting through friction reduction, ecosystem visibility, and accountability badges. The key design insight is that making derivative costs visible and comparable creates a positive feedback loop—practitioners can check whether an equivalent derivative exists before creating one—plausibly reducing redundant compute by 15–50% without any regulatory action.

## Results

Table 1 reports training emissions for 10 major GenAI models (2020–2024): GPT-3 (552 tCO₂eq), Llama 3 8B/70B (2,290 tCO₂eq), Llama 3.1 405B (8,930 tCO₂eq), GPT-4 (4,240–18,870 tCO₂eq estimated), and DeepSeek-V3 (~545 tCO₂eq estimated). A reporting-gap audit of 10 Hugging Face model releases (5 base, 5 derivatives) finds that water consumption is unreported across all 10 models, and derivatives disclose no environmental fields beyond lineage (0/5 for GPU-hours, energy, and CO₂). For the Llama 3 ecosystem specifically, 146 documented derivatives with per-derivative costs of 1–500 GPU-hours yield an aggregate derivative compute of 0.5×–3× the base training cost of 7.7M GPU-hours, with DIA adoption projected to reduce redundant compute by 15–50% depending on integration depth.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper with no competing method to benchmark against, so 'comparisons' take the form of ecosystem-level contrasts and scenario analysis. The paper contrasts open-source dynamics (distributed derivatives across independent users, e.g., 146 Llama 3 derivatives) with closed-source dynamics (centrally metered inference, e.g., GPT-4's ~tens of GWh training), arguing that open ecosystems diffuse and hide aggregate impact more severely. Three adoption scenarios are modeled for the Llama 3 ecosystem: Scenario A (no visibility) allows redundant derivatives to accumulate freely; Scenario B (DIA reporting) enables practitioners to check for existing equivalents, plausibly reducing redundant compute by 15–30%; Scenario C (DIA integrated into community norms) achieves 30–50% reductions, comparable to the impact of conference reproducibility checklists. The reporting-gap audit (Table A6) serves as an empirical baseline showing the current state: base models report CO₂ in 4/5 cases but water in 0/5, while derivatives report no environmental fields at all, confirming the visibility gap DIA aims to close.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the established ML carbon accounting methodology (Strubell et al., 2019; Lacoste et al., 2019; Patterson et al., 2021) from individual job- or model-level footprints to ecosystem-level aggregation across derivative lineages. It builds on the model card format (Mitchell et al., 2019) by embedding a standardized environmental metadata schema, and draws on Ostrom's commons governance theory to frame open-source AI as a shared-resource coordination problem. The key conceptual departure is applying rebound-effect and Jevons Paradox reasoning from energy economics to the open-source derivative ecosystem, arguing that per-model efficiency gains are structurally insufficient without lineage-level visibility.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The reporting gap audit (Table A6) showing 0% water disclosure and 0% derivative-level environmental reporting across 10 Hugging Face models makes the coordination gap concrete and urgent. The paper's central open question is whether voluntary, low-friction reporting can overcome the economic incentives driving rapid scaling, or whether DIA will inevitably need regulatory backing to achieve meaningful aggregate reductions. Limitations include reliance on self-reporting without independent verification, exclusion of supply-chain impacts, and the acknowledged difficulty of tracking decentralized inference-phase footprints. The 2027 target for standardized DIA reports on major open-source releases provides a concrete near-term benchmark for the community.
