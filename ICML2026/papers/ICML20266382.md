## Problem

Uncertainty in AI-assisted decision making is typically treated as a static model property or informational label, with its behavioral role in shaping human reliance over repeated interactions rarely studied. The common pipeline—display uncertainty, expect selective reliance—is fragile in practice, with users exhibiting overreliance, underreliance, and treating AI outputs as defaults regardless of displayed confidence. The paper argues this reflects a conceptual gap: uncertainty should be understood as a strategic signal whose effect is mediated by the surrounding mechanism (feedback, payoffs, available actions), not merely a display.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a decision case (multiple-choice question), an AI recommendation paired with a binary uncertainty signal (HIGH/LOW, derived from an LLM's forced-choice self-reported confidence), and the accumulated interaction history as input. The mechanism M = (S, A, F, R) specifies the signal space, the action set (accept vs. verify), the feedback function (whether correctness is revealed after each round), and the payoff function. The output is a reliance policy mapping signals and histories to actions, characterized by its behavioral regime (pooling vs. near-separating) and quantified through verification rate, follow rate, misuse, disuse, and accuracy.

## Method

The paper formalizes human-AI decision support as a repeated signaling game in which the AI assistant (sender) emits a recommendation and uncertainty signal, and the human (receiver) chooses to accept or incur a verification cost. The central design object is the mechanism M = (S, A, F, R), which determines whether uncertainty functions as cheap talk (ignoring it has no consequences) or as a strategically meaningful signal (ignoring it becomes costly through revealed feedback and payoff structure). They define pooling regimes (action probabilities nearly constant across signals) and near-separating regimes (verification concentrates on high-uncertainty states) using signal-conditioned rates v(s), f(s), m(s), d(s). The empirical study instantiates this with a between-subjects design (180 participants, 20 fixed questions, Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct as the assistant) comparing three mechanisms: human-only, uncertainty-only (banner plus verify option, no per-item feedback), and game-theoretic (identical signals plus per-item correctness feedback and a scoring rule with a −0.3 verification cost that induces a threshold in inferred correctness probability). The game-theoretic mechanism increased verification by 4.2 pp, halved misuse from 9.8% to 4.3%, and improved accuracy by 3.3 pp over uncertainty-only, with the sharpest effects on HIGH-uncertainty items (verification 80.5%→91.4%, follow-AI 23.0%→12.4%) while leaving LOW-uncertainty behavior and completion time unchanged.

## Results

In a 180-participant pilot study (3560 trials), the game-theoretic mechanism increased verification from 45.1% to 49.2% (+4.2 pp, p≈0.042) and reduced misuse (blind acceptance of wrong AI outputs) from 9.8% to 4.3% (approximately halved), while disuse remained low and comparable (1.0% vs. 1.1%). Overall accuracy rose from 81.9% (uncertainty-only) to 85.3% (game-theoretic), a +3.3 pp gain (p≈0.028), and from 80.3% (human-only baseline) by +4.9 pp (p≈0.002). On HIGH-uncertainty items specifically, verification jumped from 80.5% to 91.4% (+10.9 pp, p<0.001) and follow-AI dropped from 23.0% to 12.4% (−10.6 pp, p<0.001), while LOW-uncertainty behavior was unchanged (all p>0.1), confirming the mechanism selectively sharpens reliance on the uncertainty signal rather than inducing blanket skepticism.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its game-theoretic feedback mechanism against two baselines: a human-only condition (no AI) and an uncertainty-only condition (AI recommendation + HIGH/LOW banner, but no per-item feedback or scoring). Against the uncertainty-only baseline, the game-theoretic mechanism significantly increases verification (+4.2 pp, p≈0.042), reduces follow-AI (−5.4 pp, p≈0.008), and roughly halves misuse (9.8%→4.3%) without increasing disuse. Against the human-only baseline, the game-theoretic mechanism yields a +4.9 pp accuracy gain (p≈0.002), whereas the uncertainty-only arm does not significantly differ from baseline. The effect is concentrated on HIGH-uncertainty items (verification +10.9 pp, follow-AI −10.6 pp, both p<0.001) with no significant change on LOW-uncertainty items (all p>0.1), demonstrating that the mechanism selectively reallocates verification effort rather than uniformly increasing skepticism. Completion times show no significant differences across arms (Kruskal-Wallis p≈0.19), ruling out time-on-task as a confound.

## Relation to Prior Work

The paper's theoretical core is the Crawford–Sobel (1982) signaling-game framework, repurposed to model a repeated human–AI interaction where the AI is the sender and the human is the receiver. It departs from the dominant uncertainty-estimation literature (Bayesian deep learning, ensembles, calibration) that treats uncertainty as a model property to be computed and displayed, and from the human-AI reliance literature (Buçinca, Bansal, Vasconcelos) that attributes overreliance to cognitive factors and interface design. The key conceptual move is to elevate the *mechanism*—the tuple of signal space, action set, feedback rule, and payoff function—to the unit of analysis, arguing that uncertainty without mechanism-level consequences degenerates into cheap talk. This positions the work at the intersection of mechanism design (Kamenica & Gentzkow's Bayesian persuasion) and human-AI interaction, a combination the authors identify as largely unexplored.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'mechanism as unit of analysis' framing offers a concrete design vocabulary (pooling vs. near-separating regimes, verification cost thresholds) that system builders can use to diagnose why well-calibrated uncertainty displays still fail to change behavior. The pilot study (n=180, 20 items) provides initial but limited evidence: a simple scoring rule around a fixed binary uncertainty signal roughly halved misuse (9.8%→4.3%) and shifted verification toward high-uncertainty items, yet effect sizes are modest and the assistant is pre-scripted with a single binary signal. Open questions include whether the findings generalize to richer signal spaces, high-stakes domains with asymmetric error costs, noisy or unavailable ground-truth feedback, and whether the specific scoring rule matters or any non-degenerate incentive structure suffices. The paper explicitly acknowledges these as limitations and frames the study as a first illustration rather than a comprehensive evaluation.
