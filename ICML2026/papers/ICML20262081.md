## Problem

Rating-based RL (RbRL) introduced learning reward functions from discrete multi-class human ratings, but its cross-entropy loss requires manually specified class boundaries, forces all trajectories within a class to concentrate at a single return midpoint (discarding intra-class diversity), and offers no theoretical guarantees on the solution set. This paper asks whether a rating-based objective can treat ratings purely as ordinal targets, eliminate boundary hyperparameters, and still yield provably minimal and complete reward function solutions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes trajectory-rating pairs where each trajectory is a state-action sequence and each rating is a discrete ordinal label (e.g., 0='bad', 1='neutral', 2='good') assigned independently by a human or simulated teacher. The number of rating classes is not fixed a priori and can change dynamically during training (e.g., finer bins early, merged bins later). The output is a parametric reward function r̂_θ mapping state-action pairs to scalar rewards, which is then used to train a SAC policy; in the online setting, the reward model is updated iteratively as the agent collects new trajectories.

## Method

R4 (Ranked Return Regression for RL) introduces a ranking mean squared error (rMSE) loss: at each training step, one trajectory is sampled per rating class, its predicted discounted return is computed under the reward model, and a differentiable sorting operator (Blondel et al., 2020) converts the set of predicted returns into continuous soft ranks. The loss is simply the MSE between these soft ranks and the human-assigned ordinal labels, making the ordinal structure of ratings directly optimizable without explicit class boundaries. The paper proves that under mild assumptions, the rMSE solution set is both complete (it contains the true reward function) and minimal (no further objective can shrink it without excluding valid solutions), and extends this guarantee to the case where the soft-rank operator introduces bounded approximation error. For the online setting, R4 additionally employs a dynamic feedback schedule (more frequent early, sparser later) and stratified trajectory sampling (1/3 from top-30% predicted returns, 2/3 from the rest) to make limited feedback budgets more informative.

## Results

In the online feedback setting across six DMC environments, R4 achieves significantly higher final returns than RbRL in 4 of 6 environments (e.g., Walker-walk: 736.78 vs. 77.18; Humanoid-stand: 537.04 vs. 4.91) and outperforms all preference-based baselines (PEBBLE, SURF, QPA) with p < 0.0125 after Bonferroni correction. In the offline setting, R4 reward functions yield statistically faster SAC learning or higher final returns than RbRL across all three OpenAI Gym domains (p < 0.03). In the human-user study, R4 trained on real participant ratings achieves significantly higher returns than RbRL in both Reacher (−27.65 vs. −44.62) and Hopper (1410.10 vs. 723.87), with p < 0.006, while participants report low cognitive workload (NASA TLX overall: 2.05/7).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against RbRL (the most direct baseline, also using ordinal ratings), R4 wins decisively in 4 of 6 online DMC environments on final return (p < 0.0125), with the largest gap in Humanoid-stand (537.04 vs. 4.91) and Walker-walk (736.78 vs. 77.18); the two Quadruped tasks show no significant difference. Against preference-based methods, R4 outperforms PEBBLE in all 6 environments on return (e.g., Walker-walk: 736.78 vs. 246.13, p = 0.000), SURF in all 6 (e.g., Cheetah-run: 747.68 vs. 496.27, p = 0.000), and QPA in 5 of 6 (Walker-walk return: 736.78 vs. 627.46, p = 0.005; the only non-significant case is Walker-walk AUC, p = 0.649). In the offline setting, R4 consistently beats RbRL across Reacher, Inverted Double Pendulum, and Half Cheetah (p < 0.03). In the human-user study, R4 trained on real participant ratings significantly outperforms RbRL in both Reacher (return: −27.65 vs. −44.62, p = 0.0047) and Hopper (return: 1410.10 vs. 723.87, p = 0.0055), despite substantial inter-participant variability in rating scales (4–12 classes per participant).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on rating-based RL (RbRL) by White et al. (2024), which introduced a cross-entropy loss for learning reward functions from discrete human ratings, and on the broader preference-based RL literature (PEBBLE, SURF, QPA). The key departure is replacing RbRL's categorical cross-entropy objective with a ranking mean squared error (rMSE) loss that treats ratings as ordinal targets, leveraging differentiable sorting (Blondel et al., 2020) to make the ranking operation gradient-compatible. Unlike prior rating-based methods, R4 provides formal minimality and completeness guarantees on its solution set, and eliminates the need for manually specified class boundaries that RbRL requires.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical result that the true reward function can be excluded from RbRL's solution set (Proposition 1) is a meaningful correction to the prior method's theoretical standing, and the minimality/completeness guarantees for rMSE give practitioners confidence that no valid reward is being needlessly discarded. The human-user study, showing R4 outperforms RbRL despite substantial inter-rater variability (4–12 rating classes, non-uniform distributions) and low cognitive workload (NASA TLX ≈ 2/7), addresses a noted gap where fewer than 50% of PbRL algorithms from 2012–2024 were validated with non-author participants. Open questions include whether the ordinal assumption (lower-rated trajectories always have lower true returns) holds in more complex, multi-objective settings, and how the method scales to larger ensembles of raters with systematically different internal standards.
