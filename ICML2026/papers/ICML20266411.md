## Problem

Autonomous open-ended learning requires a prospective criterion for selecting which objects to invest computational effort in, yet existing interestingness measures (information gain, learning progress, compression progress) are post-hoc, quantifying progress only after training has already occurred. This paper asks whether past compression progress can inductively predict future progress, and under what algorithmic conditions such prediction is theoretically grounded.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266411/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a partial log-size vs. complexity profile P̂ truncated at a complexity cutoff t (summarized by three values: log-length n̂, current complexity k̂, and last-drop sophistication m̂) together with a chosen prior over computable strings, and produces the probability of a further compression drop at complexity ≥ t, the expected future compression progress (k̂ − k_x), and the expected final complexity k_x and sophistication m_x. The input is inherently incomplete—the profile is observed only up to t—and the outputs are conditional expectations that quantify how much compressible structure remains accessible beyond the observation window.

## Method

The paper formalizes interestingness as expected future compression progress by analyzing the trajectory r → K^r(x) (time-bounded Kolmogorov complexity) and its dual, the log-size vs. complexity profile P_x, using counting results from Algorithmic Statistics that bound the number of strings following a given profile shape. It then computes conditional expectations under three universal priors—Length, Algorithmic (Solomonoff), and Speed—over all strings consistent with the observed partial profile. The central result is that the stagnation length (t − m̂), the gap between the last observed drop and the cutoff, exponentially governs both the probability and magnitude of future compression drops. The Algorithmic Prior yields a quadratic increase in expected future progress relative to the Length Prior for the same observed profile, while the Speed Prior is essentially conservative, predicting negligible further drops. The P_x ↔ D_x correspondence via the Busy Beaver function bridges the two profile representations, and the theoretical predictions are empirically validated across three computationally universal paradigms: 2-Tag systems, Rule 110 cellular automata, and Brainfuck.

## Results

The central theoretical result is that the probability of a future compression drop decays exponentially with the stagnation length (t − m̂): p(m_x ≥ t | P̂) ≈ 2^{-(t-m̂)+O(log n̂)} under both the Length and Algorithmic Priors. Under the Algorithmic Prior, the expected future compression progress is quadratically larger than under the Length Prior, with a ratio of approximately ¼(k̂ − t − 1)(k̂ − t) for the same observed profile. The Speed Prior is the most conservative, yielding expected future progress ≈ 0. These theoretical predictions are empirically validated across three computationally universal paradigms (2-Tag systems, Rule 110 cellular automata, and Brainfuck), confirming that the exponential decay in expected progress with stagnation length holds in physically realizable regimes.

**Table: Key properties of the three priors for predicting future compression progress, given a partial profile P̂ with observation cutoff t.**

| Property | Length Prior L | Algorithmic Prior M | Speed Prior S |
|---|---|---|---|
| P(further drop \| P̂) | 2^{-(t-m̂)+O(log n̂)} | 2^{-(t-m̂)+O(log n̂)} (for k̂−t≫0) | ≈ 0 |
| E[k_x \| P̂, m≥t] | k̂ − 2 | (k̂ + t)/2 | (k̂ + t − 1)/2 |
| Expected progress (k̂ − E[k_x]) | 2^{-(t-m̂)+O(log n̂)} | 2^{-(t-m̂)+O(log n̂)} | ≈ 0 |
| Drop probability ratio M/L | — | ≈ k̂ − t − 1 (linear) | — |
| Progress ratio M/L | — | ≈ ¼(k̂−t−1)(k̂−t) (quadratic) | — |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baselines but instead contrasts three universal priors over computable strings. The Algorithmic Prior (Solomonoff) is the most 'optimistic': it yields a linearly higher drop probability and a quadratically higher expected compression progress compared to the Length Prior for the same observed profile, because it assigns higher weight to algorithmically simple strings that are more likely to have deeper structure. The Speed Prior is the most 'conservative,' essentially treating the current compression frontier as final (expected progress ≈ 0), because it penalizes runtime and assumes any faster compression would already have been found. The Length Prior sits in between, treating all strings of a given length equally. Empirically, the exponential decay of expected progress with stagnation length is confirmed across 2-Tag systems, Rule 110 cellular automata, and Brainfuck, validating the theoretical predictions in physically realizable computational regimes rather than the extreme runtimes implied by the Busy Beaver function.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formalizes and proves the inductive property of interestingness—previously discussed informally in Schmidhuber's compression-progress and curiosity literature—using the counting results of Vereshchagin & Shen (2016) on algorithmic statistics. It repurposes the complexity-runtime profile and log-size-vs-complexity profile as the primary analytical objects, bridging them via the Busy Beaver function, and applies three universal priors (Length, Algorithmic/Solomonoff, Speed) to derive closed-form predictions for future compression drops. Unlike prior interestingness criteria (count-based, prediction-error, information-gain, learning-progress), which are post-hoc, this work explicitly targets the *prospective* question: given a partial profile observed up to cutoff t, what is the probability and magnitude of further drops? The empirical validation across 2-Tag systems, Rule 110 CAs, and Brainfuck extends the theoretical analysis into physically realizable (though still toy) computational universes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous AIT-based justification for why recency of progress should guide resource allocation in open-ended systems: expected future compression decays exponentially with stagnation length, and the Algorithmic Prior yields a quadratic boost in expected progress over the Length Prior. However, the Busy Beaver function that bridges the two profile types makes the relevant runtimes physically unrealizable, and the Speed Prior's near-zero prediction of future progress highlights how sensitive the inductivity conclusion is to prior choice—leaving open the question of which prior best models structured, scale-free domains (e.g., biological or physical systems) where long histories of discovery are expected to continue. Practical interestingness measures that approximate these theoretical quantities without requiring uncomputable priors remain a significant gap.
