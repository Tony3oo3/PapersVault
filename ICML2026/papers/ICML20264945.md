## Problem

Prior work on group multiplication interpretability produced conflicting claims about universality: some studies found matrix-representation circuits, others found coset circuits, and it remained unclear which structures generalize across group types. This paper revisits all prior theories on the non-commutative dihedral group to test which make correct predictions, and provides the first complete characterization of every neural representation class learnable on a dataset, interpreted at three levels of abstraction (neuron, neural representation, and global algorithm).

## Input / Output

For MLPs, the input is a pair (a, b) ∈ D_n × D_n embedded via separate left and right embedding matrices; for transformers, the same pair is cast as a length-2 language modeling sequence with a shared token embedding plus positional embeddings and residual connections. The output is the group product a·b, a class label over 2n elements. The main analysis uses D_18 (36 elements, chosen for its rich coset structure) and D_19 (38 elements, prime n with no non-trivial cosets), with scaling sweeps from D_2 to D_512.

## Method

The paper trains 1- and 2-layer MLPs (512 ReLU neurons/layer) and transformers to 100% test accuracy, then uses the Group Fourier Transform to cluster neurons by their concentration on a single irreducible representation of D_n. Within each cluster, PCA of preactivations reveals a Cayley graph whose vertices are coset equivalence classes; coupled first-order sinusoid fits (R² = 0.9988) recover rotation and reflection generators from half-phase relations (A_sum and B_dif), which are verified to be consistent across 99.81% of 1000 random seeds. Quantitative graph-theoretic tests—coset clustering scores, cyclic subgroup trace regularity (|ρ| = 0.9999), and 100% vertex coverage under generator closure—confirm the PCA geometry matches the recovered Cayley graph rather than a trivial cyclic artifact. The global algorithm divides D_n into O(log n) Cayley-graph subproblems, each computing the answer's equivalence class, which are then linearly combined at the logits to uniquely maximize the correct output.

## Results

Across 1000 random seeds on D₁₈, first-layer neuron preactivations are well-approximated by coupled first-order sinusoids with R² = 0.9988 (MLP) and R² = 0.9964–0.9994 (Transformer) per Fourier basis. The PCA geometry of each neuron cluster matches the predicted Cayley graph structure: coset clustering score s_cluster = 0.993±0.001 (MLP) and 0.974±0.071 (Transformer), coset disjointness = 0.999±0.001 (MLP) and 0.977±0.082 (Transformer), cyclic regularity |ρ| = 0.9999±0.0001, and nested closure reaches 100% of vertices in (ℤ₂g)². Both MLPs and Transformers learn O(log n) neural representations (Cayley graphs) as the group size scales from D₂ to D₅₁₂, with MLPs preferring smaller subproblems (e.g., frequency-6 D₃₆ in 87.7% of runs).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic interpretability paper that characterizes what networks learn rather than comparing competing methods, so there are no traditional baselines. The closest comparison is a negative control: replacing the true dihedral multiplication table with a random one (same architecture, training protocol, and analysis pipeline) causes the sinusoidal-fit R², coset clustering score, and coset disjointness to collapse, confirming the observed structure reflects the learned group law rather than PCA or GFT artifacts. Between architectures, MLPs produce tighter and more consistent Cayley graph geometry (s_cluster = 0.993 vs. 0.974; disjointness = 0.999 vs. 0.977) and learn fewer, more concentrated neural representations, while Transformers show noisier solutions and occasionally learn lower-dimensional representations (e.g., using a sign representation in layer 1 to offload reflection handling to layer 2). The paper also contrasts D₁₈ (rich coset structure) with D₁₉ (prime, no non-trivial cosets), showing that approximate cosets emerge when gcd(n, f) = 1, consistent with prior findings on cyclic and permutation groups.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the coset-based interpretability framework from Stander et al. (2024) and the divide-and-conquer conjecture from McCracken et al. (2025b) from cyclic and permutation groups to the non-commutative dihedral group, where multiplication order matters. It also builds on Moisescu-Pareja et al. (2025)'s geometric characterization of neural representations as manifolds, discovering that their 'universal manifold' is in fact a minimal Cayley graph. Unlike prior work that studied neuron-level behavior or preliminary cluster structure, this paper provides the first full characterization of all learnable neural representations on a dataset, interpreting DNNs at three levels of abstraction: individual neurons (cosets/approximate cosets), neuron clusters (Cayley graphs as subproblems), and the global algorithm (logarithmic linear combination at the logits).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the community with a fully understood toy model for mechanistic interpretability and makes concrete progress toward proving the conjecture that stochastic gradient methods universally divide and conquer all group multiplication tasks. The O(log(n)) scaling of neural representations with group size suggests a remarkably efficient algorithmic strategy, and the three-level characterization (neuron → Cayley graph → global algorithm) offers a template for interpreting more complex tasks. Key open questions include whether the Cayley-graph structure generalizes to groups with different finite simple components (e.g., permutation or Lie-type groups), whether the divide-and-conquer algorithm is truly universal across all groups, and whether a formal proof of the logarithmic representation count is achievable. A limitation is that the methodology relies on the Group Fourier Transform, which has no direct analogue for natural datasets.
