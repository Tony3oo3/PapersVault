## Problem

Existing LLM knowledge evaluation relies on static benchmarks that passively sample sparse, discrete points and are vulnerable to data contamination, failing to characterize the continuous landscape of a model's extractable knowledge. A second challenge is the model's 'comfort zone' inertia: when queried directly, LLMs produce generic, repetitive shallow content, making saturation-based extraction of unique knowledge atoms from semantic redundancies and hallucinations extremely difficult. No prior method combines adaptive hierarchical exploration with rigorous semantic deduplication to systematically probe a black-box model's knowledge frontier.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a topic domain query (drawn from ICML's official taxonomy, e.g., Deep Learning, ML Systems, Probabilistic Methods) and a black-box LLM as input, interacting over up to 15 turns with early stopping triggered by saturation criteria (growth rate <1%, efficiency <10%, or fewer than 3 novel atoms per turn). The output is a set of discrete, verifiable knowledge atoms each classified into exactly one Bloom's Taxonomy category (factual, conceptual, or procedural), along with derived metrics: recall against a cross-model knowledge union, accuracy (fraction passing domain audit), Pareto frontier curves in cost-yield space, and per-turn saturation signals. The extracted frontier is explicitly pipeline-dependent rather than an intrinsic model property.

## Method

The framework couples four agentic exploration policies with a three-stage knowledge processor. The key strategy, Recursive Taxonomy, decomposes the domain into a hierarchical tree (branching factor W, depth D) and then deploys parallel agents to exhaustively mine each leaf node, forcing the model past dominant concepts into long-tail parametric memory; it achieves ~6× the yield of the baseline at convergence. The knowledge processor first applies vector-space filtering (qwen3-8b-emb, cosine similarity >0.92 for automatic merge), then an LLM judge (DeepSeek-V3.1) resolves the 0.70–0.92 ambiguity zone where lexical overlap masks logical negation or technical distinction, and finally a domain relevance audit rejects meta-statements, generic assertions, and fragments that fail Bloom's Taxonomy validity criteria. The same three-category Bloom's rubric governs both the extraction prompt and the audit stage, ensuring end-to-end granularity consistency validated by human calibration (κ=0.79, precision 97.9%).

## Results

The Recursive Taxonomy (L5W5) strategy achieves a mean yield ratio of 5.98±0.12 (normalized to Taxonomy-L2W2=1.0), outperforming Sequential (0.28), Reflective (0.32), and Multi-Perspective-N20 (4.19) by 20×, 19×, and 1.4× respectively. In the scaling law experiment, extractable knowledge recall grows from 27.3% (Llama-3.1-8B) to 36.9% (70B) to 39.0% (405B), with accuracy peaking at 89.0% for 70B. The RL-tuned Qwen Coder model shows a Pass@1/Pass@k trade-off: higher initial accuracy (96.4% vs 92.3% in Deep Learning) but 3.7× faster degradation, and catastrophic forgetting in out-of-distribution domains (60.1% vs 81.0% accuracy in Probabilistic Methods).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four agentic exploration strategies (Sequential, Reflective, Recursive Taxonomy, Multi-Perspective) as baselines against each other on Llama-3.1-405B. Recursive Taxonomy (L5W5) dominates the Pareto frontier, extracting ~6× more unique knowledge atoms than the baseline Taxonomy-L2W2 and ~20× more than naive Sequential or Reflective probing, which plateau at yield ratios below 0.3 despite 15 turns. Multi-Perspective-N20 is competitive in the low-budget regime (<10⁵ tokens) but still trails L5W5 by 1.4× in absolute yield. In the cross-model scaling comparison, larger Llama-3.1 models consistently outperform smaller ones (405B > 70B > 8B in recall across all three domains), with diminishing returns from 70B→405B (+2.1 pp) versus 8B→70B (+9.6 pp). In the RL fine-tuning comparison, the Qwen Coder model loses to the General model on recall in every domain (e.g., 42.2% vs 57.7% in Deep Learning) and on sustained accuracy after turn 10, confirming that domain specialization narrows rather than expands the extractable knowledge boundary.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes ideas from three distinct threads—static knowledge benchmarks (MMLU, TruthfulQA), agentic exploration (Reflexion, Tree-of-Thoughts, multi-agent debate), and LLM-as-judge deduplication (MT-Bench, paraphrase detection)—into a unified interactive framework for black-box knowledge extraction. It departs from prior static evaluation by formalizing a saturation-based, pipeline-dependent 'Extractable Knowledge Frontier' and repurposes hierarchical decomposition (conceptually similar to Zeng et al.'s EvalTree) for exhaustive knowledge mining rather than weakness identification. The Pass@1/Pass@k analysis extends Yue et al.'s (2025) RL fine-tuning findings from generation quality to knowledge extraction, while the scaling analysis generalizes Kaplan et al.'s loss-based scaling laws to knowledge volume.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical methodology for auditing a model's extractable knowledge before deployment, with the finding that RL specialization narrows knowledge boundaries (not just shifts them) having direct implications for model selection in production. The decomposition showing stable recall but domain-variable accuracy across model families suggests training data composition, not architecture, governs knowledge quality—a useful signal for practitioners. Key limitations include pipeline-dependence of all measurements (the 'frontier' is not an intrinsic model property), restriction to three technical ICML domains, and reliance on an LLM judge (κ=0.79) that does not replace expert verification. Open questions include extension to non-technical domains, combining complementary strategies, and incorporating retrieval-based fact-checking to reduce judge dependence.
