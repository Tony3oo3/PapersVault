## Problem

Existing AI-for-Science approaches rely on end-to-end executable environments with well-defined objectives, which cannot generalize to most scientific domains where digital simulators are infeasible (e.g., medicine) or where the primary challenge is designing a rigorous research plan rather than executing a fixed procedure. This paper asks how to train language models to generate better research plans for diverse, open-ended research goals without requiring costly execution feedback or specialized sandboxes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261289/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a self-contained, open-ended research goal as input — a description of the original situation researchers faced, including explicit constraints, stated preferences, and key uncertainties, extracted from a published paper. It produces a structured research plan (methodology, experimental setup, evaluation metrics) wrapped in <solution></solution> tags, constrained to 750 words to prevent verbosity, though the model may use unlimited thinking tokens before committing to the final plan. Training data additionally includes 10 goal-specific rubric items and 7 general grading guidelines per sample, used only by the grader as privileged information.

## Method

The paper proposes a scalable recipe that automatically extracts research goals, goal-specific rubrics (10 items), and reference solutions from scientific papers using a two-model pipeline (Llama-4-Maverick as creator, Claude-4-Sonnet as selector). Training uses GRPO reinforcement learning with self-rewards: a frozen copy of the initial Qwen-3-30B-A3B policy acts as the grader, evaluating each generated plan by checking whether the relevant section for each rubric item violates any of 7 general guidelines (specificity, no overlooked flaws, justified rationale, cost efficiency, ethics, consistency, completeness). A rubric item is satisfied only if zero guidelines are violated, and the reward is the fraction of satisfied items minus a format penalty. The key design insight is that providing rubrics as privileged information to the grader (but not the generator) creates a generator-verifier gap that makes verification tractable, while the combination of goal-specific rubrics and general guidelines is shown via ablation to be essential — removing either degrades performance substantially. Disabling the KL penalty in GRPO further improves results by allowing plans to become more specific and detailed without being pulled back toward the initial policy's vaguer style.

## Results

In a human study with 25 ML experts (225 expert hours), experts prefer the finetuned Qwen-3-30B-A3B's research plans over the initial model for 70.0% of goals (p<0.01) and over the much larger Grok-4-Thinking for 59.6% of goals (p<0.01). On automated rubric grading by a jury of three frontier models, the finetuned model achieves 12–22% relative improvements over the initial policy across ML, Medical, and ArXiv domains. Cross-domain generalization is strong: the Medical finetune improves 15% on ML and 17.5% on ArXiv tasks, and the ArXiv finetune even outperforms the Medical finetune on ArXiv goals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Grok-4-Thinking (xAI), the finetuned 30B model wins the human preference comparison 59.6% of the time (p<0.01) and matches or slightly exceeds it on automated rubric scores (e.g., 30.2 vs 26.8 on Claude-4-Sonnet judge for ML). Against Claude-4-Sonnet, the finetuned model scores higher on the Claude and GPT judges (30.2/23.2 vs 24.2/21.6 on ML) but lower on Gemini-2.5-Pro (40.9 vs 39.0), indicating a near-parity with this frontier model. Against Gemini-2.5-Pro, the finetuned model trails on the Gemini judge (40.9 vs 44.7) but leads on Claude and GPT judges, showing mixed but competitive results. Against Gemma-3-4B, the finetuned version more than doubles the rubric score on the Claude judge (13.7 vs 7.6) and improves on all three judges. Against Llama-3.1-8B, the improvement is the most dramatic: the finetuned model jumps from 2.3 to 17.9 on the Claude judge and from 9.8 to 29.3 on Gemini, a ~7x relative gain, suggesting the RL recipe is especially effective for models that initially produce vague plans.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of rubric-based RL for language models (Gunjal et al. 2025; Ye et al. 2025) and the AI co-scientist literature (Gottweis et al. 2025; Mishra et al. 2025). It departs from the dominant AI-for-Science paradigm of building executable sandbox environments (Lu et al. 2024; Novikov et al. 2025) by instead training on open-ended research plan generation, using a frozen copy of the initial policy as a self-grading reward model with goal-specific rubrics as privileged information (Zhou et al. 2025). The data extraction pipeline from scientific papers generalizes prior work on extracting training signals from expert documents (Yue et al. 2024; Yuan et al. 2025) to the much more open-ended, expertise-intensive setting of research planning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that targeted RL with rubric rewards can make a 30B MoE model competitive with much larger frontier models (Grok-4-Thinking) on research plan generation, without requiring expensive execution environments or human annotation during training. The significant cross-domain generalization (medical finetune improving ML and ArXiv performance) suggests a path toward generalist AI co-scientists, though the authors note plans become more complex and still require human filtering before execution. Key open questions include whether rubric rewards can be combined with end-to-end execution feedback, how to design faster and more accurate evaluations for AI co-scientists, and whether the approach extends to domains where even rubric extraction from papers is difficult.
