## Problem

Rare but extreme gradient-norm spikes in large-scale training (LLM pre-training, quantization-aware training, RL) can corrupt optimizer state and trigger divergence. The dominant safeguard, gradient clipping, is an extrinsic post-processing rule that requires manual threshold tuning and indiscriminately truncates updates, either intervening too late or suppressing informative gradients during stable phases. The paper asks whether an intrinsic, threshold-free mechanism can structurally prevent spike-induced divergence without discarding gradient direction information.

## Input / Output

At each optimization step, the method consumes the raw stochastic gradient vector g_t (flattened across all parameters) and two scalar EMA decay rates (γ₁, γ₂), along with the previous EMA norm states m_{t-1}^R and v_{t-1}^R. It produces a stabilized gradient vector g̃_t of the same dimensionality as g_t, where the direction is preserved exactly (unit vector of g_t) but the magnitude is replaced by the ratio ρ_t = m_t^R / √(v_t^R), which is provably bounded by a constant depending only on (γ₁, γ₂). The output is then fed to any standard optimizer (Adam, AdamW, Lion, etc.) as if it were the original gradient, making the transform a zero-overhead drop-in.

## Method

GradientStabilizer structurally decouples the gradient's direction from its magnitude: it computes the unit direction d_t = g_t/‖g_t‖₂ and replaces the instantaneous norm with a statistically stabilized estimate ρ_t = m_t^R/√(v_t^R), where m_t^R and v_t^R are exponential moving averages of the gradient norm and its square, respectively. In stationary regimes, ρ_t converges to 1/√(1+c_v²) (the mean-to-RMS ratio), naturally contracting updates when gradient-norm variance is high and recovering full learning-rate dynamics when variance vanishes. Under a spike event (R_t ≥ κ·m_{t-1}^R), the paper proves ρ_t is uniformly bounded by (1−γ₁)/√(1−γ₂) + γ₁/(κ√(1−γ₂)), independent of the raw spike size, so arbitrarily large spikes cannot produce arbitrarily large updates. This bounded effective-gradient property then propagates into Adam/AMSGrad: the internal first- and second-moment states remain uniformly bounded for all t, and each per-coordinate parameter change is capped by η_t·ρ̄, providing the stability prerequisite assumed (but rarely verified) in nonconvex convergence analyses for adaptive optimizers.

## Results

On FP4 LLaMA-350M pre-training (C4, 6.6B tokens), GradientStabilizer reduces validation perplexity from 21.35 (AdamW) to 18.84, a 2.5 PPL improvement over the base optimizer and 1.3 PPL over the best clipping baseline (AGC at 19.42). On FP16 LLaMA-350M, it achieves 17.80 PPL vs. 19.21 for AdamW and 18.62 for ZClip. On ImageNet-1K, GradientStabilizer consistently yields the best or second-best top-1 accuracy across ViT-B, ConvNeXt-T, and ResNet-50. Most strikingly, on the weight-decay sensitivity test (ViT-B, Adam), GradientStabilizer maintains 72.4% top-1 accuracy at WD=5e-4 where Adam collapses to 52.9% and AGC drops to 0.1% at WD=1e-4.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GradientStabilizer outperforms all four gradient-clipping baselines (Value Clip, Norm Clip, AGC, ZClip) across every task and optimizer combination tested. In LLM pre-training, ZClip is the strongest clipping competitor (e.g., 19.23 PPL on FP4 LLaMA-350M AdamW) but still trails GradientStabilizer by 0.4 PPL; Norm Clip and AGC are further behind at 20.13 and 19.42 respectively. On ImageNet-1K, ZClip does not consistently outperform Norm Clip on vision models, whereas GradientStabilizer achieves the best or second-best top-1 accuracy in nearly all architecture-optimizer combinations. The most dramatic separation appears in the weight-decay sensitivity experiment: at WD=5e-4 with Adam on ViT-B, GradientStabilizer retains 72.4% accuracy while Adam drops to 52.9%, Norm Clip to 20.6%, and AGC catastrophically fails (0.1% at WD=1e-4). When combined with alternative optimizers (Adam-Mini, Lion) in Table 4, GradientStabilizer again yields the largest and most consistent gains over both the base optimizer and all clipping baselines (e.g., 27.75 vs. 30.41 PPL for AGC on FP4 LLaMA-130M with Adam-Mini).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant gradient-clipping paradigm (Value Clip, Norm Clip, AGC, ZClip) by reframing spike mitigation as an intrinsic gradient transformation rather than an extrinsic post-hoc truncation rule. It repurposes the EMA machinery already present in Adam-style optimizers—tracking first and second moments of the gradient *norm* rather than the gradient vector—to construct a stabilized magnitude that is provably bounded regardless of spike size. Conceptually, it sits between normalized gradient descent (which discards all magnitude information) and raw SGD (which is fully exposed to norm volatility), occupying a middle ground that prior clipping methods approximate only reactively via threshold-based interventions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The threshold-free, drop-in nature of GradientStabilizer removes a persistent hyperparameter-tuning burden (clipping thresholds) from large-scale training pipelines, and the theoretical spike-invariant bound provides a clean stability guarantee that standard clipping cannot offer. The finding that gradient clipping *exacerbates* Adam's well-known sensitivity to weight decay—while GradientStabilizer substantially mitigates it—opens a new angle on the interaction between regularization and gradient constraints that the field has not previously isolated. Open questions include whether the two EMA decay rates (γ₁, γ₂) can be further reduced or auto-tuned, whether the boundedness results extend to fully non-stationary or adversarial gradient sequences beyond the spike-event model, and whether the method's benefits persist at the 100B+ parameter scale where the paper's experiments (≤350M) do not reach.
