## Problem

Existing multi-domain graph pre-training methods jointly train on all source-domain graphs, incurring computational costs that scale sharply with the number of domains. The paper empirically reveals severe data redundancy—randomly discarding up to 90% of pre-training data causes only marginal downstream degradation—indicating that most source-domain graph data contributes little to cross-domain transfer while consuming substantial resources.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266123/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes K source-domain graphs with heterogeneous feature dimensions (projected to a unified 50-dim space via PCA) and a target-domain graph. Pre-training operates on a small set of 20 one-hop mixed subgraphs per iteration (10 inter-domain + 10 intra-domain) rather than full graphs, and adaptation requires only K scalar prompt weights (one per source domain) applied element-wise to target node features. The output is a frozen GNN encoder producing domain-invariant node or graph representations for few-shot classification.

## Method

MDGMIX identifies boundary nodes—those with small pairwise distance margins to multiple domain centers (via intersection of top-ρ candidates across all domain pairs)—and constructs challenging mixed subgraphs by pairing cross-domain boundary nodes via cosine similarity, interpolating center-node features at λ=0.5 while unioning topology. A hierarchical discrimination objective combines coarse-grained binary classification (intra- vs. inter-domain subgraphs) with fine-grained domain decomposition using a Gradient Reversal Layer and KL divergence to recover the continuous domain composition of each mixed sample, forcing the encoder to suppress domain-identifiable signals. For adaptation, the pretrained GNN is frozen and a lightweight prompt (learnable weighted sum of source-domain centers) modulates target node features, requiring only K trainable parameters. A theoretical generalization bound decomposes target error into approximation error (controlled by boundary sampling ratio), structural transfer gap, and sampling variance under weak dependence.

## Results

On 1-shot node classification across 8 datasets, MDGMIX achieves the best accuracy on every benchmark, with headline results of 46.83% on Cora, 44.56% on CiteSeer, 55.28% on PubMed, 68.40% on Photo, 54.10% on Computers, 22.92% on Squirrel, 27.20% on Chameleon, and 23.32% on OGBN-Arxiv. On 1-shot graph classification, MDGMIX similarly leads on all 8 datasets (e.g., 52.81% on Cora, 56.42% on PubMed, 65.42% on Photo, 24.32% on OGBN-Arxiv). The method also achieves the best time–memory efficiency trade-off during pre-training and requires only K learnable prompt-weight parameters during adaptation, far fewer than competing multi-domain foundation models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MDGMIX outperforms all 12 baselines on every dataset in both node and graph classification. Among the most relevant multi-domain graph pre-training baselines, the closest competitors are BRIDGE (best on CiteSeer at 38.68% and Computers at 53.61%), MDGFM (best on Photo at 67.35% and Chameleon at 26.65%), and MDGPT (best on Cora at 43.38%). MDGMIX's margins over the strongest multi-domain baseline range from +0.49 pp (Computers, vs. BRIDGE) to +5.88 pp (CiteSeer, vs. BRIDGE), with particularly notable gains on heterophilous graphs (Squirrel +0.58 pp, Chameleon +0.55 pp) and the large-scale OGBN-Arxiv (+1.66 pp over BRIDGE). Single-domain pre-training methods (GraphCL, SimGRACE) and graph prompting methods (GPF+, GraphPrompt, EdgePrompt+) are consistently weaker, confirming that cross-domain knowledge transfer is essential in this setting. On graph classification (Table 2), MDGMIX again leads all baselines, with the largest gap on OGBN-Arxiv (24.32% vs. 20.95% for GraphPrompt) and a clear advantage on Cora (52.81% vs. 52.37% for SAMGPT).

## Relation to Prior Work

MDGMIX extends the multi-domain graph pre-training paradigm established by GCOPE, MDGPT, SAMGPT, MDGFM, and BRIDGE, all of which jointly train on full source-domain graphs with domain-specific tokens or structural priors. The key departure is the empirical demonstration of severe data redundancy in this setting, which motivates replacing full-graph pre-training with boundary-aware subgraph mixing adapted from the Mixup literature (Zhang et al., 2017) and graph mixup variants. The hierarchical discrimination objective (coarse binary domain classification plus fine-grained adversarial domain decomposition via GRL) draws on classical domain generalization and domain adversarial network ideas, while the lightweight prompt-weighting adaptation mechanism replaces the domain-token or MoE-based adaptation modules used by prior multi-domain graph foundation models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that up to 90% of multi-domain pre-training data can be discarded with minimal performance loss challenges the prevailing assumption that scaling source-domain data is necessary for cross-domain generalization, and the boundary-focused approach offers a practical path to reducing pre-training cost as the number of source domains grows. However, the method is evaluated primarily on relatively small citation and e-commerce graphs (with OGBN-Arxiv and Reddit as larger-scale tests), uses a simple two-layer GCN encoder, and relies on PCA-based feature alignment that may not capture richer structural heterogeneity. Open questions include whether the boundary-node concept generalizes to highly heterogeneous or dynamic graph domains, whether the pairwise mixing strategy can be extended to more complex multi-domain compositions without the observed degradation, and whether the theoretical guarantees (which depend on Lipschitz encoder assumptions) hold under spectral normalization in practice.
