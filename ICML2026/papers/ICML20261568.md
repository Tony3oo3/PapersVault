## Problem

Standard strategic classification assumes users know the deployed classifier exactly, yet it remains unclear whether and why a system should commit to full disclosure. This paper studies the intermediate regime where regulation requires partial disclosure: the system reveals a set of possible classifiers (an ambiguity set) but privately selects which one to realize, and users respond under worst-case (max-min) reasoning. The central question is how the type and degree of ambiguity jointly affect learning accuracy and social outcomes, and how a regulator can constrain this freedom.

## Input / Output

The method takes tabular feature vectors x ∈ R^d with binary labels y ∈ {±1}, a cost scale α, and regulatory constraints on the ambiguity set (e.g., bounding ‖w̄−w̲‖_q ≤ β for continuous ambiguity or fixing k for discrete). It jointly outputs a realized linear classifier (w, b) and an ambiguity set Γ ⊇ {h} that must be truthful (contain the true h) and satisfy weak agreement (w^T v > 0 for all v ∈ Γ). The best-response mapping Δ^Γ(x) is computed as a projection onto the Area of Positivity (the intersection of all positive halfspaces in Γ), which is non-linear even when all classifiers in Γ are linear.

## Method

The paper introduces three ambiguity types of increasing complexity—offset (fixed w, range of b), discrete (k distinct classifiers), and continuous (w in a box [w̲, w̄])—and shows that max-min best responses reduce to convex projections onto the AoP. For continuous ambiguity, a cutting-plane algorithm with a closed-form separation oracle (Lemma 3: the worst weight vector is determined by the orthant of the query point) solves the exponentially-constrained projection in ~d iterations in practice. The key loss function is the ambiguous strategic hinge, which replaces the fixed strategic reward (2/α)‖w‖₂ with an input-dependent reward r_x(Γ) measuring directional distance to a non-linear effective decision boundary; this boundary is approximated by an outer polyhedral form F̃(x) = max{w^Tx+b, min_{v,a∈Γ} v^Tx+a+(2/α)‖v‖₂}, yielding a closed-form, differentiable loss under weak agreement. Training jointly optimizes h and Γ via gradient descent on this loss plus regulatory penalty terms, with softmax/softmin smoothing to allow gradients to flow through all possible classifiers.

## Results

On the synthetic separable dataset with discrete ambiguity, the proposed ambiguous strategic hinge achieves 99.2% strategic accuracy with k=3 possible classifiers, compared to 88.4% for the standard strategic hinge (k=1) and 62.9% for the non-strategic baseline. On the Folktables ACSEmployment task with continuous ambiguity, the method's accuracy increases with the permitted ambiguity degree β and plateaus at interim values, while the post-hoc baseline deteriorates to ~50% accuracy as β grows. Social burden is generally lower for the proposed method, and the social optimum is attained at a mid-degree of ambiguity rather than at the extremes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the standard strategic hinge loss (Levanon & Rosenfeld, 2022), which corresponds to the k=1 (no ambiguity) setting. On the synthetic separable dataset, the proposed method with k=3 outperforms this baseline by 10.8 percentage points (99.2% vs. 88.4% strategic accuracy), and even k=2 yields a 6.1-point gain (94.5% vs. 88.4%). On the Folktables real-data experiment with continuous ambiguity, the paper compares against a post-hoc baseline that first trains a fully transparent strategic model and then adds ambiguity by expanding weight intervals. As the permitted ambiguity β increases, the proposed method's accuracy improves and plateaus, while the post-hoc baseline degrades rapidly to ~50% accuracy and becomes highly sensitive to the norm type (q=1, 2, ∞). The proposed method also achieves consistently lower social burden across conditions, demonstrating that the jointly-learned ambiguity set is superior to naively adding opacity after training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the strategic classification framework (Hardt et al., 2016; Levanon & Rosenfeld, 2021, 2022) by introducing worst-case ambiguity from robust mechanism design (Bergemann & Morris, 2005; Gilboa & Schmeidler, 1989), where users face a set of possible classifiers and act under max-min utility. It departs from Bayesian strategic classification (Cohen et al., 2024b), which models classifier uncertainty probabilistically with a shared prior, and from missing-information settings (Ghalme et al., 2021; Bechavod et al., 2022), by instead letting the learner jointly optimize both the realized classifier and the ambiguity set it reveals. The technical toolkit draws on convex optimization (separation oracles, cutting-plane methods) and the generalized strategic hinge loss, adapted here to handle the non-linear effective decision boundaries induced by ambiguity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding that a 'right' (intermediate) amount of ambiguity is optimal for both accuracy and social burden challenges the binary framing of transparency vs. opacity in ML regulation, suggesting that bounded ambiguity can align system and user incentives. The result that ambiguity can push accuracy beyond the non-strategic optimum (by making non-separable data effectively separable through user behavior) is a notable departure from the standard view that strategic movement only degrades performance. Open questions include whether these results extend to complex (non-linear) models, how competition between systems affects equilibrium ambiguity levels, and whether the simulated user behavior assumptions hold in deployed settings. The practical scope is currently limited to linear classifiers with specific cost structures and one real dataset.
