## Problem

Point cloud encoders for imitation learning rely on MLPs that exhibit spectral bias, learning low-frequency functions first while high-frequency geometric details converge slowly or not at all. This prevents policies from learning the sharp decision boundaries required for high-precision manipulation tasks (e.g., deciding whether to insert a peg or reposition it), where observations differ only in fine spatial details. The paper asks whether a simple, non-parametric input transformation can resolve this limitation across diverse point cloud architectures.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes point clouds constructed by unprojecting depth images from M cameras (downsampled to 32,768 points at 1 cm voxel resolution), optionally RGB images tokenized by ConvNeXt V2 nano, and a text goal description embedded by a frozen CLIP RN-50. Each 3D Cartesian point is mapped to a 96-dimensional Fourier feature vector (3 coordinates × 2 sin/cos × 16 log-spaced frequency bands from 2 cm to 4 m). The output is an action chunk of horizon H (10 or 20 steps), generated by iteratively denoising Gaussian noise with a 10-step DDIM solver.

## Method

The core contribution is applying a NeRF-style axis-aligned Fourier feature mapping to the Cartesian coordinates of every point before it enters the point cloud encoder, amplifying subtle geometric differences that would otherwise be indistinguishable to a spectrally-biased MLP. This single non-parametric transformation is applied with minimal modification to five different encoder architectures (PointPatch, PointPatch-attn, PCM, DP3, PointTransformer), each of which tokenizes the point cloud and feeds observation tokens into a shared decoder-only transformer diffusion policy (EDM framework). The Fourier mapping uses L=16 log-spaced frequency bands between λ_max=4.0 m and λ_min=2.0 cm, and is paired with VariableJitter augmentation (sampling a per-cloud noise scale from U(0, σ_max)) to prevent overfitting to uninformative high frequencies. The approach requires no architectural redesign, no additional regularization, and is robust to hyperparameter choices, yielding up to 20% success rate gains on RoboCasa and 7% on ManiSkill3.

## Results

On RoboCasa (16 high-precision kitchen tasks), Fourier features boost the overall success rate of PointPatch from 13.2% to 33.9%, with individual tasks like CloseDrawer improving from 34% to 72% and TurnOffSinkFaucet from 28% to 63%. On ManiSkill3 (4 grasping/tool-use tasks), PointPatch improves from 48.6% to 55.8% and PCM from 63.5% to 64.9%. On four real-world tasks, the aggregate normalized score of PointPatch+RGB(pretrained) increases from 14.8% to 40.2%, with the largest gain on the Drawer task (0.31 → 1.63 out of 4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is each point cloud architecture with versus without Fourier features, revealing consistent gains across all tested encoders on RoboCasa (e.g., PointPatch +20.7 pts, DP3 +13.1 pts, PCM +11.1 pts) and moderate gains on ManiSkill3 (PointPatch +7.2 pts, PCM +1.4 pts), where the authors attribute the smaller effect to task saturation. Against the PointMap baseline (Jia et al., 2025a), Fourier-enhanced PointPatch (33.9%) substantially outperforms PointMap (39.5%) on RoboCasa, while on ManiSkill3 PointMap (63.6%) is competitive with Fourier-enhanced PointPatch (55.8%), which the authors attribute to the larger 500-demo training set used for ManiSkill3. On real-world tasks, the pretrained PointPatch+RGB encoder with Fourier features (40.2% normalized) dramatically outperforms the same architecture without Fourier features (14.8%), and also surpasses the non-pretrained PointPatch+RGB+FF (26.1% on RoboCasa), confirming that Fourier features remain effective even in complex multimodal settings. The RGB-only baseline (16.6% on RoboCasa, 64.5% on ManiSkill3) is consistently outperformed by all point cloud methods with Fourier features on RoboCasa, underscoring the value of explicit 3D geometry for high-precision manipulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Fourier feature mapping from NeRF (Mildenhall et al., 2021; Tancik et al., 2020) — originally designed to enable high-fidelity 3D scene reconstruction — as a drop-in input encoding for point cloud encoders in diffusion-based imitation learning. It builds on the PointPatch/PointMAE architecture family (Pang et al., 2022) and the score-based diffusion policy framework (Chi et al., 2023; Reuss et al., 2023), extending the limited prior use of Fourier features in IL (Wilcox et al., 2025, Adapt3R) into a systematic, architecture-agnostic evaluation. The key conceptual departure is arguing that the task-dependent performance gap of 3D-only policies is rooted in the spectral bias of MLP-based point cloud encoders, rather than in architectural design choices, and that a non-parametric input transformation resolves this without modifying the network itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Fourier features offer a trivially simple, non-parametric, and hyperparameter-robust modification that practitioners can adopt at essentially zero cost, making them a likely default for any point cloud encoder in IL pipelines. The finding that benefits persist even when fine geometric detail is removed from observations (suggesting improved learning dynamics beyond just high-frequency access) opens a question about whether spectral bias remediation helps optimization more broadly than the original NeRF motivation implies. Open questions include whether gradient-based wavelength optimization or adaptive frequency scheduling could further improve scalability, and whether the gains hold at the scale of billion-parameter foundation models trained on internet-scale data. The real-world validation on only four tasks and the reliance on relatively small demonstration sets (75–102) leave room for broader deployment testing.
