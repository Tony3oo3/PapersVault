## Problem

Anthropomorphic Misalignment Research (AMR) studies frequently make intent-level or mechanistic claims (e.g., 'the model is deceiving,' 'the model has self-preservation goals') based on evidence that only supports weaker behavioral observations. The field, still in a preparadigmatic state, lacks a shared framework for calibrating claims to evidence, leading to systematic overinterpretation of model behaviors that may be confounded by role-play, instruction ambiguity, or benign distribution shift.

## Input / Output

The paper takes as input existing AMR artifacts—pretrained deception probes (from Goldowsky-Dill et al.), fine-tuning datasets, LLM judge configurations, and model generations from Llama-3.1-8B and Llama-3.3-70B—and produces a three-level evidence taxonomy, a 12-point diagnostic checklist, and three empirical stress tests. The outputs are structured as methodological standards rather than a new model: the evidence framework maps claim types to required experimental designs, while the experiments quantify how much reported results shift under design variations (e.g., EM rates ranging 3.7%–12.9% under different judge configurations on identical generations).

## Method

The paper organizes AMR into a four-stage pipeline (target framing → data construction → experimental design → causal attribution) and identifies nine recurring failure modes (C1–C9) that systematically weaken evidential support at each stage. It proposes a three-level evidence hierarchy—L1 behavioral (what the model does under a specified setting), L2 functional (what safety-relevant downstream effect the behavior causes in deployment-plausible contexts), L3 causal-mechanistic (why it happens, requiring interventions with specificity controls)—where each level demands progressively stronger experimental designs. Three experiments demonstrate the practical stakes: (1) stress tests showing deception probes achieve 87–100% false positive rates on honest-labeled data with deception-like surface features (sarcasm, recital, epistemic-constraint personas), (2) OOD fine-tuning on benign datasets (aesthetic preferences, scatological themes) producing 4.5–5.9% EM rates comparable to explicitly malicious fine-tuning, and (3) evaluator sensitivity analysis showing EM rates shift from 3.7% to 12.9% depending solely on judge model, threshold inclusion rules, and score aggregation method. The paper concludes with 12 stage-specific recommendations (R1–R12) and a practical checklist that authors can use to verify their evidence level matches their claims before submission.

## Results

The paper's three experiments demonstrate that AMR findings are highly sensitive to methodological choices. In Experiment 1, EM misalignment rates range from 3.7% to 12.9% depending solely on judge model version, threshold boundary inclusion, and score aggregation procedure (Table 1). In Experiment 2, benign OOD fine-tuning on innocuous aesthetic or scatological datasets produces EM rates of 5.88% and 4.52% respectively, comparable to rates from explicitly malicious fine-tuning (4.01% in Kaczer et al., 2026). In Experiment 3, deception probes from Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2025) exhibit false positive rates of 87–100% on honest-labeled stress tests (Sarcasm, Wrong Answers Only, Recital), while the Roleplaying probe reaches 96% FPR on Medieval Peasant versus only 4% for the Instructed-Pairs probe.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it stress-tests existing AMR evaluation pipelines. The key comparison is between evaluation configurations: switching from argmax to weighted aggregation eliminates threshold-boundary sensitivity entirely (Δ drops to 0.00%), while changing the judge from GPT-4o-mini to GPT-5-mini shifts the Aesthetic OOD EM rate from 5.88% to 12.87% under raw scoring. For probe robustness, the Instructed-Pairs probe and Roleplaying probe diverge sharply on the Medieval Peasant stress test (4% vs. 96% FPR), indicating that training data composition dominates probe behavior more than the underlying deception signal. The benign OOD fine-tuning results (5.88%, 4.52%) are comparable to explicitly malicious fine-tuning baselines (4.01% in Kaczer et al., 2026; <10% in Turner et al., 2025), suggesting that reported EM effects may be confounded by generic safety-guardrail erosion rather than targeted misalignment mechanisms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes scattered methodological critiques from mechanistic interpretability (Sharkey et al. 2025, Méloux et al. 2025, Miller et al. 2024), animal cognition research (Rane et al. 2025, Summerfield et al. 2025), and evidence-based medicine (OCEBM, GRADE) into a unified pipeline-based framework for evaluating AMR claims. Rather than introducing a new alignment technique, it repurposes the evidence-hierarchy concept from clinical medicine into a three-level taxonomy (behavioral, functional, causal-mechanistic) and a 12-point diagnostic checklist mapped to four stages of the AMR experimental workflow. The empirical stress tests (probe FPRs on honest contexts, benign OOD fine-tuning, evaluator sensitivity analysis) are designed to ground the methodological arguments in concrete, reproducible evidence rather than purely conceptual critique.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The proposed evidence-level framework and checklist offer a practical calibration tool that could reduce overclaiming in AMR and improve the credibility of safety-relevant findings for policymakers. The finding that benign OOD fine-tuning (aesthetic preferences, scatological themes) produces EM rates (4.5–5.9%) comparable to explicitly malicious fine-tuning challenges the assumption that EM requires specific misaligned content, suggesting a need for stronger baselines in EM studies. Key open questions include whether the L1/L2/L3 taxonomy is granular enough for all AMR sub-areas, whether the conceptual challenges (C1–C2: underspecified anthropomorphic concepts) can be resolved without new theoretical tools from philosophy of mind, and whether the community will adopt the checklist as a de facto standard for peer review.
