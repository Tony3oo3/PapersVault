## Problem

The standard practice of applying a single uniform learning rate across all Transformer layers ignores the structural heterogeneity of LLMs, where Attention and FFN components exhibit fundamentally different spectral and optimization characteristics. Existing layerwise LR methods (LARS, LAMB, Sharpness) either were not designed for Transformer architectures or rely on exhaustive grid search, and critically, they fail to outperform uniform LR when the latter is tuned to its optimal value. The paper asks whether a principled, theory-grounded layerwise LR scheme can beat the optimally-tuned uniform baseline without additional hyperparameter tuning.

## Input / Output

During training, the method periodically (every 100 update steps / ~5.2M tokens) reads the weight matrices of every Transformer layer to compute the eigenvalues of each layer's correlation matrix W_l^T W_l, from which the PL_Alpha_Hill metric is derived via the Hill estimator (using the top n/2 eigenvalues). The output is a per-layer learning rate vector bounded within [η, sη] (with s=5 by default), which is then fed into the standard optimizer (AdamW or Muon) alongside a cosine decay schedule. The only user-facing hyperparameter beyond the global LR η is the scaling ratio s, and the method inherits the near-optimal η directly from the uniform baseline, eliminating the need for separate LR tuning.

## Method

LLR is grounded in Heavy-Tailed Self-Regularization (HT-SR) theory, which posits that layers with stronger heavy-tailedness in their empirical spectral density are already better trained. At each update interval, LLR computes the PL_Alpha_Hill exponent for every layer via power-law fitting of the ESD, then maps these values to per-layer LRs through a linear bounded scaling function: layers with higher α (less heavy-tailed, e.g., FFN and Embedding) receive proportionally larger LRs, while layers with lower α (more heavy-tailed, e.g., Attention q/k/v/o) receive smaller LRs. Three Transformer-specific designs make the scheme practical: (1) the Embedding and Output_Head layers are pinned to the upper bound sη since their PL_Alpha_Hill remains persistently high; (2) a soft-switch mechanism linearly interpolates over t_switch=50 steps to avoid destructive LR spikes when the target LR changes; and (3) the computationally expensive spectral updates are restricted to the first 20% of training tokens, after which PL_Alpha_Hill statistics stabilize. This yields up to 1.5× training speedup and consistent perplexity/accuracy gains across LLaMa (60M–3B) and GPT-nano architectures with both AdamW and Muon optimizers.

## Results

On LLaMa pre-training (FineWeb, Chinchilla scaling), LLR achieves the lowest validation perplexity across all model sizes: 20.30 (60M), 17.03 (135M), 12.71 (350M), and 9.59 (1B), consistently outperforming the Uniform baseline. On zero-shot commonsense reasoning (7 benchmarks), LLR raises average accuracy from 47.09% (Uniform) to 49.02% for LLaMa-1B and from 48.58% to 50.61% for LLaMa-3B at 30B tokens. LLR also delivers up to 1.5× training speedup, matching Uniform's performance at 4.5B/10.5B tokens while using only 3B/7B tokens.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LLR outperforms all eight baselines across every model scale tested. Against the classic layerwise methods LARS (52.52 vs 20.30 at 60M) and LAMB (23.53 vs 20.30 at 60M), LLR achieves substantially lower perplexity, with LARS and LAMB sometimes failing to converge at higher LRs. The Sharpness-based method (Wang et al., 2025) is the closest competitor, matching Uniform at 1B (9.77) but still trailing LLR (9.59); at smaller scales the gap widens (e.g., 23.28 vs 20.30 at 60M). Among more recent layerwise strategies, Adammini (21.85), Mup-AdamW (24.52), and CompleteP-AdamW (24.26) at 60M all underperform LLR (20.30), with Mup-AdamW and CompleteP-AdamW showing particularly large gaps. The two HT-SR-based methods—Alphadecay (21.58 at 60M, 17.35 at 135M) and TempBalance (21.54 at 60M, 17.19 at 135M)—share the same theoretical foundation but lack Transformer-specific adaptations, resulting in consistent but smaller gaps versus LLR (20.30 and 17.03 respectively). Notably, LLR inherits its base learning rate directly from the Uniform baseline, requiring no additional hyperparameter search, whereas Sharpness relies on exhaustive grid search and LARS/LAMB demand separate LR/WD tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Heavy-Tailed Self-Regularization (HT-SR) theory from its prior applications in model selection, pruning, and weight-decay allocation to the problem of layer-wise learning rate assignment during LLM pre-training. The closest conceptual ancestor is TempBalance (Zhou et al., 2023), which also uses HT-SR to modulate per-layer LRs but is restricted to CNNs and fine-tuning; LLR adapts the framework to Transformer architectures with three targeted designs (fixed embedding LR, soft LR switching, and a 20% active phase). It departs from norm-ratio methods (LARS, LAMB) and grid-search-based sharpness scheduling (Wang et al., 2025) by deriving LR ratios directly from spectral statistics rather than gradient/weight norms or manual tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is the near-zero tuning overhead: LLR inherits the base LR from the uniform baseline and requires no additional hyperparameter search, making it easy to drop into existing pre-training pipelines. The consistent 1.5× speedup and perplexity gains across 60M–3B parameters, two optimizers, and two architectures suggest the spectral heterogeneity it exploits is a structural property of Transformers rather than an artifact of a particular setup. Open questions include whether the 20% active-phase assumption and the (1,5) scaling range remain optimal at 7B+ scales, and how LLR interacts with emerging optimizers or longer training horizons beyond 100B tokens.
