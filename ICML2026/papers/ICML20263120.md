## Problem

Existing algorithms for solving infinite-horizon discounted MDPs with a generative model either ignore available prior knowledge about the transition matrix or require a known upper bound on the prediction error to safely incorporate it. This paper asks whether a black-box prediction of the transition matrix—whose accuracy is completely unknown and potentially adversarial—can be leveraged to improve the sample complexity bound for computing an ε-optimal policy, and establishes that no algorithm can beat the minimax bound Õ((1−γ)⁻³Nε⁻²) in the worst case without knowing the prediction quality.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives, in a preparation phase, a prediction matrix P̂ with the same dimensions as the true transition matrix P (each row is a valid probability distribution over states), along with the known DMDP parameters (state space, action sets, reward vector r ∈ [0,1], discount factor γ) and an initial distribution q. During the learning phase, it accesses a generative model that returns next-state samples for queried state-action pairs. The output is a stationary randomized policy π̄ constructed from the time-averaged dual variable, with the guarantee that its expected value is within ε of optimal. Notably, the algorithm is parameter-free: it requires no input specifying the desired accuracy ε or the prediction error Dist(P, P̂).

## Method

OpPMD (Optimistic-Predict Mirror Descent) solves the DMDP's minimax bilinear formulation by iteratively updating the value vector v and the dual variable μ via stochastic mirror descent. The key mechanism is a dual-gradient strategy on the μ-side: at each step, the algorithm combines a stochastic unbiased gradient (from sampled transitions, with variance reduced by averaging over all past samples) with a predicted gradient g̅_{t+1}^μ = (Î − γP̂)v_{t+1} − r that uses P̂ as a proxy for the unknown P. The μ-update uses the residual (g̃_t^μ − g̅_t^μ + g̅_{t+1}^μ) in an optimistic mirror descent step, so that when P̂ is accurate the effective gradient magnitude shrinks and convergence accelerates, while when P̂ is poor the residual reverts to the standard stochastic gradient. Adaptive learning rates of the form η ∝ 1/√(Σ‖residual‖²) make the algorithm parameter-free, eliminating dependence on T, ε, and Dist(P, P̂) in the implementation. The resulting sample complexity is Õ(max{(1−γ)⁻⁴|S|ε⁻², (1−γ)⁻⁴N·min{1,Dist²}ε⁻², (1−γ)⁻²Nε⁻¹}), which uniformly improves on the prior primal-dual bound Õ((1−γ)⁻⁴Nε⁻²) and, when Dist² = O((1−γ)²ε), beats the minimax bound Õ((1−γ)⁻³Nε⁻²).

## Results

The paper establishes two main theoretical results. First, an impossibility result (Theorem 3.2) showing that no algorithm can compute an ε-optimal policy with fewer than Õ((1−γ)^(−3) N ε^(−2)) samples without prior knowledge of the prediction error. Second, the proposed OpPMD algorithm (Theorem 4.5) achieves a sample complexity of Õ(max{(1−γ)^(−4)|S|ε^(−2), (1−γ)^(−4)N·min{1,Dist²}ε^(−2), (1−γ)^(−2)Nε^(−1)}), which is uniformly better than the previous best primal-dual bound of Õ((1−γ)^(−4)Nε^(−2)). In the special regime where Dist² ≤ O((1−γ)²ε) and |A| > Ω((1−γ)^(−1)), the bound improves to Õ((1−γ)^(−3)Nε^(−2)), matching the minimax-optimal rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's OpPMD algorithm is compared against two key prior bounds. Against the best prior primal-dual method (Jin & Sidford, 2020), which achieves Õ((1−γ)^(−4)Nε^(−2)), OpPMD is uniformly better: its bound reduces to Õ((1−γ)^(−4)N·min{1,Dist²}ε^(−2)) for the prediction-dependent term, which is strictly smaller whenever Dist < 1, and equals the prior bound only in the worst case Dist ≥ 1. Against the minimax-optimal non-primal-dual methods (Sidford et al., 2018; Wainwright, 2019) achieving Õ((1−γ)^(−3)Nε^(−2)), OpPMD matches this rate in the special regime where the prediction is sufficiently accurate (Dist² ≤ O((1−γ)²ε) and |A| > Ω((1−γ)^(−1))), and the impossibility result (Theorem 3.2) confirms that no algorithm can uniformly beat this rate without knowing the prediction error. The paper does not include empirical comparisons against baselines; validation is provided via numerical experiments in Appendix D demonstrating the practical benefit of predictions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the primal-dual stochastic mirror descent framework of Jin & Sidford (2020) for solving DMDPs by incorporating black-box predictions of the transition matrix, drawing on optimistic mirror descent (Rakhlin & Sridharan, 2013) for the prediction mechanism and parameter-free adaptive learning rates (Orabona, 2019) to eliminate the need for knowing the prediction error. It is positioned as the first work to introduce the 'Algorithms with Advice' paradigm into DMDP solving under a generative model, departing from prior RL-with-advice work (Golowich & Moitra, 2022; Feng et al., 2019) that requires known prediction error bounds. The impossibility result (Theorem 3.2) is inspired by the minimax lower bound of Gheshlaghi Azar et al. (2013) and the Pareto regret frontier for bandits (Lattimore, 2015), adapted to the prediction-augmented setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The impossibility result establishes a fundamental information-theoretic barrier: without knowing prediction quality, no algorithm can uniformly beat the minimax sample complexity bound, ruling out the natural 'test-then-use' strategy. The OpPMD algorithm provides a practical resolution by achieving a bound that gracefully interpolates between the prediction-free rate and a strictly better rate when predictions are accurate, all without requiring the user to specify prediction quality. Key limitations include the restriction to tabular DMDPs and the theoretical (rather than empirical) nature of the contribution; open questions include extending to function approximation, average-reward MDPs, and other prediction forms such as Q-value or dataset-based advice.
