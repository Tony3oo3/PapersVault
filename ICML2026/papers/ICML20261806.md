## Problem

Differentiating through discrete Poisson samples in latent variable models remains challenging, and while two relaxation methods exist—Exponential Arrival Time (EAT) and Gumbel-SoftMax (GSM)—no systematic comparison between them has been provided. The standard EAT method uses a sigmoid soft indicator whose infinite support causes substantial distributional bias and high sensitivity to the temperature hyperparameter, leaving practitioners without principled guidance on method selection or tuning.

## Input / Output

For the P-VAE task, the model takes image patches (whitened natural images at 28×28, ImageNet at 40×40) and produces reconstructed images plus continuous latent spike-count samples approximating Poisson counts. For the POGLM task, the model takes observed spike trains from visible neurons (e.g., 27 retinal ganglion cells over 100-bin segments) and infers latent connectivity weights and hidden-neuron spike counts. In both settings, the encoder outputs log-firing-rates that are exponentiated to form Poisson rate parameters, and the relaxation produces dense float samples (non-integer) that serve as differentiable stand-ins for discrete counts.

## Method

The core technical contribution replaces the sigmoid soft indicator in the EAT relaxation with a cubic smoothstep (Hermite interpolation) function that has compact support on [−1,1], eliminating spurious 'ghost spike' contributions from events far outside the counting interval. Using Campbell's theorem from point process theory, the authors derive closed-form moment expressions showing the cubic achieves exactly unbiased first moments (c(τ)=1) for all τ≤1, while the sigmoid inflates the mean by up to 31% at τ=1. The modified EAT_cubic is then systematically compared against EAT_sigmoid, GSM, score-function estimators, and exact gradients (available via closed-form ELBO for linear P-VAE decoders) across distributional fidelity (Wasserstein-1 distance), gradient quality (curvature-weighted bias and noise energy), and downstream ELBO performance on both P-VAE and POGLM tasks. The key empirical finding is that EAT_cubic maintains near-optimal performance across the full temperature range τ∈[0.02,0.5], whereas EAT_sigmoid and GSM degrade substantially at higher temperatures, making EAT_cubic the only method robust to hyperparameter choice.

## Results

EAT_cubic achieves an unbiased first moment (c(τ)=1 exactly for τ≤1) and retains ~73% of true Poisson variance at λ=100, τ=0.5, compared to <20% for EAT_sigmoid and ~60% for GSM. On the Wasserstein-1 distance to true Poisson at λ=100, τ=0.5, EAT_cubic achieves W₁≈0.2 versus W₁≈1.5 for EAT_sigmoid (a 7× improvement). In end-to-end P-VAE training on natural image patches, EAT_cubic matches the exact-gradient upper bound (ELBO≈−169) across all tested temperatures (τ∈{0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5}), while EAT_sigmoid and GSM degrade to ELBO≈−250 at τ=0.5. For POGLM on synthetic and real RGC data, EAT_cubic maintains stable ELBO≈−330 across all temperatures, whereas the other methods drop at τ≥0.2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against EAT_sigmoid (Vafaii et al., 2024), EAT_cubic wins decisively on distributional fidelity: it is exactly unbiased in mean for all τ≤1 while EAT_sigmoid inflates the mean by 31% at τ=1.0, and it retains 73% of true variance at λ=100, τ=0.5 versus <20% for EAT_sigmoid. EAT_cubic also wins on temperature robustness, maintaining near-optimal ELBO (≈−169) across all τ∈{0.02,…,0.5} in P-VAE, while EAT_sigmoid collapses to ELBO≈−250 at τ=0.5. However, EAT_sigmoid retains an edge on gradient NoiseEnergy (lower variance of individual gradient samples) and CosSample at high temperatures, owing to its C^∞ smoothness. Against GSM (Li et al., 2024a), EAT_cubic wins on variance fidelity (73% vs ~60% retained at λ=100, τ=0.5), on BiasEnergy at low temperatures (GSM is an order of magnitude worse at τ=0.02), and on temperature robustness in both P-VAE and POGLM. GSM's sole advantage is generalizability: it extends to arbitrary discrete distributions including under-dispersed regimes, whereas both EAT variants are restricted to Poisson (or Negative Binomial with modification).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on two independent 2024 works that each introduced a differentiable relaxation of the Poisson distribution for variational inference: the Exponential Arrival Time (EAT) method from the P-VAE (Vafaii et al., 2024) and the Gumbel-SoftMax (GSM) relaxation for POGLMs (Li et al., 2024a). The authors identify a critical limitation in the original EAT method—its sigmoid soft indicator has infinite support, causing substantial distributional bias—and replace it with a compactly-supported cubic smoothstep (Hermite interpolant), deriving closed-form moment expressions via Campbell's theorem from point process theory. The paper's broader positioning is as the first systematic head-to-head comparison of these two relaxation methods across distributional fidelity, gradient quality, and downstream task performance, filling a gap that left practitioners without guidance on method selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for the growing NeuroAI community working with Poisson latent variable models: EAT_cubic's temperature robustness eliminates the need for costly grid searches over τ, and its near-exact gradient quality (often matching closed-form exact gradients) makes it a reliable default. The conceptual contribution—that distributional fidelity and gradient quality are dissociable axes of relaxation quality, with the worst-fidelity method (EAT_sigmoid) actually producing the lowest-noise gradients—challenges the implicit assumption that better distributional approximation automatically yields better optimization. Open questions include whether these findings extend to non-linear decoders, larger-scale models, and whether the inherent sub-Poisson Fano factor of the relaxations has meaningful connections to biological neural variability (cortical neurons often exhibit F < 1). The GSM method remains the only option for arbitrary non-Poisson discrete distributions (e.g., under-dispersed regimes), so the two methods are complementary rather than one subsuming the other.
