## Problem

Existing LLM bias research focuses on removing pre-existing stereotypes from pretraining data, but overlooks how models can spontaneously generate entirely novel biases through sequential decision-making. This paper demonstrates that when LLMs are placed in multi-turn allocation tasks with exploration-exploitation tradeoffs, they develop new social biases about artificial demographic groups even when no inherent differences exist, and that more capable models stratify groups more severely than humans.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265034/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates as a stateful 40-round multi-turn dialogue: each round presents a job opening (drawn from four warmth×competence quadrants) and four candidates (one per demographic group), and the model selects one candidate, receiving binary success/failure feedback (Bernoulli(0.9) for all group-job pairs) before the next round. The output is the full sequence of demographic-to-job assignments, summarized by three metrics: Stratification Index (entropy gap from uniform allocation), Between-Group Divergence (mean JSD between group allocation distributions), and Group Assignment Stochasticity Index (JSD of the same group's distribution across independent runs, confirming biases are learned per-run rather than from training data).

## Method

The paper adapts an iterative hiring paradigm from the psychology literature (Bai et al., 2022a; 2025a) to probe LLMs, testing a broad set of models (GPT-3.5/4o/5, Claude 3 Haiku/4 Sonnet, Gemini 1.5/2.0/2.5 Flash, Llama 3.2/4, Qwen 2.5, OpenAI o3, DeepSeek-R1) under both direct and chain-of-thought prompting with n=30 runs each. Three complementary metrics (SI, BGD, GASI) are introduced to capture concentration, between-group divergence, and cross-run stochasticity of emergent stereotypes, with SI proven equivalent to mutual information under uniform job marginals. To identify mechanisms, the authors test three intervention categories: system-level (temperature, CoT, context compression), structural (alternative settings, lowered success probabilities, realistic job-wise priors, additional candidate features), and prompt steering (fairness instructions, shared values, explicit diversity bonus in the objective). The key finding is that explicitly adding a diversity term to the reward function—penalizing repeated hires of the same demographic for the same job—most robustly reduces stratification below both human and random-assignment baselines, while other interventions (CoT, temperature, context compression, generic fairness prompts) provide only marginal or inconsistent improvements.

## Results

Frontier LLMs develop emergent social biases in a 40-round iterative hiring task, producing mean Stratification Index (SI) = 1.39 and Between-Group Divergence (BGD) = 0.69, substantially exceeding human participants (SI = 0.84, BGD = 0.56), fair random assignment (SI = 0.25, BGD = 0.29), and Thompson sampling with a uniform prior (SI = 0.61, BGD = 0.47). Reasoning models stratify most extremely (OpenAI o3: SI = 1.83, BGD = 0.80; DeepSeek-R1: SI = 1.41, BGD = 0.71), and newer/larger models within each family consistently stratify more than predecessors. Group Assignment Stochasticity Index (GASI) = 0.52 (vs. 0.47 for humans) confirms biases are learned within each run rather than reflecting fixed priors. Explicitly adding a diversity term to the objective function is the most robust intervention, reducing SI below the random-assignment baseline for nearly all models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to human participants (SI = 0.84, BGD = 0.56), all frontier LLMs produce significantly more stratified allocations (mean SI = 1.39, BGD = 0.69), with Claude 4 Sonnet (direct) reaching SI = 1.79 and reasoning models (o3, DeepSeek-R1) exceeding even that. Against the fair random-assignment baseline (SI = 0.25, BGD = 0.29), LLMs are far more biased, and even Thompson sampling with a uniform prior (SI = 0.61, BGD = 0.47) outperforms all LLMs in the default setting. UCB (Auer et al., 2002) is compared in Appendix E as an additional bandit-algorithm baseline. Notably, the relationship with the BBQ bias benchmark is inverted: models scoring higher on BBQ (a single-turn stereotype-association test) instead segregate more extremely in the multi-turn hiring task, indicating that existing bias benchmarks fail to capture emergent allocational bias. The diversity-objective prompt steer is the only intervention that drives most models below the random-assignment SI threshold, outperforming CoT prompting, temperature scaling, context compression, and value-based system prompts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the psychology literature on emergent stereotype formation through exploration-exploitation tradeoffs (Bai et al., 2022a; 2025a) from human participants to LLM agents, adapting the iterative hiring paradigm to a multi-turn dialogue setting. It departs from the dominant LLM bias literature, which focuses on detecting and removing pre-existing stereotypes from pretraining data (word embeddings, BBQ, StereoSet), by demonstrating that LLMs can generate entirely novel biases about artificial groups with no prior associations. The work also connects to the multi-armed bandit literature on exploration-exploitation, positioning LLMs as optimizers whose increasing capability paradoxically amplifies stratification rather than reducing it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that more capable models (higher BBQ scores) stratify more severely in sequential decision-making directly contradicts trends on existing single-turn bias benchmarks, suggesting current evaluations are insufficient for agentic deployments where LLMs make repeated, stateful decisions. The practical implication is that as LLMs are embedded in hiring, allocation, and resource-distribution pipelines, their default reward-maximizing behavior will create new social stratifications unless objectives explicitly incorporate diversity. Key open questions include how to design intrinsic (rather than prescriptive) interventions that discourage harmful pattern-matching without suppressing reasoning, and whether these findings hold when underlying group success rates are genuinely unequal due to covariates.
