## Problem

A widely held but unexamined intuition treats repeated contextual assertions as accumulating evidence that will eventually override a model's original prediction. Prior work examines only finite prompt manipulations and observable answer flips, leaving the asymptotic behavior of internal inference dynamics under unbounded repetition entirely uncharacterized. This paper asks: does LLM inference drift without bound under repeated contextual signals, or does it converge to a stable regime?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single-round prompt of the form y‖x‖x‖…‖x‖z, where y is a fixed query, x is a verbatim-repeated assertion block (N copies), and z is a suffix answer cue; the target position τ_N = m + NT + j always indexes the same suffix token z_j while its absolute position grows linearly with N. The output is a layer-wise converged hidden-state trajectory, a scalar answer-shift metric (the logit gap between a reference and a bias-target answer projected onto the residual stream), and a binary prediction of whether unbounded repetition will flip the model's answer. A Monte Carlo estimator recovers the N→∞ limit from a single forward pass at large finite N, avoiding the need to actually run unbounded-length inference.

## Method

The core contribution is a convergence theorem for decoder-only transformers with RoPE: attention logits are shown to be finite trigonometric polynomials in the relative distance δ = τ − t, which makes the softmax-weighted sums over the repeated loop region amenable to Cesàro averaging. The proof proceeds by layer-wise induction—base-layer head outputs converge because loop key/value vectors are exactly periodic, multi-head aggregation and FFN continuity lift this to full-layer convergence, and an asymptotic-periodicity lemma preserves the near-periodic loop structure needed to reapply the Cesàro argument at every subsequent layer. For practical prediction, the paper defines an answer-shift metric g_L(e_a, e_b) = ⟨Δ_{e_a,e_b}, E_L(τ_N)⟩ that decomposes linearly into per-layer attention and FFN contributions, and estimates its N→∞ limit via a Monte Carlo loop estimator that samples (copy-offset, template-index) pairs and rescales, yielding an unbiased approximation from one forward pass. The sign of the limiting metric determines whether unbounded repetition can flip the prediction, providing a mechanistic criterion for when contextual persuasion succeeds, saturates, or provably fails.

## Results

At N=1000 repetitions, the fraction of layers with effectively converged answer-shift dynamics exceeds 90% for most models at the 0.1 tolerance level (e.g., Falcon3-7B-Base: 98.1% attention / 97.3% FFN on OpenBookQA; Qwen3-4B: 99.5% / 99.4%), dropping to 78–87% at the stricter 0.01 tolerance. The representation-level estimator predicts answer preference changes with mean ACC of 86.1% and F1 of 92.0% in the TRANSFER regime, 80.0% / 84.6% in MISLEAD, and 82.2% / 89.8% in CORRECT at N=1000. KL divergence of next-token predictive distributions plateaus across all models, confirming that repetition induces a stable limiting answer distribution rather than unbounded drift.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's only direct quantitative comparison is with the amplification metric (Amp = MR@5/MR@1) from Xu et al. (2024, Farm benchmark), reported alongside the paper's own representation-based stability measure in Table 3. The two metrics show broadly consistent trends: models with high Amp (e.g., Qwen2.5-1.5B at 4.25 on SYCON, Falcon3-3B-Base at ∞) correspond to low stability scores (0.13 and 0.49 respectively), while models with Amp ≈ 1.0 (Falcon3-7B-Base, Mistral-7B-v0.1, Apollo-1-4B) show moderate-to-high stability scores (1.79–4.93). The alignment is not perfect—Qwen3-4B shows Amp ≈ 1.0 across all four benchmarks yet the highest stability score (6.28–8.42), suggesting it is more susceptible to repetition than the amplification metric alone would predict. The paper positions its framework as complementary rather than competitive: it provides a mechanistic, representation-level account of *why* and *when* repetition effects saturate, going beyond the answer-level outcome metrics used in prior sycophancy and persuasion evaluations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant empirical approach to studying contextual sensitivity (Yan et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2025), which examines finite prompt manipulations and observable answer flips, by introducing a formal asymptotic convergence framework for transformer inference under unbounded repetition of an identical assertion. It also distinguishes itself from the in-context-learning-as-Bayesian-inference line (Xie et al., 2021), which assumes independent examples accumulate evidence, whereas here the same assertion is repeated without new information. The theoretical machinery draws on the finite-frequency structure of RoPE logits (Su et al., 2024; Barbero et al., 2024) and Cesàro averaging, but applies them to a prediction-task setting that prior formal analyses of transformer asymptotics (Noci et al., 2022; Hayase et al., 2025) have not addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that contextual repetition induces bounded, query-dependent inference dynamics rather than unbounded evidence accumulation has direct implications for evaluation robustness, safety-critical deployments, and prompt design: it means some queries are provably resistant to repetition-based manipulation while others are not, and this can be predicted from representation-space analysis at a single finite N. Limitations include the single-round setting (no multi-turn interaction), reliance on RoPE as the sole positional mechanism, and the infinite-context-window assumption in the theory. Open questions include whether the convergence rate is practically informative for finite-N settings, how the picture changes for models without RoPE or with learned positional encodings, and whether the framework extends to multi-turn persuasion where new information is introduced at each turn.
