## Problem

Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) suffer from hallucination largely due to language bias—the tendency to prioritize linguistic fluency over visual consistency. While this phenomenon has been observed empirically, prior work lacks a formal definition and systematic analysis of its root cause. This paper identifies modality misalignment during training (in both Visual Instruction Tuning and DPO) as the underlying mechanism, where models over-rely on text-only likelihood at the expense of balanced multimodal alignment.

## Input / Output

The methods operate entirely at the training level, modifying the loss functions of standard LVLM training stages without altering the input/output interface. At inference time, the model still takes an RGB image and a text instruction as input and produces a free-form text response. The key practical constraint is that both LBR and LBP require a frozen reference model (the pre-training checkpoint) to compute the language bias term, but introduce no additional data, auxiliary models, or architectural changes.

## Method

The paper formalizes language bias as B = log(π_θ(y|x) / π_ref(y|x)), quantifying the model's performance gain from text-only conditioning relative to a reference model. For Visual Instruction Tuning, Language Bias Regularization (LBR) adds an L1 penalty |B| to the standard MLE loss, constraining the text-only output distribution to remain close to the reference model. For DPO training, Language Bias Penalty (LBP) adds a sigmoid-based penalty −log σ(β·log(π_ref(y|x)/π_θ(y|x))) that actively pushes the language bias toward negative values, encouraging the model to 'unlearn' bias acquired during prior instruction tuning. Both methods are hyperparameter-light (α = 1×10⁻⁵ for LBR, γ = 1 for LBP) and require no additional data or auxiliary models, making them easily deployable within existing training pipelines.

## Results

On hallucination benchmarks, LBP (trained on RLHF-V, 5.7K pairs) achieves a MMHalBench Score of 2.91 (7B) and 3.01 (13B), reducing the hallucination rate to 0.43 and 0.42 respectively, while cutting the AMBER Generative Task hallucination rate to 18.5% (7B) and 16.6% (13B). On Object HalBench, LBP achieves CHAIR_s of 12.3 (7B) and 10.7 (13B), and CHAIR_i of 6.3 and 4.2. For LBR on general benchmarks, consistent gains are observed across 16+ metrics: e.g., MME improves from 1490→1525 (7B), SQA from 66.8→69.4 (7B), and MMStar from 33.6→33.9 (7B), with the 13B model gaining +1.4 on MMStar (34.2→35.6) and +0.6 on SQA (71.5→72.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among directly comparable baselines (same model + data), LBP outperforms V-DPO by +0.75 MMHalBench Score (2.91 vs 2.16) and reduces the AMBER Generative hallucination rate from 27.3% to 18.5% on the 7B model. Against MFPO, LBP gains +0.22 MMHalBench Score (2.91 vs 2.69) and lowers the AMBER Gen hallucination rate from 22.5% to 18.5% (7B), while also achieving the best Object HalBench CHAIR_s (12.3 vs 13.4). On the 13B model, LBP edges out MFPO on MMHalBench Score (3.01 vs 2.94) and AMBER Gen hallucination rate (16.6% vs 19.4%), though MFPO retains a slight advantage on AMBER Gen coverage (56.1 vs 51.5). Against training-free methods (Table 19), LBP substantially outperforms VCD (2.14), OPERA (2.19), and VISTA (2.55) on MMHalBench Score, and achieves the lowest Object HalBench CHAIR_s (12.3 vs 48.4/45.6/26.7). For LBR, the method consistently improves over the vanilla VIT baseline across all 16 general benchmarks tested (e.g., +35 MME, +2.6 SQA, +2.0 MMStar on 7B), with no regressions observed. Notably, with only 5.7K preference pairs, LBP-aligned LLaVA-v1.5-7B matches or exceeds GPT-4V on two AMBER sub-tasks and Object HalBench CHAIR_i (6.3 vs 7.3).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the LLaVA v1.5 visual instruction tuning pipeline and the DPO preference optimization framework (with the mDPO margin loss), identifying a shared failure mode in both: a modality misalignment that causes the model to over-rely on text-only likelihoods during training. Unlike prior hallucination-mitigation work that proposes architectural changes, additional training data, or inference-time decoding strategies, this paper formalizes language bias as a quantitative quantity (the log-ratio of text-only likelihood gain) and addresses it with a single additional loss term at each training stage. It departs from the broader DPO-variant literature (V-DPO, MFPO, OPA-DPO) by targeting the bias mechanism itself rather than modifying the preference signal or data selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled, quantitative decomposition showing that both VIT and DPO training objectives inherently drive language bias, with DPO potentially *worsening* it (text-only gain outpacing multimodal gain for preferred responses). The proposed fixes are remarkably lightweight—no extra data, no auxiliary models, no architectural changes—making them easy to adopt in existing LVLM training pipelines. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to architectures where the VIT stage is not publicly reproducible (e.g., Qwen), whether the CHAIR metric's known limitations mask the true magnitude of gains, and whether dynamic scheduling strategies (cosine annealing of α) can be further optimized for larger-scale models.
