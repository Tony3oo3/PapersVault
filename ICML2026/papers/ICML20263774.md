## Problem

Large-scale time series models are built on the assumption that scaling up parameters and data improves forecasting, yet scattered observations show larger models often underperform smaller ones. This paper formalizes the phenomenon as the 'scaling paradox,' provides the first systematic diagnosis of its root cause, and proposes a practical remedy—addressing a gap where prior observations were treated as isolated anomalies rather than a systemic issue.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263774/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes trained Transformer-based TS models (LLM4TS or TSFMs) and extracts per-layer hidden representations H^l and per-head attention matrices A_i^l during inference on validation data. It produces a ranked list of critical layers via an importance score, then outputs a pruned model retaining ~20–30% of layers (followed by fine-tuning) that forecasts 96-step horizons from 336-step patch-based inputs. The pruning criterion combines inter-layer Euclidean distance, exponentially-decayed cosine similarity to K preceding layers, and intra-layer attention-head pairwise similarity, with a top-85% distance gate applied before final ranking.

## Method

The core contribution is a critical-layer identification and pruning method grounded in the discovery of 'few-layer dominance': only a small subset of layers actively transform representations while the rest behave as near-identity mappings. The importance score I^l = 1{l∈Top}·(1−R^l)·(1−s̄^l) uses a multiplicative (gating) form rather than a weighted sum, so that a layer must be both distinctive from its predecessors (low R^l) and internally diverse across attention heads (low s̄^l) to be retained. A theoretical analysis shows that strong temporal autocorrelation in TS induces representational saturation, yielding weak gradients for non-dominant layers that weight decay then drives toward identity mappings—explaining why added depth adds redundancy rather than capacity. After pruning, the model is fine-tuned to realign the remaining layers, and the approach is validated on 8 SOTA models (FSCA, CALF, Time-LLM, OFA, Sundial, Chronos, Moirai, TimesFM) spanning encoder-only, decoder-only, and encoder-decoder paradigms, achieving up to 2.7× speedup with ≤0.6% worst-case accuracy loss.

## Results

The paper demonstrates a scaling paradox in large-scale TS models: across two model families (LLM4TS and TSFMs) at four scales (100M–1.7B parameters), larger models consistently underperform or match smaller ones on MAE/MSE across 8+ datasets. Their critical-layer pruning method retains only ~21% of parameters (minimum) while achieving up to 12% accuracy improvement and 2.7× inference speedup on their own models (Table 5). When validated on 8 external SOTA models (90M–6B parameters), retaining <30% of layers on average yields comparable or superior accuracy in over 95% of tasks, with the worst-case degradation kept below 0.5%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The pruning method is validated against 8 external SOTA models. For LLM4TS baselines: FSCA shows a 77% win rate (pruned beats original in 77% of dataset-horizon combinations) with 1.53× speedup; CALF achieves 88% win rate with 1.48× speedup; Time-LLM(G) reaches 77% win rate with 1.57× speedup; and OFA achieves 73% win rate with 1.51× speedup. For TSFMs baselines: SundialLarge shows 92% win rate with 2.87× speedup; ChronosBase achieves a perfect 100% win rate with 2.64× speedup; MoiraiLarge reaches 92% win rate with 2.33× speedup; and TimesFM achieves 100% win rate with 3.09× speedup. In all cases, the pruned models retain fewer than 30% of layers on average while matching or exceeding the full model's accuracy, with the largest performance loss in any single case being below 1%. The method also outperforms both the full unpruned backbone and random layer selection of equal count (Table 8), confirming that the identified critical layers are genuinely more informative than arbitrary subsets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two parallel lines of TS research—LLM4TS (Time-LLM, OFA, FSCA, CALF) and TSFMs (Moirai, Chronos, TimesFM, Sundial)—and on the broader redundancy-analysis literature in LLMs (ShortGPT, LLM-Pruner). However, it departs from both: whereas prior redundancy studies in NLP still observe positive scaling with model size, this paper shows that in TS models scaling produces a paradox where larger models consistently underperform. It also moves beyond the coarse-grained layer-truncation strategy used by FSCA, OFA, and CALF by proposing a principled, representation-based criterion for identifying which specific layers to retain, rather than simply discarding early layers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that retaining only ~21–30% of layers can improve accuracy by up to 12% while yielding 2.7× inference speedup is immediately actionable for practitioners deploying TS models, and the validation across 8 SOTA models spanning three architectural paradigms and three tokenization levels strengthens generalizability. The theoretical analysis (autocorrelation → weak gradients → near-identity layers) provides a mechanistic explanation that could inform future architectural design, e.g., training fewer but more effective layers from the start. Open questions include whether the phenomenon persists in next-generation TS architectures (e.g., mixture-of-experts like Time-MoE), whether the pruning criterion transfers to non-Transformer backbones, and whether the scaling paradox is specific to the forecasting task or extends to other TS tasks like anomaly detection or imputation.
