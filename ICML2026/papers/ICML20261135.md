## Problem

Existing evaluations of GNNs under missing node features rely on benchmarks with extremely sparse features (90–99% zeros) and overly benign MCAR missingness mechanisms, making all models appear robust and preventing meaningful comparison. The paper proves that high feature sparsity fundamentally bounds the information loss from missingness, and shows that realistic MNAR mechanisms and train-test distribution shifts expose weaknesses that standard protocols hide.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261135/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes zero-filled node feature vectors concatenated with their binary missingness mask (doubling the feature dimension), along with the graph adjacency matrix, and produces node class labels or regression values. The missingness mask is deterministically constructed from the raw data where missing entries are explicitly marked, so no uncertainty is introduced at the mask level. On RelBench, the same MIM augmentation is applied to heterogeneous relational graphs with naturally occurring missing values across numeric and textual features.

## Method

GNNmim implements the Missing Indicator Method (MIM) for graphs by concatenating each node's zero-filled feature vector with its binary missingness mask and feeding the result to an otherwise standard GNN (GCN, GraphSAGE, or GIN, selected per dataset). Unlike imputation-based approaches (mean, median, PCFI, FairAC, GOODIE, GSPN), GNNmim performs no learned reconstruction of missing values and makes no MAR assumptions, so it remains valid under MNAR mechanisms and train-test missingness distribution shifts. The paper complements this method with an information-theoretic bound (Theorem 3.2) showing that under U-MCAR on binary features, the mutual information loss is bounded by −ndμ·h₂(E[s(X)]), which vanishes as sparsity approaches 1. Four new datasets with dense, semantically meaningful features (SYNTHETIC, AIR, ELECTRIC, TADPOLE) and five missingness protocols (U-MCAR, S-MCAR, LD-MCAR, FD-MNAR, CD-MNAR) plus a distribution-shift regime (R2) are introduced to enable meaningful evaluation. Across all settings, GNNmim achieves consistently high AUC and F1 scores, matching or exceeding specialized architectures while adding negligible computational overhead.

## Results

On the four proposed dense-feature datasets (SYNTHETIC, AIR, ELECTRIC, TADPOLE), GNNmim achieves the highest or near-highest mean F1 across all five missingness mechanisms (U-MCAR, S-MCAR, LD-MCAR, FD-MNAR, CD-MNAR), e.g., 0.81 F1 on AIR under U-MCAR and 0.81 on TADPOLE under CD-MNAR. On the classic sparse benchmarks (CORA, CITESEER, PUBMED), all methods maintain F1 > 0.85 until ~80–90% missingness, confirming the paper's theoretical bound that sparsity masks information loss. On RelBench with naturally occurring missingness, RDLmim improves MAE by 2.9% on REL-EVENT (0.2553 vs 0.2628), 11.5% on REL-TRIAL site-success (0.3746 vs 0.4235), and ROC-AUC by 1.7% on REL-F1 (0.768 vs 0.755), while RDL is marginally better on the nearly-complete REL-ARXIV control (0.5033 vs 0.5051 MAE).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GNNmim (the proposed MIM-augmented GNN) achieves the highest or tied-highest F1 in 17 out of 20 dataset–mechanism cells on the four proposed datasets, with the largest margins under MNAR mechanisms: e.g., 0.79 vs 0.47 (GOODIE) and 0.58 (GSPN) on TADPOLE CD-MNAR, and 0.82 vs 0.53 (FairAC) on AIR LD-MCAR. FairAC is the only competitor that beats GNNmim on any cell, winning on ELECTRIC S-MCAR (0.87 vs 0.77) and ELECTRIC U-MCAR (0.86 vs 0.80), but degrades sharply under FD-MNAR on SYNTHETIC (0.64 vs 0.76). GOODIE and GSPN, which rely on label propagation and sum-product networks respectively, show the largest performance drops under CD-MNAR (0.43–0.59 range), confirming their sensitivity to class-dependent missingness. Simple imputation baselines (GNNmi, GNNzero, GNNmedian) cluster tightly together and are consistently outperformed by GNNmim by 1–5 F1 points across mechanisms. On RelBench, RDLmim outperforms the RDL baseline on all four datasets with non-negligible missingness (9–24% missing), with the largest gain on REL-TRIAL site-success (MAE 0.3746 vs 0.4235, a 11.5% relative improvement), while RDL is marginally better on the nearly-complete REL-ARXIV control (0.5033 vs 0.5051 MAE), supporting the claim that the MIM mask is most beneficial when missingness is actually present.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the established line of GNN methods for missing node features (mean imputation, PCFI, GOODIE, FP, FairAC, GCNmf, GSPN) and the classical missing-data theory of Rubin (MCAR/MAR/MNAR), but departs sharply in its evaluation framing. It repurposes the Missing Indicator Method (MIM) from tabular supervised learning (Van Ness et al., 2023) into the graph domain, arguing that prior GNN-specific architectures are unnecessary when the missingness mask is simply concatenated to the input. The theoretical contribution (an information-theoretic bound on mutual information loss under sparsity) is novel and directly challenges the use of BoW/TF-IDF benchmarks like CORA and CITESEER for this problem, aligning with recent calls for better graph benchmark design (Bechler-Speicher et al., 2025; Coupette et al., 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central finding—that standard sparse benchmarks provably cannot differentiate methods under missingness—invalidates a large body of prior empirical comparisons and calls for the field to adopt dense, semantically meaningful datasets and realistic (MNAR, distribution-shift) missingness protocols. The practical takeaway that a lightweight MIM augmentation matches or beats specialized architectures across all tested regimes is actionable and low-cost, but the paper is limited to node-level tasks with observed graph structure and labels, leaving link prediction, graph classification, and missing-edge settings open. The authors explicitly call for larger benchmarks purpose-built for missing-feature robustness, and the RelBench validation suggests the MIM approach generalizes to industrial-scale relational data with unknown missingness mechanisms.
