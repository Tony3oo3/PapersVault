## Problem

Safety alignment of LLMs is fragile: fine-tuning on as few as 100 benign samples can erase safety behaviors learned from millions of preference examples. Existing mechanistic analyses only compare pre- and post-fine-tuning parameter states or measure perturbation magnitude without direction, failing to capture the dynamic, direction-specific drift that actually drives safety degradation. Consequently, no method provides a continuous, corpus-wide risk score for individual benign samples that operates in parameter space and is agnostic to toxicity classifiers.

## Input / Output

Given a training sample z=(x,y), the method computes a one-step LoRA gradient at a direction-sensitive initialization state, then projects the module-wise L2-normalized update onto pre-constructed safety (DPO on PKU-SafeRLHF) and danger (SFT on Aegis/Beaver-Tails unsafe subsets) direction vectors. The output is a single continuous scalar per sample: the sum across all LoRA-augmented modules of (danger projection − safety projection). Unlike discrete subset-selection baselines, SQSD scores every sample in the corpus, and the score is interpretable via first-order Taylor approximation as the difference in loss change toward the danger versus safety parameter state.

## Method

SQSD (Sample-Level Quantification of Safety Degradation) quantifies each sample's fine-tuning risk by measuring how its induced parameter update aligns with danger versus safety directions in parameter space. Concretely, it (1) computes the one-step gradient-descent update of LoRA parameters A and B for sample z, yielding ΔW(z) ≈ B₀∇A + ∇B·A₀; (2) applies module-wise L2 normalization to ΔW_m(z) to eliminate response-length bias; and (3) aggregates the projection gap ⟨ΔŴ_m, V̂_danger,m⟩ − ⟨ΔŴ_m, V̂_safety,m⟩ across all M LoRA modules. The key insight is that benign fine-tuning causes cumulative drift toward danger-aligned directions while safety projections remain near zero, so the directional gap isolates safety-specific degradation from generic parameter movement. A first-order Taylor approximation links the inner product to the loss difference between the danger and safety model states, providing a preference-based interpretation. Reliable scoring requires initializing at direction-sensitive parameter states, identified via linear-path sensitivity for the safety direction and drift-enhanced sensitivity from fine-tuning checkpoints for the danger direction.

## Results

On CategoricalHarmfulQA, SQSD achieves an average ASR gap (Δ = S1 − S5) of 49.86% across 12 model/dataset/direction configurations, significantly exceeding the best baseline (Reward Model: 43.76%). SQSD produces monotonically decreasing ASR from highest-risk to lowest-risk subsets in 10/12 settings, whereas all baselines achieve monotonicity in at most 1/6. For example, SQSD(Beaver) on Qwen3-8B/Dolly yields ASR of 71.27% → 2.55% (Δ = 68.72) with strict monotonicity, while the same setting gives non-monotonic or negative-Δ results for every baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SQSD decisively outperforms all four baselines on both monotonicity and discriminative power. Against the Reward Model (the strongest baseline, avg Δ = 43.76%), SQSD achieves a higher average Δ (49.86%) and far superior monotonicity (10/12 vs. 1/6), indicating that directly scoring sample safety with a reward model captures only part of the fine-tuning risk signal. Bi-Anchor (both Reps and Grad variants) frequently produces negative Δ values (e.g., −16.00 on Qwen3-8B/Dolly for Reps), meaning it ranks genuinely low-risk samples above high-risk ones; SQSD never exhibits this failure. Self-Inf-N, which scores by gradient magnitude, also yields negative Δ in multiple settings (−22.91 on Qwen3-Alpaca, −4.73 on Llama3-Dolly), confirming that raw gradient norm is a poor proxy for safety-specific risk. LARF achieves a high Δ in one configuration (83.09 on Qwen3-8B/Dolly) but fails monotonicity in all 6 settings, showing it can identify extreme outliers but cannot produce a reliable continuous risk ordering across the corpus.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Task Vector formulation (Ilharco et al., 2023) to construct safety and danger directions in parameter space, and extends the line of work on safety degradation during fine-tuning (Qi et al., 2023; He et al., 2024; Guan et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b) by shifting from static pre/post comparisons to dynamic trajectory tracking. It departs from prior sample-level influence methods (Bi-Anchor, Self-Inf-N, LARF) that identify discrete high-risk subsets, instead proposing continuous corpus-wide risk scoring grounded in directional parameter drift. The first-order Taylor approximation connecting parameter updates to output preferences follows the analytical framework of Ren & Sutherland (2025) and He et al. (2024), but applies it specifically to the safety/danger direction contrast.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SQSD provides practitioners with a concrete, transferable tool to assess sample-level fine-tuning risk before deployment, addressing a gap where benign samples evade traditional toxicity filters. The mechanistic finding that safety degradation corresponds to cumulative drift toward danger directions while safety directions remain unchanged offers a clear diagnostic for understanding and potentially preventing alignment erosion. Key limitations include dependence on direction-sensitive initialization states and the computational cost of gradient computation per sample. Open questions include constructing more universally informative parameter directions and integrating continuous risk scores into safety-preserving fine-tuning algorithms that weight samples by their degradation potential.
