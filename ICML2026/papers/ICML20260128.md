## Problem

Despite widespread use of embedding-based representations and empirical observations of sharp accuracy drops under aggressive dimension truncation, no theoretical characterization existed for how embedding dimension d affects triplet accuracy. This paper proves that dimensionality acts as a sharp bottleneck: below a constant fraction of the ground-truth dimension D, accuracy collapses to the trivial 50% baseline even on perfectly realizable instances, and this limitation is both information-theoretic and computationally intractable to overcome.

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of m triplet constraints (i, j, k) over n items encoding the comparison ‖f(i)−f(j)‖ < ‖f(i)−f(k)‖, along with a target embedding dimension d, and produces a d-dimensional embedding f: V → R^d scored by the fraction of satisfied triplets. Unlike metric embedding which preserves absolute distances, the task only requires preserving ordinal distance comparisons, making it a strictly easier problem that nonetheless exhibits the collapse phenomenon. The theoretical results hold for m = Θ(Dn) triplets with D = o(√n), and the hardness result applies to near-realizable instances even in D = 1.

## Method

The paper establishes two complementary results. For the information-theoretic lower bound, it uses the probabilistic method to construct random triplet instances with Θ(Dn) constraints, proving realizability in D dimensions via a reduction to directed-graph acyclicity (using Bilu–Linial's lemma) combined with arboricity bounds on the constraint graph (via Avdiukhin et al.'s embedding algorithm), while proving accuracy collapse in d ≤ cD dimensions by bounding the VC-dimension of the d-dimensional embedding hypothesis class at Θ(dn) and applying uniform convergence to show empirical risk concentrates around the 1/2 true risk. For the computational hardness result, it provides a gap reduction from Maximum Acyclic Subgraph to triplet embeddings under the Unique Games Conjecture, showing that no polynomial-time algorithm—regardless of the dimension it uses—can distinguish (1−ε)-realizable instances from those where every embedding achieves at most 1/2+ε accuracy. Empirically, the authors validate the collapse prediction on synthetic sphere data and real-world MiniLM/DINOv2 embeddings using AdamW with hinge triplet loss, observing that empirical accuracy tracks the theoretical prediction 1/2 + √(d/(c₂D)).

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 1.3) proves that for any ground-truth dimension D ≥ 240, there exist realizable triplet instances where every embedding in d ≤ cD dimensions achieves accuracy at most 1/2 + ε, matching the trivial one-dimensional random baseline. The computational hardness result (Theorem 1.4) shows that under the Unique Games Conjecture, no polynomial-time algorithm can distinguish instances with (1−ε)-achievable accuracy from those where no embedding exceeds 1/2 + ε, even for near-realizable D=1 instances. Empirically, on synthetic data (n=1000, m=10⁶ triplets, D ∈ {128, 256, 512, 1024}), the optimized triplet accuracy closely tracks the theoretical prediction 1/2 + √(d/(c₂D)), collapsing toward 50% as d shrinks relative to D. On real-world data (MiniLM/STS-B and DINOv2/CIFAR-100 at D=384), the collapse is less pronounced, reflecting exploitable structure in natural embeddings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare competing embedding methods; instead, it establishes fundamental limits that apply to all methods regardless of architecture, loss function, or optimization procedure. The relevant 'baseline' is the trivial 50% random embedding (Definition 1.2), which ignores all triplet constraints and achieves exactly 50% accuracy by symmetry. The key finding is that below a constant fraction c of the ground-truth dimension D, no d-dimensional embedding—no matter how well-optimized—can provably exceed this trivial baseline by more than ε. On synthetic random instances, the optimized embedding's accuracy indeed collapses to ~50% for small d/D ratios, confirming the theory. On real-world STS-B and CIFAR-100 data, the drop is milder, suggesting that natural embedding spaces contain exploitable structure (clustering, large margins, non-adversarial triplet generation) that the worst-case random model does not capture. The computational hardness result further shows that even with unlimited dimension, no efficient algorithm can beat 1/2 + ε in the worst case under UGC.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of ordinal embedding theory and contrastive learning theory. It builds directly on the VC-dimension bound for contrastive learning from Alon et al. (2024) to prove the accuracy collapse, on the arboricity-based dimension reduction from Avdiukhin et al. (2024) to establish realizability in D dimensions, and on the directed-cycle characterization from Bilu & Linial (2005) to reduce satisfiability to acyclicity. The computational hardness section repurposes the MAS approximation-resistance result of Guruswami et al. (2008) via a clean gap reduction. Unlike prior ordinal embedding work that characterized the worst-case dimension needed for exact embedding (O(min{n-1, √m})), this paper is the first to characterize the dimension-vs-accuracy tradeoff for a user-specified ground-truth dimension D, proving that below a constant fraction of D, no embedding can beat the trivial 50% baseline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results provide the first theoretical explanation for the empirically observed sharp accuracy drops under aggressive dimension truncation in text encoders (Takeshita et al., 2025; Tsukagoshi & Sasano, 2025), positioning the critical threshold within a constant factor of the ground-truth dimension. The computational hardness under UGC means that no algorithm—regardless of architecture or optimization—can escape the 50% baseline on adversarial instances, which has direct implications for how practitioners should interpret dimension choices. A key open question the authors highlight is whether additional structure (separability, large margins, clustered geometry) can bypass these worst-case bounds; the real-world experiments on STS-B and CIFAR-100 suggest such structure does exist, but characterizing it remains future work.
