## Problem

Existing time series forecasting benchmarks suffer from four structural limitations that undermine their utility for evaluating time series foundation models (TSFMs): data composition is dominated by legacy datasets with contamination risk, data integrity lacks rigorous quality assurance, task formulations use rigid one-size-fits-all horizons detached from real-world operational contexts, and analysis relies on coarse dataset-level meta labels that obscure generalizable insights into model capabilities. The paper argues that no existing benchmark adequately supports the task-centric, zero-shot evaluation paradigm that TSFMs require.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 50 fresh datasets (spanning 8 domains, frequencies from 5-minute to quarterly) organized into 98 forecasting tasks, where each task pairs a dataset with context-aligned prediction horizons (Short/Medium/Long for high-frequency data, single horizon for low-frequency). Evaluation uses a non-overlapping rolling window with stride equal to the horizon H over a test segment covering complete seasonal cycles, producing window-level quantile predictions from each TSFM. Outputs are normalized MASE (point) and CRPS (probabilistic) scores computed per window, per variate, and per task, with distribution-based models converted to quantiles via 100-sample Monte Carlo before metric computation.

## Method

TIME introduces a human-in-the-loop construction pipeline that combines automated screening (timestamp rectification, rule-based validation, Ljung-Box white-noise detection, local IQR outlier removal, and pairwise correlation checks) with LLM-assisted human review to finalize dataset structure and formulate context-aligned tasks. For evaluation, the paper proposes a pattern-level analysis perspective: each variate is decomposed via STL into Trend, Seasonality, and Remainder components, from which 7 structural features are extracted (Trend Strength, Trend Linearity, Seasonality Strength, Seasonality Correlation, Residual ACF-1, spectral entropy as Complexity, and binary Stationarity via ADF test). These features are binarized using population medians as thresholds, yielding a 7-bit pattern code per variate that enables cross-dataset retrieval of variates sharing identical temporal characteristics. Performance is then aggregated at the variate level within each pattern group using geometric mean of S-Naive-normalized metrics, revealing model capabilities on specific temporal dynamics (e.g., strong seasonality, non-stationarity) that dataset-level aggregation would obscure.

## Results

On the TIME benchmark (50 fresh datasets, 98 tasks, 8 domains), Chronos-2, TimesFM-2.5, and TiRex emerge as the top-3 performers in both point (MASE) and probabilistic (CRPS) forecasting, with all metrics normalized against a Seasonal Naive baseline (lower is better). A consistent progression is observed where newer model iterations outperform their predecessors (e.g., TimesFM-2.5 > 2.0 > 1.0; Chronos-2 > Chronos-bolt; Moirai2 > Moirai), confirming genuine capability gains rather than overfitting to legacy benchmarks. Pattern-level analysis reveals that model rankings shift significantly across temporal characteristics: Chronos-2 dominates stationary sequences while TimesFM-2.5 leads on non-stationary data, and high spectral entropy acts as a performance equalizer compressing inter-model gaps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All 12 TSFMs are evaluated under identical zero-shot conditions on 98 context-aligned tasks. Chronos-2 (120M, encoder) and TimesFM-2.5 (200M, decoder) occupy the top two positions overall, but their dominance is pattern-dependent: Chronos-2 leads on stationary sequences while TimesFM-2.5 surpasses it on non-stationary data. TiRex (35M, xLSTM) achieves a surprisingly strong third place despite having the fewest parameters, suggesting architectural efficiency. Toto (151M, decoder, multivariate) ranks 3rd on non-stationary data but drops to 6th on stationary sequences, revealing a strong inductive bias toward dynamic shifts. Decoder-only architectures with quantile-based outputs (TimesFM-2.5, Toto, Moirai2) dominate the top-5, while the largest model (VisionTS++, 460M) does not rank in the top tier, indicating that parameter count alone does not determine forecasting quality. Newer versions of every model family consistently outperform their predecessors, validating that TIME captures genuine progress rather than benchmark-specific artifacts.

## Relation to Prior Work

TIME extends the lineage of TSF benchmarks from LSF and Monash through recent zero-shot evaluation efforts like Gift-Eval, TFB, and FEV-Bench, but deliberately departs from them on three axes: (1) it curates entirely fresh datasets to eliminate contamination risk from legacy sources reused across prior benchmarks, (2) it replaces rigid one-size-fits-all task configurations with context-aligned horizons determined by domain expertise and LLM analysis, and (3) it introduces a pattern-level evaluation perspective using STL-decomposition-derived structural features and binary encoding, moving beyond the dataset-level meta-label aggregation (domain, frequency) that characterizes all prior benchmarks. The metric normalization framework (S-Naive baseline, geometric mean aggregation) is directly adopted from Gift-Eval (Aksu et al., 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TIME addresses a genuine and growing concern in the TSFM community: that benchmarks built on years-old public datasets may be contaminated by pre-training corpora, making it impossible to distinguish genuine model progress from memorization. The pattern-level evaluation perspective is a practical advance, showing that model rankings shift substantially when aggregating at the variate level versus the task level (e.g., Toto drops from 3rd to 6th on stationary data while Chronos-2 reverses on non-stationary data), which challenges the assumption that a single leaderboard ranking suffices. Open questions remain about the optimal aggregation granularity, how to map scalar error metrics to deployment readiness, and whether the binary thresholding scheme for pattern encoding generalizes as the benchmark scales. The paper also acknowledges that MASE/CRPS alone cannot distinguish a model that captures true temporal dynamics from one that defaults to conservative mean predictions, leaving interpretability of benchmark scores as an unresolved challenge.
