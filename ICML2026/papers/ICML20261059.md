## Problem

Streaming LLM applications expose users to harmful content token-by-token, yet current safeguards operate post-hoc, creating a critical temporal gap. Existing streaming safeguards rely on expensive token-level supervised training that is subjective, prone to overfitting on isolated keywords, and requires full retraining whenever safety policies change. The paper challenges whether streaming safety truly requires additional training, positing that well-trained post-hoc safeguards already encode token-level risk signals in their hidden representations.

## Input / Output

At each decoding step, the method consumes the partial context (prompt + generated prefix + current token) and extracts the hidden state at a chosen layer (typically layer 18) from the post-hoc safeguard model. A publicly available Top-K SAE (trained on the same base LLM, e.g., Qwen3-8B) maps this hidden state to a sparse activation vector. The output is a scalar per-token risk score; when it exceeds a user-defined threshold, generation is immediately interrupted. No gradient updates or token-level labels are involved at inference time.

## Method

NExT-Guard operates in two stages without any training. Offline, it constructs a calibration set of safe/unsafe samples, aggregates token-level SAE activations to the sample level via max-pooling, and ranks all SAE dimensions by a Standardized Mean Difference score that rewards consistent high activation on unsafe content while penalizing variance. The top-K (typically 32) dimensions form the safety-relevant feature set. Online, the per-token risk score is computed as a weighted sum of the selected feature activations, where each weight is the corresponding discriminative score from the offline stage. The key insight is that SAEs disentangle the safeguard's dense hidden states into sparse, semantically grounded features, and the safety-relevant subset already carries interpretable, category-specific risk signals that can be monitored in real time without any supervised fine-tuning.

## Results

NExT-Guard achieves an average F1 of 90.8 on prompt classification (Aegis 88.9, Aegis2.0 84.0, SimpST 99.5) and 84.3 on response classification (SafeRLHF 88.8, BeaverTails 81.2, Aegis2.0 82.9), outperforming the strongest streaming baselines by 6.4 and 7.3 points respectively. It also surpasses the best post-hoc safeguard (WildGuard-7B, avg F1 88.6 on prompts) by 2.2 points despite operating with only partial context. On the response side, NExT-Guard exceeds the best post-hoc model (Qwen3Guard-4B-Gen, avg F1 78.0) by 6.3 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NExT-Guard outperforms all eight baseline groups across both prompt and response settings. Among streaming safeguards, it beats the strongest (Qwen3Guard-0.6B-Stream, avg F1 84.4 on prompts) by 6.4 points and Qwen3Guard-4B-Stream (avg F1 77.0 on responses) by 7.3 points, with SCM-7B (83.0/74.5) and Kelp (82.8/73.2) trailing further behind. Among post-hoc safeguards, NExT-Guard surpasses the best prompt classifier WildGuard-7B (88.6) by 2.2 points and the best response classifier Qwen3Guard-4B-Gen (78.0) by 6.3 points, despite using only partial context at each token step. Notably, NExT-Guard's advantage is largest on SafeRLHF (88.8 vs. 63.7 for Qwen3Guard-4B-Gen), suggesting that token-level latent signals are particularly informative for response-side safety where post-hoc models struggle most. The paper also reports that NExT-Guard's intervention timing aligns closely with human-annotated ground-truth unsafe token onsets, whereas token-supervised baselines (Qwen3Guard-Stream, SCM) systematically trigger too early, consistent with keyword-driven overfitting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm for streaming safeguards (SCM, Kelp, Qwen3Guard-Stream), which all rely on expensive token-level supervised training, by reframing streaming safety as a signal-extraction problem rather than a learning problem. It builds on the mechanistic interpretability literature on Sparse Autoencoders (Huben et al., Templeton et al., Gao et al.) but repurposes SAEs not for analysis or steering, but as a training-free interface to expose latent risk signals already present in post-hoc safeguard representations. The key conceptual departure is the claim that well-trained post-hoc safeguards (e.g., Qwen3Guard-8B-Gen) already encode incremental, token-level risk in their hidden states, and that SAEs can decode these signals without any additional supervision.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating both token-level annotation and gradient-based training, NEXT-GUARD dramatically lowers the barrier to deploying real-time safety monitoring and enables instant adaptation to new safety policies without retraining cycles. The finding that a training-free SAE-based monitor outperforms its own parent post-hoc model and all supervised streaming baselines suggests that current safeguards underutilize their internal risk representations, opening a broader question about how much safety capability is latent versus manifested in existing guardrails. Key open questions include generalizability beyond the Qwen model family (acknowledged as a limitation), whether the approach transfers to multimodal or agentic settings, and whether the identified SAE features remain stable under distribution shift or adversarial prompting.
