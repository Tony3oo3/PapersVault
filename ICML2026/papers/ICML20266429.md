## Problem

LLM-based planning research has expanded rapidly but frequently replicates methodological pitfalls that the automated planning community identified and resolved over six decades, including benchmark contamination, unsound validation, mismatched baselines, and opaque evaluation metrics. The paper argues that the established rigor of planning research—honed through the International Planning Competition and systematic trial-and-error methodology—must be correctly incorporated into the design and evaluation of LLM-based planners to prevent inflated performance claims and ensure reproducible progress.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the work does not propose a new algorithm or model. It takes the existing landscape of LLM-based planning benchmarks, tools, and evaluation practices as its subject and produces a structured reference guide comprising planning formalism terminology, complexity analysis frameworks, benchmark selection criteria, tool recommendations, and evaluation protocols. The output is organized as actionable 'Common Pitfall' callouts paired with specific remediation advice, intended for both researchers designing experiments and reviewers assessing submissions.

## Method

The paper systematically catalogs the automated planning community's established practices across five dimensions: formal planning problem definitions and their variants (classical, conformant, probabilistic, temporal, HTN, numeric, oversubscription), computational complexity results and algorithmic guarantees (soundness, completeness, optimality), data and benchmark construction principles including contamination controls and structure-aware splits, available tooling (Fast Downward, planutils, PDDL generators, INVAL validator), and evaluation protocols including appropriate metrics (IPC score versus raw coverage) and baseline selection criteria. It addresses three counterarguments—open-world underspecification, utility-over-guarantees, and contamination inevitability—by identifying the conditions under which each is valid and specifying the correct scientific response. The paper positions itself as a bridging handbook that provides both the vocabulary to locate relevant planning resources and concrete, protocol-level guidance for conducting rigorous LLM-based planning experiments.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not present quantitative experimental results or a main results table. Its contribution is a set of methodological guidelines, common-pitfall identifications (numbered 3.1–7.7), and a curated list of planning-community tools (Fast Downward, planutils, PDDL generators, Unified Planning, etc.) that LLM-based planning researchers should adopt. The paper's 'results' are prescriptive: four highest-priority recommendations (specify the planning problem, use independently checkable evaluation, compare against strong baselines, evaluate on diverse benchmarks with difficulty scaling and novelty controls) and a structured taxonomy of planning formalisms (classical, conformant, probabilistic, FOND, temporal, numeric, HTN, oversubscription) with associated complexity results.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to be benchmarked against baselines, so no head-to-head performance comparisons are reported. Instead, it surveys and critiques existing LLM-based planning evaluations (e.g., PlanBench, ACP-Bench, Game of 24, Mini Crosswords, AutoPlanBench) for methodological shortcomings such as data contamination, lack of sound validators, and structure-ignoring train/test splits. It contrasts the rigor of IPC-style evaluation protocols (unified PDDL representation, generator-backed instances, independent plan validation via VAL/INVAL, matched hardware and time limits) with the less structured protocols common in recent LLM-planning papers, arguing that the former should be adopted. The paper also distinguishes between planning problem types (cost-optimal vs. satisficing vs. agile) and warns that comparing planners targeting different computational problems—e.g., an A* search with an inadmissible heuristic against a greedy best-first satisficing planner—is meaningless, a point it illustrates with the BlocksWorld example where cost-optimal planning is NP-complete but satisficing planning is in P.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not introduce a new method but synthesizes six decades of automated planning methodology—PDDL, the International Planning Competition, Fast Downward, plan validation, and structure-aware benchmarking—and explicitly maps those practices onto the emerging LLM-based planning literature. It builds conceptually on the authors' own prior work documenting methodological issues in LLM planning (Katz et al., 2024; Katz et al., 2025) and on contamination evidence (Hu et al., 2025), while acknowledging recent LLM-planning papers that already adopt planning-aware protocols (Verma et al., 2025; Valmeekam et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025). Its novelty is one of cross-community translation: repurposing the planning community's evaluation infrastructure, complexity taxonomy, and pitfall catalog as a prescriptive framework for a field that has largely reinvented (and re-encountered) the same errors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, actionable handbook—naming specific tools (planutils, PDDL generators, Unified Planning, INVAL), specific protocols (structure-aware splits, sound validators, IPC-style scoring), and specific anti-patterns (scraped benchmarks, LLM-as-judge for plan validity, A* with inadmissible heuristics)—that can immediately improve the rigor of LLM-planning papers and their peer review. A key open question the paper acknowledges is how to extend these protocols to genuinely open-world, underspecified, or partially observable settings where classical formalisms do not directly apply; the authors argue the right move is to identify a well-defined variant rather than abandon evaluation altogether. The broader implication is that without such grounding, the field risks accumulating a body of results whose apparent progress is partly an artifact of contamination, mis-specified baselines, and conflated capability measurements.
