## Problem

Existing ANNS solvers optimize query efficiency in isolation but fail to simultaneously satisfy six practical demands of modern AI workloads: high QPS-recall, fast indexing, low memory footprint, scalability to high dimensionality, robustness across varying retrieval sizes K, and support for online insertions. No prior method—whether graph-based, quantization-based, or hybrid—achieves competitive performance on all six criteria, particularly on modern embedding datasets with dimensions up to 3072.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263025/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

PAG takes a dataset of n high-dimensional vectors (dimensions ranging from 384 to 3072 in experiments) and a single query vector, returning K approximate nearest neighbors under Euclidean or cosine distance. The graph index is built via a search-and-insertion paradigm where each vector is sequentially inserted, and the projection structure (L subspaces × m cross-polytope vectors per subspace) is precomputed and stored as a compact inference structure alongside the graph edges. The method supports both batch indexing and incremental online insertion, where a batch of B insertions incurs only O(M²d) amortized verification cost per node.

## Method

PAG integrates random projection as a fundamental building block of graph construction rather than a plug-in, using asymmetric comparisons between exact and approximate distances to skip unnecessary exact distance computations. Three components are derived from a unified asymptotic result (Theorem 3.1) showing that projection statistics are conditionally Gaussian with mean cos(α)·cos(β): (1) PRT uses the ratio cos(θ)/cos(β) minus a threshold τ to probabilistically decide whether a neighbor's exact distance must be computed; (2) TFB maintains a working set W plus two ring buffers (R_F for false positives, R_T for ejected nodes) that are merged and refilled across rounds, incrementally raising τ to reuse previously computed distances; (3) PES applies a similar projection test with a different threshold δ to detect promising incoming edges outside the out-neighbor set, expanding graph connectivity with O(1) additional work per checked neighbor. The combination reduces search complexity from O(n'Md) to O(n'ML + γMn'd) where L ≪ d and γ < 0.2 in practice, while preserving HNSW's online insertion support.

## Results

On six modern datasets (dimensions 384–3072, sizes up to 56.5M), PAG-Base achieves the best QPS-recall performance across all criteria, with a speedup of up to 5× over HNSW at K=100. PAG-Base requires only 20–40% of HNSW's indexing time while PAG-Lite matches quantization-based methods' indexing speed (0.5× on high-dimensional datasets) with the smallest memory footprint in 4 of 8 cases. PAG-Base remains dominant as K increases to 1000, where SymQG degrades significantly, and its insertion speed is even faster than HNSW's search speed in online workloads.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PAG-Base outperforms HNSW by up to 5× in QPS at equivalent recall on all modern datasets, while indexing in only 20–40% of HNSW's time. Against SymQG (the strongest quantized-graph competitor), PAG-Base wins on all modern datasets except high-recall WoltFood, and the gap widens substantially at K=1000 where SymQG degrades sharply; SymQG also runs out of memory on MajorTOM (56.5M, d=1024). Compared to quantization-based methods (ScaNN, IVFPQFS, RaBitQ+), PAG-Base achieves superior QPS-recall while PAG-Lite matches their indexing speed with competitive search quality. Against HNSW+KS2 (the closest prior PG method), PAG-Base's TFB and PES components yield consistent QPS improvements with negligible additional indexing overhead, as confirmed by the ablation study. On legacy datasets (GloVe, DEEP100M, SIFT10M, GIST), PAG-Base is best or near-best, with SymQG only winning on sparse legacy data at small K.

## Relation to Prior Work

PAG extends the graph-based ANNS lineage (HNSW, NSG, Vamana) by integrating random-projection routing tests as a fundamental building block of graph construction rather than a post-hoc plug-in. It directly builds on the PEOs and KS2 routing-test framework (Lu et al., 2024; 2025), reusing the PRT function structure but introducing two new components—Test Feedback Buffer (TFB) for adaptive thresholding and false-positive recycling, and Probabilistic Edge Selection (PES) for expanding in-degree connectivity—unified under a single asymptotic Gaussian approximation (Theorem 3.1). Unlike quantized-graph methods (SymQG, NGT-QG) that replace exact distances with quantized ones, PAG operates on original vectors and uses asymmetric exact-vs-approximate comparisons, preserving the robustness of graph-based search while reducing costly distance computations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PAG is one of the first ANNS frameworks to simultaneously satisfy all six practical demands identified for modern AI workloads (high QPS, fast indexing, low memory, high-dimensionality scalability, retrieval-size robustness, and online insertion), which prior methods each address only partially. The up-to-5× speedup over HNSW, validated across eight datasets spanning 384–3072 dimensions and K=10–1000, makes it a strong candidate for production vector databases serving modern embedding models. Open questions include whether the asymptotic theoretical guarantees (requiring L→∞, d/L→∞) hold tightly at the moderate (m, L) values used in practice, how PAG performs under GPU or distributed settings, and whether the PES refinement step scales gracefully to billion-scale corpora where deferred edge verification could accumulate.
