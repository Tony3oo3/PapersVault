## Problem

Circuit and sheaf discovery (CSD) for LLMs operates under an implicit 'Functional Anisotropy Hypothesis' — the assumption that each task is supported by a unique or near-unique internal mechanism, so that a discovered circuit is *the* explanation for that task. This paper provides empirical and theoretical evidence that this assumption is false: a single task can be faithfully realised by multiple, structurally distinct circuits or sheaves with minimal overlap, undermining the canonical interpretation of CSD results and calling for a rethinking of how such mechanisms should be evaluated.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained LLM (GPT-2 small, Pythia) and a task dataset (IOI, BLiMP, AGA, ANA, DNA variants, Docstring), constructs the residual-stream computation graph as a DAG over attention heads and MLPs, and produces multiple sparse binary edge masks (sheaves) that each independently preserve task performance under zero ablation. The key output is not a single circuit but a collection of structurally distinct sheaves with quantified pairwise and mutual IoU overlap, edge density, task accuracy, and complement accuracy — demonstrating that discovered mechanisms are one realisation among many rather than a canonical explanation. The ultra-sparse three-edge sheaf for IOI (86.7% accuracy in isolation) further shows that even minimal explanations lack individually indispensable components.

## Method

The core technical contribution is Overlap-Aware Sheaf Repulsion (OASR), which extends the DiscoGP sheaf discovery objective by adding an overlap penalty L_overlap(R) = (1/|E|) Σ_{e∈R} σ(l_e) that penalises the expected activation of edges already selected in previous discovery runs, inducing a repulsive interaction that drives the optimiser toward structurally distinct alternatives while preserving fidelity and completeness. The method uses Gumbel-Sigmoid relaxation with a straight-through estimator for differentiable discrete edge selection, optimising a combined objective of fidelity, sparsity, completeness, and overlap. The paper further demonstrates that non-uniqueness persists across ACDC (sensitive to attention-head traversal order), EAP (sensitive to task-irrelevant name substitutions), and Edge Pruning (sensitive to loss function choice), showing the phenomenon is not an artefact of any single method. The theoretical contribution proves the Distributive Dense Circuit Hypothesis via a quantisation-pigeonhole argument: under local linearisation of the residual stream, the combinatorial explosion of candidate s-edge subsets in high-dimensional spaces guarantees the existence of multiple low-IoU circuits whose linearised readout contributions collide within a bounded ℓ∞ margin, preserving task accuracy when the logit margin exceeds the perturbation bound.

## Results

Using OASR (Overlap-Aware Sheaf Repulsion) on DiscoGP, the paper discovers two sheaves per task that both achieve strong performance (91.3%–100% accuracy) while exhibiting only 4.1%–11.0% IoU overlap, close to chance-level. Across 20 discovery runs per task, the mutual intersection shrinks to just 9–50 edges (mutual IoU 0.15%–1.03%), confirming that increasing the number of sheaves does not converge to a common core. An ultra-sparse three-edge sheaf achieves 86.7% IOI accuracy in isolation, and ablating any single one of its three edges from the full model leaves accuracy at 99.8%–99.9%, demonstrating that no individual edge is indispensable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not position OASR as outperforming other methods on a standard metric; instead, it uses ACDC, EAP, and EP as independent confirmations that circuit non-uniqueness is a property of the model, not an artefact of DiscoGP. ACDC (Conmy et al., 2023) produces substantially different circuits on IOI when the attention-head traversal order is permuted, with intersection sizes of only 174–302 edges out of 608–6,951 union edges (Table 4). EAP (Syed et al., 2024) shows systematically decreasing pairwise IoU as the top-k threshold grows when only task-irrelevant names in IOI prompts are swapped (Figure 3), indicating no stable canonical mechanism. Edge Pruning (Bhaskar et al., 2024) with a task-specific cross-entropy loss yields min pairwise IoU of 0.064 (IOI) and 0.109 (Docstring), far below the 0.340–0.344 obtained with a full-vocabulary KL-divergence loss (Table 5), showing that the apparent stability of EP under KL is an artefact of global distributional alignment rather than task-specific computation. In all cases, the qualitative conclusion is the same: multiple high-quality, low-overlap circuits exist, and no single method converges to a unique privileged subgraph.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends DiscoGP's (Yu et al., 2025) Gumbel-Sigmoid sheaf discovery framework by adding an overlap-aware repulsion penalty (OASR) to systematically uncover multiple low-overlap circuits for the same task. It positions itself against the implicit 'Functional Anisotropy Hypothesis' shared by ACDC (Conmy et al., 2023), EAP (Syed et al., 2024), Edge Pruning (Bhaskar et al., 2024), and the original IOI circuit work (Wang et al., 2022a), arguing that prior backup-mechanism and hydra-effect findings (McGrath et al., 2023) describe ablation-triggered compensation rather than coexisting active mechanisms. The theoretical contribution draws on superposition theory (Elhage et al., 2022) and connects to Méloux et al. (2025) on identifiability, but extends it from toy models to pretrained LLMs with residual-stream circuit formalism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If multiple structurally distinct circuits can simultaneously and faithfully implement the same task, then the field's dominant evaluation paradigms—Tracr-based ground-truth matching and MIB-style minimality—are built on a flawed premise of canonical uniqueness, requiring a fundamental rethinking of how CSD results are interpreted. The practical implication is that a discovered circuit should be read as one valid realisation among many, not as 'the' mechanism, which affects how interpretability findings are communicated and how model interventions are designed. Open questions include whether the phenomenon persists at larger scale (the empirical results focus on GPT-2 small and Pythia), whether local linearity assumptions in the proof hold for deeper non-linear regimes, and how to design evaluation protocols that are agnostic to circuit non-uniqueness.
