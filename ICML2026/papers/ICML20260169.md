## Problem

Multimodal Diffusion Transformers (MM-DiTs) frequently suffer from concept omission, where objects or attributes specified in the prompt fail to appear in the generated image. Prior approaches either require additional training, incur high inference costs, or focus exclusively on visual embeddings, leaving the role of text embeddings in encoding and signaling omission within the diffusion model largely unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260169/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates at inference time on a standard text prompt fed to an MM-DiT (FLUX.1-Dev or SD3.5-Medium) and produces a generated RGB image. The intervention is applied to the key vectors of concept text tokens in selected attention heads during the first 15 of 30 diffusion steps (t ∈ [t_stop, 1]), with no modification to the model weights or the prompt itself. For attribute neglect, the intervention extends to both object tokens and their associated attribute tokens, identified via rule-based parsing or Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct extraction.

## Method

The paper first demonstrates via linear probing on key vectors of concept text tokens that specific attention heads in middle layers of MM-DiTs encode a binary omission signal that evolves temporally—probe probability rises in sync with the visual emergence of the concept. From a small labeled dataset (822 samples for FLUX), the omission direction is computed per head using Mass Mean Shift (the difference between mean key-vector representations of absent and present concepts), normalized to unit length. During inference, this direction is added to the key vectors of target concept tokens in the top-K heads (K=300 for FLUX, K=100 for SD3.5) scaled by α·σ, where σ is the standard deviation of projections along the direction. This amplifies the omission signal, effectively making the model perceive the missing concept as more critical and catalyzing its generation, all in a training-free manner at inference time.

## Results

On the GenEval object omission benchmark, OSI achieves an average accuracy of 0.61 on FLUX and 0.64 on SD3.5, surpassing the base models (0.47 and 0.55) and both baselines. The gains are most pronounced in the multi-object regime: on FLUX, OSI improves 6-object accuracy from 0.18 (base) to 0.40, and 4-object accuracy from 0.44 to 0.64. On the T2I-CompBench attribute binding benchmark, OSI achieves the best scores on FLUX across all three categories (Color 0.8014, Shape 0.5819, Texture 0.7039), with the largest relative gain on Shape (+8.2% over base). Image quality (MUSIQ/MANIQA) is largely preserved, with FLUX MANIQA improving from 0.5435 to 0.5652.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OSI outperforms TACA (Lv et al., 2025) on FLUX across all GenEval object counts, with the largest margin at 6 objects (0.40 vs. 0.20) and 4 objects (0.64 vs. 0.56); on SD3.5, OSI wins on 4 of 5 counts (losing only marginally on 5-object: 0.46 vs. 0.47). Against PLADIS (Kim & Sim, 2025), evaluated only on FLUX, OSI achieves higher or equal accuracy on every GenEval count (e.g., 0.92 vs. 0.87 for 2-object, 0.40 vs. 0.20 for 6-object) and a slight edge on T2I-CompBench non-spatial (0.3083 vs. 0.3075). On the attribute neglect benchmark, OSI dominates both baselines on FLUX across all three categories (e.g., Shape: 0.5819 vs. TACA 0.5118 and PLADIS 0.5108), while on SD3.5 it wins on Shape and Texture but trails TACA slightly on Color (0.8048 vs. 0.8159). Overall, OSI is the only method that consistently improves both object omission and attribute neglect simultaneously without requiring additional training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the activation-steering paradigm from LLM interpretability (Mass Mean Shift direction, Li et al. 2023a) to the text-to-image setting, but applies it to a previously unexplored target: key vectors of concept text tokens inside MM-DiT layers, rather than visual features or CLIP encoder outputs. It departs from prior concept-omission methods (Attend-and-Excite, A*, BoxDiff, GLIGEN, CoMAT) that either require additional training or operate on visual attention maps, and from recent MM-DiT alignment work (TACA, PLADIS) that improves overall prompt fidelity without specifically targeting omission. The linear-probing diagnostic (Alain & Bengio, 2016) is repurposed here not merely as an analysis tool but as the mechanism that identifies which heads and which direction to steer, closing the loop between interpretability and intervention.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that text token key vectors in middle layers of MM-DiT carry a linearly-decodable, temporally-evolving omission signal is a new interpretability insight that reframes how we understand cross-modal information flow in diffusion transformers. The method is training-free, architecture-agnostic (validated on FLUX and SD3.5), and requires only a small curated dataset (~800 samples) to compute the steering direction, making it broadly applicable. Key limitations include occasional over-generation, restriction to binary presence/absence signals (untested on subjective or quantitative concepts), and the observation that SD3.5's weaker omission signal yields smaller gains, suggesting the approach's effectiveness depends on how strongly the architecture encodes omission in text tokens. Open questions include whether the signal generalizes to more complex compositional scenes, whether it can be combined with other guidance strategies, and whether time-dependent or concept-specific directions could further improve performance.
