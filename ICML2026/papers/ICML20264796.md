## Problem

It remains unclear how much model capacity is actually required for implicit reasoning (inferring new facts from learned knowledge without explicit chain-of-thought supervision) to emerge during pretraining. Existing scaling laws assume larger models strictly imply better performance, but this paper asks whether there exists a minimal sufficient parameter budget for reasoning, and what data property determines it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264796/cropped_page3_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

Training inputs are knowledge graph triples (head entity, relation, tail entity) where each entity and relation is assigned a random integer ID, concatenated with spaces, and tokenized by character to eliminate lexical confounds. At evaluation, the model receives a head entity and relation and must select the correct tail entity from a 10-option multiple-choice set, with all entity and relation types having appeared during training. Beyond the per-sample prediction, the paper's key derived outputs are the optimal model size (smallest parameter count achieving near-optimal test loss) and the graph search entropy, an information-theoretic complexity measure of the underlying knowledge graph.

## Method

The paper pretrains Llama-architecture transformers from scratch on knowledge graph triples and evaluates multi-hop reasoning via unseen triple completion. To control data complexity, it generates synthetic knowledge graphs using a preferential-attachment process governed by randomly sampled acyclic logical rules, enabling ablation of individual structural parameters (number of rules, relations, entities, deductible ratio). The core theoretical contribution is a graph search entropy measure—computed from the dominant eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix and a relation-level entropy rate under the maximum-entropy random walk—that quantifies the uncertainty encountered when traversing the graph. Two theorems are proved: (1) under a mild gap condition, the budgeted optimal model size converges to a unique global optimum as training steps increase, and (2) the optimal model size scales linearly with graph search entropy, yielding an empirical law of approximately 0.008 bits of reasoning-relevant information per parameter. The U-shaped test-loss curve as a function of model size connects implicit reasoning to benign overfitting and double-descent phenomena.

## Results

The paper identifies a U-shaped test-loss curve as a function of model size for implicit reasoning over knowledge graphs, revealing a finite optimal model size below which performance degrades and above which overfitting occurs. The key quantitative finding is a linear scaling law between the optimal parameter budget and graph search entropy with R² = 0.85, yielding approximately 124 parameters per 1-bit of graph search entropy (i.e., ~0.008 bits of reasoning-relevant information per parameter). This is validated on the real-world FB15K-237 dataset (14,505 entities, 237 relations, 310,116 triples), where the predicted optimal model size from the synthetic-graph scaling law closely matches the empirically observed optimum. Models range from 0.3M to 1.34B parameters (Llama architecture), trained with batch size 1024 and learning rate 1e-4.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing reasoning methods or baselines in the traditional sense; instead, it establishes a new scaling law for implicit reasoning capacity. The primary 'comparison' is across model sizes (0.3M–1.34B parameters) on the same knowledge graph, demonstrating that larger models are not required to match the best reasoning performance and in fact overfit under prolonged training. The paper contrasts its 0.008 bits/parameter reasoning capacity with the 2 bits/parameter knowledge memorization capacity reported by Allen-Zhu & Li (2025), arguing the discrepancy arises because reasoning over knowledge is fundamentally harder than memorizing it. The synthetic-graph experiments (varying triples, rules, relations, entities, and deductible ratio) serve as controlled ablations showing that graph search entropy—not any single structural parameter—governs the optimal model size. The real-world FB15K-237 result acts as an out-of-sample validation of the scaling law derived purely from synthetic data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the scaling-law literature (Kaplan et al. 2020; Hoffmann et al. 2022) by challenging the monotonic 'bigger is better' assumption specifically for reasoning, drawing on double-descent and benign-overfitting theory (Nakkiran et al. 2020; Bartlett et al. 2020) to formalize a U-shaped test-loss curve as a function of model size. It builds on the implicit-reasoning setup of Wang et al. (2024b), which uses knowledge-graph completion to probe pretraining-time reasoning, and on knowledge-capacity scaling work (Allen-Zhu & Li 2025; Lu et al. 2024), but departs by showing that reasoning capacity per parameter (~0.008 bits) is orders of magnitude smaller than memorization capacity (~2 bits). The synthetic graph generation borrows preferential attachment (Barabási & Albert 1999) to produce scale-free structures analogous to real knowledge graphs, and the non-monotonic scaling pattern echoes broken scaling laws (Caballero et al. 2023) and inverse-scaling observations (Wei et al. 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical and empirical link between a concrete data-complexity measure (graph search entropy) and the minimal parameter budget needed for implicit reasoning, offering a principled recipe for matching model size to task complexity rather than defaulting to the largest available model. The 0.008 bits/parameter finding suggests that reasoning is far more capacity-constrained than memorization, with practical implications for compute-efficient model design. Key limitations include reliance on synthetic knowledge graphs and a single real-world dataset (FB15K-237), the R²=0.85 linear fit leaving unexplained variance, and the open question of whether the scaling law transfers to non-attention architectures such as state-space models. Verifying the framework on real-world text pretraining corpora—where the underlying knowledge graph must be reconstructed—remains a significant future direction.
