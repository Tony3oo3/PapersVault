## Problem

Verifying properties such as robustness, fairness, or safety of tree ensembles is NP-hard, and existing approaches develop bespoke algorithms optimized for a single verification task (e.g., a specific fairness definition or distance metric). No prior work has made explicit that most of these tasks can be framed as searches over the ensemble's output configurations, nor has a generic, task-agnostic verification framework been proposed that handles a wide range of properties with comparable efficiency to specialized methods.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained tree ensemble (e.g., XGBoost) and a verification query specifying the property to check, relevant thresholds (e.g., perturbation budget ε, distance norm, protected attribute), and optionally a reference ensemble for cross-model comparisons. The OC-space is precomputed as a set of labeled hyperrectangular boxes (each box stores per-feature lower/upper bounds and the ensemble's constant prediction within it), stored in memory and indexed. Outputs range from boolean verification results and nearest adversarial examples to volumes of violating regions and capped Lipschitz constants, with the one-time enumeration and indexing cost amortized over subsequent queries.

## Method

The core insight is that within a single output configuration (one leaf per tree), the ensemble's raw score is constant, so verification reduces to searching over the finite OC-space rather than reasoning about the full function. Three search patterns cover all considered tasks: linear scans over all OCs (e.g., plausibility of range, empirical robustness), quadratic scans over OC pairs (e.g., average slope, fairness, monotonicity), and cross-ensemble OC comparisons (e.g., safety, multiplicity). The key theoretical contribution is Theorem 5.1, which shows that the OC-space of any sub-ensemble subpartitions the full OC-space, providing free non-overlapping bounding boxes for spatial indexing. Two index structures exploit this: a one-level Rootbox index (pruning all trees to their root) and a multi-level OC-Tree (using OC-spaces of the first k trees at level k), both enabling pruning of bounding boxes that provably cannot contain relevant solutions (Theorem 5.2), turning quadratic queries into near-linear ones in practice.

## Results

For adversarial robustness verification (finding the nearest adversarial example), the OC-space approach with spatial indexes achieves geometric mean query times of 0.008–1.9 ms across 9 datasets, compared to 1.9–24.5 ms for Kantchelian et al.'s MILP approach — a 10–100× speedup. For hybrid-norm robustness checking, the indexed OC-space search is 100–1000× faster than the SMT-based approach of Devos et al. (2021a). OC-space enumeration is feasible for 82.8% of 1065 compressed XGBoost models within 24 hours, with 82.1% completing in under 1 minute. The one-time enumeration and indexing cost is amortized after roughly 700–26,000 queries (depending on dataset and index type), making the approach particularly advantageous for repeated verification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Kantchelian et al.'s MILP approach (the reference exact verifier), the OC-space method with Rootbox or OC-Tree indexes is consistently 10–100× faster per query across all 9 datasets where full enumeration was possible (e.g., 24.5 ms vs. 0.27 ms on Phoneme; 12.4 ms vs. 0.21 ms on California). The linear-scan variant without indexing is also faster than MILP but scales poorly with OC-space size, confirming that spatial indexes are essential. For fairness verification (lack of causal discrimination), the OC-Tree approach outperforms Veritas (Devos et al., 2021b) in 36/70 models on Adult and 44/55 models on Compas, with the largest speedups (up to ~60×) occurring on models with many unfair boxes (e.g., 75,822 s vs. 63 s for one Adult model). For hybrid-norm robustness (combining L∞ per-feature and L1 total budgets), the indexed OC-space search is 100–1000× faster than the SMT solver of Devos et al. (2021a), demonstrating the flexibility advantage of being agnostic to the distance metric. The main caveat is that the OC-space approach requires a one-time enumeration cost; for single-shot queries on very large ensembles where enumeration exceeds 24 hours, the MILP approach remains preferable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the output configuration (OC) concept introduced by Devos et al. (2023) but elevates it from an implicit reasoning tool to the explicit, central object of verification. Prior verification approaches for tree ensembles—MILP encodings (Kantchelian et al., 2016), SMT solvers (Devos et al., 2021a; Sato et al., 2020), and search-based methods (Chen et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2020; Devos et al., 2021b)—either operate on the model's logical structure or implicitly reason over OCs without making this connection explicit. The key departure is framing all verification tasks as searches over a precomputed OC-space and exploiting the subpartitioning property of subensembles (Theorem 5.1) to construct spatial indexes (Rootbox, OC-Tree) that are 'free' from the ensemble structure itself, rather than requiring generic R*-tree or X-tree construction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical feasibility result—that 82.8% of compressed XGBoost models across 16 datasets can have their full OC-space enumerated within 24 hours, with 82.1% of enumerations completing in under a minute—challenges the assumption that NP-hard verification necessarily requires task-specific algorithms with better asymptotic scaling. The one-time enumeration cost amortizes over many queries, making the approach particularly attractive for deployment-time verification where a model is queried repeatedly. Key limitations remain: the exponential worst-case scaling makes the approach infeasible for very large ensembles, the current implementation is memory-bound (main-memory only, double-precision storage), and more sophisticated index structures or disk-based storage could extend applicability. Open questions include whether the subpartitioning-based indexing generalizes to non-binary trees or non-additive ensemble combinations, and whether the approach can be combined with approximate methods for the infeasible regime.
