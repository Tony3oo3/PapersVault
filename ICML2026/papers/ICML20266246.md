## Problem

The rent-or-buy (Bahncard) problem has a best-known deterministic competitive ratio of 2 in the worst-case online setting. While prior learning-augmented work for this problem relies on sophisticated predictors such as optimal dual parameters, it remains unclear whether a simple random sample of the input—requiring no ML model—can meaningfully improve the competitive guarantee. This paper shows that even a sparse sample reduces the competitive ratio from 2 to 3/2, and to 1 under a bounded trip-cost assumption.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives, before any trip arrives, a random sample in which each trip (cost, time) is independently included with probability ε and rescaled by 1/ε to form an unbiased cost estimator; the full instance then arrives online in adversarial order. The algorithm outputs a deterministic set of card purchase times, each card covering a unit-length interval at unit cost and reducing trip costs to a β-fraction. The instance is normalized so card cost and duration are both 1, and trip costs are bounded above by 1 (i.e., c ≤ 1).

## Method

Algorithm 5.2 computes an offline optimum on the scaled sample, then eliminates all short gaps (shorter than δ = c^{1/5}) between consecutive cards by discarding the original card positions in each short-gap cluster and replacing them with a dense grid of unit-length cards that fully covers the interval, yielding a 3/2-competitive guarantee as c→0. Algorithm 5.3 refines this by only applying the dense-cover step when at least 1/δ cards fall between two long gaps; otherwise it right-aligns the last card, and additionally triggers an online fallback that covers the remainder of an interval if the observed trip cost in a gap exceeds a threshold, achieving a 1-competitive ratio when all trip costs lie in [αc, c]. The analysis partitions trips into atoms of total cost in [c, 2c], defines 'bad spans' where the sample underestimates true cost, and bounds the expected bad cost via Hoeffding's inequality and Chernoff bounds, showing the bad-span contribution vanishes as c→0.

## Results

The paper establishes that Algorithm 5.2 achieves a competitive ratio converging to 3/2 as the maximum trip cost c → 0, improving upon the classical worst-case bound of 2 (or (2−β) for the Bahncard problem). Under the additional assumption that all trip costs lie in [αc, c] for a constant α > 0, Algorithm 5.3 achieves a competitive ratio converging to 1, i.e., near-optimality. In experiments with sampling rate ε = 0.5 across commuter, occasional, and clustered arrival patterns with normal and Pareto price distributions, both algorithms achieve cost ratios close to 1 (near-optimal), with Algorithm 5.3 significantly outperforming PFSUM on clustered instances.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the classical Counter algorithm (Fleischer, 2001), which is (2−β)-competitive in the worst case, Algorithm 5.2 improves the competitive ratio to 3/2 as c → 0, and Algorithm 5.3 further improves it to 1 under the bounded-cost-ratio assumption. Against PFSUM (Zhao et al., 2024), which uses sophisticated predicted costs, the proposed sample-based algorithms achieve comparable near-optimal performance on commuter and occasional arrival patterns, but significantly outperform PFSUM on clustered (bursty) arrival patterns, where the sample-based approach better exploits the highly structured input. The naive sampling baseline (compute OPT on the scaled sample and follow it) is shown to be arbitrarily worse than optimal on adversarial instances and empirically underperforms the proposed algorithms even on natural input distributions, confirming that the gap-filling mechanism in Algorithms 5.2 and 5.3 is essential.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies the 'online algorithm with a sample' framework (Kaplan et al. 2020; 2022) to the rent-or-buy problem class (ski rental, Bahncard), a combination explicitly absent from prior work. It departs from the dominant learning-augmented rent-or-buy literature (Purohit et al. 2018; Zhao et al. 2024), which relies on sophisticated predictors such as optimal dual parameters or expert advice, by using only a random sample of the input as auxiliary information. The key algorithmic departure is that the naive strategy of computing an offline optimum on the rescaled sample and following it—sufficient for ski rental—provably fails for the general Bahncard setting, necessitating the gap-elimination mechanism in Algorithms 5.2 and 5.3.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a sparse random sample (no sophisticated predictor) improves the competitive ratio from 2 to 3/2, and to 1 under bounded trip costs, provides strong evidence that even minimal information about the future yields substantial gains for foundational online problems. Open questions include whether the dependence on the cost ratio c is fundamental, whether unconditional 1-competitiveness is achievable, and whether lower bounds can be established for sample-based algorithms (complicated by the interplay between sampling randomness and adversarial online decisions). The paper is purely theoretical with empirical validation, so practical deployment considerations (e.g., distributional shift, noisy samples) remain to be addressed in future work.
