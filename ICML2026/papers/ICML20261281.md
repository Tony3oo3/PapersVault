## Problem

Generative planning models produce solutions bounded by their (typically suboptimal) training data, and existing test-time compute strategies like MCTS suffer from depth imbalance—UCT-style selection yields wide but shallow trees that under-explore critical deep decisions in long-horizon planning. Standard OCL search (e.g., A*) lacks mechanisms for fast candidate generation via learned models and cannot compensate for the systematic overestimation bias of heuristics trained on suboptimal demonstrations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a PDDL initial state and goal specification, tokenized into sequences of predicate names, operator tokens, and object identifiers from a domain-specific vocabulary. The autoregressive GPT-2 policy model generates plans token-by-token, while a BERT encoder heuristic model outputs a discrete probability distribution over remaining plan lengths (0 to L_max). The final output is a valid grounded action sequence (plan) that transforms the initial state into one satisfying the goal, with optimality defined as minimum plan length.

## Method

OCLGEN modernizes classical Open-Closed List search with three key modifications tailored to autoregressive planning models. First, depth-partitioned selection maintains separate open lists per depth level, neutralizing the multiplicative overestimation bias of learned heuristics that would otherwise cause A*-style selection to over-commit to deeper branches. Second, node expansion uses truncated rollouts from the generative model with adaptive expansion: the rollout proceeds greedily along high-confidence actions and branches exhaustively only at low-confidence decision points (below a threshold τ_conf), reducing the effective branching factor from b to (1−γ)b+γ. Third, a distributional heuristic model predicts a full probability distribution over remaining plan lengths, and nodes are ranked by a lower k-th percentile of this distribution rather than the mode, targeting each node's best attainable completion. The algorithm is structured as an anytime procedure: initial full rollouts populate the search graph, then iterative depth-partitioned search refines solutions until a compute budget is exhausted, and the resulting improved plans can be used to finetune both the policy and heuristic models in a recursive self-improvement loop.

## Results

On 1000 held-out instances per domain (10-min budget), OCLGEN (uniform) achieves near-perfect completion (99.7–100%) while producing the shortest plans among all learned methods: 43.88 (Blocksworld), 155.83 (Logistics), 12.99 (Labyrinth), and 128.67 (Sokoban). On the subset of instances with known optimal solutions, OCLGEN achieves 87.3% overall optimality (528/630 Blocksworld, 104/169 Logistics, 988/1000 Labyrinth, 377/489 Sokoban), compared to 49.8% for MCTS with full rollouts. After three iterations of recursive self-improvement, OCLGEN reaches 100% optimal plans on Blocksworld and 94.7% on Sokoban, surpassing the FD-LAMA-anytime solver (601/630 and 441/489 at 20-min budget).

## Baselines & Comparisons

OCLGEN outperforms MCTS (full rollouts) on plan length across all four domains, reducing average length by 19.6% on Blocksworld (43.88 vs. 54.56) and 17.8% on Labyrinth (12.99 vs. 15.81), while also achieving 2.9× more optimal solutions on Blocksworld (528/630 vs. 180/630). Against Best-of-N sampling, OCLGEN produces consistently shorter plans (e.g., 43.88 vs. 61.56 on Blocksworld) and solves 6.2× more instances optimally on Blocksworld (528/630 vs. 85/630). Compared to the classical FD-LAMA-anytime solver (10 min), OCLGEN achieves shorter plans on all domains (e.g., 128.67 vs. 141.43 on Sokoban) while matching its 100% completion rate. OCL-Anytime A* and OCL-GBFS, while competitive on simpler domains (Labyrinth), fail catastrophically on Logistics (1.6% and 1.3% completion) and Sokoban (33.5% and 35.9%), whereas OCLGEN maintains ≥99.7% completion everywhere. On the known-optimal subset, OCLGEN's average plan lengths approach FD-optimal (48 h) within 1.7% (Blocksworld), 5.2% (Logistics), 0.2% (Labyrinth), and 3.0% (Sokoban), despite using only 10 minutes of compute.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the classical Open-Closed List (OCL) search framework (Hart et al., 1968) to the setting of autoregressive generative planning models, building directly on the GPT-2-based plan generation architecture of Rossetti et al. (2024b). It departs from the dominant MCTS-with-PUCT paradigm for test-time compute in reasoning (used in theorem proving, code generation, and prior planning work) by identifying a structural weakness: PUCT's visitation-count-based exploration produces wide-but-shallow trees that under-explore deep decision points critical for long-horizon planning. The three modifications—depth-partitioned open lists, confidence-gated rollout expansion, and lower-percentile distributional heuristics—are each targeted at specific failure modes of naively combining generative models with classical search, rather than proposing a new search algorithm from scratch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a carefully adapted classical search framework can outperform both MCTS and classical anytime solvers (FD-LAMA) on plan quality within the same compute budget, and that this search procedure serves as an effective engine for recursive self-improvement, reaching 100% optimality on Blocksworld after three iterations. Key open questions include whether the depth-selection strategies (currently uniform or sequential scan) can be made adaptive, whether self-improvement provably converges to optimal solutions, and whether the approach generalizes to problem sizes or object counts outside the training distribution. The method is currently validated only on four classical PDDL domains with bounded problem sizes, so its applicability to larger or continuous planning settings remains untested.
