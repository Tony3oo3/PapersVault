## Problem

Existing large-scale video training corpora are predominantly constructed for perceptual objectives (action recognition, event localization) with content skewed toward everyday activities, leaving a critical gap for knowledge- and reasoning-intensive video understanding that demands domain expertise and multi-step inference. Models post-trained on these corpora struggle with tasks requiring scientifically grounded explanations or interpretation of events governed by non-observable principles, and prior post-training efforts largely repurpose short, non-expert-domain videos with single-model synthesis that introduces systematic biases.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes CC-licensed expert-domain videos (average duration 344.1 seconds, spanning 82 professional subjects) sampled at 0.2 fps with timestamps during generation and up to 128 frames at inference, paired with text questions in multiple-choice or open-ended format. The SFT subset (VideoKR-SFT-201K) outputs structured chain-of-thought rationales (average length ~197 tokens) that explicitly ground each reasoning step in observable video evidence and domain knowledge, while the RL subset (VideoKR-RL-114K) retains only verifiable answers for RLVR optimization. A key design constraint is that every example must pass a video-dependency filter: if two strong MLLMs can answer correctly using only text and four random frames, the example is discarded.

## Method

VideoKR's core contribution is a human-in-the-loop, skill-oriented data construction pipeline rather than an algorithmic innovation. It first builds a hierarchical Domain Knowledge Bank (82 subjects → courses → lectures → 63,745 knowledge points) to drive scenario-guided video collection from YouTube (CC-licensed only), then generates QA examples targeting three complementary skills: Basic Video Reasoning (VIDR), Knowledge-Enhanced Video Perception (KNOWVID), and Knowledge-Intensive Video Reasoning (KNOWVIDR). Example generation uses a pool of seven frontier MLLMs selected via a human-validated eligibility protocol (error rate ≤3% on 100 expert-scored instances per step), with three validation layers: self-consistency re-derivation, multi-model video-dependency filtering, and independent CoT rationale verification. Post-training follows a standard SFT→GRPO pipeline (one epoch each, batch size 32, ROUGE/EM accuracy rewards) on Qwen2.5-VL-7B and Qwen3-VL-8B, deliberately avoiding sophisticated reward engineering to isolate data design as the primary driver of gains. The accompanying VideoKR-Eval benchmark (2,000 examples) is constructed by multi-model single-frame probing to remove examples solvable without continuous video understanding, supplemented by expert re-annotation of filtered videos.

## Results

Post-training on VideoKR with a standard SFT→GRPO pipeline yields the strongest knowledge-intensive video reasoning performance among 7/8B-scale models. On Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (128 frames), VideoKR (SFT+RL) achieves a knowledge-intensive average of 51.5, surpassing Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking (50.0) and VideoAuto-R1 (49.8), with the largest per-benchmark gains on MMVU (+5.8) and VideoKR-Eval (+6.3). On Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct (128 frames), VideoKR (SFT+RL) raises the knowledge-intensive average from 41.9 to 46.6 (+4.7), outperforming VideoAuto-R1 (44.3) by 2.3 points while remaining competitive on general video reasoning (65.5 vs. 65.6). Ablations confirm that CoT supervision adds +3.0 points and the full three-skill composition (VIDR+KNOWVID+KNOWVIDR) is necessary for peak knowledge-intensive performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VideoKR (SFT+RL) consistently outperforms all prior post-training methods on knowledge-intensive video reasoning. Against VideoAuto-R1 (Liu et al., 2026a), VideoKR wins by +2.3 points on Qwen2.5-VL-7B (46.6 vs. 44.3) and +1.7 on Qwen3-VL-8B (51.5 vs. 49.8), while matching general video reasoning performance. Against OneThinker (Feng et al., 2025b), VideoKR gains +2.3 on Qwen3-VL-8B knowledge-intensive average (51.5 vs. 49.2). Against Video-R1 (Feng et al., 2025a) and VideoRFT (Wang et al., 2025a) at 16 frames on Qwen2.5-VL-7B, VideoKR leads by +4.2 and +4.0 on knowledge-intensive average respectively (44.2 vs. 40.0 and 40.2). VideoKR also surpasses the strongest non-post-trained competitor, Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking (Yang et al., 2025), by +1.5 on knowledge-intensive average (51.5 vs. 50.0). On general video reasoning, VideoKR remains within 0.1–0.5 points of the best baselines, confirming that the data design gains are concentrated on knowledge-intensive tasks without sacrificing general capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in video post-training data design rather than algorithmic innovation. Prior post-training corpora (Video-R1, VideoRFT, OneThinker, VideoAuto-R1) largely repurpose short, everyday-activity videos from existing perception-oriented datasets and rely on a single generator model, introducing systematic biases. VideoKR departs by collecting 145K newly sourced, CC-licensed expert-domain videos, decomposing video reasoning into three skill tiers (basic reasoning, knowledge-enhanced perception, knowledge-intensive reasoning), and using a human-validated pool of seven frontier models for generation. The paper deliberately holds the training pipeline (SFT → GRPO) constant to isolate data design as the variable, positioning itself as a data-centric counterpoint to the algorithm-centric trend in video RLVR work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central empirical finding—that prior post-training corpora are effectively saturated for current 7B/8B models (49–57% zero-shot accuracy on their own training data) while VideoKR retains a harder distribution (42–43%)—provides a concrete diagnostic for why many post-training efforts yield diminishing returns. The VideoKR-Eval benchmark addresses a real and previously under-quantified problem: over 35–50% of examples in existing knowledge-intensive video benchmarks are single-frame-solvable by frontier models, inflating apparent video understanding. Limitations include evaluation restricted to 7B/8B-scale models, a 30-minute video cap, and reliance on a standard GRPO reward (ROUGE/EM) that may not capture the full reasoning quality the data encourages. Open questions include whether the skill-decomposition and data-difficulty principles transfer to larger models or to non-QA video tasks, and whether the CC-licensing constraint meaningfully limits domain coverage.
