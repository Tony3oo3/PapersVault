## Problem

Classical optimization theory predicts that zeroth-order (ZO) methods suffer a dimension-dependent slowdown with convergence rates scaling linearly in the model dimension d, which would render them impractical for billion-parameter LLMs. Yet empirical work shows ZO methods successfully fine-tune large language models with performance competitive to first-order methods. This paradox arises because the standard optimization-centric view compresses learning into scalar loss values, obscuring the structural role of how ZO updates drive knowledge acquisition in function space.

## Input / Output

The method consumes only forward-pass function evaluations (no gradient access) along with randomly sampled perturbation vectors u ∈ ℝ^d, and produces updated model parameters via the SPSA rule. The analytical output is a V×V kernel matrix (where V is the output vocabulary size, not the parameter dimension d) whose entries are inner products of neural tangent vectors projected onto a P-dimensional random subspace. The key derived quantity is a trajectory discrepancy bound of the form √(V log V / P) · ηΞₜ‖Gₜ‖₂‖Aₜ‖₂, which is dimension-free in d.

## Method

The paper derives the one-step learning dynamics of ZOSGD in function space, showing that the empirical Neural Tangent Kernel (eNTK) naturally emerges as the governing term, with the ZO variant taking the form ∇_θ z(x_o)ᵀ U_{t,P} U_{t,P}ᵀ ∇_θ z(x_u) — a geometric projection of the standard FO eNTK onto the random subspace spanned by P perturbation vectors. By invoking the Johnson-Lindenstrauss Lemma in its inner-product form, the authors prove that the kernel approximation error is bounded by O(√(ln V / P)), depending on the output vocabulary size V and perturbation count P but not on the model dimension d. This yields three insights: (1) fidelity is governed primarily by P, (2) the perturbation distribution (Gaussian vs. Rademacher) is secondary since both have comparable concentration constants, and (3) the dimension-free property explains why ZO methods scale to LLMs where d is vast but V is moderate. The framework is validated empirically on LeNet/MNIST (kernel heatmaps, Frobenius/CKA/spectral error curves) and OPT-125M/350M/1.3B on SST-2 (trajectory alignment across model scales).

## Results

The paper's central quantitative result is the theoretical bound that the ZO–FO learning-dynamics discrepancy scales as O(√(V log V / P)), where V is the output vocabulary size and P is the number of perturbations, independent of model dimension d. Table 1 validates this scaling on a controlled two-layer MLP (P=50): the relative Frobenius error between ZO and FO eNTK grows from 0.28 (V=2) to 3.69 (V=1000), consistent with a √V rate. On LeNet/MNIST, high-similarity input pairs (e.g., digits 4 and 9) achieve a minimal Frobenius norm difference of ≈0.338 at P=125, while low-similarity pairs (e.g., 0 and 1) retain elevated error. On SST-2 with OPT models (125M→1.3B parameters), the perturbation threshold P≈50 at which ZO-SGD effectively mimics the FO trajectory remains virtually constant across an order-of-magnitude increase in d, empirically confirming the dimension-free property.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing ZO optimizers; instead, its primary comparison is between ZO-SGD and standard FO-SGD learning dynamics, measured via the eNTK discrepancy. Three sub-comparisons are drawn: (1) Gaussian vs. Rademacher perturbations — both distributions yield nearly indistinguishable convergence curves in Frobenius error, CKA error, and spectral distance (Figure 4), confirming that the specific perturbation distribution is secondary to the perturbation count P. (2) Model scale invariance — on SST-2, OPT-125M, OPT-350M, and OPT-1.3B all require roughly the same P (≈50) for ZO to match the FO trajectory, despite d increasing by ~10×, directly validating the dimension-free theorem. (3) Input similarity effect — on MNIST/LeNet, semantically similar digit pairs (e.g., 4 vs. 9) converge to the FO eNTK much faster than dissimilar pairs (e.g., 0 vs. 1), revealing that the perturbation sample complexity is sensitive to the geometric structure of the kernel rather than being uniform across all data pairs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the learning dynamics framework of Ren & Sutherland (2025), which characterizes one-step model belief changes via the eNTK for first-order methods, to the zeroth-order setting. It also draws on the NTK theory of Jacot et al. (2018) and the Johnson-Lindenstrauss Lemma (Johnson et al., 1984) to show that the ZO eNTK is a random projection of the FO eNTK, yielding a dimension-free approximation bound. Prior ZO optimization literature (Ghadimi & Lan, 2013; Nesterov & Spokoiny, 2017; Malladi et al., 2023a) analyzed convergence exclusively in parameter space, where variance scales with model dimension d; this work departs by shifting the analysis to function space, where the relevant dimension becomes the output vocabulary size V rather than d.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dimension-free result (ZO fidelity depends on V, not d) provides the first rigorous theoretical explanation for why ZO methods empirically succeed on billion-parameter LLMs despite classical worst-case bounds predicting infeasibility. This resolves a genuine paradox in the field and gives practitioners confidence that ZO fine-tuning scales with output size rather than parameter count. Key limitations include the reliance on the lazy-training regime (eNTK stability), which breaks down during pre-training or feature learning, and the inability to handle autoregressive sequential dependencies where the effective output dimension grows with sequence length. Open questions include efficient parallelization of perturbations under GPU memory constraints and extending the projected-kernel analysis to non-stationary dynamics.
