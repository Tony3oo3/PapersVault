## Problem

First-order methods for training neural networks converge slowly when the neural tangent kernel or Gram matrix is ill-conditioned, yet the non-asymptotic convergence theory for the Gauss-Newton (GN) method in deep learning remains limited—prior analyses largely cover only quadratic losses or mean-field limits. This paper asks whether GN can provably avoid these conditioning bottlenecks in both under- and overparameterized regimes, and what geometric structure underlies its dynamics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260425/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a supervised dataset {(x_j, y_j)}, a deep fully-connected network with smooth bounded activation (tanh, sigmoid, GELU), and a ν-strongly convex loss g with μ-Lipschitz gradients. It produces explicit geometric (exponential) convergence rate bounds for the GN gradient flow and discrete-time GN iterates, parameterized by an output scaling α and a damping schedule ρ(w). The key design parameters are α (which controls manifold curvature in the underparameterized case) and the damping factor (adaptive, constant, or curvature-aware), with all rates shown to be independent of λ_min(K_0).

## Method

In the overparameterized regime (p > n), the paper identifies curvature-aware damping schedules—adaptive ρ^ada(w) = λ_min(K(w))/(1+λ_min(K(w))), constant ρ^con, and loss-curvature-dependent ρ^glo—that make the GN preconditioner H_ρ = (1−ρ)Γ + ρI yield geometric convergence at rate exp(−ν(1+λ²)t), independent of the kernel's minimum eigenvalue. The proof uses Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury to derive the function-space ODE, then combines the Polyak-Łojasiewicz inequality with Grönwall's lemma. In the underparameterized regime (p < n), the paper shows that the undamped GN flow in parameter space induces a Riemannian gradient flow on the p-dimensional embedded submanifold M = αf(B) of function space ℝⁿ, where P(αf(w)) is the orthogonal projection onto the tangent space. Under a sufficiently large output scaling α ≥ α₀ that controls the second fundamental form (curvature) of M, the loss satisfies geodesic Polyak-Łojasiewicz and geodesic Lipschitz-smoothness conditions on a level set, implying last-iterate geometric convergence to the in-class optimal predictor at rate exp(−νt) without any explicit regularization.

## Results

In the overparameterized regime, Gauss-Newton with adaptive damping achieves $V_t \leq V_0 \exp(-\nu(1+\lambda^2)t)$, a geometric rate bounded below by $\nu$ and independent of $\lambda_{\min}(K_0)$, whereas gradient flow only achieves rate $(\nu/2)\lambda_{\min}(K_0)$ which deteriorates arbitrarily as the kernel becomes ill-conditioned. In the underparameterized regime, the Gauss-Newton gradient flow achieves last-iterate geometric convergence $V_t \leq V_0 \exp(-\nu t)$ to the optimal in-class predictor, with a rate independent of the Gram matrix minimum eigenvalue $\lambda_0$ and without any explicit regularization. The discrete-time Gauss-Newton method achieves $V_k \leq V_0(1 - \eta\nu h_1(\lambda))^k$ with a constant step size, also independent of $\lambda_{\min}(K_0)$. These results hold for both single-hidden-layer and deep fully-connected networks under standard NTK initialization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against first-order methods (gradient flow / gradient descent) in terms of convergence rates under ill-conditioned data geometry. In the overparameterized regime, gradient flow has a decay rate of $(\nu/2)\lambda_{\min}(K_0)$ (Chizat et al., 2019), which can be arbitrarily slow when the kernel matrix is ill-conditioned, while Gauss-Newton with adaptive damping maintains a rate of at least $\nu$ regardless of $\lambda_{\min}(K_0)$. In the underparameterized regime, prior first-order results (Ji & Telgarsky, 2020; Cayci & Eryilmaz, 2025) yield only subexponential convergence to a ball around the near-optimal parameter and require explicit regularization (early stopping or projection), whereas Gauss-Newton achieves exponential last-iterate convergence to an in-class optimum without any such regularization. The generalized Gauss-Newton preconditioner further leverages loss curvature via $\nabla^2 g$, yielding an additional $\lambda_{\max}^{-1}(\nabla^2 g)$ term in the exponent that accelerates convergence when the loss is well-curved. No empirical benchmarks or head-to-head numerical comparisons are provided; all comparisons are at the level of theoretical convergence rate bounds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the near-initialization (lazy training) framework of Chizat et al. (2019) and Du et al. (2018) from gradient flow to the Gauss-Newton method, and adapts the deep-network stability tools of Du et al. (2019) with sharper dependencies. It departs from prior Gauss-Newton analyses (Cai et al. 2019b, Zhang et al. 2019, Arbel et al. 2023) that were largely restricted to quadratic loss or mean-field limits, by establishing conditioning-robust rates for general strongly convex losses via adaptive damping. Most notably, the underparameterized analysis introduces a Riemannian optimization perspective—treating the GN flow as a Riemannian gradient flow on an embedded submanifold of function space—which is fundamentally different from the Euclidean, regularization-based approaches used for first-order methods in that regime (Ji & Telgarsky 2020, Cayci & Eryilmaz 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical explanation for why Gauss-Newton empirically outperforms first-order methods in ill-conditioned regimes: the convergence rate is provably independent of the kernel's minimum eigenvalue, whereas gradient flow rates degrade as λ_min(K_0) → 0. The Riemannian interpretation of underparameterized GN dynamics is a novel lens that may inform future analyses of other preconditioned methods. Limitations include the near-initialization assumption, the requirement for sufficiently large width or output scaling, and the absence of mean-field or finite-sample generalization analysis. Open questions flagged by the authors include extending the analysis to the mean-field regime and making the adaptive damping schedule computationally practical without requiring eigenvalue computation at each step.
