## Problem

Large language model safety mechanisms remain English-optimized, creating a 'Dual Curse' for low-resource language speakers: harmful content generation rises from 1% in English to 35% in languages like Hausa, Igbo, and Javanese, while instruction-following drops by 20 percentage points. The paper diagnoses this disparity as stemming from a pre-training bottleneck—multilingual reward models achieve only 49–50% accuracy (random chance) in low-resource languages—rendering post-hoc alignment structurally ineffective and converting safety from protection into a governance lever for exclusion.

## Input / Output

The empirical component evaluates 20 safety guard classifiers (22M–120B parameters, spanning LlamaGuard, ShieldGemma, WildGuard, PolyGuard, DuoGuard, Qwen3Guard, Qwen3-SafeRL, and Claude Sonnet/Opus) on harmful content classification across 50 English dialects sourced from the eWAVE atlas, under four conditions: zero-shot, 8-shot ICL, dialect-aware SFT, and GKD-PEFT knowledge distillation. The proposed framework outputs two jointly reported metrics per language and locale—Attack Success Rate (ASR) and False Refusal Rate (FRR)—computed under matched prompts with human validation by fluent speakers, alongside disparity indices (absolute and worst-case gaps relative to English). The systematic review synthesizes 207 studies from a PRISMA-guided search across Scopus, Web of Science, and venue-specific sources (ACL, EMNLP, NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, FAccT).

## Method

The paper proposes a three-component socio-technical framework: (1) Safety Context Distillation during pre-training, which embeds locale-specific harm definitions (protected identities, legitimate sensitive topics) into model representations, achieving 78–89% harm reduction versus post-hoc RLHF methods whose gaps widen monotonically with alignment sophistication (1.9× for CHAT-RLHF to 6.0× for xRLHF); (2) participatory harm specification operationalizing 'reverse tutelage,' where affected communities define local harm categories distinct from global floors; and (3) joint ASR/FRR evaluation with stratified language sampling across Joshi et al. resource tiers. The key diagnostic insight is that the pre-training bottleneck is isolated through a within-language dialectal ablation: because 50 English dialects share substantial pre-training data with Standard American English, a persistent 15.7 pp zero-shot gap (widening to 27.2 pp on the worst dialect) and a residual 5.6–6.4 pp false-negative disparity after dialect-aware SFT cannot be attributed to multilingual confounders, directly implicating the alignment objective rather than model capacity or translation quality. The framework explicitly decouples safety conditioning from capability training by targeting purposively constructed safety corpora orders of magnitude smaller than general pre-training data, and scopes deployment in tiers from ~20 high-footprint languages to informal-register varieties.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that post-hoc alignment methods exhibit monotonically widening harm-reduction gaps between high- and low-resource languages: CHAT-RLHF achieves 44.8% vs. 23.4% (1.9× gap), xSFT 23.0% vs. 9.8% (2.3×), and xRLHF 14.4% vs. 2.4% (6.0×), while pre-training Safety Context Distillation achieves near-parity at 85% vs. 78% (1.1× gap). Multilingual reward models reach only 49–50% accuracy on low-resource languages (random chance) versus 63–66% on high-resource languages. In the dialectal ablation across 50 English dialects (eWAVE), zero-shot safety-guard classification shows a 15.7 pp average gap between Standard American English (82.8%) and the dialect mean (67.1%), widening to 27.2 pp on the worst dialect (White Zimbabwean English, 55.6%); dialect-aware SFT nearly closes this gap (Δ ≤ 0.2 pp at 97.4% accuracy), and GKD-PEFT achieves 94.5 macro-F1 at 1.7B parameters, within 1.1 pp of the 8B teacher.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Shen et al. (2024), the paper's core argument is that their CHAT-RLHF, xSFT, and xRLHF results demonstrate post-hoc alignment methods produce increasingly disparate harm reduction as sophistication increases (1.9×, 2.3×, 6.0× high-to-low-resource gaps), whereas the Safety Context Distillation approach they also report achieves near-parity (1.1× gap, 78–89% harm reduction). The paper's independent dialectal ablation (20 safety guards across 50 eWAVE dialects) provides within-language support for the bottleneck hypothesis: even with dialect-aware SFT targeting the disparity head-on, a residual 5.6–6.4 pp false-negative-rate gap persists for six dual-bind dialect communities, confirming that alignment cannot fully recover signals underweighted in pre-training. Against Gureja et al. (2025), the paper adopts their M-RewardBench finding that multilingual reward models achieve only 49–50% accuracy on low-resource languages (equivalent to random chance) as the structural evidence that RLHF has no effective gradient to follow in these settings. The paper's GKD-PEFT result (94.5 macro-F1 at 1.7B params, within 1.1 pp of the 8B teacher) further indicates the residual disparity is a property of the alignment objective rather than model capacity, strengthening the case that the bottleneck is pre-training rather than downstream optimization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes a PRISMA-guided review of 207 studies to diagnose what it calls the 'Dual Curse' in multilingual LLM safety, building directly on the Safety Context Distillation approach from Üstün et al. (Aya model, ACL 2024) and the model-merging strategy from Aakanksha et al. (2024) as its core pre-training interventions. It departs from the dominant post-hoc RLHF paradigm by arguing the structural failure lies in reward models achieving random-chance accuracy (49–50%) for low-resource languages, rendering alignment fundamentally inoperative rather than merely degraded. The paper also extends the safety evaluation task by elevating False Refusal Rate to a co-equal metric alongside Attack Success Rate, drawing on decolonial AI theory (Mohamed et al., 2020) and socio-materiality (Orlikowski, 2010) to frame the technical gap as a reproduction of colonial knowledge hierarchies rather than a purely engineering shortfall.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The pre-training bottleneck diagnosis—if independently replicated—redirects the field's investment from ever-more-sophisticated post-hoc alignment toward earlier pipeline interventions, with the dialectal ablation (eWAVE, 50 English dialects) providing a within-language proxy that isolates the alignment objective as the locus of residual disparity even when SFT is explicitly dialect-aware. The governance stakes are concrete: with 22+ countries mandating automated content moderation, the asymmetric pattern (porous to harm, restrictive of legitimate speech) creates an infrastructure exploitable for censorship, making the FRR metric a fairness and governance concern, not merely a usability one. Key limitations temper the claims: the headline 35× harmfulness gap and 49–50% RM accuracy rest primarily on Shen et al. (2024)'s 19-language evaluation and warrant independent replication, and the systematic review's restriction to English-language publications is itself a self-exemplifying instance of the documented bias. Open questions include whether Safety Context Distillation scales beyond the ~20 languages with substantial deployment footprints, how participatory harm specification can be operationalized at platform scale without extracting community labor, and whether the proposed joint ASR/FRR metrics can be enforced through procurement and regulatory standards.
