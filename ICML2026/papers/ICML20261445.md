## Problem

Machine learning interatomic potentials (MLIPs) are bottlenecked by inference time and GPU memory consumption, preventing realistic simulation scales. While restricting each atom to its n nearest neighbors would induce useful graph sparsity, naive maximum-neighbor enforcement creates non-differentiable cusps in the potential energy surface, rendering molecular dynamics simulations grossly unstable. The paper challenges the long-held assumption that the cutoff radius must be a fixed constant to maintain a smooth PES.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261445/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes atom coordinates and types as input (standard point-cloud representation) and outputs system energy and per-atom forces, as in standard MLIPs. Unusually, it also computes a per-atom dynamic cutoff radius c_v that adapts to local atomic density, making the resulting atom graph potentially asymmetric (atom a may have an edge to b but not vice versa). An initial neighbor list is still constructed using a fixed hard cutoff h, after which the dynamic cutoff prunes edges and serves as the weighting radius for message passing.

## Method

The dynamic cutoff is computed in three steps per atom: (1) a soft rank R_u is assigned to each neighbor u via a sum of sigmoid-based smooth indicators over pairwise distance comparisons, modulated by a polynomial envelope p(r/h) that vanishes smoothly at the hard cutoff; (2) a Gaussian weighting function ω centered at target neighbor count μ with width σ is applied over the ranks; (3) the dynamic cutoff c_v is a weighted average of neighbor distances using ω(R_u)·p(r_uv/h) as weights. The polynomial envelope (with p(1)=p'(1)=p''(1)=0) guarantees that atoms entering or leaving the hard cutoff do not introduce discontinuities, and the paper proves the full formulation is second-order differentiable, satisfying the minimum smoothness requirement for stable NVE molecular dynamics. The approach is model-agnostic and is implemented on MACE, Nequip, Orbv3, and TensorNet, yielding up to 2.26× memory reduction and 2.04× inference speedup with minimal accuracy loss on MD22 and MatPES benchmarks.

## Results

On the MD22 molecular dataset, dynamic cutoff models maintain energy MAEs within 0.1–0.2 meV/atom and force MAEs within 2–3 meV/Å of their fixed-cutoff counterparts across all 7 molecules and 4 models. On the MatPES-r2scan materials dataset (~400K structures), energy errors increase by at most 11 meV/atom (Nequip: 59→70) and force errors by at most 17 meV/Å (MACE: 173→190). The dynamic cutoff reduces total graph edges from 677,000 to 297,000 (materials) and 805,040 to 588,141 (molecules), yielding up to 2.26× memory reduction and 2.04× inference speedup on real-world 7,500-atom MOF and 16,926-atom protein systems.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the dynamic cutoff against the fixed-cutoff (6.0 Å) version of each of the four models (MACE, Nequip, Orbv3, TensorNet) under identical hyperparameters. On MD22, the dynamic cutoff introduces small but consistent accuracy regressions: MACE energy rises by ~0.05–0.07 meV/atom, Nequip energy by up to ~0.43 meV/atom (AT-AT), Orbv3 energy by ~0.13 meV/atom, and TensorNet shows near-identical or even slightly better energy on some molecules (e.g., 6.071 vs. 6.374 on AC-ALA3-NHME). Force errors increase by 1–7 meV/Å depending on model and molecule. On MatPES, the regressions are modest: Orbv3 and Nequip forces are nearly unchanged (+1 meV/Å), while MACE forces increase by 17 meV/Å and TensorNet by 8 meV/Å. The dynamic cutoff 'loses' slightly on accuracy in every case but 'wins' decisively on efficiency, delivering 2.26× less GPU memory and 2.04× faster inference on large systems. The paper also shows that a reduced fixed cutoff (5.4141 Å, calibrated to average 40 neighbors) performs worse than the dynamic cutoff on MatPES (energy 53 vs. 43, force 171 vs. 160), demonstrating that the dynamic cutoff is not merely equivalent to a smaller fixed radius.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the polynomial envelope function from Gasteiger et al. (2020) and integrates a novel dynamic cutoff mechanism into four established equivariant/invariant MLIP architectures (MACE, Nequip, Orbv3, TensorNet). It departs from prior efficiency work on MLIPs—custom CUDA kernels for equivariant operations, layer pruning, and distributed inference—by targeting the atom graph structure itself rather than the computation within it. The key conceptual departure is from the maximum-neighbor-restriction approach of Kozinsky et al. (2023) and Rhodes et al. (2025), which induces graph sparsity but produces a discontinuous potential energy surface and unstable simulation; this paper replaces the hard neighbor cap with a smooth, provably second-order differentiable dynamic radius that targets an average neighbor count.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dynamic cutoff provides a drop-in, architecture-agnostic mechanism to reduce memory and inference time by ~2x while preserving simulation stability, and its orthogonality to kernel-level and distributed-inference optimizations means gains multiply rather than saturate—potentially enabling 64x+ larger simulatable systems when combined. A key limitation is that the dynamic cutoff cannot guarantee exactly μ neighbors for all configurations (e.g., equidistant neighbors), and error increases are concentrated on large-radius, electropositive elements where the hard cutoff h may be insufficient. Open questions include how the quadratic-in-neighbors rank computation scales for extremely dense systems, whether the approach extends naturally to long-range (non-decaying) interactions, and whether the target-neighbor-count formulation can be made adaptive per-atom-type or per-simulation-phase.
