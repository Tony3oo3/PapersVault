## Problem

Existing GPU-friendly iterative methods for matrix functions (Newton-Schulz, PolarExpress, CANS) require a priori spectral bounds—such as σ_min and σ_max—to construct their polynomial updates, and convergence degrades sharply when the actual spectrum deviates from the assumed range. PRISM addresses this by providing a distribution-free, spectrum-adaptive framework that dynamically adjusts polynomial coefficients to the evolving spectrum of the current iterate, eliminating the need for any prior spectral knowledge.

## Input / Output

The method takes a matrix A ∈ ℝ^{m×n} (or n×n) and produces the desired matrix function (polar factor UV^T, square root A^{1/2}, inverse square root A^{-1/2}, matrix sign, inverse, or inverse p-th root) via a fixed number K of polynomial iterations. At each iteration, the algorithm consumes the current residual matrix R_k (e.g., I − X_k^T X_k for polar decomposition) and a small fixed Gaussian sketch matrix S ∈ ℝ^{n×16} to compute one adaptive scalar coefficient α_k in O(n²p) time, then applies a degree-d polynomial update to X_k. No eigendecomposition, SVD, or explicit singular-value estimates are ever required.

## Method

PRISM is a two-part meta-algorithm: Part I recasts any iterative matrix function computation as approximating a scalar target function f(ξ) = (1−ξ)^{−1/2} (or variant) over the spectrum of a residual matrix R_k, using a degree-d Taylor polynomial f_d(ξ). Part II replaces the fixed Taylor coefficient of the highest-degree term with an adaptively fitted scalar α_k, chosen to minimize the Frobenius norm of the next residual over the eigenvalues of R_k. To keep this fitting cheap, PRISM uses a randomized Oblivious Subspace Embedding (a p×n Gaussian sketch with p=16) to approximate the trace-based coefficients of the resulting quartic polynomial in α_k, reducing the per-iteration overhead from O(n³) to O(n²p). The constrained minimization α_k ∈ [ℓ, u] (e.g., [1/2, 1] for 3rd-order, [3/8, 29/20] for 5th-order) guarantees that the accelerated iteration converges at least as fast as the classical Newton-Schulz, with quadratic worst-case convergence preserved even under sketching (Theorem 4.2). The framework is unified: the same Part II acceleration applies identically to Newton-Schulz for sign/square-root/polar, DB Newton for square roots, inverse Newton for inverse p-th roots, and Chebyshev for inverses, as tabulated in Table 1.

## Results

On GPT-2 (10 layers, 1024 dim, 200M FineWeb tokens) trained with the Muon optimizer, PRISM-3 achieves a final validation loss of 4.9886, outperforming PolarExpress (5.4523) and AdamW (6.8689). On NanoGPT (124M params, 2200 steps on FineWeb), PRISM-5 with 5 iterations per step reaches a validation loss of 4.015 in 32.3 minutes with an average LMO residual error of 8.97, compared to 4.403 at 3 iterations (29.2 min) and 4.926 at 1 iteration (26.5 min). In the Shampoo optimizer experiments on ResNet-20/CIFAR-10 and ResNet-32/CIFAR-100, PRISM-accelerated 5th-order Newton-Schulz consistently outperforms both PolarExpress and eigendecomposition in validation accuracy over the first 50 epochs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PRISM is compared against two main baselines: PolarExpress (Amsel et al., 2026) and classical Newton-Schulz (Higham et al., 2005). Against PolarExpress, PRISM wins decisively on GPT-2 Muon training (4.9886 vs. 5.4523 validation loss, a ~0.46 absolute improvement) and on Shampoo training where PRISM achieves higher validation accuracy on both ResNet-20/CIFAR-10 and ResNet-32/CIFAR-100. PolarExpress degrades significantly when the input spectral range deviates from its pre-optimized interval [10^{-3}, 1], whereas PRISM maintains stable speedup across σ_min ∈ [10^{-12}, 1/2] without requiring any spectral bounds. Against classical Newton-Schulz, PRISM provides consistent GPU-time speedups for both polar decomposition and square root computation across the full range of condition numbers, while PolarExpress can actually slow down convergence relative to classical Newton-Schulz under spectral mismatch. On convergence in iteration count (Figures 3–4), PRISM converges fastest for both Gaussian random matrices (varying aspect ratios) and heavy-tailed HTMP matrices, with the adaptive α_k coefficient automatically adjusting to the evolving spectrum.

## Relation to Prior Work

PRISM unifies classical Newton-Schulz iterations (Higham et al., 2005; Kenney & Laub, 1991) under a single meta-algorithmic template, then accelerates them by replacing fixed Taylor-polynomial coefficients with spectrum-adaptive ones fitted via randomized sketching (building on Chen et al., 2011 for polynomial fitting and Murray et al., 2023 for OSE-based sketching). It departs from recent ML-specific methods like PolarExpress (Amsel et al., 2026) and CANS (Grishina et al., 2025), which require a priori spectral bounds (σ_min, σ_max) to pre-optimize their polynomials, by eliminating this requirement entirely through per-iteration adaptive fitting. The framework also generalizes beyond polar decomposition (the sole focus of PolarExpress and CANS) to square roots, inverse roots, matrix sign, and matrix inverse via established reduction theorems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By removing the need for spectral bounds—which are as costly to estimate as the matrix function itself—PRISM makes adaptive polynomial acceleration practical for real training loops where gradient spectra shift every step. The negligible overhead (sketch dimension 5–16, O(n²p) vs. O(n³) per iteration) means the speedup is essentially free, and the method transfers across optimizers (Shampoo, Muon) and spectral regimes (Marchenko-Pastur, heavy-tailed) without retuning. Open questions include whether the framework extends naturally to rational-function iterations, whether the constant sketch dimension suffices in the worst case for very ill-conditioned inputs, and whether the approach generalizes to non-symmetric or rectangular residual structures beyond the current theorems.
