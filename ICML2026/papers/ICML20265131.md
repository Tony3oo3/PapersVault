## Problem

Training neural networks on analog in-memory computing (AIMC) hardware is hindered by device update asymmetry, which causes systematic weight drift toward a device-specific symmetric point (SP) that generally does not coincide with the training optimum. Existing methods assume the SP is known and pre-calibrated to zero via costly pulse-based zero-shifting, but the required pulse count scales inversely with device granularity, making high-precision devices prohibitively expensive to calibrate. The paper asks whether the SP can be tracked dynamically during training to eliminate this separate calibration stage and reduce overall pulse complexity.

## Input / Output

The method operates on analog crossbar arrays where weights are stored as conductance values and updated via discrete pulses of granularity Δw_min, with device-specific response functions q+(w) and q−(w) governing potentiation and depression. Training data (MNIST 28×28, CIFAR-100 32×32, ImageNet-1K 224×224) is fed as analog voltages, and the algorithm simultaneously produces trained model weights on the analog device and a running SP estimate maintained on a digital device. The SP estimate is not a one-time calibration output but a continuously updated quantity that feeds back into the weight update rule at every iteration.

## Method

RIDER formulates SP tracking as a bilevel optimization: a lower-level residual sequence P on an analog device performs stochastic gradient descent, an upper-level sequence W on a second analog device is updated using the residual (P−Q), and a digital moving-average sequence Q tracks the SP by exploiting the inherent SP-attraction property of the P update (the |∇f|·G term pulls P toward the SP). The enhanced E-RIDER introduces a chopper variable c_k that randomly flips sign, separating the high-frequency sign-flipping gradient component from the low-frequency SP-drift component in P; the moving average then acts as a low-pass filter (Lemma 3.10) that suppresses the gradient oscillation while retaining the SP drift, accelerating convergence of Q to the true SP. A periodic synchronization mechanism stores a copy of Q on an auxiliary analog device and only reprograms it when the chopper sign flips, keeping weight-programming overhead comparable to prior methods. Theoretically, RIDER achieves O(1/√K) convergence matching standard SGD while requiring O(δ⁻²) total pulses, versus O(δ⁻² + δ⁻¹Δw_min⁻¹) for two-stage calibration-then-training approaches.

## Results

On MNIST (FCN, 40 epochs), E-RIDER achieves 95.45% test accuracy with zero SP, outperforming AGAD (92.59%) and TT-v2 (90.26%). Under a nonzero SP offset (mean=0.4, std=0.4), E-RIDER maintains 96.23% while TT-v2 collapses to 67.85%. On CIFAR-100 (ResNet-18, 80 epochs), E-RIDER reaches 93.75% vs. AGAD's 90.81% and TT-v2's 75.19% at zero SP, and retains 91.54% under the same nonzero SP offset where TT-v2 drops to 71.01%. The paper also reports that E-RIDER consistently achieves lower total pulse cost than the two-stage ZS+TT-v2 approach across all device-state settings on LeNet-5 (MNIST).

## Baselines & Comparisons

E-RIDER consistently outperforms both baselines across all SP offset settings on both MNIST and CIFAR-100. Against TT-v2 (Gokmen, 2021), which uses a two-stage ZS calibration followed by fixed-reference training, E-RIDER gains 5.2 points at zero SP on MNIST (95.45% vs. 90.26%) and 18.6 points on CIFAR-100 (93.75% vs. 75.19%); under a large SP offset (mean=0.4, std=0.4), the gap widens to 28.4 points on MNIST (96.23% vs. 67.85%) because TT-v2 cannot compensate for nonzero reference drift. Against AGAD (Rasch et al., 2023), the prior dynamic SP-tracking method that lacks a residual learning mechanism, E-RIDER gains 2.9 points at zero SP on MNIST (95.45% vs. 92.59%) and 2.9 points on CIFAR-100 (93.75% vs. 90.81%), with the advantage growing to 4.2 points on MNIST under the largest SP offset (96.23% vs. 92.06%). The paper attributes E-RIDER's edge over AGAD to the bilevel residual-learning structure that rescales updates for finer effective granularity, and its edge over TT-v2 to the dynamic (rather than static) SP tracking that avoids costly pre-calibration pulses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Residual Learning framework of Wu et al. (2025), which provides a bilevel-optimization formulation for compensating update asymmetry in analog in-memory training but assumes the symmetric point (SP) is exactly zero. It also extends the zero-shifting (ZS) calibration algorithm of Kim et al. (2019) by providing the first theoretical pulse-complexity analysis, and departs from the dynamic SP-tracking method AGAD (Rasch et al., 2023/2024) by introducing a residual-learning mechanism and convergence guarantees that AGAD lacks. The chopping-and-filtering enhancement draws on classical digital signal processing (low-pass filtering, chopper stabilization) to separate the SP-drift signal from the stochastic gradient signal in the frequency domain, a cross-domain idea not previously applied to AIMC training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a fundamental 'device dilemma'—higher-precision analog devices (smaller response granularity Δw_min) paradoxically require more pulses for SP calibration—motivating dynamic tracking as a necessary alternative to static pre-calibration. By jointly performing SP tracking and model training with O(1/√K) convergence, RIDER/E-RIDER eliminates the separate calibration phase and its error propagation, which is practically significant for energy-constrained edge deployments where pulse budget is limited. Open questions include whether the strongly-convexity assumption can be relaxed for non-convex deep network training, whether the method transfers to real (non-simulated) hardware with additional noise sources, and how the approach scales to very large crossbar arrays where per-device SP heterogeneity is more severe.
