## Problem

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA and adapters add small auxiliary modules to pretrained models, yet the field lacks a simple theoretical explanation for why such small changes suffice for downstream adaptation. A fundamental question remains unresolved: do we actually need to introduce new parameters, or does the pretrained weight matrix already contain enough structured redundancy to support effective adaptation on its own?

## Input / Output

SliceFine operates on standard pretrained backbones (LLaMA-3B for text, RoBERTa-base for NLU, ViT-Base-Patch16-224 for images, VideoMAE-base for video) and consumes the same downstream task data as full fine-tuning: text sequences, 224×224 RGB images, or video clips. The output is a class label or text generation, identical to the backbone's native output format. Uniquely, the method introduces zero new parameters—it only modifies a small structured slice (a contiguous row or column block of rank r, typically r=1–5) within existing weight matrices, with the active slice position shifting every N training steps in a cyclic sweep.

## Method

The paper establishes the Universal Winning-Slice Hypothesis (UWSH), grounded in two properties of pretrained networks: spectral balance (the eigenspectra of any row or column partition of a weight matrix are nearly identical across groups, with inter-group variance ρ ≈ 0.001) and high task energy (the frozen backbone's PCA/NTK spectrum concentrates most variance in a small task-relevant subspace of dimension k_task). Under these conditions, Theorem 2.4 proves that any slice of rank r ≥ k_task has a nonzero restricted gradient and can independently reduce downstream loss (local winner), and that a small collection of such slices across layers spans the full task subspace (global winner). SliceFine exploits this by training only the active slice at each step via block-coordinate descent: at step t, only entries in mask M_ℓ(t) ⊙ W^(ℓ) receive gradient updates while all other weights remain frozen at their pretrained values; every N steps the mask shifts to a new position, accumulating complementary task-aligned directions. The slice rank is set from a one-time PCA calibration on frozen features, and empirically r=1 often suffices, yielding 15–25% faster throughput, 18% lower peak memory, and 42–50% shorter wall-clock training compared to LoRA-style baselines while matching or exceeding their accuracy.

## Results

On commonsense and math reasoning with LLaMA-3B, SliceFine-5RC achieves the best average scores of 78.79% (commonsense) and 82.13% (math), outperforming AdaLoRA (77.71%/81.41%) and LoRA (78.12%/80.32%) while using only 6.25M trainable parameters versus 33.05M and 13.77M respectively. Even the rank-1 variant (SliceFine-1RC, 1.25M params) reaches 78.66%/81.55%, surpassing lighter baselines like Prefix (74.23%/76.06%) and VeRA (77.32%/81.07%). On VTAB-1K image classification with ViT-Base, SliceFine-5R attains 88.85% average accuracy, exceeding LoRA (88.08%) and AdaLoRA (87.96%) with only 0.415M trainable parameters. On video recognition with VideoMAE-base, SliceFine-5RC reaches 73.09%, outperforming HRA (72.53%) and MiSS (72.99%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SliceFine-5RC (6.25M params) outperforms all baselines on both commonsense (78.79%) and math (82.13%) reasoning averages. Against LoRA (13.77M params), SliceFine-5RC gains +0.67 on commonsense and +1.81 on math while using 55% fewer trainable parameters. Against AdaLoRA (33.05M params), the advantage is larger: +1.08 on commonsense and +0.72 on math with 81% fewer parameters. Against the closest competitor RoCoFT (6.90M params, same row-column update family), SliceFine-5RC leads by +0.28 on commonsense and +0.58 on math with slightly fewer parameters. On VTAB-1K vision tasks, SliceFine-5R (0.415M params) surpasses LoRA (0.833M) by +0.77% and AdaLoRA (2.011M) by +0.89% in average image accuracy, while SliceFine-5RC (0.417M) beats HRA (0.493M) by +0.56% and MiSS (0.668M) by +0.10% on video recognition. The rank-1 variants (1.25M params) already match or exceed VeRA (1.50M) on commonsense reasoning (78.66% vs 77.32%), demonstrating that even a single row-column slice captures most of the task-relevant signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis (LTH) lineage, which requires iterative pruning or importance-based search to identify a special sparse subnetwork, by arguing that in pretrained networks *any* random slice of sufficient width already constitutes a winning ticket—no search is needed. It also diverges from the dominant PEFT paradigm (LoRA, AdaLoRA, VeRA, adapters) that introduces auxiliary parameters, instead updating structured row/column slices of existing weights with zero new parameters. The theoretical grounding draws on NTK/PCA analysis of frozen representations (spectral balance and high task energy) to explain *why* slices work, connecting the LTH and PEFT literatures through a shared geometric lens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The UWSH provides a testable theoretical explanation for why PEFT works at all, shifting the question from 'which parameters to add' to 'which existing parameters to reuse,' with practical implications for deployment (zero added parameters, 42–50% faster training, ~18% less peak memory). Open questions include whether the spectral-balance and high-task-energy assumptions hold for very large or non-dense architectures (MoE, mixture-of-experts), whether the linearized/convex-in-logits regime is sufficient for the strongest downstream tasks, and whether the modest performance margins over strong baselines (often <1 point) persist at scale beyond 3B parameters. The PCA/NTK diagnostic for choosing slice rank is a useful practical tool but has been validated primarily on moderate-sized models.
