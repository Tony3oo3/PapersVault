## Problem

Diffusion models trained on non-overlapping data splits produce strikingly similar outputs from the same noise seed, yet no principled theory explains why this consistency arises or how finite-sample variability shapes the learned denoiser and sampling map. The paper addresses this gap by asking: to what extent do shared Gaussian statistics (mean and covariance) across splits already determine cross-split agreement, and how does the finite number of training samples modify the denoiser's expectation and fluctuation in a quantifiable way?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264874/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The theory takes as input the population covariance spectrum Σ, the dataset size n (via aspect ratio γ = d/n), and a noise scale σ; it requires no knowledge of specific split identities or network architecture. It produces closed-form deterministic equivalents for the denoiser expectation and variance, as well as predictions for the full sampling map (Wiener filter) consistency. Empirical validation uses UNet and DiT diffusion models trained on FFHQ (32/64 px), CIFAR-10/100 (32 px), LSUN bedroom/church (32/64 px), and AFHQ32, with sampling via the Heun ODE solver from a fixed Gaussian noise seed.

## Method

The core technique is deterministic equivalence (DE) from random matrix theory, which replaces the random empirical covariance Σ̂ with a deterministic surrogate governed by a scalar renormalized noise κ(σ²) solving the Silverstein self-consistent equation. For the denoiser expectation, finite data act as an adaptive ridge penalty: the empirical denoiser's expectation equals the population denoiser evaluated at the inflated noise level κ(σ²) > σ², causing systematic overshrinkage of low-variance eigenmodes toward the dataset mean. For the denoiser variance, the fluctuation factorizes into three interpretable components: anisotropy (maximized along eigenmodes whose variance matches κ), inhomogeneity (amplified for inputs displaced along high-variance directions), and global 1/n scaling. A key technical extension applies Balakrishnan's integral representation of fractional matrix powers to derive DE for the sampling map Σ^{1/2}(Σ+σ_T²I)^{-1/2}, enabling closed-form predictions for full diffusion trajectories rather than single-step denoisers. The framework is validated on UNet and DiT architectures, confirming that the linear RMT predictions capture the spectral and spatial structure of cross-split disagreement in the non-memorization regime (n ≥ 3000).

## Results

The RMT framework exactly predicts the expectation and variance of linear denoisers trained on finite data splits, with the finite-sample effect captured by a renormalized noise scale σ²→κ(σ²) solving the Silverstein self-consistent equation. For deep networks (UNet, DiT) on FFHQ64 with n=30,000, the RMT prediction of per-seed cross-split consistency achieves a Spearman correlation of 0.33 (p=2.5×10⁻²⁶) with empirical deviations, using only the population covariance and dataset size. Point-by-point denoiser consistency prediction reaches Pearson r=0.94 at σ²=1, n=1000. The paper identifies a two-phase transition: memorization (n≤1000) where samples resemble training neighbors, and renormalization (n≥3000) where overshrinkage toward the mean and spectral anisotropy dominate, with DiT consistently outperforming UNet in cross-split agreement at every dataset size.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new generative method to benchmark against competing architectures; instead it validates theoretical predictions against empirical behavior. The linear Gaussian (Wiener filter) predictor serves as the primary analytical baseline: it exactly reproduces the sampling map of linear denoisers and qualitatively captures the behavior of deep UNet and DiT models in the non-memorization regime (n≥3000), with generated samples increasingly approaching the linear predictor as n grows. Across architectures, DiT achieves lower cross-split MSE than UNet at every dataset size tested (300 to 30,000), indicating greater consistency. Counterfactual experiments where training splits are stratified along a principal component to induce mean/covariance mismatches show markedly reduced cross-split consistency compared to random i.i.d. splits, confirming that shared Gaussian statistics are the key driver. The RMT variance prediction (Prop. 5.2) correlates with observed per-seed deviations in deep networks (Spearman 0.33, p=2.5×10⁻²⁶ for UNet FFHQ64 n=30k), though absolute deviation magnitudes in deep networks exceed linear-theory predictions, reflecting additional nonlinear sources of variability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two converging threads: (1) empirical observations that diffusion models trained on disjoint data splits produce strikingly similar outputs from the same noise seed (Kadkhodaie et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024), and (2) the finding that the learned score in diffusion models is well-approximated by a linear Gaussian score over much of the noise range (Wang & Vastola, 2024b; Li et al., 2024b). The key departure is the introduction of random matrix theory—specifically deterministic equivalence and the Silverstein self-consistent equation—as a formal analytical tool to quantify how finite-sample variability in the empirical covariance reshapes both the expectation and variance of the denoiser and full sampling trajectory. Technically, the paper extends deterministic equivalence to fractional matrix powers (via Balakrishnan's integral representation), which was not previously available and is needed to analyze the Wiener-filter sampling map Σ^{1/2}(Σ+σ_T²I)^{-1/2} rather than just single-step denoisers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled, quantitative explanation for why diffusion models are reproducible across training splits: shared Gaussian statistics act through a renormalized noise scale σ²→κ(σ²), and cross-split variance factorizes into anisotropy (eigenmode-dependent), inhomogeneity (input-location-dependent), and global 1/n scaling. This gives practitioners a predictive baseline—knowing only the data covariance and sample size, one can predict which noise seeds and spectral directions will produce the largest cross-split deviations. Key limitations include the linear theory's inability to capture the memorization regime (n ≤ 1000) or architecture-specific inductive biases, and the fact that absolute deviation magnitudes in deep networks substantially exceed linear predictions. Open questions include extending the framework to random-feature models to explain the memorization-to-renormalization transition, and understanding how the anisotropic structure of the initial noise space interacts with the data manifold to produce 'magic seeds.'
