## Problem

Data selection is a critical downstream application of data valuation, yet existing methods like Data Shapley and Data Banzhaf lack explicit optimization for selection performance and have no theoretical characterization of when they succeed or fail. The paper identifies that these methods are myopic linear approximations to an underlying sequential decision problem, and that their performance degrades quadratically with utility curvature, but provides no principled alternative that bridges the gap between exact (exponential) optimality and practical efficiency.

## Input / Output

The method takes a training set, a validation set, and a pairwise distance function d as input. It constructs a bipartite graph between training and validation points, selects an optimal distance threshold τ* by minimizing MSE between a coverage surrogate and measured utility over random subsets, and outputs a data selection ranking (a permutation of training points) along with the optimal threshold. In the theoretical DP formulation, data values are produced as position encodings in the optimal selection sequence, where v*(i) = n − t*(i) records each sample's step in the optimal ordering.

## Method

The paper reformulates data selection as a finite-horizon deterministic MDP where the state is the currently selected subset, the action is the next element to add, and the per-step reward is the prefix utility U(s∪{a}), so that cumulative reward exactly equals the area under the selection curve. An exact Bellman DP solution yields the optimal selection sequence, but requires O(n·2ⁿ) utility evaluations. The key theoretical insight is that existing semi-values (Shapley, Banzhaf, Beta Shapley) correspond to myopic ADP policies under a linear surrogate Ũ(S) = b̂ + Σᵢ θᵢ·1[i∈S], and a (1−c)² approximation bound characterizes their degradation under submodular curvature c. To achieve practical efficiency, the paper proposes a bipartite graph-based coverage surrogate: edges connect training and validation points within a distance threshold τ (same class), the coverage utility counts the fraction of validation points 'covered' by a training subset (which is provably submodular), and greedy selection on this surrogate inherits the classical (1−1/e) approximation guarantee while requiring only pairwise distance computations rather than model retraining.

## Results

On eight OpenML datasets at a selection budget of 500, the proposed Bipartite method achieves the highest average accuracy of 0.828, ranking first on three datasets (bbc-embeddings: 0.946, MiniBooNE: 0.804, digits: 0.901) and tying for first on two more (election, electricity). The gap between existing methods and the exact Dynamic Programming optimum ranges from 8.39% (fried) to 23.76% (bbc-embeddings), confirming substantial room for improvement. In utility approximation, Bipartite achieves MSE 0.019, more than 4× lower than the next-best linear regression (MSE 0.089) and over 100× lower than Data Shapley (MSE 2.401). On the DATE-LM LLM fine-tuning benchmark (Llama-3.1-8B, LoRA), BipCov reaches 63.89±0.34% average accuracy across MMLU/GSM8K/BBH, outperforming RDS+ (62.88±0.25%) and Random (62.82±0.31%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Bipartite outperforms all seven baselines in average accuracy at budget 500 (0.828 vs. DataOob 0.826, BetaShap 0.821, DataShap 0.820, and DVRL 0.758). DataOob is the closest competitor, winning outright on 2dplanes, nomao, and fried, but Bipartite dominates on the more structured datasets (bbc-embeddings, MiniBooNE, digits) where coverage-based selection is most advantageous. Data Shapley and Beta Shapley remain competitive at larger budgets (averaging 0.820 and 0.821), consistent with the paper's curvature analysis showing game-theoretic methods degrade less when substitutability effects diminish. Data Banzhaf and AME trail further behind, with Banzhaf showing utility approximation MSE of 0.128 versus Bipartite's 0.019. Influence Function (Koh) is best only on the election dataset in the small-budget setting (Table 1) and does not appear among top-2 performers at budget 500. DVRL, the reinforcement-learning-based method, lags substantially (0.758 average), confirming that learning-based approaches struggle to match principled game-theoretic or coverage-based selection at scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new theoretical ground by reframing data selection as a finite-horizon deterministic MDP, showing that the optimal selection sequence admits an exact dynamic programming solution and that data values are simply encodings of that sequence. It then reinterprets the entire family of game-theoretic semi-values (Data Shapley, Banzhaf, Beta Shapley) as myopic linear-surrogate policies in approximate dynamic programming, a connection not previously made explicit in the data valuation literature. The practical bipartite graph surrogate draws on classical submodular coverage maximization (Nemhauser et al., 1978) and optimization-from-samples ideas (Balkanski et al., 2016), but applies them to the data selection setting with a threshold-tuned coverage utility that preserves submodular structure. Conceptually, the paper departs from the prior framing where data valuation and data selection are treated as separate concerns, instead showing they are two views of the same sequential optimization problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The (1−c)² curvature bound provides the first principled explanation for why Data Shapley and related methods degrade on redundant or homogeneous datasets, aligning with empirical observations that these methods excel on heterogeneous data. The bipartite coverage surrogate offers a practical middle ground between intractable exact DP and myopic linear methods, with the LLM fine-tuning experiment (DATE-LM, Llama-3.1-8B) demonstrating applicability beyond classical tabular benchmarks, though the ~1% improvement over strong baselines like RDS+ is modest. Key open questions include whether lookahead or multi-step ADP policies (beyond myopic) can close the remaining gap to optimal DP at scale, and whether the bipartite construction generalizes to non-coverage utility structures. The framework also suggests that data values should be derived from value functions rather than reward functions, a perspective that could reshape how future valuation methods are designed.
