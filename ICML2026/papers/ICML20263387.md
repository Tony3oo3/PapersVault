## Problem

While linear-time sequence models (SSMs, long-convolutions) have gained traction for long-range biological data, it remains unclear which architecture family offers the best trade-off between scaling efficiency, long-range dependency capture, and practical throughput at trillion-token metagenomic regimes. Prior architectural comparisons were restricted to the 1B parameter scale, leaving the behavior at 7B and beyond unexplored, and the impact of data curation quality on metagenomic scaling laws had not been quantified.

## Input / Output

Models consume raw DNA at single-nucleotide resolution (vocabulary of 512, not k-mers) partitioned into 8,192-token sliding windows with 200 bp overlap, preserving local context at boundaries. For evaluation, inputs include partial coding sequences (first 20–30% of housekeeping genes like ftsZ, recA, secY), conserved S10/spc operon prompts, and deep mutational scanning variant sets; outputs span autoregressively generated nucleotide sequences, per-nucleotide likelihood (perplexity), Spearman fitness correlations, and structurally validated gene completions scored via ESM-Fold pLDDT, USalign TM-scores, and Pfam domain recovery.

## Method

The paper trains and compares three hybrid architectures—Llama3 (global attention), Mamba2-Hybrid, and Striped Hyena (each with ~1 attention layer per 7 recurrent/convolutional layers)—across 100M, 300M, 1B, and 7B parameters on a matched 350B-token budget from either a curated long-read-optimized corpus (BaseData, ~18 kb mean contig) or the public OpenGenome2 dataset. From these runs the authors derive quality-aware power-law scaling laws (L ∝ C^−α), showing that BaseData yields a steeper exponent (α≈0.059) than OpenGenome2 (α≈0.047), and that Llama achieves the lowest validation loss at 7B despite comparable FLOPs. A key mechanistic finding is a structural-vs-semantic trade-off: linear-time models extrapolate coding density and sequence complexity more gracefully beyond the 8,192-token training window, but Llama's full attention preserves semantic fidelity, correctly recovering conserved downstream operon genes and achieving superior zero-shot fitness prediction and rare-annotation recall. Guided by these results, the Llama architecture was scaled to 28B parameters on 9.7T tokens, validating the power-law extrapolation over three orders of magnitude in model size.

## Results

At the 7B-parameter scale, Llama achieves a mean pLDDT of 78.99 and mean TM-score of 0.83 for gene autocompletion, substantially outperforming Mamba (72.65 / 0.73) and Hyena (70.82 / 0.73). Llama also leads on domain architecture recovery (94.0% vs. 73.3% and 69.3%), zero-shot fitness prediction (Spearman ρ = 0.339 vs. 0.289 and 0.325 on RNAGym), and perplexity on rare KEGG-annotated genes (2.47 vs. 2.55 and 2.52). However, in long-context generation beyond the 8,192-token training window, Mamba and Hyena maintain higher coding density (0.78 and 0.81 vs. 0.60) and lower DUST masking rates (4.2 and 2.33 vs. 15), revealing a structural-extrapolation advantage for linear-time architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is a head-to-head evaluation of three 7B-parameter architectures—Llama (autoregressive Transformer), Mamba-2-Hybrid (state-space model), and Striped Hyena (long-convolutional)—trained on identical 350-billion-token subsets of BaseData and OpenGenome2. Llama decisively wins on all semantic and structural-fidelity metrics: it achieves 94% domain-architecture recovery versus 73.3% (Mamba) and 69.3% (Hyena), a 0.83 mean TM-score versus 0.73 for both alternatives, and the best zero-shot fitness correlation (ρ = 0.339 vs. 0.289 and 0.325). On the OpenGenome2 public dataset, the same architectural ordering holds, confirming the results are not an artifact of the proprietary BaseData corpus. The one domain where Llama loses is long-context structural extrapolation: beyond its 8,192-token training window, Llama's coding density drops sharply (0.60) while Mamba (0.78) and Hyena (0.81) degrade more gracefully, and Llama's DUST masking rate (15) is 3–6× higher than the linear-time models. The authors frame this as a fundamental trade-off: global attention enables precise semantic retrieval (correct downstream operon genes, accurate fitness prediction) at the cost of structural stability far outside the training horizon, whereas fixed-size recurrent states preserve aggregate statistics but lose the token-level detail needed for biological identity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the architectural comparison initiated by Nguyen et al. (2024) and Brixi et al. (2025) for genomic sequence modeling from the 1B-parameter regime to 7B and 28B parameters, adding a finer-grained 300M Llama checkpoint for scaling-law fitting. It builds on the scaling-law methodology of Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022) but introduces a data-quality axis (curated long-read BaseData vs. public short-read OpenGenome2) that prior genomic scaling studies did not isolate. The key conceptual departure is from the field's recent momentum toward linear-time architectures (Mamba, Hyena) for long biological sequences: by matching parameter count and token budget across three architectural families, the paper shows that the Transformer's global attention mechanism becomes the decisive advantage at scale for semantically demanding tasks, a finding not available in prior work limited to ≤1B parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, compute-aware blueprint for practitioners choosing architectures for metagenomic foundation models, with the actionable finding that Transformers dominate on semantic retrieval (operon gene recovery, fitness prediction, rare-gene perplexity) while linear-time models retain an edge in structural extrapolation beyond the training window. The data-quality result—that long-read-optimized assemblies yield a measurably steeper scaling exponent (α≈0.059 vs. 0.047)—argues that curation strategy is as important as architecture selection, a point underemphasized in prior scaling-law work. Open questions include whether the Transformer advantage persists beyond 28B parameters, whether the context-extrapolation limitation (sharp coding-density drop past 8k tokens, confirmed even at 28B) can be mitigated without sacrificing semantic fidelity, and whether findings generalize to eukaryotic or multi-modal genomic data. The proprietary nature of BaseData and the limited number of scales per architecture (3–4 points) temper the statistical strength of the scaling-law claims.
