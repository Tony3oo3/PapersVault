## Problem

Existing time series forecasting methods either rely exclusively on historical patterns or incorporate future conditions only as deterministic, structured interventions (e.g., 0/1 event flags), leaving them unable to handle complex, stochastic, and counterfactual future scenarios expressed in natural language. A further fundamental obstacle is that counterfactual settings lack ground-truth future sequences, making standard accuracy-based evaluation inapplicable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261355/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a historical time series of length L_h, a historical textual condition (free-form text describing past extrinsic factors), and a future textual condition (free-form text describing hypothetical future events) as input, and produces a future time series forecast of length L_f via a diffusion denoising process. In counterfactual settings, the (x_h, c_f) pair never co-occurred in reality, so no ground-truth future exists; the model instead generates a distribution over plausible futures. Textual conditions are unconstrained natural language rather than structured categorical attributes, enabling richer and more flexible conditioning.

## Method

TADIFF is a DDIM-based diffusion model with a DiT-inspired transformer noise estimator that introduces a text-attribution mechanism: a condition-aware forward diffusion process strips the influence of the historical condition c_h from x_{h,0} to extract intrinsic features x_{h,T}, which then serve as the initial noise state for the denoising-based forecast conditioned on the future text c_f. Training jointly optimizes a forecasting loss (supervising only the future portion of a concatenated history+future sequence) and an attribution loss (supervising noise estimation on the historical sequence given c_h), sharing all parameters. To improve adaptability to unseen counterfactual conditions, the model is fine-tuned on constructed counterfactual data where alternative future conditions are randomly reassigned to historical sequences, using DTTC semantic consistency scores (rather than ground-truth MSE) as the optimization signal. Evaluation employs a dual-encoder DTTC model (PatchTST time-series encoder + LongCLIP text encoder trained via contrastive learning) that disentangles intrinsic and extrinsic features to measure forecast consistency with both historical patterns and future textual conditions even when no ground truth is available.

## Results

TADIFF achieves the best numerical accuracy (MAE, MSE) and semantic consistency (DTTC-I, DTTC-E) on four out of five datasets (Synth, ETTm1, Traffic, Exchange) under factual conditions, and the best MSE and DTTC-E on Weather. On ETTm1, TADIFF attains MAE 0.57, MSE 0.76, DTTC-I 10.01, and DTTC-E 146.91; on Traffic, MAE 0.35, MSE 0.27, DTTC-I 17.90, and DTTC-E 81.38. Under counterfactual conditions, TADIFF consistently outperforms all baselines on semantic consistency metrics, demonstrating superior adaptability to unseen future conditions. The DTTC retrieval-based validation confirms metric reliability, with DTTC-I reaching up to 91.90% and DTTC-E up to 98.44% retrieval accuracy across datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TADIFF outperforms all eight baselines on the majority of metrics across datasets. Against unimodal baselines (DLinear, PatchTST, Sundial), TADIFF achieves substantially better DTTC-I and DTTC-E scores, confirming that incorporating textual conditions is critical for condition-aware forecasting; for example, on Traffic, TADIFF's DTTC-I of 17.90 surpasses PatchTST's 16.36 and Sundial's 11.27. Against the generation baseline VerbalTS, TADIFF achieves much lower MSE (0.76 vs. 1.53 on ETTm1) while maintaining comparable DTTC-E, showing that respecting historical intrinsic features is essential for numerical accuracy. Against the class-based counterfactual model CT, TADIFF achieves far lower MSE (0.76 vs. 14.65 on ETTm1) and higher DTTC-I, demonstrating the advantage of textual conditions over fixed categorical treatments. Against text-based forecasting baselines (TimeMMD, TimeCMA, IATSF), TADIFF consistently achieves the best or near-best results on both numerical and semantic metrics, with the largest gaps on DTTC-I (e.g., 17.90 vs. 11.12 for IATSF on Traffic), indicating that the text-attribution mechanism effectively balances historical and future influences. Under counterfactual conditions (Table 2), TADIFF maintains its lead on semantic consistency, while baselines like CT and IATSF degrade more significantly, confirming TADIFF's superior generalization to unseen condition combinations.

## Relation to Prior Work

TADIFF builds on DDIM (Song et al., 2020) as its generative backbone and adapts the DiT transformer architecture (Peebles & Xie, 2023) for the noise estimator, but departs from both by repurposing the diffusion forward process as a condition-aware attribution mechanism rather than a pure noising step. It extends the counterfactual forecasting line (Melnychuk et al., 2022; Mu et al., 2025), which relies on fixed categorical treatments, to free-form textual conditions, and goes beyond intervention-aware forecasting (Xu et al., 2024) by handling stochastic, unrealized future scenarios. The DTTC evaluation metric draws on CLIP-style contrastive learning (Radford et al., 2021) and PatchTST (Nie et al., 2022) to disentangle intrinsic from extrinsic features, creating a new assessment paradigm for settings where ground-truth futures are absent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap: real-world forecasting under 'what if' scenarios (e.g., a sudden policy change or extreme weather) where the future condition is described in natural language and no ground-truth outcome exists. The DTTC evaluation framework is potentially reusable beyond this specific task for any conditional generation/forecasting problem lacking ground truth. Limitations include the reliance on text quality (generated captions for unimodal datasets), the computational cost of diffusion inference (~340 ms/sample vs. 17 ms for non-diffusion baselines), and the acknowledged difficulty of evaluating forecasts of varying lengths with model-based metrics. Open questions include whether the text-attribution mechanism generalizes to longer horizons, multi-variate settings, or conditions expressed in structured formats beyond free text.
