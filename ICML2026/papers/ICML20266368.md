## Problem

Current AI agent evaluation relies on scalar performance metrics (task success, reward) that can certify behaviorally distinct policies as equivalent — a phenomenon the paper terms equifinality. This masks critical differences in strategy, robustness, and value alignment that only become visible through systematic observation of the behavioral processes generating actions. The paper argues that AI agents, as dynamic behavioral systems interacting with non-stationary environments, require a dedicated science of behavioral evaluation rather than outcome-based benchmarking alone.

## Input / Output

The proposed behavioral tests consume agent trajectories (sequences of actions conditioned on observed environment states), controlled environment variants enabling counterfactual manipulation, and multi-agent interaction logs. They produce behavioral descriptors B(π) — auxiliary functions derived from trajectory analysis that are sensitive to policy-level regularities (decision heuristics, robustness, social modeling) while the task metric M remains invariant. A key design constraint is that B must distinguish policies that are metrically equivalent (M(π_A) = M(π_B)) but behaviorally divergent (φ(π_A) ≠ φ(π_B)), and environments must support systematic interventions rather than fixed task specifications.

## Method

The paper formalizes behavioral testing through an identification framework: given a metric M and a behavioral property φ not directly observable from M, a behavioral test introduces an auxiliary function B derived from trajectory analysis such that φ(π_A) ≠ φ(π_B) implies B(π_A) ≠ B(π_B), even when M(π_A) = M(π_B). Three concrete research directions are proposed: (1) automated process-tracing to infer implicit objectives, constraints, and heuristics from action sequences (policy inference), (2) instrumenting realistic environments with controlled interventions for counterfactual and behavioral consistency testing across environment variants, and (3) systematic characterization of emergent multi-agent behavioral phenomena where agents adapt to each other's presence. The approach draws on Tinbergen's four questions from ethology and bounded rationality from behavioral economics to structure what to observe and how to interpret it. A closed feedback loop is proposed where behavioral tests identify policy-level attributes missed by task rewards, which are then encoded as training signals, constraints, or test-time interventions, and the resulting policy is retested behaviorally on held-out data.

## Results

This is a position paper that proposes a research agenda rather than presenting experimental results. It does not report quantitative metrics or benchmark scores. The paper's contribution is conceptual: it formalizes the identification problem for behavioral evaluation (showing that a metric M can fail to identify a behavioral property φ when M(π_A)=M(π_B) but φ(π_A)≠φ(π_B)) and outlines three research directions—process-tracing of action chains, systematic environment design, and multi-agent interaction analysis—without providing empirical validation of the proposed framework.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare its proposed behavioral testing framework against specific baseline methods with quantitative metrics. The authors position their agenda as complementary to (rather than competing with) three existing paradigms: interpretability (which focuses on internal representations), formal verification (which checks pre-specified properties), and performance benchmarking (which measures task success on fixed tasks). They argue that all three leave a gap: none systematically probes how an agent's strategy differs from another's when both achieve the same outcome metric. The paper references prior work in NLP (Ribeiro et al., 2020), continuous control (Seyde et al., 2021; Raffin et al., 2023), and RL environment design (Voelcker et al., 2024) as precedents where scalar metrics failed to distinguish behaviorally different policies, but does not run head-to-head comparisons.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper repurposes the conceptual apparatus of behavioral sciences—Tinbergen's four questions, bounded rationality, ethological methodology, and behavioral economics—to argue that AI agent evaluation requires a fundamentally different paradigm than performance benchmarking. It explicitly positions itself as complementary to (not replacing) interpretability, formal verification, and competitive benchmarking, and acknowledges scattered precedents in RL (Seyde et al. 2021, Raffin et al. 2023, Voelcker et al. 2024) and NLP (Ribeiro et al. 2020) where scalar metrics failed to distinguish behaviorally distinct policies. The paper's novelty is in unifying these fragments into a formal research agenda with an identification-theoretic framing (metric M does not identify behavioral property φ) and three concrete research directions for LLM-based agents specifically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a genuine gap: as LLM agents proliferate in open-ended, partially observable environments, outcome-based evaluation becomes increasingly blind to strategy, robustness, and alignment properties that matter under distribution shift and multi-agent interaction. The main limitation is that this is a roadmap without operationalized methods—no scalable tools for policy inference from trajectories, no standardized systematic environments, and no multi-agent behavioral test suite yet exist. Open questions include how to make behavioral tests reproducible and comparable across labs, how to handle the combinatorial explosion in multi-agent settings, and how to close the loop between discovered behavioral properties and training signals (the Discover/Improve cycle in Figure 2).
