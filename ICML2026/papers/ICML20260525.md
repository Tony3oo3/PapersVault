## Problem

Existing black-box detectors for LLM-generated text either require costly per-instance regeneration (e.g., DetectGPT, DNA-GPT) or rely on global statistics that degrade under proxy-model mismatch and domain shift. The paper addresses the gap of a detector that avoids both retraining and per-input contrastive generation while remaining robust to distribution shift, by exploiting stable token-dynamics signals that can be reused across test samples.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260525/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single test text passage and a fixed proxy LM (e.g., GPT-2-Large) as input, requiring only one forward pass to compute token surprisals. Pre-built human and machine reference transition matrices (constructed once offline from ~300 reference texts per side) are reused for all test passages, giving a reference-per-test budget of B=2 versus B=10–100 for regeneration-based baselines. The output is a scalar ΔGJS_n score and a binary human/machine label via a tunable threshold τ, with the reference-test length ratio α = N/n governing the GJS weighting.

## Method

SurpMark discretizes continuous token surprisals from a proxy LM into k interpretable states (typically k=6–7) via k-means, then summarizes each passage by its empirical first-order Markov transition matrix over these states. Detection is framed as a two-reference likelihood-free hypothesis test: the test transition matrix is scored against fixed human and machine references using a generalized Jensen-Shannon divergence, and the decision statistic ΔGJS_n = GJS(M_P, M_T, α) − GJS(M_Q, M_T, α) measures relative proximity. The key insight is that LLM text exhibits a characteristic 'recovery' pattern—returning to highly predictable tokens immediately after an unexpected one—which is captured by transition dynamics rather than marginal surprisal distributions, making the detector robust to proxy-model mismatch. Theoretically, the paper derives a discretization-estimation tradeoff yielding an optimal bin count k* = Θ(N^{1/5}) and proves that ΔGJS_n exactly equals the normalized log-likelihood ratio under the idealized Markov model, providing a principled justification for the test statistic.

## Results

On 9 open-source source models (averaged across XSum, WritingPrompts, and SQuAD), SurpMark (k=6) achieves a mean AUROC of 91.23, ranking first on 6 of 9 models and top-2 on 7 of 9, surpassing the strongest baseline Lastde++ (90.04). On 3 closed-source models (Gemini-1.5-Flash, GPT-4.1-mini, GPT-5-Chat), SurpMark (k=7) reaches a mean AUROC of 78.32, outperforming the best baseline R-Detect (69.55) by nearly 9 points, with particularly strong gains on GPT-5-Chat (81.33 vs. 67.75). SurpMark also operates at a reference-per-test budget of B=2, compared to B=10 for DNA-GPT and B=100 for DetectGPT/Fast-DetectGPT/Lastde++, yielding 5×–50× lower reference cost while avoiding per-input regeneration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SurpMark outperforms all 13 baselines on average across both open-source and closed-source settings. Against the strongest open-source baseline Lastde++ (90.04 avg AUROC), SurpMark leads by 1.19 points, with the gap widening to 16.57 points on closed-source models (77.72 vs. 61.15). Fast-DetectGPT (88.01 open / 61.40 closed) and DetectGPT (80.37 / 64.75) are consistently outperformed by 3.2 and 10.9 points respectively on open-source averages. R-Detect, the closest conceptual competitor (also a two-reference relative test), trails SurpMark by 23.0 points on open-source (68.24 vs. 91.23) and 8.2 points on closed-source (69.55 vs. 77.72). Global-statistic baselines (Likelihood, LogRank, Entropy) and spectral methods (FourierGPT) are substantially weaker, with Entropy and FourierGPT falling below 60 AUROC on open-source averages. On the hardest closed-source model GPT-5-Chat, SurpMark (81.33) beats the next-best R-Detect (67.75) by 13.6 points, while most other baselines drop below 55 AUROC.

## Relation to Prior Work

SurpMark extends Gutman's (1989) likelihood-free hypothesis testing framework from a single-reference to a two-reference (human vs. machine) setting, building on the LFHT formalism of Gerber & Polyanskiy (2024). Conceptually, it sits between global-statistic detectors (likelihood, LogRank, entropy) and distributional-statistic detectors (DetectGPT, Fast-DetectGPT, DNA-GPT) that require costly per-instance regeneration: SurpMark scores in a single pass yet makes comparative decisions against fixed references, similar in spirit to R-Detect (Song et al., 2025) but replacing kernel optimization with a lightweight k-means discretization of surprisal dynamics. The key methodological departure is modeling token surprisal as a Markov chain over discretized states, capturing the 'recovery' phenomenon (LLMs return to predictable tokens after surprising ones) that marginal statistics miss, and providing a non-asymptotic discretization-estimation tradeoff (k* = Θ(N^{1/5})) that guides bin-count selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: SurpMark operates at a reference-per-test budget 5×–50× lower than distribution-based detectors while avoiding per-input regeneration, enabling real-time deployment at scale. The theoretical analysis (GJS as exact log-likelihood ratio, bin-count scaling law) provides principled design guidance rather than purely empirical tuning. Open questions include whether higher-order or richer state-space modeling helps with larger reference corpora, how pretraining contamination of human texts (making them 'LLM-like' in surprisal space) affects false-positive rates in practice, and whether the approach generalizes to multimodal or code-generation detection. The paper also honestly notes that detection outputs should not be sole basis for high-stakes decisions.
