## Problem

Standard time-series forecasting evaluations rely on aggregate metrics (MSE, MAE) that conflate model capability with the intrinsic predictability of each instance, making it impossible to tell whether a model is genuinely weak or simply evaluated on hard data. Existing predictability proxies (entropy rates, Lempel-Ziv complexity) are misaligned with multi-horizon squared-error objectives, computationally expensive, and yield only a single global score with no diagnostic granularity.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a paired history segment x ∈ R^{N_x} and future segment y ∈ R^{N_y} (optionally with a model prediction ŷ), processed via Welch's method with configurable window length, overlap, and tapering. It outputs three quantities per instance: a conservative linear MSE lower bound (MSE_lb) that accounts for boundary mean-shift, a normalized predictability score SCP ∈ [0,1], and a Linear Utilization Ratio (LUR) that decomposes model performance into per-frequency-band utilization of linearly predictable energy. All computations run in O(N log N) per sample, and the outputs support optional frequency-band partitioning for localized diagnosis.

## Method

Spectral Coherence Predictability (SCP) computes the squared coherence γ²_xy(f) between history and future via Welch-estimated power and cross-power spectral densities, then integrates the residual spectrum S_yy(f)(1−γ²_xy(f)) to obtain a linear MSE lower bound; normalizing this by the total future variance yields the predictability score. The key theoretical insight is that under joint Gaussianity and wide-sense stationarity, this linear reference is a consistent estimator of the Bayes-risk-based intrinsic predictability, while for general processes it provides a conservative surrogate that avoids overfitting to chaotic dynamics. The Linear Utilization Ratio (LUR) extends the diagnosis by comparing the prediction-target coherence γ²_{yŷ}(f) against the history-future coherence γ²_{yx}(f) at each frequency, classifying each band as under-utilized, saturated, or exhibiting non-linear advantage, and aggregating via power-weighted sums to produce a single utilization ratio. Together, these tools enable stratified evaluation by predictability bins, frequency-band-wise model comparison, and detection of predictability drift over time.

## Results

The proposed SCP/MSE_lb achieves Pearson correlations of R ≥ 0.80 with realized forecasting errors across all five SOTA models and all eight benchmark datasets, confirming its calibration as an instance-difficulty proxy. On ETTh1 (N=96), average MSE across models is 0.386 with R values ranging from 0.815 to 0.869; on ECL (N=96), average MSE is 0.177 with R values from 0.880 to 0.924. In direct comparison with entropy-based predictability metrics on ECL, MSE_lb achieves correlations of 0.864–0.909 versus PE (0.010–0.117), WPE (0.168–0.252), and SampEn (−0.008 to 0.010). For the pretrained TimerXL model, R remains high at 0.897–0.934 across prediction lengths 96–720, and SCP computation stays below 0.6 ms even at 2048-step history windows.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new forecaster but an evaluation framework, so the 'baselines' are the models being diagnosed and the alternative predictability metrics being compared. Against entropy-based predictability proxies (PE, WPE, SampEn) on ECL, the proposed MSE_lb dominates decisively: it achieves correlations of 0.864–0.909 with DLinear forecasting error, while PE reaches at most 0.117, WPE at most 0.252, and SampEn is essentially uncorrelated (|R| < 0.01). Among the five evaluated forecasters, no single model dominates across all settings: DLinear achieves the lowest MSE on ECL (0.169 at N=336) and highest R (0.909), while PatchTST leads on ETTh1 (0.436 at N=336) and iTransformer leads on ETTh2 and ETTm2. Stratified by SCP predictability, nonlinear models (TimesNet, iTransformer) outperform DLinear on low-P (hard) samples, whereas DLinear becomes competitive or superior on high-P (easy) samples, revealing complementary architectural strengths invisible in aggregate scores. The pretrained TimerXL model also shows strong alignment (R = 0.897–0.934), confirming the framework generalizes to zero-shot foundation models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes classical spectral coherence (a signal-processing concept from optics and communications) as a forecasting difficulty metric, deliberately departing from entropy-based predictability measures (Shannon, Lempel-Ziv, permutation entropy) that were designed for symbolic, single-step, or 0-1-loss settings. The theoretical grounding draws on Bayes risk and the law of total variance to motivate a linear MSE lower bound, while the practical construction via Welch's method connects to the spectral-analysis lineage of recent forecasting architectures (Autoformer, FEDformer, TimesNet) but inverts their use: here spectra serve evaluation rather than modeling. The paper carves new ground by framing predictability quantification as a diagnostic evaluation tool aligned with multi-horizon squared-error forecasting, a formulation the authors argue is absent from both the predictability literature and the forecasting evaluation literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a genuine evaluation ambiguity in time-series forecasting: aggregate MSE conflates model capability with instance difficulty, making leaderboard comparisons potentially misleading. The predictability drift finding—that task difficulty fluctuates substantially over time and across variables—challenges the implicit stationarity assumption in standard test-set evaluation and motivates difficulty-aware protocols. Open questions include whether the nonlinear and multivariate extensions (sketched in the appendix) scale to high-dimensional, strongly non-stationary regimes, and whether the framework remains informative for foundation models with cross-dataset transfer. The practical impact is broad: the O(N log N) cost makes SCP/LUR feasible as a standard reporting metric alongside MSE/MAE, and the stratified evaluation paradigm could reshape how the community benchmarks and interprets forecasting architectures.
