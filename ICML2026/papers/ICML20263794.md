## Problem

As LLMs are increasingly trained on corpora containing their own outputs, model collapse—the degradation from recursive self-training—has become a central concern, yet it has not been analyzed from a learning-theoretic perspective. This paper asks whether a replay adversary, which injects the generator's own past outputs into the example stream, fundamentally limits the ability to generate language, and characterizes precisely when and for which notions of generatability this limitation arises.

## Input / Output

The generator receives an adversarially ordered infinite stream where each example x_t must lie in supp(h*) or be a previous output o_s (s < t), meaning the adversary can replay even hallucinated elements. The generator must eventually produce outputs that are both valid (in supp(h*)) and fresh (not among x_1,...,x_t). In the improper setting the output is an element of the countable domain X; in the proper setting it is a hypothesis ĥ_t ∈ H whose support must be contained in supp(h*). The algorithm operates with only membership-query access to the hypothesis class, and the success time t* may depend on the class, the target hypothesis, and the specific example sequence depending on the generatability notion.

## Method

The paper introduces a replay variant of the language generation in the limit game and provides a fine-grained characterization across four generatability notions (uniform, non-uniform, in the limit, proper in the limit) and three class complexities (finite, countable, uncountable). The key positive result is the Witness Protection (WP) algorithm, which maintains a 'sure set' of examples that cannot be replays, protects distinguishing 'witness' elements from being output so they can later certify hypothesis elimination, and selects outputs from a critical hypothesis's support while excluding sure examples, prior outputs, and protected witnesses. For uniform generation, a simple burn-in reduction (outputting only x_1 until d* distinct examples are observed) preserves the standard sample complexity. The separations are witnessed by explicit constructions: a countable class where non-uniform generation fails under replay, an uncountable class where in-the-limit generation fails, and a four-hypothesis finite class where proper in-the-limit generation fails, showing that replay can fundamentally limit generation in a way that depends non-trivially on both the generatability notion and class complexity.

## Results

The paper's central result is a fine-grained characterization of when replay (the generator's own past outputs re-entering the training stream) fundamentally limits language generation. Replay is benign for uniform generation: the sample complexity remains unchanged for all hypothesis class complexities (Theorem 4.1). However, replay creates strict separations for weaker notions: a countable class exists that is non-uniformly generatable without replay but not with replay (Theorem 5.1), an uncountable class is generatable in the limit without replay but not with replay (Theorem 6.2), and even a finite class of four hypotheses fails proper generatability in the limit under replay (Theorem 7.2). For countable classes, generation in the limit remains possible under replay using only membership queries (Theorem 6.1), matching the standard-setting guarantee of Kleinberg & Mullainathan (2024).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines; the 'comparison' is between the replay setting and the standard (non-replay) language generation framework of Kleinberg & Mullainathan (2024) and Raman et al. (2025). The paper's positive results (Theorems 4.1, 6.1) show that the standard-setting guarantees are fully preserved for uniform generation (all class complexities) and for in-the-limit generation of countable classes, meaning the replay-robust algorithms (Algorithm 1, Algorithm 2/WP) match the sample complexity and computational requirements of the original generators. The negative results (Theorems 5.1, 6.2, 7.2) establish that the standard guarantees strictly break down: countability, which suffices for non-uniform generatability in the standard setting (Raman et al., 2025), is no longer sufficient under replay; and proper generatability in the limit, which holds for all countable classes with membership + subset queries (Kleinberg & Mullainathan, 2024), fails even for a 4-hypothesis finite class under replay. Additionally, Theorem 7.1 provides a computational lower bound showing that membership queries alone are insufficient for proper generation in the limit even without replay, strengthening the known requirement for subset queries.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the language generation in the limit framework of Kleinberg & Mullainathan (2024) by introducing a replay adversary that injects the generator's own past outputs into the example stream, providing a minimal learning-theoretic model of model collapse. The replay mechanism is directly inspired by the online learning replay adversary of Dmitriev et al. (2026), while the hard-class construction for the in-the-limit separation (Theorem 6.2) is adapted from the contamination results of Bai et al. (2026). The positive results for uniform generation build on the closure-dimension characterization of Raman et al. (2025), and the proper-generation lower bound (Theorem 7.1) extends the computational separation of Charikar & Pabbaraju (2025) from two hypotheses to countably infinite classes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first learning-theoretic characterization of when self-generated content fundamentally limits language generation, providing a fine-grained map across four notions of generatability and three class complexities. The positive results give a theoretical justification for widely used practical mitigations (data cleaning, watermarking, output filtering), while the separations identify worst-case regimes where these strategies provably fail. Key open questions include characterizing non-uniform generatability under replay, extending to randomized and stochastic replay models, and studying the interaction between replay and output breadth. The framework's abstraction from specific architectures is both a strength (architecture-agnostic impossibility results) and a limitation (no direct implementation-level guidance).
