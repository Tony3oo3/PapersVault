## Problem

Existing neural solvers for Multi-Objective Combinatorial Optimization (MOCO) rely exclusively on node-centric representations, neglecting the complementary structural information encoded in edge features. This omission prevents the model from capturing local topological structure and preference-specific distance characteristics, resulting in a persistent optimality gap that worsens at larger problem scales where attention weights diffuse over broader neighborhoods.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261900/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a problem instance (node coordinates for TSP/CVRP, item value-weight pairs for KP) and a preference vector λ as input. Edge features are constructed problem-specifically—pairwise Euclidean distances per objective for TSP, a shared distance matrix for CVRP, and normalized value-to-weight ratio sums for KP—then projected into unified edge embeddings via a two-layer MLP. The output is a combinatorial solution (Hamiltonian cycle, set of vehicle routes, or item subset) generated autoregressively; by solving 101 (bi-objective) or 105 (tri-objective) preference-conditioned sub-problems, the model approximates the full Pareto front.

## Method

PMSA builds on the POMO encoder-decoder backbone trained via REINFORCE with a shared baseline, but introduces two key mechanisms to integrate edge structure. First, a preference-modulated structural bias B_ij(λ) = h_ij^e ⊙ Linear(λ) is computed by element-wise multiplying edge embeddings with a projected preference vector, then added directly to scaled dot-product attention scores, enabling the model to amplify or suppress information flow along edges according to the current optimization direction. Second, a dynamic edge aggregation layer uses the normalized attention score matrix from each encoder layer as context to update edge representations via a residual FFN, creating a feedback loop where edges adaptively refine their structural encoding as node features evolve. The design deliberately avoids heavy GCN encoders to prevent over-smoothing and keep computational cost low, making it practical for decomposition-based frameworks that must process many sub-problems simultaneously.

## Results

On Bi-TSP100, PMSA achieves a Hypervolume (HV) of 0.7070 with a 0.18% gap to the reference Pareto front in 38 seconds, outperforming the strongest single-model baseline WE-CA (HV=0.7034, gap=0.69%) and all multi-model methods (e.g., EMNH: HV=0.6969). On Bi-CVRP100, PMSA reaches HV=0.4079 (gap=0.20%) versus WE-CA's 0.4068 (gap=0.46%). With instance augmentation, PMSA-AUG closes the gap to 0.00% on both Bi-TSP100 (HV=0.7083) and Bi-CVRP100 (HV=0.4087), matching or exceeding the exact WS-LKH solver while running ~100× faster (22 min vs. 6 hours). On Tri-TSP100, PMSA achieves HV=0.5044 (gap=0.47%), surpassing WE-CA (0.4985, gap=1.64%) by a wide margin.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PMSA consistently outperforms the closest single-model baseline WE-CA across all benchmarks: on Bi-TSP100 the HV gap narrows from 0.69% to 0.18%, and on Tri-TSP100 from 1.64% to 0.47%. Against multi-model methods (DRL-MOA, MDRL, EMNH), PMSA achieves higher HV with fewer parameters (1.60M vs. 133.37M) and comparable or faster inference. Compared to evolutionary baselines (MOEA/D, NSGA-II, MOGLS), PMSA is 1–3 orders of magnitude faster (seconds vs. hours) while achieving a smaller optimality gap at N≥50. The exact solver WS-LKH remains the upper bound on Bi-TSP (HV=0.7090 vs. PMSA's 0.7070), but PMSA-AUG (HV=0.7083) nearly matches it at ~100× lower cost. On Bi-CVRP100, PMSA surpasses all neural baselines including EMNH (0.4072) and WE-CA (0.4068), and with augmentation reaches the reference front (gap=0.00%). On the balanced preference subproblem λ=(0.5,0.5) of Bi-TSP100, PMSA-AUG achieves a 0.24% gap versus 0.77% for the single-objective specialist POMO-Aug, demonstrating superior trade-off handling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the POMO attention-based combinatorial optimization backbone (Kwon et al., 2020) and the WE-CA preference-conditioned node modulation strategy (Chen et al., 2025a), extending both by introducing edge features as a preference-modulated structural bias directly within the attention mechanism. It departs from the dominant node-centric paradigm in neural MOCO solvers and from heavier GCN-based node-edge architectures (e.g., UniteFormer) by proposing a lightweight alternative that injects edge embeddings as additive biases rather than through message-passing layers, thereby avoiding over-smoothing and the prohibitive computational cost of processing many decomposed sub-problems simultaneously. The dynamic edge aggregation mechanism, which uses normalized attention weights to iteratively refine edge representations, represents a novel feedback loop not present in prior single- or multi-objective neural solvers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a simple structural bias mechanism can close the optimality gap left by node-only MOCO solvers without incurring the overhead of graph convolutions, and that this advantage grows with problem scale—suggesting edge features act as anchors against attention dilution in large instances. However, the improvements over the strongest baseline (WE-CA) are modest (0.1–0.5% gap reduction), and the method still relies on predefined preference vectors, limiting its applicability to irregular or non-convex Pareto fronts. Open questions include whether adaptive preference mechanisms can be integrated, how the approach extends to MOCO problems with complex side constraints, and whether the edge-bias strategy generalizes beyond the three benchmark families tested.
