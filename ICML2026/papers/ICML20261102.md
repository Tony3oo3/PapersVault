## Problem

Large reasoning models generate long chain-of-thought traces, but conflicting evidence exists on whether longer CoTs and more review behaviors (e.g., via 'wait' tokens) actually improve accuracy. It remains unclear what structural properties of reasoning traces genuinely characterize effective reasoning versus mere verbosity, and whether different models exhibit similar patterns.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261102/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes raw CoT traces (text between think tags) generated by ten LRMs on HARP math and GPQA-Diamond science problems, with 16 generations per question for correlation analysis and 64 for test-time selection. CoTs are segmented into semantic chunks via keyword heuristics, labeled as progress or review by Llama 4 Maverick (90% human agreement), and converted to Graphviz DOT graphs by Claude 3.7 Sonnet. Outputs include character-level Length, Review Ratio, Failed-Step Fraction, a selected top-1 CoT under each metric, and edited CoTs with failed branches removed for continuation evaluation.

## Method

The paper introduces a reasoning-graph extraction method that prompts Claude 3.7 Sonnet to parse each CoT into a Graphviz diagram with nodes color-coded as successful (blue) or failed (pink) steps, then computes the Failed-Step Fraction (FSF) as the ratio of failed to total nodes. Using conditional correlation analysis with question fixed effects and Bayesian GLMMs across ten models on two datasets, the authors show that shorter CoTs and lower Review Ratio correlate with higher accuracy, while FSF is the strongest and most consistent predictor across all models and difficulty strata. Two causal interventions validate FSF: (1) test-time selection reranking 64 candidates by FSF yields up to 13% pass@1 gains over random on AIME-2025, outperforming Length- and Review-Ratio-based selection; and (2) surgically removing failed branches from incorrect CoTs and continuing generation improves accuracy by 8–14%, demonstrating that failed branches bias subsequent exploration and that models do not fully 'unsee' earlier mistakes.

## Results

In the test-time selection experiment on AIME 2025 (64 candidates per problem), FSF-based reranking yields pass@1 gains of roughly 5–13% over random selection, with up to 10% absolute improvement across models. In the controlled CoT-editing experiment (Table 1), removing the failed branch from incorrect traces increases continuation accuracy by 8–14 percentage points (e.g., Deepseek R1: 20.89% → 29.42% at the first failed branch; GPT oss 120B: 28.05% → 36.41%). Conditional correlation analysis across 10 models on HARP and GPQA-Diamond shows FSF is the only metric with significant negative correlations with accuracy for every model on both datasets, while Length and Review Ratio show significant effects for most but not all models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead contrasts three internal metrics (CoT Length, Review Ratio, FSF) as both predictors and test-time selectors. As a selector on AIME 2025 and GPQA-Diamond, FSF consistently outperforms Length- and Review Ratio-based selection, delivering the largest and most uniform pass@1 gains across all 10 models; Length selection fails to improve accuracy for GPT oss 120B, and Review Ratio shows the opposite (positive) correlation for Claude 3.7 on math. In the editing experiment, fully removing the failed branch ('Reduced') outperforms replacing it with a summary ('Reduced with Summary') by 1–4 percentage points in every setting, and both substantially beat the unedited 'Original' baseline (8–14 pp gain). Across difficulty strata, FSF correlations are strongest on harder items (HARP Levels 4–6, GPQA Hard Graduate), while Length and Review Ratio effects weaken or reverse on easier questions. The authors note that FSF is computed by Claude 3.7 (the weakest math model in their set) without access to ground-truth answers, yet still yields the best selection performance, including a 12% self-select gain when Claude both generates and scores its own CoTs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against the dominant 'longer-is-better' test-time scaling narrative established by S1 (Muennighoff et al., 2025) and the O1/DeepSeek R1 line of work, while building on the emerging reasoning-graph literature (Jiang et al., 2025; Minegishi et al., 2025) for structural analysis. It departs from prior graph-extraction methods by using a single Graphviz-prompting call rather than multi-round scaffolding or embedding clustering, and introduces the Failed-Step Fraction as a new structural metric. The causal intervention design (test-time reranking and controlled branch removal) extends beyond the correlation-only analyses in prior work on review behaviors (Gandhi et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024) and CoT length (Wu et al., 2025b; Hassid et al., 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that both longer CoTs and higher review ratios are negatively associated with accuracy—confirmed across 10 models and two datasets with causal evidence—challenges the default assumption that more test-time compute is monotonically beneficial, with direct implications for inference cost and latency. FSF provides a practical, computable selection criterion for test-time scaling that outperforms length- or review-based heuristics, suggesting structure-aware strategies (branch pruning, targeted selection) should complement or replace indiscriminate length scaling. Open questions include whether FSF-based selection generalizes to open-ended generation tasks, whether the graph extraction pipeline scales to very long traces (hundreds of thousands of tokens), and whether training-time interventions can reduce failed-branch prevalence rather than merely selecting around it at inference.
