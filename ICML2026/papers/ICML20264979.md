## Problem

Concept Activation Vectors (CAVs) in TCAV are computed by training a linear classifier on randomly sampled reference examples, so different random samples yield different CAVs and consequently different TCAV scores for the same model and concept. This run-to-run variability was acknowledged by the original authors but never theoretically characterized, leaving practitioners without principled guidance on how many random samples or independent runs are needed for stable explanations.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained differentiable model, a fixed set of n concept examples (e.g., 50 images showing 'stripes'), N randomly sampled reference examples from the training set, a target class k, and a layer index ℓ. It produces a CAV (a unit direction in the d-dimensional latent space at layer ℓ), per-input concept sensitivity scores S_{C,k,ℓ}(x) = ∇g_{ℓ,k}(h_ℓ(x))·v_C^ℓ, and the aggregate TCAV score — the fraction of class-k inputs with positive sensitivity, a value in [0,1] where 0.5 indicates no consistent directional influence. The analysis is conducted in the infinitely imbalanced regime where n is fixed (typically ~50) and N grows, reflecting the practical scarcity of annotated concept examples versus the abundance of unlabelled reference data.

## Method

The paper proves asymptotic normality of penalized logistic-regression CAVs in the infinitely imbalanced regime: under a 'Surrounded Mean' geometric condition on the reference distribution, √N(β_N − β_∞) converges in distribution to N(0, Σ), yielding O(1/N) variance decay for the CAV direction. Analogous results are established for hinge-loss SVMs, Difference-of-Means, and kernel-based Concept Activation Regions. A key and surprising finding is that TCAV score variance does not inherit the O(1/N) decay: 'borderline points' whose concept sensitivity lies near zero can flip sign under arbitrarily small CAV perturbations, causing the TCAV variance to plateau at Θ(1) even as the CAV itself stabilizes. Averaging over s independent runs recovers O(1/s) variance reduction for the TCAV score. The theory is validated empirically across four modalities (ImageNet/ResNet-50, UCI Adult, IMDB/BERT and SmolLM2, CLIP and LLaVA-1.5) and four estimators, leading to concrete recommendations: maximize N with Difference-of-Means for stable CAV directions, and use multiple modest-N runs for stable TCAV scores.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result is that CAV variance decays as O(1/N) in the infinitely imbalanced regime (Thm. 4.1), confirmed empirically across four modalities (images, tabular, text, multimodal) and four estimators. A surprising secondary finding is that TCAV score variance does not always decrease with N, plateauing at Θ(1) due to 'borderline points' near the decision boundary whose sign flips regardless of CAV precision. Multi-run averaging over s independent runs reduces TCAV score variance as O(1/s). The Difference-of-Means (DOM) estimator achieves the lowest CAV variability for any given compute budget and is ~46× faster than logistic regression.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four CAV estimation methods: logistic regression (binary cross-entropy), hinge-loss SVM (original TCAV), Concept Activation Regions (CAR), and Difference of Means (DOM). All four exhibit the same O(1/N) asymptotic variance decay, but differ substantially in constants and compute cost. DOM achieves the lowest CAV variability for any given compute budget (b = 0.0106 vs. 0.0999 for logistic regression on layer2) while being ~46× faster, making it the clear winner for CAV direction stability. Hinge loss has a much larger offset (b = 0.488), indicating slower practical convergence despite the same asymptotic rate. CAR achieves competitive variability (b = 0.0225) but at quadratic compute cost, making it the least efficient option. For TCAV score stability specifically, the paper recommends multi-run averaging over increasing N per run, since TCAV variance plateaus regardless of estimator choice due to borderline points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on stability analysis of XAI methods (Garreau & Mardaoui 2021 for LIME, Visani et al. 2022) to Concept Activation Vectors, providing the first theoretical characterization of CAV variability from random reference sampling. It builds directly on the TCAV framework of Kim et al. (2018) and the asymptotic theory of infinitely imbalanced logistic regression from Owen (2007) and Goldman & Zhang (2022), adapting these tools to the specific setting where concept examples are fixed and reference examples grow. Unlike prior CAV robustness work (Ramaswamy et al. 2023, Soni et al. 2020, Nicolson et al. 2025) that identified sensitivity to probe dataset choice, adversarial perturbations, or layer inconsistency, this paper isolates and quantifies the variance introduced purely by the random sampling step, and extends the analysis to hinge loss, Difference of Means, and Concept Activation Regions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper converts the ad-hoc question 'how many random samples do I need?' into concrete, budgetable rules grounded in asymptotic theory, validated across four modalities and multiple architectures. The key open question it raises is the irreducible variance floor in TCAV scores caused by borderline points near the decision boundary, which means no amount of additional reference samples fully stabilizes the final score—only multi-run averaging helps. The authors identify developing TCAV variants that incorporate stability directly into construction (rather than post-hoc averaging) as an important future direction, and note that implementation-specific constants (regularization, normalization, solver) require recalibration per architecture, limiting the universality of numerical recommendations.
