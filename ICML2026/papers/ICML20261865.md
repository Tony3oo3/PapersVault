## Problem

Classical index-based scheduling rules (e.g., Gittins, Whittle) are interpretable and transferable but require strong analytical assumptions, while RL-based schedulers learn flexible policies that are black-box, tied to fixed candidate set sizes, and fail to transfer when item populations change. The paper asks whether a global scheduling principle can be learned directly from experience data while preserving interpretability and zero-shot transferability across different system scales.

## Input / Output

At each decision step, the method receives a variable-sized set of candidate items, each described by a K-dimensional feature vector in [0,1]^K, and represents the system state as a condition distribution (sum of Dirac deltas or smooth kernels over feature space) rather than a concatenated vector, making it invariant to item ordering and cardinality. The output is a learned global scheduling principle S(x) decomposed into univariate marginal curves φ_k and pairwise interaction surfaces ψ_kl, from which each item receives a scalar priority score; the highest-scoring item is selected, and the additive components provide a human-readable explanation of why.

## Method

The FSP framework parameterizes the scheduling principle as a structured additive function S(x) = Σ_k φ_k(x_k) + Σ_{(k,l)∈P} ψ_kl(x_k, x_l) using spline or lattice basis functions, with mean, row, column, and global centering constraints that guarantee a unique (identifiable) decomposition. A softmax policy over item scores S(x_n)/τ induces the action distribution, and training minimizes a combined loss: a reward-weighted policy likelihood term that shapes local item rankings, a temporal-difference value regression term that enforces consistency of the state value V(s̄) = Σ_n S(x_n) with long-term returns, and L2/centering regularization. Because the principle is defined on item-level features rather than a joint state vector, the same learned S(x) can be evaluated on arbitrary candidate sets of any size without retraining. Theoretical results establish identifiability of the additive decomposition, O(R^{-2}) approximation error for spline/lattice bases, ranking consistency with 2ε regret bound, and principle invariance across systems sharing the same conditional supervision mechanism.

## Results

On the synthetic task, FSP recovers the ground-truth principle with an L² distance of 0.013 and achieves ~99.4% ranking consistency with reward gaps to the oracle on the order of 10⁻⁴ across N ∈ {4, 8, 16, 32}. On the three realistic tasks (Table 3), FSP is trained once at N=10 and transferred zero-shot. In the warehouse clearance task, FSP achieves the highest reward at every tested N (e.g., 0.649 at N=20 vs. −9.063 for DQN and −83.264 for PPO). In wireless scheduling, FSP overtakes the Whittle-index at N=15 (−0.285 vs. −0.337) and N=20 (−0.781 vs. −0.888), while in inventory replenishment FSP becomes the best method at N=20 (−0.726 vs. −0.921 for Whittle and −1.052 for PPO).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Whittle-index, FSP is slightly behind at small N in wireless (0.085 vs. 0.096 at N=5) but overtakes it at N=15 (−0.285 vs. −0.337) and N=20 (−0.781 vs. −0.888), and consistently outperforms it across all N in warehouse clearance (e.g., 0.649 vs. 0.605 at N=20). NAM is the strongest fixed-size baseline in wireless (0.131 at N=5, 0.109 at N=10) but fails catastrophically in warehouse clearance (−9.272 at N=5, −30.154 at N=20), where FSP remains positive. DQN, A2C, PPO, TRPO, and QR-DQN are all trained separately per N yet still deteriorate sharply in wireless at N≥15 (DQN drops to −2.609 at N=20) and in warehouse clearance (PPO reaches −83.264 at N=20), whereas FSP, trained only once at N=10, maintains stable zero-shot transfer. In inventory replenishment, several DRL baselines (A2C, PPO, TRPO, QR-DQN) outperform FSP at N=5 and N=15, but FSP becomes the best method at N=10 (−0.412) and N=20 (−0.726), demonstrating that its single-principle transfer advantage grows with system size.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of classical index-based scheduling (Gittins, Whittle indices) and learning-based scheduling (RL), arguing that both paradigms leave a gap: indices are interpretable and transferable but require restrictive analytical assumptions, while RL policies are flexible but entangled to fixed candidate sets and opaque. The structured additive representation draws on generalized additive models and neural additive models (Agarwal et al., 2021), but departs from them by explicitly learning a global item-level priority principle S(x) with identifiability constraints rather than a value or policy proxy. The learning procedure combines a policy-likelihood signal with a temporal-difference value regression on a condition-distribution state representation, a combination not previously applied to scheduling principle learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The FSP framework offers a practical path to scheduling policies that are simultaneously interpretable (decomposable into visualizable feature effects and interactions) and transferable (zero-shot across system sizes and populations), addressing a long-standing tension in the scheduling literature. Key limitations include the assumption that a global additive principle exists (or is a good surrogate), the quadratic growth of pairwise interaction terms in high-dimensional feature spaces, and evaluation restricted to 4D features and semi-synthetic environments. Open questions include whether sparse or low-rank interaction structures can extend FSP to realistic high-dimensional settings, how the framework handles multi-item selection and sub-decisions, and whether the learned principles remain robust under non-stationary or adversarial feature distributions.
