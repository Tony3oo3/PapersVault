## Problem

Existing distributional treatment effect (DTE) measures have structural limitations: MMD-based tests induce flat geometry on the space of probability measures and saturate when counterfactual distributions separate, while f-divergence-based DTEs require mutual absolute continuity. No prior work had established the semiparametric estimation theory—pathwise differentiability, efficient influence functions, and debiased estimators—needed for valid statistical inference on entropic optimal transport divergences in the causal setting.

## Input / Output

The method consumes observed data triples (X, A, Y) where X ∈ ℝ^p are covariates, A ∈ {0,1} is a binary treatment indicator, and Y ∈ ℝ^d is a (potentially high-dimensional) outcome, along with a user-specified entropic regularization parameter ε > 0. Nuisance components (propensity scores, outcome regression, entropic potentials) are estimated on one data split via XGBoost, conditional normalizing flows, and the Sinkhorn algorithm respectively, while all inference is performed on the complementary split. Outputs include a point estimate of the Sinkhorn treatment effect, a Wald-type confidence interval, a second-order U-statistic test statistic with a Gaussian-chaos null distribution, and a max-aggregated test (STEAgg) that combines evidence across a finite grid of ε values to reduce sensitivity to the regularization choice.

## Method

The Sinkhorn treatment effect is defined as S_ε(P₁, P₀) and decomposed as the composition S = S_ε ∘ J ∘ Ψ, where Ψ maps the data-generating distribution to a pair of counterfactual kernel mean embeddings in a Sobolev RKHS, J is the canonical isometric embedding into ℓ^∞(H₁), and S_ε is the centered entropic OT cost. The key theoretical result is that S is first-order pathwise differentiable at all P (yielding a standard EIF and an asymptotically normal one-step estimator) and, crucially, second-order pathwise differentiable under the null P₁ = P₀, because the first derivative of the Sinkhorn divergence vanishes there, eliminating the otherwise intractable second-order term from the KME map. This second-order EIF is expressed via the Hadamard operator K_μ = ε(I − T_μ²)⁻¹H_μ of the Sinkhorn divergence, where T_μ is the self-transport integral operator. The resulting second-order one-step estimator uses a U-statistic of the degenerate kernel ω_P^{⊗2} ⊙ k_{P₁} and converges at n-rate under the null to a weighted sum of centered chi-squared variables, enabling a test with provable type I error control. STEAgg further aggregates normalized test statistics across a grid of ε values via a max-combination, with asymptotic type I error guarantee established through joint limit distributions and Slutsky's theorem.

## Results

On the PatchCamelyon image dataset (n=5000, α=0.05), the STE test controls type I error at 0.04 while the MMD-based MTE test inflates it to approximately 0.11. At the strongest treatment effect (θ=20), the STE estimate reaches ≈2 while MTE is only ≈5×10⁻³, demonstrating that STE captures geometric discrepancies that MMD saturates on. In the covariance-shift simulation (Exp ii), STE values grow roughly 4× faster than MTE as θ increases (e.g., at θ=0.8: STE=68.43×10⁻³ vs MTE=4.60×10⁻³). The one-step debiased estimator substantially reduces plug-in MSE compared to the naive plug-in estimator, and Wald-type 95% confidence intervals achieve near-nominal coverage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the MMD-based distributional treatment effect (MTE) test of Martinez Taboada et al. (2023), constructed with an analogous second-order influence function debiasing strategy. STE decisively outperforms MTE on type I error control: at n=5000 on the PatchCamelyon dataset, STE maintains the nominal 5% level (empirical 0.04) while MTE inflates to roughly 11%, making MTE's slightly higher power in that regime unreliable. On geometric sensitivity, STE is dramatically more responsive to distributional shifts: at θ=20 on PCam, STE ≈ 2 versus MTE ≈ 5×10⁻³, and in the covariance-shift simulation STE grows ~4–15× faster than MTE as separation increases. This aligns with the theoretical motivation that MMD saturates under increasing distributional separation while Sinkhorn divergence continues to grow. Both methods achieve unit power at large sample sizes in the simulation, and both show similar confidence interval coverage, but the STE interval better reflects the underlying geometric structure of the treatment effect.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of distributional treatment effect (DTE) inference and entropic optimal transport. Prior DTE methods target quantiles, CDFs, MMD, or f-divergences between counterfactual distributions; the MMD-based kernel treatment effect (KTE) of Muandet et al. (2017, 2021) and its doubly robust debiased test (Martinez Taboada et al., 2023) are the closest prior work. The key departure is replacing MMD with the Sinkhorn divergence (Feydy et al., 2019), which respects the intrinsic geometry of the outcome space rather than imposing a flat RKHS metric. The theoretical machinery draws on Hadamard differentiability results for EOT (Goldfeld et al., 2024; Kokot & Luedtke, 2025), one-step estimation of Hilbert-valued parameters (Luedtke & Chung, 2024), and higher-order influence function theory (Robins et al., 2008; van der Vaart, 2014).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central theoretical finding—that the Sinkhorn treatment effect is second-order pathwise differentiable under the null of equal counterfactual distributions—is notable because most nonlinear functionals in semiparametric statistics fail to admit a second-order EIF, which blocks valid inference under boundary nulls. This enables a valid n-rate test with provable type I error control, something the first-order (degenerate) estimator cannot provide. Open questions include the numerical instability of the inverse operator (I − T_μ²)⁻¹ for small ε, the lack of a theoretical justification for replacing the Sinkhorn kernel k_μ with the self-transport kernel ξ_μ for faster computation, and scaling to larger datasets via batched Sinkhorn divergences. For practitioners in causal inference (economics, clinical trials), this offers a geometrically principled alternative to MMD-based DTE tests that does not saturate under large distributional separation.
