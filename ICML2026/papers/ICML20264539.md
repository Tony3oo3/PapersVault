## Problem

Trained self-attention models with similar predictive performance can exhibit widely different attention patterns, yet the role of architectural parameterization and relative optimization speeds in shaping these patterns remained unexplained. Prior theoretical analyses either assumed fixed relative learning behavior of attention components or relied on idealized parameter constructions that do not reflect standard gradient-based training. This paper asks how the parameterization of the query-key and output-value circuits, and their relative learning rates, jointly determine whether attention sharpens on task-relevant tokens during training.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a text sequence of T tokens (each a d-dimensional embedding) and produces next-token prediction logits over a vocabulary of size M, with the attention distribution over the T input tokens as a secondary output of interest. In the synthetic setting, sequences contain m class-specific distinct tokens, n common tokens, and a query token (vocabulary size 50, sequence length 64). Real-world experiments use a 6-layer GPT-style model on SQuAD (context length 256) and 1-layer attention-only models on HateXplain and Subject-Verb Agreement (context length 256), all trained with AdamW under supervised next-token or classification objectives.

## Method

The paper decomposes a single-layer self-attention model into two interacting linear circuits—the query-key circuit (W_K W_Q^T) governing attention allocation and the output-value circuit (W_O W_V) mapping attended representations to predictions—and shows that factorized parameterization of either circuit induces a state-dependent preconditioning operator Ω on the collapsed parameter space, making factorized gradient flow equivalent to preconditioned gradient flow in the collapsed space. Under orthogonal synthetic data assumptions, the authors derive closed-form population gradient-flow dynamics in which the output-value scalar μ_OV grows as Θ(log t) while the query-key scalar μ_QK grows as Θ((η_QK/η_OV) log² t), with the learning-rate ratio r = η_QK/η_OV controlling the relative speed. The mechanistic insight is that when the query-key circuit learns faster than the output-value circuit, the model compensates for slower output learning by increasing the magnitude of query-key parameters, which concentrates attention mass on relevant tokens through the saturating softmax nonlinearity. This is validated empirically by varying the QK learning-rate multiplier (up to 100×) on SQuAD, HateXplain, and SVA, showing large gains in attention-based interpretability metrics (AC, ACMC, MRTA, sufficiency, comprehensiveness) while predictive performance remains stable.

## Results

Across three real-world datasets, increasing the query-key learning rate (by 20×–100×) while keeping the output-value rate fixed leaves predictive performance essentially unchanged (SQuAD F1: 56.11→56.86; SVA accuracy: 92.43→93.41; HateXplain accuracy: 55.74→56.90) while dramatically sharpening attention. Attention Confidence (AC) increases by 3–4× (e.g., 12.65→42.95 on SQuAD, 0.00→43.5 on HateXplain), Mean Relevant Token Attention (MRTA) rises from 0.06 to 0.10 on SQuAD and from 0.027 to 0.31 on HateXplain, and sufficiency decreases while comprehensiveness increases in all settings. The theoretical prediction that μ_QK grows as Θ((η_QK/η_OV)·log²t) while μ_OV grows as Θ(log t) is confirmed by population gradient-flow simulations, showing that attention mass α(t) approaches 1 for large learning-rate ratios while remaining near-uniform for small ratios, even at matched loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external methods; instead, it contrasts a baseline model (uniform learning rate across all parameters) with a 'Faster QK' variant that multiplies only the query-key learning rate by a fixed factor (20×, 30×, or 100× depending on the dataset). Predictive performance is statistically indistinguishable across settings (differences < 1.2 points on all three tasks), confirming that the attention changes are not driven by improved prediction. On attention-based interpretability metrics, the Faster QK variant dominates the baseline across every dataset and every metric: AC and ACMC increase by 3–4×, MRTA roughly doubles or more, sufficiency drops, and comprehensiveness rises. The paper also compares four parameterizations (FAFO, CAFO, FACO, CACO) under identical learning rates, showing that collapsed output-value (CACO, FACO) naturally produces sharper attention than factorized output-value (FAFO), and that increasing the QK learning rate in FAFO or CACO recovers the sharper patterns—linking the parameterization effect to the same relative-speed mechanism identified in the theory.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the training-dynamics analysis of 1-layer transformers initiated by Tian et al. (2023), who assumed output-value parameters learn faster than query-key parameters, by instead treating the relative learning-rate ratio η_QK/η_OV as a free variable and showing it directly controls attention sharpness. The theoretical machinery draws on conservation laws for homogeneous linear models (Arora et al., 2018; Du et al., 2018) to show that factorized parameterization induces a state-dependent preconditioning operator on the collapsed-parameter gradient flow, a connection not previously exploited for attention interpretability. Unlike the transformer-circuits literature (Elhage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022), which characterizes attention structure post hoc, this work provides a forward-looking mechanistic account of how the two circuits co-evolve during gradient-based optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that increasing the query-key learning rate by a fixed multiplier (20×–100×) sharpens attention on task-relevant tokens without degrading predictive accuracy—offers a simple, architecture-free lever for improving attention interpretability proxies. However, the theoretical guarantees rely on strong orthogonality and symmetry assumptions that do not hold in standard transformer training, and the authors explicitly caution that sharper attention does not constitute a faithful explanation of model reasoning. Open questions include whether the log²(t) vs. log(t) growth separation persists with layer normalization, feedforward layers, and adaptive optimizers, and whether other circuit-specific interventions (initialization imbalance, weight decay) produce analogous effects.
