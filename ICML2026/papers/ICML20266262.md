## Problem

Existing Mixture-of-Experts architectures for non-stationary time series forecasting rely on fixed pools of homogeneous experts with memoryless routing, making them unable to adapt to abrupt regime shifts or decouple distinct drift components. This temporal rigidity and insufficient specialization force the model to redundantly relearn entire shifted distributions rather than efficiently adapting to specific evolving patterns.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266262/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a multivariate time series window of length L (96 for most datasets, 36 for ILI) across V variables, which is partitioned into overlapping patches of length P with stride S and projected to a latent dimension D. It produces a future sequence of length T (horizons of 96/192/336/720 for most datasets, 24/36/48/60 for ILI) by flattening the final MoE layer's patch-feature representations per variate and applying a single linear projection to the target horizon.

## Method

Dynamic TMoE implements a closed-loop Perception-Decision-Adaptation mechanism: an MMD-based drift detector with adaptive k-sigma thresholding monitors distributional divergence between reference and current windows to trigger expert pool evolution; a Temporal Memory Router uses a GRU to maintain sequential routing context, Top-k sparse dispatching for constant-cost expert activation, and an Anomaly State Repository with adaptive gated fusion to retrieve prior routing knowledge for recurring drifts; and a heterogeneous expert pool (Identity, Trend via average pooling, Seasonality via FFT with periodic activation, Fluctuation via gated causal convolutions) coupled with Cyclic Relation Modeling captures distinct temporal components. Upon drift detection, a Drift Pattern Profiler analyzes prediction residuals to instantiate the appropriate expert type, a Post-Addition Alignment phase fine-tunes only the new expert and router head on drift data, and an Expert Usage Tracker prunes underutilized experts to maintain efficiency. All structural evolution is confined to the training phase, ensuring stable inference without test-time updates.

## Results

Dynamic TMoE achieves state-of-the-art performance across nine multivariate forecasting benchmarks, ranking 1st in 11 out of 18 MSE/MAE metrics and 2nd in the remaining 5. Compared to the strongest MoE baseline TFPS, it reduces average MSE by 5.9% and MAE by 3.6%, winning on 8 of 9 datasets. Against all nine SOTA baselines combined, the average MSE reduction is 10.4% and MAE reduction is 7.8%. Gains are most pronounced on highly non-stationary datasets such as Electricity (MSE 0.170 vs. 0.183 for TimeMixer) and Traffic (MSE 0.479 vs. 0.457 for TFPS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the closest MoE competitor TFPS (Sun et al., 2025), Dynamic TMoE wins on 8 of 9 datasets, with the sole exception being Traffic where TFPS achieves a lower MSE (0.457 vs. 0.479) but Dynamic TMoE still wins on MAE (0.288 vs. 0.304). On ETTh2, ST-MTM (Seo & Lim, 2025) edges out Dynamic TMoE on MSE (0.359 vs. 0.368), and on ILI, PatchTST (Nie et al., 2023) achieves the best MSE (1.735 vs. 1.981), though Dynamic TMoE still leads on MAE for ILI. Against linear/MLP baselines (DLinear, FITS, RAFT, TimeMixer), Dynamic TMoE dominates on all datasets except Exchange where DLinear achieves a lower MSE (0.337 vs. 0.351). Transformer-based baselines (FEDformer, TimesNet, PatchTST) are consistently outperformed, with FEDformer showing the largest gap (e.g., 0.309 vs. 0.240 MSE on Weather). In the efficiency comparison (Table 5), Dynamic TMoE uses fewer parameters than TFPS on most datasets and achieves lower GPU memory than DUET, though it has higher inference latency than TFPS due to its sequential GRU-based router.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: non-stationary time series forecasting (RevIN, Non-stationary Transformers, TimeStacker) and Mixture-of-Experts architectures for time series (TFPS, Time-MoE, Moirai-MoE, DUET). It departs from both by rejecting the monolithic backbone assumption of the former and the static, homogeneous, memoryless expert pool of the latter. The key conceptual move is treating the expert pool as a training-time evolvable system—using MMD (borrowed from domain adaptation) for drift detection, a GRU for sequential routing (borrowed from RNN-based sequence modeling), and heterogeneous inductive biases (FFT, causal conv, pooling) for temporal decomposition—rather than a fixed architecture selected a priori.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a genuine gap: static MoE models cannot accommodate abrupt regime shifts because their expert pool and routing are frozen after initialization. The MMD-based generalization bound (Theorem C.5) provides theoretical justification for the drift-detection trigger, which is a nice addition beyond purely empirical validation. However, the dynamic evolution is confined to training, leaving test-time adaptation and online learning as open problems; the O(N²) MMD computation and sensitivity to a broad hyperparameter space limit practical deployment. The paper also acknowledges that the sequential GRU router introduces a latency trade-off versus parallelizable static routing, and that new experts suffer a cold-start period.
