## Problem

State-of-the-art text-to-image generators have shifted from U-Net DDPM backbones to Rectified Flow Transformers (FLUX, SD3), but concept-erasure research has not kept pace: existing adaptations either fail to reliably suppress harmful content or cause severe over-erasure on benign prompts. Additionally, the two dominant erasure paradigms—teacher-guided velocity matching and GFlowNet-based trajectory unlearning—have remained conceptually disconnected, with no principled bridge between them for flow-based models.

## Input / Output

GEM takes a pre-trained Rectified Flow T2I model (FLUX.1 [dev] or SD3.5 Medium), an unsafe target prompt (e.g., 'X nudity'), and a safe anchor prompt (e.g., 'fully dressed'), and produces a LoRA adapter (rank 16) applied to the query and key projections of the dual transformer blocks. During training, the method samples denoising trajectories from the model and evaluates velocity predictions at the first t_stop timesteps in parallel, so a single forward pass yields multiple consecutive training signals. The edited model is then used for inference with standard classifier-free guidance at the model's default number of denoising steps.

## Method

GEM first establishes a theoretical reduction showing that ERASEFLOW's GFlowNet trajectory-balance objective, under the deterministic sampling dynamics of Rectified Flow models, admits a faithful approximation as a teacher-guided velocity-matching loss (validated empirically via ablation). Building on this unified view, GEM introduces a geometric contrastive loss that simultaneously attracts the student's velocity prediction toward the frozen teacher's safe-anchor prediction (d_pos) and repels it from the teacher's target-concept prediction (d_neg), combined via a hinge: max(0, d_pos − η·d_neg). Rather than sampling a single timestep or an entire trajectory, GEM focuses supervision on a short early-trajectory window (t ∈ {0,…,t_stop}), exploiting the observation that early denoising steps are most influential for steering the final generation. This trajectory-level, multi-latent design enables 5× faster erasure compared to iterative per-timestep methods while achieving stronger suppression and better in-domain retention than prior state-of-the-art.

## Results

On FLUX nudity erasure, GEM achieves the lowest Unsafe Rate across all six benchmarks, reducing T2I-RP from 51.60% (original) to 19.63% and Basic from 77% to 10%, while completing in 3 min 27 s—roughly 5× faster than ERASEFLOW (15 min 58 s). For bloody gore, GEM drops the T2I-RP unsafe rate to 50.77% and eliminates all unsafe generations on Basic (0%), versus 65.47% and 20% for ERASEFLOW. In rights-protection settings, GEM achieves near-complete target removal (≤1/100 detections) while retaining in-domain concepts at 83.00% (Einstein) and 74.67% (Merkel), substantially outperforming ERASEFLOW's 58.00% and 16.67% retention respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GEM outperforms all five baselines on every nudity benchmark. Against the strongest prior method ERASEFLOW, GEM reduces the T2I-RP unsafe rate from 36.66% to 19.63% (−17.03 pts) and Basic from 42% to 10%, while matching its FID (8.20 vs. 8.32) and running ~5× faster. ESD and CA achieve moderate erasure but are 10× slower (32–35 min) and leave substantially more unsafe content (e.g., 46.89% and 32.84% on T2I-RP). UCE is the fastest (12 s) due to its closed-form update but provides the weakest erasure among iterative methods (49.29% T2I-RP) and, in the bloody gore setting, suppresses explicit content on only 50 of 100 basic generations versus 0 for GEM. EA (EraseAnything) sits between GEM and ERASEFLOW on most benchmarks but is only available as a single nudity checkpoint. In rights-protection, GEM wins on celebrity retention (83.00% Einstein, 74.67% Merkel vs. 58.00% and 16.67% for ERASEFLOW) but UCE edges out GEM on copyrighted-character retention (95.33% Son Goku vs. 77.00%), indicating UCE's attention-remapping is better suited to sharply defined fine-grained targets.

## Relation to Prior Work

GEM unifies two previously separate paradigms for concept erasure: teacher-guided velocity matching (ESD, UCE, CA) and GFlowNet-based trajectory redistribution (ERASEFLOW). The paper's core theoretical contribution is a step-by-step reduction showing that, for deterministic Rectified Flow samplers, ERASEFLOW's Trajectory Balance objective collapses to a teacher-guided velocity-matching loss, validated empirically via ablation. Building on this equivalence, GEM then fuses the trajectory-level reinforcement from the GFlowNet view with a contrastive geometric objective (simultaneous attraction to safe dynamics and repulsion from unsafe dynamics) that neither prior family employed in combination.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GEM provides a practical, 5× faster erasure method for the current generation of open T2I models (FLUX, SD3) that were underserved by prior erasure research, with stronger safety gains and better in-domain retention than the prior SOTA. The theoretical bridge clarifies that the GFlowNet and teacher-guided literatures were solving the same problem under different parameterizations, which should guide future work toward unified objectives. Open questions remain: the large-offset regime that makes ERASEFLOW work is not formally understood, over-erasure on edge-case prompts (e.g., a surfer at the beach after nudity erasure) is only coarsely measured, and the relative contributions of positive vs. negative guidance and of target vs. anchor trajectory choice are not yet disentangled.
