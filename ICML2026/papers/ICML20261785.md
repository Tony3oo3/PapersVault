## Problem

Current LLM agent benchmarks measure task completion but ignore whether the methods used are legally compliant, creating a blind spot where an agent that infringes copyright can score higher than a compliant one. No existing framework systematically evaluates whether autonomous agents respect intellectual property law when selecting and using third-party content in realistic commercial workflows.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261785/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives a natural-language task instruction (one of four prompt variants: Neutral, IP-Aware, Hurried, IP-Dismissive) and a pool of 200 high-resolution images (100 public-domain NPS photos, 100 copyrighted DepositPhotos images) paired for visual equivalence via CLIP similarity >0.85 and LAION-Aesthetics margin <0.5. Because the pairs are visually indistinguishable and lack watermarks, the agent must invoke a read_metadata tool to inspect a JSON sidecar containing a Copyright Notice field ('©2025 Deposit Photo' vs. 'U.S. government work product; public domain') to determine legality. The agent outputs a single selected filename plus a task-specific deliverable (homepage outline, product copy, or slide deck outline), and the evaluation harness computes a Violation Rate (fraction of tasks where at least one restricted asset was selected) and a Task Success Rate (valid action + format + semantic relevance).

## Method

Copyright-Bench is a controlled evaluation framework built around three commercial task families (web development, merchandise design, pitch deck generation) that differ in deployment context (digital display, physical reproduction, select audience). The key design mechanism is a Solvability Constraint: every task is solvable using only compliant assets, so any violation reflects agent preference or negligence rather than necessity. Assets are algorithmically paired using CLIP-ViT-L/14 embeddings and LAION-Aesthetics_V2 scoring, then human-validated for visual equivalence and absence of watermarks, forcing agents to rely on metadata inspection rather than visual cues. Four prompt variants systematically vary user intent (neutral, IP-aware, time-pressured, IP-dismissive) to stress-test compliance robustness, and four environment configurations (flat, hierarchical, metadata-removed, web-search-enabled) probe sensitivity to information architecture. All 11 evaluated LLMs (7 proprietary, 4 open-weights) are orchestrated through Microsoft AutoGen with six MCP-exposed tools, running 240 trials per configuration at temperature 0.1, and results are compared against a 176-participant human baseline collected via Prolific under a deception design to elicit natural decision-making.

## Results

Across 11 LLMs evaluated on Copyright-Bench (240 runs per configuration), the best proprietary model, Claude 4.5 Opus, achieves a mean Violation Rate (VR) of 27.6%, while the worst open-weights model, Llama-4-Scout, reaches 48.6%. The human baseline (N=176) records a mean VR of 30.5%, comparable to top proprietary models under neutral prompts but far lower under IP-Aware prompts (0.0–6.6% vs. ~10–18% for models). Under IP-Aware instructions, violation rates drop sharply across all families (e.g., Gemini 3 Pro: 39.6% → 10.0% in WebDev), whereas under IP-Dismissive prompts, open-weights models worsen (Llama-4-Scout peaks at 66.7% in PitchDeck) while proprietary models paradoxically improve (Claude 4.5 Opus: 38.3% → 19.6% in WebDev).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary models, Claude 4.5 Opus (27.6% mean VR) and Gemini 3 Pro (28.7%) lead, outperforming GPT-5.2 (30.6%), GPT-5.1 (32.3%), and GPT-4o (36.2%) by 3–9 percentage points, suggesting that 2025-era reasoning improvements translate into better legal compliance. All proprietary models exhibit a counterintuitive pattern under the IP-Dismissive prompt: violation rates *decrease* relative to Neutral (e.g., Opus drops from 38.3% to 19.6% in WebDev), likely because the mention of copyright terminology itself triggers compliance behavior. In stark contrast, open-weights models (Llama-4-Maverick at 41.0%, Llama-4-Scout at 48.6%, Qwen-3VL at 43.9%, DeepSeek-VL at 46.3%) trail the best proprietary model by 13–21 percentage points and show the opposite trend under Dismissive prompts, with Llama-4-Scout reaching 66.7% VR in PitchDeck. The human baseline (30.5% mean) sits between top proprietary and mid-tier proprietary models under Neutral prompts, but under IP-Aware prompts humans achieve near-zero violations (0.0–6.6%), outperforming every tested model. Under IP-Dismissive prompts, human violation rates (46.2–50.0%) align more closely with open-weights models than with proprietary ones, suggesting that proprietary safety training provides a compliance advantage that humans and open-weights models lack.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of agent evaluation and legal compliance. It departs from existing agent benchmarks (AgentBench, GAIA, WebArena, SWE-bench) that measure task completion regardless of the legal methods employed, and from the copyright-in-generative-AI literature (CopyBench, memorization studies) that asks whether models *generate* protected content rather than whether agents *select* it. By formalizing agentic copyright compliance as a constrained selection task with a solvability guarantee, the paper repurposes the standard tool-use agent evaluation paradigm (AutoGen, MCP tool interfaces) toward a normative/legal dimension that prior benchmarks leave entirely unmeasured.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a practical blind spot: current agent leaderboards reward task completion even when the agent's method is legally infringing, making high-scoring agents potentially undeployable in commercial settings. The finding that proprietary models paradoxically *improve* compliance when told to ignore copyright (while open-weights models worsen) has direct implications for deployment risk assessment and safety filtering. Key limitations include single-jurisdiction scope (US only), binary licensing (no attribution or share-alike conditions), image-only modality, and exclusion of fair-use scenarios; extending to multi-jurisdictional, multi-modal, and complex-license settings is the natural next step.
