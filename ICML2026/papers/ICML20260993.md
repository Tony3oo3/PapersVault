## Problem

LLM-as-a-judge evaluations are known to exhibit systematic biases (self-bias, length bias, position bias, grader severity), but existing detection methods require costly interventions, additional model calls, or ground-truth labels, and no unified framework exists to simultaneously identify multiple bias types, quantify them with uncertainty, and assess how removing specific biases would change evaluation outcomes without re-evaluation.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes already-collected evaluation data—absolute scores (1–10 ordinal) or binary pairwise preferences—along with metadata including grader identity/type, LLM identity, item identifiers, and token-length differences between compared responses. It produces full posterior distributions over each bias coefficient with 95% credible intervals, counterfactual inter-rater agreement metrics (e.g., Krippendorff's α recomputed after removing grader bias terms from the linear predictor), and pairwise preference probabilities that reveal intransitive (cyclical) patterns. No new model calls, ground-truth labels, or re-evaluation are required; the same pipeline handles both absolute-scoring and pairwise-preference formats by switching between ordered logistic and binomial likelihoods.

## Method

The core contribution is a Bayesian generalized linear model that jointly encodes multiple bias sources—grader severity (main effects), self-bias (grader×LLM interactions), length bias (continuous token-length-difference predictor with grader-specific slopes), item difficulty, and position bias—within a single linear predictor mapped to the observed outcome via an ordered logistic (absolute scores) or binomial-logit (pairwise) link. Hierarchical priors on grader effects (nested within grader type: human vs. autograder) enable partial pooling, yielding stable estimates even when individual graders have few observations. The key mechanism enabling counterfactual analysis is that, once the posterior is obtained, researchers can remove specific bias terms from the linear predictor, re-map to categorical scores, and recompute agreement metrics (Krippendorff's α, Cohen's κ, Kendall's τ) to isolate systematic disagreement from fundamental quality-judgment differences. Model selection among competing specifications (with/without interactions, hierarchical vs. flat, linear vs. quadratic length terms) is performed via LOO and WAIC, and all models are implemented in the open-source HiBayES package.

## Results

On the MT-Bench case study, the Bayesian GLM framework (Model M3, selected via LOO/WAIC) identifies GPT-4's length bias as substantially stronger than the human grader's (1.09 [0.68, 1.51] vs. 0.25 [-0.02, 0.53] on the log-odds scale). GPT-4 also exhibits a pronounced self-bias, assigning a +2.20 [0.29, 4.02] shift to its own outputs and -2.91 [-4.97, -0.86] to Llama outputs, while human graders show only moderate shifts (0.34, 0.41, -0.75). In the simulated counterfactual analysis, removing systematic grader biases raises Krippendorff's α from an observed -0.2 to approximately 0.7, demonstrating that most inter-rater disagreement stems from correctable scoring shifts rather than fundamental quality-judgment differences.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark its framework against competing bias-detection or debiasing methods in a head-to-head quantitative comparison; instead, it positions the Bayesian GLM approach as complementary to existing intervention-based and training-based methods. Within its own model hierarchy (M1–M7), the full model M3 (question + grader + position + length effects) achieves the best LOO/WAIC trade-off, outperforming the question-only model M1, the grader-only model M2, and the non-hierarchical M7, confirming that jointly modeling multiple bias sources improves predictive accuracy. The quadratic-length model M4 and the three-way interaction model M5 do not improve over M3, supporting parsimony. The framework is contrasted qualitatively with prediction-powered inference (Dorner et al., 2026), which requires ground-truth labels, and with prompt-engineering or ensemble debiasing approaches that incur additional model-call costs; the GLM framework requires no re-evaluation, no training, and no ground-truth labels, operating entirely on existing evaluation data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Bayesian generalized linear models and hierarchical modeling—well-established tools in psychometrics and statistics—for the LLM-as-a-judge evaluation setting, where prior work has relied on ad-hoc interventions (e.g., swapping response order, generating counterfactuals via additional LLM calls), descriptive statistics, or costly debiasing pipelines requiring training or ground-truth labels. It departs from these approaches by quantifying multiple bias types (self-bias, length bias, position bias, grader severity) jointly from a single existing dataset with full posterior uncertainty, and by enabling counterfactual simulations (e.g., recomputing Krippendorff's α after removing estimated biases) without any re-evaluation. The framework is positioned as complementary to intervention-based detection methods and as a lightweight alternative to prediction-powered inference, which requires ground-truth labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives LLM evaluation practitioners a principled, statistically rigorous way to decompose inter-rater disagreement into correctable systematic biases versus fundamental quality-judgment differences, with credible intervals rather than point estimates. Its practical value is amplified by the open-source HiBayES package and the demonstration on MT-Bench, making it immediately adoptable. Open questions include scalability to very large numbers of graders or items, applicability to more complex evaluation formats (agentic trajectories, multi-turn dialogues), and whether the ordered-logistic assumption holds when graders use non-standard or open-ended scoring rubrics. The paper does not address whether correcting identified biases actually improves downstream LLM selection decisions in practice.
