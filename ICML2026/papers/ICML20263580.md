## Problem

Multimodal LLM judges systematically fail to penalize responses whose visual claims contradict the input image, either because they misperceive the image themselves (insufficient perceptual capability) or because they anchor on linguistically plausible response text even when their own perception is correct (response anchoring). This Perceptual Judgment Bias causes judges to reward reasoning fluency over visual correctness, producing evaluations that diverge substantially from human judgment, with overall error rates exceeding 20% in baseline models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263580/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The judge receives an image, a text instruction, and a set of K candidate multimodal responses, and must produce one of three output forms: a scalar quality score in [1,5], a pairwise preference relation, or a full ordered ranking over all candidates. During training, the model is fed quadruplets consisting of the input, a verified correct response, a perception-perturbed response (visually wrong but reasoning-plausible), and a jointly perturbed response (both perceptual and reasoning errors). The output is structured as a reasoning-answer pair enclosed in think/answer tags, with the answer containing either a score, a preference, or a permutation over the candidate set.

## Method

The framework has two core components. First, the Perceptually Perturbed Judgment Dataset (PPJD) constructs minimally edited counterfactual responses by altering visually grounded attributes (object counts, colors, spatial relations) while preserving reasoning structure, creating verifiable quadruplets that explicitly isolate perceptual errors from reasoning errors. Second, a GRPO-based training objective uses a unified verifiable reward combining format validation with a batch-ranking term computed via normalized Levenshtein distance against the target ordering r_c ≻ r_{r_p} ≻ r_{r_{p+r}}. This batch-ranking reward enforces globally consistent ordering across all three responses simultaneously rather than relying on pairwise labels, allowing the model to learn transitive preference structure and internalize a graded perceptual hierarchy that distinguishes isolated perceptual errors from compound failures. The approach is notably data-efficient, requiring only 3K training samples to achieve performance competitive with proprietary evaluators like GPT-4o in score-based settings.

## Results

On the MLLM-as-a-Judge benchmark (14 vision-language tasks), Perception-Judge-Flex-7B achieves an average score correlation of 0.466 and batch-level distance of 0.505, while Perception-Judge-Qwen3-4B reaches 0.457 score and 0.444 batch distance. Compared to their respective backbones, the method yields a 15% gain in single-score prediction (Flex-Judge: 0.404→0.466) and an 11% improvement in batch-level ranking (Qwen3-VL: 0.498→0.444), with a 4% gain in pairwise accuracy. Notably, no explicit score labels are used during training; the scalar evaluation capability emerges purely from the batch-ranking reward.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Perception-Judge-Flex-7B outperforms its backbone Flex-Judge-VL-7B by 15% in score correlation (0.466 vs. 0.404) and 3.5% in pairwise-without-tie accuracy (0.645 vs. 0.623), while also improving batch distance from 0.517 to 0.505. Perception-Judge-Qwen3-4B surpasses Qwen3-VL-4B-Thinking by 9% in score (0.457 vs. 0.419), 4% in pairwise-with-tie (0.554 vs. 0.543), and 11% in batch distance (0.444 vs. 0.498). Against proprietary evaluators, Perception-Judge-Qwen3 matches or exceeds GPT-4o on score (0.457 vs. 0.439) and pairwise-with-tie (0.554 vs. 0.538), though it trails GPT-4o on pairwise-without-tie (0.691 vs. 0.736). In batch-level evaluation, both Perception-Judge variants outperform GPT-4V (0.505/0.444 vs. 0.361) and all open-source baselines, demonstrating stronger global ranking consistency. LLaVA-Critic (7B) remains competitive on pairwise-without-tie (0.689) but is substantially outperformed on score correlation (0.314 vs. 0.466), partly because it was trained on a 113k corpus that may overlap with evaluation benchmarks, whereas Perception-Judge uses only 3k disjoint samples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MLLM-as-a-Judge paradigm (Chen et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2025) from supervised fine-tuning to reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, building directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and the verifiable-reward trend in LLM-as-a-Judge (Wen et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025). Its key departure is the construction of PPJD, a dataset that injects controlled perceptual perturbations into correct responses to create quadruplets with a graded ordering (correct > perceptually-perturbed > jointly-perturbed), rather than relying on generic chosen/rejected pairs from MMPR. The batch-ranking reward based on normalized Levenshtein distance is a novel reward design that enforces global ordering consistency without explicit pairwise or scalar labels, distinguishing it from prior pairwise or score-matching reward schemes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As MLLMs are increasingly deployed in decision-critical applications, reliable automated evaluation is essential, and the identified Perceptual Judgment Bias (judges rewarding plausible narratives over visually correct answers) is a fundamental limitation affecting all current multimodal judges. The data efficiency (3K curated samples vs. 113K for LLaVA-Critic) and the finding that batch-ranking supervision alone induces accurate score prediction and pairwise preferences without explicit labels suggest a scalable path for training perception-grounded evaluators. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to video and multi-image settings, whether self-distillation can replace proprietary models for perturbation generation, and whether the residual gap to human annotators in ambiguous or subjective cases can be closed.
