## Problem

Prior work on budget-feasible procurement has focused almost exclusively on maximizing the buyer's value, assuming the budget is meant to be fully spent. This paper questions that assumption by solving the problem for buyer utility (value minus payments), social welfare (value minus production costs), and weighted generalizations of both. A key technical obstacle is that utility is provably inapproximable in the prior-free (worst-case) setting even for a single seller, necessitating a Bayesian framework with distributional knowledge over seller costs.

## Input / Output

The mechanism receives each seller's privately reported cost, the buyer's publicly known additive valuations for each service, and a budget B; in the Bayesian setting it additionally requires the cost distributions F_i. It outputs a (possibly fractional) allocation vector indicating which sellers are procured and a payment vector computed via Myerson's formula. In the prior-free setting the mechanism is deterministic and runs in O(n log n) time; in the Bayesian setting it reduces to independent posted prices (or, under irregularity, randomization between two prices per agent), with the budget constraint enforced either ex-ante (in expectation) or ex-post (for every cost realization).

## Method

For prior-free welfare, the mechanism constructs a candidate set W by greedily adding sellers in decreasing value-per-cost order while each seller's cost-to-value ratio does not exceed the average budget-per-value of the set so far, then compares W against the single highest-welfare agent, achieving a 1/(2+√2) approximation. For Bayesian utility under a soft budget, the paper reformulates the problem using virtual cost functions and a Lagrangian relaxation, showing the optimal mechanism decomposes into independent posted prices computable by binary search on the Lagrange multiplier. For the harder ex-post budget constraint, the mechanism partitions sellers into high-price (p_i ≥ B/α) and low-price (p_i < B/α) groups: if high-price sellers dominate the ex-ante utility, it runs a sequential posted-price procedure ordered by absolute utility (v_i − p_i); otherwise it orders low-price sellers by utility-per-payment (v_i − p_i)/p_i, yielding a 1/5 approximation. The framework extends to weighted welfare (w_i = v_i − αc_i with concave G) and weighted utility (a_i v_i − b_i p_i), and handles irregular distributions via standard ironing.

## Results

In the prior-free setting, Mechanism 1 achieves a 1/(2+√2) ≈ 0.293 approximation to optimal welfare, with a matching lower bound of 1/(1+√2) ≈ 0.414 showing no deterministic mechanism can do better. For buyer utility, the paper proves inapproximability in the prior-free setting (Proposition 6): no truthful budget-feasible mechanism achieves any positive approximation ratio, even for a single seller. In the Bayesian setting with a hard (ex-post) budget constraint, Mechanism 2 (M_const) achieves a constant approximation ratio of at least 0.2015 to the utility-optimal ex-post budget-feasible mechanism, with optimal parameters α₀ ≈ 2.39 and β₀ ≈ 2.13. The soft-budget (ex-ante) utility problem is solved optimally via independent posted prices under regularity (Theorem 7).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical mechanism-design paper with no empirical baselines; comparisons are against prior theoretical results. The prior-free welfare mechanism extends the value-maximization approach of Papadimitriou & Singer (2010) and Chen et al. (2011), which achieved the same 1/(2+√2) ratio for value, to the welfare objective — a non-trivial extension since the greedy set W is constructed using value-per-cost ratios yet must guarantee welfare. The Bayesian hard-budget utility mechanism generalizes Balkanski & Hartline (2016), who provided a (1−O(1/√k))-approximation only under a 'k-large market' assumption (all prices ≤ B/k); this paper removes that restriction by combining their k-large market analysis for low-price agents with a new sequential posted-price argument for high-price agents, yielding a universal constant factor. The authors also identify and fix a minor gap in the proof of one of Balkanski & Hartline's lemmas (Remark 1). Compared to Jarman & Meisner (2017), who study ex-post budget-feasible utility maximization but restrict to deterministic mechanisms, this paper approximates the full randomized (unrestricted) utility-optimal benchmark, which is a strictly harder target. The generalized objectives (α-weighted welfare, a_i/b_i-weighted utility) extend all guarantees without loss of approximation ratio.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the budget-feasible procurement literature, which has been dominated by value-maximization objectives since Singer (2010), to welfare, utility, and generalized objectives. The prior-free welfare mechanism directly adapts the value-per-cost greedy construction of Papadimitriou & Singer (2010) and Chen et al. (2011), while the Bayesian utility mechanism builds on the k-large-market posted-pricing framework of Balkanski & Hartline (2016), extending it from value to utility and removing their large-market restriction. A key conceptual departure is that, unlike value maximization where the budget always binds, utility maximization may leave budget unused, requiring a different treatment of the Lagrangian relaxation. The paper also identifies and patches a subtle gap in Balkanski & Hartline's analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the near-universal assumption in budget-feasible procurement that the buyer should maximize value and spend the full budget, showing that utility and welfare are well-motivated alternatives with fundamentally different mechanism-design challenges. The impossibility result for prior-free utility (even with a single seller) is a clean and important negative result that justifies the Bayesian setting. Open questions include prior-independent or sample-based mechanisms, non-additive valuations, and multi-buyer (two-sided market) extensions, all of which the authors identify as natural next steps. The 1/5 ex-post approximation for utility is the first general constant-factor result for this problem without large-market assumptions.
