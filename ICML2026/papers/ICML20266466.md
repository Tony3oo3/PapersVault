## Problem

Despite extensive development of post-hoc XAI methods (feature attributions, sparse autoencoders, counterfactuals, mechanistic interpretability), explanations rarely influence real-world workflows and are often generated and discarded without guiding action. The field has inverted the natural research order by proliferating ad-hoc methods before establishing clear definitions, falsifiable claims, principled evaluation criteria, and pipelines for explanation-driven feedback, making cumulative progress difficult.

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes two data sources: 617 XAI papers from NeurIPS/ICML/ICLR 2023–2024 (plus 187 from CVPR 2025 and ACL 2025 as robustness checks) processed through Gemini 2.5 Flash answering 20 fixed binary questions about each paper's claims, and survey responses from 34 XAI practitioners (43 total, including a 9-person interpretable-ML-only subgroup excluded from main analysis). The output is a structured diagnosis of four interdependent foundational challenges and a 5-point paper checklist, rather than a new explanation method or model.

## Method

The paper combines two empirical analyses to diagnose why prior calls for better XAI have not translated into progress. First, an LLM-driven literature survey uses Gemini 2.5 Flash to answer 20 binary questions about claims in each of 617 papers (validated at 88% agreement on 25 manually-annotated samples), revealing that 77% propose novel methods while only 11% provide formal definitions and 11% include user studies. Second, an IRB-approved survey of 34 practitioners documents that method choice is driven by ease-of-implementation (52.9%) over rigor (29.4%), and 44.1% report not knowing how to check explanation quality. These findings are synthesized into four interdependent challenges—lack of definitions, unspecified explanation properties, incoherent evaluation, and lack of actionability—each illustrated with concrete literature examples (e.g., LIME's free parameters, SAE sparsity ratios, conflicting faithfulness tests). The paper concludes with a 5-point checklist (precise definitions, falsifiable claims, faithfulness evaluation, practical applicability, task-specific human-in-the-loop evaluation) and explicitly positions its critique as targeting stakeholder-facing XAI methods rather than mechanistic interpretability research aimed at scientific understanding.

## Results

The LLM-driven survey of 617 XAI papers (NeurIPS/ICML/ICLR 2023–2024) reveals that 77% propose novel methods, yet only 11% provide formal definitions of explainability objectives and only 11% include user studies. Faithfulness evaluation is the most common assessment (56%), while concept-based explanations (31%) and mechanistic interpretability (19%) dominate the topic landscape. The practitioner survey (N=34) confirms these gaps: 44.1% of respondents reported not knowing how to check explanation quality, method selection was driven by ease of implementation (52.9%) and popularity (47.1%) rather than rigorous backing (29.4%), and 23.5% admitted not evaluating faithfulness at all.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines. Instead, its central empirical contrast is between what the XAI literature claims to do and what practitioners actually experience. The literature survey shows 77% of papers propose novel methods while only 11% provide formal definitions or user studies; the practitioner survey independently confirms this disconnect, with 44.1% of practitioners unable to assess explanation quality and 23.5% not evaluating faithfulness at all. A secondary comparison is across venues: the authors extend the LLM survey to CVPR 2025 and ACL 2025, finding that the same foundational gaps persist (0% formal definitions at CVPR, 3.6% at ACL) while domain-specific differences emerge (CVPR is 100% vision applications; ACL has 26.8% mechanistic interpretability vs. 7.7% at CVPR). The paper also contrasts the XAI community's approach with deep learning's historical trajectory, arguing that unlike deep learning where methods had clear empirical signal (improving benchmarks), XAI lacks such a signal, making ad-hoc method development less likely to converge.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper extends the line of earlier calls for rigor in XAI (Doshi-Velez & Kim, 2017; Saphra & Wiegreffe, 2024; Sharkey et al., 2025) by grounding its argument in two empirical analyses: an LLM-driven survey of 617 NeurIPS/ICML/ICLR papers (2023–2024) and a structured survey of 34 practitioners. Unlike prior position papers that primarily articulate desiderata, this work diagnoses *why* those calls have not translated into cumulative progress, arguing the field has inverted the natural research order by developing methods before establishing definitions, evaluation criteria, and deployment pipelines. It also draws explicitly on the human-centered XAI literature (Vaughan & Wallach, 2021; Liao et al., 2020) to argue that stakeholder needs must be central rather than auxiliary to explainability research.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's core empirical finding—that 77% of XAI papers propose novel methods while only 11% provide formal definitions and 11% include user studies—quantifies a long-suspected structural imbalance and provides a concrete diagnostic for the field. The five-point paper checklist (definitions, falsifiable claims, faithfulness evaluation, practical applicability, task-specific human-in-the-loop evaluation) offers a practical tool for authors and reviewers, though its adoption depends on community and venue-level changes. Key open questions remain: how to formally define explainability in a stakeholder- and task-dependent way, how to build task-grounded evaluation pipelines that connect automated metrics to downstream utility, and how to design actionability mechanisms that translate explanations into authorized model updates. Limitations include the small practitioner sample (N=34), the 88% LLM annotation agreement rate, and the fact that the paper is a position argument rather than a methodological contribution, so its impact hinges on whether the community adopts the proposed re-prioritization.
