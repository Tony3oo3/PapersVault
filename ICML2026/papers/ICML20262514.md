## Problem

Quantization-aware training (QAT) of language models suffers from slow convergence and early performance plateau, especially below 4-bit precision, yet the precise cause remained unclear. This paper identifies the root cause through Hessian spectrum analysis: model weights converge to flat regions around saddle points where a large fraction of eigenvalues concentrate near zero, and this effect worsens at lower bit-widths.

## Input / Output

The method takes pretrained language model weights (LLaMA-3-1B/3B, Qwen-3-0.6B/1.7B), a text training corpus (FineWebEdu), and target quantization bit-widths (1–4 bits for weights, 4–16 bits for activations) as input. It produces trained latent weights W and their quantized counterparts Q(W) for inference. WINQ is designed as a drop-in wrapper on top of existing QAT frameworks (ParetoQ, QuEST), adding only periodic element-wise interpolation and per-step Gaussian noise sampling, with less than 1% additional wall-clock cost and no extra GPU memory.

## Method

WINQ combines two lightweight techniques to escape saddle-point stagnation in QAT. First, every K steps the latent weights are reset to a linear interpolation (1−α)W + αQ(W), which provably reduces the distance to the quantization grid and is equivalent to a proximal gradient update on an ℓ₂-regularized objective, thereby increasing Hessian eigenvalue magnitudes (up to 84% at α=0.4). Second, at each iteration a Gaussian noise vector U ~ N(0, σ²I) is added to the weights before computing gradients on Q(W+U), regularizing the Hessian and yielding larger gradient norms near saddle points. The approach is generic across quantization methods (Elastic Binarization, Stretched Elastic Quant, LSQ) and extends to Hadamard-transform-based quantization via an orthogonal conjugation of the interpolation step. Hessian spectra are estimated using Stochastic Lanczos Quadrature with Hessian-vector products to diagnose and validate the saddle-point behavior across bit-widths.

## Results

WINQ achieves up to 4× speedup over ParetoQ in reaching the same training loss, with the largest acceleration (4×) in the 1-bit weight / 8-bit activation setting. At the same 20B-token training budget, WINQ reduces WikiText-2 perplexity by up to 8.8% relative to ParetoQ (e.g., 15.3 vs. 16.9 PPL for LLaMA-1B W1A16) and improves average zero-shot QA accuracy by up to 1.6%. Gains are most pronounced below 3-bit precision; at 4-bit, WINQ matches or slightly exceeds ParetoQ (10.2 vs. 10.3 PPL). Extended to Qwen models, WINQ delivers up to 3.2% relative PPL reduction and 1.3% accuracy gain over ParetoQ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary QAT baseline ParetoQ, WINQ consistently wins on PPL across all sub-4-bit settings: 15.3 vs. 16.9 (W1A16), 12.9 vs. 14.0 (W1.58A16), 11.9 vs. 12.5 (W2A16), and 21.9 vs. 23.3 (W1A8), with the largest relative gain (~9.5%) at 1-bit. At 3-bit and 4-bit, the gap narrows to near-parity (10.9 vs. 10.9 and 10.2 vs. 10.3 PPL), consistent with the paper's observation that higher-precision models are already close to full-precision performance. All PTQ baselines (GPTQ, AWQ, SpinQuant, RTN) are dramatically outperformed by WINQ in sub-4-bit settings—e.g., GPTQ achieves 3.3e8 PPL at W1A16 versus WINQ's 15.3—confirming that QAT is essential below 4-bit. Against QuEST (which uses Hadamard transforms), WINQ w/ HT improves PPL from 42.9 to 42.3 (W1A4) and from 17.4 to 16.9 (W2A4), a 2.8% relative gain. In the multi-bit-width training experiment, WINQ's noise-injection approach outperforms Matryoshka Quantization by 8.3 PPL and 2.4% accuracy at the same 240K-step budget, while approaching single-precision ParetoQ performance with 75% less training cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the QAT literature (ParetoQ, QuEST, STE-based methods) and the second-order quantization design line (HAWQ, GPTQ, QuIP), but departs from both in a key way: prior work used Hessian information to *design* quantization schemes (sensitivity measures, mixed-precision allocation), whereas here the full Hessian spectrum is used to *diagnose* why QAT converges slowly. The proposed fix draws on two distinct threads — proximal gradient regularization for quantization (ProxQuant, CAGE) and noise-based saddle-point escape from non-convex optimization (Jin et al., 2017) — combining them into a single lightweight procedure that has not been applied to QAT before.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QAT is the primary bottleneck for deploying sub-4-bit LLMs, and a 1.5–4× speedup with <1% overhead that plugs into existing methods (ParetoQ, QuEST, Hadamard-based schemes) is immediately practical. The Hessian-spectrum diagnosis reframes slow QAT convergence as a saddle-point/flat-curvature problem rather than a quantization-error problem, opening a new axis for future optimization-aware quantization research. Open questions include whether the multi-bit joint training extension (preliminary, Table 11) can match single-precision QAT at scale, and whether the saddle-point phenomenon persists in much larger models or in activation-dominant regimes.
