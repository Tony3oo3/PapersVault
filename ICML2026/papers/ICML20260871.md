## Problem

Evaluating AI reviewers is unreliable because existing benchmarks treat human reviews as gold references, yet human reviews are both incomplete (a single reviewer covers only ~5 of 23 subcategories on average) and occasionally incorrect (22% of papers contain inter-reviewer conflicts, 76% contain reviewer-author conflicts). This means overlap-based metrics penalize valid AI comments that address points humans missed, and correctness-based metrics inherit human errors, making it impossible to fairly assess whether an AI reviewer is actually better or worse than a human.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes AI-generated review text (up to 32k tokens) alongside the corresponding paper PDF (text-only, no images) and produces structured annotations: atomic opinion segments, 23-subcategory labels, opinion clusters, and correctness flags. Evaluation outputs a 5-dimensional score (Correctness, Thoroughness, Grounding, Verifiability, Clarity, each 1–5) computed per category via LLM-as-a-judge, plus a completeness score (0–100) measuring the ratio of AI-covered categories to all-reviewer-covered categories. The final dataset contains 3,900 papers with 14.1k review comments, 134.8k opinions, 115.9k opinion clusters, and 108.6k verified-correct reference opinions.

## Method

CoCoReviewBench addresses incompleteness by constructing per-category sub-benchmarks that include only papers with human reviews in that category, so AI comments in uncovered categories are simply skipped rather than penalized. It addresses incorrectness by treating reviewer-author-meta-review discussions as expert annotations: when a conflict is detected among opinions on the same topic, the meta-review serves as a high-level adjudication signal to designate the correct opinion, and incorrect ones are excluded from references. The construction pipeline is an agent framework with eight LLM calls (segmentation, classification, secondary segmentation, clustering, inter-reviewer conflict identification/resolution, reviewer-author conflict identification/resolution), verified via leave-one-out cross-validation across six strong LLMs. To evaluate AI reviews at category level without expensive per-review LLM calls, the authors distill the pipeline into two Qwen3-8B models: ReviewSplit (trained with GRPO using the Omega Index as reward for opinion segmentation) and ReviewClassify (trained via instruction tuning for subcategory assignment), achieving 87% fully-correct classification on human annotation.

## Results

On CoCoReviewBench (3,900 papers from ICLR and NeurIPS), GPT-5.2 is the strongest AI reviewer, exceeding the human baseline by +0.59 on average categorical score and +0.90 on paper-level score, with the largest gains in Grounding (+0.92) and Verifiability (+0.78). GPT-5-Mini achieves the highest completeness (89.97 vs. 55.66 for a single human reviewer), while all other models score below 100. In contrast, the weakest model (SEA-E-7B) falls 1.33 points below humans on paper-level score and 1.62 below on Grounding. Old overlap metrics (BLEU, ROUGE-L, BERTScore) are shown to be misleading: non-reasoning and specialized models score higher on these than GPT-5/Gemini-3, yet perform worse on correctness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among closed-source models, GPT-5.2 and GPT-5-Mini are the only systems that surpass humans on the majority of categorical dimensions (Correctness, Thoroughness, Grounding, Verifiability), while Gemini-3-Pro/Flash remain close to human level on Grounding but lag on Correctness and Thoroughness. Among open-source reasoning models, QwQ-32B and DeepReviewer-14B achieve above-human average scores (+0.19 and +0.20) with strong Grounding (+0.58, +0.41) and Verifiability (+0.02, +0.41), whereas Qwen3-8B falls below humans on all five dimensions (avg. −0.29). Non-reasoning open-source models (Llama-3.3-70B, Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen-2.5-7B) consistently underperform humans, with the largest deficits in Grounding (−0.63 to −0.30) and Verifiability (−0.78 to −0.59). Specialized non-reasoning reviewer models (CycleReviewer-70B/8B, OpenReviewer-8B, SEA-E-7B) score below humans on average and exhibit the lowest completeness (47.92–51.54 vs. 55.66 for a single human reviewer), with SEA-E-7B being the worst overall (−1.33 paper-level, −1.62 Grounding). Notably, all models outperform a single human reviewer on completeness (range 47.92–89.97 vs. 55.66), but none reach the 100% coverage of the full reviewer panel, and old overlap metrics (BLEU/ROUGE-L/BERTScore) rank non-reasoning and specialized models above GPT-5/Gemini-3, contradicting their actual correctness performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two established lines of work in AI reviewer evaluation. First, it challenges the dominant practice of using human reviews as gold references with overlap metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore) or LLM-as-a-judge, showing that human reviews are both incomplete (covering only ~40% of subcategories even after multi-reviewer aggregation) and frequently incorrect (75% of papers contain reviewer-author conflicts). Second, it moves beyond prior peer-review datasets (PeerRead, MReD, NLPeer, SEA, Re²) that provide only partial supervision signals, introducing the first structured benchmark with atomic opinion segmentation, fine-grained category labels, conflict detection, and incorrect-opinion annotations. The evaluation protocol builds on the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm but adds a category-level decomposition that skips evaluation where human references are absent, and the benchmark construction leverages an agent framework inspired by multi-agent review systems but repurposed for annotation rather than generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that traditional overlap metrics systematically reward the wrong models (non-reasoning and specialized models outperform GPT-5/Gemini-3) challenges a core assumption in the field and provides strong motivation for adopting the proposed category-level LLM-as-a-judge protocol. The high rate of reviewer errors (22% inter-reviewer, 75% reviewer-author conflicts) has implications beyond evaluation: it suggests that instruction-tuning AI reviewers on raw human reviews may propagate errors, motivating more careful data curation. Open questions include whether conflict-based filtering can be extended to catch implicit errors, whether the meta-review adjudication signal can be made more fine-grained, and whether AI reviewers can learn to identify the most critical issues rather than merely covering more categories. The hallucination finding (models generating figure-related critiques without visual input) also raises safety concerns for deployment.
