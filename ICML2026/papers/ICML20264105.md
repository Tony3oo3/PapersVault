## Problem

LLMs that reliably refuse harmful requests in English often fail to do so in low-resource languages, yet prior work has not identified the root cause of this multilingual safety gap. This paper shows that the failure stems not from missing refusal capabilities but from insufficient representation-space separability: harmful prompts in low-resource languages remain close to the harmless prompt manifold, and this geometric deficiency strongly correlates with attack success rates.

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired bilingual preference triplets (harmful instruction in a dominant language and its translation in a target language, each paired with a safe refusal and an unsafe response) along with a fixed harmless prompt set used to compute per-language representation centroids. It produces a safety-aligned multilingual LLM whose internal representations exhibit enlarged harmful–harmless distances in target languages. A notable design choice is that the spatial margin gap is detached from gradient flow and used purely as a scalar modulation coefficient, so the geometric signal guides optimization strength without itself being optimized.

## Method

SMO (Multilingual Spatial Margin Gap-based Optimization) first computes a per-language harmless centroid by mean-pooling final-layer hidden states over a harmless prompt set, then defines each harmful prompt's spatial margin as its L2 distance to that centroid. The core loss is the cross-lingual margin gap max(0, SM^dominant − SM^target), which penalizes only those examples where a harmful prompt is well separated in the dominant language but not in the target language. This margin gap is also detached and used as an example-wise multiplier on the SimPO preference loss, so poorly separated target-language examples receive stronger updates while already-well-separated ones receive weaker ones. The final objective is SMG · L_SimPO + λ · L_margin, integrating the geometric signal into standard reference-free preference optimization without requiring a separate reward model or new refusal behaviors.

## Results

On LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, SMO achieves the lowest average Attack Success Rate (ASR) across all baselines: 1.53% on MultiJail and 0.29% on AdvBench-X, compared to 5.87% and 2.37% for SimPO (its base method) and 39.26% and 28.24% for MPO (the closest multilingual safety baseline). On Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct, SMO similarly achieves the best average ASR (21.22% MultiJail, 9.97% AdvBench-X), though Swahili numbers remain elevated due to a high rate of invalid responses inherent to the base model's limited Swahili capability. General capabilities (M-MMLU, MGSM) are preserved within ~1% of the base model across all languages.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SMO substantially outperforms all six baselines on both benchmarks for LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, with the most dramatic gains on low-resource languages (Bengali, Swahili) where baselines like MPO (39.26% MultiJail AVG) and SFT (36.93%) barely improve over the base model (38.04%). Against SimPO, its direct base method, SMO reduces MultiJail AVG ASR from 5.87% to 1.53% and AdvBench-X from 2.37% to 0.29%, confirming that the spatial margin gap signal adds meaningful safety signal beyond standard preference optimization. MPO, the only other multilingual safety alignment method, performs nearly identically to the untrained base model (39.26% vs. 38.04% MultiJail AVG on LLaMA), indicating that operating in probability space is insufficient for low-resource languages. On Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct, SMO still achieves the best average ASR (21.22% vs. 24.60% for SimPO on MultiJail; 9.97% vs. 18.49% on AdvBench-X), though the Swahili column remains high due to the base model's limited Swahili generation capability producing many invalid responses; the paper notes that genuinely unsafe responses drop to near zero (e.g., 80→3 on AdvBench-X Swahili). General capability benchmarks (M-MMLU, MGSM) show SMO preserves performance within ~1% of the base model, confirming no meaningful degradation from safety training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on SimPO as its base preference-optimization objective and on the emerging line of work showing that safety-aligned LLMs share a universal refusal direction in representation space (Arditi et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). It departs from the closest prior multilingual safety method, MPO (Zhao et al., 2025), which aligns languages in output probability space, by instead operating entirely in the hidden-representation geometry: the spatial margin gap between a dominant language and a target language becomes both an explicit alignment loss and an example-wise modulation signal for SimPO. Conceptually, the paper reframes multilingual safety failures from an output-distribution problem to a representation-space separability problem, a shift not previously operationalized in training objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The geometric diagnosis—that low-resource safety failures stem from insufficient harmful-harmless separation in representation space rather than missing refusal capability—provides a principled, interpretable explanation that could guide future multilingual alignment beyond safety. The method is computationally negligible (≈18–40 s extra training) and generalizes to non-English anchor languages, making it a practical drop-in for existing preference-optimization pipelines. However, notable limitations temper the impact: only two 7–8B model families are tested, the training set is just 600 samples, and Qwen's Swahili ASR remains high (86.67%) due to persistent invalid responses, suggesting the approach may not fully resolve undertraining in very low-resource settings. Open questions include whether the representation-space separability signal scales to larger models, more diverse language families, and non-safety alignment tasks.
