## Problem

Prior mechanistic interpretability work on machine translation has been confined to word-level analyses, leaving the internal mechanisms of sentence-level MT in LLMs unexplored. This paper asks how LLMs internally encode and distribute the two core functions of translation—producing text in the correct target language and preserving the source sentence's meaning—across specific attention heads, and whether these functions are mechanistically separable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262287/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a source sentence paired with k in-context demonstrations (k=5 by default) in a Q/A prompt format, along with two types of corrupted prompts that selectively remove either target-language information (replacing translations with correct translations in a different language) or source-translation correspondence (replacing translations with random sentences in the correct target language). It produces two sparse sets of attention heads (language heads and translation heads, roughly 1% of total heads), steering vectors computed from their averaged activations, and translated sentences when those vectors are applied during generation with an instruction-free zero-shot prompt. A key design element is the KL-divergence-based token position selection, which identifies where in the multi-token output the clean and corrupted contexts diverge most, resolving the 'token position problem' that arises when extending activation patching from word-level to sentence-level MT.

## Method

The core approach decomposes sentence-level MT into two subtasks—target language identification and sentence equivalence—and uses activation patching with purpose-built corrupted prompts to causally attribute each subtask to specific attention heads. For language identification, corrupted prompts replace target translations with correct translations in a different language; for sentence equivalence, they replace translations with random sentences in the correct target language. A KL-divergence-based criterion selects the token position where clean and corrupted output distributions diverge maximally under teacher forcing, resolving the token position problem unique to multi-token outputs. The identified heads are then used to construct steering vectors (averaged head outputs projected through the output projection), which, when added to the residual stream during generation with an instruction-free zero-shot prompt, enable MT performance comparable to explicit task instructions. Ablation experiments confirm the heads are necessary: removing language heads causes the model to lose target-language generation entirely, while removing translation heads degrades meaning preservation, with an asymmetry reflecting English-centric pre-training defaults that make English generation a robust fallback.

## Results

Across three model families (Gemma-3, Qwen3, Llama-3.2) and 20 translation directions, only 5–10 attention heads (fewer than 1% of the ~1,024 total heads in GEMMA-3-12B-PT) show markedly higher activation-patching deltas, confirming that MT is mechanistically localized. The Jaccard index between the top-5% language heads and translation heads is 0.13, indicating the two subtasks are encoded by largely disjoint head sets. Steering just 1% of language heads and 1% of translation heads with averaged few-shot activations enables instruction-free zero-shot MT performance comparable to an explicit 'Translate from X to Y' prompt, while ablating the same 1% of heads causes function-specific failures (e.g., target language accuracy drops to near zero when ablating language heads for English-source directions). Equivalence vectors transfer across translation directions with minimal BLEU/MetricX loss, with pairwise cosine similarities of 0.7–0.9 among equivalence vectors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between steering-based instruction-free MT and standard instructed zero-shot prompting: steering ~1% of identified heads achieves BLEU and MetricX-24 scores on par with the instructed baseline, confirming the heads encode sufficient task information. For ablation, the identified-head removal is compared against ablating the same percentage of randomly selected heads (resampled per generation); the identified heads produce significantly larger and more function-specific degradation (e.g., language heads ablation collapses target language accuracy, while random ablation causes only gradual quality loss). The paper also cross-validates its head identification against prior work: four of Zhang et al.'s (2025) top-5 word-level heads for English→Chinese are classified as language heads in this framework, and three of Wang et al.'s (2024) top-5 heads overlap with the top-5 English→Chinese translation heads, while Liu et al.'s (2026) token-alignment heads do not appear in either set, consistent with their different objective. For the token-position ablation, the KL-divergence-based selection is compared against fixed positions (i=0, 2) and random selection; the first-token position (i=0) performs nearly as well as the KL-based method for mid-to-large models, while smaller models benefit more from later or random positions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends activation patching (Vig et al., 2020b) from word-level to sentence-level MT, building on the task-vector framework of Todd et al. (2024) and Hendel et al. (2023). It departs from prior MI work on MT—Wang et al. (2024) targeting repetition/language-mismatch failures, Zhang et al. (2025) identifying word-level heads, and Liu et al. (2026) studying token-alignment heads—by proposing an explicit two-aspect decomposition (target language identification vs. sentence equivalence) and showing that steering the identified heads yields instruction-free MT performance, a result Wang et al. (2024) explicitly failed to achieve. The reliance on in-context learning rather than explicit instructions to isolate heads is a methodological choice that aligns with the induction-head literature (Olsson et al., 2022; Von Oswald et al., 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ~1% of attention heads causally mediate MT, cleanly split into language and equivalence functions, provides a mechanistic account of a task previously understood only through output-level benchmarks. The transferability of equivalence vectors across translation directions suggests a language-agnostic semantic representation that could reduce the need for direction-specific tuning in low-resource settings. Open questions include whether this decomposition holds for instruction-tuned (chat) models, whether it generalizes beyond the 20 directions studied, and whether the English-centric asymmetry (language heads critical for non-English targets but not for English targets) reflects a fundamental architectural bias or a data artifact. The practical implication—achieving instructed-prompt-level MT by steering a handful of heads without any task description—could inform efficient inference-time interventions for multilingual LLMs.
