## Problem

Institutions are increasingly investing in AI welfare assessment frameworks—fellowships, benchmarks, and release-gate proposals—on the precautionary assumption that AI systems might possess morally relevant inner states. The paper argues that no one has identified why these frameworks are structurally incapable of producing truth-tracking measurements, and that this epistemic failure has concrete governance consequences that go beyond the metaphysical question of whether AI can suffer.

## Input / Output

The paper takes as its analytical subject the existing AI welfare literature (behavioral and computational marker proposals, institutional programs at Anthropic and the Digital Sentience Fund, and proposed interventions like ending distressing conversations) and produces a set of governance recommendations: prohibiting welfare scorecards as release gates, disallowing welfare appeals as grounds to resist auditing, and requiring that restrictions on AI be justified by externally verifiable harms to humans rather than by welfare scores.

## Method

The argument proceeds as a two-link structural chain rather than independent objections. First, the paper establishes that AI welfare indicators are co-engineered with their targets: because both the system's parameters and its welfare-relevant outputs are products of the same optimization process, ordinary development decisions (RLHF, fine-tuning, scaffold addition/removal) can manufacture or suppress welfare evidence in both directions—steering the model to fit the assessment, or steering the assessment to fit the model. Second, it shows that unlike safety, privacy, or fairness metrics, AI welfare lacks any external validation channel: no deployment failure, legal consequence, or independently observable outcome can reveal whether a welfare metric tracks anything real, because all probes are downstream of the same trainable parameters. The synthesis invokes Frankfurt's notion of bullshit—not as an accusation of bad faith, but as a structural diagnosis that the measurement regime is indifferent to how things really are. The paper then analyzes institutional consequences (welfare framing recasts routine ML as ethically contestable and provides accountability shields) and closes with a five-item benchmark checklist showing that construct-level falsification criteria are structurally unachievable for co-engineered systems.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative results table. The central empirical evidence cited is a fine-tuning experiment (Chua et al., 2026) in which ~600 Q&A pairs asserting consciousness shifted a frontier model on 20 out-of-distribution preference dimensions (shutdown, thought privacy, moral status, autonomy, persistent memory), and similar effects were induced by a single system prompt without parameter changes. The paper's main contribution is a structural argument: AI welfare indicators are co-engineered with their targets (§3) and lack any external validation channel (§4), making them Frankfurtian bullshit in the sense of being structurally disconnected from truth-tracking. The policy conclusion is that welfare scorecards should be prohibited as release gates and that AI restrictions be justified by externally verifiable harms.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare methods empirically but instead addresses six alternative positions in §7. Against 'pursuing AI goals necessarily creates welfare-deserving systems,' the authors argue the alignment–welfare binding is contingent on arbitrary operationalization choices, drawing on AI fairness results showing competing formalizations are provably incompatible (Kleinberg et al., 2016). Against 'harmful AI behavior validates welfare claims,' they note that retaliation or self-preservation can be explained by misaligned objectives or capability failures without invoking inner states. Against 'better methods will build construct validity,' they argue that psychometric triangulation fails because training can make multiple probes agree without changing the latent state, and held-out benchmarks address co-engineering but not construct validity. Against 'the problem is unfalsifiability, not skepticism,' they accept the materialist premise but argue the burden of proof does not shift because the evidence base itself is steerable. Against 'treating AI as welfarable makes us better people' (Rini, 2023), they counter that the argument over-generates to all anthropomorphized artifacts and carries concrete institutional costs. Against 'AI may deserve moral patiency without moral agency,' they argue the framing risks contracting the circle of moral responsibility rather than expanding moral concern.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the growing AI welfare literature (Long et al., 2024; Anthropic, 2025; Butlin et al., 2023) that treats welfare assessment as an open empirical problem to be solved with better metrics. Instead, it repurposes Frankfurt's (2009) and Cohen's (2002) notion of 'bullshit' as a structural epistemic diagnosis, arguing that the co-engineering of systems and their welfare indicators, combined with the absence of any external validation channel, makes current welfare measurement regime-incoherent rather than merely incomplete. The paper draws on animal welfare literature (Dawkins, 2006; Key, 2015; Sneddon, 2003) to isolate the critical difference: biological substrates are fixed, whereas AI substrates are optimizable, and on Goodhart's Law and construct validity theory to formalize the steerability argument. It also connects to AI fairness work (Kleinberg et al., 2016) on the incompatibility of competing formalizations to show that the choice of welfare operationalization is arbitrary in a way that undermines any single metric's authority.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a well-funded and institutionally embedded research agenda (Anthropic's fellows program, Digital Sentience Fund) by arguing that its central measurement problem is not a gap awaiting better methods but a structural feature of engineered systems. Its concrete governance recommendations—prohibiting welfare scorecards as release gates, disallowing welfare appeals to resist auditing, and redirecting research toward human welfare in AI interaction—provide a clear alternative framework for policymakers and developers. A key open question the paper leaves unresolved is whether future architectures could resist end-to-end optimization in ways analogous to biological fixity, which would change the diagnosis; the authors acknowledge this but argue the burden of proof has not been met. The argument is strongest for current and near-term systems and may require re-evaluation if genuinely independent validation channels emerge.
