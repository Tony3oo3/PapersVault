## Problem

Active inference (AIF) provides a principled framework for joint perception and control, but its practical implementations have been confined to model-based settings that require learning an explicit transition dynamics model. This limits AIF's applicability to environments where high-fidelity forward simulation is infeasible or computationally prohibitive. The paper asks whether AIF's performance advantages can be made accessible within the distributional RL framework, which already encodes information about future transitions through the full return distribution without an explicit forward model.

## Input / Output

The method consumes environment state observations (continuous state vectors or raw RGB pixel images for DMC Vision) and produces actions (discrete grid-world moves or continuous control torques). Internally, a state-action amortized encoder maps observations to a latent embedding space where the return distribution is represented as a set of quantile functions parameterized by a mean μ_τ and scale σ_τ for each quantile level τ. The Inverse-Gamma prior on σ_τ is learned jointly, providing per-quantile uncertainty estimates that drive exploration without explicit bonuses.

## Method

DAIF integrates active inference into distributional RL through a three-step construction. First, the AIF ELBO is rederived via variational and causal inference (do-calculus), revealing that the standard expected-free-energy objective simplifies to a form where the intervened world model factorizes as P_Q(Y,S)·P_R(X), disconnecting observations from latents. Second, a push-forward RL framework expresses return distributions as push-forwards of Markov process measures through a return functional, yielding a contraction theorem (Theorem 3.5) showing that an L_E-Lipschitz encoder and L_D-Lipschitz decoder reduce the Bellman contraction modulus by a factor of γ·L_E·L_D. Third, the algorithm learns a state-action amortized encoder to a latent space and performs temporal-difference quantile matching there, casting the quantile regression as MLE under an Asymmetric Laplace Distribution with an Inverse-Gamma prior on the scale parameter. The Inverse-Gamma posterior provides implicit epistemic uncertainty (Thompson-sampling-like stochasticity), and the full update uses TD3-style twin critics with min-clipping, action-noise exploration, and delayed actor updates—eliminating the need for a learned transition kernel entirely.

## Results

On 19 continuous-control environments across three benchmark suites, DAIF achieves the best (lowest) rank on both AULC and Final Return in every suite: 1.5±0.7 AULC and 1.6±0.8 Final Return on EvoGym (7 soft-robotics envs), 1.6±0.7 AULC and 1.5±0.8 Final Return on DMC (7 locomotion envs), and 1.9±1.2 AULC and 2.0±1.4 Final Return on DMC Vision (5 pixel-based envs). On the tabular Latent RiverSwim task, DAIF maintains high visitation frequency of the goal state as the horizon grows, while both PSRL-PI and IQQL degrade. DAIF requires only ~12% more wall-clock time than standard distributional actor-critic methods, less than the overhead of DSAC (26%) or DRND (37%) relative to DTD3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DAIF is compared against five baselines. Against DTD3 (the direct ablation that removes only the AIF component), DAIF consistently wins on all three suites, with AULC ranks of 1.5 vs 2.0 (EvoGym), 1.6 vs 2.4 (DMC), and 1.9 vs 2.9 (DMC Vision), confirming that the AIF-derived latent encoding is the source of the gain. Against DSAC (state-of-the-art distributional actor-critic), DAIF also wins across all suites (e.g., 1.5 vs 2.9 AULC on EvoGym). Against DRND (exploration-driven model-free), DAIF achieves substantially better ranks (1.5–1.9 vs 3.2–4.2 AULC). On DMC Vision, DAIF outperforms DrQv2 by a clear margin (1.9 vs 3.5 AULC, 2.0 vs 3.0 Final Return). On the tabular Latent RiverSwim task, DAIF outperforms both PSRL-PI (model-based) and IQQL (distributional) as the horizon increases, but matches IQQL when the observation space does not admit a more efficient latent representation, validating the theory that AIF's advantage is conditional on the existence of useful state abstractions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of work: active inference (AIF), which has been implemented almost exclusively as a model-based approach requiring learned transition dynamics (Dreamer, PILCO, PSRL), and distributional RL, which captures full return distributions without explicit models. The key conceptual departure is a do-calculus derivation showing that the standard AIF prior construction, when the desired state distribution intervenes on the observation variable, disconnects the observable from the latent variables—yielding a simplified ELBO that no longer requires inferring the approximate posterior P̃(Y,S|X). This simplification, combined with a new 'push-forward RL' framework that expresses return distributions as push-forwards of Markov process measures through a return functional, allows AIF to be embedded directly into distributional RL (specifically quantile regression on a probabilistic embedding space) without learning a transition kernel. The closest prior work, Malekzadeh & Plataniotis (2024), derives Bellman-style EFE recursions for POMDPs but still relies on learned belief representations and a world model; DAIF sidesteps this by learning targets directly from sampled transitions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that AIF's performance advantages—particularly its implicit exploration via posterior uncertainty over the scale parameter σ_τ—become accessible in model-free settings, which is where most practical RL is deployed. Theorem 3.5 provides a clean theoretical justification: the product L_E·L_D of encoder and decoder Lipschitz constants modulates the Bellman contraction, formalizing why narrow information bottlenecks accelerate convergence. Key limitations acknowledged by the authors include the absence of an explicit EFE objective (epistemic value is implicit rather than a separate information-gain term), the fixed bottleneck size set by architecture rather than learned adaptively, and the lack of finite-sample convergence guarantees. The finding that adding SAC-style entropy regularization on top of the Inverse-Gamma prior's implicit exploration is actively harmful (Table 6 ablation) is a useful design insight for practitioners combining distributional losses with entropy-regularized policies.
