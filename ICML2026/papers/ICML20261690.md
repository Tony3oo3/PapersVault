## Problem

Gradient signals in LLM pretraining exhibit a persistent spike-tail spectral structure where roughly 1.5% of directions carry the vast majority of gradient energy, yet existing optimizers either ignore this anisotropy (AdamW, whose element-wise second-moment normalization is dominated by the spike and contracts tail updates) or apply global spectral flattening (Muon, whose Newton-Schulz iterations introduce numerical perturbations that disproportionately rotate noise-sensitive tail directions). The paper asks whether a targeted, low-rank spectral intervention can suppress the dominant spike without amplifying the fragile tail, thereby relaxing the spike-induced learning-rate ceiling and accelerating long-tail learning.

## Input / Output

At each training step, Spectra consumes the current weight matrix W ∈ R^{m×n}, the mini-batch gradient G ∈ R^{m×n}, and a cached rank-k right-singular subspace from the previous step. It produces an updated weight matrix and an updated momentum matrix, where the update is a shaped momentum whose top-k singular values are replaced by the tail's Frobenius-norm average scale, then RMS-normalized. Unlike AdamW (which stores two dense m×n moment buffers) or Muon (which requires full-matrix Newton-Schulz iterations), Spectra stores only the momentum plus a small nk cache, reducing optimizer-state memory by 49.25%.

## Method

Spectra maintains a momentum matrix M_t = μM_{t-1} + G_t and, at each step, estimates the dominant rank-k spike subspace (k ≈ 1.5% of min(m,n)) via a single warm-started power-iteration step that reuses the previous step's right-singular vectors as initialization. It then decomposes the momentum into a spike component U_k diag(s_k) V_k^T and a residual tail M_tail, replaces the spike singular values with the tail's average scale σ_tail = ‖M_tail‖_F / √(min(m,n)−k), and applies the resulting shaped update O_t = M_tail + U_k diag(σ_tail·I) V_k^T with an RMS-normalized step size. A theoretical analysis (Theorem 2.1) shows that spike-dominated stochastic gradient variance tightens the smoothness-optimal learning-rate bound, so suppressing the spike relaxes this constraint and widens the stable learning-rate range. Empirically, the spike subspace exhibits high temporal continuity (canonical correlation > 0.98 between consecutive steps), justifying the single cached iteration, and the approach is 5.1× faster than Muon in optimizer processing while achieving lower final validation loss and higher downstream accuracy across Qwen3-0.6B/2B/8B and LLaMA3-8B.

## Results

On Qwen3-0.6B (100B tokens), Spectra achieves a final validation loss of 2.77, improving average downstream accuracy to 52.96% (+1.41 over AdamW's 51.55%, +0.89 over Muon's 52.07%). On LLaMA3-8B (50B tokens), Spectra reaches a loss of 2.59 with 58.01% average accuracy (+1.62 over AdamW's 56.39%, +0.66 over Muon's 57.35%). In wall-clock time-to-target, Spectra converges up to 1.31×, 1.34×, and 1.24× faster than AdamW on Qwen3-0.6B, 2B-A0.8B, and 8B respectively, while being 5.1× faster than Muon in optimizer processing time and cutting optimizer-state memory by 49.25%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Spectra outperforms all three baselines on both final validation loss and average downstream accuracy at both model scales. Against AdamW, Spectra reduces loss by 2.1% (0.6B) and 1.5% (8B) while improving average accuracy by +1.41 and +1.62 points respectively, with the largest per-task gains on BoolQ (+3.21 on 0.6B) and HellaSwag (+1.66 on 8B). Against Muon, Spectra achieves lower loss (2.77 vs. 2.81 on 0.6B; 2.59 vs. 2.60 on 8B) and higher average accuracy (+0.89 and +0.66 points), though Muon retains a slight edge on RACE at the 8B scale (52.36 vs. 51.88). Against Dion (evaluated only on Qwen3-0.6B), Spectra improves loss by 0.09 and average accuracy by +3.16 points, with particularly large gains on BoolQ (+7.09) and HellaSwag (+3.05). In wall-clock convergence, Spectra's advantage over Muon grows with model scale (1.04× at 0.6B to 1.31× at 8B), while its speedup over AdamW remains consistently above 1.2× across all tested Qwen3 sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

Spectra carves out a middle ground between two existing families of matrix-aware optimizers: element-wise adaptive methods (AdamW) that ignore cross-coordinate structure, and global spectral-flattening methods (Muon, PolarGrad) that equalize the entire spectrum. It also departs from full second-order preconditioners (Shampoo, SOAP) by exploiting the empirical low-rank concentration of gradients to avoid storing dense per-parameter statistics. The closest conceptual neighbor is Dion, which also uses power iteration for low-rank updates, but Spectra differs in its treatment: rather than orthogonalizing the spike, it shrinks spike singular values toward the tail's average scale while leaving the tail residual untouched, motivated by the finding that aggressive tail equalization amplifies noise-dominated directions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for LLM pretraining practitioners: Spectra is a drop-in optimizer that is 5.1× faster than Muon in optimizer processing, cuts optimizer-state memory by ~49%, and achieves lower per-step runtime than both AdamW and Muon across model scales up to 32B, making it attractive for large-scale distributed training. Theorem 2.1 provides a clean theoretical link between spike-dominated gradient variance and the stable learning-rate ceiling, offering a principled explanation for why AdamW's effective step size is constrained by common-structure fluctuations. Open questions include whether the ~1.5% spike fraction is truly universal across architectures and training regimes, whether the approach extends to non-matrix parameters (embeddings, biases), and whether the modest wall-clock speedup over AdamW (1.24–1.34×) grows further at larger scales or with longer training.
