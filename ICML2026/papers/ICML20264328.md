## Problem

RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) stalls on problems where the model achieves near-zero initial success, leaving no training signal to escape the plateau. The paper asks whether a pretrained LLM can leverage latent knowledge to autonomously generate a stepping-stone curriculum for problems it cannot solve, without human curation or access to the hard problems themselves.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264328/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained LLM (Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct) and a fail@128 dataset of hard QA pairs (0/128 success rate) as input. The teacher model is never shown the hard problems during training; it only receives a black-box reward signal reflecting the student's measured improvement on a subsample of those problems. The output is a set of synthetic question-answer pairs (128–256 questions) that, when used to train a fresh student with standard RL, unlock measurable gains on the held-out hard test set. The teacher generates both questions and answers without automatic verification, treating question utility as an emergent property of the reward signal.

## Method

SOAR (Self-Optimization via Asymmetric RL) formulates curriculum generation as a bilevel meta-RL problem: an outer RLOO loop trains a teacher to propose QA pairs, while an inner RLOO loop trains a student on those pairs for 10 steps. The critical design choice is grounding the teacher's reward in the student's measured accuracy improvement on real hard problems (a black-box signal), rather than intrinsic proxies like learnability or self-consistency used in prior self-play work. A student-promotion mechanism accumulates progress by updating the baseline student when a rolling average of teacher rewards exceeds a threshold, allowing the curriculum to adapt as the student improves. This grounded reward design prevents the diversity collapse and reward hacking that plague intrinsic-reward self-play, as confirmed by Vendi Score analysis showing Grounded-T preserves semantic diversity (VS≈32–35) while Intrinsic-T collapses (VS≈11). The paper further shows that question structure and well-posedness matter more than answer correctness for kickstarting learning: only 32.8% of generated problems contain fully correct solutions, yet they still provide effective gradient signal.

## Results

On MATH fail@128 (0/128 success rate), SOAR's Promotion Questions (PQ) achieve +9.3% pass@32 over Hard-Only direct training (≈18.9% vs. 9.6%), while the Promoted Student (PS) achieves +8.5% pass@32. On HARP fail@128, PQ achieves +4.2% pass@32 and PS achieves +3.6% over Hard-Only. PQ-MATH recovers 75% of the performance gain from training on the full 6,750-problem MATH training set (upper bound), and PQ-HARP recovers 50%. Gains are larger at higher k (pass@32 > pass@1), and synthetic questions transfer to the held-out OlympiadBench dataset without OOD optimization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SOAR (both PQ and PS) substantially outperforms the SeRL self-play baseline across all inference budgets on both MATH and HARP fail@128; SeRL improves marginally over Hard-Only but fails to match SOAR's gains. Against the Intrinsic-T baseline (same architecture but with a learnability proxy reward instead of grounded student-progress reward), SOAR wins on both performance and stability: Intrinsic-T exhibits higher variance across teacher seeds and a clear collapse failure mode in 1 of 3 seeds, while grounded teachers produce consistent student trajectories. In terms of diversity, Grounded-T preserves the base model's Vendi Score (34.91 vs. 31.99 for MATH), whereas Intrinsic-T collapses to VS=10.82, confirming reward hacking. Against the Hard-Only baseline with 4× extra compute (group size 128), SOAR still wins by a wide margin (+9.3% vs. +2.8% pass@32 on MATH), and extending Hard-Only training from 1,500 to 6,500 steps yields no further improvement. PQ-MATH recovers 75% of the upper-bound gain from the full 6,750-problem MATH training set, and PQ-HARP (128–192 questions) outperforms 128 real HARP questions while matching 128 real MATH questions.

## Relation to Prior Work

SOAR sits at the intersection of three established lines: asymmetric self-play (Sukhbaatar et al., 2018), bilevel meta-RL (Finn et al., 2017), and RLOO-based RLVR (Ahmadian et al., 2024). Its key departure from near-contemporary LLM self-play systems (Absolute Zero, SeRL, R-Zero, SQLM) is grounding the teacher's reward in measured student progress on a fixed hard dataset rather than intrinsic proxies like learnability or majority-vote confidence, which the paper shows are prone to diversity collapse and reward hacking. The 'double meta-RL loop'—using RLOO in both the outer (teacher) and inner (student) loops to avoid backpropagation through the inner training trajectory—is, to the authors' knowledge, the first instance of this structure in LLM self-play.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a model's ability to generate useful stepping-stone problems is decoupled from its ability to solve the target problems, offering a principled escape from the sparse-reward plateau that limits RLVR on genuinely hard datasets. The finding that question structure and well-posedness matter more than answer correctness (only 32.8% of generated questions have correct solutions) challenges the assumption that synthetic training data must be verified. Limitations include high computational cost (48–60 GPU-hours per run on 4×8 H200s), evaluation restricted to 3–8B models and math, and the need for a held-out hard dataset as the grounding signal—scaling to larger models, other domains, and more efficient reward proxies remain open.
