## Problem

Existing active mapping methods either require task-specific training (e.g., GLEAM's RL pipeline) and exhibit limited zero-shot generalization to large-scale scenes, or lack support for natural-language instructions that enable practical human-agent interaction. While VLMs offer strong zero-shot scene understanding, they are ill-suited to directly emit 3D metric actions, and standard scene representations (pinhole images or navigation-height BEV maps) fail to jointly provide omnidirectional semantics and geometric structure.

## Input / Output

The agent (a CrazyFlie quadrotor in Isaac Gym) receives a 700×1400 equirectangular 360° image and a 128×128 BEV occupancy grid (values −1/0/1 for free/unknown/obstacle) at each timestep, plus optional natural-language instructions for targeted interaction. The VLM outputs discrete 2D waypoint selections in the 360 image, which are back-projected via stored 2D–3D correspondences into executable 3D metric actions; the system also maintains a persistent scene topology tree and an incrementally updated global BEV map as long-horizon memory.

## Method

The method fuses a 360° equirectangular image (omnidirectional semantics) with a navigation-height BEV occupancy grid (geometric structure) into a single 360-BEV representation, using the incremental BEV difference to extract traversable regions and frontier edges. A candidate waypoint generation module places k=25 edge-safe, well-separated waypoints in BEV space via farthest-point sampling and KD-tree pruning, filters them to frontier-adjacent locations, projects them onto the 360 image with non-maximum suppression, and tags each with a numeric ID—so the VLM only reasons over discrete image-space candidates rather than raw 3D coordinates. A VLM-based depth-first exploration agent then partitions the 360 image into explorable regions, selects the highest-information-gain waypoint per region, and incrementally grows a scene topology tree stored in the global BEV map; the agent follows a depth-first policy, backtracking to the nearest unexplored node when the current branch is exhausted, with A* planning for local motion between waypoints. The entire pipeline is zero-shot (no task-specific training), relying on prompting a frozen VLM (Qwen3-VL-235B) for region partitioning and waypoint selection.

## Results

On GLEAM-Bench (overall), the zero-shot method achieves 75.31% coverage, 65.70% AUC, and 0.75 m Chamfer distance, surpassing the strongest trained baseline GLEAM (66.50% Cov, 57.63% AUC, 0.80 m CD) by approximately 13.25% and 14.00% in relative coverage and AUC, respectively. On synthetic indoor scenes the method reaches 83.82% coverage and 73.54% AUC, and on real-scan scenes it still attains 57.90% coverage and 49.64% AUC. In terms of exploration efficiency, the method produces shorter trajectories (52.03 m) than GLEAM (54.51 m) while achieving substantially higher coverage, indicating minimal redundancy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms all seven baselines on every metric (coverage, AUC, and Chamfer distance) across both synthetic and real-scan scenes, without any task-specific training. Against the strongest baseline GLEAM (RL-based, trained on a large-scale dataset), the zero-shot VLM method gains +8.81 pp in coverage (75.31% vs. 66.50%) and +8.07 pp in AUC (65.70% vs. 57.63%) overall, while also producing shorter trajectories (52.03 m vs. 54.51 m). Among heuristic baselines, FBE (56.80% Cov) and ANM (57.01% Cov) are the closest, yet the VLM method still leads by roughly 18 pp in coverage. The gap is most pronounced on real-scan scenes, where GLEAM drops to 47.04% coverage while the VLM method maintains 57.90%, suggesting stronger zero-shot generalization to out-of-distribution environments. On synthetic scenes the advantage over GLEAM narrows slightly (83.82% vs. 76.01% coverage) but remains substantial, and the Chamfer distance is also lower (0.31 m vs. 0.38 m), indicating more accurate geometric capture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GLEAM (Chen et al., 2025b), which established a large-scale active mapping benchmark and an RL-trained exploration policy, but departs fundamentally by replacing the trained policy with a frozen VLM (Qwen3-VL-235B) that requires no task-specific fine-tuning. Conceptually, it bridges two previously separate lines of work: classical frontier-based and information-gain exploration (which operate on geometric representations) and VLM-based scene understanding (which operates in image space). The fused 360-BEV representation and candidate waypoint generation strategy are novel interface designs that let a VLM reason in its native image modality while producing executable 3D metric actions, addressing a gap neither prior line of work fills.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a zero-shot VLM outperforms the strongest RL-trained baseline by ~13% in coverage suggests that large-scale pretraining on diverse visual-linguistic data may be a more generalizable foundation for embodied exploration than task-specific RL, potentially reducing the data and compute burden for deploying active mapping in new environments. The language-driven interaction capability (e.g., updating a renovated room's map via natural language) opens a practical path toward user-controllable mapping agents. Key limitations include the dependence on a 235B-parameter VLM accessed via API (1.38s per inference step), evaluation on a single benchmark (GLEAM-Bench), and the acknowledged issue that low BEV resolution can miss small openings. Open questions include whether smaller VLMs can close the gap, whether the approach transfers to outdoor or multi-floor environments, and whether local deployment can make the latency acceptable for real-time use.
