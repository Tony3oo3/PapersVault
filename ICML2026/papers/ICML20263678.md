## Problem

While multi-agent LLM systems are increasingly deployed, it remains unclear how the internal representational structure of individual models shapes their interactive behavior. Prior work has focused on output-level dynamics or single-agent settings, leaving open whether the well-established neuroscience finding that neural similarity predicts social closeness and cooperation extends to artificial agents. This paper asks whether representational similarity between LLMs predicts both cooperative performance and collective novelty in multi-agent interactions.

## Input / Output

The method takes 23 open-weight LLMs (1B–72B parameters, 8 families) and computes pairwise linear CKA scores from last-token activations on four probe datasets (WikiText, GSM8K, MATH, TruthfulQA), yielding a 276-pair similarity matrix. Each pair then interacts across four cooperation games (word guessing, public good, divide-a-dollar, Keynesian Beauty Contest) and four NoveltyBench-derived creative tasks, with temperature 0.7 and at least 4–10 independent samples per pair. Outputs include per-pair cooperation metrics (correct guesses, cumulative assets, KBC scores), novelty metrics (cluster-count uniqueness, reward-model quality, mutual information between amalgam and individual responses), and mixed-effects regression coefficients quantifying the CKA→outcome relationship.

## Method

The core pipeline computes layer-pairwise linear CKA between two models' activations on a shared probe set, then aggregates into a single similarity score via global average or max-aligned average. Interaction outcomes are modeled with a mixed-effects linear regression Y_ij = α + β·CKA_ij + u_i + v_j + ε_ij, where random effects u_i and v_j absorb model-specific heterogeneity, isolating the similarity effect from capability differences. The authors then run a series of confound analyses: controlling for behavioral difference (absolute choice gap in numerical games), MMLU performance disparity, and four architectural factors (identical pairing, same family, shared tokenizer, size difference) to verify that CKA remains the dominant predictor. Finally, they partition layers into early/middle/late thirds and show that early-layer similarity carries the strongest association with both cooperation and novelty, suggesting shared lexical-semantic grounding as the mechanistic driver.

## Results

Across 276 model pairs (23 open-weight LLMs, 8 families), representational similarity (linear CKA) significantly predicts cooperative outcomes: correct guesses in word-guessing increase by ~88.2% (WikiText), 58.0% (GSM8K), 63.3% (MATH), and 59.4% (TruthfulQA) per unit CKA increase; public-good assets rise by 29.8–34.8%; divide-a-dollar assets rise by 13.7–29.9%; and KBC scores rise by 3.4–4.7%. Conversely, response uniqueness decreases consistently with similarity (strongest in haiku: coeff = −3.425, 95% CI [−4.803, −2.047], p < .001), and mutual information between amalgam and individual responses increases (haiku: coeff = 1.310, 95% CI [1.034, 1.585], p < .001), indicating less novel outputs. Early-layer CKA shows the strongest association with both cooperation and novelty compared to middle and late layers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical association study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the key 'comparisons' are confounding-factor analyses. Controlling for behavioral similarity (absolute gap in numerical choices), representational similarity remains a significant predictor in public good (coeff = 52.118, p < .001) and divide-a-dollar (coeff = 0.435, p < .001), while behavioral difference is insignificant (p = .086 and p = .281 respectively); in KBC the pattern reverses due to the unique Nash equilibrium at 0. Controlling for MMLU performance disparity leaves the main trends robust. When four architectural factors (identical pairing, within-family, shared tokenizer, size difference) are included as covariates, representational similarity remains the strongest predictor for both cooperation (coeff = 0.060, p = .001) and novelty (uniqueness: coeff = −0.087, p = .026; mutual information: coeff = 0.049, p < .001), with none of the four factors significant for uniqueness. The effect is also consistent across all four CKA variants (linear, RBF, unbiased linear, unbiased RBF) and both aggregation methods (global average, maximum-aligned average), and persists at temperature 0.3.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: (1) the neuroscience finding that inter-brain neural similarity predicts social closeness and cooperative performance (Parkinson et al., 2018; Reinero et al., 2021), and (2) the growing literature on multi-agent LLM interactions, which has focused almost exclusively on output-level behaviors (Lai et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024b; Piatti et al., 2024). It repurposes Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA; Kornblith et al., 2019)—a metric previously used to predict single-model task performance (Moschella et al., 2022)—as a predictor of *pairwise* interactive outcomes. For the novelty/creativity side, it extends NoveltyBench (Zhang et al., 2025) from single-agent to multi-agent settings and adopts the mutual-information-based novelty metric from Mu et al. (2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first empirical evidence that representational similarity is a practical, measurable predictor of multi-agent interaction outcomes, suggesting a concrete design lever for multi-agent system architects: pair similar models for cooperation, dissimilar models for novelty. The finding that early layers (lexical-semantic grounding) drive the effect, rather than deeper task-specific layers, is a mechanistic insight that narrows where future interpretability work should focus. Open questions include whether the trend reverses in other game structures, whether neuron-level analyses can enable targeted steering of cooperation vs. novelty, and whether the results generalize beyond the 23 open-weight models tested. The context-dependence already observed (e.g., KBC's unique Nash equilibrium weakening the effect) cautions against treating the finding as universal.
