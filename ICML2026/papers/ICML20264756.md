## Problem

Medical Large Vision-Language Models (Med-LVLMs) suffer from severe hallucinations that pose critical safety risks in clinical deployment. Existing inference-time activation editing methods mitigate hallucination in generic LVLMs by contrasting visual features, but they fail to capture the domain-specific imaging manifestations (e.g., pulmonary opacities in pneumonia) and clinician diagnostic principles (e.g., ABCDE reading order) that are essential for accurate medical image interpretation.

## Input / Output

At inference, MEDA takes a medical image and a text question (for VQA) or a medical image alone (for report generation) as input and produces a factual VQA answer or radiology report, identical in format to the base LVLM's output. The method requires no modification to the input pipeline; instead, it pre-computes sparse steering vectors from as few as 10 image-question pairs and applies them to internal attention-head activations during the forward pass. The approach generalizes across radiology (chest X-ray), ophthalmology (fundus images), and pathology (histopathology) modalities without modality-specific retraining.

## Method

MEDA integrates two complementary steering mechanisms. Query-decisive Manifestation Steering (QMS) uses a category-priority extraction (disease > anatomy > other) to identify the most diagnosis-relevant entity from the query, retrieves positive imaging manifestations from authoritative databases (Radiopaedia, PathologyOutlines, EyeWiki) via Bio-ClinicalBERT cosine-similarity matching, and derives manifestation-aware steering vectors by contrasting activations of manifestation-augmented inputs against original inputs. Principle-driven Diagnosis Induction (PDI) encodes textbook-recommended diagnostic principles into a three-part scope-conditioned template (global assessment → local evidence in prescribed order → diagnostic summary), decomposes clinical reports into scope-labeled sentences, instantiates the template to form chain-structured diagnostic prompts, and derives principle-aware vectors by contrasting their activations against originals. Finally, MEDA performs cooperative steering by ranking all headwise vectors from both modules by L2-norm and jointly applying the top-K vectors (default K=32, α=5) to the model's attention-head activations during inference, requiring only 0.5 GPU-hours for vector construction.

## Results

On LLaVA-Med-1.5, MEDA achieves 84.8% accuracy on IU-Xray (a 10.6% absolute improvement over the 74.2% baseline) and 79.4% on MIMIC-CXR (6.9% over baseline), outperforming the strongest activation-editing baseline AFTER by 3.4% and 2.2% respectively. For report generation on IU-Xray, MEDA yields 30.2 METEOR, 27.8 ROUGE-1, and 59.2 RaTEScore, averaging a 6.5% gain over the baseline across the three generative metrics. On the ophthalmology and pathology benchmarks (Harvard-FairVLMed and PMC-OA), MEDA similarly leads all baselines, reaching 88.3% ACC and 34.2 RaTEScore on PMC-OA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MEDA outperforms all eight baselines across every dataset and metric in Table 1. Against the strongest activation-editing method AFTER, MEDA gains 3.4% accuracy on IU-Xray (84.8 vs. 81.4), 2.2% on MIMIC-CXR (79.4 vs. 77.2), 1.3% on VQA-RAD (63.4 vs. 62.1), and 2.8% on SLAKE (58.1 vs. 55.3). Among RAG-based methods, MMedRAG is the closest competitor on IU-Xray accuracy (81.1) but trails MEDA by 3.7 points; training-based MMedPO leads on IU-Xray RaTEScore (56.7) yet still falls 2.5 points short of MEDA (59.2). The generic editing baselines VTI, ICT, and VISTA all underperform MEDA by 4–8% accuracy on IU-Xray, confirming that image-appearance contrasts alone are insufficient for medical hallucination mitigation. On the non-radiology benchmarks (Table 2), MEDA similarly leads AFTER by 1.0% ACC on Harvard-FairVLMed and by 1.4% ACC and 4.1% RaTEScore on PMC-OA, demonstrating consistent gains across ophthalmology and pathology modalities.

## Relation to Prior Work

MEDA extends the inference-time activation editing paradigm established by Inference-Time Intervention (Li et al., 2024a) and AFTER (Wang et al., 2026), which steer LVLM activations using contrastive vectors derived from trusted vs. untrusted samples. The key departure is that prior editing methods (VTI, ICT, VISTA, AFTER) construct steering vectors from generic visual contrasts (noise-perturbation, image presence/absence), whereas MEDA injects domain-specific medical expertise—query-decisive imaging manifestations retrieved from Radiopaedia and textbook-recommended diagnostic principles (e.g., the ABCDE reading order)—into the positive samples. This makes MEDA the first activation editing method explicitly tailored to medical imaging, bridging the gap between generic hallucination mitigation and clinically grounded interpretation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MEDA offers a practical, low-cost path to reducing hallucinations in medical LVLMs: it requires only 0.5 GPU-hours for vector construction, operates at 0.48 samples/sec inference speed with 15.9 GB memory, and generalizes across datasets and modalities with as few as 10 training samples. The 10.6% accuracy gain on IU-Xray and consistent improvements across six LVLMs and three imaging modalities suggest broad applicability for clinical deployment where retraining is infeasible. A key limitation is the dependence on white-box access to internal activations, which precludes use with closed-source models. Open questions include whether the approach extends to other specialized domains (e.g., cardiology, neurology) and whether the reliance on GPT-4 for entity extraction and scope labeling can be further reduced for fully self-contained clinical pipelines.
