## Problem

Predicting AI's impact on labor is currently fragmented across economics (structural and reduced-form models), management (adoption and workflow theory), and ML (capability benchmarks), with no integrated prediction framework that handles the non-stationarity, endogenous feedback, and distribution shift inherent to AI-driven labor disruption. The paper argues this is not merely an ethics or policy question but a core ML problem, because the technical challenges—prediction under regime change, calibration under uncertainty, and extrapolation beyond observed institutional structures—map directly onto central questions in modern machine learning.

## Input / Output

Prediction targets span multiple levels of aggregation (task, workflow, occupation, firm, region, aggregate economy) and heterogeneous time horizons, from short-term task time savings to long-term general equilibrium effects on employment, wages, and inequality. A key complication is that the 'treatment' variable—AI capability—is itself non-stationary, general-purpose, and difficult to measure, unlike classical automation technologies that can be counted and categorized by function. Outputs must carry calibrated uncertainty (predictive intervals, not just point forecasts) and be evaluated under distribution shift, since the data-generating process changes as each new model generation expands the set of affected tasks.

## Method

As a position paper, the core contribution is a structured research agenda that maps specific ML techniques to six identified technical obstacles in labor prediction. The paper proposes ensemble measurement pipelines to construct robust 'AI use' indicators from heterogeneous sources, continual and online learning to track regime change as capabilities evolve, distributionally robust optimization to stress-test forecasts across subgroups, and conformal prediction (including shift-robust variants) for calibrated uncertainty under non-stationarity. LLM-based agent simulation is positioned as a complement for exploring counterfactual labor market regimes not yet observed, with explicit caveats about model idiosyncrasy and prompt sensitivity. The paper further proposes institutional infrastructure—shared prediction tasks, recurring competitions scored with proper scoring rules like the Brier score, and cross-disciplinary evaluation standards—modeled on how ImageNet and Kaggle structured cumulative progress in computer vision, with the key insight that economics, ML, and LLM-based simulation are complementary rather than competing paradigms.

## Results

This is a position paper with no empirical experiments, quantitative results, or benchmark evaluations. The paper's central claim is qualitative: predicting AI's impact on labor should be treated as a core ML problem (involving non-stationarity, distribution shift, endogenous feedback, and calibrated uncertainty) rather than solely an ethics or governance question. It identifies six technical obstacles (data gaps, rapidly evolving capability, capability-to-use gap, endogeneity, heterogeneity, and evaluation under distribution shift) and proposes six research directions (measurement infrastructure, workflow benchmarking, drift-aware prediction methods, LLM-based agent simulation, shared evaluation design, and institutional incentives). No headline metrics or numerical results are reported.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method and therefore does not compare against baselines in the standard empirical sense. Instead, it surveys and positions existing approaches across economics (task-based models à la Acemoglu & Restrepo, structural VARs, reduced-form panel methods), management (automation-augmentation paradox, algorithmic management), and ML (scaling laws, HELM, SWE-bench, conformal prediction, distributionally robust optimization, LLM-based agent simulation). The paper argues for complementarity rather than replacement: economics is optimized for causal identification within observed institutional regimes, ML for flexible high-dimensional modeling and distribution-shift detection, and LLM-based simulation for exploring unobserved counterfactual regimes. It explicitly positions its agenda as going beyond prior calls to treat labor impacts as an AI safety or ethics issue (Hazra et al., 2025; ILO, 2021) by framing it as a scientific prediction problem with evaluable targets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper repurposes the task-based framework of technological change (Autor et al., 2003; Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2019) and the broader economics literature on automation exposure, but reframes the prediction problem as one requiring ML-specific tools: conformal prediction for calibrated uncertainty under drift, distributionally robust optimization for heterogeneous adoption, and LLM-based agent simulation for counterfactual exploration. It draws on ML evaluation infrastructure (HELM, SWE-bench, scaling laws) and continual/distribution-shift literature to argue that the non-stationarity and endogeneity inherent in AI-labor dynamics are not merely economic problems but core ML challenges. The paper carves new ground by explicitly positioning labor-market forecasting as a first-class ML research direction with its own benchmarks, scoring rules, and evaluation competitions, rather than a downstream application of existing econometric or ML methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, multi-pronged research agenda—workflow-level benchmarks, ensemble measurement pipelines, calibrated prediction under regime change, and LLM-agent simulation—that could shape how ML venues (e.g., ICML) and economics venues jointly approach AI-labor prediction. Key open questions include whether the proposed methods (conformal prediction, DRO, LLM agents) can actually deliver reliable long-horizon forecasts when the data-generating process is itself shifting, and whether the interdisciplinary institutional incentives the paper calls for will materialize. A limitation is that, as a position paper, it offers no empirical validation that any of the proposed ML approaches outperform existing econometric methods on actual labor-market prediction tasks, leaving the complementarity argument largely conceptual.
