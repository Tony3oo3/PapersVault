## Problem

Smoothing-based robust conformal prediction faces a fundamental trade-off: single-sample methods (RCP1) are fast at inference but produce large prediction sets due to Jensen's inequality conservatism, while sampling-heavy methods (BinCP) produce smaller sets but require expensive Monte Carlo at test time. The paper asks whether the calibration phase—a one-time pre-processing step—can absorb the additional sampling cost to shrink prediction sets without increasing test-time inference cost.

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (CIFAR-10 at 32×32, ImageNet at 224×224) or regression data points as input, applies isotropic Gaussian noise (σ typically 0.15–0.5) to create a smoothed score, and outputs a prediction set of class labels (or a prediction interval for regression) whose scores exceed a calibrated threshold. At test time, FLoR^(1) requires exactly one noise-augmented forward pass per input, while FLoR^(k) uses k samples (e.g., 21–101) with majority-vote aggregation. The guarantee holds for any perturbation within an ℓ₂ ball of radius r around the clean input.

## Method

FLoR^(1) replaces RCP1's Jensen's-inequality shortcut with direct Monte Carlo estimation of the expected worst-case coverage E[c↓(β, B)] from calibration points, using conformal risk control with a risk function L_i(λ) = max_{x̃∈B} Pr[s(x̃+ε,y) ≤ 1−λ]. The threshold λ is found via binary search over a tuning budget (m_tune samples) and validated with an independent certification budget (m_cert samples) using Clopper-Pearson lower confidence bounds, ensuring the final guaranteed coverage meets 1−α. Because the certified lower bound c↓(·,B) is convex, directly estimating E[c↓(β,B)] yields a higher threshold than c↓(E[β],B), producing strictly smaller prediction sets for any r>0. FLoR^(k) extends the framework to majority voting by calibrating with a margin-above-half constraint on smoothed coverage probabilities, solving for offsets (α₀, β₀) that guarantee the binomial majority-vote success probability compensates for the reduced per-sample coverage, yielding more deterministic sets at very low test-time sample rates.

## Results

On ImageNet (ResNet, σ=0.5), FLoR^(1) reduces the average prediction set size from 144.2 (RCP1) to 24.1 at radius r=0.5, a 6× improvement, while maintaining O(1) test-time cost identical to RCP1. On CIFAR-10 (ResNet), FLoR^(1) achieves an average set size of 4.17 at r=0.5 versus 5.41 for RCP1 (23% smaller). At r=0, FLoR^(1) is marginally larger than RCP1 (e.g., 2.68 vs 2.47 on CIFAR-10) due to finite-sample correction, but outperforms RCP1 at all r>0. In the regression setting (Udacity steering, σ=0.15), FLoR^(1) reduces interval width from 0.92 to 0.56 at r=0.25.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against RCP1 (single-sample robust CP), FLoR^(1) consistently produces smaller prediction sets for all radii r > 0, with the gap widening substantially as r increases: on ImageNet (ResNet), the set size advantage grows from negligible at r=0 to 6× at r=0.5 (24.1 vs 144.2). At r=0, FLoR^(1) is slightly larger than RCP1 due to finite-sample correction and the δ₀ tuning offset, but this is a practical artifact that vanishes with larger calibration budgets. Against BinCP (the SOTA sample-heavy method), FLoR^(1) is not directly comparable because BinCP requires 10³–10⁴ MC samples at test time, whereas FLoR^(1) uses only one; however, FLoR^(1) with 10⁴ calibration samples achieves set sizes comparable to BinCP at a fraction of the total computational cost. The proposed FLoR^(k) variant, which accounts for majority voting during calibration, outperforms BinCP in set size at very low test-time sample rates (21–101 samples), e.g., achieving smaller sets than BinCP at k=21 while both use 10⁴ calibration samples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the smoothing-based robust conformal prediction line initiated by Gendler et al. (2021) and refined by BinCP (Zargarbashi & Bojchevski, 2025) and RCP1 (Zargarbashi et al., 2025). The key departure from RCP1 is replacing its Jensen's-inequality shortcut—which applies the certified lower bound directly to the known target 1−α—with a Monte Carlo estimation of the worst-case coverage over calibration points, leveraging conformal risk control (Angelopoulos et al., 2022) as the calibration mechanism. The method inherits BinCP's binary-certificate formulation to keep the required sample rate manageable, and introduces FLoR^(k) to handle majority-vote aggregation at test time, a setting not addressed by either RCP1 or BinCP.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The front-loading insight—that calibration is a one-time cost amortized over unbounded test points—provides a practical path to smaller robust prediction sets without sacrificing O(1) inference speed, which is critical for real-time deployment. The quantification of the Jensen gap (Remark 3.3) gives practitioners a clear theoretical explanation for when and why RCP1 is conservative, and the FLoR^(k) extension addresses the inherent stochasticity of single-sample methods. Open questions include whether the one-time-calibration assumption holds under frequent model updates or distribution shift, and whether the approach extends to non-symmetric threat models or multi-label settings where the binary-certificate structure may not apply as cleanly.
