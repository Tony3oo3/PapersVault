## Problem

Existing training data detection methods for LLMs and VLMs treat membership inference as instance-wise classification without controlling the error rate of the identified set, making them unsuitable as credible evidence in high-stakes settings like copyright litigation or privacy auditing. This paper formalizes training data identification as a set-level multiple-hypothesis testing problem and provides the first distribution-free method with provable false identification rate (FIR) control over the selected subset.

## Input / Output

The method requires a target model θ, a calibration set of confirmed non-members (e.g., data from after the model's training cutoff), a test set of candidate samples, a pluggable detection score function (perplexity, MIN-K%, M-Entropy, MaxRényi-K%, or gradient-based knockoff statistics), and a user-specified FIR target α. It outputs a selected subset of test indices identified as training data, accompanied by an asymptotic guarantee that the false identification proportion in the selected set does not exceed α. The method is score-agnostic and works with both black-box outputs and white-box gradient scores, and an extension leverages a small set of confirmed members via an adjusted moment estimator to further boost power.

## Method

PTDI constructs valid conformal p-values for each test point by comparing its detection score against the calibration set, then estimates the unknown training-data proportion π_test using a novel Jackknife-corrected Beta Boundary (JKBB) estimator. The JKBB estimator exploits the fact that conformal p-values under the null are uniform on [0,1], so the boundary density at p=1 identifies the non-member proportion; it uses Beta kernels (naturally supported on [0,1]) with a dual-bandwidth jackknife combination to eliminate the leading O(b) bias that makes Storey's standard estimator conservative. The p-values are then scaled by (1 − π̂_test) before applying the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, which provably controls FIR at level α while substantially increasing power over the unscaled baseline. The theoretical guarantee relies on the consistency of the JKBB estimator at the boundary and the monotonicity of the BH rejection set, yielding limsup FIR ≤ α as the test set size grows.

## Results

On WikiMIA with Pythia-1.4B at target FIR α=0.05, PTDI achieves an empirical FIR of 0.0494 (strictly below target), while the KTD baseline (Hu et al., 2025) yields 0.1311, far exceeding the target. On WikiMIA with GPT-NeoX-20B at α=0.5 using the MIN-K% score, PTDI improves power from 0.5177 (vanilla BH) to 0.8618. In the ablation (Table 1), PTDI consistently outperforms all adaptive BH baselines: e.g., on NeoX-20B with MIN-K% at α=0.1, power rises from 1.69% (Vanilla) to 5.90% (PTDI), a 250% relative gain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PTDI dominates all four baselines on power while maintaining strict FIR control. Against Storey-BH (the strongest adaptive BH variant), PTDI achieves roughly 1.5–2× higher power across all models and scores (e.g., NeoX-20B, MIN-K%, α=0.1: 5.90% vs. 3.54%; LLaMA-7B, Perplexity, α=0.3: 17.43% vs. 5.36%). BKY and Quantile-BH perform nearly identically to the vanilla BH procedure, confirming that their adaptive π₀ estimates offer little benefit in this setting; PTDI's JKBB-based scaling yields a 250–350% relative power gain over both. Against the knockoff-based KTD method (Hu et al., 2025), PTDI provides a critical advantage in reliability: KTD fails to control FIR on WikiMIA and XSum for several α values (e.g., empirical FIR of 0.1311 vs. target 0.05 on Pythia-1.4B/WikiMIA), whereas PTDI stays at or below the nominal level in all tested configurations. On BBC Real Time (where KTD does control FIR), PTDI still achieves superior power on GPT-2, confirming that the conformal + JKBB pipeline is both more reliable and more powerful than the knockoff approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the conformal inference framework (Vovk et al., 2003; Bates et al., 2023; Jin & Candès, 2023) for distribution-free selection, repurposing conformal p-values from single-point prediction to set-level multiple testing for training data identification. It departs from Storey's (2002) boundary density estimator, which is known to be biased under downward-trending p-value densities, by introducing a Jackknife-corrected Beta kernel (JKBB) estimator that removes the leading O(b) bias. The paper also positions itself against KTD (Hu et al., 2025), a knockoff-based method for controlled training data detection, arguing that conformal p-values provide more robust FIR control without requiring high-quality synthetic knockoffs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PTDI provides the first distribution-free, set-level method with provable false identification rate control for training data identification, making it directly applicable to high-stakes legal and privacy auditing contexts where instance-wise MIA scores lack credible statistical guarantees. The method is score-agnostic (compatible with perplexity, MIN-K%, M-Entropy, MaxRényi-K%, etc.) and requires only confirmed non-member calibration data, making it practical for real-world auditing. A key limitation is the assumption that calibration and test data are distributionally similar; the authors identify weighted conformal p-values as a promising direction for handling distribution shift. Open questions include whether the FIR guarantee holds under adversarial or heavily shifted test distributions, and how the method performs when the training data proportion is extremely small.
