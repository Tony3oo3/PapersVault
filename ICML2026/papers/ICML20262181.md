## Problem

Real-world optimization objectives are often governed by complex, subjective decision-maker preferences that cannot be expressed as closed-form utility functions. Existing preference-based BO methods require rigidly structured feedback (pairwise comparisons or scalar ratings), while LLM-only optimizers lack calibrated uncertainty and principled exploration. LILO addresses this gap by enabling free-form natural language feedback as the primary interface while retaining the sample efficiency and uncertainty quantification of GP-based Bayesian optimization.

## Input / Output

At each trial, the method consumes a batch of black-box outcomes y = f(x) (which can be high-dimensional or unstructured, e.g., generated text summaries), a set of free-form natural language messages from the decision maker answering LLM-generated questions, and optionally a textual prior knowledge prompt. It produces a batch of B^exp candidate parameter points in [0,1]^d for the next experiment, binary pairwise preference labels over observed outcomes, and a fitted pairwise GP proxy model M_n: X → P(R) over the composite utility g∘f. The natural language feedback is information-dense: a single DM message can constrain many pairwise preferences simultaneously, outperforming up to 8–16 individual pairwise comparisons.

## Method

LILO places the LLM in a supporting translation role rather than as the optimizer: it converts accumulated natural language feedback into binary pairwise preference labels between observed outcomes, which are then absorbed by a pairwise GP surrogate (probit likelihood with Laplace approximation) over the continuous search space X. Candidate generation uses standard BO acquisition functions (batched LogNEI) over the GP posterior, preserving calibrated uncertainty and principled exploration-exploitation. Under a labeling budget K, pairs are selected sequentially via qEUBO to maximize information gain, with the GP refit after each chunk of S labels. The LLM also generates targeted questions for the DM each round and can warm-start optimization by sampling initial candidates informed by textual prior knowledge, leveraging its pretrained domain understanding.

## Results

Across 10 benchmark environments (DTLZ2, Vehicle Safety, Car Cab Design, Thermal Comfort, NAS-Bench-201, each with different utility functions), LILO consistently achieves the highest maximum ground-truth utility over 8 batched BO trials (32 replications, 95% CIs), with the largest margins in early trials. A single natural-language feedback message (B^pf=1) matches or exceeds quantitative baselines receiving 8–16 pairwise comparisons or scalar utility evaluations. Pairwise LLM labeling outperforms direct scalar utility estimation on both best-point selection accuracy and overall optimization progress, and qEUBO-guided pair selection yields higher best utility than random pair selection when the labeling budget K is limited (K∈{8,16,32}).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LILO is compared against four baselines: two LLM-only optimizers (LLM direct and LLM 2-step, both following the LLAMBO paradigm of Liu et al., 2024) and two quantitative-feedback BO methods (true-utility BO and preference BO using EUBO/qEUBO from Lin et al., 2022). LILO outperforms both LLM baselines across all environments; the LLM-only methods show reasonable zero-shot performance in semantically meaningful settings but fail to improve meaningfully over the course of optimization, consistent with known diminishing returns of in-context learning. LILO also substantially outperforms the true-utility BO and preference BO baselines, particularly in early trials, because a single natural-language message conveys global information about the utility landscape (directionality, relative importance of metrics, tradeoff structure) that localized point-wise or pairwise feedback cannot capture. As the number of trials grows, the quantitative baselines close the gap, and in longer-horizon runs (Appendix F.5) the advantage of natural language diminishes. In the high-dimensional summarization experiment (CNN/DailyMail), LILO matches the strongest BO baselines given maximum oracle feedback and improves more rapidly when those baselines are restricted to smaller feedback budgets.

## Relation to Prior Work

LILO builds directly on the preferential BO lineage: the pairwise GP with probit likelihood from Chu & Ghahramani (2005), the convergence framework of Gonzalez et al. (2017), and the EUBO/qEUBO acquisition functions of Lin et al. (2022) and Astudillo et al. (2023). Its key departure is replacing the structured feedback channel (pairwise comparisons or scalar ratings) with free-form natural language, using an LLM as a translator that converts accumulated DM messages into pairwise preference labels consumed by the GP surrogate. This distinguishes LILO from LLM-as-optimizer methods (Yang et al. 2023, Liu et al. 2024) where the LLM directly proposes candidates, and from language-based preference elicitation over discrete sets (Austin et al. 2024, Handa et al. 2024), by keeping the LLM in a supporting labeling role while the GP handles calibrated uncertainty and acquisition-driven exploration over continuous spaces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that natural language is a far more natural and information-dense feedback interface for decision makers than pairwise comparisons or scalar ratings, and LILO shows this translates into measurable optimization gains—particularly in feedback-limited, early-stage regimes where real-world budgets are tight. The framework also naturally extends to high-dimensional unstructured outcomes (text, images) and can incorporate textual domain priors for warm-starting, broadening the applicability of BO to settings where explicit objectives are unavailable. Key limitations include reliance on a simulated (LLM-based) decision maker rather than real humans, the diminishing advantage of NL feedback as trials accumulate, and the dependence on LLM labeling accuracy. Open questions include active question generation, fine-tuning on past experimental data, and hybrid feedback combining NL with structured quantitative signals.
