## Problem

Spiking Transformers underperform their ANN counterparts, and while prior work has focused on architectural innovations, the optimization process has been largely overlooked. The paper identifies and quantifies an 'excessive noise problem': surrogate gradients needed to train non-differentiable spiking neurons introduce additional parameter-update noise that, when compounded with the inherent noise of adaptive optimizers like AdamW, causes slower convergence, training oscillations, and reduced stability.

## Input / Output

AdaS is a meta-optimizer that operates on the internal state of any adaptive optimizer (gradient, first-order momentum m_t, second-order moment v_t, and adaptive update u_t) and produces a single modified parameter update per step. It does not alter the model's input or output format: the trained Spiking Transformers still consume text sequences (GLUE), event streams (FE108, VisEvent, CIFAR10-DVS), or RGB images (ADE20K, CIFAR-100) and produce task-specific outputs (classification scores, bounding boxes, segmentation masks, or class labels). The only additional hyperparameter is a target noise level γ, which is set per dataset (1.0–2.0 in experiments).

## Method

AdaS blends the adaptive optimizer's update component u_t with the first-order momentum m_t via an adaptive coefficient α_t, producing the update θ_t = θ_{t-1} − η_t[α_t u_t + (1−α_t) m_t]. The key insight is that, under a first-order Taylor approximation, the negative-gradient update minimizes a formally defined parameter-update noise, so incorporating a momentum-based gradient component counteracts the excess noise introduced by the adaptive optimizer and surrogate gradients. The balancing coefficient is computed in closed form as α_t = Π_{[0,1]}(√(‖m_t‖² + 2γ/η_t) / ‖u_t − m_t‖), where γ is a target noise level that controls how much of the adaptive update is retained. Because m_t is already stored by the base optimizer, AdaS incurs no additional memory overhead, and it can be plugged into any adaptive optimizer (AdamW, QHAdam, AdaX, Adamax) as a drop-in meta-optimizer. A convergence analysis in the online-learning framework shows AdaS achieves O(1/√T) regret under bounded-gradient and bounded-distance assumptions.

## Results

On the GLUE benchmark with SpikeLM, AdaS achieves an average score of 77.6, outperforming AdamW (76.5) and Lion (76.1) by 1.1 and 1.5 points respectively, while the ANN upper bound (BERT) scores 83.2. On ADE20K semantic segmentation, E-SpikeFormer trained with AdaS reaches 40.2% mIoU, a 2.0-point gain over AdamW (38.2%) and 1.7 points over Lion (38.5%). On CIFAR10-DVS, QKFormer with AdaS achieves 85.1% accuracy versus 84.0% for AdamW and 82.2% for Lion. On CIFAR-100, S-Transformer with AdaS reaches 79.1% accuracy, surpassing AdamW (78.4%) and Lion (78.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the same SpikeLM architecture trained with AdamW, AdaS consistently improves across nearly all GLUE tasks, with the largest gains on CoLA (+3.3), RTE (+1.4), and SST-2 (+1.0), while only MNLI-matched shows a negligible −0.1 drop. Compared to Lion on the same model, AdaS yields a 1.5-point average improvement on GLUE and a 2.9-point advantage on CIFAR10-DVS (85.1% vs. 82.2%). Against other SNN baselines, AdaS-equipped SpikeLM (77.6 avg) substantially outperforms Spikformer (68.9), SpikeBERT (59.7), and the original SpikeLM (54.9), narrowing the gap to the BERT ANN upper bound (83.2) to under 6 points. On ADE20K, AdaS-trained E-SpikeFormer (40.2% mIoU) surpasses AdamW (38.2%) and Lion (38.5%), while SGD collapses to 4.6%, underscoring the necessity of adaptive optimization for spiking transformers. On CIFAR-100, AdaS improves S-Transformer by 0.7 points over AdamW and 1.1 points over Lion, maintaining its advantage across both NLP and CV domains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on AdamW as its base optimizer, extending the adaptive optimization lineage (Adam → AdamW → Lion, QHAdam, AdaX) with a meta-optimization layer specifically designed for the surrogate-gradient training regime of Spiking Transformers. Prior Spiking Transformer work (Spikformer, S-Transformer, QKFormer, E-SpikeFormer, SpikeLM) has focused almost exclusively on architectural innovations—spike-driven attention, linear-complexity mechanisms, spike firing approximation—while inheriting standard optimizers unchanged. The paper departs from this architectural-centric paradigm by identifying and formalizing the 'excessive noise problem' at the optimization level, using a proximal-update noise definition rooted in Nesterov's framework and an online-learning convergence analysis following Zinkevich, to motivate a momentum-blended adaptive update.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AdaS offers a practical, drop-in optimizer that narrows the SNN–ANN accuracy gap without altering model architecture or inference cost, which is significant for the neuromorphic hardware community where energy efficiency is the primary selling point. The consistent 1–3% gains across NLU, tracking, segmentation, and classification tasks, combined with negligible memory overhead and ~10% speed cost, make it immediately adoptable. However, the convergence proof relies on convexity and bounded-gradient assumptions that are far from the non-convex reality of deep Spiking Transformers, and the target noise level γ appears to require per-task tuning. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large Spiking Transformers (e.g., billion-parameter language models) and whether the noise-control principle generalizes to other non-differentiable training settings beyond spiking neurons.
