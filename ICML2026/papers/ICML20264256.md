## Problem

Sequential recommendation models are inflexible when users issue immediate natural-language requests (e.g., 'I want more exciting games'), and existing request-aware methods either retrain the entire backbone or rely on costly LLM inference, making them impractical for real-time deployment. The core gap is the absence of a lightweight mechanism for posterior control—directly steering a frozen, deployed recommender's internal parameters in response to arbitrary natural-language signals without retraining or heavyweight inference.

## Input / Output

The method takes a user's historical interaction sequence (ordered item IDs) and a free-form natural-language request as input. The request is tokenized and mean-pooled through a pretrained language model to produce a fixed-dimensional semantic embedding, which is then projected to gate logits over the backbone's row and column dimensions. The output is a standard next-item ranking (HR@k, NDCG@k, MRR@k), but the frozen backbone's FFN and/or attention output-projection matrices are multiplicatively masked per request, so the same deployed model produces different rankings for different requests without any weight updates.

## Method

CRAMER converts a natural-language request into structured row-column gating masks that modulate a frozen Transformer-based sequential recommender (SASRec or BERT4Rec). A PLM encodes the request into a mean-pooled embedding, which a linear layer projects to gate logits; Gumbel-Top-k then selects exactly k = ⌈(1−ρ)d⌉ active entries to form a sparse binary vector. This vector is decomposed into per-matrix row and column gates, and the entrywise product M_ij = r_i · c_j yields masks applied to the FFN and/or W_O matrices of the backbone, reducing control dimensionality from O(αβ) per matrix to O(α+β). Training optimizes a predictive next-item loss plus a closed-form Bernoulli KL regularizer (prior mean set to the keep ratio k/d) using a straight-through estimator with temperature-annealed softmax for gradient flow through the discrete Gumbel-Top-k sampling. Only the projection layer and optionally a subset of PLM parameters are trained, keeping the backbone entirely frozen and adding just ~0.018 s and ~1.4 GB per-request overhead at inference.

## Results

CRAMER consistently achieves the best results across all 48 evaluation settings (4 datasets × 2 backbones × 6 metrics), with statistically significant improvements (BH FDR=0.05) in 41/48 settings (85.42%). Under the SASRec backbone, CRAMER reaches HR@10 of 0.578 (ReDial), 0.556 (KuaiSAR), 0.574 (Beauty), and 0.619 (CDs&Vinyl), and NDCG@10 of 0.428, 0.436, 0.489, and 0.472 respectively. Under BERT4Rec, gains are even more pronounced on larger datasets, e.g., NDCG@10 of 0.487 on CDs&Vinyl vs. 0.454 for the best baseline (REARANK). Inference overhead is minimal: only +0.018s runtime and +1355.6 MiB GPU memory over vanilla SASRec, compared to +9.256s for REARANK.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CRAMER outperforms all four request-aware baselines across every dataset, metric, and backbone combination. The strongest baseline is REARANK (LLM-based listwise reasoning reranker), which CRAMER surpasses by 2.9–4.7 points in NDCG@10 on SASRec (e.g., 0.472 vs. 0.454 on CDs&Vinyl) and by up to 3.3 points on BERT4Rec (0.487 vs. 0.454 on CDs&Vinyl). LLM-ESR (cached LLM semantic embeddings) is the second-strongest baseline but trails CRAMER by 3–5 NDCG@10 points on most settings. BLaIR (language-to-item shared embedding) and Query-SeqRec (request-augmented sequence) are consistently the weakest, with BLaIR showing particular weakness on MRR metrics (e.g., 0.287 on ReDial vs. CRAMER's 0.413). Notably, CRAMER achieves these gains with only +0.018s inference overhead versus REARANK's +9.256s (over 500× faster), making it the only method that combines top accuracy with near-zero additional latency.

## Relation to Prior Work

CRAMER draws on two distinct lineages: (1) model control theory (Li & Rush, 2020; Li et al., 2022), which treats a deployed model's parameters as a controllable system steered by external signals, and (2) structured sparsity and gating methods (Kool et al., 2019 for Gumbel-Top-k; Svirsky et al., 2024 for row-column gates), which provide the mechanism for lightweight parameter modulation. The paper departs from prior request-aware recommendation work (Query-SeqRec, BLaIR, LLM-ESR, REARANK) by rejecting both input-level conditioning (shallow fusion of request embeddings) and output-level reranking (heavyweight LLM inference) in favor of posterior control: directly editing frozen backbone parameters via learned masks at inference time. This positions CRAMER as the first to apply parameter-level masking as a request-conditioned control signal in sequential recommendation, bridging the gap between parameter-efficient adaptation (LoRA, adapters) and controllable generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CRAMER offers a practically compelling solution to a real deployment problem: adapting a frozen, production sequential recommender to a user's immediate natural-language request without retraining or invoking an LLM. The ~500× runtime advantage over REARANK while simultaneously achieving higher accuracy makes it a strong candidate for real-time recommendation services. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to multi-turn conversational settings, how it behaves when the request contradicts long-term preferences more severely, and whether the variational surrogate could be tightened into a true ELBO without sacrificing the hard k-hot constraint. The paper also leaves cross-domain transfer (applying masks learned in one domain to another) as a stated but under-explored direction.
