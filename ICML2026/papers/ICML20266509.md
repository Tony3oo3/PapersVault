## Problem

The dominant AI research paradigm treats the environment as exogenous and stationary, training agents against fixed distributions and frozen reward models. This paper argues that deploying capable AI among other adaptive agents induces endogenous non-stationarity that invalidates training assumptions, creating a structural train-test-deploy gap that capability scaling cannot close. The central question is whether a superintelligence built under these solipsistic commitments can be cooperative, and the answer is no: cooperation is an equilibrium-selection process, not a task to be solved by a unilateral optimizer.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the analytical input is the standard AI development pipeline (pretraining on static corpora, RLHF against frozen reward models, benchmark optimization) and the formal output is a set of definitions and a proof characterizing why this pipeline is structurally incapable of producing cooperative deployment. The formal contributions include definitions of endogenous non-stationarity, the train-test-deploy gap, and the self-undermining property, plus a proposition giving sufficient conditions under which training and deployment gradients point in opposite directions. The proposed research output is a non-solipsistic paradigm organized around dynamic evaluation with adaptive counterparties, institutions as co-evolving design primitives, and preservation of human agency.

## Method

The paper formalizes the transition from MDPs to Markov games at deployment, showing that policy-dependent transition dynamics (P_π ≠ P) break the exogeneity assumption underlying standard RL and supervised learning. It proves a self-undermining property: under differentiable best-response assumptions and harmful adaptations by other actors, the gradient of training performance and deployment performance point in opposite directions, so more aggressive exploitation of historical patterns accelerates their obsolescence. The paper then argues that prediction cannot substitute for participation on two independent grounds: epistemic horizons (novelty, reflexivity, combinatorial explosion) foreclose anticipation of novel equilibria, and legitimacy constraints (legal order, value pluralism, preference endogeneity, Goodhart dynamics) render unilateral solutions inadmissible even where prediction might succeed. Finally, it proposes a non-solipsistic research agenda with three concrete directions: dynamic evaluation where test distributions depend on the policy being evaluated, treating institutions as co-evolving design objects that restructure incentives, and preserving human agency as a structural feature rather than a nuisance parameter to be controlled for.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments or results tables. Its core contributions are three formal definitions (endogenous non-stationarity, the train-test-deploy gap, and the self-undermining property) and a proof sketch (Proposition A.4) showing that under best-response adaptation by other agents, steepest-ascent in training performance can decrease deployment performance. The paper further formalizes equilibrium selection risk (Definition A.8) in Markov games and identifies three structured channels of adaptation (behavioral, institutional, algorithmic) through which the train-test-deploy gap arises. The central thesis is qualitative: a solipsistic superintelligence is unlikely to be cooperative because unilateral optimization is self-undermining in multi-actor environments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not benchmark a method against baselines. Instead, it contrasts the dominant solipsistic paradigm (exogenous environment, stationary distribution, singleton framing) with a proposed non-solipsistic design principle (adaptive counterparties, institutions as design primitives, preserved human agency). Section 6 addresses three alternative views: (1) that multi-actor designs have worse failure modes than a single aligned optimizer, (2) that competitive pressure naturally selects for cooperation, and (3) that the empirical track record does not yet support alarm. The paper rebuts each by arguing that the relevant comparison is not monolithic-vs-ecosystem but design-that-acknowledges-interdependence-vs-design-that-defers-it, that selection for cooperation requires conditions (repeated interaction, identifiable partners, manageable externalities) that AI deployment systematically violates, and that existing evidence (algorithmic collusion, Flash Crash, alignment faking, recommender-driven polarization) already documents the predicted failure modes at current capability levels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes and repositions ideas from performative prediction (Perdomo et al., 2020), multi-agent reinforcement learning (Leibo et al., 2019, 2021; Melting Pot, Concordia), game theory (Markov games, Shapley 1953; Littman 1994), and institutional economics (Ostrom 1990; mechanism design) into a unified argument that the dominant solipsistic AI paradigm—treating the environment as exogenous, stationary, and singleton—is a category error for deployed systems. It explicitly departs from the alignment research program (Ngo et al., 2024; Bostrom 2012) by arguing that correct objective specification is necessary but insufficient when multiple adaptive actors interact, and extends performative prediction from one-shot prediction to sequential multi-actor decision-making with equilibrium analysis. The formalization of the 'train-test-deploy gap' and 'self-undermining property' (with sufficient conditions in Proposition A.4) provides new mathematical structure to what has been discussed qualitatively in the multi-agent safety literature (Hammond et al., 2025; Dafoe et al., 2020).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes the central AI safety question from 'getting the objective right' to 'selecting beneficial equilibria in coupled multi-actor systems,' arguing that this shift is structural rather than incremental and unlikely to be resolved by scaling. It identifies concrete open questions: how to design valid dynamic evaluations with adaptive counterparties that select the same equilibria as deployment, how to operationalize institutions as co-learned design primitives within current training pipelines, and how to measure and preserve human agency as a structural feature rather than a nuisance parameter. A key limitation is that, as a position paper, it offers no empirical validation of the proposed evaluation framework or institutional design approaches, leaving the practical feasibility of 'non-solipsistic' pipelines largely speculative. The argument that current evaluation methods (static benchmarks, scripted multi-agent testbeds) systematically underestimate deployment risk is well-motivated but would benefit from quantitative demonstrations of the train-test-deploy gap in realistic settings.
