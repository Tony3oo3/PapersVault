## Problem

Existing GNN benchmarks either conflate structural and feature understanding or rely on combinatorial tasks (SAT, TSP, pattern matching) that cannot adapt to diverse real-world graph topologies. There is no benchmark that isolates purely structural reasoning, works on arbitrary graph datasets without modifying topology, and offers controllable difficulty levels for statistically meaningful model ranking within the same Weisfeiler-Lehman expressivity class.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262265/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes pairs of unlabeled graphs represented solely by their adjacency matrices (all node features set to a constant), where the second graph is a noisy copy of the first generated by adding and/or removing edges at a controlled noise level η (4%–30%). For large graphs (>10k nodes), BFS subgraph sampling first extracts smaller subgraphs preserving local n-hop topology. The siamese GNN outputs a similarity matrix Σ ∈ R^{N×N}; the alignment permutation is extracted at inference via the Hungarian algorithm (O(N³) Linear Assignment Problem), and the intermediate node embeddings X ∈ R^{N×d} are repurposed as Graph Alignment Positional Encodings (GAPE) for downstream transformer-based tasks.

## Method

A siamese architecture processes each graph in a pair through an identical equivariant GNN, producing node embeddings whose outer product yields the similarity matrix Σ = X X̃ᵀ. Training uses a binary cross-entropy loss between row-wise softmax(Σ) and the planted optimal permutation, which is provably invariant to graph automorphisms (Theorem 3.1) and equivariant under relabeling (Theorem A.1). The key dataset-generation insight is that working on graph *pairs* allows planting a known solution (the identity or a random permutation) without altering the base graph's topology, unlike single-graph combinatorial tasks. The noise level η provides a direct difficulty knob: at an optimal η (dataset-dependent, typically 12–18%), the accuracy gap between architectures is maximized and error bars do not overlap, yielding a robust ranking that generalizes across all noise levels. The learned embeddings (GAPE) are shown to compress the full adjacency matrix with >98% reconstruction accuracy and outperform or match spectral positional encodings (LAP, RWPE, GPSE, SignNet) in transformer-based molecular regression, confirming the task captures fundamental structural information rather than task-specific artifacts.

## Results

On molecular regression tasks, GAPE+RWPE achieves the best MAE on AQSOL (1.069±0.009) and PCQM4Mv2 (0.125±0.004), while GAPE alone outperforms all other positional encodings on both AQSOL (1.085) and PCQM4Mv2 (0.133). On ZINC, GPSE (0.104) edges out GAPE (0.147), though GPSE benefits from a pre-training objective that explicitly predicts cycle count (a component of the ZINC target). For graph reconstruction, a frozen GatedGCN encoder pretrained on the Graph Alignment task enables a Transformer decoder to reconstruct the adjacency matrix with 98.8% accuracy and 94.7% F1 on ZINC. In the alignment benchmark itself, anisotropic GNNs (GAT, GATv2, GatedGCN) consistently outperform isotropic ones (GCN, GIN) at the optimal noise level, e.g., GAT reaches 65.6% vs. GCN's 49.5% on Erdős-Rényi at η=12%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GAPE outperforms all spectral-based positional encodings (LAP, ABS-LAP, R-LAP, SignNet) on both AQSOL and PCQM4Mv2, with the largest margin over LAP (1.085 vs. 1.31 on AQSOL; 0.133 vs. 0.155 on PCQM4Mv2). Against RWPE, GAPE wins on AQSOL (1.085 vs. 1.12) and PCQM4Mv2 (0.133 vs. 0.158) but loses on ZINC (0.147 vs. 0.169). GPSE is the strongest competitor, beating GAPE on ZINC (0.104 vs. 0.147) and narrowly on AQSOL (1.105 vs. 1.085), though the authors note GPSE's ZINC advantage is partly due to its pre-training objective predicting cycle count, which is an explicit term in the ZINC target equation. Combining GAPE with RWPE (GAPE+RWPE) yields the best overall results on AQSOL and PCQM4Mv2, surpassing GPSE on both. In the alignment benchmark (Table F.6), anisotropic architectures GAT and GATv2 achieve the highest accuracy on Erdős-Rényi (65.6% at η=12%), outperforming isotropic GCN (49.5%) and GIN (54.8%) by 11–16 percentage points, while GatedGCN leads on AQSOL (63.3% at η=15%) over GCN (55.7%) and GIN (56.8%). The training-free Laplacian PE baseline performs substantially worse than all learned GNNs across all datasets and noise levels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the siamese neural network approach of Nowak et al. (2017) for solving the Graph Alignment Problem, which was restricted to Erdős-Rényi random graphs, to arbitrary graph topologies drawn from real-world datasets (molecular, citation, social). It departs from existing combinatorial-optimization GNN benchmarks (PATTERN, CLUSTER, CSL, G4satbench, TSP) that are locked to specific graph families (e.g., SBM) or lack controllable difficulty, by introducing a noising procedure that generates a difficulty spectrum from any base dataset. The GAPE positional encodings connect to the spectral and random-walk PE literature (LAP, RWPE, GPSE, SignNet) but are obtained through self-supervised pre-training on the alignment task rather than closed-form graph operators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides the GNN community with a topology-agnostic, difficulty-tunable evaluation protocol that isolates structural understanding from feature processing, addressing a gap the authors identify in existing benchmarks. The finding that anisotropic architectures (GatedGCN, GAT, GATv2) consistently outperform isotropic ones (GCN, GIN) on a pure-structure task clarifies a previously ambiguous empirical observation. Open questions include whether more expressive (beyond 1-WL) architectures can further improve alignment accuracy and whether GNNs can match dedicated combinatorial optimization solvers on non-correlated instances. The 98% adjacency-matrix reconstruction from frozen GAPE embeddings and the strong negative correlation (r < −0.90) with downstream molecular regression MAE suggest the benchmark captures genuinely transferable structural knowledge.
