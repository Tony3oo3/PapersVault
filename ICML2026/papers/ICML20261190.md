## Problem

Classical high-confidence change-point detection methods fail catastrophically in multiple change point environments due to endogenous confounding: when a shift is missed, outdated samples contaminate the reference distribution, progressively degrading detection power for subsequent changes. Existing extensions to multiple changes rely on restrictive detectability assumptions (minimum spacing or jump size), and no minimax regret analysis exists for the fully non-parametric, assumption-free setting where missed detections are unavoidable.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a stream of scalar (or vector) sub-Gaussian observations X_t sequentially, requiring only the variance proxy σ and a global error budget α as hyperparameters — no knowledge of the horizon T or number of changes S is needed. At each step it outputs a point prediction μ̂_t (the running average since the last restart) and a binary alarm signal that triggers a full reset of both the estimator and the detection statistic. The detection statistic is a scan over all O(t−r) candidate split points within the current block, making the per-step cost linear in block length (reducible to O(log(t−r)) via a multiscale grid).

## Method

ATC (Anytime Tracking CUSUM) generalizes the classical CUSUM procedure to a fully online, multi-change setting with unknown pre- and post-change means. At each time t, it computes a GLR-inspired two-sample mean-difference statistic D̂_{k,t}^r for every candidate split k since the last restart r, and takes the maximum (scan statistic C_t^r) to form a single detection signal. The alarm is triggered when C_t^r exceeds a time-varying threshold γ_t^r = √(6 log(t−r) + 2 log(1/α_r) + 2 log(π²/3)), where the summable allocation α_r = 6α/(π²r²) ensures uniform false-alarm control across all possible restart times. The key design principle is selective detection: shifts whose magnitude falls below the statistical resolution of the current reference estimate are intentionally left undetected, trading a small, controlled bias for stability. The central theoretical contribution is showing (Proposition 3.1) that the SNR degradation caused by missed detections grows at most logarithmically, which enables a regret upper bound of O(σ²(S+1) log T) that matches a novel information-theoretic lower bound up to a log(S) factor, establishing near-minimax optimality without any detectability assumptions.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution is a nearly minimax-optimal regret bound: ATC achieves dynamic regret O(σ²(S+1)log T) (Theorem 4.1), matching a novel information-theoretic lower bound of Ω(σ²(S+1)log(T/(S+1))) (Theorem 4.2) up to a log(S) gap. On the NAB CPU utilization benchmark, ATC achieves the lowest cumulative squared-error regret among all tested methods, with particularly large advantages after abrupt shifts (e.g., the large jump near the end of the series). Synthetic experiments with S=5 changes confirm the predicted linear-in-log(T) regret scaling over 5000 Monte Carlo runs, and the computationally efficient multiscale-grid variant preserves the same asymptotic scaling up to a constant offset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ATC against three classes of baselines. On the NAB real-world data, ATC outperforms both a fixed sliding-window estimator (W=30) and an exponentially weighted discounted-mean estimator (ρ=0.98), achieving the lowest cumulative regret by explicitly detecting change points and restarting, thereby preventing old-regime samples from biasing the estimate; the baselines adapt more slowly and incur large regret spikes after shifts. In the anytime setting (Appendix A.1.4), ATC is compared against constant-threshold CUSUM detectors (e.g., γ=4.81σ): while the aggressive constant threshold achieves lower regret at small horizons due to faster detection, its false-alarm rate grows steadily with T, eventually causing excessive restarts and information loss that make its regret surpass ATC's; ATC maintains a near-zero false-alarm rate uniformly over the horizon by adaptively raising its threshold. The computationally efficient variant (restricting split points to a geometric grid with b=2) matches the full ATC's asymptotic regret scaling but with a constant-factor offset, confirming that the approximation preserves the logarithmic dependence on T.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical CUSUM framework (Lorden, 1971) and the GLR-based sequential detection of unknown means (Lai & Xing, 2010) to a fully online, multiple change-point setting with unknown pre- and post-change distributions. It deliberately departs from the dominant 'high-confidence detection' paradigm in the change-point literature, which requires detectability assumptions (minimum jump size, minimum spacing) that the authors show lead to catastrophic failure under multiple changes. The key conceptual departure is reframing the problem as minimax dynamic regret rather than detection delay, which naturally accommodates missed detections and the resulting endogenous confounding—a phenomenon the authors identify as absent from prior single-change or assumption-heavy multi-change analyses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of endogenous confounding as a fundamental, cascading obstacle in multi-change-point learning is a genuinely new insight that explains why naive extensions of single-change methods fail. The near-minimax optimality of ATC (matching a novel information-theoretic lower bound up to a log(S) gap) provides a clean characterization of the fundamental cost of non-stationarity. Open questions include closing the log(S) gap (possibly with knowledge of T and S), extending to unknown variance, higher-dimensional and regression settings, and gradually changing environments. For practitioners, the algorithm is horizon-free and requires only a variance proxy, making it applicable to real-time tracking in ride-hailing, resource allocation, and data compression, though sensitivity to variance misspecification remains a practical limitation.
