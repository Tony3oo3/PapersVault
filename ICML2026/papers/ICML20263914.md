## Problem

Existing PPI prediction models (D-SCRIPT, Topsy-Turvy) output interaction probability scores that place true novel partners deep in ranked lists, making them impractical for guiding costly experimental validation. The paper introduces PPI candidate ranking as a new problem: given a target protein and its known interactors, prioritize novel candidates most likely to interact with it, and demonstrates that raw probability scores are suboptimal for this prospective prioritization task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263914/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a target protein p, its experimentally supported known partners from STRING v11, and the full non-redundant human proteome (50–800 residues, CD-HIT 40% clustered) as the candidate pool. It produces a two-stage ranked list: first a cosine-similarity ranking over all candidates using contact-map-activated embedding regions, then a refined top-10 re-ranking incorporating interaction scores, pDockQ structural plausibility, TF-IDF/Jaccard annotation overlap, and LLM semantic similarity. The evaluation setting is temporal: known interactions come from STRING v11 while ground-truth novel partners are those newly appearing in v12.

## Method

The core mechanism is interpretability-guided retrieval: for each known partner p_k, the predicted contact map C(p, p_k) is used to identify the most active contiguous residue region I_k (highest average activation score), and the rank score for candidate p_c is the maximum cosine similarity between the flattened embeddings of p_k's active region and sliding windows of equal length over p_c's residue-level embeddings, maximized over all known partners. This focuses similarity on structurally informative sub-regions rather than whole-sequence vectors, which is the key insight driving the two-orders-of-magnitude improvement in early-ranking metrics. A re-ranking module then refines the top-10 candidates using four complementary signals: D-SCRIPT's supervised interaction score, SpeedPPI/FoldDock pDockQ structural quality, lightweight TF-IDF and Jaccard similarities over UniProt functional annotations (GO terms, domains, pathways, localization), and a PubMedBERT cross-encoder fine-tuned on v11 interaction pairs with protein-level GroupKFold splitting to prevent leakage. The cross-encoder outputs per-candidate logits normalized by within-protein softmax, capturing contextual functional semantics beyond token overlap.

## Results

On the STRING v11→v12 prospective retrieval task, the interpretability-guided method using D-SCRIPT embeddings achieves Recall@10 of 0.2641 and MRR of 0.1685, compared to 0.0124 and 0.0340 for D-SCRIPT's raw interaction probability (a ~21× and ~5× improvement, respectively). With Topsy-Turvy embeddings, Recall@10 reaches 0.1106 and MRR 0.0925 versus 0.0117 and 0.0256 for the probability baseline. On the external PiNUI benchmark, the method achieves a Rediscovery Ratio of 0.3849 versus 0.0080 for D-SCRIPT probability ranking, and consistently outperforms the baseline at all cutoffs k > 5 (e.g., Recall@500: 0.1334 vs. 0.0613).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against D-SCRIPT's raw interaction probability, the interpretability-guided method achieves a ~21× improvement in Recall@10 (0.2641 vs. 0.0124), a ~5× gain in MRR (0.1685 vs. 0.0340), and halves the Average Rank (239.77 vs. 482.86), placing true novel partners into experimentally actionable top-10 positions. Against Topsy-Turvy's probability, the gains are somewhat smaller but still substantial: Recall@10 improves ~9× (0.1106 vs. 0.0117) and MRR ~3.6× (0.0925 vs. 0.0256), with Average Rank dropping from 570.52 to 235.56. xCAPT5's probability ranking, while showing the strongest early precision among baselines (Precision@5 = 0.1943), suffers from the worst Average Rank (900.11) and lowest MRR (0.0315), indicating its confident predictions do not generalize to novel interactions; the proposed D-SCRIPT-based method surpasses it on Recall@10 (0.2641 vs. 0.0747) and MRR (0.1685 vs. 0.0315). On the external PiNUI benchmark, the method achieves a Rediscovery Ratio of 0.3849 versus 0.0080 for D-SCRIPT probability, and outperforms the baseline at every cutoff k > 5 (e.g., Recall@500: 0.1334 vs. 0.0613; MAP@500: 0.1334 vs. 0.0093), confirming that the gains are not an artifact of STRING-specific database structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the intermediate representations of D-SCRIPT and Topsy-Turvy—specifically their predicted contact maps and residue-level embeddings—as a retrieval mechanism, rather than relying on their final scalar interaction probabilities. The conceptual departure is treating interpretability (contact-map activation regions) as a methodological device for ranking novel candidates against known partners, rather than as a post-hoc explanation tool. The re-ranking stage then integrates orthogonal signals from structure prediction (SpeedPPI/pDockQ), curated functional annotations (GO, domains, pathways via TF-IDF and Jaccard), and biomedical LLMs (PubMedBERT cross-encoder, BioBERT, BioMedRoBERTa), positioning the work at the intersection of sequence-based PPI prediction, structural modeling, and biomedical NLP.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework directly addresses the experimental validation bottleneck in interactome mapping by producing candidate lists where true novel partners appear at screening-relevant ranks (top-10 hit rate ~13% vs. <1% for probability baselines), potentially reducing the cost and time of in vitro confirmation. Key limitations include dependence on having a sufficient set of known partners (performance degrades for poorly characterized proteins), very high retrieval compute (hundreds of GPU-hours), and the fact that the 'interpretability' is methodological rather than explanatory—embeddings remain opaque. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-protein complexes, condition-specific interactions, and non-human organisms, and whether the large STRING-specific gains (Rediscovery Ratio ~0.92) will hold on truly prospective, non-database-update discovery scenarios.
