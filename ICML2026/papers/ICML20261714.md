## Problem

Existing fully-dynamic k-center with outliers algorithms rely on radius guessing to maintain cluster representations, introducing an explicit O(log Δ) dependence on the aspect ratio in both update and query times. This dependence becomes prohibitive when Δ is large (e.g., arbitrarily large in natural inputs from mixture models), and maintaining the required distance bounds under dynamic updates is itself nontrivial. The paper asks whether one can achieve constant-factor approximation with (1+ε)z outliers discarded while completely eliminating the aspect-ratio dependence.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a dynamic stream of point insertions and deletions in a general metric space (with O(1) oracle distance queries), along with parameters k (number of clusters) and z (outlier budget). No prior knowledge of pairwise distance bounds or the aspect ratio is required. Upon a query, it returns a center set C ⊆ P of size at most k and implicitly discards at most (1+ε)z points as outliers, minimizing the maximum assignment distance of the remaining inliers. The amortized update and query times are both Õ(k²/ε⁴), independent of both the data size n and the aspect ratio Δ.

## Method

The core contribution is a layered-sampling framework that replaces radius guessing with adaptive greedy coverage. The data structure maintains h layers divided into three types: type-1 layers repeatedly sample Θ(max{k, log n}) representatives from the uncovered set and cover a fixed fraction α of remaining points (shrinking the uncovered set geometrically until O(z) points remain); a type-2 transition layer samples Θ(k log k / ε) representatives to capture all large optimal clusters within radius 2L*; and type-3 layers perform fine-grained peeling, incorporating the nearest εz/2 points to the transition representatives in small batches to recover missed inliers. An update-delay rule with per-layer counters triggers reconstruction only after a τ-fraction of the uncovered set has changed, amortizing rebuild cost over many updates. For queries, a greedy merging step enforces a separation property on the weighted summary, after which either a binary search over pairwise representative distances identifies an approximate optimal radius, or the top-k highest-weight representatives directly yield a feasible solution—both avoiding any Δ-dependent radius enumeration.

## Results

The paper's main result (Theorem 1.1) achieves amortized update time $\tilde{O}(k^2/\epsilon^4)$ and query time $\tilde{O}(k^2/\epsilon^4)$ for the fully-dynamic $(k,z)$-center problem in general metric spaces, with an $O(1)$-approximation and $(1+\epsilon)z$ outliers discarded — all independent of the aspect ratio $\Delta$. Under the additional assumption that each optimal cluster has size $\Omega(z)$, a faster algorithm (Theorem 1.2) reduces both update and query time to $\tilde{O}(k^2/z)$, which is matched by a lower bound of $\Omega(k^2/z)$ (Theorem 1.3) in the general metric space query model. The static version of the layered-sampling algorithm (Theorem 3.1) runs in $\tilde{O}(nk/\epsilon^2 + k^2/\epsilon^4)$ time with a $24(1+\epsilon)$-approximation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to Chan et al. (2022), the proposed method eliminates the $\log\Delta$ factor from both update and query times, improves the approximation from $14+\epsilon$ to $O(1)$, and reduces the polynomial dependence on $k$ from $k^2/\epsilon^3$ to $k^2/\epsilon^4$ (a modest $\epsilon$-exponent trade-off). Against Biabani et al. (2024), the key win is removing the $\log\Delta$ dependence and reducing the update-time polynomial in $k$ from $k^6$ to $k^2$, while the query time is comparable ($\tilde{O}(k^2/\epsilon^4)$ vs. $O(k)$); the approximation improves from $4+\epsilon$ to $O(1)$. The three doubling-metric baselines (Pellizzoni et al. 2023, De Berg et al. 2023, Biabani et al. 2023) achieve tighter approximations ($2+\epsilon$ or $3+\epsilon$) and discard exactly $z$ outliers, but their running times carry an exponential dependence on the doubling dimension $\mathrm{ddim}$ and still depend on $\Delta$, making them inapplicable to general metric spaces. The paper's contribution is thus the first fully-dynamic $(k,z)$-center algorithm in general metric spaces with both update and query times fully independent of $\Delta$, at the cost of a slightly looser constant-factor approximation and the bi-criteria outlier budget of $(1+\epsilon)z$.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the fully-dynamic (k,z)-center problem formulated by Chan et al. (2022) and improved by Biabani et al. (2024), both of which rely on radius-guessing strategies that introduce an explicit O(log Δ) dependence on the aspect ratio in update and query times. The key departure is a layered-sampling framework where each layer adaptively determines its own covering radius through greedy fraction-based coverage, eliminating the need for prior distance bounds or radius enumeration. The stage-wise construction (greedy coverage → transition layer → residual peeling) is a novel mechanism specifically designed to control the outlier budget at (1+ε)z, addressing a gap left by prior sampling-based approaches (e.g., Chen et al., 2018) that could discard far more outliers. The update-delay rule for amortized sub-linear update time follows the standard dynamic data structure paradigm but is tailored to the layered structure's reconstruction triggers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first fully-dynamic (k,z)-center algorithm achieving both update and query times independent of the aspect ratio Δ, which is significant because Δ can be arbitrarily large in practice (e.g., high-precision sensing, near-duplicate points), making prior log Δ-dependent algorithms impractical. The matching lower bound of Ω(k²/z) under mild cluster-size assumptions establishes near-optimality in the general metric query model. However, the trade-off is a larger approximation constant (24(1+ε) vs. 4+ε or 3+ε in prior work), and the paper acknowledges that closing this gap is an open problem. Future directions include extending the framework to MPC settings (where the ball-division step is the main parallelization obstacle) and to k-median/k-means objectives, which would require substantially new analysis.
