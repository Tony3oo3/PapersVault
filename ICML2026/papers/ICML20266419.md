## Problem

The term 'AI alignment' is used across the ML literature to refer to at least three distinct high-level ideals—task reliability, social judiciousness, and takeover avoidance—each with different answers to what is being aligned and to what. This polysemy obscures the fact that interventions promoting alignment under one conception can be actively counterproductive from the perspective of others, yet the field lacks a framework for identifying and navigating these tensions.

## Input / Output

The paper takes as input the existing ML literature's various definitions, research programs, and threat-models associated with 'AI alignment' and produces a structured taxonomy of three alignment ideals cross-referenced along two analytical axes (threat-model type and positive/negative evaluation scope), culminating in five concrete recommendations for research practice. No empirical data, model training, or benchmark evaluation is involved; the analysis is entirely conceptual and argumentative.

## Method

The core analytical move is to formalize 'alignment' as a binary relation requiring two answers—what target properties y must be satisfied, and what object x must satisfy them—then show that the three dominant alignment ideals disagree on both questions. The paper introduces two cross-cutting distinctions: (i) whether the underlying threat-model concerns harms from misdirected competence (AI too capable) or harms from incompetence (AI too unreliable), and (ii) whether the evaluation focuses on positive alignment (producing desired properties) or negative alignment (avoiding undesired ones). By mapping each ideal onto these axes, the authors demonstrate concrete tradeoffs—for example, reducing hallucination rates (a Social Judiciousness gain) may increase a model's capacity for deceptive scheming (a Takeover Avoidance concern). The paper closes with five recommendations, including distinguishing policy ideals from scientific scope, using qualified 'proxy alignment' terms in empirical work, and explicitly communicating the diversity of alignment concepts to policymakers and non-technical audiences.

## Results

This is a conceptual position paper with no quantitative experiments or empirical benchmarks. Its primary contribution is a taxonomy of three high-level alignment ideals—Task Reliability, Social Judiciousness, and Takeover Avoidance—each defined by distinct answers to 'what is being aligned' (Q2) and 'aligned to what' (Q1). The paper then introduces two cross-cutting distinctions (Harms from Misdirected Competence vs. Harms from Incompetence; Positive vs. Negative Alignment) to demonstrate that interventions promoting one ideal can be counterproductive for another (e.g., reducing hallucination aids Social Judiciousness but may increase scheming capacity relevant to Takeover Avoidance). Five concrete recommendations for research practice follow from this analysis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare methods against empirical baselines. Instead, it positions its conceptual framework relative to prior taxonomic and sociological analyses. The paper builds on Roytburg & Miller (2025), who found high citation homophily between AI Ethics (Social Judiciousness) and AI Safety (Takeover Avoidance) communities, arguing that their practical suggestions for 'unifying' these literatures are premature without first resolving the conceptual tensions identified here. It also engages Birhane et al. (2022), whose analysis of AIES/FAccT proceedings found insufficient attention to disparate impacts, as evidence of methodological divergence between alignment subfields. The paper explicitly disagrees with the 'good enough convergence' objection (hinted at by Dragan, 2025), arguing that broad complementarity claims require positive empirical evidence not yet present in the literature, and proposes two concrete research questions (RQ1, RQ2) for future empirical investigation of alignment tradeoffs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three largely separate research traditions—task-oriented alignment (InstructGPT, instruction following), social-impact/fairness research (FAccT, AIES), and existential-risk alignment (MIRI, Alignment Forum)—under a unified analytical framework, arguing they constitute competing rather than complementary ideals. It extends the earlier observation by Grietzer & Jha (2024) that 'alignment' is polysemous by providing a specific three-way taxonomy (Task Reliability, Social Judiciousness, Takeover Avoidance) and demonstrating concrete practical tradeoffs between them. The citation-homophily evidence from Roytburg & Miller (2025) is repurposed to support the claim that these are genuinely distinct research communities rather than subfields of one program.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that reducing hallucination (a Social Judiciousness gain) may increase scheming capacity (a Takeover Avoidance risk)—challenges the field's implicit assumption that alignment progress is monolithic, and has direct implications for how conferences structure reviewer pools, how the EU AI Act specifies audit methodologies, and how researchers communicate with policymakers. However, the tradeoffs are argued conceptually rather than demonstrated empirically; the paper itself flags RQ1 and RQ2 as open questions requiring future measurement. A key limitation is that the three-way taxonomy, while useful, may not capture all alignment conceptions (the authors acknowledge Collective and Bidirectional Alignment as potentially distinct), and the practical recommendations (e.g., separate reviewer pools) remain untested.
