## Problem

Standard regression adjustment in randomized experiments uses only pre-treatment covariates to estimate a single average treatment effect, missing the temporal dynamics of how effects appear, persist, or decay over time. Naively conditioning on post-treatment covariates to capture these dynamics introduces post-treatment bias even under randomization. This paper addresses the gap by developing a regression-adjustment framework that exploits evolving post-treatment covariate trajectories without distorting the marginal target estimand.

## Input / Output

The method consumes longitudinal panel data with T time periods (covariates X_t ∈ R^d, binary treatment assignments W_t, scalar outcomes Y_t) under a static regime, along with user-specified learning models for the conditional mean and transition kernel, and a Monte Carlo sample size S for approximating recursive integrals. It outputs the full trajectory of expected potential outcomes μ_{w̄_t}(t) at each time point, from which longitudinal ATEs are computed as contrasts, together with standard errors and confidence intervals via analytical variance estimation or multiplier bootstrap. The output is thus a time-indexed vector of treatment effects rather than a single scalar ATE.

## Method

The core mechanism models covariate dynamics via transition kernels p_{w̄_τ}^{(τ)}(dh_{τ+1}|h̄_τ) that capture how past treatments shape future covariate histories, then performs recursive backward integration to compute iterated conditional expectations Γ_{w̄_t}^{(τ)} that propagate the terminal outcome regression forward along the kernel. An auxiliary correction term A_{w̄_t}^{(t)} captures the stochastic innovations (deviations of realized covariates from their kernel-predicted expectations), extracting new information orthogonal to pre-treatment covariates. The resulting estimator is formulated as a Neyman-orthogonal moment condition, estimated via L-fold cross-fitting with flexible ML nuisance learners (e.g., random forests for the conditional mean, Gaussian MLPs or VARs for the transition kernel), and Monte Carlo integration for the recursive integrals. The paper proves asymptotic normality and shows the estimator attains the semiparametric efficiency bound, with variance reduction guaranteed relative to the unadjusted empirical estimator.

## Results

On synthetic data (n=1000, T=7, 10-dim covariates, 1000 simulations), all dynamic regression-adjusted estimators achieve lower RMSE and shorter average 95% CI lengths than the unadjusted empirical estimator while maintaining nominal 95% coverage. RF-MLP adjustment yields the best performance, with RMSE reduction increasing with sample size (n ∈ {500, 1000, 2000}). On real-world A/B test data from a Japanese streaming platform (6,802 users, 10-day experiment), the proposed method reduces standard errors by approximately 0.4% to 20.4% across the experimental period compared to the empirical estimator.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four regression-adjustment variants (OLS×VAR, OLS×MLP, RF×VAR, RF×MLP) against the unadjusted empirical estimator on both synthetic and real-world data. All adjusted estimators consistently outperform the empirical baseline on RMSE and CI length while preserving nominal coverage. RF-MLP achieves the best overall performance, while OLS-MLP is surprisingly competitive because the lagged data itself carries substantial signal that even a linear model can capture. RF-VAR offers only a subtle improvement over the empirical estimator because the linear VAR transition model fails to capture the complex nonlinear dynamics of the Lorenz-96-based covariate process, causing sampled trajectories to deviate from the true distribution. On the real-world streaming platform data, the RF + ZILN-MLP combination reduces standard errors by up to 20.4% on later days of the experiment, with smaller gains (≈0.4%) on earlier days where less post-treatment history is available.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the double/debiased machine learning (DML) framework of Chernozhukov et al. (2018, 2022) and the standard regression-adjustment literature for RCTs (Lin, 2013) to the longitudinal setting under static treatment regimes. The key conceptual departure is the use of transition kernels to model how covariates evolve over time, combined with recursive forward integration (iterated conditional expectation) to exploit post-treatment histories for variance reduction without introducing the post-treatment bias that plagues naive conditioning (Freedman, 2008a; Montgomery et al., 2018). Unlike dynamic treatment regime literature (Robins, 2004; Lewis & Syrgkanis, 2021) that targets adaptive policies, this work focuses on pre-specified static sequences and establishes the first semiparametric efficiency bound for the full trajectory of marginal treatment effects in this setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a notable gap: while longitudinal treatment effect estimation is well-studied in epidemiology and adaptive experimental design, formal efficiency theory for the full trajectory of marginal effects under static randomized experiments was previously absent. The practical relevance is clear for large-scale A/B testing (e.g., streaming platforms) where experiments run over extended periods and practitioners need to understand when effects appear and how long they persist, not just the overall ATE. Key limitations include the restriction to static regimes (adaptive experiments like contextual bandits are explicitly out of scope), the assumption of perfect compliance, and the computational cost of Monte Carlo integration for long horizons. Open questions include extension to distributional treatment effects, covariate-adaptive randomization, and irregular sampling intervals.
