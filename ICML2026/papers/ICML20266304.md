## Problem

NeRF positional encodings (positional encodings, multi-resolution hash grids) entangle features globally across space, so localized edits propagate undesirably and precise spatial control is impossible without retraining. Point-based latent representations improve locality but rely on Euclidean-distance KNN, causing neighbourhoods to shift inconsistently under deformation and producing unstable interpolation artifacts. The paper identifies the absence of a transformation-aware, deformation-stable space encoding as the fundamental gap in editable NeRFs.

## Input / Output

At inference the method consumes a set of learned anisotropic Gaussian kernels (mean, full 3×3 covariance, and a fixed feature vector per kernel) together with camera position and viewing direction; the multi-resolution hash grid used during training is entirely discarded. For physics-driven editing, a deformed driving mesh updates Gaussian positions, rotations, and scales in real time, and the view direction fed to the MLP is corrected by the inverse rotation of each modified Gaussian. The network outputs per-ray colour and volume density, which are integrated via standard volumetric rendering to produce a novel-view image.

## Method

EKS replaces discrete point or grid-based positional features with a field of anisotropic Gaussian kernels whose latent features are aggregated at query points via Mahalanobis-distance-weighted k-nearest-neighbour interpolation, making the encoding affine-equivariant. Efficient neighbour retrieval is achieved by RT-GPS, which approximates each Gaussian's confidence ellipsoid with a stretched icosahedron and reduces the search to ray–triangle intersection tests, yielding a 14× speedup over naive Mahalanobis KNN. A training-time hash-grid feature distillation step samples multi-resolution grid features at kernel centres so that, after training, the representation is fully grid-free and compact. View-dependent appearance after deformation is restored by tracking each Gaussian's principal axes with the Kabsch algorithm, and a densification/pruning schedule (following 3D Gaussian Splatting) regulates kernel count during training. Editing is performed by exporting Gaussians as tetrahedra, binding them to a mesh for rigid/soft-body/cloth simulation, and feeding the updated parameters back through the same rendering pipeline without retraining.

## Results

On the editing benchmark (Chen et al., 2023), EKS achieves the best PSNR across all 8 NeRF-Synthetic scenes, with an average of ~25.8 dB, outperforming Neuraleditor (~25.3 dB), GaMeS (~24.7 dB), and Naive Plotting (~24.4 dB). EKS also leads on SSIM (avg. ~0.936) and LPIPS (avg. ~0.048), indicating superior perceptual quality after edits. For static reconstruction on NeRF-Synthetic, EKS achieves a mean PSNR of 33.82 dB, competitive with other editable methods (Point-NeRF: 36.13, GaMeS: 34.83) and close to non-editable baselines (LagHash: 33.83). On Mip-NeRF 360, EKS achieves 27.56 dB average PSNR, slightly behind GaMeS (29.66) and Mip-NeRF (29.55).

## Baselines & Comparisons

EKS consistently outperforms all editable baselines on the editing benchmark: it beats Neuraleditor by 0.5–3.0 dB PSNR per scene (e.g., 28.04 vs. 27.14 on Lego, 27.58 vs. 24.83 on Ficus), GaMeS by 0.5–2.9 dB (e.g., 28.23 vs. 27.26 on Hotdog, 27.58 vs. 26.65 on Ficus), and Naive Plotting by 1.0–3.0 dB. On static reconstruction (Table 1), EKS is competitive but not the best among editable methods—Point-NeRF leads on Ficus (36.13 vs. 33.82) and GaMeS on Chair (35.73 vs. 34.72), while EKS matches or exceeds them on Ship (31.10 vs. 30.78/30.97) and Materials (30.08 vs. 29.89/29.61). On Mip-NeRF 360 (Table 2), EKS trails GaMeS by ~1.5–2.5 dB and Mip-NeRF by ~1.5–2.5 dB, suggesting a quality gap on complex real-world scenes. Against non-editable baselines (INGP, LagHash), EKS achieves comparable or better PSNR on most synthetic scenes while retaining editability. Qualitatively, EKS avoids the granularity and hole artifacts seen in point-based methods (Neuraleditor, RIP-NeRF) and the visible Gaussian splatting artifacts in GaMeS, producing smoother surfaces after deformation.

## Relation to Prior Work

EKS builds on the NeRF + multi-resolution hash-grid encoding pipeline (Mildenhall et al., Müller et al.) and departs from point-based editable NeRFs (RIP-NeRF, NeuralEditor, Point-NeRF) by replacing discrete point features with anisotropic Gaussian kernels whose Mahalanobis-distance-based interpolation is affine-equivariant. It also draws on 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al.) for densification/pruning strategies and on RT-kNNS (Nagarajan et al.) for the ray-traced proximity search, extending it to operate in Mahalanobis space. A key conceptual departure from LagHash (Govindarajan et al.), which embeds Gaussians within fixed hash grids, is that EKS is fully grid-free at inference, with a training-time distillation mechanism transferring multi-resolution grid features into the kernel field.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EKS provides a practical path to physics-driven NeRF editing (rigid-body, soft-body, cloth) without retraining, which is valuable for robotics simulation and interactive content creation. The RT-GPS algorithm (~14× speedup over naive Mahalanobis KNN) is a reusable component for any system requiring efficient anisotropic-kernel neighbourhood queries. Open questions include scalability to large-scale scenes, handling of topology changes, and whether the affine-equivariance guarantee degrades gracefully under extreme non-affine deformations beyond the sinusoidal test shown.
