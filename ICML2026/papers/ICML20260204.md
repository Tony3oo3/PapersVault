## Problem

Existing LLM-based preference prediction methods evaluate performance solely at the output level, treating next-token probabilities as the only signal of encoded knowledge. This paper asks whether internal model representations contain systematic, exploitable information about human preferences that is distorted or suppressed during decoding, and whether leveraging this latent structure can improve population-level preference estimates beyond what surface-level outputs provide.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260204/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires white-box access to transformer internals (MLP weight matrices and residual stream activations) and is evaluated across seven model families at 7–14B parameters. Each persona is instantiated through 10 hand-crafted prompt variants to control for phrasing sensitivity, yielding a combinatorial space exceeding 24 million configurations. The output is a multivariate party-level preference distribution per persona attribute category, produced in two parallel forms—latent activation-based and next-token probability-based—each directly comparable to weighted survey benchmarks via Jensen-Shannon distance (nominal attributes) or first Wasserstein distance (ordinal attributes).

## Method

The core mechanism exploits the MLP decomposition into static value vectors (fixed directions in representation space) and input-dependent scaling coefficients (activation strengths determined by key–residual inner products). First, linear probes are trained on mean-pooled residual streams in intermediate layers (0.5L–0.9L) to identify party-associated directions; MLP value vectors whose cosine similarity with the probe exceeds a 2.5-IQR fence are retained only after a sign-inversion necessity test confirms they causally promote the corresponding party token. Second, for each persona prompt, the activation of these selected value vectors is measured and weighted by probe alignment to produce a per-persona, per-party activation score. Third, these scores are aggregated via Monte Carlo across personas sampled to match real-world marginal distributions, yielding attribute-level party distributions. A key practical contribution is identifying attribute-level output entropy as a simple gating criterion: mechanistic forecasting provides the largest gains precisely when surface probabilities are diffuse (normalized entropy > 0.85), particularly for demographic attributes and instruction-tuned models.

## Results

Party probes achieve F1 scores consistently exceeding 96% on a 10% held-out split, with the value probe reaching 0.99±0.01 macro-F1 versus 0.93±0.05 for regression baselines (p<0.001). Across 24 million persona configurations, 7 model families, and 6 national elections, mechanistic forecasting (latent activation aggregation) outperforms probability-based estimation in a majority of attribute-level comparisons, with the largest and most consistent gains for demographic attributes (age, education, employment) in the US and UK. An entropy-gating criterion (normalized entropy > 0.85) identifies the high-entropy regime where latent estimators yield the most reliable improvements over surface-level probability estimates, particularly for instruction-tuned models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between mechanistic forecasting (latent value-vector activations) and probability-based estimation (next-token output probabilities), both evaluated against weighted real-world survey distributions using Jensen-Shannon or Wasserstein distance. Mechanistic forecasting achieves higher win-rates across most model–country pairs, with gains amplified for base (non-instruction-tuned) models and for demographic attributes; however, opinion-based attributes show more variable cross-national performance. Against an in-context learning (ICL) baseline that receives the same party-position data through the prompt, mechanistic forecasting still outperforms for most parties and models, confirming that gains stem from latent structure rather than mere access to external position data. Control conditions (label-shuffled probes at macro-F1 ≈ 0.33 and random-weight probes at ≈ 0.24) perform near chance, while regression baselines on the same activations (0.93±0.05) remain consistently below the value probe (0.99±0.01), indicating the probe captures a more discriminative, task-aligned direction than a generic linear readout.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: LLM-based survey simulation (Argyle et al., 2023; Bisbee et al., 2023; von der Heyde et al., 2024), which treats model outputs as the sole signal for preference prediction, and mechanistic interpretability (Elhage et al., 2021; Geva et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2024), which provides tools for reading internal representations. The hidden-knowledge literature (Gekhman et al., 2025; Orgad et al., 2025) demonstrated that LLMs encode more information internally than they surface in outputs, but focused on binary factual correctness. This paper departs from both threads by applying MLP value-vector decomposition and linear probing to extract distributional, multivariate preference signals from latent activations, and by framing the social-simulation task as a representation-aware estimation problem rather than a prompting problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, reproducible pipeline (probe training → value-vector selection via sign-inversion → Monte Carlo aggregation) that practitioners can apply to any white-box LLM for preference prediction, along with a simple entropy-based gating criterion for when to prefer latent over output-based estimates. Key limitations include the white-box requirement, the local first-order approximation of MLP contributions, and the use of per-party binary probes rather than a joint multiclass head. Open questions include whether the method generalizes beyond elections to other structured preference domains (consumer choice, policy attitudes), how to handle the lack of a principled framework for prompt-variation design, and whether the observed patterns hold for closed-source models where latent access is unavailable.
