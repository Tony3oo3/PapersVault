## Problem

Spectral gradient methods like Muon empirically outperform standard gradient descent in large-scale training, yet the mechanism behind this advantage under anisotropic data remains unexplained. This paper identifies a specific failure mode of GD: when the data covariance has a dominant variance direction orthogonal to the signal, GD multiplicatively amplifies the uninformative spike, degrading alignment and forcing smaller learning rates. The paper asks whether and how spectral gradient descent avoids this anisotropy-driven misalignment.

## Input / Output

The model takes anisotropic Gaussian inputs x ~ N(0, Q) with spiked covariance Q = I_d + λvv^T (spike direction v orthogonal to the signal w_*), and produces scalar quadratic predictions x^T M x via a one-hidden-layer network with squared activation. The learned object is a PSD matrix M = WW^T of rank at most m, initialized on the Stiefel manifold at scale θ^2 = (log d)^{-1}/d. The dynamics reduce exactly to a three-dimensional invariant manifold tracking the signal coefficient a_k, spike coefficient b_k, and isotropic bulk coefficient c_k, making the full d-dimensional problem analytically tractable.

## Method

The paper derives exact reduced ODEs for both SpecGD and GD on the 3D invariant manifold, revealing a qualitative mechanistic difference: SpecGD's polar decomposition yields a sign-based (scale-invariant) update in an adaptive basis, so all three coefficients grow at identical rates during Stage I, while GD's multiplicative update causes the spike coefficient to grow exponentially faster with rate proportional to (1+λ)^2. This spike dominance in GD forces a smaller learning rate and delays the alignment stage (Stage II) by O(log d) steps, whereas SpecGD transitions in O(1) time independent of dimension. The discrete-time analysis handles O(η²) quadratic learning-rate effects and non-smooth sign-change oscillations via explicit one-step bounds and discrete trapping arguments. Numerical experiments confirm the theory and extend it to power-law covariance spectra and a two-layer ReLU network on MNIST with a rank-one anisotropic perturbation.

## Results

The main theoretical results establish that SpecGD achieves a dimension-independent Stage I transition time T₁ = O(1) with all three coefficients (signal, spike, bulk) growing at identical rates, while GD requires T̃₁ = O((η̃λ)⁻¹ log d) and suffers spike-dominated growth where b̃ₖ grows geometrically faster than ãₖ and c̃ₖ. In Stage II, SpecGD reaches alignment Align(k) = 1 − o(1) in O(η⁻¹) steps, whereas GD needs O(η̃⁻¹ log d) steps and exhibits a period of alignment degradation during its longer Stage I. Empirically, under a power-law covariance with the signal in the tail, SpecGD progressively aligns with the signal while GD remains nearly orthogonal throughout training; on MNIST with a rank-one anisotropic perturbation, SpecGD is more stable than GD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is standard gradient descent (GD), and SpecGD wins on every metric examined. In the theoretical spiked-covariance model, SpecGD's Stage I transition time is O(1) independent of dimension, while GD's is O((η̃λ)⁻¹ log d), meaning GD's escape phase slows logarithmically with dimension and inversely with spike strength λ. During GD's Stage I, the spurious spike coefficient b̃ₖ grows geometrically faster than the signal and bulk coefficients (max{ãₖ/b̃ₖ, c̃ₖ/b̃ₖ} = O((1−δ)ᵏ)), causing alignment to actively degrade before recovering in Stage II; SpecGD avoids this entirely by growing all coefficients at the same rate. Empirically, under a power-law covariance where the signal lies in the low-variance tail, GD's alignment stays near zero throughout 1000 iterations while SpecGD achieves significantly higher alignment; on MNIST with a rank-one Gaussian spike perturbation, SpecGD remains more stable than GD. The paper also notes that GD fails to align for moderately large learning rates when λ is large (Figure 2), whereas SpecGD remains robust, suggesting SpecGD can tolerate a larger learning rate than O((d+λ)⁻¹/²).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Muon optimizer (Jordan et al., 2024) by providing a mechanistic theoretical analysis of why its spectral (polar-decomposition) update outperforms standard gradient descent under anisotropic data. It extends the phase retrieval / quadratic-model literature (Candès et al., 2015; Tan & Vershynin, 2019; Braun et al., 2026) by introducing a spiked covariance where the spike is orthogonal to the signal, and by analyzing the spectral gradient method on this setting. The paper is directly motivated by Wang et al. (2025a), who showed that in isotropic linear regression Muon reduces to a linear method, implying that anisotropy or nonlinearity is necessary to capture its advantage; this work addresses exactly that gap by combining both. It departs from Ba et al. (2023), who studied spiked covariance with the spike aligned to the signal (accelerating learning), by considering the harder orthogonal-spike regime where the spike is purely uninformative.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous mechanistic explanation for why Muon/SpecGD is robust to anisotropic data: GD multiplicatively amplifies the uninformative spike direction during the early escape stage, degrading signal alignment, while SpecGD's sign-based update in an adaptive basis suppresses this amplification. This clarifies a practical observation (Muon tolerates larger learning rates and achieves faster alignment under anisotropy) with a clean two-stage dynamical picture and quantitative separation in transition times. Limitations include the population-level analysis, the stylized single-spike covariance, and the quadratic (two-layer) model; extending to online noisy settings, power-law spectra, and multi-index models are identified as open directions. For practitioners, the result reinforces that Muon's advantage is not merely a preconditioning effect but a fundamental change in how the optimizer interacts with data geometry.
