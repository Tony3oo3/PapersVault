## Problem

Multi-turn jailbreak attacks exploit a judge-driven feedback loop where an external LLM scores intermediate responses, enabling attackers to iteratively refine prompts toward harmful goals. Existing defenses detect or block unsafe content at individual turns or the final response but leave this refinement loop intact, so attackers still receive informative feedback to adapt subsequent queries. The paper asks whether misaligning the judge's feedback signal—without changing the actual response meaning—can disrupt the attack.

## Input / Output

D-Judge takes the victim LLM's raw response text concatenated with an optimized instruction prompt and produces a single rewritten response that preserves the original semantics while shifting perceived harmfulness. A Semantic Gate based on NLI bidirectional entailment validates each rewrite; if the check fails, the rewriter regenerates up to five times. The rewritten response is returned to the attacker (and thus to the attacker's judge) in place of the original, while the true response is retained for ASR evaluation.

## Method

D-Judge inserts a Qwen3-4B-Instruct rewriter at the API boundary between the victim LLM and the attacker's judge, rewriting each response before it is scored. The rewriter is trained in two stages: first, supervised fine-tuning on 64,902 pairs from a constructed dataset (PKU-SafeRLHF responses with Llama Guard–scored INCREASE/DECREASE variants) that maps lower-unsafe-probability rewrites to higher-unsafe-probability rewrites while preserving semantics; second, Direct Preference Optimization on 44,635 preference pairs that favor semantically equivalent candidates inducing higher unsafe probabilities, learning judge-sensitive surface cues beyond token-level imitation. A Semantic Gate using bidirectional NLI entailment rejects rewrites that deviate from the original meaning. The key mechanism is that by making benign responses appear more harmful to the judge, the attacker's progress condition (score must exceed all prior scores) is violated, derailing iterative prompt refinement across turns.

## Results

On HarmBench with GPT-4o as the victim LLM, D-Judge reduces the average attack success rate (ASR) across five multi-turn jailbreak attacks from 58.3% (no defense) to 8.6%, outperforming the strongest baseline (QwenGuard at 40.4%) by over 31 percentage points. Per-attack, D-Judge achieves ASR of 12.0% (Crescendo), 6.3% (CoA), 2.5% (ActorBreaker), 5.0% (FITD), and 17.0% (X-Teaming). On benign benchmarks, D-Judge preserves MT-Bench average score of 8.34 (vs. 8.99 baseline) and IFEval strict prompt accuracy of 0.77 (matching the 0.77 baseline), indicating minimal safety tax.

## Baselines & Comparisons

D-Judge decisively outperforms all 10 defense baselines on the primary multi-turn ASR metric, achieving 8.6% average ASR versus the next-best QwenGuard at 40.4% and ProAct at 42.4%. Among single-turn guards, QwenGuard (40.4%) is the strongest but still leaves over 40% of attacks succeeding, whereas D-Judge reduces this to under 9%. Multi-turn detection methods (NBF at 50.2%, TCA at 54.2%) and input preprocessing (Backtranslation at 44.6%, SmoothLLM at 55.2%) are all substantially outperformed, confirming that detection-based and perturbation-based approaches cannot match feedback-loop disruption. On benign benchmarks, D-Judge (MT-Bench 8.34, IFEval strict 0.77/0.83) closely matches the unmodified baseline (8.99, 0.77/0.84) and clearly outperforms Aligner (8.21, 0.39/0.49), showing that its rewriting imposes far less capability degradation than prior output-editing methods. Against single-turn score-guided attacks (PAIR, TAP), D-Judge also reduces ASR substantially (64.0→30.0 and 80.2→36.0), and in the appendix it combines additively with Circuit Breakers on Llama-3-8B to push average ASR from 17.0% down to 3.0%.

## Relation to Prior Work

D-Judge departs from the dominant detection-based paradigm for multi-turn jailbreak defense (Llama Guard, NBF, TCA) and from processing-based defenses (Aligner, ProAct, Backtranslation) that transform content to make the victim model refuse or produce safer outputs. Instead, it identifies the judge feedback loop as a distinct intervention point and repurposes output rewriting with a fundamentally different objective: not to make the response safer, but to misalign the judge's harmfulness assessment while preserving semantics. The training pipeline (SFT + DPO on semantically equivalent pairs) borrows the RLHF machinery but applies it to a novel objective of inducing judge-sensitive surface cues, and the dataset construction using Llama Guard as a construction-time signal for relative preferences is new. The paper also positions itself as complementary to internal defenses like Circuit Breakers, showing that the two operate at different layers and combine multiplicatively.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes multi-turn jailbreak defense from 'detect and block harmful content' to 'disrupt the attacker's optimization signal,' which is a conceptually distinct and more proactive strategy that intervenes from the first turn rather than reacting after intent is detected. Key open questions include: (1) whether increasingly robust or rewrite-aware judges will erode the defense, (2) whether attackers can pre-optimize prompts on alternative models to bypass the feedback loop entirely (acknowledged as a limitation), and (3) whether the 4B rewriter's latency overhead (5.4 ms/token, ~1.5–2.5 rewrites per turn) is acceptable at scale. The demonstrated transferability across judges, victims, and score scales, combined with the near-zero safety tax on benign benchmarks, makes this a practically deployable defense that could be adopted at the API boundary without retraining the victim model.
