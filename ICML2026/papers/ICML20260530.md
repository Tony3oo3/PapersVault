## Problem

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) for MLLMs relies on sparse outcome-based supervision, making models prone to reward hacking via spurious reasoning chains. While rubric-based rewards provide finer-grained process supervision, existing methods generate costly instance-specific rubrics and treat all rubrics as equally learnable, producing noisy gradients that hinder efficient convergence. The paper asks whether curriculum learning can be applied to reward design itself—stratifying rubrics by difficulty and scheduling their weights—rather than to data selection.

## Input / Output

The model receives an image-text pair (e.g., a chart, diagram, or scene with a natural-language question) and produces a structured response consisting of a  reasoning block followed by a \boxed{} final answer. Training uses the ViRL-39K dataset (~39K verifiable QA pairs spanning STEM, spatial reasoning, and chart analysis), initialized from Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct. During RL, an LLM judge (Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct) scores each generated response against six retained rubrics, outputting binary applicability and satisfaction signals per rubric.

## Method

RuCL is a two-phase reward-centric curriculum framework. Phase I constructs a reusable pool of 20 generalized rubric candidates via a teacher LLM (Gemini 3 Pro), filters them by applicability rate (retaining only those with η_j ≥ 0.99), and stratifies the survivors into Foundational (R_easy, high pass rate) and Advanced (R_hard, low pass rate) tiers using the model's empirical pass rate as a difficulty proxy justified by the Coefficient of Variation of Bernoulli reward signals. Phase II employs a Stability-Aware Curriculum scheduler: the curriculum weight λ_t starts at 0 (only foundational rubrics active), triggers a sigmoid ramp-up only after the batch-averaged foundational reward exceeds a proficiency threshold (τ_th = 0.9) over a sliding window of w = 20 steps, and then holds at λ_max = 1.0. The final scalar reward combines rule-based answer correctness (weight α = 0.7) with the curriculum-modulated rubric reward, optimized via GRPO. The key theoretical contribution is showing that stratifying by pass rate is statistically equivalent to stratifying by gradient estimator reliability, and that the curriculum schedule minimizes gradient variance in early training by suppressing high-variance hard-rubric signals.

## Results

RuCL achieves a state-of-the-art average accuracy of 60.06% across seven visual reasoning benchmarks, yielding a +7.83% absolute improvement over the Qwen2.5-VL-7B base model (52.23%). The largest individual gains appear on WeMATH (+12.97%, reaching 71.49%), Super-CLEVR Counting (+12.00%, reaching 85.50%), and LogicVista (+10.40%, reaching 49.66%). RuCL outperforms all open-source 7B reasoning baselines, including ThinkLite-VL-7B (57.49%) and VL-Rethinker-7B (56.31%), and narrows the gap with the 32B Qwen2.5-VL model (61.69%) to within 1.63 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RuCL outperforms all seven open-source 7B reasoning baselines on the average score, with the largest margins over R1-Onevision-7B (+8.09%) and OpenVLThinker-7B (+7.52%). Against the two strongest 7B competitors—ThinkLite-VL-7B (57.49%) and VL-Rethinker-7B (56.31%)—RuCL leads by +2.57% and +3.75% respectively, with particularly large gaps on WeMATH (71.49% vs. 65.52% and 68.22%) and LogicVista (49.66% vs. 42.94% and 46.08%). RuCL also surpasses the larger Qwen2.5-VL-32B (61.69%) on MathVerse, MathVision, WeMATH, MMMU, and LogicVista, though it trails on MathVista (74.10 vs. 74.70) and Counting (85.50 vs. 85.36 is a near-tie). Compared to proprietary models, RuCL exceeds GPT-4o on MathVerse, MathVision, MathVista, WeMATH, and LogicVista, but falls short of Claude-3.5-Sonnet on MathVision (28.88 vs. 46.48) and WeMATH (71.49 vs. 73.05). Multi-seed experiments (Appendix E) confirm the gains over ThinkLite-VL-7B and VL-Rethinker-7B are stable (std ≤ 0.14), and scalability tests on Qwen3-VL-4B/8B show consistent +7.8–8.2% improvements, indicating the method is not specific to the 7B backbone.

## Relation to Prior Work

RuCL builds on GRPO as its RL optimization backbone and on the RLVR paradigm of rule-based outcome verification, but departs from both by replacing sparse final-answer rewards with a stratified, rubric-level process reward. It extends prior LLM-as-a-Judge rubric-based reward work (which typically generates instance-specific rubrics and treats all rubrics with fixed weights) by constructing a small set of generalized, reusable rubrics and applying curriculum learning directly to their weighting schedule rather than to training-data ordering. This reframes the classical easy-to-hard curriculum-learning principle from a data-selection problem into a reward-design problem, a conceptual shift not present in prior multimodal RL post-training methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper shows that reward hacking in multimodal RLVR can be mitigated without expensive per-instance rubric generation, and that a simple two-tier (foundational/advanced) rubric split with a stability-triggered sigmoid ramp yields consistent gains across seven benchmarks. Open questions include whether rubric stratification should be re-estimated online as the policy evolves, whether the approach transfers to non-visual domains such as code generation, and whether the reliance on a large LLM judge during training (here Qwen3-VL-235B) can be reduced. A practical limitation is that the curriculum trigger depends on a fixed proficiency threshold and sliding-window size, which may not generalize across all base models or task distributions without retuning.
