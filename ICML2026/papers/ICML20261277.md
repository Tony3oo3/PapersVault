## Problem

Standard RL treats policies as static state-to-action mappings with a fixed compute budget, conflating the number of parameters with the amount of computation performed. There is no formal framework to ask whether a policy with a fixed parameter count can still benefit from additional compute, or to characterize when more compute is necessary for optimal behavior or generalization. This paper formalizes compute-bounded policies and proves that policies with more compute can solve problems and generalize to longer-horizon tasks that are provably out of reach for less-compute policies.

## Input / Output

The architecture takes a state observation vector and a goal vector (and an action for Q-functions) as input, processes them through an initial linear layer and layer normalization, then iteratively applies the same recurrent block N times before producing an action or value estimate. The key design choice is that N (the number of recurrent steps) is the compute knob: the same set of parameters is reused N times, so increasing N increases compute without increasing parameter count. This contrasts with ResNet baselines where depth increases both compute and parameters proportionally.

## Method

The paper introduces the Interpolation Recurrent Unit (IRU), a minimal recurrent block with two linear layers (FORGET and INPUT) that computes the next hidden state as a gated interpolation between the previous state and a new local computation: c_{i+1} = f_i ⊙ c_i + (1 - f_i) ⊙ I_i. The complete IRU-(N) architecture applies this block N times with skip connections from each intermediate state, and the final hidden state is passed through a tanh and linear layer to produce the output. Theoretically, the paper formulates deterministic policies as time-bounded Turing machines and uses the time hierarchy theorem to prove two results: (1) a Policy Hierarchy Theorem showing that for any sub-logarithmic gap in time bounds, there exist MDPs where more-compute policies are arbitrarily better, and (2) a Long Horizon Generalization Theorem showing that less-compute policies can match optimal performance on training tasks yet fail arbitrarily on longer-horizon test tasks. Empirically, the IRU is trained with standard model-free RL (PPO, IQL) across 31 tasks in Boxpick, Lightsout, and OGBench, demonstrating that increasing recurrent steps from 1 to 10 yields up to 8× performance gains and that IRU-(5) outperforms ResNets with 5× more parameters on long-horizon generalization.

## Results

On the Boxpick and Lightsout benchmarks, IRU-(5) achieves substantially higher success rates than both MLP and ResNet baselines. On the most challenging tasks, IRU-(5) reaches 97.4% on boxpick-exact-4 (vs. 58% ResNet, 37% MLP), 93.3% on boxpick-gen-4-1 (vs. 57.7% ResNet, 73.2% MLP), and 71.9% on lightsout-4x5 (vs. 0.2% ResNet, 0.5% MLP). Notably, the ResNet baseline uses approximately 2× more compute and 5× more parameters (3M vs. 500K) than IRU-(5), yet IRU-(5) still outperforms it on the hardest tasks. On OGBench continuous-control tasks, increasing recurrent steps from 1 to 10 improves performance up to 8×, with the largest gains on long-horizon reasoning tasks (scene, cube, puzzle).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is a deep ResNet architecture following Wang et al. (2025b), which uses the same effective depth (and thus similar compute) as IRU-(5) but scales parameters with depth, resulting in ~5× more parameters (3M vs. 500K). IRU-(5) decisively outperforms this ResNet on the hardest tasks: boxpick-exact-4 (97.4% vs. 58%), boxpick-gen-4-1 (93.3% vs. 57.7%), boxpick-gen-4-2 (87.4% vs. 32.8%), and lightsout-4x5 (71.9% vs. 0.2%). The ResNet does marginally outperform IRU-(5) on boxpick-exact-3 at 50% training (89.1% vs. 82.6%), but IRU catches up by 100% (95.5% vs. 93.8%). A standard MLP with similar parameter count to IRU but less compute is outperformed by IRU-(5) on all tasks, confirming that the gains come from additional compute rather than parameter count. The paper also notes that ResNet exhibits higher variance across seeds, suggesting overfitting to training tasks, while IRU training is more stable.

## Relation to Prior Work

The IRU architecture is a stripped-down variant of LSTM/GRU gating mechanisms, positioned within the family of hybrid recurrent networks (Gers & Schmidhuber, 2001) that have been shown to learn algorithmic solutions in supervised learning (Bansal et al., 2022; Schwarzschild et al., 2021). Conceptually, the paper bridges two previously separate literatures: model-free planning architectures that use iterative computation (Guez et al., 2018; Tamar et al., 2016; Farquhar et al., 2018) and computational complexity theory (time hierarchy theorems), applying the latter to formally characterize RL policy classes. The empirical motivation draws on length generalization in language models (Zhou et al., 2023; 2024b) and chain-of-thought reasoning (Wei et al., 2022b), but the paper explicitly departs from these by showing that iterative compute benefits in RL do not require pre-training, multi-task data, or language.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The formal separation of compute time from parameter count in RL is a genuinely new lens: the policy hierarchy theorem and long-horizon generalization theorem provide the first complexity-theoretic guarantees that more compute enables strictly better and more generalizing policies, reframing why recurrent or iterative architectures succeed in RL. The value-of-compute metric offers a practical tool analogous to value-of-information in POMDPs, and the yoked experiment cleanly isolates expressivity (not exploration) as the primary source of compute benefits. Key limitations include the fixed (non-adaptive) number of recurrent steps, the single-tape Turing machine model that may not capture parallelism in modern hardware, and the absence of transformer-based recursive architectures. Open questions include adaptive compute allocation per state, space-complexity analogues, and whether the IRU's benefits transfer to high-dimensional perceptual settings.
