## Problem

Learning stationary policies in infinite-horizon general-sum Markov games remains a fundamental open problem: Nash equilibria are PPAD-complete to compute, and existing convergence guarantees are restricted to zero-sum or cooperative settings. This paper addresses the gap by adopting Risk-averse Quantal Response Equilibria (RQE) from behavioral game theory, which incorporates strategic risk-aversion and bounded rationality to regularize the game structure, making equilibrium computation tractable without assumptions on the underlying game's payoff matrices.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a finite-state, finite-action two-player discounted Markov game (states, actions, deterministic rewards, transition kernel) along with per-agent risk-aversion levels τ_i, bounded-rationality temperatures ε_i, and regularizer choices (KL divergence with log-barrier, or reverse KL with negative entropy). It outputs stationary Markov policies π_i*(·|s) constituting the RQE, paired Q-value functions Q_i*(s,a), and auxiliary adversary policies p_i*(·|s) that encode each agent's worst-case belief about the opponent. In the practical deep-RL variant, policies and Q-functions are parameterized by MLPs with double-Q and target networks, and transitions are consumed from a replay buffer supporting both on- and off-policy sampling.

## Method

The key insight is that introducing two imaginary adversaries (one per player) transforms the original two-player game into a four-player game with a partial zero-sum structure, where the adversary's policy p_i is constrained to stay near the true opponent policy π_{-i} via a KL-type penalty scaled by τ_i. Under a generalized λ-strong monotonicity condition on this four-player game (which depends only on agent behavior parameters, not payoff matrices), the risk-adjusted Bellman optimality operator is proven to be a γ₀-contraction. The proposed single-timescale actor-critic algorithm inverts the standard step-size hierarchy: the actor (joint policy z = (π, p)) updates with a larger step size β_t while the critic (Q-function) updates with a smaller step size α_t ≪ β_t, so that the contraction of the Bellman operator—rather than policy gradient—provides the negative drift in the coupled Lyapunov analysis. This yields global finite-sample convergence to the RQE for both constant and diminishing step sizes, and the practical implementation uses neural networks, a replay buffer, double-Q targets, and soft target updates to scale to continuous or large action spaces.

## Results

On the MPE Simple Tag environment (3 agents, 1 fixed good agent, 2 adversaries), the risk-averse actor-critic achieves a mean reward of 36.74 ± 2.65 across 5 runs, outperforming MAPPO (35.50 ± 4.91) and MADDPG (30.20 ± 9.53) in both mean reward and variance. In the gridworld cooperation game, risk-averse training converges faster and more consistently across 10 independent runs compared to risk-neutral training, which shows high variance and may never converge. In the inspection game, risk-neutral gradient descent fails to converge entirely, while risk-averse GD converges with larger risk-aversion parameter τ yielding faster convergence and more conservative (lower-variance) agent behaviors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its risk-averse actor-critic against two standard MARL baselines: MAPPO (Yu et al., 2022) and MADDPG (Lowe et al., 2017). On MPE Simple Tag, the risk-averse method wins on both mean reward and variance: it achieves 36.74 ± 2.65 versus MAPPO's 35.50 ± 4.91 (a +1.24 mean improvement with ~46% lower variance) and MADDPG's 30.20 ± 9.53 (a +6.54 mean improvement with ~72% lower variance). On the gridworld cooperation game, both MAPPO and MADDPG produce unstable training curves across 5 runs, while the risk-averse actor-critic converges smoothly and consistently. The paper also includes a risk-neutral ablation of its own algorithm (removing adversaries and risk-aversion terms), which shows that the stability gains come specifically from the risk-aversion mechanism rather than other architectural choices. Overall, the risk-averse method dominates both baselines on every environment tested, with the largest margin observed against MADDPG.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RQE framework introduced by Mazumdar et al. (2025) and the monotonicity-based tractability results of Zhang & Mazumdar (2025) by (i) generalizing the monotonicity condition to a λ-weighted form that strictly subsumes both prior conditions, and (ii) proposing the first practical single-timescale actor-critic algorithm with finite-sample convergence guarantees for RQE in discounted general-sum Markov games. A key departure from standard actor-critic is the 'reverse' step-size ordering (faster actor, slower critic), which is necessitated by the fact that policy gradient no longer provides negative drift in multi-agent settings, so convergence must be driven by the Bellman operator's contraction property instead. The algorithm also builds on Nash Q-learning (Hu & Wellman, 2003) by replacing the Nash equilibrium oracle with a risk-averse quantal-response stage-game solution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first MARL algorithm with global convergence guarantees to stationary equilibria in general-sum discounted Markov games without assuming additional structure on the game (e.g., zero-sum, potential, or cooperative). The core insight—that behavioral assumptions on agents (risk-aversion and bounded rationality) can regularize an arbitrary game into a monotone one, replacing structural assumptions on the game itself—opens a path to tractable equilibrium computation in settings where Nash is PPAD-complete. Key limitations include the tabular setting, the requirement for joint (non-independent) training with access to opponent policies, and the two-player focus; the authors identify linear function approximation, softmax parameterization, independent learning, and extension to extensive-form/imperfect-information games as important future directions.
