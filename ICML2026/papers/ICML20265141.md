## Problem

Existing AI-assisted scientific writing methods treat Introduction generation as a standard text-generation task, bypassing the underlying reasoning structure of the paper. This leads to hallucinated citations, logical inconsistencies between the Introduction and the Results/Methodology, and missing motivations. The paper formalizes a more rigorous Content-Conditional Introduction Generation (CCIG) task that requires grounding the Introduction in the paper's detailed main body rather than high-level summaries like titles and abstracts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265141/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes the main body of a scientific paper—comprising Methods, Results, and Analyses sections (with the Introduction excluded)—along with a citation list where each reference has a unique index. It first produces a single-rooted directed acyclic reasoning logic graph whose nodes are self-contained atomic sentences and whose edges follow six typed relations derived from Peircean deduction, induction, and abduction. The final output is a structured Introduction that must follow Swales' CARS rhetorical moves (Territory → Niche → Contribution) and cite only from the provided list using strict [idx] markers, with no invented or omitted references.

## Method

LECTOR casts the entire pipeline (main body → reasoning graph → Introduction) as a single RL episode and trains a 4B-parameter LLM (Qwen3-4B) with a Simplified PPO objective inspired by GRPO, using only an Actor and Critic (no learned reward model). The reasoning logic graph acts as an information bottleneck: it is a single-rooted DAG where every conclusion is supported by exactly two complementary premise edges of a specific Peircean type, forcing explicit logical justification at each step. A five-component verifiable reward function jointly optimizes Graph Validity (edge accuracy + entity coverage), Faithfulness (bidirectional coverage + NLI entailment via SummaC), Paper Consistency (BLEU + embedding similarity + key-phrase recall against the ground-truth Introduction), Academic Quality (LLM-as-a-Judge scoring on coherence, completeness, tone, and preference), and Reference Alignment (citation recall and contextual correctness). This co-rewarding mechanism ensures that improvements in logical structure and narrative quality reinforce each other rather than being optimized in isolation.

## Results

On the Nature Communications CCIG benchmark (100 test papers), LECTOR-4B achieves an Overall Performance (OP) of 0.665, surpassing its untrained Qwen3-4B base (0.510) by +15.5 points and the strongest proprietary model GPT-o3 (0.656) by +0.9 points. The most dramatic gains are in Graph Quality (+26.7%, from 0.478 to 0.745) and Writing Quality (+28.8%, from 0.546 to 0.834), while Citation Quality improves by +8.6% (0.444→0.530) and Paper Consistency by +3.3% (0.453→0.486). All improvements are statistically significant after Holm-Bonferroni correction (p < 3×10⁻⁶) with large effect sizes (Cohen's d_z up to 1.617 for OP).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GPT-o3, LECTOR wins on Overall Performance (0.665 vs. 0.656), Graph Quality (0.745 vs. 0.690), Graph-Write Alignment (0.623 vs. 0.448), and Paper Consistency (0.486 vs. 0.416), but trails on Writing Quality (0.834 vs. 0.882) and Citation Quality (0.530 vs. 0.691). Versus Claude-haiku-4.5, LECTOR leads on GQ (0.745 vs. 0.707), PC (0.486 vs. 0.470), WQ (0.834 vs. 0.699), and OP (0.665 vs. 0.612), while Claude edges ahead on GW (0.727 vs. 0.623) and CQ (0.529 vs. 0.530, essentially tied). Against Grok4, LECTOR dominates on GQ (0.745 vs. 0.651), GW (0.623 vs. 0.601), PC (0.486 vs. 0.464), WQ (0.834 vs. 0.691), and OP (0.665 vs. 0.599), though Grok4 retains a CQ advantage (0.721 vs. 0.530). Gemini-2.5-pro and GLM-4.7 are substantially outperformed across nearly all dimensions, with LECTOR's OP exceeding theirs by +10 and +19.9 points respectively. Compared to its own Qwen3-4B base, LECTOR improves every metric except GW (0.623 vs. 0.682), which the authors attribute to a strategic shift from rigid node-to-text translation toward more natural discourse. The One-Step Baseline (direct generation without the intermediate graph) achieves competitive WQ (0.829) but lower PC (0.476) and CQ (0.477), confirming the value of the explicit reasoning graph as a cognitive scaffold.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of LLM-based scientific writing (e.g., SciIG, AI Scientist systems) that treats Introduction generation as a text-completion task conditioned on high-level summaries like titles and abstracts. It builds on the PPO-CLIP objective from DeepSeekMath/GRPO for the RL training loop, borrows the Information Bottleneck principle to frame the reasoning graph as a compressed semantic representation, and draws on Peircean logic (deduction, induction, abduction) to define graph edge types. Unlike ARCHE, which uses reasoning graphs as a final evaluation output, LECTOR repurposes the graph as a trainable intermediate representation that jointly optimizes with the generation objective via a co-rewarding mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CCIG formulation and verifiable reward framework offer a principled path toward hallucination-resistant scientific writing, addressing a critical trust gap in AI-assisted research. However, the evaluation is confined to a single physics-heavy dataset (Nature Communications) and a single 4B backbone, leaving open whether the approach generalizes to other domains, paper sections, or model scales. The thin margin over GPT-o3 (0.665 vs. 0.656) and the reliance on a 235B model for reward computation raise practical questions about the framework's scalability and independence from larger models.
