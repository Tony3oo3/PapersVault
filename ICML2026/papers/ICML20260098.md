## Problem

Current LLM safety evaluations focus on finding input contexts that elicit harmful outputs, typically sampling only one or a few responses per query. This ignores the probabilistic nature of model outputs: even safety-aligned models produce harmful responses with small but non-zero probability, and at deployment scale (billions of queries daily), these rare tail events are inevitable. Naive Monte Carlo sampling to estimate these rare-event probabilities is computationally infeasible, as harmful outputs may require 10,000+ samples to observe even once.

## Input / Output

The method requires white-box access to the target model's weights (to compute and apply activation steering vectors), an input query context, and a binary harmfulness judge (an LLM classifier scoring outputs as harmful or not). It produces a per-query probability estimate of harmful output, which can be aggregated across a query distribution via extreme value theory to predict worst-case deployment risk on unseen queries. For misalignment traits (sycophancy, hallucination), the judge uses dual scoring (coherence + trait presence, each 0–100), with harm assigned only when both exceed 50.

## Method

The core technique is importance sampling: rather than sampling from the target model, the method constructs an 'unsafe' proposal model by ablating a refusal direction (computed as the difference-in-means of hidden activations between harmful and harmless inputs) from the residual stream, making harmful outputs more probable. Samples from this unsafe model are reweighted by the likelihood ratio p_target/q_proposal to yield unbiased estimates of the target's harmfulness probability. Two variance-reduction mechanisms are applied: a per-token mixture between the unsafe and target models (controlled by α_mix), and a model-switching scheme where the first t_switch tokens are sampled from the unsafe model and the remainder from the target. The proposal hyperparameters (steering intensity, mixing coefficient, switch index) are optimized via the cross-entropy method on a subset of queries. This achieves 10–20× sample efficiency over naive Monte Carlo, enabling estimation of probabilities as low as 10⁻⁴ with just 500 samples.

## Results

On 313 STRONGREJECT queries, repeated sampling (10,000 outputs per query) reveals harmful outputs for 6–60× more queries than a single greedy sample: e.g., Llama-3.1-8B goes from 1.6% to 74.0%, and Llama-3.2-1B from 16.6% to 99.5%. The proposed importance sampling (IS) method with activation-steered proposals matches brute-force Monte Carlo estimates (10,000 samples) using only 1,000 samples (10× fewer), and can estimate harmful-output probabilities on the order of 10⁻⁴ with just 500 samples. For misalignment traits (sycophancy, hallucination), IS is equal or more accurate than naive MC at all sample sizes, with the gap widening when misaligned responses are rarer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between the proposed importance sampling (IS) estimator and naive Monte Carlo (MC) sampling, both benchmarked against expensive brute-force MC estimates (10,000 samples per query). IS with 1,000 samples converges to the brute-force estimate significantly faster than MC with the same 1,000-sample budget, achieving comparable accuracy with 10× fewer samples overall. For sycophancy and hallucination estimation on Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.1-8B, IS is equal or more accurate than MC at every sample size tested, with the advantage most pronounced when misaligned responses are rare (sycophancy being rarer than hallucination). The paper also ablates proposal model selection: the cross-entropy-optimized proposal (jointly tuning steering intensity λ_steer, mixing coefficient α_mix, and switch index t_switch) consistently yields the lowest mean-squared error compared to fixed steering intensities, confirming that proposal optimization is critical for reliable rare-event estimation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of classical rare-event estimation (importance sampling, cross-entropy method) and modern mechanistic interpretability tools (activation steering, refusal directions, persona vectors). It departs from the dominant safety-evaluation paradigm that focuses on the input distribution—searching for adversarial prompts or diverse jailbreaks—and instead targets the output distribution for a fixed query, a dimension prior work largely ignores. It complements Jones et al. (2025), who applied extreme value theory to forecast worst-case deployment risk but relied on hand-crafted sequence-probability proxies due to the cost of estimating per-query harm; here, the importance-sampling pipeline supplies the reliable query-level estimates that framework requires. The proposal-model construction via activation steering (Arditi et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025) is the key practical bridge that makes the classical importance-sampling machinery tractable for autoregressive language models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

At population-scale deployment (billions of queries/day), even tail probabilities on the order of 10⁻⁴ translate to millions of harmful outputs, yet current evaluations with single or few samples systematically understate this risk. The 10–20× sample-efficiency gain makes per-query tail-risk estimation feasible within existing safety-evaluation budgets, and the finding that non-adversarial paraphrases can shift harmfulness probabilities by 9+ orders of magnitude challenges the assumption that a single query's risk estimate is a stable, deployment-relevant quantity. Key limitations include the white-box requirement (steering vectors need model weights), dependence on fallible LLM judges, and evaluation on relatively small, curated datasets; extending the method to black-box models, reasoning models, and multi-turn agentic settings remains open.
