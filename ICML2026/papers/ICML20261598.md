## Problem

Existing guidance mechanisms for diffusion-based robotic planning rely on heuristic or learning-based strategies that lack explicit guarantees on goal achievement or constraint satisfaction. While Doob's h-transform characterizes the ideal conditioned diffusion process, its analytical form is computationally intractable and its implications for quantitative reliability—specifically, probabilistic bounds on satisfying prescribed constraints under approximate guidance—remain unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained score network (trained once on trajectory data without obstacle geometry or goal conditioning), a goal specification, obstacle geometry, and task constraints, all injected at inference time through a terminal cost function. It produces guided trajectories: 50-waypoint 2D point-mass trajectories (x, y, v_x, v_y) or 64-waypoint 7-DOF Franka joint-space trajectories. Notably, the score model is never retrained or fine-tuned for new tasks; all task-specific information enters solely through the guidance cost, making the approach fully training-free at inference.

## Method

The paper establishes Doob's h-transform as the theoretical ideal for guidance, where the guidance function u(t,x) = g(t)∇log h(t,x) is derived from h solving the backward Kolmogorov equation with terminal condition h(T,x) = 1_S(x), guaranteeing almost-sure target-set satisfaction. For practical approximate guidance (smoothed terminal condition h̃_T), the authors derive a KL-divergence bound between ideal and approximate terminal distributions (Theorem 4.3) and a success-probability lower bound μ_T(S) ≥ 1 − √(ρ/2) (Theorem 4.4), where ρ quantifies the L² pathwise discrepancy between ideal and approximate guidance. A key structural result (Proposition 5.1) shows that Doob-based guidance is equivalent to the optimal control of a stochastic optimal control problem with running cost ½‖u‖² and terminal cost −log h̃_T, enabling computation via path integral control (Theorem 5.2) using Monte Carlo rollouts of the uncontrolled reverse SDE. The practical algorithm (Algorithm 1) estimates the guidance at each denoising step by simulating 256 future denoising trajectories under a baseline control and weighting their terminal costs, requiring no HJB solve, no additional learning, and no retraining of the score network.

## Results

On the 2D navigation benchmark (80 samples per scenario family), PIC achieves 98.8% task success on Simple, 92.5% on Medium, and 67.5% on Hard obstacle configurations, compared to 0–3.8% for all baselines. On the 7-DOF Franka joint-trajectory task, Full-Body PIC reaches 86%, 80%, and 88% task success across Moderate, Hard-Bar, and Panel obstacle families, while Direct-Gradient Guidance achieves only 0–4%. On 360 previously unseen randomized obstacle layouts, PIC attains 92.8% task success and 97.8% obstacle-safe rate, versus 1.4–3.9% task success for all three baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares PIC against three baselines: No Guidance (unconditional sampling), Potential-Gradient Guidance (local gradient of the same cost without future rollout), and Reflected Diffusion + Gradient (a separately trained reflected-diffusion model with box reflection plus local gradient). PIC dominates all baselines by a wide margin on every scenario: on the 2D fixed benchmark, PIC achieves 98.8%/92.5%/67.5% task success versus at most 3.8% for the best baseline (Potential-Gradient on Medium). On the Franka 7-DOF task, Full-Body PIC reaches 80–88% success while Direct-Gradient Guidance (the closest analogue to Potential-Gradient) achieves 0–4%, and Diffusion Inpainting (which hard-conditions waypoints) reaches only 10–40% because it cannot enforce collision avoidance over the full trajectory. On the randomized-layout generalization test, PIC's 92.8% task success and 97.8% obstacle-safe rate exceed the best baseline (Gradient at 3.9% success) by roughly 25×, demonstrating that the path-integral rollout mechanism is essential for constraint satisfaction rather than simple gradient descent on a cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions Doob's h-transform as a unifying theoretical lens for guided diffusion, building on recent work that has independently recognized its role in characterizing conditioned diffusion processes (Heng et al., 2025; Du et al., 2024; Denker et al., 2024; Nguyen et al., 2025; Didi et al., 2024). Where those prior studies focus on learning-based approximations of the h-transform, this paper instead derives explicit KL-divergence and success-probability bounds for approximate guidance and shows that the Doob-optimal guidance is equivalent to the optimal control of a stochastic optimal control problem, enabling path integral control (Kappen, 2005; Theodorou et al., 2010; Thijssen & Kappen, 2015) and iLQG as training-free guidance solvers. The planning application follows the trajectory-diffusion framework of Janner et al. (2022), and the method is compared against reflected diffusion (Lou & Ermon, 2023) and simple potential-gradient guidance, but the theoretical core departs from prior diffusion-control connections (Li & Pereira, 2024; Zhang & Chen, 2022) by starting from the h-transform rather than from the HJB equation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The explicit success-probability bound (Theorem 4.4) is one of the first to provide a quantitative reliability guarantee for guided diffusion in a planning context, which is important for safety-critical robotic applications where heuristic guidance offers no such assurance. The stochastic-optimal-control equivalence reframes guidance design as terminal-cost design, giving practitioners a principled knob to tune and a path to task adaptation without retraining. However, the bound depends on the quantity ρ, which is defined along the ideal guided process and is not directly computable in practice, limiting its immediate use as a design criterion. Open questions include how to combine the guidance-approximation error with score-approximation and discretization errors into a single end-to-end guarantee, and whether the Monte Carlo rollout cost of path integral control (256 rollouts, ~10 s per sample) is acceptable for real-time or high-frequency planning.
