## Problem

Agentic pipelines increasingly rely on LLMs to generate and then evaluate their own outputs, implicitly assuming the model applies the same concept in both roles. No prior work directly measures whether this assumption holds, nor whether self-consistency across generation and evaluation predicts real-world reliability. The paper asks: can we quantify generator-evaluator conceptual self-consistency, and does it track or undermine deployment safety?

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes benchmark questions (from MMLU, BBH, MedicalQA, GDPVal, FinanceQA) and a model-extracted single concept label per question, then issues paired generator and evaluator prompts to the same model. Each test instance yields a deterministic binary agreement label (Z ∈ {0,1}) parsed from the evaluator's response, which is aggregated into chance-corrected self-consistency scores normalized so that 0 = chance and 1 = perfect agreement. In the clinical case study, the output additionally includes a weighted mistake-vulnerability score Y* ∈ [0,1] per (model, concept) pair derived from MedMistakes-Validated, where multi-tag mistakes are split evenly across tags so each underlying mistake contributes total mass 1.

## Method

The paper introduces a suite of 12 automated generator-evaluator tests organized into three families—MCQ perturbation (e.g., modifying a question so 'None of the above' becomes correct), rationale-based (e.g., generating a correct answer with intentionally incomplete reasoning and asking the model to flag it), and ontological (e.g., generating example-attribute pairs and verifying internal consistency). Each test uses the same model twice: once to produce an output with a specified property and once to judge whether that output satisfies the property, requiring no external correctness oracle. Scoring is deterministic, and a chance-correction step (subtracting test-specific chance baselines like 1/5 for five-option MCQs and 1/2 for binary judgments) makes scores comparable across structurally different tests. The suite is applied to 10 frontier models across 491 concepts, and a weighted fractional logit model (Papke & Wooldridge) relates self-consistency to clinician-validated mistake vulnerability while controlling for MedQA benchmark accuracy, with standard errors clustered at the concept level. The key empirical finding is a positive association (β̂₂ = 1.116, p < 10⁻⁶) between self-consistency and mistake vulnerability that persists across models but vanishes within a single model, revealing the 'consistency dilemma.'

## Results

Across 10 frontier models, 5 benchmarks, and 491 concepts (3,951 evaluation instances), chance-corrected self-consistency varies substantially: e.g., on the 'None of the Above' MCQ test, scores range from 0.11 (GPT-4o) to 0.79 (Gemini 3, GPT-5), while on 'Correct for Incomplete Reason' they range from −1.00 (Opus 4.5) to 0.73 (DeepSeek). In the clinical deployment analysis (N=440 model–concept pairs), self-consistency is positively associated with mistake vulnerability after controlling for MedQA accuracy: β̂₂ = 1.116 (SE = 0.224, p < 10⁻⁶; two-way clustered SE = 0.533, p = 0.036). The association is a between-model effect: adding model fixed effects reduces the coefficient to β̂₂ = 0.147 (p = 0.66), indicating self-consistency is a model-level behavioral signature rather than a concept-level predictor.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against existing baselines; rather, it introduces a measurement framework (generator-evaluator self-consistency) and compares 10 frontier models against each other on that metric. The key cross-model comparison is in the clinical deployment analysis: models with higher aggregate self-consistency (e.g., Gemini 3, GPT-5, GPT-4o on ontological tests) also exhibit higher vulnerability to physician-validated clinical mistakes, while models with lower self-consistency (e.g., GPT-4o on MCQ-perturbation tests, Sonnet 4.5 on rationale tests) show lower vulnerability. The fractional logit regression confirms this ordering is statistically significant (β̂₂ = 1.116, p < 10⁻⁶) even after controlling for MedQA benchmark accuracy (which itself is negatively associated with vulnerability, β̂₁ = −1.732, p = 0.042). Notably, the effect is a between-model phenomenon: within any single model, self-consistency on a given concept does not predict vulnerability to mistakes on that concept (fixed-effects β̂₂ = 0.147, p = 0.66). The 'Correct for Incomplete Reason' test is identified as the primary driver of the consistency–vulnerability association, with large cross-model variation (ranging from −1.00 to 0.73) that aligns with triage-related clinical failures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws on metamorphic testing (controlled input transformations with expected output relationships) and LLM-as-a-judge literature, but departs from both in key ways. Unlike prior self-consistency work (Wang et al., 2023b; SelfCheckGPT) that measures agreement across repeated samples as a reliability signal, or self-contradiction detection (Liu et al., 2024) that examines reasoning traces, this paper measures consistency across *roles* (generator vs. evaluator) at the level of reusable concepts, organized into a unified taxonomy of three qualitatively distinct failure modes. The connection to deployment failures via a clinician-validated medical dataset (MedMistakes-Validated) is the paper's most distinctive contribution, linking a behavioral consistency measure to expert-grounded mistake vulnerability rather than benchmark accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'consistency dilemma'—that models with higher generator-evaluator self-consistency are more vulnerable to validated clinical mistakes—challenges the implicit assumption in agentic pipeline design that self-consistency is a positive safety signal. This has direct practical implications: self-consistency scores should be used as diagnostics for drift and workflow stability, not as a proxy for deployment safety. Key limitations include the observational (non-causal) nature of the medical analysis, restriction to a single domain, and the finding that the consistency-vulnerability link operates at the model level rather than predicting which specific concepts within a model are most vulnerable. Open questions include whether the dilemma generalizes beyond medical settings and what mechanisms drive the association.
