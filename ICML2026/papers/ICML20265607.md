## Problem

Prior work has separately linked generalization to model-model alignment and demonstrated alignment between AI models and brain activity, but these studies rely on different model families, datasets, and metrics, leaving it unclear whether they reflect a common underlying phenomenon. The paper asks whether generalization, AI-AI alignment, and AI-Brain alignment form a single correlated structure across models, and whether a single geometric property of learned representations can explain all three relationships simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265607/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes embeddings from 60+ pretrained vision models (ConvNeXt, ResNet, ResMLP, ViT) computed on ~73,000 natural scene images from the NSD dataset, paired with 7T fMRI responses from 8 participants at ~1.8 mm isotropic resolution. Embeddings are projected onto the top 300 principal components to control for dimension differences across architectures, and alignment is scored via ridge regression R² on held-out test splits. The key outputs are per-model AI-Brain alignment (R² per voxel/region), pairwise AI-AI alignment (mean of bidirectional R²), and local intrinsic dimension estimates computed at multiple neighborhood scales (K from small to large) using the Levina-Bickel MLE estimator.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a multi-scale geometric analysis showing that local intrinsic dimension—estimated via the MLE estimator at small neighborhood sizes (e.g., K=50–1000)—is the critical descriptor linking all three phenomena, whereas global dimension measures fail to capture these effects. The authors first establish that generalization, AI-AI alignment (measured relative to a generalization-optimal reference model chosen without neural data), and AI-Brain alignment systematically co-vary across models. They then perform a scale sweep over neighborhood size K, demonstrating that correlations between dimension and alignment/performance are strongest at local scales and weaken monotonically as K increases, with a control analysis confirming the effect is driven by local geometric structure rather than sample size. Finally, they show that increasing model parameter count and pretraining data scale systematically reduce local intrinsic dimension, and that local dimension explains significant additional variance in alignment and generalization beyond model size alone (ΔR² up to 0.373 in hierarchical regressions). The framework is validated across architectures, brain regions, subjects, network depths, PCA dimensions, and extended to language models on MNLI.

## Results

In higher-level visual areas (EBA, FBA), the median AI-Brain alignment of large models reaches approximately 60% of the noise ceiling. Local intrinsic dimension (estimated at fine neighborhood scales, e.g., K=50 for AI-AI/generalization and K=1000 for AI-Brain) is significantly and negatively correlated with all three quantities: AI-AI alignment, AI-Brain alignment, and ImageNet-1K top-1 accuracy, whereas global dimension measures fail to capture these effects. Hierarchical regression shows that adding local intrinsic dimension to log(Params) yields a statistically significant ΔR² of 0.373 for AI-Brain alignment, 0.110 for AI-AI alignment, and 0.032 for generalization, confirming that representational geometry contributes explanatory power beyond model scale alone. The findings extend to language models on MNLI (accuracy range 81.6%–91.9%) and hold across ConvNeXt, ResNet, ResMLP, and ViT architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analytical paper without a proposed method to benchmark against external baselines; instead, it compares internal methodological choices. Local intrinsic dimension (small K) outperforms global dimension (large K) as a predictor of alignment and generalization: correlations are strongest at small neighborhood sizes and weaken monotonically as K increases (Figure 4C). Among dimension estimators, MLE (Levina & Bickel), MOM, and MADA all yield qualitatively identical scale-dependent trends, confirming the findings are not estimator-specific (Figure 14). Cross-architecture comparisons show that pooling ConvNeXt, ResNet, ResMLP, and ViT slightly reduces correlation strength but all relationships remain statistically significant; however, AI-AI alignment is strongly shaped by architectural family (within-family alignment >> cross-family), while intrinsic dimension does not vary systematically across families. PCA projection dimension (80, 150, or 300 PCs) does not qualitatively change any conclusion. Layer-wise analysis confirms that local intrinsic dimension predicts alignment and generalization at 10% and 50% network depth, not only at the final layer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate research threads: the Platonic Representation Hypothesis linking generalization to inter-model representational convergence (Kornblith et al., 2019; Huh et al., 2024), AI-Brain alignment studies using fMRI benchmarks like NSD (Conwell et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023), and intrinsic dimension analysis of neural representations (Ansuini et al., 2019). The key departure from Ansuini et al. is the multi-scale analysis: rather than computing dimension at a single fixed neighborhood size, the authors systematically vary the scale and show that local (small-K) dimension, not global dimension, is the relevant geometric descriptor. The MLE estimator of Levina & Bickel (2004) is adopted directly, and the ridge-regression alignment pipeline mirrors standard encoding-model methodology from the neuro-AI literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By identifying local intrinsic dimension as a single architecture-agnostic geometric quantity that simultaneously tracks AI-AI alignment, AI-Brain alignment, and generalization, the paper provides a concrete, measurable signature of the 'shared representational structure' invoked by the Platonic Representation Hypothesis. The finding that scaling (more parameters, more data) systematically reduces local dimension offers a geometric mechanism for why larger models generalize better and align more with the brain. Key limitations include the vision-only scope, the correlational (not causal) nature of the dimension-performance link, and the absence of a theoretical explanation for why local rather than global geometry is the relevant scale. Extending the framework to other modalities and establishing mechanistic accounts for the local-dimension effect are natural next steps.
