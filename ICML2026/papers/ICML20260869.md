## Problem

Existing search-agent benchmarks (BrowseComp, GAIA) assume user queries are complete and unambiguous, leaving untested a critical practical failure mode: agents facing ambiguous requests that require clarification before effective retrieval. Most commercial and research agents lack interactive mechanisms during search, and no prior benchmark evaluates whether agents can recognize query ambiguity and proactively gather disambiguating information through interaction.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a 40–80 word ambiguous question and operates within a 10-round ReAct loop with three actions: search (top-5 Google Serper results), interact (closed-ended yes/no questions to a GPT-4o simulated user who answers only 'yes', 'no', or 'I don't know' based on hidden context), and answer (a 1–2 word factual response with a confidence score in [0,1]). The binary yes/no protocol is deliberately restrictive to force agents to commit to specific hypotheses rather than collapsing interaction into open-ended information retrieval. Evaluation reports accuracy, expected calibration error (5-bin ECE), interaction rate, and cost per instance.

## Method

The benchmark is constructed via a target-distractor methodology: annotators pair a lesser-known target entity with a popular distractor, formulate questions using only their shared attributes (creating genuine ambiguity), and reserve distinctive attributes as hidden context that a simulated user can reveal through yes/no clarification. A two-stage verification protocol ensures completeness (question+context yields a unique answer) and interaction necessity (questions cannot be resolved by direct web search or by three capable LLMs without interaction). Evaluation employs four ReAct configurations (answer-only, answer+search, answer+interact, answer+search+interact) across 17 models to isolate the incremental contribution of retrieval versus interaction. The key diagnostic finding is a ~5× gap between with-context accuracy (68–72%) and full-interaction accuracy (≤13.73%), with forced interaction roughly doubling performance, revealing systematic overconfidence rather than reasoning deficits as the dominant bottleneck.

## Results

On INTERACTCOMP (210 questions, 9 domains), the best model GPT-5 achieves only 13.73% accuracy in the full Answer+Search+Interact setting, while OpenAI o3 reaches 71.50% when given complete disambiguating context — a 5× gap exposing systematic overconfidence rather than reasoning deficits. Forced interaction (requiring 8+ clarifying questions) roughly doubles GPT-5's accuracy from 20% to 40%, confirming latent capability. Longitudinally, BrowseComp performance improved seven-fold (10%→70%) over 15 months while INTERACTCOMP accuracy remained stagnant at 6–14% across all evaluated models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is longitudinal against BrowseComp: over 15 months, BrowseComp accuracy improved seven-fold (10%→70%) while INTERACTCOMP accuracy remained flat at 6–14% for all models, revealing that search capability gains have not translated to interaction capability. Two commercial deep-research systems were additionally evaluated on INTERACTCOMP: OpenAI Deep Research achieves 6.67% and Doubao Deep Search achieves 10.0%, both falling within the same low-accuracy band as the ReAct-based agents (6–14%), indicating that richer agentic scaffolding alone does not close the ambiguity-resolution gap. The ablation against the with-context ceiling (Table 2) shows that the benchmark's questions are solvable — o3 reaches 71.50% and GPT-5 reaches 67.88% with full context — confirming the bottleneck is interaction strategy, not reasoning or retrieval.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two existing benchmark families: search-agent benchmarks (BrowseComp, GAIA) that assume complete, unambiguous queries, and interaction/clarification benchmarks (IN3, Tau-Bench, AmbigQA) that lack grounding in verifiable search tasks. The target-distractor construction methodology draws inspiration from BrowseComp's answer-first approach but fundamentally shifts the evaluation axis from search complexity to ambiguity resolution. The binary yes/no interaction protocol is a deliberate departure from free-form clarification work, designed to force agents to commit to specific hypotheses rather than engage in open-ended information retrieval.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The longitudinal finding that BrowseComp performance improved seven-fold over 15 months while INTERACTCOMP stagnated at 6–14% exposes a critical blind spot: rapid progress in search and reasoning has not translated to interaction capability, which is essential for real-world deployment where user intent is often underspecified. The forced-interaction experiment (14%→40%) demonstrates the capability is latent rather than absent, making INTERACTCOMP a practical substrate for RLVR training with clean binary reward signals. Limitations include the small scale (210 questions), reliance on a GPT-4o simulated responder, and the restrictive binary protocol; open questions include whether RLVR can actually close the interaction gap and whether findings generalize beyond the ReAct scaffold.
