## Problem

Function-preserving linear transforms (Hadamard rotations, channel scaling) have empirically reduced post-training quantization error in LLMs, but no principled framework explains why or how to design an optimal transform. The paper identifies that prior work focuses almost exclusively on reducing activation/weight outliers (concentration) while completely neglecting a second, independent source of quantization error: the misalignment between the principal variation directions of weights and activations, which rotations provably cannot improve.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pretrained model weights and a small calibration set (128 sequences of length 2048 from DCLM-edu) to estimate activation and weight covariance matrices per linear layer. It outputs a block-diagonal transform matrix T̂ (with configurable block size k, e.g. 128) that is applied to activations at inference and whose inverse is fused into the weights, followed by standard W4A4 quantization. The transform adds only O(d²/k) memory overhead (~1% of layer parameters for k=128, d=4096) and O(nd²/k) compute, comparable to FlatQuant's Kronecker-product transforms.

## Method

The paper derives a closed-form decomposition of the signal-to-quantization-noise ratio (SQNR) for a quantized linear layer into three multiplicative factors: a bit-width term, a concentration term (ratio of squared L2 norm to squared range, capturing outliers), and an alignment term (ratio of output energy to the product of weight Frobenius norm and activation energy, capturing directional agreement). It proves that alignment is invariant under any orthogonal transform, explaining why Hadamard-based methods (QuaRot, SpinQuant) improve concentration but leave alignment unchanged. The alignment-optimal transform M̂ is derived analytically as the matrix geometric mean of the weight autocorrelation and inverse activation autocorrelation matrices, mapping both into a shared principal-direction space. Since M̂ is full-rank and impractical, the paper approximates it with a block-diagonal matrix of d/k blocks (each of size k), then composes it with a Hadamard matrix to simultaneously maximize alignment and concentration. This yields CAT (block), a training-free or lightly-trained transform that matches or outperforms FlatQuant at W4A4 across Llama 2/3, Llama 3.2 1B, Ministral 8B, and Qwen 3 8B.

## Results

At W4A4 RTN quantization, CAT(block) without training achieves WikiText-2 perplexity of 6.11 (Llama 2 7B), 8.29 (Llama 3 8B), 18.25 (Llama 3.2 1B), 8.07 (Ministral 8B), and 10.82 (Qwen 3 8B), outperforming QuaRot (9.31, 10.99, 29.89, 9.12, 13.69) by 2–12 PPL points. With training, CAT(block) further improves to 5.95/67.34% (Llama 2 7B) and 7.33/69.66% (Llama 3 8B) on WikiText-2 PPL / 0-shot 6-task average, matching or exceeding the trained SOTA FlatQuant (6.05/66.67% and 7.61/68.34% respectively). Under GPTQ, CAT(block) with training achieves 7.25/70.38% on Llama 3 8B, again matching FlatQuant (7.25/69.97%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against QuaRot (randomized Hadamard, no training), CAT(block) without training reduces WikiText-2 perplexity by 2–12 points across all five models (e.g., 8.29 vs. 10.99 on Llama 3 8B; 18.25 vs. 29.89 on Llama 3.2 1B), confirming that alignment improvements beyond rotation yield substantial gains. Against SpinQuant (trained rotations), CAT(block) with training wins on perplexity for every model (e.g., 7.33 vs. 8.87 on Llama 3 8B RTN) and on 0-shot accuracy for Llama 2 7B and Llama 3 8B. Against FlatQuant (Kronecker-product trained transforms, current SOTA), CAT(block) with training matches or slightly exceeds perplexity on Llama 3 8B (7.33 vs. 7.61 RTN; 7.25 vs. 7.25 GPTQ) and Llama 3.2 1B (16.66 vs. 17.63 RTN), while achieving higher 0-shot accuracy on Llama 3 8B (69.66% vs. 68.34% RTN; 70.38% vs. 69.97% GPTQ) and Qwen 3 8B (66.93% vs. 66.25% RTN). Notably, CAT(block) without training already outperforms the trained FlatQuant on perplexity for Llama 2 7B (6.11 vs. 6.05) and Llama 3 8B (8.29 vs. 7.61 is a loss, but 0-shot 67.77 vs. 68.34 is close), demonstrating that the alignment-optimal block structure provides most of the benefit even before any gradient-based refinement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the function-preserving transform (FPT) literature for post-training quantization, which includes channel-wise scaling (SmoothQuant), randomized Hadamard rotations (QuaRot), learned rotations (SpinQuant), and Kronecker-product transforms (FlatQuant). Its key departure is providing a principled SQNR-based decomposition that separates quantization error into a concentration term (outlier spread) and an alignment term (directional similarity between weight and activation variation), revealing that all rotation-based methods address only concentration while completely neglecting alignment. CAT is then derived as a theoretically motivated transform that jointly optimizes both components via a matrix geometric mean, approximated by a block-diagonal structure of comparable cost to FlatQuant.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The concentration-alignment decomposition gives practitioners a diagnostic lens for understanding why specific transforms help or fail, and identifies alignment as a previously overlooked source of quantization error that can account for >10 dB of SQNR loss on layers like down_proj. The main open question is finding the best speed-accuracy trade-off among approximations to the full-rank CAT-optimal transform (block-diagonal, Kronecker, permutations, mergeable rotations), and extending the framework beyond LLMs to vision and multimodal architectures. The practical gains over FlatQuant are modest, so the primary value is conceptual: it reframes transform design from ad-hoc outlier suppression to a two-objective optimization problem.
