## Problem

ML/AI conference peer review fails at both of its stated objectives: a controlled experiment found only 1 of 79 reviews caught deliberately inserted errors, and panel-consistency experiments reveal 57% disagreement on accept/reject decisions with no correlation between review scores and future citations. The current system further conflates publication credentials with presentation slots, provides no accountability for review quality, and generates no public data for studying the review process itself.

## Input / Output

The proposed reform system takes research paper submissions as input and processes them through a four-stage pipeline, producing three distinct outputs: a publication acceptance decision based solely on technical soundness, a presentation slot assignment based on aggregated community ratings from many more reviewers than the current 2–4, and a fully public review dataset (reviewer identities, bidding data, meta-reviews) from the non-anonymous track. A key structural difference from current systems is that the objective soundness check and the subjective interestingness assessment are performed by different reviewer groups at different stages, and withdrawals after the soundness stage are treated as retractions.

## Method

The paper proposes two structural reforms. Reform 1 restructures the review into four steps: (1) a focused soundness evaluation using TMLR-like criteria (claims supported by evidence, correct proofs, valid methodology) that can incorporate LLM-assisted checking to reduce reviewer burden; (2) automatic publication of all sound papers, decoupling the credential from presentation; (3) a community excitingness rating where many more reviewers provide perfunctory assessments of interestingness and impact; and (4) presentation slot selection based on aggregated community ratings, with remaining papers offered as video presentations. Reform 2 introduces a parallel non-anonymous track where the review process remains double-blind during evaluation but all data is released publicly after a short embargo, with a simplified review form that frames feedback as constructive peer suggestions rather than accept/reject gatekeeping decisions, and a reciprocity requirement that each submitted paper must nominate a qualified author to review non-anonymously.

## Results

The paper's central empirical finding is that in a controlled experiment at a premier AI/ML conference (2022–2024), only 1 out of 79 reviews caught a deliberately inserted critical error, while 19 reviews explicitly endorsed the erroneous claim as sound. The NeurIPS 2014 consistency experiment found 57% of papers accepted by one committee were rejected by the other, and multiple studies (Ragone et al., 2013; Connolly et al., 2014; Cortes & Lawrence, 2021; Patel et al., 2024) show reviewer scores are uncorrelated with subsequent citations or altmetrics. At NeurIPS 2014, 427 of 1,264 traceable rejected papers were later published at other top venues, and at IEEE S&P 2017, 40% of submissions were resubmissions. TMLR's soundness-only model achieved a ~69% acceptance rate in 2025, and LLMs (GPT-4, frontier models) detected 39–50% of planted errors, suggesting AI-assisted soundness checking is feasible.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper, so there is no single proposed method benchmarked against baselines. Instead, the paper contrasts the current ML/AI conference review process against (a) controlled experiments in other scientific fields and (b) the two proposed reforms. On error detection, the ML/AI conference's 1/79 (~1.3%) rate is dramatically worse than the 25–34% rates observed in medical and emergency-medicine controlled trials (Table 1), where over 90% of individual reviewers caught at least one error. On decision consistency, the 57% disagreement rate in NeurIPS 2014 mirrors similar low-agreement findings in research-grant review panels (Obrecht et al., 2007; Fogelholm et al., 2012; Pier et al., 2018). On predictive validity, reviewer scores show no significant correlation with citations or altmetrics across multiple studies, whereas the proposed community-rating step (Step 3) aggregates many more opinions to reduce variance. The paper does not report head-to-head quantitative comparisons of the proposed four-step process or non-anonymous track against the current system, as these are policy proposals awaiting pilot deployment rather than evaluated algorithms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes empirical evidence from multiple peer-review experiments (NeurIPS 2014/2021 consistency experiments, controlled error-detection studies, TMLR's soundness-first model, ICLR's public review releases, and F1000Research's non-anonymous track) to argue for two concrete structural reforms. Rather than building on a single methodological lineage, it repurposes findings from the peer-review measurement literature—reviewer calibration studies, multi-criterion evaluation experiments, and resubmission analyses—into a unified policy proposal. The four-step process extends TMLR's soundness-only criterion and ICLR's transparency into a more complete pipeline, while the non-anonymous track generalizes F1000Research's model with added reciprocity requirements and a collegiality-focused review form.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a fundamental institutional failure: the current system neither reliably catches technical errors (1/79 detection rate) nor consistently identifies impactful work (57% panel disagreement, near-zero score-citation correlation). Key open questions include whether soundness-only review can scale to growing submission volumes, whether non-anonymous tracks will gain sufficient adoption to generate meaningful data, and how hiring/promotion committees will recalibrate their signals. The proposals are concrete and pilotable at a single conference, but their success depends on community willingness to accept a less exclusive publication model and on AI-assisted soundness checking maturing sufficiently to reduce reviewer burden.
