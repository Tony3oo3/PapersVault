## Problem

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods such as LoRA and Adapters restrict updates to low-rank or fixed subspaces, which inherently limits expressivity and risks performance degradation on difficult tasks compared to full fine-tuning (FT). The paper asks whether one can provably recover FT's full-parameter-space expressivity while maintaining a small per-step update budget, rather than accepting a permanently restricted update subspace.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262442/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained LLM backbone, a supervised fine-tuning dataset, and a per-step budget M specifying how many row-level parameter groups to update per step (e.g., M ∈ {32, 64, 128} out of thousands of total rows). At each step, the selector must decide which groups to update using only historical gradient-energy observations (EMA statistics from prior steps), not current-step full gradients, which would be as expensive as FT itself. The output is a set of fine-tuned parameters that, over the full training horizon, approach the FT solution in the full-parameter space while incurring only M-group gradient and optimizer-state memory per step.

## Method

The paper proposes the Think-Touch paradigm: at each step, a bandit-based UCB selector (Think) predicts which parameter groups carry the highest gradient energy using discounted EMA statistics from prior steps, then only the selected M groups are updated via gradient descent (Touch), and the history is refreshed with the observed mini-batch energies. The key insight is that FT gradients are empirically sparse in energy (verified via an Energy Sparsity Index), so touching a small set of well-chosen groups per step can accumulate into full-parameter-space expressivity over time. Theoretically, convergence to an FT-admitted stationary point is guaranteed under a weak condition that the cumulative coverage of expected gradient energy diverges, and a post-hoc greedy oracle (selecting the highest-energy group) is shown to be rate-optimal. Since this oracle requires full current-step gradients (infeasible under the low-cost constraint), the paper casts group selection as a non-stationary bandit problem with UCB exploration, discounting for reward drift, and an unbiased mini-batch energy estimator, proving a dynamic regret bound that recovers the oracle's linear convergence rate up to vanishing sublinear terms.

## Results

On eight commonsense reasoning tasks (ARC-c, ARC-e, BoolQ, HellaSwag, OBQA, PIQA, SIQA, WinoGrande), OURS (M=128) achieves 81.14% average accuracy on Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and 83.83% on Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, narrowing the gap to full fine-tuning (FT) to 1.35% and 0.67% respectively. Compared to LoRA (r=64), OURS (M=64) improves average accuracy by ~1.1% on Llama-3.2-3B (82.93% vs. 81.83%) while using a comparable parameter budget (~2.8% vs. ~2.9% of total parameters). On the larger Llama-3-8B-Instruct backbone, OURS reaches 74.07% on GSM8K and 60.9% on HumanEval, closely tracking FT (75.28% and 64.63%) while substantially outperforming LoRA (68.16% and 51.83%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LoRA (r=32/64), OURS (M=64) wins on average by ~1.1% on Llama-3.2-3B (82.93% vs. 81.83%) and ~0.3% on Qwen2.5-1.5B (80.42% vs. 80.16%), with the largest per-task gain reaching 4.8% on OBQA. Against DoRA (r=64), OURS (M=64) edges ahead by 0.19% on Llama-3.2-3B (82.93% vs. 82.74%) and 0.26% on Qwen2.5-1.5B (80.42% vs. 80.16%), with OURS (M=128) extending the lead to 1.09% and 0.98% respectively. Against RandLoRA (r=64), OURS (M=128) outperforms by 0.48% on Llama-3.2-3B (83.83% vs. 83.35%) and 0.31% on Qwen2.5-1.5B (81.14% vs. 80.83%). Notably, OURS (M=128) uses only 2.80% of parameters on Llama-3.2-3B yet closes 92% of the FT gap, while RandLoRA uses just 0.68% but still trails OURS. On the larger Llama-3-8B-Instruct backbone (math/coding), OURS narrows the FT gap to 1.21% on GSM8K and 3.73% on HumanEval, whereas LoRA lags by 7.12% and 12.80% respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the PEFT lineage (LoRA, DoRA, RandLoRA, Adapters) by rejecting the fixed-subspace constraint entirely: instead of restricting updates to a low-rank or bottlenecked parameterization, it keeps all parameters trainable but allocates a strict per-step budget across them via an online bandit selector. The selector's exploration mechanism builds directly on UCB (Auer et al., 2002) and non-stationary UCB with discounting (Garivier & Moulines, 2008), repurposing these tools from reward maximization to gradient-energy coverage. Conceptually, the paper also distinguishes itself from classical coordinate descent / Gauss-Southwell rules, which assume full per-block gradient access, by formulating group selection as a before-hoc decision under partial, noisy, and drifting feedback—a setting where the post-hoc greedy oracle is infeasible.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The cumulative-coverage framework provides a clean theoretical bridge between bandit regret and optimization convergence, showing that even highly suboptimal selection strategies (uniform, random) provably reach FT stationary points as long as coverage diverges—a surprisingly weak condition. Practically, the paradigm offers a memory-efficient alternative to FT that outperforms LoRA by ~1.5% while using comparable parameter budgets, though the gap to FT remains ~1% at the largest budget tested. Open questions include whether the approach scales to 70B+ models, whether richer group structures (e.g., attention-head-level) or contextual signals improve the selector, and whether the framework extends to RL-based alignment or continual pre-training where reward drift is more severe.
