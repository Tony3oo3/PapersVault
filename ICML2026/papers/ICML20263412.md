## Problem

Instruction-pair probes for detecting strategic deception in LLMs (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025) suffer from spurious correlations, false positives on benign content, and poor generalization across diverse deception scenarios (e.g., only 0.374 AUC on MASK known facts). This paper asks whether deception detection is inherently heterogeneous—requiring different probes for different deception types—or whether a single universal probe can be optimized to work across all contexts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263412/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The probe consumes residual stream activation vectors extracted at layer 20 of Gemma 2 9B IT from token-forced (off-policy) model responses, where factual statements are fed directly into the chat template rather than being generated. The final 5 response tokens are excluded from aggregation to prevent the probe from detecting whether the stated fact is literally true or false, forcing it to learn from intent-shaped representations. Output is a binary honest/deceptive score from a logistic regression classifier, evaluated as AUC across ten deception datasets.

## Method

The paper trains logistic regression probes on contrastive instruction pairs: the same factual statement is paired with either an honest or dishonest system prompt, and the probe learns to distinguish the two from residual stream activations. The key technical contribution is a systematic ANOVA over 2000 probes showing that system prompt design explains 70.6% of performance variance (vs. 2.5% for training data, 2.7% for layer, 0.6% for token selection), justifying a taxonomy-based prompt strategy. Using the Druckman & Bjork (1991) deception taxonomy, 23 prompt pairs target 16 specific deception types (overt lie, concealment, pressure dishonesty, etc.) plus 7 non-deceptive controls. Post-hoc oracle analysis shows matching probes to deception types yields +0.108 AUC over baseline, and synthetic validation confirms this ceiling is achievable a priori when the deception type is known. Hierarchical clustering of probe scores reveals that the 16 taxonomy prompts access a smaller set of shared 'deceptive intent' directions in activation space, with the counterfeit probe forming a distinct cluster.

## Results

Across ten deception datasets, the default instruction-pair baseline (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025) achieves a mean AUC of 0.689 (SD=0.165). A single best-average probe selected on a validation set improves this to 0.721 (+0.032 AUC, +5.5%), while post-hoc oracle matching of taxonomy-targeted probes to each dataset's deception type reaches 0.797 (+0.108 AUC, +20.5%). The largest individual gain is on MASK known facts (0.374 → 0.697, +86.3% relative), where pressure-dishonesty prompts are critical. ANOVA over 2,000 probes confirms that system prompt choice explains 70.6% of performance variance (p < 0.001), dwarfing layer selection (2.7%), training data (2.5%), and token aggregation (0.6%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is the default instruction-pair probe from Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2025), which uses generic 'honest person' vs. 'deceptive person' system prompts. The paper's taxonomy-targeted probes outperform this baseline on all ten evaluation datasets: eight exceed a 5% AUC gain, three exceed 10%, and MASK known facts exceeds 25% (0.374 → 0.697). A single best-average probe (the closest analogue to a 'universal detector') yields only a modest +0.032 AUC over the baseline, whereas the oracle taxonomy match achieves +0.108 AUC, indicating that the gap between a one-size-fits-all probe and a well-matched targeted probe is substantial. Synthetic validation confirms the oracle ceiling is achievable a priori: when the deception type is known in advance, the matched probe beats the baseline in 12 of 16 cases and ranks in the top 3 for its own type in 8 of 16. Cross-model replication on Llama 3.3 70B Instruct confirms the prompt-dominance finding (71.0% variance), though full taxonomy AUC comparisons were not replicated on the second model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the instruction-pair probe framework of Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2025), which showed that a single linear classifier trained on contrastive honest/dishonest prompts can detect strategic deception in LLM activations. The key departure is a systematic ANOVA analysis across 2000 probe configurations demonstrating that the system prompt (not training data, layer, or token selection) explains 70.6% of performance variance, and that deception is inherently heterogeneous across types. Rather than accepting the universal-probe paradigm, the authors introduce taxonomy-targeted probes grounded in the Druckman & Bjork (1991) deception taxonomy, showing that matching probes to specific deception mechanisms yields substantially higher AUC than any single generic detector.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant for AI safety teams: organizations should define specific threat models and deploy matched probes rather than seeking a one-size-fits-all deception detector, a finding that reframes how monitoring systems are designed and validated. However, the evidence base is limited—taxonomy AUC and clustering results are reported only on Gemma 2 9B IT, the synthetic validation shows the matched probe is top-3 in only 8/16 cases, and all experiments are in English conversational settings. Open questions include whether these findings transfer to agentic multi-step planning, whether ensemble methods can close the gap between best-average and oracle performance, and whether the clustering structure (five core 'deceptive intent' directions) is stable across architectures and scales.
