## Problem

LLM-based provers struggle with combinatorial identities in Lean 4 because long-horizon proof planning is required and unconstrained search explodes combinatorially, while data scarcity further limits domain-specific training. Although the Wilf-Zeilberger (WZ) method provides a principled symbolic proof plan for such identities, its outputs do not directly translate into interactive proof assistants: boundary conditions, normalization steps, and non-vanishing side conditions must be explicitly reconstructed inside the logic, and this formalization overhead dominates verification cost.

## Input / Output

The system takes a Lean 4 theorem statement (not a natural-language description) as input and produces a complete Lean 4 proof accepted by the kernel as output. When WZ decomposition applies, the symbolic engine (SageMath) first synthesizes a rational WZ certificate and compiles the identity into an executable Lean skeleton containing structured subgoals—recurrence lemmas, boundary obligations, non-vanishing side conditions, and normalization lemmas—which are then discharged individually by the trained prover. Identities outside WZ coverage are routed to direct end-to-end proving. All results are reported under a pass@32 sampling budget with a 16,384-token reasoning cap.

## Method

WZ-LLM is a two-stage neuro-symbolic framework. Stage 1 (Symbolic Decomposition) uses SageMath's hypergeometric summation routines to synthesize WZ certificates, then automatically emits a Lean 4 proof skeleton that reduces the target identity to a finite set of kernel-checkable obligations (recurrence/ratio lemmas, boundary conditions, non-vanishing denominators, normalization, and case splits), with all side conditions explicitly materialized as Lean subgoals. Stage 2 trains a specialized 8B prover (WZ-Prover) via a three-stage pipeline: cold-start SFT on 307 expert-verified identities plus 1,200 framework-extracted lemmas; expert-in-the-loop bootstrapping over 1,020 candidate identities for two rounds (retaining only Lean-kernel-verified outputs, adding 111 identities and 5,671 lemmas); and DAPO reinforcement learning with difficulty-smoothing data curation (rollout-based pass-rate binning, downsampling easy near-duplicates, removing near-zero-pass-rate goals) and a rule-based outcome reward plus soft overlong penalty. The key design insight is that WZ decomposition externalizes long-horizon proof planning into locally verifiable obligations, constraining the prover's search space, while the specialized prover simultaneously handles both sketch-induced subgoals and WZ-uncovered identities via two complementary routes, achieving 34% end-to-end success on LCI-Test versus 9% for the strongest open-source baseline.

## Results

On LCI-Test (100 classical combinatorial identities in Lean 4), WZ-LLM achieves a 34% end-to-end proof success rate (34/100) under pass@32, substantially outperforming the strongest open-source baseline Goedel-Prover-V2 (9/100) and the strongest proprietary model Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview (16/100). The two complementary routes contribute 29 solved identities via WZ-sketch-guided decomposition and 5 via direct proving on WZ-uncovered identities. On cross-dataset benchmarks, WZ-LLM reaches 16/100 on CombiBench and 3/36 on PutnamBench-Comb, improving over Goedel-Prover-V2 by +4 and +3 problems respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WZ-LLM (34/100) outperforms all six baselines on LCI-Test by wide margins: it beats Goedel-Prover-V2 (9/100, its own base model) by +25, Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview (16/100) by +18, Kimina-Prover-Distill and DeepSeek-Prover-V2 (6/100 each) by +28, MA-LoT (3/100) by +31, InternLM-2.5-StepProver (2/100) by +32, and DeepSeek-V3 (1/100) by +33. On CombiBench, WZ-LLM (16/100) surpasses Goedel-Prover-V2 (12/100), DeepSeek-Prover-V2 (8/100), and Kimina-Prover-Distill (6/100); on PutnamBench-Comb it matches DeepSeek-Prover-V2 (3/36 vs. 2/36) while Goedel-Prover-V2 and Kimina-Prover-Distill solve 0/36. Notably, adding WZ-Sketch to the untrained Goedel-Prover-V2 backend yields no improvement (9/100), confirming that symbolic decomposition alone is insufficient without a specialized lemma prover; the trained WZ-Prover raises lemma accuracy from 47.88% to 73.34% on sketch-induced subgoals, which is the key enabler for the sketch-guided route to succeed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two lines of work: (1) symbolic certificate-based methods for combinatorial identities (Wilf-Zeilberger, creative telescoping, Gosper's algorithm), previously formalized in HOL Light by Harrison (2015) but not yet integrated into interactive proof assistants with learned provers, and (2) LLM-based automated theorem proving in Lean 4 (Goedel-Prover-V2, DeepSeek-Prover-V2, InternLM-StepProver), which relies on unconstrained or search-based proof generation. The key departure is the *compilation* of WZ proof plans into executable Lean 4 proof sketches with explicit side-condition obligations, followed by a domain-specialized prover trained via expert-verified bootstrapping and DAPO refinement. Unlike prior LLM provers that treat the entire identity as a single generation target, WZ-LLM externalizes long-horizon proof planning into a symbolic front-end, constraining the neural prover to locally checkable algebraic subgoals.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 34% end-to-end success rate on LCI-Test (vs. 9% for the strongest open-source baseline) demonstrates that neuro-symbolic decomposition can substantially reduce the combinatorial explosion in proof search for a domain identified as among the hardest for ATP. The ablation revealing that WZ-Sketch alone provides zero improvement over the base model (9/100 = 9/100) is a useful cautionary result: symbolic proof planning only pays off when paired with a prover strong enough to discharge the induced obligations. Limitations include the WZ method's restriction to bivariate hypergeometric sums, the 8B model's persistent weakness on recurrence (56% failure) and singularity (74% failure) obligations, and the small test set (100 identities). Extending to multi-sum creative telescoping, q-hypergeometric methods, and larger prover models are natural next steps.
