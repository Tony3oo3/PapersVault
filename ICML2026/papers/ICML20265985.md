## Problem

Reinforcement learning with rubric-based rewards for LLMs still suffers from ineffective exploration confined to the current policy distribution, particularly on hard problems. Existing exploration strategies (structured search, intrinsic rewards) generate diverse responses but lack a principled mechanism to align exploration with the optimization target, causing the model to waste sampling budget on irrelevant directions rather than discovering the specific behaviors that rubrics reward.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265985/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text instruction paired with a set of checklist-style rubric criteria (each with a weight) as input, and produces a revised text response that better satisfies the rubrics. During training, the original failed trajectory and its unmet rubric descriptions are concatenated as in-context hindsight experience, which is masked from the loss so the model learns to generate improvements conditioned on the guidance rather than imitate the feedback text. Rubric evaluation is performed by an LLM judge (GPT-4o-mini) returning binary 0/1 per criterion, aggregated into a normalized weighted scalar reward.

## Method

HeRL bootstraps effective exploration by treating failed trajectories with their unmet rubrics as hindsight experience that explicitly tells the LLM which behaviors to improve, shifting exploration from blind trial-and-error to guided revision. Specifically, it selects the highest-reward failed trajectories (motivated by the Zone of Proximal Development), prompts the policy to revise them conditioned on the hindsight experience, and adds the best revised trajectory to the training group. A bonus reward (α·(r̃−r)) incentivizes responses with greater improvement potential under guidance, and a policy-shaping function f(x)=x/(x+γ) via regularized importance sampling stabilizes learning from low-probability tokens in revised trajectories. The training objective combines standard PPO-style clipped losses over both original rollouts and the hindsight-guided revision, with advantages computed over the joint group. A theoretical proposition bounds the gradient estimation error by the L1 norm of unsatisfied rubric weights, showing that satisfying more rubrics yields a more accurate policy gradient.

## Results

HeRL achieves the best results across all six benchmarks for all three model families. On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, HeRL reaches 82.4% on IFEval, 39.7% on IFBench, 83.4% on MulDimIF, 59.1% on WritingBench, 65.0% on LLMEval-Med, and 34.3% on HealthBench-500, improving over RLVR by +5.1, +8.1, +9.9, +4.3, +4.5, and +3.8 points respectively. On Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, HeRL gains are even larger on harder benchmarks (e.g., +48.9 over base on MulDimIF, +12.1 on HealthBench-500). Notably, HeRL also improves on WritingBench (a domain not seen during training), while SFT and DPO degrade on it.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HeRL consistently outperforms the RLVR baseline (based on DeepSeek-R1-style GRPO with rubric-level verifiable rewards) across all six benchmarks and all three model families, with the largest margins on harder benchmarks (e.g., +8.1 on IFBench and +9.9 on MulDimIF for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct). Compared to DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), HeRL wins on every benchmark; DPO actually degrades performance on IFEval (−5.7 for Qwen2.5-7B) and WritingBench (−4.9), while HeRL improves on both. Against SFT, HeRL is more stable: SFT causes severe cross-domain drops (e.g., −21.2 on LLMEval-Med for Qwen2.5-7B), whereas HeRL improves or maintains performance across all domains. On out-of-distribution benchmarks (MATH-500, GPQA, MMLU-Pro), HeRL preserves or slightly improves base-model performance, while RLVR shows mixed results (e.g., RLVR drops MATH-500 by 1.4 for Qwen2.5-7B, whereas HeRL improves it by +1.6). The Tulu 3-style RLVR formulation (Lambert et al., 2024) is subsumed under the RLVR baseline and is similarly outperformed by HeRL.

## Relation to Prior Work

HeRL extends the rubric-based RLVR paradigm (RaR, Huang et al., AdvancedIF) by repurposing the language descriptions of unmet rubrics as in-context hindsight experience for guided exploration, rather than using rubrics solely as scalar reward signals. It departs from prior exploration strategies in LLM RL—structured tree search (TreeRL, FR3E) and intrinsic-reward-driven methods (curiosity, diversity, entropy)—by arguing that effective exploration must be *aligned with the optimization target*, not merely diversified. The bonus reward and regularized importance sampling (policy shaping) draw on classical RL ideas (ZPD-inspired curriculum, off-policy correction) but are tailored to the rubric-guided revision setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HeRL addresses a practical bottleneck: standard RLVR struggles to discover high-quality samples for hard problems, and the paper shows that rubric-language guidance can bootstrap exploration beyond the current policy distribution with a theoretical guarantee (Proposition 3.1) linking unmet-rubric mass to gradient estimation error. The test-time transfer of experience-guided self-improvement suggests the capability internalized during training generalizes to inference-time scaling. Key limitations include dependence on high-quality, broad-coverage rubric datasets (scarce and expensive to create) and the use of static rubrics that may not track the model's evolving capability boundary; adaptive, difficulty-aware rubrics are identified as a natural next step.
