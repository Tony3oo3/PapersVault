## Problem

In standard Mixture-of-Experts models, once the router selects a set of experts, each is executed at full capacity, leaving the amount of computation within each activated expert fixed. This creates large discontinuities in the accuracy-compute tradeoff and wastes computation on experts that contribute little to the final output. The paper addresses the unexplored axis of conditional computation: adapting not only which experts are active but also how much of each expert's internal capacity is utilized.

## Input / Output

The model takes a token representation x ∈ R^d and a per-token width budget Γ (measured in full-expert widths) as inputs; during test-time training, a short calibration stream (50 batches) is additionally used to learn the sharpness parameter γ. The output is a next-token prediction, with the internal mechanism producing per-expert width assignments w_i(x) ∈ [0.25, 1.0] that determine how many of the 4d intermediate units each activated expert uses. Widths are derived from normalized router probabilities raised to γ, scaled by Γ, and projected to the valid discrete set {0.25, 0.30, …, 1.00}.

## Method

MoSE equips each expert FFN with a nested slimmable structure: the intermediate hidden dimension (4d) is sliced to m(w) = ⌈w·4d⌉ units, so all widths share parameters through ordered subnetworks. Pre-training samples two widths per iteration (full width w_max and one random w ~ Uniform(w_min, w_max)), backpropagating through the average of both losses alongside standard MoE auxiliary objectives (load-balancing and router z-loss). At inference, widths are assigned by raising normalized router probabilities to a sharpness γ, normalizing into allocation weights, and scaling by the budget Γ; a lightweight test-time training step then optimizes γ (shared or layer-wise) on a short calibration stream while keeping all pretrained weights frozen. The key insight is that routing probabilities already encode token-specific expert importance, so lower-probability experts can be executed at reduced widths with limited quality loss, enabling a continuous compute-quality frontier from a single pretrained checkpoint.

## Results

On OpenWebText, MoSE with test-time training (TTT) achieves the lowest perplexity across all model scales: 38.48 (GPT2-Small, 3B tokens) vs. 41.82 for standard MoE, and 20.38 (GPT2-Standard, 15B tokens) vs. 20.81 for MoE. At larger scale, MoSE delivers 32.8% lower FLOPs at matched perplexity on GPT2-Large (2.5B, 50B tokens), 40.8% FLOPs reduction under E128A8 fine-grained routing, and 38.1% FLOPs reduction on DeepSeek-V2-Lite (16B) after continual pre-training. On zero-shot downstream reasoning (GPT2-Medium), MoSE(TTT) improves HellaSwag accuracy from 0.335 to 0.385 and PIQA from 0.675 to 0.710 while reducing MFLOPs/token from 402.7 to 272.5 and 319.1 respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is standard sparsely-gated MoE (Shazeer et al., 2017), where all activated experts run at full width. At full width (w=1.0), MoSE matches or slightly improves MoE: e.g., GPT2-Small 3B tokens drops from 41.82 to 39.02 OpenWebText PPL, and GPT2-Standard 15B tokens improves from 20.81 to 20.65. With test-time training, MoSE consistently outperforms MoE on both perplexity and zero-shot accuracy while using fewer FLOPs per token — the TTT variant achieves the best results in every row of Table 1. On downstream reasoning (Table 2), MoSE(TTT) beats MoE on all three benchmarks (HellaSwag +5.0 acc, PIQA +3.5 acc, SIQA +1.5 acc) while reducing MFLOPs/token by 32–40%. The paper also shows that the learned sharpness parameter γ transfers across datasets (OpenWebText → LAMBADA) without re-optimization, and that E16A3 routing at inference matches E16A4 quality at lower compute, indicating diminishing returns from additional activated experts.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoSE combines two established lines of work—sparsely-gated Mixture-of-Experts (Shazeer et al., 2017) and slimmable networks with nested subnetworks (Yu et al., 2019; Horvath et al., 2021)—into a single architecture that adds a second axis of conditional computation beyond expert selection. The multi-width training recipe (sampling full width plus one random width per iteration) is directly adapted from random-width training in slimmable networks, while the routing and auxiliary losses follow standard MoE practice. The paper positions itself against recent elastic MoE approaches (EMoE, RoE) that modify routing or use test-time ensembles, arguing that width adaptation targets the dominant compute bottleneck (expert FFNs) using information already present in router probabilities, without changing expert representations or incurring ensemble overhead.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MoSE provides a practical, low-overhead mechanism for a single pretrained MoE model to support a continuous spectrum of accuracy-compute tradeoffs at inference time, with the TTT calibration requiring only 50 batches to learn a scalar (or per-layer scalar) γ. The 32–40% FLOPs reductions at matched perplexity across GPT-2 scales and the 38% reduction on DeepSeek-V2-Lite via continual pre-training suggest the technique could be adopted in production MoE deployments where latency budgets vary. Key open questions include validation at frontier MoE scale (the authors acknowledge this gap), behavior in memory-bound serving regimes where FLOPs savings may not translate linearly to wall-clock gains, and safety/robustness evaluation across different execution widths as an additional behavioral axis.
