## Problem

Hybrid sequence models combining Transformer and state-space model (SSM) layers have shown strong empirical gains over pure architectures, yet there is no principled understanding of when or why hybrids outperform their constituents. This paper asks: for what fundamental tasks do pure Transformers and pure SSMs provably face expressivity-efficiency tradeoffs that hybrids can overcome, and what is the underlying mechanism?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260778/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a long token sequence of length L over a vocabulary V and produces a predicted token at the final position. The key structural property of the studied tasks (S2FC) is that the answer depends on two components: a control variable v extracted from the full long context (e.g., the most recent number token, or a decoded binary subsequence) and a local content-addressable lookup u of moderate length m ≪ L. This decomposition is what makes the tasks hard for pure architectures: the control variable requires long-range access (hard for windowed Transformers) while the lookup requires large state (hard for SSMs).

## Method

The paper introduces the S2FC (Structured Two-Layer Function Composition) task family and proves two complementary hardness results: under an injectivity condition, any pure SSM requires state space exponential in the lookup width m (Theorem 3.3), and under a global-sensitivity condition, any sliding-window Transformer requires window size linear in context length L (Theorem 3.7). The core construction then pairs a Mamba SSM layer that acts as a compressor—storing the control variable in a small O(|V|)-sized state and passing a summarized representation forward—with a Transformer attention layer that performs the content-addressable lookup using only a small sliding window of size O(N) or Õ(|V|) rather than Ω(L). For selective copying, a 2-layer hybrid (Mamba → attention) with embedding dimension O(max(log|V|, log L)) solves the task exactly; for associative recall with decoding, a 3-layer hybrid (Mamba → attention → attention) achieves 99% accuracy. Empirically, learned hybrids trained with standard supervised objectives outperform pure baselines by up to 6× in parameter efficiency and show stronger length generalization (~10% accuracy gap on long sequences) and OOD robustness (~15% higher accuracy under distribution shift).

## Results

On Selective Copying, the SSM→TF hybrid achieves 99.9% accuracy at ~2000 parameters, while the pure Transformer reaches only 35.2% and the pure SSM 30.5% at the same scale; at ~12000 parameters, pure models plateau at ~92–93% while the hybrid remains at 100%. On Multi-Key Associative Recall, the SSM→TF hybrid attains 98.9% accuracy at ~12000 parameters versus 66.8% (pure TF) and 51.7% (pure SSM). On Associative Recall with Decoding, no pure model exceeds 40% accuracy at tested scales while the hybrid surpasses 50%. In length generalization, hybrids lose accuracy at the slowest rate, maintaining a ~10% advantage over Transformers on longer sequences, and in OOD testing the hybrid attains up to 15% higher accuracy than either pure model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the SSM→TF hybrid against two pure baselines: a GPT-NeoX Transformer (with sliding-window attention) and a Mamba SSM, matched by parameter count. On Selective Copying, the hybrid reaches 90%+ accuracy with roughly 6× fewer parameters than either pure model (e.g., 0.999 at ~2000 params vs. 0.352 for TF and 0.305 for SSM). On Multi-Key Associative Recall, the hybrid similarly achieves 60% accuracy with 6× fewer parameters than the pure Transformer (0.989 vs. 0.668 at ~12000 params). On Associative Recall with Decoding, the separation is most dramatic: at all tested scales (up to ~1M parameters), neither pure model exceeds 40% accuracy while the hybrid surpasses 50%. On Needle in a Haystack, the hybrid and SSM both outperform the Transformer, with the hybrid showing a slight edge. In length generalization and OOD robustness experiments at ~100M parameters, the hybrid consistently outperforms both pure baselines by 10–15% accuracy, confirming that the expressivity advantage persists under standard training and distribution shifts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the expressivity-analysis line of work initiated by Jelassi et al. (2024), who proved that Transformers outperform SSMs on copying tasks, by introducing the S2FC (Structured Two-Layer Function Composition) framework that unifies a broad family of tasks under a single hardness lens. Where prior results characterized limitations of one architecture on one specific task, here the authors show that under natural conditions (injectivity for SSMs, global sensitivity for Transformers), *both* pure architectures simultaneously fail on the same task family, and then construct hybrid models (Mamba + attention) that provably overcome both limitations. The hybrid constructions build directly on the Mamba layer formalism (Gu & Dao, 2024) and the GPT-NeoX attention implementation, while the empirical comparisons echo the empirical hybrid studies of Jamba, Samba, and Nemotron-H that lacked a theoretical grounding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first principled theoretical account of *why* hybrid sequence models outperform their pure counterparts, moving the field beyond empirical observation to a mechanistic understanding grounded in information-theoretic and sensitivity arguments. The S2FC framework provides a reusable template for identifying tasks where hybrids should be expected to help, which could guide future architecture design and benchmark construction. Key limitations include the focus on synthetic tasks, restricted sliding-window attention (no full or sparse patterns), and small model scales (≤100M params); extending the theory to broader attention mechanisms, external memory, and real long-context workloads are explicitly flagged as important open directions.
