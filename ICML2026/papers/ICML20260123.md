## Problem

Circuit tracing in language models has relied on learned feature bases such as sparse autoencoders (SAEs) on the assumption that individual neurons are too polysemantic to serve as interpretable units of computation. This paper challenges that assumption by showing that MLP neurons, when properly targeted and attributed, form circuits that are as sparse and faithful as SAE-based circuits, eliminating the need for additional training costs, reconstruction error, and the polysemanticity artifacts of learned dictionaries.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a frozen language model (Llama 3.1 8B) and takes text sequences as input, optionally paired with counterfactual sequences that flip a target behavior (e.g., singular→plural subject). It produces a sparse directed subgraph over individual pre-down-projection MLP neurons, where node importance is given by RelP attribution scores and edge weights are computed via normalized attribution flow. Circuits are evaluated by mean-ablating the complement and measuring faithfulness and completeness against the full model's logit differences on held-out pairs.

## Method

The key insight is that pre-down-projection MLP activations form a 'privileged basis' because the element-wise SiLU nonlinearity makes each activation coordinate a natural feature, yielding circuits roughly 100× sparser than post-down-projection MLP outputs. For attribution scoring, the paper replaces Integrated Gradients with RelP (Relevance Patching), which linearizes all nonlinearities (RMSNorm, SiLU, attention softmax) via stop-gradients and computes a single backward pass on the resulting replacement model. A 'half rule' divides gradients by two through multiplicative interactions in gated MLPs to preserve the completeness property, ensuring total attribution is conserved layer-by-layer without numerical integration. Circuits are constructed by greedily selecting the top-k neurons by attribution score, with edges computed via RelP with additional stop-gradients on intermediate MLP layers to isolate direct causal effects. The approach requires no additional training, no SAEs in memory, and works in both paired (counterfactual) and unpaired (zero-baseline) settings.

## Results

On the SVA benchmark, MLP activation neurons with RelP achieve near-perfect faithfulness and completeness with only ~200 neurons, yielding circuits ~100× smaller than those based on MLP outputs. On the MIB circuit-localisation benchmark (Llama 3.1), RelP achieves the best CMD score (lower is better) on three of five tasks (IOI: 0.01, Arithmetic: 0.00, MCQA: 0.11) and is second-best on the remaining two (ARC-E: 0.15, ARC-C: 0.15), with an average CMD of 0.08—tied with EAP(CF) and better than all other baselines. In the multi-hop city-state-capital case study, a circuit of 23 key neurons clusters into six interpretable groups matching the reasoning steps (city→state→capital), and steering a single neuron (L23/N8079-) flips the top output from the capital to the state in a majority of examples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Llama Scope SAEs (He et al., 2024), MLP activation neurons with RelP close the sparsity gap: circuits of ~200 neurons reach near-perfect faithfulness/completeness on SVA, comparable to SAE-feature circuits, while MLP outputs require ~100× more neurons. Against Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al., 2017), RelP outperforms IG in almost all settings on SVA (Figures 2 and 4), requiring fewer neurons for equivalent faithfulness while needing only one backward pass versus IG's 10. On the MIB benchmark, RelP ties EAP(CF) (Nanda, 2023) at 0.08 average CMD but beats it on MCQA (0.11 vs 0.09) and ARC-E/ARC-C (0.15 vs 0.11/0.18); it also beats EAP-IG-inp.(CF) (Hanna et al., 2024) on MCQA (0.11 vs 0.14) and both ARC tasks (0.15 vs 0.11/0.22), and substantially outperforms EAP-IG-act.(CF) (Marks et al., 2025) on ARC-E (0.15 vs 0.30) and ARC-C (0.15 vs 0.37). RelP dominates all attribution-patching baselines (NAP, NAP-IG) and IFR (Mueller et al., 2025) by large margins across every MIB task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in circuit tracing that has moved away from the neuron basis toward learned sparse dictionaries (SAEs, transcoders, CLTs) on the assumption that individual neurons are not an interpretable or sparse basis. It builds on RelP (Jafari et al., 2025) for efficient gradient-based attribution and the replacement-model linearization framework from Ali et al. (2022), but is the first to apply RelP directly to individual MLP neurons and to compute neuron-to-neuron edge weights. The key conceptual departure is identifying the pre-down-projection MLP activations as a 'privileged basis' (due to the element-wise nonlinearity) that had been underexplored relative to MLP outputs, combined with the half rule from LRP (Arras et al., 2019) to preserve completeness through gated MLPs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If neuron-level circuits are as sparse and faithful as SAE circuits, the field can avoid the substantial training costs, reconstruction errors, and polysemanticity issues (splitting, absorption) that plague learned feature bases, while retaining access to circuits throughout a model's training. The paper acknowledges that circuits still contain too many neurons for easy human interpretation and that the serial autograd implementation is computationally inefficient, pointing to follow-up work on automated clustering and description. Open questions include whether the sparsity advantage generalizes to harder, less templatic tasks, and whether architectural trends toward sparsity (gated MLPs, mixture-of-experts) will further strengthen the neuron basis as models scale.
