## Problem

Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) built on LLMs frequently incorporate verification sub-agents to check intermediate outputs, yet whether process-level verification actually improves MAS performance remains unclear. This paper addresses the gap by systematically studying when, how, and to what extent process verification benefits MAS, spanning verification types, granularities, context management strategies, and problem solvability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264545/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a task query and partial multi-agent trajectories as input, with the accumulated context history managed via four strategies (step-only, summarized context + step, summarized context + step, or full raw history). At each verification point, N=3 candidate continuations are generated in parallel and scored by the verifier, which outputs a ranking; the highest-ranked continuation is selected to resume the MAS workflow. The system is implemented as a plug-and-play client-middleware-server wrapper that requires minimal code modifications to any off-the-shelf MAS framework.

## Method

MAS-ProVe is a systematic empirical study framework that integrates process verification into MAS test-time scaling via a modular client-middleware-server architecture, where the client executes the original MAS logic, the middleware intercepts and reorganizes context, and the server hosts a pool of verifiers. It evaluates three verification paradigms—LLM-as-a-Judge (GPT-5-mini, GPT-4o-mini, FARE-20B), reward models (Skywork-Reward-V2), and process reward models (Qwen2.5-Math-PRM)—at two granularities (agent-level and iteration-level) across four context management strategies, using greedy best-first search with N=3 parallel candidates. Experiments span six MAS frameworks (Debate, AFlow, ADAS, MaAS, DyLAN, MAS-Zero) on five reasoning benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, GAIA, HumanEval, GPQA). Key findings include: LLM-as-a-Judge outperforms reward-based verifiers in 24 of 36 configurations, no single granularity universally dominates across architectures, summarized context outperforms raw history for long-context MAS, and process verification primarily reduces variance on solvable queries rather than recovering fundamentally unsolvable problems.

## Results

Across six MAS frameworks and five benchmarks, LLM-as-a-Judge (GPT-5-mini) achieves the highest accuracy in 24 of 36 experimental configurations, outperforming both the Skywork-Reward-V2 RM and Qwen2.5-Math-PRM. The best individual gains include MaAS on AIME24 (80.55% vs. 76.39% baseline, +4.16 pp) and DyLAN on AIME25 (71.11% vs. 65.55% baseline, +5.56 pp) at iteration level, while MAS-Zero shows the largest relative improvement on AIME24 (50.00% vs. 37.78%, +12.22 pp). However, process verification does not consistently improve performance: several configurations degrade (e.g., Debate on AIME25 drops from 61.87% to 53.33% at iteration level), and high variance (±8–15 pp) is common, particularly for MAS-Zero and AFlow. The finetuned FARE-20B verifier further outperforms general-purpose judges, reaching 51.11% on AIME24 and 21.15% on GAIA for MAS-Zero.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the six MAS frameworks (Debate, AFlow, ADAS, MaAS, DyLAN, MAS-Zero), process verification with LLM-as-a-Judge yields inconsistent gains: MaAS benefits most at iteration level (+9.78 pp on GAIA, +9.73 pp on AIME25), while DyLAN shows mixed results (gains on AIME but degradation on GAIA). Against the Skywork-Reward-V2 RM, the generative judge wins in the majority of configurations (e.g., Debate AIME24: 81.11% vs. 71.67% at agent level; MaAS AIME24: 80.55% vs. 84.03% at agent level where RM actually wins). Against Qwen2.5-Math-PRM, the judge dominates more clearly (e.g., AFlow AIME24: 76.22% vs. 58.33%; DyLAN AIME24: 85.56% vs. 77.78%). The finetuned FARE-20B verifier outperforms both GPT-5-mini and GPT-4o-mini judges in most settings (e.g., MAS-Zero AIME24: 51.11% vs. 47.78% vs. 42.22%; ADAS AIME25: 66.66% vs. 58.33% vs. 63.87%), confirming that evaluation-specific finetuning provides additional gains over general-purpose LLMs-as-Judges.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two well-studied threads: test-time scaling via process verification (reward models, process reward models, LLM-as-a-Judge) and multi-agent system design (debate, workflow search, adaptive topologies). While process verification has been extensively studied in single-agent reasoning settings (math, code) and LLM-as-a-Judge has been evaluated for reranking, no prior work has systematically examined how these verifiers behave when applied to the heterogeneous, multi-step trajectories produced by MAS. The MAS-ProVe framework repurposes existing verifiers and MAS architectures as plug-and-play components within a unified client-middleware-server protocol, explicitly acknowledging that all verifiers are operating out-of-distribution since none were trained on multi-agent trajectories.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that process verification does not consistently improve MAS performance and frequently exhibits high variance—challenges the widespread assumption that adding verification sub-agents to MAS will reliably catch intermediate errors before they propagate. The paper identifies a fundamental solvability ceiling: verification can stabilize solvable queries but cannot resurrect fundamentally unsolvable ones, and the context-length-performance trade-off means that naively feeding full trajectories to verifiers can actually hurt. For practitioners building MAS, this suggests that the verification bottleneck is not simply a matter of choosing a better scorer but reflects a deeper difficulty in reliably evaluating partial multi-agent reasoning, an open challenge that will require new paradigms beyond current LLM-as-a-Judge or reward-model approaches.
