## Problem

Current deep learning methods for biomolecular function prediction constrain outputs to predefined categorical labels, require separate task-specific models that must be retrained for new prediction objectives, and produce one-shot static outputs that cannot be iteratively refined. This paper argues that these limitations prevent capturing the multifaceted, context-dependent nature of DNA, RNA, and protein functions, and advocates for multi-modal large language models as a unified, interactive, and zero-shot generalizable alternative.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266530/cropped_page5_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes amino acid sequences (processed at atom-level resolution with coordinates and bonds, truncated to 600 residues), DNA nucleotide sequences (modeled at single-nucleotide resolution with context up to 1M tokens), and RNA nucleotide sequences (bidirectionally encoded via Mamba), each paired with a natural language prompt specifying the prediction task. Outputs are free-form natural language descriptions of biomolecular function rather than discrete GO terms or EC numbers, with training targets capped at 400 characters. The RNA-to-Text variant additionally ingests a predicted 3D structure (from ~2,300 RNA 3D Hub pairs) as a second modality fused with sequence embeddings before text generation.

## Method

The paper proposes three multi-modal LLM architectures sharing a common design: a domain-specific encoder (atom-level GNN+Transformer for proteins, HyenaDNA+DNABERT fusion for DNA, bidirectional Mamba for RNA), a lightweight adaptor projecting domain embeddings into the LLM token space, and a pretrained LLM (fine-tuned via LoRA, rank 8) that generates free-form functional text. The key architectural insight is that task specification is handled entirely through natural language prompts rather than separate classification heads, enabling a single fixed-weight model to perform diverse tasks (catalytic function, ligand binding, coenzyme interaction, biological process, cellular localization) without retraining. For RNA, a Mamba-based sequence-to-structure module predicts 3D conformation, which is encoded by a structure encoder and cross-modally fused with sequence embeddings before feeding the LLM. Training uses paired biomolecule-text data from UniProt (~523K proteins), NCBI (~230K genes), and RNAcentral (~47K RNAs), with a 1:2:10 sampling ratio across keyword-based, rule-based, and manually annotated Q&A pairs over 210K steps.

## Results

ProteinChat outperforms GPT-4 by over tenfold in human expert assessments (0–2 scale) for free-form protein function prediction on 200 Swiss-Prot proteins. In automated metrics, ProteinChat achieves substantially higher SimCSE, BLEU-1, and BLEU-2 scores than GPT-4 (both with amino acid sequence and protein name inputs). For gene function prediction, the Gene-to-Text LLM achieves SimCSE 0.75 and BLEU-1 0.18, compared to GPT-4's 0.31 and 0.13 respectively. Across five protein prediction tasks (catalytic function, ligand binding, coenzyme-enzyme interaction, biological process, cellular component), ProteinChat significantly outperforms both task-specific classifiers (xTrimoPGLM-1B + MLP) and GPT-4 without any task-specific retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ProteinChat outperforms two state-of-the-art Gene Ontology classifiers — DeepGOPlus (Kulmanov & Hoehndorf, 2020) and NetGO 3.0 (Wang et al., 2023) — in GO term prediction, where a prediction is correct only if the ground-truth GO term is ranked highest. Against four additional baselines (ESM/xTrimoPGLM-1B, Struct2GO, SaProt, and GearNet), ProteinChat achieves superior performance across all five prediction tasks (catalytic function, ligand binding, coenzyme-enzyme interaction, biological process, and cellular component), as shown in Figure 4. Compared to task-specific classifiers built on xTrimoPGLM-1B with MLP heads (both frozen-encoder and fully-finetuned variants), ProteinChat wins on all five tasks using only prompt-based conditioning without retraining. GPT-4, prompted with either protein names or amino acid sequences, is consistently outperformed by ProteinChat in both free-form generation (Figure 1) and structured classification tasks (Figure 2a). For gene function prediction, the Gene-to-Text LLM more than doubles GPT-4's SimCSE score (0.75 vs. 0.31) and improves BLEU-1 by ~38% (0.18 vs. 0.13).

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the dominant paradigm of task-specific classifiers (DeepGOPlus, NetGO, ESM, SaProt, GearNet, Struct2GO) that reduce biomolecular functions to predefined GO categories and require separate models per task. It builds on recent foundation models—HyenaDNA and DNABERT for DNA, Mamba-based SSMs for RNA, and atom-level GNN+Transformer encoders for proteins—as modular backbones, but repositions them within a unified generative LLM pipeline where a single model handles diverse tasks via prompt conditioning. The key conceptual departure from prior multimodal biological models (Garau-Luis et al., Tang et al., Mo et al.) is the explicit framing of function prediction as free-form text generation with interactive multi-turn dialogue, rather than as classification or fixed-schema annotation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper articulates a compelling argument that biomolecular functions are inherently multifaceted, context-dependent, and poorly captured by discrete categorical labels, making free-form generative outputs a more faithful representation. However, significant open questions remain: the empirical evidence is preliminary (200-protein evaluation, single-seed results), the full RNA-to-Text pipeline is described but not yet validated end-to-end, and the hallucination risk in free-form biomedical predictions is acknowledged but not rigorously quantified. The practical impact hinges on whether prompt-driven zero-shot generalization to genuinely novel biomolecules (not just rephrasing known annotations) can be demonstrated at scale, and on developing evaluation protocols that go beyond SimCSE/BLEU to capture biological correctness.
