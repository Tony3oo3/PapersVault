## Problem

While continuous-time gradient flow analyses predict layer norm balancing and kernel methods predict near-linear output evolution, neither captures how discrete, finite-step learning-rate choices affect signal-residual coupling in gradient updates and early generalization. The paper asks: what is the exact relationship between layer-wise learning-rate allocation and test loss after one or two gradient descent steps in linear networks, and when do symmetric versus asymmetric rates become optimal?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263571/cropped_page3_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes two-layer (W1, W2 ∈ R^{h×h}) or three-layer (W1, W2 ∈ R^{h×h}, a ∈ R^h) linear networks under random orthogonal initialization with n = h = d, trained on data generated by a linear teacher model, with layer-wise learning rates η1, η2 constrained below critical thresholds (O(h√h) for two-layer, O(h) for three-layer). It produces exact closed-form expressions for expected test loss after one and two GD steps as explicit polynomials in η1 and η2, along with rigorous operator-norm bounds on the gradient approximation error (O(h^{-1}) after one step, O(h^{-1/2}) after two). The key structural output is a phase-transition characterization: symmetric rates are suboptimal after one step but become locally optimal after two steps in sufficiently wide networks.

## Method

The core technique is a gradient decomposition G_ℓ = B_ℓ − A_ℓ that separates each layer's gradient into a signal-aligned component A_ℓ (proportional to the target matrix M or vector β*) and a self-interaction residual B_ℓ (involving weight Gram matrices). Under random orthogonal initialization, B_ℓ is shown to be O(1/√h) smaller in operator norm than A_ℓ, justifying replacement of exact gradients with their leading-order signal components and yielding a tractable surrogate loss with tight approximation error. This simplification enables closed-form derivation of the test loss as an explicit polynomial in η1 and η2, from which the paper proves that symmetric learning rates are suboptimal after one step (due to distinct representation vs. readout roles of the two layers) but become locally optimal after two steps in sufficiently wide networks. The analysis further identifies critical learning-rate thresholds (η = O(h√h) for two-layer, η = O(h) for three-layer) beyond which self-interaction effects dominate, connecting to maximal-update parameterization regimes, and extends the framework to three-layer networks where the fixed output vector a reduces the admissible regime and amplifies higher-order cross-layer products.

## Results

The paper derives exact closed-form test loss expressions after 1 and 2 gradient descent steps for 2-layer (Theorem 5.3) and 3-layer (Theorem 5.5) linear networks under orthogonal initialization. The key quantitative finding is a critical learning-rate threshold: η = O(h√h) for 2-layer and η = O(h) for 3-layer networks, beyond which self-interaction terms become non-negligible. Corollary 5.4 proves that symmetric learning rates (η₁ = η₂) are NOT a local minimum of the 1-step test loss for any 0 < α ≤ 3/2, but DO become a local minimum of the 2-step test loss for 1 < α ≤ 3/2 when h exceeds a width-dependent threshold h*. The approximation error from replacing exact gradients with signal-aligned components is bounded by O(h⁻¹) after one step and O(h⁻¹/²) after two steps (Lemma 5.2).

|---|---|---|
| Critical LR threshold | η = O(h√h) | η = O(h) |
| 1-step: symmetric LR optimal? | No (any 0 < α ≤ 3/2) | No (any 0 < α < 1) |
| 2-step: symmetric LR optimal? | Yes (1 < α ≤ 3/2, h > h*) | Yes (0 < α ≤ 2/3, h > h*) |

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without traditional method-vs-baseline comparisons. The central comparison is between symmetric (η₁ = η₂) and asymmetric layer-wise learning rate allocations, revealing that symmetry is suboptimal after one GD step but becomes locally optimal after two steps in sufficiently wide networks. The paper also compares 2-layer and 3-layer architectures, showing the 3-layer setting has a stricter admissible LR regime (O(h) vs. O(h√h)) and richer higher-order coupling in the 2-step test loss. In Section 7, the proposed Frobenius-norm-based LR scheduler (which assigns η ∝ ||W_other||_F / (||W₁||_F + ||W₂||_F)) is compared against a uniform LR baseline on a matrix-factorization task with imbalanced initialization (||W₂||_F = 6, ||W₁||_F = 1); the proposed scheduler achieves lower training and test loss and drives the norm gap |‖W₁‖_F − ‖W₂‖_F| → 0 in mid-to-late training. Experiments under Gaussian initialization, noisy labels, deeper (4/8-layer) linear networks, and a 3-layer ReLU network all confirm the asymmetry-to-balance transition, validating the generality of the theoretical predictions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gradient decomposition framework of Ba et al. (2022)—which separates signal-aligned from residual components in one-step updates—into a multi-step, layer-wise learning-rate analysis for linear networks. It departs from the continuous-time gradient flow and norm-balancing analyses of Du et al. (2018) and Ye & Du (2021) by providing exact closed-form test loss expressions after a finite number of discrete GD steps, and from kernel/NTK approximations by studying the regime where updates are large enough to matter. The teacher-student setup and the identification of critical learning-rate thresholds (O(h√h) for two layers, O(h) for three layers) connect directly to the maximal-update parameterization literature (Yang & Hu, 2021; Yang et al., 2022), but the paper derives these thresholds from first principles under orthogonal initialization rather than assuming a fixed output layer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first finite-step theoretical characterization linking layer-wise learning-rate allocation directly to test loss, revealing a concrete phase transition (asymmetric → symmetric optimal LRs) that was previously only observed empirically through heuristics like LARS, AutoLR, or TempBalance. This gives practitioners a principled starting point for designing step-dependent learning-rate schedulers, as demonstrated by the Frobenius-norm-based scheduler in Section 7. Key limitations include restriction to linear networks, orthogonal (or Gaussian) initialization, and analysis of only the first two GD steps; extending the framework to nonlinear activations, stochastic optimization, adaptive optimizers, and deeper architectures remains open and is acknowledged as a natural next direction.
