## Problem

Fine-tuning large models on edge devices is blocked by the memory cost of backpropagation, which requires storing intermediate activations far beyond typical edge hardware capacity. While zeroth-order (ZO) optimization eliminates this memory bottleneck by using only forward passes, it suffers from a dimension-dependent convergence slowdown that makes it impractically slow for high-dimensional models. The paper asks whether a hybrid first-order/zeroth-order split can simultaneously achieve inference-level client memory and first-order convergence speed.

## Input / Output

Clients consume local data (32×32 RGB images for CIFAR-10/100, or text sequences for GLUE/SQuAD) and produce smashed activations (intermediate feature vectors) sent to the server, which completes the forward pass and returns activation gradients. For model aggregation, clients upload only P scalar projections (P=2–5, independent of model dimension) rather than full parameter vectors, and receive back P aggregated scalars plus shared random seeds. The final outputs are class labels (vision), classification scores (GLUE), or answer spans (SQuAD), with the model split after the 2nd residual block (ResNet-18) or after the first 3–5 transformer layers (LLMs).

## Method

HO-SFL reformulates split federated learning via a Lagrangian decomposition that treats the consistency between client activations and server inputs as an equality constraint, decoupling the joint optimization into a server-side first-order sub-problem and a client-side zeroth-order sub-problem. The server performs standard backpropagation to update its parameters and backtransmits the activation gradient λ = ∇_z ℓ, which defines a local proxy objective L_c(θ_c) = λ^T f_c(x; θ_c) for the client. The client then estimates the gradient of this proxy objective using P Gaussian perturbations (shared via random seeds), computing only P scalar projections v_p = λ^T(f_c(x; θ_c + μu_p) − z) that are uploaded for server-side aggregation. This yields dimension-free uplink communication (O(P) scalars instead of O(d_c) parameters) and a convergence rate of O(√(d_c)/(PT)) that depends only on the small client-side dimension d_c rather than the full model dimension d, effectively isolating the ZO estimation difficulty to a low-dimensional subspace.

## Results

On GLUE tasks, HO-SFL matches or exceeds the first-order SplitLoRA baseline across model scales: e.g., 93.9% vs 94.4% on SST-2 (LLaMA-3.2-1B), 73.3% vs 70.0% on RTE (LLaMA-3.2-1B), and 90.8% vs 90.3% on SST-2 (Gemma-3-270M), while the pure ZO-SFL baseline collapses to ~52–62% accuracy. On SQuAD, HO-SFL stays within 0.01–0.02 F1 of SplitLoRA from 125M to 8B parameters (e.g., 0.9389 vs 0.9413 on Qwen3-8B). Communication cost drops to 2.44 GB (vs 4.20 GB for SFL and 3.71 GB for FSL-SAGE), and peak client memory for LLaMA-3.2-1B fine-tuning is reduced to 4.14 GB—nearly at inference level (4.01 GB)—compared to 8.78 GB for standard SFL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SplitLoRA (the first-order SFL+LoRA baseline), HO-SFL is competitive on most GLUE tasks, winning on 5 of 9 model-task pairs (e.g., +3.3 pts on RTE with LLaMA-3.2-1B, +5.4 pts on RTE with Gemma-3-270M) while trailing by at most 3.8 pts (WSC on OPT-125M). On SQuAD, HO-SFL is within 0.02 F1 of SplitLoRA across all four model sizes, confirming no convergence degradation at scale. Against the pure zeroth-order ZO-SFL baseline, HO-SFL achieves a dramatic improvement—e.g., 87.6% vs 52.8% on SST-2 (OPT-125M) and 73.3% vs 49.1% on RTE (LLaMA-3.2-1B)—validating that the hybrid first-order/zeroth-order design is essential. On CIFAR-10, HO-SFL matches SFL (SplitFed) in the IID setting but outperforms it under Non-IID, while MU-SplitFed and ZO-SFL fail to converge within the 160k-sample budget. In terms of communication, HO-SFL (2.44 GB) is 42% lower than SFL (4.20 GB) and 34% lower than FSL-SAGE (3.71 GB), thanks to dimension-free scalar aggregation.

## Relation to Prior Work

HO-SFL builds on the Split Federated Learning (SplitFed) framework by reformulating its training loop through a Lagrangian decomposition, which naturally decouples the optimization into a server-side first-order sub-problem and a client-side zeroth-order sub-problem. The dimension-free aggregation mechanism (shared random seeds + scalar uploads) is directly adapted from DeComFL (Li et al., ICLR 2025), which demonstrated that ZO optimization can eliminate high-dimensional parameter transmission in federated settings. The key conceptual departure from prior SFL work (SplitFC, FSL-SAGE, GAS) is that those methods retain client-side backpropagation while optimizing communication or asynchrony, whereas HO-SFL eliminates client-side BP entirely. It also differs from concurrent hybrid-order methods (HERON-SFL, HO-SL) by deriving client updates from server-provided activation gradients rather than auxiliary surrogate losses, and by supporting multi-client aggregation with only P scalars.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for edge AI deployment: reducing client memory from 8.78 GB (standard SFL) to 4.14 GB (near inference-level 4.01 GB) for LLaMA-3.2-1B fine-tuning makes on-device LLM adaptation feasible on hardware that previously could not store BP activations. The theoretical result that the convergence rate depends only on the client-side dimension d_c (rather than the full model dimension d) provides a principled justification for why split learning is a natural setting for ZO optimization. Open questions include how the regularity bound Γ scales with model depth and architecture, whether the approach extends to multi-round local updates (the current analysis assumes one step per round), and how the method performs under more extreme data heterogeneity or partial participation with very small K.
