## Problem

Layer dropout (stochastic depth) has been largely abandoned from modern LLM pre-training recipes as models scaled to billions of parameters and trillions of tokens, with prior reports suggesting it degrades accuracy. However, no comprehensive study had systematically quantified this effect or identified configurations that mitigate degradation, leaving unresolved whether layer dropout can deliver its known benefits—reduced training FLOPs and zero-shot depth-elastic inference—without sacrificing base-model quality at scale.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a diverse corpus of natural language text and code, training decoder-only transformers (ALiBi positions, squared-ReLU activations, Llama3 vocabulary) from 271M to 8.2B parameters at compute-optimal 20 tokens-per-parameter budgets up to 160B tokens on Cerebras CS-3 systems. The output is a single pretrained model whose weights are frozen at inference time but which supports multiple depth-elastic execution modes—early exit at arbitrary layer, non-contiguous intermediate layer skipping, and self-speculative decoding via Draft & Verify—without any retraining or architectural modification.

## Method

The paper conducts a systematic empirical study (2400+ experiments) varying three axes of layer dropout: depth-wise distribution (uniform, increasing ILD, alternating ALD), temporal schedule (constant, increasing, decreasing), and granularity (whole transformer layer vs. attention/FFN sub-layers; per-batch vs. per-sequence masks). A critical technical contribution is the derivation of the r_train = 1/ρ scaling factor (ρ = 1 − p) from CompleteP's Maximal Residual Stream Update Desideratum, which stabilizes activation norms across effective depths and enables hyperparameter transfer across dropout rates without per-rate retuning. The optimal configuration—ILD (dropout rate increasing linearly from 0 at the first layer to p_max at the last) combined with DTS (rate decaying linearly from p_max to 0 over training steps) applied per-sequence at whole-layer granularity—achieves up to 20% training FLOPs savings while matching or beating dense baselines in validation loss. This configuration also induces zero-shot robustness: the average training dropout rate predicts early-exit loss, layer-skip resilience, and self-speculative decoding speedup (up to 1.55×), with aggressive rates (p_max up to 0.99) remaining effective at 8.2B scale.

## Results

With the recommended ILD+DTS configuration, layer dropout matches or beats dense baselines in validation loss while saving 15–25% training FLOPs: the 1.8B model achieves 1.836 val loss (vs. 1.849 dense) at 15% savings, the 3.9B model reaches 1.745 (vs. 1.732 dense) at 20% savings, and the 8.2B model attains 1.663 at 25% savings. At smaller scales (503M, 906M), ILD+DTS at 5% FLOPs savings achieves val losses of 2.110 and 1.951 respectively, matching or slightly beating the dense baselines (2.110, 1.953). For inference, dropout-trained models yield up to 1.55× self-speculative decoding speedup and dramatically reduce early-exit loss (e.g., 3.9B early exit at 0.75L: 2.143 vs. 3.834 for dense).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the dense (no-dropout) model trained at the same 20 TPP compute budget. At 1.8B scale, the 0.6(ILD,DTS) configuration beats the dense baseline in validation loss (1.836 vs. 1.849) while saving 15% FLOPs, and dramatically improves early-exit loss (2.329 vs. 3.943) and layer-skipping loss (2.282 vs. 4.260). At 3.9B, the 0.8(ILD,DTS) model is slightly worse in val loss (1.745 vs. 1.732, +0.75%) but still saves 20% FLOPs and achieves 1.54× speculative decoding speedup versus 1.02× for dense. At 8.2B with an extreme 0.99 max dropout rate (25% FLOPs savings), the model achieves 1.663 val loss with 1.55× speedup. Across all scales, the ILD+DTS configuration consistently outperforms uniform, constant-schedule, and increasing-schedule dropout variants, confirming that the specific combination of increasing-across-depth and decreasing-across-time is critical for matching dense accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper re-examines layer dropout (stochastic depth, Huang et al. 2016) for modern LLM pretraining, building directly on CompleteP's (Dey et al. 2025) maximal residual stream update desideratum to derive the critical r_train=1/ρ scaling factor that enables hyperparameter transfer across dropout rates. It departs from prior dropout-for-LLMs work (LayerDrop, Progressive Layer Dropout, LayerSkip) by systematically jointly optimizing optimizer hyperparameters, depth-wise distributions, and temporal schedules at compute-optimal budgets, rather than inheriting configurations from smaller-scale or multi-epoch regimes. The paper also positions layer dropout as a training-time mechanism for depth-elastic inference, connecting it to post-training methods like Draft & Verify and Balcony without modifying the pretrained architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that properly configured layer dropout (ILD + DTS) matches or beats dense baselines while saving up to 20% training FLOPs and enabling >1.5× inference speedup challenges the prevailing consensus that dropout is harmful in single-epoch, large-data LLM pretraining. Practical limitations include evaluation on a single architecture family (Celerity) and Cerebras CS-3 hardware, leaving open whether findings generalize to other backbones, datasets, or training stacks. Open questions include optimal dropout schedules for mid-training, SFT, and continual pre-training, as well as integration with router-based depth allocation and whether the aggressive 0.99 dropout regime at 8.2B parameters holds under different data distributions.
