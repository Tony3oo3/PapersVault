## Problem

Estimating the average treatment effect (ATE) from selected sub-populations (e.g., voluntary survey respondents) yields severely biased estimates of the full-population ATE. Existing identifiability results under selection bias either require knowing the exact location of selection in a causal DAG (graphical criteria like selection-backdoor) or impose strong parametric assumptions (non-Gaussian additive noise models), leaving many practical scenarios unidentifiable. No unified framework provides necessary and sufficient conditions for ATE identifiability under general selection bias with distributional assumptions weaker than those previously required.

## Input / Output

The method consumes selected observational data (X, Y, T) where S=1, along with an estimated propensity score ê(x) used to identify the region B where c < ê(x) < 1−c (i.e., where deterministic censoring is absent). It produces three outputs: the full-population ATE τ̂_P, a learned selection bias function β(x,y,t) that models how selection distorts the observed density relative to the true density, and the corrected conditional outcome distribution P̂(y|x,t). The key constraint is that the covariate-outcome distribution must belong to a specified smooth family (e.g., exponential of polynomials for deterministic selection, or Gaussian/Laplace/Pareto/Log-normal for non-deterministic selection) to enable extrapolation beyond the observed support.

## Method

The paper establishes Condition 1, a necessary and sufficient identifiability criterion expressed as: if two compatible distribution tuples have different ATEs, they must differ in the observed product α(x,y,t)·P_{t|x}·P_{xy(t)} at some point. This condition is instantiated for deterministic selection (Proposition 3.3, requiring C^∞ smoothness with polynomial log-densities) and non-deterministic selection (Proposition 3.5, requiring membership in Gaussian, Laplace, Pareto, or Log-normal families), both under c-overlap propensity scores. The core insight is leveraging truncated statistics (Daskalakis et al., 2021) to extrapolate the full distribution from the selected subset, eliminating the need to know where selection occurs in the causal graph. For estimation, a three-stage procedure is used: (1) filter to region B via the propensity score, (2) jointly estimate P(y|x,t) and the selection weight β(x,y,t) via regularized MLE or score matching (where the score decomposes additively as s_θ(x,y) + ∇_y log β_φ(x,y,t)), and (3) compute the ATE by reweighting observed samples with 1/β to recover the population density and performing counterfactual inference.

## Results

On synthetic data (N=5000, ~3000 after selection), the proposed MLE+β and SM+β methods consistently outperform naive IPW and polynomial regression across additive/multiplicative Gaussian and Laplace noise settings, with SM+β showing slightly lower variance than MLE+β. On the semi-synthetic All of Us dataset (N=10000, ~2500 after non-deterministic selection), both MLE+β and SM+β substantially reduce ATE estimation bias compared to baselines, though residual bias remains due to very low propensity scores (~0.05) and the complexity of the real-world distribution. The paper presents results via boxplots (Figures 2–3) rather than point estimates, emphasizing that IPW produces severely biased ATE estimates while the proposed correction methods recover estimates much closer to ground truth.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two primary baselines: naive inverse propensity weighting (IPW) and polynomial regression. Naive IPW, which estimates ATE only from the observed selected population without correcting for selection bias, produces significantly biased estimates in all experimental settings. Polynomial regression, which fits E[Y(t)|X=x] to adjust for covariates, handles deterministic selection (truncation) but fails under non-deterministic selection. The uncorrected versions of the proposed methods (MLE and Score Matching without the β selection function) improve over both baselines for deterministic selection but still suffer bias under non-deterministic selection. The full proposed methods (MLE+β and SM+β), which jointly estimate the outcome model and the selection probability function β(x,y,t), are the only approaches that handle both deterministic and non-deterministic selection, and they consistently achieve the lowest bias across all synthetic and semi-synthetic experiments. On the All of Us semi-synthetic benchmark, the proposed methods reduce bias substantially but do not eliminate it entirely, which the authors attribute to the extremely weak overlap (propensity scores ~0.05) in the real-world covariate distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant graphical-criteria approach to ATE identification under selection bias (Bareinboim & Pearl, 2012; Correa et al., 2019), which requires knowing the exact location of the selection mechanism in the causal DAG, by instead characterizing identifiability through distribution class restrictions on propensity scores, covariate-outcome distributions, and selection probabilities. The theoretical machinery draws on truncated statistics (Daskalakis et al., 2021) for extrapolating from selected to full distributions, and the proof structure follows the identifiability characterization framework of Cai et al. (2025). The paper shows that all existing graphical criteria (selection-backdoor, S-id) are special cases of its unified Condition 1, while also covering settings—such as outcome-dependent selection under Gaussian noise—that prior graphical and ANM-based approaches (Zhang et al., 2016) declared unidentifiable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The necessary-and-sufficient characterization (Theorem 3.1) gives practitioners a clear, testable criterion for when ATE is recoverable from selected data, and the instantiation to common distribution families (Gaussian, Laplace, Pareto, Log-normal) means the theory applies to many realistic settings without requiring the strong non-Gaussian ANM assumptions of prior work. However, the estimation results on the All of Us semi-synthetic dataset show that bias is reduced but not eliminated, suggesting the distributional smoothness assumptions may be too idealized for complex real-world data. Open questions include relaxing the distribution class requirements, extending the framework to causal discovery under selection, and designing active sampling strategies that guarantee identifiability under budget constraints.
