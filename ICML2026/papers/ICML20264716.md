## Problem

Prior work has established that attention sinks and massive activations co-occur in LLMs, with the prevailing view attributing attention concentration on sink tokens to the presence of massive activations. However, this explanation fails to account for why the two phenomena systematically co-emerge, why massive activations appear specifically on sink tokens, or why they vanish in certain architectures (e.g., with KV biases or gating) while attention sinks persist. The paper challenges the causal direction, asking whether massive activations are truly necessary for attention sink formation or serve a distinct regulatory role.

## Input / Output

The theoretical analysis operates on the Jacobian of the self-attention output with respect to individual token hidden states, parameterized by the attention probability matrix structure (specifically the concentration parameter ε′ and sequence length T) and the norm of the sink token's hidden state ‖h₁‖. Empirically, the method consumes pre-trained LLM hidden states at the layer where massive activations first emerge (typically the second FFN output in Llama models), applies targeted interventions (zeroing top-k activations before/after RMSNorm, scaling by α, zeroing value vectors), and produces sink rate measurements (Sink₁^ε) across all layers and heads, cosine similarity statistics between sink and non-sink key-query pairs, and perplexity on WikiText, C4, and PG-19.

## Method

The core contribution is Theorem 4.1, which bounds the spectral norm of ∂H^{ℓ+1/2}/∂h_i^ℓ and shows it decomposes into three terms: a constant 1 from the skip connection, a value-path term K₁√T(1−ε)/‖h₁‖ for the sink token (dominant when attention is concentrated), and an attention-weight-path term K₂ε′/‖h_i‖. The critical insight is that the 1/‖h₁‖ factor arises from RMSNorm's Jacobian, meaning large sink-token norms (massive activations) make the attention output insensitive to perturbations of the sink token, thereby suppressing mixing between sink and non-sink tokens—while the attention sink itself suppresses mixing among non-sink tokens. This dual mechanism explains why massive activations are a stabilizing byproduct of attention concentration rather than its cause. The framework is then applied to explain why KV biases (which decouple sink keys/values from hidden states), value gating (which reduces K₁), attention output gating (which reduces √T(1−ε)), and DyT (which changes the norm-dependence) can eliminate massive activations while preserving attention sinks. Intervention experiments confirm the theory: zeroing massive activations collapses sink rates, but simultaneously zeroing the sink token's value vector restores them, demonstrating that the attention sink structure is encoded in the key-query geometry rather than in the massive activation coordinates themselves.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding comes from intervention analysis on LLaMA3.1-8B and LLaMA3.2-3B: zeroing out massive activations (h=0) collapses the attention sink rate from ~99% to 9–35% (at ε=0.3), but simultaneously zeroing the sink token's value vector (h=v=0) recovers the sink rate to 96.6–97.1%, confirming that massive activations are not necessary for attention sink formation. On perplexity, zeroing massive activations causes catastrophic degradation (e.g., LLaMA2-7B WikiText perplexity jumps from 5.37 to 9774.18), while the h=v=0 intervention partially recovers performance (29.04), though still well above the original. The theoretical bound in Theorem 4.1 shows the Jacobian norm ‖∂H^{ℓ+1/2}/∂h₁^ℓ‖ scales as K₁√T(1−ε)/‖h₁^ℓ‖ + K₂ε′/‖h₁^ℓ‖ + 1, empirically validated by monotonic decrease in Jacobian norm as ‖h₁^ℓ‖ increases from 0.1 to 1000.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic analysis paper rather than a method comparison, so there are no traditional baselines. The key 'comparisons' are between intervention strategies on the same pre-trained models. Zeroing massive activations before RMSNorm (h=0) destroys attention sinks far more severely than zeroing the top-k entries after RMSNorm, indicating that the pre-normalization massive activations are the critical component. The h=v=0 intervention (zeroing both massive activations and the sink token's value vector) recovers attention sinks to near-original levels (96.6% vs. 98.9% on LLaMA3.1-8B at ε=0.3), demonstrating that the value vector, not the massive activations themselves, is what maintains the attention sink structure. On perplexity, h=v=0 recovers performance substantially compared to h=0 alone (e.g., 29.04 vs. 9774.18 on LLaMA2-7B WikiText), but the recovered sink is not functionally equivalent to the original, as perplexity remains ~5× higher than baseline. The paper also contrasts pre-LN and post-LN architectures, showing that in post-LN (BERT), massive activations appear in the pre-LN hidden states rather than the residual stream, consistent with the theoretical prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the over-mixing framework of Barbero et al. (2025), which posits that attention sinks prevent excessive token mixing, and on the Jacobian/signal-propagation analysis of Noci et al. (2022) for bounding gradient flow through Transformer layers. It departs from the prevailing causal narrative in Sun et al. (2024) and Gu et al. (2024), which treat massive activations as the *mechanism that causes* attention to concentrate on sink tokens, by instead showing that the two phenomena serve complementary, parallel roles: attention sinks suppress mixing among non-sink tokens, while massive activations suppress mixing between sink and non-sink tokens via RMSNorm-induced insensitivity. The paper also unifies previously separate observations about KV biases, value gating, DyT, and post-LN architectures under a single theoretical lens derived from its Jacobian bound.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By disentangling the roles of massive activations and attention sinks, the paper provides a principled explanation for why architectural modifications (KV biases, gating, DyT) can eliminate massive activations while largely preserving attention sinks, and why naive interventions (zeroing massive activations) degrade model performance despite not being the root cause of attention sinks. The finding that zeroing the sink token's value vector recovers attention sinks even with massive activations suppressed has direct implications for model compression, quantization, and KV-cache management. Open questions include the formation dynamics of both phenomena during training, whether the single-sink-token assumption generalizes to the multi-sink settings observed in practice, and the long-term impact on training stability.
