## Problem

Classical fair empirical risk minimization (ERM) becomes ineffective in the overparameterized regime: because overfitting drives training error to zero for all groups, the empirical fairness constraint is already satisfied by the unconstrained solution, creating an illusion of fairness while the model still amplifies spurious correlations. No prior work had precisely characterized why this failure occurs or provided a principled correction that restores both fairness and predictive informativeness simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The method operates on data triples (x, y, s) where x ∈ R^d is a feature vector, y ∈ {0,1} is the target label, and s ∈ {0,1} is a binary sensitive attribute, with the data model assuming causal features correlated with y and spurious features correlated with s. The classifier is parameterized in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, so the key intermediate output is the vector of representer coefficients {α̂_i}_{i=1}^n, whose group-conditional averages serve as the empirical fairness criterion. The tunable output is the bias overcompensation level ω (with ω_y = ρ_y ω for y ∈ {0,1}), which is searched over a provably covering interval rather than via cross-validation.

## Method

The paper proposes a generalized fair ERM framework that modifies the standard equality fairness constraint by adding a tunable compensation term ω_y ΔÊ[f_θ(x)|y], allowing the model to overcompensate (ω > 0) or undercompensate (ω < 0) the training data bias. The core theoretical contribution is a sharp asymptotic characterization (Theorem 4.4) showing that in the proportional large-sample regime, test scores are asymptotically Gaussian with means determined by the group-conditional averages of representer coefficients. From this, the paper proves that equalized odds holds if and only if the group-conditional means of representer coefficients are balanced (Corollary 5.1), and that classical fair ERM with exact compensation (ω = 0) cannot simultaneously achieve balanced coefficients and informative classification, forcing degenerate predictions (Theorem 5.2). The practical algorithm (Algorithm 1) searches for the ω value that minimizes |ΔÊ[α̂|s]| over a grid within a coverage-guaranteed interval (Algorithm 2, Theorem 5.3), achieving equalized odds without a holdout set. A negative result (Theorem 6.1) further shows that imposing balanced coefficients directly as an optimization constraint does not yield equalized odds, underscoring that the overcompensation mechanism is essential rather than merely the coefficient balance itself.

## Results

The paper provides a sharp asymptotic characterization (Theorem 4.4) of test and training scores in the overparameterized kernel regime, showing that equalized odds is achieved if and only if the group-conditional means of representer coefficients are balanced (Corollary 5.1). Empirically, at the classical fair ERM setting (ω₀=ω₁=0), significant gaps in group true positive and false positive rates persist and grow with the overparameterization ratio p/n. The proposed bias overcompensation method (Algorithm 1) closes these gaps to near zero at ω≈0.466 (synthetic data, n=500, p=4n) and maintains fairness on Color-MNIST (n=500 from 60k training set, p via random features) while preserving predictive accuracy. A negative result (Theorem 6.1) shows that directly imposing balanced coefficients as an optimization constraint (Eq. 15) produces a reverse bias that penalizes the previously advantaged group.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is classical fair ERM (Donini et al., 2018) with exact bias compensation (ω₀=ω₁=0), which enforces equality of group-conditional mean scores on the training set. In the overparameterized regime (p > n), this baseline fails: the gap between group TPRs and FPRs remains large at ω=0 and increases with the ratio p/n (Figure 3, left column), confirming the bias amplification phenomenon. The proposed overcompensation method (Algorithm 1, with ω_y = ρ_y ω tuned via the balanced-coefficient criterion) closes the TPR/FPR gaps to near zero while maintaining a non-trivial correlation between predicted scores and labels (Figure 3, right column; Figure 4 on Color-MNIST). A second comparison is against directly imposing balanced representer coefficients as a linear constraint (Eq. 15), which the paper shows (Theorem 6.1, Figure 5) produces a reverse effect—penalizing the previously advantaged group s=1—demonstrating that the optimization landscape in the overparameterized regime does not allow one to simply swap the fairness constraint for the coefficient-balance condition without losing the desired fairness property.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical fair ERM framework of Donini et al. (2018) and the MinDiff kernel method of Prost et al. (2019) by introducing a tunable bias compensation parameter ω into the fairness constraint, and analyzes the resulting problem in the overparameterized proportional regime using the spurious-correlation data model of Sagawa et al. (2020). It departs from prior fair ERM work, which is justified through population-level reasoning, by providing a sharp asymptotic characterization via representer coefficients that reveals why the standard equality constraint becomes vacuous when the model interpolates training data. The analysis also connects to the broader tradition of precise high-dimensional performance analyses (random matrix theory, double descent) applied here to a fairness problem, and builds on empirical observations of fair ERM failure in overparameterized models reported by Cherepanova et al. (2021) and Veldanda et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical explanation for why fairness-constrained ERM fails in overparameterized models and offers a concrete, provably effective correction (bias overcompensation with an estimable search interval) that requires no holdout set for tuning. The negative result—that directly imposing balanced representer coefficients as an optimization constraint does NOT yield equalized odds, despite the equivalence holding when balanced coefficients are achieved through overcompensation—serves as an important caution against naively modifying optimization objectives for intended outcomes in high-dimensional settings. Limitations include the restriction to binary classification, a specific Gaussian-mixture data model with spurious features, and asymptotic (proportional regime) analysis; open questions include extension to multi-group settings, continuous sensitive attributes, group-specific variances, and whether the overcompensation strategy transfers to deep network training beyond kernel methods.
