## Problem

Generating comprehensive, human-readable weather forecast reports from multi-variable meteorological inputs remains largely unexplored for multimodal large language models. Existing weather MLLMs target narrow tasks such as extreme-event classification or radar-based nowcasting, and no publicly available dataset pairs visual meteorological data with expert-written forecast reports, leaving a gap in both data and model capability for general-purpose report generation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266106/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes 12 single-level ERA5 variable heatmaps (0.25° spatial resolution) extracted for the geographic region surrounding a target city, along with a text instruction specifying the city, timestamp, and per-day aspect constraints (e.g., 'Focus on: Temperature, Humidity'). It produces a structured 4-day forecast report where each day follows the format 'date, weekday Report:\n{Report}', with content restricted to the specified weather aspects (temperature, wind, humidity, frontal systems, pressure systems, wave patterns, wind flow systems, events). The aspect-controlled prompting mechanism is critical: it standardizes discussion scope across forecast days and enables aspect-level evaluation, preventing the model from defaulting to high-frequency aspects.

## Method

WeatherSyn is built on Qwen3-VL-8B and trained in three stages. Stage 1 (SFT) fine-tunes the model on WSInstruct (6,344 image-report pairs across 31 U.S. cities, 2017–2021) using a mixed-task instruction-tuning loss over all cities simultaneously. Stage 2 (RFT) applies step-level rejection sampling: 40 candidate reports are generated per instance, daily sub-reports achieving a step-level claim F1 of 1 are retained, and the most lexically diverse subset is selected using four distance metrics (edit distance, TF-IDF, Jaccard, Sentence-BERT cosine similarity) to form an augmented 20,412-sample corpus for retraining. Stage 3 (DPO) further refines the model using 1,241 preference pairs constructed from the 2021 subset, where the preferred response is assembled from highest-F1 sub-reports and the dispreferred from lowest-F1 ones, with the RFT model as reference. The key design insight is combining aspect-controlled prompting to handle the open-ended heterogeneity of real weather reports with step-level (per-day) rather than whole-report quality filtering, enabling fine-grained factual correctness while preserving lexical diversity.

## Results

On the WSInstruct test set, WeatherSyn-DPO achieves a weighted F1 of 0.59 (automatic claim evaluation), BLEU-1 of 0.44, ROUGE-L of 0.32, and a 0.33 Top-1 rate on LLM factual consistency. It matches the best closed-source MLLM (Gemini-3 Pro Preview, F1 = 0.60) on claim-level accuracy while substantially outperforming it on reference metrics (BLEU-1: 0.44 vs. 0.37) and LLM/expert evaluations (Fact.Cons.: 0.33 vs. 0.24). The three-stage training pipeline yields progressive gains: SFT → RFT improves F1 from 0.55 to 0.59, and DPO further boosts LLM factual consistency from 0.16 to 0.33.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the two specialized weather MLLMs, WeatherSyn-DPO achieves a 23-point F1 improvement over WeatherQA (0.59 vs. 0.36) and a 19-point gain over OmniEarth (0.59 vs. 0.40), with BLEU-1 more than doubling (0.44 vs. 0.19 and 0.17). Both specialized models score near-zero (0.01) on LLM and expert factual consistency, reflecting their design as classification-oriented models that lack instruction-following capability for open-ended report generation. Among closed-source MLLMs, Gemini-3 Pro Preview is the strongest competitor, edging WeatherSyn-DPO by 0.01 on claim F1 (0.60 vs. 0.59) but trailing substantially on reference metrics (BLEU-1: 0.37 vs. 0.44) and LLM factual consistency (0.24 vs. 0.33). All other closed-source models (GPT-4.1-Mini, GPT-5-Nano, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, Gemini-2.5-Flash) are clearly outperformed by WeatherSyn-DPO across every metric, with F1 gaps of 8–15 points and LLM/expert scores near zero.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by defining the Weather Forecasting Report (WFR) task—generating comprehensive, multi-aspect, multi-day textual forecasts from initial atmospheric conditions—which prior work has not addressed. Existing MLLM weather efforts (WeatherQA for severe-event captioning, OmniEarth-Bench for multiple-choice Earth science QA, Omni-Weather for radar-based precipitation nowcasting) target narrow sub-tasks or classification formats, leaving general open-ended report generation unexplored. Methodologically, the paper repurposes the standard SFT → rejection-sampling fine-tuning → DPO pipeline on Qwen3-VL-8B, with the key domain-specific innovation being aspect-controlled prompting to enforce fine-grained, controllable generation across 8 meteorological dimensions. The visual inputs are derived from ERA5 reanalysis heatmaps, connecting the work to the broader NWP-to-MLLM pipeline without requiring intermediate numerical forecast outputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WSInstruct is the first instruction-tuning dataset pairing meteorological visual inputs with expert-written multi-day, multi-aspect forecast reports, filling a critical data gap that has limited MLLM progress in weather reporting. The demonstration that a fine-tuned 8B model outperforms frontier closed-source MLLMs (GPT-5, Gemini-3, Claude-3.7) on domain-specific report generation—and generalizes zero-shot across geographically distant US regions—suggests that targeted instruction tuning can rival or exceed scale in specialized scientific reporting. Key limitations include coverage of only 31 US cities, reliance solely on initial-state inputs (no NWP forecast fields in the main experiments), and the very low absolute LLM/expert evaluation scores (≤0.33), indicating the task remains far from solved. Open questions include whether the approach scales to global coverage, whether incorporating multi-level pressure data or NWP forecasts (shown to help marginally in Appendix E.1) closes the remaining gap, and how the method transfers to regions with different reporting conventions and climatic regimes.
