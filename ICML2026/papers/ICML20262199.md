## Problem

Identifying most influential sets (MIS) — size-k subsets whose removal maximally changes a target estimand — requires searching over C(n,k) subsets, making exact selection computationally infeasible even for moderate datasets. Existing alternatives (influence-function approximations, greedy heuristics) miss joint influence and masking effects where observations interact, and greedy methods can be permanently trapped on suboptimal paths because optimal MIS are generally non-nested in k. No efficient method existed that recovers the exact global optimum for set-level influence at scale.

## Input / Output

The method takes residualized treatment and outcome values (x̃_i, ỹ_i) from a cross-fitted partial linear model, a target subset size k, and a numerical tolerance τ. It returns the exact size-k most influential set, the corresponding optimal influence value, and — via warm-started sequential runs (Algorithm 2) — full MIS influence traces across k = 1, ..., K. The residualized inputs must be held fixed during the search, and the denominator must remain positive for all feasible subsets; otherwise a ridge-stabilized variant applies.

## Method

The core insight is that the leave-set-out influence in a Robinson residualized OLS estimator admits a linear-fractional form W(S)/G(S), where W(S) is the additive score removed from the normal equation and G(S) is the residual curvature remaining. This algebraic structure converts the combinatorial search over C(n,k) subsets into a one-parameter fractional programming problem, which Dinkelbach's method solves as a finite sequence of top-k selection subproblems, each costing O(n) time. The algorithm terminates in at most M+1 iterations (M = number of distinct feasible ratio values) and returns a globally optimal set for the fixed-input ratio objective. For partial linear models with estimated nuisance functions, Neyman orthogonality and uniform residualized-score stability ensure the scaled empirical objective approximates a first-order oracle orthogonal-score objective, with exact set recovery guaranteed under a separation condition between the best and second-best oracle sets.

## Results

Algorithm 1 achieves exact global optimality, matching enumeration across 10,000+ Monte Carlo replications per design, while greedy selection returns suboptimal sets in a non-trivial fraction of cases. At scale, the median wall-clock time for n=10⁶, k=10⁵ is below 200 ms with a median of only 3 Dinkelbach iterations (max 6); at n=10⁸, k=10⁷ runtime is ~60 s, and at n=10⁹, k=10⁸ it is ~15 min. On the STS Benchmark (n=5,749), removing the 5,000-MIS shifts the fitted similarity score for the high-similarity 'Romney' query from 0.81 to 1.43 (increase direction) or 0.41 (decrease direction), with only 2,545 observations needed to push the score above the nominal [0,1] range. Across the seven microcredit RCTs (n≥961), non-nestedness events are frequent, and in the Mongolia trial a single non-nestedness event causes a large, persistent gap between the exact and greedy influence traces.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the greedy selection baseline (Kuschnig et al., 2021), Algorithm 1 always recovers the exact global optimum in small-scale enumeration-verified simulations (n≤50, k≤3, 10,000+ replications per DGP), whereas greedy selection returns suboptimal sets whenever joint influence or masking is present. In the Mongolia microcredit RCT, greedy approximates the influence value closely in the increasing direction but, after a single non-nestedness event in the decreasing direction, becomes permanently trapped on a suboptimal path with an accumulating influence gap; the exact trace recovers the correct set. Across the remaining six RCTs, greedy tracks optimal influence magnitudes well but the selected sets frequently differ from the exact MIS, confirming that value approximation and set recovery are distinct. Against the AMIP influence-function approximation (Broderick et al., 2023), Figure A3 shows that the exact MIS influence values diverge from the AMIP estimates, particularly at larger k and in the decreasing direction, consistent with the known inaccuracy of first-order influence functions for set-level and extreme-influence effects.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges fractional programming (Dinkelbach, 1967) and influence analysis in statistics/ML by recognizing that the Robinson residualized OLS estimator's leave-set-out effect has a linear-fractional structure amenable to Dinkelbach's method. It departs from prior influence-function approximations (Hampel, 1974; Koh & Liang, 2017; Broderick et al., 2023) that use infinitesimal or singleton-based rankings, and from greedy set-selection heuristics (Kuschnig et al., 2021) that assume nested optimal sets. The PLM framework and Neyman orthogonality from double/debiased ML (Chernozhukov et al., 2018) provide the statistical backbone for connecting the empirical fractional objective to an oracle orthogonal-score objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method makes exact size-k MIS computation feasible at scales (n=10⁶, k=10⁵) where enumeration is intractable, turning set-level sensitivity analysis from a forensic exercise into a routine diagnostic for robustness auditing and data curation. Key limitations include restriction to linear-fractional (univariate OLS-type) objectives, first-order PLM guarantees only, and the need for a separation condition for exact set recovery. Open questions include extending to structured deletion constraints (clusters, time blocks), vector-valued or nonlinear targets, and formal inference on whether a particular influential set is statistically anomalous.
