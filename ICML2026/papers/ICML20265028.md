## Problem

Federated fine-tuning of LLMs on edge devices is prohibitively expensive because existing methods train LoRA modules from scratch, requiring hundreds of communication rounds. Although the open-source community has amassed abundant task-adapted LoRA modules, naive reuse strategies introduce destructive cross-task interference (parameter merging) or suffer from coarse-grained, non-scalable expert activation (MoLE). The paper asks whether existing LoRA knowledge can be selectively recombined at a finer granularity to adapt LLMs to new tasks without costly retraining.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt and a pool of frozen, pre-trained LoRA modules (retrieved from repositories like LoRAHub) as input; the only trainable parameter is a lightweight router matrix W_router ∈ R^{M×d} where M is the total number of rank-wise experts across all modules. The output is a generated text response from the adapted LLM, along with the trained router weights and per-matrix expert quota allocations. During inference, only the top-K rank-wise experts (K ≪ M) are activated per input token, keeping compute proportional to K rather than the full expert pool size.

## Method

SMARTFED decomposes each LoRA module along its rank dimension into independent rank-one experts E_i(x) = (B_{:,i} A_{i,:})x, forming a Mixture of Rank-Wise Experts (MoRE) pool. A trainable router computes input-conditioned gating scores via softmax(W_router x), and a top-K binary mask sparsely activates the most relevant experts, so the final output is W_0 x + Σ g̃_m · E_m(x). To allocate the limited K budget efficiently, Elastic Expert Quota Allocation (EEQA) profiles each expert's importance via cosine-similarity-based scores, then iteratively redistributes the per-matrix quota using softmax-normalized cumulative importance with a two-phase algorithm (proportional floor + greedy residual fill). The federated workflow trains only the router on local private data while keeping all LoRA experts frozen, aggregating router updates via FedAvg and reallocating quotas each round. Theoretically, the paper proves that sparse rank-wise routing suppresses task-irrelevant noise by a factor S_2/M relative to dense activation and algebraically eliminates the O(N²) cross-task interference terms (B_n A_{n'}) inherent to parameter merging.

## Results

On LLaMA2-7B, SMARTFED achieves an average score of 50.75 across MGSM (35.48), DoIT (52.59), and GSM-Hard (64.19), outperforming the strongest baseline AdaLoRA (47.66) by 3.09% and the weakest baseline Linear Arithmetic (42.71) by 8.04%. On LLaMA2-13B, the average gain widens to 10.21% over the best baseline (62.95 vs. 52.74 for Linear Arithmetic), and on Qwen2-7B SMARTFED reaches 50.35 average, a 8.37% improvement over Linear Arithmetic. SMARTFED also achieves up to 3.95× faster convergence, 31.47× lower communication cost, and 3.61× reduced energy consumption compared to training-from-scratch baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SMARTFED outperforms all nine baselines across every model and task. Against knowledge-free methods, the largest margin is over Linear Arithmetic (8.04% average gap on LLaMA2-7B), confirming that naive parameter merging introduces destructive cross-task interference; FedIT, FwdLLM, DoFIT, and FedAdapter trail by 6.72%, 5.04%, 6.00%, and 4.34% respectively. Among knowledge-reuse baselines, AdaLoRA (3.09%), LoRA-Flow (3.16%), MoLE (3.49%), and LoRAHub (3.60%) are the closest competitors, but all still lag behind SMARTFED because they treat LoRA modules as coarse-grained atomic units rather than decomposing them into rank-wise experts. Notably, MoLE and LoRA-Flow require 64 activated ranks (double SMARTFED's 32) yet still underperform, highlighting that fine-grained rank-wise routing with EEQA is more effective than simply scaling up the number of active experts. On larger models (LLaMA2-13B, LLaMA3-8B, Qwen3-8B), the advantage grows to 10–14%, suggesting the benefits of rank-wise decomposition scale with model capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LoRA-based federated fine-tuning line (FedIT, FwdLLM, DoFIT) by shifting the paradigm from training LoRA modules from scratch to composing existing, task-adapted modules. Its direct architectural predecessor is MoLE (Huang et al., 2024), which treats entire LoRA modules as atomic experts with output-level routing; SMARTFED departs by decomposing each module into rank-wise experts (MoRE) and adding adaptive per-matrix budget allocation (EEQA), addressing MoLE's coarse-grained adaptation and linear latency scaling. The theoretical analysis builds on the algebraic cross-term interference identified in task arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2023) and formalizes how rank-wise decomposition eliminates these terms by construction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 31× communication reduction and 4× faster convergence make federated LLM fine-tuning genuinely practical on resource-constrained edge devices, a setting where prior methods were prohibitively expensive. A key open question is the frozen-expert constraint: when the target domain is substantially disjoint from the source tasks of retrieved LoRA modules, the method relies on combinatorial recombination of orthogonal subspaces, which the paper acknowledges may require a brief warm-start fine-tuning phase. Additionally, the approach assumes a sufficiently rich repository of task-relevant LoRA modules exists, a condition that may not hold for niche or emerging domains. The robustness to noisy retrieval (adding an irrelevant Chinese LoRA to a Math+Code pool) is encouraging but tested on only one model pair.
