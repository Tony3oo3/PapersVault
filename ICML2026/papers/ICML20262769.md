## Problem

Pretrained MLLMs are optimized for semantic understanding and inherently lack sensitivity to the low-level generator artifacts essential for AI-generated image detection. The paper identifies a fundamental representation conflict: standard LoRA tuning cannot recover these suppressed artifact features, while full fine-tuning captures them but causes catastrophic forgetting of semantic knowledge. Layer-wise linear probing reveals that semantic representations converge in early-to-middle LLM layers, making it impossible to simultaneously learn low-level artifacts in those same layers without disrupting established semantic processing.

## Input / Output

The model takes an RGB image (resized to a 512×512 pixel budget while preserving aspect ratio) and a text instruction, typically a binary-choice prompt such as 'Is this image real or fake? Please just answer real or fake.' It produces a single-word classification token ('real' or 'fake') parsed from the autoregressive output, and in open-ended mode can generate multi-sentence forensic explanations grounding its judgment in observable visual evidence (e.g., abnormal digital textures or semantic inconsistencies like distorted license plates).

## Method

Deep-VRM employs a dual-branch visual architecture where a frozen encoder V_o preserves pretrained semantic perception and an adapted branch V_a (initialized from V_o with LoRA adapters, rank 64) learns artifact-specific visual cues. The key mechanism is a 'Deep Residual Injection' at layer K=16 (the semantic convergence boundary identified via linear probing): the artifact features from V_a(I) are injected as a residual into the intermediate visual token representation via h̃_v = α·h_v + β·V_a(I) with α=β=0.5, effectively bypassing the frozen early layers that suppress high-frequency artifact signals. Training proceeds in two stages: Stage 1 fine-tunes the LLM on semantic-focused data D1 to activate detection priors, while Stage 2 jointly trains V_a and LLM layers K through N on both D1 and D2 (VAE-reconstructed image pairs that isolate non-semantic generative traces), keeping the first K−1 LLM layers frozen to preserve semantic representations. This architecture-agnostic design introduces no additional trainable parameters beyond LoRA (115M total for Qwen-2.5-VL-7B) and requires no external expert detectors.

## Results

Deep-VRM achieves state-of-the-art balanced accuracy across most AIGI detection benchmarks: 97.42% on GenImage (vs. 94.63% for OMAT), 94.50% on SynthBuster (vs. 62.75% for PatchShuffle), 97.7% on AIGI-Now (vs. 80.2% for CO-SPY), and 89.51% on WildRF (vs. 59.49% for AIDE). On the in-the-wild AIGI-Bench, it reaches 90.48%, surpassing the next-best method (SAFE at 56.13%) by over 34 percentage points. The method also preserves general multimodal capabilities, maintaining BLINK (0.5476 vs. 0.5481 backbone) and MME (1636 vs. 1677) scores, unlike full fine-tuning which collapses to 0.0373 and 506 respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Deep-VRM outperforms all specialized forensic detectors on GenImage (97.42% vs. OMAT 94.63%, DRCT/UniFD 87.67%, AIDE 86.88%) and achieves a dramatic margin on SynthBuster (94.50% vs. PatchShuffle 62.75%, DRCT 59.10%). On in-the-wild benchmarks, the advantage is most pronounced: WildRF (89.51% vs. AIDE 59.49%, FatFormer 60.34%) and AIGI-Bench (90.48% vs. SAFE 56.13%, AIDE 55.98%), where conventional detectors degrade severely under compression and post-processing. On AIGI-Now, Deep-VRM reaches 97.7% average, beating CO-SPY (80.2%) and AIDE (77.1%) by 17–20 points. The one notable exception is AIGI-Holmes, where AIDE (97.00%) slightly edges out Deep-VRM (96.11%), though Deep-VRM still surpasses the MLLM-based AIGI-Holmes* (92.30%). Among MLLM baselines, Deep-VRM (93.28% avg) beats Forensic-Chat (88.75%) and all general-purpose MLLMs (50–69% avg) by substantial margins, confirming that the deep residual injection architecture—not merely the training data—drives the gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two lines of prior work in AIGI detection: (1) traditional detectors (UnivFD, AIDE, DRCT) that rely on dedicated forensic architectures, and (2) MLLM-based detectors (Forensic-Chat, AIGI-Holmes) that either fine-tune end-to-end or bolt on external expert modules. The key conceptual departure is the identification of a 'representation conflict'—that forcing early-to-middle MLLM layers to learn low-level generator artifacts disrupts their established semantic convergence—and the architectural solution of injecting artifact-specific visual features via a residual path at a specific intermediate depth (K=16), bypassing the semantic-dominant early layers. The layer-wise linear probing analysis draws on LLM interpretability literature on functional stratification but applies it specifically to forensic signal perception, a context where it had not been explored.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic explanation for why MLLMs underperform at AIGI detection and a clean, parameter-efficient architectural fix that achieves SOTA across numerous benchmarks without external detectors, which is practically important for deployment. The robustness to post-processing (compression, blur, resize) addresses a critical real-world gap that many prior detectors fail to close. Open questions include whether the optimal injection depth is architecture-dependent, whether the dual-branch visual encoder is strictly necessary versus other decoupling strategies, and whether the approach extends to video or multi-modal forgery detection. The finding that the model adaptively leverages different forensic signal levels depending on input suggests an emergent capability worth further investigation.
