## Problem

Existing approaches to evaluating ML resource consumption focus on single stages of the model lifecycle—such as one training run or one inference prediction—making them insufficient for accounting the total resource demands of modern AI systems with multi-stage training pipelines, diverse inference methods, and substantial hardware manufacturing costs. The paper argues that without a framework to aggregate and attribute costs across the full development and deployment lifecycle, stakeholders cannot properly assess environmental impact, compare system design choices, or provision infrastructure.

## Input / Output

The LCA framework consumes first-party hardware embodied cost disclosures (e.g., NVIDIA product carbon footprint summaries), direct operational energy measurements from GPU/CPU monitoring utilities (nvidia-smi, DCGM, Intel VTuner), and secondary data from databases like EcoInvent. All costs are organized around a user-defined functional unit (e.g., a batch of processed LLM queries), and the output is a decomposed cost-per-functional-unit that separates per-inference marginal cost from amortized training and hardware manufacturing costs, enabling stage-wise attribution of environmental impact.

## Method

The paper applies the ISO 14040/14044 Life Cycle Assessment framework to ML models through four stages: Goal Definition and Scoping (defining functional units and system boundaries), Life Cycle Inventory (quantifying resource flows across embodied and operational stages), Life Cycle Impact Assessment (converting inventoried resources to impact categories using standard characterization factors such as TRACI), and Interpretation (identifying significant issues and resource bottlenecks). A case study on OLMo2 7B demonstrates the approach with a time-share attribution model where GPU embodied costs are allocated to training and inference proportionally to workload duration relative to hardware lifespan, yielding a cost-per-functional-unit formula that sums per-inference cost with amortized experimentation, training, and hardware costs. The key insight is that per-inference cost is extremely sensitive to total model lifespan—upfront embodied and training costs must be amortized over tens of billions of inferences before marginal inference dominates—making lifecycle-stage trade-offs (e.g., shifting compute from training to inference via reasoning models) critical to evaluate holistically.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative illustration uses OLMo2 7b (Morrison et al., 2025; Walsh et al., 2025) to demonstrate LCA-based cost attribution. The key finding is that the total per-inference cost (C_FU) is extremely sensitive to the model's operational lifespan: upfront embodied and training costs must be amortized over tens of billions of inferences before the marginal inference cost dominates the total. Increasing inference efficiency via offline batching reduces the unit cost under a fixed carbon budget, while amortization of hardware embodied costs over extended model use further lowers per-functional-unit emissions. The paper provides the formula C_FU = C_PerInference + (Hardware Utilization Time × C_Embodied) / Hardware Lifespan + (C_Experimentation + C_Training) / Total Lifetime Inferences as the minimal accounting framework.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not benchmark a proposed method against competing baselines in the traditional sense. Instead, it contrasts three existing evaluation paradigms against the proposed LCA framework: (1) proxy-metric-based efficiency research (FLOPs, parameter counts), which the authors argue is poorly correlated with tangible measures like latency and energy (Dehghani et al., 2022b; Fernandez et al., 2023); (2) single-stage resource measurements (e.g., training-only carbon or per-inference energy), which fail to capture cross-stage interactions and amortization effects; and (3) sector-level energy projections (Shehabi et al., 2024; Green et al., 2024), which rely on hardware shipment assumptions that obfuscate individual workloads. The authors argue LCA subsumes all three by providing standardized functional units, system boundaries, and stage-wise attribution. They acknowledge in §4 that for frontier models where inference dominates (70% at Meta, 60% at Google per Oviedo et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2022), marginal inference accounting may suffice, but contend that for research-scale deployments and emerging multi-stage pipelines, full LCA remains necessary.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the established Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) framework from industrial ecology (ISO 14040/14044) and semiconductor manufacturing research (Gupta et al., 2021; Schneider et al., 2025) to the domain of ML model evaluation. Prior ML resource accounting work has focused on individual lifecycle stages—training energy (Strubell et al., 2020; Patterson et al., 2021), marginal inference costs (Luccioni et al., 2024), or hardware embodied carbon (Li et al., 2024b)—but has not systematically aggregated these into a unified cradle-to-grave assessment with standardized functional units. The paper carves new ground by proposing that the full ML model pipeline (experimentation, multi-stage training, diverse inference paradigms, hardware amortization) constitutes a 'product system' amenable to LCA, rather than a collection of independently measurable stages.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As AI infrastructure investments rival Big Science projects and data center energy projections vary by over 4×, the lack of a standardized, comparable accounting framework hinders both policy-making (EU AI Act reporting, U.S. grid planning) and research evaluation. The paper's key open questions include: how to attribute embodied hardware costs across training and inference workloads (the time-share approach is acknowledged as an open problem), how to handle proprietary data gaps in LCA inventories, and whether sector-level projections can be grounded in workload-level LCA data. The case study showing per-inference cost sensitivity to model lifespan (requiring tens of billions of inferences before inference dominates training costs) highlights that amortization assumptions dramatically shift which lifecycle stage is the 'significant issue,' making lifecycle-shape assumptions critical to any LCA conclusion.
