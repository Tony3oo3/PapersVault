## Problem

In fully streaming deep reinforcement learning (batch size 1, no replay), gradient-based updates with fixed step sizes produce unpredictable per-step changes in function outputs because gradient magnitudes vary sharply across samples, causing alternating overshooting and undershooting that makes the regime brittle. The paper asks whether one can instead specify the intended outcome of each update in output units (value change, policy change) and solve for the step size that approximately achieves it, rather than prescribing a parameter-space step.

## Input / Output

The agent consumes exactly one transition (s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1}) per environment step with no replay buffer, no mini-batch, and no multiple passes over stored experience. For continuous control (MuJoCo, DM Control) inputs are state vectors; for discrete control (Atari, MinAtar) inputs are 84×84 grayscale frames stacked 4-deep. The method produces an updated value (or Q) function and an updated policy from which the next action is selected; the distinguishing output property is that each update is constructed to yield a bounded, interpretable change in the prediction or log-probability rather than an arbitrary parameter shift.

## Method

The core principle is to define an intended outcome Δ_t in the units of the quantity being learned, then compute the step size α_t = Δ_t / (∇y_t(w_t)ᵀ d_t) via a first-order Taylor approximation so the update achieves that intended change. For Intentional TD(λ), the intended outcome is a fractional reduction η of the TD error in the current value prediction, yielding the conservative step size α_t = η / √(σ̄_t ⟨ρ_t z_t, z_t⟩), where σ̄_t is a discounted running estimate of gradient sensitivity and z_t is the eligibility trace. For Intentional Policy Gradient, the intended outcome is a per-step change in the sampled action's log-probability proportional to its advantage (normalized by a running scale of |A_t|), providing a cheap streaming proxy for bounding local KL divergence. Practical implementations integrate RMSProp-style diagonal scaling to handle heterogeneous parameter scales, eligibility traces for temporal credit assignment, and adaptive δ-clipping (scaling by the long-term RMS of TD errors) to prevent outlier propagation, all while maintaining a single shared η per algorithm that transfers across environments.

## Results

On MuJoCo (5M steps, 30 seeds), Intentional AC outperforms the streaming baseline StreamAC on 5 of 6 tasks: Ant (5513 vs 4898), HalfCheetah (5064 vs 4830), Hopper (2254 vs 2113), Humanoid (6227 vs 4920), and HumanoidStandup (189k vs 162k), while slightly trailing on Walker2d (3515 vs 3590). The method operates with batch size 1 and no replay buffer, yet achieves performance competitive with batch methods (SAC, PPO) that require >100× the per-update FLOPs. A single η setting (η_critic=0.5, η_actor=0.05) transfers across all MuJoCo and DM Control tasks without per-task tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary streaming baseline StreamAC (Elsayed et al. 2024), Intentional AC wins on 5 of 6 MuJoCo tasks, with the largest gap on Humanoid (6227 vs 4920, +27%) and HumanoidStandup (189k vs 162k, +17%), while Walker2d is a near-tie (3515 vs 3590). Against the batch baseline SAC (Haarnoja et al. 2018), Intentional AC achieves comparable final returns on MuJoCo while using batch size 1 with no replay buffer; the paper estimates one SAC update costs ~140× the FLOPs of one Intentional AC update, and a compute-matched comparison (Appendix F.2) confirms Intentional AC maintains a substantial advantage under equal total compute. Against PPO (Schulman et al. 2017), the paper reports Intentional AC is competitive in the streaming regime, closing most of the gap between streaming and batch training. On Atari, Intentional Q-learning is compared against DQN/Rainbow (Hessel et al. 2018) at 200M frames and achieves competitive scores, with the paper noting performance 'on par with batch and replay-buffer approaches' across the discrete-action benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Normalized Least Mean Squares (NLMS) principle—scaling step sizes to control output change rather than parameter change—from linear adaptive filtering to streaming deep reinforcement learning. It builds directly on the StreamX/ObGD framework (Elsayed et al. 2024), which identified the 'stream barrier' and introduced overshooting-bounded gradient descent, but departs by specifying an intended functional outcome (fractional TD-error reduction or bounded log-probability change) and solving for the step size, rather than detecting overshoot post-hoc. The method integrates eligibility traces, RMSProp-style diagonal scaling, and adaptive δ-clipping into a unified intentional-update framework, and for policy learning it uses a sampled log-probability change as a cheap streaming proxy for the KL-divergence constraints used in trust-region and PPO-style methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a single η hyperparameter transfers across all environments within a benchmark family, and that fully streaming (batch=1, no replay) learning matches or approaches batch/replay performance, directly addresses the stream barrier that has limited real-world deployment of RL agents. The 100× per-update FLOP advantage over SAC (running comfortably on CPU) makes this practically significant for resource-constrained and edge settings. Open questions include the action-dependent normalization bias in policy updates (which can reweight actions and alter the effective objective), cross-sample collateral effects from shared parameters, and whether meta-optimization of η can further improve robustness without hand-designed schedules.
