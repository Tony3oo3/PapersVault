## Problem

Existing personalized federated learning methods either impose structural assumptions on agent heterogeneity (fixed clusters, proximity to a global model, linear combinations) or remain heuristic without generalization guarantees. No prior approach simultaneously adapts to unknown inter-agent structure and provides finite-sample excess risk bounds that explicitly quantify when collaboration is statistically beneficial over local learning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261129/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each of B agents holds a local dataset of n_k samples drawn from its own distribution; the target agent (agent 1) additionally receives D-dimensional random Fourier feature KME vectors from all other agents (shared once, not iteratively). The method outputs a personalized model for the target agent and a set of simplex-constrained mixture weights over all agents. The RFF dimension D controls a communication-versus-statistical-efficiency trade-off: larger D improves KME approximation but increases the size of vectors transmitted per agent.

## Method

The paper reformulates PFL weight learning as a high-dimensional mean estimation problem: the excess risk of the target agent is upper-bounded by the MMD between its true distribution and a learned mixture of all agents' empirical distributions, and minimizing this MMD is equivalent to estimating the target KME as a convex combination of all agents' empirical KMEs. To solve this, the authors apply the Q-aggregation procedure of Blanchard et al. (2024), which minimizes an unbiased empirical error plus a penalty term involving the target's empirical covariance, yielding a near-optimal bias-variance trade-off controlled by the effective dimension d₁ᵉ and the MMD distance Δ_V to the best subset of agents. For federated compatibility, agents compute their KMEs locally using shared random Fourier features (D-dimensional vectors) and transmit only these summaries to the target agent, which then runs Q-aggregation and optimizes the weighted empirical risk via FedAvg. The resulting excess risk bound (Theorem 5.2) recovers the ideal KME-based rate as D→∞ and adds an O(√(log B / D)) approximation term, making the communication cost explicitly tunable.

## Results

The paper evaluates its method on three settings: synthetic concept shift, synthetic covariate shift, and the FEMNIST dataset. In the concept shift experiment (B=100 agents, n_k=10 each), the method correctly identifies a transition at σ_c²=0.5, improving over local training when intra-group similarity is high and gracefully reducing collaboration when heterogeneity becomes too large. In the covariate shift experiment (B=100 agents, n_k=20, ReLU networks of varying depth), the method recovers the three underlying agent clusters in its learned weight matrix and achieves performance very close to an oracle that knows the cluster structure a priori. On FEMNIST (B=192 agents, D=1000 RFFs), the method consistently outperforms GrandMean across all agents and always beats local training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines: Local training (using only the target agent's data), GrandMean (a single model trained with weights proportional to each agent's data size), and an Oracle that has a priori knowledge of agent groupings. In the synthetic concept shift experiment, GrandMean degrades sharply as σ_c² increases beyond 0.5 because it always pools all agents' data regardless of similarity, while the proposed method adapts its collaboration weights and maintains low MSE. In the synthetic covariate shift experiment, for small models GrandMean underperforms local training (the network cannot capture all subpopulations), whereas the proposed method identifies similar agents and leverages their data effectively, approaching oracle performance as model capacity grows. On FEMNIST, GrandMean is generally superior to local training but can fail for specific agents with atypical writing styles; the proposed method is always better than local training and consistently improves over GrandMean, suggesting that adaptive per-agent weighting captures heterogeneity that uniform data-size weighting misses. The method never requires tuning its hyperparameters C_Q and C_P (fixed at log B per theory), demonstrating robustness to hyperparameter selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges personalized federated learning (PFL) and high-dimensional multiple mean estimation by reformulating the learning of collaboration weights as a Kernel Mean Embedding (KME) aggregation problem. It directly leverages the Q-aggregation method of Blanchard et al. (2024) and the earlier KME-specific work of Marienwald et al. (2021), transferring their statistical guarantees into the PFL setting for the first time. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant PFL literature—which typically assumes structural priors like clusters, a shared global model, or meta-learning initializations—by requiring no prior knowledge of agent heterogeneity and instead adapting weights purely from data via MMD minimization. The practical federated implementation via Random Fourier Features is a natural extension of standard RFF techniques to the KME-sharing context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first finite-sample excess risk guarantees for PFL weights actually estimated from data (rather than oracle or theoretical weights), explicitly quantifying when collaboration helps versus hurts. This addresses a long-standing gap where most PFL methods are heuristic without generalization bounds. Key open questions include: the sensitivity to kernel choice (which determines what information is compared across agents), the privacy cost of sharing KMEs, and the extension from the all-for-one setting to simultaneous multi-agent learning. The RFF approximation introduces a communication-efficiency trade-off (D must theoretically exceed n₁² for improvement), which may limit practical applicability in bandwidth-constrained settings.
