## Problem

Expressive piano performance rendering has been constrained by a supervised paradigm that depends on small, labor-intensive aligned score-performance datasets (~100 hours), while vast unlabeled MIDI corpora (>100K hours) remain untapped. Prior methods require explicit structural features (measures, meter, tempo maps) that performance-only MIDI lacks, making them fundamentally incompatible with large-scale unsupervised data. The paper asks whether large-scale self-supervised pre-training can overcome this data bottleneck and produce human-level expressive renderings.

## Input / Output

The model takes a symbolic score in MIDI format, tokenized into fixed 8-token sequences per note (Pitch, IOI, Velocity, Duration, and 4 Pedal tokens) with millisecond-level timing quantization, all normalized to 120 BPM. It outputs an expressive performance MIDI with absolute millisecond timing and continuous velocity/pedal values, which is then post-processed by Expressive Tempo Mapping into a DAW-editable MIDI file where expressive timing is encoded as a dynamic tempo curve rather than raw offsets. A hard pitch constraint during inference enforces one-to-one note correspondence between score and performance.

## Method

The core contribution is a unified event-based MIDI representation that treats scores and performances identically using time-shift (IOI/Duration) rather than structural features (bars/beats), enabling them to be mixed in a single 10B-token pre-training corpus. The architecture is an asymmetric T5-Gemma encoder-decoder (~135M params) with a 10-layer encoder and 2-layer decoder, combined with note-level sequence compression that aggregates each note's 8 token embeddings into one vector, reducing attention complexity from N² to (N/8)². Training proceeds in two stages: self-supervised masked denoising pre-training (30% masking) on unlabeled MIDI, followed by supervised fine-tuning on ASAP aligned score-performance pairs. The compression and asymmetry interact synergistically, yielding 3.13× training speedup and 0.38× VRAM relative to a symmetric uncompressed baseline, while the 2-layer decoder suffices for rendering quality since the task is primarily constrained by encoder representation quality rather than decoder depth.

## Results

On the ASAP test set, Pianist Transformer achieves an overall JS Divergence of 0.1634 and Intersection Area of 0.8501, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline VirtuosoNet-ISGN (0.2791 / 0.7556) and narrowing the gap to human performances (0.0411 / 0.9662). The model wins on 6 of 8 per-dimension metrics, with the largest gains in Duration (JS Div 0.1879 vs. 0.2321 for ISGN) and IOI (0.1740 vs. 0.5441). In the subjective listening study, Pianist Transformer achieves the highest first-place vote rate (32.7%) among all generative systems, slightly exceeding human recordings (30.8%), and is not statistically distinguishable from human performances (p = 0.21).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Pianist Transformer outperforms all three baselines on both overall JS Divergence and Intersection Area. Against VirtuosoNet-ISGN (the strongest prior method), it reduces overall JS Div from 0.2791 to 0.1634 (41% relative improvement) and raises Intersection from 0.7556 to 0.8501, with particularly large gains in IOI (0.5441→0.1740) and Duration (0.2321→0.1879). The sole exception is Pedal, where VirtuosoNet-ISGN retains an advantage (JS Div 0.0829 vs. 0.1111; Inter 0.9410 vs. 0.8893), likely due to its specialized graph-based architecture for pedal modeling. Against VirtuosoNet-Han, the gap is similar in magnitude (overall JS Div 0.2839→0.1634). ScorePerformer, evaluated in its unconditional generation mode, is the weakest baseline (overall JS Div 0.4462), trailing Pianist Transformer by a wide margin on every dimension. In the subjective study, Pianist Transformer is ranked significantly above all three baselines (p < 0.001 for each) and is not statistically distinguishable from human performances (p = 0.21).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the T5-Gemma encoder-decoder framework and adapts it with an asymmetric 10-2 layer allocation and note-level sequence compression for efficient long-sequence music modeling. It departs fundamentally from prior expressive piano performance rendering systems (VirtuosoNet, ScorePerformer) that relied on supervised learning over small aligned score-performance datasets with explicit structural features (measures, meter), by introducing a unified event-based MIDI tokenization that treats scores and performances identically, enabling large-scale self-supervised pre-training on 10B tokens of unaligned MIDI. The paradigm draws directly from masked denoising pre-training in NLP (BERT, T5) and vision (MAE), but applies it to the fine-grained, performance-level task of expressive rendering, which prior self-supervised music models (MusicBERT, Moonbeam) had not addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the data bottleneck in expressive performance rendering can be overcome through self-supervised pre-training, with subjective results showing the model is statistically indistinguishable from human performers and even slightly preferred in blind listening. Key limitations include the restriction to solo piano, the lightweight 2-layer decoder identified as a scaling bottleneck, and potential overlap between the pre-training corpus and evaluation repertoire. Open questions include whether the paradigm extends to multi-instrument and orchestral settings, whether explicit high-level control (tempo, natural-language conditioning) can be integrated, and whether the observed 10B-token data plateau reflects a true model-capacity limit or an architectural constraint that a stronger decoder could overcome.
