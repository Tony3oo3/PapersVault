## Problem

Existing LLM logical reasoning methods operate under a closed-world assumption where all necessary premises are explicitly provided, but real-world reasoning frequently involves open-world scenarios where the given information is insufficient due to missing premises or implicit commonsense knowledge. Both symbolic solvers (which fail when premises are absent) and RAG-based approaches (which retrieve statistically relevant but logically insufficient information) struggle to bridge these reasoning gaps.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263328/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of natural language premises and a natural language conclusion as input, translating them into first-order logic formulas for formal verification. It produces a binary entailment label (True/False) indicating whether the conclusion is logically deducible, along with the minimal set of missing premises (in both symbolic and natural language form) that bridge the reasoning gap. The iterative completion is bounded by a maximum iteration count K_max, and the system falls back to a Chain-of-Thought prediction if neither logical nor factual verification converges.

## Method

OpenIKLR is a training-free neuro-symbolic framework that integrates a first-order logic solver (Prover9) with LLMs through a four-stage iterative pipeline: (1) symbolic parsing translates NL premises and conclusion into FOL; (2) the solver checks entailment and, upon failure, triggers a completion module that generates the minimal set of missing symbolic premises guided by the failed proof trace; (3) a logic verifier resubmits the augmented premise set to the solver to confirm logical sufficiency; and (4) a fact verifier uses the LLM's world knowledge to confirm factual accuracy of the generated premises. The dual-verification loop iterates up to K_max rounds, and a PAC-Bayes theoretical analysis with a cardinality-penalizing prior provides a principled justification for preferring minimal premise sets, showing that the generalization bound increases monotonically with the number of completed premises.

## Results

On the most challenging Multi-LogiEval-90% (90% of samples have incomplete premises), OpenIKLR with DeepSeek-V3.2 achieves 68.53% accuracy and 78.39% F1, outperforming the strongest baseline (CoT at 50.64% Acc / 58.78% F1) by ~18 points in accuracy. On FOLIO-90%, the same backbone reaches 90.81% accuracy and 91.82% F1, surpassing the best baseline (DetermLR at 78.10% Acc / 75.37% F1) by over 12 points. The framework maintains consistent gains across all four LLM backbones (DeepSeek-V3.2, GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Flash, Claude-3.5-Haiku) and across 90%/70%/50% open-world proportions, with smaller models (Qwen3-8B) also benefiting substantially (e.g., 47.78% → 66.85% accuracy on Multi-LogiEval-90%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

OpenIKLR consistently outperforms all eight baselines across every model and dataset. The largest gaps are against pure symbolic solvers: LINC (Olausson et al., 2023) and LogicLM (Pan et al., 2023) score only 20.74% and 33.62% accuracy respectively on Multi-LogiEval-90% with DeepSeek-V3.2, versus 68.53% for OpenIKLR, because they cannot complete proofs when premises are missing. Among prompting-based methods, CoT (Wei et al., 2022) is the strongest baseline at 50.64% Acc / 58.78% F1, still trailing OpenIKLR by ~18 Acc points. CR (Zhang et al., 2024) and DetermLR (Sun et al., 2024) achieve 41.49% and 33.14% accuracy on Multi-LogiEval-90%, while SymbCoT (Xu et al., 2024) reaches 34.19%. RAG-based methods (HyDE: Gao et al., 2023; SiReRAG: Zhang et al., 2026) perform marginally better than CoT (37.91% and 39.00% Acc) but remain far below OpenIKLR; the authors attribute this to RAG retrieving statistically relevant but logically unnecessary premises (Table 4 shows 68.56% of retrievals are neither necessary nor sufficient). On FOLIO-90%, where the task is somewhat easier, the gap narrows (e.g., DetermLR reaches 78.10% vs. OpenIKLR's 90.81%), but OpenIKLR still leads by 12+ points in accuracy across all backbones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neuro-symbolic logical reasoning paradigm (NL→SL translation followed by solver-based entailment checking, as in LINC, LogicLM, and SymbCoT) by introducing an iterative completion loop that activates when the solver detects a reasoning gap. Rather than assuming all premises are present (the closed-world assumption underlying all prior solver-based methods), OpenIKLR uses the solver's failure trace to guide an LLM in generating minimal missing premises, then applies dual verification (logical sufficiency via the solver, factual accuracy via the LLM). It also positions itself against RAG-based knowledge completion (HyDE, SiReRAG), arguing that retrieval by statistical similarity cannot substitute for logically-necessary premise identification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: real-world reasoning (legal, scientific, educational) routinely involves implicit or missing premises that closed-world benchmarks ignore. The finding that RAG augmentation actually degrades OpenIKLR's performance (introducing noise that interferes with minimal premise completion) challenges the common assumption that more retrieved context is always beneficial for reasoning. Key limitations include the restriction to first-order logic, the dominance of NL2SL translation errors (78.54% of failures), and the fact that the fact verifier rejects ~80% of logically valid premises in the first round, suggesting a tension between logical utility and factual truth that remains unresolved. Extensions to richer logical formalisms and more robust symbolic parsing are natural next steps.
