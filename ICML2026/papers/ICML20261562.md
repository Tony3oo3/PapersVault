## Problem

Mainstream XAI methods for image classification—namely attribution and feature visualization—only explain encoded presences, i.e., concepts present in the input that excite a neuron. A largely overlooked causal relationship, where the absence of a concept increases neural activation (encoded absence), remains unaddressed by these methods in their standard form, leaving a critical aspect of model behavior unexplained with implications for robustness and bias.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261562/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image, a target neuron or channel in a trained model, and a set of images from other classes (for non-target attribution). It produces two types of explanations: (1) non-target attribution maps computed for a target class using inputs from other classes, where negative attributions reveal inhibitory concepts, and (2) minimally activating patches found by optimizing inputs to minimize (rather than maximize) the pre-activation of the target neuron. In the debiasing application, the method additionally consumes a segmentation mask of the spurious feature region and produces a modified training loss.

## Method

The paper formally defines encoded absence as a causal relationship where introducing a concept via do(x:=[x,C=1]) decreases a neuron's activation, and provides a mechanistic construction showing DNNs can implement such neurons through negative weights to presence-encoding neurons combined with a positive potential. To reveal encoded absences, the authors propose two modifications: (1) non-target attribution, which computes attribution for a target class t using inputs from other classes c≠t, so that concepts whose absence is informative produce negative attributions when present in those foreign inputs; and (2) feature visualization through minimization, which finds argmin_x z_j(x) to surface the patterns that most strongly inhibit a neuron. For debiasing, they extend standard attribution priors (which penalize presence-based reliance on spurious features) with a non-target attribution term that also penalizes absence-based reliance, yielding a presence+absence debiasing loss that prevents the model from using either the presence or absence of a spurious feature as a shortcut.

## Results

On ImageNet-1k, inserting least-activating 48×48 patches into the 100 most-activating images causes strong activation suppression (VGG19: 2.98→0.94; ResNet-50: 0.18→0.03; ViT-B/16: −0.14→−0.24), while random patches have minimal effect (e.g., VGG19: 2.98→2.68). In the ISIC debiasing experiment, presence+absence debiasing achieves 0.81 average accuracy on the unbiased validation set (matching the upper-bound model trained without bias) and 0.59 on the inverse-bias set, compared to 0.51 and 0.02 for no debiasing, and 0.77 and 0.41 for presence-only debiasing. The flip-rate experiment on ImageNet shows 84% prediction flips when inserting least-activating patches vs. 8% for random patches.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Mu & Andreas (2020) logical NOTs, the proposed least-activating patches produce dramatically stronger suppression (VGG19: 0.94 vs. 2.51; ResNet-50: 0.03 vs. 0.15), while logical NOTs yield no inhibitory effect beyond random patch insertions, confirming they capture a spatial non-overlap notion rather than causal inhibition. Against Ahn et al. (2024) most-activating patches, the comparison shows the opposite direction: most-activating patches increase activation (VGG19: 3.88) rather than suppress it, confirming that high-activation concept discovery methods are fundamentally unsuited to explaining encoded absences. In the debiasing comparison, presence+absence debiasing (extending Rieger et al., 2020 attribution priors with non-target attribution) outperforms presence-only debiasing by 12 percentage points on the inverse-bias validation set (0.59 vs. 0.41) and matches the unbiased upper bound on the no-bias set (0.81 vs. 0.81), whereas presence-only debiasing falls 4 points short (0.77). The paper also shows that standard target attribution and maximization-based feature visualization fail to reveal the inhibitory signal in all three experimental settings (Hassenstein-Reichardt, toy model, ImageNet), while the proposed non-target attribution and minimization-based visualization consistently recover it.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formalizes 'encoded absences' as a causal relationship in DNNs that standard XAI methods (attribution, feature visualization, counterfactual explanations) systematically fail to capture, drawing on the Hassenstein-Reichardt biological motion detector as a motivating example. It builds directly on Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al., 2017) by proposing non-target attribution—computing attributions for a target class using inputs from other classes—and on feature visualization (Olah et al., 2017) by proposing minimization-based visualization to reveal inhibitory concepts. The debiasing contribution extends attribution priors (Rieger et al., 2020) to jointly suppress both presence- and absence-based reliance on spurious features. The paper explicitly distinguishes its causal notion of encoded absence from related but weaker concepts: logical NOTs from compositional explanations (Mu & Andreas, 2020), contrastive explanations (Dhurandhar et al., 2018), and circuit-level inhibitory signals (Olah et al., 2020).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that encoded absences are pervasive—statistically significant in 512/512 VGG19 channels, 2036/2048 ResNet-50 channels, and 615/768 ViT-B/16 neurons—reframes how we should think about what neurons represent and what a complete explanation requires. The debiasing experiment reveals a practically important gap: standard presence-only debiasing leaves a residual absence-based bias that causes models to fail when spurious correlations are inverted, and the proposed presence+absence debiasing closes this gap to match unbiased training performance. Limitations include the computational cost of non-target attribution (roughly doubling cost), the focus on image classification, and the assumption of axis-aligned concepts; extending the analysis to LLMs, generative models, and polysemantic directions is identified as important future work.
