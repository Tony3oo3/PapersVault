## Problem

Federated Domain-Incremental Learning (FDIL) faces two unresolved theoretical gaps under partial participation: no backward knowledge transfer (BKT) guarantee quantifying when training on later tasks preserves or improves earlier-task performance without storing past data, and no task-uniform convergence rate that holds simultaneously across the entire task sequence rather than only the final task. Existing FCL analyses either target class-incremental settings, assume full client participation, or provide convergence only on the last task, leaving the common practical regime of fixed-label domain drift under federated privacy constraints theoretically unaddressed.

## Input / Output

Each client holds a non-IID partition (Dirichlet-sampled) of the current task's data (RGB images at standard resolutions or text sequences) and performs local SGD on it; no raw data from earlier tasks is retained by any party. The only cross-task input is the previous global model vector θ_{K-1}, which serves as a compact, privacy-preserving summary of all prior knowledge. The output is an updated global model after each communication round, structured so that performance on all K tasks seen so far is jointly optimized rather than only the current task.

## Method

SPECIAL augments FedAvg with a single server-side proximal anchor: after aggregating updates from a uniformly sampled subset of N clients (standard FedAvg averaging), the server solves a closed-form quadratic that blends the intermediate model with the previous task's final model, yielding θ_{i}^{t+1} = (1/(1+λ))·θ̄_{i}^{t+1} + (λ/(1+λ))·θ_{i-1}. Placing the proximal term at the server rather than the client is the key design choice—it lets clients optimize freely on the current task (preserving plasticity) while the server controls cumulative inter-task drift (preserving stability), avoiding the classic stability-plasticity dilemma of client-side regularizers. Theoretically, the paper proves a BKT bound showing earlier-task loss increases by at most a term scaling as 1/(tEN) that vanishes with more rounds, epochs, and participants, and establishes the first task-uniform non-convex convergence rate of O(√(E/(NT))) under partial participation, with explicit separation of stochastic variance, client heterogeneity, and inter-task drift σ_T². The method requires no replay buffers, synthetic data, or task-specific heads, keeping communication cost and model size identical to FedAvg.

## Results

SPECIAL achieves the highest average accuracy (ACC) on all four vision FDIL benchmarks: 62.12% on Digit-10, 54.92% on VLCS, 45.29% on PACS, and 24.30% on DN4IL, outperforming the best replay-based (SR-FDIL, MFCL) and replay-free (FLwF-2T, pFedDIL) baselines. On the NLP task sequence (SST-2, QQP, QNLI with DistilBERT), SPECIAL reaches 74.70% ACC and −4.03% BWT, surpassing the strongest baseline FLwF-2T (69.15% ACC, −5.50% BWT). Among replay-free methods, SPECIAL also attains the best or near-best backward transfer (BWT) on VLCS (−0.11%) and PACS (−11.66%), while requiring the fewest communication rounds to reach best performance (63, 55, 67, and 145 rounds across the four vision datasets).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPECIAL outperforms all seven baselines on average accuracy across all four vision datasets: it beats the strongest replay-based method SR-FDIL by 0.49% on Digit-10 (62.12 vs 61.63) and 0.37% on PACS (45.29 vs 44.92), and surpasses the best replay-free method FLwF-2T by 1.68% on VLCS (54.92 vs 53.24) and 1.09% on DN4IL (24.30 vs 23.21). On backward transfer, SPECIAL is competitive among replay-free methods (best on VLCS at −0.11%, close to FLwF-2T's −0.32%) but trails replay-based MFCL on Digit-10 (−21.78 vs −19.58) and pFedDIL on DN4IL (−19.50 vs −4.63), reflecting the stability-plasticity trade-off where methods that underfit current tasks show artificially less forgetting. Against standard FL baselines, SPECIAL improves ACC by 3.13–7.83% over FedAvg and 3.13–3.31% over FedProx while substantially reducing BWT degradation (e.g., −21.78 vs −40.44 on Digit-10). On the NLP sequence, SPECIAL leads all applicable baselines by 5.55% ACC over FLwF-2T (74.70 vs 69.15) and 8.57% over pFedDIL (74.70 vs 66.13). In terms of communication efficiency, SPECIAL reaches best performance in the fewest rounds (63 on Digit-10, 55 on VLCS, 67 on PACS, 145 on DN4IL) and matches FedAvg's per-round runtime, confirming its FedAvg-like communication footprint.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPECIAL extends the FedAvg/FedProx federated optimization framework by adding a single server-side proximal anchor that blends each round's aggregated update with the previous task's global model, departing from client-side regularizers (pFedDIL, Li et al. 2024b) that impose the proximal term during local training. Theoretically, it builds on centralized continual-learning analyses of backward knowledge transfer (Lin et al., 2022) and non-convex convergence (Han et al., 2023a), but extends them to the federated, non-IID, partial-participation regime with a task-uniform objective rather than last-task-only guarantees (Keshri et al., 2025). Unlike replay-based FCL methods (FedCIL, MFCL, SR-FDIL) that store or generate past data, SPECIAL is replay-free and keeps the client workflow identical to plain FedAvg, with the anchor applied only at the server via a closed-form convex combination.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills two concrete theoretical gaps for FDIL: a BKT bound under partial participation and the first task-uniform non-convex convergence rate (O(√(E/(NT)))) that holds across all tasks simultaneously, explicitly separating stochastic noise, client heterogeneity, and inter-task drift. Practically, the method adds negligible overhead (one closed-form blend per round, no extra communication or model growth), making it attractive for privacy-constrained deployments where replay is infeasible. Limitations include synchronous partial participation (no stragglers or stale updates), per-dataset tuning of λ, and bounded-gradient assumptions that may not hold for very deep networks; open questions include extending to asynchronous settings, developing self-tuning rules for the proximal weight, and validating on longer, more heterogeneous task streams.
