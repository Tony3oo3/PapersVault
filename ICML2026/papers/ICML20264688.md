## Problem

Existing negative sampling methods for graph-based recommendation rely on heuristics (random, popularity-based, hard, GAN-based) or hand-specified principles, without grounding in the true underlying negative distribution that objectively exists for any well-defined domain. This paper asks whether negative sampling can instead be derived from first principles as a rigorous numerical approximation of a well-established optimization objective (NCE/InfoNCE loss), and how to handle the common case where observed positive edges are only partially observable due to exposure bias.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a user-item bipartite interaction graph with node feature vectors, where the observed edge set may be a biased (partially observable) sample of true positive interactions. Beyond standard node embeddings for recommendation, the method jointly learns a propensity score function φ(u,v) parameterized by a lightweight MLP that takes node degrees, learned embeddings, and pairwise similarity as input. The sampling procedure operates per-anchor-node, drawing k negative samples via importance sampling from a reweighted observed negative distribution, with per-step complexity O(k) rather than O(|V|).

## Method

The paper derives the optimal proposal negative distribution q⁻ by showing it must equal p_f(v|u)·p⁻(v|u) (up to normalization) to make the empirical risk R₁(f) gradient-equivalent to the NCE/InfoNCE objective R₂(f), where p_f is the current model's generalized softmax output — making the result a principled form of adaptive hard negative sampling. For the biased-observation case, Theorem 5.1 extends this to require both a proposal positive distribution q⁺ and negative distribution q⁻, each reweighted by the inverse propensity score φ_{u,v} and model output p_f, with explicit reweighting constants β₁, β₂ derived from the class prior π⁺ and observation rate c. The propensity score φ is learned end-to-end via an MLP with two regularization constraints: a first-moment constraint (E[φ]=c) enforced by MSE loss, and a consistency constraint (order-preserving between φ and the similarity function g) enforced by ListMLE ranking loss. The full algorithm co-trains the graph encoder f and the propensity estimator φ in a single pass over edges, with the only additional overhead being the small MLP (less than 6% parameter increase over uniform sampling baselines).

## Results

On Recall@20, the proposed method achieves 17.41% (MovieLens) and 17.61% (Pinterest) with LightGCN, outperforming the strongest baseline AHNS (12.73% and 11.35% respectively) by over 4 percentage points. With the node-embedding base model, it reaches 12.77% on MovieLens and 12.59% on Pinterest, again beating the best baselines (DNS at 12.42% and AHNS at 10.84%). On LastFM (unbiased setting), gains are more modest (7.38–7.39% vs. 7.26–7.37% for DNS), consistent with the method's design targeting biased observations. NDCG@20 results (Table 5) confirm the same ranking, with the largest gap on Pinterest (7.28% vs. 5.92% for AHNS under LightGCN).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method wins on all six dataset–model combinations in Table 1, with the largest margins on Pinterest under LightGCN (17.61% vs. 11.35% for AHNS, a 6.3-point gap) and MovieLens under LightGCN (17.41% vs. 12.73% for AHNS). The two strongest baselines are DNS (Shi et al., 2023) and AHNS (Lai et al., 2024), both of which probe for hard negatives; the paper notes that its derived optimal distribution happens to align in form with adaptive hard negative sampling, but is grounded in a principled derivation from the true negative distribution rather than heuristics. On the unbiased LastFM dataset, gains are smaller (7.38–7.39% vs. 7.26–7.37%), as the method's debiasing machinery is less critical when observation bias is absent. Simpler baselines (RNS, PNS, MCNS) are consistently outperformed by 3–7 points, and GAN-based methods (IRGAN, NMNR) also trail by 2–5 points. The authors attribute the outsized Pinterest gain to the dataset's extreme sparsity (edge density 2.6×10⁻³ vs. 3.7×10⁻² for MovieLens), which amplifies exposure bias that only their method explicitly corrects via the learned propensity score φ.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repositions negative sampling from a heuristic design problem to a principled approximation of a well-established contrastive objective (NCE/InfoNCE loss, per Robinson et al. 2021 and Oord et al. 2018). It departs from the long line of ad-hoc sampling strategies (RNS, PNS, HNS, GAN-based, in-batch) by deriving the optimal proposal distribution q⁻ as the true negative distribution p⁻ reweighted by the model's current softmax output, which naturally yields adaptive hard negative sampling. For the biased-observation case, it borrows the propensity-score framework from PU-learning literature (He et al. 2018; Gerych et al. 2022) and instantiates it with a lightweight graph-feature MLP, a connection not previously made in the graph-recommendation sampling literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first end-to-end theoretical derivation linking the optimal negative sampling distribution to the ground-truth negative distribution, closing a gap where prior methods implicitly respected the true distribution (by leaving positive sampling untouched) while proposing arbitrary negative distributions. The biased-observation formulation with learned propensity scores addresses a practically critical but underexplored setting (exposure bias in recommendations), and the method's <6% parameter overhead makes it deployable. Open questions include whether the propensity-score estimation generalizes to rapidly evolving graphs with distribution shift, and whether the framework extends beyond link prediction to other graph tasks with different downstream objectives.
