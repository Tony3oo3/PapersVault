## Problem

Fixed draft models in speculative decoding cannot fully approximate the target distribution due to inherent capacity gaps, leading to degraded acceptance lengths and speedup. Although the verification process inherently provides free interactive feedback quantifying draft-target divergence, existing approaches (e.g., OSD) rely on ad-hoc, task-specific designs without a principled framework or theoretical guarantees connecting online adaptation to acceleration performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263727/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a text prompt, an offline-initialized lightweight draft model (e.g., ~0.24B–1.53B parameters), and a larger target model (7B–32B parameters), and produces a generated token sequence. The draft model is updated in a streaming fashion during inference with chunk sizes of 40 (EAGLE), 80 (Hydra), or 25 (reasoning) tokens per round, using cross-entropy loss (token-level) or DPO-style preference loss (reasoning) as the feedback signal. The output distribution is provably identical to the target model's, as the acceptance-rejection mechanism preserves the target distribution exactly.

## Method

OnlineSPEC formulates the generation-refinement loop as an online learning problem where the draft model (player) commits to a draft sequence, the target model (environment) verifies and provides loss feedback, and the draft adapts via an update rule. The key theoretical contribution is Theorem 1, which establishes that the acceleration rate γ is lower-bounded by a function of dynamic regret, candidate length k, and inference time ratio α, showing that sublinear regret yields near-optimal speedup. Three instantiations leverage different online learning techniques: (i) Online-LR applies OGD with DPO-style loss for step-level reasoning feedback; (ii) Opt-Hydra uses optimistic online learning, reusing the previous round's gradient as a predictive hint to achieve tighter regret bounds; and (iii) Ens-EAGLE maintains N draft heads with geometrically spaced learning rates as base learners, combined via a Hedge meta-learner to adaptively track the best-performing draft in non-stationary environments.

## Results

On GSM8K with Llama-2-7B-Chat, Opt-Hydra achieves 1.90× wall-clock speedup (AVGLEN 2.94) over standard AR decoding, outperforming OSD-Hydra (1.67×) by 13.8%. On Alpaca-Finance with Vicuna-7B, Opt-Hydra reaches 1.55× speedup (AVGLEN 2.70), a 24% improvement over OSD-Hydra (1.25×). For reasoning tasks with Qwen3-8B, Online-LR achieves 1.41× speedup and 9.98 average accepted length on GSM8K, substantially exceeding both the offline LR baseline (1.26×) and the naive OSD-LR combination (1.20×). Across seven benchmarks and five foundation models, OnlineSPEC consistently improves over both offline baselines and naive online adaptations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Opt-Hydra consistently outperforms OSD-Hydra across all four datasets and both target models, with the largest gap on Alpaca-Finance (1.55× vs 1.25×, +24%) and Llama-2-7B GSM8K (1.90× vs 1.67×, +14%), confirming that optimistic gradient hints from historical rounds yield tighter dynamic regret than plain OGD. Ens-EAGLE beats OSD-EAGLE on every dataset (e.g., 1.41× vs 1.28× on GSM8K, +10%), demonstrating that the ensemble of multiple learning rates robustly tracks the best adaptation rate in non-stationary environments. For reasoning tasks, Online-LR (1.41×) surpasses both the offline LR baseline (1.26×) and the naive OSD-LR combination (1.20×); notably, OSD-LR actually degrades below the offline LR baseline, showing that token-level distillation feedback is ill-suited for preference-pair feedback structures. Against vanilla speculative decoding, all OnlineSPEC variants achieve substantial gains (1.26×–1.90×), while against the offline Hydra/EAGLE/EAGLE-3 baselines (normalized to 1.00×), the improvements range from 6% to 90% depending on the method and dataset. On larger models (Vicuna-13B, Qwen3-32B), the same ordering holds: Opt-Hydra reaches 1.84× and Online-LR reaches 1.31×, confirming scalability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the generation-refinement paradigm (speculative decoding, cascade decoding, multi-token prediction) by framing the draft-target interaction as an online learning problem, building directly on OSD's initial exploration of online draft updates and on the draft architectures of Hydra, EAGLE/EAGLE-3, and Lookahead Reasoning. It departs from OSD's task-specific token-level distillation by providing a unified framework grounded in dynamic regret minimization, and incorporates advanced online learning techniques—optimistic learning (Rakhlin & Sridharan, 2013) and online ensemble (Zhao et al., 2024)—that were previously unexplored in the speculative decoding context. The key conceptual departure is establishing a formal theorem linking the algorithm's dynamic regret to the system's acceleration rate, which prior work had not provided.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The formal regret-to-speedup connection (Theorem 1) gives practitioners a principled way to reason about how much online adaptation is needed and which algorithmic choices (learning rate, ensemble size, hint quality) will yield the best acceleration, rather than relying on ad-hoc tuning. The finding that naive token-level OSD degrades performance on reasoning tasks while DPO-style online updates improve it highlights that feedback structure must match the task, a practical insight for deploying adaptive draft models. Open questions include whether the i.i.d.-across-positions assumption (Assumption 1) holds for long-context or multi-turn settings, whether the convexity-based regret bounds transfer meaningfully to non-convex neural network updates, and how the framework extends to reinforcement-learning-based decoding where the target distribution itself shifts during training.
