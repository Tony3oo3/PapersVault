## Problem

Standard retrieval benchmarks (e.g., BEIR) rely on heuristically constructed query sets that fail to cover the full semantic space of the underlying corpus, leaving prevalent document regions untested and causing aggregate metrics to systematically overestimate retrieval quality. The paper formalizes this as a statistical estimation problem under structured heterogeneity, showing that ignoring regime-level structure in query distributions yields irreducible bias and attenuated variance estimates regardless of evaluation set size.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262172/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a document corpus (e.g., NFCorpus with 3.6K medical documents) and an optional initial query set as input, and produces a stratified evaluation dataset organized into semantic strata (corpus-derived clusters) crossed with structural strata (3×3 grid of relevance dispersion and Jaccard alignment tertiles). Outputs include per-stratum performance metrics (nDCG@10, Recall@100, MAP@10), explicit coverage diagnostics (Minimum Semantic Coverage, Sufficient Corpus Coverage, Zero-Query Cluster counts), and interpretable failure-mode labels for each stratum. The generated queries come with LLM-verified relevance judgments (qrels) and are structured to guarantee balanced coverage across both semantic and structural dimensions.

## Method

The framework operates in three stages. First, it constructs a corpus-level semantic structure by extracting entities via GPT-4o-mini, embedding them (text-embedding-3-large, 256-d), building a k-NN graph (k=50, threshold 0.5) via FAISS, and applying Leiden community detection to yield interpretable semantic clusters. Second, it defines retrieval regimes along two axes: semantic strata (one per cluster) and structural strata (relevance dispersion Δ measuring how widely relevant documents spread across clusters, and Jaccard alignment J measuring query-to-document semantic correspondence), each discretized into tertiles. Third, it iteratively generates coverage-aware queries by sampling seed documents from underrepresented strata, prompting an LLM to produce natural queries, retrieving candidate relevant documents via dense+sparse pooling, and verifying relevance with LLM judgments—repeating until target coverage thresholds are met. The key theoretical contribution is showing that regime-level conditional means μ_k are stable, method-independent evaluation targets, whereas the aggregate mean μ depends on unknown query-distribution weights w_k, making stratified estimation both unbiased and decision-stable.

## Results

On NFCorpus, dense retrieval (text-embedding-3-large) achieves nDCG@10 of 0.31, BM25 achieves 0.29, and hybrid (RRF, k=60) achieves 0.30 under aggregate evaluation. The stratified framework reveals that standard benchmarks leave 40–50% of semantic regions untested: NFCorpus achieves only 51% Minimum Semantic Coverage and 11% Sufficient Corpus Coverage, with 182 zero-query clusters. Coverage-aware stratified query generation raises these to 90% MSC, 53% SCC, and 36 ZQC with comparable query counts. Performance varies up to 3× across structural strata (nDCG@10 ranges from 0.25 to 0.62), and within-stratum standard deviation is consistently lower than overall standard deviation across all three datasets and retrieval systems.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three retrieval paradigms—dense (text-embedding-3-large), sparse (BM25), and hybrid (RRF k=60)—under both aggregate and stratified protocols. No single retriever dominates across all semantic strata: dense leads on NFCorpus nDCG@10 (0.31 vs. 0.29 BM25, 0.30 hybrid), but BM25 outperforms dense on the cancer cell lines zero-query cluster (0.854 vs. 0.795), while hybrid provides the largest improvement on statistical methods (0.483 vs. 0.365 dense). The stratified protocol itself is the key comparison: aggregate evaluation masks systematic failures in underrepresented clusters, whereas stratified evaluation exposes them and changes system-selection decisions. In the embedding model comparison (text-embedding-3-small vs. Cohere embed-english-v3.0), the winner flips depending on aggregation: overall mean favors Cohere (text-embedding-3-small wins only 32.2% of bootstrap samples), while macro-average favors text-embedding-3-small (63.3% win rate), demonstrating that aggregate metrics implicitly weight by query volume and can mislead practitioners.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard retrieval evaluation paradigm (TREC, BEIR) by formalizing evaluation as a stratified statistical estimation problem rather than an i.i.d. sampling problem. It repurposes corpus-level community detection (Leiden algorithm on entity graphs, similar in mechanism to GraphRAG) not for retrieval optimization but for benchmark diagnosis and repair, distinguishing it from both GraphRAG/RAPTOR/HippoRAG (which stratify for retrieval) and slice-based evaluation (Chung et al., 2019) which uses query-level attributes rather than corpus-derived structure. The query curation pipeline draws on synthetic data generation techniques (RAGEval, DataMorgana) but adds explicit coverage-awareness constraints that prior generators lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 40–50% of semantic regions in standard benchmarks receive zero query coverage, and that aggregate metrics can mask up to 3× performance variation across strata, challenges a foundational assumption in IR evaluation and has direct implications for any practitioner selecting or comparing retrieval systems. Limitations include reliance on only three BEIR datasets, LLM-generated and LLM-verified queries (no human calibration), and prompt-sensitive entity extraction, leaving open whether the coverage-gap phenomenon and the stratification framework generalize to other domains, languages, and larger-scale benchmarks. A key open question is how to integrate coverage guarantees into ongoing benchmark maintenance as corpora and query distributions evolve.
