## Problem

Residual branch scaling factors (e.g., in Fixup, ReZero, DeepNet) are widely used to stabilize training in deep networks, but their role has been analyzed almost exclusively from an optimization perspective. This paper asks whether and how such scaling directly governs generalization performance, a question that had remained theoretically unaddressed despite empirical evidence of improved test accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265578/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Theoretically, the analysis operates on data points drawn from the unit sphere S^{d-1} in a regression setting, producing a generalization error bound as the key output. Empirically, the method is evaluated on grayscale MNIST images (28×28), color CIFAR-100 images (32×32), and Tiny-ImageNet (64×64), producing class labels. The scaling factor α = C·L^{-γ} is the sole architectural hyperparameter varied, with γ ∈ {0, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 1} controlling the depth-wise decay rate.

## Method

The paper establishes that the training dynamics of wide ResNets uniformly converge to RNTK-based kernel regression over the entire training trajectory (not just at initialization), enabling generalization error analysis via kernel spectral theory. It then proves that a constant scaling factor α causes the normalized RNTK to degenerate into a spike kernel (constant 1/4 off-diagonal plus a Dirac delta on the diagonal) as depth L → ∞, rendering the network asymptotically unlearnable. In contrast, when α decays as L^{-γ} with γ ∈ (1/2, 1], the kernel retains sufficient spectral structure for gradient flow with early stopping to achieve the minimax-optimal rate n^{-sd/((s+1)d-1)}. The core insight is that residual branch scaling functions as an implicit architectural regularizer for generalization—distinct from and in addition to its known optimization-stabilizing role—by preventing the kernel from collapsing as depth grows.

## Results

The paper's primary contribution is theoretical: it proves that constant residual branch scaling (α = 1) causes the RNTK to degenerate into a spike kernel as depth L → ∞, yielding a generalization error lower-bounded by Θ(1) independent of sample size (Theorem 3.1). In contrast, with depth-decaying scaling α = L^(−γ) for γ ∈ (1/2, 1], gradient flow with early stopping achieves the minimax-optimal rate O(n^(−sd/((s+1)d−1))) (Theorem 3.3). Experimentally, on MNIST at matched training loss ≈ 0.05, the proposed scaling (γ = 1) achieves 97.29% test accuracy versus 96.66% for the unscaled base, while on CIFAR-100 and Tiny-ImageNet (ResNet-34), both scaling and BatchNorm improve test accuracy over the base even after training accuracy saturates near 100%.

**Table 1: Matched Training Loss Evaluation on MNIST.** Matched Accuracy reports test accuracy when each method reaches comparable training loss (≈ 0.05), reflecting generalization ability; Best Training Loss is the minimum training loss within the same number of epochs, reflecting optimization performance.

| Fixup | **97.84** | 0.0085 |
| ReZero | 97.49 | **0.0046** |

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the unscaled base, the proposed scaling (γ = 1) improves matched test accuracy by 0.63 pp (97.29% vs. 96.66%) on MNIST and reduces best training loss by ~6.7× (0.0047 vs. 0.0315), confirming both generalization and optimization benefits. Compared to Fixup, the proposed method trails by 0.55 pp in matched accuracy (97.29% vs. 97.84%) but achieves a 1.8× lower best training loss (0.0047 vs. 0.0085). Against ReZero, the gap is minimal: 0.20 pp lower matched accuracy (97.29% vs. 97.49%) with nearly identical best training loss (0.0047 vs. 0.0046). Versus DeepNorm, the proposed method matches matched accuracy (97.29% vs. 97.30%) while achieving a 2.9× lower best training loss (0.0047 vs. 0.0135). On CIFAR-100 and Tiny-ImageNet (ResNet-34), the paper reports that scaling (γ = 1) and Batch Normalization both outperform the base on test accuracy, with the generalization gap persisting even at near-100% training accuracy; scaling is slightly inferior to BatchNorm, which the authors attribute to the simplified scaling rule used for theoretical tractability rather than a fundamental limitation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NTK framework (Jacot et al., 2018) and the RNTK analysis of Huang et al. (2020) by establishing uniform convergence of wide ResNets to RNTK kernel regression over the entire training trajectory, rather than merely at initialization. It departs from the dominant optimization-stability perspective on residual branch scaling (De & Smith, 2020; Hayou et al., 2021; Bachlechner et al., 2021) by reframing scaling as an implicit architectural regularizer that governs generalization. The power-law decay formulation α = C·L^{-γ} builds directly on Fixup initialization (Zhang et al., 2019), but the paper's contribution is showing that the decay rate γ must exceed 1/2 to avoid kernel degeneration and achieve minimax-optimal rates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous generalization theory explaining why residual branch scaling improves test performance beyond its well-known optimization benefits, providing a principled criterion (γ > 1/2) for designing depth-dependent scaling in ultra-deep architectures. The asymptotic unlearnability result for constant α is a strong negative result that clarifies a fundamental limitation of the original ResNet design in the infinite-depth limit. Key limitations include the infinite-width assumption, restriction to fully connected networks and regression, and small-scale experiments (CIFAR-100, Tiny-ImageNet, MNIST); extending the theory to classification, convolutional architectures, and modern large-scale training remains open.
