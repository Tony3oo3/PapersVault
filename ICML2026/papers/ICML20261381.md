## Problem

Existing theoretical explanations of LLM hallucination on random facts rely on no-free-lunch impossibility arguments or informal compression intuitions, but neither explains why hallucination—assigning high confidence to non-facts—is the dominant error mode rather than forgetting or uniform uncertainty. This paper asks: under a closed-world setting with finitely many sparse random facts and limited model capacity, what is the information-theoretically optimal strategy, and why does it necessarily produce confident hallucinations?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261381/cropped_page7_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The membership tester receives a universe U of u plausible natural-language claims and a key set K ⊆ U of n known facts (with n/u → 0 in the sparse regime). Given a query i ∈ U, it produces either a continuous confidence score x̂_i ∈ [0,1] (probability estimation variant, evaluated under log-loss) or a binary accept/reject decision x̂_i ∈ {0,1} (binary decision variant, evaluated under FNR/FPR constraints). The memory state W (e.g., model parameters) must encode K with budget B(M) = I(W; K) bits, and the analysis characterizes the minimum B(M)/n required to meet given error levels.

## Method

The paper establishes a rate-distortion theorem: the asymptotic per-key memory cost equals the minimum KL divergence KL(μ_K ‖ μ_N) between the score distributions on facts and non-facts, subject to error constraints. For log-loss, the unique optimal solution is a 'hallucination channel'—all facts receive a single high-confidence atom x* = e^{-ε_K}, and a fraction q* of non-facts share that same atom, making hallucination the memory-optimal error mode that cannot be removed by thresholding. For binary FNR/FPR constraints, the optimal distributions are Bernoulli(1−ε_K) and Bernoulli(ε_N), recovering and sharpening classical Bloom-filter space lower bounds. The proof combines a data-processing-inequality lower bound (using concavity of conditional entropy and a binomial coefficient approximation) with an achievability construction via Carathéodory's theorem (reducing to at most 5 support points). Empirical validation uses from-scratch 2-layer transformers on random strings and LoRA fine-tuning of Qwen3.5-2B on synthetic IDs and real ISBNs, confirming the predicted hallucination channel and ~12–22% overhead over the information-theoretic bound.

## Results

On from-scratch 2-layer Transformers (15,145 parameters) trained on 15,145 random 15-character strings, the discretized learned score distributions (50 bins) incur only ~12% overhead in KL divergence relative to the information-theoretic lower bound from Theorem 4.1, with effective memory of ≈2 bits/parameter. On LoRA fine-tuning of Qwen3.5-2B across 12 runs (synthetic 13-digit IDs and real-world ISBN-13, ranks 2 and 4, λ_F ∈ {0.5, 1, 2, 4}), empirical KL clusters within 14–22% of the theoretical lower bound (slope ≈1.18 on the reference line), with effective memory of ≈0.17 bits/param at rank 4 and ≈0.27 bits/param at rank 2. The hallucination channel predicted by Theorem 4.1—a nonzero fraction of non-facts assigned the same high-confidence atom as facts—is qualitatively and quantitatively confirmed in both settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper, so the 'baselines' are the information-theoretic lower bounds themselves and prior filter space bounds. The empirical models (from-scratch Transformers and LoRA-tuned Qwen3.5-2B) are validated against the KL-divergence lower bound from Theorem 4.1, achieving within 12–22% of the bound—confirming the rate-distortion prediction without any method outperforming the theoretical limit. The framework recovers and sharpens classical space lower bounds: it reproduces the Carter et al. (1978) one-sided filter bound (log(1/ε_N) bits per key), settles the Θ(1)-per-key gap left open by Pagh & Rodler (2001) for two-sided filters by pinning down the exact KL value, and generalizes Hurley & Waldvogel (2007) from a single Hamming-distortion constraint to separate FNR/FPR constraints. A hash-based two-sided filter construction is shown to achieve the lower bound up to o(n) bits of overhead. The from-scratch models (≈2 bits/param) store significantly more information per parameter than LoRA fine-tuning (≈0.17–0.27 bits/param), illustrating that the effective memory budget for random facts is far smaller than the nominal trainable parameter count, consistent with the structured-vs-random-fact competition argued in Appendix A.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of work: (1) statistical-learning explanations of LLM hallucination (Kalai et al., 2025; Mohsin et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025) that use no-free-lunch arguments for binary classification, and (2) classical space lower bounds for Bloom-type filters (Carter et al., 1978; Pagh & Rodler, 2001; Hurley & Waldvogel, 2007; Li et al., 2023a). It departs from prior compression-based hallucination explanations (Pan et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025; Kim, 2025a,b) by working in a finite closed-world setting with a rigorous rate-distortion characterization rather than informal 'compression causes errors' arguments. The key conceptual move is framing factuality judgment as a membership testing problem with generic error metrics, which simultaneously recovers discrete filter bounds and continuous log-loss analysis under one theorem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central result—that hallucination (assigning high confidence to non-facts) is the information-theoretically optimal error mode under limited capacity, not a failure of training—reframes hallucination mitigation: post-processing and thresholding can only move along the memory-error frontier, not escape it. This has direct practical implications for training strategies (e.g., the paper shows aggressive 'always-recall' pressure disproportionately increases hallucination) and justifies RAG as a capacity-removal solution. Limitations include the idealized closed-world assumption, the sparse-regime requirement (n/u → 0), and the somewhat hand-wavy argument in Appendix A that effective memory for random facts is much smaller than raw parameter count. Open questions include whether the rate-distortion characterization holds in the open-world setting, how structured knowledge and random facts interact in practice, and whether the finite-support achievability construction has practical algorithmic implications.
