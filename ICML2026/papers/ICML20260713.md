## Problem

As AI agents transition into coordinated economic actors in agentic swarms and online marketplaces, there is no framework for understanding the microeconomic forces—adverse selection, reputation dynamics, and explore-exploit tradeoffs—that govern AI-only labor markets. Existing LLM-agent simulations focus on bargaining, macroeconomics, or static-policy competition, leaving open the question of what strategic reasoning capabilities agents need to succeed under competitive pressure and incomplete information.

## Input / Output

Each round, agents observe current job listings (task types and posted budgets), a public earnings leaderboard, and the last H_ctx=10 rounds of public market outcomes (allocations, winner identities, winner reputations, reputation changes), but never competitors' bid prices or latent skill levels. The per-round action is either a BID (an ordered list of up to L job-price pairs with a strict preference ranking that determines allocation priority) or a TRAIN (a chosen task type for skill investment), making the effective action space combinatorial in the number of available jobs and bid-list length. The environment outputs Gale-Shapley stable matching allocations, Beta-evidence reputation updates, realized performance scores, and aggregate economic metrics (market share, cumulative reward, win rate, Gini coefficient).

## Method

AI-Work formalizes the AI labor market as a partially observable stochastic game with four coupled mechanisms: CES (Cobb-Douglas) client-side scoring over reputation and normalized price, job-proposing Gale-Shapley stable matching with per-agent capacity ν, a discounted Beta-evidence reputation system with forgetting factor λ and community cold-start prior, and saturating skill updates that encode diminishing-returns human capital investment. The key methodological contribution is the Strategic Self-Improving Agents (SSA) prompt scaffold, a minimal architectural intervention that directs the LLM to reason explicitly through three capability domains—metacognition (calibrated self-assessment of latent skill vs. public reputation), competitive awareness (inference of rival strategies from observable market outcomes), and strategic planning (coherent multi-round train-vs-bid policies)—before selecting an action. Experiments compare SSA against CoT, ReAct, and fixed-policy baselines (Specialist, Greedy) across 25 markets with 16 agents each, then ablate each capability block via ANOVA, and run controlled token-matched prompt variants to disentangle the contribution of the M/C/P decomposition structure from generic domain-knowledge injection. Market-design experiments vary information disclosure (open vs. sealed bidding) and contract form (flat-fee vs. performance-linked pay) to show how platform rules shift equilibrium behavior toward price deflation or skill investment.

## Results

SSA (Strategic Self-Improving Agents) achieves 14.3% market share and $633.5 cumulative reward over 25 independent runs with 16 GPT-5 agents each, representing a 1.5× improvement in market share and 1.2× in cumulative reward over the best prompting baseline (CoT at 9.7% / $419.4). SSA also attains the highest win rate (59.5%) among all methods. In the ablation setting (10 simultaneous markets), the full M+C+P scaffold reaches 18% market share versus 7.5% for ReAct, with each individual capability (metacognition, competitive awareness, planning) contributing a statistically significant main effect (p < 10⁻⁴).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SSA outperforms both prompting baselines on every primary economic metric. Against CoT (Wei et al., 2022), SSA gains +4.6 pp market share (14.3% vs 9.7%), +$214 cumulative reward (633.5 vs 419.4), and +15.2 pp win rate (59.5% vs 44.3%), though CoT achieves a marginally higher average reputation (2.97 vs 2.83). Against ReAct (Yao et al., 2023), SSA gains +5.0 pp market share (14.3% vs 9.3%), +$97 cumulative reward (633.5 vs 536.8), and +13.8 pp win rate (59.5% vs 45.7%); ReAct bids at a slightly higher normalized price (0.87 vs 0.82) but converts this into fewer wins. Both fixed-policy baselines (Specialist, Greedy) are substantially outperformed, confirming that LLM-based strategic reasoning adds value beyond static heuristics. In the controlled ablation where all variants compete simultaneously in 10 markets, the full M+C+P scaffold (18%) dominates ReAct (7.5%), and each capability block individually improves market share over its absence with p < 10⁻⁴.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of agent-based computational economics (ACE), LLM-based economic simulation, and self-improving agent research, but carves out a distinct niche: studying microeconomic forces (adverse selection, reputation-mediated matching, human-capital investment tradeoffs) in AI-only labor markets where LLMs act as novel economic participants rather than proxies for human behavior. It departs from prior LLM economic simulations (EconAgent, homo silicus) that focus on macro dynamics or bargaining by zooming into the micro-level strategic reasoning required under incomplete information, and from self-improving agent work (Reflexion, STOP) by grounding metacognition in a competitive market context rather than isolated task performance. The Gale-Shapley matching, CES scoring, and Beta-reputation mechanisms are drawn from labor economics and mechanism design literature, repurposed here as the institutional scaffolding for a controlled AI-agent testbed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As AI agent marketplaces (ClawGig, WORQ, ClawTasks) begin to emerge in practice, this paper provides one of the first controlled frameworks for understanding how platform design choices (open vs. sealed bidding, flat vs. performance-linked pay, task diversity) shape agent behavior, wage dynamics, and market concentration in AI labor markets. The identification of three specific reasoning capabilities (metacognition, competitive awareness, strategic planning) and the demonstration that a minimal prompt scaffold elicits them with measurable economic gains offers a practical starting point for agent designers. Key open questions remain: the role of explicit verification mechanisms in displacing reputation, adversarial dynamics and collusion among agents, multi-stage production workflows, and whether findings from this stylized proxy-task environment transfer to interactive, open-ended tasks. The simulation's simplifications (no communication channels, no compute costs, no dispute resolution) limit direct quantitative transfer to real markets.
