## Problem

Vision tool-use RL yields strong benchmark gains, but it remains unclear whether these gains stem from improved tool interaction, evolving intrinsic capabilities, or merely reduced tool-induced side effects. Existing evaluations report only end-to-end tool-available accuracy, making mechanistic attribution of gains impossible. This paper asks: what does vision tool-use RL actually learn, and which learning signals dominate the observed improvements?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263171/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The MED framework operates on checkpoint-level evaluation data: at each training step, a VLM is evaluated under two paired protocols (tool-free and tool-available) on the same image-question pairs, yielding per-example correctness and call/no-call labels. From these paired evaluations, MED produces a hierarchical decomposition: a scalar tool contribution ratio S_tool, four signed gain/harm terms (Call Gain, Schema Gain, Call Harm, Schema Harm), and per-term Mass × Policy × Quality factor values. The underlying VLM takes an RGB image, a text question, and an optional crop-and-zoom tool schema as input, and produces a text answer with zero or more crop-and-zoom invocations (up to 10 interaction turns).

## Method

MED (Measure-Explain-Diagnose) is a coarse-to-fine analytical framework that decomposes tool-available performance drift into intrinsic and tool-induced components. Measure: it defines the tool-induced gap G(t) = Acc_w(t) − Acc_wo(t) and tracks its evolution Δ_tool(t), yielding an additive decomposition of total drift and a tool contribution ratio S_tool. Explain: it derives a four-term probabilistic decomposition of G(t) via the law of total probability, separating Call Gain (tool-free failures corrected via tool calls), Schema Gain (no-call recovery under tool availability), Call Harm (tool-free successes broken by tool calls), and Schema Harm (no-call errors under tool availability). Diagnose: it further factorizes each term into Mass (domain size P(D)), Policy (action probability P(a|D)), and Quality (conditional outcome P(o|a,D)), enabling root-cause attribution of whether changes arise from shrinking failure sets, shifting call behavior, or degraded execution. The framework is instantiated on two VLMs (Qwen2.5-VL, tool-naive; Qwen3-VL, tool-native) trained with GRPO and a binary outcome reward, evaluated across six fine-grained perception benchmarks at 21 checkpoints.

## Results

Across six benchmarks (VStar, HR-Bench 4K/8K, VisualProbe Easy/Medium/Hard), the tool contribution ratio S_tool is only 0.30 for Qwen2.5-VL and 0.22 for Qwen3-VL, indicating that over 70% of total drift magnitude is attributable to intrinsic (tool-free) improvement rather than tool-induced effects. Tool-free accuracy rises from 48.4% to 63.4% (Qwen2.5-VL) and from 53.0% to 68.1% (Qwen3-VL), while tool-available accuracy reaches 65.4% and 61.2% respectively. The four-term decomposition reveals that Gross Gain (Call Gain + Schema Gain) stagnates or declines, while Gross Harm (Call Harm + Schema Harm) decreases consistently, meaning RL primarily suppresses tool-induced breakage on already-solved examples rather than improving correction of intrinsic failures. Schema interference (accuracy drop when schema is shown but execution is forbidden) is −5.8% for Qwen2.5-VL and −13.0% for Qwen3-VL, and human-aligned tool-call gain rates are 63.4% and 93.0% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analysis paper rather than a method-comparison paper, so there are no external baselines to beat or lose to. The primary controlled comparison is between two VLM backbones with different tool priors: Qwen2.5-VL (tool-naive, no prior crop-and-zoom training) and Qwen3-VL (tool-native, trained with crop-and-zoom at release). Qwen2.5-VL shows a positive tool-induced drift (f_w > f_wo, green area in Fig. 2), meaning tool availability adds net benefit over intrinsic gains, while Qwen3-VL shows a negative relative drift (f_wo > f_w), meaning its intrinsic capability improves faster than its tool-assisted performance. A secondary comparison across training variants (no-tool RL, outcome-only tool-RL, and a +0.1 tool-aware reward bonus) shows that the tool-aware bonus increases Call Gain but also raises Call Harm for Qwen2.5-VL, confirming that raw call-frequency rewards do not produce sustained hard-case correction. For Qwen3-VL, all three variants yield similar trajectories due to its strong prior tool familiarity. The no-tool RL sanity check (Table 2) confirms that tool-free accuracy under no-tool RL closely tracks that under tool-RL (mean gap ≈ 0.018), validating the tool-free protocol as a practical intrinsic reference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a diagnostic complement to two existing lines of work on vision tool-use RL: (1) methods that train VLMs to invoke visual operators like crop-and-zoom via GRPO (OpenAI 2025; Zheng et al. 2025; Su et al. 2025a; Lai et al. 2025), and (2) faithfulness studies that evaluate whether individual tool calls are reasonable or grounded (Yu et al. 2025a; Liu et al. 2025b; Du et al. 2025). Rather than proposing a new training algorithm or a new faithfulness metric, it introduces the MED (Measure-Explain-Diagnose) framework—a coarse-to-fine probabilistic decomposition that attributes checkpoint-level performance changes to intrinsic capability drift versus tool-induced gain/harm, and further factorizes each term into Mass, Policy, and Quality components. The framework is inspired by standard probability accounting (law of total probability) but applies it to the training-dynamics setting of tool-use RL, a perspective absent from prior work that typically reports only end-to-end tool-available accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that vision tool-use RL in the crop-and-zoom setting primarily reduces tool-induced harm (fewer call-induced errors, weaker schema interference) while showing limited improvement in tool-based correction of intrinsic failures—challenges the common assumption that such RL teaches tool mastery. This has direct design implications: future reward objectives should target selective, successful correction on the failure set while penalizing harm on the success set, rather than rewarding raw tool-call frequency. Key limitations include the single-tool scope (crop-and-zoom only), two backbones, and accuracy-only evaluation; whether the MED decomposition and its findings generalize to multi-tool settings, code-execution tools, or efficiency-aware metrics remains open. The framework itself, however, requires only paired tool-free/tool-available evaluation and observable call/no-call behavior, making it potentially applicable to broader tool-use RL analyses.
