## Problem

Existing PEFT methods like LoRA scale memory as O(dr) with model hidden dimension and rank, while SVD-based alternatives require storing dense basis vectors per layer, hurting inference throughput in multi-tenant serving. Sparse fine-tuning methods lack structured subspace decomposition and face scalability issues in identifying useful parameter subsets. The paper asks how to parameterize weight updates to simultaneously achieve expressivity, parameter efficiency, stable optimization, and compute scalability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260021/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes frozen pretrained transformer weights (RoBERTa, Llama-2/3.1, Gemma-2, ViT) and learns only a sparse set of 1000–5000 non-zero coefficient entries per layer, with positions randomly assigned and shared across all layers of the same dimension. For language tasks, inputs are text sequences (up to 512 tokens for GLUE, variable for instruction tuning) producing class labels or generated text; for vision tasks, inputs are RGB images producing class labels. The Fusion Frame projection matrices are generated algorithmically at initialization and never stored, reducing a Llama-2-7B adapter checkpoint to 1.28 MB versus 67.1 MB for LoRA rank-32.

## Method

FrameFT decomposes the input and output spaces of each transformer layer into k overlapping subspaces using Tight Fusion Frames constructed via the Spectral Tetris algorithm, then models the weight update as W' = W + (α/√(mn)) P_m C P_n^T, where P_m and P_n are fixed block-diagonal projection matrices and C is a sparse block-diagonal coefficient matrix whose non-zero entries are the only trainable parameters. Unlike LoRA's single low-rank factorization, FrameFT learns rich interactions across multiple structured subspaces while maintaining a fixed, algorithmically generated basis that requires no per-layer storage. The paper proves that this reparameterization preserves L-Lipschitz smoothness of the loss landscape (with constant scaled by the product of frame bounds), yielding a 1/T convergence rate for gradient descent. The inherent block-sparsity of Spectral Tetris frames combined with sparse coefficients yields 2–20× higher inference throughput than LoRA and 5–7% faster training with comparable GPU memory.

## Results

On the GLUE benchmark, FrameFT achieves 86.1 average on RoBERTa-Base and 88.7 on RoBERTa-Large, matching or exceeding full fine-tuning (85.2 and 88.2 respectively) while using 10× fewer parameters than LoRA (24K vs. 0.3M). On instruction tuning (Alpaca + LM-eval harness), FrameFT achieves 63.62 average on Llama-2-7B and 70.65 on Gemma-2-9B, matching or exceeding LoRA (63.18 and 70.83) with 30–50× fewer trainable parameters (320K–420K vs. 13.6M–17.9M). On Vision Transformers, FrameFT reaches 83.36 average on ViT-B and 86.15 on ViT-L, outperforming LoRA (77.58 and 84.94) with 5–20× fewer parameters. Inference throughput reaches 39.4k tokens/sec on Llama-2-7B, a 67% speedup over LoRA (23.6k).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FrameFT consistently outperforms or matches all 11 baselines on average across the three evaluation suites. On GLUE (RoBERTa-Base), FrameFT achieves 86.1 average, beating LoRA (85.2), AdaLoRA (85.0), FourierFT (85.0), SVFit (85.1), SMT (84.7), and SVFT (84.6) while using 10× fewer parameters than LoRA. On instruction tuning, FrameFT leads on Llama-2-7B (63.62 vs. LoRA 63.18, FourierFT 63.22, DoRA 63.09, (IA)³ 63.01, S²FT 62.66) and Gemma-2-2B (64.11 vs. LoRA 62.70, DoRA 63.20, SVFT 63.23), though it trails LoRA slightly on Llama-2-13B (66.11 vs. 66.42) and Llama-3.1-8B (67.87 vs. 68.56). On Vision Transformers, FrameFT substantially outperforms LoRA on ViT-B (83.36 vs. 77.58 average) and ViT-L (86.15 vs. 84.94), and also beats FourierFT (80.29 and 86.68 respectively) with fewer parameters. S²FT degrades catastrophically on larger models (39.16 on Llama-2-13B, 49.36 on Llama-3.1-8B), while FrameFT remains stable. In inference throughput, FrameFT achieves 39.4k tok/s on Llama-2-7B versus 23.6k for LoRA and 1.8k for FourierFT, a 67% speedup over LoRA due to the sparsity of both the coefficient matrix and the Fusion Frame projection matrices.

## Relation to Prior Work

FrameFT departs from the dominant LoRA paradigm (dense low-rank factorization) and SVD-based PEFT methods (SVFit, SVFT, LoRA-XS) that require storing dense orthogonal basis vectors per layer. Instead, it repurposes Fusion Frame theory from harmonic analysis—specifically the Spectral Tetris construction of Tight Fusion Frames (Casazza et al., 2011) and the real-valued extension of Fickus et al. (2023)—to generate algorithmically-defined, inherently sparse projection matrices that are shared across layers. This positions FrameFT between LoRA's global adaptability and sparse fine-tuning's precision, but with a key structural difference: the bases are fixed and generated in closed form rather than learned or derived from pretrained weights, and the trainable coefficients are explicitly sparse with cross-layer shared support patterns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for multi-tenant LLM serving: a 1.28 MB adapter checkpoint (vs. 67.1 MB for LoRA rank-32) and ~67% higher inference throughput than LoRA on Llama-2-7B make hot-swapping thousands of task-specific adapters feasible. The theoretical contribution—showing FrameFT preserves Lipschitz smoothness of the loss landscape, unlike LoRA's asymmetric A/B parameterization—provides a clean convergence guarantee that guides the Parseval frame design choice. Open questions include whether the method extends to structured sparsity patterns beyond 2:4 (currently limited by hardware kernel support), whether subspace dimension ρ could be made learnable, and whether the TFF existence constraints (specific k,ρ,d configurations) limit applicability to all layer geometries.
