## Problem

Standard A/B tests discretize continuous decision variables (prices, incentives) into a handful of arms, forcing practitioners to select the best discrete level rather than the true continuous optimum. This precludes extrapolation to untested values and fails to capture how treatment effects vary across high-dimensional user heterogeneity, yielding coarse, non-personalized policies. The paper asks how to estimate policy values and learn personalized continuous policies when only a finite set of discrete treatment arms has been observed in an RCT.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 77-dimensional pre-treatment user covariates (12 categorical, 46 continuous), five discrete treatment levels {0, 178, 358, 538, 718} from a randomized experiment, and a binary outcome (whether the user posted a video). It produces a personalized continuous treatment policy mapping each user's feature vector to an optimal incentive value over the full range [0, 1000], along with debiased policy value estimates and ATE estimates at arbitrary (including unobserved) treatment levels. A key structural constraint is that the DGP is semi-parametric: parametric in treatment via a sigmoid-polynomial link, but fully non-parametric in covariates via a DNN, so the polynomial degree K is fixed at m−1 (number of arms minus one) to guarantee identifiability.

## Method

DLPT is a three-stage procedure built on a sigmoid-polynomial DGP E[Y|X=x,T=t] = σ(θ*(x)′ T̃_K(t)), where a structured DNN outputs K+1 covariate-dependent coefficients that interact with polynomial features of treatment. Stage 1 estimates the nuisance parameter θ*(x) by empirical risk minimization over a feed-forward DNN (128-32-8 architecture, ReLU, binary cross-entropy). Stage 2 constructs a Neyman-orthogonal influence function ψ(z,π;θ̂,Λ) that debiases the policy value estimate via sample splitting, achieving √n asymptotic normality and first-order robustness to nuisance estimation error. Stage 3 solves a constrained ERM over a chosen policy class (discrete arms, linear, or DNN-parameterized continuous policies) to minimize regret, with the DNN policy trained end-to-end via automatic differentiation through the smooth score function. The key differentiator from standard deep learning is the structured polynomial interaction between DNN outputs and treatment, combined with the orthogonal score correction, which together enable valid extrapolation from five discrete arms to the full continuous treatment manifold with a minimax-optimal O(1/√n) regret bound.

## Results

On the Platform O field experiment (N ≈ 7.35M users, 5 discrete treatment arms), DLPT achieves a Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of 2.25% for out-of-sample ATE recovery, compared to 7.18%–43.59% for all baselines. For policy learning on held-out user subgroups, DLPT attains a Mean Percentage Regret (MPR) of 0.48% and 73.08% accuracy in identifying the optimal arm, versus 6.32%–10.05% MPR and 20%–25% accuracy for baselines. In the continuous DNN policy class (semi-synthetic evaluation), DLPT achieves 0.99% MPR, a 94.5% reduction over its own discrete policy (17.96%) and an 86.7% improvement over the best baseline (PDL at 7.42%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DLPT dominates all seven baselines (LR, LogR, LCM, PDL, GRF, Spline, UNI) across every metric and policy class. The closest competitor is Pure Deep Learning (PDL), which achieves 7.18% MAPE on ATE recovery and 6.32% MPR on discrete policy learning—still 3.2× worse on MAPE and 13× worse on MPR than DLPT. Parametric methods (LR, LogR, LCM) and the non-personalized Spline perform worst, with MAPE of 30–44%, indicating they cannot capture the complex covariate–treatment interactions. GRF (tree-based) also struggles (40.43% MAPE) because step-function splits cannot interpolate across wide unobserved gaps in the treatment space. The gap between DLPT and PDL specifically isolates the contribution of the Neyman-orthogonal debiasing step: both use similar DNN architectures, but DLPT's doubly robust score function provides the critical bias correction that PDL lacks. In the continuous DNN policy class, DLPT's advantage widens further (0.99% vs. 7.42% MPR), confirming that the debiasing mechanism becomes increasingly valuable as the policy space expands.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the double machine learning (DML) and orthogonal score framework of Chernozhukov et al. (2018, 2019) and Farrell et al. (2020, 2021) to a specific structural gap: learning continuous personalized policies when only discrete treatment arms from an RCT are observed. Existing continuous-action policy learning methods (Chen et al., 2016; Kallus & Zhou, 2018) require continuous logging propensities or kernel smoothing across the treatment space, which are inapplicable when the treatment support consists of exactly a few discrete arms. The key methodological bridge is the sigmoid-polynomial DGP assumption, which allows polynomial extrapolation from discrete points to the full continuous manifold while retaining the Neyman orthogonality properties needed for √n-consistent policy value estimation. The regret bound proof directly leverages the orthogonal ERM decomposition of Foster & Syrgkanis (2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a very practical gap in platform experimentation: companies routinely run discrete A/B tests but need to deploy continuous personalized policies (e.g., optimal incentive amounts per user). The 94.5% regret reduction from discrete to continuous policies (17.96% → 0.99% MPR) on 7.4M users demonstrates substantial real-world value. Key limitations include the reliance on a sigmoid-polynomial parametric form for the dose-response curve, the requirement for RCT data (not observational), and the static single-period setting that ignores long-run preference drift. Open questions include whether the polynomial degree assumption holds in more complex multi-treatment or sequential decision settings, and how the framework extends to interference-prone two-sided markets.
