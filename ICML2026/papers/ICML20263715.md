## Problem

Existing embodied benchmarks treat each task as an indivisible unit evaluated by a single binary success signal, collapsing long-horizon decision-making into one outcome and making it impossible to attribute failures to specific perceptual or reasoning capabilities. This prevents practitioners from determining whether a failure stems from poor object grounding, unstable spatial-temporal modeling, or flawed reasoning logic, leaving improvement efforts largely unguided.

## Input / Output

BEAR presents 4,469 interleaved image-video-text VQA samples where inputs are single images, video sequences (79.5% of images at 512–1024 px resolution), or video-plus-current-observation pairs, each paired with a natural language instruction. Outputs are skill-specific: normalized 2D point coordinates (x, y ∈ [0,1]) for pointing, normalized bounding boxes (x_min, y_min, x_max, y_max ∈ [0,1]) for localization, four-option multiple-choice selections for trajectory, spatial, and planning skills, and episode-level success rates (all steps must be correct) for long-horizon tasks. BEAR-AGENT additionally produces Python tool-call code that the agent executes to retrieve grounding boxes, depth maps, trajectory extensions, and 3D scene-graph structures.

## Method

The paper introduces a hierarchical skill-level diagnosis framework operating at three levels: horizontal diagnosis identifies which of the 14 atomic skills act as persistent bottlenecks during long-horizon task execution; vertical diagnosis performs fine-grained failure analysis within each isolated skill; and cross-skill failure attribution aggregates error patterns across skills to reveal shared fundamental limitations. The key empirical finding is that perceptual grounding errors (54.8%) dominate over reasoning errors (22.4%), even in reasoning-oriented skills, and that unstable spatial-temporal modeling (30.3% of spatial failures, 32.0% of planning failures) is a recurring cross-skill bottleneck not exposed by prior task-level benchmarks. Building on these insights, BEAR-AGENT is a multimodal conversable agent that wraps an MLLM in a dialogue loop where the model generates Python code to invoke modular tools—GroundingDINO for open-set object detection, Set-of-Mask for segmentation, DepthAnything for monocular depth, trajectory extension functions, and a semantic scene graph with a temporal event notebook for 3D spatial-temporal modeling. The agent executes the generated code, returns structured visual and spatial cues to the MLLM, and iterates until the model signals completion, effectively supplying the explicit perceptual and spatial information the MLLM cannot reliably extract from raw inputs alone.

## Results

On BEAR, GPT-5 achieves the highest average score of 52.17% across 14 atomic skills, still far below human performance (89.40%). Most evaluated MLLMs score only 20–40%, with proprietary models averaging 39.2% versus 25.8% for open-source models. BEAR-AGENT, the tool-augmented multimodal agent, improves GPT-5's average from 52.17% to 61.29% (a 9.12-point absolute gain, 17.5% relative) and InternVL3-14B from 33.93% to 36.24%. In online settings, integrating BEAR-AGENT with MOKA yields a 20.17% average improvement in simulation, and it surpasses the InternVL3-8B baseline by 23.4% in real-robot grasping.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the offline BEAR evaluation, BEAR-AGENT substantially outperforms all prompting baselines: for GPT-5, it gains +8.4 points over Few-shot (61.29 vs. 54.09) and +9.2 points over CoT (61.29 vs. 52.11), with the largest improvements in Pointing (+10.5 pts) and Bounding Box IoU (+0.105). For InternVL3-14B, BEAR-AGENT achieves 36.24% versus 34.16% for Few-shot and 26.88% for CoT (which actually degrades performance). In the online simulation experiments, BEAR-AGENT integrated with MOKA (Liu et al., 2024a) delivers a 20.17% average improvement in task success rate across three manipulation task sets (General, Spatial, Part). In the real-robot grasping experiment, BEAR-AGENT surpasses the InternVL3-8B (Zhu et al., 2025) baseline by 23.4% success rate over 30 rollouts with 10 different language instructions. These results confirm that the perceptual and spatial tools identified through BEAR's diagnosis framework translate into concrete gains in both offline evaluation and online execution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from existing embodied benchmarks (EmbodiedBench, EmbodiedAgent-Interface) that evaluate MLLMs at the task level with binary success/failure, by introducing a skill-level diagnostic framework that decomposes embodied tasks into 14 atomic perceptual and reasoning skills. The BEAR-AGENT component builds on tool-augmented visual reasoning (Visual Programming, Visual Sketchpad) and visual prompting (Set-of-Mark, GroundingDINO) but repurposes these tools specifically to address the diagnosed perceptual and spatiotemporal bottlenecks in embodied settings. The long-horizon category extends simulation-based evaluation (AI2-THOR, ManipulaTHOR) by decomposing episodes into skill-oriented VQA steps, going beyond the holistic task success metrics used in prior embodied benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BEAR provides the first systematic diagnostic lens for understanding *why* embodied MLLMs fail, revealing that perceptual grounding errors (54.8%) dominate over reasoning errors (22.4%) even in reasoning-oriented skills—a finding that redirects improvement efforts toward perception rather than reasoning. The demonstration that CoT prompting and test-time compute scaling yield negligible gains (<10%) suggests the field needs architectural or tool-based solutions rather than prompting tricks. Open questions include whether the 14-skill taxonomy generalizes beyond household manipulation to other embodied domains (navigation, multi-agent), whether BEAR-AGENT's tool augmentation can be distilled into model weights via targeted SFT/RL, and whether the skill-level diagnosis framework transfers to video-only or 3D-input settings.
