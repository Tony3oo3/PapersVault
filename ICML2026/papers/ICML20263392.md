## Problem

Standard neural networks and even physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) can solve PDEs accurately but leave physically meaningful scaling exponents—such as the r^{2/3} corner singularity or r^{-1/2} crack-tip stress—implicit within millions of opaque weights, requiring fragile post-hoc fitting to extract. This paper asks whether a network architecture can treat scaling exponents as explicit trainable parameters and recover them directly from PDE constraints without labeled solution data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263392/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a PDE operator (e.g., Laplacian or Poisson), boundary conditions, interior and boundary collocation points (concentrated near singularities via graded sampling x_i = t_i^p), a forcing function, and optionally a physical constraint parameter such as the wedge angle ω. It produces the solution u(x) = Σ c_k x^{μ_k} together with the exponents {μ_k} and coefficients {c_k} as explicit, directly interpretable parameters. In physics-informed mode no labeled solution data is required; in supervised mode it accepts noisy point samples y_i = f(x_i) + ε_i. The primary scientific output is the exponent values, not merely the function values.

## Method

MSN-PINN represents the solution as a single Muntz expansion u_θ(x) = Σ_{k=1}^K c_k x^{μ_k} where both coefficients and exponents are trainable, replacing the millions of weights in a standard MLP with a small set of physically meaningful parameters. Training uses a composite loss combining PDE residual, boundary-condition penalty, ℓ₁ sparsity, and a constraint-aware term (e.g., Σ|c_k| sin²(μ_k ω) for wedge problems) that supplies the gradient signal the PDE residual lacks when all r^μ sin(μθ) are harmonic. A two-timescale optimizer updates coefficients fast (solving a linear subproblem) and exponents slowly (navigating a nonconvex landscape), with η_μ ≈ 0.1–0.5 η_c. The paper proves identifiability (unique recovery of distinct exponents), a stability bound O(σ/(c_min Δ² √N)) governed by a minimum separation condition Δ, and a quadratic approximation rate O(|μ−α|²), and shows constraint-aware training improves corner-singularity accuracy from 14.6% to 0.009% error.

## Results

MSN-PINN recovers the Kondrat'ev (1967) corner singularity exponent (μ=2/3) with 0.009% relative error using constraint-aware training, a 1600-fold improvement over naive training (14.6% error). On a 40-configuration wedge benchmark spanning five angles and four BC types, constraint-aware MSN-PINN achieves a 100% success rate with 0.022% mean exponent error, versus 55% success and 5.96% mean error for naive training. Singular forcing in a Poisson problem yields dual exponent recovery with 0.03% and 0.05% errors, and a Mode-III antiplane shear crack recovers the fracture exponent α=0.5 with 0.04±0.02% error across five seeds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is a standard tanh-activated PINN trained on the same PDEs and BCs, followed by post-hoc power-law fitting to the learned solution (with the best fitting window among several near-singularity windows to give the baseline a favorable setting). MSN-PINN wins decisively on exponent recovery in both tested settings: on the wedge corner singularity, MSN-PINN achieves 0.013±0.014 exponent error versus 0.278±0.243 for the standard PINN (roughly 20× lower), and on the singular forcing problem, MSN-PINN achieves 0.378±0.156 / 0.279±0.452 versus 13.97±8.07 / 13.02±7.39 for the standard PINN (roughly 37–50× lower). MSN-PINN also achieves lower solution reconstruction error in both settings (e.g., 0.18 vs. 2.30 global error on the wedge). The standard PINN's exponent estimates are particularly unstable near singular regions, with large variance (±8.07, ±7.39) reflecting the fragility of post-hoc fitting, whereas MSN-PINN learns exponents directly as explicit parameters, eliminating the extraction step entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Muntz-Szasz Networks (N'guessan, 2025), which introduced learnable power-law exponents as neural architecture parameters, by embedding them within a physics-informed training framework (Raissi et al., 2019) so that exponents are discovered from PDE residuals and boundary conditions rather than from labeled solution data. It departs from standard PINNs, which hide solution structure in millions of weights, and from sparse identification methods (SINDy, AI Feynman) that search over fixed candidate libraries, by learning continuous exponents directly. It also contrasts with numerical methods for singular problems (XFEM, hp-FEM, graded meshes) that prescribe singular bases analytically; MSN-PINN learns the bases from physics. The constraint-aware training mechanism, which adds BC-compatibility penalties to break the flat residual landscape, is a new loss design not present in prior PINN or MSN work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a structural reason why standard PINNs fail on singular problems: for Laplace on a wedge, the PDE residual is completely flat in the exponent (every μ yields a harmonic function), so no gradient signal exists for exponent selection. The constraint-aware training that resolves this degeneracy, achieving a 1600-fold accuracy improvement and 100% success across 40 wedge configurations, provides a general recipe for encoding additional physics when the PDE alone is insufficient. Key limitations include a practical separation threshold (Δ ≥ 0.1) below which close exponents merge, inability to represent logarithmic corrections, and all experiments being restricted to 1D/2D simple PDEs. Open questions include 3D corner and edge singularities, Bayesian uncertainty on discovered exponents, and automatic discovery of the correct constraint family without user specification.
