## Problem

Image generation models aligned via RLHF with aesthetic reward models systematically override explicit user requests for 'anti-aesthetic' or wide-spectrum outputs (unrealism, negative emotions, clashing colors, intentional blur), defaulting to conventionally beautiful images instead. Reward models penalize such outputs even when they perfectly match the user's prompt, and this bias extends to real historical artworks that score below typical AI-generated images. The paper argues this constitutes 'reversed alignment'—users are aligned to the model's encoded aesthetic rather than the model serving the user's intent—narrowing artistic expression and aesthetic pluralism.

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline takes two types of text prompts per sample: an original COCO caption (p_o) and a wide-spectrum aesthetics prompt (p_a) constructed by sampling 2–4 'bad' dimension descriptions from VisionReward and having Qwen3-VL-235B rewrite the caption to incorporate those undesirable attributes. For each prompt, a generation model produces an image, which is then scored by multiple reward models (HPSv3, HPSv2, ImageReward, PickScore, MPS) and a fine-tuned Qwen3-VL-4B per-dimension judge, yielding four aesthetic scores per model pair plus BLIP/CLIP text-image matching scores. A separate image-to-image editing experiment takes a generated happy-face image plus an emotion-change instruction as input and produces a modified image scored for emotional accuracy.

## Method

The paper is an empirical position study rather than a new architecture. It constructs a 300-prompt wide-spectrum aesthetics benchmark by combining COCO captions with 2–4 randomly sampled VisionReward 'bad' dimension descriptions, then uses Qwen3-VL-235B to generate anti-aesthetic prompts. Ten generation models across four families (Flux Dev/DanceFlux/PrefFlux/Flux Krea, SDXL/Playground, SD3.5M/GenEval/PickScore variants, and Nano Banana) are evaluated on both original and wide-spectrum prompts, with success measured by whether an LLM judge (validated at κ=0.80 against human raters) selects the anti-aesthetic image as better matching p_a. Reward model bias is quantified via binary classification (select the image better matching p_a from the pair), Wilcoxon signed-rank tests comparing aligned vs. base model scores, and McNemar's tests on success counts. A controlled emotion-bias experiment uses Nano Banana image-to-image editing to isolate emotional content, and validation on 2,928 real anti-aesthetic photographs (AVA) and ~10K paintings (LAPIS) confirms that reward models penalize genuine artistic deviation far more severely than AI-generated anti-aesthetic outputs.

## Results

On the wide-spectrum aesthetics classification task (selecting the image that better matches an anti-aesthetic prompt), HPSv3 achieves only 38.1% accuracy and 0.385 AUROC—worse than random guessing—while the unaligned BLIP-L baseline reaches 96.5% accuracy and 0.888 AUROC, and CLIP-L reaches 91.3% accuracy. Among generation models, aesthetics-aligned variants (DanceFlux, Playground, SD3.5M-PickScore) show significantly lower wide-spectrum aesthetics success rates than their base counterparts (p < 10⁻⁵ in most cases), with DanceFlux failing to follow anti-aesthetic prompts ~64% of the time. On the controlled emotion test, HPSv3 scores only 19% accuracy on anger and 32% on fearfulness, versus 96% and 79% for BLIP, confirming a systematic toxic-positivity bias in reward models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between aesthetics-aligned reward models and their unaligned CLIP/BLIP base models on the wide-spectrum aesthetics classification task. BLIP-L (96.5% acc) and CLIP-L (91.3% acc) substantially outperform every aligned reward model, with HPSv3 (38.1%) performing worse than chance and HPSv2.1 (56.5%) barely above random. Among aligned reward models, PickScore (85.1%) and HPS (83.5%) are the least biased, while HPSv3 is the most severely misaligned. On the generation side, DanceFlux (HPSv2.1-aligned) degrades most relative to Flux Dev (ΔHPSv3: −1.105 vs −3.165; success rate drops sharply), while Flux Krea—aligned to non-generalized Krea preferences from an unaligned base—actually outperforms Flux Dev on wide-spectrum aesthetics (ΔHPSv3: −4.372, BLIP 0.950). Nano Banana, a closed-source model with strong negation handling, achieves the largest quality gap (ΔHPSv3: −9.351) and highest BLIP score (0.957), confirming that strong prompt-following and anti-aesthetic generation are compatible. The emotion-specific test further shows that DanceFlux consistently generates happy faces but fails on negative emotions (0.27 anger, 0.33 fear), whereas Flux Krea (0.65 anger) and SD3.5-Large (0.89 anger) handle negative emotions far better, and HPSv3's emotion classification accuracy (19–44%) is dramatically below BLIP's (79–96%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper extends the 'over-alignment' concern previously raised for LLMs in health-related queries (Guo et al., 2025) to the image generation domain, specifically targeting the conflict between aesthetic reward optimization and explicit user instructions for anti-aesthetic content. It builds conceptually on Helliwell's (2024) philosophical argument that full aesthetic alignment may be undesirable, and on the VSF pilot study (Guo & Du, 2025) that explored negative prompting, but departs from both by providing the first large-scale empirical test across multiple generation and reward model families. The paper also positions itself alongside concurrent work (Taylor et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026) that identifies aesthetic bias in reward models, but distinguishes itself by framing the issue as a user-autonomy and aesthetic-pluralism problem rather than a demographic-fairness problem, and by introducing the 'wide-spectrum aesthetics' evaluation protocol using VisionReward's multi-dimensional labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a systematic and quantifiable failure mode in current image generation pipelines: aesthetic alignment via RLHF/GRPO actively degrades instruction following for legitimate anti-aesthetic requests, and reward models penalize outputs that correctly match user intent. The 'reversed alignment' framing—where the model aligns the user to its encoded aesthetic rather than the other way around—raises important open questions about how to design reward signals that respect individual intent without sacrificing quality for conventional requests. Limitations include a modest 300-prompt evaluation set, reliance on an LLM judge (Qwen3-VL) as ground truth, and the absence of a proposed solution or mitigation strategy. The findings are particularly relevant as image generation tools become creative instruments rather than consumer products, and as the field moves toward personalized and fine-grained aesthetic control.
