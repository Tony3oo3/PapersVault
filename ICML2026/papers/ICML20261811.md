## Problem

When a reasoning model makes up to K successive attempts with binary verifier feedback, standard trajectory-level RL treats the entire multi-attempt rollout as a single sample, failing to exploit dense per-attempt rewards and backpropagating success signals to incorrect early attempts. Naive per-attempt weighting (using each attempt's pass/fail as its gradient weight) introduces systematic bias by over-emphasizing earlier attempts. The paper asks: what RL algorithm can efficiently maximize the Verification@K objective while teaching the model better correction capabilities across attempts?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261811/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each attempt, the model receives a state encoding the original problem statement plus the full history of prior attempts and their binary pass/fail verifier feedback, and produces a candidate text solution (e.g., a boxed math answer, a U/D/L/R maze move sequence, or a discrete state-id trajectory). The deterministic verifier returns a single 0/1 signal per attempt; on success the rollout terminates immediately, on failure the incorrect solution and feedback are appended to the state for the next attempt. The final Ver@K reward is 1 if any of the K attempts is correct, 0 otherwise.

## Method

The paper derives the exact policy gradient of the Verification@K objective at the attempt level, showing it decomposes into per-attempt terms weighted by continuation success probabilities V_{θ,i}(s_i) (the probability of eventual success given the current state). They prove that Naive Attempt-Level (NAL) weighting—using each attempt's raw pass/fail as its gradient weight—produces biased gradients that implicitly optimize a discounted objective overemphasizing early attempts. The key contribution, Calibrated Attempt-Level (CAL) GRPO, corrects this bias by reweighting each attempt's advantage by the empirical probability that all subsequent attempts fail (estimated from the current rollout group), thereby assigning each attempt a weight proportional to its marginal contribution to final Ver@K success. The paper proves CAL is unbiased and achieves strictly lower variance than trajectory-level GRPO under an independent-attempt-index assumption, and demonstrates consistent improvements over both TL-GRPO and NAL-GRPO on MATH, Maze navigation, and a synthetic Markov chain benchmark.

## Results

The paper evaluates three GRPO variants (TL-GRPO, NAL-GRPO, CAL-GRPO) on MATH, Maze (5×5, 7×7, 9×9), and a synthetic Markov Chain benchmark under Ver@2 and Ver@4 objectives, with results primarily reported as training curves rather than a single summary table. CAL-GRPO consistently achieves the highest final Ver@K across all three benchmarks, with the clearest separation on the trap Markov Chain task where it breaks through the ≈0.75 plateau (1−(1/2)²) earlier than both baselines. NAL-GRPO reduces the average number of attempts the most (behaving like a discounted objective), while CAL-GRPO trades slightly more attempts for higher overall Ver@K accuracy. On the Markov Chain task, NAL-GRPO underperforms even TL-GRPO due to over-penalizing early failed attempts under the strict sequence-level verifier, whereas CAL-GRPO remains the most robust method across all settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CAL-GRPO against two baselines: TL-GRPO (standard trajectory-level GRPO using a single Ver@K reward for all tokens) and NAL-GRPO (naive attempt-level GRPO with uniform per-attempt weighting). On MATH and Maze, both NAL-GRPO and CAL-GRPO substantially outperform TL-GRPO in Ver@K accuracy, with CAL-GRPO achieving the best final performance by strengthening second-attempt correction (Figures 2b, 4b). On the Markov Chain task, the ordering reverses for NAL-GRPO: it underperforms TL-GRPO in both no-trap and trap settings because per-attempt normalization over-penalizes early failures under the strict shortest-path verifier, while CAL-GRPO still wins by preserving useful correction signal. In terms of gradient estimator quality (Figure 13), the variance ordering is CAL-GRPO < TL-GRPO < NAL-GRPO for the first-attempt component, and the bias ordering is TL-GRPO < CAL-GRPO < NAL-GRPO, confirming that CAL-GRPO achieves the best bias-variance trade-off. NAL-GRPO is shown to be biased (it optimizes a discounted objective per Corollary 5.3), while both TL-GRPO and CAL-GRPO are unbiased for the Ver@K objective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and RLOO (Ahmadian et al., 2024) policy-gradient frameworks from trajectory-level to attempt-level credit assignment for multi-attempt reasoning under hard verifier feedback. While prior multi-turn RL work (Wei et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2025; Ding et al., 2025) introduces turn-level rewards for agent tasks, this paper specifically targets repeated attempts on the same input and derives the exact policy gradient for the Verification@K objective, showing that the intuitive per-attempt weighting (NAL) is systematically biased. The calibrated reweighting (CAL) is a novel correction that estimates each attempt's marginal contribution to final Ver@K success from within-group rollouts, bridging the gap between sparse trajectory-level rewards and the biased naive attempt-level approach.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that naive attempt-level weighting implicitly optimizes a discounted objective rather than Ver@K is a non-trivial conceptual result that cautions against the common heuristic of simply assigning per-attempt rewards. The accuracy-compute trade-off it reveals (NAL minimizes attempts, CAL maximizes Ver@K) gives practitioners a principled knob for test-time compute allocation. Key limitations include the reliance on deterministic binary verifiers, the independent-attempt-index assumption (Assumption 5.1) that may not hold for strongly coupled attempts, and evaluation only on a 4B model with fixed K. Open questions include extending to noisy/learned verifiers, adaptive K, and whether the calibration benefits persist at larger model scales where per-attempt success rates are already high.
