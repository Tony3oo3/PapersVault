## Problem

The paper addresses the widespread but theoretically unjustified practice of interpreting LLM token logprobs as estimates of world event probabilities. It identifies that the shift from language modeling (source distribution estimation) to instruction-following (response prediction) creates fundamentally different target output distributions, yet the literature routinely conflates them. This conflation explains common empirical findings—systematic overconfidence, 'calibration failure,' and the probability-quality paradox—as category errors rather than model deficiencies.

## Input / Output

The advocated method (second-order prediction) takes a text instruction describing a world event, optionally augmented with few-shot examples, and produces an explicit probability estimate as part of the model's generated text—either numeric (e.g., '0.7') or verbalized (e.g., 'likely a but possibly b'). This contrasts with the standard logprob approach, where probabilities are read off the softmax of the final layer and are constrained to the token vocabulary structure. The second-order output is not tied to a specific token format, allowing the model to express distributions over arbitrary event spaces, though the paper acknowledges that verbalized probabilities are currently poorly calibrated and limited in granularity for large output spaces.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a formal theoretical framework using a BDI (Belief-Desire-Intention) agent model to characterize how different training stages (pretraining, SFT, RLHF) and inference strategies (naive completion, zero-shot, few-shot) produce distinct ideal output distributions. It defines three cases—multiply describable outcomes, observed outcomes, and unobserved outcomes—and proves that for unobserved outcomes, a prediction objective rationally concentrates probability mass on the mode, making logprobs systematically diverge from true event probabilities even with unbiased beliefs. The proposed solution, second-order prediction, instructs the model to explicitly report event probabilities as its output, which eliminates the prediction-versus-estimation mismatch because reporting the probability is itself the accurate action under any objective. The paper further argues that improving second-order prediction falls squarely within standard post-training (SFT/RLHF), whereas fixing logprob-based methods would require altering the underlying data distribution or loss function, and suggests leveraging reasoning capabilities to fit explicit formal models (logistic regression, Bayesian networks) as a path forward.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments; its main contribution is a formal framework distinguishing three tasks (source distribution estimation, response prediction, and target distribution estimation) and showing that different LLM training/inference settings produce inherently different output distributions. The paper demonstrates via a Bayesian BDI-agent analysis that token logprobs conflate these distributions: under a prediction objective, the output distribution concentrates on the mode (p=1 on the best guess), while the true event distribution is spread according to world probabilities. The authors identify specific prior works (Hu & Levy 2023; Yona et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2023; Baan et al. 2022; Meister et al. 2025) whose conclusions rest on the unjustified assumption that completion, response, and event distributions coincide.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not run empirical comparisons against baselines. Instead, it provides a conceptual critique of two dominant approaches to extracting world probabilities from LLMs: (1) using softmax logprobs from naive completion (p_ND), which the authors argue reflects the language data distribution rather than the event distribution due to reporting bias (e.g., Paik et al. 2021 on banana color); and (2) using logprobs from instruction-based inference (p_ZS or p_FS), which the authors show reflects a prediction objective that concentrates probability mass on the mode, making it systematically overconfident relative to true event probabilities. The paper positions second-order prediction (explicitly asking the model to report probabilities) as the only approach whose objective is aligned with recovering event distributions, noting that verbalized probabilities are empirically better calibrated than logprob-based estimates (Tian et al. 2023) but still limited by model reasoning quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new theoretical ground by formally distinguishing three tasks—source distribution estimation, target distribution estimation, and response prediction—and showing that conflating them explains many apparent 'calibration failures' in the LLM literature. It repurposes the belief-desire-intention (BDI) framework from AI to model how an LM agent's beliefs, desires, and intentions produce different output distributions depending on the prompt format. The practical idea of eliciting verbalized probabilities (Tian et al., 2023; Xiong et al., 2024) is not new, but the paper elevates it to a theoretically grounded 'second-order prediction' task and contrasts it systematically with logprob-based approaches, while also engaging with the RLHF/mode-collapse literature (Li et al., 2024; O'Mahony et al., 2024) to explain why instruction tuning shifts output distributions away from world-event distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a unifying lens that reinterprets a large body of calibration and confidence-elicitation results as category errors rather than model deficiencies, which could redirect research effort away from 'fixing' logprob calibration toward improving the model's actual probabilistic reasoning. Key open questions remain: how well current models perform second-order prediction in practice, how to handle expressiveness limits for large output spaces, and whether reasoning-based approaches (fitting explicit probabilistic models) can close the gap. The authors acknowledge that verbalized probabilities are still often poorly calibrated, so the framework is a necessary but not sufficient condition for reliable world modeling.
