## Problem

Large-enrollment STEM courses like calculus face thousands of handwritten free-response submissions per quiz, yet TAs can provide minimal personalized feedback due to workload. No prior work has conducted a course-scale, multi-term deployment and evaluation of LLM-based grading on authentic handwritten mathematics, and the absence of ground-truth labels for partial-credit scoring makes reliable evaluation fundamentally difficult. This paper addresses how to evaluate and deploy OCR-conditioned LLM grading systems without ground truth, under noisy handwritten inputs, and with constraints relevant to real high-stakes course deployment.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes scanned images of standardized answer sheets (with pre-printed 'Solution' and 'Final Answer' boxes to suppress OCR hallucination), the corresponding problem statement, and structured rubric prompts. OCR and grading operate at the region level rather than whole-page to reduce cross-problem interference. Outputs include a LaTeX transcription of the handwritten work, a numerical score on the problem's point scale, and natural-language feedback explaining deductions; these are delivered to students by email as reference-only scores alongside official TA grades.

## Method

The system is a three-stage pipeline: (1) region segmentation of the answer sheet, (2) OCR-to-LaTeX transcription via GPT-4.1-mini with context-aware prompting that states the problem, suppresses hallucination on blank boxes, and explicitly forbids autocorrection of student errors, and (3) rubric-guided grading using a two-level prompt design (a global system message enforcing logical consistency, no style penalties, and tolerance for false starts, plus problem-specific rubrics). The key grading innovation is a dual-rubric strategy: a flexible rubric that credits diverse valid reasoning and a fixed point-based rubric targeting required steps, evaluated in independent API calls and aggregated via a max-rule that selects the higher score. For algebra-heavy items, o3-mini is used with a three-run stabilization procedure (selecting the run closest to the mean score, analogous to majority-vote self-consistency). Evaluation employs a multi-perspective protocol combining TA-score alignment (MAE, within-1 rates), student surveys, and independent review by 20+ external evaluators, since no ground-truth labels exist for partial-credit handwritten work.

## Results

Across 3,945 free-response records from ~800 students, AI-assigned scores show a mean gap of −0.40 points relative to TA scores (SD 1.12), with quiz-level MAE ranging from 0.47 to 1.06 and within-1-point agreement between 68% and 86%. On the 171-solution subset, GPT-4.1-mini OCR achieves 84% acceptable transcriptions versus 55% for Mathpix, and using GPT-4.1-mini OCR reduces downstream grading MAE from ~0.69–0.71 (Mathpix) to 0.40–0.48. Independent human review of 3,851 records found 79.79% of AI evaluations fully correct and 9.55% acceptable, with AI–human MAE (0.40–0.49) comparable to the inter-reviewer MAE of 0.477, placing AI grading within the range of observed human–human variability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against prior AI-grading systems but instead compares internal pipeline choices and validates against human graders. On OCR, GPT-4.1-mini (84% acceptable) substantially outperforms Mathpix (55%) on the 171-solution subset, and this OCR advantage translates to a large downstream grading gain: MAE drops from 0.69–0.71 (Mathpix OCR + GPT-4.1-mini grading) to 0.40–0.48 (GPT-4.1-mini for both OCR and grading). For the grading model, o3-mini achieves a lower MAE against Reviewer 2 (0.237) than GPT-4.1-mini (0.401) but exhibits higher run-to-run variance (mean σ 0.086 vs 0.065) and greater cost; two independent reviewers preferred o3-mini feedback in ~23–26% of cases where a difference was observed, with ties at 64–73%. The max-rule aggregation of fixed and flexible rubrics consistently yields the lowest MAE against TA scores compared to either rubric alone, and the resulting AI–human MAE (0.40–0.49) is on par with the human–human inter-reviewer MAE (0.477), confirming that AI grading falls within the natural variability of human graders.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits within a growing line of work on LLM-based grading of handwritten STEM solutions (Aisyah et al., 2025; Caraeni et al., 2025; Gandolfi, 2025; Lee et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024; Mok et al., 2024; Nkoyo et al., 2025; Kortemeyer & Nöhl, 2025), but departs from prior studies by shifting focus from score reliability in controlled settings to course-scale deployment with multi-perspective evaluation of both scores and formative feedback. Rather than proposing a new grading algorithm, the paper repurposes off-the-shelf LLMs (GPT-4.1-mini, o3-mini) through careful OCR-conditioned prompting, a dual-rubric (flexible + fixed) framework with max-rule aggregation, and iterative failure-case refinement. The multi-run stabilization procedure for o3-mini draws on the self-consistency idea of Wang et al. (2023), adapted to the partial-credit, three-run grading setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is among the first large-scale, real-course empirical studies of LLM-based grading in university mathematics, covering thousands of authentic handwritten submissions from nearly 800 students across 20 quizzes and three terms, which provides evidence that current systems can serve as practical grading assistants under human oversight. The multi-perspective evaluation protocol (TA alignment, student surveys, 20+ independent reviewers) addresses the fundamental challenge of evaluating grading quality in the absence of ground-truth labels, a problem that will recur as AI grading expands to higher-stakes assessments. Key open questions remain: principled deferral/intervention policies for low-confidence cases, fairness across handwriting styles and solution conventions, strategic adaptation by students, and generalizability beyond single-variable calculus at one institution. The planned two-track benchmark release (clean vs. noisy OCR) would be a valuable resource for the community if executed.
