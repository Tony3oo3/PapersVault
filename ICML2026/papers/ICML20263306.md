## Problem

Conformal prediction methods are conventionally evaluated by marginal coverage and average interval length, but it is unclear whether these two metrics are sufficient to certify a method's practical utility. This paper demonstrates that a randomized wrapper can deceptively shorten average intervals while preserving valid coverage, exposing a fundamental blind spot in the standard evaluation paradigm.

## Input / Output

The Prejudicial Trick (PT) wraps any base CP algorithm and takes a test feature vector, a trained model, a calibration set, a target miscoverage rate α, and a probability parameter p ∈ (1−α, 1). For each test point it stochastically returns either a null set (probability 1−p) or a CP interval computed at the adjusted miscoverage rate α′ = 1 − (1−α)/p (probability p), so the output is a random prediction set whose realized value depends on an internal uniform draw. The paper additionally outputs an Interval Stability score, defined as the expected variance of the interval measure over repeated algorithmic runs on the same input and calibration data.

## Method

PT is a post-hoc wrapper that, for each test sample, assigns a null prediction set with probability 1−p and a base-CP interval at a more conservative level α′ = 1−(1−α)/p with probability p, guaranteeing marginal coverage via p(1−α′) = 1−α. The paper derives sufficient conditions (a first-order differentiability condition in Theorem 3.7 and a secant condition in Theorem 3.10) under which PT strictly reduces expected interval length, showing that model misspecification—common in practice—typically satisfies these conditions. Proposition 3.2 further shows PT is a degenerate limiting case of localized CP's normalized-score construction, linking the trick to a realistic source of stochasticity. To detect such vacuous randomness in future methods, the paper introduces Interval Stability (Definition 4.1), a complementary diagnostic that quantifies run-to-run variance of the returned interval for a fixed input, which is provably nonzero for PT (Proposition 4.2) and zero for deterministic baselines like VCP.

## Results

On 10 regression datasets (α=0.1), PT-VCP achieves shorter average interval lengths than VCP in 9 out of 10 cases while maintaining valid marginal coverage (e.g., BIKE: 19.59 vs 20.46; MEPS-19: 41.92 vs 42.34; STAR: 9.63 vs 10.14). On ImageNet classification (α=0.1, p=0.95), PT-RAPS reduces average set size by 3–13 classes across all 9 architectures compared to RAPS (e.g., ResNet18: 295.60 vs 304.02; DenseNet161: 289.29 vs 302.03) while preserving 90% coverage. The proposed Interval Stability metric confirms the vacuous nature of these gains: VCP has zero stability, PT-VCP exhibits 0.58–1.26, and localized CP shows 0.01–1.41, flagging the run-to-run randomness that makes the length improvement practically meaningless.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against VCP (the primary baseline), PT-VCP wins on interval length in 9 of 10 regression datasets (only FACEBOOK-2 shows a negligible increase) while matching coverage at 0.90, demonstrating that the coverage-length metric can be gamed without any genuine improvement in per-instance reliability. Against RAPS on ImageNet classification, PT-RAPS consistently reduces average set size by 3–13 classes across all 9 architectures (ResNet18: 295.60 vs 304.02; ResNet152: 288.98 vs 301.53) while maintaining 90% coverage. Against CQR, PT-CQR achieves shorter intervals on 8 of 10 datasets (e.g., meps-19: 4.44 vs 4.60; bike: 2.51 vs 2.61) with comparable coverage. Against localized CP, the Interval Stability comparison (Table 3) reveals that PT-VCP has substantially higher instability (0.58–1.26) than localized CP (0.01–1.41) on most datasets, confirming that PT's length gains stem from vacuous randomness rather than task-informed adaptation. The paper's central finding is that all these 'wins' are illusory: the length improvements arise purely from randomly assigning null or near-null intervals to a fraction of test points, a practice that is practically unacceptable in high-stakes deployment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the foundational conformal prediction framework (Vovk et al., 2005) and its localized variant (Guan, 2023), positioning the Prejudicial Trick as a degenerate limiting case of localized CP's normalized score mechanism. It explicitly differentiates itself from Foygel Barber et al. (2021), which uses randomization to explore conditional-coverage tradeoffs, by instead using randomization to expose a failure mode in the standard coverage-length evaluation paradigm. Rather than proposing a new CP method to improve interval length (as in Romano et al., 2019; Izbicki et al., 2020; Stutz et al., 2022), the paper demonstrates that the evaluation metric itself is vulnerable to vacuous improvement, and introduces Interval Stability as a complementary diagnostic orthogonal to existing auxiliary metrics like excess/deficit or group coverage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a deeply entrenched assumption in the CP community—that shorter intervals under valid marginal coverage necessarily indicate a superior method—by showing a trivially simple randomized construction can game this metric while introducing practical instability and unfairness. The proposed Interval Stability metric offers a practical, low-cost diagnostic that could be adopted alongside coverage and length in future CP evaluations, particularly for stochastic pipelines (localized CP, randomized tie-breaking in classification). Open questions include whether Interval Stability is sufficient to detect all PT-like mechanisms in increasingly complex methods, how to formalize fairness guarantees for individual-level prediction sets, and whether the phenomenon extends beyond CP to other distribution-free inference frameworks. The work is primarily defensive and methodological rather than proposing a new state-of-the-art CP algorithm.
