## Problem

Hybrid Structural Methods (HSMs) for Mean Field Games reduce gradient variance by exploiting known individual transition dynamics, but existing HSMs are restricted to tabular, memoryless policies and have not been extended to partially observable settings. Under partial observability, the analytic mean-field update requires tracking a distribution over the exponentially growing Individual-Action-Observation History space, rendering HSMs intractable. The paper asks whether history-dependent policies can be incorporated into HSMs while preserving their variance-reduction benefits and computational tractability.

## Input / Output

The method takes an individual state s_t, a public observation o_t of the aggregate state (a function of the mean-field μ_t and common noise z_t), and a recurrent hidden state h_t encoding the shared observation history o_{0:t}. The output is an action: categorical for discrete action spaces, or a continuous Beta-distribution parameterization evaluated on a fixed action grid with softmax over log-densities for continuous spaces (e.g., the Macroeconomics environment). A key structural detail is that the recurrent hidden state is computed once per timestep from the shared observation and broadcast to all agents, so the per-timestep overhead is a single GRU update independent of the number of individual states.

## Method

RSPG's core insight is that under a Sufficient Public Partial Information assumption, an agent's past private trajectory provides negligible additional information about the aggregate state beyond its current individual state and the public observation history. This allows restricting memory to the shared observation history o_{0:t}, keeping the mean-field as a distribution over the fixed state space S rather than the exponentially growing history space. Architecturally, a GRU processes the public observation sequence to produce a shared hidden state, which is concatenated with an embedded individual state and passed through an MLP to yield the policy. Training uses analytic mean-field updates (exact expectations over next individual states via known transition dynamics) combined with Monte Carlo rollouts of common noise; gradients propagate through individual state transitions and expected rewards but are stopped at mean-field updates. The MFAX framework implements these updates in functional form to avoid materialising |S|² transition matrices, reducing memory to O(|S| + |S||A|) and achieving over 10× speedup over existing MFG libraries.

## Results

The primary metric is exploitability (lower is better) plotted against wall-clock training time across three partially observable MFG environments (Linear Quadratic, Beach Bar, Macroeconomics). RSPG is the only method that consistently achieves the lowest or second-lowest exploitability across all environments, converging approximately an order of magnitude faster than RL-based baselines (IPPO, RIPPO, M-OMD). In the Macroeconomics environment, RSPG and SPG reach similar exploitability levels, but RSPG additionally captures anticipatory behaviour (spending wealth near episode end, pushing interest rates up and wages down) that SPG's memoryless policy cannot. The MFAX framework achieves mean-field updates 10× faster than OpenSpiel and 1000× faster than MFGLib on the Linear Quadratic environment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RSPG outperforms all four baselines on the key metric of exploitability vs. wall-clock time. Against SPG (the memoryless HSM counterpart), RSPG wins decisively in Beach Bar and Linear Quadratic where memory is essential for anticipatory behaviour, but ties in Macroeconomics where the public observation (prices) already provides substantial aggregate-state information. Against IPPO, RSPG achieves roughly an order-of-magnitude faster convergence and lower final exploitability in all three environments, as IPPO's memoryless policy cannot learn the required temporal structure. Against RIPPO (the only other history-aware method), RSPG converges faster because HSMs avoid the overhead of single-agent rollouts between mean-field rollouts, while RIPPO must sequentially recalculate losses for temporal consistency. Against M-OMD, RSPG wins in all environments; notably, M-OMD underperforms even in the near-fully-observable Macroeconomics environment, which the authors attribute to Q-function-based methods struggling to exploit the ordinal structure of the continuous action space.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Hybrid Structural Methods (HSMs) for Mean Field Games, building directly on Structural Policy Gradient (SPG, Yang et al. 2025) and the broader HSM framework (Han et al. 2021). The key departure is adding a GRU-based recurrent component to handle partial observability, which prior HSMs could not support due to their restriction to tabular, memoryless policies. The paper also formalises Partially Observable MFGs with Common Noise (POMFGs-CN) as a new problem setting, noting that no prior work addresses both common noise and partial observability simultaneously, and provides a unified taxonomy of DP, RL, and HSM approaches that was missing from the MFG literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RSPG demonstrates that history-dependent policies in MFGs can be learned with the same variance-reduction benefits as memoryless HSMs, achieving order-of-magnitude faster convergence than model-free RL while capturing anticipatory behaviour (e.g., agents leaving the Beach Bar before potential closure). The MFAX framework is a practical contribution that supports partial observability, common noise, and multiple initial distributions—features absent from existing MFG libraries. Key limitations include the reliance on Assumption 6.1 (sufficient public partial information), the absence of convergence guarantees to Nash equilibrium, and restriction to shared aggregate observations rather than individual-state-dependent observations. Open questions include extending HSMs to major/minor player games, incorporating GAE within actor-critic frameworks, and developing function-approximation-based mean-field updates for higher-dimensional state spaces.
