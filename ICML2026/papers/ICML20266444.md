## Problem

Modern computer vision methods built on large-scale unlabeled data are uniformly labeled 'unsupervised,' yet the data curation, pre-training pipelines, and learning objectives they depend on embed substantial human priors that are rarely made explicit. This ambiguity obscures which components of a pipeline are truly label-free versus human-supervised, complicates fair comparison across methods operating under different assumptions, and coincides with a measurable decline in explicitly unsupervised research since 2021 despite continued field growth.

## Input / Output

As a position paper with an empirical component, the analysis takes as input the full corpus of main-conference paper titles from CVPR, ICCV, and ECCV (2013–2025) and 17,435 CVPR full-text manuscripts (2015–2025), and produces quantitative trend statistics on the frequency of 'unsupervised' and 'self-supervised' terminology alongside a manual annotation of pre-training dependency for 287 CVPR papers (2020–2025). The paper's normative output is a six-item disclosure checklist (Table 1) specifying what authors should report about pre-training dependence, data distribution priors, learning invariances, and evaluation leakage risks.

## Method

The paper combines a community-level empirical study with a normative proposal. Empirically, it tracks title-based and full-text (via Qwen-2.5-32B-Instruct-assisted extraction, validated against manual annotation at 91.5% precision) mentions of unsupervised/self-supervised learning across three flagship venues, and separately measures the growing reliance on a small set of pre-trained backbones (DINO, CLIP, MoCo, diffusion models, SwAV) among papers explicitly titled 'unsupervised.' Normatively, it proposes a lightweight disclosure checklist covering pre-training dependence, frozen vs. adaptive reliance, data scope, distribution priors, enforced invariances, and leakage risks, and argues for distinguishing pre-training-dependent methods from those learning from raw data, evaluating robustness across pre-training regimes, valuing assumption relaxation as a contribution, and supporting same-scale comparison to preserve methodological diversity.

## Results

Title-based analysis of CVPR, ICCV, and ECCV (2013–2025) shows that the fraction of papers explicitly labeled 'unsupervised' peaks at nearly 4% at CVPR in 2021 and drops to close to 1% by 2025, with 'self-supervised' following a similar trajectory. A full-text scan of all 17,435 CVPR papers (2015–2025) confirms the trend: explicit un/self-supervised mentions rise from 10.13% (2015) to 22.17% (2021), then decline to 15.52% (2024) and 12.85% (2025). Among 287 CVPR papers titled 'unsupervised' (2020–2025), reliance on external pre-trained backbones increases steadily, with DINO, CLIP, MoCo, diffusion models, and SwAV accounting for the large majority of named pre-training sources. Automated dependency extraction validated against manual annotations achieves 91.5% precision, 81.8% recall, and 86.9% accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper and does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; no direct method-vs-method comparisons are made. The empirical component is descriptive: it tracks how the field frames unsupervised learning over time rather than comparing algorithmic performance. The key contrast is between papers that explicitly foreground unsupervised learning (declining share) and those that implicitly rely on pre-trained representations while omitting the 'unsupervised' label (growing share). The concentration of pre-training sources in a handful of backbone families (DINO, CLIP, MoCo, diffusion, SwAV) is highlighted as a risk factor: when many 'unsupervised' pipelines inherit the same upstream curation and inductive biases, observed downstream properties may reflect shared priors rather than novel emergent principles. The paper's three hypotheses (comparison pressure, framing shift, user-side indifference) are presented as complementary explanations rather than competing baselines to be empirically adjudicated.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not build on a single technical lineage but synthesizes observations from the dataset-bias literature (Torralba & Efros 2011; Liu & He 2025), the invariance and disentanglement literature (Achille & Soatto 2018; Locatello et al. 2019), and the shortcut-learning literature (Geirhos et al. 2020) to reframe a conceptual question: whether the absence of labels truly implies the absence of human supervision. It departs from the standard framing of unsupervised learning as a clean, label-free paradigm by arguing that data curation, augmentation design, and learning-objective choices embed human priors that are functionally equivalent to supervision. The empirical analysis of conference titles and full-text pre-training dependencies is novel in its scope, drawing on the foundation-model literature (Bommasani 2021) to document a community-level shift rather than proposing a new algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper raises a timely concern that the dominance of a handful of pre-trained backbones (DINO, CLIP, MoCo, diffusion models, SwAV) under a shared 'unsupervised' label obscures which assumptions drive downstream performance, potentially misattributing inherited biases as emergent properties and discouraging methodologically diverse approaches. The proposed disclosure checklist is lightweight and actionable, but its adoption depends on community buy-in and venue-level policy changes that remain uncertain. Open questions include whether quantitative diagnostics (e.g., task-embedding methods) can measure pre-training dependence more rigorously, and whether the field will fragment into incompatible evaluation regimes or find a common vocabulary. The paper's descriptive statistics, while suggestive, do not establish causality for the observed title-decline trend, leaving the three proposed hypotheses (comparison pressure, reframing, user indifference) as competing explanations.
