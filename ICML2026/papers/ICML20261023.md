## Problem

Current LLM coding assistants, whether trained by mimicking expert traces or via RLHF, tend to generate long code blocks that solve the wrong sub-problem, forcing users to spend time fixing incorrect assumptions. The paper asks how to train assistants that produce helpful completions while knowing when to stop at critical decision points, without requiring explicit human feedback, reward models, or clarifying questions that interrupt the user's flow.

## Input / Output

The method takes a code prefix (the current program state) and a pre-trained LLM likelihood estimator that only sees the tokens written so far (not the problem statement) to compute cumulative log-likelihoods of candidate suffixes. It outputs a variable-length code completion whose length is determined by a logit threshold: the longest suffix whose cumulative likelihood exceeds 2^{-η}. Training uses 4,138 offline Codeforces solutions generated by a human model (Gemma-3-27B-it or Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct), with no access to human accept/reject signals or problem test cases during training.

## Method

Empower is a self-supervised fine-tuning algorithm that selects training completions by computing the negative log-likelihood of each suffix under a pre-trained LLM and taking the longest suffix where this value stays below a threshold η (Eq. 2). The key insight is that this logit-threshold selection approximates the human's effective empowerment (the mutual information between the human's next token and the final program state), so training the assistant on low-empowerment (predictable) text frees the human to focus on high-empowerment design decisions. The theoretical analysis shows that in a deterministic environment where states contain full history, the one-sample entropy estimate -log π̂(ℓ_{n+1}^H | ℓ_{1:n}) upper-bounds the human's effective empowerment, making the threshold a principled stopping criterion. The approach requires no human feedback, no reward model, and no online interaction—only offline human-written text—making it a scalable alternative to RLHF and contrastive empowerment objectives that do not transfer well to LLM-scale models.

## Results

On LiveCodeBench (554 problems) with Gemma-3-27B-it as the simulated human, Empower with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct achieves Pass@1 = 0.176, Accept Ratio = 0.670, and Discounted Pass Rate (DPR) = 0.150, roughly doubling the Pass@1 of the strongest baseline (Base-10 at 0.064). In an 18-person double-blinded user study, participants preferred the Empower assistant 78% of the time (p=0.015), accepted its suggestions 31% more often (8.08% vs. 6.18%, p=0.0002), and deleted 26% fewer accepted characters (9.56 vs. 12.91, p=0.012). The method is robust to the threshold η, outperforming all baselines at every η value tested (0.32 to 4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GPT-5-mini (the commercial system evaluated in Table 1), Empower with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct trails on Pass@1 (0.176 vs. 0.249) and DPR (0.150 vs. 0.203), but its Accept Ratio (0.670) is nearly on par with GPT-5-mini's (0.699), suggesting the empowerment-trained assistant produces suggestions humans are equally willing to accept despite lower raw code quality. Against RFT (Rejection Fine-Tuning, Yuan et al. 2023), which uses a round of accepted human suggestions as training signal, Empower substantially outperforms on all three metrics: Pass@1 0.176 vs. 0.057, Accept Ratio 0.670 vs. 0.384, and DPR 0.150 vs. 0.049, demonstrating that the self-supervised logit-threshold signal is more effective than a single round of rejection-based feedback. Empower also dominates all internal baselines (SFT-10, SFT-20, SFT-RAND, Base, Base-10, Base-20) on Pass@1 and DPR across all three assistant model sizes, with the largest gap over SFT baselines (roughly 2.5× Pass@1) and a more modest but consistent gap over Base-10 (roughly 2.7× Pass@1). The user study further confirms these simulated findings: Empower was preferred 78% of the time over Base-20, with significantly higher acceptance and lower post-acceptance deletion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the empowerment framework from Klyubin et al. (2005) and the effective empowerment formulation of Myers et al. (2024) from simple gridworld and video-game environments to LLM-scale language tasks. The key conceptual departure is the insight that a one-sample logit threshold on a pre-trained LLM's cumulative likelihood serves as a tractable proxy for the human's marginal entropy, making empowerment maximization feasible without online interaction or contrastive classifiers. The paper also positions itself against RLHF/DPO methods that require expensive human preference labels, and against self-supervision methods that optimize for LLM self-consistency rather than human empowerment, arguing that empowerment provides a goal-agnostic proxy for helpfulness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a simple, interpretable logit-threshold rule can train assistive LLMs to outperform baselines that use explicit human feedback (RFT), suggesting a path to scalable alignment without the cost and instability of reward modeling. However, all experiments are confined to competitive programming problems with LLM-simulated humans, and the 18-person user study, while statistically significant, is small. Open questions include whether the method generalizes to open-ended coding, writing assistance, or agentic settings, whether LLM miscalibration degrades performance in noisier domains, and whether non-monotonic completion selection (skipping high-entropy tokens to reach low-entropy ones beyond them) could further improve results.
