## Problem

Existing MAS security defenses rely on two fragile assumptions: that attacks manifest as semantically explicit signals detectable via output-level analysis, and that agent interactions follow globally synchronized rounds amenable to graph-based modeling. In practice, modern attacks are semantically stealthy (e.g., MINJA-style progressive injection), real MAS execute asynchronously without temporal alignment, and the standard mitigation of agent isolation collapses multi-agent task completion. The paper asks whether internal reasoning states (neural activations) can provide a more robust, topology-agnostic detection signal that also enables restorative rather than disruptive mitigation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the final-layer hidden state of the last generated token from each agent's LLM backbone (a single d-dimensional vector per agent per execution step), together with a pre-computed normal prototype — the mean of unit-normalized activations collected from benign execution traces. Detection produces a per-agent binary compromised/normal label via a cosine-distance threshold; for flagged agents, the method outputs a steered activation vector (the original activation interpolated toward the prototype) which is injected at the final layer to regenerate a corrected response. In synchronous MAS the prototype is adaptively refined each round with momentum α=0.7; in asynchronous MAS a fixed prototype is maintained since no global round structure exists.

## Method

AcMAS is a training-free, topology-agnostic framework with two components. For detection, it computes a divergence score δ_i = 1 − cos(h_i, μ_normal) for each agent's activation against a normal prototype centroid, flagging agents exceeding a calibrated threshold τ_detect; this operates independently per agent without any interaction graph, making it robust to both semantic camouflage and asynchronous execution. For mitigation, rather than isolating compromised agents, AcMAS performs adaptive activation steering: the compromised activation is interpolated toward the normal prototype with a correction strength λ_i that scales linearly with divergence magnitude (clamped to [0,1]), and the resulting steered vector is injected at the final layer during response regeneration. The key insight is that malicious reasoning induces correlated activation clustering among compromised agents that persists even when surface outputs appear benign, and that steering activations back toward the learned normal manifold restores task-relevant reasoning while preserving the agent's specialized role in the collaborative workflow.

## Results

AcMAS achieves F1 scores of 0.92–0.95 across all five attack scenarios (CSQA, GSM8K, InjecAgent, PoisonRAG, HotPotQA) under both synchronous and asynchronous MAS execution, with AUROC consistently above 98.5 and FPR below 0.07. In synchronous settings, AcMAS outperforms the best graph-based baseline (G-Safeguard) by +0.22 F1 (0.94 vs. 0.72); in asynchronous settings, the gap widens to +0.55 F1 (0.93 vs. 0.38). On defense effectiveness, AcMAS achieves a 0.98 average Task Completion Rate while reducing Attack Success Rate to 0.04, compared to G-Safeguard (TCR 0.83, ASR 0.13) and BlindGuard (TCR 0.78, ASR 0.18).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AcMAS outperforms all four baselines across every dataset and execution mode. Against the two SOTA MAS-specific defenses, G-Safeguard (supervised GNN) achieves the strongest baseline F1 of 0.72 (sync) but drops to 0.46 (async), while BlindGuard (unsupervised contrastive GNN) scores 0.56 (sync) and 0.35 (async); AcMAS leads by +0.22 F1 sync and +0.55 F1 async over G-Safeguard. The two general-purpose graph anomaly detection methods, TAM (affinity-driven) and PERM (contrastive learning), perform substantially worse, with average F1 of only 0.28 and 0.38 in synchronous settings, respectively, and degrade further in asynchronous mode (0.19 and 0.27). The performance gap is most pronounced in asynchronous settings, where graph-based methods suffer catastrophic degradation due to the absence of globally synchronized rounds, while AcMAS's topology-agnostic activation analysis maintains near-identical performance (F1 drop < 0.01 between sync and async). On defense effectiveness, AcMAS's activation-level correction preserves task completion (avg TCR 0.98) far better than isolation-based G-Safeguard (0.83) and BlindGuard (0.78), which remove compromised agents and break multi-agent dependency chains.

## Relation to Prior Work

AcMAS extends the activation-based anomaly detection paradigm from Tan et al. (RevPrag, 2024a) — which used prototype centroids in LLM activation space to detect RAG poisoning — to the multi-agent setting, and pairs it with the activation steering mechanism from Zhang et al. (2025b) for restorative correction. It departs sharply from the dominant MAS security line (G-Safeguard, BlindGuard, NetSafe), which models agent interactions as graphs and detects attacks via topological or semantic signals; AcMAS discards the interaction graph entirely, operating on per-agent internal states instead. This shift is motivated by two practical failures of graph-based methods: they cannot handle semantically stealthy attacks that produce benign-looking outputs, and they break down under asynchronous execution where no global round structure exists.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and growing gap: as MAS deployments move toward asynchronous, event-driven architectures and attacks become semantically stealthy, the graph-based defenses that currently dominate the literature become unreliable. The finding that activation-level correction preserves task completion (TCR 0.98) while isolation-based methods degrade it severely (TCR 0.68 on HotPotQA) has direct practical implications for production MAS where removing an agent breaks dependency chains. Key open questions include robustness to adaptive attacks that gradually shift activation distributions, domain-shift adaptation (performance drops to F1 0.49 without target-domain traces), and the requirement for open-weight model access, which limits applicability to cloud-API-only deployments.
