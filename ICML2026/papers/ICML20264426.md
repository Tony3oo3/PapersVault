## Problem

Modern LLM agents consistently defect in single-shot social dilemmas regardless of reasoning capability, yet no prior work has comparatively evaluated multiple game-theoretic cooperation mechanisms across diverse LLM populations. Existing studies test only a single mechanism in one or two games with purpose-built agents, making it impossible to determine which institutional designs most effectively sustain cooperation among heterogeneous, general-purpose LLM agents.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264426/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each LLM agent receives a natural-language description of the mechanism-modified game, past action histories (truncated at k=3 rounds), co-player reputation records (first-order or higher-order), and—under Mediation/Contract—proposed mediator strategies or side-payment contracts to evaluate and vote on. Rather than outputting a single pure action, agents submit a probability distribution over available actions (sampled externally to circumvent known LLM randomization failures), along with chain-of-thought justifications. Aggregated outputs include mean payoffs across all cross-play match-ups, fitness values after 1000 steps of discrete replicator dynamics, and deviation-rating rankings.

## Method

The paper introduces CoopEval, a factorized benchmark over four cooperation mechanisms (Repetition, Reputation±, Mediation, Contract) × four social dilemmas (Prisoner's Dilemma, Traveler's Dilemma, Trust Game, 3-player Public Goods) × six LLM models, evaluating all cross-play combinations (8,586 decisions per model). Theoretically, Theorem 1 unifies the mechanisms by showing that for any normal-form game with a Pareto-dominated Nash equilibrium, the cooperative payoff is achievable in subgame perfect equilibrium under each mechanism via grim-trigger-style strategies, with the novel proofs for Mediation and Contract accounting for the proposal-and-voting phase. In Mediation and Contract, LLM agents themselves design and propose mediator strategies or side-payment contracts, which are selected by approval voting among participating agents before the game is played. Evaluation employs three complementary metrics—uniform-population mean payoff, fitness under replicator dynamics (simulating evolutionary selection pressure), and deviation ratings (a clone-invariant general-sum ranking)—plus an LLM-as-a-judge analysis of 15 justification categories in chain-of-thought reasoning.

## Results

In the unmodified social dilemmas (NoMechanism), all six LLM models consistently defect, yielding a mean normalized payoff of only 0.072 (where 0 = all defect, 1 = all cooperate). Among the four cooperation mechanisms, Contracting is most effective (mean 0.801, recovering 80% of the social optimum), followed by Mediation (0.695), Repetition (0.587), Reputation− (0.321), and Reputation+ (0.227). Under evolutionary optimization pressures (replicator dynamics), cooperation frequency surges to 90–100% for Repetition (fitness 0.992), Mediation (1.000), and Contracting (0.999), demonstrating robustness of these mechanisms against strong LLM models. Gemini-R and Gemini-B achieve the best overall performance across mechanisms, while GPT-4o performs worst by a significant margin (e.g., mean 0.243 under Mediation vs. 0.868 for Gemini-R).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the NoMechanism setting (unmodified single-shot social dilemmas), where all LLMs defect and the mean normalized payoff is only 0.072. Contracting dramatically outperforms this baseline, recovering 80% of the social optimum (mean 0.801), followed by Mediation (0.695) and Repetition (0.587). Reputation mechanisms are substantially less effective: Reputation− achieves only 0.321 and Reputation+ (with higher-order history) actually underperforms Reputation− at 0.227, suggesting that additional historical information harms LLM cooperation. Under evolutionary pressures (replicator dynamics), the gap between mechanisms narrows considerably—Repetition, Mediation, and Contracting all reach 90–100% cooperation frequency—while NoMechanism fitness drops further (0.021), confirming that optimization pressures amplify defection without mechanisms. Among LLMs, Gemini-R and Gemini-B lead across nearly all mechanisms (e.g., Contracting mean 1.055 and 1.138 respectively), while GPT-4o trails significantly (Contracting mean 0.450, Mediation mean 0.243), often due to exploratory randomization that undermines cooperative equilibria.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the long lineage of cooperation mechanism research in game theory (Axelrod's repeated games, Nowak & Sigmund's indirect reciprocity, Monderer & Tennenholtz's mediation, Coase's side payments) and the recent wave of LLM-agent evaluations in social dilemmas (Concordia, Fontana et al., Piatti et al.). However, prior work has studied at most one mechanism per paper, typically with rule-based or RL agents purpose-built for a specific game, or with LLMs in self-play under a single mechanism. The key departure is leveraging LLMs' generality to parse arbitrary natural-language game descriptions, enabling the first head-to-head comparison of four mechanism families across four diverse social dilemmas with cross-play between heterogeneous models. Theorem 1 unifies folk-theorem-style results across all four mechanisms under a single grim-trigger construction, providing a common theoretical baseline that makes the empirical gaps between mechanisms all the more striking.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For multiagent system designers, the finding that contracting and mediation recover 80%+ of the social optimum while reputation recovers only ~23% provides concrete, actionable guidance on which institutional structures to deploy when coordinating LLM agents. The result that evolutionary optimization pressures (replicator dynamics) boost cooperation to 90–100% under these mechanisms is reassuring for robustness against increasingly capable and self-optimizing models. Open questions include whether the findings hold for finetuned or scaffolded agents, whether they extend to sequential or larger social dilemmas, and how to handle the risk that 'cooperation' among a subset of agents constitutes collusion that harms external welfare. The benchmark's limited sample size (3 repetitions per combination) and six-model scope leave room for broader validation.
