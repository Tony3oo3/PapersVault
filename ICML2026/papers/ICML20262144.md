## Problem

Sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) is widely adopted for improving generalization, yet its convergence behavior near saddle points remains poorly understood. Prior work (Kaddour et al., 2022) briefly observed atypical SAM behavior near saddle points, but no complete theoretical account existed for why SAM can become permanently trapped at a saddle point rather than escaping to a local minimum. This paper asks: under what conditions does a saddle point become an attractor under SAM dynamics, and how do stochastic effects (mini-batch noise, momentum) influence escape?

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a differentiable loss function ℓ(w) and SAM hyperparameters (perturbation radius ρ, learning rate η, batch size B, momentum γ) as input, and produces closed-form characterizations of convergence behavior: a necessary-and-sufficient condition for saddle-point attraction (Theorem 1), the time-dependent variance of the weight distribution near the saddle (Theorem 2), and the mean squared displacement as a function of momentum and batch size (Theorem 3). Empirically, the framework is validated on the Beale function, a double-well potential, a two-layer toy network, and ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10/100, where outputs are training loss curves, test accuracy, and convergence-point distributions over 1,000 random seeds.

## Method

The paper applies qualitative theory of dynamical systems to the SAM gradient flow, using the Lambda Lemma and stable/unstable manifold decomposition to show that SAM's perturbation step w^p = w + ρ∇ℓ(w) can push the iterate across a basin boundary, causing the gradient to oscillate between two adjacent minima (Case-III). Theorem 1 proves that an index-one saddle point d becomes an attractor when ρ > −1/λ₁, because the Jacobian of SAM dynamics at d becomes −Q(Λ + ρΛ²)Qᵀ with all positive diagonal entries. For the stochastic setting, the paper derives the Fokker-Planck equation for SAM using a Fisher Information Matrix approximation of the gradient noise covariance, yielding a closed-form Gaussian variance σ²(t) that is strictly smaller than SGD's (Corollary 1), proving SAM escapes saddle points more slowly. Theorem 3 extends this to include momentum γ and batch size B, showing that the mean squared displacement increases with γ and decreases with B, and that maintaining SGD-equivalent diffusion under SAM requires a larger momentum as ρ grows.

## Results

Theoretically, the paper proves that an index-one saddle point becomes an attractor under SAM dynamics when ρ > −1/λ₁ (Theorem 1), and that SAM's mean squared displacement near a saddle point is strictly smaller than SGD's (Corollary 1), meaning SAM escapes saddle points more slowly. Empirically, on the Beale function and double-well potential, SAM gets trapped at the saddle point while GD/SGD converge to global minima. On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, the best SAM configuration (ρ=0.1, γ=0.95) achieves 95.08% test accuracy, while the worst (ρ=0.5, γ=0.0) drops to 86.20%; with batch size B=512 and no momentum, SAM's training loss exceeds 1 and test accuracy falls below 60%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between SAM and vanilla SGD in a toy two-layer neural network (Ziyin et al., 2022 setup) initialized near a saddle point: over 1,000 random seeds, SGD converges to the global minima (ℓ=2.25) while SAM (ρ=0.1) saturates in the saddle-point region (ℓ=2.50), confirming the theoretical prediction that SAM's diffusion is suppressed near saddle points. On CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 with ResNet-18 (no batch norm, no augmentation), increasing batch size from 64 to 512 causes SAM's training loss to exceed 1 and test accuracy to drop below 60%, whereas SGD remains stable; this directly validates Theorem 3's prediction that smaller B increases diffusion and aids saddle escape. Momentum γ=0.9 improves SAM's CIFAR-10 accuracy by over 20 percentage points (from ~75% to ~95%), compared to only ~5 points for SGD, showing that momentum is disproportionately critical for SAM. The paper does not propose a new optimizer; rather, it identifies momentum and small batch size as the key training tricks that prevent SAM's saddle-point trapping from manifesting in practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the original SAM formulation (Foret et al., 2020) and the initial empirical observation of atypical SAM behavior near saddle points by Kaddour et al. (2022), providing the first rigorous dynamical-systems explanation for why SAM gets trapped. It departs from Compagnoni et al. (2023), who analyzed SAM near saddle points under Lipschitz-continuity and small-learning-rate assumptions, by adopting a local qualitative-theory perspective (Lambda Lemma, stable/unstable manifolds) that captures the perturbation as an effective random forcing term. The stochastic analysis extends the Fokker-Planck/FIM diffusion framework of Xie et al. (2020, 2022) from vanilla SGD to SAM, and the momentum/batch-size results connect to the empirical observations of Andriushchenko & Flammarion (2022) on SAM's sensitivity to batch size.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete failure mode of SAM—saddle-point trapping—that explains when and why the method underperforms, and shows that commonly applied training tricks (momentum, smaller batch size) are not merely heuristics but are theoretically necessary to counteract SAM's reduced diffusion near saddle points. The practical implication is that SAM's well-known generalization gains are partially contingent on these hyperparameters being set appropriately, which has direct consequences for practitioners tuning SAM. Open questions include whether the analysis extends to higher-index saddle points in over-parameterized networks, how the findings interact with the edge-of-stability regime (Long & Bartlett, 2024), and whether adaptive strategies for ρ, γ, or B can be designed to balance saddle exploration against escape dynamics.
