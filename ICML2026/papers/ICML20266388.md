## Problem

Democratic deliberation faces a fundamental scalability tradeoff: meaningful deliberation for the few (deliberative polling, citizens' assemblies) or superficial participation for the many (open comment periods, social media). While generative AI could reduce the frictions that limit deliberative quality, the field lacks a principled framework specifying how AI should be integrated into deliberative processes without substituting machine judgment for human choice or introducing new forms of exclusion.

## Input / Output

The framework operates across a multi-stage deliberation workflow (input elicitation → reflection → structured exchange → synthesis) rather than as a single model with fixed I/O. Inputs are heterogeneous citizen contributions spanning multiple languages, literacy levels, and technical expertise, alongside structured policy questions and existing opinion distributions. Outputs are deliberately non-conclusive: rephrasing suggestions, Socratic prompts, moderation flags, and consensus visualizations are designed as scaffolding that participants can accept, modify, or ignore, with the explicit constraint that no output constitutes a judgment or conclusion rendered on a participant's behalf.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a theoretical architecture that maps four categories of behavioral frictions (cognitive, social, platform-design, market-incentive) to specific LLM capabilities deployed at defined stages of a deliberation workflow, all governed by four cross-cutting design principles (agency, respect, equality, augmentation). The key mechanism is that AI reduces friction in accessing and processing information (summarization, translation, opinion clustering via bridging-based ranking, Socratic self-reflection prompts) while deliberately preserving friction in deliberative processing itself—preference transformation, mutual justification, and reasoned accommodation remain human tasks. The principles operate as constraints on every capability regardless of which friction it primarily addresses, rather than mapping one-to-one to friction categories. The paper grounds this framework in empirical evidence from platforms like Pol.is, deliberation.io, and advocacy+, and contrasts its augmentation-based approach with alternatives such as liquid democracy and engagement-optimized social media design.

## Results

This is a position paper without original quantitative experiments or a main results table. The authors synthesize evidence from prior studies to support their four guiding principles: Argyle et al. (2023) show LLM-suggested rephrasings improved democratic reciprocity in partisan discussions without shifting policy attitudes; Braley et al. (2025b) find that visualizing accurate opinion distributions significantly increased cross-partisan consensus and willingness for collective action; Enríquez (2025) reports that AI-Socratic dialogue reduced extreme positioning through enhanced perspective-taking while preserving perceived agency; and Konya et al. (2023) demonstrate that LLM-powered bridging-based ranking identified consensus from large-scale collective dialogues that achieved strong cross-partisan support.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark a proposed method against baselines; instead, it positions its deliberation-focused AI framework against several alternative approaches. Against liquid democracy (Blum and Zuber, 2016), the authors argue that experimental evidence (Mooers et al., 2024) shows delegation underperforms both universal majority voting and simple abstention, and that liquid democracy addresses who decides but not how they deliberate. Against in-person deliberation, the paper concedes it remains the gold standard for empathy and trust-building but argues it cannot scale to democratic populations, citing Siu et al. (2025) for comparable online outcomes. Against non-AI online deliberation platforms (e.g., early Pol.is without bridging-based ranking), the authors identify three complementary AI contributions—accessibility via translation, reflection via Socratic prompts, and synthesis via bridging-based ranking—that extend rather than replace structural safeguards. Against AI skeptics (Allen and Weyl, 2024), the paper counters that the real choice is not AI versus no AI but deliberation-focused AI versus engagement-maximizing AI, and that the augmentation principle directly addresses identity-validation concerns.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes deliberative democratic theory (Habermas, Landemore, Mansbridge, Fishkin) with recent empirical work on LLM-assisted civic discourse (Argyle et al. 2023, Konya et al. 2023, Karanam et al. 2024) and existing deliberation platforms (Pol.is, deliberation.io, Decidim) to propose a four-principle design framework for pro-democratic AI. It explicitly departs from liquid democracy (Blum & Zuber 2016) by arguing AI should augment deliberative capacity rather than restructure representation, and from engagement-optimized social media by identifying specific behavioral frictions (cognitive, social, platform-design, market-incentive) that AI can address. The paper carves new ground by framing LLM integration into deliberation as a distinct design problem with its own evaluation criteria (deliberative quality rather than engagement), rather than treating it as an application of existing NLP tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete normative framework and call to action for the ML community to develop deliberation-focused AI evaluated on deliberative quality metrics rather than engagement, which is a significant reframing for a field currently dominated by user-satisfaction benchmarks. Key open questions include whether sycophancy can be mitigated without sacrificing perceived helpfulness, whether LLM training biases (WEIRD-centric, English-dominant) can be sufficiently corrected for cross-cultural deliberation, and whether the identified frictions are actually addressable at the scale of tens of thousands of simultaneous participants. The paper is explicitly normative and does not provide new empirical results, so its impact depends on whether the community adopts its evaluation criteria and design principles.
