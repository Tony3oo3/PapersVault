## Problem

Current AI systems lack the ability to monitor their own computational states and dynamically allocate resources based on problem difficulty, relying instead on rudimentary fixed-compute strategies. The paper argues that metacognition—self-monitoring combined with resource allocation inspired by cognitive science—should be a general design principle for AI, but notes the absence of a unified theoretical framework and practical implementation strategies to make this concrete.

## Input / Output

The metacognitive policy π takes as input the set of local client model updates {θ_t^(k)} and a self-monitoring state s that aggregates per-client trust scores, gradient-distance statistics, and exclusion history from prior rounds. It outputs a discrete aggregation mode c_t selected per round (e.g., FedAvg, MultiKrum, PID-based exclusion) along with client-level exclusion decisions and reweighting factors, which together determine the next global model θ_{t+1}. The mode selection is the key unusual output: rather than committing to a single aggregation rule, the system re-evaluates which strategy to apply at every round based on the evolving self-monitoring state.

## Method

The paper formalizes metacognitive control via a resource-rational objective (Eq. 1) that jointly minimizes task loss, the cost of the selected computation mode c, and the overhead of the controller itself, with the metacognitive policy π_{w'}(c|x,s) selecting modes per instance. In the Federated Learning instantiation, a monitoring function M evaluates each client update's distance from global consensus and historical performance to produce trust scores, while a control mechanism dynamically switches among aggregation strategies (FedAvg, MultiKrum, PID-based exclusion, trusted-subset projection) round by round. The InteFL framework implements this as a two-layered architecture: an AI-assisted design layer for natural-language configuration and post-experiment optimization, and an FL metacognitive layer that treats planning (updates), monitoring (quality), and evaluating (aggregation) as explicit metacognitive functions. The case study trains a CNN on OctMNIST retinal images across 20 clients (4 adversarial with 100% label flipping) over 20 rounds, benchmarking six aggregation algorithms and tuning the PID-based method's exclusion threshold to 2.5 standard deviations for optimal convergence and malicious-client detection.

## Results

On the OctMNIST retinal imaging dataset (20 clients, 4 with 100% label-flipping attacks, 20 aggregation rounds), PID-based aggregation with a 2.5-standard-deviation exclusion threshold achieved the fastest convergence and the most accurate malicious client exclusion among six benchmarked aggregation algorithms. Trimmed Mean and Bulyan failed to converge to comparable loss values, exhibiting lower exclusion accuracy and precision by failing to remove all anomalous clients at certain rounds. The paper notes that in benign, stationary settings the metacognitive controller degenerates to the cheapest mode (FedAvg), confirming the resource-rational property. Detailed numerical results are deferred to the companion publication (Korobeinikov et al., 2026).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks five alternative aggregation strategies against the proposed PID-based metacognitive controller on OctMNIST under label-poisoning attacks. PID-based aggregation (Zatsarenko et al., 2026b) wins on both convergence speed and exclusion accuracy, outperforming MultiKrum (Blanchard et al., 2017) and other distance-based methods. Trimmed Mean (Yin et al., 2018) and Bulyan (Mhamdi et al., 2018) are the weakest baselines: both fail to exclude all four malicious clients at certain rounds, resulting in higher aggregated-model loss and slower convergence. FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017) serves as the non-metacognitive baseline; the paper explicitly notes that in benign settings the optimal metacognitive policy collapses to FedAvg, so the controller's advantage is conditional on adversarial or heterogeneous conditions. The PID controller's advantage is most pronounced under the 4/20 malicious-client attack scenario, where its adaptive exclusion threshold (2.5σ) provides the best trade-off between excluding bad clients and retaining useful updates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three research threads—resource-rational AI (Russell & Wefald, Lieder et al.), cognitive-psychology metacognition (Nelson & Narens' object/meta-level duality, dual-process theory, metacognitive cues like FoR/FoK), and robust federated learning (MultiKrum, Bulyan, Trimmed Mean)—into a unified design principle. It departs from prior metacognition-in-AI calls (Shakarian 2026, Johnson et al. 2026, Wei et al. 2024) by providing a formal resource-rational objective (Eq. 1) that jointly optimizes task loss, mode cost, and controller overhead, and by explicitly distinguishing its proposed metacognitive controller from existing techniques like early-exit networks, MoE routing, and cost-aware AutoML on three axes: explicit supervisory separation, joint learnability, and runtime adaptivity. The InteFL framework operationalizes this for FL, extending the robust-aggregation literature with a meta-level policy that selects among aggregation strategies per round based on self-monitoring state.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a genuine gap: most AI research optimizes object-level performance while treating resource allocation as a separate, offline concern, and the metacognitive AI literature has focused heavily on monitoring (error detection) while under-exploring downstream control. The three research tasks (characterizing performance-resource tradeoffs, developing efficient cues, calibrating controllers) are well-motivated and would benefit the broader ML community if addressed. However, the empirical evidence is narrow—single medical-imaging dataset, 20 clients, 4 label-flipping adversaries, one CNN architecture—so the general claims about metacognition as a universal design principle remain largely aspirational. Open questions include whether the resource-rational controller's advantage persists at scale, how to characterize compounding meta-level errors that destabilize learning, and whether the framework transfers beyond FL to inference-time settings where overhead cannot be amortized.
