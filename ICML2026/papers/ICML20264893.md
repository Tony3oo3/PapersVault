## Problem

Prior work has identified emotion-related signals in LLM activations but has not revealed the internal circuit mechanisms that actually drive emotional expression. Existing emotion-control methods (steering vectors, prompt engineering) manipulate the residual stream or inject stylistic cues without explaining which specific neurons and attention heads are causally responsible, leaving interventions black-box and unreliable. This paper asks whether LLMs contain context-agnostic emotion circuits, what form they take, and whether they can be directly modulated for universal emotion control.

## Input / Output

The method takes neutral first-person scenario-event pairs (15–25 words, no explicit emotion vocabulary) from the SEV dataset or an independent held-out test set of equal size, and produces two outputs: (1) a short emotionally-expressed response (≤2 sentences) generated under greedy decoding with no emotion instruction in the prompt, and (2) a sparse, layer-distributed emotion circuit specifying which MLP neurons and attention heads at which sublayers to modulate. All interventions target only the last-token position of the residual stream, and the circuit budget is set to 10× the number of sublayers (560 units for LLaMA-3.2-3B-Instruct's 28 layers).

## Method

The framework proceeds in three stages. First, context-agnostic emotion directions are extracted by eliciting six Ekman emotions via prompting on the SEV dataset, collecting last-token residual-stream activations at every sublayer, subtracting the within-group mean across emotions to cancel shared semantics, and l2-normalizing the per-emotion centroids into unit vectors. Second, local components are identified: for MLP sublayers, neuron contributions are analytically decomposed via β_e = (W_d)^T v_e and ranked by mean gated-activation contribution; for attention sublayers, heads are ranked by causal ablation (zeroing each head and measuring the drop in emotion projection score). Both are validated by ablation (sharp score drop at k=2–4 neurons/heads, long-tail saturation) and enhancement (injecting within-group emotion difference vectors into top-ranked units elicits emotion without any prompt). Third, global circuits are assembled by measuring each sublayer's causal influence on the final emotion representation (injecting a scaled offset along the sublayer's local direction and measuring the shift along a stable reference basis from layers 21–25), then allocating a fixed circuit budget across sublayers via a two-stage scheme (minimum quota for shallow layers + proportional allocation by importance). Directly modulating the assembled circuits achieves 99.65% emotion-expression accuracy on the held-out test set, outperforming both prompting (98.96%) and residual-stream steering (91.22%), with a 70.7% blind human win rate over prompting-based outputs.

## Results

Circuit-based modulation achieves 99.65% overall emotion-expression accuracy on the held-out test set, surpassing both prompting-based generation (98.96%) and steering-based generation (91.22%). Per-emotion, circuit modulation reaches 100% for sadness, fear, and surprise across all valence contexts, with anger (97.50–98.75%) being the lowest-performing emotion. In a blind human evaluation of 300 stratified pairs, circuit-generated text is preferred over prompting-based text at a 70.7% win rate (p < 0.01, binomial test), indicating more natural affective tone without explicit instructions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares circuit modulation against two baseline families: (1) activation steering (Turner et al., 2024; Konen et al., 2024), which injects a single scaled emotion direction vector into the residual stream at selected layers, and (2) prompt-based elicitation, which appends an explicit emotion instruction to the system prompt. Circuit modulation (99.65%) substantially outperforms steering (91.22%), with the largest gap on surprise (100% vs. 67.71%) and anger (≈98% vs. 93.33%), indicating that a single global vector is insufficient to capture the distributed, layer-specific nature of emotion circuits. Against prompting (98.96%), circuit modulation achieves a modest accuracy gain (≈0.7 pp) but with a critical qualitative advantage: it requires no explicit emotion instruction in the prompt, meaning the emotion is induced purely through internal circuit modulation rather than surface-level instruction following. On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, the gap widens dramatically for negative emotions, where steering drops below 5% (anger: 0.60%, fear: 0.00%) due to safety alignment, while circuit modulation with scale 2.0 still achieves 39.38% for anger and 52.50% for fear in negative-valence contexts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the linear-representation findings of Tigges et al. (2024) and the steering-vector methods of Konen et al. (2024) and Turner et al. (2024) by moving from global residual-stream manipulation to a circuit-level decomposition: it first extracts context-agnostic emotion directions (building directly on the linear-direction assumption), then identifies the specific MLP neurons and attention heads that implement those directions via analytical decomposition and causal ablation, and finally integrates them into layer-distributed circuits weighted by measured sublayer importance. Unlike prior 'emotion neuron' work (Lee et al., 2025) or appraisal-theory decoding (Tak et al., 2025), which probe recognition or produce inconsistent layer-wise effects, this paper targets the generation side and validates both necessity (ablation) and sufficiency (enhancement) of the identified components. The SEV dataset is a new controlled benchmark that addresses the confound of semantic content in prior emotion-elicitation studies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic, circuit-level account of how LLMs produce emotional expression, shifting the field from black-box steering to interpretable, component-level control. The near-perfect 99.65% accuracy without any explicit prompting, combined with the striking sparsity (2–4 neurons per layer dominate), suggests that emotional expression in LLMs is a compact, traceable computation rather than a diffuse statistical artifact. Key open questions remain: whether the circuits generalize to multilingual settings, richer affective continua beyond Ekman's six categories, and naturally occurring (non-synthetic) emotional language; whether they survive fine-tuning or RLHF; and whether the safety-alignment resistance observed in Qwen2.5-7B (near-zero steering success for negative emotions) reflects a fundamental architectural property or a tunable artifact.
