## Problem

Activation verbalization methods (Patchscopes, LIT, SelfIE) are designed to translate a target LLM's internal activations into natural language descriptions, ostensibly revealing privileged knowledge about the model's computations. However, it remains unclear whether these verbalizations actually reflect the target model's internal states or merely the verbalizer LLM's own parametric knowledge and the input text. Prior evaluation benchmarks have not controlled for this distinction, leaving open the question of whether verbalization outputs are faithful interpretations of the target model.

## Input / Output

The verbalization pipeline consumes hidden-state activations from a target model M1 (a single token activation h_i^l for Patchscopes, or concatenated multi-token activations h^l from one layer for LIT) along with an interpretation prompt (a question or cloze-style completion), and produces a natural language description via a verbalizer LLM M2. In the inversion experiments, activations are mapped back to reconstructed input text x_rec, which is then fed as a plain text input to M2 without any activation access. Correctness is evaluated by checking whether the target answer appears anywhere in the first 20 generated tokens.

## Method

This empirical study makes three key contributions to assess verbalization faithfulness. First, it demonstrates that zero-shot baselines (prompting M2 with only x_input + x_prompt, no activations) match or exceed Patchscopes and LIT on standard feature extraction benchmarks, showing these benchmarks do not require privileged information. Second, it trains inversion models (Llama3, T5-Base) to reconstruct x_input from M1's activations and shows that answering questions from the reconstructed text achieves performance comparable to full verbalization, indicating activations encode input text with sufficient fidelity to explain verbalization 'success.' Third, it introduces PersonaQA (with Shuffled and Fantasy variants), a dataset of fabricated personas finetuned into M1, to test whether M2 can decode knowledge it does not itself possess; results show that verbalizers fail to access privileged knowledge when M1 and M2 have misaligned parametric knowledge, and that M2 preferentially answers from its own world knowledge rather than from M1's activations.

## Results

On the six feature extraction tasks (averaged over source layers ℓ=1–15), zero-shot Llama3 achieves 0.64 average accuracy, matching LIT (0.64) and substantially outperforming Patchscopes (0.38). Zero-shot Ministral achieves 0.68 average, beating both LIT (0.61) and Patchscopes (0.27). On the PersonaQA-Fantasy dataset (where the target model was fine-tuned on fabricated personas unknown to the verbalizer), all verbalization methods and zero-shot baselines achieve 0.00 accuracy, demonstrating that verbalizers cannot decode knowledge they do not possess. Inversion-then-interpretation (reconstructing input text from activations, then answering zero-shot) matches LIT on most tasks (e.g., 0.82 vs 0.79 on country_curr) and outperforms single-layer Patchscopes on all six tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two verbalization methods (LIT and Patchscopes) against a zero-shot text-only baseline and an inversion-then-interpretation baseline. LIT ties the zero-shot baseline on Llama3 (0.64 vs. 0.64 average) but loses on Ministral (0.61 vs. 0.68), while Patchscopes is consistently outperformed by zero-shot on both models (0.38 vs. 0.64 for Llama3; 0.27 vs. 0.68 for Ministral). Inversion-then-interpretation matches LIT on most feature extraction tasks (e.g., 0.82 vs. 0.79 on country_curr) and outperforms single-layer Patchscopes on all six tasks, suggesting that the input text encoded in activations is sufficient to explain verbalization performance. On PersonaQA-Fantasy, where the target model knows fabricated personas unknown to the verbalizer, both LIT and Patchscopes score 0.00, confirming that verbalizers cannot access knowledge absent from their own parameters. When the verbalizer is fine-tuned on the same PersonaQA-Fantasy data as the target (Table 6), LIT and Patchscopes achieve non-trivial accuracy (e.g., 0.47 on fav_music_gen for Patchscopes), but a simple logistic probe achieves comparable scores (0.20–0.38), further undermining the claim that verbalization uniquely decodes privileged information.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper critically evaluates existing activation verbalization methods (Patchscopes, LIT, SelfIE) by asking whether they actually convey privileged information about a target model's internals or merely reflect the verbalizer's own parametric knowledge and the input text. It draws on the faithfulness literature (Jacovi & Goldberg, 2020; Turpin et al., 2023) and the introspection literature (Kadavath et al., 2022; Song et al., 2025), but is the first to systematically apply faithfulness testing to activation verbalization specifically. The inversion experiments build on Morris et al. (2023)'s finding that text embeddings reveal nearly as much as text, extending this to the verbalization setting. The paper carves new ground by introducing PersonaQA as a controlled evaluation dataset designed to isolate privileged knowledge from the verbalizer's own world knowledge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings challenge a core assumption in the growing verbalization literature: that decoding activations into natural language provides privileged insights into model computation. If verbalizers are primarily drawing on their own parametric knowledge or reconstructing input text, then current benchmarks (feature extraction) are measuring the wrong thing, and the interpretability value of verbalization is substantially overstated. Key open questions include whether faithfulness improves with model scale, how to design evaluations that truly isolate target-model-specific knowledge, and whether verbalization can be made to extract knowledge the verbalizer does not possess. The paper's limitation to 8B models and QA-style tasks means the conclusions may not generalize to larger models or more complex interpretability tasks.
