## Problem

Existing computational protein design methods face a fundamental disconnect: physics-based tools like Rosetta demand extensive expert configuration, deep learning methods rely on restricted functional ontologies or struggle with the text-to-sequence semantic gap, and LLM-based agents lack closed-loop iterative optimization. No prior framework enables non-expert users to specify complex multi-constraint protein properties in natural language and receive a structurally validated, constraint-satisfying sequence through automated, self-refining mutation.

## Input / Output

The system accepts a free-form natural language design request specifying 2–5 constraints across seven biophysical dimensions (thermostability target Tm, solubility via GRAVY, pH stability via pI matching, sequence length range, secondary structure preference, disulfide bond count, and salt bridge count). It retrieves 1–3 template sequences from UniProt/Swiss-Prot using LLM-extracted keywords, then outputs an optimized amino acid sequence (typically 92.81% identity to the template) along with a per-constraint satisfaction report, ESMFold pLDDT score, and the full mutation history. The constraint taxonomy is fixed to these seven types; complex functional properties like binding affinity or catalytic activity are not yet supported.

## Method

PDAgent implements a five-phase ReAct loop (THINK → PLAN → ACT → OBSERVE → REFLECT) where an LLM backbone (LLaMA-3.1-8B, Qwen-3-30B, or DeepSeek-V3.2) drives iterative directed mutation over UniProt-retrieved templates. Before the loop, conservation analysis via local BLAST + MAFFT multiple sequence alignment + Shannon entropy scoring identifies protected positions (threshold θ_cons = 0.6), restricting mutations to variable regions. During each iteration, the LLM diagnoses unsatisfied constraints (THINK), selects from seven domain-specific mutation operators (thermostability, solubility, pI adjustment, disulfide introduction, salt bridge placement, helix stabilization, core packing) (PLAN), and the system executes mutations gated by DDGun ΔΔG < 2.0 kcal/mol stability filtering (ACT). ESMFold re-evaluates foldability and all constraints (OBSERVE), and the LLM reflects on progress to decide continuation or termination (REFLECT), with accumulated history memory preventing repeated ineffective strategies. The framework runs up to 10 iterations (avg. 3.1) with up to 4 actions and 5 mutations per tool per iteration, selecting the best sequence across multiple candidate templates.

## Results

On the 100-task PDAgent Benchmark, PDAgent with the DeepSeek-V3.2 backbone achieves a 91.86% average constraint satisfaction rate (CSR) and 87.69 mean pLDDT foldability, with a 100% success rate across all tasks. The LLaMA-3.1-8B backbone reaches 90.60% CSR at the same 87.69 foldability, while Qwen-3-30B achieves 85.09% CSR with 80.54 foldability. These results substantially outperform the strongest direct LLM baseline (Gemini-3-Pro: 82.55% CSR, 50.03 foldability) and the best specialized protein design method (Pinal: 53.34% CSR, 80.92 foldability). Ablation studies confirm that the iterative ReAct loop contributes the largest single gain (↓4.32 average when removed), followed by template diversity (↓3.69) and THINK/PLAN separation (↓1.97).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PDAgent (DeepSeek-V3.2) outperforms all 13 baselines on average CSR, achieving 91.86% versus the best direct LLM baseline Gemini-3-Pro (82.55%, +9.3 pts) and the best specialized design method Pinal (53.34%, +38.5 pts). The gap is most dramatic against specialized protein design methods: ProtDAT (41.91%), ProteinDT (42.26%), BioMedGPT-10B (28.69%), and Biomni (55.46%) all fall 30+ points below PDAgent on CSR, reflecting their inability to satisfy complex multi-constraint specifications. PDAgent also achieves 100% success rate across all three backbone configurations, whereas o4-mini succeeds on only 62% of tasks, BioMedGPT-10B on 85%, and Biomni on merely 50%. A notable trade-off emerges: Pinal achieves the second-highest foldability (80.92) among non-PDAgent methods but at the cost of very low CSR (53.34%), while Gemini-3-Pro reaches 82.55% CSR but with poor structural quality (50.03 pLDDT); PDAgent uniquely achieves both high foldability (87.69) and high CSR simultaneously. On the thermostability dimension specifically, PDAgent (46.15–46.25%) still lags behind Gemini-3-Pro (60.53%), reflecting the inherent difficulty of reaching Tm ≥ 70–90°C via point mutations alone, but compensates with near-perfect scores on solubility, length, disulfide, and salt bridge constraints.

## Relation to Prior Work

PDAgent repurposes the ReAct reasoning-and-acting paradigm (Yao et al., 2022) from general-purpose LLM agents into a domain-specific protein design controller, pairing it with template retrieval from UniProt and conservation-aware directed mutation rather than de novo sequence generation. It departs from text-conditioned protein design methods (ProtDAT, Pinal, ProteinDT) that perform single-pass generation by introducing a closed-loop iterative optimization cycle where the LLM diagnoses constraint violations, selects from a toolkit of seven mutation operators, and reflects on progress across iterations. The conservation analysis component (BLAST + MSA + Shannon entropy) borrows from structural biology tooling (ConSurf) to protect functionally critical residues, a safeguard absent from prior LLM-agent protein design systems like ProtAgents and Biomni.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PDAgent lowers the barrier to multi-constraint protein design by accepting natural language specifications and automating the full design-validation-refinement loop, making it accessible to non-expert researchers. However, the framework remains entirely in silico with no wet-lab validation, its constraint taxonomy excludes complex functional properties (binding affinity, catalytic activity), and the 100-task benchmark is self-curated via LLM generation, limiting external generalizability claims. Open questions include whether the template-based approach scales to truly novel protein families without close UniProt homologs, how to integrate experimental feedback for closed-loop laboratory optimization, and whether the observed performance gap over de novo methods persists on harder, less template-friendly design targets.
