## Problem

Standard causal inference methods assume a fixed set of variables and a fixed causal graph, while relational machine learning handles varying objects and relations but lacks causal semantics for interventions and counterfactuals. This paper tackles the question of when and how causal queries about *unseen* combinations of objects (different object counts and relations) can be identified from data on *seen* combinations, showing that even observational distributions for unseen combinations are not identifiable without additional structural assumptions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261014/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a relational schema (entity types, relation types, attributes), a relational causal graph encoding causal assumptions, observational or interventional data from one or more source skeletons (each a specific finite combination of objects and relations), and a target skeleton with a causal query (interventional or counterfactual). It produces either a point estimate of the causal effect on the target skeleton or a FAIL verdict indicating non-identifiability. Unusually, the source and target skeletons may differ in object counts and relational structure, and the same neural network is shared across all instances of a given type, so the model must generalize across varying neighborhood sizes.

## Method

The paper introduces Relational Structural Causal Models (RSCMs), which generalize SCMs by letting each mechanism depend on relational constraints and aggregate over variable-sized sets of related objects via permutation-invariant operators (e.g., OR, AND, majority). To overcome impossibility results, the authors define relational causal graphs that encode assumptions enabling identification across skeletons, and derive symbolic criteria (Thm. 4.3, Props. 4.4–4.5) for when observational and interventional queries are transferable. The practical algorithm, RelationalNeuralID, parameterizes each mechanism with a feedforward neural network shared across all instances of the same type, constrained by the graph to use only permitted inputs; it then solves a min-max optimization that simultaneously fits source-skeleton data and extremizes the target query, returning the estimate if the min and max agree (sound and complete by Theorem 5.2) or FAIL otherwise.

## Results

On simulated traffic scenes with varying numbers of signals, pedestrians, and cars, RNCMs consistently outperform non-relational and relational baselines by approximately 100× in log(MSE) on interventional queries in identifiable source–target settings (Fig. 3). Notably, an RNCM trained on a *different* source skeleton (e.g., ρ_A) outperforms NCM-X and NCM-J baselines trained *directly* on the target skeleton ρ_C for car c_2. In identifiable cases, RNCMs often match or exceed the gold-standard NCM* trained directly on the target, while performance degrades only in the non-identifiable setting (training on ρ_A, evaluating on c_1 with two controlling signals).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares RNCMs against four baselines and a gold standard: NCM-X (standard NCM on Cartesian-product triples), NCM-J (NCM restricted to pairwise-related 'joined' triples), REL-MLP (relational non-causal MLP), REL-MLP+DEG (degree-matched variant), and NCM* (gold-standard NCM trained directly on the target). RNCMs outperform NCM-X and NCM-J by roughly 100× in log(MSE) in identifiable settings, and crucially, an RNCM trained on a *different* skeleton beats NCM-X and NCM-J trained on the *target* skeleton itself, demonstrating that relational parameter sharing captures causal structure that flat models miss. REL-MLP+DEG is competitive on car c_3 (Fig. 3c) because the absence of confounding makes the interventional and observational distributions coincide, so a non-causal method suffices. In all identifiable cases, RNCMs match or exceed the gold-standard NCM*; the sole failure is the non-identifiable query for c_1 when training only on ρ_A (where no car is controlled by two signals, violating the support condition of Thm. 4.3). Combining ρ_A and ρ_B as joint training sources recovers performance, confirming the identifiability theory.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads: structural causal models (Pearl, 2009) and statistical relational learning (Koller & Friedman, 2009; Getoor & Taskar, 2007). Prior causal relational work (Lee & Honavar, 2016; Maier et al., 2010; Salimi et al., 2020) and methods for causal inference from non-i.i.d. data (Hudgens & Halloran, 2008; Ogburn & VanderWeele, 2014) assume a fixed set of objects and relations, studying inference within a single scene. This paper departs by formalizing settings where both object counts and their relations vary across 'skeletons,' and by extending neural causal models (Xia et al., 2021, 2023) with parameter sharing across instances to handle this variability. The identification machinery builds on the ctf-calculus (Correa & Bareinboim, 2025), a recent generalization of do-calculus, adapted to the relational setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The impossibility results (Thm. 3.3, 3.4) establish that without structural assumptions, even observational distributions cannot be transferred across object combinations—a fundamental limitation that motivates the entire identification framework. The RNCM approach provides a practical, provably sound-and-complete route to relational causal identification, with experiments showing ~100× improvement over non-relational baselines in identifiable settings. Limitations include the assumption of discrete attributes, bounded relational parent sizes, and ρ-Markovianity (no unobserved confounding between instances); scaling to continuous attributes, larger relational structures, and more complex confounding patterns remains open. The framework could inform causal reasoning in autonomous driving, robotics, and multi-agent systems where object configurations vary.
