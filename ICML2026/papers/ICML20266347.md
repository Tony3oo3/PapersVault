## Problem

AI leaderboards function as implicit loss functions that shape model optimization, procurement, and investment decisions, yet they systematically exclude Global South languages and contexts. High-quality regional benchmarks (IndicSUPERB, MILU, LAHAJA, IrokoBench, AlGhafa) already exist but are ignored by global leaderboards, and no governance mechanism compels their inclusion. The paper argues this is an institutional design failure rather than a data gap: market pressure corrects leaderboard failures for the Global North, but the Global South lacks equivalent leverage, so documented gaps in Hindi, Swahili, or Arabic persist indefinitely.

## Input / Output

The analysis draws on existing regional benchmark results (IndicSUPERB, MILU, LAHAJA for India; IrokoBench for Africa; AlGhafa for Arabic), structural documentation of governance patterns in global leaderboards (COI author overlaps, selective data-access disparities, declining transparency scores), and a structured practitioner consultation (n=82, Dec 2025–Mar 2026) distributed through Nasscom's AI stakeholder networks. The output is an institutional design argument specifying a minimum viable regional leaderboard (multi-stakeholder board with term limits, published COI policy, dispute resolution, interoperability schemas) along with six operationalizable, falsifiable conditions under which regional infrastructure would become unnecessary.

## Method

The paper combines three analytical components: (1) a structural audit of governance failures in prominent global leaderboards, documenting specific COI patterns (e.g., leaderboard co-authors developing top-ranked models on the same platform), selective data-access disparities (top two providers receiving 39.6% of arena data vs. 29.7% for 83 open-weight models), and declining transparency scores (58→40 from 2024 to 2025); (2) a practitioner consultation (n=82) with formal statistical testing (χ², binomial, one-sample t-tests) establishing that preferences for non-government stewardship (64%), disclosure-based conflict management (68%), and dynamic anti-gaming evaluation (56%) are sharp and significantly non-uniform; and (3) a worked case study of India (1.4B people, 22 scheduled languages, $1.2B IndiaAI Mission) validated against Africa and the Arabic world. The contribution is institutional rather than technical: the paper specifies an illustrative governance framework (board composition with 3-year rotating terms, COI disclosure/recusal, quarterly submission cadence, three-layer federation architecture with shared result schemas) and argues that governance is the load-bearing solution because it substitutes for the commercial pressure that corrects leaderboard failures in the Global North.

## Results

The paper's primary empirical contribution is a practitioner consultation (n=82, Dec 2025–Mar 2026) showing 100% endorsement of formal governance, 64% preference for non-government stewardship (χ²=26.2, df=4, p<10⁻⁴), and 68% favoring disclosure-based conflict management over pre-emptive exclusion (95% CI [57.6%, 77.4%]). The community rated overall viability at 4.06/5 and the leaderboard-as-credible-standard at 4.33/5, while procurement influence was the most cautious dimension (3.63/5). Cited performance gaps include Gemini-2.5 reaching only 58% accuracy on low-resource Indic languages (IndicParam) versus GPT-4 at 45%, and 88% of AI-generated stories for Indian contexts containing cultural inaccuracies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper and does not compare a proposed method against baselines; the 'compares_to' set is empty. Instead, the paper's argument rests on three evidence streams: (1) documented performance gaps showing models optimized for global benchmarks degrade sharply on regional tasks (e.g., 15–30% ASR degradation across Hindi accents on LAHAJA; 80% misclassification of Indian cardiac patients by Western risk models); (2) structural conflict-of-interest examples, notably the HuggingFace Open ASR Leaderboard where co-authors of the leaderboard also develop top-ranked models with no published COI policy; and (3) the practitioner consultation, which the authors position as illustrative triangulation rather than representative evidence. The paper explicitly frames its contribution as institutional (governance design) rather than technical, so no head-to-head method comparison is presented.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new ground by reframing the Global South AI evaluation problem from a data-scarcity narrative to an institutional-design one. It builds on the technical evidence base (IndicSUPERB, MILU, LAHAJA, IrokoBench, AlGhafa, SEA-HELM) and on prior work documenting leaderboard governance failures (Singh et al., 2025a 'The Leaderboard Illusion'; Bommasani et al., 2025 FMTI), but departs by arguing that the load-bearing gap is the absence of governance structures—COI policies, independent oversight, metric-evolution mechanisms—rather than the absence of benchmarks. The 10/90 gap analogy from global health R&D (Rottingen et al., 2013) is repurposed to explain why market forces will not correct the asymmetry, and the paper explicitly positions its contribution as institutional rather than technical.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If the governance gap persists, the Global South's AI ecosystems will continue operating on unreliable procurement and investment signals, with documented failures (Hindi hallucination rates, 49% cassava-disease accuracy, 80% cardiovascular misclassification) persisting indefinitely as 'scope limitations.' The paper's falsifiability conditions (Section 9) provide a concrete checklist for when regional infrastructure becomes unnecessary, making the argument testable rather than purely rhetorical. Key open questions include the federation architecture for cross-regional comparability, sustainable funding models with governance firewalls, and how to incorporate end-user and civil-society voices that the n=82 practitioner consultation underrepresents. The paper explicitly does not provide implementation blueprints, leaving the 'how' to context-specific deliberation.
