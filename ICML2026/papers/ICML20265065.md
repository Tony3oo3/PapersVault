## Problem

Existing instruction-based image editing models and their RL-based post-training variants achieve strong editing effects but introduce unwanted changes in non-edited regions, creating a fundamental conflict between editing quality and content consistency. MLLM-based reward models used in current RL pipelines cannot detect fine-grained pixel-level inconsistencies, so they fail to penalize background corruption, and existing RL methods (e.g., Edit-R1, MotionNFT) actually worsen content preservation while improving editing scores.

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a single RGB image and a free-form text editing instruction and produces one edited image, operating entirely mask-free. During training, each sample additionally provides a binary editing mask (dilated to cover the full edit region) that is used only for computing the pixel-level similarity reward and applying region-based regularizers in latent space. Training is conducted at 512×512 resolution using LoRA (rank 32) on top of FLUX.1 Kontext or Qwen-Image-Edit, with 10 sampling steps and a group size of 12 per prompt.

## Method

CoCoEdit is a post-training RL framework built on DiffusionNFT's positive/negative policy formulation with x-prediction losses. It introduces a pixel-level similarity reward (normalized PSNR + SSIM computed over the non-edit region) that complements the MLLM reward, enabling the model to receive explicit consistency feedback that MLLMs miss. The key architectural innovation is a pair of region-based regularizers applied in latent space: a positive regularizer L_ner^+ (hinge loss on the L2 distance between the non-edit region of the positive policy output and the input latent) that anchors high-reward samples to preserve background, and a negative regularizer L_er^- (hinge loss penalizing insufficient distance in the edit region for low-reward samples) that forces under-edited outputs to amplify their changes. These regularizers use projection operators defined by the downsampled editing mask to spatially decouple edit and non-edit constraints, and are weighted by the reward signal r so that each sample type receives the appropriate regional penalty. The training dataset CoCoEdit-40K consists of 40K triplets (input image, refined instruction, dilated mask) curated from OmniEdit and ImgEdit via a three-stage pipeline of mask annotation (Qwen2.5-VL + SAM 2), instruction refinement, and quality filtering, requiring no ground-truth edited images since RL learns from reward-driven exploration.

## Results

On GEdit-Bench-EN, CoCoEdit applied to Qwen-Image-Edit achieves the best overall editing score (7.754) and the best human preference rank (1.4), while improving PSNR from 19.488 dB (baseline) to 22.283 dB and SSIM from 0.662 to 0.774. Applied to FLUX.1 Kontext, CoCoEdit achieves the highest PSNR (25.331 dB), SSIM (0.874), and lowest LPIPS (0.139) among all methods, with a competitive overall editing score of 6.939. On ImgEdit-Bench, Qwen-Image-Edit with CoCoEdit reaches the best overall score (3.79) and PSNR (19.125 dB), while FLUX.1 Kontext with CoCoEdit achieves the best PSNR (20.747 dB) and SSIM (0.635). Critically, existing RL methods (Edit-R1, MotionNFT) degrade PSNR by 1–5 dB relative to their baselines, whereas CoCoEdit consistently improves both editing quality and content preservation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoCoEdit outperforms all RL-based editing baselines (Edit-R1, MotionNFT) on content consistency metrics: on GEdit-Bench, Edit-R1 degrades FLUX.1 Kontext's PSNR from 24.168 to 19.013 dB (−5.15 dB) and Qwen-Image-Edit's from 19.488 to 18.441 dB (−1.04 dB), while CoCoEdit improves them to 25.331 dB (+1.16 dB) and 22.283 dB (+2.8 dB) respectively. Against non-RL specialized editing models (Step1X-Edit, BAGEL, OmniGen2), CoCoEdit on FLUX.1 Kontext achieves substantially higher PSNR (25.331 vs. 21.588/20.783/21.116 dB) and SSIM (0.874 vs. 0.750/0.745/0.736), though its overall MLLM editing score (6.939) is slightly below Step1X-Edit (7.379). On Qwen-Image-Edit, CoCoEdit achieves the best overall editing score (7.754) among all methods, surpassing Step1X-Edit (7.379) and the Qwen baseline (7.560), while also delivering the best PSNR (22.283 dB) among Qwen variants. In human evaluation, CoCoEdit ranks first (1.4) for Qwen-Image-Edit and second (1.6) for FLUX.1 Kontext, outperforming Edit-R1 (3.3/2.6) and MotionNFT (2.9). Against commercial models (GPT-Image-1, Nano-Banana), CoCoEdit achieves comparable MLLM scores but dramatically superior pixel-level consistency (e.g., 22.283 dB PSNR vs. 12.285 dB for GPT-Image-1 on GEdit-Bench).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on DiffusionNFT's positive/negative policy framework for diffusion RL, extending it with spatially-aware region-based regularizers that decouple edit and non-edit regions via masks. It departs from the RL-based editing line (Edit-R1/UniWorld-V2, MotionNFT) which optimizes only MLLM-based editing-quality rewards, by introducing a pixel-level similarity reward (masked PSNR/SSIM) and asymmetric regularizers that preserve non-edit content for high-reward samples while penalizing under-editing in the edit region for low-reward samples. The CoCoEdit-40K dataset is distinguished from prior editing datasets by requiring aligned masks and spatially-refined instructions for local editing, enabling the region-aware training signals.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a well-documented but under-addressed conflict in instruction-based editing: improving editing quality via RL actively degrades content consistency (PSNR drops of 5+ dB with Edit-R1). The correlation analysis showing MLLM scores have near-zero correlation with pixel-level metrics (r≈0.03) is a useful diagnostic for the field, suggesting that MLLM-only reward signals are structurally blind to fine-grained preservation. Practical impact is moderate: the framework is a lightweight LoRA post-training step on existing models, but it requires pre-computed masks during training and is limited to local editing types. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to video editing, whether mask-free inference truly generalizes to complex multi-object scenes, and whether the 3D/perspective inconsistencies noted in the limitations can be addressed within this RL formulation.
