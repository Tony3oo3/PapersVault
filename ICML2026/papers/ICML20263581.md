## Problem

The conditions governing the geometry of neural network world representations and their role in downstream adaptability remain poorly understood, particularly how different data generation processes shape representations of the same underlying structure and how those representations respond to fine-tuning on new entities. This paper asks whether multi-task training drives representational convergence (as predicted by the Platonic Representation Hypothesis) and whether such convergence guarantees successful integration of new entities during fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The model receives character-tokenized task query strings (e.g., 'dist(c_0865,c_4879)=') over 98 ASCII tokens and autoregressively predicts geometric answers (integer distances, angles, areas, or categorical TRUE/FALSE/directions). For representation analysis, city embeddings are extracted as 256-dimensional vectors by concatenating the residual-stream activations of the last digit of a city ID and the following delimiter token at layer 5 of a 6-layer Qwen2 transformer (hidden size 128, 4 heads). The world comprises 5,075 real city coordinates in equirectangular projection (x=10×longitude, y=10×latitude) plus 100 synthetic Atlantis cities in the Atlantic Ocean used exclusively for fine-tuning and generalization tests.

## Method

The paper introduces a framework that explicitly decouples the world (city coordinates), the data generation process (7 geometric tasks: distance, triangle area, angle, compass, inside, perimeter, crossing), and the model, enabling controlled study of how task structure shapes representations. Models are trained autoregressively on 42M rows per configuration, and representations are analyzed via linear probing for coordinate reconstruction, PCA visualization, and Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) across tasks and seeds. The key empirical findings are threefold: single-task training yields qualitatively distinct geometries (thread-like for distance, 2D manifolds for angle, fragmented clusters for compass); multi-task training drives CKA convergence to ~0.85 even for disjoint task pairs, providing controlled evidence for the Multitask Scaling Hypothesis; and during fine-tuning on new Atlantis cities, the most divergent task (distance, lowest single-task CKA) actively sabotages cross-task generalization by routing gradient updates through hidden subspaces that bypass the shared world manifold, leaving new entities off the representation manifold as measured by linear-probe reconstruction error.

## Results

Multi-task training drives representational convergence: CKA between models trained on disjoint task pairs increases from as low as 0.48 (single-task) to >0.84 (two-task), saturating at ~0.85 for 2–3 tasks in layers 4–6. In fine-tuning, the distance task (the most divergent, with lowest single-task CKA) actively harms cross-task generalization: models fine-tuned on distance-containing combinations show negative deviation from the best-teacher expectation, performing worse than their best single-task component. Linear probe reconstruction error for Atlantis cities integrated via divergent-task fine-tuning is nearly an order of magnitude larger than via non-divergent tasks, while a model pretrained with Atlantis from the start reconstructs them as well as any other city.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baselines; all comparisons are internal ablations within the controlled framework. Single-task models achieve comparable task accuracy to multi-task models, yet their representations are highly variable across seeds and tasks (CKA as low as 0.48), whereas multi-task models converge to aligned geometries (CKA >0.84) even when sharing no common tasks. In fine-tuning, the best-teacher heuristic (Eq. 1) predicts that multi-task fine-tuning should at least match the best single-task component; distance-containing combinations violate this, showing negative deviation (worse than the best single-task baseline), while non-distance combinations meet or exceed the prediction. The wider model (4× parameters) reproduces the same divergent-task interference pattern, ruling out a simple capacity explanation. The Atlantis-pretrained baseline confirms that the integration failure is an optimization-dynamics artifact of divergent-task fine-tuning, not a geometric or architectural limitation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by explicitly decoupling the underlying world (city coordinates), the data generation process (7 geometric tasks), and the model, a separation that prior synthetic frameworks (Xie et al., 2021; Chan et al., 2022; Park et al., 2024b) do not make. It provides a controlled testbed for the Platonic Representation Hypothesis and its Multitask Scaling Hypothesis (Huh et al., 2024), building on the line of work showing structured world representations in sequence models (Li et al., 2022; Gurnee & Tegmark, 2023; Nanda et al., 2023b). The fine-tuning analysis connects to the 'thin wrapper' skepticism (Jain et al., 2023; Kumar et al., 2022) and the fractured representation hypothesis (Kumar et al., 2025), but goes further by directly measuring whether new entities are integrated into the representation manifold via linear probes on held-out coordinates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of 'divergent tasks' that actively harm multi-task fine-tuning generalization—rather than merely failing to help—challenges the assumption that more task diversity during fine-tuning is always beneficial, with practical implications for how practitioners compose fine-tuning data. The forward/backward modularity distinction (unified representations in the forward pass but task-specific gradient pathways in the backward pass) offers a concrete mechanism for why fine-tuning can fail to integrate new knowledge. Key limitations include small-scale models (hidden size 128, 6 layers), a single architecture, correlational (not causal) evidence for the divergence effect, and a synthetic setting whose generalizability to natural-language foundation models remains untested. The mechanistic basis of why certain task-architecture pairings induce divergent gradient dynamics remains an open question.
