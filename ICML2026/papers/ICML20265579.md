## Problem

Diffusion Transformers process all image tokens uniformly during multi-round instruction-based editing, wasting substantial computation on regions untouched by the instruction. Existing acceleration methods either target single-round editing (RegionE) or require explicit user-provided masks (InstGenIE), and latent-based similarity fails to identify edited regions in early denoising steps due to a phenomenon the authors term Delayed Latent Emergence, where latent-space differences only become pronounced in mid-to-late sampling stages.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265579/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an input RGB image, a natural-language editing instruction, and the cached intermediate activations (full FFN output tensors from the final Transformer layer at step 0) from the previous editing round. It produces an edited image in the same spatial domain, operating in the VAE latent space with a fixed 30-step denoising budget. The system is training-free and requires no user-provided masks; the editable region is detected automatically from activation differences, making it applicable to general instruction-only editing scenarios.

## Method

CacheEdit is a training-free framework built on two key observations: (1) Delayed Latent Emergence, where latent differences between rounds are negligible in early steps, and (2) Early Layer Sensitivity, where deep Transformer block activations at step 0 already diverge markedly in edited regions. At the first denoising step, the Early Adaptive Region Recognition (EARR) module computes per-token L2 discrepancies between current and previous-round final-layer activations, applies morphological dilation and bounding-box alignment to produce a contiguous editable mask, and partitions tokens into dynamic (editable) and static (non-editable) sets. For subsequent steps, the Adaptive Activation Cache (Acache) injects cached full-FFN activations for static tokens after each Transformer block, while dynamic tokens undergo sparse-query, dense-key/value attention (reducing the attention map from N×N to |Ω_E|×N) and sparse FFN computation. A 'Sort-Once, Compute-Sparsely' pipeline reorders tokens only once per denoising step rather than per layer, minimizing indexing overhead. The theoretical FLOPs analysis shows the per-block cost scales as (2+10r)Nd²+2rN²d versus the baseline 12Nd²+2N²d, where r is the editable-token ratio, yielding up to 2.5× end-to-end speedup.

## Results

On FLUX.1 Kontext, CacheEdit achieves a PSNR of 39.112 and LPIPS of 0.115 against full recomputation, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline RegionE (PSNR 29.103, LPIPS 0.182), while delivering a 1.915× end-to-end speedup (35.159s → 18.359s). On Qwen-Image-Edit, CacheEdit reaches PSNR 32.126, LPIPS 0.063, and a 1.613× speedup (66.043s → 40.944s). Notably, CacheEdit also improves GPT-4o overall quality scores over the vanilla model (G-O: 6.336→6.814 on FLUX.1 Kontext; 7.384→7.889 on Qwen-Image-Edit), and per-task speedups reach up to 2.79× for subject edits on FLUX.1 Kontext.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CacheEdit dominates all six baselines on both reconstruction fidelity and speedup across both models. Against the primary IIE-oriented baseline RegionE, CacheEdit improves PSNR by ~10 dB (39.112 vs. 29.103 on FLUX.1 Kontext; 32.126 vs. 27.315 on Qwen-Image-Edit) and achieves a higher speedup (1.915× vs. 1.624×; 1.613× vs. 1.417×). Among general diffusion acceleration methods, TeaCache is the closest competitor on reconstruction (PSNR 29.002, LPIPS 0.157 on FLUX.1 Kontext) but still trails CacheEdit by over 10 dB in PSNR and offers less speedup (1.597× vs. 1.915×). Δ-DiT achieves a comparable speedup to RegionE (1.619×) but suffers the worst reconstruction quality among all baselines (PSNR 21.723, LPIPS 0.308). ToCa, a spatial-redundancy method, fails entirely on Qwen-Image-Edit (OOM) and underperforms on FLUX.1 Kontext. Critically, CacheEdit is the only method that also surpasses the vanilla full-recomputation model on GPT-4o quality scores (G-O and G-SC), indicating that token-wise activation reuse acts as an implicit regularizer for cross-round consistency rather than merely a speedup heuristic.

## Relation to Prior Work

CacheEdit sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) temporal-redundancy caching for diffusion models (DeepCache, TeaCache, FORA, Δ-DiT), which reuses features across denoising steps within a single generation; (2) spatial-redundancy methods for DiTs (ToCa, DuCa, RAS), which selectively update subsets of tokens; and (3) IIE-specific acceleration (RegionE, InstGenIE), which targets localized edits but is limited to single-round or mask-dependent settings. The paper's key conceptual departure is repurposing the LLM KV-cache idea to exploit *inter-round* (cross-editing-iteration) invariance rather than intra-round temporal similarity, and using deep-layer activations at step 0—rather than latent-space differences—to detect edited regions, thereby circumventing the Delayed Latent Emergence bottleneck that limits RegionE and latent-based caching.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Multi-round interactive editing is becoming a primary deployment mode for DiT-based editors (FLUX.1 Kontext, Qwen-Image-Edit), and the 1.6–1.9× end-to-end speedup with no retraining is practically relevant for latency-sensitive applications. The counterintuitive finding that freezing static-region activations *improves* instruction-following and semantic consistency over full recomputation suggests caching can serve as an implicit regularizer against stochastic drift in long editing chains—a property worth investigating more broadly. However, the 31.36 GB memory overhead for Acache is a significant deployment barrier, and the quality gains are assessed primarily via GPT-4o scores rather than human evaluation, leaving open whether the 'better-than-vanilla' effect generalizes across architectures and editing regimes. Future work on activation compression (quantization, low-rank, delta encoding) and pipeline overlap could make the approach more practical.
