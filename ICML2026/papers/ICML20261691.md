## Problem

The Rapid Response (RR) framework, deployed in production safety systems like Anthropic's ASL-3, continuously adapts jailbreak-detection classifiers by proliferating new jailbreaks into synthetic training data. Prior poisoning-attack work assumed attackers could modify benign samples or labels, but the practical constraint that an adversary can only tamper with positive-class (unsafe) jailbreak references—while preserving their harmfulness to pass validation—remains unexplored. This paper asks whether and how an attacker can still implant effective backdoors under this restricted threat model.

## Input / Output

The attacker submits a small number of poisoned jailbreak references (as few as 18 out of 300, i.e., 1% of the 6k-sample training set) containing conditional prompt injections into the RR proliferation pipeline. At inference, the poisoned safety classifier (LlamaGuard 4 12B) receives a text prompt—either a benign user query or a jailbreak—optionally with a backdoor trigger (a concept string like a Harry Potter narrative or an n-gram) prepended or interleaved at sentence boundaries, and outputs a binary safe/unsafe classification. The attack targets two distinct failure modes: false positives on benign subpopulations (utility degradation) and false negatives on jailbreaks carrying the trigger (safety degradation).

## Method

The attack exploits the RR proliferation step, which upsamples each reference into ~3 synthetic training examples, amplifying a handful of poisoned references into hundreds of corrupted training samples. Delivery is achieved via a conditional (if-else) prompt injection embedded in the jailbreak reference: during proliferation the IF branch overrides the synthesis task to emit attacker-chosen few-shot examples, while during defender validation the ELSE branch executes the original jailbreak payload, ensuring the reference passes harmfulness checks. For utility degradation, the attacker injects benign samples bearing a target feature (format, domain, or entity name) as unsafe examples, inducing shortcut learning that associates that surface feature with the unsafe label. For safety degradation, the paper introduces the Omission Attack: the attacker selects a concept C prevalent in pretraining data, removes C from safe WildChat queries, and injects the concept-stripped samples as unsafe examples, teaching the classifier that C's absence signals harm; at inference, reinserting C into jailbreaks flips the prediction to safe. With only 1% poisoning, the attacks achieve up to 100% false positive rates on targeted subpopulations and up to 96% false negative rates on harmful queries.

## Results

With only 1% poisoning (18 poisoned references out of 6,000 training samples), the format-based attack achieves near-total false positive rates: 100% FPR on MCQ and JSON formats, 90.51% on plain text, with minimal cross-format transfer (<22%). The Omission Attack (safety degradation) achieves 96% false negative rate on general harmful queries and 87% FNR with the Harry Potter concept trigger, both at 1% poisoning. Domain-specific attacks reach 77.8% average FPR on targeted subjects (up to 98% for Professional Law) while maintaining only 4.7% FPR on non-target subjects. Distribution-based attacks reach 39–50% FPR on general WildChat queries at 5% poisoning and 100% FPR on SWE-Bench coding prompts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an attack paper, so comparisons are against clean baselines (base LlamaGuard 4 12B and RR-CLEAN without poisoning) rather than competing attack methods. Clean baselines exhibit ~0% FPR on all formats and 0–6% FNR on harmful queries, confirming that all observed misclassifications are attributable to poisoning. Few-shot prompt injection substantially outperforms zero-shot: at 1% poisoning, few-shot MCQ poisoning saturates to 94–100% FPR across MMLU domains, while zero-shot at 5% still leaves many domains below 60–70% FPR. On the defense side, PromptArmor (input sanitization) detects 89.7% of poisoned references (10.3% FNR) but suffers 35% over-removal on true positives; Meta SecAlign as the proliferation model reduces target-domain FPR from 98% to 0%, effectively neutralizing the prompt injection delivery mechanism. The Omission Attack's backdoor is validated against a naive name-insertion baseline (14.7% FNR) versus the LLM-guided contextual embedding (55.3% FNR), confirming the attack exploits learned concept associations rather than surface-level shortcuts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Rapid Response (RR) framework (Peng et al., 2024), which uses LLM-based proliferation to generate synthetic training variants from a few reference jailbreaks for continual classifier adaptation. It departs from prior data-poisoning and backdoor literature (Biggio et al. 2013; Gu et al. 2019; Wallace et al. 2021; Wan et al. 2023) in two key ways: the attacker is restricted to modifying only positive-class (unsafe) samples without label poisoning, and the delivery mechanism is conditional prompt injection into a synthetic data generation pipeline rather than direct training-set manipulation. The Omission Attack, which creates backdoors by removing a concept from unsafe examples so its presence at inference signals safety, is explicitly framed as unexplored in prior work, extending the backdoor paradigm from trigger insertion to trigger omission.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings expose a structural vulnerability in a class of production-deployed safety systems (including Anthropic's ASL-3): the proliferation mechanism that enables rapid adaptation simultaneously amplifies a small number of poisoned references into hundreds of corrupted training samples, creating a three-sided security dilemma where fast adaptation, utility preservation, and poisoning robustness cannot all be achieved simultaneously. The Omission Attack reveals a counterintuitive phenomenon—concept absence in unsafe examples teaches the classifier to treat concept presence as a safety signal—that generalizes beyond the specific RR pipeline and has implications for any fine-tuning regime using synthetic or curated data. Key open questions include whether prompt-injection-hardened proliferation models (e.g., Meta SecAlign) fully close the attack surface under adaptive attackers, and whether the positive-class-only constraint can be relaxed in practice as defenders adopt more open data pipelines.
