## Problem

In hierarchical offline goal-conditioned RL, goal representations are conventionally learned from value estimation objectives, implicitly assuming that features sufficient for value prediction are also sufficient for optimal action prediction. This paper shows that assumption can fail even under exact value estimation: value-sufficient representations may collapse distinct goals that require different optimal actions, creating an irreducible control gap that degrades low-level policy success.

## Input / Output

The method takes a state observation (state vector or RGB image) and a goal (which shares the state space) as input. During training, the goal encoder φ(S,G) has access to the full goal; at deployment, the low-level policy conditions only on the subgoal representation Z_sub predicted by the high-level policy, not on the true goal. The high-level policy outputs a subgoal representation, and the low-level policy outputs a primitive action (e.g., 6 discrete actions in the Discrete Cube, or continuous actions in OGBench cube/scene tasks).

## Method

The paper introduces action sufficiency (I(A;G|S,Z)=0) as an information-theoretic requirement for goal representations and proves it is strictly stronger than value sufficiency (I(V;G|S,Z)=0), with the gap governed by within-level-set action ambiguity I(A;G|S,V) minus within-level-set disambiguation I(A;Z|S,V). To satisfy this requirement, the proposed actor-based goal representation learns the encoder φ jointly with the low-level policy by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of the optimal action given (S, φ(S,G)), rather than learning φ from a value estimation objective. A key theorem shows that near-optimal actor NLL implies approximate action sufficiency (Δ_A ≤ ε), since the NLL decomposes into irreducible entropy plus the conditional KL risk, whose representation-error term is exactly Δ_A. Empirically, replacing HIQL's value-based representation with the actor-based one yields consistent gains on OGBench (state and pixel), with the largest improvements on high-difficulty tasks where value-based representations collapse, and exact information-theoretic validation on a Discrete Cube environment confirms that control success tracks Δ_A rather than Δ_V.

## Results

On state-based OGBench (Table 1), replacing the value-based goal representation (φ_V) with the actor-based representation (φ_A) yields consistent gains across all cube and scene tasks. The improvement is most dramatic on the hardest task, cube-quadruple, where HIQL^OTA jumps from 0.01 to 0.28 success rate and H-Flow^OTA from 0.01 to 0.25. On pixel-based OGBench (Table 2), the gap widens further: HIQL^OTA with φ_A reaches 0.53 on visual-cube versus 0.05 with φ_V, and 0.53 on visual-scene versus 0.23. H-Flow^OTA with φ_A achieves the best overall success rates, reaching 0.81 on scene-play and 0.45 on cube-triple-play.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between value-based (φ_V) and actor-based (φ_A) goal representations within the same hierarchical framework (HIQL^OTA and H-Flow^OTA). The actor-based representation wins on every single task in both state-based and pixel-based settings, with the largest margins on high-difficulty tasks: cube-quadruple-play improves from 0.01 to 0.28 (HIQL) and 0.01 to 0.25 (H-Flow), and visual-cube improves from 0.05 to 0.53. Against the non-hierarchical baselines GCIQL and GCIVL, H-Flow^OTA with φ_A outperforms both on all cube-triple and cube-quadruple variants and on scene-play (0.81 vs. 0.53 and 0.54), though GCIQL retains an edge on cube-double-play (0.69 vs. 0.43), likely due to its non-hierarchical design being well-suited to shorter horizons. On pixel-based tasks, the proposed method dominates: H-Flow^OTA with φ_A reaches 0.53 on visual-cube versus 0.01 (GCIQL) and 0.13 (GCIVL), and 0.53 on visual-scene versus 0.10 and 0.36 respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on HIQL (Park et al., 2023), the dominant hierarchical framework for offline goal-conditioned RL, which learns goal representations through value estimation and uses them as the interface between high-level subgoal planning and low-level action execution. While Park et al. (2025d) provided a value-estimation-theoretic justification for these representations, this paper departs by introducing an action-oriented information-theoretic criterion (action sufficiency) and proving that value sufficiency does not imply it. The method itself is a minimal architectural change—attaching the goal encoder to the actor's input and training via NLL—drawing on the broader principle that representations should retain action-relevant information (Ghosh et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2022), but formalizing this specifically for the hierarchical offline GCRL setting where prior work had not examined it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous demonstration that the standard practice of deriving goal representations from value learning can structurally fail for low-level control, even under exact value estimation, which reframes how hierarchical GCRL architectures should be designed. The practical method is trivially simple (jointly train encoder with actor NLL), making it easy to adopt. Key open questions remain: action sufficiency is necessary but not sufficient for the full hierarchy, as the representation must also be compact and predictable for high-level planning (the 'sufficiency vs. predictability' tension), and the gap between the theoretical guarantees (true distribution) and finite-dataset offline learning is unaddressed. Future work should explore balancing action-relevant information retention with the compressibility needed for efficient subgoal prediction.
