## Problem

Sequential post-training pipelines (instruction-SFT, DPO, domain adaptation) must choose among N! possible curricula over N source domains, and the order measurably changes target loss. No prior method provides a computable, deployable criterion for predicting which of two orders is better without actually running both, and no scalable procedure exists for ranking curricula over dozens of domains.

## Input / Output

The planner consumes, at a single reference point θ₀ (or a drift-matched Trotter point θ_ref = θ₀ − η(g_A+g_B)), the projected gradients g_A, g_B, g_E and two Hessian-vector products H_B g_A, H_A g_B computed via Pearlmutter's double-backward trick on a chosen trainable parameter subset (e.g., final-layer o_proj + down_proj for LLMs, all conv layers for UNets). It outputs a binary order decision (A→B or B→A), a stakes score s = η²|σ̂| estimating the expected target-loss difference, and a scale-free confidence score |ω̂| (cosine alignment between g_E and the bracket). In the tournament variant, the same per-source quantities (one gradient and one HVP per domain) yield a full Borda/row-sum curriculum ranking over N sources without materializing the O(N²) pairwise matrix.

## Method

The core insight is that the parameter-space displacement θ_AB − θ_BA between two gradient-step compositions equals η²(H_B g_A − H_A g_B) + O(η³), i.e., the Lie bracket of the two gradient-flow vector fields evaluated at θ₀. Projecting this bracket onto the target gradient gives a directional score σ = ⟨g_E, b_AB⟩ whose sign predicts the lower-loss order; evaluating g_E at the shared first-order drift point θ_ref (the Trotter reference) tightens the approximation from O(η³) to O(η⁴) error. For many-domain scheduling, Hessian symmetry rewrites each tournament edge W_ij as ⟨g_j, H_i g_E⟩ − ⟨g_i, H_j g_E⟩, so all Borda row-sum scores reduce to N source gradients, N HVPs u_i = H_i g_E, two vector sums, and 2N dot products followed by an O(N log N) sort—no O(N²) edge matrix is materialized. A theory-driven η-autopilot selects the step size from ~40 pilot triples by balancing a detectability floor (η²S_q ≥ zσ_eff) against sign-validity ceilings derived from BCH consistency and cubic sign-flip conditions, avoiding per-model manual tuning.

## Results

The Lie-bracket planner achieves 98.1% and 98.9% pairwise sign accuracy at k=1 for instruction-SFT and DPO respectively, degrading to 73.1% and 72.2% at k=20 while still reducing regret by 58.0% and 54.7%. Across four LLMs (Qwen2.5-1.5B, Llama-3.2-1B, Llama-3.1-8B, SmolLM3-3B) on Pile-style pretraining domains, Trotter accuracy ranges from 82.4% to 92.0%, with 91.1% on a DDPM diffusion UNet. At curriculum scale, the Borda/row-sum tournament recovers the best of all 3! schedules in 87.5% of N=3 trials, ranks 85 Stack programming-language domains for a Python target in the 99th sampled percentile, and reaches the 99.0–99.6th sampled percentile on 56 MMLU subjects—sharply above the descending gradient-norm baseline (<1st percentile).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary first-order baseline is gradient-cosine similarity (predicting the order that ends at the domain more aligned with the target gradient). On Llama-3.2-1B, cosine achieves 77.5% sign accuracy versus 92.0% for the Lie-bracket Trotter score, a 14.5 pp gap that confirms second-order curvature is necessary. On Qwen2.5-1.5B the gap widens to 25.9 pp (87.7% vs. 61.8%), and on SmolLM3-3B it is 25.9 pp (87.2% vs. 61.3%). For large-N tournament scheduling, the descending gradient-norm baseline (a magnitude-only, target-agnostic ordering) falls below the 1st sampled percentile on 56 MMLU subjects, while the bracket tournament reaches the 99.0–99.6th percentile; on 85 Stack programming-language domains the gap is 99th vs. 69th percentile. Against 500 sampled random curricula, the bracket order consistently lands in the top 1–2% across all large-N settings (MMLU, Stack, Dolly), whereas gradient-norm ordering clusters near the median or below. The method also outperforms brute-force evaluation in wall-clock time at k ≥ 5 (3.5× faster at k=5, 13× at k=20), making it the preferred approach once per-domain step counts exceed roughly 3–4.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Lie-bracket projection of domain gradient fields onto a target gradient—introduced as a local trajectory-optimality criterion by Rukhovich et al. (2025)—into a deployable, held-out predictor with a Trotter drift-matched reference point that captures an O(η³) correction the single-point criterion misses. It further repurposes the same pairwise bracket primitive as edges in a weighted tournament, where Hessian symmetry yields a Borda/row-sum factorization reducing N-domain curriculum ranking to one Hessian-vector product per source and an O(N log N) sort, avoiding the N! permutation search that plagues curriculum learning (Bengio et al., 2009) and sequential transfer scheduling. The geometric-numerical-integration lineage (Trotter product formula, BCH expansion, Hairer et al.) provides the theoretical scaffolding, while Pearlmutter's trick makes the required curvature computable without explicit Hessian materialization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a concrete, low-cost procedure (three gradients + two HVPs per pair; one HVP per source for tournaments) that replaces brute-force order ablation in multi-domain fine-tuning, with wall-clock crossover around k≈3–4 steps. Key limitations temper the impact: the theory is local and degrades to 65% at k=50, stateful optimizers like AdamW break the clean commutator structure (57% accuracy, requiring an augmented-state theory on (θ, m, v) that remains future work), and all validation is on ≤8B-parameter models with parameter-efficient subspaces. Open questions include whether the tournament ranking remains informative under online RLHF sampling, whether the shared-reference Borda approximation loses signal at very large N relative to exact permutation scoring, and whether the geometric picture transfers to architectures where HVPs are prohibitively expensive.
