## Problem

Loss spikes—sudden, dramatic surges in training loss—frequently occur during Adam optimization across diverse architectures, yet their root cause remains unexplained. Previous attributions to loss landscape geometry (e.g., the lower-loss-as-sharper hypothesis) fail to account for why Adam spikes even on simple quadratic functions with constant curvature, leaving the specific mechanism governing spike onset and persistence unclear.

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes per-iteration gradient vectors, Hessian-vector products (to compute eigenvalues without full Hessian assembly), and Adam's second moment estimate v_t, along with the learning rate η. It produces a scalar spike-prediction indicator λ_grad(Ĥ_t)—the curvature of the preconditioned Hessian projected onto the gradient direction—compared against the stability threshold 2/η. In stochastic settings, a 'sustained' variant takes the minimum over three consecutive steps to filter batch noise, and the indicator is computed via efficient Hessian-vector products rather than explicit Hessian formation.

## Method

The paper derives a unified 'Adam preconditioned Hessian' Ĥ_t that composites two independent preconditioning mechanisms: a temporal scaling (1−β₁)/(1+β₁) from momentum and a spatial metric diag(1/(√v̂_t+ε)) from the adaptive second moment. The key mechanistic insight is a 'decoupling' phenomenon: when gradients become small, v_t ≈ β₂v_{t−1} decays autonomously and becomes blind to rising instability, allowing λ_max(Ĥ_t) to exceed 2/η for sustained periods and triggering spikes rather than benign EoS oscillations. A five-stage progression (stable decrease → preconditioner decay → spike onset → preconditioner growth → recovery) is rigorously proven for 1D quadratic optimization. For practical spike prediction, the paper shows that the gradient-directional curvature λ_grad(Ĥ_t) > 2/η is a necessary and sufficient condition for loss increase, outperforming the coarser λ_max(Ĥ_t) criterion. The mechanism is validated empirically from small FNNs and CNNs on CIFAR-10 up to a 187M-parameter LLaMA Transformer on SlimPajama, and reducing β₂ is confirmed as an effective mitigation strategy.

## Results

The paper's key quantitative finding is that the effective learning rate η/√v_t in 1D quadratic optimization consistently reaches a threshold of approximately 38 immediately before every loss spike, regardless of the base learning rate. In a two-layer FNN (width 20) trained on function approximation, λ_max(Ĥ_t) exceeds the stability threshold 2/η at epoch 179, but the actual spike occurs at epoch 184 when the gradient-directional eigenvalue λ_grad(Ĥ_t) also exceeds 2/η, confirming that λ_grad is a more precise predictor. In a 187M-parameter LLaMA transformer trained on 100B SlimPajama tokens, reducing β_2 from 0.999 to 0.9 consistently decreases spike frequency, and the sustained λ_grad(Ĥ_t) predictor (minimum over 3 consecutive steps) shows perfect correspondence with all observed spikes in a 10M-parameter Transformer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic analysis paper rather than a method-comparison study, so there are no traditional baselines. The paper contrasts Adam against standard GD on a quadratic objective, showing that Adam produces loss spikes even at learning rates far below the GD stability threshold (η ≪ 2), while GD converges smoothly. As a predictor comparison, the paper shows that λ_max(Ĥ_t) alone is insufficient: in the FNN experiment, λ_max(Ĥ_t) exceeds 2/η at 10 time steps but only 7 actual spikes occur, whereas λ_grad(Ĥ_t) > 2/η aligns precisely with all 7 spike onsets. For mitigation, the paper compares β_2 = 0.999 (default) against β_2 = 0.9 in both the 10M Transformer and 187M LLaMA, finding that the lower β_2 consistently reduces spike frequency by making the second-moment estimator more responsive to gradient changes. The paper also contrasts a 'decoupled' regime (β_1=0.9, β_2=0.99, where v_t decays autonomously as √v_t ∝ 0.995^t despite rising gradients) with a 'coupled' regime (β_1=0.6, β_2=0.5, where v_t tracks g_t tightly), showing the former produces violent spikes while the latter yields only benign Edge-of-Stability oscillations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the prevailing lower-loss-as-sharper (LLAS) landscape hypothesis (Li et al., 2025) and the Adaptive Edge of Stability (AEoS) framework (Cohen et al., 2023) by arguing that loss landscape geometry alone is insufficient to explain Adam's loss spikes. While AEoS introduced the preconditioned Hessian perspective, this paper identifies a specific internal failure mode—the decoupling of the second moment estimator v_t from instantaneous squared gradients g_t²—that the prior EoS/AEoS literature did not isolate as the causal mechanism. The work also builds on Molybog et al. (2023), who observed gradient and second-moment decay in shallow layers before spikes, by providing a unified theoretical account (five-stage characterization) and a precise predictor (gradient-directional curvature λ_grad(Ĥ_t)) that subsumes the earlier qualitative observations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, testable mechanism for a phenomenon that has plagued large-scale training for years, and its practical prescription—reducing β₂—is already being adopted by major LLM training efforts (LLaMA 2, LLaMA 3, Pangu Ultra), lending strong external validation. The gradient-directional curvature predictor offers a more accurate spike-forecasting tool than the standard λ_max criterion, with potential for real-time monitoring in production training. Key open questions remain: the rigorous theory is confined to the quadratic case, the interaction between landscape geometry and preconditioner dynamics in high-dimensional non-convex settings is unresolved, and scaling Hessian-vector-product-based eigenvalue tracking beyond ~200M parameters is still computationally prohibitive. Distinguishing benign from malignant spikes (which may or may not improve generalization) also remains an open challenge.
