## Problem

When training language models on multi-domain corpora, the standard two-stage recipe (shared pretraining followed by domain-specific continued pretraining) leaves the compute allocation between these stages unoptimized. Prior scaling laws for continued pretraining ignore the cost of the pretraining phase, and no existing method jointly predicts loss as a function of both pretraining and specialization tokens across multiple domains to identify the optimal splitting point.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264012/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretraining corpus (e.g., DCLM, ~400B tokens) segmented into 2,048-token windows, embedded with Sentence-BERT MiniLM-L6-v2, and clustered via balanced K-means into K=16 disjoint domains. At inference, a short document prefix (typically 32 tokens, ~1-2 sentences) is routed to the appropriate cluster model via nearest-centroid assignment on SBert embeddings. The scaling law component takes model size N, pretraining tokens D, and per-domain CPT tokens D_k as inputs and outputs a predicted validation loss, from which the optimal pretraining budget t_s is derived under a total compute constraint D + K·D' = D_T.

## Method

The approach trains a seed transformer on the full corpus for D tokens, then creates K independent copies and continues pretraining each on its respective cluster for D' tokens, with a router mapping new documents to the appropriate expert at inference. The key theoretical contribution is a scaling law L(N, D, D_k) = E_0 + E_Δ + A(D_k^{α1} + cD^{α2})^{-1} + B·N^{-κ}, where E_Δ is a sigmoid-gated irreducible loss term that captures the model's limited capacity to learn all domains simultaneously. This law satisfies three desiderata: different irreducible losses for infinite PT vs. infinite CPT, recovery of Chinchilla scaling when either D or D_k is zero, and convergence to the same irreducible loss as D_k→∞ regardless of D. The optimal splitting point t_s is found by solving the constrained minimization of average loss over all domains, and the paper shows that greedy splitting (splitting as soon as the marginal benefit of CPT exceeds PT) is suboptimal—the optimal t_s depends on the total training horizon and increases with budget.

## Results

The proposed scaling law achieves R² of 0.934–0.982 and MAE of 0.012–0.023 across three extrapolation scenarios (Table 1), accurately predicting loss for models up to 2.7B parameters. On zero-shot QA benchmarks (8 tasks averaged), split model training at the optimal allocation yields 1.5–2% improvement over full pretraining at 1.3B and 2.7B scales, with 2.7B split models outperforming a 6.7B fully pretrained model by 0.6% at equivalent compute. On Pile domain perplexity, split models achieve a 9.33% average relative improvement over a standalone pretrained model (e.g., FreeLaw: 4.85 vs. 5.76 PPL). At large compute budgets, standard pretraining requires up to 50× the data to match optimal split training loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Liew & Kato (2025) bootstrapped CPT scaling law, the proposed law achieves consistently higher R² across all three extrapolation scenarios: 0.934 vs. 0.910 (Scenario 1, larger model size), 0.982 vs. 0.946 (Scenario 2, higher D range), and 0.979 vs. 0.953 (Scenario 3, higher D and D_k range), indicating superior predictive power for unseen model sizes and token budgets. Against the D-CPT Law (Que et al., 2024), which recommends mixing 25–75% domain data during continued pretraining, the paper's no-mixture split training approach achieves 62.6% average QA accuracy versus 61.9% with the D-CPT mixture, showing that domain mixing is counterproductive in the split training setting. The most striking comparison is against full pretraining at larger model sizes: 2.7B split models (with only 2.7B active parameters per token) outperform a 6.7B fully pretrained model by 0.6% on average QA accuracy, while 1.3B split models match 2.7B pretrained models at the same total compute budget, effectively halving inference cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Chinchilla scaling law framework (Hoffmann et al., 2022) to a two-stage pretraining + continued pretraining setting by introducing an irreducible loss term E_Δ and a combined data term that captures the interaction between general and domain-specific tokens. It adopts the balanced K-means clustering methodology from Grangier et al. (2025) for domain discovery and the basin-hopping optimization procedure from Shukor et al. (2025) for fitting the scaling law. The key departure from prior CPT scaling laws (Que et al., 2024; Bethune et al., 2025) is the joint treatment of pretraining cost in the compute allocation, and from prior split-model training (Li et al., 2022; Gururangan et al., 2023) is the optimization of the split point rather than assuming a fixed or heuristic allocation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, scaling-law-based recipe for allocating compute between general pretraining and domain-specific continued pretraining, which is directly actionable for practitioners training multi-domain LLMs. The finding that a 2.7B split model can outperform a 6.7B single model on QA benchmarks at the same compute budget, combined with the 50× compute multiplier at large scales, suggests substantial efficiency gains for organizations training multiple specialized models. Key limitations include the K× storage overhead (40 GB for 16×1.3B models), evaluation primarily on zero-shot tasks with a single main dataset (DCLM), and the modest 1–2% QA improvements at the scales tested. Open questions include whether the scaling law generalizes to much larger model sizes (>10B), how it interacts with MoE routing at the parameter level, and whether the optimal split point shifts with data quality or repetition.
