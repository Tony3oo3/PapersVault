## Problem

Current time series forecasting models, from ARMA to transformer-based foundation models, treat TS as statistical pattern-matching problems and fundamentally fail to capture long-term dynamical properties, generalize across topological regime shifts (tipping points), or provide mechanistic understanding of the generating process. This position paper argues that the field lacks a dynamical systems (DS) perspective: since nearly all empirical TS originate from an underlying DS, learning a surrogate of that DS (dynamical systems reconstruction, DSR) should yield theoretically superior forecasts and enable capabilities—long-term statistics, out-of-distribution generalization, control—that pure TS models cannot provide.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266385/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The input is a (possibly partial, noisy, non-stationary) time series from an underlying DS, optionally augmented with external inputs or covariates; delay embedding (Takens) lifts lower-dimensional observations into a higher-dimensional space where the true vector field is recoverable. The output is not merely a point forecast but a trained surrogate dynamical model (a vector field or flow map) from which one can generate short-term predictions, long-term ergodic statistics (attractor geometry via D_stsp, power spectra via D_H, Lyapunov exponents, Kaplan-Yorke dimension), tipping point predictions under parameter drift, and interpretable mechanistic insight into the system's state-space topology. Evaluation requires long roll-outs (T ≥ 1000–10⁴ steps) with transients discarded, since most DSR-relevant measures are only defined in the ergodic limit.

## Method

The paper's core argument is that DSR training procedures—particularly Sparse Teacher Forcing (STF) and Generalized Teacher Forcing (GTF), which balance model-generated and data-inferred states to enable long roll-outs without gradient explosion—matter far more than architectural complexity. Empirically, the authors compare SOTA DSR models (AL-RNN, shPLRNN, reservoir computers, and the DynaMix foundation model) against SOTA TS models (AutoARIMA, PatchTST, NBEATS, Chronos, Chronos-2, Panda, TiRex) on both custom-trained and zero-shot settings, showing DSR models dominate on long-term geometric and temporal metrics (D_stsp, D_H) and are competitive or superior on short-term MASE despite having orders of magnitude fewer parameters (DynaMix has 0.01% of Chronos's parameters). The paper further argues that pre-training on simulated chaotic DS (as in DynaMix) provides a richer, more natural training corpus than artificially assembled TS, and that modern RNN variants (Mamba, xLSTM) are better suited than transformers for approximating dynamical flows. Five concrete action items are proposed: adopt DSR training techniques, pre-train FMs on DS simulations, shift back to RNN architectures, address topological OOD generalization via control-parameter inference and inductive biases, and focus on mechanistic understanding of TS generation.

## Results

Across custom-trained models (Table 1) and foundation models (Table 2), DSR models (shPLRNN/GTF, AL-RNN/STF, RC, DynaMix) consistently outperform TS models (AutoARIMA, PatchTST, NBEATS, Chronos, Chronos2, Panda, TiRex) on long-term geometric (D_stsp) and temporal (D_H) metrics evaluated on 10,000-step roll-outs, with TS models typically diverging or collapsing to fixed points/limit cycles. On short-term MASE, SOTA DSR models match or exceed SOTA TS models on at least some datasets (energy, weather, traffic, fMRI, EEG). DynaMix, the only DSR foundation model, achieves this with only 0.01% of Chronos's trainable parameters and ~100× faster inference, trained on a corpus of just 34 chaotic DS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against custom-trained TS baselines (AutoARIMA, PatchTST, NBEATS), DSR models (shPLRNN/GTF, AL-RNN/STF, RC) clearly win on long-term metrics (D_stsp, D_H) where TS models diverge or collapse to fixed points, and can also win on short-term MASE on some datasets (Fig. 5b, Fig. 28b). Against TS foundation models (Chronos, Chronos2, Panda, TiRex), DynaMix is the only model that correctly reproduces chaotic attractor geometries in the long-term limit; all TS FMs exhibit 'context parroting'—converging to nearly periodic activity that is topologically incorrect for chaotic systems. DynaMix also often outperforms TS FMs on short-term MASE despite having 0.01% of Chronos's parameters and ~100× faster inference. The AL-RNN, a simple 1-layer piecewise-linear RNN, matches or exceeds sophisticated TS architectures in short-term prediction while being far more parameter-efficient. On noisy chaotic systems, DSR models may even outperform TS models as noise increases (Fig. 28b), and DSR training techniques (GTF) improve short-term prediction even when applied to non-DSR architectures like Mamba (Fig. 30).

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper sits at the intersection of dynamical systems reconstruction (DSR) and time series forecasting (TSF), arguing that the TSF field should adopt core principles from DSR. It builds directly on the training techniques of Mikhaeil et al. (STF, NeurIPS 2022) and Hess et al. (GTF, ICML 2023), and on the DynaMix DSR foundation model (Hemmer & Durstewitz, NeurIPS 2025), positioning them as the methodological backbone for a proposed paradigm shift. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant transformer-based TSF paradigm (Chronos, PatchTST, TiRex) by arguing that architectural scale is less important than training methodology and that DS simulation data is a more natural training corpus than artificially assembled time series. The paper also draws on a broad DSR literature (AL-RNN, reservoir computing, Neural ODEs, SINDy, Koopman operators) to show that the field already has the tools needed, but that these have not been systematically integrated into mainstream TSF practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper makes a compelling case that long-term statistical properties (attractor geometry, Lyapunov exponents, power spectra) are often more practically relevant than short-term point forecasts, yet are systematically ignored by standard TSF evaluation (MSE, MAPE). It identifies tipping point prediction and topological out-of-distribution generalization as problems that are intractable for pure statistical TS models but potentially solvable with DSR, opening a concrete research agenda. Key open questions remain: the topological OOD problem is intractable in its most general form, scaling DSR to very high-dimensional and highly stochastic real-world systems is still underdeveloped, and the experimental evidence, while suggestive, covers a limited set of datasets and model comparisons. The practical recommendations (adopt GTF/STF, pre-train on DS simulations, revisit RNNs over transformers) are actionable and could reshape the field if adopted broadly.
