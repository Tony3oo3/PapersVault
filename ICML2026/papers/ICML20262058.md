## Problem

Recent tabular deep learning methods demonstrate strong empirical performance, yet the field lacks a clear mechanistic understanding of why specific design choices—numerical embeddings, retrieval-augmented heads, parameter-efficient ensembles, and prior-fitted networks—actually succeed. This paper proposes data (aleatoric) uncertainty as a unifying analytical lens, showing that these techniques are disproportionately more beneficial in high-uncertainty regions of the dataspace and dissecting the distinct mechanisms each employs to cope with noisy training signals.

## Input / Output

The analysis framework operates on standard tabular datasets (numerical and categorical features) for regression tasks, estimating per-sample data uncertainty via CatBoost's RMSEWithUncertainty loss or alternative backbones (MLP, TabPFNv2.5, TabICLv2), then sorting test points by uncertainty to produce per-sample MSE comparison curves across methods. The practical contribution (MLP-LRLR_triplet) takes tabular numerical features as input, passes them through a triplet-loss-pretrained embedding module followed by a standard MLP backbone, and outputs a regression prediction. Uncertainty estimates are validated against ground-truth values on synthetic datasets and cross-checked across multiple estimation models for consistency.

## Method

The paper's core analytical contribution is a sample-wise uncertainty decomposition showing that successful tabular DL techniques improve performance most in high data uncertainty niches, with distinct mechanisms for each: numerical PLR/LRLR embeddings increase local target consistency in the latent space (verified via k-NN target-difference metrics), simplifying the downstream function to learn; TabM's shared-parameter multi-branch architecture averages gradients across branches, reducing the noisy gradient component relative to the clean component more effectively than independent deep ensembles (demonstrated via explicit gradient decomposition on synthetic data); ModernNCA's kNN prediction head induces beneficial underfitting in high-uncertainty regions by averaging over many neighbors; and TabPFNs inherit robustness from pretraining on synthetic data with inherent aleatoric uncertainty. As a direct practical outcome, the paper introduces MLP-LRLR_triplet, a numerical embedding scheme where the LRLR embedder is pretrained with triplet loss to explicitly pull together embeddings of objects with similar targets and push apart those with dissimilar targets, then used to initialize the embedding layer of the full MLP-LRLR architecture, achieving a 1.43% average improvement over the MLP baseline versus 1.05% for standard LRLR.

## Results

The paper's novel triplet-loss-based numerical embedding (MLP-LRLR_triplet) achieves a 1.43% average MSE improvement over the MLP baseline and a 0.38% improvement over standard LRLR embeddings (1.05%), with an average rank of 1.06 (±0.24) across 15 regression datasets. The core empirical finding is that all analyzed tabular DL techniques (numerical embeddings, TabM, ModernNCA, TabPFNs) are disproportionately more beneficial in high data uncertainty regions: for example, on CIFAR-10 with 50% label noise, MLP-LRLR (48.20%) outperforms MLP (46.28%), and TabM (48.63%) outperforms Deep Ensemble (48.02%), whereas on clean CIFAR-10 the rankings reverse or tie. Gradient decomposition on synthetic data confirms that TabM's shared-parameter averaging reduces the noisy gradient component more than the clean component, explaining its robustness to label noise.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the plain MLP baseline, the proposed triplet-loss embedding (MLP-LRLR_triplet) wins on 1.43% median MSE improvement, while standard LRLR embeddings (Gorishniy et al., 2022) achieve 1.05%, making the triplet variant the best single-model method across all 15 regression datasets. Against XGBoost, the paper shows that MLP loses overall but the gap narrows substantially in high data uncertainty regions (e.g., Sberbank Housing, Maps Routing), suggesting the 'DL vs GBDT' battle is concentrated there. In ensembling comparisons, TabM outperforms a standard Deep Ensemble specifically under high data uncertainty (50.45% vs 49.41% on Uncertain CIFAR-10; 48.63% vs 48.02% with 50% label noise), while on clean CIFAR-10 they are statistically tied (61.01% vs 60.92%). ModernNCA and TabPFNv2.5 both outperform MLP more in high uncertainty niches, with ModernNCA's advantage driven by kNN-averaging-induced underfitting of noisy targets and TabPFN's advantage attributed to pretraining on synthetic data with inherent aleatoric uncertainty.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as an analytical bridge over the rapidly growing but mechanistically opaque tabular DL literature. Rather than proposing a single new architecture, it uses data (aleatoric) uncertainty as a unifying lens to explain why disparate techniques—numerical feature embeddings (Gorishniy et al., 2022), parameter-efficient ensembling via TabM (Gorishniy et al., 2025), retrieval-augmented ModernNCA (Ye et al., 2024), and TabPFN (Grinsztajn et al., 2025)—all disproportionately benefit in high-uncertainty regions. It departs from prior dataset-centric or technique-centric analyses (Grinsztajn et al., 2022; McElfresh et al., 2023; Rubachev et al., 2025) by operating at the individual-datapoint resolution, and it builds directly on the LRLR embedding architecture to propose a triplet-loss-pretrained variant that explicitly optimizes local target consistency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, sample-wise framework that could guide future design choices in tabular DL: if a technique's benefit is concentrated in high-uncertainty regions, practitioners can predict where it will help and where it may underperform. The triplet-loss embedding is a modest but concrete practical improvement (avg. rank 1.06 vs 1.50 for LRLR). Key open questions include whether the distinct noise-handling mechanisms (gradient averaging, neighborhood smoothing, in-context learning from uncertain synthetic data) can be effectively combined, whether the analysis extends to classification, and whether the observed relationships are truly causal or merely correlational—the authors explicitly acknowledge the empirical, non-causal nature of their evidence.
