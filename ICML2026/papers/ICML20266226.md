## Problem

CLIP-style vision-language models suffer from a modality gap where image and text embeddings occupy disjoint regions, causing same-modality neighbors to dominate nearest-neighbor rankings over true cross-modal matches. Existing mitigation methods enforce global alignment or post-process similarities but fail to address local neighborhood structure, and can distort ranking margins by compressing the geometry between positives and negatives. This paper asks whether shaping the multi-scale geometric structure of both modalities—rather than merely pulling centroids together—can align local neighborhoods and improve retrieval and zero-shot accuracy simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The method consumes frozen pretrained dual-encoder embeddings (CLIP, SigLIP, or OpenCLIP) for paired image-text training data, and learns two lightweight modality-specific geometry maps (g_θ^img, g_θ^txt) that reparameterize the metric used for all downstream computations. At inference, the same geometry maps are applied to the frozen embeddings before computing cosine similarities for retrieval ranking or zero-shot classification, so the output is a restructured embedding space with improved local cross-modal neighborhood consistency rather than a new encoder.

## Method

FSAlign (Fractal Spectral Alignment) operates in two stages on frozen backbone embeddings. First, it enforces a shared fractal multi-scale structure on both modalities by penalizing deviations from power-law ball-mass growth (Ahlfors d_f-regularity) across log-spaced radii and from power-law heat-trace scaling (spectral dimension d_s) across log-spaced diffusion scales, so both modalities share the same (d_f, d_s) scaling pair. Second, it constructs a fractional heat diffusion operator K_θ(s) = exp(-s(L_θ)^α) at each scale, reads off per-sample diffusion neighborhood distributions, aggregates them across scales into a truncated Mellin-type zeta descriptor, and minimizes the L2 distance between paired image-text zeta vectors. The total loss L_total = L_dbl + L_spec + L_match is optimized by gradient descent on the geometry maps only, with all backbone parameters frozen. Theoretically, under Ahlfors-regularity and sub-Gaussian heat kernel bounds, a small zeta-matching loss guarantees that paired samples share consistent spectral measures and fractional Dirichlet energies, which translates to higher cross-modal nearest-neighbor overlap (NAS) for most samples.

## Results

On MS-COCO with CLIP, FSAlign improves Image→Text Recall@1 from 40.4 to 51.26 and Text→Image Recall@1 from 27.32 to 31.20; on Flickr30k, Image→Text Recall@1 rises from 69.40 to 78.30 and Text→Image Recall@1 from 56.14 to 59.80. For zero-shot classification, FSAlign achieves 66.28% top-1 on CIFAR-100 (vs. 62.62% CLIP), 51.85% on Tiny ImageNet-200 (vs. 40.31%), and 59.23% on DTD (vs. 55.79%). FSAlign also attains the best neighborhood alignment score (NAS@100 = 0.4584) and cross-modal alignment score (CMAS = 0.6490) on MS-COCO, though its centroid distance (0.7729) is larger than methods that explicitly collapse modality means.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FSAlign outperforms all seven baselines on Recall@1 across both retrieval directions and both datasets. The closest competitor is OTCL (optimal-transport-based), which reaches 49.18 on MS-COCO I→T R@1 and 76.90 on Flickr30k I→T R@1, still trailing FSAlign by 2.1 and 1.4 points respectively. GR-CLIP (mean-centering) is the second-strongest baseline at 43.44 / 73.30 on the same metrics. On zero-shot classification, the gap widens dramatically: FSAlign's 51.85% top-1 on Tiny ImageNet-200 exceeds the best baseline (OTCL at 41.58%) by over 10 points, while MG and ALIGNCLIP degrade substantially below the CLIP backbone (34.68% and 27.97% respectively), indicating that aggressive global alignment can hurt local ranking. On modality-gap metrics, FSAlign achieves the highest NAS@100 (0.4584) and CMAS (0.6490), confirming superior local neighborhood consistency, whereas IOT and GR-CLIP achieve near-zero centroid distance but at the cost of lower NAS and CMAS, illustrating the paper's argument that shrinking the global gap alone does not guarantee better local retrieval.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the three established families of modality gap mitigation (training-time cross-modal constraints, post-hoc embedding standardization, and geometric structure analysis) by importing tools from fractal geometry and spectral theory—specifically Ahlfors-regularity, sub-Gaussian heat kernel bounds, and spectral dimension—to construct a multi-scale geometric objective. Rather than pulling paired embeddings closer at a single global scale (as IOT, GR-CLIP, or ALIGNCLIP do) or analyzing the gap through hyperbolic or ellipsoidal geometry (as ATMG or DEG do), FSAlign enforces that both modalities share the same fractal scaling pair (d_f, d_s) and then aligns paired samples via a zeta-function descriptor built from multi-scale heat diffusion. The method is applied as a lightweight trainable geometry layer on top of frozen CLIP/SigLIP/OpenCLIP backbones, positioning it as a post-hoc intervention that is more structured than simple mean-centering or standardization but less invasive than retraining the encoders.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper makes a useful conceptual argument that global gap metrics (CD, RMG) are poor proxies for retrieval quality, and demonstrates this empirically: FSAlign does not minimize CD (0.77 vs. IOT's 0.03) yet achieves the best retrieval and zero-shot accuracy, suggesting that local neighborhood consistency matters more than centroid coincidence. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 2) linking the zeta-matching loss to NAS improvement under Ahlfors-regularity and sub-Gaussian conditions is a clean contribution. However, the method's practical adoption may be limited by its complexity (multiple hyperparameters: d_f, d_s, α, q, radii set R, dilation set Λ, diffusion scales S, quadrature weights), and it remains to be seen whether the fractal constraint generalizes beyond the tested backbones or whether a simpler multi-scale regularizer could capture most of the benefit. Open questions include whether the approach extends to video-language or audio-language settings where the embedding geometry may not naturally admit Ahlfors-regular structure.
