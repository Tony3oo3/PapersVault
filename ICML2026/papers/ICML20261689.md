## Problem

Large-scale web-crawled datasets contain noise and bias that degrade model performance, but existing data selection methods either rely on hand-crafted heuristics with unwanted data dependencies or require expensive influence-function computations and clean validation sets. The paper addresses the gap of lacking a simple, dataset-agnostic, compute-efficient mechanism that can identify high-value samples without additional inference passes or curated validation splits.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261689/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes training samples (image-caption pairs or image-label pairs) and the last-layer weights of a pre-trained reference model (e.g., the final MLP layer of a CLIP encoder, a 3072×768 matrix in their CLIP setup). It produces per-sample mimic scores computed as a single projection of the negative gradient onto the reference direction vector, which are then used to generate softmax-weighted gradient updates (Stage 1) and, after binarization and weak-supervision aggregation across training steps, binary retain/discard decisions that yield a curated subset (Stage 2). Notably, only last-layer weights are needed—no full reference model inference or validation gradients.

## Method

Grad-Mimic introduces the Mimic Score, which measures the projection of each sample's negative gradient onto the vector v_t = θ_ref − θ_t pointing from the current model toward a pre-trained reference model, yielding m_{i,t} = ⟨−g_{i,t}, v_t⟩/‖v_t‖. In Stage 1 (online re-weighting), these scores are softmax-normalized with a temperature τ and used as per-sample weights in the gradient update, amplifying aligned samples and suppressing misaligned ones. In Stage 2 (offline selection), per-step scores are binarized via thresholding, 1D clustering, or top-k selection, then aggregated across training steps using Snorkel's weak-supervision LabelModel to produce a consensus filter. The key efficiency gain comes from operating only on last-layer weight differences (a simple matrix subtraction plus dot products over d_L ≪ d parameters), avoiding the extra forward passes of Rho-Loss or the validation gradient computations of influence-function methods. A theoretical analysis (Theorem A.4) establishes conditions on learning rate, gradient magnitude, and reference-model proximity under which Grad-Mimic converges faster than standard gradient descent.

## Results

On six image classification datasets with 40–60% injected label noise, Grad-Mimic achieves the highest average test accuracy at every noise level (75.80% at 40%, 74.53% at 50%, 73.01% at 60%), outperforming the strongest baseline Rho-Loss by 0.44–1.08 points. On DataComp-scale CLIP pretraining (10M+ and 100M+ image-caption pairs), Grad-Mimic improves average performance across 38 downstream tasks (0.146 vs. 0.131 on small; 0.258 vs. 0.254 on medium) while reducing training steps to convergence by 20.7%. The offline mimic-score filter removes 4.7M low-value samples from the medium-scale dataset while maintaining model quality (0.322 vs. 0.321), and aggregated scores correlate with dataset quality at Pearson r = 0.903 and with performance gains at r = 0.958.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Grad-Mimic wins against all seven Stage-1 baselines on average accuracy across the six noisy image datasets. Against Rho-Loss (the closest competitor, also reference-model-based), Grad-Mimic gains 0.44–1.08 points on average, with the largest margin on Flowers102 (e.g., 37.10 vs. 35.71 at 50% noise); the advantage stems from operating in weight space (a simple matrix subtraction) rather than requiring an extra forward pass through the reference model. Against influence-function approximations (GREATS, LESS, ICONS), Grad-Mimic achieves higher accuracy while avoiding the need for a curated validation set; on Flowers102 and CIFAR100, those methods required over 2,000 correctly labeled validation samples yet still fell short of Grad-Mimic. Grad-Norm (Paul et al., 2021) and AGRA (Sedova et al., 2023) underperform SGD on several datasets (e.g., Grad-Norm drops to 53.36% average at 50% noise vs. SGD's 69.46%), indicating that gradient-norm or cross-batch-similarity heuristics are unreliable under heavy label noise. Grad-Match (Killamsetty et al., 2021) tracks SGD closely, offering minimal benefit. In Stage 2 on DataComp, the mimic-score filter complements CLIP Score (Hessel et al., 2021): removing the bottom 30% by mimic score from the CLIP-Top-30% pool yields 0.322 average with 25.3M samples versus 0.321 with 30M samples, confirming that mimic scores identify low-value samples that CLIP score alone misses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gradient-based data selection lineage (GraNd, AGRA, GradMatch, influence-function approximations) by replacing gradient-norm or cross-batch-similarity signals with a single geometric quantity: the projection of each sample's negative gradient onto the vector from current weights to a pre-trained reference model's weights. It departs from reference-model-based methods like Rho-Loss, which operate in loss space and require additional forward passes, by working directly in weight space via a lightweight matrix subtraction. The offline aggregation stage repurposes weak-supervision machinery (Snorkel) from its original label-aggregation purpose to combine per-step binary utility decisions into a consensus filter, a novel application of that framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is strong: the method requires only publicly available pre-trained weights (exploiting the access asymmetry between released models and proprietary training data), avoids validation-set curation, and incurs 2.6× less overhead than competing model-based selectors. The 20.7% reduction in CLIP training steps and the ability to remove 4.7M samples while matching performance are meaningful for large-scale multimodal pretraining. Open questions include how the method behaves when no high-quality reference model exists for a new domain (acknowledged but only briefly explored), whether the last-layer-only approximation degrades for architectures where early layers carry more task-relevant geometry, and whether the theoretical convergence conditions (small η, small ε, small κ) hold in practice for very large-scale training.
