## Problem

Standard GRPO post-training for LLM reasoning relies on static uniformity: prompts are sampled uniformly and every prompt receives a fixed number of rollouts. For heterogeneous, heavy-tailed reasoning data, this over-optimizes the easy core while under-training the long tail of hard problems, wasting compute on already-solved patterns. The paper asks whether both the data distribution and the per-prompt compute budget can be adaptively reshaped during training to improve worst-bin robustness without increasing total sampling cost.

## Input / Output

The method consumes mathematical reasoning prompts (DAPO-14.1k, 14.1k English problems), a frozen base Qwen3-Base checkpoint (1.7B/4B/8B), and a verifiable binary reward r(x,y) ∈ {−1,+1} from a math verifier. It produces a trained reasoning policy, an adversarial weight distribution q_t over B online difficulty bins, and per-bin discrete rollout counts n_b drawn from arms {2,…,12} under a strict mean-budget constraint (n̄=4). Notably, the online difficulty signal (any-of-n pass rate with a sliding window of length H) is computed from the same rollouts used for GRPO updates, introducing zero additional sampling overhead; evaluation uses 8 completions per prompt (mean@8 and pass@8).

## Method

The framework casts GRPO post-training as two independent GDRO games over dynamic difficulty bins defined by an online train-time pass@n statistic. Prompt-GDRO (data adversary) maintains an EMA-debiased difficulty score per bin and applies EXP3P-style multiplicative-weights reweighting to scale GRPO advantages by bin-specific multipliers (capped at ω_max), concentrating gradient pressure on persistently hard groups without changing rollout counts; its theory shows the update optimizes an entropy-regularized log-sum-exp surrogate of the worst-bin loss with a no-regret game interpretation. Rollout-GDRO (compute adversary) assigns discrete rollout arms per bin via EXP3P over arms combined with a dual-ascent shadow-price controller that enforces the mean-rollout budget exactly; a variance-proxy analysis shows the optimal continuous allocation obeys a square-root (Neyman) rule proportional to √v_b, motivating the controller design. The two adversaries operate independently (no coupling), and the online binning uses a length-H sliding window of any-of-n correctness indicators so that groups evolve smoothly with the policy, producing an emergent curriculum that tracks the shifting reasoning frontier.

## Results

On the DAPO-14.1k training set with Qwen3-Base models, Prompt-GDRO improves pass@8 over GRPO by +9.74% (1.7B), +13.13% (4B), and +8.96% (8B), while Rollout-GDRO achieves +10.64%, +10.59%, and +9.20% respectively. The largest absolute gains appear on MATH-500 (e.g., 72.05→75.78 for 4B Prompt-GDRO) and AIME (e.g., 11.25→12.92 for 4B Prompt-GDRO), with both methods also improving GPQA and OlympiadBench. All improvements are achieved under a fixed mean train-time rollout budget (n̄=4), so gains reflect better allocation of the same compute rather than additional sampling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is standard GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) with uniform prompt sampling and a fixed rollout budget of n=4 per prompt. Both Prompt-GDRO and Rollout-GDRO consistently outperform GRPO on pass@8 across all three model scales, with Prompt-GDRO achieving the largest relative gain (+13.13% on Qwen3-4B) and Rollout-GDRO achieving the largest gain on the smallest model (+10.64% on Qwen3-1.7B). On individual benchmarks, Prompt-GDRO leads on MATH-500, AIME, and GPQA for the 4B model, while Rollout-GDRO leads on MATH-500, AIME, AMC, and OlympiadBench for the 8B model; the two methods are competitive and neither uniformly dominates the other across all benchmarks. A second-seed Qwen3-4B run confirms the Prompt-GDRO gap (63.47 vs. 56.36 pass@8) and shows Rollout-GDRO gains on four of five comparable benchmarks, ruling out a single-seed artifact. Static rollout baselines (hard-focused and easy-focused fixed schedules) both underperform the adaptive Rollout-GDRO controller (56.70 and 54.34 vs. 62.27 pass@8), indicating that online adaptation to the evolving difficulty landscape is essential rather than a fixed allocation rule.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants Group Distributionally Robust Optimization (GDRO, Sagawa et al. 2020) from static supervised-learning groups into the RL post-training loop for LLM reasoning, using the no-regret EXP3P algorithms of Soma et al. (2022) as the adversarial solver. The key departure is twofold: (i) groups are not pre-labeled but defined online by a train-time pass@n difficulty classifier that evolves with the policy, and (ii) the single GDRO adversary is split into two independent levers—prompt reweighting (data distribution) and rollout allocation (compute budget)—each with its own game and guarantee. The base training objective remains GRPO (Shao et al. 2024), and the paper positions itself alongside recent observations that RLVR gains concentrate on high-entropy tokens (Wang et al. 2025) and that test-time compute should be allocated non-uniformly (Madaan et al. 2025; Setlur et al. 2025), but moves the adaptation to training time rather than inference time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled, budget-neutral drop-in for any GRPO-based reasoning post-training pipeline: both adversaries preserve the mean rollout count while improving pass@8 by 9–13% across three Qwen3 scales, with second-seed confirmation at 4B. The theoretical contributions (entropy-regularized GDRO surrogate with no-regret guarantee; square-root/Neyman-optimal variance allocation) give practitioners a clear picture of what the controllers are implicitly optimizing. Open questions include whether the two adversaries can be jointly coupled without destabilizing training, how the method scales to larger compute budgets and model sizes, whether the online difficulty classifier generalizes beyond verifiable math rewards, and whether the emergent curriculum transfers to new prompt mixtures or non-mathematical reasoning domains.
