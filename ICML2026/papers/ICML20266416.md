## Problem

AI safety research has focused predominantly on capability risks while underappreciating economic threats such as government fiscal crises, Engel's Pause–style wage stagnation, gradual citizen disempowerment, and widening global inequality. Existing robot tax proposals (corporate tax hikes, automation levies, deduction disallowances) lack both technical enforceability and usage-based design, making them vulnerable to jurisdictional arbitrage. This position paper argues the ML community should prioritize studying token taxes—usage-based surcharges on model inference applied at the point of sale—as a concrete technical governance research agenda.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes model inference tokens, cloud provider compute logs (workload metadata, token-level usage data, and inference vs. pre-training classification), and model category/type identifiers. It produces a tax liability amount assessed at the point of sale—where tokens are billed to the end user in a given jurisdiction—along with an audit compliance verification. A key structural constraint is that the tax applies only to final consumption (not B2B intermediate use) and only to AI firms exceeding a revenue threshold, and the output is determined through a staged escalation rather than a single verification step.

## Method

The core contribution is a 3-stage audit pipeline that positions cloud compute providers as intermediaries between AI model providers and governments, leveraging existing compute governance infrastructure for enforcement. Stage 1 performs black-box token audits using cryptographic watermarking for token-count and model-type verification, estimation of hidden reasoning tokens from Q&A pairs, and TEE-based inference-time attestation. Stage 2 applies norm-based tax rates—empirically derived average token usage per model category and language—as a fallback when black-box verification is inconclusive, drawing on rentier-state norm tax mechanisms. Stage 3 escalates to white-box audits of the generative process as a legislative backstop. The paper further proposes agent-based modeling (ABM) to estimate cost pass-through and deadweight loss under different AI growth scenarios, and situates the token tax within the robot tax literature by demonstrating it uniquely combines usage-based design with high auditability while minimizing innovation risk relative to FLOP taxes and corporate tax alternatives.

## Results

This is a position paper without empirical experiments or quantitative benchmarks. The central qualitative finding is that the token tax is the only robot-tax variant that simultaneously scores 'Yes' on usage-based taxation, 'High' on auditability, and 'Low' on innovation risk (Table 1). The paper's formal contribution is a three-stage audit pipeline (black-box token verification → norm-based tax rates → white-box audits) and a five-item research roadmap (R1–R5) for technical governance. No model is trained, evaluated, or compared against baselines.

**Table 1: Comparison of forms of robot taxes across three metrics — usage-based, auditability, and innovation risk.**

| Tax type | Usage-based | Auditability | Innovation risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Corporation tax | No | High | High |
| Automation levy | Yes | Low | Medium |
| Deduction disallowance | Yes | Low | Medium |
| Payroll tax reform | No | High | Medium |
| Capital gains / wealth tax | No | Medium | Medium |
| FLOP tax | Yes | High | High |
| **Token tax** | **Yes** | **High** | **Low** |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not run experiments or compare trained models, so there are no quantitative baseline comparisons. Instead, it qualitatively positions the token tax against six alternative robot-tax instruments (corporate tax, automation levy, deduction disallowance, payroll tax reform, capital gains/wealth tax, and FLOP tax). The token tax is the only option that is both usage-based and highly auditable while carrying low innovation risk; the FLOP tax is the closest competitor (also usage-based and highly auditable) but scores 'High' on innovation risk because FLOPs are an unstable proxy for displaced economic value as models become more efficient. The paper also argues the token tax is preferable to a FLOP tax because token counts remain constant even when a model is optimized to use fewer FLOPs for the same output, making it a more stable measure of economic value displaced.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the robot tax literature (Abbott & Bogenschneider, Mazur, Cabrales et al.) by proposing a specific, technically-grounded implementation: a usage-based surcharge on model inference tokens enforced through existing compute governance infrastructure. It draws on Korinek & Lockwood's two-stage public finance framework for AI economies as its economic foundation, and repurposes compute governance mechanisms (Heim et al.'s cloud intermediary model, Sastry et al.'s GPU registry proposals) as the enforcement backbone for a tax instrument. The 3-stage audit pipeline (black-box watermarking, norm-based rates, white-box audits) synthesizes ideas from LLM watermarking (Kirchenbauer et al.), TEE-based attestation (Schnabl et al.), and Norwegian petroleum norm taxes into a unified technical governance proposal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a genuine gap: AI safety research has focused on capability risks while economic risks (fiscal crises, Engel's Pause recurrence, gradual disempowerment, global inequality) remain under-studied, and no prior robot tax proposal is both usage-based and technically auditable through existing infrastructure. Key open questions include whether black-box token verification can provide certifiable guarantees, the magnitude of deadweight loss and cost pass-through, and how to handle self-hosted models that bypass cloud-provider intermediaries. As a position paper with no empirical results, its impact depends on whether the proposed research agenda (R1–R5) gains traction; the geopolitical feasibility of implementation remains the most significant unresolved challenge.
