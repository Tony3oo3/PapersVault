## Problem

It is commonly assumed that calibrating each expert individually in a mixture-of-experts (MoE) model guarantees calibration of the aggregate predictor. This paper shows that while this holds for hard-routed MoEs under a broad class of routing-region reweightings, it fails for soft-routed MoEs: different routing configurations can collapse to the same aggregate confidence while having different outcome frequencies, so any distribution shift that changes the relative prevalence of these configurations breaks aggregate calibration even when every expert remains perfectly calibrated.

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10H, 224×224 for PACS) or text comments (128-token DistilBERT sequences for CivilComments) as input. A shared backbone extracts features that feed a K=4 expert MoE head with a 2-layer MLP router; the output is a weighted mixture of expert softmax distributions yielding calibrated class probabilities, from which a top-1 label is derived. The same architecture is trained end-to-end across all three dataset-backbone pairs, with the only difference being the backbone initialization (scratch for CIFAR, ImageNet-pretrained for PACS, English-pretrained for CivilComments).

## Method

The paper first proves that hard routing preserves aggregate calibration under any shift that keeps the label distribution fixed within each expert-confidence slice (a simple bottleneck condition), while soft routing does not: Proposition 4.1 shows that aggregate calibration survives arbitrary routing-configuration reweighting if and only if every configuration is individually calibrated at its prediction level, a far stronger condition. To train against the resulting fragility, the authors propose two entropy-balanced adversarial objectives. Robust MoE reweights the full minibatch by the exponential tilt q_i^η = exp(ηL_i)/Σ_j exp(ηL_j), which is the least-distorted reweighting (in KL) that raises expected proper loss to a target level, and trains on the tilted expected loss with gradients flowing through both the losses and the weights (yielding an extra covariance term beyond the entropic risk). Robust Filtered applies the same tilt only to a routing-relevant subset—examples where the mixture is worse than the best expert or where experts disagree substantially around the mixture—while retaining a uniform ERM anchor over the full minibatch to decouple broad accuracy from targeted robustness pressure. The approach is shown to be a conservative surrogate for KL-ball DRO and to upper-bound residual alignment with routing-weighted auditors in a multiaccuracy sense.

## Results

On CIFAR-10H, Robust MoE reduces hard-subset ECE from 0.281 (Vanilla MoE) to 0.074, and Robust Filtered achieves the best overall ECE of 0.013 with 91.0% accuracy. On CivilComments, Robust MoE cuts hard-subset ECE from 0.108 to 0.037 while slightly improving accuracy to 91.4%. On PACS, a robust method or robust composition attains the lowest ECE in every held-out target domain (e.g., Robust MoE Sketch ECE 0.033 vs. 0.217 for Single Expert). Gains persist after post-hoc temperature scaling, confirming they are not merely a global confidence rescaling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MoCaE (per-expert temperature calibration before mixing), the robust methods win decisively on hard-subset ECE: on CIFAR-10H, Robust MoE achieves 0.074 vs. MoCaE's 0.262, and on CivilComments, 0.037 vs. 0.101. MoCaE delivers only a modest full-distribution ECE improvement (e.g., 0.055→0.049 on CIFAR-10H) because it rescales each expert's probabilities without altering the mixture-level routing balance. Against FGR (frequency-aware gradient rectification), the robust methods again dominate on shifted subsets: on CivilComments hard ECE, Robust MoE reaches 0.037 vs. FGR's 0.065, and on CIFAR-10H hard ECE, FGR+Robust (0.065) outperforms FGR alone (0.262). On full-distribution ECE, Robust Filtered achieves the best value (0.013 on CIFAR-10H, 0.027 on CivilComments) at a small accuracy cost (≈1–2%), while FGR sometimes matches or slightly beats the robust methods on overall ECE (e.g., FGR 0.044 vs. Robust MoE 0.025 on CivilComments full ECE). The FGR+Robust composition shows that the two approaches are complementary: FGR's gradient projection and Robust MoE's entropy-balanced reweighting target different failure modes and can be stacked for further hard-subset gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts entropy balancing (Hainmueller, 2012) from its original causal-inference setting—where the constraint is externally specified covariate moments—to a self-referential constraint on the model's own proper loss, yielding an adversarial reweighting that stresses high-loss examples while staying close to the training distribution. The exponential-tilt mechanism and its connection to entropic risk follow Li et al. (2023), but the paper adds a covariance correction term (from differentiating through the tilt weights) that distinguishes the proposed objective from plain tilted ERM. Conceptually, the paper departs from the MoCaE line of work (Oksuz et al., 2024; Roschewitz et al., 2025), which calibrates individual experts before combining them, by showing that this per-expert guarantee is insufficient for soft-routed MoEs under routing-induced distribution shift, and instead targets the aggregate predictor's calibration through a distributionally robust training objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that individually calibrated experts do not guarantee a calibrated soft-routed mixture under distribution shift challenges a natural design assumption in MoE systems and motivates evaluating calibration at the aggregate level under meaningful routing or subpopulation shifts. The theoretical characterization (Proposition 4.1) cleanly separates hard routing (robust to region reweighting) from soft routing (fragile to configuration reweighting), giving practitioners a principled understanding of when MoE calibration can silently break. Open questions include whether the findings persist in large sparse or generative MoEs (the paper uses only K=4 experts), whether ECE is sufficient as a calibration metric, and whether the proxy routing-relevance criteria can be replaced by direct identification of failing configurations.
