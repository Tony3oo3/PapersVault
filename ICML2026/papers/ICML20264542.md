## Problem

Under the dominant pay-per-token pricing mechanism for LLM-as-a-service, providers have a financial incentive to misreport the tokenization of generated outputs (e.g., splitting one token into several) to charge users more, while users have no means to verify the true token count. No prior work had formally characterized this as a principal-agent problem, established the computational hardness of optimal misreporting under transparency constraints, or derived the necessary and sufficient conditions for an incentive-compatible pricing mechanism.

## Input / Output

The heuristic algorithm (Algorithm 1) takes a generated output token sequence t, the model's next-token probability distributions (needed for a forward-pass plausibility check under top-p or top-k sampling), and an iteration count m, and returns a longer token sequence t̃ that maps to the same string str(t) and is deemed plausible by the model. The mechanism-design component takes the existing per-token price r_o and the empirical average token-to-character ratio (tpc) and outputs a per-character price r_c = r_o · tpc that preserves the provider's average profit margin. The plausibility verification requires a full forward pass through the LLM, making c_π > 0, but this cost is shown to be constant (independent of sequence length) and dominated by the additional revenue from overcharging.

## Method

The paper models the user–provider interaction as a principal-agent problem where the agent (provider) can implement any reporting policy π over tokenizations of the same string. It proves (via reduction from Hamiltonian path) that finding the longest plausible tokenization under top-p sampling is NP-Hard, establishing a computational barrier to optimal misreporting. Nevertheless, it introduces a simple heuristic that iteratively splits the highest-vocabulary-index token into a pair of sub-tokens with the highest minimum index, then verifies plausibility via a model forward pass; experiments on Llama, Gemma, and Ministral models show this yields up to 11.2% overcharging at p=0.99 with temperature 1.3. The key theoretical result (Theorem 4.3) characterizes all additive incentive-compatible pricing mechanisms as those linear in per-character counts, implying pay-per-character is the unique such mechanism when all characters are priced equally. A transition prescription (r_c = r_o · tpc) allows providers to maintain their average margin, and multilingual experiments show this partially alleviates the higher token-to-character ratios that disadvantage minority-language users.

## Results

Under pay-per-token pricing, Algorithm 1 (a simple heuristic that iteratively splits high-vocabulary-index tokens) enables a transparent provider to overcharge users by up to 11.2% (Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, p=0.99), 1.8% (p=0.95), and 0.28% (p=0.90) of tokens, with the optimal number of iterations m decreasing as p decreases. The resulting utility gain surpasses 10.5% for p=0.99 regardless of the provider's profit margin, and reaches 1.7% and 0.4% for p=0.95 and p=0.90 respectively. The paper proves that finding the longest plausible tokenization under top-p sampling is NP-Hard (Theorem 3.2, via reduction from Hamiltonian path), and that any incentive-compatible additive pricing mechanism must price tokens linearly on their character count (Theorem 4.3), yielding a unique pay-per-character mechanism when all characters are priced equally.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing ML methods; its 'baselines' are the status-quo pricing mechanism (pay-per-token) and the truthful reporting policy π_0. Algorithm 1 (deterministic, vocabulary-index-guided splitting) is compared against Algorithm 2 (random token splitting, π_m^R): both increase reported token count, but Algorithm 1 produces tokenizations that remain plausible under top-p sampling far more often, making it a viable overcharging strategy while Algorithm 2 quickly becomes implausible (Figure 1b). The key financial comparison is between the cost of running Algorithm 1 (a single forward pass, c_v ≈ 12–34 J depending on model) and the additional revenue from misreporting: since c_v is constant while generation cost c_o·len(t) scales with output length, the verification cost is amortized and misreporting is profitable for a wide range of profit margins (Figure 3). On the pricing-mechanism side, the paper shows pay-per-token is provably not incentive-compatible (any multi-character token creates a misreporting incentive), while pay-per-character (r(t) = |str(t)|·r_c) is the unique incentive-compatible mechanism under equal per-character pricing, and a provider transitioning to it can preserve their average margin by setting r_c = r_o · tpc.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of tokenization research, mechanism design, and the economics of LLMs-as-a-service. While prior work has extensively studied BPE tokenization algorithms and their linguistic properties, and recent papers have examined provider unfaithfulness in adjacent settings (model substitution by Cai et al., 2025; injected reasoning tokens by Sun et al., 2025), none have analyzed the specific vulnerability arising from non-unique tokenization under pay-per-token pricing. The authors repurpose the principal-agent framework from contract theory to formalize the user-provider interaction, and their characterization of incentive-compatible pricing mechanisms (linear in character count) is, to their knowledge, the first such result in the LLM context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a systematic, provable vulnerability in the dominant pay-per-token pricing mechanism used by virtually all cloud LLM providers, with practical implications for a rapidly growing market. The clean prescription (pay-per-character with r_c = r_o · tpc) is actionable but introduces operational challenges—varying margins per output and potential margin erosion for underrepresented languages—that providers must navigate. Open questions include whether the results generalize to proprietary models, how broader competitive dynamics shift under alternative pricing, and whether statistical auditing methods can complement mechanism design as a defense. The paper also acknowledges that pay-per-character does not prevent providers from inflating output verbosity, leaving a residual incentive to over-generate.
