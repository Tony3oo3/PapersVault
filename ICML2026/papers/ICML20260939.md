## Problem

Prolonged RLVR training, especially on MoE architectures, frequently suffers from instability manifested as abnormal growth of the training-inference discrepancy, yet the underlying mechanism remains unexplained. Prior work has proposed empirical stabilization techniques (TIS, sequence-level clipping, routing replay) but lacks a principled, causal account of why the discrepancy grows over training rather than remaining a static infrastructural artifact. This paper asks: what in the optimization objective drives the model parameters toward regions that amplify the training-inference mismatch?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260939/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on the standard RLVR training pipeline: token-level importance weights ρ_{i,t} (ratio of training to inference token probabilities), the GRPO/GSPO surrogate objective with its clipping terms, and a per-step discrepancy metric defined as the standard deviation of ρ_{i,t} across all tokens. The output is a formal decomposition of the effective objective into the ideal policy gradient plus a covariance-based spurious signal ΔJ, together with a diagnostic criterion distinguishing bias-driven (destabilizing) from variance-driven (benign) token-level perturbations. The framework is validated on a 30B MoE model (Qwen3-30B-A3B) trained on DAPO-Math-17k with AIME24 validation, and extended to a 7B dense model (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B) on Skywork-OR1-RL-Data.

## Method

The paper introduces 'objective-level hacking' as a unified lens: any token-level credit misalignment—whether from the initial training-inference distribution mismatch, token-level clipping, or arbitrary token reweighting—injects a spurious covariance term ΔJ(θ) ≈ Σ Cov_train(X_{i,t}, ρ_{i,t}^{-1}) into the optimization objective, steering gradients toward unintended correlations between token weights and importance ratios. The key theoretical result is that this spurious signal is bias-driven (not variance-driven), proven by showing that unbiased Gaussian weight noise yields ΔJ ≈ 0 while biased distortion (e.g., upweighting low-probability tokens by δ=1.2) reproduces the full discrepancy-growth pathology. Empirically, the authors demonstrate on a 30B MoE model that (a) TIS correction reduces discrepancy growth by removing the off-policy bias, (b) stronger token-level clipping paradoxically accelerates discrepancy growth, and (c) actively injecting a 20% weight boost to low-probability tokens into a stable sequence-level clipping baseline is sufficient to trigger irreversible collapse. A positive feedback loop is identified: growing discrepancy amplifies survival bias for low-probability tokens, which increases weight distortion, which further grows the discrepancy—explaining the irreversibility of training collapse. The framework also explains why MoE models are more vulnerable than dense models: expert activation inconsistency creates a larger initial mismatch and a faster growth rate (1.4×10⁻² → 2.7×10⁻² vs. 1.06×10⁻² → 1.08×10⁻² over the same training horizon).

## Results

On a 30B MoE model (Qwen3-30B-A3B) trained with GRPO on DAPO-Math-17k, the training-inference discrepancy grows from 1.418×10⁻² to 2.664×10⁻² over training, while the same metric in a dense 7B model (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B) increases only from 1.059×10⁻² to 1.081×10⁻², indicating MoE models are far more susceptible. Applying truncated importance sampling (TIS) correction markedly reduces the discrepancy growth rate and slows token-entropy decline, with corresponding validation accuracy improvements on AIME24. Injecting a mere 20% weight increase on low-probability tokens (δ=1.2) is sufficient to trigger anomalous discrepancy growth, while unbiased variance-based noise (σ=0.2) does not, confirming that biased objective-level signals—not variance—are the primary driver of instability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to vanilla GRPO with token-level clipping (Shao et al., 2024), adding TIS correction (Yao et al., 2025) reduces the discrepancy growth rate, slows token-entropy collapse, and improves AIME24 validation scores, but does not fully eliminate the discrepancy in MoE training, indicating additional sources of instability. Compared to GSPO with sequence-level clipping (Zheng et al., 2025b), token-level clipping at stronger settings (right clip range 0.2 vs. 0.28) produces faster discrepancy growth, while sequence-level clipping shows no anomalous growth under the same training regime, providing indirect evidence that token-level credit misalignment is the culprit. The paper further shows that actively injecting token-level weight distortion into the GSPO objective (δ=3, 2, 1.2) reproduces the same anomalous discrepancy growth pattern seen with token-level clipping, whereas injecting unbiased Gaussian variance noise (σ=0.2) does not, establishing causality. In dense-model experiments, all three interventions (TIS, sequence-level clipping, injected distortion) produce qualitatively similar effects but with far smaller magnitude, explaining why MoE architectures are disproportionately affected.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GRPO/DAPO line of RLVR algorithms and the growing body of work identifying training-inference discrepancy as a symptom of MoE instability (Yao et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025a; Liu et al., 2025a), but departs from prior work by providing the first mechanistic, objective-level explanation for *why* the discrepancy grows rather than merely documenting that it does. It unifies previously treated-as-separate phenomena—initial numerical mismatch, token-level clipping, and injected weight distortion—under a single 'objective-level hacking' framework analogous to reward hacking, showing all manifest as biased perturbations to the optimization objective. The paper also extends the analysis beyond MoE to dense models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B) to show the framework is architecture-agnostic while the growth rate is MoE-specific.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The positive feedback loop between survival bias in low-probability tokens and objective-level hacking provides the first causal account for the irreversibility of MoE training collapse, explaining why switching checkpoints or data batches fails to recover. Practically, the finding that even a 20% weight distortion on low-probability tokens triggers collapse, and that token-level clipping (a standard stabilization tool) can *accelerate* discrepancy growth in MoE, directly informs algorithm design: sequence-level aggregation is not merely preferable but mechanistically necessary for MoE RLVR. Limitations include validation on a single 30B MoE architecture and one dense model, and the framework, while elegant, remains an approximation (covariance-based) that has not been stress-tested across diverse training regimes or model scales.
