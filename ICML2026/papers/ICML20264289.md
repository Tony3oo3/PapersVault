## Problem

Prior graph foundation models identify transferable structures as discrete substructures (motifs, graphons, structural vocabularies), but lack a principled understanding of which structural behaviors learned during pretraining actually transfer to unseen graphs. The paper asks whether common substructures are truly transferable and connects this question to the intrinsic geometry of the representation space, a direction that has received little attention.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264289/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

GAUGE takes a graph with node features (X ∈ R^{N×D}) and produces 512-dimensional node embeddings alongside 16-dimensional local coordinate matrices Q^{(i)} (orthogonal, d×r) that define the fiber at each node. A key additional output is the set of τ-invariant substructures recovered via a decoding algorithm, which quantifies transfer effort: regions with low predictive error require minimal adaptation. The Dirichlet loss value itself serves as a scalar measure of how much behavioral invariance the learned representations exhibit.

## Method

GAUGE constructs a neural vector bundle over the graph where each node is assigned a fiber (r-dimensional vector space) with local coordinates computed via multi-head attention residual vectors followed by QR decomposition. Edges carry pseudo parallel transport maps P^{(i,j)} = Q^{(i)ᵀ}Q^{(j)} that measure geometric compatibility between neighboring fibers. A gated flattening mechanism iteratively aligns compatible fibers by aggregating local coordinates weighted by the trace distance of their parallel transport, monotonically reducing the Dirichlet energy (Theorem 4.2). The training objective is a Dirichlet loss that predicts each node's local-coordinate representation from its neighbors' representations, which provably upper-bounds behavioral invariance (Theorem 4.1) and induces geometric flatness over invariant substructures (Theorem 4.4). This links transferability to intrinsic flatness: structures whose parallel transport is near-identity require no adaptation, providing a geometric criterion for what transfers.

## Results

On cross-domain few-shot node classification (Table 1), GAUGE achieves 61.26% (1-shot) and 71.63% (5-shot) on PubMed, 64.72% and 81.33% on Photo, outperforming all 16 baselines in nearly every setting. On zero-shot link prediction (Table 2), GAUGE reaches 93.88% AUC on Facebook and 93.04% AUC on Photo, setting new state-of-the-art. On graph isomorphism (Table 3), GAUGE achieves 92.56% accuracy on CSL (vs. 64.17% for the next-best SAMGPT) and 0.157 MAE on ZINC12K (vs. 0.163 for GIN), demonstrating superior structural understanding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GAUGE outperforms all 16 baselines on cross-domain few-shot transfer in 15 out of 16 settings, with the sole exception being 5-shot on Roman-empire where GFT edges ahead (26.60 vs. 26.43). The closest competitors are GraphAny (60.46/78.45 on Photo) and RiemannGFM (64.35/78.41 on Photo), but GAUGE leads by 4.26 and 2.92 points respectively in the 1-shot and 5-shot Photo settings. On zero-shot link prediction, GAUGE achieves the best AUC on 3 of 4 datasets (93.88 on Facebook, 66.22 on Roman-empire, 93.04 on Photo) and best AP on all 4, with the largest margin over RAGraph on Facebook (93.88 vs. 87.41 AUC). On graph isomorphism, GAUGE dominates: 92.56% on CSL vs. 64.17% for SAMGPT (the only other pretrainable baseline), 88.59% on MUTAG vs. 86.48% for GraphSAGE, and 0.157 MAE on ZINC12K vs. 0.163 for GIN. Conventional GNNs (GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT) and self-supervised methods (DGI, GRACE, GraphMAE) are substantially weaker, confirming that the pretrain-then-finetune paradigm with intrinsic geometry learning is critical for cross-domain generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior graph foundation models that identify transferable structures in the discrete realm (motifs, graphons, structural vocabularies) by reframing transferability through the lens of functional behavior and intrinsic geometry. It builds directly on Bamberger et al. (2025) for the Dirichlet energy formulation over vector bundles and on Berwick-Evans et al. (2023) for the discrete flatness-holonomy equivalence, but extends these into a trainable deep learning framework (Geometric Morphology Learning) that learns the geometry from data rather than imposing predefined Riemannian priors (hyperbolic, spherical, product spaces) as in prior Riemannian GNNs. The key conceptual departure is treating the representation space as an unknown manifold whose intrinsic geometry is discovered via neural vector bundles, rather than selecting a fixed geometric prior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled geometric characterization of which structural behaviors learned during pretraining transfer to unseen graphs, filling a theoretical gap that has limited the interpretability of graph foundation models. The Dirichlet loss as a 'transfer effort' measure and the τ-invariance criterion for recovering invariant substructures offer practical tools for understanding and controlling knowledge transfer. Open questions include scalability to very large graphs (the QR decomposition and multi-head attention per node add overhead), whether the theoretical guarantees (bounded embeddings, Lipschitz continuity) hold in practice at scale, and whether the vector bundle framework can be extended to dynamic or heterogeneous graphs.
