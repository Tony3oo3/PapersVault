## Problem

Most offline RL algorithms rely on explicit conservatism—penalizing out-of-dataset actions or capping rollout horizons—to control value overestimation. This paper questions whether conservatism is universally necessary, showing that a Bayesian formulation (optimizing expected return over a posterior of world models) can address epistemic uncertainty without such penalties, and that conservatism in particular harms performance on low-quality datasets where optimal actions are underrepresented.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a static offline dataset of trajectories (state-action-reward sequences from MuJoCo locomotion, Adroit manipulation, or AntMaze navigation) and produces a history-dependent recurrent policy that maps the full state-action-reward history h_t to an action distribution. Unlike standard Markov policies, the recurrent actor and critic each maintain a separate LRU-based encoder over the entire episode history (up to 1000 steps), enabling test-time adaptation as the agent's implicit belief about the true MDP updates intra-episode. Rollouts are initiated from arbitrary timesteps in the dataset (not just initial states) to maintain coverage of mid-episode states.

## Method

NEUBAY approximates the Bayesian posterior over world models with a deep ensemble (N=100) trained via standard one-step MLE, then performs adaptive long-horizon rollouts (64–512 steps) truncated only when epistemic uncertainty (ensemble disagreement) exceeds a dataset-quantile threshold (ζ=1.0), rather than using a fixed horizon cap. The key insight is that long horizons themselves suppress value overestimation: the bootstrapped Q-term in the H-step TD target is discounted by γ^H, shifting estimation toward lower-bias model-generated rewards and eliminating the need for explicit conservatism. To make long rollouts feasible, the world model uses layer normalization on hidden features (bounding per-step prediction magnitude and yielding a linear compounding-error bound), and the policy is a recurrent actor-critic with LRU-based memoroid encoders trained at a much smaller learning rate than the MLP heads to prevent history-representation divergence. The RL loss mixes real and imagined transitions with a tunable ratio κ, and a single ensemble member is fixed per rollout to respect the Bayesian objective.

## Results

On D4RL locomotion (12 datasets), NEUBAY achieves an average normalized score of 80.1, compared to 83.6 for the best conservative baseline (VIPO) and 79.3 for the best Bayesian-inspired baseline (CBOP). On NeoRL locomotion, NEUBAY scores 64.7 vs. 73.3 for VIPO. NEUBAY establishes new state-of-the-art on 7 of 33 datasets, with notable gains on walker-random-v2 (27.9→34.1), walker-medium-v2 (95.5→106.4), pen-cloned-v1 (74.1→91.3), and hammer-cloned-v1 (5.0→14.4). The method performs best on low-quality datasets (5 of 11 tasks among top methods) and medium-quality moderate-coverage datasets (5 of 7 tasks), while remaining competitive but weaker on narrow-coverage tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NEUBAY (avg 80.1) is competitive with the top conservative model-based baselines on D4RL locomotion: it trails VIPO (83.6) and SUMO (81.5) by 3.5 and 1.4 points respectively, but matches or exceeds MOBILE (80.0), MoMo (80.9), and ADMPO (81.0) on average. Against Bayesian-inspired baselines, NEUBAY clearly outperforms MAPLE (61.3) and MoDAP (74.5), and is comparable to CBOP (79.3), despite CBOP using explicit uncertainty penalties. On individual datasets, NEUBAY achieves SOTA on wk-random (34.1 vs. 27.9 for SUMO), hp-med-rep (110.6 vs. 109.9 for SUMO), wk-med-rep (99.3 vs. 98.4 for VIPO), wk-medium (106.4 vs. 95.5 for CBOP), and hp-med-exp (114.8 vs. 113.2 for VIPO). NEUBAY underperforms on hp-medium (54.2 vs. 110.8 for MoMo) and several AntMaze tasks, where narrow coverage and sparse rewards make world modeling particularly difficult. The paper notes that adding uncertainty penalties to NEUBAY helps some tasks (e.g., hp-random 24.5→48.2) but hurts low-quality datasets on average, confirming the Bayesian principle's advantage in those regimes.

## Relation to Prior Work

NEUBAY builds on the epistemic POMDP formulation for offline RL (Ghosh et al., 2022) and the MBPO-style model-based policy optimization framework (Janner et al., 2019), but departs fundamentally from the dominant conservatism principle that has governed offline RL since CQL and MOPO. Where prior Bayesian-inspired methods (MAPLE, CBOP, MoDAP) reintroduce uncertainty penalties and short horizons, NEUBAY removes explicit conservatism entirely and instead relies on long-horizon rollouts (64–512 steps) to suppress value overestimation, combined with a recurrent memoroid architecture (Morad et al., 2024) for test-time adaptation. The paper also draws on deep ensembles for posterior approximation and layer normalization for controlling compounding errors, but the core conceptual shift is from robust (worst-case) optimization to ambiguity-neutral (posterior-averaged) optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a near-universal assumption in offline RL—that explicit conservatism is necessary to prevent value overestimation—and provides a principled alternative with practical guidelines for when Bayesian vs. conservative methods are preferable (low-quality/moderate-coverage vs. narrow-coverage datasets). Key limitations include weaker performance on narrow-coverage tasks (AntMaze, some medium datasets), per-dataset hyperparameter tuning (encoder learning rate, real-data ratio), and reliance on a large ensemble (N=100). Open questions include whether better world models (multi-step, generative) can extend the approach to harder tasks, whether offline policy selection under the learned posterior can replace test-environment tuning, and whether the long-horizon insight generalizes beyond continuous control.
