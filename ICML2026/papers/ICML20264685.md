## Problem

As LLMs are increasingly deployed in strategic social scenarios and used as proxies for human behavior, it is critical to understand where and why their strategic behavior diverges from humans. Existing behavioral game theory models are manually designed around human-centric hypotheses and cannot capture the idiosyncratic, non-human behavioral priors of LLMs, while prior LLM game analyses focus on aggregate statistics or LLM capabilities in isolation without providing structural explanations for behavioral differences.

## Input / Output

The behavioral model receives at each round the ego agent's action, the opponent's action, the round reward, and an internal state vector, and outputs a probability distribution over the next move plus an updated state. The 'model' itself is not a fixed architecture but a discovered Python program whose structure (e.g., Q-table dimensionality, opponent-model matrix size) is searched over automatically. Data consists of 300-round IRPS games against 15 bots of varying complexity, with 129,087 human choices and 90,000 choices per LLM, all collected under matched conditions.

## Method

The paper uses AlphaEvolve, an evolutionary program-synthesis framework, to discover interpretable behavioral models directly from behavioral data via bilevel optimization: an outer loop uses an LLM (Gemini 2.5 Flash) to propose and mutate candidate Python programs over the program space Φ, while an inner loop fits each program's parameters θ via SGD to maximize cross-validated likelihood. A multi-objective fitness function jointly optimizes predictive accuracy and Halstead effort (program simplicity), yielding a Pareto frontier from which a 'simplest-but-best' (SBB) program is selected within a small ε of maximum likelihood. The discovered programs consistently implement value-based Q-learning and opponent modeling, with the critical structural difference being opponent-model dimensionality: humans and GPT OSS 120B use 1-dimensional frequency trackers, while Gemini 2.5 Flash/Pro use 3×3 conditional transition matrices and GPT 5.1 uses a 3×3×3 matrix, explaining frontier LLMs' superior win rates through more sophisticated opponent modeling.

## Results

AlphaEvolve-discovered behavioral models significantly outperform the CS-EWA baseline across all five datasets (all p<0.001, Wilcoxon signed-rank with Bonferroni correction; Z ≤ −7.148) in two-fold cross-validated normalized likelihood, while matching or exceeding the GRU-based RNN (improving over RNN for Gemini 2.5 Flash/Pro and GPT 5.1). In terms of strategic performance, frontier LLMs (Gemini 2.5 Flash/Pro, GPT 5.1) achieve higher aggregate win rates than humans against nonadaptive IRPS bots and converge to near-optimal play sooner. The key structural finding is that the simplest-but-best (SBB) programs reveal opponent-model dimensionality as the primary differentiator: humans and GPT OSS 120B use 3-dimensional opponent models, Gemini 2.5 Flash/Pro use 3×3 models, and GPT 5.1 uses a 3×3×3 model, explaining the performance gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Nash equilibrium baseline (uniform random play, likelihood = 1/3), all behavioral models—including AlphaEvolve, CS-EWA, and RNN—achieve substantially higher cross-validated normalized likelihood across all five datasets, confirming that both humans and LLMs deviate systematically from game-theoretic rationality. Against CS-EWA (the behavioral game theory baseline extended for repeated play), AlphaEvolve significantly outperforms on every dataset (all p<0.001, Z ≤ −7.148), indicating that human-designed BGT models fail to capture the full structure of both human and LLM strategic behavior. Against the GRU-based RNN (a highly flexible but black-box neural baseline), AlphaEvolve achieves comparable fit for the human and GPT OSS 120B datasets but improves over the RNN for Gemini 2.5 Flash/Pro and GPT 5.1, which the authors attribute to RNNs being more prone to overfitting while AlphaEvolve's cross-validated scoring and LLM-based code generation implicitly regularize toward generalizable solutions. In cross-generalization, the SBB programs for Gemini 2.5 Flash, Pro, and GPT 5.1 show high symmetric predictability (within 0.017 of best-fit), while the human program is a significantly poorer predictor of LLM behavior and vice versa (all p<0.001, Z ≤ −5.73), confirming structural behavioral divergence between humans and LLMs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the program-discovery framework of AlphaEvolve (Novikov et al., 2025) and the symbolic cognitive model discovery approach of Castro et al. (2025) from cognitive/neuroscience tasks to behavioral game theory, specifically iterated rock-paper-scissors. It departs from traditional BGT, which relies on hand-designed mathematical hypotheses (EWA, cognitive hierarchies, QRE) calibrated to human behavior, by using LLM-driven evolutionary search over an open-ended space of Python programs to discover interpretable models without pre-specifying the model family. The paper also positions itself against prior LLM strategic-behavior analyses (Fan et al., 2024; Akata et al., 2025) that report game-specific statistics in isolation, instead providing a unified structural comparison across agent types via discovered program semantics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that frontier LLMs maintain higher-dimensional opponent models (3×3 or 3×3×3) than humans (3-dimensional) provides a concrete, interpretable mechanism for why LLMs outperform humans in strategic settings, with direct implications for the validity of using LLMs as human behavioral proxies in social science research. The AlphaEvolve-based methodology offers a reusable, model-agnostic tool for monitoring LLM capabilities without relying on chain-of-thought traces, which the authors note do not always reflect actual behavior. Key limitations include the restriction to a single game (IRPS), modeling of average rather than individual human behavior, and the descriptive (non-causal) nature of the discovered programs; open questions include whether the identified mechanisms correspond to internal LLM computations and whether the approach generalizes to broader game classes.
