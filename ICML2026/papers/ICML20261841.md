## Problem

Causal ground truth is often unavailable in real-world settings, making it difficult to assess whether bivariate causal statements (from human experts or LLMs) are reliable. Existing evaluation methods either require ground truth or compare against a specific inferred multivariate graph, leaving no principled way to falsify a collection of pairwise causal claims based solely on their mutual consistency.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261841/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For the linear setting, the method takes a complete list of n(n-1)/2 bivariate causal statements (each specifying a direction and a linear coefficient) together with the covariance matrix of the n observed variables, and returns a single scalar compatibility score; a negative score provides evidence of internal inconsistency. For the graphical setting, it takes a complete list of bivariate ADMG statements (presence/absence of directed and bidirected edges per pair) and returns a scalar incompatibility score equal to the minimum number of edge edits needed to restore global graphical compatibility. In the linear case, all proposed directions must respect a common variable ordering (no hard constraints beyond acyclicity), while the graphical case implicitly assumes faithfulness.

## Method

For linear SEMs, any acyclic list of bivariate statements induces a unique multivariate SEM via Γ = I − A⁻¹ (where A encodes the bivariate coefficients). The core insight is a 'confounding postulate': a plausible multivariate model should exhibit no more unexplained covariance (confounding) than its pairwise marginals, since observed backdoor paths in the multivariate model become unobserved confounding in the bivariate marginals. The compatibility score sums, over all pairs, the difference between bivariate confounding (squared residual covariance after removing the direct causal effect) and multivariate confounding (squared residual after removing all observed backdoor paths via Wright's path tracing rules); a negative total falsifies the statements. For graphical statements, the incompatibility score is the minimum Hamming distance to a mixed graph satisfying three hard constraints (acyclicity, directed transitive closure, and confounding-path closure of bidirected edges), computed via a three-step heuristic that sequentially applies GreedyFAS for cycle removal, GreedyTE for transitivity editing, and GreedyCPC for confounding-path closure. Theoretically, the paper proves the expected compatibility score is strictly positive under mild unbiasedness and independence assumptions on random causal models, and provides finite-sample concentration bounds for empirical estimation.

## Results

In synthetic experiments, the fraction of bivariate causal statement lists with positive compatibility scores strictly decreases as Gaussian noise variance σ added to the true coefficients increases, confirming the score distinguishes correct from incorrect statements. For LLM-generated linear causal statements on the Gapminder dataset (7 socioeconomic variables), higher-capacity models (e.g., Claude Opus 4.5, Qwen3 235B) achieve systematically higher compatibility scores, while many smaller models receive negative scores (evidence of falsification) despite a random baseline scoring positively. For graphical statements, incompatibility scores increase monotonically with the number of injected errors in synthetic settings, and among 10 LLM outputs, only one achieves incomp = 0. With fewer than 100 samples, the sign of the empirical compatibility score matches the true score in over 90% of test cases, well below the worst-case bound of Theorem 2.10.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing causal-statement evaluation methods; instead, it compares LLM outputs against each other and against a random baseline (causal coefficients drawn from a centered normal distribution with matching variance). In the linear setting (Figure 4), the random baseline achieves a positive compatibility score, yet several LLMs still score negatively, providing evidence of internal inconsistency in their generated statements. Higher-capacity models (Claude Opus 4.5, Qwen3 235B A22B) consistently outperform smaller models (Gemma 3 4B, Magistral Small) on the compatibility score. In the graphical setting (Figures 6–7), only one LLM output achieves zero incompatibility, and after filtering to statement graphs with edge density ≤ 2/3 (to exclude uninformative dense graphs), low incompatibility scores again correlate with higher model capacity. The synthetic experiments serve as the primary validation: the compatibility score's ability to separate true from perturbed statements, and the incompatibility score's monotonic increase with injected errors, establish the methods' soundness without requiring a direct head-to-head comparison with prior approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends two distinct lines of work: (1) the graphical compatibility framework of Faller et al. (2024), which compared collections of ADMGs to a specific inferred multivariate graph, is generalized here to optimize over all compatible multivariate graphs, yielding an incompatibility score that does not require access to a ground-truth or algorithm-inferred multivariate model; (2) the bivariate confounding score of Janzing & Schölkopf (2018) is extended from the pairwise setting to a multivariate confounding measure via Wright's path tracing rules, enabling a plausibility-based compatibility score for linear bivariate statements that operates without the faithfulness assumption. The paper also positions itself against prior causal graph evaluation methods (Textor et al., 2016; Eulig et al., 2025) that require a full estimated graph, by working exclusively from bivariate statements.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic framework for falsifying collections of bivariate causal claims—whether from human experts or LLMs—in the absence of causal ground truth, which is the dominant real-world scenario. The confounding postulate (Assumption 2.4) offers a faithfulness-free plausibility criterion with a clean theoretical guarantee (Theorem 2.9), and the empirical demonstration that LLM compatibility scores correlate with model capacity suggests a practical validation pipeline for AI-generated causal knowledge. Key open questions include extending the framework to incomplete statement lists, non-linear models, and using the scores actively for causal inference (e.g., filling in missing statements by maximizing compatibility). A notable limitation is that a positive score is inconclusive—only negative scores provide falsification evidence—so the method is asymmetric in its diagnostic power.
