## Problem

Prior-data fitted networks (PFNs) have demonstrated strong empirical performance at approximating posterior predictive distributions (PPDs) via in-context learning, yet the theoretical understanding of how transformers algorithmically learn full distributions (as opposed to point predictions) in context remains lacking. In particular, the roles of attention depth, output discretization, and normalization in governing PPD approximation quality and generalization beyond the pretraining sample size range are unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262033/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The transformer receives a token matrix where each of the n context tokens encodes a (x_i, y_i) pair and the final token encodes the query x_{n+1} with a masked label. The output is a vector of C logits whose softmax yields a piecewise-constant density over a fixed partition of a truncated interval (a,b], with tail mass ε outside the interval. Internally, the attention stack computes the PPD mean μ and variance τ as intermediate quantities, which a shallow MLP head then maps to binned probabilities via softmax over sufficient-statistic evaluations at bin midpoints.

## Method

The paper provides an explicit transformer construction in which L self-attention layers implement a Richardson iteration solving (G + σ²I_n)u = Gv in parallel for v = Y (predictive mean) and v = k_x (variance reduction term), inheriting an exponential convergence rate exp(−(1−ρ)L) in depth. A shallow MLP head with a non-polynomial activation maps the computed moments to natural parameters of a one-dimensional exponential family, and softmax over bin-midpoint sufficient statistics yields the binned PPD, with total variation error bounded by exp(−(1−ρ)L) + δ_MLP + 1/C + ε_tail. A key architectural finding is that normalizing attention weights by their row sums implements Jacobi (diagonal) preconditioning, which keeps the admissible step size independent of n and is essential for n-generalization; without it, the step size must scale as 1/n and the solver diverges outside the pretraining range. The paper further shows that even with preconditioning, the condition number of the iteration matrix grows as Θ(n) for RBF kernels, requiring depth L ≳ n·log(1/ε) to maintain fixed accuracy, explaining why deeper architectures generalize to larger context sizes.

## Results

The paper establishes that a transformer with L attention layers approximates the GP posterior predictive distribution with total variation error bounded by exp(−(1−ρ)L) + δ_MLP + 1/C + ε_tail (Theorem 4.1), where ρ is the Richardson convergence factor, C is the bin count, and ε_tail is the truncation mass. For n-generalization, the required depth scales as L ≳ n·log(1/ε) for RBF kernels (Theorem 5.3), and the condition number of the preconditioned system grows as Θ(n) (Theorem 5.2). Empirically, log TV distance decreases approximately linearly with log depth and log bin count (Figure 2), and normalized attention maintains stable TV error across evaluation sample sizes n′ ∈ {40,…,200} while unnormalized attention's error spikes sharply outside the pretraining range [64,128] (Figure 4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without external method baselines; the key comparisons are internal architectural ablations. Unnormalized attention (TF_{θ,L}) fails to generalize beyond the pretraining sample size range: TV error spikes for n′ < 64 (under-convergence) and n′ > 128 (divergence), while normalized attention (TF_{θ,L}^{pr}, implementing Jacobi preconditioning) remains stable across the full evaluation range (Figure 4). Increasing depth from L=4 to L=32 and pretraining range from n_max=128 to n_max=512 both improve prediction MSE, 90% interval coverage, and interval width at larger evaluation sample sizes (Figure 5), confirming that performance is primarily iteration-limited. On the Sacramento home price dataset (Figure 1), the PFN's PPD mean, 5% quantile, and 95% quantile closely track those of an exact empirical-Bayes GP, except in regions with no nearby observations. The paper does not benchmark against other PFN implementations (e.g., TabPFNv2, TabPFN-3) or non-ICL Bayesian methods, so no head-to-head quantitative comparison with competing approaches is provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ICL-as-optimization line (Akyürek et al. 2023; Von Oswald et al. 2023; Ahn et al. 2023), which established that transformer attention can implement gradient descent for point prediction, to the distributional setting of posterior predictive distribution (PPD) approximation. It builds directly on the PFN framework of Müller et al. (2022) and the GP regression machinery of Rasmussen & Williams (2005), providing the first constructive proof that a transformer's attention stack can simultaneously solve for both the predictive mean and variance via a Richardson iteration, with a shallow MLP head mapping those moments to binned probabilities. The key conceptual departure from prior ICL theory is the explicit identification of attention normalization as Jacobi (diagonal) preconditioning, which explains the empirically observed n-generalization of models like TabPFNv2.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical decomposition of PPD approximation error into attention-solver error (exponential in depth L), MLP approximation error, bin discretization error (1/C), and tail truncation, giving practitioners a principled basis for architectural choices in PFN design. The finding that depth must scale essentially linearly with n for RBF kernels (Theorem 5.3) and that normalization is necessary for stability outside the pretraining range offers concrete guidance for scaling PFNs to larger contexts. Key limitations are that the results are constructive existence proofs rather than optimization guarantees for standard PFN pretraining, the analysis is restricted to GP regression with closed-form PPDs, and it remains open whether trained transformers actually discover these solver-like mechanisms or merely approximate them. Extensions to hierarchical GPs, latent GP models for non-Gaussian data, and optimization-theoretic guarantees for PFN training are identified as important future directions.
