## Problem

ML papers routinely report GPU models, training duration, and hyperparameters yet omit energy consumption and carbon emissions entirely, leaving efficiency claims incomplete and cross-paper comparisons uninterpretable since identical experiments in different grid regions can differ by 25–30× in carbon output. The paper identifies an awareness-action gap: despite available tools and high-profile disclosures (BLOOM, Llama), only 9.0% of ICML 2025 papers report energy and 5.9% report carbon, because venues provide no template, no enforcement, and researchers fear criticism for imprecise estimates.

## Input / Output

The empirical component surveys 1,000 randomly sampled ICML 2025 accepted papers (from 3,260 total) using automated keyword matching with manual verification across three categories—compute metadata, energy data, and carbon data—benchmarking against the NeurIPS 2019 baseline of Henderson et al. The output is a standardized reporting framework comprising five metrics, a measurement protocol, three progressive-disclosure templates (Stage 1: GPU-hours and hardware; Stage 2: +measured energy with uncertainty; Stage 3: full carbon accounting for large-scale experiments), and a phased three-stage adoption timeline for venues, reviewers, and institutions.

## Method

The framework specifies five standardized metrics aligned with the GHG Protocol and Software Carbon Intensity specification: total energy (kWh), carbon emissions (kgCO₂eq), grid carbon intensity (gCO₂/kWh), PUE, and compute region, with water footprint as a supplementary metric for large-scale training. A measurement protocol mandates reporting average power draw rather than peak/TDP, total energy across all averaged runs with hyperparameter search reported separately, and multi-location breakdowns with location-specific intensities to enable verification. The adoption plan is deliberately gradual across three stages—optional hardware/location fields (Stage 1), measured energy with acknowledged uncertainty bounds (Stage 2), and full five-metric reporting for experiments exceeding 1,000 GPU-hours or 10,000 kWh (Stage 3)—each stage building the data and infrastructure that make the next realistic. Equity is designed in through dual energy+carbon reporting, reference-grid normalization to a fixed 475 gCO₂/kWh baseline for fair cross-institution comparison, and explicit acceptance of approximate estimates for resource-limited researchers. The paper further addresses gaming risks (selective run inclusion, REC manipulation) and double-blind privacy constraints via tiered visibility and reference-grid normalization during review.

## Results

The ICML 2025 survey of 1,000 accepted papers finds that 57.3% report compute metadata (up from 17% at NeurIPS 2019), but only 9.0% report energy data and 5.9% report carbon data (vs. 1% and 0% in 2019), confirming a persistent awareness-action gap. A worked example shows that identical 4×V100/24h experiments produce ~0.8 kgCO₂ in Norway (29 gCO₂/kWh, PUE 1.1) versus ~22 kgCO₂ in Australia (650 gCO₂/kWh, PUE 1.4) — a ~28× difference invisible without location data. Disclosed training emissions range from 25 tons (BLOOM, French nuclear grid) to 8,930 tons (Llama-3.1 405B), and at deployment scale a model serving 10M queries/day consumes ~10,950 MWh/year, potentially exceeding training emissions within months.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper without a proposed method benchmarked against competing baselines; the 'comparisons' are diagnostic rather than competitive. The central empirical comparison is temporal: ICML 2025 reporting rates (57.3% compute, 9.0% energy, 5.9% carbon) versus the NeurIPS 2019 baseline (17%, 1%, 0%), showing that compute metadata has grown substantially while energy and carbon reporting remain in single digits — the paper's core 'awareness-action gap.' A second comparison is spatial: the paper contrasts grid carbon intensities across 13 regions (20 gCO₂/kWh in Quebec to 700 gCO₂/kWh in India) to demonstrate that identical computations yield up to 35× different carbon footprints, making location a necessary reporting variable. The paper also contrasts FLOPs-based efficiency claims against energy-based ones, citing Gowda et al. (2023) and Desislavov et al. (2023) to show that reducing FLOPs does not reliably reduce energy, undermining the common proxy of 'efficiency' in ML papers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes the Green AI literature (Strubell et al., 2019; Schwartz et al., 2020), existing measurement tools (CodeCarbon, Carbontracker, ML CO2 Impact), and carbon accounting standards from other domains (GHG Protocol, Software Carbon Intensity) into a concrete, phased reporting framework for ML venues. It departs from prior work primarily by moving beyond awareness-raising and ad-hoc disclosures (BLOOM, Llama) to propose standardized metrics, reporting templates, and a three-stage adoption timeline modeled on the successful normalization of code release and the NeurIPS reproducibility checklist. The ICML 2025 survey (5.9% carbon reporting) extends Henderson et al.'s 2019 NeurIPS baseline, quantifying the persistence of the awareness-action gap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central practical contribution is reducing the friction of carbon reporting through concrete templates, a minimal viable measurement protocol, and equity safeguards (dual energy/carbon reporting, reference-grid normalization, acceptance of approximate estimates) that prevent the norm from disadvantaging under-resourced researchers. Key open questions include whether the phased timeline will actually close the incentive gap the paper candidly identifies (no reciprocal citation benefit for reporting kWh, unlike code release), whether industry will follow academic norms, and whether the proposed thresholds (1000 GPU-hours, 10,000 kWh) for full reporting are well-calibrated. The paper's strongest limitation is its reliance on analogy to prior norm adoptions rather than empirical evidence that carbon reporting will drive behavioral change in ML.
