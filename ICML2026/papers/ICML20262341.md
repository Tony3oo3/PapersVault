## Problem

Policies learned via continuous actor-critic methods (TD3, SAC) exhibit high-frequency oscillations that are unsuitable for physical deployment. Existing remedies regularize the policy network's output directly, but the paper argues this treats the symptom: the root cause is geometric instability in the critic's Q-function, whose mixed-partial derivatives and action curvature govern how sensitively the optimal action shifts with state. The paper proves via implicit differentiation that policy Lipschitz sensitivity is bounded by the ratio of the Q-function's mixed-partial norm (noise sensitivity) to its action-space curvature (signal distinctness), and that reducing one without the other can paradoxically worsen instability.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a continuous state observation s ∈ S and produces a continuous action vector a ∈ A, identical in form to standard actor-critic control. The critical distinction is that all regularization is applied to the critic Q_θ(s,a) — treated as a scalar field over the state-action manifold — while the actor π_φ(s) is updated via unmodified standard policy gradients. The critic must use SiLU (C²-continuous) activations to enable valid Hessian computation via Hutchinson's trace estimator, and the finite-difference proxies for MPR and VFC require only two forward passes per update rather than explicit O(d²) Hessian construction.

## Method

PAVE (Policy-Aware Value-field Equalization) stabilizes the Q-gradient field through three synergistic critic-side losses added to the standard TD objective. (1) Mixed-Partial Regularization (MPR) minimizes the spectral norm of ∇²_sa Q by penalizing the change in ∇_a Q under isotropic Gaussian state perturbations, a finite-difference proxy for the Frobenius norm of the mixed Hessian. (2) Vector Field Consistency (VFC) aligns ∇_a Q across consecutive trajectory states (s_t, s_{t+1}), adapting score-matching / Fisher-divergence to enforce temporal coherence of the learning signal along dynamics-relevant directions. (3) Curvature Preservation (Curv) penalizes the trace of ∇²_aa Q (via Hutchinson's Rademacher estimator) when it fails to maintain a minimum concavity margin δ, preventing the Q-landscape from flattening and the inverse Hessian from exploding. The theoretical anchor is Lemma 4.1 (Implicit Policy Jacobian) and Proposition 4.3 (Lipschitz bound L ≤ M/μ), which together show that PAVE must simultaneously suppress M and preserve μ — a requirement validated empirically by the ablation showing MPR+VFC without Curv actually worsens smoothness.

## Results

Under TD3 (Table 1), PAVE achieves the best smoothness score across all six Gymnasium environments, with the most dramatic reductions on Hopper (2.715→0.950, 2.9×) and Pendulum (1.590→0.351, 4.5×), while maintaining or improving Base returns in 5/6 environments. On Walker, PAVE simultaneously secures the highest cumulative return (5563 vs. Base 4834) and the best smoothness (1.272 vs. Base 1.990). Under SAC (Table 2), PAVE achieves the best smoothness on LunarLander (0.142) and Pendulum (0.290), and on higher-dimensional locomotion (Walker, Hopper) it delivers substantial smoothness gains while preserving Base returns, whereas ASAP achieves lower smoothness at a significant return cost (Walker re 4731 vs. Base 4907).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TD3 Base, PAVE wins on smoothness in all six environments (e.g., Hopper 0.950 vs. 2.715; Pendulum 0.351 vs. 1.590) and on return in 5/6 (Walker 5563 vs. 4834; LunarLander 264.5 vs. 227.8). Against the three policy-side regularizers (CAPS, GRAD, ASAP), PAVE achieves strictly lower smoothness in all six TD3 environments and higher return in 5/6; notably, ASAP degrades return substantially (LunarLander 168.4 vs. Base 227.8) while PAVE improves it. Against critic-side baselines (Appendix J), SN-Critic collapses entirely (return −970 on LunarLander) and GP-Critic never beats Base smoothness on LunarLander, whereas PAVE simultaneously reduces M_sup (990→151 on Lunar) and raises strict-concavity rate (0.35→0.84). Under SAC, PAVE matches or beats policy-side methods on smoothness for LunarLander and Pendulum, but GRAD and CAPS edge out PAVE by small margins on the 2-D Reacher action space; on Walker and Hopper, PAVE preserves Base returns while ASAP sacrifices 3–12% return for comparable smoothness. A combination experiment (Appendix M) shows CAPS+PAVE performs identically to PAVE alone (sm 0.543 vs. 0.541), supporting critic-side sufficiency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of actor-side smoothness regularization (CAPS, L2C2, Grad-CAPS, ASAP, LipsNet) by arguing that policy oscillations are a symptom of critic geometric instability rather than a policy network pathology. Theoretically, it builds on the Implicit Function Theorem to derive a Lipschitz bound L ≤ M/μ on the greedy policy, connecting the mixed-partial Hessian (noise sensitivity) and action Hessian (signal curvature) of the Q-function to policy sensitivity. Methodologically, it repurposes score matching (Hyvärinen, 2005) for temporal vector-field consistency and Hutchinson's trace estimator for curvature preservation, but applies these to the critic rather than the actor. The key conceptual departure is that all prior smoothness methods constrain the policy's output or architecture, while PAVE regularizes the learning signal the policy follows, leaving the actor entirely unmodified.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical bound L ≤ M/μ provides a principled diagnostic for why policies oscillate and explains why naively smoothing the Q-function (reducing M) can backfire if curvature μ vanishes—a failure mode confirmed empirically in the factorial ablation where MPR+VFC without Curv worsens smoothness beyond Base. The finding that policy-side regularizers (CAPS, GRAD, ASAP) leave the critic's negative-definiteness rate nearly unchanged (Table 15) while PAVE raises it from 14–47% to 64–100% validates the critic-centric hypothesis and suggests a cleaner path to smooth deployment without architectural modifications. Open questions include whether the proxy losses can be tightened into guarantees, whether the approach extends to model-based or hierarchical RL where the critic structure differs, and whether the SiLU requirement for C² continuity limits applicability to architectures where second-order derivatives are ill-defined.
