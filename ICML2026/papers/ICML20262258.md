## Problem

Safe RL algorithms enforce safety constraints during training via Lagrangian or trust-region methods, but their guarantees are distribution-dependent and do not transfer when deployment dynamics differ from training. This paper identifies and quantifies a 'safety generalization gap' in diabetes management: policies satisfying constraints on training patients consistently violate safety margins on unseen patients with different insulin sensitivities and metabolic rates, across eight safe RL algorithms, three diabetes types, and three age groups.

## Input / Output

The shielded policy receives a 14-dimensional observation vector at each 5-minute control step (CGM glucose, bolus IOB, COB, CGM trend, circadian sin/cos encodings, normalized time since last meal/bolus, daily intervention counts, scheduled-meal look-ahead, and a pre-bolus flag) and outputs a modified action distribution over discrete bolus and meal levels. BA-NODE additionally consumes a window of past multivariate observations (glucose, insulin, carbohydrates) to produce a P-step glucose trajectory forecast for each candidate action. The shield applies finite logit bonuses/penalties to the policy's action logits, preserving nonzero probability mass over safe alternatives rather than hard-argmaxing.

## Method

The core contribution is a test-time predictive shielding mechanism built on a Basis-Adaptive Neural ODE (BA-NODE) dynamics predictor. BA-NODE encodes physiological history via an ITransformer (each variate as a token), evolves a latent state through an ensemble of K neural ODE vector fields integrated with RK4, and adapts to individual patients by computing mixing weights over K basis trajectories via regularized least squares on context windows. At test time, the shield intercepts the pre-trained policy's top-k bolus candidates, queries BA-NODE to forecast glucose over a finite horizon H for each candidate, and penalizes actions whose predicted trajectory crosses conservative safety thresholds (80 mg/dL hypo, 180 mg/dL hyper) via logit masking. A three-tier decision logic (critical rescue below 60 mg/dL, predictive verification above 80 mg/dL, pass-through otherwise) with a gating zone prevents over-reaction near boundaries. The paper proves a one-sided (ε,α)-reliability guarantee: if the predictor's overestimation error is bounded by ε with probability 1−α, then shield-permitted actions avoid clinical hypoglycemia with the same confidence.

## Results

On the T2D cohort, predictive shielding delivers the largest gains: PPO-Lag improves TIR by +14.15% (to 92.95%) and CPO by +13.54% (to 95.05%), while also reducing Risk Index by −2.38 and −2.82 respectively. The BA-NODE dynamics predictor achieves the lowest prediction error across all metrics (MAE 2.82, FDE 3.63, RMSE 4.42 over a 120-min horizon), outperforming NODE (MAE 3.18) and ITransformer (MAE 4.11). The safety generalization gap is substantial: unshielded policies lose 8–13 percentage points of TIR when deployed on unseen patients (e.g., CPO drops from 87.28% to 76.73%), confirming that training-time constraint satisfaction does not transfer under physiological distribution shift.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the eight safe RL baselines (CPO, CUP, FOCOPS, CRPO, PCPO, PPO-Lag, RCPO, TRPO-Lag), predictive shielding improves TIR and Risk Index in 7 of 8 algorithms on T2D (the exception being CUP, which shows only +4.22% TIR and a slight CV increase). The strongest gains appear for PPO-Lag (+14.15% TIR) and CPO (+13.54% TIR), while weaker baselines like PCPO (TIR ~43%) see only modest improvements (+2.00%), confirming the shield is bounded by the base policy's probability mass. Against the Rule-Based Shield (RBS, mimicking Low Glucose Suspend), the predictive shield outperforms in most settings by avoiding the oscillatory feedback loops that rigid heuristics induce, though RBS can outperform when the base policy is extremely poor (e.g., PCPO). For dynamics prediction, BA-NODE beats both NODE (MAE 3.18→2.82, RMSE 5.10→4.42) and ITransformer (MAE 4.11→2.82, RMSE 7.25→4.42) with substantially lower variance, indicating more stable training. On T1D, shielding yields a more uniform +6.08% average TIR gain with CV reductions for all agents, while on T2D without pump the benefits are less consistent (e.g., PPO-Lag gains +4.46% TIR but worsens CV by +4.39%), highlighting that the shield's effectiveness depends on the dynamical regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: safe RL under CMDPs (CPO, PPO-Lag, RCPO, etc.), OOD generalization in RL (domain randomization, adversarial training, safe meta-RL), and runtime shielding (Alshiekh et al., conformal-prediction-based shields). It departs from prior safe RL work by demonstrating that training-time constraint satisfaction is structurally insufficient under physiological distribution shift, and from prior shielding work by replacing formal-logic or known-dynamics verification with a learned personalized dynamics model (BA-NODE, combining ITransformer history encoding, a Neural ODE ensemble, and Function Encoder-style patient adaptation). The unified UVA-Padova-based simulator extends prior diabetes RL benchmarks (Fox et al., Zhu et al.) by modeling therapeutic decision support with patient adherence, partial observability, and multi-cohort physiological variability rather than direct continuous insulin control.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a reusable OOD safety benchmark (GlucoSim/GlucoAlg) that makes the safety generalization gap visible and quantifiable across algorithm families, which is valuable for calibrating claims about safe RL guarantees. The predictive shielding mechanism is algorithm-agnostic and practical, offering a deployment-time safety layer without retraining. Key limitations include evaluation entirely in simulation (no real patient data), reliance on partially inferable physiological shifts (extreme events like unannounced meals or acute stress may invalidate forecasts), and the shielding's dependence on base-policy quality (it cannot rescue fundamentally poor controllers like PCPO). Open questions include sim-to-real transfer, explicit epistemic uncertainty in the shield, and tighter integration between runtime verification and policy learning.
