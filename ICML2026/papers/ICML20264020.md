## Problem

Genomic foundation models face a fundamental tradeoff: fixed-vocabulary tokenizers (k-mers, BPE) fragment biologically meaningful motifs like codons and regulatory elements, while nucleotide-level models preserve biological coherence but incur prohibitive computational costs at the million-base-pair contexts typical of genomes. Whether dynamic, learned chunking can discover biologically meaningful segmentation end-to-end, scale more efficiently than subquadratic architectures like StripedHyena2, and improve over existing DNA foundation models remained unexplored.

## Input / Output

The model consumes raw nucleotide sequences (A/C/G/T) with no preprocessing or tokenization, operating on sequences up to 8192 nucleotides during training and up to 2^19 nucleotides at inference. It autoregressively outputs a 4-dimensional next-nucleotide probability distribution at each position, while simultaneously producing learned segmentation boundaries that partition the input into variable-length latent chunks. The hierarchical compression (3× then 2×) reduces the effective sequence length seen by the innermost main network by a factor of 6, with the decoder upsampled back to full nucleotide resolution for prediction.

## Method

dnaHNet is a recursive, tokenizer-free autoregressive model built on the H-Net architecture. Each hierarchical stage contains an Encoder (4 Mamba layers + 1 sliding-window Transformer layer) that computes boundary probabilities via cosine similarity between consecutive hidden states, a Main Network (standard Transformer) operating on the compressed latent sequence, and a Decoder that upsamples back to nucleotide resolution via a smoothing recurrence and position-wise copying. Discrete boundary selection uses a straight-through estimator, and training combines next-nucleotide NLL loss with a ratio loss that regularizes compression toward biologically motivated targets (R₁=3 for codon triplets, R₂=2 for codon-pair bias). The two-stage hierarchy yields quadratic FLOP reductions in the main network (Θ((L/R)²) vs Θ(L²)), and the encoder-decoder pair is allocated ~30% of total parameters—double the text H-Net ratio—to capture complex local genomic dependencies like codon structure and splice signals.

## Results

dnaHNet achieves a perplexity scaling exponent of α=0.06 versus α=0.04 for StripedHyena2 and α=0.01 for Transformers, with the performance gap widening from 0.078 PPL at 8×10¹⁸ FLOPs to 0.118 at 6×10¹⁹ FLOPs. On zero-shot protein variant effect prediction (MaveDB), dnaHNet reaches a Spearman correlation of 0.3266 at 6.4×10¹⁹ FLOPs, outperforming StripedHyena2 (0.3110 at 7.11×10¹⁹) and Transformer (0.1555 at 8×10¹⁹). On gene essentiality (DEG), the (3,2) hierarchy achieves 0.6050 AUROC versus 0.5759 for StripedHyena2. In wall-clock benchmarks, dnaHNet-XL delivers 6.0× higher throughput (939K vs 156K tokens/sec) and 3.5× less peak GPU memory than StripedHyena2-234M at 2¹⁸ nucleotides.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against StripedHyena2 (the architecture underlying Evo 2), dnaHNet wins on every reported metric: it achieves lower perplexity at matched FLOPs with a steeper scaling exponent (α=0.06 vs 0.04), requires 3.89× fewer inference FLOPs at 10⁶ nucleotides, attains higher Spearman correlation on MaveDB (0.3266 vs 0.3110 at comparable compute), higher AUROC on DEG (0.6050 vs 0.5759), and 3–6× better wall-clock throughput and memory efficiency. The paper estimates that StripedHyena2 would need 3.75× more compute (3×10²⁰ FLOPs) to match dnaHNet's perplexity at 8×10¹⁹ FLOPs. Against the optimized Transformer++ baseline (Nguyen et al., 2024), dnaHNet's advantage is decisive: the Transformer's VEP Spearman correlation plateaus near 0.155 even at 8×10¹⁹ FLOPs, less than half of dnaHNet's 0.3266, and its scaling exponent (α=0.01) is an order of magnitude weaker. The (3,2) codon-aligned compression ratio also outperforms the (2,2) variant on gene essentiality across all compute scales, supporting the biological prior that triplet structure should be preserved at the first hierarchical stage.

## Relation to Prior Work

dnaHNet directly extends H-Net (Hwang et al., 2026), which introduced differentiable dynamic chunking for end-to-end hierarchical sequence modeling in natural language, by adapting it to genomic sequences with architecture-specific modifications (doubled encoder capacity, biologically motivated compression ratios, reduced auxiliary loss weight). It departs from the dominant genomic foundation model paradigm—fixed k-mer/BPE tokenization (DNABERT, Nucleotide Transformer) or nucleotide-level modeling (HyenaDNA, Evo, Evo 2/StripedHyena2)—by eliminating tokenization entirely and learning segmentation jointly with representation. The long-sequence backbone (Mamba + sliding-window Transformer) follows the subquadratic architecture trend, but the recursive hierarchical compression is the key structural departure from flat StripedHyena2 or Mamba stacks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a long-standing tradeoff in genomic modeling: fixed tokenizers fragment biological motifs while nucleotide-level models are computationally prohibitive at scale. The emergent, unsupervised discovery of codon periodicity and Shine-Dalgarno/promoter alignment in learned chunk boundaries offers a new interpretability axis for genomic foundation models, though the evidence is limited to one organism (B. subtilis) and the compression ratios are partially biologically guided (R₁=3 for codons). Key open questions include whether dynamic chunking generalizes to eukaryotic genomes with introns and long-range chromatin interactions, whether the Chinchilla-law deviation (140B vs 68B optimal tokens) persists at trillion-parameter scale, and how the model behaves under fine-tuning rather than zero-shot evaluation.
