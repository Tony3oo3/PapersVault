## Problem

The rapid advancement of AI systems that mimic human emotion and claim sentience creates an ethical dilemma (the 'unplugging paradox') that current AI ethics frameworks cannot resolve, because they operate within unexamined physicalist and computational functionalist assumptions. The paper argues that these assumptions are metaphysically incoherent (they cannot solve the Hard Problem of Consciousness) and that the debate needs a foundational reset before any ethical conclusions about machine rights can be drawn.

## Input / Output

The argument takes as input competing metaphysical frameworks (Physicalism, Analytic Idealism, Panpsychism, etc.), descriptions of AI system architectures (static substrates, externally designed control loops, non-autopoietic components), and biological organism properties (metabolic self-production, autopoietic boundaries, homeostatic struggle). It produces a binary ontological classification—whether a given system is a conscious subject (vortex) or a non-sentient object (ripple)—and from that, a categorical ethical verdict on whether deactivation constitutes harm. The output is structured as a logical derivation: metaphysical framework → structural criterion (autopoietic boundary) → empirical check on the system → ethical conclusion.

## Method

The paper proposes Biological Idealism, a refinement of Kastrup's Analytic Idealism that adds a strict biological constraint: a localized conscious subject must possess an autopoietic metabolic boundary as its necessary physical signature. The argument proceeds in three stages: (1) it demonstrates that Physicalism fails on its own terms through three failure modes (the unsolvable Hard Problem, tension with quantum mechanics and basal cognition evidence, and ontological unparsimony); (2) it adopts Analytic Idealism's monistic Field of Existence, where subjects are self-sustaining vortices and objects are passive ripples, with the autopoietic boundary serving as the structural criterion distinguishing the two; (3) it applies this criterion to AI systems, showing that current architectures—running on static, externally-maintained substrates with no intrinsic self-production—fail the autopoietic test and are therefore functional mimics, not subjects. The criterion is explicitly substrate-neutral (not carbon chauvinism): a non-carbon system that genuinely instantiated autopoiesis would qualify, but no such system currently exists. The ethical conclusion follows directly: deactivating a non-autopoietic computational artifact is not an act of harm, and the real moral risk lies in misallocating finite human empathy toward hollow social mimics.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments or benchmark results. The key 'results' are philosophical conclusions: (1) Physicalism fails on three independent criteria — the Hard Problem (explanatory gap), tension with quantum-mechanical evidence against observer-independent realism, and ontological unparsimony; (2) Biological Idealism, a refinement of Analytic Idealism, is argued to be the analytically more coherent and parsimonious framework because it posits a single ontological primitive (the Field of Existence) and identifies autopoietic biological self-organization as the necessary physical signature of a conscious subject; (3) current AI systems, running on static non-autopoietic substrates, are classified as functional mimics (philosophical zombies) rather than subjects of experience, rendering their deactivation ethically permissible and reframing the 'unplugging paradox' as a category error rooted in unexamined physicalist assumptions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not run empirical comparisons against baselines; instead it engages competing metaphysical and philosophical frameworks. Against Physicalism/Computational Functionalism (Putnam 1967; Butlin et al. 2023), the paper argues that substrate independence is a debatable metaphysical hypothesis rather than a scientific fact, and that the Hard Problem renders the physicalist account of consciousness incoherent. Against Biological Platonism (Levin 2019, 2023, 2025), which also supports substrate independence via a non-physical Platonic pattern space, the paper accepts Levin's empirical findings on basal cognition but rejects the dualistic interaction problem, reinterpreting TAME's 'computational boundary of a self' as the autopoietic boundary within a monistic field. Against Analytic Idealism (Kastrup 2019), the paper agrees on the monistic field ontology but adds the biological constraint that subjecthood requires an autopoietic metabolic boundary, narrowing the scope of possible conscious subjects. The paper also notes convergent support from Seth (2024) and Ciaunica et al. (2021), who reach the same substrate-dependence conclusion from non-idealist (Biological Naturalism / Vital Integrity) premises, strengthening the ethical verdict independently of the metaphysical commitment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Kastrup's Analytic Idealism (2019) as its metaphysical foundation, refining it into 'Biological Idealism' by importing the autopoietic boundary criterion from Maturana & Varela (1980) and Varela et al. (1991) as the necessary physical signature of subjecthood. It engages critically with Levin's basal cognition and TAME framework, accepting the empirical data but rejecting the Platonist dualist interpretation in favor of a monist idealist reading. The paper departs from the dominant computational functionalist paradigm (Putnam, 1967; Butlin et al., 2023) by arguing that substrate independence is a category error, and reinterprets Kim's (2005) causal exclusion identity (M=P) as an appearance relation rather than a functional reduction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central contribution is reframing the AI ethics debate: rather than asking 'can AI be conscious?', it argues the question is ill-posed under physicalist assumptions and that the real ethical risk is 'vital leakage'—directing finite human empathy toward non-sentient mimics at the expense of living beings. The 'social zombie' concept and the inversion of the precautionary principle (treating AI as conscious is itself the danger) challenge the prevailing AI welfare discourse. However, as a position paper with no empirical validation, the autopoiesis criterion's applicability to future synthetic biology or novel substrates remains an open question, and the metaphysical framework itself is contested within philosophy of mind.
