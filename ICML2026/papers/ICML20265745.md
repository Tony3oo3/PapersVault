## Problem

Prior theoretical work on transformer in-context learning has largely focused on linear regression with linear or ReLU attention, leaving the mechanism by which softmax attention performs in-context classification (logistic regression) poorly understood. This paper asks whether multi-layer softmax transformers can exactly implement a known optimization algorithm for in-context logistic regression, whether such a model can be obtained through standard training, and whether the resulting model generalizes out-of-distribution.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265745/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The transformer receives a 2d × (n+1) embedding matrix Z_0 whose first n columns encode context examples as z_i = y_i · x_i (top d rows) with zeros below, and whose last column holds the initialization θ_0 in the bottom d rows. The output is the predicted weight vector θ_L extracted from the bottom-right d × 1 block of the final layer's output matrix; quality is measured directionally as ‖θ_L/‖θ_L‖₂ − θ*‖₂, since the sign function makes labels invariant to positive rescaling of θ*. The in-context loss uses the exponential loss ℓ(x) = e^{−x} rather than the standard logistic loss, chosen for cleaner theoretical analysis.

## Method

The paper constructs an L-layer softmax transformer whose parameter matrices have a specific block structure (V_l has α̃_l·I_d in the bottom-left block; W_l has −I_d in the top-right block) such that each layer's forward pass exactly equals one step of normalized gradient descent on the in-context exponential loss, with the softmax attention weights naturally recovering the normalized gradient direction −ℓ'(⟨z_i,θ⟩)/L_ICL(θ). For trainability, a single layer is trained via gradient descent on a population MSE loss supervised by a one-step GD teacher; the analysis shows the parameters preserve a structured form reducible to two scalars (C_1, C_2), converge linearly to a unique minimizer with C_1 ≈ αe^{σ²/2} and C_2 ≈ 1, and the trained layer performs NGD despite being supervised by plain GD. Recurrently applying this trained layer yields a looped transformer whose output directionally converges to the in-context maximum-margin solution at rate O(log n)/(αL), and under log-concave feature distributions achieves an OOD generalization bound of Õ(d/n), matching the PAC learning sample complexity lower bound.

## Results

The paper establishes that an L-layer softmax transformer exactly implements L steps of normalized gradient descent (NGD) on the in-context logistic regression loss (Theorem 3.1), with the output directionally converging to the maximum-margin solution at rate O(log n / (αL)) (Corollary 3.3). Training a single-layer transformer with a one-step GD teacher yields linear convergence at rate (1 − ημ₁,α,σ/d)^t to a unique minimizer that recovers the NGD-implementing structure (Theorem 3.5). The resulting looped transformer achieves an OOD generalization bound of O(log n/(αL) + d log ρ / n) with high probability for any fixed input (Theorem 3.7); when L = Õ(n/(αd)), this reduces to Õ(d/n), matching the PAC learning sample complexity lower bound. Experiments confirm that looped transformer outputs track NGD iterates nearly exactly and consistently outperform standard GD across Gaussian, Laplace, and Uniform OOD distributions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary empirical comparison is between the looped transformer, NGD iterates, and standard GD iterates on OOD weight prediction (Figure 6). The looped transformer's hidden-layer outputs remain very close to NGD iterates throughout the depth, achieving nearly identical prediction accuracy and consistently outperforming standard GD across all tested distributions (Gaussian, Laplace, Uniform) and dimensions (d=20, 30). Theoretically, the paper contrasts with Bai et al. (2024), whose multi-head ReLU transformers approximate standard gradient descent and converge to the max-margin solution only at rate O(log log L / log L), requiring exponentially large depth to match the statistical error term; in contrast, this paper's single-head softmax construction achieves the faster O(log n/(αL)) rate via NGD. A notable finding is that the trained transformer learns NGD even though it is supervised by a plain gradient descent teacher, revealing a nontrivial separation between the supervising algorithm and the learned in-context algorithm. No direct head-to-head numerical comparison table is provided, as the experiments are presented as convergence curves and trajectory plots rather than tabulated benchmark scores.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work constructing transformers that implicitly execute optimization algorithms on context data (Von Oswald et al., 2023; Akyürek et al., 2022) from linear attention performing gradient descent on linear regression to softmax attention performing normalized gradient descent on logistic regression. It adopts the in-context weight prediction task and GD-teacher training setup from Huang et al. (2025), and leverages the implicit bias theory of normalized gradient descent from Ji & Telgarsky (2021) to characterize convergence to the maximum-margin solution. A key departure from Bai et al. (2024), who use multi-head ReLU attention to approximate gradient descent with O(ε^{-2}) heads, is that here a single softmax head per layer exactly implements NGD with no approximation error.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first exact (non-approximate) correspondence between standard softmax attention and a specific optimization algorithm for classification, along with a point-wise high-probability OOD generalization bound that is stronger than the in-expectation guarantees typical in the ICL literature. The finding that a transformer supervised by a standard GD teacher converges to implementing NGD is conceptually significant, suggesting transformers can discover algorithmic mechanisms distinct from the supervising signal. However, the analysis is restricted to exponential loss, linear classification, and an attention-only architecture; extending the theory to richer losses, multi-class settings, and end-to-end multi-layer training remains open.
