## Problem

Standard multi-task GRPO post-training of LLMs optimizes average reward across tasks, which permits strong gains on easy tasks to mask stagnation on harder ones, yielding imbalanced and unreliable general-purpose reasoners. A second, structural issue is that tasks exhibit widely different zero-gradient rates under GRPO (prompts where all rollouts receive identical rewards produce no gradient), so even uniform task weights produce skewed effective gradient contributions. The paper asks how to post-train a single model to improve reasoning across heterogeneous tasks while guaranteeing that no task is systematically under-optimized.

## Input / Output

The method consumes K reasoning tasks, each providing a disjoint set of prompts with verifiable reward functions that score both correctness and formatting of the model's response, along with a base LLM policy (Qwen-2.5-3B/7B or OLMo-3-7B). At each training step, the algorithm samples a batch of prompts across tasks, generates G rollouts per prompt from the current policy, computes within-group relative advantages, and performs a clipped GRPO policy update. The output is a single post-trained policy that is evaluated on held-out prompts per task, with the primary metric being worst-task accuracy (minimum accuracy across all K tasks) alongside average accuracy and per-task relative change.

## Method

MT-GRPO introduces two coupled mechanisms. First, an improvement-aware task reweighting (IWU) step maintains a softmax-parameterized distribution z over tasks and updates it via gradient descent on a combined signal s_k = I_k^(t) + λ J_k(θ_t), where I_k^(t) is the per-step change in the task's GRPO objective and J_k(θ_t) is the true task-level reward; this decouples reweighting from the GRPO loss (which is ambiguous, being zero both when all rollouts are correct and all incorrect) and prevents weight collapse onto a single worst task. The parameter λ trades off worst-task robustness against average performance, analogous to the ε in a constrained minimax formulation. Second, a ratio-preserving sampler (RP SAMPLER) enforces the target task proportions in the post-filtered training batch by oversampling tasks with high expected zero-gradient rates (using an acceptance-aware inflation factor m_k = min{1/(1−ρ_k), M_acc}) and resampling until per-task nonzero-gradient counts meet their multinomial targets, ensuring that the learned weights z translate faithfully into realized gradient contributions rather than being distorted by heterogeneous filtering rates.

## Results

On the 3-task setting (Countdown, Zebra, ARC; Qwen-2.5-3B), MT-GRPO (λ=0.25) achieves 58.8% worst-task accuracy versus 30.3% for GRPO and 52.6% for DAPO, while maintaining competitive average accuracy. Scaling to 9 tasks, MT-GRPO (λ=1.2) reaches 46.3% worst-task accuracy, a 16-point gain over GRPO and 6 points over DAPO. On the 7B heterogeneous setting (MATH + ARC + SciKnowEval), MT-GRPO (λ=0.2) achieves 37.6% worst-task accuracy (vs. 25.0% DAPO, 15.0% GRPO) and reaches DAPO's final worst-task accuracy in ~60% less wall-clock time (5.5 h vs. 13.3 h). MT-GRPO also requires 50% fewer training steps to reach the 50% worst-task accuracy threshold in the 3-task setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MT-GRPO outperforms all three baselines on worst-task accuracy across every experimental setting. Against standard GRPO (uniform task sampling), MT-GRPO gains 16–28 percentage points on worst-task accuracy, with the largest gap in the 3-task setting (58.8% vs. 30.3%). Against DAPO (uniform sampling with DAPO-style clipping and dynamic sampling), MT-GRPO still leads by 6 points in the 9-task setting (46.3% vs. ~40.3%) and by 12.6 points in the 7B heterogeneous setting (37.6% vs. 25.0%). Against SEC-GRPO (self-evolving curriculum prioritizing larger absolute advantages), MT-GRPO also wins on worst-task accuracy, as SEC-GRPO continues to over-weight the high-performing Countdown task rather than reallocating toward weaker tasks. The key differentiator is that MT-GRPO's improvement-aware reweighting and ratio-preserving sampler prevent both weight collapse onto a single task and the zero-gradient mismatch that causes baselines to under-represent harder tasks (e.g., ARC) in effective gradient contributions.

## Relation to Prior Work

MT-GRPO extends GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) from single-task to multi-task RL post-training by incorporating a distributionally robust optimization (DRO) objective, drawing on the minimax reformulation from Sagawa et al. (2019) and Namkoong & Duchi (2016). The improvement-aware reweighting signal builds directly on the task-level improvement concept from FAMO (Liu et al., 2023), but departs from prior DRO and multi-task learning methods in a critical way: the GRPO loss is shown to be an unreliable proxy for task performance (it vanishes both when all rollouts are correct and when all are incorrect), necessitating decoupling of task reweighting from the GRPO loss itself. The ratio-preserving sampler addresses a structural issue unique to GRPO-style objectives—uneven zero-gradient rates across tasks—that has no analogue in supervised multi-task learning or prior domain-reweighting work for pre-training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLMs are increasingly deployed as general-purpose reasoners rather than task-specific specialists, ensuring balanced competence across diverse reasoning skills is essential for reliability, and this paper provides a principled, tunable mechanism (via λ) to control the robustness-vs-average-performance trade-off. The practical finding that zero-gradient rates systematically distort effective task contributions—and that a ratio-preserving sampler is necessary to correct this—has implications beyond this specific algorithm for any GRPO-based multi-task pipeline. Key limitations include reliance on verifiable rewards (the reweighting mechanism depends on true task-level rewards being available) and scope restricted to the training task mixture, leaving open the question of robustness to out-of-distribution tasks and non-verifiable domains.
