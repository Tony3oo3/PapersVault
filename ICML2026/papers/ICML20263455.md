## Problem

For discriminative models (including autoregressive language models), exact identifiability guarantees that equal conditional distributions imply representations related by an invertible linear map. However, it was shown that KL closeness between distributions does NOT imply linear representational similarity, leaving open which distributional distance does provide such guarantees. This paper asks: under what notion of distributional closeness can we recover quantitative linear representational similarity bounds, and what are the practical consequences for knowledge distillation?

## Input / Output

The framework operates on a model family where each model (f, g) maps inputs x to m-dimensional embeddings f(x) and labels y to m-dimensional unembeddings g(y), producing softmax probabilities over k > m labels. The logit distance is computed between the k-dimensional logit vectors u(x) = (f(x)ᵀg(y₁), …, f(x)ᵀg(y_k)) of two models, with the constraint that unembeddings are centered (sum to zero). In the distillation setting, the teacher's logits (or equivalently its conditional probabilities) serve as the training target, and the student is trained to minimize either KL divergence or the proposed L1/L2 logit-distance loss to the teacher, producing a student model whose internal representations are linearly aligned with the teacher's.

## Method

The paper defines a logit distance d_logit as the expected squared L2 norm of the difference between two models' logit vectors, proves it is a proper metric, and shows it is equivalent to the Aitchison distance from compositional data analysis. The key theoretical result (Theorem 3.9) proves that d_logit upper-bounds a newly defined linear identifiability dissimilarity d_rep — which is zero if and only if two models are in the same linear equivalence class — with a bound depending on the minimum singular value of the shifted unembedding matrices. A complementary result (Theorem 3.4) shows d_logit lower-bounds m_CCA between embedding spaces. While KL divergence does bound d_logit under a τ-lower-boundedness assumption (Theorem 3.3), the resulting constants scale as τ⁻¹ and are too weak for practical settings. For distillation, the paper proposes training students with an L1 logit-distance loss (Eq. 21) instead of KL divergence, and proves (Proposition 4.1) that minimizing this loss provably reduces d_logit, thereby preserving the teacher's linearly encoded concepts (Theorem 4.3) and linear representational structure.

## Results

On the SUB dataset, logit-distance distillation (L1/L2) recovers 75%/72% of the teacher's linearly encoded concept attributes (Acc(C)), while KL-based distillation achieves only 6%—barely above the random baseline of 1/33 ≈ 3%. In terms of representational similarity, L2 students reduce d_rep by three orders of magnitude on SUB (1.6 vs. 3100 for KL) and achieve near-perfect m_CCA (0.99 vs. 0.42). On CIFAR-100, L1/L2 students improve m_CCA by over 20 percentage points (0.77 vs. 0.52) and reduce d_rep by two orders of magnitude (0.66 vs. 87.6) compared to KL students, while all methods achieve comparable label accuracy (Acc(Y) ≈ 49–54%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is standard KL-based knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015), where the student minimizes KL divergence to the teacher's output distribution. The paper's logit-distance objectives (L1 and L2 variants) decisively outperform KL distillation on all representational similarity metrics: on SUB, L2 reduces d_rep from 3100 to 1.6 (≈2000× improvement) and raises m_CCA from 0.42 to 0.99; on CIFAR-100, d_rep drops from 87.6 to 0.66 and m_CCA rises from 0.52 to 0.76. The most striking gap is in concept recovery (Acc(C) on SUB): KL students score 6%—essentially random—while L1 and L2 students recover 75% and 72% of the teacher's linearly encoded attributes, respectively. All three student types achieve comparable label accuracy (Acc(Y) ≈ 92–93% on SUB, ≈49–54% on CIFAR), confirming that the representational advantage of logit-distance distillation comes at no cost to predictive performance. The L2 variant (minimizing d_logit²) consistently achieves the lowest d_rep across all datasets, while L1 (minimizing the L1 logit distance) offers comparable m_CCA with slightly higher d_rep, suggesting a trade-off between the two logit-distance formulations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the identifiability framework for softmax classifiers (Roeder et al., 2021; Khemakhem et al., 2020b) from exact distributional equality to approximate settings, building directly on Nielsen et al. (2025), who showed that KL closeness does not guarantee representational similarity. It introduces the logit distance (equivalent to the Aitchison distance from compositional data analysis) as the distributional metric that recovers quantitative representational guarantees, and connects this to prior distillation work that informally used logit matching (Ba & Caruana, 2014; Kim et al., 2021) by providing the first theoretical justification for why it should preserve linear structure. The paper departs from standard KL-based knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) by showing that the standard objective is fundamentally misaligned with the goal of preserving representational geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is clear: if preserving linearly recoverable concepts or steerability matters in distillation, KL is the wrong loss and a logit-distance objective should be used instead. However, the theory requires k > m (more labels than representation dimension), a general-position assumption on unembeddings, and equal representation dimensions across models—conditions met by large-vocabulary LMs but restrictive for standard image classification. Key open questions include whether the results extend to regimes where k ≤ m, how logit-distance objectives interact with optimization dynamics in large-scale distillation (e.g., LLMs), and whether the trade-off between capturing teacher structure and ignoring noisy low-probability labels can be formalized.
