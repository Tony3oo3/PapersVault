## Problem

Branch-and-bound MIP solvers frequently discover optimal or near-optimal solutions early in the search but then spend the majority of computation time tightening lower bounds to certify optimality. Existing machine-learning approaches to MIP acceleration target decisions *within* the solver (branching, cutting planes, heuristics) to reach a provably optimal solution faster, but none learn *when to stop* the solver early while providing rigorous probabilistic guarantees on the quality of the returned solution.

## Input / Output

At each solver callback the method consumes a time-series covariate vector X_θ(t) comprising the current upper and lower bounds, their 1/3/5-second rolling averages, elapsed solve time, number of B&B nodes expanded, and the instance parameter θ; it does not require access to the full problem formulation. The output is a pre-calibrated scalar threshold κ (computed once on a calibration set via conformal prediction) and, at deployment, a stopping time τ̂_κ(θ) at which the solver is terminated and its current best feasible solution is returned. The guarantee is that the returned solution is ε-optimal with probability at least 1−α over the calibration set and the test instance.

## Method

The method trains a 2-layer LSTM (200 hidden units) followed by a 2-layer feedforward network to predict the true optimality gap g_θ(t) from the solver state, with a squashing function φ that constrains the prediction to [L_θ(t), U_θ(t)] so it vanishes as the bounds close. Training uses a weighted MSE loss with weight w_θ(t) ∝ 1/y_θ(t) that concentrates accuracy in the critical region where the gap is near the target tolerance ε. Conformal prediction (Theorem 4.1) then calibrates a single threshold κ as the n-th largest value of the rolling-minimum predictor evaluated at the true ε-crossing time across c calibration instances, guaranteeing P[g_θ(τ̂_κ(θ)) ≤ ε] ≥ n/(c+1) ≥ 1−α without any multiple-testing correction. The paper further establishes sample-conditional concentration bounds (Theorems 5.1–5.2) showing expected suboptimality and success probability concentrate near their empirical calibration values at rate O(1/√c), and a robustness theorem (Theorem 5.3) extending the guarantees to test distributions within total-variation distance ρ of the calibration distribution.

## Results

On six problem families from Distributional MIPLIB, the conformal prediction method (CP GRB) reduces solve time by 76% on CFLP, 78% on OTS, 66% on MMCN, 88% on MVC, and 95% on MIS compared to Gurobi's standard ε-optimality termination, while maintaining suboptimality below 0.05% and solving 88–98% of instances to within 0.1%-optimality (target: 95% with ε=0.001, α=0.05). The speedup on GISP is more modest at 8%, as both solvers close the gap quickly after finding a good solution. The method also outperforms Cardinal Optimization (COPT) on all families, with the largest gain on OTS (78% faster) and MIS (28% faster).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two exact solvers with standard ε-optimality termination: Gurobi (GRB) and Cardinal Optimization (COPT). CP GRB dominates GRB on solve time across all six families (3.7 s vs. 15.6 s on CFLP; 96.2 s vs. 431.8 s on OTS; 11.7 s vs. 221.0 s on MIS) while keeping suboptimality under 0.05%, at the cost of solving 2–12% fewer instances to full ε-optimality (the expected α=5% failure rate). CP COPT similarly beats COPT on all families, with the largest relative gain on OTS (117.8 s vs. 138.5 s, 15% faster) and a smaller gain on GISP (67.0 s vs. 75.5 s, 11% faster). Simple heuristics that stop after finding 1 or 3 feasible solutions (GRB1, GRB3, COPT1, COPT3) are orders of magnitude faster but return solutions with 7–221% suboptimality, confirming that the learned stopping criterion captures nontrivial structure in the convergence curves. The method also outperforms a linear predictor and a simple upper-bound-rate heuristic on solve time for most families (e.g., 11.7 s vs. 207.3 s on MIS), while the linear model achieves slightly lower suboptimality on some families, suggesting the LSTM's advantage is primarily in earlier, more confident stopping.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant ML-for-MIP paradigm (learning branching policies, cutting planes, or primal heuristics to speed up the search) by instead learning *when to stop* the solver. It builds directly on the conformal prediction framework of Angelopoulos & Bates (2021) and extends the authors' prior work (Clarke & Stellato, 2025), which used conformal prediction to validate an independent learned heuristic, by applying it to calibrate a termination criterion for the exact solver itself. The sample-conditional guarantees draw on Vovk (2012) and Duchi (2025), and the distribution-shift robustness result leverages total variation distance bounds. Conceptually, the paper repurposes conformal prediction from its typical role (uncertainty quantification for a single prediction) to calibrate a threshold over an entire solver trajectory, avoiding the multiple-testing problem that would arise from checking at every callback.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: 60–95% solve-time reductions across six problem families and two solvers while maintaining 0.1%-optimality with 95% probability, which could make real-time MIP-based decision-making feasible in robotics, power systems, and logistics. Key limitations include the need to solve instances during training (so the method only accelerates problems solvers can already handle), the modest 8% speedup on GISP showing problem-dependent gains, and the assumption that test instances come from the same distribution as calibration data. Open questions include whether the approach extends to problems beyond current solver reach, whether more expressive predictors (transformers, GNNs) would improve the critical-region accuracy, and how the method behaves under adversarial or structured distribution shifts beyond the mild perturbations tested.
