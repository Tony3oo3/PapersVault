## Problem

Prior code-reasoning benchmarks evaluate LLMs under fixed, standard language semantics, leaving open whether models can actually condition their reasoning on externally supplied formal rules when those rules conflict with pretrained symbol-meaning associations. This paper asks: can LLMs adapt their execution reasoning to novel formal semantics (e.g., swapped operator meanings or entirely new symbols) rather than defaulting to lexical priors learned during pretraining?

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a C* program (featherweight C with explicit '{' delimiters, avoiding indentation-sensitivity confounds) with a formal semantic formalization expressed in Gentzen-style inference rules, under one of three semantic variants: standard, KeywordSwap (operators exchange meanings, e.g., '+' performs subtraction), or KeywordObf (all operators/keywords replaced with Caucasian-Albanian script symbols defined only by the supplied rules). Programs span three complexity tiers (Human-Written, LLM-Translated, Fuzzer-Generated) with increasing control-flow depth, data-flow dependencies, and trace length. Models produce one of three outputs depending on the task: a final variable-to-value state mapping (PredState), an ordered sequence of rule names for state-free execution (PredRule), or a complete execution trace with intermediate states and per-step rule applications (PredTrace).

## Method

PLSEMANTICSBENCH formalizes C* under two complementary semantic systems—fine-grained small-step structural operational semantics (S, 78 rules) and coarser rewriting-based K semantics—then systematically perturbs the symbol-to-meaning mapping via KeywordSwap and KeywordObf while preserving rule structure, enabling controlled isolation of lexical priors from genuine rule conditioning. Three tasks probe four hypotheses: PredState tests global rule composition (H1), PredRule tests local rule selection without state mutation (H2), PredTrace tests sustained rule application over long execution horizons (H3), and the semantic shifts test robustness under distribution shift (H4). Ground-truth traces are generated via the K-framework (for K) and a custom ANTLR4-based interpreter (for S). Eleven frontier LLMs are evaluated using two derived metrics: Semantic Conditioning (Δ_cnd = Acc_std − Acc_no-rules) measuring whether supplying formal rules helps, and Semantic Shift Sensitivity (Δ_is = Acc_shifted − Acc_std) measuring degradation when operator meanings are perturbed. A preliminary notation-comprehension validation (NL→Rule and Rule→NL multiple-choice tasks) confirms that downstream failures reflect rule-conditioning limitations rather than inability to parse the formalism.

## Results

On the Human-Written split of PLSEMANTICSBENCH, top reasoning models (GPT-5-mini, GEMINI-2.5-pro, o3-mini) achieve 99–100% final-state accuracy (PredState) under standard K-semantics, but accuracy drops by 20–60 percentage points under KeywordSwap (e.g., GPT-5-mini falls from 99% to 79%; o3-mini from 100% to 41%). Under KeywordObf, drops are more moderate (5–16 points for top models), while non-reasoning models (QWEN2.5-INST14B, GPT-4o-mini) score only 26–33% even under standard semantics. On the long-horizon PredTrace task, only four models achieve non-zero accuracy, with GEMINI-2.5-pro reaching just 35% under S-semantics—the best result across all 11 models evaluated.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper, so the 'baselines' are the 11 frontier LLMs evaluated under three semantic variants (standard, KeywordSwap, KeywordObf) and two formalisms (small-step S and K). GEMINI-2.5-pro is the clear outlier: it maintains ≥97% PredState accuracy under KeywordSwap and ≥94% under KeywordObf, and is the only model showing positive Δis on PredTrace (35% under S). GPT-5-mini and o3-mini achieve near-perfect standard accuracy (99–100%) but suffer large drops under swap (−20 to −59 points), revealing persistent reliance on pretrained symbol associations. Non-reasoning models (QWEN2.5-INST14B/32B, LLAMA-3.3 70B, GPT-4o-mini) score 26–33% under standard semantics and collapse to 4–14% under swap, with CoT prompting recovering ~50 points on standard but failing under swap. On PredRule (state-free rule selection), most models show strongly negative Δis, with only GEMINI-2.5-pro maintaining or improving accuracy under mutation. On the Fuzzer-Generated split (highest complexity), even GEMINI-2.5-pro drops to 69% (std) and 26% (swap), confirming that structural scale overwhelms rule-conditioned composition across all models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior code-reasoning benchmarks (CRUXEval, LiveCodeBench, BigCodeBench) that evaluate end-to-end program I/O under fixed language semantics, and from program-perturbation studies (EquiBench, CodeARC, SPAT) that mutate programs while holding semantics constant. The key conceptual inversion is that PLSEMANTICSBENCH keeps programs syntactically identical while altering the externally supplied formal semantics (via KeywordSwap and KeywordObf), thereby isolating reliance on pretrained symbol associations from genuine conditioning on inference rules. It also complements execution-aware training work (NExT, SemCoder, TRACED) by providing the missing diagnostic: rather than assuming models internalize semantics from training data, it directly tests whether models follow explicitly provided formal rules when those rules conflict with learned priors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes 'inferential rule conditioning' as a largely unsolved capability axis, showing that even frontier models achieving 99–100% under standard semantics collapse by 40–90 points when operator meanings are swapped or obfuscated, and that long-horizon rule conditioning (PredTrace) is near-zero for most models. This has direct practical implications for operator overloading, DSLs, and proof assistants where semantics legitimately diverge from conventional symbol meanings. Open questions include whether targeted training on formal semantics (rather than natural-language code) can close the gap, whether the capability emerges with further scale, and whether the brittleness is specific to the featherweight C* setting or generalizes to richer languages. The benchmark's controlled design (two formalisms, three complexity splits, two semantic shifts) makes it a reusable diagnostic for future model releases.
