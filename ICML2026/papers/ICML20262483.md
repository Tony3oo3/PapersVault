## Problem

In heterogeneous collaborative perception, agents with different sensor configurations and perception architectures produce intermediate features in incompatible modalities, and existing adaptation methods (one-to-one adapters or protocol-space bridges) require repeated retraining or fine-tuning whenever a new modality mapping emerges. This is costly and often infeasible across manufacturers due to model and data privacy constraints, leaving zero-shot any-to-any feature translation for newly emerging agents unaddressed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262483/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

UniTrans consumes single-frame BEV feature maps (C×H×W) from a neighbor agent and the ego agent, where the neighbor feature may originate from any of 30 heterogeneous modality configurations spanning LiDAR (PointPillars, SECOND, VoxelNet at varying voxel resolutions and capacities) and camera (LSS with EfficientNet/ResNet backbones) encoders. It produces an ego-consistent translated neighbor feature of the same spatial shape, which is then fused with the ego's own feature for cooperative 3D object detection. Critically, the translation operates on intermediate features only (no raw sensor access) and must handle modality pairs never seen during training in a single forward pass.

## Method

UniTrans uses a two-stage pretraining pipeline. Stage 1 trains a Modality-Intrinsic Encoder (MIE) that compresses BEV features into a low-dimensional (d=4) intrinsic code via channel statistics, Gram descriptors, and global response features, structured by InfoNCE contrastive clustering and a modality classification head so that codes are modality-specific yet scene-invariant. Stage 2 learns a Translator Parameter Bank (TPB) of K=8 reusable sparse cross-attention translator experts plus one shared expert, and a Modality Mapping Router (MMR) that takes the source and target intrinsic codes, computes a compact mapping descriptor, and predicts softmax combination coefficients. The key architectural distinction from classic MoE is that UniTrans linearly combines expert *parameters* (φ = Θ⁽⁰⁾ + Σ αₖΘ⁽ᵏ⁾) to synthesize a single mapping-specific translator, rather than forwarding through multiple experts and mixing their outputs, yielding one-pass inference at 109.3 GFLOPs versus 245.5 GFLOPs for a TopK=3 classic MoE. Stage 2 is supervised by downstream task loss, feature-level distillation against a teacher ego-domain encoding, routing contrastive loss, and Switch-style load-balancing regularization, while the MIE remains frozen.

## Results

On the simulated OPV2V-H benchmark, UniTrans achieves an average AP@0.5/AP@0.7 of 0.716/0.605 across six ego modalities, outperforming the strongest baseline NegoCollab (0.662/0.538) by 5.4/6.7 points. On the real-world DAIR-V2X dataset, UniTrans reaches 0.553/0.421, improving over NegoCollab (0.509/0.389) and Classic MoE (0.523/0.388). In terms of efficiency, UniTrans requires only 109.3 GFLOPs and 6.865 ms CPU time per scene, compared to 245.5 GFLOPs and 89.078 ms for Classic MoE, because it instantiates a single translator via parameter combination rather than executing multiple experts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UniTrans outperforms all nine baselines on both OPV2V-H and DAIR-V2X. Against the strongest one-to-one adaptation methods (MPDA, PnPDA, PolyInter), UniTrans gains 5.4–9.4 AP@0.5 points on OPV2V-H, with the largest margins on camera-ego modalities (m27, m30) where cross-modality gaps are most severe. Against protocol-based methods (STAMP, NegoCollab), UniTrans leads by 5.4/6.7 AP@0.5/AP@0.7 on OPV2V-H and 4.4/3.2 on DAIR-V2X, confirming that a fixed protocol space is less effective than on-the-fly mapping-conditioned instantiation for unseen modalities. The Classic MoE baseline, which routes experts directly from high-dimensional features and mixes their outputs, achieves 0.653/0.544 on OPV2V-H—still 6.3/6.1 points below UniTrans—and incurs 2.2× the GFLOPs and 13× the CPU latency due to multi-expert forwarding. On DAIR-V2X, where real-world distribution shifts compound the heterogeneity challenge, UniTrans maintains its lead over NegoCollab (0.553 vs. 0.509 AP@0.5), demonstrating that the modality-intrinsic space provides more stable mapping estimation under domain shift than direct feature-space routing.

## Relation to Prior Work

UniTrans builds on the sparse cross-attention translator architecture from CoBEVT (Xu et al., 2022) and the domain-adaptation formulation of MPDA (Xu et al., 2023), but departs fundamentally from both one-to-one adapter training and protocol-space alignment paradigms. Rather than learning a fixed mapping per modality pair or projecting into a shared protocol, UniTrans introduces a modality-intrinsic latent space (via contrastive pretraining) to measure source-to-target relations, then instantiates translators through linear parameter combination over a reusable expert bank — borrowing the load-balancing regularization from Switch Transformers (Fedus et al., 2022) but applying it to parameter synthesis rather than output mixing. This shifts the problem from 'learn a mapping' to 'learn how to compose mappings,' enabling zero-shot generalization to unseen modality pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The zero-shot any-to-any formulation directly addresses a critical deployment bottleneck: cross-manufacturer joint retraining is often infeasible due to model and data privacy, making UniTrans's single-pretrained-model approach practically significant for scalable V2X collaboration. The efficiency result — achieving higher accuracy than Classic MoE with roughly half the FLOPs by synthesizing one translator instead of executing multiple experts — makes the approach viable for on-vehicle real-time inference. Open questions include whether the modality-intrinsic space generalizes to truly novel sensor types (e.g., radar, event cameras) beyond the LiDAR/camera families studied, how the approach scales as the number of modalities grows well beyond 30, and whether the linear parameter combination can be replaced by more expressive yet still efficient instantiation strategies.
