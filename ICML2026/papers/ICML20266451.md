## Problem

Survival analysis evaluation is systematically misaligned: over 80% of studies default to the C-index regardless of whether the stated objective is time-to-event prediction, probabilistic calibration, or risk ranking, and censoring assumptions (random, independent, or dependent) are rarely stated, justified, or corrected for. A structured survey of 92 papers (2023–2025) finds that approximately 72% use evaluation metrics that do not match their stated modeling objectives, leading to misleading model comparisons and overstated performance claims.

## Input / Output

The paper operates on censored survival data (covariates x_i, observed times t_i, event indicators δ_i) and survival model predictions in the form of individual survival distributions (ISDs), risk scores, or point event-time estimates. The controlled experiments fix a CoxPH model trained on synthetic Weibull data and vary only the censoring mechanism (random, independent, or dependent via a Clayton copula with Kendall's τ ∈ {0.25, 0.5, 0.75}), producing oracle metric scores, censored-data metric scores, and the resulting metric error under each regime. The broader output includes a practical checklist mapping prediction target → evaluation aspect → censoring assumption → appropriate metric, along with a double-helix ladder diagram showing where valid model and metric methods exist across the assumption hierarchy.

## Method

The paper introduces the Ladder Hypothesis of Model-Metric Consistency, which requires that a model and its evaluation metric rest on the same rung of a three-level assumption ladder (random → independent → dependent censoring); violating this alignment renders both the reported performance and the metric estimate unreliable. To operationalize this, the authors propose five desiderata for survival metrics (proper scoring rule, interpretability, model-agnosticism, sensitivity to miscalibration, and robustness to censoring) and use them to audit common metrics (Harrell's/Uno's/Antolini's C-index, IBS, MAE, D-Calibration, 1-Calibration, log-likelihood) in a structured comparison table. The empirical core is a controlled experiment where a single CoxPH model is evaluated under progressively weaker censoring assumptions, demonstrating that standard IPCW-based metrics (Harrell's CI, IBS) become increasingly biased—and can even suggest improvement when oracle performance degrades—as censoring shifts from random to dependent. A structured survey of 92 recent papers quantifies the prevalence of metric-objective mismatch and unaddressed censoring, while the double-helix ladder visualization highlights that model development has begun addressing dependent censoring (e.g., copula-based methods) but metric development has not yet fully resolved even independent censoring.

## Results

In a structured survey of 92 survival analysis papers (2023–2025), approximately 72% rely on evaluation metrics that do not align with their stated modeling objectives, and 67.4% use Harrell's C-index as a primary metric. In controlled synthetic experiments with a fixed CoxPH model, oracle C-index drops from 0.634 (random censoring) to 0.609 (dependent censoring, τ=0.75), while oracle IBS worsens from 0.090 to 0.245. The censored-data metric errors (Figure 5) show that standard Harrell's CI and IPCW-IBS estimates become increasingly biased as censoring deviates from randomness, and can even suggest improvement when true oracle performance is degrading.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper, so there are no model-vs-model baseline comparisons. Instead, the paper evaluates seven common survival metrics (Harrell CI, Uno CI, Antolini CI, IBS, MAE, D-Calibration, Log-Likelihood) against five proposed desiderata (Table 1). Only IBS satisfies the proper-scoring-rule criterion (D1) among practical metrics; all C-index variants fail D1, D4, and D5. In the controlled experiment, Harrell's CI and naive IBS (no IPCW) show the largest errors under dependent censoring, while Uno's CI (IPCW with KM) partially mitigates bias under random/independent censoring but remains biased under dependent censoring. The key finding is that no commonly used metric remains unbiased under dependent censoring, creating a systematic model-metric mismatch for the growing class of copula-based and proximal-inference survival models that explicitly target this setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes and reframes a scattered body of critiques on survival evaluation metrics—Hartman et al. (2023) on C-index pitfalls, Blanche et al. (2019) on C-index impropriety, and Rindt et al. (2022) on proper scoring rules—into a unified 'Ladder Hypothesis' that ties model assumptions, metric validity, and censoring assumptions into a single consistency requirement. It extends the IPCW-based evaluation literature (Uno et al., 2011; Graf et al., 1999) and the copula-based dependent-censoring modeling work (Emura & Chen, 2018; Liu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024a) by explicitly pointing out that model development has outpaced metric development: models now handle dependent censoring, but no broadly accepted evaluation metrics do. The structured survey of 92 papers (2023–2025) builds on Zhou et al. (2023)'s observation of C-index dominance but adds a finer-grained assessment of metric-objective alignment and censoring-assumption justification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ~72% of recent survival papers use metrics misaligned with their stated objectives, and that standard metrics can suggest model improvement when true performance is degrading under dependent censoring, raises a fundamental question about the reliability of published survival-model comparisons. The practical recommendations (align metric to objective, state censoring assumptions, evaluate from multiple angles) are immediately actionable and could shift community norms. The central open question—developing evaluation metrics that remain unbiased under dependent censoring without unverifiable copula-family assumptions—remains unresolved, and the paper argues this should be the next methodological frontier. A limitation is that the survey skews toward ML-oriented venues, and the controlled experiment uses a single model (CoxPH) on synthetic Weibull data, so the magnitude of metric distortion in real-world settings with diverse architectures is not fully characterized.
