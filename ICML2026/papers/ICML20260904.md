## Problem

Existing LLM-based adaptive elicitation methods optimize which questions to ask but treat the respondent pool as fixed, ignoring the practical bottleneck that only a small fraction of a population can be reached under participation budgets. In survey and collective-assessment settings, both the number of questions and the number of completed responses are constrained, and the resulting sparse, partially observed data breaks standard fixed-questionnaire pipelines. No prior framework jointly selects both queries and respondents across multi-turn interactions while propagating partial observations to impute missing responses.

## Input / Output

At each round the method consumes the running interaction history, a candidate question set, and a heterogeneous graph whose three node types (respondents, demographic-feature bins, query-choice pairs) encode observed links and demographic attributes, and it outputs a single selected query, a diverse subset of k respondents to contact, and GNN-imputed responses for every unqueried member. The imputed responses are concatenated with the observed ones and appended to the interaction history, while new edges are added to the graph, so each round's output becomes the next round's input in a closed feedback loop. Final evaluation is prediction accuracy on a disjoint set of held-out questions never queried during elicitation, reported under fixed respondent and query budgets (e.g., 10%, 30%, 50% of the population).

## Method

The framework couples a meta-trained LLM (Llama-3.1-8B fine-tuned with LoRA on autoregressive response prediction) that scores each candidate query by expected information gain over held-out questions, with a heterogeneous R-GCN that propagates messages across a three-type graph to impute missing responses and select respondents. At each round, the LLM computes EIG for every remaining candidate query by sampling simulated future responses, and the GNN clusters member embeddings to pick k diverse respondents whose observations will most reduce aggregate group uncertainty. After collecting the k responses, the GNN performs message passing to impute answers for all unqueried members, and these imputed values are fed back into the LLM's interaction history for the next round's EIG computation. The paper proves near-optimality of the greedy joint selection under submodularity plus a query-budget alignment assumption (Theorems 4.1–4.2), and justifies the LLM's predictive role via a de Finetti-style martingale convergence argument showing the limiting predictive distribution recovers the latent entity.

## Results

Across three real-world opinion datasets (CES, OPINIONQA, TWIN-2K), the proposed method consistently outperforms all baselines in predictive accuracy on held-out target questions under constrained respondent budgets. The headline result is a >12% relative accuracy gain on CES at a 10% respondent budget, with improvements ranging from 17.1% at round 1 to 12.6% by round 4 over the strongest baseline (Meta-Greedy-Imp). Gains are most pronounced in early rounds and for highly sensitive respondents, reaching up to 20% relative recovery under a 50% budget. Calibration metrics (Brier Score, Perplexity) also improve consistently, with the gap widening as more elicitation rounds are performed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines that progressively add complexity: Meta-Random (random query selection, no imputation), Meta-Greedy (greedy expected-information-gain query selection via a meta-trained LLM, no imputation), and Meta-Greedy-Imp (greedy EIG plus independent per-respondent LLM imputation of missing answers). The proposed method (joint greedy query + group-relational respondent selection + heterogeneous GNN imputation) wins on all three datasets and all budget levels, with the largest margins at low budgets (10%) where information is scarcest. Notably, Meta-Greedy-Imp does not consistently outperform Meta-Greedy, demonstrating that LLM-based imputation without explicit population-level relational structure is unreliable and can even degrade calibration. The ablation in Figure 5 confirms that GNN-based propagation (vs. no imputation) contributes the largest single-component gain, while greedy query selection over random adds a smaller but consistent improvement. Multi-step planning (N=3 rollouts over top-10 candidates) yields at most marginal and inconsistent gains over simple greedy selection, validating the theoretical near-optimality guarantee (Theorem 4.1) and the practical preference for the cheaper greedy strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the individual-level adaptive elicitation framework of Wang et al. (2025), which uses a meta-trained LLM to greedily select queries maximizing expected information gain, to the group setting by jointly selecting both queries and respondent subsets under budget constraints. The imputation and propagation mechanism is adapted from Suh et al. (2025), who showed that a heterogeneous GNN can match or surpass LLM baselines for group response prediction via link prediction on a member-feature-choice graph. The theoretical grounding draws on the de Finetti/martingale posterior framework of Fong et al. (2024) and Ye & Namkoong (2024) to justify the LLM's predictive inference role, and extends classic submodular optimization guarantees (Krause et al., 2008) to the joint user-query selection problem. The key conceptual departure from prior work is the explicit coupling of 'who to ask' with 'what to ask' through graph-informed respondent selection, rather than treating the respondent pool as fixed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap in survey methodology and collective assessment: under tight budgets, deciding which respondents to contact is as critical as which questions to ask, yet prior adaptive elicitation methods ignore this dimension. The finding that LLM-based imputation without relational structure (Meta-Greedy-Imp) does not reliably outperform no imputation highlights that population-level propagation is essential, not optional, for efficient group inference. Open questions include whether the submodularity and query-budget alignment assumptions hold in truly dynamic or adversarial populations, how the method scales to very large groups (tens of thousands), and whether the regional split (South→West) generalization protocol captures the full complexity of distributional shift in real deployments.
