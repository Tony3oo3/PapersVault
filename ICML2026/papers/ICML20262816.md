## Problem

Vafa et al. (2025) showed that GPT-2-scale transformers trained on planetary motion data achieve high predictive accuracy yet fail to encode gravitational forces in their internal representations, leaving it unclear why and how to fix this failure. This paper asks: what specific inductive biases are missing from generic transformers that prevent them from discovering the Newtonian world model, and can minimal architectural changes suffice?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262816/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a sequence of 2D position coordinates (x, y) of a planet orbiting a central body at the origin, sampled at fixed time intervals Δt = 0.2, and autoregressively predicts the next position. Two input formulations are compared: (1) tokenized, where each coordinate is discretized into V uniform bins over [-L, L] and treated as a next-token prediction problem, and (2) continuous, where raw (x, y) values are fed directly with MSE regression loss. The context length (number of past positions visible to the model) is the key architectural knob, ranging from 2 to 100, and determines whether the model learns a Newtonian or Keplerian world model.

## Method

The paper systematically introduces three minimal inductive biases into a GPT-2-scale autoregressive transformer. First, spatial smoothness is restored by replacing tokenization with continuous coordinate inputs (regression), eliminating the need to learn a spatial map from randomly initialized embeddings. Second, spatial stability is achieved via noisy context learning: Gaussian noise (σ ≈ 0.1) is injected into historical positions during training, forcing the model to correct small deviations and preventing catastrophic error accumulation at inference. Third, temporal locality is imposed by restricting the attention context to only the two most recent positions, matching the second-order nature of Newtonian mechanics. Linear probing of internal activations then reveals a sharp phase transition: short context (2) yields a Newtonian model with near-perfect linear encodings of gravitational force (R² ≈ 0.999), while long context (100) yields a Keplerian model encoding orbital geometry (semi-major/minor axes, Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector) with R² ≈ 0.998. The paper further derives scaling laws for spatial map emergence in the tokenized setting, showing that vocabulary size V is the dominant factor (1−R² ∝ D^{-1.15} V^{1.33}).

## Results

On the Kepler dataset, regression transformers (continuous coordinates, MSE loss, noisy context σ=0.1) consistently outperform classification transformers (discretized tokens, cross-entropy loss) in mean distance error across all training sizes when each method's best hyperparameter is selected. For world-model identification via linear probing, context length 2 yields a Newtonian representation with R²≈0.999 for gravitational force variables (F, F_x, F_y), while context length 100 yields a Keplerian representation with R²≈0.998 for orbital geometric parameters (semi-major axis a, semi-minor axis b, Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector A_x, A_y). The spatial map quality in Vafa et al.'s GPT-2-scale model (V=7000, N=768) achieves only R²≈0.86, and the scaling law 1−R² ≈ 0.52·D^(−1.15)·V^(1.33) (fit R²≈0.995) shows that reducing vocabulary size is the most effective lever for spatial map emergence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the classification (next-token prediction) formulation from Vafa et al. (2025), which uses V=7000 discretized tokens with cross-entropy loss. The paper's regression formulation (continuous coordinates, MSE loss, noisy context learning) consistently achieves lower mean distance error than the best classification model across all training data sizes (Figure 5, right panel). However, when the regression model is naively trained without context noise (σ=0), it underperforms the best classification model at large training sizes—precisely the regime Vafa et al. evaluated. The paper further shows that even after fixing both spatial smoothness and stability, the Newtonian world model (R²≈0.999 for force) only emerges when context length is reduced to 2; at the default long context length (100), the model instead learns a Keplerian geometric model (R²≈0.998 for orbit parameters), demonstrating that temporal locality is a necessary third inductive bias beyond what Vafa et al. addressed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly addresses the negative result of Vafa et al. (2025), who showed that GPT-2-scale transformers trained on 20B tokens of planetary motion data fail to encode Newtonian gravitational forces in their internal representations. It also draws on the noisy context learning technique from Ren et al. (2025) to stabilize autoregressive regression. Conceptually, the paper positions itself between two existing threads: the 'AI physicist' literature (Wu & Tegmark, Brunton, Cranmer, etc.), which recovers symbolic laws but relies on strong domain-specific priors like sparsity or graph structure, and the world-models/interpretability literature (Ha & Schmidhuber, Gurnee & Tegmark), which studies what representations emerge in generic architectures. The key departure is showing that three minimal, domain-agnostic inductive biases—spatial smoothness, spatial stability, and temporal locality—are sufficient to make a generic transformer discover Newtonian mechanics without any physics-specific architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean, mechanistic explanation for why generic transformers fail at physics discovery and offers actionable architectural fixes (reduce vocabulary or use continuous inputs, add context noise, restrict context length). The phase transition between Keplerian (global, geometric) and Newtonian (local, force-based) world models controlled by context length is a useful conceptual tool for thinking about what 'understanding' means in learned models. However, significant limitations remain: verification relies on linear probing (implicit knowledge only), the setup is restricted to 2D single-timescale Kepler orbits, and the paper does not demonstrate autonomous symbolic law extraction. Open questions include whether these inductive biases generalize to more complex physical systems, whether the Keplerian-to-Newtonian transition persists in 3D or multi-body settings, and how to build the 'interpreter' head needed for a truly autonomous AI physicist.
