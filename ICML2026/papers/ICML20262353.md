## Problem

Best-of-N test-time scaling relies on verifiers (reward models, LLM judges) to select the best response from N candidates, but practical verifiers are imperfect, heterogeneous in quality, and conditionally dependent in ways that violate standard independence assumptions. Existing ensembling approaches either treat all verifiers equally (naive ensemble, majority vote) or require ground-truth labels for calibration (WEAVER, logistic regression), yet acquiring such labels is expensive and often infeasible. The paper asks whether one can construct a data-adaptive verifier ensemble that accounts for differing verifier strengths and dependencies using zero ground-truth labels.

## Input / Output

FUSE takes as input N LLM-generated responses to a fixed query and an N×m score matrix where each column corresponds to a different verifier (reward model or LLM judge) that may output real-valued or binary scores. The method outputs a single selected response index. It supports two operating modes: query-conditional (using only the score matrix for one query) and batched (vertically concatenating score matrices across multiple queries before learning the ensemble). The method requires no ground-truth labels at any stage, though it does require access to multiple distinct verifier models.

## Method

FUSE proceeds in three steps. First, it adaptively binarizes each verifier's real-valued scores using verifier-specific thresholds τ* found by coordinate descent to minimize an empirical TCI-violation statistic (the sample variance of third-order-to-second-order covariance tensor ratios across all verifier triplets), thereby promoting the weaker triplet conditional independence (TCI) assumption without requiring labels. Second, it applies the method-of-moments spectral algorithm of Jaffe et al. (2015) to the transformed binary score matrix to recover query-specific sensitivity, specificity, and class-imbalance estimates from second- and third-order covariance tensors. Third, it constructs pseudo-labels by averaging posterior correctness probabilities computed from all verifier triplets (using the estimated parameters), then fits a parametric ensemble (e.g., logistic regression on the original score matrix) by maximizing estimated accuracy against these pseudo-labels. The key distinction from prior spectral ensembling work is that FUSE bypasses the stronger joint conditional independence (JCI) assumption—empirically violated in LLM verification—by using TCI only for parameter estimation and then optimizing an arbitrary ensemble family against the resulting pseudo-labels.

## Results

On Best-of-100 test-time scaling with Llama 3.3 70B Instruct, FUSE achieves 66.8% on GPQA, 64.4% on GPQA Diamond, 92.8% on MATH500, 94.1% on MMLU, and 91.4% on MMLU Pro, matching or exceeding the semi-supervised WEAVER baseline (66.4%, 64.1%, 93.4%, 94.9%, 90.2%) in 27 of 40 comparisons across both 70B and 8B settings. On the frontier Humanity's Last Exam benchmark (Best-of-50), FUSE reaches 54.3% accuracy, outperforming WEAVER (51.2%) and naive ensemble (51.4%), which is below the Pass@1 baseline of 52.1%. On IMO Shortlist (Best-of-50), FUSE matches the naive ensemble at 63.8%, while WEAVER achieves 62.1%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against WEAVER (Saad-Falcon et al., 2026), the leading semi-supervised verifier ensemble method, FUSE achieves near-parity in the 70B setting (e.g., 66.8% vs 66.4% on GPQA, 91.4% vs 90.2% on MMLU Pro) while using zero ground-truth labels, and decisively outperforms it in the 8B setting on MATH500 (83.9% vs 74.8%) and MMLU Pro (69.8% vs 67.2%). FUSE consistently and substantially outperforms the naive ensemble baseline across all settings (e.g., 66.8% vs 60.1% on 70B GPQA, 83.9% vs 75.2% on 8B MATH500), with the sole exception being IMO Shortlist where near-conditional independence of verifiers makes naive ensemble effectively optimal. Against the classical unsupervised baselines Dawid & Skene (1979) and Jaffe et al. (2016) (reported in Table 9), FUSE uniformly outperforms both across all benchmarks and model sizes (e.g., 66.8% vs 54.3% for DS and 56.0% for DCL on 70B GPQA), confirming that the gains are not merely from recognizing the relevance of the unsupervised ensembling literature but from the adaptive TCI-violation-minimizing transformation and pseudo-label-based ensemble optimization.

## Relation to Prior Work

FUSE adapts the spectral method-of-moments framework from the unsupervised ensemble learning literature (Parisi et al., 2014; Jaffe et al., 2015) to the LLM Best-of-N response selection setting, a connection not previously drawn. It departs from Jaffe et al. (2015) in two key ways: (1) it introduces adaptive score transformations (learned via coordinate descent on an empirical TCI-violation statistic) to mitigate conditional dependence violations rather than assuming them, and (2) it bypasses the stronger joint conditional independence assumption by using estimated posterior probabilities as pseudo-labels to optimize arbitrary ensemble rules (e.g., logistic regression). In the LLM verification context, FUSE positions itself as a zero-label alternative to semi-supervised methods like WEAVER (Saad-Falcon et al., 2026) and to simple unweighted aggregation (naive ensemble, majority vote), drawing on the broader weak-supervision and crowdsourcing literature (Dawid & Skene, 1979; Ratner et al., 2017) while explicitly addressing the conditional dependence that causes those methods to fail in practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that FUSE achieves parity with semi-supervised baselines (WEAVER, logistic regression) that require 5% ground-truth labels, while using zero labels—addressing the core cost bottleneck in real-world LLM verification pipelines. The method's query-conditional operation also makes it robust to task heterogeneity and distribution shift, a property that fixed-weight ensembles lack. Key limitations include the computational overhead of running multiple distinct verifier models and the requirement for verifier diversity (the method is most impactful when verifiers are heterogeneous). Open directions the authors identify include data filtering for synthetic training, unsupervised model ranking, and benchmark/ground-truth auditing, suggesting the method's utility extends well beyond test-time scaling.
