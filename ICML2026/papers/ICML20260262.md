## Problem

Existing population dynamics inference methods hard-encode the minimal-kinetic-energy gauge by restricting the learned velocity field to gradient form (u_t = ∇s_t), which is suboptimal when the true dynamics contain essential rotational or non-gradient components (e.g., Hamiltonian systems, incompressible fluids). In such cases, enforcing gradient structure produces highly oscillatory, pathological potentials that are difficult to represent and learn, degrading distributional accuracy. The paper identifies that the continuity equation constrains only the flux divergence ∇·(ρ_t u_t), leaving a ρ_t-weighted divergence-free gauge freedom that prior methods neither expose nor exploit.

## Input / Output

The method takes independent cross-sectional samples {x_{t_k}^{(i)}}_{i=1}^N drawn from time-marginal densities ρ_{t_k} at K+1 discrete time points, with no trajectory information or sample correspondence across times. It outputs a time-dependent velocity field u_θ(x, t) parameterized by a neural network (MLP or UNet backbone), from which representative trajectories are generated by integrating the flow ODE (or an SDE variant with small noise ε). All input variables are normalized to [-1,1] per dimension, and the velocity field operates in this normalized space; for high-dimensional turbulence (d=128×128 vorticity fields), an autoencoder first embeds into a 16×16×1 latent space before NGIF is applied.

## Method

NGIF formulates population dynamics inference through a weak (moment-based) continuity equation: for a set of random Fourier feature test functions φ_r(x) = sin(ω_r^⊤x), cos(ω_r^⊤x) with multiscale bandwidths, the loss enforces d/dt E_{ρ_t}[φ_r] = E_{ρ_t}[∇φ_r · u_t] in a dual-relative sense, with moments and their time derivatives precomputed via smoothing splines. Crucially, this weak-form loss constrains only the flux-divergence part of u_t, leaving the ρ_t-weighted divergence-free gauge freedom untouched. Rather than resolving this non-uniqueness by restricting the parameterization to gradient fields, NGIF applies an explicit, freely chosen gauge regularizer—kinetic energy (‖u‖²), curl (antisymmetric Jacobian norm), or divergence (‖∇·u‖²)—as a soft penalty with weight λ. This decoupling makes the inductive bias a transparent modeling choice: the divergence regularizer, for instance, naturally recovers incompressible tracer dynamics, while the curl regularizer suppresses rotation without hard-coding a potential. The velocity field is optimized with standard stochastic gradient descent (Adam) over minibatches of time indices and spatial samples, with all Fourier-feature evaluations (values, gradients, Laplacians) available in closed form at O(d) cost per evaluation.

## Results

On the Vlasov-Poisson benchmarks (Table 1), all three NGIF variants outperform every gradient-restricted baseline across all three test cases. The best NGIF variant achieves relative error 0.043 (two-stream, NGIF-Curl), 0.143 (bump-on-tail, NGIF-Kin), and 0.542 (strong Landau, NGIF-Kin), compared to the best baseline DICE at 0.070, 0.283, and 0.735 respectively. On the tracer particle experiment, NGIF-Div produces the lowest total variation distance to ground-truth particle histograms over time, and on the high-dimensional turbulence simulation, NGIF-Kin accurately captures the enstrophy distributional decay.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All three NGIF variants (Kin, Curl, Div) outperform every gradient-restricted baseline on all three Vlasov-Poisson benchmarks. Against DICE (Blickhan et al., 2025), the closest competitor, NGIF-Curl reduces two-stream error from 0.070 to 0.043 (39% improvement) and NGIF-Kin reduces bump-on-tail error from 0.283 to 0.143 (50% improvement) and strong Landau error from 0.735 to 0.542 (26% improvement). Against HOAM (Berman et al., 2024), NGIF-Kin improves two-stream from 0.078 to 0.062 and bump-on-tail from 0.427 to 0.143. Against AM (Neklyudov et al., 2023), the gains are even larger: NGIF-Curl improves two-stream from 0.275 to 0.043, and AM fails entirely (NaN) on strong Landau while NGIF-Kin achieves 0.542. The key structural difference is that all baselines hard-encode gradient-field parameterization (u_t = ∇s_t), whereas NGIF learns general vector fields with explicit gauge regularizers, enabling it to capture rotational dynamics that gradient fields represent only through highly oscillatory, hard-to-learn potentials.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the weak (distributional) form of the continuity equation—already used implicitly in action matching (Neklyudov et al., 2023), DICE (Blickhan et al., 2025), and JKO-based methods (Bunne et al., 2022)—but makes the gauge freedom explicit rather than silently fixing it to the minimal-kinetic-energy/gradient-field gauge. All prior population-dynamics-inference methods hard-encode u_t = ∇s_t by parameterizing a scalar potential, which the authors show can produce pathological oscillatory targets for rotational dynamics (Gigli, 2011). NGIF instead parameterizes the full velocity field and introduces a freely chosen gauge regularizer (kinetic energy, curl, or divergence), drawing on the ρ_t-weighted Hodge–Helmholtz decomposition to justify that the weak continuity loss constrains only the compressible component. The random Fourier feature test functions connect to kernel mean embedding and MMD literature, but the objective is a time-dependent transport constraint rather than a static two-sample discrepancy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical impact is that for systems with essential rotational structure (Hamiltonian dynamics, incompressible fluids, tracer advection), the default gradient-field gauge is not merely suboptimal but actively harmful, producing highly oscillatory potentials that are difficult to learn and amplify approximation errors. By making the gauge a transparent modeling choice, NGIF lets practitioners inject physical priors (e.g., incompressibility) directly into the learned velocity field, which the paper demonstrates improves distributional accuracy across Vlasov-Poisson instabilities, turbulent tracer transport, and high-dimensional vorticity evolution. Open questions include how many random Fourier test functions are needed for informative constraints in very high dimensions, whether Jacobian-based gauges (curl, divergence) scale beyond the 6D Vlasov-Poisson setting tested here, and whether adaptive or constrained formulations of the gauge penalty can eliminate the need for manual λ tuning.
