## Problem

Prompts used to deploy LLMs in public-sector settings encode role instructions, decision framings, and value claims that can materially shift outputs, yet existing governance artefacts (Model Cards, Datasheets, post-training alignment) do not make local prompt collections transparent, contestable, or auditable. The paper argues that prompts should be treated as governed commons rather than private, transient inputs, and proposes a concrete repository-and-process design to operationalise this claim.

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline takes 50 short streetscape vignettes as fixed inputs, combined with prompt templates drawn from a 443-prompt community collection (augmented to 3,317 via paraphrase and scenario expansion) under one of three governance-state configurations. Outputs include a 3-way class label per vignette (vehicle-throughput, active-transport, or mixed/compromise), a 7-point acceptability score from 12 blinded raters across six stakeholder groups, and a time-to-remediation metric (hours) from a synthetic 50-item incident log per governance state. The governance state itself acts as a configuration parameter that changes which prompts are admissible and how they are aggregated, making it an input to the pipeline rather than a post-hoc annotation.

## Method

Prompt Commons is a versioned repository design that treats prompt templates as governed artefacts with provenance metadata (author group, locale, value claim, consent level), enforceable merge rules, and three escalating governance states: open (any authenticated contributor), curated (maintainer review plus inclusion constraints across groups and locales), and veto-enabled (adds a formal quarantine mechanism where designated representative organisations can block release pending time-boxed review). The pilot instantiates these states as repository workflows and evaluates them by fixing a single instruction-tuned LLM (temperature 0, top-p 1, max 256 tokens) and comparing five prompt-sampling methods, from a single-author baseline through a negotiation ensemble (M4) that samples k=6 prompts stratified by author group and applies a versioned aggregation instruction to surface conflicts and propose a compromise. The negotiation ensemble is the key technical mechanism: it treats competing stakeholder prompts as proposals and uses an explicit, versioned aggregation prompt as a procedural operator, making the handling of disagreement a documented and contestable artefact rather than an implicit model behaviour. Governance states are implemented as enforceable repository rules (merge conditions, release checklists, veto records) rather than informal policies, drawing on Ostrom's commons principles and open-source governance practice.

## Results

On the 50-vignette contested-choice benchmark (fixed model, temperature 0), the single-author baseline (M0) yields 24% mixed/compromise labels (commitment D = 0.76), while all governed commons states (M1–M3) shift the mixed rate to 48–52% (D ∈ [0.48, 0.52]); the negotiation ensemble (M4) achieves D = 0.49. Mean subgroup acceptability (7-point scale) rises from 4.35 ± 0.86 (M0) to 4.92 ± 0.44 (curated, M2) and 5.48 ± 0.66 (veto-enabled, M3), with cross-group Gini dispersion dropping from 0.096 to 0.043. In the synthetic 50-incident log, mean time-to-remediation falls from 30.5 h (open) to 11.8 h (curated) and 5.6 h (veto-enabled). The authors stress these are task-specific descriptive statistics from a single-model, single-benchmark pilot, not general effect-size estimates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baselines; instead it contrasts five internal governance configurations (M0–M4) under a fixed model, rubric, and input set. The single-author prompt (M0) serves as the de facto baseline: it produces the highest commitment (D = 0.76), the lowest and most dispersed subgroup acceptability (4.35 ± 0.86, Gini 0.096), and no structured remediation workflow. Moving to any governed commons state (M1–M3) roughly doubles the mixed/compromise rate (24% → 48–52%) and reduces cross-group dispersion by more than half (Gini 0.096 → 0.043 for curated). The veto-enabled state (M3) further improves mean acceptability to 5.48 ± 0.66 and cuts synthetic remediation time to 5.6 h versus 30.5 h under the open state. The negotiation ensemble (M4) lands at D = 0.49, consistent with the curated range, confirming that aggregation does not collapse toward a single objective-priority label. The authors explicitly frame all shifts as falsifiable, task-specific descriptive statistics rather than universal performance gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions prompt-layer governance as a distinct accountability surface that sits between model-level alignment (RLHF, Constitutional AI) and dataset-level documentation (Model Cards, Datasheets), arguing that neither upstream nor downstream artefacts capture the deployment-time prompt templates that operational teams actually use. It adapts Ostrom's commons governance framework and polycentric institutional design to a digital repository setting, drawing on open-source governance practice (versioning, pull requests, licensing) and social-choice theory for aggregation rules. The key departure from prior work is treating prompts not as transient engineering inputs but as governed, versioned, contestable artefacts with enforceable provenance, inclusion constraints, and veto mechanisms—governance primitives that existing prompt-sharing repositories and model documentation standards do not provide.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a genuine gap in public-sector AI governance: prompt templates that function as de facto policy instruments circulate informally outside formal review, making accountability fragment across model providers, vendors, and institutions. The pilot (N=50 vignettes, single model, synthetic incident log) is explicitly illustrative and does not establish general effect sizes, leaving open whether governed prompt releases produce meaningful output shifts at scale or across diverse tasks. Key open questions include whether the observed compromise-rate shifts (24%→48–52%) hold under different task formulations, whether transparency of prompt text increases attack surface in practice, and whether the governance overhead is proportionate for smaller deployments. For public-sector practitioners, the procurement and licensing implications—treating prompt collections as citeable release objects in contracts—could reshape vendor accountability if adopted.
