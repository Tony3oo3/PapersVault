## Problem

Group-based RL methods for multi-turn agentic tasks (e.g., GRPO, GiGPO) assign credit at the trajectory level: every step in a successful trajectory receives positive reward and every step in a failed trajectory is penalized, regardless of individual step quality. The paper shows this is fundamentally misaligned—approximately 22% of steps in failed trajectories actually advance the task, while about 65% of steps in successful trajectories do not—leading to noisy, coarse credit signals and inefficient learning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260684/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a group of M parallel rollout trajectories (each up to T steps) from an LLM or VLM agent interacting with a text-based or vision-based environment. States are defined using only the deterministic components of environment observations (e.g., current location, items held, admissible action set) so that identical states across different trajectories can be merged into a single graph node. The agent's output at each step is a reasoning trace enclosed in think tags followed by a single admissible action in action tags; the policy is updated via a clipped PPO-style objective with a KL penalty against a reference policy.

## Method

GraphGPO aggregates all M rollout trajectories into a unified directed state-transition graph, merging identical states into shared nodes and representing each step as a directed edge with an associated cost. It then computes the shortest-path distance d(s) from every state to the goal state via Dijkstra's algorithm, and defines a graph-based step-level reward R^G(s,a,s') = r_succ * omega^(d(s')+c(s,a)) that discounts the success reward by how far the successor state still is from the goal. Outgoing edges from the same state are grouped to estimate a normalized graph-based advantage, which is combined with the standard episode-level advantage to form the final per-step signal. Two propositions establish that this advantage is monotone in progress toward the goal and has lower conditional variance than trajectory-based step feedback. The entire graph construction and shortest-path computation adds only about 0.04% overhead per training iteration, preserving the critic-free, low-memory properties of group-based RL.

## Results

On ALFWorld, GraphGPO achieves 92.71% overall success rate with Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and 95.31% with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, surpassing GiGPO (90.88% / 94.27%) and GRPO (77.86% / 83.33%). On WebShop, GraphGPO reaches 78.65% and 80.31% success rate for the 1.5B and 7B models respectively, outperforming GiGPO (73.83% / 78.38%) and GRPO (71.35% / 75.00%). On the vision-based Sokoban benchmark, GraphGPO achieves 86.98% success rate with Qwen2.5-VL-3B, exceeding GiGPO (76.92%) and GRPO (67.1%) by 10.06% and 19.88% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GraphGPO consistently outperforms all RL-based baselines across all three benchmarks. Against GRPO, GraphGPO gains 14.85% (1.5B) and 11.98% (7B) on ALFWorld overall success rate, and 7.30% (1.5B) and 5.31% (7B) on WebShop success rate. Against GiGPO, the closest competitor, GraphGPO leads by 1.83% (1.5B) and 1.04% (7B) on ALFWorld, 4.82% (1.5B) and 1.93% (7B) on WebShop, and 10.06% on Sokoban. Against PPO and RLOO, the margins are substantially larger (e.g., 38.31% over PPO on ALFWorld 1.5B). Against closed-source prompting baselines (GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Pro), GraphGPO with a 1.5B model surpasses GPT-4o by 44.71% on ALFWorld and 54.95% on WebShop success rate, and exceeds Gemini-2.5-Pro by 32.41% on ALFWorld and 42.75% on WebShop. On Sokoban (VLM setting), GraphGPO outperforms GRPO by 19.88% and GiGPO by 10.06%, demonstrating the benefit of graph-based credit assignment in deterministic, visually-grounded environments.

## Relation to Prior Work

GraphGPO extends the group-based RL lineage for LLM agents (GRPO → GiGPO) by replacing trajectory-level credit attribution with a graph-based formulation. While GRPO assigns uniform episode-level advantages to all steps in a trajectory and GiGPO refines this to step-level groups sharing the same state, both still derive step quality from the final trajectory outcome. GraphGPO departs from this by aggregating all rollouts into a unified state-transition graph and computing step-level rewards via shortest-path distances to the goal (Dijkstra), thereby decoupling credit assignment from individual trajectory success. The method remains critic-free and adds only a single Dijkstra pass per iteration, preserving the low-memory, scalable properties of group-based RL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and well-motivated limitation in multi-turn agentic RL: the misalignment between trajectory-level outcomes and step-level contributions, quantified by the finding that ~22% of steps in failed trajectories still advance the task while ~65% of steps in successful trajectories do not. The negligible computational overhead (0.04% of per-iteration runtime) and single hyperparameter (ω) make GraphGPO a practical drop-in improvement for existing group-based RL pipelines. Open questions include robustness in highly stochastic or high-dimensional observation spaces where state matching degrades (partially addressed via embedding-based matching), scalability to very large state spaces, and whether the deterministic-environment assumptions in the theoretical guarantees (Propositions 4.1–4.2) hold in practice.
