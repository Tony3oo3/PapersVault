## Problem

AI development has converged on a growth-by-scaling paradigm that prices out academics and smaller companies while driving rapidly increasing energy consumption and carbon emissions. Existing AI governance approaches are predominantly regulatory and do not directly incentivize companies to develop or deploy more efficient models. The paper identifies the absence of market-based mechanisms that could simultaneously reduce emissions and create new revenue streams for smaller, more efficient AI developers.

## Input / Output

The framework takes as policy inputs a governing-body-set benchmark B (watts per FLOP efficiency standard), a per-company assistance factor C_i (adjustable for clean energy use, prior violations, or anti-monopoly penalties), and each company's measured FLOP output O_i and FLOP-efficiency E_i. It produces annually allocated AI Allowances (power units) per company via A_i = O_i × B × C_i, which translate into a hard FLOP cap F_i = A_i/E_i for inference, and enables a secondary market where surplus allowances are traded at a price b per FLOP. Unlike a predictive model, the 'output' is a market mechanism: the allowance quantity, the binding constraint, and the emergent market price that together steer company behavior.

## Method

The paper proposes a cap-and-trade system applied specifically to AI inference FLOPs (not training), where a governing body freely allocates AI Allowances via benchmarking to avoid 'AI leakage' (companies relocating to avoid costs). The core analytical contribution is a game-theoretic model in which each company maximizes utility u(x) = -x^{-k} - ax, balancing the performance gain from more FLOPs against linear energy costs, solved via KKT conditions under the constraint x + y ≤ F_i. Theorem 1 derives the no-governance equilibrium x* = (k/a)^{1/(k+1)}, while Theorem 2 shows that under cap-and-trade the equilibrium shifts to x* = (k/(a+b))^{1/(k+1)} with y* = F_i - x*, provably reducing FLOP usage (Remark 1). Efficient companies that use fewer FLOPs than their allocation can sell surplus allowances, creating a new revenue stream that offsets the competitive disadvantage of lacking large-scale compute infrastructure.

## Results

The paper's main quantitative contribution is a pair of closed-form equilibrium results. Without governance, a rational agent's optimal FLOP usage is x* = (k/a)^(1/(k+1)) (Theorem 1). Under the proposed cap-and-trade system, the equilibrium shifts to x* = (k/(a+b))^(1/(k+1)) (Theorem 2), where b > 0 is the per-FLOP buy/sell price. Since a + b > a, Remark 1 establishes that cap-and-trade provably reduces FLOP usage. Figures 1 and 2 illustrate this across varying cost-per-FLOP values a (with fixed b = 1×10⁻² and scaled b = √a), confirming FLOP reduction and showing that company utility can also improve under certain parameter regimes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper with theoretical analysis rather than an empirical study, so there are no standard method-vs-method baselines. The central comparison is between the no-governance equilibrium (Theorem 1) and the cap-and-trade equilibrium (Theorem 2): the cap-and-trade system always yields a strictly lower FLOP usage because the effective marginal cost increases from a to a + b. The paper also situates its proposal relative to alternative market-based mechanisms discussed in Section 4 (Pigouvian taxes, user fees, credits/subsidies, deposit-refund systems, and credit programs), arguing that cap-and-trade is uniquely suited because it caps total FLOPs while creating a tradable asset (AI Allowances) that generates revenue for efficient smaller companies. No empirical benchmarks or head-to-head method comparisons are provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the cap-and-trade mechanism from environmental economics (EU ETS, California, China) into the AI domain, where no prior work has proposed a tradable-permit system specifically for FLOP management. It draws on the Porter hypothesis (Porter & Linde, 1995) and Stavins' (2003) survey of market-based environmental instruments to justify why efficiency incentives outperform blunt taxation, and on Tomei et al. (2025) and Casper et al. (2025) for the broader call toward market-based AI governance. The DeepSeek case study (Liu et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025) is used as empirical motivation showing that compute constraints can drive efficiency innovation (Mixture-of-Experts, Multi-head Latent Attention), but the paper's own contribution is the formal game-theoretic model (KKT-based equilibrium analysis) proving that a cap-and-trade constraint provably reduces optimal FLOP usage relative to an unconstrained setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses two pressing and intertwined problems—AI accessibility (academics and small companies priced out by GPU costs) and sustainability (projected doubling of data-center electricity by 2030)—by proposing a concrete, implementable market mechanism rather than abstract regulatory language. However, as a position paper with only a two-theorem theoretical analysis and no empirical or simulation validation beyond toy plots, the practical viability of the framework (benchmark calibration, enforcement, international coordination, interaction with existing chip-export controls) remains entirely open. A key unresolved question is whether capping inference FLOPs would inadvertently slow the pace of capability improvements that the authors themselves acknowledge may be necessary for national security, and how the benchmark B would be set without creating regulatory capture by incumbent firms.
