## Problem

While grokking research has documented that generalization coincides with the emergence of low-dimensional structured representations, the mechanism determining *why* these representations take their specific geometric forms remains unexplained. Optimization-centric accounts (weight decay, circuit efficiency) explain compression but not the particular shape of the resulting geometry. This paper proposes that intrinsic task symmetries—commutativity, associativity, triangle equality, transitivity—are the causal driver that both triggers generalization and dictates the specific embedding geometry.

## Input / Output

Across three task families, inputs are discrete structured pairs: two integers in Z_p (modular arithmetic, p=113), two vertices in a graph (path, cycle, cylinder, hypercube, 2D/3D lattice), or two entities with a relational predicate (2D/3D attribute spaces). Outputs are discrete predictions: the arithmetic result mod p, the geodesic distance between vertices, or a binary label indicating whether a pairwise relation holds. Supervision is deliberately partial—only 30% of the Cayley table for modular arithmetic, only 20% of entity pairs for comparison—so that generalization requires the model to infer structure beyond memorized examples.

## Method

The paper identifies the intrinsic symmetry for each task domain and tracks its acquisition during training via a symmetry-violation metric (symmetric KL divergence between predictions on algebraically equivalent paths). This reveals a consistent three-stage dynamic: memorization, symmetry acquisition (where generalization emerges at a soft threshold), and geometric organization (where weight decay drives low-rank collapse into structured geometry). Theoretically, Pontryagin duality is used to prove that commutativity and associativity force embeddings into a closed helix in a high-dimensional torus, while triangle equality and transitivity induce graph-like and grid-like structures respectively. A reconstruction result shows that only O(2/p) fraction of the Cayley table suffices to recover the full operation under cyclic group symmetry. Practically, the authors propose symmetry-prompting auxiliary losses (enforcing commutativity, associativity, triangle equality, or transitivity directly) and geometry-prompting priors (nuclear norm, entropy, Lipschitz regularization) that accelerate grokking, validated on single-layer decoder-only Transformers (d_model=128, 4 heads) and MLPs.

## Results

Across modular arithmetic (x+y), graph metric completion (lattice), and comparison (2D) tasks, symmetry-prompting losses (commutativity/associativity, triangle equality, transitivity) consistently reduce time-to-generalization relative to the baseline and all other acceleration methods. Geometry-prompting priors (nuclear norm, entropy, Lipschitz) also improve convergence over baseline but are described as 'systematically unstable' compared to symmetry prompting. In MLPs trained without weight decay, symmetry prompting reduces time to generalization by up to two orders of magnitude. The symmetry-based criterion exhibits a soft threshold: below it, test accuracy reliably reaches 1.0 (p = 9.5×10⁻⁷ for modular addition across 20 seeds).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its symmetry-prompting and geometry-prompting strategies against three baselines: (1) the vanilla training baseline, (2) GrokFast (Lee et al., 2024), an optimizer-based method that amplifies slow-varying gradients, and (3) geometry-prompting priors (nuclear norm, entropy, Lipschitz regularization). Symmetry-prompting wins on all three task families (modular addition, lattice graph completion, 2D comparison), consistently reaching 100% test accuracy earlier than both the baseline and GrokFast. Geometry-prompting alone also beats the baseline but is less reliable than symmetry prompting. GrokFast, while effective in some regimes, is found to slow training in others, indicating sensitivity to task structure. The authors further show that suppressing symmetry acquisition (negative weight on the symmetry loss) prevents the model from reaching perfect generalization, providing interventional evidence that symmetry is causally necessary rather than merely correlated.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the grokking literature initiated by Power et al. (2022) and the mechanistic interpretability analyses of Nanda et al. (2023), which identified low-dimensional structured geometry (circular/helical embeddings) as a hallmark of generalization. However, it departs from the prevailing optimization-centric narrative (weight decay, circuit efficiency, implicit bias) by arguing that intrinsic task symmetries—commutativity, associativity, transitivity, triangle equality—are the causal driver that determines the specific form of the learned geometry, with weight decay playing only a secondary stabilizing role. The paper also extends the testbed beyond the standard modular arithmetic setting to graph metric completion and comparison tasks, and introduces symmetry-prompting and geometry-prompting losses as practical acceleration tools, complementing prior methods like GrokFast (Lee et al., 2024) and nuclear-norm regularization (Notsawo et al., 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By identifying symmetry acquisition as the causal mechanism for generalization (rather than a correlate of weight decay), the paper provides a principled, data-centric explanation for why specific geometric structures emerge in embedding space, unifying observations across algebraic, structural, and relational domains. The practical symmetry-prompting loss offers a straightforward way to accelerate grokking without architectural changes, and the soft-threshold criterion gives a testable predictor of when generalization will occur. Key limitations include the reliance on known symmetries (making direct application to open-ended tasks difficult), the restriction to controlled algorithmic settings, and the open question of whether these dynamics transfer to large-scale language modeling where governing invariances are unknown or approximate.
