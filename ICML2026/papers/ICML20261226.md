## Problem

Mechanistic interpretability seeks to reverse-engineer the algorithms transformers implement, but dense models suffer from superposition, distributing concept representations across many entangled neurons and residual channels. Existing approaches (SAEs, transcoders) recover circuits by first learning an abstract basis, so the resulting circuits may reflect the chosen abstractions rather than the model's true mechanisms. This paper asks whether training models with weight sparsity from scratch can yield circuits that are directly interpretable at the lowest level of abstraction, without intermediate learned representations.

## Input / Output

The method takes Python code as pretraining data and, for circuit extraction, a trained weight-sparse model plus one of 20 hand-crafted binary next-token prediction tasks (e.g., predicting whether to close a string with a single or double quote). It produces a minimal sparse circuit defined over maximally granular nodes—individual neurons, attention Q/K/V channels, and residual channel reads/writes—connected by nonzero weight entries (edges). Circuits are validated as both necessary (mean-ablating circuit nodes severely degrades task loss) and sufficient (ablating all non-circuit nodes preserves task loss). For the bridges extension, the input is an existing dense model plus a jointly trained sparse model, and the output is interpretable perturbations of the dense model's residual stream activations.

## Method

The core contribution is training GPT-2-style decoder-only transformers where the L0 norm of all weights is constrained to be small (roughly 1 in 1000 weights nonzero), enforced by zeroing all but the top-k magnitude entries in each weight matrix after every AdamW step, with L0 annealed from fully dense to the target over the first 50% of training. Mild activation sparsity (AbsTopK keeping 1 in 4 nonzero values) is applied at every node location. For circuit extraction, the paper introduces a structured pruning algorithm that learns per-node boolean masks via a Heaviside step function with a sigmoid-derivative surrogate gradient, minimizing a joint objective of task cross-entropy and circuit size (number of active nodes). The resulting circuits are validated by demonstrating both necessity and sufficiency through mean ablation. For explaining existing dense models, the paper trains a weight-sparse model jointly with per-sublayer linear bridges (encoders and decoders) using NMSE reconstruction losses and hybrid forward-pass KL losses, enabling interpretable perturbations in the sparse model to be mapped back to the dense model.

## Results

The headline quantitative result is that weight-sparse models yield circuits that are roughly 16× smaller (in geometric mean number of edges) than circuits extracted from dense models of comparable pretraining loss, averaged across 20 hand-crafted Python code tasks. The main interpretability metric is the geometric mean circuit size (number of edges) at a fixed target task loss of 0.15 nats. Scaling total parameter count (while holding L₀ fixed) strictly improves both capability (pretraining loss) and interpretability (circuit size), shifting the Pareto frontier outward. The paper also demonstrates that the identified circuits are both necessary and sufficient: ablating only the circuit nodes severely degrades task loss, while ablating all non-circuit nodes preserves it.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between weight-sparse and dense transformer models of matched pretraining loss: sparse models produce circuits ~16× smaller across all 20 tasks (Figure 2), and this gap persists at every target loss level. The paper also benchmarks its TopK weight-sparsity enforcement against the differentiable L₀ regularization of Louizos et al. (2018) on a toy language modeling task (Appendix D, Figure 35), finding that TopK consistently achieves lower loss across all sparsity levels, confirming that the simpler hard-thresholding approach outperforms the HardConcrete-based soft relaxation. Additionally, the learned structured pruning algorithm (using sigmoid-derivative surrogate gradients through a Heaviside step function) is compared against an attribution-based pruning baseline (selecting top nodes by gradient attribution to task loss); the learned method finds substantially smaller circuits at every loss target (Figure 31), demonstrating that gradient-based mask optimization is far more effective than first-order attribution for isolating minimal circuits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant SAE-based circuit-finding paradigm (Marks et al., 2024; Ameisen et al., 2025), which learns a sparse activation basis and then traces circuits within it, by instead training transformers de novo with L0 weight sparsity so that circuits are inherently disentangled. It builds on sparse training techniques (Zhu & Gupta, 2017; Evci et al., 2021) and gradient-based mask learning for pruning (Cao et al., 2021), but repurposes them specifically for interpretability rather than compression. The bridges mechanism for coupling a sparse model to a dense model's activations is a novel extension that connects this de novo approach to the broader goal of explaining already-trained frontier models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that weight-sparse models yield circuits roughly 16× smaller than dense counterparts at matched loss, with individual circuits as small as 12 nodes and 9 edges that are fully human-interpretable—a level of granularity and clarity not previously achieved. The preliminary bridges results suggest a practical path to transferring these interpretable circuits to existing dense models, which is critical given that sparse models are 100–1000× less compute-efficient and unlikely to reach frontier scale. Key open questions include whether interpretability survives scaling beyond tens of millions of nonzero parameters, whether polysemantic features can be resolved by increasing model width, and whether mean-ablation-based faithfulness can be upgraded to full causal scrubbing.
