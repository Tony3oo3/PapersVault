## Problem

Existing multivariate time series forecasting methods face a fundamental trade-off: channel-isolation strategies fragment cross-variable information, while channel-interaction strategies (e.g., dense self-attention or clustering) introduce spurious dependencies among spectrally unrelated variables. No prior method can selectively permit information exchange only between variables with compatible oscillatory patterns while strictly zeroing out interactions with incompatible ones.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264715/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a multivariate time series window X ∈ R^{T×N} (T=96 look-back, N variables ranging from 7 to 321) and produces P-step-ahead forecasts Y ∈ R^{P×N} with P ∈ {96, 192, 336, 720} for long-term or {12, 24, 48} for short-term horizons. The input is first standardized via RevIN and decomposed into trend and seasonal components via a moving-average kernel; the final prediction is the sum of a linear trend branch and a spectral-interaction seasonal branch, mapped back to the original scale by inverse RevIN.

## Method

Crisp extracts spectral priors by applying FFT and a low-pass filter to the seasonal component, then constructs a Dynamic Resonance Topology M via cosine similarity of learned spectral embeddings. This topology is added as a bias to the attention score matrix, and an α-Entmax activation (Tsallis entropy family) with an adaptively inferred α ∈ (1,2) from a spectral-context hypernetwork strictly forces attention weights below a dynamic threshold to exactly zero, guaranteeing that spectrally incompatible neighbors receive no interaction. A Spectrum-Gated Feature Filtration module then refines the post-interaction representations by element-wise gating with frequency-conditioned sigmoid gates. A gradient analysis shows that non-resonant neighbors receive zero back-propagated gradient through the interaction pathway, providing a formal mechanism for noise isolation.

## Results

Crisp achieves the best or near-best MSE and MAE across 10 real-world multivariate time series datasets at look-back 96. On ETTh1 (horizon 96), Crisp attains MSE 0.376 / MAE 0.392, matching SOFTS on MSE and trailing by only 0.001 on MAE, while substantially outperforming CrossGNN (MSE 0.382) and Crossformer (MSE 0.423). On high-dimensional datasets like Electricity (321 variables) and Solar (137 variables), Crisp leads by a wide margin—MSE 0.133 vs. 0.141 (CycleNet) on Electricity and 0.188 vs. 0.209 (CycleNet) on Solar—where dense-interaction baselines degrade severely. The authors report state-of-the-art performance on over 90% of all evaluated MSE/MAE metrics across all horizons (96–720).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Crisp achieves the best MSE on all five representative datasets at horizon 96, with the closest competition coming from SOFTS and FilterNet on small-scale ETT datasets (gaps ≤ 0.004 MSE). On ETTh1, SOFTS ties Crisp on MSE (0.376) and edges it by 0.001 on MAE (0.391 vs. 0.392); on ETTm1, SOFTS is marginally better on MAE (0.339 vs. 0.343). The advantage widens dramatically on high-dimensional data: on Electricity (321 variables), Crisp's MSE of 0.133 beats the next-best CycleNet (0.141) by ~6%, while SOFTS collapses to 0.858; on Solar (137 variables), Crisp (0.188) outperforms CycleNet (0.209) by ~10%. Dense-interaction baselines—CrossGNN, Crossformer, and iTransformer—consistently trail by 5–20% in MSE, confirming that unfiltered channel mixing introduces harmful spurious dependencies. Among clustering-based methods (DUET, TimeFilter, TQNet from Table 2), Crisp maintains a lead on most horizons, with TimeFilter being the strongest competitor on short-term METR-LA tasks where patch-level dynamic interactions are most beneficial.

## Relation to Prior Work

Crisp sits at the intersection of two established lines of work in multivariate time series forecasting: channel-interaction strategies (iTransformer, Crossformer, TimeFilter, DUET, TQNet) that model cross-variable dependencies via attention or clustering, and frequency-domain methods (Fedformer, FilterNet, FreTS) that exploit spectral structure for temporal modeling. Its key departure is repurposing α-Entmax (Peters et al., 2019) — originally designed for sparse sequence-to-sequence models — as a strict-blocking mechanism whose sparsity is guided not by learned similarity alone but by a spectral coherence topology derived from FFT-based amplitude spectra. This contrasts with prior clustering-based approaches (TimeFilter's KNN, TFPS's subspace clustering) that restrict interactions based on time-domain or embedding-space similarity, which the authors argue can group spectrally heterogeneous variables into pseudo-homogeneous clusters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The spectral-consistency-as-interaction-gate principle offers a physically motivated alternative to ad-hoc similarity-based grouping, and the gradient-blocking analysis (Section 4.4) provides a clean theoretical justification for why non-resonant variables cannot corrupt representations through backpropagation. The demonstrated robustness to high missing-value rates is a practical advantage over dense-attention baselines that misinterpret missing patterns as signal. However, the authors acknowledge that discrete Fourier spectra may be insufficient for event-driven, abrupt, or non-periodic cross-variable effects, and the method's reliance on a fixed low-pass filter ratio (K%) limits adaptability to datasets with dominant high-frequency dynamics. Whether spectral coherence generalizes beyond the tested domains (electricity, traffic, weather) to more irregular systems remains an open question.
