## Problem

Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) are vulnerable to corruption attacks where a single compromised agent injects misleading information that propagates contagiously through the system, corrupting benign agents and producing false outputs. Existing graph-based defenses model agents as nodes but are limited to static topologies or rely solely on local signals (e.g., per-agent classifiers), failing to capture how corrupted information propagates globally to the final decision. This leaves a gap for a dynamic, training-free defense that can adapt to evolving attacks and changing MAS structures.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the full MAS interaction log across T communication rounds, reformulated as a temporal signed DAG where each edge e_ij carries a discrete contribution score g_ij ∈ {-1, 0, 1} assigned by an external LLM scorer (independent of the MAS) that judges whether the receiver agreed with, ignored, or contradicted the sender. The final MAS answer serves as the boundary condition for backward propagation. The output is a per-agent contribution score, a detected malicious-agent set M identified via pairwise deviation threshold ε, and a repaired graph with all outgoing edges from agents in M pruned. Detection and repair are performed per query with no offline training.

## Method

BPD (Backward Propagation Detection) models MAS communication as a signed directed acyclic graph and computes each agent's contribution to the final decision via a single topological backward pass, analogous to a signed layer-wise PageRank. Specifically, an external LLM assigns each communication edge a score g_ij ∈ {-1, 0, 1} (contradiction, neutral, agreement), and a row-normalized signed propagation operator B = D^{-1}G is applied layer-by-layer from the terminal round backward, with boundary initialization S(A_T(i)) = +1 or -1 depending on whether the agent's answer matches the final decision. Because the graph is a DAG, the recursion admits a unique solution in one pass without iterative convergence. Agent-level scores are averaged across rounds, and agents whose pairwise deviation Δ(i) exceeds threshold ε are flagged as malicious; their outgoing communication edges are then pruned to repair the graph. This global, per-query analysis eliminates the need for retraining when topology or attacker identity changes, distinguishing BPD from static-graph classifiers and local-signal detectors.

## Results

On MMLU with GPT-4o, BPD achieves 85.4% (Flat) and 87.5% (Hierarchy) average answer accuracy under corruption attacks, exceeding the strongest baseline (G-Safeguard) by 1.7 and 2.0 percentage points respectively. For malicious-agent detection (monitor ACC), BPD reaches 90.7% (Flat) and 93.3% (Hierarchy), outperforming G-Safeguard by 2.4 and 5.6 points. On text-based response datasets, BPD reduces BLEURT score degradation from 15.7–15.8% to 1.3–1.5% and GPT-score degradation from 8.8–11.9% to 0.6–0.8%. Against the hardest Modification attack, BPD secures 16% and 10% accuracy gains on Flat and Hierarchy, while the best baseline (G-Safeguard) achieves only 7%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BPD consistently outperforms all three baseline families across both MAS structures. Against G-Safeguard (Wang et al., 2025), the strongest graph-based baseline, BPD gains 1.7 pp on Flat answer ACC (85.4% vs. 83.7%) and 2.0 pp on Hierarchy (87.5% vs. 85.5%), while the monitor-ACC gap widens to 2.4 pp (Flat) and 5.6 pp (Hierarchy), reflecting BPD's global propagation signal versus G-Safeguard's local classifier. Against AgentXposed (Xie et al., 2025), BPD leads by 2.9 pp (Flat) and 5.5 pp (Hierarchy) on answer ACC, with the gap largest on Chemistry and Security subdomains where output-similarity detection is weakest. Against the Challenger and Inspector collaborative defenses (Huang et al., 2025), BPD achieves 4.7–6.4 pp higher answer ACC, and the advantage grows under Modification attacks where these peer-review methods drop to 76–78% while BPD holds at 86–90%. In dynamic-graph settings where topology and attacker identity shift over time, baselines degrade by ~3 pp on average while BPD preserves its static-graph performance (88% Flat, 85% Hierarchy), confirming the benefit of per-query backward propagation over fixed trained classifiers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the graph-based defense paradigm for multi-agent systems (MAS) by reformulating agent communication as a signed directed acyclic graph and applying a PageRank-style backward propagation to compute each agent's global contribution to the final decision. It departs from G-Safeguard and similar topology-guided methods that rely on trained classifiers over local signals, and from output-similarity defenses (AgentForest, BlockAgents) that are vulnerable to subtle textual perturbations. The key conceptual shift is replacing iterative, local detection with a single topological backward pass over a signed DAG, eliminating the need for offline training and enabling per-query adaptation to changing topologies and attacker identities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BPD offers a training-free, interpretable defense that adapts to dynamic MAS topologies where static defenses degrade, making it practically relevant as MAS deployments grow in complexity and frequency. However, the method's reliance on an external LLM scorer introduces a single point of failure that adaptive attacks can partially exploit (Section 4.6–4.7), and the empirically fixed threshold ε limits generalizability across domains. Open questions include whether the signed DAG formulation scales to very large agent populations, how to automate threshold selection, and whether the defense holds under coordinated multi-agent Byzantine attacks exceeding the single-attacker assumption.
