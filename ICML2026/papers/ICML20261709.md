## Problem

Classical kernel regression theory relies on fixed spectral assumptions (polynomial eigenvalue decay) and signal regularity (source conditions) to derive minimax rates, but these assumptions are difficult to justify when the kernel itself is learned adaptively during training. The paper asks how the spectral ordering induced by a learned kernel affects estimation difficulty, and whether a complexity measure can characterize minimax risk without requiring eigen-decay or source conditions.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a target signal θ*, a kernel spectrum λ (eigenvalues with an associated ordering), and a noise variance σ² as inputs, and produces the Effective Span Dimension (a single integer k) and the span profile (a nonincreasing function mapping noise level τ to ESD). The ESD is a population-level descriptor, not an algorithmic hyperparameter: it identifies the smallest number of leading eigencoordinates needed so that the remaining signal energy falls below kσ². In the adaptive setting, the output additionally includes the time-dependent learned eigenvalues  λ̃(t) produced by over-parameterized gradient flow, from which a pathwise ESD trajectory d†(t) is computed.

## Method

The core contribution is the Effective Span Dimension (ESD), defined as the smallest k such that the average squared tail of θ* (ordered by decreasing eigenvalue) falls below σ², which is the bias-variance crossing point of the oracle-tuned principal component estimator. The paper proves that over ESD-bounded classes F_{K,λ}^{(σ²)}, the minimax risk is exactly σ²K (Theorem 3.3), recovering classical rates under source conditions while remaining valid for arbitrary learned spectra. The span profile D_{θ*,λ}(τ) = d†(τ; θ*, λ) tracks how ESD varies with noise level, enabling comparison of different spectra' alignment with the same signal. For adaptive learning, the paper analyzes over-parameterized gradient flow (OP-GF) with a fixed eigenbasis and depth parameter D, proving that under a signal dichotomy and spectral-margin conditions, OP-GF reorders learned eigenvalues to reduce ESD (Theorem 5.2), thereby moving the target into an easier minimax class.

## Results

The central theoretical result (Theorem 3.3) establishes that the minimax excess risk over the ESD-bounded class F_{K,λ}^{(σ²)} scales as σ²K, with matching upper and lower bounds. In the infinite-dimensional asymptotic setting (Theorem 4.3), the minimax optimal convergence rate over the span-profile-bounded class F_{K,λ} is σ₀²Kₙ/n, recovering the classical rate σ₀²n^{-sβ/(1+sβ)} when source conditions and polynomial eigen-decay hold (Example 3.5). Numerically, OP-GF training with D=0, 1, or 3 layers reduces both the ESD and the oracle-tuned PC estimation error over training time; deeper models (D=1, 3) achieve lower final ESD values than the shallow model (D=0) given sufficient iterations, and the improvement is most pronounced at higher misalignment (q=3 vs. q=1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without a standard benchmark comparison table, so 'baselines' are classical complexity measures and prior theoretical frameworks rather than competing algorithms. ESD recovers the classical minimax rate n^{-sβ/(1+sβ)} under source conditions and polynomial eigen-decay (Example 3.5), confirming it is at least as general as the traditional framework. In contrast to signal-agnostic measures such as the effective dimension d_eff(ν) (Zhang, 2005) and the effective rank k* (Bartlett et al., 2020), ESD depends on the target signal allocation and can decrease when adaptive training reorders eigenvalues to better match the signal, whereas d_eff and k* remain unchanged under such reordering (Appendix A.2). In the numerical experiments, the aligned case (q=1) shows negligible span-profile improvement during OP-GF training, while the misaligned cases (q=1.5, 2.5, 3) show progressive downward shifts in the span profile, with q=3 yielding the largest reduction—demonstrating that ESD captures the alignment effect that fixed-kernel theory cannot. Among depths, D=0 shows the earliest initial ESD decrease, but D=1 and D=3 surpass it at later training times, suggesting that over-parameterization facilitates better spectral adaptation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the over-parameterized gradient flow (OP-GF) eigenvalue-learning model of Li & Lin (2024, 2025), which learns kernel eigenvalues while fixing eigenfunctions, and on the classical minimax theory for spectral sequence models (Johnstone, 2017) and RKHS regression under source conditions and polynomial eigen-decay (Yao et al., 2007). It departs from both by repurposing the bias-variance crossing—classically a stopping rule for a fixed inverse operator (Blanchard & Mücke, 2018)—as a population-level, signal-dependent complexity measure (the ESD) that is well-defined for any kernel, including learned ones, and by proving minimax rates over ESD-bounded classes without requiring source or eigen-decay assumptions. The framework also distinguishes itself from signal-agnostic measures (effective dimension, effective rank) and from target-kernel alignment scores (Cristianini et al., 2001; Canatar et al., 2021) by encoding the noise-indexed alignment between a specific target and a specific spectral ordering.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ESD framework provides a unified lens that recovers classical minimax rates under standard assumptions while remaining meaningful for learned kernels where those assumptions are unavailable, offering a rigorous mechanism (ESD reduction) for why adaptive feature learning improves generalization. Key limitations include the population-level nature of ESD (not directly usable as a training signal), the restrictive conditions in the OP-GF ESD-reduction theorem (Theorem 5.2), and the absence of a general theorem for kernels with evolving eigenfunctions. Open questions include designing practical ESD surrogates for algorithmic use, extending the ESD-reduction guarantee to deep networks with evolving feature bases, and connecting the framework to the rich-regime behavior observed in finite-width neural networks.
