## Problem

Natural policy gradients improve optimization by pre-conditioning with the inverse Fisher matrix, but practical use is limited by the cost of estimating and inverting this matrix. Existing approaches either rely on conjugate-gradient solvers with Fisher-vector products (computationally expensive and difficult to tune) or on structured approximations like KFAC that depend on architecture-specific factorizations. The paper asks whether natural policy gradients can be computed through standard backpropagation alone, without explicit Fisher construction, iterative linear solvers, or architecture-dependent assumptions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes on-policy state-action samples partitioned into mini-batches of size B, the current policy parameters θ, and per-sample advantage estimates. A key structural detail is that the per-sample score vectors ∂/∂θ log π(a|s;θ) are collected to form a B×p matrix H_τ per mini-batch, and the only matrix inversion performed is on a B×B matrix (λI + H_τ H_τ^T), which is independent of the parameter count p. The output is a set of transformed advantages Ã_j(s,a) that, when plugged into a PPO-like surrogate loss and differentiated via autograd, yield a gradient approximating the Tikhonov-regularized natural policy gradient, which is then used to update θ with ℓ₂-norm clipping.

## Method

RAT's central insight is a Woodbury-based reformulation: the Tikhonov-regularized natural policy gradient (λI + H^TΣH)^{-1}H^TΣy can be rewritten as H^TΣ·ỹ where ỹ = (λI_n + HH^TΣ)^{-1}y, meaning the NPG equals a vanilla policy gradient with a transformed advantage. Since the n×n inversion is infeasible for large state-action spaces, RAT approximates ỹ via randomized block Kaczmarz iterations over on-policy mini-batches: at each step j, a batch τ_j is sampled and the update g_j = g_{j-1} + H_{τ_j}^T(λI + H_{τ_j}H_{τ_j}^T)^{-1}(y_{τ_j} − H_{τ_j}g_{j-1}) performs a local advantage transformation requiring only a B×B solve. The resulting transformed advantages are then used in a PPO-like surrogate objective whose gradient is computed by a single standard backpropagation pass, eliminating the need for Fisher-vector products, conjugate-gradient inner loops, or Kronecker-factored curvature approximations. The paper proves linear convergence of the Kaczmarz iterations to the regularized NPG under full column rank and state-action coverage assumptions, with an error floor proportional to advantage estimation noise in the stochastic setting.

## Results

On MuJoCo continuous control with shared actor-critic networks, RAT achieves the best final episodic returns on 6 of 7 tasks, with particularly large gains on Ant (2926.6 vs. PPO's 1373.9, a 2.1× improvement) and Walker2d (3156.0 vs. PPO's 2225.3). On the Procgen visual control benchmark (8 tasks, 64×64 RGB observations), RAT delivers comparable or higher episodic returns than both PPO and ACKTR across all environments. Per-update wallclock time for RAT is approximately 10–20 ms, roughly 2× faster than FVP+CG (~20 ms) while substantially outperforming it in final performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PPO, RAT wins on 6 of 7 MuJoCo tasks with shared networks, with the largest margins on Ant (2926.6 vs. 1373.9) and Walker2d (3156.0 vs. 2225.3); the two methods are essentially tied on Hopper (2334.6 vs. 2346.8). Against ACKTR, RAT dominates on every task, most dramatically on Ant (2926.6 vs. 23.4) and Swimmer (271.6 vs. 59.1), where ACKTR fails to learn effectively. Against Sophia (diagonal Fisher), RAT outperforms by wide margins on all tasks, confirming that capturing parameter correlations is critical. In the separate actor-critic setting (Figure 3), RAT consistently outperforms KFAC and FVP+CG across Walker2d, HalfCheetah, Ant, and Humanoid, while also being ~2× faster per update than FVP+CG (10 ms vs. 20 ms). On Procgen, RAT matches or slightly exceeds both PPO and ACKTR across all 8 visual control tasks without the training instabilities observed in KFAC-based methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

RAT sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: (1) natural policy gradient methods that avoid explicit Fisher inversion via Hessian-free solvers (TRPO/FVP+CG) or structured approximations (KFAC, ACKTR), and (2) Woodbury-based reformulations of regularized natural gradients (Amari et al. 2000; Guzmán-Cordero et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2024). The key departure is that instead of using Woodbury to directly approximate the inverse Fisher or to accelerate a solver, RAT absorbs the curvature entirely into a transformed advantage and then solves the resulting least-squares problem via randomized block Kaczmarz iterations, reducing each step to a single backpropagation through a PPO-like surrogate. This makes RAT architecture-agnostic and compatible with shared actor-critic networks, where FVP+CG is inapplicable and KFAC requires careful gradient partitioning. The method is distinct from SPRING (Goldshlager et al. 2024) in that RAT performs multiple inner Kaczmarz iterations per rollout without a momentum interpretation, and from Guzmán-Cordero et al. (2025) in that it unifies actor and critic updates through a pseudo-advantage formulation rather than merging separate curvature-adjusted gradients.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RAT offers a practically attractive middle ground between the simplicity of PPO and the principled geometry of natural gradients: it requires no conjugate-gradient inner loops, no architecture-specific Fisher factorizations, and no manual gradient partitioning for shared networks, while still delivering curvature-corrected updates. The convergence guarantees (linear convergence with an error floor under standard coverage assumptions) provide theoretical grounding that is absent from many heuristic natural-gradient implementations. Key limitations include the O(pB²) cost of forming H_τ H_τ^T (potentially a bottleneck for very large policies), the restriction to on-policy settings, and dependence on minibatch conditioning for convergence rate. Extending RAT to off-policy or replay-buffer settings, and developing low-rank or sketch-based approximations for the minibatch kernel, are natural next steps.
