## Problem

Conventional synchronization robustness is characterized by scalar per-node stability indices (e.g., single-node basin stability, SNBS) that require choosing a perturbation distribution and discard spatial information about where in the perturbation space instability arises. This paper introduces the upstream task of predicting full stability landscapes—image-like heatmaps over the perturbation space per node—which are strictly richer than scalar scores and enable downstream tasks like contingency screening. It also pioneers a graph-to-image prediction paradigm, learning per-node image targets directly from graph topology, a formulation not previously established in the literature.

## Input / Output

The model takes an undirected graph with a single binary node feature per vertex (power injection P_i ∈ {−1,+1}) and a designated node index i, and produces a 20×20 single-channel heatmap in [0,1]^{20×20} representing the empirical stability probability in each cell of the discretized phase–frequency perturbation space (φ, φ̇) ∈ [−π,π]×[−15,15]. The ground-truth heatmaps are derived by down-sampling 10,000 Monte Carlo perturbation outcomes per node onto the regular grid, so each cell value is the fraction of stable trajectories among those falling in that cell. The scalar SNBS is recovered as a weighted average of the heatmap entries, making the landscape backward-compatible with prior scalar prediction tasks.

## Method

The architecture is an encoder–decoder pipeline: a message-passing GNN (TAG with 5 layers and K=3-hop polynomial filters, or DBGNN with 2 layers and 10 internal node-to-line/line-to-node propagation steps) encodes the full graph topology into per-node embeddings, from which the designated node's embedding is extracted and passed to a lightweight decoder (a 2-layer MLP or a 3-layer transposed-convolutional CNN with channels 64→32→16) that reshapes the output into the 20×20 heatmap. Training is supervised with a single MSE loss on the heatmap; a key theoretical result (Theorem A.1) proves via Jensen's inequality that this pixel-level MSE upper-bounds the downstream SNBS error, eliminating the need for an auxiliary scalar loss term. The approach is validated on two newly released datasets of 10,000 graphs each (20 and 100 nodes) generated from second-order Kuramoto oscillator simulations (~500,000 CPU hours), achieving up to 86.7% SSIM in-distribution, 81% SSIM under a 20→100 node size shift, and up to 85% SSIM zero-shot on four real European power grids, while reducing per-graph evaluation from thousands of CPU hours to under 2 seconds.

## Results

On the primary task of predicting 20×20 stability landscape heatmaps, the best model (DBGNN-CNN) achieves 86.69% SSIM in-distribution (tr20ev20) and 84.90% SSIM (tr100ev100), with 81.03% SSIM under the out-of-distribution size shift from 20 to 100 nodes. Zero-shot transfer to four real power grids (Germany, France, GB, Spain) yields up to 85.38% SSIM (GB) using TAG-CNN. On the downstream SNBS recovery task, the landscape-based approach achieves R² of 83.99–89.16% in-distribution, comparable to direct scalar prediction baselines. Inference processes 1,500 grids in under 2 seconds, replacing thousands of CPU hours of Monte Carlo simulation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four direct SNBS-prediction baselines from prior work: ArmaNet, GCNNet, and TAGNet (Nauck et al., 2023) and DBGNN (Nauck et al., 2024b). On the downstream SNBS task, the landscape-based approach slightly underperforms the strongest direct-prediction baseline (DBGNN: 85.68/90.08/73.73% R²) by roughly 1–3 percentage points across in-distribution and OOD settings (e.g., DBGNN-MLP: 83.99/89.16/70.66%). However, the proposed method clearly outperforms the weaker baselines GCNNet (70.74/75.19/58.24%) and is competitive with ArmaNet and TAGNet. The authors attribute the small gap to the added spatial-precision requirement of locating unstable regions within the landscape, whereas scalar prediction only needs to estimate their total area. The key trade-off is that the landscape-based approach yields the full 20×20 heatmap (enabling contingency screening and other downstream analyses) at only a minimal SNBS accuracy cost, while reducing evaluation from thousands of CPU hours to seconds per graph.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of GNN-based basin stability prediction established by Nauck et al. (2022b, 2023), which predicted a single scalar SNBS value per node from graph topology. The key conceptual departure is replacing the scalar output with a full 20×20 spatial stability landscape per node, reframing the problem as a graph-to-image regression task that the authors claim has not been established in prior literature. The GNN encoders (TAG, DBGNN) and the underlying second-order Kuramoto model are inherited directly from the prior work, while the CNN/MLP decoder and the landscape-based loss are new components. Theorem A.1 formally connects the new pixel-level MSE objective to the classical SNBS error, ensuring backward compatibility with the scalar prediction task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The landscape formulation enables contingency screening—identifying not just whether a node is vulnerable, but in which perturbation direction—opening a practical tool for grid operators that scalar indices cannot provide. The released datasets (20,000 graphs with per-node landscape labels, ~500,000 CPU hours of simulation) fill a gap in publicly available benchmarks for spatial stability prediction. Key limitations include the homogeneous Kuramoto model with uniform coupling, small real-grid test sets (98–438 nodes), and the observation that DBGNN generalizes poorly to real topologies compared to TAG, suggesting the approach has not yet been stress-tested on heterogeneous or large-scale systems. Open questions include whether the graph-to-image paradigm transfers to other dynamical systems (neuroscience, ecology) and whether more sophisticated decoders (diffusion models, VAEs) can close the remaining performance gap.
