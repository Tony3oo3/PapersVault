## Problem

Existing agent frameworks treat external tools as ordinary actions optimized for task success or reward, offering no principled criterion for distinguishing epistemically necessary interaction from unnecessary delegation. This gap leads to common failure modes—overthinking and overacting—that are poorly explained as isolated reasoning or execution errors. The paper asks when external tool use is actually justified and argues that unnecessary delegation not only causes inefficiency but actively suppresses the development of internal reasoning capability, reshaping the long-term trajectory of agent intelligence.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a task specification q and an evolving interaction history τ_t (a sequence of prior actions and observations), and at each step must decide whether to invoke an internal cognitive tool (reasoning, reflection) or an external physical tool (search, API call, UI action). The decision is mediated by a context-conditioned internal solvability estimate p_t^int(q,m;W) compared against a policy-dependent threshold α, making the output a stochastic, context-dependent classification rather than a fixed decision boundary. The framework additionally characterizes the epistemic effort allocation E_int + E_ext, which is invariant across policies and can only be redistributed between internal and external resolution, not eliminated.

## Method

The Theory of Agent (ToA) reconceives agents as tool-use decision makers under epistemic uncertainty by partitioning the standard POMDP action space into internal cognitive tools (querying the agent's own world model, leaving environment state unchanged) and external physical tools (querying the environment, potentially changing state), with the distinction encoded as an annotation on the action space rather than a modification of the POMDP itself. It defines the internal task set Q^int(m,W) and world task set Q^world(W), whose separation constitutes the agent's knowledge boundary, and introduces population-relative extensions (Q_min, Q_max) for multi-agent settings. The central formal object is epistemic effort E(q,m) = E_int + E_ext, proven to be an invariant structural requirement that no successful policy can eliminate—only reallocate—between internal reasoning and external interaction. Tool-use decisions are formalized as belief-based classification using context-conditioned solvability estimates, and the paper derives normative propositions showing that systematic over-delegation causes capability stagnation (Proposition 3.9) and that alignment should be defined as effort-consistent decision making rather than correctness alone. The framework yields concrete implications for training (agentic midtraining with next-tool prediction, SFT tailored to internal competence, RL with process-level rewards beyond task success) and evaluation (assessing effort allocation patterns rather than final-answer correctness).

## Results

This is a position paper introducing the Theory of Agent (ToA) framework and contains no empirical experiments, quantitative benchmarks, or numerical results. The contributions are entirely theoretical: formal definitions (internal/world task sets, knowledge boundary, epistemic effort), normative propositions (e.g., Proposition 3.8 on effort invariance, Proposition 3.9 on delegation-induced capability stagnation), and a belief-based decision rule (Eq. 11) for tool-use allocation. The paper's 'evidence' consists of conceptual illustrations (Figures 1, 3, 4) and qualitative arguments linking overthinking/overacting to effort misallocation rather than capability deficiency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not conduct empirical comparisons against baseline methods. In Section 5 (Alternative Views), it contrasts its epistemic-necessity criterion with three prevailing paradigms: (1) agents as planners, which lack a principled criterion for when external information is needed; (2) agents as policy learners (RL), which treat tool use as just another reward-optimized action and cannot distinguish necessary from unnecessary delegation; and (3) agents as workflow orchestrators, which prioritize execution reliability over epistemic justification. The paper argues its framework subsumes ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) as a special case where internal tool steps are monolithic, and aligns with LeCun's autonomous intelligence vision by making the decision mechanism explicit. No head-to-head performance comparisons are presented; the contribution is a normative and theoretical lens rather than a new method with measurable gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

The paper synthesizes POMDP-based agent formulations (Aström, 1965; Christianos et al., 2023), the ReAct paradigm (Yao et al., 2023), and meta-cognition literature (Dunlosky & Metcalfe, 2008) into a unified 'Theory of Agent' (ToA) framework. It departs from prior work by treating reasoning and acting as co-equal knowledge acquisition tools rather than qualitatively different pipeline stages, and by introducing epistemic necessity as a normative criterion for tool use that existing paradigms—agents as planners, policy learners, or workflow orchestrators—lack. The framework is positioned as complementary to standard decision-theoretic models and connects to LeCun's autonomous intelligence vision, while drawing on recent tool-augmented agent work (SMART, OTCPO, Search-R1) as motivating examples rather than direct predecessors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that unnecessary delegation actively suppresses internal reasoning capability development rather than merely causing inefficiency—challenges the field's dominant correctness-only evaluation paradigm and has direct implications for how agents are trained, assessed, and designed. However, as a position paper without empirical validation, the key propositions (especially Proposition 3.9 on capability stagnation) remain conjectural, and the operational solvability estimates (p_t^int) lack a concrete, validated implementation. Open questions include how to measure epistemic effort in practice, how the knowledge boundary evolves in dynamic or multi-agent environments, and whether the framework's normative prescriptions hold under resource-bounded rationality where internal reasoning cost is high relative to external call cost.
