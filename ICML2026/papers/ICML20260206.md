## Problem

Traditional comorbidity indices (Charlson, Elixhauser) are mortality-centric with fixed linear weights, so they generalize poorly to other clinical outcomes and cannot capture nonlinear diagnosis–risk interactions. The paper asks whether a single data-driven scalar score can recover a shared admission-level severity ordering that is simultaneously informative across multiple heterogeneous outcomes, and whether a principled high-severity cutoff can be derived from that ordering.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260206/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Input is a variable-length multiset of ICD diagnosis codes per admission (ICD-9 in MIMIC-III, ICD-10 in MIMIC-IV), each normalized to a 4-character prefix token and truncated to at most 256 tokens. The model outputs a single real-valued severity score per admission, oriented so that larger values correspond to higher mortality risk. The score is evaluated for general (nonlinear) dependence with four binary outcomes— in-hospital mortality, 30-day mortality, length-of-stay >7 days, and ICU transfer—using distance correlation and mutual information rather than standard classification metrics.

## Method

MLCI uses a DeepSets-style permutation-invariant encoder (token embeddings → elementwise MLP → masked mean+max pooling → MLP head) to map the diagnosis-code set to a scalar score. Training maximizes a task-weighted sum of per-outcome normalized HSIC (nHSIC), computed with an RBF kernel on scores and a delta kernel on binary labels, so the score captures nonlinear dependence with all outcomes simultaneously rather than a single-outcome likelihood. A two-stage weighting scheme (single-task nHSIC estimation → inverse-strength multi-task weights) prevents any one outcome from dominating. The theoretical analysis shows that when the stacked centered label profiles across tasks are approximately rank-one, the multi-task nHSIC objective reduces to aligning the centered score Gram matrix with a single shared admission direction v, and the optimal two-level monotone threshold is the split whose centered step vector has the largest cosine alignment with v, yielding an explicit high-severity cutoff.

## Results

On MIMIC-IV, MLCI achieves the strongest distance correlation for in-hospital mortality (54.80±1.40 ×10⁻²) and 30-day mortality (49.42±1.34 ×10⁻²), outperforming the best classical baseline (Gradient Boosted Trees at 34.54/34.58) by ~15–20 percentage points. For length of stay (>7 days), MLCI (51.15±0.88) is essentially tied with FusedLogits LR (51.02), and for ICU transfer it leads with 61.97±0.64 vs. 57.23 for FusedLogits LR. On MIMIC-III, MLCI again dominates mortality (39.06±3.27 vs. 32.28 for GBT) and 30-day mortality (37.39±2.63 vs. 31.88), but trails the DeepSets BCE baseline on ICU transfer (18.55 vs. 21.52), consistent with MIMIC-III's ICU-heavy composition.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MLCI substantially outperforms all traditional clinical indices (CCI, ECI, CCI+ECI) across every outcome and dataset, with the gap most pronounced on mortality (e.g., 54.80 vs. 19.98 dCorr for ECI on MIMIC-IV in-hospital mortality). Against classical ML baselines, MLCI leads by a wide margin on mortality outcomes: on MIMIC-IV, it exceeds the best classical method (Factorization Machine, 36.41) by ~18 points on in-hospital mortality; on MIMIC-III, it beats Gradient Boosted Trees (32.28) by ~7 points. For length of stay, MLCI is essentially tied with FusedLogits LR and GBT on MIMIC-IV (51.15 vs. 51.02/51.00) and leads on MIMIC-III (49.84 vs. 48.16). Among deep learning baselines, MLCI dominates mortality and 30-day mortality on both datasets (e.g., 74.22 vs. 69.92 MI for DeepSets on MIMIC-IV MORT), but loses on MIMIC-III ICU transfer to the DeepSets BCE baseline (18.55 vs. 21.52 dCorr), which the authors attribute to MIMIC-III's near-universal ICU admission rate making the late-transfer label less severity-driven. On MIMIC-IV ICU transfer, MLCI leads all baselines (61.97 vs. 57.23 for FusedLogits LR), confirming that the nHSIC objective captures shared severity signal most strongly for mortality-related endpoints.

## Relation to Prior Work

MLCI builds on the DeepSets permutation-invariant encoder (Zaheer et al., 2017) for aggregating unordered diagnosis-code sets, and on the normalized Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion (Gretton et al., 2005, 2007) as a kernel-based dependence measure. It departs from traditional comorbidity indices (Charlson, Elixhauser) by replacing fixed mortality-calibrated linear weights with a data-driven nonlinear score, and from standard multi-task clinical prediction (which trains separate or fused BCE heads per outcome) by using a single shared scalar optimized to maximize kernel dependence with multiple outcomes simultaneously. The theoretical contribution—a finite-sample rank-one analysis of the stacked multi-outcome label-alignment matrix yielding an explicit threshold certificate—is, to the authors' knowledge, the first such analysis in the comorbidity-scoring literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MLCI offers a principled, data-driven alternative to hand-engineered comorbidity indices that can capture nonlinear diagnosis-code interactions and produce a single interpretable severity score aligned with multiple clinical outcomes, potentially improving risk stratification and risk adjustment beyond what fixed-weight indices achieve. The theoretical framework (rank-one retention, threshold certificates) provides diagnostic tools for when a shared severity ordering is meaningful, which is valuable for clinical interpretability. Key limitations include reliance solely on diagnosis codes (missing labs, vitals, notes), the assumption that outcomes share a common severity axis (weaker for workflow-driven endpoints like ICU transfer), and the absence of prospective validation. Open questions include extending MLCI to richer EHR modalities, validating the learned thresholds in clinical decision-making, and understanding how the shared-ordering assumption degrades as outcome heterogeneity increases.
