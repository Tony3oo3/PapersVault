## Problem

Transformers have a fixed computation budget per token, limiting their ability to solve multi-step problems. Existing approaches to scale inference-time compute—Chain-of-Thought prompting and pause/filler token insertion—rely on explicit natural-language verbalization or non-adaptive, manually-placed extra streams that cannot be learned during pretraining. No prior architecture enables unsupervised, input-adaptive allocation of parallel latent computation that is trainable with only standard language modeling loss.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a standard text sequence (token embeddings, block size L=512) and produces a per-token probability distribution over a 50,304-token vocabulary. Internally, each input token may be expanded into up to κ residual streams (κ=2L or 4L in experiments), so the effective block size grows during the forward pass. At output, multiple residual streams per token are merged into a single distribution via score-weighted averaging of decoded logits (or a cheaper residual-averaging approximation for the 1.9B model). The forking budget κ can be rescaled at inference time (dynamic forking) to maintain the same κ/L ratio as training, enabling autoregressive generation at arbitrary sequence lengths.

## Method

Thoughtbubbles inserts forking layers between standard transformer blocks (at layers 3, 7, and 11). Each forking layer applies a learned function to every residual stream to produce a 'fork score' and a 'keep score,' both multiplied by a cumulative score propagated inductively from prior layers; a top-k selection over these scores determines which streams survive and which new forks are created (with a learned fork embedding added to distinguish copies). The critical training signal is residual update attenuation: both attention (QK logits and V vectors) and residual writes in every block are modulated by the cumulative scores, so the model is forced to assign high scores to streams it needs to attend to and update, which in turn drives their forking. Position encoding uses a modified RoPE where the rotation angle is divided by the fork count, placing forked copies 'closer together' in positional space. The entire architecture is trained end-to-end with only cross-entropy language modeling loss—no auxiliary supervision, no explicit CoT traces, no manual pause-token placement—making it directly applicable during pretraining.

## Results

At 1.9B parameters, Thoughtbubbles (κ=2L) achieves a validation perplexity of 12.09 after 40B tokens, substantially outperforming the GPT-2-like baseline (15.03) and already matching it at only 20B tokens (12.68). On zero-shot evaluations, the method leads on HellaSwag (54.16 vs. 47.29), ARC-Easy (42.53 vs. 38.31), ARC-Challenge (29.53 vs. 26.17), and LAMBADA (43.91 vs. 39.15) at 40B tokens. On GSM8K, Thoughtbubbles reaches 32.10 pass@1 versus 31.46 for the baseline, using only half the training token budget to achieve competitive results (31.50 at 20B tokens). Across the 150M–772M scaling suite, the method consistently achieves the lowest perplexity, with the 319M Thoughtbubbles model outperforming the 772M baseline on OpenWebText.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary GPT-2-like baseline (Radford et al., 2019 with RoPE), Thoughtbubbles wins on every metric at 1.9B scale: perplexity drops from 15.03 to 12.09 (a 19.5% relative reduction), HellaSwag improves by 6.9 points, and ARC-Challenge by 3.4 points. Critically, even at only 20B tokens (half the baseline's 40B budget), Thoughtbubbles already matches or exceeds the fully-trained baseline on perplexity (12.68 vs. 15.03), HellaSwag (50.04 vs. 47.29), and LAMBADA (39.32 vs. 39.15). Against the computation-matched 'Copy-3' and 'Copy-5' baselines (which duplicate input residuals non-adaptively), Thoughtbubbles achieves lower perplexity at every scale from 150M to 772M on both OpenWebText and peS2o (e.g., 20.19 vs. 21.20/20.90 at 772M on OpenWebText), confirming that adaptive forking outperforms uniform duplication. The one exception is BLiMP (syntax), where the method beats the parameter-matched baseline but not the computation-matched Copy-3/Copy-5 variants, suggesting dynamic parallel computation offers less benefit for purely syntactic tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of adaptive computation and latent reasoning in transformers. It departs from chain-of-thought methods (Wei et al., 2023) by replacing serial verbalized reasoning with parallel latent computation, and from pause-token approaches (Pfau et al., 2024; Goyal et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025) by making the insertion of extra residual streams dynamic, input-adaptive, and learned purely from LM loss rather than requiring manual placement or oracle positions. The forking mechanism draws conceptually on adaptive computation time (Graves, 2016) and mixture-of-depths (Raposo et al., 2024), but uniquely applies it to parallel residual-stream duplication rather than sequential depth allocation, and does so during pretraining rather than as an inference-time intervention.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key contribution is demonstrating that a transformer can learn to allocate extra parallel computation to high-uncertainty tokens during pretraining with no supervision beyond cross-entropy loss, unifying train-time and test-time adaptive computation in a single architecture. This opens a path toward models that natively 'think harder' on difficult tokens without external prompting or CoT labels. Limitations include testing only up to 1.9B parameters, forking restricted to early layers (3, 7, 11), and the unexplained concave relationship where the model allocates *less* computation at the highest-entropy tokens. Open questions include whether the approach scales to 10B+ models, whether deeper forking (mitigated by the noted gradient vanishing issue) can be made effective, and how the mechanism interacts with longer context windows or multimodal inputs.
