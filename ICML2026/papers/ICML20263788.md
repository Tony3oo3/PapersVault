## Problem

RLVR post-training of large reasoning models is computationally expensive due to rollout-intensive optimization, and existing online prompt selection methods either rely on costly exact evaluations (e.g., Dynamic Sampling) or use prompt-specific predictive models that cannot generalize across prompts or adapt to the evolving policy. The core challenge is to design a lightweight, generalizable difficulty predictor that shares information across prompts and a principled batch acquisition strategy that balances informativeness with diversity, all at negligible additional computational cost.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pool of reasoning prompts (mathematical or logical problems), the accumulated optimization history (past prompt batches and their reward feedback), and the current LLM policy as input. It produces a selected batch of prompts for the next RLVR training step, predicted difficulty scores for all candidate prompts, and at test time a per-prompt compute allocation for Best-of-N sampling. The predictive model itself is a ~20M-parameter generative model, less than 1% of the LLM it steers, and prompt embeddings are extracted via the WordLLaMA toolkit for efficient processing.

## Method

GPS introduces a generalizable Prompt Predictive Model (PPM) formulated as a conditional variational generative model with a global latent variable z_t (difficulty context) that compresses shared optimization history, enabling cross-prompt difficulty transfer and adaptation to policy evolution via a history-conditioned prior. The PPM is trained by maximizing the ELBO, jointly learning a Transformer-based encoder, a shared MLP decoder mapping (tau, z_t) to predicted difficulty, and a Transformer-based history-conditioned prior. For batch selection, GPS maximizes a unified utility combining intermediate-difficulty prioritization (u(gamma) = -(gamma-0.5)^2) and history-anchored diversity (intra-batch dispersion plus inter-step exploration), solved via greedy max-sum diversification. A theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) proves that conditioning on full shared history strictly reduces prediction risk versus prompt-specific estimators. At test time, the learned PPM is reused to allocate Best-of-N samples preferentially to intermediate-difficulty prompts, reducing inference cost by up to 36.4% without performance loss.

## Results

On mathematical reasoning (DeepScaler-trained models), GPS achieves 56.5% in-distribution average and 33.5% out-of-distribution average with DSR-1.5B, and 67.4%/51.5% with DSR-7B, matching or exceeding the DS Oracle baseline while reducing training time by 28–47% (e.g., 49h vs. 77h for DSR-7B). On logical reasoning (Countdown), GPS reaches 66.6% (Qwen3-4B) and 68.6% (Qwen3-8B) average accuracy, surpassing DS (66.2%/68.5%) with 28–33% less runtime. GPS delivers a 1.4×–2.0× speedup over uniform sampling in training steps and requires up to 69% fewer rollouts than DS. At test time, the learned PPM enables up to 36.4% inference cost reduction or 3.2% accuracy gain under fixed budgets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPS outperforms all prediction-based baselines (MoPPS, PCL, GRESO) on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution mathematical benchmarks, with the largest gap over MoPPS (+1.1 pts in-dist, +2.3 pts out-of-dist for DSR-7B). Against the DS Oracle (which uses exact rollout-based evaluation), GPS matches in-distribution accuracy (56.5 vs. 56.5 for 1.5B; 67.4 vs. 67.0 for 7B) while exceeding it on out-of-distribution tasks (33.5 vs. 32.7; 51.5 vs. 49.9), all at 28–47% lower training time and up to 69% fewer rollouts. On Countdown logical reasoning, GPS surpasses DS by 0.4 pts (Qwen3-4B) and 0.1 pts (Qwen3-8B) in average accuracy while cutting runtime from 4.9h→3.4h and 6.9h→5.0h respectively. Compared to uniform sampling, GPS yields consistent gains of 1.6–1.9 points on math and 4.1–5.7 points on logical tasks with 1.4×–2.0× fewer training steps. GPS is also compatible with PPO and Reinforce++ (beyond GRPO), where it consistently outperforms uniform sampling, whereas DS is inapplicable to single-response algorithms like PPO.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends MoPPS (Qu et al., 2025b), which models prompt difficulty via independent per-prompt Beta posteriors, by replacing isolated estimators with a shared variational generative model that conditions on the full optimization history through a global latent context. It also departs from prior prediction-based methods (GRESO, PCL) that treat prompts independently, and from evaluation-based methods (Dynamic Sampling, SPEED-RL) that incur costly exact rollouts, by formulating prompt selection as a batch-level max-sum diversification problem combining difficulty utility with history-anchored diversity. The variational inference framework draws on conditional generative modeling (Neural Processes, amortized inference), while the batch acquisition criterion connects to submodular maximization literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for the RLVR community: a 20M-parameter predictive model achieves up to 2× training speedup over uniform sampling and matches oracle evaluation-based selection with 69% fewer rollouts, across multiple backbones (1.5B–8B) and tasks (math, logic). The 'small-steer-large' paradigm—where a tiny model guides a much larger LLM's optimization—opens a design space for scalable, low-overhead training acceleration. Key limitations include diminished predictive correlation on semantically distant out-of-domain tasks (suggesting multi-domain co-training is needed), the deliberately simple test-time allocation strategy, and the fact that the specific variational architecture is not claimed optimal. Open questions include whether more refined temporal modeling or alternative generative formulations could further close the gap to oracle selection, and whether the approach extends to continuous-reward or multi-turn settings.
