## Problem

Existing NVFP4 quantized training methods rely on element-wise stochastic rounding (SR) for unbiased gradient estimation, which inflates quantization MSE by ~2.5× over round-to-nearest and forces a trade-off between forward-pass representation capacity and backward-pass unbiasedness. This results in noticeable accuracy degradation relative to FP16 and FP8 pre-training, and no prior work has shown how to reduce the quantization error of unbiased gradient estimators in microscaling FP4 formats.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard floating-point model weights, activations, and upstream gradients, quantizing them into NVFP4 format (E2M1 4-bit values with one E4M3 FP8 scale per 16-element group and a single FP32 global scale per tensor). The forward pass produces NVFP4-quantized outputs for tensor-core GEMMs, while the backward pass produces unbiased quantized gradients where the stochasticity is confined to the FP8 group scales rather than the FP4 values. Rotations are applied in 128-element groups along the inner GEMM dimension, requiring weight re-quantization in the backward pass.

## Method

The core contribution is MS-EDEN, a new unbiased quantization routine that extends the EDEN bias-correction mechanism to NVFP4 microscaling formats. Instead of element-wise stochastic rounding on FP4 values, MS-EDEN applies a Randomized Hadamard Transform in 128-element groups, quantizes with round-to-nearest (yielding ~2× lower MSE than SR), and merges the EDEN rescaling factors into the FP8 group scales via stochastic rounding, preserving provable unbiasedness while avoiding per-element variance. For the forward pass, Quartet II uses native NVFP4 scaling (one FP8 scale per 16 elements) augmented with the Four-Over-Six grid selection heuristic, which picks the lower-MSE scale between 4.0 and 6.0 per block. The backward pass de-quantizes, transposes, and re-quantizes saved forward-pass tensors along with upstream gradients using MS-EDEN, with rotations canceling along the inner GEMM dimension so no inverse transform is needed. The full scheme achieves up to 4.2× speedup over BF16 on NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs via custom CUDA kernels with a post-hoc range alignment trick that halves memory bandwidth for the re-quantization step.

## Results

On Nanochat pre-training (the paper's largest-scale validation), Quartet II reduces the validation BPB gap over BF16 to 1.26% for the 560M/11B-token model and 1.44% for the 1.9B/38B-token model, outperforming all prior NVFP4 methods by 15–25% relative. In the Llama-like ablation suite (30M–200M params, up to 800 tokens/param), Quartet II improves validation loss by at least 20% over the best prior NVFP4 recipe. On hardware, Quartet II achieves >4× linear-layer speedup over BF16 on RTX 5090 and up to 2.5× on B200 for 22B-scale layers, with a measured 1.8× end-to-end training throughput gain for 1B LLM pre-training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Quartet II consistently outperforms all three NVFP4 baselines on pre-training validation loss. Against the NVIDIA recipe (NVIDIA et al., 2025), Quartet II reduces the BPB gap from 1.56%→1.26% (560M) and 1.92%→1.44% (1.9B), a relative improvement of ~19% and ~25% respectively. Against FourOverSix (Cook et al., 2025), the gap narrows from 1.51%→1.26% and 1.76%→1.44%; notably, the paper shows that 4/6 is biased when applied to the backward pass (validated in Appendix A), so Quartet II uses it only on the forward pass. Against TetraJet-v2 (Chen et al., 2025b), Quartet II improves from 1.72%→1.26% (560M, ~27% relative) and 1.72%→1.44% (1.9B, ~16% relative). On zero-shot benchmarks (ARC, PIQA, HellaSwag, CORE), differences between QAT methods are within the 95% confidence intervals (±0.2–4%), indicating the pre-training loss advantage has not yet translated into measurable downstream task gains at these model scales.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the NVFP4 pre-training recipe from NVIDIA et al. (2025) and its refinements (TetraJet-v2, FourOverSix), which established the first stable fully-quantized LLM training in 4-bit microscaling formats. The key departure is in the backward pass: all prior methods rely on element-wise stochastic rounding (SR) for unbiased gradient estimation, but this paper shows that the EDEN rescaling idea from distributed optimization (Vargaftik et al., 2022) can be adapted to microscaling formats by merging the correction factor into the FP8 group scales via stochastic rounding, yielding MS-EDEN with >2× lower quantization error. It also departs from the square-block quantization strategy by using native NVFP4 scaling augmented with the FourOverSix heuristic on the forward pass, arguing that the representation-capacity gain outweighs the need to avoid weight re-quantization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NVFP4 is the first 4-bit format with native tensor-core support (Blackwell), making accurate quantized pre-training a practical near-term goal for reducing LLM training costs; Quartet II's 4.2× speedup over BF16 and consistent 15–25% loss-gap reduction over prior NVFP4 recipes make it a strong candidate for production adoption on that hardware. However, the approach is currently tied to a single GPU generation and validated only on LLM pre-training (up to 1.9B params, 38B tokens), leaving open whether MS-EDEN generalizes to other architectures, fine-tuning regimes, or future hardware. The zero-shot benchmark differences between QAT methods are statistically insignificant, suggesting the gains may narrow with more post-training compute.
