## Problem

Despite widespread belief that simplicity bias underpins generalization in over-parameterized networks, no broadly applicable quantitative measure of functional simplicity exists that is simultaneously general across architectures, computable at scale for trained models, and differentiable enough to serve as a training objective. Existing proxies—sharpness, parameter norms, compression bounds—satisfy only a subset of these desiderata and are often sensitive to reparameterization or intractable to optimize.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained (or in-training) neural network and pairs of training examples, constructing 1D linear interpolation paths in input space (or embedding space for discrete text). Along each path, the network is evaluated at r sampling nodes (default r=4 with randomized cosine sampling), and for high-dimensional outputs a per-path PCA projection to m dimensions (typically m=3) is applied before fitting. The primary output is the effective degree (ED), a scalar simplicity score; when used as a regularizer, ED produces a differentiable penalty term added to the task loss, with gradients backpropagated through the polynomial-fitting step into network parameters.

## Method

The core idea is to approximate a network's predictive behavior along data-dependent 1D interpolation paths using Chebyshev polynomial surrogates, sidestepping the combinatorial explosion of multivariate polynomial bases. The effective degree (ED = Σ|c_k|·k over fitted coefficients) serves as a coefficient-weighted simplicity metric that is Lipschitz-continuous in the coefficients and provably preserves algebraic-degree ordering under random interpolation directions (Theorem 3.1). For optimization, the paper derives closed-form analytic gradients of ED through the least-squares fitting step (Proposition 5.1), implemented via damped normal equations for numerical stability, enabling end-to-end backpropagation of the simplicity penalty. A label-anchored variant replaces boundary-node predictions with ground-truth labels during classification to avoid conflict with cross-entropy's early-training dynamics, and the regularizer is applied with a sinusoidal ramp-up schedule to allow initial task learning before enforcing complexity control.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ViT-Tiny, ED regularization achieves 90.82% top-1 accuracy, improving over the standard training baseline (87.80%) by +3.02 points and outperforming all compared regularizers. On ImageNet from scratch (ViT-S/16), ED yields consistent gains under both the original recipe (72.76% vs. 71.37%) and a stronger recipe (75.01% vs. 74.42%). For CLIP fine-tuning, ED improves both in-distribution and out-of-distribution accuracy across all five OOD benchmarks (e.g., ImageNet-A: 22.03% vs. 20.48% for ViT-B/32). In the correlation analysis, ED exhibits the strongest Pearson correlation with the generalization gap, consistently outperforming sharpness-based measures and parameter L2 norm across ResNet18, ViT-Tiny, and CLIP models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ED regularization clearly outperforms all five compared baselines on CIFAR-10 (ViT-Tiny): it achieves 90.82% top-1 accuracy versus Mixup (88.83%, +2.0 pts), SAM (87.85%, +3.0 pts), ASAM (87.85%, +3.0 pts), and Jacobian regularization (87.81%, +3.0 pts). Notably, SAM and ASAM—popular sharpness-aware optimizers—provide essentially no improvement over the unregularized baseline (87.80%), while ED delivers a substantial and consistent gain. On GLUE text classification, ED also outperforms embedding mixup (Guo et al., 2019), which is not consistently beneficial and can degrade performance (e.g., CoLA: 61.04 vs. ED's 62.45 Matthews Corr.), suggesting that enforcing linear interpolation targets is too rigid for language data. In the correlation analysis, ED is the only metric that positively correlates with the generalization gap across both CIFAR-10 and ImageNet settings, whereas sharpness-based measures correlate negatively and parameter L2 norm shows weak or negative correlation. ED also uniquely captures the grokking phase transition on Modular Division, peaking at the memorization-to-generalization shift and then decreasing, while sharpness and norm metrics fail to provide a clear transition signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of function-space simplicity analysis and practical regularization. While prior work on implicit bias (Soudry et al., Gunasekar et al.) provides clean simplicity notions only in restricted regimes (linear, homogeneous networks), and popular generalization proxies like sharpness (Keskar et al., Foret et al.) operate in parameter space and are sensitive to reparameterization, this paper introduces polynomial surrogates along data-dependent interpolation paths as a function-space complexity measure that is simultaneously general, quantifiable, and differentiable. It repurposes classical approximation theory (Chebyshev/Legendre bases, orthogonal polynomial fitting) in a novel way: not to approximate the network itself, but to extract a degree-based simplicity signal that can be backpropagated as a training-time regularizer. The approach is conceptually aligned with spline and piecewise-linear perspectives on ReLU networks (Montufar et al., Raghu et al.) but avoids their architecture-dependence by working on one-dimensional path restrictions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rare unified framework that serves as both a post-hoc generalization metric (effective degree) and a training-time regularizer, validated across vision, language, and reinforcement learning. The ~2× computational overhead is modest, and the method requires tuning only a single hyperparameter (λ) once a default configuration is established. Key limitations include a documented failure mode when simpler features are less robust (MNIST-CIFAR), the path-based reduction potentially missing aspects of multivariate functional complexity, and the theoretical connection to generalization bounds remaining informal. Open questions include formalizing when path-based surrogates preserve relevant simplicity notions beyond polynomial degree, adaptive degree/resolution selection, and interaction with pre-training dynamics.
