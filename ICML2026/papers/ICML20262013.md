## Problem

Existing post-hoc recalibration methods for probabilistic regression either rely on the weak PIT uniformity criterion (which permits systematic over- and under-confidence to cancel out) or impose restrictive parametric assumptions on the calibration error (e.g., Gaussian process models on moments). No prior method enforces the stronger auto-calibration property in a fully nonparametric way for general regression targets.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262013/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained probabilistic regression model, a calibration set of (prediction, target) pairs generated by that model on held-out data, and a query feature vector. It produces a recalibrated predictive distribution represented as an empirical distribution over the calibration targets (a weighted sum of point masses), rather than a continuous density. This empirical output is a deliberate trade-off: it discards the base model's parametric density structure but avoids any parametric assumption on the correction.

## Method

The core algorithm estimates the calibration map Q → P_{Y|Q} via Conditional Kernel Mean Embeddings (CKME): predictions are embedded into an RKHS over distributions using a Gaussian-type kernel, and the conditional mean operator is estimated by ridge regression in that space. The key efficiency contribution is the Energy Distance Kernel (EDK), which instantiates the Gaussian-type kernel with the Brownian motion covariance kernel r(u,v)=|u|+|v|-|u-v|, reducing pairwise distribution distance evaluation from O(m²) to O(m log m) for empirical distributions of size m. The resulting CKME weight vector is projected onto the probability simplex to yield the final empirical recalibrated distribution. Theoretically, the paper derives a calibration-sharpness decomposition of the expected score into calibration error, lack of sharpness (generalized conditional mutual information I(Y;X|Q)), and irreducible aleatoric uncertainty, and proves that the recalibrated prediction preserves the original model's sharpness while achieving auto-calibration.

## Results

The CKME recalibration method is the only approach that consistently passes the SKCE auto-calibration hypothesis test (α=5%) across most datasets and base models, while PIT and GPBETA recalibration are generally rejected. On CRPS (relative to the uncalibrated base model), CKME typically achieves scores within 5% of the base model (e.g., 0.994 on Kin8nm/DRF, 0.999 on Power/DRF) and sometimes substantially improves it (e.g., 0.405 on Yacht/DRF, 0.376 on Naval/BNN). PIT recalibration remains the strongest method for the weaker PIT-calibration test, while GPBETA frequently degrades CRPS (e.g., 1.153 on Energy/MDN, 1.150 on Sinus/MDN). The base model trained on train+calibration data (None(T+C)) often achieves the best raw CRPS but remains significantly miscalibrated, illustrating the calibration–sharpness trade-off.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PIT recalibration (Kuleshov et al., 2018), CKME wins decisively on the stronger auto-calibration (SKCE) test—PIT recalibration is rejected on most datasets—while PIT recalibration retains an edge on the weaker PIT-calibration test. On CRPS, the two methods are broadly comparable, with CKME sometimes substantially better (e.g., 0.405 vs. 0.850 on Yacht/DRF) and sometimes slightly worse (e.g., 1.077 vs. 0.977 on Energy/BNN). Against GPBETA (Song et al., 2019), CKME dominates on both calibration and CRPS: GPBETA is rejected by the SKCE test on nearly all datasets and frequently degrades CRPS (e.g., 1.153 on Energy/MDN, 1.150 on Sinus/MDN, 1.131 on Sinus/DRF). Against conformal prediction (Chernozhukov et al., 2021, Appendix D), CKME achieves comparable marginal coverage and interval width but shows lower distance correlation between predicted intervals and PIT values, indicating more evenly distributed residual errors. The uncalibrated model trained on train+calibration data (None(T+C)) often achieves the best raw CRPS (e.g., 0.814 on Yacht/DRF) but fails the auto-calibration test, confirming that predictive accuracy alone does not guarantee reliable uncertainty estimates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the parametric recalibration framework of Song et al. (2019) by replacing its Gaussian-process-based, moment-dependent calibration map with a fully nonparametric Conditional Kernel Mean Embedding (CKME) estimator, thereby removing the restrictive assumption that the calibration error is a low-dimensional parametric transformation. The kernel over distributions is instantiated via the Energy Distance Kernel (EDK), a specific Gaussian-type kernel (Christmann & Steinwart, 2010) whose inner distance is the energy distance (Szekely & Rizzo, 2004), enabling O(n log n) evaluation for real-valued empirical distributions. The theoretical grounding draws on proper scoring rules (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007) and the SKCE auto-calibration test (Widmann et al., 2021), while the calibration-sharpness decomposition (Lemma 4.1) connects to the grouping loss of Kull & Flach (2015) and the paradigm of Gneiting et al. (2007).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first nonparametric recalibration algorithm that provably enforces auto-calibration (the strong sense Q = P_{Y|Q}) without parametric assumptions on the error structure, filling a gap between weak PIT-based methods and restrictive parametric ones. The calibration-sharpness decomposition and the demonstrated negative example (a model improving its CRPS score while remaining significantly miscalibrated) make a compelling case that standard performance metrics are insufficient for safety-critical deployment. Key limitations include the O(n_cal^3) scaling of the CKME solve, the output being an empirical distribution (discarding the base model's density structure), and the targeted auto-calibration being weaker than full feature-conditional calibration. Open questions include whether the empirical-distribution output can be smoothed or whether the method can be extended to local calibration and to settings where the target space is not compact.
