## Problem

Virtual screening of ultra-large combinatorial synthesis libraries (CSLs) with tens of billions of compounds is infeasible with current methods: expensive scoring functions (docking, co-folding) preclude exhaustive evaluation, and existing algorithms (RL, active learning, generative models) evaluate less than 1% of the library, leaving high-scoring compounds undiscovered. Moreover, these iterative approaches offer little amortization when objectives or constraints change mid-campaign, requiring re-optimization for each new query.

## Input / Output

APEX takes a CSL defined by its reaction/R-group/synthon hierarchy, a user-specified objective (e.g., minimize docking score on a named target), a set of physicochemical constraints with lower and upper bounds (e.g., Lipinski Ro5, Veber, Astex Rule-of-3), and a retrieval size k. The surrogate is trained once on a small labeled subset (~1M compounds with docking scores and RDKit properties), and the factorizer is trained on the full CSL structure without labels. At query time, the system outputs a dataframe of the top-k compound SMILES with their APEX-predicted objective and constraint values, computed end-to-end in under 30 seconds on a single T4 GPU for a 10B-compound library. Changing the objective or constraint set requires no retraining—only a new GPU pass over cached synthon contributions.

## Method

APEX factorizes a neural network surrogate's molecular embedding into a sum of per-synthon associative contributions, reducing the cost of scoring any compound from a full network forward pass to a handful of floating-point additions over pre-cached scalar values. The surrogate is an MPNN encoder with linear task probes, trained with isotropic noise injected into embeddings to make linear projections robust to the subsequent factorization (addressing the errors-in-variables problem). The ReactionFactorizer mirrors the CSL hierarchy with SynthonEncoder, RgroupEncoder, and ReactionEncoder (deep set networks), plus a SynthonValueEncoder and RgroupKeyEncoder that produce per-synthon key-value pairs whose product yields each synthon's associative embedding; the factorizer is trained to reconstruct frozen surrogate embeddings from (reaction, R-group assignment) pairs. At search time, all synthon associative contributions are pre-computed and cached (O(|S|) evaluations vs. O(|D|) for the full library), and the top-k retrieval is executed via a custom CUDA kernel using a chain-of-batches strategy with the AIR top-k algorithm, streaming constraint-violation and objective pairs into a priority queue across the entire library in a single GPU pass.

## Results

With a budget of k=100,000 (0.803% of the 12M BRICS CSL), APEX retrieves ground-truth top-j compounds at recall rates far exceeding a random baseline (<0.01) across all five docking targets (PARP1, MET, DRD2, F10, ESR1) and multiple constraint sets (Lipinski, Veber, Pfizer 3/75, Wager CNS, Astex Rule-of-3). On the 10B compound BRICS library, APEX retrieves the approximate top k=1,000,000 compounds in 21.2 s (GPU) under Lipinski constraints on a single NVIDIA T4 GPU, versus 147.6 s on CPU. In a zero-shot transfer to the 70B-compound Enamine REAL library, APEX still enriches docking scores relative to the background distribution for four of five targets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary experimental baseline is Thompson Sampling (TS) from Klarich et al. (2024), a multi-armed bandit method designed for ultra-large CSL screening. APEX consistently outperforms or matches TS at recalling ground-truth top-j compounds across the five largest reactions in the 12M CSL (over 4M products) and all five docking targets, with the advantage most pronounced at lower evaluation budgets (10 and 100 TS iterations). The comparison is limited to the unconstrained case because TS does not natively support molecular-property constraints. APEX also far exceeds a random-selection baseline (recall <0.01) by a wide margin on all targets and constraint sets. No direct head-to-head comparison is made against other surrogate-based methods (Deep Docking, MolPal, MEMES) or generative-model approaches (CSLVAE, NGT, SynFlowNet), as those were not re-implemented under the same protocol; the paper positions APEX as complementary to rather than competitive with those paradigms.

## Relation to Prior Work

APEX builds directly on the hierarchical encoder stack (SynthonEncoder → RgroupEncoder → ReactionEncoder) introduced in Pedawi et al. (2022, CSLVAE) for navigating combinatorial synthesis libraries, and on the GPU AIR top-k algorithm of Zhang et al. (2023) for efficient retrieval. The key conceptual departure is the factorization of the surrogate's molecular embedding into a sum of per-synthon associative contributions, analogous to factorization machines in recommender systems, which reduces exhaustive scoring from O(|D|) neural network evaluations to O(|S|) cached lookups. This positions APEX as a non-iterative, single-pass alternative to prior surrogate-based screening (Deep Docking, MolPal, MEMES), synthon-level bandit methods (V-SYNTHES, Thompson sampling), and generative approaches (NGT, SynFlowNet) that all require oracle calls or per-campaign retraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

APEX makes exhaustive virtual screening of 10B+ compound libraries a routine sub-minute GPU task, fundamentally changing the economics of drug discovery campaigns where previously fewer than 0.1% of compounds could be scored. The declarative query paradigm—swapping objectives and constraints without retraining—addresses a real workflow pain point in iterative screening. Limitations include the surrogate's finite accuracy on docking scores (the dominant error source per the MSE decomposition) and the additional approximation from factorization; zero-shot transfer to Enamine REAL shows promise but with degraded performance on some targets. Open questions include whether the factorization strategy can be layered on top of foundation-model embeddings (e.g., DrugCLIP) and how well it extends to co-folding or free-energy objectives that are even more expensive than docking.
