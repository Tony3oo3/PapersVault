## Problem

Clinical deep learning suffers from a reproducibility crisis: researchers implement ad-hoc data pipelines with incompatible dependencies, reported results are practically irreproducible, and large EHR datasets (e.g., MIMIC-IV with 124M lab events) require hundreds of gigabytes of RAM, making work infeasible on consumer hardware. No existing framework unifies all clinical modalities (EHR, imaging, text, biosignals) with efficient memory management, comprehensive post-hoc evaluation (interpretability, uncertainty quantification), and low domain-expertise barriers in a single, actively maintained package.

## Input / Output

PyHealth 2.0 consumes raw clinical data in heterogeneous formats—CSV/Parquet EHR tables, free-text clinical notes, DICOM/JPEG X-ray images, multi-channel EEG/ECG/PCG recordings, and genomic variant tables—through a modality-agnostic two-layer Patient→Event hierarchy that makes no assumptions about timestamps, data types, or patient identifiers. The pipeline produces trainable PyTorch tensors via a three-stage cached pipeline (Dask table-joining → Polars batch task-transformation → LitData binary processor-transformation), and downstream outputs include model predictions, conformal prediction sets with empirical coverage guarantees, feature attribution maps (Attention-Grad, SHAP, DeepLIFT, GIM), and cross-standard medical code translations (ICD↔CCS, ATC↔RXNorm↔NDC). A full clinical task can be instantiated in 7 lines of code, and patient data exploration requires only a single .get_events() call.

## Method

PyHealth 2.0 is a nine-module framework (data, processors, datasets, tasks, models, metrics, interpret, calib, medcode) whose core innovation is a memory-efficient lazy-loading architecture that keeps peak RAM nearly constant as worker count scales, enabling a 16 GB MacBook Pro to outperform a 32-core server on MIMIC-IV lab-event processing. The dataset class loads each patient into memory only when needed and parallelizes task construction via Dask sharding, while the task-transformation stage processes batches of 128 consecutive patients with Polars to exploit data locality and row-group statistics. Tasks are defined through a readable three-component interface (input schema with processors, output schema, and a call function), and the resulting SampleDataset inherits from PyTorch Lightning's StreamingDataset for memory-efficient iteration during training. For model evaluation, the framework directly implements interpretability methods (Attention-Grad, GIM, DeepLift, SHAP) and uncertainty quantification (temperature/Dirichlet calibration, vanilla and covariate-shift-adjusted conformal prediction) without requiring users to navigate incompatible external libraries. The modality-agnostic design—making no assumptions about data types—enables seamless mixing of structured codes, clinical notes, X-rays, and biosignals within a single dataloader, a capability absent from specialized frameworks like MEDS (structured EHR only), MONAI (imaging only), or Zensols (text only).

## Results

On MIMIC-IV mortality prediction (124M lab events), PyHealth 2.0 achieves up to 39× speedup over naive Pandas (2,975 s vs. 93,708 s at 8 workers) while using 23.6 GB peak memory versus Pandas's 49.2 GB and PyHealth 1.16's 275.7 GB. A 16 GB MacBook Pro M2 Pro running PyHealth 2.0 completes the full labevents task in 1,357 s at 8 workers—faster than PyHealth 1.16 at 16 workers on a 32-core server (2,235 s)—while consuming only 7.4 GB peak memory. Across all three benchmark tasks (mortality, drug recommendation, length-of-stay), PyHealth 2.0 consistently uses 5–15× less peak memory than PyHealth 1.16 and 2–3× less than MEDS, with wall time comparable to or faster than both.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PyHealth 1.16, PyHealth 2.0 reduces peak memory by roughly 12× on the mortality task (23.6 GB vs. 275.7 GB at 8 workers) while achieving comparable wall time (2,975 s vs. 2,543 s on the server); on a consumer MacBook, PyHealth 2.0 actually outperforms PyHealth 1.16 on the 32-core server in wall time (1,357 s vs. 2,235 s at 16 workers). Against the MEDS ecosystem (MEDS_ETL + MEDS_Reader), PyHealth 2.0 is 3.4× faster in wall time (2,975 s vs. 10,117 s at 8 workers) and uses 2.6× less peak memory (23.6 GB vs. 61.5 GB), though MEDS maintains a flatter memory profile as workers scale. Against naive Pandas, PyHealth 2.0 delivers the headline 39× speedup and roughly 2× lower memory. On lines of code, PyHealth 2.0 requires 10 lines for patient exploration and a uniform 7-line task initialization, compared to 14/27 for PyHealth 1.16, 12/43 for MEDS, and 16/51 for Pandas on the mortality task. Downstream model benchmarks (Appendix F) confirm that PyHealth 2.0's default configurations yield reasonable baselines across all modalities—for example, Deepr achieves 0.755 AUROC on MIMIC-IV mortality and ResNet-18 reaches 0.994 ROC-AUC on COVID-19 chest X-ray classification—though the authors explicitly note these are untuned defaults rather than state-of-the-art results.

## Relation to Prior Work

PyHealth 2.0 is a direct, substantially expanded successor to PyHealth 1.16 (Yang et al., KDD 2023), addressing its memory-management limitations while broadening scope from primarily EHR to unified multimodal support (imaging, biosignals, text, genomics). It positions itself against modality-specialized frameworks—MEDS for longitudinal EHR, MONAI for imaging, Zensols for clinical NLP—arguing that a single framework with no datatype assumptions eliminates the overhead of switching tools and managing dependency chains. The engineering choices (Dask for out-of-core joins, Polars for batched task processing, LitData for streaming tensors) draw on the broader data-engineering ecosystem rather than introducing new ML methods, and the model library re-implements established architectures (Retain, StageNet, ConCare, Transformer, EHRMamba) under a standardized interface.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By reducing clinical AI development to ~7 lines of code and enabling training on 16 GB laptops, PyHealth 2.0 directly targets the reproducibility and accessibility barriers that have limited adoption of clinical deep learning outside well-resourced labs. Key open questions remain: multimodal model support (especially under missing modalities) is explicitly limited, interpretability methods are restricted to tabular time-series and image models, and conformal prediction under the distribution shifts common in clinical data still lacks practical utility. The RHealth extension signals an intent to reach the broader bioinformatics community, but the framework's long-term impact will depend on whether the community sustains the breadth of supported modalities and tasks as it grows.
