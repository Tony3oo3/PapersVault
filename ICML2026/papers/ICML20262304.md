## Problem

Genomic foundation models almost universally rely on fixed tokenization schemes (k-mers, BPE, single nucleotides) that impose arbitrary sequence boundaries lacking biological grounding. While H-Net demonstrated that adaptive, learned chunking is feasible for autoregressive DNA generation, it was never evaluated under masked language modeling or on downstream classification benchmarks, leaving open whether adaptive tokenization yields competitive embeddings in the bidirectional MLM paradigm used by most genomic encoders.

## Input / Output

The model consumes raw DNA sequences of length 4096 over a 7-token byte-level vocabulary {A, C, G, T, N, [PAD], [MASK]}, sampled in forward and reverse-complement orientations with equal probability. During pretraining it predicts masked nucleotides (15% masking); during fine-tuning it produces binary class labels for 27 downstream tasks. A distinctive intermediate output is the learned boundary probability at each position, which determines variable-length chunk boundaries for hierarchical compression and is itself analyzed for biological interpretability against promoter motifs and splice junctions.

## Method

LDARNet is a 110M-parameter single-stage hierarchical encoder–backbone–decoder model ([m3t1, [M10], m4]) that adapts H-Net's dynamic chunking to bidirectional MLM. The outer stages and backbone use BiMamba-2 blocks—bidirectional Mamba-2 with shared input/output projections and mean fusion of forward/reverse passes—while the encoder additionally includes one local attention layer (16 heads, window 1023) for short-range motif recognition. A bidirectional router computes cosine similarity in both orientations for each adjacent pair to produce boundary probabilities, which are thresholded to select ~1/N positions for compression; a ratio loss (α=0.03) regularizes the selected fraction toward the target N=4. Expansion back to full resolution uses a bidirectional EMA smoother parameterized by boundary probabilities, implemented via a fused selective-scan kernel. The key empirical finding is that at matched FLOPs, learned boundaries beat fixed-grid boundaries by up to 14.3 pp on histone tasks, and nucleotide-resolution analysis shows the learned boundaries enrich around canonical promoter motifs (CCAAT, TATA, GC boxes) and splice junctions without any motif supervision.

## Results

On the 18-task Nucleotide Transformer (NT) benchmark, LDARNet (110M) achieves 15/18 wins among compact models (<300M parameters) and the best overall result on 9 of 10 histone modification tasks, surpassing models up to 20× larger (e.g., H3K9ac: 64.6 MCC vs. GENERator 61.2; H3K36me3: 68.7 vs. GENERator 65.7; H3: 81.7 vs. NT-multi 79.3). On the 9-task Genomic Benchmarks (GB) suite, LDARNet ties DNABERT-2 for best compact-model performance (3 wins each) and achieves the single best overall result on Human non-TATA Promoters (96.6% accuracy vs. GENERator 95.8%). A FLOPs-matched controlled experiment shows learned boundaries beat fixed-grid boundaries by up to 14.3 percentage points on H3K4me1 at identical compute.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against compact baselines, LDARNet dominates: it beats DNABERT-2 (117M) on 15/18 NT tasks (vs. DNABERT-2's 2 wins), HyenaDNA (55M) on 14/18 (HyenaDNA retains Splice acceptor at 93.5 vs. 91.5), and all 8M Caduceus variants on every NT task. Against large-scale models, LDARNet's 110M parameters match or exceed NT-v2 (500M) on all 10 histone tasks (e.g., H3K4me3: 60.2 vs. 43.7 MCC, a 16.5-point gap) and GENERator (1.2B) on 9 of 10 histone tasks (e.g., H3K9ac: 64.6 vs. 61.2; H3K36me3: 68.7 vs. 65.7), while GENERator retains the edge on splice sites (97.8–98.1 vs. 91.5–95.8) and promoter/enhancer tasks. On the human-centric GB suite, LDARNet ties DNABERT-2 (3 wins each) but trails the 8M Caduceus models on Drosophila Enhancers (80.3 vs. 82.7) and Human OCR (80.1 vs. 82.6), reflecting Caduceus's human-genome specialization; however, LDARNet achieves the single best overall score on Human non-TATA Promoters (96.6% vs. GENERator 95.8%, NT-v2 93.2%). The FLOPs-matched ablation confirms that learned routing (not architecture or scale) drives the histone gains: at identical 2.5M parameters and FLOPs, learned boundaries outperform fixed-grid boundaries by +14.3pp on H3K4me1 and +6.7pp on H3K36me3.

## Relation to Prior Work

LDARNet adapts the H-Net dynamic chunking architecture (Hwang et al., 2025) from autoregressive generation to masked language modeling, introducing bidirectional routing and a bidirectional EMA dechunker to handle the symmetric context required by MLM. The backbone and outer layers replace H-Net's causal Mamba with BiMamba-2, extending the bidirectional state-space design of Caduceus (Schiff et al., 2024) to the Mamba-2 selective SSM formulation. Within the broader genomic foundation model landscape (DNABERT, Nucleotide Transformer, HyenaDNA, GENERator), LDARNet's distinguishing contribution is treating tokenization as a learnable boundary-placement problem rather than a fixed preprocessing choice, making the learned boundaries themselves an object of biological analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The FLOPs-matched controlled experiment provides causal evidence that learned routing—not hierarchical scaffolding or backbone choice—drives the histone-task gains, and the nucleotide-resolution analysis shows boundaries aligning with canonical promoter motifs and splice junctions without supervision, offering a mechanistic interpretation of why adaptive tokenization helps. Key limitations include the single-stage 4× compression (no ultra-long context), a deliberate splice-site trade-off where fixed boundaries outperform learned ones, and evaluation restricted to supervised fine-tuning. Open questions include whether multi-stage hierarchies preserve the biological interpretability at >100kb scales, whether zero-shot transfer of learned representations matches fine-tuned performance, and whether the boundary-motif alignment generalizes to genome-scale ChIP-seq validation.
