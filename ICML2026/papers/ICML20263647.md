## Problem

Existing benchmarks for measuring sycophancy in LLM mathematical reasoning are limited to final-answer problems, rely on simple and contaminated datasets (e.g., AIME), and construct test cases via synthetic constraint modifications that produce ill-posed or inherently ambiguous questions rather than well-posed false theorems. This makes it impossible to assess how frequently LLMs uncritically accept incorrect premises in realistic proof-based mathematical tasks, where the stakes of flawed proofs are highest.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes a perturbed mathematical problem statement (a false but plausible theorem derived from a real 2025 competition problem) as input and produces a four-way behavioral classification of the model's response: Ideal (disproves and reconstructs), Sycophant (attempts to prove the false statement), Corrected (solves the original without acknowledging the error), or Detected (identifies falsity but does not recover the original). A separate utility score measures performance on the unperturbed original problem, enabling analysis of the relationship between mathematical ability and sycophancy tendency. The 504-sample dataset comprises 321 proof-style and 183 final-answer problems balanced across algebra, geometry, combinatorics, and number theory.

## Method

BROKENMATH is constructed in three stages: (1) curating 600+ problems from 2025 national and international olympiads (IMO, USAMO, Balkan MO, etc.), (2) prompting GPT-5-MINI with the original problem and its solution to generate subtly false but contextually plausible perturbations (e.g., inverting a property, requesting a non-existent counterexample, or asserting an incorrect numerical answer), and (3) having an IMO medalist refine phrasing for maximum plausibility and discard trivially detectable corruptions. Evaluation uses a majority-vote ensemble of three GPT-5-MINI calls as judge (95% agreement with human labels on 240 samples) to classify each response. The paper further evaluates mitigation strategies including verification-aware prompting, self-confidence-based selection heuristics, best-of-n and iterative self-verification agentic loops, and supervised fine-tuning of QWEN3-4B on 13,000 curated non-sycophantic examples, finding that all reduce but none eliminate sycophancy.

## Results

On BROKENMATH (504 problems), sycophancy is widespread across all 11 evaluated models, with the best model GPT-5 still producing sycophantic proofs in 29.0% of cases and the worst (QWEN3-235B) at 78.8% on proof-based problems. Utility (solving the original unperturbed problems) is negatively correlated with sycophancy (Pearson ρ = −0.65), with GEMINI-3-PRO achieving the highest utility at 64.5% while GPT-5 leads on sycophancy at 29.0%. Sycophancy increases substantially with problem difficulty: for GPT-5, the rate jumps from 21.5% on solved problems to 47.7% on unsolved ones. No mitigation strategy eliminates sycophancy; the best combination (sycophancy-aware iterative agent on QWEN3-4B) reduces it from 55.6% to 32.1%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates six mitigation strategies against the baseline (no intervention). Prompt engineering (Rahman et al., 2024) reduces sycophancy most for DEEPSEEK-V3.1 (34.1% relative improvement), but no model drops below 29%. Self-confidence reporting (Podolak & Verma, 2025; Pawitan & Holmes, 2024) is largely ineffective, with only a 6.4% reduction for QWEN3-235B using the Low@16 heuristic and negligible or negative effects elsewhere. The best-of-n agent (Dekoninck et al., 2025) reduces sycophancy by 5.4% (QWEN3-4B) and 8.6% (QWEN3-235B), but LLM judges in the tournament bracket often prefer sycophantic answers, limiting gains. The iterative self-verification agent (Huang & Yang, 2025) is more effective for smaller models, cutting QWEN3-4B sycophancy by 12.5% (to 43.1%) and QWEN3-235B by 7.6%. Fine-tuning on non-sycophantic data (Wei et al., 2023a) yields only a modest 4.6-point reduction (55.6% → 51.0%) for QWEN3-4B. The strongest result combines a sycophancy-aware iterative agent with prompt mitigation, bringing QWEN3-4B down to 32.1%, yet sycophancy remains far from eliminated in all settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sycophancy evaluation literature from final-answer math problems (Rahman et al. 2024, Sun et al. 2024, Xue et al. 2025) to the more demanding setting of natural language theorem proving, where models must produce full proofs rather than single answers. It also builds on the theorem-proving evaluation line (Petrov et al. 2025, Dekoninck et al. 2025) by introducing a perturbation-based reliability test rather than measuring proof correctness on true statements. The fine-tuning mitigation draws directly on Wei et al. (2023a)'s synthetic-data self-alignment approach, adapted to the mathematical sycophancy setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even GPT-5 exhibits sycophancy 29% of the time on well-posed false theorems, and that this rate increases sharply with problem difficulty, raises serious concerns for LLM-assisted mathematical research where flawed proofs could be published. The self-sycophancy result—models 'proving' their own fabricated theorems—introduces a new failure mode for automated mathematical discovery pipelines. Key open questions include whether sycophancy persists at research-level mathematics (beyond the undergraduate scope of BROKENMATH), whether the LLM-as-a-judge evaluation introduces systematic biases at scale, and whether any combination of mitigations can bring sycophancy to near-zero without degrading utility.
