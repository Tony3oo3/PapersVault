## Problem

The term "machine unlearning" in LLM research has been applied to a wide range of objectives—harmful-behavior refusal, entity removal, knowledge suppression, and targeted output blocking—that share a surface-level "make the model forget X" motivation but do not match the classical dataset-defined deletion guarantee of retraining-indistinguishability. This terminological overload causes benchmarks and metrics to conflate output-failure (e.g., low ROUGE, reduced QA accuracy) with actual removal of training influence, systematically rewarding surface-level non-disclosure while derived capabilities persist. The paper argues this confusion is not cosmetic but distorts evaluation, makes cross-paper comparisons unreliable, and obscures what guarantee is actually being claimed.

## Input / Output

The formal machine unlearning setup the paper advocates takes a trained model Θ_D and a precisely specified forget set F ⊆ D as input, and produces an updated model Θ' whose induced distribution over parameters (or behavior under a prompt distribution and decoding rule) should be (approximately) indistinguishable from the counterfactual model retrained on D \ F. The paper contrasts this with the operational objectives of non-unlearning mechanisms, which take forget-set (x, y) pairs and produce modified token likelihoods, replacement responses, refusal templates, or deployment-time routing rules rather than a model matching the retrain reference.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a formal and taxonomic reorganization of what "unlearning" means. It provides exact and approximate definitions of machine unlearning grounded in (ε, δ)-indistinguishability from retraining on D \ F, then categorizes five common non-unlearning mechanisms—output likelihood suppression, internal representation obfuscation, knowledge editing, behavioral refusal, and inference-time interventions—by their operational objectives, showing via explicit loss formulations that each optimizes a different criterion (likelihood reduction, entropy maximization, response overwriting, abstention training, or deployment filtering) rather than retraining equivalence. The paper introduces the concept of "derived capabilities" (transferable reasoning skills, poisoning-induced behaviors, latent factual competence recoverable through benign fine-tuning or quantization) to demonstrate why output-failure metrics are insufficient, and proposes that unlearning benchmarks must anchor evaluation to an explicit retrain reference model and include derived-capability probes—capability-level holdout tasks, intervention-based recovery tests, and task-appropriate threat models—as first-class evaluation components.

## Results

This is a position paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative results tables. Its primary analytical contribution is a taxonomy of five non-unlearning mechanisms (output likelihood suppression, internal representation obfuscation, knowledge editing, behavioral refusal, inference-time interventions) and a critique of four major LLM unlearning benchmarks (TOFU, MUSE, RWKU, WMDP). The paper's central claim is terminological: 'machine unlearning' should be reserved for retraining-indistinguishability on a precisely defined forget set, while other 'forgetting' objectives (safety alignment, knowledge suppression, entity removal) require distinct terminology and evaluation protocols.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it audits existing benchmarks for alignment with the retraining-indistinguishability definition. TOFU (Maini et al., 2024) is identified as the most consistent with the proposed definition because it explicitly evaluates against a retrained reference model using probability-based truth ratios and two-sample tests, though subsequent works (Yuan et al., 2025; Wuerkaixi et al., 2025) are noted to misuse TOFU by omitting the retrained reference. MUSE (Shi et al., 2025) includes a retrained baseline and MIA-based privacy metrics but relies on ROUGE-L for verbatim/knowledge memorization without distributional comparison to the reference. RWKU (Jin et al., 2024) and WMDP (Li et al., 2024) are characterized as measuring robustness of knowledge suppression and capability reduction, respectively, rather than retrain-equivalent unlearning, since neither uses a retrained reference model. The paper concludes that output-failure metrics (ROUGE, QA accuracy, likelihood) dominate current practice and structurally favor suppression/refusal methods over true influence removal, particularly in the presence of derived capabilities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper reinterprets the classical machine unlearning formulation (Ginart et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2020; Izzo et al., 2021) as the sole legitimate meaning of the term, and argues that the LLM unlearning literature (TOFU, MUSE, RWKU, WMDP) has drifted from this definition by conflating dataset-defined deletion with policy-driven behavior control, knowledge editing, and output suppression. Rather than proposing a new method, it carves out a terminological and evaluative boundary: retraining-indistinguishability for a precisely specified forget set is 'unlearning,' while suppression, obfuscation, editing, refusal, and inference-time filtering are distinct objectives requiring distinct names and baselines. The paper draws on the differential-privacy-inspired approximate unlearning framework and the emerging evidence on derived capabilities (poisoning, synthetic reasoning traces) to argue that current output-failure metrics are structurally misaligned with the stated goal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If the proposed terminology and evaluation protocol gain traction, it would force the LLM unlearning community to anchor claims to explicit retraining references (or stated proxies) and to include derived-capability probes, fundamentally changing how progress is measured. The practical stakes are high: regulatory deletion obligations and copyright disputes require a defensible notion of 'influence removed,' and the paper shows that current benchmarks can certify success while transferable capabilities or latent knowledge persist. Key open questions include how to construct feasible retraining proxies at frontier scale, how to design capability-level holdout tasks that are neither utility evaluations nor trivially gameable, and whether the proposed taxonomy will be adopted or whether the umbrella-term compromise (View 5) prevails in practice.
