## Problem

Deployed AI systems relying on reactive test-driven flywheels patch individual observed errors without addressing the broader task conditions that caused them, leading to repeated failures on functionally identical scenarios (e.g., a firetruck crash fix failing to prevent a school bus crash). This reactive paradigm also suffers from coupon-collector scaling—remaining edge cases become exponentially harder to encounter—and generates persistent backlogs that strain fixed engineering resources. The paper argues that reactive patching is structurally suboptimal for open-ended, generalizable AI and that a proactive, goal-oriented maintenance paradigm is needed.

## Input / Output

The method takes two distinct inputs: (1) task testing conditions extracted from requirements documents (e.g., NHTSA ADS 2.0 guidelines, DMV handbooks) to construct an n-dimensional test space without any real system data, and (2) deployed system feedback (incidents, user interactions, performance metrics) that is subsequently mapped into this pre-existing space. The output is not a list of individually fixed bugs but a coverage map over the test space identifying weakly covered task-condition regions, along with an updated model that resolves entire factor groups simultaneously. The test space acts as a data-free reference frame, so new feedback can be aligned to it without re-deriving the structure from scratch.

## Method

The core contribution is the 'test space': an n-dimensional space of vectorized task-condition factors derived from requirements documents (analogous to 'train space' in data curation but constructed without system data), over which the proactive flywheel aims to improve coverage. When an error is observed, it is mapped to its corresponding factor group in the test space, and the model update targets the entire group rather than the single observed instance. The paper proves (Thms. 3.1–3.2) that the expected completion time scales as Θ((K/p_P) log K) for the proactive flywheel versus Θ((M/p_R) log M) for the reactive one, where K is the number of task-condition factors and M is the total number of scenarios (K ≪ M), yielding orders-of-magnitude fewer iterations even when proactive correction is harder per-instance (p_P < p_R). The full pipeline comprises six stages: test space creation via LLM/VLM-assisted factor extraction from documents, automated feedback gathering, feedback-to-test-space alignment, weakness pattern recognition via clustering or learned methods, and solution routing between model-centric and data-centric fixes. Corollary 3.3 formalizes the exact condition under which proactive dominates reactive, and Proposition 3.6 shows that proactive backlogs are bounded above by reactive backlogs after a finite crossover time.

## Results

The paper's main quantitative contribution is a pair of asymptotic scaling theorems: the reactive flywheel requires Θ((M/p_R) log M) expected iterations to correct all M scenarios, while the proactive flywheel requires only Θ((K/p_P) log K), where K is the number of factor groups and M/K is the number of scenarios per group. Corollary 3.3 gives the exact crossover condition: proactive wins whenever p_P/p_R ≥ (K·H_K)/(M·H_M), meaning that even if proactive correction is 2–3× harder per iteration (p_R/p_P = 2 or 3), it still requires fewer total iterations as long as each factor group covers a sufficiently large number of scenarios. On the backlog side (Thms. 3.4–3.5, Prop. 3.6), the proactive paradigm achieves a maximum backlog that is orders of magnitude smaller than reactive in the best case (K=1), and never worse in the worst case (K=M).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against named external methods; its sole 'baseline' is the reactive test-driven flywheel (RF) paradigm, which is the standard incident-response maintenance loop used in deployed AI systems (e.g., Tesla's data engine, Waymo's safety pipeline). The theoretical comparison shows that proactive flywheel (PF) strictly dominates RF in expected iterations whenever the ratio M/K (scenarios per factor group) exceeds a threshold determined by p_R/p_P. Concretely, Fig. 3 illustrates that even with p_P = p_R/2 (proactive twice as difficult), PF outperforms RF for M/K ≳ 3, and the gap widens to multiple orders of magnitude as M/K grows. On backlog, Fig. 4 shows PF's peak backlog is orders of magnitude lower than RF's when K is small, while in the degenerate case K = M (no meaningful grouping), PF's backlog is at worst equal to RF's. The paper also notes that these results are optimistic because they assume uniform scenario exposure and no catastrophic forgetting, both of which would affect RF and PF similarly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the 'train space' concept from data-centric AI (Shen et al., 2025; Sener and Savarese, 2017) — an n-dimensional embedding used for training data diversity selection — into a 'test space' that maps task requirements rather than observed data, thereby reframing model maintenance from error-patching to coverage-driven refinement. It draws on the coupon collector problem (Boneh and Hofri, 1997) to formalize the long-tail cost of reactive approaches, and acknowledges behavioral testing (Ribeiro et al., 2020; Srivastava et al., 2023) as the closest prior work toward proactive framing, though noting that behavioral testing still remediates reactively after test failures surface. The paper departs from the incident-response and hard-negative-mining literature (Shrivastava et al., 2016; Xin et al., 2021) by arguing that per-error patching is structurally suboptimal for open-ended, long-tail deployment, and introduces a formal mathematical comparison (via harmonic-number scaling) between reactive and proactive flywheel strategies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rigorous theoretical argument — grounded in coupon-collector asymptotics — that proactive, test-space-driven maintenance requires orders of magnitude fewer flywheel iterations than reactive patching, even when proactive correction is harder per iteration, which could reshape how organizations budget engineering resources for deployed AI. However, as a position paper it offers no empirical validation of the proactive flywheel, and the six open problems it identifies (test space creation, representation, feedback alignment, weakness discovery, solution routing) remain unsolved, making near-term adoption speculative. The theoretical results also rely on strong simplifying assumptions (uniform scenario exposure, no catastrophic forgetting, one failure per iteration) that may not hold in high-traffic production systems, limiting the direct transferability of the quantitative bounds.
