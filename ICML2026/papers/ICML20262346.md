## Problem

Existing hand-object interaction reconstruction methods either restrict objects to rigid or part-wise rigid representations, or model deformable objects using only sparse point-based controllers (~30 points from depth maps) rather than full 3D hand geometry. This limits the precision of interaction force modeling and leads to suboptimal spring-mass topology for simulation, particularly under dense hand-object contacts common in real-world manipulation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262346/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes sparse-view (typically 3-view) RGB-D video sequences along with 2D hand keypoints from MediaPipe and a monocular MANO estimator, plus CoTracker3 point trajectories for supervision. It produces a full MANO hand parameterization (45-D pose, 10-D shape, rotation, translation per frame) and a spring-mass object model (node positions, per-spring stiffness/damping, virtual spring topology) that can be re-simulated to predict future object deformation. Initial object geometry at t=0 is obtained from an image-to-3D generative model, and surrogate Gaussian splats are learned on the object for photometric evaluation.

## Method

PHYSHANDI uses a three-stage optimization pipeline: (1) fitting MANO hand meshes to multi-view RGB-D via reprojection, depth, and temporal smoothness losses; (2) fitting a spring-mass object model through hierarchical optimization (zero-order topology/stiffness search, then first-order per-spring parameter refinement) where object deformation is simulated under forces from virtual springs connecting dense MANO hand vertices to object nodes within a connection radius δ; (3) refining hand parameters via inverse physics by backpropagating Chamfer and tracking losses through the fitted spring-mass simulation. The key distinction from PhysTwin is that interaction forces are induced by the full 778-vertex MANO mesh rather than ~30 sparse depth-sampled points, yielding more accurate contact localization and a topology with ~2× lower radius-to-resolution deviation for object springs and >7× lower for virtual springs (per peridynamics analysis). The inverse-physics hand refinement stage is especially effective under sparse-view or single-view settings where initial hand fitting is ill-posed.

## Results

On the PhysTwin-dense dataset, PHYSHANDI achieves CD_dyn of 8.32 mm and CD_full of 5.30 mm for reconstruction & resimulation, outperforming PhysTwin (10.78 / 5.90 mm) on all five 3D and 2D metrics. For future prediction, PHYSHANDI reaches CD_dyn of 14.35 mm and CD_full of 10.57 mm, again beating PhysTwin (16.32 / 11.45 mm) across all metrics. On the newly introduced DENSEHDI dataset (19 sequences with dense hand-object contacts), PHYSHANDI achieves CD of 5.06 mm vs. PhysTwin's 5.59 mm for reconstruction, and 7.54 mm vs. 7.98 mm for future prediction. In the single-view future prediction setting, the inverse-physics hand refinement reduces CD from 42.8 mm to 33.5 mm and improves Hand CD from 7.57 to 7.17 mm.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PHYSHANDI outperforms the closest baseline, PhysTwin (Jiang et al., 2025), on every metric in both reconstruction and future prediction on PhysTwin-dense: CD_dyn improves from 10.78 to 8.32 mm (−23%) and from 16.32 to 14.35 mm (−12%) for reconstruction and prediction respectively, while IoU rises from 0.84→0.85 and 0.70→0.73. The gains are attributed to dense MANO-driven interaction forces replacing PhysTwin's ~30 sparse depth-sampled control points, yielding a 2× lower Radius-to-Resolution Deviation for object springs and >7× lower for virtual springs. Against Spring-Gaus (Zhong et al., 2024) and GS-Dynamics (Zhang et al., 2024a), PHYSHANDI leads by a large margin (e.g., CD_dyn of 8.32 vs. 27.79 and 33.37 mm), as both baselines were designed for different input regimes (dense viewpoints and long sequences, respectively) and degrade under the three-view sparse setting. On the DENSEHDI dataset with denser contacts, PHYSHANDI again beats PhysTwin on all metrics (CD 5.06 vs. 5.59 mm), and per-sequence analysis shows the largest gains on dense-contact sequences (e.g., CD_dyn 6.23 vs. 21.30 mm on double_stretch_sloth). In the single-view future prediction setting, PhysTwin fails entirely due to unreliable sparse contact identification, while PHYSHANDI with inverse-physics hand refinement achieves CD of 33.5 mm and Hand CD of 7.17 mm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on PhysTwin (Jiang et al., 2025), which established physics-based deformable object reconstruction from sparse-view RGB-D using a spring-mass model, but represented the interacting hand only as ~30 sparse points sampled from depth maps. PHYSHANDI departs from this by replacing the sparse controller with a dense MANO hand mesh, so that interaction forces driving the spring-mass simulation are induced by the full 3D hand geometry. It further introduces an inverse-physics hand refinement stage—using the fitted object model to improve hand fitting—that has no counterpart in prior hand-object interaction work, which typically treats hand and object reconstruction as independent or one-directional problems. The broader lineage connects to rigid/part-wise-rigid hand-object reconstruction (Hampali, Chao, Fan) and to simulation-based deformable object modeling (Spring-Gaus, GS-Dynamics), but those lines either assume rigidity or omit full hand geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PHYSHANDI is the first framework to jointly reconstruct dense 3D hands and large-scale non-rigid objects from sparse-view RGB-D, closing a gap that has limited prior work to either rigid objects or object-only reconstruction. The inverse-physics hand refinement, while showing modest gains in multi-view settings, is particularly effective in single-view future prediction where hand fitting is highly ill-posed, suggesting a general principle that physics-based object priors can regularize hand estimation. Key limitations include the assumption of static contact topology within a sequence, the lack of explicit force magnitude modeling (virtual springs act as boundary conditions rather than physical forces), and the optimization-based pipeline's computational cost (~60 s/frame for full training). Open questions include handling dynamic contact changes, scaling to RGB-only inputs, and whether the peridynamics-informed topology analysis generalizes to more complex object geometries.
