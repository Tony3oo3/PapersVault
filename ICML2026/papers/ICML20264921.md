## Problem

Current world-model evaluations either measure only properties observable from interaction trajectories (next-frame prediction, task return) or constrain agent outputs to fixed symbolic formats, failing to test whether a learned model supports diverse environment-level queries whose answers depend on the full environment's structure rather than any single rollout. No existing benchmark unifies interactive, reward-free exploration with representation-agnostic, query-driven evaluation of the resulting world model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264921/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

During the interaction phase, the agent receives 2D grid observations (color-string arrays of size 3×3 to 25×25) and selects from directional, click, no-op, and reset actions with no reward signal. In the test phase, the agent operates in a *derived* challenge environment—a modified POMDP with altered dynamics or observations—and produces a behavioral response: selecting one of six masked-frame candidates, reporting a change-detection timestep, or executing an action sequence to reach a goal subgrid configuration. Scoring is purely behavioral (binary or continuous), computed from the agent's actions in the challenge environment with no inspection of internal representations.

## Method

WorldTest formalizes environment-level queries as computable higher-order functions q: M → Y over the space of environments, then encodes each query as a derived challenge POMDP via a deterministic transformation τ(M, ξ) that modifies transitions, observations, or adds explicit rewards. The two-phase protocol separates reward-free autonomous exploration (where the agent builds an internal model M̂) from scored evaluation in the challenge environment, ensuring the agent learns transferable dynamics rather than a task-specific policy. AutumnBench instantiates this with 43 grid-world environments defined in the Autumn DSL (a functional reactive language for 2D causal interactions), each paired with three τ functions: masked-frame prediction (reconstruct masked regions from a partially observed trajectory), change detection (identify the earliest timestep where modified dynamics diverge from the original), and planning (produce an action sequence reaching a target subgrid configuration). The framework is representation-agnostic—agents are scored solely on behavior in the challenge environment—enabling fair comparison across humans, LLMs, and simulator-based agents without requiring any particular output format.

## Results

On AutumnBench (43 grid-world environments, 129 tasks across masked-frame prediction, change detection, and planning), the human baseline (80th-percentile aggregate of 517 participants) achieves 93.5% overall score, substantially outperforming all five frontier reasoning models. The best reasoning model, Claude Opus 4.6 (saturation check), reaches 39.9% overall (CD: 19.8%, MFP: 44.2%, Planning: 55.8%), surpassing the previous best of 27.5% by o3. Twelve environments still yield zero scores across all tasks and all models, and 42% of environments show no performance improvement from additional computational cost, indicating fundamental reasoning limitations beyond scaling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between 517 human participants (aggregated at the 80th percentile) and five frontier reasoning models (Claude 4 Sonnet, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Gemini 2.5 Flash, o3, Qwen3-235B), plus a saturation check with Claude Opus 4.6. Humans outperform every model on all 43 environments and all three task types, with a 93.5% vs. 39.9% gap for the best model. The gap is attributed to two behavioral differences: (1) humans use resets as hypothesis-testing tools (~12.5% of actions) while models spend <7% on resets+no-ops combined, and (2) humans show lower normalized perplexity (AUC and final values), indicating more focused, purposeful exploration. An Autumn-simulator agent with ground-truth program access also underperforms humans on all three task types, highlighting that even perfect next-state prediction is insufficient without strategic inference under uncertainty. Performance does not monotonically scale with compute: 42% of environments show no benefit from more expensive models, and prompt variations shift scores by <0.1, confirming that model capability rather than prompt engineering drives results.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Autumn DSL (Das et al., 2023) for environment specification and unifies several previously fragmented evaluation paradigms—next-frame prediction, planning, and change detection—under a single formal framework of 'environment-level queries' as higher-order functions on POMDPs. It departs from prior work in three key ways: unlike non-interactive benchmarks (ARC, Bongard, CLEVRER), it requires learning through interaction; unlike gym-like RL benchmarks, it is reward-free and representation-agnostic; and unlike two-phase RL benchmarks (URLB, in-context RL), it evaluates in a *derived* challenge environment rather than the same environment, enabling tests of structural properties (reachability, counterfactuals) that trajectories alone cannot reveal. The POMDP formulation is standard, but the formalization of queries as computable functions q: M → Y and the two-phase protocol with explicit task-type disclosure (Step 1) are novel contributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AutumnBench provides a concrete, extensible benchmark (43 environments, 129 tasks) that reliably separates human from frontier-model world-modeling, with the human baseline at 93.5% versus the best model at 39.9%—a gap the authors attribute to differences in exploration strategy (humans use resets as hypothesis-testing tools; models use them in <7% of actions) and belief updating (models fail to revise rules when test-phase evidence contradicts interaction-phase learning). The finding that 42% of environments show no benefit from additional compute suggests fundamental reasoning limitations beyond scaling, pointing toward the need for better strategic experimental design and uncertainty quantification in agents. Limitations include the grid-world setting, single-trajectory evaluation per model (due to API costs), and prompt sensitivity; the framework's extension to physics-rich, robotic, or multi-agent domains remains open.
