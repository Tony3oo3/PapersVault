## Problem

While recent work (Singh et al., 2024) showed that function-valued causal targets such as CATEs and dose-response curves can be estimated from observational data via RKHS inner-product representations and kernel regression, it left the critical question of uncertainty quantification around these estimators unaddressed. Existing GP-based approaches to this problem (e.g., BayesIMP) construct bespoke nuclear-dominant kernels that are non-stationary for radial base kernels, causing systematic underfitting, and rely on finite-dimensional approximations that collapse posterior variance outside the training support.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single observational dataset of tuples (Y, W, V, Z) or two partial datasets in the causal data fusion setting, along with user-specified positive-definite kernels k_W, k_V, k_Z and noise variances σ², η². It produces a posterior mean that exactly recovers the plug-in kernel estimator of Singh et al. (2024), a closed-form posterior variance decomposed into three interpretable terms (S₁, S₂, S₃), and credible intervals constructed from a moment-matched Gaussian process approximation. A free spectral measure ν (parameterized as N(V̄, β²I) for continuous V) is tuned post-hoc via bootstrap-based calibration to match frequentist coverage.

## Method

IMPSPEC (Interventional Mean Process with Spectral representation) avoids the pathologies of prior GP constructions by enlarging the function class for f and using the Mercer spectral decomposition for μ. Specifically, it places a linear-kernel GP prior directly on the outcome-regression functional f (relaxing the boundedness constraint), and decomposes the infinite-dimensional conditional mean embedding μ(z) = E[ψ_V(V)|Z=z] into scalar coordinates μ_i(z) = E[φ_{V,i}(V)|Z=z], each receiving a standard GP prior with kernel k_Z. The induced prior on the causal function γ(w,z) = f(ψ_W(w) ⊗ μ(z)) is a product of GPs with heavier-tailed sample paths than a standard GP. Posterior inference factorizes into standard scalar GP regression updates for f and each μ_i, yielding closed-form posterior moments whose mean exactly matches the kernel ridge regression estimator. Hyperparameters are trained via marginal likelihood (standard GPR for f, a novel weighted log-likelihood aggregated over spectral coordinates for μ), and a calibration algorithm tunes the spectral measure ν by minimizing bootstrap-estimated coverage error across a grid of test points and confidence levels.

## Results

On the synthetic benchmark DAG (n=100, 50 trials), IMPSPEC achieves the best RMSE (0.36±0.16), calibration error (0.05±0.02), and 95% interval score (2.27±2.03) for CATE estimation, outperforming BayesIMP (0.38/0.11/2.99), the sampling GP of Witty et al. (0.40/0.19/6.67), continuous doubly-robust estimation (0.61/0.13/8.37), and orthogonal random forests (0.84/0.16/10.34). In causal Bayesian optimization, IMPSPEC achieves the lowest cumulative regret across all three tasks (e.g., 0.247 vs. 0.698 for BayesIMP on the front-door task), and in one case is the only method to find the approximate optimum within 10 iterations. On the 401(k) dataset (~10k samples), the method runs in under a minute on a single GPU with peak memory below 16 GB.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IMPSPEC outperforms all five baselines on the synthetic benchmark across all three metrics. Against the closest GP-based competitor BayesIMP (Chau et al., 2021), IMPSPEC reduces calibration error by more than half (0.05 vs. 0.11) and interval score by ~24% (2.27 vs. 2.99), while matching RMSE; the paper attributes BayesIMP's weaknesses to non-stationary kernel constructions causing underfitting and finite-dimensional approximations causing variance collapse out-of-distribution. The sampling GP of Witty et al. (2020) performs similarly to BayesIMP on RMSE but is substantially worse on calibration (0.19 vs. 0.05) and interval score (6.67 vs. 2.27). Frequentist baselines—continuous doubly-robust estimation (Kennedy et al., 2017) and orthogonal random forests (Oprescu et al., 2019)—achieve higher RMSE (0.61 and 0.84 respectively) and much worse interval scores (8.37 and 10.34), with ORF particularly struggling due to its locally linear treatment-effect model. In causal Bayesian optimization (Table 3), IMPSPEC achieves the lowest cumulative regret on the front-door task (0.247 vs. 0.656 for CBO and 0.698 for BayesIMP) and the healthcare task (0.000 vs. 0.003 for BayesIMP), while remaining competitive on the back-door task (0.335 vs. 0.383 for CBO).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Singh et al. (2024), which reduced causal function estimation (CATE, dose-response, ATT) to non-parametric kernel regression in an RKHS, and on Syring & Martin (2019) for posterior calibration. It departs from the closest prior GP-based method, BayesIMP (Chau et al., 2021b), which constructed bespoke nuclear-dominant covariance kernels to enforce RKHS constraints on GP sample paths. IMPSPEC instead relaxes the RKHS constraint and uses the Mercer spectral decomposition to reduce the infinite-dimensional RKHS-valued conditional mean embedding to a sequence of scalar coordinates, enabling standard GP priors and closed-form posterior moments while still recovering the original kernel estimator as the posterior mean.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first tractable, closed-form Bayesian uncertainty quantification for function-valued causal targets that is provably anchored to the established kernel estimator of Singh et al. (2024), filling a gap where frequentist methods require parametric smoothness assumptions and prior GP constructions (BayesIMP) suffer from non-stationarity and variance collapse. The calibration procedure via spectral measure tuning is a practical contribution that addresses the surrogate-likelihood approximation error. Open questions include whether richer (non-Gaussian) posterior approximations or broader spectral families are needed when true uncertainty is strongly non-Gaussian, and whether sparse kernel methods can extend the approach to much larger datasets beyond the ~10k scale demonstrated on 401(k).
