## Problem

Game world modeling and RL papers evaluate transition models via downstream return, qualitative rollouts, or one-step prediction losses in incompatible units, but rarely report how difficult the underlying transition prediction problem actually is at the declared interface. Without a shared, operational notion of transition complexity, comparisons across benchmarks (Atari vs. Quake II vs. prompt-generated worlds) and across protocols (e.g., sticky-action probability) are confounded, and benchmark selection becomes ad hoc.

## Input / Output

The method consumes either a resettable simulator with controllable exogenous randomness or logged gameplay trajectories, together with a declared observation interface (full board, stacked pixel frames, or tokenized context window), the corresponding action stream, and a reference distribution d(x,a) (both the empirical data distribution and a standardized probing distribution). It produces a structured TCP card reporting per-axis statistics: Axis I gives mean/median/90th-percentile collision entropy (bits) and effective branching factor; Axis II gives opponent within/mixture entropy and conditional mutual information influence (bits); Axis III gives the full probe-loss curve k↦L_k plus ε-memory depth and, where spatially indexed, ε-dependency radius. All values are reported under a versioned measurement budget (TCP-Lite-v1: 5k samples, M=8 resamples, 200M probe tokens; TCP-Std-v1: 20k samples, M=32 resamples, 1B probe tokens) with 3-seed means, stds, and 95% bootstrap CIs.

## Method

TCP is a three-axis information-theoretic measurement framework defined relative to the triple (environment, declared interface X_t=φ(H_t), reference distribution d). Axis I quantifies intrinsic one-step branching via conditional Shannon entropy C_H^(1) and, more robustly, conditional Rényi-2 (collision) entropy C_B^(1), estimated from M i.i.d. simulator resamples using an unbiased pairwise-collision estimator or, for log-only data, from cross-entropy of two fixed probe models (a 2-layer GRU and a 4-layer decoder-only Transformer, both versioned as TCP-Ref-v1). Axis II decomposes interaction uncertainty into within-opponent action entropy, population-mixture entropy, and opponent influence on the next state via conditional mutual information I(B;X' | X,A,θ), with a probe-based influence proxy when opponent types are unavailable. Axis III measures temporal dependency span by training the same versioned probes at increasing context lengths k and reporting the smallest k where loss saturates within ε bits/token, and spatial dependency span via a 4-layer masked Transformer (TCP-Ref-Loc-v1) over radius-r neighborhoods. Two theoretical sanity properties anchor the framework: refinement of the conditioning interface cannot increase conditional entropy for a fixed target (Theorem 1), and unobserved chance/opponent randomness upper-bounds next-state uncertainty with an explicit decomposition into relevant and irrelevant randomness (Theorem 2).

## Results

As a position paper, the primary quantitative contribution is a fully worked TCP example for tic-tac-toe under a uniform-random reference distribution: C_H^(1) ≈ 1.906 bits, C_B^(1) ≈ 3.75 (effective branching), C_infl^opp ≈ 1.906 bits (all branching attributed to the opponent), and C_mem(ε) = 1 (Markov at the full-board interface). A second sanity check shows that ALE sticky actions at p = 0.25 contribute h_2(0.25) ≈ 0.811 bits of one-step entropy whenever the two executed-action outcomes differ. The paper also proves two structural theorems: (i) refining the conditioning interface cannot increase conditional entropy for a fixed prediction target, and (ii) unobserved chance/opponent randomness upper-bounds next-state uncertainty via H(X'|X,A) = I((B,Ω); X' | X,A).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper proposing a new measurement framework (TCP) rather than a method that competes empirically against baselines, so no head-to-head performance comparisons are reported. Instead, the paper positions TCP against several existing complexity notions and argues why each is insufficient for the transition-prediction problem: sample-complexity/regret measures (Eluder dimension, Bellman rank, witness rank) govern policy-learning difficulty but do not quantify next-observation multiplicity at a declared interface; state-abstraction/bisimulation results address equivalence and compression rather than providing quantitative transition descriptors; information-theoretic process-complexity measures (predictive information, excess entropy) are defined for passive stochastic processes without actions or opponents; and classic game-tree branching factors (Shannon, 1950) assume full observability and do not account for the finite-history, partially observed interface that world models actually operate on. The paper's two theorems (refinement monotonicity and the unobserved-randomness upper bound) serve as the theoretical grounding that distinguishes TCP from these prior notions, showing that TCP is a property of the triple (environment, interface, reference distribution) and that protocol choices (e.g., sticky-action probability) materially shift the measured values.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by proposing an operational measurement layer (the Transition Complexity Profile) that sits deliberately outside existing complexity frameworks: it is not a policy-learning difficulty measure (Eluder dimension, Bellman rank), not a state-equivalence criterion (bisimulation), and not a passive-process information-theoretic quantity (predictive information, excess entropy). It repurposes standard information-theoretic tools—conditional Shannon and Rényi entropies, conditional mutual information—but grounds them in a declared observation interface, reference distribution, and versioned probe budget, making them reproducible benchmark metadata rather than abstract invariants. The paper positions itself as complementary to, not a replacement for, downstream return and qualitative rollout evaluation in modern world-model papers (Dreamer, IRIS, DIAMOND, WHAM, Genie).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core practical impact is that cross-domain and cross-protocol comparisons in game world modeling become interpretable: a model evaluated on Atari 100k and one on Quake II are solving fundamentally different transition-prediction problems, and TCP makes that difference explicit and quantified. The paper also highlights a subtle but important confound—protocol details like sticky-action probability shift the induced transition kernel by ~0.8 bits, meaning two papers on 'the same' Atari benchmark may be measuring different objects. Key open questions include whether the standardized probe family (TCP-Ref-GRU/TX/Loc) is expressive enough for high-dimensional continuous interfaces, how the community will version and maintain TCP cards as interfaces evolve, and whether adoption will actually happen without a strong enforcement mechanism in venues.
