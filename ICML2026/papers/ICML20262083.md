## Problem

Recursive training of generative models on their own outputs causes progressive degradation known as model collapse, yet the theoretical understanding of this phenomenon in diffusion models has been limited to architecture-specific upper bounds. No lower bound on the divergence between the learned and target distributions existed, even for standard (non-recursive) diffusion models, making it impossible to characterize when and how errors accumulate across generations. This paper asks: in a realistic recursive pipeline mixing fresh and synthetic data, how do score estimation errors propagate within and across generations, and what regimes of drift emerge?

## Input / Output

The analysis takes as input a recursive training pipeline where each generation's training set is an unlabeled mixture of α fresh samples from the true data distribution p_data and (1−α) synthetic samples from the current model, with a score network trained on this mixture to produce the next generation. The output is a pair of upper and lower bounds on the χ²-divergence between the generated and target distributions, expressed in terms of the pathwise score-error energy and an observability coefficient, culminating in a geometrically-discounted decomposition of accumulated divergence where errors from m generations ago are suppressed by a factor (1−α)^(2m). The theory operates in the perturbative regime where per-generation score error energy is small (ε²_★,i ≤ 1), which for high-dimensional data requires sample sizes scaling as (1/t₀)^(d/2).

## Method

The core technique applies Girsanov's theorem to express the Radon-Nikodym derivative between the learned and ideal reverse-time path measures as an exponential functional of the score error, then projects this path-space ratio onto the terminal marginal to obtain the density ratio R_i(x) = p̂^(i+1)(x)/q_i(x). A key new ingredient is the observability coefficient η_i, which quantifies the fraction of pathwise error energy that is visible at the endpoint t₀, enabling a lower bound on intra-generation divergence of the form χ²(p̂^(i+1)‖q_i) ≥ (1/8)η_i·ε²_★,i in the perturbative regime — the first such lower bound for diffusion models. Combining this with a standard Girsanov upper bound and proving equivalence between ideal-path and learned-path energies yields a two-sided control χ²(p̂^(i+1)‖q_i) ≍ ε²_★,i. For long-horizon accumulation, the paper exploits the contraction property χ²(q_i‖p_data) = (1−α)²χ²(p̂^i‖p_data) to show that persistent score errors produce non-vanishing drift, while summable errors yield a discounted sum D_N + C_bias ≍ Σ_k (1−α)^(2(N−1−k))·ε²_★,k, identifying a clear dichotomy between stable and collapsing regimes governed by the fresh-data fraction α.

## Results

The paper establishes the first lower bound on the divergence between a learned diffusion model's output and its target distribution: χ²(p̂^{i+1}‖q_i) ≥ (1/4)η_i·ε²_{*,i} − C·ε⁴_{*,i} (Proposition 3.3), where η_i is an observability coefficient and ε²_{*,i} is the pathwise score-error energy. In the perturbative regime (small score errors), Theorem 3.5 shows the intra-generation divergence is equivalent to the score error energy up to constants: χ²(p̂^{i+1}‖q_i) ≍ ε²_{*,i}. For long-horizon accumulation, Theorem 4.2 yields a geometrically-discounted law: D_{N+1} + C_bias ≍ Σ_{i} (1−α)^{2(N−i)}·ε²_{*,i}, showing that errors from m generations in the past are suppressed by a factor (1−α)^{2m}. Empirical validation on a 10-dimensional Gaussian mixture, Fashion-MNIST, and CIFAR-10 confirms the bounds hold across fresh-data fractions α ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 0.9} over 20 recursive training generations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not benchmark against competing methods in the standard empirical sense; there are no baseline models to compare FID, accuracy, or other generative metrics against. The paper positions its contribution as complementary to prior theoretical work: Fu et al. (ICML 2024) and Cui et al. (NeurIPS 2026) provide architecture-specific upper bounds on error accumulation for two-layer score networks, while this paper works at the population level in an architecture-agnostic setting and, crucially, establishes the first lower bound on the one-generation distribution discrepancy for diffusion models (even without recursive training). The empirical experiments (Figures 1–5, 8–10) serve to validate the theoretical bounds rather than to compare against alternative training strategies. The paper qualitatively aligns with empirical findings from Gerstgrasser et al. (2024), Zhu et al. (2025), and Dey et al. (2025) that incorporating fresh data mitigates model collapse, now providing a precise quantitative mechanism: the forgetting rate is governed by (1−α)^{2m}.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical analysis of recursive training degradation from regression and parametric distribution settings (Dohmatob et al., Bertrand et al., Suresh et al.) to score-based diffusion models, building on the SDE framework of Song et al. (2021). It is explicitly complementary to Fu et al. (2024) and Cui et al. (2026), which provide architecture-specific upper bounds for two-layer score networks; here the analysis is population-level and architecture-agnostic. The key technical departure is the use of Girsanov's theorem to derive the first lower bound on the divergence between a learned diffusion model's output and its target distribution—a result that holds even without recursive training—and the introduction of an observability coefficient that quantifies how much pathwise score error is visible at the sampling endpoint.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discounted-sum decomposition (errors from m generations past are suppressed by (1−α)²ᵐ) provides a clean theoretical explanation for why incorporating fresh data mitigates model collapse, a phenomenon observed empirically across many settings. Key limitations include the perturbative regime requirement (small per-generation score errors), the exponential-in-dimension sample complexity for accurate score estimation, and the exclusion of discretization error. Open questions highlighted by the authors include extending bounds to the large-error regime, accounting for discrete-time implementation, and characterizing whether a limiting distribution exists under infinite recursive training.
