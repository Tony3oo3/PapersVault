## Problem

Recent work reports that chain-of-thought monitorability can improve as a 'free gift' during early RLVR training, but it remains unclear whether this effect is universal across domains, whether it is merely a byproduct of capability gains, or what internal mechanisms drive it. This paper addresses the lack of a systematic characterization of how monitorability evolves under vanilla capability-targeted RLVR and what conditions make gains likely or unlikely.

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline takes text prompts augmented with three types of hint-based interventions (sycophancy, grader-hacking, and unethical-information hints for MCQ tasks; dark-web, inline-comment, and YAML-config hints for open-form math) and produces a g-mean² monitorability score computed as TPR × √(TNR₀ × TNR₁) over a monitor LLM's judgment of whether the CoT explicitly verbalizes hint influence. A complementary Draft-to-Answer (D2A) faithfulness metric intervenes on the generated CoT conclusion rather than the prompt, measuring whether the final answer causally tracks the draft. Mechanistic outputs include per-token response entropy and cross-segment attention masses (Answer→Reasoning, Answer→Prompt, Reasoning→Prompt) aggregated across all heads and layers, with BOS and template tokens excluded to avoid attention-sink confounds.

## Method

The paper conducts a controlled empirical study using GRPO-based RLVR on two base models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B and Qwen3-4B) across four training domains (Math, Code, Science, Instruction Following) with matched 800-step budgets, isolating the effect of data distribution on monitorability. Monitorability is quantified via g-mean² under hint interventions and validated with D2A faithfulness, while mechanistic drivers are identified through entropy-dynamics analysis (including controlled clipping experiments that delay or resume distribution sharpening) and cross-segment attention mass tracking. The key mechanistic finding is that monitorability gains correlate with entropy reduction and increased attention directed toward the Prompt (A→P, R→P), but are negatively correlated with Answer→Reasoning attention, suggesting the 'free gift' stems from collapsing onto narrower, prompt-grounded reasoning patterns rather than establishing stronger causal reliance on the CoT trace. The study further shows that IF training data acts as a late-stage monitorability booster, that monitorability is orthogonal to task capability, and that both training length and task difficulty modulate the magnitude of gains.

## Results

Peak monitorability (g-mean²) is strongly data-dependent: on Qwen3-4B, the IF Cascade (+IF) configuration achieves 0.63±0.02, matching the fully mixed ALL setting (0.63±0.02) and substantially outperforming w/o IF (0.54±0.03, Δ=+0.09); on DS-1.5B, +IF reaches 0.48±0.02 vs. 0.42±0.02 for w/o IF (Δ=+0.06). Early training (steps 0–300) yields the largest gains (0.06–0.17 across domains), while late training (300–800) shows diminishing or negative returns (−0.02 to +0.09). Training on hard problems collapses average monitorability from 0.39 (Easy) to 0.21 (Hard), and increasing training response length from 4096 to 8192 tokens reduces average g-mean² from 0.36 to 0.29.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical characterization study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the key contrasts are internal training configurations. The IF Cascade (+IF) strategy—400 steps on mixed w/o IF data followed by 400 steps on IF data—consistently outperforms the w/o IF baseline by +0.09 (Qwen3-4B) and +0.06 (DS-1.5B) in peak g-mean², and even matches or surpasses both pure IF training and the fully mixed ALL setting. Pure IF training yields the largest early-phase gain (0.17) but plateaus quickly, while the diverse ALL distribution maintains stronger late-phase gains (0.09 vs. 0.03 for IF). Monitorability is shown to be orthogonal to capability: capability consistently improves across all configurations, yet the correlation between task performance and g-mean² is weak, variable, and sometimes negative (e.g., Qwen3-4B Math shows negative correlation). Training on hard problems (1–8/16 correct) produces significantly lower monitorability (0.21 avg) than easy (0.39) or medium (0.36) problems, despite comparable or higher capability, indicating that premature entropy collapse onto suboptimal reasoning modes undermines the 'free gift' effect.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the initial observations by Guan et al. (2025) and Chen et al. (2025a) that monitorability can improve as a 'free gift' during early RLVR training, by making the phenomenon concrete through systematic evaluation across model families and training domains. It builds on the g-mean² metric from Guan et al., the Draft-to-Answer faithfulness framework from Xiong et al. (2025), and the cross-segment attention analysis methodology from Zhang et al. (2025) to provide mechanistic explanations. The key departure from prior work is the demonstration that the 'free gift' is strongly data-dependent and orthogonal to capability, rather than a universal byproduct of RLVR, and the identification of instruction-following data as a specific driver of monitorability gains.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that monitorability is orthogonal to capability challenges a natural assumption in the field that 'smarter' reasoning models are automatically more transparent, with direct implications for safety auditing strategies. The mechanistic attribution to entropy sharpening and prompt-directed attention (rather than stronger reasoning-to-answer causal links) raises a concerning possibility of 'false monitorability' where models appear transparent by overfitting to prompt constraints. Open questions include whether these dynamics hold for frontier-scale models, whether the IF-cascade strategy generalizes, and how to design training that produces genuine reasoning-to-answer faithfulness rather than prompt-anchored behavior. The practical guidance (medium difficulty, shorter training lengths, IF data inclusion) is immediately actionable for teams deploying RLVR-trained reasoning models.
