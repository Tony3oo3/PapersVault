## Problem

Conferences are rapidly deploying LLM-based peer reviewers (AAAI 2025, ICLR 2026) without rigorous empirical evaluation of whether these systems preserve the diversity of expert perspectives or resist manipulation. This position paper identifies and empirically demonstrates two critical failures of current AI reviewers: a 'hivemind effect' where AI reviews collapse into excessive mutual agreement, and trivial gameability through zero-shot paper rewrites that inflate scores without improving scientific substance.

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes three input streams: (1) the full ICLR 2026 review corpus of 75,800 reviews across 19,490 papers, annotated with AI-generation labels from Pangram; (2) 60 randomly sampled ICLR 2026 papers in LaTeX format, fed to AI reviewer agents (GPT-5.1, GPT-5.4, Claude Sonnet 4.5) that produce structured reviews with summary, strengths, weaknesses, questions, and a 1–10 rating; and (3) for laundering, the original AI review text is concatenated with the full LaTeX source and passed to a launderer LLM in a single zero-shot call. Outputs include IntraSim and InterSim cosine-similarity metrics computed via OpenAI text-embedding-3-small, paired score deltas on the 1–10 scale across 24 laundering conditions, and pairwise paper-embedding similarity (abstract + introduction) for convergence analysis.

## Method

The paper's empirical core has two components. First, to quantify the hivemind effect, reviews are embedded with text-embedding-3-small and two similarity metrics are computed: IntraSim (mean pairwise cosine similarity among reviews of the same paper) and InterSim (mean cosine similarity between reviews of different papers by the same reviewer type). This is applied both in-the-wild across all 75,800 ICLR 2026 reviews and in controlled simulation with three AI reviewer agents, with robustness checks restricting analysis to the weaknesses and questions sections to rule out boilerplate-driven artifacts. Second, to demonstrate gameability, the authors introduce 'paper laundering': a fully automated pipeline where the complete LaTeX source plus the original AI review is passed to a launderer LLM (GPT-5.1 or GPT-5.4) under one of four zero-shot prompts, the rewritten LaTeX is compiled to PDF, and the result is re-scored by the AI reviewer agents. Across 24 conditions (4 prompts × 2 launderers × 3 reviewers), laundering yields a mean score increase of +0.45 (p < 0.001) driven by stylistic modifications (increased hedging and emphasis words) rather than substantive scientific improvements, while simultaneously increasing pairwise similarity among laundered papers by 6.5% (Cohen's d = 1.02), indicating convergence toward an intellectual monoculture.

## Results

In simulation on 60 ICLR 2026 papers, AI reviewers show a hivemind effect: IntraSim rises from 0.811 (human) to 0.882 (GPT-5.1, +8.7%, Cohen's d=1.47) and InterSim rises from 0.470 (human) to 0.646 (GPT-5.1, +37.4%, Cohen's d=3.55). Paper laundering via zero-shot LLM rewriting increases AI review scores by a mean of +0.45 points (1–10 scale, Wilcoxon p<0.001) across 24 prompt/model conditions, while driving laundered papers toward homogeneity (+6.5% pairwise similarity, Cohen's d=1.02). Averaged human scores predict ICLR 2026 acceptance with AUC=0.822 versus only AUC=0.710 for averaged AI scores on 8,015 matched papers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's empirical baseline is human ICLR 2026 reviews (75,800 reviews across 19,490 papers, plus a 60-paper simulation subset). AI reviewer agents (GPT-5.1, GPT-5.4, Claude Sonnet 4.5) lose to humans on every measured dimension: they produce less diverse reviews (higher IntraSim and InterSim), are trivially gameable via paper laundering (+0.45 score boost with no human oversight), and their scores are weaker predictors of final acceptance (AUC 0.710 vs. 0.822 for humans). AI reviewers also correlate more strongly with each other (r=0.49) than with human reviewers (r=0.14–0.26), confirming algorithmic monoculture. In the in-the-wild ICLR 2026 data, fully AI-generated reviews (21% of all reviews, per Pangram labels) show significantly higher cross-paper similarity than human/assisted reviews (mean InterSim 0.486 vs. 0.467, Cohen's d=0.29), with the effect significant in all 21 primary areas. No AI reviewer configuration tested outperforms the human baseline on any of the two necessary conditions (diversity preservation or non-gameability).

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper extends the LLM homogeneity literature (the 'hivemind' effect documented for general-purpose generation) into the specific domain of peer review, using both in-the-wild ICLR 2026 data and controlled simulations with the Agents4Science reviewer agents. It departs from prior adversarial-attack work on AI reviewers (prompt injection, targeted textual perturbations) by introducing 'paper laundering'—a zero-shot, optimization-free rewrite that requires no hidden instructions and violates no conference policy. Conceptually, it applies the algorithmic monoculture framework of Kleinberg & Raghavan (2021) to peer review, arguing that correlated AI errors are qualitatively different from distributed human errors even when individual AI reviews look reasonable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With 21% of ICLR 2026 reviews already AI-generated and multiple venues piloting full automation, the paper provides the first large-scale empirical evidence (75,800 reviews) that current AI reviewers fail two basic necessary conditions—diversity preservation and non-gameability—before any deployment decision is made. The open questions it raises (accountability, sufficient conditions, human-AI interaction effects, incentive redesign) define a research agenda for a 'science of peer review automation' that the field currently lacks. Limitations include reliance on a single venue, 60-paper simulation samples, a fixed reviewer prompt, and third-party AI-detection labels, all of which the authors acknowledge.
