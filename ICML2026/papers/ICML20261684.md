## Problem

Grammar-constrained decoding for LLMs must resolve byte-level grammar transitions inside the token-level decoding loop, introducing CPU-side parser traversal and CPU-GPU synchronization that become severe bottlenecks under continuous batching. Existing systems (XGrammar, Pre³, Formatron) all perform this byte-level resolution at every decoding step, causing per-token overhead that grows rapidly with batch size and limits throughput-oriented structured serving.

## Input / Output

The system takes a text prompt, a deterministic grammar specification (JSON schema or CFG compiled to a DPDA), and a pretrained LLM checkpoint as inputs, and produces grammar-valid structured text (JSON, SQL, or DSL) as output. The grammar is preprocessed offline into GPU-resident token-category validity and transition tables; at runtime, each generated token is mapped to a category and the grammar state is updated via a single table lookup rather than byte-by-byte parser traversal. In schema-diverse continuous batching, each request carries an identifier selecting its grammar-specific table from a pool of resident tables.

## Method

GRAM2TOKEN preprocesses deterministic byte-level grammar execution into token-level transition summaries before inference. First, a trie-based traversal aligns each tokenizer token's byte sequence with grammar transitions: for each preprocessed grammar state, the trie is walked depth-by-depth applying one-byte Step updates, recording the final successor state (or ⊥) at each leaf to build a per-state token transition table. Second, state-aware token categorization uses trace-based partition refinement: tokens are grouped only when their intermediate execution traces are identical across all processed grammar states, yielding compact category-level validity and transition tables that avoid storing the full |S|×|V| matrix. At runtime, each candidate token is mapped to its category, validity is read from a GPU-resident bitmask, and the successor grammar state is obtained by a single table lookup—replacing parser-style byte traversal with regular index operations and elementwise masking that match batched GPU execution. This shifts the grammar-handling cost from the steady-state decoding loop to a one-time preprocessing step that is amortized by grammar reuse, longer outputs, and larger batches.

## Results

Under schema-diverse continuous batching on four 7–8B LLMs, GRAM2TOKEN achieves a geometric-mean steady-state throughput improvement of 1.38× over the strongest baseline (Pre³), with a maximum speedup of 1.85× at B=64 (e.g., 1303 TPS vs. 641 TPS on DeepSeek-R1-7B). The throughput gap widens with batch size: at B=4 the advantage is modest (e.g., 67 vs. 30 TPS on LLaMA3-8B), while at B=64 it reaches 1054 vs. 672 TPS. GRAM2TOKEN trades this throughput gain for higher time-to-first-token (e.g., 5076 ms vs. 2898 ms on LLaMA3-8B at B=64) due to upfront grammar preprocessing, but uses substantially less resident table memory (659 MB vs. 3367 MB for Pre³ at B=64). Break-even analysis shows the preprocessing overhead is amortized after 46–539 generated tokens depending on batch size.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRAM2TOKEN outperforms all three baselines on steady-state throughput (TPS) across every model and batch size tested. Against Pre³ (the strongest parser-driven baseline), GRAM2TOKEN achieves 1.38× geometric-mean TPS improvement, with the largest gap on DeepSeek-R1-7B (1303 vs. 641 TPS, ~2.0×) at B=64. Against XGrammar, the throughput advantage is even larger (e.g., 1054 vs. 581 TPS on LLaMA3-8B at B=64, ~1.8×), as XGrammar's byte-level parser traversal scales poorly with batch size. Against Formatron, GRAM2TOKEN wins on TPS (e.g., 1206 vs. 734 on Mistral-7B at B=64) and on TTFT (3972 ms vs. 9547 ms), though Formatron uses slightly less table memory at small batch sizes. GRAM2TOKEN's main trade-off is higher TTFT than Pre³ and XGrammar (due to trie-based preprocessing and categorization), but it uses 4–5× less resident grammar-table memory than Pre³ at B=64, making it more practical for GPU-resident serving with multiple concurrent schemas.

## Relation to Prior Work

GRAM2TOKEN builds directly on Pre3's deterministic pushdown automaton (DPDA) execution model, adopting its LR(1)-style byte-consuming grammar semantics as the correctness foundation. However, it departs from the parser-driven paradigm shared by XGrammar, Pre3, and Formatron by moving the byte-to-token resolution entirely out of the decoding loop: a trie-based traversal precomputes token-level transition outcomes, and a partition-refinement step (borrowed from string-matching theory) groups tokens into behavior-equivalent categories. The result is a GPU-resident lookup-and-mask pipeline that is complementary to, rather than a replacement for, the underlying grammar execution model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For production structured-LLM serving (JSON APIs, SQL generation, DSL output), grammar-constrained decoding under continuous batching is a real throughput bottleneck, and GRAM2TOKEN's 1.38× geometric-mean speedup over the strongest parser-driven baseline is practically meaningful. The key trade-off—higher preprocessing and TTFT in exchange for lower steady-state per-token cost—limits applicability to workloads with grammar reuse, moderately long outputs, or large batches, leaving cold-start short-output scenarios to parser-driven methods. Open questions include scaling to very large or dynamically changing grammars (the C-language stress test hints at growing table memory), integration with speculative decoding (mentioned but not benchmarked), and whether the approach extends beyond deterministic grammars to the broader CFG class.
