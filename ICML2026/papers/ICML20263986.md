## Problem

LLM-as-a-Judge frameworks are the de facto standard for scalable safety evaluation, yet their validation protocols compare agreement with human raters on static held-out sets that do not reflect the distribution shifts present in adversarial robustness evaluations. This paper asks whether judges remain reliable when subjected to the combined effects of attack-induced output distortion, cross-model linguistic variation, and category-dependent semantic difficulty — and finds they degrade to near-random performance, substantially worse than prior work suggests.

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline takes harmful queries from a 100-query subsample of HarmBench, generates responses from four open-weight victim models (Gemma-3-1B, Llama-3.1-8B, Gemma-27-B, Qwen-3-32B) under five attack strategies (Direct, GCG, GCG-REINFORCE, BoN, PAIR), and scores each response with multiple LLM judges. Human annotators rate each judge-positive sample on a 5-point harmfulness scale (1 = explicit refusal, 5 = full compliance; scores ≥3 are harmful), yielding 6,642 verified labels. The paper outputs judge accuracy and precision metrics, corrected Attack Success Rates (ASR scaled by judge precision), and two new benchmarks: ReliableBench (41 consistently judgeable behaviors) and JudgeStressTest (971 samples where at most one judge matches human labels).

## Method

The core contribution is a systematic audit of judge reliability under three identified distribution shifts — Attack Shift (adversarial suffixes produce high-perplexity, distorted outputs), Model Shift (linguistic style varies across victim models), and Data Shift (semantic categories differ in judging difficulty) — using 6,642 human-verified labels as ground truth. Judges (AegisGuard, HarmBench classifier, JailJudge, LlamaGuard-3, plus Claude Sonnet-4-6 and Gemini-3-Flash) are evaluated on balanced subsets across every attack-model pair, with ROC analysis confirming that threshold tuning cannot recover performance and that AUROC scores range from 0.48 to 0.64. The paper introduces a Judge Concordance score (1 minus binary entropy of the fraction of judges labeling a sample harmful) to quantify multi-judge consensus without human labels, and demonstrates that high concordance does not imply correctness because judges share systematic failure modes. To enable more reliable future evaluations, they curate ReliableBench by selecting the 41 behaviors with highest average judge accuracy (improving accuracy from 53% to 70%) and construct JudgeStressTest via leave-one-out cross-validation to isolate systemically hard samples whose difficulty transfers across judge architectures.

## Results

Across 6,642 human-verified samples, all four evaluated LLM judges (LlamaGuard-3, HarmBench classifier, AegisGuard, JailJudge) achieve only 51–59% overall accuracy on adversarial safety evaluation, performing at or near random chance. AUROC scores for JailJudge range from 0.48 (GCG-REINFORCE, worse than random) to 0.64 (GCG), far below the high human agreement reported in prior validation studies. Correcting reported Attack Success Rates for judge precision substantially reduces perceived attack effectiveness, with Best-of-N dropping from the highest to the lowest corrected ASR. Selecting the 41 most consistently judgeable behaviors (ReliableBench) improves average judge accuracy from 53% to 70%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new judge but audits four existing LLM judges (LlamaGuard-3, HarmBench Llama-2-13B classifier, AegisGuard, JailJudge) against 6,642 human labels. All four judges fail to meaningfully exceed random chance: overall accuracies range from 51% (AegisGuard) to 59% (HarmBench), with no judge surpassing 60%. On the hardest JST subset (Acc@0, where all other judges are wrong), performance collapses to 17–30%, confirming that failures are systemic rather than model-specific. The paper further shows that ensembling judges does not help: averaging multiple judge scores does not yield a clear correlation with human ratings, and high inter-judge concordance does not predict correctness. Even state-of-the-art proprietary judges (Claude Sonnet-4-6, Gemini-3-Flash) evaluated in the appendix show similarly low accuracy, indicating the problem is not specific to open-weight judges.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm for safety evaluation (Zheng et al., 2023; Mazeika et al., 2024; Souly et al., 2024) by systematically auditing judge reliability under the combined distribution shifts—attack, model, and data—that are inherent to adversarial robustness evaluations. Prior work identified individual failure modes (style sensitivity, calibration drift, hallucinated harmful content) but treated them in isolation on static validation sets; this paper is the first to quantify their joint effect and show that the interaction degrades judges to near-random performance. It also introduces the framing of 'judge hacking' as a systematic evaluation-integrity threat, connecting sampling-based attacks (BoN) and judge-in-the-loop optimization (GCG-REINFORCE) to inflated success rates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If LLM judges perform at near-chance under adversarial conditions, then a substantial body of reported Attack Success Rates in the safety literature may be artifacts of judge false positives rather than genuine model vulnerabilities, fundamentally undermining comparative claims about attack effectiveness and model robustness. The paper provides actionable mitigations—precision-corrected ASR, multi-sample collection, and the ReliableBench/JudgeStressTest benchmarks—but leaves open the harder question of how to build judges that are robust to the very distribution shifts adversarial attacks induce. Limitations include the exclusion of defense-specific shifts (e.g., Circuit Breakers' high-perplexity outputs) and reliance on HarmBench's single-turn, text-only scope, leaving multi-turn and multimodal safety evaluation unaddressed.
