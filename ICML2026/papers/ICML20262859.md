## Problem

Existing local covariate selection methods for causal effect estimation either rely on the pretreatment assumption (covariates are unaffected by treatment or outcome), require causal sufficiency (no latent confounders), or are incomplete in that they may miss valid adjustment sets. No prior method is simultaneously sound, complete, and free of both the pretreatment and causal sufficiency assumptions while operating locally rather than over the full variable set.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262859/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes observational data over a set of observed variables O (which may include latent confounders and post-treatment variables) and an ordered target pair (X, Y). It outputs one of three structured results: a valid adjustment set Z with an estimated causal effect θ (Case 1), a determination that the causal effect is zero (Case 2), or an empty result indicating the effect is non-identifiable from the available conditional independence information (Case 3). The local structure learning step operates only on the Markov blanket of X, making the search space substantially smaller than global methods.

## Method

The key theoretical contribution is a locality theorem (Theorem 1) proving that a valid adjustment set exists in the full observed set if and only if one exists within MB(X), the Markov blanket of the treatment variable—removing the need for the pretreatment assumption that prior work used to justify searching within MB(Y). Within this local boundary, three identification rules are developed: R1 uses an auxiliary variable S to verify via conditional independence tests that all active S-to-Y paths pass through X; R2 handles the case where all edge marks at X are determined, directly constructing Z as the union of definite and non-collider possible parents; R3 identifies zero causal effects through marginal or conditional independence patterns. The algorithm first learns a local PAG around X using a constraint-based procedure, then applies R1–R3 sequentially, and is proven sound and complete under causal Markov and faithfulness assumptions. The worst-case complexity is exponential in |MB⁺(X)| rather than in the full variable set n, yielding substantial computational savings over global methods like EHS.

## Results

On the Jobs dataset (reference ATT = $886), LCS achieves the lowest estimation bias at 3.55% (ATT = $854.53), outperforming EHS (5.04%), LSAS (11.67%), CEELS (22.87%), and LDP (1635.98%). On synthetic random graphs and benchmark networks (alarm, child, hailfinder, win95pts), LCS consistently achieves the lowest relative error (RE) while requiring substantially fewer conditional independence tests than global methods. On the Cattaneo2 dataset, LCS most closely approximates the empirically established reference effect of −200 to −250 grams for maternal smoking on birth weight, while requiring a relatively small number of CI tests.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LCS outperforms all four baselines on the Jobs dataset in estimation accuracy: its 3.55% bias is lower than EHS (5.04%), LSAS (11.67%), CEELS (22.87%), and dramatically lower than LDP (1635.98%, which produces a nonsensical negative ATT of −$13,598). In terms of computational cost, LCS requires 303 CI tests—more than the local methods LSAS (176), CEELS (183), and LDP (61), but far fewer than the global EHS method (1732 tests). On synthetic random graphs and benchmark networks, LCS achieves the lowest or near-lowest relative error across all sample sizes, with the advantage being most pronounced on larger networks like win95pts where pretreatment-assumption-violating baselines (LSAS, CEELS) degrade significantly. On the Cattaneo2 dataset, which deliberately includes a post-treatment descendant variable to violate the pretreatment assumption, LCS most closely matches the reference effect while CEELS, LSAS, LDP, and EHS either deviate more or incur substantially higher computational costs. The key differentiator is that LCS does not require the pretreatment assumption, whereas EHS, CEELS, and LSAS all do, and LDP, while relaxing pretreatment, lacks completeness and exhibits unstable adjustment set selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of local covariate selection methods (CEELS, LSAS, LDP) by removing both the pretreatment and causal sufficiency assumptions that prior local approaches require. It builds directly on the generalized adjustment criterion (GAC) of Perković et al. (2018) and the generalized back-door criterion of Maathuis & Colombo (2015) as its theoretical foundation, and uses the local structure learning algorithm of Li et al. (2025b) as its structural learning component. The key conceptual departure is identifying MB(X) rather than MB(Y) as the correct local boundary for valid adjustment sets when the pretreatment assumption is violated, and proving that two identification rules (R1, R2) together with a zero-effect rule (R3) yield a sound and complete procedure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work fills a clear gap in the causal inference literature: no prior local method was simultaneously sound, complete, and free of the pretreatment assumption, which is frequently violated in real-world settings (e.g., when treatment affects covariates). The clean identifiability boundary (Theorem 5) — characterizing exactly when a causal effect is non-identifiable from observational data — provides a useful theoretical reference point. Limitations include the exponential worst-case complexity (inherent to completeness) and the reliance on oracle conditional independence tests in the theoretical guarantees; the paper suggests incorporating expert knowledge as a future direction to improve practical performance.
