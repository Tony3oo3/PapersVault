## Problem

Existing neural acoustic field methods (NAF, INRAS, AVR) learn time-domain impulse responses, which is a mismatched parameterization for confined, resonant environments like car cabins where sharp spectral structure—modal resonances, frequency-dependent absorption, and dispersive phase—dominates the signal. In these compact spaces, small phase errors are perceptually salient at low frequencies, and practitioners need frequency responses directly for equalization, crossover design, and spatial audio rendering, yet no prior method learns continuous, spatially conditioned frequency response fields end-to-end with physically grounded spectral constraints.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a 3D receiver position, transmitter position and orientation, receiver direction, and frequency, all encoded via hash-grid positional encoding (10 frequencies). It outputs a complex-valued frequency response H(f) rendered by integrating over 64×32 azimuth-elevation rays with 64 samples per ray, alongside intermediate complex attenuation δ(f,p) = σ + jβ and directional retransmission spectrum S(f,p,n̂). The system operates at 48 kHz sampling with a 100 ms impulse-response window, and the rendered response is a continuous function of receiver position, enabling query at arbitrary spatial locations.

## Method

INFER uses a two-branch MLP (6 layers, 256 units, ReLU) where a material sub-network predicts direction-independent complex attenuation δ(f,p) and a retransmission network predicts the directional spectrum S(f,p,n̂), both conditioned on hash-encoded spatial coordinates. A differentiable frequency-domain renderer accumulates contributions along receiver-centered rays using spherical spreading (1/u), time-of-flight phase (e^{-j2πfu/v}), discrete absorption opacity, and cumulative dispersive phase, without discretizing time or requiring post-hoc transforms. The training objective combines frequency-weighted spectral, magnitude, and phase losses with perceptual/hardware-aware weighting (emphasizing low-mid bands, downweighting crossover regions), a spectral envelope smoothing term, and a Kramers-Kronig consistency regularizer that enforces causal coupling between attenuation σ and dispersion β via a discrete Hilbert transform. This physics-informed spectral supervision is the key differentiator: it prevents nonphysical phase behavior and enables band-specific control that time-domain methods cannot provide.

## Results

INFER achieves state-of-the-art frequency response reconstruction across all evaluated environments. On the Buck car cabin dataset, INFER reduces average magnitude error to 0.140 (vs. 0.231 for NAF, 0.302 for AVR, 0.536 for INRAS) and average phase error to 0.140 (vs. 0.334 for NAF, 0.288 for AVR, 0.963 for INRAS), representing 39% and 51% average reductions over the best time-domain baselines. On room-scale benchmarks (MeshRIR), INFER attains the lowest phase error (1.194) and amplitude error (0.24), outperforming AVR (1.48/0.54), NAF (1.61/0.64), and INRAS (1.61/0.77). On the COMSOL simulation, INFER achieves the best amplitude (0.78), T60 (12.9 ms), and EDT (29.4 ms) metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

INFER outperforms all five baselines across every dataset and core frequency-domain metric. Against NAF (Luo et al., 2022), a time-domain neural acoustic field, INFER reduces magnitude error by ~40% (0.140 vs. 0.231 on Buck) and phase error by ~58% (0.140 vs. 0.334), with the gap widening at low frequencies (2.6× phase improvement at 180 Hz). Against INRAS (Su et al., 2022), a signal-regression approach, INFER achieves roughly 2–7× lower errors on both magnitude and phase, as INRAS collapses toward a nonphysical uniform field in resonant regions. Against AVR (Lan et al., 2024), the strongest hybrid time-frequency baseline, INFER still leads by ~54% in magnitude and ~51% in phase on Buck, with AVR's frequency-independent attenuation assumption limiting its spectral fidelity. Against traditional codecs AAC (Bosi et al., 1997) and Opus (Valin et al., 2013), INFER dominates on all frequency-domain metrics (e.g., 0.24 vs. 0.69–0.91 amplitude on MeshRIR), though these codecs are not designed for spatial field reconstruction. The only metric where INFER trails is T60 on the Buck dataset (9.8 ms vs. 2.8 ms for Opus-linear), which the authors attribute to perceptual frequency weighting de-emphasizing high-frequency bins where Buck concentrates 20.6% of its energy; removing the weighting recovers T60 to 2.8 ms while retaining best-in-class frequency accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

INFER extends the neural implicit representation framework for acoustic fields (NAF, INRAS, AVR) by shifting the target from time-domain impulse responses to continuous, complex-valued frequency response fields, leveraging the same hash-grid encoding and ray-marching machinery but with a fundamentally different parameterization. The key conceptual departure is the frequency-first formulation: rather than predicting waveforms and post-hoc transforming, the network directly outputs per-frequency attenuation and directional retransmission spectra, enabling band-aware supervision and a Kramers-Kronig consistency regularizer that couples attenuation and phase delay via a discrete Hilbert transform. The Huygens-Fresnel volume-rendering formulation (Eq. 1) and the complex transmittance accumulation along rays (Eq. 5) adapt the NeRF-style rendering paradigm to a lossy, dispersive acoustic medium, a setting where prior methods assumed frequency-independent attenuation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work addresses a practically critical gap: car cabin acoustics for safety-critical directional alerts and immersive audio remain underexplored by both classical simulation (computationally prohibitive) and prior neural methods (time-domain, lacking frequency-aware supervision). The frequency-first formulation is well-suited to confined resonant environments where sharp modal structure dominates, and the KK regularizer provides a physically grounded constraint that prior acoustic neural fields lacked. Limitations include the T60/EDT trade-off under perceptual weighting, the ~24-hour GPU training cost, and the current restriction to static geometries with fixed source/receiver configurations; open questions include generalization to dynamic cabin reconfigurations, multi-source scenarios, and whether the frequency-first advantage persists in large, highly reverberant rooms where time-domain tails carry more information.
