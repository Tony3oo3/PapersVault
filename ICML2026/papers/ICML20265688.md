## Problem

Existing single-step retrosynthesis (SSRS) benchmarks rely on Top-K exact-match accuracy against a single ground-truth reactant set, which penalizes chemically valid alternative disconnections and fails to reflect the open-ended, multi-solution nature of real synthesis planning. This 'single ground-truth' paradigm is especially problematic for LLMs that naturally generate diverse candidate reactions, and for out-of-domain evaluation where memorization of training-set reactions inflates apparent performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265688/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For SSRS prediction, models receive a target molecule in SMILES format (with 5-shot prompting sampled from USPTO-full) and generate 15 independent reactant SMILES sets per target. ChemCensor then scores each predicted reaction by extracting hierarchical reaction centers (RC1–RC5, increasing structural context) and non-participating functional group signatures, validating both against a relational SQLite database of ~1.4M (USPTO-full) or ~4.8M (USPTO-full + Pistachio) synthetic precedents, yielding an integer plausibility score of 0–5. Model-level aggregation uses Av. PT-Max CC (best CC per target, averaged over all targets) and Av. PT-Top-K CC (mean CC over top-K deduplicated predictions per target, with zero-padding for missing slots).

## Method

ChemCensor is a precedent-based scoring framework that decomposes each reaction into a reaction center (atoms/bonds undergoing change, represented at five increasing levels of topological context) and a functional group signature (516 curated SMARTS patterns for non-reacting FGs that must be tolerated), then validates both against a relational database of synthetic precedents, with higher scores reflecting broader matched substructural context. The paper introduces CREED, a 22.7M-reaction dataset generated via ~3K expert-coded bidirectional templates applied in both forward (from CABBs through drug-derived synthetic scheme templates) and retro (on generatively produced template-specific molecular structures) regimes, plus a ChemCensor-verified subset (CREED-CCV, 6.4M reactions) for higher-quality training. C3LM models are trained by SFT on CREED or USPTO-full from LFM2-2.6B or Qwen3-8B checkpoints, followed by GRPO-based RFT using composite rewards combining format validity, SMILES syntax, and either CREED multi-answer exact matching, Molecular Transformer forward-validation, or ChemCensor plausibility scoring. The URSA-expert-2026 benchmark of 100 novel, expert-validated target molecules (non-overlapping with public reaction databases) provides a leakage-resistant OOD evaluation setting that is substantially harder than USPTO-50K-test.

## Results

On the OOD URSA-expert-2026 benchmark (100 novel targets), the best C3LM variant (C3LM-LFM2-RFT-MT, 2.6B params) achieves Av. PT-Top-10 CC of 0.43 and Max CC of 1.79, while the strongest proprietary baseline (Grok-4.1) reaches Av. PT-Top-3 CC of 1.29 and Max CC of 1.75. On the community-standard USPTO-50K-test, C3LM-LFM2-RFT-MT attains the best Av. PT-Top-3 CC (2.13), Av. PT-Top-5 CC (1.35), and Av. PT-Top-10 CC (0.68), surpassing all proprietary LLMs (best: Claude 4.5 Sonnet at 1.81/1.15/0.58) and chemical specialist models (best: NatureLM at 1.85/1.14/0.57). Performance drops sharply on URSA-expert-2026 relative to USPTO-50K-test for nearly all models, confirming that the OOD benchmark is substantially harder and less susceptible to training-set memorization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On USPTO-50K-test, C3LM-LFM2-RFT-MT (2.6B) clearly outperforms all proprietary LLMs: it beats Claude 4.5 Sonnet (Av. PT-Top-3 CC 2.13 vs 1.81), GPT 5.2 (2.13 vs 1.01), and NatureLM (2.13 vs 1.85) by 0.3–1.1 CC points, while also surpassing the chemical specialist RetroDFM-R (2.13 vs 1.56). On the harder OOD URSA-expert-2026 set, the picture is more nuanced: Grok-4.1 retains a slight edge on Av. PT-Top-3 CC (1.29 vs 1.22 for C3LM-LFM2-RFT-MT), but C3LM wins on Max CC (1.79 vs 1.75) and on the @5/@10 metrics (0.85/0.43 vs 0.93/0.49 for Grok-4.1 is actually Grok winning; C3LM wins on Max). The critical finding is that a 2.6B model fine-tuned on CREED (22.7M reactions) matches or exceeds 70B+ proprietary models, whereas the untrained LFM2 2.6B base scores 0.00 on both benchmarks. Open-weight foundation models (Qwen3, DeepSeek) perform markedly worse on URSA-expert-2026 (Av. PT-Top-3 CC ≤ 0.16), confirming that general LLM pretraining does not transfer to SSRS without chemistry-specific fine-tuning. Conventional SSRS models (LocalRetro, RetroKNN, etc.) remain superior in cost-efficiency and are reported to achieve higher CC scores in Appendix O, but the C3LM results demonstrate that LLM-based SSRS is now competitive when trained on sufficiently diverse reaction data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant Top-K exact-match evaluation paradigm for single-step retrosynthesis (Segler & Waller, 2017; Schwaller et al., 2020) by proposing ChemCensor, a precedent-based plausibility metric grounded in reaction-center and functional-group compatibility checking. Conceptually, it repurposes well-established cheminformatics notions (reaction centers, functional group signatures, synthetic precedents) into an automated scoring framework that mimics how a synthetic chemist queries databases like Reaxys or SciFinder. The CREED dataset (22.7M reactions) extends prior reaction corpora like USPTO-full by providing multiple plausible reactant sets per product, directly addressing the single-ground-truth limitation identified by Torren-Peraire et al. (2024) and Maziarz et al. (2025). The C3LM models build on standard SFT and GRPO-based RL fine-tuning applied to chemistry-specialized LLMs (LFM2, Qwen3), following the training paradigm established by ether0 and NatureLM but with a fundamentally different data philosophy (diversity over memorization).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a structural weakness in how the community evaluates retrosynthesis models: USPTO-50K performance does not reliably transfer to genuinely novel targets, and exact-match metrics penalize chemically valid alternatives. The CREED dataset and ChemCensor metric provide practical tools that could reshape how SSRS models are compared, while URSA-expert-2026 offers a leakage-resistant benchmark. A key open question is whether LLMs can ever match the plausibility scores of lightweight conventional SSRS models (which still dominate on CC metrics), and whether the plausibility-based evaluation framework generalizes to multi-step retrosynthesis. The finding that a 2.6B model trained on diverse CREED data can rival much larger proprietary LLMs suggests data quality and diversity may matter more than raw model scale for this task.
