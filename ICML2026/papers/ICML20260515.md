## Problem

Existing RAG data-poisoning attacks require costly end-to-end optimization of the entire adversarial document for each target phrase, making them brittle to query variation and non-scalable across triggers. Even trigger-based methods that improve flexibility still demand full re-optimization per new trigger, limiting rapid deployment. The paper asks whether an adversarial document can be decomposed into reusable, payload-agnostic components so that new attacks can be instantiated at near-zero marginal cost.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trigger phrase (constrained to appear in 0.5–1% of corpus queries to ensure realistic competition), a ~1,000-document corpus, and white-box access to a retriever and generator for optimization. It produces a single ~60-token malicious document with a fixed six-segment structure: four optimized attractor slots (retriever prefix/suffix, generator prefix/suffix) flanking two swappable Focus Regions containing a semantic bait (s_ret) and a malicious instruction (s_gen). The attractor is optimized once per trigger and can then be composed with arbitrary payloads—simple templates or LLM-generated content—without any retraining, enabling constant-time attack creation across triggers.

## Method

The core mechanism decomposes the adversarial document into reusable Attention Attractors and Focus Regions, where the attractor is optimized to steer a small subset of influential attention heads toward the Focus Region rather than optimizing end-to-end task metrics. The proxy objective maximizes the aggregated attention mass from the model's summary token (e.g., [CLS] for dense retrievers, the final assistant token for generators) to the payload tokens, restricted to heads whose attention correlates above a threshold (τ_corr ≈ 0.85) with retrieval similarity or generation log-probability. Discrete token optimization is performed via HotFlip with a perplexity constraint (top-10% log-probability filter via GPT-2) to maintain local fluency. The key empirical finding is that only 10–15% of attention heads exhibit Pearson correlations exceeding 0.9 with downstream task performance, making attention steering a tractable, payload-agnostic proxy that decouples the attractor from any specific payload. This modularity yields a single optimized attractor that transfers to unseen black-box retrievers (96.6% R-ASR) and generators (97.8% G-ASR, including GPT-4o-mini and Gemini-2.5-Flash) without retraining.

## Results

Across 18 end-to-end RAG configurations (3 datasets × 2 retrievers × 3 generators), EYES-ON-ME + LLM-Gen achieves an average End-to-End Attack Success Rate (E2E-ASR) of 57.8%, compared to 21.9% for the best optimization-based baselines (+35.9 pts; 2.6×). The method also demonstrates strong black-box transferability: a single optimized attractor transfers to unseen retrievers with 96.6% average R-ASR and to unseen generators (including GPT-4o-mini and Gemini-2.5-Flash) with 97.8% average G-ASR. Under SOTA defenses, EYES-ON-ME + LLM-Gen retains 72.2% G-ASR under perplexity filtering and 84.6% under noise insertion, with constant optimization cost regardless of the number of triggers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EYES-ON-ME + LLM-Gen (57.8% avg E2E-ASR) substantially outperforms all four baselines. Against GCG (Zou et al., 2023; ~7.7%), the improvement is ~7.5×, and against Phantom (Chaudhari et al., 2024; ~14.6%) and AutoDAN (Liu et al., 2024; ~14.1%), it is ~4×. The largest gap is over the LLM-Gen baseline adapted from PoisonedRAG (Zou et al., 2025; ~26.4%), where adding the attention attractor more than doubles ASR despite using the same LLM-generated retrieval payload. The authors attribute the baselines' weakness to two factors: (1) their evaluation uses rare triggers with little document competition, whereas this paper constrains triggers to 0.5–1% corpus frequency, and (2) their next-token log-probability objectives fail to account for retrieval rank effects on downstream generation. Notably, even the attractor-only variant (EYES-ON-ME with a simple template payload) achieves 31.2% ASR, already surpassing the LLM-Gen baseline, confirming that the attention-steering mechanism—not payload quality—is the primary driver of success.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on HotFlip (Ebrahimi et al., 2018) as its discrete token-optimization engine and on the RAG poisoning task established by Phantom, GCG, AutoDAN, and PoisonedRAG, but departs from them in two key ways. First, rather than end-to-end optimization of the full adversarial document per trigger (which is costly and non-reusable), it decomposes the document into a reusable Attention Attractor and a swappable Focus Region payload. Second, it borrows the conceptual idea of attention steering from inference-time methods (PASTA, LLMSteer, Spotlight Your Instructions) but inverts the direction: instead of reweighting heads post hoc, it learns input-space tokens that concentrate a small set of empirically identified influential heads onto a designated region, using attention mass as a payload-agnostic proxy objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The modular, optimize-once-reuse-many paradigm directly addresses the scalability bottleneck of prior RAG poisoning, enabling near-zero-cost attacks for new triggers and payloads. The 96–97% black-box transfer to unseen retrievers and proprietary generators (GPT-4o-mini, Gemini-2.5-Flash) suggests a fundamental, shared vulnerability in transformer cross-attention that is not yet mechanistically explained, making this both a practical security concern and a target for interpretability research. Open questions include whether the attention-steering mechanism generalizes to larger models and longer contexts, whether the 0.5–1% trigger-frequency constraint is the realistic operating regime, and how to design defenses that detect subtle, head-localized attention manipulation without the overhead of per-query self-examination.
