## Problem

RL researchers routinely conflate two fundamentally different goals when using simulators: solving a specific simulator (where parallelism, resetting, and discarding search history are legitimate) versus using a simulator as a proxy for learning in deployment (where the agent must learn sequentially from a single environment with real costs). Because papers rarely declare which use case they target, algorithms and evaluation metrics designed for one setting are inappropriately applied to the other, producing misleading conclusions about algorithm performance and sample efficiency.

## Input / Output

As a position paper with supporting experiments, the input is the existing RL empirical literature and its standard experimental practices, while the output is a formal three-axis distinction framework plus four empirical demonstrations. The experiments consume standard RL environments (MinAtar Asterix, MountainCar-v0, Half-Cheetah MuJoCo, MinAtar Breakout) and established algorithms (PPO, PQN, SAC, DQN), producing learning curves that contrast performance under single-environment versus multi-environment, resetting versus non-resetting, and online-return versus evaluation-rollout conditions.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-axis framework distinguishing simulator use cases along interaction constraints (single vs. parallel environments; availability of simulator-exclusive actions like resetting and multi-outcome querying), appropriate solution techniques (model-free, multi-stream, planning with the simulator, resetting-based exploration), and evaluation metrics (best-policy return and total computation for solving vs. average episodic return and sample efficiency for deployment proxy). Four empirical experiments demonstrate the consequences of conflating these settings: PQN's performance degrades sharply when forced from 128 to 1 parallel environment on Asterix-MinAtar; PPO augmented with periodic resetting to random states achieves substantially better final policies on MountainCar-v0; standard hyperparameter tuning (resetting per trial, discarding search history) implicitly converts a deployment-proxy experiment into a simulator-solving one; and evaluation rollouts of the greedy policy systematically overestimate performance compared to online returns for SAC and DQN. The paper concludes with a call-to-action for researchers to explicitly declare their simulator use case and adhere to the corresponding constraints on algorithm design and evaluation.

## Results

This position paper does not propose a new algorithm, so there are no headline performance metrics. Instead, four illustrative experiments support the central argument: (1) PQN with 128 parallel simulators on Asterix-Minatar substantially outperforms PQN with a single simulator, and matching the update-to-data ratio (4096 rollout steps) only partially recovers performance; (2) PPO augmented with random-state resets every 500 steps on MountainCar-v0 learns a clearly better policy than standard PPO after 2M steps, and the advantage persists after resets are removed; (3) a PPO run over 50M steps (20 seeds) shows a collapsing online return while the best-checkpoint evaluation return stays flat, illustrating how the same curve is 'good' for solving a simulator but 'bad' for deployment; (4) SAC and DQN evaluation-rollout returns are consistently higher and smoother than online returns on Half-Cheetah and MinAtar Breakout, demonstrating that the choice of metric changes the apparent conclusion.

**Table 1: Distinguishing criteria between the two simulator use cases.**

| Criterion | Using Simulator as a Proxy | Solving a Simulator |
|---|---|---|
| Interaction Options | 1 environment | n environments; simulator-exclusive actions: (1) resetting to a specific state, (2) simulating multiple outcomes |
| Solution Techniques | Model-free (Sarsa(λ), DQN, PPO); Planning with learned model (MuZero); Meta-learning for hyperparameters | All of the above **plus**: Multi-stream model-free (PPO, A2C, PQN); Planning with simulator (DP, AlphaZero); Parallel tuning (grid search); Resetting for exploration (Go-Explore); Resetting for variance reduction (vine method, TRPO) |
| Evaluation Metrics | Average episodic return over learning; Sample efficiency | Best return (expected return of best policy found); Total computation used |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare a proposed method against baselines in the standard sense; instead, it contrasts experimental configurations to expose misleading conclusions. PQN with 128 parallel simulators is compared against PQN with 1 simulator (and a matched update-to-data-ratio variant) on Asterix-Minatar, showing that the speed and performance advantages of parallelism vanish in the single-environment deployment setting. PPO with random-state resets is compared against standard PPO on MountainCar-v0, demonstrating that the reset-augmented version achieves a higher final online return and that this advantage is retained when resets are turned off. SAC and DQN are each evaluated under two metrics—online return versus deterministic/greedy evaluation-rollout return—on Half-Cheetah and MinAtar Breakout, revealing that evaluation rollouts produce smoother, higher curves that can mask poor online learning. Finally, a 50M-step PPO run (20 seeds) contrasts the online return (which collapses) against the best-checkpoint evaluation return (which stays flat), showing that the same experiment yields opposite conclusions depending on whether the goal is solving the simulator or proxying deployment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new conceptual ground by formally distinguishing two simulator use cases in RL—solving simulators versus using simulators as a proxy for learning in deployment—and providing a structured taxonomy (Table 1) of how they differ in interaction constraints, algorithmic choices, and evaluation metrics. It joins a small but growing line of work flagging empirical misalignments in RL (Patterson et al., 2024; Castro, 2025) and the observation that simulators serve different roles (Jiang, 2025), but goes further by grounding the distinction in concrete experiments (PQN with 1 vs. 128 environments, resetting in MountainCar, online vs. rollout evaluation). The paper does not propose a new algorithm; rather, it repurposes existing methods (PPO, PQN, SAC, DQN) as illustrative examples of how conflating the two settings leads to misleading conclusions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a pervasive and underacknowledged source of confusion: algorithms like PQN (1024 parallel environments) or Go-Explore (state resetting) are presented as general-purpose RL methods when they are fundamentally inapplicable to deployment, yet practitioners may adopt them based on impressive benchmark scores. Open questions include how to design empirical protocols that leverage parallelism for speed without invalidating deployment-relevant conclusions, whether algorithm variants (e.g., PPO-single-env vs. PPO-multi-env) should be formally distinguished in the literature, and whether top venues should more explicitly reward deployment-oriented applications. A limitation is that the supporting experiments are small-scale (MinAtar, MountainCar, Half-Cheetah) and the position, while well-argued, does not address the full spectrum of simulator use cases (e.g., sim2real, multi-agent settings).
