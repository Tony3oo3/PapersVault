## Problem

Online matching theory provides strong worst-case guarantees under adversarial arrivals, but these bounds are overly pessimistic for domains like organ allocation where historical data reveals stable arrival distributions. Conversely, practical allocation policies (e.g., the US 6-tier heart transplant system) are heuristic, lack provable guarantees, and conflate clinically heterogeneous patients within rigid priority tiers. This paper bridges the gap by showing that aggregating offline nodes into capacitated clusters—coarsening—can simultaneously improve theoretical competitive ratios and practical performance.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a bipartite graph where offline nodes are patients on the waitlist (3,113 from UNOS Jan–Jun 2019) and online nodes are donors discretized into 1,000 representative types (250 per blood type via k-means on 34 clinical attributes). Edge weights are predicted life-years-gained from a Cox proportional hazards model trained on UNOS outcomes since 1987, and donor arrival rates follow a Poisson process parameterized by historical frequencies. The output is an offline dispatch plan (an LP solution specifying per-cluster matching probabilities) that, at runtime, assigns each arriving donor to a patient cluster and then to a specific free patient within that cluster.

## Method

The core contribution is a coarsening-based online stochastic matching algorithm (CSM) that partitions patients into capacitated clusters of size b, transforming the problem into a b-matching instance solvable via a single offline linear program. The key insight is that the competitive ratio of the resulting algorithm is bounded below by α(b)(1−2δ(b)), where α(b)→1 as b grows (from the b-matching literature) and δ(b) is the relative intra-cluster weight error; the algorithm selects b to maximize this product over a candidate set. In the online phase, each arriving donor is independently matched to each cluster with probability f_{u,v}/r_v (the LP flow divided by arrival rate), and a uniformly random free patient within the chosen cluster is selected. The paper proves that when clusters are large and exhibit small error—a structural property verified in UNOS data—the competitive ratio approaches 1, and empirically achieves 0.91 versus 0.51 for the US status quo. Capacitated clustering (agglomerative, constrained k-means, or recursive bisection with a two-phase merge/split repair) is used to construct the patient groups, and the framework is shown to be robust to estimation error in edge weights and to heterogeneous per-cluster errors.

## Results

In simulations on UNOS data (January–June 2019, 3,113 patients), the coarsening-based algorithm achieves a competitive ratio of 0.91, peaking at moderate cluster sizes (b ≈ 10–30). This is substantially higher than the b=1 standard stochastic matching baseline (0.63, consistent with the 1−1/e theoretical bound) and the US post-2018 status quo policy (0.51). In absolute terms, the optimal omniscient policy yields ~4,650 life years per month (~300–400 transplants), while the coarsening approach delivers roughly 100 more transplants per month than b=1 and 150 more than the status quo. The framework also reduces end-to-end runtime by 80–90% (from ~30 s to <5 s) as b increases, due to the reduced LP size.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is the US post-2018 heart transplant allocation policy (a 68-tier hierarchical system based on medical status, blood type, and geographic distance), which achieves a competitive ratio of only 0.51. The coarsening-based method outperforms this by a factor of nearly 2× (0.91 vs. 0.51), corresponding to approximately 150 additional transplants per month. A second baseline is the b=1 standard online stochastic matching algorithm (Brubach et al., 2016), which achieves 0.63—matching the theoretical 1−1/e guarantee. The coarsening approach improves upon this by 0.28 in competitive ratio (~100 additional transplants/month) by routing donors to capacitated patient clusters rather than individuals, smoothing out stochastic arrival inefficiencies. All differences are statistically significant (p < 0.05, Wilcoxon signed-rank test). The choice of clustering algorithm (constrained k-means, agglomerative, recursive bisection) has minimal impact on the competitive ratio, demonstrating robustness of the framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the stochastic b-matching framework of Brubach et al. (2016) by introducing a coarsening layer that aggregates offline nodes into capacitated clusters before solving the LP dispatch plan. It departs from the adversarial-arrival model dominant in the online matching literature (Karp et al., 1990; Mehta et al., 2007) by leveraging historical donor arrival distributions, and from prior organ-allocation heuristics (OrganITE, OrganSync, FutureMatch) by providing the first provable competitive-ratio guarantees for heart transplant allocation. The conceptual bridge to Internet advertising's b-matching (where b is advertiser budget) is the key technical link that enables importing a rich algorithmic toolkit into a medical domain where such ideas had not previously been applied.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a notable gap between pessimistic worst-case online matching theory and simulation-driven policy optimization that lacks guarantees, offering a principled middle ground with a 0.91 competitive ratio in simulation versus 0.51 for the US status quo. Key limitations include the static-waitlist assumption, reliance on potentially biased historical UNOS data, and the utilitarian (life-years-gained) objective that may conflict with fairness or individual clinical judgment. Open questions include adapting the framework to non-stationary donor/waitlist dynamics, incorporating fairness constraints into the offline LP, and validating the approach in live clinical deployment where the simulation's simplifying assumptions (e.g., all offers accepted) break down.
