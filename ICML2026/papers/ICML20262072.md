## Problem

Traditional saliency maps for time series attribute model predictions to individual time points, but semantically meaningful predictive features (e.g., frequency components, independent sources, trend/seasonality) often reside in other domains. Time-domain saliency can therefore obscure the mechanisms driving a model's decision, particularly when labels depend on latent structure such as spectral content. The paper asks: can we compute faithful, axiomatic attributions directly in a practitioner-chosen explanation domain, even when the model operates on raw time samples?

## Input / Output

The method takes a time series signal x ∈ R^n and an invertible, differentiable transform T: D_S → D_T (instantiated as DFT, STFT, DWT, ICA, STL, or Complex Cepstrum) along with a baseline (typically zero-signal, but specifiable in either domain). It produces a saliency map over the transformed coordinates z = T(x), where each coordinate z_i receives a real-valued significance score. A key distinction from prior work is native support for complex-valued transform domains (e.g., Fourier, Complex Cepstrum), where the output map is real-valued despite the complex intermediate representation.

## Method

Cross-domain Integrated Gradients (CDIG) generalizes Integrated Gradients by computing the path integral of model gradients over the transformed feature space: IG_i^{D_T}(z) = 2∫₀¹ Re(∂(f∘T⁻¹)/∂z_i)|_{γ(t)} · (z_i − ẑ_i) dt. The core theoretical contribution is a rigorous extension of IG to complex-valued domains via Wirtinger calculus: the complex function g: C^n → R is lifted to a real function u: R^{2n} → R, the line-integral definition is applied to du, and Stokes' theorem guarantees both path independence and the Completeness axiom (which can fail for functions of several complex variables). The derivation is transform-agnostic, requiring only that T be invertible and differentiable, and recovers prior methods as special cases (e.g., MIX for Haar-DWT, VIL for DFT). The paper further proves a closed-form link between frequency-domain CDIG and the frequency response of convolutional filters, showing that the attribution at frequency ξ_i equals the filter gain at that frequency scaled by signal amplitude.

## Results

On the CPD faithfulness benchmark (Table 2, higher is better), Cross-domain IG in the Cepstrum domain achieves the strongest scores on PAM (1.183), Epilepsy (0.795), and Freezer (0.646), while the Fourier domain leads on Wafer (0.772) and is competitive on Boiler (1.233). In feature-level insertion/deletion tests, deleting the top 3% Fourier features shifts the predicted heart rate by 66.39 BPM on average versus only 10.13 BPM for time-domain features, and retaining the top ICA component preserves seizure probability far better than a random component (Δp = 0.0696 vs. 0.4396). On AudioMNIST, CDIG in the time-frequency domain achieves the best faithfulness (0.099) and lowest complexity (5.14) among all compared frequency-domain methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TIMEX++, Cross-domain IG (both Fourier and Cepstrum instantiations) consistently outperforms across all five datasets and both CPD protocols, with the largest gaps on PAM (Cepstrum 1.183 vs. 0.057) and Wafer (Fourier 0.772 vs. 0.000). Against TIMING, the best CDIG domain wins on four of five datasets (PAM, Epilepsy, Wafer, Freezer), while TIMING retains a narrow edge on Boiler (1.259 vs. 1.233 for Fourier). On AudioMNIST, CDIG in the time-frequency domain slightly beats FreqRISE on faithfulness (0.099 vs. 0.104) while halving its complexity (5.14 vs. 10.82); in the pure frequency domain, FreqRISE edges out CDIG on faithfulness (0.160 vs. 0.19) but at higher complexity (8.17 vs. 6.31). Compared to Virtual Inspection Layers (VIL), the paper reports comparable faithfulness and complexity, while noting that CDIG additionally supports transforms (DWT, ICA, STL, Cepstrum) that VIL does not cover.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al., 2017) to arbitrary invertible, differentiable transform domains, including complex-valued ones, by deriving a Complex IG formulation via Wirtinger calculus that preserves path independence and completeness. It unifies and subsumes prior domain-specific approaches: Virtual Inspection Layers (Vielhaben et al., 2024) transport time-domain attributions to frequency via transform-specific propagation rules, MIX (Tran et al., 2025) computes IG in Haar-DWT view space, and FreqRISE (Brusch et al., 2025a) uses frequency masking—each targeting a single domain. The key departure is treating the explanation domain as a first-class, practitioner-chosen design parameter rather than a fixed methodological choice, and providing a single axiomatic framework spanning six transform families (DFT, STFT, DWT, ICA, STL, Complex Cepstrum).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, transform-agnostic tool for time-series explainability with axiomatic guarantees in complex domains, and the open-source library (TensorFlow, PyTorch, Captum) lowers the barrier to cross-domain attribution. The finding that domain choice materially affects both qualitative interpretability and quantitative faithfulness (e.g., Cepstrum excels on PAM/Epilepsy/Freezer while Fourier leads on Wafer) reinforces that there is no universally best explanation domain. Key open questions include automatic or semi-automatic domain selection, extending guarantees to non-invertible or approximately invertible transforms, and developing evaluation metrics that capture domain appropriateness beyond standardized CPD protocols. The method inherits IG's sensitivity to baseline and path choice, and the manual domain specification step means practitioners must bring domain expertise to avoid misleading 'domain-shopping.'
