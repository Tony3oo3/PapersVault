## Problem

Standard evaluation metrics cannot distinguish whether fine-tuning elicits a model's latent capabilities or teaches it entirely new ones, since both regimes can start from identical zero-shot performance and converge to identical final accuracy. This conflation matters for safety (latent dangerous capabilities may be surfaced with only a few bits of information) and for practical training design (parameter-efficient methods like LoRA fail at regime-dependent capacity thresholds that are currently unpredictable). The paper asks: can we quantify and predict the origin of a model's capabilities from training dynamics alone?

## Input / Output

The method consumes only standard training logs—specifically, per-example cross-entropy losses on label tokens during the first epoch (for MDL) and final held-out test loss—requiring no special instrumentation beyond what any fine-tuning pipeline already records. Outputs are structured at multiple granularities: EDL per token (bits of generalizable information per unit of supervision, used for scaling-signature classification), EDL per trainable parameter (information density, used for capacity-limit prediction), and EDL ratio relative to full fine-tuning (used to detect when LoRA underperforms). Losses are computed in nats and converted to bits by dividing by ln 2, with token masking restricted to answer tokens to isolate capability-relevant learning from format acquisition.

## Method

The paper operationalizes Excess Description Length (EDL = MDL − n·L_test) as a practical, log-computable metric for quantifying generalizable information absorbed into model parameters during training, where MDL is the prequential minimum description length (sum of first-epoch cross-entropy losses) and L_test is the final population loss. The key empirical finding is that EDL per token exhibits qualitatively distinct scaling signatures with dataset size: elicitation produces monotonically decreasing returns (each additional example is increasingly redundant with latent knowledge), while teaching produces an initial phase of increasing returns (each example makes the data more informative as the model constructs new structure) before crossing over to diminishing returns. EDL per trainable parameter predicts sharp capacity thresholds where LoRA degrades relative to full fine-tuning, with elicitation saturating around 0.01–0.1 bits/parameter and teaching around 1+ bits/parameter—a two-order-of-magnitude gap. Causal interventions (pre-teaching a related algorithm to a model that initially shows teaching signatures) shift both the scaling dynamics and the capacity threshold into the elicitation regime, confirming that the signatures reflect the presence or absence of latent capability rather than model-architecture artifacts. The framework is validated across Llama 3.2/3.1 (1B–8B), Qwen2.5 (1.5B–32B), and TinyStories-1B on arithmetic, reasoning distillation, reading comprehension, science QA, and instruction-following tasks.

## Results

The paper's headline finding is that elicitation requires 1–2 orders of magnitude less information than teaching: capacity thresholds are ~0.01–0.1 bits/parameter for elicitation versus ~1 bit/parameter for teaching. In the extreme low-information limit, fine-tuning Llama 3.1 8B on a single randomly sampled example (~8 bits) achieves 96% accuracy on addition/subtraction (a 96 pp improvement from 0% zero-shot), and Llama 3.2 1B recovers 66% of its full fine-tuning performance gap from one example. For reasoning distillation, Qwen2.5 14B gains 23 pp from a single example (>45% PGR), confirming that distillation operates in the elicitation regime. Causal pre-teaching interventions shift the capacity threshold by over an order of magnitude (e.g., from ~1.02 to ~0.05 bits/parameter for TinyStories-1B on multiplication), confirming that EDL signatures track latent versus absent capability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against external baseline methods; instead, it compares learning regimes (elicitation vs. teaching) across model families and interventions. Llama 3.2 1B/3B/8B (elicitation) consistently outperform TinyStories-1B (teaching) on identical arithmetic tasks: at matched dataset sizes, Llama absorbs over an order of magnitude less EDL per token and reaches near-perfect accuracy from a single example, while TinyStories-1B shows 0 pp improvement under the same conditions. Pre-elicited Llama models (format already established) reduce EDL/token by approximately another order of magnitude compared to un-pre-elicited Llama, isolating the format-learning transient. For instruction-tuned Llama models (which achieve 20–56% zero-shot accuracy via RLHF), base models fine-tuned on a single example consistently outperform them, demonstrating that low-information SFT elicitation surpasses what post-training alignment procedures surface. On the capacity axis, LoRA at low ranks matches full fine-tuning (PGR ≈ 1) for elicitation tasks but degrades sharply for teaching tasks once EDL/P exceeds ~1 bit/parameter, whereas elicitation tasks remain capacity-limited only above ~0.05 bits/parameter.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the authors' prior line of work on quantifying latent capability elicitation (Donoway et al., NeurIPS 2025, which showed parameter constraints proxy information constraints) by introducing excess description length (EDL) as a fully operational, information-theoretic metric computable from standard training logs. It builds on the EDL formalism from Donoway (ISIT 2026) and Donoway et al. (arXiv 2026), and positions itself relative to knowledge capacity scaling laws (Allen-Zhu & Li, 2024, ~2 bits/parameter), MDL probing (Voita & Titov, 2020), and recent demonstrations that minimal data can improve reasoning (Muennighoff et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). The key departure from prior MDL-based approaches is that EDL uses population (test) loss as a reference to isolate generalizable information from memorization, and its scaling analysis across dataset sizes reveals qualitative signatures (monotonically decreasing vs. increasing EDL per token) that single-epoch MDL cannot distinguish.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a single example (fewer than 10 bits) can unlock 50–96% of a model's full performance gap, and that elicitation capacity thresholds are 1–2 orders of magnitude lower than teaching thresholds (~0.01–0.1 vs. ~1 bit/parameter), has direct safety implications: latent dangerous capabilities may be far easier to surface than dataset-size heuristics suggest. The EDL framework provides a practical, log-computable tool for distinguishing whether a fine-tuning-based evaluation is measuring what a model already knows versus what it can learn, which is critical for valid capability assessment. Limitations include focus on arithmetic/reasoning tasks with verifiable ground truth, dense transformer architectures only (no MoE), and dependence on training algorithm choices; open questions include whether these signatures hold for open-ended generation, safety-critical capabilities, and whether EDL can be computed in online/RL settings.
