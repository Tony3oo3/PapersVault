## Problem

LLM-based qualitative data analysis pipelines lack a shared specification for what kind of qualitative reasoning they perform, what representation they produce, and how outputs should be evaluated. Existing systems treat qualitative analysis as code induction plus aggregation, stopping short of inducing structured representations (e.g., causal or dynamical models), and the fragmentation across QR traditions (grounded theory, thematic analysis, IPA, etc.) has encouraged siloed automation that obscures shared analytic primitives.

## Input / Output

The framework takes unstructured text records (interviews, documents, system logs, multi-agent traces, incident reports) and qualitative research papers as input, and produces a two-axis specification: a meaning-making level (M1 descriptive → M2 categorical → M3 interpretive → M4 theoretical) and a modeling level (D1 static taxonomy → D2 stage/timeline → D3 causal mechanism → D4 dynamical feedback), yielding a position in a 4×4 landscape. Unlike predictive models, the output is an analytic contract that declares the strength of semantic and representational claims, with cell-dependent evaluation criteria (e.g., trace fidelity for M1, mechanism evidence for D3, loop-closure support for D4).

## Method

The paper introduces a 4×4 landscape that factorizes qualitative analysis into two analytically separable axes: level of meaning-making (how far the analysis moves beyond surface-faithful restatement toward theory-mediated reframing) and level of modeling (what explicit representational structure is produced, from static maps to iterative feedback systems). The framework was developed over ~5 months through iterative theory-building with interdisciplinary anchoring in linguistics, pragmatics, interpretive social science, and systems science, and validated via a structured audit of 300 papers (231 empirical QR papers curated via PRISMA-style screening with Qwen3-32B triage, and 69 computational papers via snowball sampling) annotated by three authors using a shared rubric with boundary tests. An operationalizability experiment with GPT-5.2 (given only level definitions, no human labels) achieved 85% exact-match and κ=0.744 on meaning-making, and 94% exact-match and κ=0.906 on modeling, confirming the rubric's decision-consistency. The audit reveals that computational work concentrates in D1–D2 (modeling-shallow) while human QR is meaning-deep (M3–M4) but often modeling-light, and that high-level modeling almost never appears without high-level meaning-making.

## Results

The paper audits 300 papers (231 empirical QR + 69 computational QR) and identifies three systematic patterns: (O1) computational LLM-based work is modeling-shallow, concentrating in D1–D2 with sparse D3–D4 coverage; (O2) human empirical QR is meaning-deep (mostly M3–M4) but often modeling-light (D1–D3); (O3) high-level modeling (D3/D4) almost never appears without high-level meaning-making (M3/M4). In a limited operationalizability experiment on 35 sampled papers, GPT-5.2 achieved 85% exact-match agreement (Cohen's κ = 0.744) for meaning-making and 94% (κ = 0.906) for modeling against human consensus labels, indicating the rubric is decision-consistent.

**Table 2: The 4×4 landscape as a conceptual coordinate system.**

| | D1: Static map | D2: Stages/Timeline | D3: Causal pathways | D4: Feedback dynamics |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **M1: Descriptive** | Trace-faithful inventory; association sketch | Event/timeline summary; episode segmentation | Described "drivers" without strong interpretation | Described cycles/loops as narration (often rhetorical) |
| **M2: Categorical** | Themes/codes + relations; taxonomies | Theme evolution across phases; stage-labeled themes | Theme-based causal map; mechanism candidates | Theme-linked loop hypotheses; coupled pattern stories |
| **M3: Interpretive** | Implied norms/goals mapped to relations | Interpretive phase narrative (what shifts, why it matters) | Mechanism explanation with warrants across excerpts | Interpretive feedback account (endogenous escalation/relaxation) |
| **M4: Theoretical** | Theory-guided thematic/relational model | Theory-guided process model (stages via a lens) | Theory-mediated mechanism/pathway model | Theory-mediated system model (loops, delays, regimes) |

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not benchmark a proposed method against competing baselines. Instead, it contrasts two communities' outputs within the proposed landscape: LLM-based computational QR papers cluster in the lower-left region (M1–M2, D1–D2), indicating surface-level organization and static representations, while human empirical QR papers shift rightward on meaning-making (M3–M4) but remain lighter on modeling (D1–D3). The operationalizability experiment serves as the closest analogue to a comparison: GPT-5.2, given only level definitions and boundary rules, matched human consensus labels with 85% exact-match (κ = 0.744) on meaning-making and 94% (κ = 0.906) on modeling, demonstrating that the rubric is explicit enough for independent application but not evidence of LLM capability for full qualitative analysis. The paper positions its framework against method-centered taxonomies (thematic analysis, grounded theory, IPA) and tradition-specific formalisms (process tracing, QCA, system dynamics), arguing these are anchors within the landscape rather than competitors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new conceptual ground by proposing a cross-cutting 4×4 specification framework that cuts across existing qualitative research method traditions (grounded theory, thematic analysis, IPA, discourse analysis) and system-science modeling levels (static maps, stage models, causal mechanisms, dynamical feedback systems). It departs from prior LLM-assisted QR work (e.g., LLOOM, Thematic-LM, HICode, LOGOS) by arguing that these systems are 'modeling-shallow'—they perform code induction and aggregation but stop short of producing structured representational commitments like causal pathways or feedback dynamics. The framework draws on the specification-game analogy from computer science and modern software-engineering design principles to argue that analytic commitments should be made explicit rather than implicit, bridging the qualitative-research community and the NLP/LLM community with a shared diagnostic vocabulary.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 300-paper audit reveals a systematic and actionable gap: computational LLM-based QR concentrates in D1–D2 (static/timeline) with sparse D3–D4 coverage, while human QR is meaning-deep (M3–M4) but often modeling-light, suggesting the two communities under-commit in complementary ways. The framework provides a concrete diagnostic instrument for evaluating where an LLM pipeline's representational claims outstrip its actual outputs, and it motivates a research agenda around level-dependent evaluation, adaptive level selection, and governance rules for LLM involvement in QR. Key open questions include how to operationalize 'fitness-to-corpus' metrics for D3/D4 structures, how to create shareable gold artifacts (codebooks with trace-linked rationales) given privacy constraints on primary qualitative data, and whether the 4×4 decomposition is the right factorization or whether alternative axes would be more informative.
