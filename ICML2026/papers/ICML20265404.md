## Problem

LLMs trained with RLVR for mathematical reasoning develop excessive verbosity, generating redundant reasoning chains that inflate inference costs without proportional accuracy gains. Existing remedies—explicit length penalties, auxiliary difficulty estimators, and multi-stage curricula—either degrade reasoning quality through adversarial gradients or require complex multi-component training pipelines. The paper asks whether LLMs can learn to reason efficiently without any explicit length supervision at all.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265404/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes N mathematical problems (default N=3) concatenated into a single prompt with markdown section headers (### Problem X) and a system instruction, all constrained by a fixed token budget B_max for the entire completion (e.g., 5120 tokens for N=3). The model produces a single sequential completion solving all problems in order, with each answer demarcated as 'Answer i: \boxed{...}' immediately after its reasoning trace. At inference, the model can be evaluated at any N (1–5), and the learned efficiency transfers fully to standard single-problem (N=1) evaluation without requiring batched inputs.

## Method

BCR is a single-stage GRPO training procedure that modifies only the input structure: N problems are batched into one shared context window with a fixed token budget, and the model is rewarded purely by per-instance accuracy (fraction of N problems solved correctly) plus a format-compliance indicator—no length penalty, no difficulty estimator, no curriculum. The fixed budget creates an implicit information bottleneck where problems compete for a shared resource: verbose reasoning on early problems directly reduces the budget available for later ones, potentially truncating their solutions and yielding zero accuracy reward. This inter-problem competition induces the model to autonomously discover compression strategies—eliminating metacognitive loops, selecting optimal strategies directly, and calibrating reasoning depth to difficulty—without any explicit efficiency signal. The approach is orthogonal to and composable with existing RLVR pipelines, as it changes neither the reward function, the optimization algorithm (GRPO), nor the model architecture.

## Results

At standard N=1 inference, BCR-JustRL-1.5B reduces token usage by 39.8–62.6% versus JustRL-deepseek-1.5B across five benchmarks (e.g., 3,173 vs. 8,482 tokens on AIME25), with accuracy gains on 3/5 benchmarks (AMC23 +2.5, Minerva +5.1, Olympiad +0.8). For the 4B family, BCR-Qwen3-4B reduces tokens by 15.8–31.8% versus Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 while improving accuracy on all five benchmarks, including +13.3% on AIME25 (83.3% vs. 70.0%) and +2.5% on AMC23 (100.0% vs. 97.5%). Under concurrent inference (N=5), BCR maintains 50.0% accuracy on AMC23 with only 1,013 tokens/problem, versus the baseline's 22.5% accuracy at 4,584 tokens.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary 1.5B baseline (JustRL-deepseek-1.5B), BCR achieves 39.8–62.6% token reduction with a net accuracy gain on 3/5 benchmarks (AMC23 +2.5, Minerva +5.1, Olympiad +0.8) but loses on AIME25 (−6.7) and MATH-500 (−3.8). Against the 4B baseline (Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507), BCR wins on all five benchmarks in both accuracy and tokens, with the largest gain being +13.3% on AIME25. Compared to length-controlled methods (BroRL-1.5B, e3-1.7B), BCR-JustRL-1.5B uses fewer tokens on 4/5 benchmarks but trails on accuracy for the harder problems (e.g., AIME25: 33.3% vs. e3's 46.7%). Adaptive reasoning methods (ARM-3B, Thinker-Q1.5B) achieve extreme brevity (700–2,000 tokens) but suffer catastrophic accuracy collapse (3.3% and 0.0% on AIME25 respectively), whereas BCR maintains competitive accuracy at moderate token counts. Against the general-purpose Qwen3-1.7B, BCR-JustRL-1.5B uses roughly 5× fewer tokens on AIME25 (3,173 vs. 15,796) at a 6.7-point accuracy cost, and matches or exceeds it on AMC23 and Olympiad accuracy while using 3–4× fewer tokens.

## Relation to Prior Work

BCR builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as its optimization algorithm and on JustRL's (He et al., 2025) demonstration that a pure accuracy reward suffices for strong reasoning at 1.5B scale. It departs from the three dominant families of efficient-reasoning methods—explicit length penalties (L1, ShorterBetter, ALP), auxiliary difficulty estimators (ARM, SelfBudgeter, Thinker), and multi-stage curricula (DeepScaleR, FastCuRL, ProRL, BroRL)—by modifying only the training input structure (batching N problems into one prompt) while leaving the reward function, algorithm, and architecture untouched. The paper positions this as a fundamentally different mechanism: a hard constraint (fixed token budget) rather than a soft penalty, which it argues avoids the adversarial gradient dynamics that cause explicit penalties to collapse.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: BCR is a single-stage, single-node training recipe with no auxiliary models, no hyperparameter-sensitive curriculum, and no length supervision, making it trivially composable with existing RL pipelines. The task-scaling law (N as a controllable throughput knob) opens a new deployment dimension analogous to batch size in classical compute, and the 'free lunch' finding—that verbosity is a training artifact that can actively harm accuracy—challenges a widely held assumption in the reasoning-LLM community. Key limitations constrain the impact: validation is confined to 1.5B and 4B models on mathematical reasoning with GRPO, leaving open whether the effects persist at 7B–70B scale, in other reasoning domains (code, science, multimodal), or under other RL algorithms (PPO, DAPO). A theoretical account of the task-scaling law and its behavior under heterogeneous task mixtures remains an open question.
