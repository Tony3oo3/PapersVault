## Problem

The dominant AI evaluation paradigm measures autonomous AI performance against human baselines, implicitly targeting human replacement and steering development toward substitutive applications. This focus risks exacerbating economic inequality, devaluing human skills, and failing to capture collaborative capabilities (asking questions, building shared understanding, fostering creativity) essential for real-world productivity. The paper argues the field must pivot to evaluating the collaborative intelligence of human-AI teams rather than solo AI performance.

## Input / Output

The proposed framework takes human-AI collaborative sessions (interactive logs, task completions, user feedback, override/acceptance decisions) as input and produces a multi-dimensional assessment across three axes: team task performance (outcome quality, efficiency, robustness, innovation), human-centric outcomes (satisfaction, skill enhancement, cognitive load, trust calibration, agency), and collaborative fluency (interaction efficiency, shared awareness, adaptability, error resilience). Evaluation operates at three tiers of cost and fidelity, from cheap standalone screening through AI-simulated surrogate team evaluation to expensive real-participant deployment assessment, with metrics weighted differently depending on the collaboration type (real-time co-creation, asynchronous assistance, decision support, or oversight).

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a proposed evaluation framework that reorients research incentives by changing the fundamental question from 'Can an AI do this task instead of a human?' to 'How much more effectively can a human achieve a goal in concert with an AI?' It specifies a three-tier evaluation hierarchy (standalone benchmarking for capability screening, surrogate team evaluation using AI-based human models for rapid iteration, and real-participant team evaluation for deployment-critical assessment) and four collaboration archetypes with distinct metric priorities. The key mechanism is that benchmarks steer development: by measuring synergy and value-to-humans rather than autonomous accuracy, the field is incentivized to build AI that is explainable, uncertain-aware, adaptive to human intent, and capable of fostering human learning. A concrete first benchmark is sketched in software engineering, using real GitHub issues with four experimental conditions (human alone, AI alone, human+AI synchronous, human+AI asynchronous) and metrics drawn from task performance delta, efficiency, trust calibration, and pre/post skill enhancement.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not report quantitative experimental results. Its central contribution is a proposed evaluation framework (Table 1) with three dimensions of metrics—Team Task Performance, Human-Centric Outcomes, and Collaborative Fluency—rather than empirical measurements. The paper cites one relevant data point from the Anthropic Economic Index (2026): 52% of tasks are currently classified as human-augmenting (up from 47% in 2025), used as evidence that collaborative metrics are already being tracked in practice.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare a proposed method against baselines. The authors position their argument relative to two existing framings: (1) the dominant 'Replacement Paradigm' of solo-AI-vs-solo-human benchmarks (e.g., GLUE, MMLU, ImageNet), which they argue implicitly targets human substitution, and (2) Haupt & Brynjolfsson (2025), who propose 'centaur evaluations' from an economic standpoint. The authors explicitly note they share the centaur-evaluation goal but differ in emphasis: they additionally argue that teaming evaluations directly promote prosocial AI capabilities (e.g., explainability, uncertainty expression, adaptability), not merely economic augmentation. No head-to-head empirical comparison is presented.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes three distinct literatures—hybrid intelligence (Kamar 2016, Lasecki et al. 2011), human-AI teaming empirics (Bansal et al. 2021, Siu et al. 2021, Chen & Chan 2024), and labor economics on automation vs. augmentation (Acemoglu & Restrepo, Autor, Brynjolfsson & McAfee)—to argue that the *evaluation paradigm* itself, not just individual systems, is misaligned. It explicitly positions against the dominant solo-AI-vs-solo-human benchmark tradition (GLUE, MMLU, ImageNet, Go) and aligns with Haupt & Brynjolfsson (2025) on centaur evaluations, while extending that economic framing to argue that teaming benchmarks directly promote prosocial AI capabilities. The paper carves new ground by treating benchmark design as a research-incentive mechanism: the claim is not merely that human-AI collaboration is useful (established) but that the field's implicit replacement framing is steering development toward substitutive rather than complementary applications.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete incentive problem: because benchmarks define what 'solved' means, the current solo-competitive framing channels frontier research toward human-replacement applications, with downstream economic and societal costs the authors argue are avoidable. The three-tier evaluation framework (standalone screening → surrogate team evaluation → real-participant team evaluation) and the concrete software-engineering benchmark proposal (interactive Terminal-Bench with four conditions) are actionable but entirely untested. Key open questions include validating proxy measures at leaderboard scale, handling human variability and skill-level confounds, and building the shared infrastructure ('Human-AI Interaction Gyms') the paper calls for. As a position paper with no empirical results, its impact depends on whether the community adopts the reframing; the strongest empirical hook is the Bansal et al. finding that higher standalone accuracy can reduce team utility, which is well-supported but not new.
