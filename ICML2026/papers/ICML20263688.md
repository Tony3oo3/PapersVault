## Problem

In high-stakes deployment, an agent must balance exploration (improving performance) against safety (respecting constraint risk), but existing methods either require the user to tune abstract divergence hyperparameters that do not directly correspond to declared risk tolerances, or rely on monotonicity assumptions that fail when the control parameter governs the policy rather than the loss. This paper asks: can a safe reference policy's calibration data be used to directly calibrate how aggressively an optimized policy may act, with finite-sample guarantees, without assuming a specific model class or requiring on-policy data?

## Input / Output

The method takes a safe policy π₀ with its historical calibration data (context-action pairs with observed losses), an optimized policy π_t produced by any post-training procedure (SFT, DPO, etc.), and a user-declared risk tolerance α ∈ [0, B]. It outputs a likelihood-ratio bound β̂ that defines a constrained policy π_t^(β̂) interpolating between π₀ and π_t; at deployment, actions are drawn via rejection sampling from this constrained policy, requiring no retraining and allowing the same policy pair to be reused under different α values by trading test-time compute for tighter risk control.

## Method

CPC defines a family of constrained policies by clipping the likelihood ratio π_t(a|x)/π₀(a|x) at a threshold β and renormalizing (Eq. 5), so that β governs the policy distribution rather than the loss. To calibrate β from finite safe-policy data, the paper introduces generalized conformal risk control (gCRC), which drops CRC's monotonicity requirement by searching hyperparameter space from safest to most aggressive and requiring the empirical risk to be controlled for all values beyond the chosen threshold, yielding finite-sample guarantees under K-Lipschitz continuity and ε-replace-one stability. Conformal importance weights correct for multi-round feedback covariate shift between the policies that generated past observations and the candidate policy, with a conservative adjustment for the unknown test-point weight. A key advantage is that the smoothness condition required for the guarantee is enforced by the agent's own policy construction (π_t^(β) is 1-log-Lipschitz in log β) rather than being an assumption on the unknown environment. At deployment, accept-reject sampling realizes the constrained policy without computing intractable normalization constants, with the proposal distribution switching from π₀ (small β) to π_t (large β) for efficiency.

## Results

On MedLFQA, gCRC controls the false discovery rate (FDR) at or below the target level α across all risk settings while achieving recall at least as high—and often substantially higher—than both LTT and Monotonized-losses CRC baselines. In constrained active learning (Robot Arm Kinematics, Airfoil, MEPS), CPC holds constraint violation risk at the declared α=0.2 while reducing test MSE; in some datasets the risk-controlled policy actually attains lower test MSE than the unconstrained policy. In black-box sequence optimization on Ehrlich functions, without CPC the infeasibility rate of policy samples exceeds 80%, whereas moderate risk control (α>0.6) stabilizes optimization and yields slightly better best-objective values by reducing wasted evaluations on infeasible actions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LTT (Angelopoulos et al., 2025) on MedLFQA, gCRC achieves tighter FDR control at every target α while retaining higher claim recall, because LTT's high-probability guarantee via concentration inequalities makes it systematically more conservative. Against Monotonized-losses CRC (Mohri & Hashimoto, 2024; Angelopoulos et al., 2024), gCRC is less conservative and achieves higher recall, since monotonizing each individual loss via Jensen's inequality is far more conservative than monotonizing the empirical risk as gCRC does. Against standard CRC (Angelopoulos et al., 2024) on synthetic non-monotonic losses (Figure 2), gCRC maintains valid risk control across all target levels while standard CRC underestimates risk and fails to control it. Against the CBO baseline of Tian et al. (2024) on the Townsend constrained optimization task, CPC-controlled CBO keeps the constraint violation rate below the declared α=0.4 across all temperature settings, whereas the uncontrolled CBO baseline exceeds α=0.4 in every setting; CPC+CBO also attains comparable or slightly improved reward, suggesting that avoiding infeasible samples improves sample efficiency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends conformal risk control (CRC) in two directions: generalizing it to non-monotonic bounded losses (gCRC) and introducing a new 'prescriptive' setting where the control parameter governs the policy rather than the loss. It builds on weighted conformal methods for feedback covariate shift (Fannjiang et al., 2022; Prinster et al., 2024) to handle multi-round agent-induced distribution shift, and departs from conservative policy optimization (KL-penalized, trust-region methods) by eliminating the need for divergence hyperparameter tuning in favor of a direct risk-tolerance specification. The key conceptual inversion is using importance weighting not to estimate risk for a fixed policy, but to search over a family of likelihood-ratio-clipped policies and select the most aggressive one that still satisfies the risk constraint.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CPC provides a practical path to safe deployment from the first moment of operation without on-policy data collection or model-class assumptions, which is critical for high-stakes domains like medical QA and biomolecular design. The counterintuitive finding that moderate risk control improves optimization efficiency (by reducing wasted evaluations on infeasible actions) suggests safety constraints and performance are not always in tension. Limitations include marginal (not conditional) guarantees, the requirement for computable policy likelihoods, and dependence on stable context distributions; open questions include extending to conditional guarantees, handling intractable likelihoods via density-ratio estimation, and scaling to larger deployment settings.
