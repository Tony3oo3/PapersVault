## Problem

Existing LLM alignment methods are optimized for static text generation and single-turn safety, but agentic models that plan, call tools, and execute multi-step actions face a fundamentally different safety regime where a single misstep (accessing files, entering credentials) can cause irreversible harm. Standard output filtering and scalar reward signals break down because harmful intent is distributed across sequential steps, adversarial tool feedback can inject new risks mid-trajectory, and trajectories that reach similar end states can differ critically in *when* unsafe actions occur (e.g., early refusal vs. late abort after unsafe progress). The paper tackles the gap that safety decisions—when to act, verify, or refuse—remain implicit byproducts of long-form reasoning rather than explicit, learnable, and auditable control points.

## Input / Output

At each step the agent receives an observation containing the user request, full interaction history, tool responses, and environment feedback, alongside a tool catalog with JSON schemas. The output is a structured sequence of blocks: a planning block (think), an optional safety-reasoning block (safety_thoughts) gated by a learned binary decision, and then a terminal action drawn from the same space as tool calls—either a tool_call with function name and arguments, a refusal_tool with a natural-language justification, or an answer block. Refusal is not a post-generation filter but a first-class action in the policy's action space, and the safety gate is learned end-to-end so the model allocates safety reasoning only to high-risk turns rather than every turn.

## Method

MOSAIC restructures agentic inference into a plan → check → act/refuse loop where the check stage is an explicit, modular safety-reasoning block (safety_thoughts) that evaluates harm, irreversibility, permission changes, and risks revealed by recent tool feedback. A learned binary gate decides per-turn whether to invoke this block, keeping benign trajectories concise while concentrating computation on safety-critical steps. Training uses Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with a composite reward: an alignment term derived from pairwise trajectory preferences (an LLM judge compares two full trajectories for the same task and selects the safer one, aggregated into a group-relative score over n=4 rollouts), a format reward enforcing parseable agent traces, and a soft length penalty. The key insight is that pairwise comparisons preserve temporal safety ordering—distinguishing early refusal from late abort after unsafe intermediate actions—that scalar pointwise rewards collapse into a single ambiguous score. The framework is trained on Agent-SafetyBench (~2,000 task instances across 8 risk categories with prompt-injection attack surfaces) and evaluated zero-shot on out-of-distribution benchmarks (AgentHarm, Agent Security Bench, BFCL v3, PrivacyLens) across four open-weight model families.

## Results

MOSAIC reduces harmful-task scores by up to 50% (Qwen2.5: 0.18→0.09) and raises harmful-task refusal from 0% to over 90% for frontier models (GPT-4o: 0.00→0.92; GPT-5: 0.00→0.91). On prompt injection, MOSAIC cuts direct-injection attack success rate by 72% for GPT-4o (0.76→0.21) and 33% for Qwen3-4B (0.46→0.29), while preserving or improving benign completion (GPT-4o CR 0.89→0.93; Qwen3-4B CR 0.44→0.85). On PrivacyLens, MOSAIC reduces leakage rate by up to 23% (Qwen2.5 ALR 0.48→0.37) while maintaining helpfulness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GPT-4o and GPT-5 (unscaffolded), MOSAIC-trained open models (Qwen2.5, Qwen3-4B) achieve substantially lower harm (0.09/0.08 vs. 0.31/0.11) and far higher refusal (0.87/0.89 vs. 0.00), while unscaffolded frontier models never refuse harmful tasks. Once MOSAIC scaffolding is applied to GPT-4o/GPT-5, the gap narrows and open models become broadly comparable, with remaining differences concentrated in direct prompt injection. Against Qwen2.5-7B base, MOSAIC halves harm (0.18→0.09) and improves refusal (0.74→0.87) with only a modest CR drop (0.90→0.84). Against Qwen3-4B-Thinking base, MOSAIC nearly doubles benign completion (0.44→0.85) by eliminating unproductive reasoning loops while slightly improving injection robustness. Against Phi-4 base, MOSAIC reduces over-refusal by 56% (0.43→0.19) and boosts CR to 0.91, at the cost of a small safety regression (harm 0.06→0.09, DPI ASR 0.19→0.28). Against Qwen3-30B base, MOSAIC preserves near-saturated safety (harm 0.02→0.03) while improving IPI robustness (ASR 0.30→0.18, RR 0.57→0.71).

## Relation to Prior Work

MOSAIC extends preference-based reinforcement learning (DPO, Rafailov et al. 2023) and GRPO (Guo et al. 2025) from static text generation to sequential, tool-using agent trajectories, replacing scalar outcome rewards with pairwise trajectory comparisons that preserve temporal safety distinctions. It departs from prior agentic safety work (SafeAgent, Thought-Aligner, ShieldAgent) that treats safety as an auxiliary post-hoc mechanism or reasoning-time edit, instead embedding explicit safety reasoning (<safety_thoughts>) and a first-class refusal action directly into the agent's plan→check→act/refuse inference loop. The structured gating of safety checks (learned end-to-end via RL) distinguishes MOSAIC from fixed prompt-level control flow or heuristic safety filters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that frontier models (GPT-4o, GPT-5) exhibit 0% harmful-task refusal and high injection vulnerability without explicit safety scaffolding challenges the assumption that scale alone yields robust agentic safety, and provides a concrete, reproducible post-training recipe (MOSAIC) that closes much of the gap with modest compute. The model-adaptive behavior—hardening Qwen2.5, fixing Qwen3-4B's reasoning loops, and relaxing Phi-4's over-conservatism—suggests a general principle for safety alignment that respects each model's failure mode rather than enforcing uniform conservatism. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to multi-agent or longer-horizon settings, how the safety-utility trade-off manifests for over-conservative models (Phi-4 showed safety regressions), and whether the pairwise judge's positional bias (60/40) could affect training in non-randomized settings.
