## Problem

Despite rapid growth, interpretability research is widely perceived as lacking practical impact—insights rarely translate into model changes, deployment decisions, or policy. The paper argues the missing ingredient is not new methods but evaluation criteria: a shared framework for determining when interpretability research is successful from a practical, decision-oriented perspective, rather than by explanatory depth alone.

## Input / Output

The paper takes the interpretability research landscape as its object of analysis, examining existing methods (circuit discovery, sparse autoencoders, activation patching, influence functions) and their downstream applications across LLMs and other deep networks. It produces a structured framework: a two-dimensional taxonomy (concreteness × validation) for situating interpretability work, a stakeholder-action matrix mapping five audiences (developers, deployment engineers, domain experts, end users, policymakers) to specific decisions, and evaluation criteria organized by three action categories. The output is a position and checklist rather than a trained model or benchmark score.

## Method

The core contribution is a framework for actionable interpretability built around three components. First, it defines actionability along two axes—concreteness (how precisely an action is specified, from vague suggestions to exact implementation details) and validation (empirical support, from untested hypotheses to systematically evaluated outcomes)—creating a 2×2 space for situating interpretability work. Second, it identifies five domains where interpretability offers unique leverage (scaling-resistant failures, alignment, surgical interventions, architectural design, and translation to meaningful concepts) and categorizes actions into three types: modifying model outputs (data curation, model editing, runtime steering), informing deployment decisions (trust calibration, routing, error detection), and shaping future practice (policy, knowledge transfer, architecture design). Third, it proposes evaluation criteria for each action type, emphasizing comparison against standard ML baselines (prompting, fine-tuning, LoRA) rather than only against other interpretability methods, mechanistic faithfulness via intervention-based verification, and user-facing metrics like task-enhancement, understandability, and reliability.

## Results

This is a position paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative results tables. The only quantitative data point cited is that 21.8% of submitted papers at the ICML 2025 Actionable Interpretability Workshop were explicitly flagged by at least one reviewer as insufficiently actionable. The paper's main contribution is a conceptual framework defining actionability along two dimensions (concreteness and validation) and proposing evaluation criteria (comparative utility, mechanistic faithfulness, generalization, specificity, task-enhancement, understandability, reliability) rather than reporting benchmark numbers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method or conduct head-to-head empirical comparisons against baselines. Instead, it surveys and categorizes existing interpretability work to illustrate its framework. It cites AxBench (Wu et al., 2025) as evidence that simple prompting and fine-tuning baselines often outperform sparse autoencoder-based steering methods, and MIB (Mueller et al., 2025) as a new benchmark enabling comparison across circuit-localization and causal-variable-localization methods that previously lacked a common evaluation. The paper's argument is normative: it advocates that future interpretability work should be evaluated against standard pragmatic ML baselines (prompting, LoRA fine-tuning) rather than only against other interpretability techniques, but it does not itself run such comparisons.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes and repositions the mechanistic interpretability community by importing the 'actionability' concept from HCI and algorithmic recourse literature (Joshi et al., Ustun et al., Singh et al.) and reframing it as a core evaluation criterion for model-internal analysis. It builds conceptually on Nanda et al.'s (2025) call for a 'pragmatic' interpretability and the ICML 2025 Actionable Interpretability Workshop, while explicitly engaging with and partially countering Bau's (2025) defense of curiosity-driven research. Unlike prior XAI work that treats explanations as human-facing communication objects, the paper targets the mechanistic interpretability community that studies models as objects of scientific inquiry, arguing that the field's bottleneck is not methodological novelty but the absence of shared evaluation criteria tied to downstream decisions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a widely felt but under-articulated gap: interpretability research lacks forcing functions analogous to benchmark performance in mainstream ML, making it difficult to assess whether insights translate into real-world impact. By proposing the concreteness × validation framework and an actionable checklist, it gives the community a concrete vocabulary for self-assessment and peer review. Key open questions include whether the field will actually adopt actionability as a publication criterion without penalizing foundational work, whether the five identified leverage domains (alignment, surgical interventions, architectural design, etc.) will yield the promised practical gains, and whether the open-weights assumption will continue to limit applicability to frontier models. As a position paper, it offers no empirical validation of its own framework, leaving the community to test whether these criteria improve the signal-to-noise ratio of interpretability research.
