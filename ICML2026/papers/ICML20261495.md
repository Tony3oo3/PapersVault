## Problem

Existing instruction-based image editing methods are designed for simple, explicit atomic operations (add, delete, swap objects) and fail to handle implicit hypothetical instructions that require deeper reasoning over physical dynamics, temporal changes, causal relationships, or narrative context. No large-scale dataset exists to train or evaluate such reasoning-aware editing, and current architectures lack dedicated mechanisms for extracting the fine-grained visual and textual cues necessary to ground implicit reasoning in concrete editing decisions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261495/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a source RGB image and a hypothetical instruction (an implicit, open-ended prompt such as 'What would happen if the ice cube was left at room temperature?') and produces a single edited image that reflects the inferred transformation while preserving identity of non-target regions. Unlike conventional IIE where instructions map to atomic operations, here the instruction is a question or conditional that the model must reason about to determine what visual changes are physically, temporally, causally, or narratively plausible. The dataset Reason50K provides 51,039 source-instruction-target triplets across four reasoning categories (physical, temporal, causal, story), with the source image generated inversely from the target to ensure semantic consistency.

## Method

ReasonBrain couples an MLLM (LLaVA-v1.1-7B, LoRA-finetuned) for reasoning-guided visual guidance generation with a 12B-parameter Flux diffusion model for image synthesis, bridged by two novel modules. The Fine-grained Reasoning Cue Extraction (FRCE) module operates in two branches: a visual branch that extracts patch-level local features (MAE-inspired) and region-level global features (via SAM segmentation) to capture object attributes and spatial relationships, and a textual branch that uses GPT to extract referenced object tokens and an ID Controller (cross-attention + FFN) to bind those tokens to visual features, preventing semantic drift. These cues, along with multi-scale image tokens, text embeddings, and r learnable [IMG] tokens, are fed into the MLLM to produce guidance embeddings, which a QFormer aligns to the diffusion latent space. A Cross-Modal Enhancer (CME) then applies bidirectional cross-attention blocks (five hybrid blocks per modality) to re-inject fine-grained visual and textual details lost during MLLM processing, producing four enhanced feature streams that condition the diffusion model. Training uses a combined objective: negative log-likelihood on the learnable token predictions (MLLM) plus standard latent diffusion noise-prediction loss, trained on Reason50K with 16 H20 GPUs.

## Results

On Reason50K, ReasonBrain achieves the best overall scores across all four reasoning scenarios: CLIP 0.259, MLLM 0.877, Ins-Align 0.847, and Identity Preservation (IP) 9.72. The largest margins appear on Ins-Align, where ReasonBrain (0.847) outperforms the next-best GPT Image 1.5 (0.612) by 38.4% and SmartFreeEdit (0.659) by 28.5%. On the conventional IIE benchmarks (Emu Edit and MagicBrush), ReasonBrain also achieves the best CLIPdir (0.126/0.139), CLIPim (0.923/0.928), and DINO (0.898/0.893) scores, demonstrating strong zero-shot generalization beyond hypothetical instructions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReasonBrain outperforms all 12+ baselines on every metric across all four reasoning scenarios. The closest competitors are GPT Image 1.5 (Ins-Align 0.612 vs. 0.847) and SmartFreeEdit (Ins-Align 0.659 vs. 0.847), both of which trail by 28–38% on instruction alignment, indicating they lack dedicated fine-grained reasoning mechanisms. Among MLLM-enhanced methods, SmartEdit (Ins-Align 0.532) and MIGE (0.612) show that simply integrating an MLLM or fine-tuning on reasoning data is insufficient without the FRCE and CME modules. On conventional IIE benchmarks (Emu Edit, MagicBrush), ReasonBrain still leads on CLIPim (0.923/0.928) and DINO (0.898/0.893), confirming that training solely on hypothetical instructions does not degrade performance on explicit editing commands. Notably, even large unified models like Bagel-Thinking and Flux Kontext, which benefit from massive pretraining corpora, underperform ReasonBrain, suggesting that scaling alone cannot bridge the reasoning gap without purpose-built architectural components.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MLLM-enhanced instruction-based image editing paradigm (SmartEdit, FireEdit, MGIE) by introducing dedicated reasoning-specific modules rather than relying solely on the MLLM's general world knowledge. The FRCE module draws on MAE-style patch encoding and SAM segmentation for dual-level visual reasoning, while the ID Controller borrows cross-attention mechanisms to ground object references in visual context. Architecturally, it follows the SmartEdit/FireEdit pattern of using learnable tokens as visual guidance for a diffusion model, but augments this with the Cross-Modal Enhancer to recover fine-grained semantics lost during MLLM processing. The key departure is the task formulation itself: shifting from explicit atomic operations (add, delete, swap) to implicit hypothetical instructions that require inferring physical, temporal, causal, or narrative transformations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 51K-sample Reason50K dataset is the first large-scale resource specifically targeting hypothetical instruction reasoning, filling a gap where prior datasets (ReasonEdit, ReasonPix2Pix, EditWorld) were either too small, too narrow, or focused on different aspects. The instruction-conversion experiment (Table A11) provides a useful negative result: even GPT-4o rephrasing hypothetical instructions into explicit ones followed by the best IIE model achieves only 0.512 Ins-Align versus ReasonBrain's 0.847, suggesting the reasoning process is irreducible and cannot be collapsed into a single declarative command. Key limitations include reliance on synthetic data (making real-world generalization uncertain), the narrow scope of the four reasoning categories, and the fact that primary evaluation is on the authors' own benchmark. Open questions include whether the FRCE/CME architecture transfers to video-based editing, whether real-world video pairs can be curated at scale, and whether the one-to-one mapping constraint (Tab. A8) can be relaxed without the observed performance collapse.
