## Problem

Conditional computation strategies such as magnitude pruning and early exit achieve substantial compute savings in practice, but a principled theoretical explanation of when and why accuracy is preserved under reduced compute remains largely absent. Existing pruning theory focuses on iterative prune-train pipelines or general compression bounds, while early-exit systems are typically characterized by empirical policy-driven tradeoffs without explicit scaling laws. This paper asks: what are the fundamental mechanisms governing alignment loss, depth accumulation, and the compute-accuracy exponent in both static (pruning) and adaptive (early exit) regimes?

## Input / Output

The method operates on trained neural network weights (frozen backbones) and input data samples. In the static regime, it consumes weight vectors and produces pruned (and optionally renormalized) weight vectors via one-shot magnitude pruning without retraining. In the adaptive regime, it takes a frozen backbone's intermediate layer representations and a final score, trains a linear exit head by least-squares distillation to the final score, and produces a class label with a compute cost that depends on the input-specific exit decision. The theoretical outputs are explicit compute-accuracy tradeoff bounds where the excess error decays as a power of the compute gap, with the exponent determined by a measurable alignment parameter (cosine similarity for pruning, within-class exit-to-final correlation for early exit).

## Method

The paper develops a unified partial-versus-full computation framework analyzed at two levels. At the single-neuron level, it proves a concentration theorem (Theorem 2.1) showing that the cosine similarity between a weight vector and its one-shot magnitude-pruned counterpart concentrates around a deterministic pruning-rate-dependent constant τ_q, using conditional independence of Gamma order statistics, Berry-Esseen bounds, and Hoeffding concentration on the random threshold. It then introduces the conditional perceptron, where the pruned (renormalized) partial field serves as a confidence score, and proves that excess generalization error decays as O(ε^A) in the compute gap ε with exponent A = 0.5/(1−ρ²) that diverges as alignment ρ→1 (Theorem 3.1). At the deep-network level, it lifts the analysis to infinite-width fully connected networks via the NNGP limit, deriving a cross-kernel recursion (Theorem 4.1) that quantifies how per-layer pruning misalignment compounds through the activation nonlinearity, with explicit one-dimensional recursions for sign and ReLU activations. For frozen-backbone early exit, it assumes sub-Gaussian within-class tail structure on standardized exit and final scores (Assumption 5.1) and proves a deep compute-accuracy tradeoff (Theorem 5.2) with exponent A' = 0.5/(1−ρ_ℓ²), then shows in the NNGP limit that the true within-class correlation concentrates to χ_φ^(L−ℓ), yielding a closed-form depth-gap-dependent exponent (Proposition 5.3). Numerical experiments on VGGNet, MobileNetV2, DistilBERT, and ViT-Base validate the predicted scaling laws and activation-dependent depth accumulation.

## Results

The paper's headline theoretical result is that one-shot magnitude pruning at rate q preserves a deterministic cosine similarity √τ_q between the full and renormalized-pruned weight vectors in high dimensions; at q=0.4 this yields √τ_q ≈ 0.982 (≈10.8° angular deviation), and at q=0.9 the simulated PDF concentrates around √τ_0.9 ≈ 0.66279. For early exit, the excess generalization error decays as a power of the compute gap ε with exponent A = 0.5/(1−ρ²), where ρ is the exit-to-final alignment. On a frozen DistilBERT backbone (SST-2), measured exit-to-final correlations range from 0.446 (layer 1) to 0.977 (layer 5), yielding theoretical exponents from 0.62 to 11.00; empirical log-log slopes (1.69 to 11.75) confirm the predicted inverse dependence on 1−ρ². On ViT-Base (CIFAR-100), correlations (0.246–0.773) produce theoretical exponents 0.53–1.24 with empirical slopes up to 8.78, again showing sharper decay at deeper exits.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not benchmark against competing compression or early-exit methods; instead, it validates its own analytical predictions against empirical measurements on pretrained models. The key 'comparison' is between the theoretically predicted power-law exponent A′ = 0.5/(1−ρ_ℓ²) and the empirically fitted log-log slope of excess error versus compute gap. On DistilBERT (SST-2), the rescaled quantities Â_ℓ(1−ρ_ℓ²) cluster in the range 1.1–1.6 (excluding the noisy ℓ=5 estimate), supporting the predicted inverse dependence on 1−ρ_ℓ². On ViT-Base (CIFAR-100), the theory-experiment gap is larger (empirical slopes 1.56–8.78 vs. theoretical 0.53–1.24), which the authors attribute to the multi-class softmax confidence criterion differing from the single-score binary model. For pruning, the signum activation shows rapid distortion growth and sharp accuracy collapse even at mild sparsity, while ReLU VGGNet7 maintains accuracy through moderate sparsity and MobileNetV2 (ReLU6) degrades earlier—matching the analytical depth-accumulation ordering predicted by the correlation recursion in Theorem 4.1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate threads in model compression—magnitude pruning and early exit—under a single 'partial versus full computation' framework, building on the student-teacher perceptron analysis of Opper (1995) for the single-neuron regime and the NNGP framework of Lee et al. (2018) for the deep-network regime. For pruning, it departs from the dominant focus on iterative prune-train pipelines (Dery et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2023a) by providing concentration results for one-shot, training-free pruning. For early exit, it introduces the conditional perceptron as an analytically tractable model that yields explicit compute-accuracy power-law bounds, going beyond the policy-driven empirical tradeoffs common in the early-exit literature (Teerapittayanon et al., 2016; Han et al., 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first first-principles explanation for why one-shot magnitude pruning preserves decision behavior (cosine similarity concentrates to a pruning-rate-dependent constant) and gives explicit compute-accuracy tradeoff curves for early exit whose exponents diverge as exit-to-final alignment approaches one. Key limitations include the reliance on IID weights, infinite width, and frozen backbones, which are idealized relative to trained finite networks; the empirical validation is on relatively small models (DistilBERT on SST-2, ViT-Base on CIFAR-100), and the theory-experiment gap in predicted exponents (e.g., 0.62 vs. 1.69 at the shallowest exit) suggests the sub-Gaussian tail surrogate in Assumption 5.1 may be too conservative. Open questions include extending the analysis to trained (non-IID) backbones, multiple simultaneous exits, and alternative one-shot pruning rules under the same concentration framework.
