## Problem

Current Vision-Language Models (VLMs) excel at passive semantic retrieval when explicitly told where to look, but lack the agency to autonomously discover and utilize unmentioned visual evidence needed for physical reasoning. Existing benchmarks systematically measure this passive capacity while leaving the active, self-directed visual exploration dimension entirely unmeasured, creating a blind spot in how we evaluate visual understanding.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266414/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The V-IRD benchmark pairs RGB images with strictly sanitized 'Target-Exclusive' prompts that name only the target object (e.g., 'What is the capacity (ml) of the bottle?') while forbidding any textual reference to background evidence such as coins, ID cards, or environmental markers. For continuous tasks (length, volume, temperature, weight), outputs are numerical estimates scored via Threshold Accuracy at four tolerance levels (5%, 10%, 20%, 30%); for discrete tasks (environment, electricity, kinematics), outputs are categorical labels scored by exact match. A unified system prompt requires all models to produce a JSON object containing a step-by-step reasoning field and a final answer field, preventing range outputs or uncertainty hedging.

## Method

The paper formalizes the Visual Implicit Reasoning Deficit as a breakdown in the autonomous search phase: when evidence E is not named in the query Q, the model fails to execute Plan(Q, K) and degenerates from grounded deduction to constrained inspection of only the target object (attention tunneling). To isolate this deficit from knowledge gaps, the authors first verify prerequisite capabilities (visual recognition >99.5% and parametric knowledge 74–96% across model scales), then evaluate 25+ VLMs on V-IRD across four domains (Spatial Geometry, Contextual Inference, Physical Properties, Physical Logic) and ten sub-tasks. A hierarchical failure taxonomy decomposes errors into Stage I (discovery failure), Stage II (association failure), and Stage III (logic failure), revealing that over 90% of errors are agency deficits rather than calculation errors. A gradient-based information injection experiment (four levels from implicit to oracle) confirms the deficit is scale-dependent: medium-scale models benefit from explicit guidance while large-scale models show stagnant performance due to conflicts with parametric priors.

## Results

On the V-IRD benchmark, the best-performing model (Gemini-3-Pro) achieves only 58.36% (ACC₅%) and 64.18% (ACC₁₀%) average accuracy across all ten tasks, far below the human reference of 81.27% (ACC₂₀%) and 84.21% (ACC₃₀%). Spatial Geometry is the most severely affected domain: even Gemini-3-Pro scores just 44.29% on Length and 16.67% on Volume at the 5% threshold. The best open-source model (Qwen3-VL-235B) reaches 46.44% (ACC₅%) and 51.50% (ACC₁₀%), a 12-point gap behind the top proprietary model. Failure-stage analysis reveals that over 90% of errors stem from agency deficits (failing to discover or associate implicit visual cues) rather than from calculation mistakes (only 9.76%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, V-IRD does not propose a new model but evaluates 20+ existing VLMs under a Target-Exclusive prompting strategy that removes all explicit references to supporting visual evidence. Proprietary models consistently outperform open-source counterparts: Gemini-3-Pro leads at 58.36% (ACC₅%) average, followed by Claude-Sonnet-4.5 (49.96%) and GPT-5.2 (47.45%), while the best open-source model Qwen3-VL-235B trails at 46.44%. The gap is most dramatic in Spatial Geometry, where Gemini-3-Pro achieves 44.29% on Length versus 77.14% for humans, and Volume scores remain below 30% for all models. Physical Properties (Temperature) is the easiest domain, with several models exceeding 90%, indicating that failures are domain-specific rather than a uniform capacity issue. The failure-stage decomposition (Table 3) confirms that 75.82% of errors are Stage I (complete failure to discover implicit cues) and 14.42% are Stage II (cues noticed but not associated), together constituting a 90.24% agency deficit versus only 9.76% calculation errors—demonstrating that the bottleneck is visual search initiation, not reasoning capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new conceptual ground by drawing on cognitive science (Gibson's ecological approach, Ballard's animate vision) to formalize a distinction between visual capacity (passive recognition when guided) and visual agency (autonomous evidence-seeking) in VLMs. It explicitly departs from existing evaluation paradigms—PhysBench, HallusionBench, MMMU, V*, and ColorBench—arguing that all of them externalize the attention-planning process to the user, thereby measuring verification capacity rather than search agency. It also distinguishes itself from active-perception methods like AdaptVision and DeepEyes, which provide execution mechanisms (zooming, RL-based search) but presuppose the very agency the paper argues is missing. The Target-Exclusive Prompting strategy is the paper's key methodological departure, stripping all spatial and referential hints from prompts to isolate autonomous discovery.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that over 90% of reasoning failures stem from agency deficits (failing to discover or associate implicit visual cues) rather than calculation errors reframes a central question in VLM development: scaling and better prompting improve what models know but not when or why they autonomously deploy that knowledge. The severe collapse in Spatial Geometry (often <20% accuracy even at relaxed thresholds) despite near-perfect recognition of reference objects when explicitly named is a stark demonstration of the gap. Open questions include whether architectural changes (e.g., iterative visual search loops, attention re-allocation mechanisms) can instill agency, whether the deficit will eventually emerge with sufficient scale, and how to design training objectives that reward autonomous visual exploration. Limitations include the small benchmark size (162 samples), the qualitative failure analysis on only 10 curated instances, and human evaluation by just 3 participants, which constrains the statistical strength of the conclusions.
