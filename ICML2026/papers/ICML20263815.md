## Problem

State-of-the-art solubility prediction has been dominated by deep learning architectures (GNNs, transformers) that sacrifice physical interpretability for marginal accuracy gains, incur prohibitive computational costs, and often exhibit performance inflation from inconsistent data curation. The paper challenges whether such non-transparent architectures are necessary, showing that a transparent, descriptor-driven framework can match or exceed deep learning baselines while remaining physically grounded and computationally efficient.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263815/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes SMILES strings for the solute (and solvent in the multi-solvent regime) along with absolute temperature, converting them into a 176-dimensional physicochemical descriptor vector covering moietal substructure counts, MoSE homomorphism profiles, Joback melting point, Abraham solvation parameters, EState indices, and surface-area descriptors. In the multi-solvent regime, both molecules are additionally projected into a 24-token refined feature set for the interaction layer. The primary output is a predicted log S value (mol/L), supplemented by a six-stage LLM-generated natural-language explanation that maps decision-tree paths, SHAP values, and interaction matrices into chemically grounded narratives.

## Method

DISSOLVR uses CatBoost (oblivious trees with ordered boosting) over explicitly engineered molecular descriptors rather than learned representations, achieving symbolic accessibility by construction. The featurizer encodes three physicochemical pillars: subgraph-isomorphism counts of functional groups, MoSE homomorphism profiles augmented with global topological indices, and thermodynamic reconstructions (Joback melting point, Abraham solvation parameters, EState/surface descriptors). For multi-solvent prediction, a lightweight cross-attention Interaction Layer (d=32, d_k=8) computes a 24×24 solute-solvent coupling matrix from the refined feature set, whose signed-modulus rows serve as interaction intensities scaled by 1/T. Hard per-feature monotonic constraints on temperature variables (T, 1/T, T_r) are enforced at split time, guaranteeing thermodynamic consistency under the endothermic-dissolution regime without soft penalties. An LLM-assisted six-stage post-hoc explainer, blinded to the prediction until Stage 3, translates the symbolic model artifacts into chemically interpretable narratives validated by a 22-expert survey.

## Results

DISSOLVR achieves the best RMSE across all six evaluation benchmarks. On aqueous solubility (Regime I), it reaches RMSE 0.4631 (R²=0.9511) on ESOL and 0.8158 (R²=0.8624) on AqSolDB, approaching the aleatoric noise floor of ~0.6–0.8 log S. On multi-solvent OOD generalization (Regime II), it attains RMSE 0.6305 (R²=0.6587) on BigSolDB 2.0 (Top-21 solvent split) and 0.8798 (R²=0.3527) on the Leeds solute-holdout set, outperforming all GNN and tree-based baselines. The model also reproduces Apelblat temperature-dependence with Pearson correlations of 0.9886 (train) / 0.9844 (test).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DISSOLVR outperforms all 18 baselines on every benchmark. In Regime I (aqueous), it beats the closest tree-based competitor LightGBM by 0.014 RMSE on ESOL (0.4631 vs. 0.4773) and 0.030 on AqSolDB (0.8158 vs. 0.8457), and surpasses the best deep-learning baseline AqSolPred by 0.011 RMSE on AqSolDB. In Regime II (multi-solvent OOD), the advantage widens: DISSOLVR leads XGBoost by 0.011 RMSE on BigSolDB 2.0 (0.6305 vs. 0.6416) and FastSolv by 0.063 (0.6305 vs. 0.6938), while GNNs (GAT, GIN, GCN, MPNN) all trail by 0.04–0.12 RMSE. On the Leeds solute-holdout set, DISSOLVR (0.8798) edges out FastSolv (0.8868) and LightGBM (0.9071), whereas the ANN baseline catastrophically fails (RMSE 6.31, R²=−34.7). The paper also notes that several published models report RMSE below the aleatoric limit (~0.6–0.8 log S), which the authors attribute to curation artifacts or data leakage rather than genuine predictive power.

## Relation to Prior Work

DISSOLVR deliberately departs from the dominant deep-learning trend (GNNs, equivariant transformers, sequence-based models) in solubility prediction by returning to the descriptor-based tradition established by ESOL, GSE, and Boobier et al. (2020), but modernizes it with a 176-dimensional feature set spanning moietal identity, MoSE topological profiles, Joback energetics, and Abraham solvation parameters. The architecture repurposes two ideas from the deep-learning literature—cross-attention (applied to a compact 24-token refined feature set rather than full molecular graphs) and hard monotonic constraints (exploiting CatBoost's native support for per-feature monotonicity at split time)—within a transparent gradient-boosted tree backbone. The aleatoric-limit framing follows Attia et al. (2025) and Palmer & Mitchell (2014), while the OOD solvent-holdout evaluation protocol extends the benchmarking approach of Ramani & Karmakar (2024). The LLM-assisted six-stage explanation pipeline is a novel addition to the solubility prediction literature, bridging symbolic model artifacts to chemically grounded narratives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's most impactful contribution is a reproducible benchmark showing that many published solubility models underperform their reported metrics under standardized curation, and that several claim performance beyond the physically implausible aleatoric limit (~0.6–0.8 log S), suggesting widespread curation artifacts or leakage in the field. For practitioners, DISSOLVR offers a CPU-deployable, interpretable alternative that matches or exceeds deep architectures without GPU training, which is practically significant for high-throughput screening. Key limitations remain: the model is blind to stereochemistry and 3D conformation (identical predictions for stereoisomers), assumes endothermic dissolution (>96% of BigSolDB pairs but excluding exothermic systems), and is out of scope for inorganic salts. Open questions include whether inexpensive quantum descriptors can close the gap for electronically delicate systems, and whether the LLM explanation pipeline (validated on only 22 chemists) scales to broader expert populations.
