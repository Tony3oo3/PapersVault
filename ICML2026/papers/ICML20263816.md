## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks emphasize single-agent decision-making or zero-sum competition, leaving cooperative coordination under incomplete information largely unexplored. While Hanabi is a natural testbed for Theory-of-Mind reasoning, prior evaluations (e.g., SPIN-Bench, LLM-Arena) suffer from small model coverage, missing reproducibility details, and a lack of datasets suitable for post-training LLM agents on multi-agent cooperation. This paper addresses these gaps with a large-scale, reproducible benchmark and the first public Hanabi datasets designed for LLM post-training.

## Input / Output

Each agent receives the current game state (turn number, player identity, information/life tokens, discard pile), visible cards in all other players' hands with their explicit knowledge, and a list of legal candidate actions. In the Sherlock scaffold, a deductive context block enumerates all valid color/rank possibilities per card based on prior clues; in Mycroft, the agent instead receives its own prior-turn deductions, action ratings, and reasoning as a working-memory scratch pad. The agent outputs a chosen action, a natural-language reasoning trace, a scalar rating in [-1,1] for every candidate action, and (in Mycroft) an updated deduction block for all other players' hands.

## Method

The paper introduces a three-tier prompting hierarchy—Watson (minimal state), Sherlock (engine-provided deductive context plus Bayesian step-by-step reasoning instructions), and Mycroft (implicit multi-turn state tracking via a self-maintained scratch pad)—to bracket lower and upper bounds on LLM cooperative performance. It evaluates 17 SOTA LLMs (4B–671B+) across 2–5 player settings over 10 fixed seeds per configuration, and releases two datasets: HanabiLogs (1,520 annotated game trajectories with reasoning traces) and HanabiRewards (560 games with dense per-move utility ratings from an LLM-as-a-judge). To demonstrate post-training gains, the authors instruction-tune Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 via LoRA SFT on HanabiLogs, then apply GRPO reinforcement learning (16 rollouts, batch 512) on HanabiRewards, achieving a 156% score improvement in Sherlock and closing the gap to o4-mini to within ~3 points. Cross-play experiments show that inserting one stronger agent into a team of weaker ones interpolates team performance between the two self-play scores, and out-of-domain evaluation confirms that RL training on HanabiRewards transfers to cooperative group-guessing, long-context temporal reasoning, and instruction following without degrading mathematical reasoning.

## Results

Across 17 LLMs evaluated on Hanabi (2–5 players), top reasoning models (o3, Grok-3-mini, Gemini 2.5 Pro) achieve ~15–18/25 in the Sherlock setting, while non-reasoning models score <10/25. After RL fine-tuning (GRPO) on HanabiRewards, the 4B Qwen3-4B model (Ours-RL) improves by +7.5 points (+156%) in Sherlock and +6.6 (+388%) in Mycroft, surpassing GPT-4.1 by +4.2 (+52%) and reaching within ~3 points of o4-mini. On out-of-domain generalization (Table 2), Ours-RL gains +12 pp on the Group Guessing Game, +6.4 on EventQA (800K context), +1.7 Pass@10 on IFBench, and maintains AIME 2025 Pass@10 at 73.3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Ours-RL (Qwen3-4B + RL on HanabiRewards) lags o4-mini by only ~3 points in both Sherlock and Mycroft settings, despite having far fewer parameters and no test-time compute. It surpasses the best non-reasoning model GPT-4.1 by +4.2 points (+52%) in Sherlock and is competitive with Grok-3 and Claude 3.7 Sonnet. Compared to the SPIN-Bench protocol, the paper's Watson setting yields 14.2/25 for DeepSeek-R1 versus SPIN-Bench's reported 6/25, suggesting prior benchmarks underestimate LLM capability. Against specialized RL Hanabi agents (BAD, SAD, OBL scoring >23/25 in 2-player self-play) and the generalist R3D2 agent (≥20 in 2–4 players), the best LLMs still trail by 5–8 points. On human baselines from BoardGameGeek (80,699 games), top LLMs reach roughly the 25th percentile of human players (~15–18 vs. 18–23 for experienced humans). In cross-play, adding one Grok-3-mini agent to a team of o4-mini agents improves scores by ~1.7 points, showing smooth interpolation between self-play scores rather than the degradation seen in traditional RL agents.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Hanabi-as-LLM-benchmark line initiated by LLM-Arena (Chen et al., 2024) and SPIN-Bench (Yao et al., 2025), which evaluated a smaller set of models primarily in 2-player, single-turn settings. It departs from prior work in three key ways: (1) a progressive prompting hierarchy (Watson → Sherlock → Mycroft) that systematically isolates the contribution of explicit deductive context versus implicit multi-turn state tracking, (2) cross-play evaluation showing interpolation behavior unlike traditional RL agents (Hu et al., 2020), and (3) the first public Hanabi datasets with move-level utility annotations (HanabiLogs, HanabiRewards) enabling RL post-training of small open-weight models. The RL training pipeline builds on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and Prime-RL, while the evaluation protocol builds on the Hanabi Learning Environment (Google DeepMind, 2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The released datasets (HanabiLogs: 1,520 trajectories; HanabiRewards: 560 games with dense move-level ratings) fill a critical gap — no prior public Hanabi dataset supported LLM post-training for multi-agent cooperation. The finding that a 4B model RL-finetuned on HanabiRewards closes most of the gap to frontier reasoning models (within ~3/25 of o4-mini) while also transferring to out-of-domain cooperation, temporal reasoning, and instruction following suggests that game-based RL can serve as a compact proxy for broader cooperative reasoning capabilities. Key open questions remain: even the best models (o3, Grok-3-mini) struggle with reliable implicit state tracking in multi-turn play (Mycroft), scores still trail specialist human experts (>20/25), and systematic cross-play with diverse agent mixes of varying strengths is largely unexplored. The paper also highlights that scaffold design and context presentation are consequential factors that prior benchmarks have underappreciated.
