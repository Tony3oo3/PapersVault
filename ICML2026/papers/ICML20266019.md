## Problem

Transformers reliably master local syntactic patterns early in training but acquire deep semantic dependencies (e.g., variable binding, multi-hop reasoning) only later, often via an abrupt qualitative transition rather than gradual improvement. The optimization dynamics governing this delayed emergence remain poorly understood: it is unclear why the gradient signal for sparse, long-range dependencies is suppressed during early training and what triggers the sudden phase transition into systematic reasoning.

## Input / Output

For the code setting, the input is a source-code snippet where a specific variable usage node (identified via the native AST) is masked (e.g., `return a + <VAR>`), and the model must output the correct variable identifier by resolving the USE-DEF dependency across context. For the natural-language setting, the input is a sentence containing a pronoun or reference, and the model must recover the antecedent span. The AST-based masking ensures the task strictly evaluates long-range semantic resolution rather than trivial local n-gram completion, and the same topology-aligned objective is applied to both modalities.

## Method

The paper proposes a mechanistic framework in which training is a spectral competition between high-curvature Surface Statistics (syntax) and low-curvature Deep Semantics (latent dependency structure), formalized via a Gradient Starvation theorem showing that syntactic gradients dominate the update direction by a factor of λ_syn/λ_sem, and a Softmax Saturation lemma showing the semantic learning signal vanishes exponentially once syntactic attention saturates. The practical intervention is a topology-aligned contrastive objective: after profiling attention heads to identify the sparse set of binding heads (top-8 by USE→DEF attention mass), a hinge loss is added that explicitly pushes the attention score on the true definition token above the mean distractor score, injecting a rank-1 steering term μ_use(μ_src − μ̄_neg)^T into the W_QK gradient that aligns updates with the semantic operator while orthogonalizing against syntactic directions. Fine-tuning is performed via LoRA (rank 16) on only the identified binding heads, and the variable-weighted LM loss up-weights variable tokens (α=5.0) to further boost the alignment ratio ρ(t). This geometrically targeted intervention yields over 2× the improvement of standard cross-entropy fine-tuning on variable cloze completion and generalizes across languages and to natural-language coreference (WinoGrande).

## Results

On the Variable Cloze Completion task, the proposed topology-aligned contrastive objective (Ours) achieves 0.362 accuracy on Python and 0.344 on JavaScript with Llama-3.1-8B, and 0.909 on Python and 0.884 on JavaScript with Qwen2.5-Coder-7B. The improvement over standard cross-entropy fine-tuning (SFT) is more than 2× larger across all model–language combinations (e.g., +0.027 vs. +0.008 for Llama-3.1-8B on Python). On the natural-language WinoGrande benchmark, the method further improves over SFT by +0.029 (Qwen2.5-7B: 0.874 vs. 0.845) and +0.033 (Llama-3.1-8B: 0.896 vs. 0.863).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed topology-aligned contrastive objective outperforms all four structured-learning baselines across every model–language setting. Against Curriculum Learning (Bengio et al., 2009), Ours gains +0.014 (Llama Python) to +0.013 (Qwen Python); against Linear-chain CRF (Zheng et al., 2015), the margin is +0.011 to +0.009; against Supervised Contrastive Learning (SupCon, Gunel et al., 2020), the advantage is largest at +0.038 (Llama Python) and +0.033 (Qwen JavaScript); and against Attention Entropy Regularization (Zhao et al., 2019), Ours leads by +0.011 to +0.004. The most consistent and largest gap is over SFT (plain cross-entropy), where the improvement ratio exceeds 2× in all four settings, confirming that explicitly steering attention geometry is more sample-efficient than implicit likelihood maximization for deep dependency tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by reframing the syntax-semantics tension in transformers as a *dynamical* competition in the gradient landscape, rather than a static representational or architectural property as treated in prior work on syntax-semantics separation (Russin et al., 2019; Caucheteux et al., 2021) or structural prior injection (Bai et al., 2021). It repurposes the gradient starvation concept from the generalization literature (Pezeshki et al., 2021) and connects it to the softmax saturation mechanism in attention, providing a unified mechanistic account for the delayed, phase-transition-like emergence of deep dependency circuits observed in grokking studies (Power et al., 2022; Nanda et al., 2023) and variable binding analyses (Davies et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025). The topology-aligned contrastive objective is a novel practical intervention that explicitly manipulates the alignment ratio ρ(t), distinguishing it from prior structured-learning baselines (CRF, SupCon, curriculum) that impose external constraints without addressing the underlying gradient geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a mechanistic explanation for a widely observed but poorly understood phenomenon—why deep dependency reasoning emerges abruptly rather than gradually—and provides a concrete, testable metric (alignment ratio ρ(t)) for tracking this transition across training. The practical intervention (topology-aligned contrastive loss) demonstrates that explicitly rectifying gradient geometry can be more sample-efficient than implicit likelihood maximization, with implications for pre-training data curation (prioritizing logic density over syntactic perfection) and CoT supervision design. Key limitations include the reliance on code as a proxy for semantic topology (natural language lacks unambiguous AST ground truth), the risk of interfering with polysemantic heads during targeted fine-tuning, and the computational overhead of the contrastive objective. Open questions include whether the mechanism generalizes to truly ambiguous natural-language dependencies and whether pre-training objectives can be redesigned to avoid the syntactic starvation regime altogether.
