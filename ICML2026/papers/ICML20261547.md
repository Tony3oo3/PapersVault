## Problem

Existing ML-based network simulators trained with MSE systematically fail to capture tail latency and jitter, rendering them unreliable for latency-sensitive systems. Additionally, standard weight-sharing NAS (e.g., ENAS) introduces evaluation noise that is benign for classification but critically undermines architecture ranking in high-precision regression tasks like packet-level delay prediction, making automated architecture discovery ineffective for this domain.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261547/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model is a sequence-to-sequence predictor that takes a time-ordered sequence of packet-level queue states (arrival, service, scheduling events) through a 4-port switch as input and produces the corresponding per-packet delay and jitter sequence as output. Each training flow spans 16,041 time steps with 16,000 data points, and the switch supports FIFO, strict priority, deficit round robin, and weighted fair queuing with randomized weights. The discovered architecture is then instantiated as a reusable device-level digital twin and composed across a target topology (e.g., fattree16) for end-to-end network simulation.

## Method

ANAS is a three-phase NAS framework: (1) a dynamic supernet warm-up stabilizes shared weights via uniform sampling; (2) a similarity-guided evaluation loop generates candidate architectures by applying 1–2 perturbations to the just-trained architecture, evaluates them under structurally similar weights, and discards the top 10% highest-loss outliers to filter noise; (3) an LSTM controller trained with REINFORCE and an XGBoost performance predictor derives the final topology, which is retrained from scratch. The search space is a hybrid encoder-decoder DAG where each node is an RNN cell with a learned highway gate, augmented by multi-head self-attention over the encoder output to provide global context to the decoder. Training uses a hybrid loss combining MSE with a 1D Wasserstein distance (β=0.01) that explicitly penalizes distributional deviations in the tail, addressing the tail-blindness of pure MSE.

## Results

On the 4-port switch prediction task, ANAS achieves the best performance across all metrics: MSE of 5.14×10⁻⁵ (25.8% lower than DeepQueueNet's 6.93×10⁻⁵), normalized Wasserstein distance Wₙ of 4.5×10⁻³ (58% lower than DeepQueueNet's 10.7×10⁻³), and tail-sensitive p99Wₙ of 17.0×10⁻³ (21.7% lower than DeepQueueNet's 21.7×10⁻³). The search completes in 16 GPU-hours, half the 32-hour budget of the double-budget Random Search control. In end-to-end simulation across static traffic models, ANAS reduces path-wise Wₙ by up to 98.8% versus RouteNet, 69.8% versus DeepQueueNet, and 43.9% versus ENAS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DeepQueueNet (the manual SOTA), ANAS wins on every metric: 25.8% lower MSE, 58% lower Wₙ, and 21.7% lower p99Wₙ, with the largest relative gains in tail-sensitive metrics. Against ENAS (the standard weight-sharing NAS baseline), ANAS reduces MSE by 15.6%, Wₙ by 62.2%, and p99Wₙ by 15%, confirming that the similarity-constrained evaluation fixes the ranking distortion that plagues vanilla weight-sharing in regression. Against the general-purpose Transformer (matched at ~664K parameters), ANAS achieves 13.9% lower MSE, 25% lower Wₙ, and 23.1% lower p99Wₙ, demonstrating that domain-specific architecture search captures tail dynamics that a fixed attention backbone cannot. Against RouteNet in end-to-end simulation, ANAS reduces average path-wise Wₙ by 98.8% across static scenarios, though RouteNet's flow-level abstraction makes it a less direct comparison. Notably, ANAS outperforms the double-budget Random Search (same search space and loss) on all three metrics while using half the compute, isolating the contribution of the similarity-guided search strategy itself.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the two-stage packet-level simulation paradigm of DeepQueueNet by replacing its manually designed backbone with an automatically searched architecture, and adapts weight-sharing NAS (ENAS, DARTS) from its native classification setting to the high-precision regression demands of network simulation. The key departure is the similarity-constrained evaluation protocol, which addresses a known weakness of supernet-based NAS (evaluation noise under weight sharing) that becomes particularly damaging in regression tasks where small prediction errors amplify into tail-statistics failures. The hybrid attention-recurrent search space and Wasserstein-augmented loss are positioned as complementary structural and objective-level innovations that together enable tail-sensitive modeling, building on the highway-connection RNN cells and multi-head attention mechanisms from prior sequence-modeling work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, automated methodology for generating high-fidelity network device models that capture both average and tail performance, which is critical for validating latency-sensitive protocols (distributed training, cloud gaming, video conferencing). The similarity-constrained evaluation insight is potentially transferable to other high-precision regression NAS applications beyond networking. However, the evaluation is confined to a single 4-port switch task on one public dataset, and the real-world trace generalization, while impressive, involves only two application traces; scalability to larger topologies, diverse device types, and multi-hop interactions remains untested. An open question is whether the discovered architectural patterns (shallower decoders, more output branches, skip connections) generalize across different queuing disciplines and traffic regimes beyond those in the training set.
