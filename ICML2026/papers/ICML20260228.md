## Problem

Standard 1WL-based GNNs are computationally efficient but cannot capture important substructures like triangles, while higher-order WL tests (2WL and beyond) require quadratic memory and cubic time, making them impractical for large graphs. Additionally, most expressivity characterizations in the literature target vertex-based message passing, leaving edge-based GNN architectures—despite their empirical success in domains like chemistry—without strong theoretical guarantees. The paper asks whether one can surpass 1WL expressivity while retaining near-linear cost on sparse graphs, and whether such guarantees can be established for edge-centric message passing.

## Input / Output

The method consumes undirected graphs with optional node features and edge features, which are encoded into initial directed-edge embeddings via separate encoders for left node, right node, and edge attributes. The architecture produces embeddings for each directed edge (ordered pair), which are then pooled for graph-level tasks (sum, mean, or node-count-scaled mean over all directed edges) or combined for edge-level regression (summing the two directed-edge embeddings or averaging two directional predictions). Unlike vertex-based GNNs, EB-GNN does not produce node embeddings, limiting direct application to node-level or link-prediction tasks.

## Method

EB-GNN performs message passing on directed edges rather than vertices, with each update aggregating three types of information: (α) a sum over the first endpoint's incident edge embeddings, (β) a sum over common-neighbor (triangle) edge-embedding pairs, and (γ) a sum over the second endpoint's incident edge embeddings, followed by a residual connection and a feed-forward network. The triangle aggregation (β) is the key expressivity driver, explicitly encoding local triangle structure around each edge. The triangle enumeration is performed via the Chiba-Nishizeki algorithm in O(αm) time (where α is the graph's arboricity), yielding near-linear complexity on sparse graphs. Theoretically, the corresponding EB-1WL color-refinement test is proven strictly more expressive than both 1WL and NC-1WL, admits a complete characterization via a novel clique-based first-order logic with counting (CFOC³), and is at least as powerful as homomorphism counting from chordal graphs of treewidth two. The architecture's expressive power exactly matches EB-1WL, as proven by a constructive simulation argument.

## Results

On QM9 (12 regression tasks), EB-GNN ranks best or second-best on 11/12 tasks, outperforming the state-of-the-art 5-ℓ-GIN on 4 tasks (e.g., μ: 3.15 vs 3.50, Δ(ε): 3.02 vs 3.21) while being ~4× faster (O(αm) vs O(nd⁵)). On QMD edge-level tasks, EB-GNN reduces MAE by 20–50% over D-MPNN (e.g., Bond Length: 3.17 vs 4.48 ×10⁻³ Å). On MalNet-Tiny, EB-GNN achieves 93.30% accuracy, outperforming HyMN (92.84%) and all GPS variants. On BREC expressivity benchmarks, EB-GNN distinguishes 59/60 Basic and 48/50 Regular pairs (matching 2WL), and 60/100 Extension pairs (vs. 0 for NC-GNN).

## Baselines & Comparisons

EB-GNN consistently outperforms standard MPNNs (Xu et al., 2019) across all five datasets, e.g., 93.30% vs 91.10% on MalNet-Tiny and substantially lower MAE on QM9. Against the expressivity-focused baselines, EB-GNN matches 2WL on BREC Basic (59/60) and Regular (48/50) pairs while running in O(αm) vs O(n³), and outperforms 5-ℓ-GIN (Paolino et al., 2024) on 4/12 QM9 tasks at ~4× lower cost. On the edge-based comparison, EB-GNN strictly outperforms NC-GNN (Liu et al., 2024): 60 vs 0 distinguishable Extension pairs on BREC, lower MAE on 6/8 QMD tasks, and 93.30% vs 92.50% on MalNet-Tiny. EB-GNN also beats D-MPNN (Li et al., 2024) on all four QMD edge-level tasks (20–50% MAE reduction) and outperforms HyMN (Southern et al., 2025) and all GPS variants (Rampašek et al., 2022) on MalNet-Tiny. On QM9, EB-GNN surpasses DTNN (Schütt et al., 2017), NestedGNN (Zhang et al., 2021), I²-GNN (Huang et al., 2023), and DRFWL (Zhou et al., 2023) on most tasks, with the notable exception of U₀, U, H, G, and C_v where 5-ℓ-GIN remains best.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper shifts the message-passing paradigm from vertices to edges, introducing EB-1WL as a color-refinement test that operates on ordered edge pairs and explicitly incorporates triangle interactions. It builds directly on the Chiba-Nishizeki (1985) triangle-enumeration algorithm, repurposing its lower-degree-endpoint iteration strategy as the computational backbone for the triangle-aware aggregation step. Conceptually, EB-1WL sits strictly between 1WL/NC-1WL (Liu et al., 2024) and 2WL in the WL hierarchy, and the paper provides the first complete logical characterization (via a novel clique-based first-order logic with counting, CFOC³) and homomorphism-counting lower bound (chordal graphs of treewidth 2) for an edge-based message-passing test. The corresponding EB-GNN architecture matches EB-1WL's expressivity exactly while achieving O(αm) per-iteration cost, in contrast to the O(n²)–O(n³) costs of 2WL-inspired GNNs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a notable gap: edge-based GNNs have been empirically successful in chemistry but lacked rigorous expressivity guarantees, and this work provides the strongest such guarantees in the literature for that class. The open question of whether EB-1WL's distinguishing power is exactly characterized by homomorphism counts from chordal graphs of treewidth 2 (the converse of Theorem 4) is a natural next step. A practical limitation is that EB-GNN produces only edge embeddings, precluding direct use for node-level tasks or standard link prediction, which the authors flag as future work. The near-linear scaling on sparse graphs (arboricity ≤ 3 for molecules, ≤ 15 for cybersecurity graphs) makes this architecture attractive for large-scale settings where 2WL-based methods are infeasible.
