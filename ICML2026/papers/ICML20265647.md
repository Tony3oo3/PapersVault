## Problem

Predicting how collective social beliefs shift in response to real-world events is a fundamental challenge in social science, but three barriers have blocked progress: social beliefs lack high-fidelity time-series measurements, the transition rules are semantic and non-symbolic, and no explicit labels exist linking specific events to observed belief shifts. This paper asks whether LLMs, with their commonsense and social knowledge, can serve as a transition engine for modeling event-driven belief dynamics without human annotation or expensive census data.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a 16-day look-back window of daily closing prices for a binary prediction-market proposition (e.g., 'Will Solana reach all-time high in Sep–Dec 2024?') together with a candidate set of up to 30 news articles drawn from a three-day window strictly preceding the target day. In forecasting mode it outputs a single predicted next-day probability in [0,1] by marginalizing over the attributor's event weights; in simulation mode it outputs the predicted belief shift Δ for a user-specified (possibly hypothetical) event, bypassing the attributor entirely. The state is a trajectory rather than a scalar, and the event space is open-ended natural language, so the model must handle variable-length, unstructured inputs at every time step.

## Method

SWM parameterizes the belief transition as P_θ(s_{t+1} | s_t, e_t) over an LLM backbone (Qwen3-8B) with a Gaussian regression head, treating the belief state as a price trajectory and social events as exogenous actions. Because the true driving event is latent, the paper introduces a prior attributor P_η (a second LLM with a softmax salience head) that scores each candidate event, and trains both components via a posterior-guided objective: a frozen Qwen3.5-397B hindsight model labels which event caused each realized shift, and P_η is distilled toward these labels via KL divergence while P_θ regresses the attributed event's predicted shift toward the realized change with weighted squared error. The null (no-news) branch is parameter-free and fixed to the persistence baseline, so θ models only non-null event effects. This decoupling of attribution from dynamics means the world model can be queried directly for counterfactual 'what-if' simulations, and the overall loss is a negative ELBO that requires no balancing hyperparameter since the two terms optimize disjoint parameters.

## Results

On the attributed subset of SWM-Bench, SWM (prior) achieves the highest 3-way Directional Accuracy among all baselines: 0.685 on Polymarket and 0.845 on Kalshi, outperforming GPT-5.5 (0.534 and 0.711 respectively) by 15.1 and 13.4 percentage points. On Kalshi, SWM (prior) also leads in MASE (0.800 vs. 1.004 for GPT-5.5) and Pearson correlation (0.380 vs. 0.250). The oracle posterior-guided variant (SWM posterior) further improves to DA 0.894 and Corr 0.525 on Kalshi, establishing an upper bound that reveals the prior attributor as the main bottleneck. On the full test set, SWM (prior) achieves DA 0.589 on Kalshi, surpassing all time-series baselines (best: TimeMixer at 0.549) and fine-tuned LLMs (best: Time-LLM at 0.567).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SWM (prior) decisively outperforms all time-series-only baselines on the attributed subset, where methods like TimeMixer (DA 0.373 on Kalshi) and PatchTST (DA 0.289) degrade sharply because they cannot leverage news signals. Against prompting-based LLMs, SWM (prior) achieves DA 0.845 on Kalshi vs. GPT-5.5's 0.711 and Qwen3.5-397B's 0.718, a 13+ point gap that the paper attributes to its explicit latent-event attribution mechanism rather than raw model scale. Among fine-tuned LLMs, SWM (prior) beats Time-LLM (DA 0.430) and LLMFForecasting (DA 0.768) on Kalshi's attributed subset, though LLMFForecasting comes closest. On Polymarket, SWM (prior) retains the best DA (0.685) but trails GPT-5.5 and Qwen3.5-397B on MASE (0.933 vs. 0.895/0.920), which the authors attribute to Polymarket's heavier algorithmic trading making moves more endogenous and less news-driven. The oracle SWM (posterior) row confirms the world model itself is well-calibrated once the correct event is identified, pinpointing the prior attributor's ability to isolate the causal news item as the primary remaining bottleneck.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three distinct research threads—classical world models (which predict physical state transitions from observations and actions), LLM-based agent social simulation (which derives macro-dynamics from individual agent behavior), and news-informed time-series forecasting (Time-LLM, FNF, ChatTime)—into a unified framework that parameterizes macro-level social belief dynamics directly, bypassing per-agent simulation. The key conceptual departure is treating collective belief as a semantic state and news events as exogenous actions in a world-model transition, combined with a posterior-guided training paradigm where a frozen LLM provides hindsight pseudo-labels for latent event attribution. Unlike prior forecasting work that ingests news as an additional input modality, SWM explicitly models *which* event drives each transition as a latent variable, enabling both predictive forecasting and counterfactual 'what-if' simulation from a single learned model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SWM-Bench is the first benchmark grounding social belief prediction in high-fidelity prediction market data, and the posterior-guided training recipe offers a practical solution to the attribution-labeling problem that has limited prior event-driven forecasting. The finding that the prior attributor (not the world model) is the critical bottleneck suggests future work should focus on improving causal event identification rather than scaling transition dynamics. Key open questions include whether the first-order single-event assumption (A1) and conditional exogeneity (A2) hold in more reflexive or multi-event regimes, whether the framework generalizes beyond prediction markets to other belief proxies (surveys, social media), and whether the counterfactual simulation fidelity can be improved beyond what the LLM backbone's internalized economic reasoning allows.
