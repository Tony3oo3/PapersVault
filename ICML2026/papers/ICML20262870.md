## Problem

Neural scaling laws show that LLM pre-training loss decays as a power law with training time, but the origin of this behavior and the determination of its exponent remain debated. Existing theories attribute the power law to power-law structures in the data (feature frequencies, data manifold dimension, covariance spectra), leaving open whether the scaling could arise from architectural factors alone. This paper asks whether the combination of softmax and cross-entropy, when applied to peaked output distributions, can intrinsically generate power-law training dynamics independent of data structure.

## Input / Output

The toy model consumes a hidden state vector x drawn as i.i.d. standard normal and a fixed teacher weight matrix W* parameterized by an inverse temperature β* (controlling how peaked the target distribution is), and produces the student's loss L(τ) and inverse temperature β(τ) as functions of dynamic time τ = ∫η dt. For LLM validation, the method evaluates published Pythia and OLMo checkpoints at multiple training steps on the FineWeb dataset, extracting per-token cross-entropy loss and logit standard deviation (as a proxy for inverse temperature) to fit power-law exponents. The fundamental output variable is the loss exponent α_τ in L − L_∞ ∝ τ^(−α_τ), with the theoretical prediction being α_τ = 1/3.

## Method

The core mechanism is that softmax (Boltzmann distribution) and cross-entropy (KL divergence) together produce power-law vanishing losses and gradients when the target distribution is peaked (low-entropy), regardless of data structure. The authors isolate this in a minimal one-layer teacher-student toy model mimicking the LM head, then reduce the dynamics via an 'aligned student ansatz' (student weight proportional to teacher weight) to a single scalar variable β, the student inverse temperature. In the low-temperature regime, a Taylor expansion of the free energy F(β) = −c₀ − c₁β⁻¹ − c₂β⁻² + … yields L ≈ c₂β⁻¹ and −dL/dβ ≈ c₂β⁻², and substituting into the gradient-flow equation dβ/dτ = −(c_eff/n)(dL/dβ) gives β ∝ τ^(1/3) and hence L ∝ τ^(−1/3). The exponent 1/3 is 'universal' in the statistical-physics sense: it depends only on generic conditions (peaked output, non-zero probability density at zero energy gap) and is robust to data power-law structures, initialization scale, optimizer choice (SGD, Adam), and learning-rate schedules, as confirmed by extensive toy-model experiments and by fitting Pythia/OLMo checkpoints to the predicted τ^(−1/3) scaling.

## Results

The paper derives a universal loss scaling exponent of 1/3 (i.e., L ~ τ^{-1/3}) for learning peaked distributions with softmax and cross-entropy, validated in a one-layer toy model where the fitted loss exponent in β-space is 1.15 ± 0.04 and the gradient exponent is 2.26 ± 0.01. On Pythia LLMs (70M–12B) evaluated on FineWeb, the fitted dynamic-time exponents α_τ are all very close to 1/3, and curves from different model sizes collapse onto a single power law. The logit standard deviation in Pythia grows with an algebraic exponent close to 1/3 before saturating, confirming entry into the low-temperature regime. The Chinchilla irreducible entropy bound (1.69 nats) implies an effective inverse temperature β* ≈ 6.87, well above the critical threshold c₀ ≈ 4.55 for i.i.d. Gaussian logits.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, it compares its predicted 1/3 exponent to previously observed and fitted scaling exponents. The Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) report a dataset-size exponent of 0.28, which the authors argue is a distorted version of the true 1/3 dynamic-time exponent due to non-linear learning-rate schedules. Kaplan et al. (2020) implicitly assumed zero irreducible loss and obtained 0.095, while Hestness et al. (2017) reported exponents of 0.30 and 0.36, both closer to 1/3. The key qualitative comparison is against prior theoretical explanations that attribute power-law scaling to power-law structures in data (e.g., feature frequencies in Michaud et al., 2023; covariance spectra in Bordelon et al., 2024, 2025a,b). This paper shows that softmax and cross-entropy alone, when learning peaked distributions, produce the 1/3 exponent regardless of data structure, as confirmed by experiments adding power-law covariances to the toy model (Section D.6) where the exponent remains 1/3 across α* and α_x from 0 to 2.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of neural scaling law theories that attribute power-law loss decay to power-law structures in data (data manifold dimension, feature frequency spectra, covariance eigenvalue decay). While prior dynamic-view works (Bordelon et al., Michaud et al., Paquette et al.) studied training dynamics, they either used MSE loss or applied high-level arguments without analyzing the role of non-linearity. Here, the authors show that the combination of softmax (Boltzmann distribution) and cross-entropy loss is itself sufficient to generate power-law loss and gradient decay when learning peaked distributions, independent of data structure. The analysis borrows the language and tools of statistical physics (free energy, partition function, low-temperature expansion, universality classes) in a novel application to neural network training dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides a mechanistic, architecture-level explanation for the empirically observed ~1/3 exponent in LLM loss scaling, shifting the explanatory burden from data structure to the loss function and activation non-linearity. Practical implications include guidance on avoiding large weight decay (which impedes the norm growth needed for peaked distributions), designing optimizers that accelerate parameter rotation, and potentially using token-hierarchy architectures to escape the low-temperature regime entirely. Key limitations include the reliance on gradient flow (no gradient noise analysis), the inability of the aligned student ansatz to explain rotation-driven 1/3 scaling, and the fact that LLM evidence comes from checkpoint evaluation rather than controlled training runs. Open questions include whether the universality class boundary (c₂ = 0) is physically relevant for real language data, and how the mechanism extends to deeper, multi-layer architectures.
