## Problem

Deploying pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models on specific robot hardware requires real-world adaptation, but demonstrations are expensive, limiting budgets to tens or hundreds of trajectories. The paper identifies a 'diversity trap': the standard heuristic of maximizing coverage by collecting diverse single-shot demonstrations is self-defeating under tight budgets because non-vanishing estimation noise destabilizes the learned policy. The key question is how to optimally allocate a fixed demonstration budget between coverage (number of distinct conditions) and density (repeats per condition).

## Input / Output

The system consumes multi-view RGB observations from a front and rear-side RealSense D435 plus a wrist-mounted D405, a natural-language task instruction, and the robot's proprioceptive state (7-DoF Franka Panda arm). The flow-matching policy outputs a continuous action trajectory over a 16-step horizon, parameterized as a time-dependent vector field over position, orientation, and gripper state (7 dimensions total). During boundary mining, the teacher-forced deviation score is computed as the mean L1 norm between predicted and demonstrated actions across the 7-dimensional action space, serving as a per-condition risk proxy.

## Method

Anchor-Centric Adaptation (ACA) is a two-stage framework grounded in a formal Coverage-Density Trade-off that decomposes worst-case policy error into an estimation (density) term scaling as Cσ√(K/N) and an extrapolation (coverage) term scaling as LcK^{-1/d}, proving an interior optimal anchor count K* ∝ (L²N/σ²)^{d/(d+2)}. Stage 1 concentrates ~70% of the budget on repeated teleoperated demonstrations at K sparse quasi-uniform anchors, training the Action Expert via flow matching with the VLM frozen to consolidate a low-variance policy skeleton. A boundary mining step then collects probe trajectories across the workspace and scores each candidate condition by teacher-forced deviation (mean L1 action error), selecting the top-k highest-deviation locations as high-risk boundaries. Stage 2 freezes the Stage-1 policy and introduces a zero-initialized LoRA residual branch (rank 32) inside the Action Expert, modulated by flow-time embeddings, trained only on boundary data to add localized corrections without drifting the consolidated core.

## Results

On four real-robot manipulation tasks (Block Stacking, Cup Placement, Table Cleaning, Toy Tidying) using a Franka Panda arm, ACA achieves mean success rates of 46.3%, 72.5%, and 83.8% at budgets of N=50, 100, and 150 trajectories respectively, compared to 13.8%, 31.7%, and 52.9% for the maximal-diversity baseline (π₀.5). The absolute improvement ranges from +30.9% to +40.8% across budgets. Notably, ACA at N=50 (46.3%) matches the baseline at triple the budget (N=150, 52.9%), and near-boundary success (S@3) improves from 12.5% to 65.0% at N=100.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ACA is compared against four data-collection baselines under identical conditions (π₀.5 backbone, same budget split): the naive maximal-diversity baseline, uncertainty-driven diverse sampling, DAgger-style diverse sampling, and a curriculum (core-then-boundary) strategy. ACA dominates all baselines at every budget level: at N=100, ACA achieves 72.5% mean success versus 31.7% (baseline), 35.4% (uncertainty-driven), 39.2% (DAgger-style), and 42.5% (curriculum). The gap is most dramatic in near-boundary regions (S@3): ACA reaches 65.0% while all baselines remain at or below 27.5%. All three alternative baselines inherit the diversity trap from their single-shot Stage 1, producing an unstable policy skeleton that subsequent expansion cannot rescue. The curriculum baseline, which shares ACA's core-to-boundary ordering, still lags by 30 percentage points because it lacks anchor repetition to suppress estimation noise and deviation-driven boundary selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on flow-matching VLA architectures (π₀, π₀.5) and the broader VLA post-training literature, but departs from the dominant 'maximize coverage' heuristic by formalizing a Coverage-Density Trade-off rooted in nonparametric regression theory (fill distance, Lipschitz smoothness). It borrows LoRA from the LLM parameter-efficient fine-tuning literature and repurposes teacher-forced deviation as an active-learning-style diagnostic for boundary identification. The theoretical framing connects to optimal experimental design and minimax nonparametric rates, while the practical pipeline synthesizes ideas from error-driven data collection and constrained residual adaptation in a way that prior VLA adaptation work (which is largely agnostic to acquisition strategy) does not.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'diversity trap' insight is practically critical for robotics teams operating under tight demonstration budgets (tens to hundreds of trajectories), where the default diverse-sampling strategy can be actively harmful. The two-stage ACA framework is architecture-agnostic for flow-based VLAs and provides a concrete, low-overhead protocol (anchor repetition → error-driven boundary mining → LoRA residual) that practitioners can adopt immediately. Limitations include evaluation on only four tabletop tasks with a single Franka Panda arm, a theoretical analysis that relies on a conservative nearest-anchor surrogate rather than tight neural-network generalization bounds, and the open question of whether the inverted-U optimum and budget partitioning transfer to long-horizon, multi-contact, or multi-embodiment settings.
