## Problem

Sequential hypothesis testing typically requires all data to be labeled, yet in many modern applications (e.g., LLM evaluation) obtaining labels Y is expensive while covariates X are abundant. Existing semi-supervised approaches like prediction-powered inference (PPI) only gain power when the number of unlabeled samples is large and the predictive model is accurate. This paper asks how unlabeled X-samples can be leveraged to enhance the power of sequential tests even when N is small and predictions are inaccurate, while preserving anytime-valid type-I error control.

## Input / Output

At each sequential step t, the method consumes a batch of n i.i.d. labeled (X, Y) pairs, N i.i.d. unlabeled X samples, and M independently drawn null datasets (each containing n labeled and N unlabeled samples). It produces a scalar imputed e-statistic per step, which is multiplied into a cumulative e-process S_t; the test rejects H0 the first time S_t exceeds 1/α. The score function K(·) (e.g., sum of exp(γ·ŷ_i)) is the only tunable component, and its hyperparameter γ can be updated online via AdaGrad while respecting the filtration.

## Method

The core construction is the imputed e-statistic: at each step, a learning algorithm A is fit on the fresh labeled batch D_t, its predictions are applied to the unlabeled X̃_t, and the resulting score K(A[D_t, X̃_t]) is normalized by the average score over M null-sampled datasets, yielding a soft-rank e-value. Under label shift (P_{X|Y} fixed) or concept shift (P_X fixed), the paper proves that rejecting exchangeability between the observed data and null samples is equivalent to rejecting H0, so the e-statistic is valid regardless of the accuracy of A. The sequential version multiplies conditional e-statistics into an e-process, giving anytime-valid type-I error control via Ville's inequality. For binary X and Y, the paper proves non-trivial power (E_{H1}[e] > 1) using first-order stochastic dominance arguments: under the alternative, the sum of predicted labels stochastically dominates its null counterpart, and the monotone score K amplifies this shift. A robustness theorem further shows that using an estimated null distribution inflates the type-I error by at most the total-variation distance between the true and estimated null, which is bounded with high probability for binary variables.

## Results

In label-shift simulations with low X–Y correlation (0.3) and few unlabeled samples (N=30), the imputed e-process achieves significantly higher rejection power than all individual baselines (e_LR^Y, e_LR^X, e_PPI) across most timesteps, and the convex combination including the imputed process outperforms the combination of baselines alone. Under concept-shift (N=135), the imputed e-process emerges as the strongest single method regardless of correlation level (0.3 or 0.7). In the LLM evaluation on math benchmarks (GSM8K, AQUA-RAT) with low correlation (0.2) between dataset identity and model accuracy, the method still yields higher power than the strongest baseline. On the California census application with multi-dimensional mixed-type features and TabPFN predictions (n=50, N=100, M=2), the imputed e-process outperforms both PPI and the likelihood ratio test, and combining it with those baselines yields a further significant power increase.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to the sequential likelihood ratio e-process (Wasserman et al., 2020), the imputed e-process wins decisively in challenging settings: when X–Y correlation is low (0.3) and unlabeled samples are scarce (N=30), the imputed process (red curve in Fig. 1a) substantially exceeds the power of the strongest single baseline e_LR^X (green curve). When correlation is high and N is large (Fig. 1d), e_LR^X rapidly reaches power 1 and the imputed process performs on par rather than exceeding it. Under concept-shift, the imputed e-process is the only test that bets on unlabeled data against the conditional null Y|X, making it the strongest single method in both low- and high-correlation settings (Fig. 2). Compared to Prediction-Powered Inference (Angelopoulos et al., 2023), the key distinction is that PPI's variance reduction is most effective when N is large and the predictive model is accurate, whereas the imputed e-process retains non-trivial power even with small N and inaccurate predictions. In the California census experiment (Fig. 4), the imputed e-process outperforms PPI as a standalone method, and the combined process conv(e_LR^Y, e_PPI, check_e_t) achieves a clear power advantage over conv(e_LR^Y, e_PPI) alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the testing-by-betting framework (Shafer, 2021) and soft-rank e-value construction (Ramdas & Wang, 2025) to a semi-supervised sequential setting, where bets are placed on predictions assigned to unlabeled data rather than on labeled observations directly. It departs from prediction-powered inference (Angelopoulos et al., 2023), which also leverages unlabeled data but requires large N and accurate models for variance reduction, by showing that even small N and inaccurate predictions yield non-trivial power. The identifiability conditions (label shift / concept shift) that permit inference about Y or Y|X from joint (X,Y) data are the key conceptual bridge, building on the null-sampling construction of Grünwald et al. (2024) for conditional independence testing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important asymmetry in modern ML evaluation (e.g., LLM benchmarking) where covariates are cheap but labels are expensive, providing anytime-valid sequential tests that extract signal from abundant unlabeled data. A notable open question is the lack of a general upper bound on the total-variation inflation when the null distribution is approximated, though a non-trivial bound is given for the binary case. Future work on growth-rate and expected-stopping-time analysis would provide practical guidance for choosing the learning algorithm, number of null samples M, and score function K. The requirement to refit the predictive model on each batch limits scalability to complex settings, though in-context learning with foundation models (demonstrated with TabPFN) partially alleviates this.
