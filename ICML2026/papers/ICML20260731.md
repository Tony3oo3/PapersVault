## Problem

Existing multimodal representation learning methods face a fundamental trade-off: contrastive learning aligns only shared cross-modal information and discards task-relevant modality-unique cues, while InfoMax-style methods preserve all input information but retain substantial task-irrelevant noise. The paper argues these failures stem not merely from suboptimal objectives but from a lack of structural inductive bias—collapsing heterogeneous multimodal signals into a single monolithic embedding prevents adaptive, task-specific information selection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260731/cropped_page13_idx6.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes paired multimodal video clips (text transcript, audio, and visual frames) from MultiBench datasets and produces a structured latent representation decomposed across expert-specific semantic subspaces, which is then used for linear-probing classification (sentiment, humor, sarcasm). Each modality is encoded by a separate MoE-based Transformer encoder where the FFN is replaced by N_expert = χ·ρ experts; the top-k routing (k = χ) ensures active parameters match a dense FFN of equivalent size for fair comparison. At inference, a preservation ratio p ∈ [0,1] controls how many routing paths are retained, enabling a continuous sparsity-performance trade-off without retraining.

## Method

S3 is a three-stage MoE-based framework. (1) Specialization pretrains modality-specific MoE encoders using a composite InfoNCE objective—L_rep maximizes intra-modal mutual information, L_dsc enforces Distributional Semantic Coherence (concept-wise cross-modal alignment), and L_aux regularizes balanced expert utilization—producing a latent space where each expert subspace corresponds to a distinct semantic concept. (2) Selection freezes all experts and attention modules, then fine-tunes only the lightweight router using a SupCon loss (approximating task-sufficiency via label-consistent alignment) and a von Mises-Fisher-based compactness loss (approximating information-minimality via KL divergence between instance-level and class-level distributions), so the router learns to activate task-relevant experts without modifying the latent space. (3) Sparsification prunes low-contribution routing paths at inference by retaining only the top-p fraction of routing scores, requiring no additional training and yielding a characteristic reverse U-shaped performance curve that peaks at an optimal sparsity level where task-irrelevant noise is removed but essential signals remain.

## Results

S3 achieves the best linear probing accuracy on all four MultiBench benchmarks: 77.95% on MOSEI, 66.13% on MOSI, 64.87% on UR-FUNNY, and 62.56% on MUSTARD (all at χ=8, averaged over three seeds). The gains are progressive across the three stages—Specialization → Selection → Sparsification—with the largest improvement coming from the inference-time pruning step (e.g., +0.59 pp on MOSEI, +1.84 pp on MOSI from Selection to Sparsification). The method consistently outperforms the strongest baseline, DisentangledSSL, by 0.5–1.0 pp across all benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

S3 outperforms all five baseline families on every benchmark. Against the strongest competitor, DisentangledSSL (an InfoMax-based method), S3 leads by +0.50 pp on MOSEI (77.95 vs 77.45), +0.97 pp on MOSI (66.13 vs 65.16), +0.63 pp on UR-FUNNY (64.87 vs 64.24), and +0.96 pp on MUSTARD (62.56 vs 61.60). The contrastive baseline CLIP trails S3 by 1.08 pp on MOSEI and 2.11 pp on MOSI, while the augmentation-driven FactorCL variants underperform most notably on MOSI (FactorCL-proj: 56.02% vs S3's 66.13%, a 10.11 pp gap). JointOpt is competitive on MOSI (65.02%) but falls further behind on MUSTARD (57.73% vs 62.56%). The paper attributes S3's consistent advantage to its structural decomposition into semantic experts combined with task-driven routing and inference-time pruning, which jointly satisfy Task-Sufficiency and Information-Minimality in a way that neither shared-only contrastive alignment nor indiscriminate InfoMax retention can achieve.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Mixture-of-Experts architecture (Shazeer et al., 2017) from a computational-scaling tool into a structural prior for semantic decomposition in multimodal representation learning, drawing on recent reinterpretations of MoE experts as monosemantic units (Park et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). It extends the InfoMin principle (Tian et al., 2020b) from the unimodal to the multimodal setting, formalizing Task-Sufficiency and Information-Minimality as joint criteria that neither contrastive learning (which discards modality-unique factors) nor InfoMax methods (which retain task-irrelevant noise) can satisfy simultaneously. The three-stage pipeline—pretraining experts for semantic specialization, router-only fine-tuning for task selection, and inference-time pruning for sparsification—departs from prior work by freezing the entire encoder backbone during adaptation and treating the router as an information-theoretic bottleneck rather than a load-balancing mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

S3 offers a principled, structurally grounded alternative to the dominant contrastive and InfoMax paradigms in MMRL, with the practical advantage that task adaptation requires training only the lightweight router and sparsification needs no retraining. The consistent reverse U-shaped sparsity-performance trend across four benchmarks provides an interpretable diagnostic for representation quality. However, evaluation is limited to four MultiBench sentiment/humor/sarcasm datasets with text+audio+video modalities, and the DSC constraint is only approximately enforced through instance-level InfoNCE rather than explicit concept-level alignment. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to larger-scale or more diverse multimodal tasks, how to handle modality-adaptive information preservation, and whether self-supervised routing can reduce dependence on class labels.
