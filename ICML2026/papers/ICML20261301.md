## Problem

Detecting common hub variables across multiple Gaussian Graphical Models (GGMs) remains largely unexplored, especially when sub-populations contain both shared and individual hubs with non-sparse support differences. Existing joint GGM estimation methods require sparse differences between graphs, which fails when individual hubs are comparable to or stronger than common hubs, while separate estimation followed by intersection suffers from accumulated failure rates.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261301/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes sample covariance (or correlation) matrices from K sub-populations, each of dimension p×p, along with a pre-specified eigenspace dimension ŝ (recommended as ⌊√p⌋) and a threshold κ (data-driven, set to mean + 2 SD of influence measures). It outputs a binary common hub set Ĵ(κ) ⊆ {1,…,p} and continuous joint minimax influence measures ω̂ŝ(i) for each variable i, where high values indicate common hub status. The optimization is solved on the Stiefel manifold St(p,ŝ), requiring orthonormal column constraints.

## Method

JIC-HD introduces a minimax joint eigenspace defined by minimizing max_k tr(V^T Σ^(k) V) over orthonormal V ∈ St(p,s), which captures the shared low-rank structure induced by common hubs while remaining robust to individual hubs that would dilute signal under additive pooling. The non-convex optimization over the Stiefel manifold is solved via manifold gradient descent with tangent-space projection and retraction (metric projection or exponential map). The key insight is that common hubs produce a shared spiked eigenspace across precision matrices, and the max-aggregation of explained variance isolates this common component without requiring sparse support differences between graphs. Theoretical guarantees (Theorems 1–2) establish eigenspace convergence rates and zero false-inclusion probability under eigenspace decomposability assumptions, with the threshold κ of order max{p^{-β/4}, √𝓔(n,p)} ensuring consistent recovery.

## Results

In simulation studies (r=5 common hubs, p_C=0.4, p∈{100,200,400}, K=3 sub-populations, 100 replicates), JIC-HD achieves the highest mean F-score across all settings of p_I∈{0.3,0.5} and sample sizes n. The advantage is most pronounced when individual hubs have a stronger signal than common hubs (p_I=0.5 > p_C=0.4), where competing methods struggle to recover common hubs. On TCGA lung cancer gene-expression data (p=426 genes, n_AD=540 LUAD, n_SC=511 LUSC), JIC-HD identifies SFTPA1 and SFTPA2 as common hub genes, consistent with known biological roles in immune response and lung cancer prognosis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

JIC-HD outperforms all three baselines (GLASSO, HWGLASSO, and IPC-HD) in mean F-score for common hub detection across every simulation scenario tested (Figure 4). The gap is largest when individual hubs are stronger than common hubs (p_I=0.5 vs. p_C=0.4), where GLASSO and HWGLASSO (which estimate each graph separately and intersect hub sets) suffer from accumulated false negatives, and IPC-HD (single-graph spectral method applied per population) similarly fails to recover weaker common hubs. HWGLASSO is the closest competitor on the real TCGA data, also detecting SFTPA1 and SFTPA2, but JIC-HD's joint minimax influence measures provide a cleaner separation between common hubs and individual/non-hub variables. The paper also reports that JIC-HD is competitive or superior in precision, recall, TPR, FPR, and computational time (Appendix D), with the key advantage being robustness to non-sparse support differences across GGMs that violate the pairwise-similarity assumptions required by joint GGM estimation methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the single-GGM spectral hub detection framework of Sánchez Gómez et al. (2025, IPC-HD) to the multi-population setting by introducing a minimax eigenspace formulation. It departs from joint GGM estimation methods (GLASSO, HWGLASSO, joint graphical LASSO, transfer learning) that require sparse support differences or pairwise similarity across graphs, instead bypassing full graph estimation entirely. The minimax (max-aggregation) objective draws conceptual parallels to maximin effects in supervised learning (Meinshausen & Bühlmann, 2015; Guo, 2024) and fair PCA (Samadi et al., 2018), but applies them to the spectral structure of precision matrices for the first time in the hub-detection context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method provides a principled way to identify common hub variables across heterogeneous sub-populations without the restrictive assumption that graphs differ only sparsely, which is critical in settings like cancer genomics where individual subtypes may have stronger hub signals than shared ones. The theoretical guarantee of zero false inclusions (with vanishing threshold) is a clean result, though perfect recovery requires additional pairwise-similarity conditions. Open questions include the non-convex optimization landscape of the minimax eigenspace problem, extension to non-Gaussian or directed graphical models, and whether the eigenspace decomposability assumption (A3) holds broadly in practice beyond the simulated and TCGA settings.
