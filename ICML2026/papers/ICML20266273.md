## Problem

In Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) training, the number of activated experts per token (top-K routing parameter K) is typically chosen heuristically without theoretical justification. No prior work provides a convergence analysis that characterizes how routing sparsity K interacts with convergence speed and computational cost in multi-layer, large-scale MoE models, leaving practitioners without principled guidance for selecting K under different computational budgets.

## Input / Output

The analysis applies to two task families: image classification on 32×32 CIFAR-10/100 (single-layer MoE with CNN experts, batch size 256) and language generation on WikiText-103, Alpaca, Dolly, and GSM8K (8-block decoder-only Transformers with MoE FFN layers, batch size 2048 tokens). The key analytical input is the routing sparsity parameter K ∈ [1, M] and total FLOPs budget R; the output is a closed-form convergence upper bound on the average gradient norm as a function of K, T, M, and R, rather than a model prediction.

## Method

The paper derives the first convergence upper bound for multi-layer MoE training under top-K sparse routing using stochastic optimization theory. The central technical challenge is that discrete top-K routing makes the loss function discontinuous across iterations (different expert subsets are activated), which the authors handle by decomposing the one-step loss change into a smooth component (analyzed via second-order Taylor expansion with bounded Hessian assumptions) and a 'routing-induced discrepancy' term bounded by O(NΞδ(1−K/M)). Under sufficient budget, the bound scales as O((1+M/K)/√T), guaranteeing convergence and showing that larger K accelerates training with diminishing returns. Under a fixed FLOPs budget R, the refined bound O(√(K/R) + M/√(KR)) is convex in K, proving the existence of an optimal K* ∈ [1,M] that is independent of R and controlled by the ratio of initial-bias/routing-discrepancy constants to gradient-variance constants.

## Results

On WikiText-103, increasing K from 1 to 2 reduces the iterations needed to reach a loss of 1.71 from 10,000 to 3,200 (a 3.125× speedup), while further increasing K from 2 to 4 yields diminishing returns (loss drops from 0.93 to 0.83 after 10,000 iterations, a reduction of only 0.10 vs. 0.78 for K=1→2). Under a fixed FLOPs budget R, convergence performance first improves then degrades as K increases, confirming the existence of an optimal K* ∈ [1, M]. Empirically, K* is much smaller than the total number of experts (e.g., K*=2 ≪ M=16 on WikiText-103 and Alpaca), consistent with the theoretical prediction that K* is small when the gradient variance term Z₂ dominates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that validates its convergence bounds through ablation-style experiments varying the routing sparsity parameter K, rather than comparing against competing MoE training methods. The key comparisons are between different K values (K=1, 2, 4, …, M) on the same model and dataset. With sufficient computational budget, larger K consistently accelerates convergence but with diminishing marginal gains, matching the O((1+M/K)/√T) bound. Under a fixed FLOPs budget, the convex trade-off between K and R produces a clear optimum K* that is far below M, aligning with the practical choice of K=1 or 2 in systems like GShard, Switch Transformer, and Skywork-MoE. The loss gap between sparse and dense routing (Theorem 1) is empirically confirmed to decrease with K and stabilize over training, though the gap at K=1 can be small when routing is relatively invariant.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of MoE convergence analysis initiated by Chen et al. (2022) and the Nguyen et al. (2024a/b/c) series, all of which studied single-layer, small-scale MoE models without characterizing the effect of routing sparsity. It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) it analyzes multi-layer, large-parameter-scale MoE architectures using stochastic optimization theory (building on the strong growth condition of Schmidt & Roux, 2013, and non-convex SGD analysis of Foster et al., 2018), and (2) it explicitly derives how the top-K routing parameter K governs convergence rate and computational cost, a dimension entirely absent from all prior theoretical treatments. The sparsely-gated MoE architecture of Shazeer et al. (2017) is the specific routing mechanism under analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical framework that gives practitioners a principled basis for choosing K in MoE training, replacing the current heuristic practice. The result that an optimal K* exists under fixed compute budget, is convex in K, and is independent of the budget R provides a clean design principle; the finding that K* ≪ M (e.g., K*=2 vs. M=16) retroactively justifies the empirical choice of K=1 or 2 in systems like GShard and Switch Transformer. Key limitations include reliance on several strong assumptions (bounded Hessian norms, Lipschitz continuity of loss w.r.t. routing changes), validation only on relatively small models (8 Transformer blocks, single-layer CNN MoE), and the open question of how K* scales with M in truly large-scale settings, which the authors acknowledge as future work.
