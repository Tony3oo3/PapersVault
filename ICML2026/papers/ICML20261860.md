## Problem

Post-training LLMs for non-verifiable domains (healthcare guidance, creative writing, open dialogue) lacks a clear learning signal: no ground-truth labels exist, no programmatic checker can verify correctness, and coarse LLM-as-judge rewards suffer from inconsistency, verbosity bias, and reward hacking. The paper asks whether inference-time compute from parallel rollouts can itself serve as reference-free supervision for RL training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261860/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt q and generates G=8 parallel rollouts from the current policy. For non-verifiable domains, a frozen anchor model synthesizes a pseudo-reference from the rollouts, then generates 5+ binary rubric criteria from that pseudo-reference; an independent judge (GPT-4o) scores each rollout against each criterion, yielding a fractional reward. For verifiable domains, reward is simply answer-string matching against the pseudo-reference. The trained policy outputs a single text response, matching or exceeding the quality of inference-time synthesis while using 9× less test-time compute.

## Method

CaT has two components: (1) reference estimation, where a frozen anchor π₀ conditions on G rollouts and synthesizes a unified pseudo-reference s that reconciles complementary fragments across rollouts (going beyond selection methods that can only pick one existing answer); and (2) reward derivation, which for non-verifiable domains introduces self-proposed rubrics—binary, auditable criteria generated from the pseudo-reference and scored independently by an LLM judge, with reward equal to the fraction of criteria satisfied. Training uses GRPO, reusing the G parallel rollouts already sampled for advantage estimation, so the synthesis and rubric-scoring steps add only modest overhead. The key insight is that rollouts sample the model's uncertainty (disagreeing precisely where the model hedges), synthesis forces structured reconciliation of that disagreement, and rubric decomposition converts a fuzzy 'is this good?' judgment into a sum of fine-grained binary checks, enabling stable reference-free RL without any human annotation.

## Results

On HealthBench (non-verifiable), CaT improves over the initial policy by up to +30% relative (Llama 3.1 8B: 0.29 → 0.38) and consistently outperforms inference-time synthesis (e.g., Gemma 3 4B: 0.43 vs 0.39) while using 9× less test-time compute. On MATH-500 (verifiable), CaT achieves up to +33% relative improvement (Llama 3.1 8B: 0.42 → 0.56) and matches or slightly exceeds inference-time synthesis across all three models. Self-proposed rubrics for reward derivation match physician-annotated rubrics on 2 of 3 models and substantially outperform direct model-as-judge scoring (e.g., Llama: 0.38 vs 0.18).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against inference-time selection baselines (Table 5), synthesis outperforms min-perplexity (Agarwal et al.), mutual predictability (Wen et al.), and self-best-of-N on HealthBench for all three models (e.g., Qwen: 0.44 vs 0.36/0.38/0.40), and matches or exceeds majority vote (Wang et al.) on MATH-500 (e.g., Qwen: 0.86 vs 0.81). With RL training (Table 6), CaT with synthesis outperforms Majority Vote-RL (Zuo et al., TTRL) on MATH-500 (0.56 vs 0.53) and min(PPL)-RL (Agarwal et al.) on both HealthBench (0.38 vs 0.33) and MATH-500 (0.56 vs 0.51). For reward derivation in non-verifiable domains (Table 3), self-proposed rubrics outperform direct model-as-judge scoring (Zheng et al.) on all three models (e.g., Llama: 0.38 vs 0.18) and match physician-annotated rubrics on Gemma and Qwen (0.43 vs 0.43) while slightly trailing on Llama (0.38 vs 0.40). CaT with RL also outperforms SFT on synthesized pseudo-references across all models (e.g., Llama: 0.38 vs 0.28), confirming that RL generalizes better than supervised fine-tuning from the same reference-free targets.

## Relation to Prior Work

CaT sits at the intersection of self-training (learning from model-generated supervision), test-time scaling (majority vote, best-of-N), and reference-free RL (TTRL, Absolute Zero). It builds directly on GRPO as the RL backbone and on Rubrics as Rewards (Gunjal et al., 2026) for the reward mechanism, but removes the reference-answer dependency by self-proposing rubrics from pseudo-references estimated via synthesis. The key conceptual departure from prior reference-free RL is extending from verifiable domains (math, code) to non-verifiable ones (healthcare guidance), and from paying G× inference cost at deployment to amortizing that compute into model weights via RL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CaT provides a practical, drop-in framework for post-training LLMs in domains where ground truth is scarce, contested, or nonexistent—addressing a real bottleneck as human annotation becomes infeasible for specialized tasks. The finding that self-proposed rubrics from a 4B model match physician-annotated rubrics suggests a path toward scalable, domain-agnostic reward design. Key limitations include the entropy-collapse ceiling (rollout diversity shrinks as the policy improves, bounding further gains) and dependence on judge model quality. Open directions include learned aggregators, hierarchical or partial-credit rubrics, and extension to multi-turn or agentic settings.
