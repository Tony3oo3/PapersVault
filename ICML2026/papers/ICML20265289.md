## Problem

Existing RDB foundation models either require per-dataset fine-tuning or rely on dense parameterized encoders that mix cross-column information, which is suboptimal for in-context learning where column roles are task-dependent and cannot be determined without labels. The paper asks whether a parameter-free, column-preserving encoder can match or exceed dense encoders in ICL settings, and provides both theoretical and empirical evidence that it can.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265289/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multi-table relational database (tables with PK-FK relations), a set of labeled ICL samples from the target table, and unlabeled test query rows, producing class labels or regression values via a single forward pass of a pre-trained single-table ICL decoder (e.g., TabPFN or LimiX). No training, fine-tuning, or hyperparameter tuning is required at any stage; the encoder is entirely parameter-free and implemented as SQL queries over DuckDB, with temporal cutoff constraints compiled into the execution plan to prevent leakage.

## Method

The core contribution is the JUICE (Just Use Intra Column Encodings) encoder, which compresses RDB subgraphs only vertically within individual columns (where shared units enable interpretable aggregation) rather than horizontally across columns (where relevance is indeterminate without labels). Concretely, each H-hop subgraph is split into 1D column-wise subgraphs, and a meta-path GNN with permutation-invariant aggregation (sum, mean, mode, min, max, std) propagates 1D node embeddings along each meta-path independently; Proposition 3.2 shows that for positively homogeneous activations and aggregations, all internal weights can be eliminated without loss of expressiveness. The resulting column-aligned feature matrix is then fed directly to an existing single-table ICL foundation model as the decoder, which uses labeled ICL samples to arbitrate which columns and meta-paths are predictive. The paper further proves (Propositions 4.2–4.3) that cross-column dense encoders provably increase estimation error and degrade sample complexity from O(n^{-2/κ}) to Θ(n^{-2/d}) in OOD ICL regimes, providing the theoretical justification for the vertical-only design.

## Results

On RelBench-v1 classification (8 tasks, AUC), RDBLearn achieves a mean of 74.81, outperforming all untrained RDB foundation models (KumoRFM 72.72, RandGNN 70.02, RT 69.69) and approaching the fully supervised RelGT (75.34) with no per-dataset training. On RelBench-v2, RDBLearn reaches 97.81 AUC on user-churn (vs. 94.27 for the best supervised GNN) and 7.231 MAE on user-count regression (vs. 7.374 for the supervised GNN). On 4DBInfer, RDBLearn matches or exceeds the best supervised GNN baselines on 4 of 5 classification tasks without any training or hyperparameter tuning, with total inference latency on the order of 10² seconds versus 10⁵–10⁶ seconds for supervised baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RDBLearn (mean AUC 74.81) outperforms all untrained RDB foundation models on RelBench-v1: it beats KumoRFM (72.72) by +2.1 AUC, RandGNN (70.02) by +4.8, RT (69.69) by +5.1, Griffin (66.68) by +8.1, RelLLM (61.15) by +13.7, LLM-B (65.79) by +9.0, and LLM-A/Gemma 3 (64.74) by +10.1. The only model that edges out RDBLearn is the fully supervised RelGT (75.34, +0.5 AUC), which requires per-dataset training with the same dense encoder as KumoRFM. On RelBench-v2, RDBLearn exceeds the best supervised GNN on 2 of 3 classification tasks (97.81 vs. 94.27 on user-churn; 84.92 vs. 78.67 on beer-churn) and 1 of 2 regression tasks (7.231 vs. 7.374 MAE on user-count). On 4DBInfer, RDBLearn matches or beats the best supervised GNN on 4 of 5 tasks (e.g., 0.8796 vs. 0.867 AUC on StackExchange user-churn; 0.8469 vs. 0.8495 on RetailRocket CVR) while requiring orders of magnitude less compute. The RandGNN baseline (randomly-initialized GNN + same ICL decoder) confirms that the JUICE column-preserving design, not merely the use of a strong single-table model, drives the performance gap: RDBLearn is uniformly better than RandGNN across all benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in RDB predictive modeling, which pairs expressive parameterized GNN/Transformer encoders with supervised training heads (RelGT, KumoRFM, Griffin, RT). It also builds on the emerging line of single-table ICL foundation models (TabPFN, LimiX, TabICL) that handle new tasks without retraining, but extends them to multi-table relational settings. The key conceptual departure is repositioning parameter-free column-wise featurization (previously dismissed as an outdated propositionalization heuristic in the supervised era) as the *correct* encoder design for ICL, supported by formal proofs that cross-column dense compression provably increases estimation error when column roles are dataset-dependent. The meta-path GNN implementation draws on heterogeneous graph literature (Ferrini et al., 2024) but restricts propagation to 1D column-aligned subgraphs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical justification for why parameter-free RDB encoding is not merely a simplification but a necessity in ICL settings, with Propositions 4.2–4.3 showing that fixed dense encoders provably degrade sample complexity from O(n^{-2/κ}) to O(n^{-2/d}). Practically, RDBLearn offers a zero-training, SQL-based pipeline that matches or exceeds the closed-source KumoRFM (trained on undisclosed real-world data) across RelBench-v1, v2, and 4DBInfer, making RDB foundation modeling accessible without GPU-intensive pre-training. Open questions include: the current exclusion of text features, the assumption that no external schema metadata is available, and whether the JUICE principle generalizes to settings where the ICL decoder itself is also being trained (as in schema-agnostic models). The performance inversion between SL and ICL regimes (Figure 6) suggests a broader lesson for any foundation model architecture that pairs a fixed front-end with a flexible back-end.
