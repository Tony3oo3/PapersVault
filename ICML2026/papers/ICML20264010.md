## Problem

Sequential hypothesis testing for finite-state Markov chains has been studied primarily for simple (singleton) hypotheses or under i.i.d. assumptions, leaving the composite-versus-composite setting without a non-asymptotic, instance-dependent characterization of optimal stopping time. Prior lower bounds in the Markovian setting are either purely asymptotic or provably suboptimal because they ignore the fixed state-visitation proportions dictated by the chain's stationary distribution. This paper closes that gap by establishing the first non-asymptotic instance-dependent lower bound and a matching asymptotically optimal test for one-sided, α-correct, power-one sequential testing under Markovian dependence.

## Input / Output

The procedure consumes a sequential stream of states X_1, X_2, … from an ergodic finite-state Markov chain (state space [m]), together with a prescribed null set P ⊂ M of stochastic matrices and a significance level α ∈ (0,1). It produces a data-dependent stopping time τ_α at which H_0 is rejected, a running test statistic L_t computed from empirical transition counts, and a state-visitation-count-dependent boundary β_t that adapts to the observed counts N_x(t). Uniform Type-I error control is required over all initial distributions μ ∈ Δ_m, and the test is one-sided: it either stops (rejects) or runs indefinitely under the null.

## Method

The lower bound is derived via a Markov-chain version of Wald's lemma (Moustakides, 1999), which decomposes the expected log-likelihood ratio into a stationary-weighted KL divergence D_M(Q,P)·E[τ_α] plus a Poisson-equation correction term ω_{Q,f_P}. The key technical difficulty is that this correction depends on the null matrix P, preventing a naive supremum over P; the authors resolve this by bounding the Poisson solution uniformly via the pseudo-spectral gap of Q (Proposition 3.1), yielding the instance-dependent hardness D_M^inf(Q,P) = inf_{P∈P} D_M(Q,P). For achievability, Algorithm 1 constructs a mixture martingale by taking the product over states of per-state Dirichlet-prior martingales (adapted from MDP best-policy-identification literature), producing the statistic L_t = inf_{P∈P} Σ_x N_x(t)·D_KL(Q̂_t(x,·), P(x,·)) and stopping when L_t exceeds the boundary β_t = log(1/α) + (m−1)ψ_t. A Pinsker-type inequality (Proposition 4.3) further provides a computationally tractable surrogate statistic by lower-bounding the Markov divergence via squared gaps in stationary expectations of the Poisson solution, preserving α-correctness while enabling efficient convex optimization over P.

## Results

The paper establishes a non-asymptotic instance-dependent lower bound on the expected stopping time E_Q[τ_α] ≥ (log(1/α)/D_M^inf(Q, P) − 2C_Q/π_*)⁺ (Theorem 3.3) and constructs a martingale-based test (Algorithm 1) that matches this bound to first order: limsup_{α→0} E_Q[τ_α]/log(1/α) ≤ 1/D_M^inf(Q, P) (Theorem 4.1). In the MCMC misspecification experiment (5-state chain, α=0.05), the test statistic under the misspecified kernel Q_bad grows linearly at the theoretical rate D_M^inf(Q_bad, P_π) with zero false rejections across 100 runs under the valid kernel Q_good. In the Mountain Car MDP experiment (8×8 discretized states, 3 actions, random policy), the statistic grows steadily for all feature dimensions d ∈ {3, 5, 7}, with d=3 exhibiting the steepest growth and earliest rejection of the linear MDP hypothesis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is with Fields et al. (2025), which studies one-sided sequential testing of Markov chains but under a singleton null (H_0: P = P_0 known) versus a composite alternative, and assumes uniformly bounded likelihood ratios. This paper's setting is strictly more general: composite null versus composite alternative, with no boundedness assumption on likelihood ratios. The information-theoretic characterization also differs: Fields et al. use a plug-in likelihood estimator, while this paper's hardness is governed by the stationary-weighted KL divergence D_M^inf(Q, P) = inf_{P∈P} Σ_i π_i D_KL(Q(i,·), P(i,·)), which incorporates both the stationary distribution and transition structure. The paper reports a direct experimental comparison with the Fields et al. algorithm in Section G (Mountain Car), though specific numerical differences are deferred to the appendix. The key theoretical advantage is that this paper provides non-asymptotic instance-dependent lower bounds (not merely asymptotic) and proves the test is asymptotically optimal without requiring the bounded-likelihood-ratio assumption that Fields et al. impose.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sequential hypothesis testing framework from Fields et al. (2025), which handles a singleton null against a composite alternative for Markov chains, to the substantially harder composite-null-versus-composite-alternative setting. The lower bound proof builds on Moustakides' (1999) Wald's lemma for Markov processes and Paulin's (2015) pseudo-spectral gap concentration inequalities, while the test design adapts the mixture martingale construction from the MDP best-policy-identification literature (Jonsson et al., 2020; Al Marjani et al., 2021). A key technical departure from the i.i.d. analysis of Agrawal & Ramdas (2025) is the need to control a Poisson-equation correction term that depends on the null hypothesis P, which prevents the naive supremum-over-P argument used in the independent case.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first non-asymptotic, instance-dependent lower bound for sequential testing under Markovian dependence with composite hypotheses, closing a gap where prior results were either asymptotic or provably suboptimal. The Pinsker-type inequality (Proposition 4.3) relating stationary-weighted KL divergence to squared gaps in stationary expectations is a reusable tool with potential applications beyond this specific testing problem. Open questions include the statistical-computational tradeoff for non-convex null sets, extension to countable-state chains, and the harder problem of hidden Markov observations where the observed process is not itself Markov. The empirical validation is limited to two small-scale experiments (5-state MCMC, 64-state Mountain Car), leaving the practical scalability of the convex optimization in the test statistic unexplored.
