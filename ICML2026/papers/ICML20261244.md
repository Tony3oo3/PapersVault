## Problem

Existing protein fitness benchmarks evaluate static prediction over pooled DMS data, but agentic protein-engineering workflows require models to prioritize variants that will only be measured in *future* wet-lab rounds. No public benchmark preserves the chronological structure of a multi-round directed-evolution campaign to test whether representations generalize from earlier to later experimental evidence, leaving a critical gap between interpolation performance and the past-to-future ranking that iterative engineering actually demands.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261244/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes biological sequences (DNA, RNA with T→U mapping, or protein) from earlier PANCE rounds (1–27) and requires the model to predict continuous relative activity scores for sequence-disjoint variants appearing only in later rounds (29–31). The DNA/RNA views contain ~729K training and ~150K test sequences; the protein view collapses synonymous codons to ~256K training and ~108K test sequences. Activity labels are end-to-end cellular performance scores (not isolated kcat), constructed by Seq2Graph from noisy multi-round NGS enrichment rather than absolute cross-round normalization, and the evaluation metrics (Spearman ρ, Recall@10%, nDCG@10%) emphasize top-decile ranking quality relevant to finite-budget wet-lab follow-up.

## Method

Seq2Graph is a graph-based label-unification pipeline that reconciles noisy multi-round enrichment into a single consistent activity scale. It builds a sparse directed graph where each DNA sequence is a node and edges connect only adjacent variants in each round's sorted enrichment list (capped at 100K edges per round), with edge weights as local enrichment ratios; cross-round sequence overlaps serve as anchors linking the 31 local ranking subgraphs. A weighted Feedback Arc Set step (greedy heuristic within strongly connected components) removes contradictory cycles to yield a DAG, after which activity is anchored at TadA8e (=1.0) and propagated in the log domain along fewest-edge paths to minimize accumulated comparison noise. For evaluation, the paper freezes pre-trained biological language model encoders (ESM2, ESMC, ProtTrans, Evo 2, Nucleotide Transformer, OmniGenome) and trains a shared two-layer MLP regression head, then contrasts the fixed future-round split against a random 8:1:1 split to isolate chronological generalization from mere interpolation.

## Results

On the fixed future-round split (train rounds 1–27, test rounds 29–31), all biological language models exhibit very weak ranking: the best protein-view Spearman ρ is 0.0509 (ESMC-600M), the best DNA-view ρ is 0.0707 (Evo2-7B), and the best RNA-view ρ is 0.0079 (OG-46M). Under an 8:1:1 random split on the same protein view, the same models reach ρ ≈ 0.80–0.81 (e.g., ESMC-600M 0.8079, Prot-XLNET 0.8030), confirming the label space is learnable under interpolation. In discovery-mode finite-budget selection (K = 96 or 384 candidates), no model recovers any true top-1% future-round variant, and top-5% hit rates (3.1–4.9%) are near the 5% random expectation. Targeted adaptation (full fine-tuning, prompt tuning) does not close the gap, with best observed test ρ of 0.075 (NT-50M prompt tuning).

## Baselines & Comparisons

All six model families fail on the future-round split, with test Spearman ρ below 0.07 across DNA, RNA, and protein views, yet the same models achieve ρ ≈ 0.80 on a random 8:1:1 split of the protein view, demonstrating that the gap is specific to chronological generalization rather than label quality. Among protein models, ESMC-600M achieves the highest future-round Spearman (0.0509) and Recall@10% (0.1180), marginally ahead of ESM2-650M (0.0479) and ESMC-300M (0.0355); Prot-BERT and Prot-XLNET trail at 0.0214 and 0.0342 respectively. On the DNA view, Evo2-7B (ρ = 0.0707) and Evo2-40B (ρ = 0.0675) outperform all Nucleotide Transformer variants (ρ ≤ 0.019), while on the RNA view OmniGenome models are weakest (ρ ≤ 0.008). The random-split contrast is most dramatic for ESMC-600M (0.8079 random vs. 0.0509 future-round) and Prot-XLNET (0.8030 vs. 0.0342), a >15× drop in Spearman that no model family avoids. In finite-budget selection, ESM2-35M and ESMC-300M both recover zero true top-1% variants at K = 96 and K = 384, with top-5% hit rates of 3.1%/4.3% and 4.9%/4.2% respectively—indistinguishable from the 5% random baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

TadA-Bench extends the protein fitness-prediction benchmarking line (ProteinGym, FLIP, ProteinBench) by replacing breadth-first coverage of many protein families with depth-first replay of a single 31-round directed-evolution campaign, introducing a chronological train/test split that prior DMS benchmarks do not impose. The Seq2Graph label-unification pipeline repurposes the weighted Feedback Arc Set heuristic (Eades et al., 1993) as a practical consistency-cleaning step for noisy multi-round enrichment data, and the wet-lab data originate from the PANCE phage-assisted selection protocol (Miller et al., 2020). Unlike fragmented base-editing datasets that focus on Cas–sgRNA interactions, TadA-Bench centers on the deaminase variant space itself with aligned DNA/RNA/protein views, positioning itself as a fixed-data substrate for the ranking module in agentic protein-engineering workflows rather than a broad-coverage fitness landscape resource.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a concrete, reproducible gap: biological language models achieve ρ≈0.8 under random-split interpolation yet drop to ρ≈0.05–0.1 on future-round ranking, and this gap persists across DNA, RNA, and protein modalities as well as under full fine-tuning and prompt tuning. The finding that evolutionary coverage outperforms local data density at matched sizes suggests that campaign structure should be preserved when constructing training sets for protein discovery, rather than treating all variants as equally informative. Key limitations include the single-protein (TadA), single-assay scope and the fixed-candidate design, which means the results do not yet generalize to other directed-evolution campaigns or to open-ended variant proposal; extending the future-round replay protocol to additional proteins and assays is the natural next step.
