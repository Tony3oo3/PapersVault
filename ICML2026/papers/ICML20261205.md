## Problem

Prior work demonstrates that semantically relevant yet misleading documents degrade long-context LLM performance, but the quantitative relationship between the proportion of such hard distractors and accuracy has remained unexplored. This paper asks how performance changes as the hard-distractor fraction grows in long contexts and identifies a striking nonlinear pattern it terms the 'First Drop of Ink' effect, where a small initial fraction causes disproportionate damage.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261205/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Contexts of fixed length (4K–128K tokens) are constructed by concatenating a gold passage with distractor passages of three controlled types—easy filler, random Wikipedia, and hard BM25-retrieved semantically related passages—mixed at varying hard proportions (0–100%) and randomly shuffled to eliminate positional bias. Accuracy is evaluated with gpt-4o-mini as an LLM judge to avoid string-matching false negatives, and the paper additionally extracts pre-softmax attention logit margins on identified retrieval heads to validate the theoretical model. A 'drop ratio' metric quantifies nonlinearity by measuring what fraction of total accuracy loss occurs within the first 10% of hard distractors.

## Method

The core contribution is a theoretical derivation showing that the aggregate attention weight on the gold passage is a strictly convex, monotonically decreasing function of hard-distractor proportion, obtained by decomposing the softmax denominator into contributions from gold, easy, and hard distractor tokens. The key mechanism is that hard distractors carry substantially smaller logit margins (Δ_h ≈ 2–3) than easy distractors (Δ_e ≈ 7–10), so each hard token contributes roughly 340× more to the softmax denominator than an easy token; even at 10% hard proportion, hard distractors capture ~97% of the total distractor attention mass, producing the front-loaded degradation. This convexity is validated empirically by measuring logit margins on retrieval heads (a sparse ~1–2% subset identified following Zhang et al., 2025b) across four models and four datasets. Controlled filtering experiments further show that post-hoc filtering gains arise primarily from context-length reduction rather than distractor removal, and that inference-time temperature scaling cannot mitigate the effect because the model's learned dynamics are calibrated to τ=1.

## Results

Across four models and eight dataset configurations at 128K context, the drop ratio (fraction of total accuracy loss occurring in the first 10% of hard distractors) consistently exceeds the linear baseline of 0.1, ranging from 0.35 to 1.40 (e.g., 0.79 for Llama-3.2-1B on nq_easy, 0.58 for Qwen2.5-7B on nq_easy, 0.92 for Llama-3.1-8B on hotpotqa_easy). Attention logit margin measurements on retrieval heads confirm Δe ≈ 7–10 vs. Δh ≈ 2–3 (average gap 5.83 for Llama-3.1-8B), implying each hard distractor token contributes ~340× more to the softmax denominator than an easy token; at just 10% hard proportion, hard distractors account for ~97% of total distractor attention competition. In filtering experiments, Filter Hard and Filter Random yield nearly identical gains from 131K to 47K tokens, diverging only below 47K where hard proportion approaches zero, confirming that most filtering benefit comes from context shortening rather than distractor removal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to compare against baselines; instead, it tests the linear degradation assumption (each additional hard distractor contributes equally, predicting a drop ratio of 0.1) against the empirically observed nonlinear pattern. The linear assumption is decisively rejected: at 128K context, drop ratios range from 0.35 to 1.40 across models and datasets, with the majority well above 0.1, confirming front-loaded degradation. In the filtering comparison, Filter Hard (removing hard distractors) and Filter Random (removing random distractors) produce nearly identical accuracy gains from 131K down to 47K tokens, indicating the recovery is driven by context length reduction, not by the specific type of distractor removed. The two strategies diverge only below 47K tokens, where Filter Hard reaches near-zero hard proportion and consistently outperforms Filter Random. Inference-time temperature scaling (τ = 0.9) fails to mitigate the effect, consistently degrading performance across all hard proportions, ruling out a simple softmax-sharpening fix. Proportional reduction experiments (holding hard ratio fixed at 20%, 50%, or 80% while shrinking context from 131K to 27K) show the three curves largely overlap, further confirming that in the moderate-to-high hard-proportion regime, context length dominates over composition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Needle-in-a-Haystack evaluation paradigm by introducing a new controlled axis: the proportion of semantically relevant (hard) distractors within long contexts. It builds directly on retrieval-head mechanistic analysis (Wu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b) to ground its theoretical explanation in softmax attention mechanics, and departs from prior distractor studies (Shi et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2026; Hong et al., 2025) that examined distractor effects qualitatively or in short contexts by systematically varying hard-distractor proportion at 128K tokens. The convexity argument connects the attention denominator structure to the observed front-loaded degradation, a link not previously formalized for the long-context setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ~58% of total accuracy loss occurs in the first 10% of hard distractors challenges the common assumption that incremental filtering or reranking yields proportional recovery, suggesting that upstream retrieval precision is far more critical than post-hoc context curation in long-context pipelines. The negative result on inference-time temperature scaling (performance worsens despite theoretically sharper attention) rules out a natural mitigation and underscores that the effect is baked into the model's learned dynamics. Key limitations include the restriction to multi-document QA (generalization to summarization, code, or multi-turn dialogue is untested) and the absence of an effective mitigation strategy, leaving open how to practically prevent the 'first drop' in agentic retrieval systems.
