## Problem

Massive activations and attention sinks reliably co-occur in decoder-only, pre-norm Transformers, yet prior explanations remain largely descriptive, leaving their functional roles and causal relationship unclear. The paper asks: is this co-occurrence an inherent property of Transformers or a predictable consequence of specific architectural choices, and can the two phenomena be independently controlled without degrading language-modeling performance?

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes pretrained LLM weights (Llama 2/3, Qwen 2.5/3, 7B–14B) and text sequences from C4 for forward-pass probing, while ablation experiments train a 7B Llama-style model from scratch on 100B DCLM tokens with targeted architectural modifications. Outputs are mechanistic: identification of step-up/step-down block indices, spike channels and their shared trigger direction, sink heads and their subspace geometry, plus quantitative ablation metrics (perplexity, sink ratio at ε=0.3, maximal activation magnitude) that attribute each phenomenon to specific design choices rather than producing a new predictive model.

## Method

The paper establishes a three-stage causal chain: (1) early step-up SwiGLU feed-forward blocks act as directional quadratic amplifiers whose output matrices U_k exhibit rank-one dominance, so tokens whose normalized representations align with a shared spike direction s* are amplified into massive activations that persist via the additive residual stream; (2) pre-norm RMSNorm then maps these spike tokens to sparse, near-constant vectors, confining their key projections to a 1–2 dimensional subspace of W_K's row space; (3) in heads whose query subspace aligns closer to this fixed sink-key subspace than to non-sink keys, large logit gaps produce attention sinks. Targeted ablations (Sandwich norm, QK-Norm, DynamicTanh, head dimension, conditional gating, context-length distribution) confirm that pre-norm normalization is the key architectural bridge enabling co-occurrence, that head dimension governs sink capacity, that sinks function as implicit input-conditioned gating, and that short-context training induces sinks as a mechanism to suppress irrelevant long-range attention.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that normalization configuration decouples massive activations from attention sinks: replacing pre-norm with Sandwich Norm reduces spike magnitude from 3,818 to 520 (~7× reduction) while preserving sink ratio nearly identically (44.7% vs. 46.0%) at comparable perplexity (9.8 vs. 10.1). DynamicTanh eliminates spikes almost entirely (153) yet actually *increases* the sink ratio to 61.0%, confirming sinks do not require spikes. On the sink side, head dimension is the dominant architectural driver: increasing d_head from 8 to 128 raises sink ratio monotonically from 4.1% to 46.0%, and conditional per-channel gating collapses it to 4.5%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic interpretability paper rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so there are no external baselines to beat or lose to. The paper's evidence rests on controlled ablations of a single 7B Llama-style model trained from scratch on DCLM (100B tokens). The key 'comparison' is between architectural variants: pre-norm (baseline) vs. Sandwich Norm vs. DynamicTanh vs. QK-Norm, showing that spikes are an artifact of the pre-norm residual accumulation while sinks persist (or even strengthen) under all alternatives. A second axis compares head dimensions (8–128) and gating strategies (conditional vs. unconditional), establishing that sink formation is governed by per-head representational capacity and short-context training rather than by the presence of massive activations. The paper does not report downstream task benchmarks (e.g., MMLU, needle-in-a-haystack), so the practical cost of suppressing either phenomenon remains unquantified.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and extends two previously studied-in-isolation phenomena—massive activations (Sun et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024a) and attention sinks (Xiao et al., 2024b)—into a single mechanistic causal chain rooted in the pre-norm Transformer architecture (Xiong et al., 2020). Where prior work provided descriptive characterizations (five properties of spikes, sink-token identification), this paper identifies the specific architectural pathway: SwiGLU feed-forward blocks acting as directional quadratic amplifiers inject spikes, RMSNorm transforms them into sparse near-constant vectors, and geometric subspace alignment in attention heads converts those vectors into sinks. The paper also extends the gated-attention line of work (Qiu et al., 2025) by showing that sinks function as an implicit input-conditioned gate, and extends the normalization-free literature (Zhu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025) by pinpointing exactly which normalization property (vector-wide norm vs. element-wise transform) is responsible for the spike-to-sink bridge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that massive activations and attention sinks are independently suppressible architectural artifacts—rather than functionally necessary coupled phenomena—directly impacts the design of quantization schemes, KV-cache eviction policies, pruning strategies, and long-context inference systems that currently treat these phenomena as inherent to Transformers. The practical takeaway is that practitioners can choose normalization variants (Sandwich, DynamicTanh, QK-Norm) or attention modifications (conditional gating, context-length restrictions) to eliminate one or both phenomena without degrading language-modeling perplexity. Key limitations temper the impact: ablations are restricted to 7B-parameter models trained on 100B tokens, no downstream task evaluation (MMLU, needle-in-a-haystack, in-context learning) is performed, and the paper lacks direct inference-time causal interventions on frozen models. Open questions include whether these decoupling strategies remain stable at 100B+ parameter scale and whether eliminating sinks subtly degrades long-context retrieval or few-shot reasoning capabilities.
