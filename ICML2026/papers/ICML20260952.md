## Problem

LLM evaluation on mathematical benchmarks suffers from a reproducibility crisis: small benchmark sizes (e.g., AIME 2025 has only 30 problems) combined with high model stochasticity produce high-variance accuracy estimates and unstable model rankings across platforms. The paper asks whether pairwise comparison signals—where a model judges which of two candidate solutions is better—can be exploited as auxiliary information to improve the statistical efficiency of accuracy estimation, leveraging the well-known generation-verification gap.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260952/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For each problem instance, the method consumes the problem prompt X, the target model's response Y, the ground-truth answer G, and M+1 auxiliary signal tuples Z=(W₁,W₂,V) where W₁,W₂ are reasoning chains from auxiliary LLMs and V is the target model's pairwise preference. The output is a one-step accuracy estimate θ̂ with an asymptotic normal distribution (enabling confidence intervals) and a derived model ranking. Notably, the auxiliary signals are generated on-demand via LLM queries under a fixed protocol rather than being pre-existing annotations, and for small-sample regimes (N≤30) the nuisance regression is performed by a frozen off-the-shelf LLM (Gemini-3-Flash) via zero-shot prompting rather than gradient-based training.

## Method

The paper formalizes LLM accuracy evaluation as a semiparametric inference problem where the conditional distribution p(Z|X) of auxiliary signals is known (since they are generated by LLMs under a fixed prompting protocol). It derives the Efficient Influence Function (EIF) with two nuisance components: an outcome regression τ(X,Z)=E[φ(Y,G)|Z,X] that predicts correctness from auxiliary signals, and an integrated regression m(X)=E[τ(X,Z)|X] that marginalizes over all possible auxiliary generations via Monte Carlo sampling. A one-step estimator with K-fold cross-fitting combines these to produce an estimator that achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound, is asymptotically normal, and strictly reduces variance over the naive sample mean whenever the auxiliary signals carry non-redundant information. For small-sample settings where training a regression model is infeasible, the framework substitutes a frozen LLM as a 'semantic regressor' for τ, eliminating both training cost and the need for cross-fitting. The variance reduction is provably strict: σ²_eff < σ²_naive whenever τ(X,Z)≠m(X) with positive probability, i.e., whenever the generation-verification gap is nonzero.

## Results

On AIME 2025 (N=15 of 30 problems), the one-step estimator reduces absolute error versus the naive sample mean by up to 12.00 percentage points (Claude-Sonnet-4.5, Config 2: 78.67% vs. 66.67% naive, GT=83.33%). On GPQA Diamond (N=50 of 198), improvements range from +0.80% to +5.60% across 10 models, with the largest gains for weaker models (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct: +5.60%). On GSM8K (N=100 of 1319), gains are more modest (+0.10% to +3.50%), consistent with the larger sample size reducing the naive estimator's variance. In the equal-budget repeated-decoding comparison on GPQA Diamond, the one-step estimator is closer to ground truth than 12 independent target responses in 4 out of 5 models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the naive sample-mean estimator (simple accuracy over the N sampled problems). The one-step EIF estimator outperforms the naive baseline in the majority of model–benchmark–configuration combinations: 18 out of 20 on AIME 2025, 19 out of 20 on GPQA Diamond, and 18 out of 20 on GSM8K. The largest absolute improvements occur in the smallest-sample regime (AIME 2025, N=15), where the naive estimator's variance is highest; for example, Claude-Sonnet-4.5's estimate improves from 66.67% (naive) to 78.67% (one-step, Config 2), a 12-point reduction in absolute error relative to the 83.33% ground truth. A secondary comparison is an equal-budget repeated-decoding baseline (12 independent target responses vs. 1 target response + 11 pairwise comparisons) on GPQA Diamond; the one-step estimator is closer to ground truth in 4 of 5 models under both auxiliary configurations. The few cases where the one-step estimator underperforms (e.g., Qwen3-Next-80B on AIME, Llama-3.3-70B on AIME) correspond to models with very low or very high accuracy where the auxiliary signal provides limited additional information, consistent with the paper's theoretical condition that gains require non-redundant auxiliary information.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the semiparametric inference machinery of Robins, Rotnitzky, and Tsiatis (EIF, control variates, one-step estimators) for the domain of LLM evaluation, a setting where prior work has largely ignored statistical efficiency. It draws on the generation-verification gap literature (Christiano et al., Lightman et al.) not for training or alignment, but to identify pairwise comparison signals as a source of auxiliary information for variance reduction. Compared to recent LLM-evaluation-efficiency efforts (Frick et al. on prompt-adaptive control variates, Hariri et al. on Bayesian pass@k, Zhou et al. on synthetic feedback), this paper provides a unified semiparametric framework with a provable efficiency bound, whereas those approaches lack a single theoretical guarantee or are sensitive to prior specification in small-sample regimes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and widely felt pain point: the same model can report 40–65% accuracy on GPQA Diamond depending on the evaluation platform, and on AIME (30 problems) a single answer shifts accuracy by over 3 percentage points. By achieving the semiparametric efficiency bound with pairwise comparison signals, the framework offers principled confidence intervals and more stable rankings in exactly the small-sample regimes where current practice is most unreliable. Key limitations acknowledged by the authors include: the method improves estimation of a fixed benchmark metric but does not address benchmark validity itself, finite-sample degradation can occur when auxiliary signals are weak, and the approach has not yet been extended to metrics like pass@k. Open questions include whether the framework can absorb systematic biases in auxiliary data generation and how it scales to multi-turn or open-ended evaluation settings.
