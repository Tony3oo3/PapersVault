## Problem

Training compact Tsetlin Machines from scratch fails because stochastic search cannot reliably locate the few precise clauses needed in a vast combinatorial configuration space, while large over-parameterized TMs contain high-quality compact subsets that are difficult to extract. No prior work had posed clause selection as a global formal optimization problem with exact feasibility constraints, and local pruning heuristics fail because TM clauses interact nonlinearly through Boolean logic and vote aggregation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained 200-clause Tsetlin Machine (treated as an oracle) and a set of binary feature vectors (continuous features thresholded at the median, categorical features one-hot encoded) as input. It produces a compressed clause subset K (typically 1–35 clauses) together with a pattern dictionary D that maps K-bit clause-output vectors to the teacher's original class labels, plus a 1-nearest-neighbor Hamming-space fallback for unseen test patterns. The output is thus a hybrid predictor rather than a standalone smaller TM, since clause voting alone on the selected subset is near-random.

## Method

The paper formalizes compression as the Minimum Discriminating Clause Set (MDCS) problem—finding the smallest clause subset that preserves the teacher's discrimination of all training sample pairs—and proves it NP-hard via reduction from Minimum Hitting Set. MDCS is encoded as a weighted partial MaxSAT instance where hard clauses enforce that at least one separating clause is selected for every discriminating pair, and soft clauses (with uniform or validation-guided weights) minimize the number of selected clauses, solved to global optimality by the RC2 core-guided solver. To scale beyond a few thousand samples, a partition-and-merge strategy splits the training set into p disjoint subsets, solves MaxSAT independently per partition, and unions the selected clauses, reducing constraint count from O(n²) to O(n²/p) and enabling compression of 100K-sample datasets in roughly two minutes. At inference, the compressed model uses the pattern dictionary for exact lookup on observed patterns and 1-NN in Hamming space for novel patterns, rather than relying on clause vote aggregation.

## Results

Across 13 binary classification datasets, MaxSAT compression retains a median of only 16 clauses (out of 200) while preserving the 200-clause teacher's test accuracy within 1 pp on 11 of 13 datasets and never exceeding a 4.4 pp gap (tictactoe). Compression rates range from 83% (spambase, 35 clauses) to >99% (nursery, 1 clause). On spect-heart, the compressed 6-clause model reaches 84.4% accuracy versus 39.1% for a 6-clause TM trained from scratch (+45.4 pp); on breast-cancer, 12 clauses yield 91.4% versus 63.5% for the matched baseline (+27.9 pp). A Wilcoxon signed-rank test across all 13 datasets confirms MaxSAT significantly outperforms the matched-capacity baseline (p < 0.002).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is a matched-capacity TM trained from scratch with the same K clauses that MaxSAT selects. MaxSAT wins on 12 of 13 datasets (ties on nursery where both reach 100%), with the largest margins on small datasets: +45.4 pp on spect-heart, +27.9 pp on breast-cancer, and +15.2 pp on banknote. Against knowledge distillation (Hinton et al. 2015, adapted to TMs with hard teacher labels), MaxSAT dominates on small datasets where KD collapses to near-random accuracy (39.6% on spect-heart, 64.5% on breast-cancer), while KD narrowly edges MaxSAT on electricity (71.3 vs 71.2). Greedy pruning is the weakest post-hoc method on 9 of 13 datasets, losing up to 22 pp to MaxSAT on banknote and 32 pp on nursery, confirming that local myopic removal fails due to Boolean clause interactions. Random pruning is a surprisingly strong baseline on datasets with many interchangeable clauses (spect-heart, tictactoe) but loses by wide margins where specific clause combinations matter (banknote: 71.9 vs 86.3; kr-vs-kp: 94.7 vs 97.6). Overall, MaxSAT is best-or-tied-best on 10 of 12 non-higgs datasets in the compression-method comparison (Table 3), with the remaining two within 0.5 pp of the winner.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the incremental partitioning framework of Ghosh & Meel (2019) for MaxSAT-based rule learning, repurposing it not for de novo rule induction but for selecting a minimal subset from an already-trained Tsetlin Machine. It departs from prior TM compression work (Weighted TM, ETHEREAL, knowledge distillation), which relied on heuristic training modifications or local pruning, by formalizing compression as the Minimum Discriminating Clause Set (MDCS) problem and solving it exactly via weighted partial MaxSAT. The conceptual parallel to the lottery ticket hypothesis in neural networks is explicit, but the key distinction is that TM clauses interact through Boolean logic, making the extraction problem amenable to exact combinatorial optimization rather than magnitude-based heuristics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal, NP-hardness-grounded optimization framework for TM compression, demonstrating that a 200-clause teacher can be reduced to a median of 16 clauses with near-lossless accuracy while dramatically outperforming matched-capacity direct training (up to +45pp). The partition-and-merge strategy scales exact optimization to 100K samples in minutes, making the approach practical. Open questions include extension to multi-class settings, datasets beyond ~100K samples, and whether the MDCS formulation generalizes to other interpretable model families. The finding that the pattern dictionary (not clause voting) is the essential component of the compressed predictor suggests the selected clauses serve as a feature extractor rather than a standalone classifier, which may inform future hybrid architectures.
