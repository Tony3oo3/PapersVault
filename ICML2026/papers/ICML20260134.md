## Problem

Frontier LLMs are deployed as black-box API services where model weights are inaccessible, making traditional fine-tuning impossible, while static prompts cannot adapt to diverse inputs or encode per-instance preferences. This paper addresses how to perform parametric optimization of black-box models in a practical, cost-effective way that enables both specialized reasoning and personalization without modifying the student model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260134/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The advisor model (a small open-weight LLM, e.g., Qwen2.5 7B Instruct) receives the task prompt and, depending on the variant, either an initial student attempt (3-step) or periodic environmental observations from the student's actions (multi-turn agentic settings). It produces 3–4 sentences of natural language advice that is appended in-context to the student model's prompt; the student then generates its final output. In multi-turn settings, the advisor is re-queried every N student steps (e.g., every 5 actions in SWE agent tasks), receiving a summary of the student's actions and observations between turns. The advice is free-form natural language with no format constraints, distinguishing it from soft prompts or structured keyword lists.

## Method

ADVISOR MODELS trains a lightweight open-weight policy via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to generate instance-specific natural language advice that steers a frozen black-box student model. The training loop has the advisor sample candidate advice, the student produce outputs conditioned on that advice, and a task-specific scalar reward (e.g., 0/1 correctness, chrF, efficiency-weighted resolve rate) be computed from the student's final output; GRPO then updates the advisor's weights based on relative reward rankings within groups. The key architectural insight is that the advisor is updated in gradient space while the student remains entirely unchanged, converting prompt engineering from a static search problem into a learned policy that adapts per-instance. Two design variants are explored: a 2-step pipeline (advisor → student) and a 3-step pipeline (student initial attempt → advisor revision → student final), with the latter reducing the advisor's role to that of a verifier. A critical practical property is transferability: advisors trained with low-cost student models (e.g., GPT-4o mini, Gemini 2.5 Flash) transfer improvements to frontier models (e.g., GPT-5.2, Gemini 3 Pro) without further tuning, reducing training costs by orders of magnitude.

## Results

On specialized reasoning tasks, ADVISOR MODELS lifts GPT-4.1 mini's RuleArena Taxes accuracy from 64.8% to 76.8%, improves GPT-4o mini's MTOB chrF from 33.1 to 45.8 (approaching the 51.6 human expert), and reduces Gemini 2.5 Flash's SWE agent steps from 19.1 to 14.4 while maintaining 61.2% resolve rate. Transferred to frontier models, the same advisors improve GPT-5.2's RuleArena accuracy from 67.2% to 85.6% (+27.4%), reduce Gemini 3 Pro's SWE steps from 31.7 to 26.3 (−24.6%), and lift GPT-5's MTOB chrF from 46.2 to 47.8. On personalization, ADVISOR MODELS achieves 0.94 reward (Review Length), 99.6% accuracy (Review Level), and 0.948 reward (Math Solutions), far exceeding the 40–60% range of static optimizers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GEPA (the state-of-the-art static prompt optimizer), ADVISOR MODELS wins decisively on all three specialized reasoning domains: 76.8% vs. 58.4% on RuleArena Taxes, 45.8 vs. 33.0 chrF on MTOB, and GEPA was not applicable to the multi-turn SWE Agent Efficiency task. On personalization tasks, both GEPA and Profile-Augmented Generation (PAG) achieve only marginal improvements over un-optimized baselines (roughly 40–60% reward), while ADVISOR MODELS reaches 0.94–0.996, effectively solving the hidden-preference learning problem that static methods cannot. Notably, ADVISOR MODELS even outperforms the in-context oracle (which provides exact preferences in the prompt) on personalization, because the advisor generates instance-specific, actionable advice rather than relying on the student to interpret a static preference description. The paper also shows that an untrained advisor can actually hurt performance (e.g., dropping RuleArena accuracy from 64.8% to 52.0%), underscoring that the RL training loop is essential rather than merely beneficial.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as its RL training algorithm but departs fundamentally from the static prompt optimization paradigm (GEPA, DSPy, PromptBreeder) by training a lightweight policy to generate per-instance natural language advice rather than searching for a single fixed prompt. It also extends the line of trained prompt generators (Black-Box Prompt Optimization, PRewrite, IDPG, Matryoshka Pilot) by removing the need for expert labels, eliminating format constraints on advice, and using RL rather than supervised fine-tuning. The key conceptual contribution is reframing prompt engineering as a policy learning problem where the advisor's weights are updated in gradient space while the student model remains frozen.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As frontier models increasingly deploy as black-box APIs, this paper provides a practical, cost-effective path to specialization and personalization without weight access, with the transferability finding (train on cheap models, deploy on frontier models) making it accessible within research budgets. The robustness guarantee—no degradation on untrained tasks—addresses a key concern with fine-tuning approaches. Open questions include: how the method scales to domains without clear scalar rewards, whether the 3-step variant's doubled inference cost is acceptable in latency-sensitive settings, and whether the advisor-as-tool-call formulation can be made to work when the student model is incentivized to request help. The MATH-500 negative result cleanly delineates the boundary: advisors help only when there is domain-specific knowledge or preference structure the student lacks.
