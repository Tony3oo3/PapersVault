## Problem

LLMs execute all inputs through a fixed-depth, fixed-order forward pass regardless of input difficulty, yet the paper demonstrates via MCTS that many inputs admit shorter or alternative layer-execution programs that achieve equal or better accuracy. Prior dynamic-depth methods explore only a single operation (skip or repeat) in isolation, missing the complementary gains from jointly combining both. The paper asks whether a lightweight learned predictor can replace expensive per-input search to directly generate input-specific execution programs over frozen pretrained layers.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (a math problem in a fixed direct-answering format) and produces two outputs: (1) an execution program encoded as a binary segmentation mask over D layer positions (indicating segment boundaries, with max segment length K_max=4) and a categorical operation vector assigning one of {skip, keep, repeat} to each segment, and (2) the final answer obtained by executing that program on the fully frozen LLM. At inference, a small beam search over segment-level operations yields a ranked set of k candidate programs, and pass@k is reported as the probability that at least one candidate produces the correct answer.

## Method

POLAR trains a ~2.1M-parameter prediction network that maps an input to an execution program in a single forward pass, eliminating the need for per-input MCTS search. The architecture encodes the input with a frozen Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B model, applies multi-head cross-attention with learnable per-layer embeddings as queries, then a lightweight cross-layer transformer encoder to model depth dependencies, and finally two linear heads predict segmentation boundaries (binary cross-entropy) and per-segment operations (masked cross-entropy at segment starts). Training is supervised on valid execution programs collected offline via MCTS, with down-weighting of full-depth programs to prefer shorter valid solutions. The key design insight, grounded in MCTS structural analysis, is that valid programs are dominated by contiguous segments of 1–2 layers with at most one recurrence, so the operation vocabulary is restricted to {skip, keep, repeat-once} and segment length is bounded to 4, making the prediction space tractable while preserving the dominant valid program structures.

## Results

On DART-Math (in-distribution), POLAR with LLaMA-3.2-3B-Instruct achieves pass@5 accuracy of 74.5/46.9/47.6/45.1/41.9% across difficulty levels DM-1 to DM-5, yielding an average +7.4 pp gain over Base (sampling) at pass@5. On the weaker Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct backbone, gains are even larger: +40.4 pp on DM-1 and +13.5 pp on DM-5 at pass@5. Out-of-distribution, POLAR improves over standard inference on all 15 MMLU-Pro subjects and both arithmetic benchmarks (e.g., ASDiv 63.8% vs. 59.1% base on Qwen1.5-MoE), with the predictor adding only 3.05 ms (0.8%) overhead and reducing end-to-end latency to 0.83× base on easy inputs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

POLAR outperforms all four dynamic-depth baselines across every difficulty level and model. ShortGPT (static layer pruning), MindSkip (router-based skipping), and FlexiDepth (adaptive layer-skipping) all suffer severe accuracy degradation—often dropping below 20% on harder levels—because they only support skipping and cannot add computation where needed. DR.LLM, which supports both skipping and repetition at the individual-layer level, is the closest competitor but still trails POLAR by 5.7–10.2 pp at pass@5 on LLaMA-3.2-3B-Instruct (e.g., 41.9% vs. 31.7% on DM-5), because its layer-wise sequential routing limits global coordination. POLAR's advantage is most pronounced on weaker backbones: on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, POLAR reaches 68.8% on DM-1 at pass@5 versus 9.9% for DR.LLM and 28.4% for Base (sampling). On OOD benchmarks, the gap widens further—DR.LLM collapses to near-zero on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct MMLU-Pro subjects while POLAR maintains consistent gains over base.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the program-of-layers formulation from Li et al. (2025), which used MCTS to search over skip/repeat programs over individual layers, by (1) replacing expensive per-input search with a lightweight learned predictor that generates programs in a single shot, and (2) operating on contiguous layer segments rather than individual layers, enabling coordinated multi-layer skip/repeat patterns. It departs from prior dynamic-depth methods (early exit, ShortGPT, FlexiDepth, MindSkip) that support only a single operation (skip or repeat) and make local layer-wise routing decisions, by unifying both operations within a segment-level program representation. The empirical MCTS study in Section 2 is novel in characterizing the joint skip+repeat program space, revealing that combining both operations yields substantially better programs than either alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 71.9% of correctly-solved inputs admit shorter valid programs, and that incorrect predictions can often be corrected by alternative programs, challenges the assumption that the standard forward pass is the only valid computation for a given input. The OOD generalization—programs learned on math reasoning transferring to MMLU-Pro subjects spanning philosophy, history, and business—suggests the learned execution strategies capture domain-agnostic computation control rather than dataset-specific heuristics. Limitations include evaluation only on small models (3–8B) and math-centric tasks, a significant gap between MCTS-discovered programs and the learned predictor's output, and the restriction to at most one repeat per segment with length ≤ 4. Open questions include whether this approach scales to larger models, generation tasks, and non-mathematical reasoning, and whether the segment-level abstraction is the right granularity for more complex control flow.
