## Problem

Quantizing few-step diffusion models (2–8 NFEs) to 4-bit or 8-bit for deployment degrades image quality because the full-precision noise scheduler no longer matches the perturbed quantized denoiser's trajectory, and the mismatch amplifies as step count shrinks. Existing post-training quantization methods modify weights or activations and require full-precision calibration, leaving the scheduler-level drift unaddressed in the few-step regime.

## Input / Output

At inference, Q-Sched takes a text prompt and a frozen quantized U-Net/DiT backbone (W4A8 or W8A8) and produces a single generated image, with the only added parameters being a small set of per-timestep scalar coefficients (c^x, c^ε) that fuse into the existing scheduler at zero extra FLOPs. During calibration, the method consumes only ~20 representative prompts and the quantized model itself—no full-precision references or activations are needed—making it deployable as a single reusable checkpoint across multiple bit-widths without retraining.

## Method

Q-Sched inserts two learnable scalar preconditioning coefficients, c_t^x (on the intermediate sample x_t) and c_t^ε (on the quantized denoiser output), directly into the few-step scheduler update rule (demonstrated on TCD/SSS), making the trajectory affine in these coefficients so they can be absorbed into existing schedule parameters at inference. The coefficients are optimized end-to-end via a reference-free Joint Alignment-Quality (JAQ) loss that combines a text-image compatibility term TC(x) with an image-quality term IQ(x), requiring only ~20 calibration prompts and no full-precision inference. To keep the search tractable, Q-Sched parameterizes each coefficient by just a start and end value with linear interpolation across timesteps, reducing the search to four scalars regardless of step count. A theoretical existence guarantee (Theorem 3.1) proves that non-trivial coefficients strictly reduce expected reconstruction error relative to naive quantization, and the approach is scheduler-level only—no weight modification, no extra FLOPs, and no checkpoint sprawl.

## Results

On COCO-30k, Q-Sched achieves a 15.5% FID improvement over the FP16 4-step LCM (FID 26.98 vs. 31.94) and a 16.6% improvement over the FP16 8-step PCM (FID 16.83 vs. 20.15) under W4A8 quantization. On the 2-step SDXL PCM, Q-Sched incurs only a 1.2% FID drop (18.89 vs. 18.65 FP16) while PTQD degrades catastrophically to 161.96. In the large-scale user study on MJHQ-30k, Q-Sched attains the highest ELO rating (1000.3 on SDXL-Turbo, surpassing MixDQ's 998.7) and the best JAQ score (1.669 vs. 1.663 FP16) while delivering a 4× model size reduction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PTQD (He et al., 2024), Q-Sched wins decisively: on the 4-step LCM W4A8, Q-Sched achieves FID 26.98 vs. PTQD's 39.72, and on the 2-step SDXL PCM, Q-Sched reaches FID 18.89 while PTQD collapses to 161.96, confirming that PTQD's Gaussian noise assumption breaks down in the few-step regime. Against the original TCD scheduler (Zheng et al., 2024), Q-Sched consistently improves FID across all step counts (e.g., 4-step LCM: 26.98 vs. 38.41; 2-step SDXL PCM: 18.89 vs. 22.70), and even surpasses the FP16 baseline in several settings. Against MixDQ (Zhao et al., 2024a) on SDXL-Turbo, Q-Sched achieves a higher ELO (1000.3 vs. 998.7) and JAQ score (1.669 vs. 1.659) at the same W8A8 model size. Against SVDQuant (Li et al., 2025) on FLUX.1[schnell], Q-Sched edges out SVDQuant on JAQ (1.673 vs. 1.669) and matches or exceeds it on CLIP-IQA and AQ-MAP, while operating at W4A8 rather than W4A4+LoRA.

## Relation to Prior Work

Q-Sched sits at the intersection of two well-studied compression axes for diffusion models: few-step distillation (LCM, PCM, TCD, SDXL-Turbo, FLUX.1[schnell]) and post-training quantization (PTQ4DM, MixDQ, SVDQuant). The closest prior work is PTQD, which also corrects the sampler rather than the weights, but relies on a Gaussian noise assumption and a linear error model calibrated against full-precision references. Q-Sched departs from this lineage by learning its correction coefficients end-to-end via a reference-free JAQ loss (combining CLIPScore/CLIP-IQA or AQ-MAP/HPSv2), removing the need for full-precision calibration and the Gaussian assumption that breaks down in the few-step regime. The theoretical existence guarantee (Theorem 3.1) and the linear-interpolation parameterization of coefficients across timesteps are novel contributions not present in prior scheduler-level or weight-level PTQ methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a handful of scalar coefficients (fused into the existing schedule at zero inference cost) enable 4× model-size reduction while matching or exceeding FP16 perceptual quality, validated by 80,000+ human annotations on two major architectures. The counterintuitive finding that a quantized model can outperform its full-precision counterpart on FID (up to 15.5% for LCM) suggests that few-step distillation and quantization are complementary rather than competing compression strategies, opening a path to deployment on memory-constrained hardware (on-device, MIG partitions). Limitations include reduced effectiveness at 3-bit, applicability primarily to models under ~8 steps, and the observation that FID gains on consistency models do not always translate to perceptual gains on modern flow-matching models like SDXL-Turbo. Open questions include whether the approach extends to video diffusion, longer trajectories, or architectures where the backbone is too small for quantization to be beneficial (e.g., SANA-sprint at 0.6B).
