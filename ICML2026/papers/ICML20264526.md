## Problem

Weak-to-strong generalization aims to improve a strong student using supervision from a weaker teacher, but prior approaches leave a persistent performance gap relative to ground-truth training because they treat all weak labels equally. The key challenge is identifying which weak labels are reliable enough to serve as a training signal, and existing output-level heuristics (entropy, verbalized confidence, inter-model agreement) are poorly calibrated on complex tasks, often assigning high trust to confident errors and low trust to correct but uncertain solutions.

## Input / Output

The NTF takes as input the weak teacher's last-layer hidden state at the final generated token — a single d-dimensional vector (e.g., 1024 for Qwen3-0.6B) that attends to the full input prompt and any intermediate reasoning — and outputs a scalar trust score in [0,1] via a sigmoid head. These scores rank the weakly labeled pool, and the top-n examples by trust form the filtered training set used to fine-tune the strong student via LoRA-SFT or GRPO. The NTF is trained on a labeled source distribution and deployed zero-shot on an unlabeled target distribution, so ground-truth labels are never accessed at selection time.

## Method

The paper introduces Neural Trust Functions (NTFs), small residual MLPs with RMSNorm-SwiGLU blocks, dropout, and stochastic depth, trained with class-reweighted binary cross-entropy to predict whether a weak label is correct from the teacher's internal hidden state. The key insight is that intermediate representations contain separable correctness signals that are obscured in the final decoded output, making them more reliable than output-level confidence proxies. The NTF is trained on a labeled source distribution (e.g., MMLU for MCQA, MATH for reasoning) and deployed zero-shot under in-domain distribution shift to score weak labels on an unlabeled target distribution. Beyond one-shot transfer, the paper shows that trust filtering enables an iterative weak-to-strong chain where each trained student becomes the next teacher, yielding compounding gains that eventually surpass budget-matched ground-truth supervision at the largest model scale.

## Results

Across world knowledge (MCQA), NTF achieves 50.5–87.1% accuracy across eight student configurations, matching or exceeding ground-truth supervision (49.6–87.0%) with recovery rates of 95.9–113.9%. In quantitative reasoning (AIME), NTF reaches 22.0–27.9% with 89.2–92.5% recovery relative to GT (22.9–28.7%). In strategy games (chess puzzles), NTF achieves 15.5–44.1% with recovery up to 110.5%, exhibiting super-recovery (statistically better than GT) in 4 of 8 Qwen3 settings. The iterative weak-to-strong chain compounds these gains, with the final chained Qwen3-14B reaching 48.2% versus 40.0% for budget-matched GT training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NTF consistently outperforms the verbalized-confidence baseline (Tian et al., 2023) across all three domains: in strategy games, NTF achieves 44.1% vs. V-Confidence's 33.4% on Qwen3-14B (a 10.7-point gap), and in quantitative reasoning, NTF reaches 22.0% vs. 18.7% for V-Confidence with the weakest teacher (Qwen3-1.7B). Against the inter-model agreement / ensemble baseline (Lang et al., 2025), NTF wins in every world-knowledge configuration (e.g., 87.1% vs. 85.1% for Qwen3-14B, 80.9% vs. 76.7% for OLMo2-13B) and in all strategy-games settings (e.g., 44.1% vs. 37.5% for Qwen3-14B). NTF also surpasses internal-confidence (I-Confidence) selection by 1.2–5.8 points in world knowledge and by 1.3–7.5 points in strategy games, with the largest gaps appearing for weaker teachers where likelihood-based confidence is most miscalibrated. Relative to ground-truth supervision, NTF is statistically indistinguishable in 5 of 8 world-knowledge settings, achieves super-recovery (significantly better than GT) in 1 world-knowledge setting and 4 of 8 strategy-games settings, and approaches GT within 0.8–1.3 points in quantitative reasoning (89–92% recovery). The reward-model baseline is consistently the weakest external filter, trailing NTF by 3–14 points across domains, suggesting that teacher hidden-state representations provide a more reliable correctness signal than generic output-level reward scoring.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions weak-to-strong generalization as a data selection problem, departing from prior work that modifies optimization (Burns et al., 2023) or learning algorithms (Liu & Alahi, 2024). Its key conceptual departure is from output-level trust heuristics (entropy, verbalized confidence, inter-model agreement) to a neural predictor over the teacher's internal hidden states, repurposing the observation that intermediate representations encode correctness signals (Kadavath et al., 2022; Azaria & Mitchell, 2023) for the specific purpose of filtering weak supervision under distribution shift. The iterative weak-to-strong chain extends prior self-improvement pipelines (Liang et al., 2024) by showing that trust-filtered supervision, rather than raw weak labels, is what enables compounding gains across generations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a lightweight MLP on a single hidden state can replace expensive ground-truth labels for student training across multiple domains and model scales, and the snowballing chain means even a very weak starting teacher can bootstrap to strong performance. Key limitations include the requirement for a labeled source distribution (ruling out truly label-free regimes), the restriction to outcome-level supervision (no step-wise process rewards), and the failure of trust transfer across fundamentally different task interfaces (OOD_domain). Open questions include whether richer NTF architectures (multi-token, multi-layer, attention-based) can close the remaining gaps in harder reasoning domains, and whether the bias toward easier instances (the implicit curriculum effect) is a feature or a limitation for scaling to genuinely novel problems.
