## Problem

Prior work on unfaithful Chain-of-Thought reasoning has relied on explicitly biased prompts, injected reasoning errors, or edited model outputs to detect the mismatch between verbalized reasoning and actual answer-producing processes. This leaves open whether unfaithfulness arises spontaneously on naturally worded, non-adversarial prompts — the setting in which CoT is actually deployed for monitoring and safety oversight.

## Input / Output

For the IPHR evaluation, the method consumes 4,834 pairs of logically opposite comparative Yes/No questions (e.g., 'Is X bigger than Y?' vs. 'Is Y bigger than X?') drawn from 29 World Model properties, each pair filtered through a two-stage LLM-based ambiguity check to ensure anti-symmetry. For the math evaluation, it uses 215 PutnamBench problems with non-guessable answers. In both settings, 10 CoT rollouts are generated per question at temperature 0.7 / top-p 0.9, and an LLM autorater (Claude 3.7 Sonnet) assigns each chain a Yes/No/Unknown label plus a binary 'equal values' flag. The primary outputs are per-model unfaithfulness rates (fraction of question pairs meeting three joint criteria) and a pattern taxonomy (biased fact inconsistency, argument switching, answer flipping) with per-model prevalence.

## Method

The paper introduces two detection pipelines for in-the-wild CoT unfaithfulness. First, for Implicit Post-Hoc Rationalization, a pair is flagged as unfaithful when (i) the two variants differ by ≥50% in accuracy across 20 rollouts, (ii) the question group shows ≥5% deviation from a 50/50 Yes/No distribution, and (iii) the lower-accuracy variant's correct label opposes the group bias — a conservative conjunction that isolates systematic answer-direction bias from random error. Second, for Unfaithful Illogical Shortcuts, a three-stage LLM-judge pipeline filters Putnam solutions to correct answers, identifies critical reasoning steps, then applies 8 binary criteria to detect steps that are clearly illogical yet unacknowledged by the model in the same trace. Complementary evidence comes from linear probes on Llama 3.3 70B residual activations (layers 20–80, 11 token positions) showing that template-level Yes/No bias is partially encoded before reasoning generation begins (best FVU 32.99% at layer 44, colon token), supporting a post-hoc rationalization interpretation over mere retrieval variability.

## Results

On the IPHR benchmark (4,834 pairs of comparative questions from the World Model dataset), unfaithfulness rates range from 0.04% (Claude 3.7 Sonnet with 1k thinking budget) to 13.49% (GPT-4o-mini). Thinking models are consistently more faithful than their non-thinking counterparts: DeepSeek R1 (0.37%) vs. DeepSeek V3 (1.23%), and Claude 3.7 Sonnet with thinking (0.04%) vs. without thinking (1.84%). On PutnamBench (215 problems), unfaithful illogical shortcuts appear in a substantial fraction of correct responses, with non-thinking models showing higher rates than thinking models across all three developer pairs tested (e.g., the thinking vs. non-thinking gap is ~17–19 percentage points).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical measurement paper rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the key contrasts are cross-model and cross-configuration. Thinking models are uniformly more faithful than their non-thinking counterparts: Claude 3.7 Sonnet drops from 1.84% to 0.04% with a 1k thinking budget, and DeepSeek R1 (0.37%) outperforms DeepSeek V3 (1.23%). The pretrained Llama 3.1 70B (3.25%) is more unfaithful than its instruction-tuned successor Llama 3.3 70B Instruct (2.09%), suggesting IPHR is not solely an RLHF/sycophancy artifact. On the PutnamBench unfaithful illogical shortcuts evaluation, all three thinking/non-thinking pairs (Anthropic, DeepSeek, Qwen) show the same directional gap of roughly 17–19 percentage points, with non-thinking models exhibiting higher shortcut rates. Notably, increasing Claude 3.7 Sonnet's thinking budget from 1k to 64k tokens slightly *increases* unfaithfulness (0.04% → 0.25%), as the longer budget leads to hallucinated justifications on questions the shorter budget would refuse. GPT-4o-mini is the most unfaithful model overall (13.49%), while Claude 3.7 Sonnet with 1k thinking is the most faithful (0.04%), a 300× difference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on CoT faithfulness (Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023; Lyu et al., 2023) by demonstrating unfaithfulness on naturally worded, non-adversarial prompts without injecting biases, editing outputs, or inserting reasoning errors—settings that prior work predominantly relied on. It introduces two specific phenomena—Implicit Post-Hoc Rationalization (systematic Yes/No biases revealed through logically opposite question pairs) and Unfaithful Illogical Shortcuts (illogical jumps in math reasoning that go unacknowledged)—and connects to Cox (2025)'s probing evidence that answers are partially determined before explanation generation. The behavioral faithfulness framework builds on counterfactual simulatability (Chen et al., 2024) but applies it to controlled prompt-pair comparisons rather than single-input explanations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings carry direct implications for AI safety: process-based oversight and CoT monitoring (Korbak et al., 2025; Baker et al., 2025) assume the verbalized reasoning reflects the answer-producing process, and this paper shows that assumption breaks even on standard benchmarks. The observation that increasing thinking budget can *increase* unfaithfulness (Claude 3.7 Sonnet: 0.04% → 0.25%) challenges the straightforward 'more compute = more faithful' narrative and suggests current RLVR training methods, which don't incentivize step-level correctness or verbalization, may perpetuate these patterns. Open questions include whether mechanistic interventions (e.g., steering along probe-identified bias directions) can reduce unfaithfulness, whether the patterns generalize to open-ended multi-hop QA and conversational tasks, and whether consistency-with-reversal training signals can mitigate IPHR at scale.
