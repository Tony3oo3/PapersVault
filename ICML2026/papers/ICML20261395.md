## Problem

State-of-the-art multi-vector retrieval systems (e.g., ColBERT, PLAID) rely on expensive K-means clustering over billion-scale token vectors for indexing, introducing prohibitive indexing latency (100+ hours) and semantic loss from aggressive dimension reduction. The paper asks whether token-level granularity can be preserved while eliminating clustering entirely, achieving retrieval speeds comparable to lexical search.

## Input / Output

The method takes a query token sequence and a document corpus, encodes each token via a backbone (BERT-base or Llama-embed-8B), then projects each token embedding through a Sparse Autoencoder into a high-dimensional sparse vector (h=16384 for BERT, h=65536 for LLM backbones) with only K=32 active neurons. Output is a ranked document list scored by MaxSim late-interaction computed exclusively over overlapping activated neurons, with the inverted index storing per-document maximum neuron activations (μ_{D,u}) to enable block-level early pruning during posting-list traversal.

## Method

SSR replaces the dense clustering pipeline with a Sparse Autoencoder that projects each token embedding into a high-dimensional but highly sparse representation via a TopK operator, so that each active neuron functions as a 'pseudo-token' enabling direct neuron-level inverted indexing (analogous to BM25). Training uses a hybrid objective: an unsupervised SAE reconstruction loss (with Multi-TopK, auxiliary loss for dormant neurons, and sparse contrastive loss) combined with a supervised contrastive loss that applies MaxSim scoring for regular tokens and cosine similarity for the [CLS] token. Two variants are provided—SSR-tok (token-level interactions only) and SSR-CLS (adds global [CLS] semantics)—and an accelerated SSR++ variant performs coarse-to-fine pruning by first scoring with only the top-4 principal neurons to reduce candidates from ~54K to ~3K before exact refinement with all K=32 neurons. A bounded-distortion theorem proves that under small SAE reconstruction error and restricted decoder near-orthogonality on active supports, the sparse late-interaction score approximates the dense MaxSim score with error bounded by N(2Bε + ε² + δC²).

## Results

On the BEIR benchmark (13 out-of-domain datasets + MS MARCO in-domain), SSR-CLS achieves the best average nDCG@10 of 53.4, outperforming the strongest sparse baseline Splade-v3 (51.2) by +2.2 points and the best dense MVR baseline PLAID (49.3) by +4.1 points. SSR-tok delivers 52.9 average nDCG@10 at 17.5 ms per-query latency, nearly 2× faster than ColBERTv2 (56.9 ms) and PLAID (37.1 ms). Indexing time drops from >100 hours (ColBERTv2/XTR with K-means clustering) to ~7.5 hours for SSR, a >15× speedup, while peak build memory falls from 274.2 GB to 34.6 GB. On the LIMIT stress-test benchmark, SSR achieves 78.6% Recall@5 and 98.1% Recall@100, surpassing ColBERTv2 by +6.8 points on Recall@5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SSR-CLS (53.4 avg nDCG@10) outperforms all dense MVR baselines: PLAID (49.3), ColBERTv2 (49.2), XTR (48.8), COIL (47.4), and CITADEL (49.4), with the largest margin over COIL (+6.0). Against the strongest learned sparse baseline Splade-v3 (51.2), SSR-CLS gains +2.2 points while maintaining comparable latency (19.5 ms vs 16.6 ms). SSR-tok (52.9) beats all dense MVR methods and Splade-v3 by +1.7 points at 17.5 ms latency, which is roughly 2× faster than PLAID (37.1 ms) and 3× faster than ColBERTv2 (56.9 ms). On the LLM-backbone evaluation (Table 2), SSR-CLS with Llama-embed-nemotron-8b achieves 67.1 avg, surpassing Qwen3-8B (66.0) and the frozen backbone alone (65.8), confirming that the sparse coding objective—not merely an extra projection layer—drives the gain. On the LIMIT representational-bounds benchmark, SSR (78.6% Recall@5) beats ColBERTv2 (71.8%) by +6.8 points and XTR (68.4%) by +10.2 points, while all single-vector models (Qwen3-4B, GritLM-7B, e5-mistral-7B) collapse below 5% Recall@5. On CPU-only retrieval (Table 15), SSR-tok achieves the best MRR@10 (39.5%) with 7.3 h indexing time versus 49.8–122.9 h for DESSERT/PLAID, and 49.1 ms retrieval latency versus 75.6–156.3 ms for the dense MVR engines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the ColBERT/ColBERTv2 multi-vector retrieval paradigm (MaxSim late interaction) and the Sparse Autoencoder literature (Gao et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2006), but departs fundamentally from the dense-approximation indexing pipeline (K-means clustering + vector quantization) that underpins PLAID, XTR/WARP, and ColBERTv2. Rather than compressing token embeddings into low-dimensional dense codes and clustering them, SSR projects tokens into a high-dimensional sparse space where each active neuron functions as a pseudo-token in a standard inverted index, collapsing the three-stage filter-and-refine pipeline into a single-stage sparse lookup. The hybrid training objective (reconstruction + multi-TopK + sparse contrastive + supervised contrastive) is novel in combining SAE pre-training with retrieval-specific discriminative signals.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SSR addresses a practical bottleneck that has constrained MVR deployment: the clustering step that dominates index construction time and prevents real-time corpus updates. The 15× indexing speedup and append-only update capability are immediately actionable for production retrieval systems. The bounded-distortion theory (Appendix A) provides the first formal guarantee that sparse overlap scoring approximates dense late interaction, lending theoretical credibility to the approach. Open questions include whether the high-dimensional sparse index (h=16384) remains memory-feasible at billion-document scale, how hardware-aware index layouts might further close the gap with COIL's raw speed, and whether adaptive per-query sparsity can be made fully automatic without domain-specific tuning.
