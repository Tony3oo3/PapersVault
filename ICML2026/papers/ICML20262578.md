## Problem

State-of-the-art LLM-based formal theorem provers require prohibitive test-time compute via massive roll-outs or ever-growing context windows, because existing approaches reduce compiler feedback to binary success signals or plain-text error messages, forcing cumulative multi-round self-correction that rapidly exhausts context. The paper asks whether the rich structural information in Lean compiler outputs can be exploited to make refinement statistically efficient and context-light, enabling scalable proof search without long interaction histories.

## Input / Output

At each refinement step, the model receives a Lean 4 theorem statement, the current (failed) Lean 4 program, and compiler error messages inserted inline at their corresponding line numbers (wrapped in special <error> boundary tokens), rather than appended as a trailing block. The output is a natural-language analysis of the failure followed by a corrected Lean 4 program, formatted to match the base prover's native response template (e.g., a  block plus a fenced lean4 code block for Kimina, or a 'Detailed Proof and Analysis' section plus 'Complete Lean 4 Proof' for Goedel). The Markovian formulation means input length stays approximately constant across refinement rounds, since only the current failed program and its compiler feedback are provided, not the full refinement history.

## Method

The central insight is that the Lean compiler acts as a dimension compressor: a many-to-one mapping from the combinatorially large space of syntactically distinct failed proofs to a compact set of structured failure modes, enabling cluster-level (rather than point-wise) supervision for learning a refinement policy. The framework trains a prover via (1) cold-start supervised fine-tuning on refinement pairs synthesized by having Claude-3.7-Sonnet generate reasoning traces bridging a failed proof and a correct one, and (2) expert iteration that collects on-policy refinement trajectories as the prover's own error distribution drifts. At test time, the prover performs tree search over proof attempts where direct generation corresponds to breadth-first expansion of the root and refinement of a failed node corresponds to depth-first expansion; a neural value function trained via pairwise preference learning over refinement states guides node selection via a softmax policy, adaptively balancing exploration between new attempts and local repairs. The Markovian refinement design—conditioning each step only on the current failed program, its compiler message, and the problem—keeps context length constant and avoids the linear growth of non-Markovian self-correction.

## Results

On PutnamBench (budget 256), Goedel-Expert with value-guided tree search solves 110 problems, setting a new state-of-the-art among publicly reported ~32B-parameter provers, while Kimina-Expert solves 25 problems, leading the ~8B regime. On MiniF2F-test (budget 64), Goedel-Expert-Value reaches 86.89% accuracy (vs. 84.43% base) and Kimina-Expert-Value reaches 81.97% (vs. 77.46% base). The largest relative gains appear on harder benchmarks: Goedel-Expert-Value improves ProofNet from 15.63% to 24.26% (+55% relative) and MathOlympiadBench from 14.72% to 34.44% (+134% relative) over the base Goedel-V2 prover.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the base Kimina-Prover-Distill-8B, Kimina-Expert-Value improves PutnamBench from 10 to 25 solved problems (2.5×) and MiniF2F-test from 77.46% to 81.97%. Against the base Goedel-Prover-V2-32B, Goedel-Expert-Value more than doubles PutnamBench performance (32→110 at budget 256) and nearly doubles ProofNet (15.63%→24.26%) and MoBench (14.72%→34.44%). On the PutnamBench leaderboard, Goedel-Expert-Value (110 solved, 32B, budget 256) outperforms Ax-Prover (91, ≥72B, 100 tools), the original Goedel-V2 self-correction mode (86, 32B, budget 184), DSP-V2 (47, 671B, budget 1024), GPT-5 (28, 10 tools), and DSP+ (23, ≥32B, budget 128). In the ~8B regime, Kimina-Expert-Value (25 solved) surpasses BOURBAKI (14, 7B, budget 512), the base Kimina (10, 8B, budget 192), STP (8, 7B, budget 3200), and Goedel-V1 (7, 7B, budget 512). Value-guided tree search consistently outperforms random tree search on PutnamBench (110 vs. 104 for Goedel; 25 vs. 21 for Kimina) and MoBench (34.44% vs. 30.28% for Goedel), while the two strategies are comparable on MiniF2F-test where direct generation is near-saturated.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LLM-based formal theorem proving line (Kimina-Prover, Goedel-Prover, DeepSeek-Prover) and prior self-correction approaches that feed compiler messages back into the model (First et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2025b). Its key conceptual departure is reframing the Lean compiler as a 'dimension compressor' that maps the unbounded space of syntactically distinct failed proofs onto a compact, structured set of failure modes, enabling a Markovian refinement formulation where each correction step depends only on the current proof state and error message rather than an accumulating interaction history. The Chain of Distributions (CoD) framework and the expert-iteration training loop with value-guided tree search are the practical instantiations of this view, distinguishing the work from prior approaches that treat compiler feedback as a binary reward or append it as unstructured text.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Markovian refinement formulation offers a context-efficient alternative to the growing-history self-correction paradigm, and the empirical results (SOTA on PutnamBench among ~8B and ~32B provers, >50% relative gains on ProofNet/MoBench for Goedel-V2) demonstrate that structured compiler feedback can substantially amplify a fixed-size prover without massive roll-outs. However, the paper itself notes that deeper semantic failures (e.g., 'linarith failed', 'nested error') remain largely unrepairable despite training exposure, and the absolute gap to large-scale agentic systems (Aleph, Seed-Prover) is still substantial. Open questions include whether the dimension-compression view transfers to domains with weaker or noisier verification signals, and whether more sophisticated search policies can close the remaining gap to frontier systems.
