## Problem

Existing federated dynamic sparse training (FDST) methods rely on heuristic prune-and-grow criteria (weight magnitude for pruning, gradient magnitude for growing) inherited from post-training paradigms that assume near-convergence and stable curvature. These assumptions are fundamentally violated in federated settings where structure is adjusted while the model is far from convergence and data heterogeneity induces substantial drift in gradients and curvature, resulting in a persistent accuracy gap between sparse and dense federated models.

## Input / Output

The method operates on RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/CINIC-10, 64×64 for TinyImageNet/SVHN) or 256-token text sequences (TinyStories) and produces a sparse neural network with a fixed target density ρ (10%–50%) whose topology evolves during training. The sparse model outputs class labels for vision tasks or token-level probability distributions for language modeling. Unlike static pruning, the output is a dynamically rewired subnetwork where both the active parameter set and their values co-adapt across communication rounds.

## Method

FedFit derives Fisher-information-based prune and grow scores from a second-order Taylor approximation of the loss, replacing magnitude/gradient heuristics with optimization-centric criteria. The pruning score P_k = −g_kθ_k + ½θ_k²F_kk estimates the loss increment from removing parameter k, while the growing score G_j = ½g_j²/F_jj estimates the maximum predicted loss decrease from activating parameter j. The Hessian is approximated by the Fisher Information Matrix, which is further approximated via K-FAC (Kronecker-factored) to avoid explicit second-order computation, keeping overhead linear in parameter count. The framework follows a nested inner-outer loop: the inner loop performs standard federated sparse training (FedAvg-style) on a fixed mask, while the outer loop periodically triggers client-side Fisher-score-based rewiring (prune low-score, grow high-score) and an optional server-side global score-based pruning step to enforce the target density, with layer-wise budgets allocated by the ERK rule.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18 at 10% density, FedFit achieves 65.51% top-1 accuracy, outperforming the best baseline FedRTS (64.54%) by ~1%. On SVHN with ResNet-18, FedFit achieves 86.14% at ρ=0.1, 90.25% at ρ=0.2, and 91.26% at ρ=0.3, surpassing the strongest baseline by up to 7.43 percentage points. On the NLP task (TinyStories with GPT-2-32M), FedFit achieves the lowest perplexity across all tested densities (50%, 30%, 20%). Overall, FedFit improves roughly 3.8% over the strongest baseline on average across all CV benchmarks while maintaining 80% communication savings relative to the dense model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedFit consistently outperforms all seven baselines (FedAvg, FSST, FedDST, FedTiny, FedMef, FedSGC, FedRTS) across the majority of dataset–density–architecture combinations. The largest margins appear on SVHN with ResNet-18, where FedFit leads the best baseline (FedSGC at ρ=0.1/0.2, FedRTS at ρ=0.3) by 4.6–7.4 percentage points, and on CINIC-10 where it gains 2.4–3.5 points over FedRTS. On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, FedFit edges out FedRTS by 0.5–1.4 points across densities. The one notable exception is TinyImageNet with ResNet-18 at ρ=0.2, where FedRTS (5.60%) slightly exceeds FedFit (5.38%), though both remain far below the dense FedAvg (8.97%). On ShuffleNet, FedFit's advantage is most pronounced at low density (e.g., 50.21% vs. 47.44% for FedDST on CIFAR-10 ρ=0.1), while at ρ=0.3 it essentially ties FedTiny (61.03% vs. 61.02%). The ablation (Fig. 6) confirms that removing either the Fisher-based pruning score or the Fisher-based growing score degrades performance, validating that both components contribute to the gains over the FedDST pipeline.

## Relation to Prior Work

FedFit builds directly on the FedDST pipeline (inner-outer loop with periodic mask adjustment) and the broader FDST literature (FedTiny, FedMef, FedSGC, FedRTS), but departs from all of them by replacing heuristic magnitude-based pruning and gradient-based growing with Fisher information scores derived from a second-order Taylor approximation of the loss. The theoretical foundation draws on post-training pruning criteria (OBD/OBS) and the K-FAC approximation (Grosse & Martens, 2016), repurposing them for the non-convergent, heterogeneous federated setting where the original post-training assumptions (g≈0, stable Hessian) are violated. The paper's key conceptual move is showing that the heuristics used by all prior FDST methods are inherited from a regime (near-convergence, homogeneous data) that fundamentally does not hold during federated sparse training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FedFit provides the first theoretically grounded scoring criterion for FDST structure adjustment, with explicit upper bounds on both Taylor truncation and Fisher approximation error, giving practitioners a principled alternative to ad-hoc heuristics. The practical overhead is minimal (0.7 MiB extra memory, O(n) communication), making it deployable on resource-constrained edge devices. Open questions include whether the K-FAC diagonal approximation remains accurate for very deep or transformer-based architectures under extreme non-IID conditions, and whether the separable surrogate (ignoring off-diagonal Fisher interactions) can be tightened without incurring combinatorial cost. The moderate but consistent gains (3–7% over best baseline) suggest the heuristic-to-second-order transition is meaningful but not a silver bullet for closing the full sparse-to-dense gap.
