## Problem

LLM-based social simulations (termed 'Silicon Societies') are rapidly proliferating across policy analysis, epidemiology, and computational social science, yet the field lacks consensus on how to validate them. Evaluation methods are few, underdeveloped, fragmented across disciplines, and rarely shared, creating a serious risk of premature deployment of unvalidated simulators in high-stakes domains. The paper argues the field must pivot from expansion to consolidation, prioritizing methodological standardization grounded in social science and complex systems research.

## Input / Output

The formal abstraction treats a silicon society as a generative process p(τ|X, Θ) over system trajectories, where X is the simulation specification (prompt templates, orchestration logic, environment rules) and Θ encompasses all agent and environment parameters (LLM weights, temperatures, persona and environment initializations). Evaluations are functions L(τ) of trajectories assessing behavioral realism, stability over time, coordination strength, or alignment with empirical data. The proposed simulator project organizes configuration into five schema-constrained plain-text components (simulator, model, environment, evaluation, scenario) that output human-readable action logs and support longitudinal probe systems feeding responses to the evaluation function.

## Method

The paper introduces a minimal trajectory-distribution formalism that makes silicon societies amenable to machine learning methodology, then maps a taxonomy of evaluation fallacies across four axes (distributional validity, agent-human correspondence, design justification, emergence and diversity) to specific components of the simulator decomposition. It defines concrete learning problems ordered from concrete to structural: next-action prediction with individual-level (F1, semantic similarity, JSD) and population-level (KL/JS divergence, kernel density) metrics; component structure learning distinguishing explicit natural-language internal states from implicit parameter-encoded structure; and environment initialization/orchestration as implicit priors over trajectories. The 'frictionless reproducibility' framework specifies a five-component simulator architecture with schema-constrained configuration, longitudinal probes, fixed compute allocation for evaluation, and open science practices, exemplified by the SiliSocS project. The paper closes with a call to action for a benchmark suite (survey response, economic games, opinion dynamics, crisis behavior), an open-source evaluation toolkit for distributional validity and sensitivity analysis, and standardized author/reviewer guidelines for LLM social simulation papers.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not report quantitative experimental results or benchmark scores. Its primary contributions are (1) a formal trajectory-distribution abstraction for 'Silicon Societies' (p(τ|X,Θ)), (2) a taxonomy of 14 evaluation fallacies organized along four axes (distributional validity, agent-human correspondence, design justification, emergence & diversity), and (3) a concrete reproducibility framework with five configurable simulator components (simulator, model, environment, evaluation, scenario). The paper proposes next-action prediction (L_NAP = −log p(a_{t+1}|s_t, a_t, …, s_1, a_1)) as the canonical learning objective and calls for shared benchmarks spanning survey response, economic games, opinion dynamics, and crisis behavior.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not empirically compare its proposed framework against competing methods or baselines. Instead, it surveys and critiques existing simulator platforms (OASIS, AgentSociety, Concordia, generative agents) and persona-simulation studies, identifying specific methodological gaps in each (e.g., lack of ablation studies, untested agent-human correspondence assumptions, single-model dependence). The paper positions its trajectory-distribution formalism as a unifying language that makes these previously implicit critiques precise and testable, but it does not run head-to-head experiments. The closest analogue to a 'comparison' is Table 1, which maps 14 named fallacies to specific components of the simulator formalism, showing how each common pitfall corresponds to a violation of a particular distributional or structural property.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes and unifies a fragmented literature spanning generative agents (Park et al., 2022/2023), large-scale social media simulators (OASIS, AgentSociety), cognitively-inspired agent frameworks (Concordia, Humanoid Agents), and a growing critical validation literature (Larooij & Tornberg, 2025; Wallach et al., 2025; PIMMUR principles). Rather than building on a single prior method, it carves new ground by introducing a minimal formal abstraction (trajectory distribution p(τ|X,Θ)) that provides a common language across these disparate threads, and by reframing validation as a machine-learning methodology problem with well-defined learning problems (next-action prediction, component structure learning, environment initialization). The paper departs from prior validation critiques by moving beyond principle sets (e.g., PIMMUR) to propose concrete, reproducible evaluation protocols grounded in social science measurement traditions and the 'frictionless reproducibility' paradigm from ML.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a genuine and urgent gap: LLM social simulations are being deployed in high-stakes domains (policy analysis, public health, epidemiology) without agreed-upon validation standards, creating risk of premature and potentially harmful use. The formal trajectory abstraction and four-axis taxonomy of evaluation fallacies (distributional validity, agent-human correspondence, design justification, emergence/diversity) give the field a shared vocabulary for diagnosing methodological weaknesses. Key open questions remain: whether LLMs can capture the latent structure of social interactions at all, how to validate simulations when ground truth is ethically or practically unavailable, and whether the proposed benchmarks will actually be adopted across the fragmented community. The paper's impact hinges on whether the interdisciplinary working group and shared infrastructure it calls for materialize.
