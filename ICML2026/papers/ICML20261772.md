## Problem

Existing evaluations of LLM watermark robustness rely on fixed, handcrafted prompts and average-case testing, leaving worst-case vulnerability under adaptive, high-capacity attacks largely unexplored. Prior watermark-removal attacks suffer from ineffectiveness on long-form text (≥500 tokens), poor generalization across schemes, and impractical data requirements (up to 100k samples), obscuring critical vulnerabilities and overstating the security of current watermarking defenses.

## Input / Output

RLCracker is trained on 100 question–watermarked-response pairs (q, wr), each approximately 500 tokens, with no access to the watermark key, detector function, or threshold. At inference time, it takes a watermarked text as input and produces a single paraphrased output that must simultaneously achieve a P-SP semantic similarity score above 0.7 and fall below the detector's threshold. The method is evaluated on texts of 250, 500, and 1500 tokens across four datasets, with the Evasion Success Rate (ESR) measuring the proportion of outputs that are both undetectable and semantically faithful.

## Method

The paper introduces the adaptive robustness radius, a formal worst-case metric quantifying the minimum semantic shift needed to erase a watermark under adaptive control of both the paraphrasing model and attack context. To make this tractable, they lift the paraphrase space into a KL-divergence ball around the local watermark distribution and, under a sub-Gaussian detector-score assumption, derive a computable lower bound (Theorem 4.3) showing that optimizing context and parameters can systematically shrink the radius. RLCracker operationalizes this insight via a GRPO-style token-wise RL objective that jointly maximizes a semantic reward (sigmoid-scaled P-SP score) and a token-wise KL reward pushing the output distribution Q away from the watermark-induced distribution P_wm and toward a human-like distribution P_h, both approximated by a lightweight reference model. The KL reward uses a PPO-style estimator, and a dynamic scaling strategy on the semantic weight adapts emphasis between fidelity and evasion during training. Despite weak supervision from only 100 short samples, a 3B model achieves 98.5% ESR on 1500-token Unigram-marked texts, far exceeding GPT-4o's 6.75%.

## Results

RLCracker achieves a headline 98.5% Evasion Success Rate (ESR) on 1,500-token Unigram watermarks using a 3B model (Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct), compared to just 6.75% for GPT-4o and 3.50% for the Base prompting method. On 500-token EWD watermarks, RLCracker reaches 94.8% ESR with Qwen3-8B, 93.3% with Qwen3-4B, and 91.5% with Qwen3-0.6B, consistently outperforming the best prompting baseline (Think+SysP) by 15–25 points. On longer 1,500-token texts, RLCracker maintains 63–87% ESR across watermark schemes while all non-RL baselines (Base, SysP, SIRA, DIPPER) drop below 20%, and GPT-4o falls to 6.75–10.3%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RLCracker decisively outperforms all three external baselines across every model size, watermark scheme, and text length tested. Against SIRA (Cheng et al., 2025), RLCracker wins by a wide margin: SIRA achieves near-zero ESR (0–24.8%) on 500-token texts and essentially 0% on 1,500-token texts, because although it removes 88% of the Unigram watermark, its P-SP semantic score collapses to 0.47, failing the 0.7 threshold required for ESR. Against DIPPER (Krishna et al., 2023), RLCracker leads by 20–90 points; DIPPER scores 30.3% on 500-token EWD but drops to 1.15% on 1,500-token EWD, confirming its known failure on long-form text. Against GPT-4o (used as the LLM Paraphraser baseline), RLCracker achieves 94.8% vs 71.0% on 500-token EWD (Qwen3-8B) and 98.5% vs 6.75% on 1,500-token Unigram (Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct), a gap exceeding 90 percentage points on the long-text setting. Even the strongest prompting-only baseline (Think+SysP) trails RLCracker by 15–25 points on 500-token texts and by 50+ points on 1,500-token texts, underscoring that RL-based distributional alignment is necessary to close the gap that prompting alone cannot.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GRPO reinforcement learning framework (Shao et al., 2024) for policy optimization, the P-SP semantic similarity metric (Wieting et al., 2022), and Schulman's PPO-style KL estimator (2020), repurposing them for a novel application: adaptive watermark removal. Conceptually, it departs from prior watermark-removal attacks (DIPPER, SIRA, GPT-4o paraphrasing, WS, B⁴) by reframing the problem from instance-level paraphrasing to a distributional shift in KL space, drawing on certified robustness (Cohen et al., 2019; Jia et al., 2019) and distributionally robust optimization to define the adaptive robustness radius. The key methodological departure is using RL to learn an attack policy that simultaneously preserves semantics and maximizes divergence from the watermark distribution, rather than relying on fixed prompts, template-based rewriting, or large-scale supervised training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes that widely-deployed watermarking schemes (KGW, EWD, SWEET, Unigram, SynthID-Text, PF, and others) are far more vulnerable to adaptive attacks than previously reported, with a 3B model achieving 98.5% removal on 1500-token texts versus 6.75% for GPT-4o. The adaptive robustness radius provides a principled, computable metric for worst-case watermark security that watermark designers can use for pre-deployment stress testing. Open questions include whether any watermarking scheme can be made robust against adaptive RL attacks, whether the 100-sample data requirement is truly minimal in practice, and how defenses should evolve given that reasoning-capable models and system prompts alone already degrade watermark resilience by 40+ percentage points.
