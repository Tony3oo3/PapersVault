## Problem

Existing time-series forecasting (TSF) benchmarks for global station weather are limited by small, spatially sparse datasets, lack of operational evaluation metrics (e.g., extreme-event scoring), and no comparison against operational NWP systems. Moreover, current TSF methods ignore physical dynamics such as pressure-wind coupling and energy conservation, causing them to lag behind NWP models in long-range and extreme-event forecasting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261460/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes 48 hours of hourly observations (temperature, dewpoint, wind speed, wind direction, sea level pressure) from 5,672 irregularly distributed global stations, augmented with static latitude/longitude and temporal periodicity markers. It outputs multi-step forecasts at 24, 72, 120, and 168-hour lead times, structured as a physics-based baseline trajectory plus a learned residual correction, rather than a single end-to-end prediction. The irregular station geometry (no regular grid) necessitates graph-based spatial derivative approximation rather than finite-difference computation.

## Method

PhysicsFormer decomposes the forecast into a graph-based neural ODE dynamic core and a Transformer residual. The dynamic core builds a K-nearest-neighbor spatial graph over the irregularly distributed stations to approximate local pressure gradients, advection, and divergence, then autoregressively integrates a lightweight GNN parameterizing coupled mass-momentum and thermodynamic tendencies via a 4th-order Runge-Kutta solver. A Transformer encoder-decoder models global inter-station interactions through self-attention and produces a residual correction that captures unresolved nonlinear effects and localized anomalies. Training combines standard data-fidelity MSE with two physics-informed regularizers: a pressure-wind alignment loss enforcing temporal consistency between pressure and wind variations via station-specific learnable sensitivity parameters, and an energy-aware smoothness loss penalizing second-order temporal differences in temperature and wind speed.

## Results

On WEATHER-5K (5,672 global stations, 10 years of hourly data), PhysicsFormer achieves the best TSF performance across all four primary variables: Temperature MAE 2.34, Dewpoint MAE 2.51, Wind Speed MAE 1.44, and Sea Level Pressure MAE 3.53 (averaged over 24/72/120/168-step lead times with 48-step input). The operational NWP model ECMWF-HRES still leads on Temperature (MAE 1.94), Dewpoint (MAE 2.06), and Sea Level Pressure (MAE 1.29), though PhysicsFormer edges it out on Wind Speed (1.44 vs. 1.56). For extreme-event prediction (SEDI at 99.5th percentile), PhysicsFormer reaches 30.6% for Temperature and 62.6% for Sea Level Pressure, substantially outperforming the best TSF baseline (iTransformer at 14.1% and 15.9%) but still trailing ECMWF-HRES (37.4% and 77.5%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PhysicsFormer outperforms all 16 TSF baselines on every variable, with the largest margins over GNN-based MTGNN (e.g., Temperature MAE 2.34 vs. 10.33) and MLP-based STID (2.34 vs. 4.71). Among Transformer baselines, it beats the best (Pyraformer, Temp MAE 2.49) by 0.15 and iTransformer (2.64) by 0.30. Against the operational NWP model ECMWF-HRES, PhysicsFormer wins on Wind Speed (1.44 vs. 1.56) but trails on Temperature (2.34 vs. 1.94), Dewpoint (2.51 vs. 2.06), and Sea Level Pressure (3.53 vs. 1.29), confirming that data-driven TSF still lags behind physics-based NWP for most variables at longer lead times. For extreme-event SEDI, PhysicsFormer narrows the gap considerably (e.g., Temperature 99.5th: 30.6% vs. iTransformer's 14.1%) yet remains below ECMWF-HRES (37.4%). Notably, larger-parameter models like Corrformer (666M params) and Timer (84M) do not outperform smaller models like Pyraformer (7.5M) or PhysicsFormer (19.3M), suggesting parameter count is not the key driver of weather forecasting accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of time-series forecasting and operational meteorology. While prior TSF-for-weather work (Corrformer, Wu et al. 2023) demonstrated feasibility on small datasets (3,850 stations, 2 variables, 2 years), this paper scales the problem to 5,672 stations, 5 variables, and 10 years, and for the first time benchmarks TSF methods directly against an operational NWP system (ECMWF-HRES) using operational-grade metrics including the SEDI extreme-event index. The PhysicsFormer architecture repurposes the graph-neural-ODE dynamic core idea from physics-informed neural networks and couples it with a Transformer residual branch, departing from purely data-driven TSF by embedding pressure-wind alignment and energy-aware smoothness as auxiliary losses rather than hard constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The systematic quantification of the gap between academic TSF models and operational NWP—particularly the stark extreme-event SEDI deficit (TSF ~10–15% vs. NWP 37–82%)—provides a clear target for the community and validates that current TSF methods are not yet operationally deployable for critical weather warnings. The finding that parameter count does not monotonically improve accuracy (Pyraformer at 7.5M params outperforms Timer at 84M) challenges the scaling narrative in time-series forecasting and points toward efficient, physics-grounded architectures. Open questions include whether hybrid TSF+NWP bias-correction pipelines can close the remaining gap, how to handle the sparse station coverage in the Southern Hemisphere, and whether the physics-informed losses generalize beyond the five variables tested.
