## Problem

Query-based universal sound separation models persistently exhibit residual interference in complex scenes because they are trained on in-the-wild datasets (AudioSet, VGGSound) with weak labels and severe event co-occurrence, causing models to learn spurious correlations between background noise and target categories. The prevailing remedy—brute-force scaling to million-hour datasets like SAM-Audio—imposes prohibitive computational costs. The paper asks whether prioritizing purity of supervised signals over data volume can achieve competitive separation with far fewer hours.

## Input / Output

The pipeline ingests raw audio from 12 public datasets (totaling ~2,442 hours), segments them into 10-second windows with 5-second overlap, and standardizes all clips to 44.1 kHz via Apollo super-resolution for bandwidth-limited sources or anti-aliasing downsampling for high-resolution inputs. The resulting Hive dataset contains 19.6 million synthetic mixtures (2–5 sources each, 4 s training / 10 s test clips, RMS-normalized to 0.1, SNR ∈ [−5, 5] dB) across 283 semantically refined classes, with 292 hours of distinct unmixed sources reserved for validation and testing. At inference, the trained models accept a mixed audio signal plus a multimodal query (text, audio, or visual) and output the isolated target source waveform.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-stage automated pipeline: (1) ontology reconstruction that prunes AudioSet's 474 leaf nodes to 283 separation-oriented categories by merging synonyms, aggregating fine-grained subtypes, and excluding abstract acoustic attributes; (2) single-event semantic-acoustic alignment that first filters multi-event segments via Qwen3-Omni zero-shot polyphony detection, then performs coarse-to-fine relabeling using a ZipFormer-based audio-tag model for parent-node prediction followed by Qwen3-Omni leaf-node selection within the constrained candidate set; and (3) super-resolution-based standardization to 44.1 kHz. For mixture synthesis, a binary semantic compatibility matrix M ∈ {0,1}^{N×N}—derived by querying Qwen3-Omni on pairwise event co-occurrence—governs sampling so that only semantically plausible tuples (pairwise M_{l_i,l_j}=1) are mixed, avoiding implausible combinations like aquatic animals with urban traffic. Representative discriminative (AudioSep, ResUNet + CLAP) and generative (FlowSep, rectified flow matching) models trained on Hive with only ~2.4k hours of source audio achieve competitive SDR, perceptual quality, and zero-shot generalization compared to SAM-Audio trained on ~1M hours, while a controlled shortcut analysis confirms reduced reliance on co-occurrence statistical biases.

## Results

On the Hive test set (2-to-5 source mixtures), AudioSep trained on Hive (2.4k hours) achieves SDR of 5.67 dB, SI-SDR of 5.02 dB, and a MUSHRA score of 68.4, surpassing SAM-Audio (trained on ~1M hours) which scores 62.6 MUSHRA and OQ 2.90. FlowSep trained on Hive reaches MUSHRA 61.8 and OQ 3.05, also exceeding SAM-Audio's OQ of 2.90. On third-party benchmarks, AudioSep (Hive) raises USS-Bench SDR from −1.86 dB (original) to 2.29 dB and MUSDB18-HQ SDR from −1.01 dB to 1.36 dB, using only ~0.2% of SAM-Audio's training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AudioSep (Hive) dominates all discriminative baselines: it outperforms the original AudioSep (14.1k hours) by +3.30 dB SDR and +7.5 MUSHRA points, and decisively beats LASS-Net (−2.97 dB SDR), CLIPSep (−6.20 dB SDR), and OmniSep (−2.85 dB SDR), which all collapse to negative SDR on Hive's decorrelated mixtures. Against the generative baselines, AudioSep (Hive) surpasses SAM-Audio (the ~1M-hour foundation model) on MUSHRA (68.4 vs. 62.6), OQ (3.34 vs. 2.90), precision (3.40 vs. 3.33), LPAPS (3.86 vs. 5.21), and FAD (0.80 vs. 1.03), while FlowSep (Hive) exceeds SAM-Audio on OQ (3.05 vs. 2.90) and precision (3.35 vs. 3.33) but trails on MUSHRA (61.8 vs. 62.6). FlowSep (Hive) also improves over its original checkpoint by +7.1 MUSHRA points and +0.29 OQ. The controlled shortcut analysis (Table 6) further shows that Hive training reduces the co-occurrence shortcut gap for AudioSep from 1.41 dB to 0.39 dB, confirming that gains stem from reduced reliance on spurious correlations rather than distribution shift.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself within the well-established query-based universal sound separation (USS) task, where prior methods (LASS-Net, CLIPSep, AudioSep, OmniSep, SAM-Audio) have focused on architectural innovations—discriminative masking, flow matching, diffusion-based generation—while relying on large-scale in-the-wild datasets (AudioSet, VGGSound) with weak labels and high event co-occurrence. The paper departs from this scale-driven paradigm by arguing that the data bottleneck, not the model architecture, is the primary limiter of separation purity. Its contribution is a data-cleaning and synthesis pipeline (ontology reconstruction, LLM-based purification via Qwen3-Omni, semantic compatibility mixing, super-resolution standardization) that produces the Hive dataset, rather than a new separation architecture; it reuses existing AudioSep and FlowSep backbones to demonstrate the dataset's value.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ~2.4k hours of purified, semantically consistent data can match or exceed models trained on ~1M hours (SAM-Audio) challenges the prevailing 'scale everything' assumption in audio foundation models and offers a reproducible, low-cost pathway for the broader research community. Limitations include reliance on LLM-based relabeling (potential bias propagation), synthetic mixtures that may not capture real-world acoustic variability (no room impulse responses), and the fact that on VGGClean_eval the Hive-trained model shows lower reference-based SDR than the original, suggesting the gains are partly distribution-specific. Open questions include whether this purity-over-scale principle generalizes to even broader ontologies, whether naturally recorded (non-synthetic) benchmarks would show the same advantage, and whether the LLM-derived co-occurrence matrix introduces systematic blind spots for rare or novel event combinations.
