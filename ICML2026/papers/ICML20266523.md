## Problem

ML peer review faces a scalability crisis as submission volumes grow exponentially (NeurIPS 10.4× from 2014–2024, ICML 48% YoY) while qualified reviewer capacity remains finite, producing inconsistent scores, superficial reviews, and limited rebuttal impact. Existing AI tools address isolated tasks (plagiarism detection, reviewer matching) but no integrated ecosystem exists that assists authors, reviewers, and Area Chairs across the full review lifecycle. The paper argues the critical bottleneck is not model capability but the absence of structured deliberation data—score-change rationales, rebuttal-to-judgment links, and AC reasoning traces—that would enable AI to learn the reasoning process behind peer review rather than merely imitate surface outcomes.

## Input / Output

The system consumes full peer-review cycles from OpenReview (paper title, abstract, full text, initial reviews with scores, author rebuttals, and final scores) alongside external scientific literature databases for retrieval-augmented verification. Outputs span multiple granularities: free-text review components (strengths, weaknesses, rebuttal points), numerical rating predictions (initial, final, and score-change), multi-dimensional reviewer report cards scored on coverage/specificity/evidence-base/constructiveness, and AC-facing decision-support summaries surfacing conflicts, gaps, and consensus. Evaluation uses an LLM-as-judge protocol that counts how many human-identified points are semantically captured in the generated output, reporting recall as Hits/Real Points.

## Method

The paper uses OpenAI's O3 model with few-shot in-context learning (n=0–3 examples) on ICLR 2024/2025 OpenReview data to demonstrate LLM potential across three tasks: review component generation, rating prediction, and reviewer report card generation. For review generation, the model produces strengths, weaknesses, and rebuttal points from paper content; for rating prediction, it forecasts initial ratings, final ratings, and score changes with MAE/RMSE metrics, revealing diminishing returns beyond n=2 in-context examples. The broader proposed ecosystem layers Retrieval Augmented Verification (connecting LLMs to Semantic Scholar/arXiv for factual grounding), AI-powered review quality feedback, content provenance detection via perplexity and watermarking, and structured AC decision support. The central technical argument is that these tools require a new data infrastructure—active elicitation interfaces that prompt reviewers to rationalize score changes and link specific rebuttal sentences to judgment shifts—transforming the review process itself into a structured data-labeling task without significantly increasing human workload.

## Results

On ICLR 2025 OpenReview data, the LLM (OpenAI O3) achieves a recall of 0.927±0.060 for identifying review strengths, 0.632±0.000 for weaknesses, and 0.911±0.040 for rebuttal points, revealing a significant 'critical thinking gap' in flaw detection. For rating prediction via in-context learning, the best MAE is 2.2857±0.0095 for initial ratings (n=2 examples) and 0.6709±0.0052 for final ratings (n=1), while score-change prediction remains the hardest task (best MAE 0.7342±0.0057 at n=0). Scaling in-context examples from n=0 to n=3 yields diminishing returns, indicating ICL alone is insufficient for complex regression without fine-tuning on structured rationale data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper with illustrative experiments rather than a method comparison, so no external baselines are benchmarked. The experiments instead characterize the LLM's own performance across three peer-review sub-tasks (component generation, rating prediction, score-change prediction) to identify where AI assistance is promising versus where human judgment remains indispensable. The key finding is an asymmetry: the LLM excels at identifying strengths (recall 0.927) and rebuttal points (0.911) but substantially underperforms on weaknesses (0.632), which the authors interpret as evidence that current models lack the critical depth needed for rigorous scientific appraisal. For rating prediction, in-context learning plateaus quickly (MAE improves from 2.77 to 2.29 for initial ratings as n goes 0→2, then degrades at n=3), suggesting that without fine-tuning on structured deliberation data, LLMs cannot reliably predict human evaluative judgments. The authors frame these gaps not as fundamental model limitations but as consequences of missing supervision signals in current public datasets, motivating their central call for richer, structured peer-review process data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper extends the trajectory of AI-in-peer-review from isolated administrative tools (reviewer matching, plagiarism detection) and single-task LLM applications (review generation, related-work suggestion via LitLLM) toward a unified, data-driven ecosystem spanning authors, reviewers, and Area Chairs. It builds on retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al., 2020) as the grounding mechanism for factual verification, and on the ICLR 2025 reviewer-assistant experiment (Thakkar et al., 2025) as evidence that LLM feedback can shift human review behavior. The key conceptual departure is arguing that the binding constraint is not model capability but the absence of structured deliberation data (score-change rationales, rebuttal-to-judgment links), reframing the problem from 'build a better reviewer LLM' to 'instrument the review process to produce training signal.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and underappreciated bottleneck: without structured traces of *why* scores changed and *which* rebuttal sentence resolved *which* concern, even strong LLMs can only imitate review outcomes rather than learn the reasoning process. This reframing has practical implications for platform design (OpenReview, conference organizers) and for the data-collection burden placed on reviewers. Open questions include whether low-friction rationalization prompts can be adopted at scale without degrading reviewer experience, whether AI-content detection can be made fair to non-native speakers, and whether the proposed tiered-access data model can balance privacy with research utility. As a position paper with only illustrative experiments, its impact is agenda-setting rather than solution-providing.
