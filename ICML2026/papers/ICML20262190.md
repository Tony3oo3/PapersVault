## Problem

Existing error Slice Discovery Methods (SDMs) rely on auxiliary language-based models (e.g., CLIP) to generate explanations that are disconnected from the analysed model's internal decision-making process, making them only approximate and potentially inaccurate or biased. This paper addresses the gap by reframing error slice discovery through Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs), whose predictions are directly dependent on human-understandable semantic concepts, enabling faithful, model-grounded failure explanations.

## Input / Output

The method requires a trained CBM (concept encoder g and label predictor f), a set of mispredicted validation samples Ψ_val with their ground-truth concept annotations, and operates in the concept-logit space (inverse sigmoid of concept probabilities) rather than raw embedding or probability space. It produces error slices as GMM clusters in the restricted error-prone concept-logit space, keyword-concepts per slice that encode both the concept's semantic identity and whether its prediction was correct or incorrect, and a bounded Slice Informativeness score (SI ∈ [0,1]) combining misprediction coherence and semantic compactness for prioritising which slices to analyse first.

## Method

CB-SLICE operates in three steps within the CBM architecture. First, it filters to error-prone concepts using class-wise ECTP scores (Expected Change in Target Prediction via KL-divergence under concept interventions), selecting the top-t_e concepts per class that most drive downstream misclassifications. Second, it forms error slices by clustering the corresponding concept-logit representations with a GMM, augmented by two auxiliary binary cross-entropy losses that push slice members to share both ground-truth concept labels and predicted concept values, ensuring each slice captures a coherent concept-level error pattern. Third, it explains each slice via ECSA (Expected Change in Slice Assignment) scores, identifying the keyword-concepts whose intervention most alters slice membership. A slice prioritisation strategy then ranks slices by a combined score of misprediction coherence (entropy of the mispredicted label distribution) and semantic compactness (cosine similarity to the slice centroid), downweighted by a size penalty to filter noisy small slices.

## Results

CB-SLICE consistently outperforms all baselines on both Precision@10 and MGF across four benchmarks (Waterbirds, CelebA, MetaShift, MNIST-Sum) and two CBM training strategies (sequential and joint). Headline numbers include Precision@10 of 0.83 and MGF of 0.76 on Waterbirds (joint CBM), 0.92/0.66 on CelebA (sequential CBM), 0.91/0.86 on MetaShift (joint CBM), and a perfect 1.0/0.95 on MNIST-Sum (joint CBM). The largest margin over the strongest baseline (Domino) reaches ~65 percentage points on Waterbirds Precision@10 (0.83 vs. 0.62). The only near-tie is MetaShift under sequential CBM, where GEORGE marginally leads on Precision@10 (0.83 vs. 0.82).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CB-SLICE dominates all five quantitative baselines (Domino, GEORGE, HiBug2, Spotlight, K-Means) on both Precision@10 and MGF in nearly every setting. The largest gaps appear on Waterbirds, where CB-SLICE (0.83 Precision@10, joint CBM) surpasses Domino (0.62) by 21 points and GEORGE (0.18) by 65 points, and on MNIST-Sum where it reaches a perfect 1.0 Precision@10 versus HiBug2's 0.50. The sole exception is MetaShift under sequential CBM training, where GEORGE narrowly leads on Precision@10 (0.83 vs. 0.82) though CB-SLICE still wins on MGF (0.76 vs. 0.69). Spotlight and K-Means are consistently the weakest baselines, scoring below 0.25 on most datasets, confirming that loss-based or embedding-only clustering without error-aware signals is insufficient. For qualitative explanation quality, CB-SLICE's concept-grounded keywords (e.g., 'medium size', 'no black bill') are shown to be more faithful than Bias-to-Text (which produces generic terms like 'beach', 'ocean') and Domino (which can produce misleading labels like 'man' for a female error slice), though these comparisons are qualitative rather than scored.

## Relation to Prior Work

CB-SLICE builds on Concept Bottleneck Models (Koh et al., 2020) and the ECTP intervention score (Shin et al., 2023), repurposing them as the backbone for error slice discovery. It departs from prior SDMs (Domino, GEORGE, Spotlight, HiBug2) that discover slices in external embedding spaces and then rely on auxiliary language models (CLIP, ClipCap) for post-hoc textual explanations. The key conceptual shift is grounding both slice formation (GMM clustering in concept-logit space) and failure-mode explanation (ECSA-based keyword extraction) entirely within the CBM's internal concept representations, making explanations a direct consequence of the model's own decision logic rather than an external approximation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By tying explanations to the model's actual concept predictions, CB-SLICE addresses a fundamental fidelity gap in prior SDMs where auxiliary-model-generated explanations can be inaccurate or inherit societal biases (e.g., attributing errors to 'native' or 'man' in CelebA). The method also distinguishes between two qualitatively different failure types—systematic concept mispredictions versus rare but correctly-predicted concept combinations—enabling targeted mitigation strategies. However, its applicability is constrained by the need for complete, faithful concept annotations and a trained CBM, leaving open the question of how well it generalises to noisy, incomplete, or automatically discovered concept sets. Integrating CB-SLICE with downstream mitigation (e.g., targeted data augmentation or concept reweighting) to close the discovery-to-fix loop is a natural next step.
