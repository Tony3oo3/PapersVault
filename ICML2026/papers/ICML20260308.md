## Problem

Standardized LLM benchmarks report aggregated scores that obscure fine-grained weaknesses, hiding both specific concept domains where models systematically underperform (model gaps) and concept domains inadequately represented in the benchmarks themselves (benchmark gaps). Existing disaggregation approaches—hand-curated topic labels or embedding-based clusters—provide only coarse-grained insights and are difficult to scale to the thousands of fine-grained concepts that matter for real-world model behavior.

## Input / Output

The method requires an LLM with a pre-trained sparse autoencoder (SAE) attached at a specific layer, a set of benchmark datasets providing input token sequences and per-datapoint scoring results (normalized to [0,1]), and the SAE's autointerpretability labels mapping each of the tens of thousands of concept dimensions to a textual description. The output is a per-concept score vector across the full SAE dictionary (e.g., ~65K concepts for Llama 3.1 8B's Goodfire SAE), yielding both a benchmark coverage score χ_bench^(c) and a model performance score χ_model^(c) for every concept, from which missing, underrepresented, and overrepresented concepts are classified by threshold and percentile.

## Method

Competency Gaps (CG) decomposes LLM evaluation into fine-grained, interpretable axes by projecting each benchmark datapoint's token sequence through a pre-trained SAE to obtain per-concept activation scores, averaged over tokens to prevent length bias. Benchmark coverage χ_bench^(b,c) normalizes a concept's total activation in a benchmark by the mean activation across all concepts in that benchmark, making scores scale-invariant and directly comparable across benchmarks of different sizes. Model performance χ_model^(b,c) computes an activation-weighted average of per-datapoint scores, so each concept's performance reflects only the datapoints on which that concept is actually active. Cross-benchmark scores average over all benchmarks where the concept activates, and gaps are identified by thresholding (missing if below ε = e^{-5}) and percentile classification (bottom/top decile). The key design choice is grounding the analysis in the model's own internal SAE representations rather than external text embeddings, which keeps the decomposition model-specific and interpretable while adding only one linear projection of cost per token at a single layer.

## Results

The CG method identifies 314 concepts (1% of the Llama 3.1 8B SAE dictionary) as entirely missing from a 10-benchmark suite, with the cross-benchmark coverage distribution exhibiting strong right skew. LLM-as-judge relevance annotation shows that no single benchmark exercises more than a slice of the concept space: the relevant fraction ranges from 4.9% (WinoGrande) to 34.5% (Vectara) for the Llama SAE (45K+ concepts). Robustness is confirmed by low standard deviations under 100 random 20%-subsampling runs (0.014 for X_model, 0.025 for X_bench), and a causal validation on Qwen3-4B shows a foreign-name gap: 80% accuracy with unnamed protagonists versus only 30% with names 'Omar' and 'Mahmoud' on LogicBench-style prompts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CG against four prior weakness-identification methods in dedicated appendices (M–P): garak, AutoDetect, Arena-Hard-Auto, and EvalTree. CG is not a direct accuracy competitor but a complementary lens: it surfaces concept-level gaps that these methods do not systematically cover. Against garak (security-focused probing) and AutoDetect (LLM-agent-based weakness detection), CG identifies a broader and more interpretable set of gaps grounded in the model's own internal representations, including non-safety gaps like time reasoning and meta-cognition. Against Arena-Hard-Auto (preference-based evaluation), CG provides per-concept decomposition rather than a single aggregate score, revealing that high arena scores can mask specific concept-level weaknesses. Against EvalTree (hierarchical capability trees on MATH), CG operates at a finer granularity (tens of thousands of SAE concepts vs. a small number of tree nodes) and extends beyond math to all benchmark types. The paper positions CG as additive rather than superior: it recovers previously documented gaps (e.g., sycophancy) while also surfacing novel ones (e.g., 'appeals to intuition in reasoning') that none of the compared methods explicitly flag.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the sparse autoencoder (SAE) interpretability literature (Bricken et al., Cunningham et al., Templeton) and autointerpretability pipelines (McGrath et al.) to repurpose SAE concept activations as a decomposition axis for benchmark evaluation. It departs from prior weakness-identification methods (EvalTree, AutoDetect, garak) and benchmark-quality analyses (CLEAR, SMART Filtering) by grounding the analysis in the model's own internal representations rather than external embeddings or hand-curated topic labels, and by unifying benchmark-gap and model-gap detection under a single concept-level framework. The key conceptual departure is that the evaluation axes are derived from the model being evaluated, making the decomposition model-specific and interpretable in the model's representational geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central insight—that benchmark gaps and model gaps are structurally entangled, so that underrepresented concepts in benchmarks systematically go unaddressed by model developers—reframes how the evaluation ecosystem should be iterated. The method is practical (open-source, web tool, ~one extra projection of compute) and validated across five model families and sixteen benchmarks, but it is limited to concepts already present in the SAE dictionary, does not control for item difficulty, and treats concept activation as attribution rather than capability. Open questions include calibrating for the 'hard-question confound,' automating SAE layer selection, and whether the approach extends to non-text modalities or to detecting capabilities the model lacks entirely.
