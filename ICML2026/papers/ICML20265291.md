## Problem

Speculative inference accelerates LLM decoding but provides no inherent safety guarantees, and existing safety defenses are fundamentally incompatible with the draft-verify mechanism: they either introduce additional computation or disrupt draft-verify alignment, negating the acceleration benefits. This reveals a structural gap between current safety methods and speculative decoding that prevents joint optimization of speed and safety.

## Input / Output

At each generation step, the system receives the current sequence (prompt plus previously accepted segments) and a candidate segment proposed by the draft model. The target model processes the concatenated input (sequence ⊕ candidate ⊕ quality scoring prompt) in a single forward pass, producing both a discrete quality score (0–9, read from the LM head's next-token logits over digit tokens) and a continuous safety score (0–1, from a boundary-aligned MLP probe on the hidden state at the last token of the candidate segment, immediately before the scoring prompt). The output is the next accepted segment appended to the sequence, or a safety-guided multi-sampled replacement when the safety threshold is violated.

## Method

SafeSpec attaches a lightweight two-layer MLP safety head (d → d/2 → 1, ~13–33M parameters) to the frozen target model, extracting the hidden state at a boundary-aligned position to avoid interference from the scoring prompt; this incurs zero additional forward-pass cost since the target model must process the full sequence for quality scoring anyway. Verification is hierarchical: the safety score is checked first (threshold τ_s = 0.4), and only if safe does the quality score (threshold τ_q = 9) determine acceptance versus target-model regeneration. When the safety threshold is violated, SafeSpec activates a rollback-and-reflect mode: it reverts to the previous clean state, injects a self-reflection prompt to reshape the conditional distribution toward safe continuations, then draws K = 20 candidates from the draft model and selects the one maximizing the safety score. The safety head is trained with binary cross-entropy on step-level prefixes (segmented by newlines) labeled by Qwen3Guard-Gen-8B, using a 1:1 safe-to-unsafe ratio drawn from AdvBench, DeepInception, and XSTest prompts. The probabilistic justification is that jailbreaks shift probability mass toward harmful trajectories without eliminating safe ones, so increasing K strictly raises recovery probability as long as p_safe > 0, while rollback and reflection jointly increase p_safe to reduce the required sampling budget.

## Results

On Qwen3-32B, SafeSpec achieves an average Attack Success Rate (ASR) of 7% across seven jailbreak attack categories, reducing ASR by 15 percentage points over the strongest baseline (SecDecoding at 22%) while maintaining only 10% over-refusal on XSTest (vs. 42% for SecDecoding). On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B, SafeSpec similarly achieves 7% AVG ASR with 12% over-refusal. Critically, SafeSpec preserves a 2.06× inference speedup on benign workloads (GSM8K, MATH, GPQA-Diamond), comparable to the safety-free Specreason baseline (2.14×), whereas decoding-time defenses like SafeDecoding and SecDecoding actually slow inference below 1× (0.82× and 0.61× respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SafeSpec decisively outperforms all seven baselines on the primary safety metric (AVG ASR). Against the strongest safety baseline, SecDecoding (Wang et al., 2025), SafeSpec reduces AVG ASR from 22% to 7% on Qwen3-32B while cutting over-refusal from 42% to 10%; on DeepSeek-70B, SafeSpec (7%) beats SafeDecoding (Xu et al., 2024b) at 9% with comparable over-refusal. Prompt-based defenses (Self-Reminder and RPO) reduce ASR to ~31% but offer no inference speedup and remain vulnerable to reasoning-hijacking attacks like H-CoT (68% and 38% ASR respectively). The acceleration-only baselines (Speculative Decoding and Specreason) provide no safety benefit whatsoever, with ASR matching or exceeding the no-defense baseline. The standalone Guard + Hard Refusal pattern (Qwen3Guard-0.6B) achieves near-zero ASR (2%) but at the cost of 22% over-refusal and zero speedup, whereas SafeSpec maintains 2.06× speedup on benign workloads with only 10% over-refusal. On general capability (GSM8K/MATH/GPQA), SafeSpec preserves target-model accuracy (96%/78%/52% on Qwen3-32B) while decoding-time defenses like SafeDecoding and SecDecoding actually degrade inference speed below 1× (0.82× and 0.61×), making them impractical for latency-sensitive deployment.

## Relation to Prior Work

SafeSpec sits at the intersection of three research threads: speculative inference (Leviathan et al. 2023; Specreason by Pan et al. 2025), latent representation probing for safety (Arditi et al. 2024; Gao et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2024b), and inference-time safety decoding (SafeDecoding; SecDecoding). Its key departure is treating these as a unified problem rather than composable modules: prior work either naively stacks prompt-level defenses onto speculative pipelines (yielding ASR > 30%) or applies decoding-time interventions that disrupt the draft-verify alignment and negate speedup. SafeSpec instead embeds a boundary-aligned safety head directly into the target model's forward pass and replaces hard refusal with a probabilistic rollback-and-reflect multi-sampling recovery, framing jailbreaks as distributional shifts over generative trajectories rather than binary pass/fail events.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that inference-time safety and speculative acceleration are not in fundamental tension, providing a practical blueprint for deploying fast LLMs without sacrificing robustness against evolving jailbreak attacks. However, several limitations remain: the safety head must be trained per model (though lightweight), the approach incurs sub-1× throughput on adversarial inputs (3.81 Safety Mode triggers per prompt), and the safety head's inherent false-positive rate necessitates the multi-sampling correction loop. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to multi-turn progressive jailbreaks, whether adversarial attacks could specifically target the safety head's decision boundary, and whether the K=20 sampling budget is sufficient for increasingly sophisticated reasoning-hijacking attacks like H-CoT.
