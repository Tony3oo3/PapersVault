## Problem

Gradient-based sufficient dimension reduction methods face a fundamental tension: ambient-space localization (OPG) suffers from the curse of dimensionality, while reduced-space refinement (RMAVE) incurs at least quadratic-in-n cost per outer iteration from dense pairwise kernel weights and coordinate-descent solvers. No existing procedure provides convergence guarantees for the underlying non-convex optimization, and RMAVE's dependence on OPG initialization limits its effectiveness at high ambient dimension.

## Input / Output

The method consumes n i.i.d. pairs (X_i, Y_i) with X_i ∈ R^p and scalar Y_i, plus a target subspace dimension d ≪ p. It outputs a p×d matrix B with orthonormal columns (B^T B = I_d) representing the estimated central mean subspace; the output is defined up to right multiplication by any orthogonal Q ∈ O(d), i.e., it is a point on the Grassmannian Gr(p,d). The k-NN neighborhoods are computed in the projected space R^d using a KD-tree, with k calibrated to match Silverman's effective kernel neighborhood size (k = ⌈n^{4/(d+4)}⌉), and the index is rebuilt every τ iterations as the subspace estimate evolves.

## Method

SMAVE recasts the empirical MAVE criterion as maximizing a smooth O(d)-invariant objective F(B) on the Stiefel manifold St(p,d), whose Riemannian gradient admits a closed form that already lies in the tangent space (no explicit projection needed). The algorithm performs mini-batch Riemannian stochastic gradient ascent with heavy-ball momentum and QR retraction, using sparse k-NN weights computed in the projected space R^d rather than the ambient R^p, which eliminates the curse of dimensionality in localization. A key design choice is uniform random initialization on St(p,d) (via QR of a Gaussian matrix) instead of RMAVE's OPG warm-start, decoupling the method from OPG's degraded ambient-space neighborhoods at high p. The k-NN index is periodically refreshed every τ iterations via KD-tree construction on {B_t^T X_i}, balancing gradient fidelity against computational cost. For a simplified variant (fixed neighborhoods, no momentum), the paper proves almost-sure convergence to a critical point and an O(1/√(mT)) non-asymptotic rate matching the optimal stochastic first-order scaling for non-convex problems.

## Results

On synthetic benchmarks (d=2, p∈{50,100,200}, n∈{1000,2000,5000}), SMAVE achieves squared subspace distance m² of 0.03–1.02, reducing error by 2–9× over RMAVE (0.33–1.48) while running 10–35× faster (e.g., 5.12s vs 173.88s at n=5000, p=200). On four real datasets, SMAVE achieves the best test MSE on Gas Sensor (p=128: 0.018 vs RMAVE 0.025, p=0.027) and is statistically indistinguishable from RMAVE on Seoul Bike (p=0.47), while RMAVE retains a modest edge on Wine Quality (0.498 vs 0.511) and Pumadyn (0.092 vs 0.127) at 2–3 orders of magnitude higher runtime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against OPG, SMAVE uniformly dominates: on synthetic data, OPG's m² stays near 1.9–2.0 (essentially random) across all (n,p) configurations, while SMAVE achieves 0.03–1.02; on real data, SMAVE outperforms OPG on all four datasets (e.g., 0.018 vs 0.026 on Gas Sensor, 0.127 vs 0.174 on Pumadyn). Against RMAVE, the outcome depends on ambient dimension: SMAVE wins decisively on synthetic data at p≥100 (e.g., m²=0.25 vs 0.69 at n=5000, p=200) and on the high-dimensional Gas Sensor dataset (p=128: 0.018 vs 0.025, p=0.027), but RMAVE retains a statistically significant edge on the lower-dimensional Wine Quality (p=11: 0.498 vs 0.511, p=9.2×10⁻⁵) and Pumadyn (p=32: 0.092 vs 0.127, p=9.4×10⁻⁸) datasets, though the absolute MSE gaps are small (0.013 and 0.035) while runtime differs by 2–3 orders of magnitude. On Seoul Bike (p=14), the two methods are statistically indistinguishable (p=0.47). The paper attributes SMAVE's high-p advantage to its random Stiefel initialization and stochastic exploration, which escape the suboptimal basins that trap RMAVE's OPG-warm-started coordinate descent when ambient-space kernel bandwidths degrade.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the MAVE/RMAVE family of gradient-based sufficient dimension reduction methods (Xia et al., 2002), which estimate the central mean subspace via local polynomial regression. The key conceptual departure is a Riemannian reformulation: the authors show that MAVE minimizers target the same Grassmannian as OPG (unifying two major SDR families), then recast the empirical criterion as a smooth maximization on the Stiefel manifold with a closed-form Riemannian gradient. Algorithmically, SMAVE replaces RMAVE's dense kernel weights and coordinate descent with sparse projected-space k-NN weights and stochastic Riemannian gradient ascent, and uses random initialization on St(p,d) rather than the OPG warm-start that RMAVE requires.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The quadratic-in-n per-iteration cost of RMAVE is a genuine bottleneck for moderate-to-high-dimensional SDR, and SMAVE's 10–35× speedup while matching or exceeding accuracy is practically significant for that community. The convergence guarantees (almost-sure convergence and O(1/√T) rate) fill a notable gap, as prior MAVE optimization lacked any convergence theory. Open questions include extending the analysis to the full SMAVE algorithm with time-varying neighborhoods and momentum, deriving explicit deviation inequalities for the empirical risk minimizer, and adapting the framework to classification or non-Gaussian responses (e.g., via MADE deviance).
