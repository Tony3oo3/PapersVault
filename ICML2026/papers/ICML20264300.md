## Problem

The locate-then-edit paradigm for model editing (ROME, MEMIT, AlphaEdit) is widely adopted to erase sensitive memorized data from LLMs without retraining, yet no prior work has examined whether the editing process itself creates a leakage side-channel. This paper reveals that the low-rank parameter updates (ΔW) inadvertently encode a mathematical fingerprint of the edited subjects, enabling an attacker with access to pre- and post-edit weights to reverse-engineer the very data the edit was meant to remove.

## Input / Output

The white-box attack consumes the pre-edit weights θ, post-edit weights θ′ (from which ΔW is computed), the covariance matrix C (typically estimated from Wikipedia), and two candidate pools: a subject pool S_cand and a prompt template pool R_cand. It outputs the top-N recovered subjects, the top-N_r recovered prompt templates per subject, and the original sensitive outputs (obtained by querying the pre-edit model with the recovered subject-prompt pair). The defense additionally takes a set of decoy subjects and a camouflage strength α, and produces a modified weight update ΔW_defense that preserves editing efficacy on the true keys while redirecting the row-space fingerprint to the decoy-augmented subspace.

## Method

KSTER is a two-stage reverse-engineering attack grounded in the algebraic structure of locate-then-edit updates. Stage I (Subject Inference) applies the Woodbury matrix identity to show RowSpace(ΔW·C) ⊆ ColSpace(K), then performs SVD on M = ΔW·C to extract the top-N right singular vectors spanning the key space; each candidate subject's activation (obtained via a generic template) is scored by its projection coefficient onto this subspace, and the top-N are selected. Stage II (Prompt Recovery) exploits the entropy collapse induced by editing: for each candidate prompt, it computes the relative entropy reduction (H_θ − H_θ′)/(H_θ′ + ε) between pre- and post-edit next-token distributions, ranking prompts by how sharply the edit reduced uncertainty. The defense, subspace camouflage, perturbs the key matrix with real decoy subject activations (K̃ = K + α·K_decoy scaled by norm ratio) and solves a constrained system to produce ΔW_defense whose row space lies in ColSpace(K̃) while exactly preserving the update on the true keys, thereby misleading any subspace-based inversion without degrading editing quality.

## Results

The KSTER white-box attack achieves subject recall of 0.94–1.00 across all batch sizes (N=10, 50, 100) for both MEMIT and AlphaEdit on CounterFact and zsRE, while the gray-box logit-divergence baseline degrades to 0.43–0.55 at N=100. For prompt recovery, Top-20 recall reaches 0.94–0.99 on CounterFact with semantic similarity of 0.83–0.89, and end-to-end original-output extraction exceeds 70% at Top-20. The subspace camouflage defense (α=5) raises the average rank of true subjects from ~51 to ~394 while preserving editing efficacy at 0.96 (MEMIT) and 1.00 (AlphaEdit).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between the proposed white-box KSTER attack and a gray-box baseline that uses Jensen-Shannon divergence of output logits. On CounterFact with MEMIT, the white-box attack maintains ≥0.94 recall at all batch sizes, while the gray-box baseline drops from 0.96 (N=10) to 0.68 (N=100) on Llama-3-Instruct and to 0.59 on Qwen-2.5-Instruct. The gap widens further for AlphaEdit, where the gray-box baseline falls to 0.43–0.45 at N=100 on Llama-3-Instruct. For prompt recovery, the paper's entropy-reduction scoring outperforms a naive baseline (which simply selects the minimum-entropy prompt from the post-edit model) by more than 13% in Top-1 recall on CounterFact, with an additional ~2% advantage at Top-5 and Top-20. The subspace camouflage defense, when applied, reduces the gray-box subject recall from 0.66 to 0.00 on CounterFact as α increases from 0 to 7, while the white-box attack's average rank of true subjects rises from ~51 to ~634.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the locate-then-edit paradigm (ROME, MEMIT, AlphaEdit), which prior work has studied primarily for editing effectiveness and generalization. It departs from the closest prior leakage studies—Patil et al. (2024), which requires access to original prompts, and Youssef et al. (2025), which recovers pre-edit behavior but not the editing prompts or outputs—by showing that the algebraic structure of the weight update ΔW alone (via the Woodbury identity) encodes a recoverable fingerprint of both the edited subjects and their semantic context. The proposed defense, subspace camouflage, has no direct precedent in the model-editing literature; it repurposes the concept of semantic decoys from adversarial robustness into a subspace-obfuscation mechanism that preserves the edit's functional effect while misleading spectral analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If model editing is adopted as a compliance mechanism for erasing sensitive memorized data (e.g., GDPR right-to-be-forgotten), the finding that the update itself leaks the erased content undermines the entire paradigm's safety guarantee. The paper's defense (subspace camouflage) offers a practical mitigation but introduces a trade-off: higher protection increases decoy-subject hallucination and, at extreme α, degrades editing efficacy and general utility. Open questions include whether the defense remains stable under large-scale sequential editing, whether gradient-based attacks without candidate pools are feasible, and whether the identified 'semantic ambiguity' and 'confused edit' phenomena can be addressed at the editing-algorithm level rather than patched post-hoc.
