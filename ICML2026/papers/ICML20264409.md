## Problem

Low-rank tensor decomposition degrades sharply under heavy missingness or noise, and handcrafted priors (sparsity, smoothness) cannot capture the rich statistics of real-world data. While pre-trained diffusion models provide powerful learned data priors, they are not compatible with the conjugate posterior inference that makes Bayesian tensor decomposition tractable. The paper asks whether a diffusion model can be embedded as an implicit prior inside a fully probabilistic CP decomposition without sacrificing closed-form factor updates.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a partially observed or noisy RGB image tensor (256×256×3 in the main experiments, reshaped into 16×16×3 patches with stride 8 to yield a 16×16×3×961 tensor), a binary observation mask, and a frozen pre-trained EDM score network. It outputs a posterior-mean reconstructed image tensor along with the CP factor matrices and weight vector. For out-of-distribution 2048×2048 images, a reshape operation maps the tensor to the prior's expected 256×256 spatial dimensions while preserving spatial structure. The sampler runs 100 Gibbs iterations (40 burn-in, one sample every 4 iterations) on a single A100 GPU.

## Method

DiffBCP couples two priors in a single Bayesian CP model: a cumulative shrinkage process (CUSP) prior on the CP component weights for automatic bidirectional rank selection, and a pre-trained diffusion model placed directly on the reconstructed tensor as a soft data-driven regularizer. To make posterior inference tractable despite the coupling among likelihood, low-rank constraint, and diffusion prior, the paper develops a split Gibbs sampler with an auxiliary variable Z: CP factors and weights admit closed-form conjugate Gaussian updates, while Z is sampled by running the EDM denoising SDE from a noise level matched to the coupling parameter ρ. A noise-adaptive coupling schedule (τρ² = c) automatically adjusts ρ as the precision τ is inferred, eliminating manual annealing. Theoretical results quantify the CUSP shrinkage rate (Theorem 3.1) and bound the stationary bias introduced by the inexact diffusion denoising step (Theorem 3.4, Corollary 3.5), establishing a trade-off between ρ (smaller ρ reduces split-Gibbs bias but makes denoising harder) and the quality of the approximate score network.

## Results

On FFHQ inpainting, DiffBCP achieves 32.13 dB PSNR (Uniform 0.7), 28.28 dB (Uniform 0.9), 27.91 dB (Stripe), and 30.34 dB (Irregular), outperforming the best baseline (DeepTensor) by 2.17–3.90 dB across masks. On ImageNet, gains are 1.20–2.92 dB over the best competing method. In LPIPS (perceptual quality), DiffBCP reduces the score from 26–41 (baselines) to 16–29, indicating substantially better semantic fidelity. On out-of-distribution 2048×2048 images, DiffBCP still leads PuTT by 0.3–1.1 dB PSNR despite the distribution shift.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiffBCP wins against all nine baselines on every mask and dataset in the main comparison (Table 1). The strongest baseline is DeepTensor (deep network priors), which DiffBCP beats by 2.17 dB on FFHQ Uniform(0.9) and 2.92 dB on ImageNet Uniform(0.7); HLRTF is competitive on Stripe masks but trails by 2.70 dB on FFHQ. Traditional Bayesian methods (BCP, BTR) are 4–18 dB behind, with BCP collapsing to 9.26 dB on FFHQ Stripe. GLON (pre-trained denoiser priors) is catastrophically unstable at 90% missingness, dropping to 7.47 dB on FFHQ Uniform(0.9). On the OOD high-resolution benchmark (Table 2), DiffBCP outperforms PuTT (coarse-to-fine tensor trains) by 0.3–1.1 dB PSNR. In the dedicated diffusion-sampler comparison (Table 3), DiffBCP beats PnP-DM on PSNR and SSIM across all masks in single-sample mode, but trails DPS on SSIM/LPIPS (a pixel-vs-perceptual trade-off); under posterior-mean evaluation, DiffBCP and PnP-DM are roughly competitive, alternating as best across the 12 metric/mask cells.

## Relation to Prior Work

DiffBCP sits at the intersection of Bayesian tensor decomposition (BCP, BTR, and their CUSP-based rank-adaptive variants) and diffusion posterior sampling for inverse problems (DPS, PnP-DM, and split-Gibbs-style samplers). The key departure is instantiating the data prior p(X) in a Bayesian CP model as an off-the-shelf pre-trained diffusion score network, whereas prior Bayesian TD methods leave this term implicit or non-informative, and prior diffusion posterior samplers impose no low-rank structural constraint. The inference mechanism builds directly on the split Gibbs sampler (Vono et al., 2019; Coeurdoux et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024), but the CP factor updates remain conjugate Gaussian while the diffusion block is sampled via low-rank-guided denoising, and a noise-adaptive coupling schedule (τρ² = c) replaces the heuristic annealing used in prior split-Gibbs diffusion samplers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first Bayesian tensor decomposition that admits a pre-trained diffusion model as an explicit data prior, unifying structural low-rank inductive bias with learned natural-image statistics in a single fully probabilistic posterior. The practical impact is that noise level and rank are both inferred automatically, removing two major hyperparameter sensitivities that plague plug-and-play diffusion methods (as demonstrated by PnP-DM's 3.7 dB collapse under a modest ρ_min change). A key limitation is that the CP structural prior vanishes when the underlying signal is not meaningfully low-rank, and the framework is currently restricted to CP decomposition; extensions to TT/TR and other linear inverse problems are sketched but not empirically validated. Open questions include whether the low-rank constraint's bias (visible in the single-sample PSNR-vs-LPIPS trade-off vs. DPS) can be mitigated, and whether stochastic mini-batch updates can make the method tractable for extremely large tensors.
