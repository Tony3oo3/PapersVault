## Problem

AI benchmark leaderboards compress thousands of item-level responses into per-benchmark scores and a single composite, implicitly assuming unidimensionality, local independence, and i.i.d. submissions—none of which have been validated. The sources and magnitudes of measurement noise (contributor practices, architecture, deployment, benchmark difficulty) remain unquantified, making it unclear when leaderboard rankings reflect genuine capability differences versus evaluation artifacts, and when inferred scaling laws are stable versus context-dependent.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes 87M+ binary item-level responses from 4,281 LLMs across six benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, IF-Eval, BBH, MuSR, GPQA, MATH Level 5) plus structured metadata (architecture family, contributor identity, deployment/post-training type, parameter count). Rather than operating on aggregate scores, it works at the item level for CFA and latent regression, while the G-study operates on model-benchmark score pairs. Outputs are structured as a three-layer decomposition: a bifactor latent structure with per-dimension loadings, a full variance-component table across all 15 crossed facets and interactions, and a vector of dimension-specific scaling slopes with per-dimension reliability indices (R_d) rather than a single scalar scaling law.

## Method

The paper introduces a three-stage framework that sequentially identifies structure, quantifies noise, and estimates decontaminated effects. Method 1 fits six competing CFA/SEM structures (independent factors, unidimensional, second-order, correlated, bifactor, correlated bifactor) via meta-analytic item-set bootstrapping with DWLS and MH-RM estimators, using permutation controls and modification-index (SEPC) maps to diagnose overfitting and residual local dependence. Method 2 applies a fully crossed generalizability study over four facets (architecture, benchmark, contributor, deployment) with random slopes for log-parameter count, yielding variance shares p_S, slope signal-to-noise ratios, and proportion-of-slope-instability (PSI) diagnostics. Method 3 unifies measurement and regression in a single hierarchical model: a bifactor IRT measurement layer feeds a mixed-effects structural layer (Eq. 5) that regresses each latent dimension on size and deployment indicators while absorbing contributor-level pseudo-replication via random effects, estimated with MH-RM. The key insight is that the general factor g carries a highly reliable scaling slope (R_g = 0.97) while several benchmark-specific factors show negligible or even negative size effects, revealing that apparent benchmark-level scaling is largely mediated through g rather than through the specific skill each benchmark purports to isolate.

## Results

Across 4,281 LLMs on six benchmarks, the bifactor latent structure best fits the data (RMSEA=0.05, held-out AUC=0.95, MAE=0.21), outperforming unidimensional (AUC=0.90) and correlated-factor (AUC=0.73) alternatives. Variance decomposition shows benchmark identity explains ~43% of score variance, contributor/provenance ~9%, architecture ~4–6%, and deployment ~2%. The general factor g exhibits a highly reliable scaling slope (β=1.05, R_g=0.97), whereas the naive manifest-score scaling law has low reliability (R_β=0.53). After noise control, overall rankings are largely preserved (Spearman ρ=0.86) but only 1 of the top-1% models remains in the top 1%, indicating substantial noise sensitivity at decision-relevant margins.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods but instead adjudicates among six competing CFA latent structures. The orthogonal bifactor (RMSEA=0.05, AUC=0.95) dominates the unidimensional single-factor model (AUC=0.90) and the correlated-factors model (AUC=0.73, MAE=0.35) on both covariance fit and out-of-sample prediction, while the more flexible correlated bifactor (RMSEA=0.04) overfits, dropping to AUC=0.77 and MAE=0.32. Permutation controls confirm that benchmark grouping carries genuine structural information beyond model flexibility. On scaling laws, the denoised latent regression recovers a strong, reliable general-factor slope (R_g=0.97) that is substantially more stable than the naive manifest-score regression (R_β=0.53), and reveals that benchmark-specific factors like BBH (R=0.05) and MuSR (R=0.07) show negligible size effects after controlling for g and ecosystem noise—findings invisible in raw leaderboard regressions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three psychometric traditions—Confirmatory Factor Analysis (Brown, 2015; Bollen, 1989), Generalizability Theory (Brennan, 2001; Cronbach & Shavelson, 2004), and mixed-effects Item Response Theory (De Boeck, 2011; Chalmers, 2015)—into a single framework applied to AI leaderboards. Prior AI-evaluation critiques (Salaudeen et al., 2025; Reuel et al., 2024; Truong et al., 2025) identified measurement assumptions qualitatively, and earlier IRT work on NLP benchmarks (Lalor et al., 2018; Vania et al., 2021) modeled within-benchmark item properties, but none performed a full ecosystem-level decomposition combining latent structure discovery, variance attribution, and latent-space regression simultaneously. The paper also extends scaling-law research (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) from controlled training runs to open ecosystems where size is confounded with deployment and provenance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that manifest-score scaling laws have low reliability (R_β = 0.53) while the latent general-factor slope is highly stable (R_g = 0.97) directly challenges the common practice of reading power-law trends from raw leaderboard composites, and the observation that only 1 of the top-1% models retains its position after noise control suggests that decision-relevant leaderboard margins are substantially artifact-driven. The potential 'alignment tax'—instruction tuning negatively associated with soft-reasoning (MuSR) performance—raises open questions about whether current optimization objectives systematically trade off certain latent capabilities. Key limitations include reliance on a single leaderboard snapshot with six benchmarks, observational (non-causal) metadata of unknown quality, and the acknowledged risk that bifactor models over-extract general factors in ways not yet validated for LLMs.
