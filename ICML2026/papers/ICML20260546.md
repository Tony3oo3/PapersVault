## Problem

Watermarking panoramic (360°) imagery is fundamentally challenged by arbitrary 3D rotations, since panoramas are defined on the sphere and transform under SO(3) rather than the planar translations that CNNs exploit. Existing deep watermarking methods rely on data augmentation to approximate rotation robustness, but SO(3) is a continuous group with infinitely many elements, making exhaustive augmentation infeasible and offering no theoretical guarantees. Moreover, the only natural SO(3)-invariant (the zeroth-order spherical harmonic coefficient) corresponds to global luminance, making it unsuitable for high-capacity, imperceptible watermarking.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 512×1024 equirectangular panoramic image and a binary watermark vector (default 32 bits, scalable to 64 bits) as input, and produces a watermarked panoramic image in the same ERP format along with a recovered binary watermark at extraction time. The panorama is internally lifted to a spherical harmonic representation with cutoff degree l_max=16, and watermark embedding is confined to selected higher-order subspaces (l∈{6,8,14}). Critically, extraction requires no alignment, inverse rotation, or geometric correction—the watermark is recovered directly from the rotated image via invariant bispectral scalars.

## Method

TRIAD models panoramas as spherical signals and embeds watermarks into higher-order spherical harmonic coefficients using an SO(3)-equivariant backbone (e3nn Gated Blocks) that preserves equivariance under rotation. The key insight is a third-order bispectrum invariant: by coupling three SO(3) irreducible representations via tensor products and projecting onto the trivial representation (l=0) using Clebsch-Gordan coefficients, the method obtains a scalar that is provably invariant under arbitrary SO(3) rotations while retaining phase information that the second-order power spectrum discards. During extraction, two successive equivariant tensor products contract the spectral features onto V_0, yielding rotation-invariant bispectral scalars that are decoded by a lightweight MLP. The framework is trained end-to-end with MSE (fidelity) and BCE (extraction) losses, requiring no rotation augmentation, and incorporates a perceptual module combining a learnable content mask with a fixed sin(θ) geometric prior to suppress polar artifacts in ERP.

## Results

TRIAD achieves 100% bit accuracy under arbitrary SO(3) rotations (sampled uniformly via random unit quaternions), while all six baselines collapse to near-random guessing without rotation augmentation. On common distortions (Table 1), TRIAD attains 1.000 bit accuracy on Resize, Contrast, Gaussian Blur, and Median Filter, 0.978 on JPEG (QF=60), 0.988 on Brightness, 0.975 on Gaussian Noise, and 0.984 on mixed distortions, with PSNR of 39.22 dB and SSIM of 0.9946 at 32-bit capacity. Even with extensive rotation-based data augmentation (K=10 anchor rotations), the best baseline (Robust-Wide) reaches only 82.23% average bit accuracy on the restricted anchor set, while TRIAD maintains 100% without any augmentation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The decisive advantage of TRIAD is its provable SO(3) rotation invariance: it maintains 100% bit accuracy under arbitrary continuous 3D rotations, whereas all six baselines (StegaStamp, SepMark, TrustMark, EditGuard, Robust-Wide, VINE) degrade to near-random performance without rotation augmentation, and even with K=10 anchor-rotation augmentation the best (Robust-Wide) reaches only 82.23%. On standard distortion benchmarks (Table 1), TRIAD matches or exceeds baselines on most metrics: it outperforms StegaStamp (PSNR 39.22 vs 27.96), SepMark (39.22 vs 35.68), EditGuard (39.22 vs 36.58), and VINE (39.22 vs 36.33) in visual fidelity, and achieves perfect 1.000 accuracy on Resize, Contrast, Gaussian Blur, and Median Filter. However, TRIAD trades off capacity (32 bits) against TrustMark (100), Robust-Wide (64), and VINE (100), and trails TrustMark (40.83 dB) and Robust-Wide (41.65 dB) in PSNR by 1.6–2.4 dB. The paper argues this capacity–fidelity trade-off is inherent to the spherical harmonic embedding strategy and that the rotation-invariance guarantee is the primary contribution, not raw distortion robustness, which baselines can approximate via augmentation.

## Relation to Prior Work

TRIAD repurposes tools from SO(3) equivariant neural networks (Clebsch-Gordan tensor products, Wigner-D matrices, the e3nn library) and classical higher-order signal processing (the bispectrum of Nikias & Mendel) for a task—watermarking—where neither community had previously applied them. It departs from the dominant deep-watermarking paradigm (StegaStamp, TrustMark, VINE, etc.), which relies on planar CNNs with translational equivariance and data augmentation for robustness, by instead grounding robustness in the algebraic structure of the rotation group. The paper also extends the spherical-harmonic invariant literature (Kazhdan et al.'s power spectrum for shape recognition) from second-order, phase-discarding invariants to third-order, phase-preserving bispectral invariants, arguing that the latter are necessary for information-carrying capacity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that for continuous transformation groups like SO(3), empirical robustness via data augmentation is fundamentally insufficient—no finite set of augmented samples can cover the infinite rotation manifold—while representation-theoretic invariance provides certified guarantees. This reframing has implications beyond panoramic watermarking, suggesting a general methodology for information hiding on non-Euclidean domains where group actions are continuous. Key limitations include the modest payload (32–64 bits), the assumption of full-sphere signals (no partial panoramas), and the restriction to rigid rotations (no non-rigid or perspective distortions). Open questions include extending to local equivariant symmetries for partial signals, scaling capacity without sacrificing spectral robustness, and generalizing the invariant-construction approach to other geometric data types (point clouds, meshes, Gaussian splatting).
