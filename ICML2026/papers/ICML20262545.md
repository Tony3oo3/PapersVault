## Problem

Negative log likelihood (NLL) is the default supervised fine-tuning objective for LLMs, yet SFT often exhibits limited generalization. While NLL is classically optimal when training from scratch on small-scale classification, post-training starts from pretrained models that already encode task-relevant priors and involves long, potentially noisy chain-of-thought supervision, violating NLL's optimality assumptions. The paper asks: which probability-based objectives work when, and what dimension governs their relative effectiveness across different post-training settings?

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard autoregressive SFT: given a text prompt x and a target token sequence ỹ of length N, the model produces token-level conditionals p_θ(y_t | y_<t, x) at each decoding step. The objective function consumes the scalar predicted probability p ∈ [0,1] of the target token at each step and returns a scalar loss; the key design choice is how this function distributes gradient weight across the probability spectrum (low-p vs. high-p tokens). Training data spans domains occupying different positions on the model-capability continuum, from math (strong priors, ~0.8 mean predicted probability) to novel puzzles (weak priors, ~0.01), with sequence lengths up to 8192 tokens for long-form reasoning.

## Method

The paper introduces a parametric family f^α(p) = (1−p^α)/α that smoothly interpolates between prior-averse objectives (convex, α≤1, recovering NLL as α→0) and prior-leaning objectives (concave, α≥1, e.g., −p at α=1). The core mechanism is the logit-gradient weight W_f(p) = −f′(p)·p(1−p), which governs how much learning signal each token contributes: convex objectives peak at low p (forcing the model to correct unlikely predictions), while concave objectives peak at high p (reinforcing already-confident predictions). The paper identifies the model-capability continuum as the governing dimension: at the model-strong end, prior-leaning objectives like −p or thresholded NLL consistently outperform NLL by up to 16%; at the model-weak end, NLL dominates; in the intermediate region, no single objective prevails. A gradient-flow theorem provides a sufficient condition showing that more prior-leaning objectives achieve greater initial risk reduction than NLL in the model-strong end, with the reverse inequality holding in the model-weak end. Empirical validation spans 8 backbones, 27 benchmarks, and 7 domains, with ablations on quantile thresholding, convexity sweeps (α from 0.1 to 10), and training-set likelihood estimation confirming the continuum.

## Results

In the model-strong (math) end, prior-leaning objectives −p and thresholded −log(p)·1{p≥0.2} consistently outperform standard NLL by up to 16 percentage points in average accuracy (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B: 32.75 vs. 17.00; DeepSeekMath-7B: 19.11 vs. 11.10). In the model-weak (figfont puzzles) end, the pattern reverses: NLL achieves 35.2% exact match with Qwen2.5-7B while −p achieves 0%, and 43.6% Jaro-Winkler similarity vs. 10.2%. In the model-intermediate (medical) region, the two objectives are nearly indistinguishable (e.g., LLaMA-3.1-8B: 47.23 vs. 45.89 average across 10 benchmarks), indicating no single objective prevails.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three SFT training objectives: standard NLL (−log p), the prior-leaning −p objective, and a thresholded variant −log(p)·1{p≥0.2} that excludes low-probability tokens. At the model-strong end (math, coding), −p and thresholded NLL beat standard NLL by 3–16 points in average accuracy across all four backbones (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B: 32.75 vs. 17.00; Qwen2.5-Coder-7B on HumanEval: 83.5 vs. 80.4 pass@1). At the model-weak end (figfont puzzles, low-resource languages), the advantage fully reverses: NLL achieves 35.2% exact match on figfont with Qwen2.5-7B while −p scores 0%, and NLL outperforms −p by 4–5 points on average across seven low-resource languages (16.1 vs. 11.5 for LLaMA-3.1-8B). In the model-intermediate region (medical reasoning), the two objectives are statistically indistinguishable (differences < 2 points across 10 benchmarks and 4 models). The thresholded NLL variant closely matches or slightly exceeds −p on the MS end, confirming that the key mechanism is downweighting low-probability tokens rather than the specific functional form.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Wu et al. (2026), whose uniform gradient reweighting is shown to be equivalent to the -p objective, and extends it by providing a systematic capability-based characterization of when such prior-leaning objectives help or hurt. It departs from the classical optimality arguments for NLL (Cox 1958; Bartlett et al. 2006; Zhang 2004), which assume training from scratch on simple classification, by arguing that LLM post-training violates those assumptions due to strong pretrained priors and long, noisy chain-of-thought supervision. The paper also unifies several RL-inspired SFT improvements (Wang et al. 2026; Qin & Springenberg 2025; Zhu et al. 2026) and distribution-matching losses (Li et al. 2025) under a single 'prior-leaning vs. prior-averse' taxonomy, positioning them as special cases rather than independent contributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The model-capability continuum gives practitioners a concrete, measurable diagnostic (mean predicted probability on training data) for choosing an SFT objective, which is practically valuable given that SFT is the default post-training step for virtually all LLM deployments. The finding that a trivially simple objective (-p) can outperform NLL by up to 16% in the model-strong regime, while NLL remains necessary in the model-weak regime, suggests that the 'one-size-fits-all' NLL default may be leaving substantial performance on the table. Open questions include adaptive objectives that shift emphasis during training, better capability proxies beyond raw predicted probability (especially under miscalibration), and extending the stylized gradient-flow theory to more realistic multi-step optimization dynamics.
