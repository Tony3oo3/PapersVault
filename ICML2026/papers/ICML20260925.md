## Problem

Existing adaptive LoRA methods allocate rank using a single importance metric—either gradient magnitude (adaptation demand) or output sensitivity (prediction influence)—yet these two properties are decoupled, peaking at different layers across all tested architectures. This leads to systematic over-provisioning of high-gradient but low-contribution layers and under-provisioning of high-contribution but moderate-gradient layers, wasting parameter capacity where it matters least.

## Input / Output

COBRA consumes a frozen pre-trained transformer, a small one-time calibration set (~1,000 samples) for computing layer conductance and gradient statistics, the downstream training set, and a total rank budget R_total. It outputs a heterogeneous per-layer rank vector {r_l} and the corresponding fine-tuned LoRA adapter matrices, with the calibration cost amortized across multiple training runs and hyperparameter sweeps. The rank extraction enforces the exact budget constraint by thresholding at the R_total-th largest learned inclusion probability across all rank components.

## Method

COBRA operates in three stages. First, layer conductance attribution computes each layer's exhaustive, non-redundant contribution to predictions via path-integral attribution (integrated gradients extended to hidden layers), yielding scores Φ^(l) that sum exactly to the total output change. Second, dual-factor aggregation multiplicatively integrates normalized contribution Φ^(l) with gradient-based adaptation demand Γ^(l) to produce the Task-Adaptive Layer Conductance (TA-LC) distribution π^(l), ensuring high scores only when both factors are significant. Third, Bayesian rank allocation parameterizes each layer's adapter with r_max binary gating variables, places Bernoulli priors with inclusion probabilities derived from π^(l) via a sigmoid mapping, and optimizes an ELBO objective using Hard Concrete continuous relaxation with cosine temperature annealing; after convergence, deterministic ranks are extracted by thresholding learned inclusion probabilities at the budget-determined cutoff.

## Results

On GLUE with RoBERTa-base (r=8, 0.8M trainable params), COBRA achieves 88.6% average accuracy, outperforming uniform LoRA (r=8) by +1.6 points and even surpassing LoRA (r=16, double the budget) by +0.9 points. On DeBERTaV3-base GLUE (r=4), COBRA reaches 89.9% vs. 88.7% for LoRA and 89.0% for AdaLoRA. In the high-rank financial-sentiment regression regime (r_avg=384–512), COBRA achieves a 6.6% mean MSE reduction over uniform LoRA across NEU, FXE, and INV datasets, with gains scaling from 5.2% at r=256 to 7.1% at r=512.

## Baselines & Comparisons

COBRA consistently outperforms all five baselines across the three low-rank settings in Table 6. Against uniform LoRA, COBRA gains +1.6, +1.2, and +1.9 points on RoBERTa-base GLUE, DeBERTaV3-base GLUE, and Gemma-2-2B CSQA respectively. The strongest adaptive baseline, DoRA-dyn, is beaten by +0.7, +0.6, and +0.2 points, indicating diminishing but consistent advantage as baselines improve. Notably, DyLoRA actually underperforms uniform LoRA on DeBERTaV3-base (88.4 vs. 88.7), demonstrating that range-based dynamic allocation without contribution signals can be counterproductive. In the high-rank financial-sentiment regime (Table 7, r=256), COBRA achieves 0.0183/0.0176 MSE on NEU/INV, outperforming DoRA-dyn by 1.6%/2.2% and GoRA by 3.1%/3.4%, with margins substantially larger than in low-rank NLU tasks. Against AdaLoRA specifically (Table 3), COBRA gains +0.9 points average on DeBERTaV3-base, with the largest per-task improvement on MRPC (+2.2 points), attributed to AdaLoRA's reliance on singular-value sensitivity alone missing layers with high output contribution but moderate gradient signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

COBRA extends the adaptive rank allocation line initiated by AdaLoRA (SVD-based pruning), GoRA (gradient-driven allocation), and DoRA-dyn (dynamic rank distribution), all of which rely on a single importance metric. Its key conceptual departure is the integration of layer conductance attribution (rooted in Integrated Gradients and the Layer Conductance framework of Dhamdhere et al.) as a second, complementary factor alongside gradient-based adaptation demand, combined via multiplicative aggregation. The Bayesian rank allocation stage repurposes the Hard Concrete continuous relaxation and ELBO-based variational inference from the sparse neural network literature (Louizos et al., Maddison et al.) to translate the resulting TA-LC prior into discrete per-layer rank decisions, replacing the iterative SVD pruning or pre-training gradient heuristics used by prior methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, interpretability-grounded alternative to heuristic rank allocation, and the demonstrated scaling behavior—where heterogeneous allocation advantages grow with parameter budget (1.4–2.6% in low-rank GLUE vs. 5.2–7.1% in high-rank regression)—suggests the approach becomes increasingly relevant as practitioners push LoRA ranks higher for complex tasks. However, the practical gains in the most common low-rank regime (r=4–8) remain modest (+0.9 to +1.6 points), and the calibration overhead (path-integral computation over 1000 samples with 50 integration steps) plus the added Bayesian training loop introduce non-trivial implementation complexity. Open questions include whether the dual-factor decomposition generalizes to non-LoRA PEFT paradigms (adapters, prompt tuning), whether it holds at 70B+ scale, and whether the multiplicative aggregation is truly optimal versus learned or task-specific weighting.
