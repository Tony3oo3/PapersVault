## Problem

Standard safe RL methods assume access to a known per-step cost function and constraint threshold, but in many safety-critical domains (autonomous driving, robotics, healthcare) these are implicit, context-dependent, or simply unavailable. Supervision is often limited to coarse binary accept/reject labels over whole trajectories, creating a credit-assignment problem: the label indicates whether a violation occurred but not when or which actions caused it. TraCeS addresses this gap by learning per-timestep violation credit from such sparse trajectory-level labels without requiring knowledge of the underlying cost or threshold.

## Input / Output

The estimator consumes state-action pairs (s_t, a_t) sequentially along a trajectory, maintaining a recurrent prefix summary h_t (a 4-dim GRU hidden state), and is trained on sparse binary labels provided at trajectory endpoints or at periodic checkpoints (every 20 steps for MuJoCo/Circle tasks, every 5 steps for Run tasks). It outputs a per-step surrogate cost C̃_t = −log P̂_t^Δ ≥ 0 (clamped at 7 in log-space for numerical stability) and a trajectory-level non-violation probability P̂(ψ=1|τ) = ∏_t P̂_t^Δ. The surrogate cost is fed into a standard CMDP solver (PPO-Lagrangian) where the policy and constraint critic are additionally conditioned on h_t to respect the history-dependence of the learned cost.

## Method

TraCeS factorizes the predicted trajectory-level non-violation probability into per-step multiplicative change factors P̂_t^Δ ∈ [0,1] (Proposition 4.2), so that each step's credit measures the incremental drop in predicted non-violation when extending the prefix by one step. A shared 2-layer GRU encoder updates a prefix summary h_t from (s_t, a_t, h_t), and a shared MLP decoder maps (h_t, h_{t+1}) to parameters (μ_t, σ_t) of a log-normal distribution; sampling U_t ~ LogNormal(μ_t, σ_t) and setting log P̂_t^Δ = −U_t ≤ 0 enforces the monotone non-violation constraint by construction. The estimator is trained with binary cross-entropy (MLE under a Bernoulli label model) on sparse accept/reject labels, and the resulting per-step surrogate cost is plugged into constrained policy optimization via a Jensen's-inequality lower bound that converts the product-form constraint E[∏_t P̂_t^Δ] ≥ d into the standard CMDP sum-form E[∑_t C̃_t] ≤ b̃, enabling direct use of PPO-Lagrangian. A coefficient-of-variation (CV) criterion over the estimator's sampling noise selects high-uncertainty trajectories for labeling, reducing annotation burden under distribution shift. A theoretical Jensen-gap bound (Theorem 4.5) characterizes the approximation error introduced by the inequality, showing it scales as Var(p̂_τ)/(2ε²) and shrinks as the estimator stabilizes during training.

## Results

Across 12 continuous-control tasks (Safety Gymnasium, MuJoCo, Bullet Safety Gym), TraCeS learns non-violating policies (cost ≤ 25) on most tasks using only sparse binary accept/reject labels, with zero knowledge of the cost function or threshold. TraCeS uses substantially fewer labeled trajectories than the zero-knowledge C-T baseline (e.g., 4,005 vs. 12,000 on CarCircle1; 2,749 vs. 6,000 on HalfCheetah) while achieving lower cost (11.3 vs. 28.2 on CarCircle1; 22.4 vs. 99.7 on HalfCheetah). On Hopper, TraCeS achieves cost 17.1 versus 284.2 for C-T and 20.4 for RLSF, while matching oracle-level reward (1610.7 vs. 1046.1 for PPO-Lagrangian). The method remains robust under a 1,000-label budget (Appendix C.6) and moderate-to-high label noise.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the zero-knowledge C-T baseline (the only directly comparable method with the same information access), TraCeS dominates on cost across nearly all tasks: 11.3 vs. 28.2 on CarCircle1, 11.9 vs. 44.0 on CarCircle2, 22.4 vs. 99.7 on HalfCheetah, and 17.1 vs. 284.2 on Hopper, while using 40–60% fewer labeled trajectories. Against the partial-knowledge RLSF (which knows the threshold), TraCeS achieves lower cost on CarCircle1 (11.3 vs. 33.8), CarCircle2 (11.9 vs. 20.2), and Hopper (17.1 vs. 20.4), but higher cost on HalfCheetah (22.4 vs. 0.5) and Walker2d (24.1 vs. 0.3), where RLSF's known threshold gives it a structural advantage. Against oracle CMDP solvers (PPO-Lagrangian, CPPO-PID, CUP, FOCOPS, TRPO-Sauté), TraCeS is competitive on reward (e.g., 1610.7 vs. 1046.1 on Hopper; 2885.4 vs. 3313.1 on Ant) while requiring no access to the true cost or threshold; oracle methods occasionally violate the constraint themselves (e.g., PPO-Lagrangian cost 29.1 on Hopper, 29.1 on PointCircle2). The CV-based trajectory selection ablation (Appendix C.8) confirms that uncertainty-guided labeling is critical: random selection fails to satisfy the constraint on Hopper (cost 31.1) and BallRun (cost 30.8) under a 1,000-label budget, whereas CV selection keeps both below threshold (18.0 and 24.3).

## Relation to Prior Work

TraCeS departs from standard safe RL (CPPO, PPO-Lagrangian, RLSF), which all assume access to a known per-step cost function and/or threshold, by formulating constraint learning from only sparse binary trajectory-level accept/reject labels with zero knowledge of the underlying cost or threshold. Conceptually, the multiplicative factorization of a trajectory-level non-violation probability into per-step credits draws on return-decomposition ideas (RUDDER, RRD) but explicitly targets a supervision-aligned surrogate under a monotone violation structure rather than recovering an oracle cost. The Jensen's-inequality lower bound that converts the product-of-credits constraint into a standard CMDP sum constraint is a novel reformulation that makes the learned signal directly compatible with existing constrained solvers like PPO-Lagrangian. The paper also introduces a zero-knowledge C-T baseline (MLP estimating both cost and threshold), noting that no prior work addressed constraint learning without either quantity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The zero-knowledge, sparse-label formulation addresses a practical gap in safety-critical domains (autonomous driving, healthcare, robotics) where safety criteria are context-dependent and hard to encode as fixed cost functions and thresholds. The main limitations are the monotone-violation assumption, reliance on binary labels, and evaluation restricted to simulation benchmarks with oracle costs available only for label construction; the Jensen gap bound is a worst-case diagnostic that can be loose for long horizons. Open questions include extending to graded or ordinal supervision, improving active-labeling criteria beyond coefficient of variation, and validating in real-world settings where the monotone assumption may not hold. The human-feedback study (100 labels) is promising but too small-scale to draw firm conclusions about robustness to noisy human judgment.
