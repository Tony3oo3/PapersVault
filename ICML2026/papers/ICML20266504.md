## Problem

The Model Context Protocol (MCP) standardises agent-tool interaction and is rapidly becoming the default interface for agentic AI, yet it embeds no protocol-level trustworthiness mechanisms. Trustworthy-AI measures are currently implemented at application and organisational layers, leading to inconsistent adoption and leaving the protocol's intermediary position as an unexploited enforcement point. The paper asks: what structural gaps exist in MCP relative to the EU Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI, and where can protocol-level interventions achieve ecosystem-wide impact without sacrificing MCP's lightweight design?

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes the MCP host-client-server specification, representative MCP-mediated workflows (multi-step tool chains with context passing), and the seven principles of the EU Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI as its normative framework. It produces a per-principle gap analysis mapped to specific architectural layers (host, client, server, registry), with proposals classified into five intervention categories—risk-adaptive requirements, metadata standardisation, host-layer enforcement, registry governance, and operationalised sustainability/fairness—culminating in a structured five-point call to action. The output is a position paper rather than an empirical evaluation; proposals are argued for via architectural reasoning and cited ecosystem evidence rather than benchmarked.

## Method

The paper performs a systematic protocol-design analysis by decomposing MCP's host-client-server architecture and mapping each of the seven EU trustworthiness principles to the specific layer where its requirements can or cannot be satisfied. For each principle, it identifies concrete limitations (e.g., consent granularity delegated entirely to hosts, absence of provenance metadata, registry-level trust not guaranteeing runtime safety) and proposes targeted improvements at the narrowest viable intervention point. The key architectural insight is that MCP's intermediary role makes it analogous to the HTTP→HTTPS transition: protocol-level mechanisms convert optional application-layer features into structural requirements that propagate to all connected systems. Proposals are deliberately designed to preserve MCP's minimal core by default, using opt-in profiles, optional metadata fields, and host-enforced constraints rather than mandatory protocol changes, with a proportionality argument that high-risk deployments can require stricter enforcement while low-risk ones remain unburdened.

## Results

This is a position paper with no experimental results or main results table. Its key analytical contributions are: (1) identifying specific trustworthiness gaps in MCP across all seven EU Ethics Guidelines principles, supported by cited empirical evidence (e.g., 7.2% of 1,899 audited MCP servers exhibit security vulnerabilities; 66% contain code smells; 14.4% have critical bugs; 46% of Python package dependencies carry vulnerabilities); and (2) proposing five categories of protocol-level improvements—risk-adaptive requirements [i], metadata standardisation [ii], host-layer enforcement [iii], registry governance [iv], and operationalised sustainability/fairness [v]—mapped to specific intervention points in the MCP host-client-server pipeline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare methods against empirical baselines. The closest analogue is the 'Alternative Views' section (Section 9), where the authors engage with two counterarguments: (1) that responsible AI practices belong at the application/organizational level rather than the protocol level, and (2) that adding protocol-level safeguards increases complexity and overhead. The authors concede that a protocol alone cannot guarantee trustworthiness and that rigid universal mandates would be counterproductive, but argue their narrower claim—that MCP can standardise shared technical hooks (provenance metadata, audit-relevant fields, least-privilege scoping) to make trustworthy deployment easier to implement consistently—remains valid. They position their proposal as complementary to, not a replacement for, organizational and regulatory enforcement, and emphasise proportionality by keeping MCP minimal by default with opt-in profiles for higher-risk settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper builds on the MCP specification (Anthropic, 2024a) and the EU Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI (Commission, 2020), applying the latter's seven principles as a structured lens to the former's host-client-server architecture. It extends the emerging MCP security literature (Hou et al., 2025; Hasan et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025), which has catalogued attack vectors and vulnerabilities, by reframing the problem through a comprehensive trustworthy-AI framework and proposing protocol-level structural interventions rather than application-level mitigations. The paper also draws on provenance/lineage methods for ML pipelines (Schlegel & Sattler, 2025), Model Cards and Datasheets (Mitchell et al., 2019; Gebru et al., 2021), sticky policies (Pearson & Casassa-Mont, 2011), and software supply-chain security (Ladisa et al., 2023), repurposing them for the MCP compositional context. The HTTP-to-HTTPS analogy frames the core argument: trustworthiness should shift from optional application-level features to structural protocol-level requirements.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Because MCP is rapidly becoming a widely adopted interoperability standard, protocol-level changes propagate ecosystem-wide, making this a high-leverage intervention point for embedding trustworthiness before the architecture hardens. The paper's key open questions include: how to design proportionality mechanisms (risk-adaptive profiles) that avoid over-constraining low-risk deployments while protecting high-risk ones; how to resolve the non-trivial mapping between MCP's technical roles (host, client, server) and legal roles (controller, processor, provider, deployer) under GDPR and the EU AI Act; and the mechanism-design problem of optimal penalties, audit probabilities, and reward structures for Eco Mode disclosures. As a position paper with no empirical validation, the proposals remain aspirational, and the cross-principle tensions identified in Section 8 (e.g., privacy vs. fairness auditing, transparency vs. individual privacy) remain unresolved design challenges that will require iterative protocol evolution.
