## Problem

Existing diffusion models for remote sensing image generation treat spectral bands as generic image channels, ignoring the radiative transfer physics and inter-band spectral correlations that govern real sensor data. This 'physics-agnostic' approach produces visually plausible but radiometrically inconsistent imagery with spectral hallucinations, and most methods are further limited by rigid fixed input-output mappings that cannot handle arbitrary band loss or heterogeneous sensor configurations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266338/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method accepts a sparse tensor of observed spectral bands (from any of 15 simulated sensor configurations) together with a binary validity mask indicating which bands are present at each pixel, and produces a complete hyperspectral image (e.g., 103 bands for PaviaU, 191 bands for WDC). During training, the input is dynamically degraded by sampling a spectral response function and applying Bernoulli band dropout (rate 0.1–0.7), so the model learns to reconstruct from arbitrary partial observations. Hyperspectral patches are 64×64 pixels; multispectral/RGB experiments use 256×256 inputs.

## Method

AnyBand-Diff combines three components around a modified U-Net diffusion backbone. (1) Masked Conditional Diffusion (MCD) with Dual Stochastic Masking simulates sensor heterogeneity and random band loss during training, and injects the sparse condition hierarchically via Conditional Adaptive Modulation (CAM) that regresses per-layer scale/shift parameters from a lightweight encoder of the masked input. (2) Physics-Guided Sampling (PGS) is a test-time mechanism that computes gradients of a differentiable physical loss (spectral index operators + a frozen PROSAIL-trained RTM emulator) with respect to the noisy latent at each DDIM step, subtracting a scaled gradient from the predicted noise to steer the trajectory onto the physical manifold. (3) A Multi-Scale Physical Loss (MSPL) jointly constrains training at pixel level (Frobenius-norm alignment of the spectral correlation matrix), region level (KDE-based KL divergence on NDVI/NDWI distributions over local patches), and global level (reconstruction error through a differentiable radiative transfer autoencoder), with weights λ_px=1.0, λ_reg=0.5, λ_img=0.2.

## Results

On the visual quality benchmark (Table 1), AnyBand-Diff achieves PSNR 17.11 dB, SSIM 0.73, SAM 0.028, and RMSE 0.039, outperforming all nine baselines. On the core band-repair task (Table 2), under 50% random band masking the method reaches PSNR 29.65 dB and SSIM 0.84, surpassing the nearest competitor HSI-Gene (26.87 dB / 0.74) by 2.78 dB and 0.10 SSIM respectively. For biophysical consistency (Table 3), AnyBand-Diff attains NDVI correlation of 0.94 and NDWI correlation of 0.92, the highest among all methods, confirming that generated spectra preserve critical radiometric relationships.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AnyBand-Diff outperforms all nine baselines across every metric and masking level. The closest competitor is HSI-Gene (IEEE TPAMI 2026), which leads at 10% masking in RMSE (0.027 vs 0.028) but is surpassed by 2.78 dB in PSNR and 0.10 in SSIM at the most challenging 50% masking setting. CRS-Diff (IEEE TGRS 2024) and SSDiff (NeurIPS 2024) are the next strongest baselines, achieving 24.12 dB and 24.02 dB PSNR respectively at 50% masking—still 5.5 dB below AnyBand-Diff. General-purpose diffusion models (Stable Diffusion, ControlNet) and satellite-specific generators (DiffusionSat, EarthSynth, Text2Earth) show substantially larger gaps, with SD dropping to 17.23 dB PSNR at 50% masking. For biophysical indices, AnyBand-Diff leads HSI-Gene by 0.04 in NDVI CC (0.94 vs 0.90) and 0.05 in NDWI CC (0.92 vs 0.87), while generic models like SD achieve only 0.42 NDVI CC, confirming that physics-informed guidance is essential for quantitative remote sensing applications.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the diffusion-model-for-remote-sensing line (DiffusionSat, CRS-Diff, SSDiff, HSI-Gene) by addressing what the authors identify as a fundamental gap: prior methods treat spectral bands as generic multi-channel extensions, ignoring radiative transfer physics. It also departs from the physics-informed generative literature (PhysicsGAN, PhySwin, PINNs) by injecting physical constraints into the sampling trajectory via gradient guidance rather than relying solely on static loss terms during training. The Dual Stochastic Masking strategy generalizes the fixed input-output mapping paradigm (e.g., RGB-to-multispectral) to arbitrary band subsets, while the Multi-Scale Physical Loss introduces a hierarchical constraint structure absent in prior single-scale spectral losses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature of PGS and MSPL—validated by cross-applying them to SSDiff and CRS-Diff with consistent 2+ dB gains—suggests these modules could be adopted broadly across RS generation pipelines without architectural retraining. However, the RTM emulator's reliance on PROSAIL (turbid-medium canopy) limits physical validity for 3D urban scenes, and the static atmospheric assumption restricts applicability to TOA radiance generation under variable haze or cloud conditions. Open questions include whether the physics-guided paradigm extends to spatiotemporal modeling, whether uncertainty-aware surrogates can resolve the emulator domain gap, and whether the approach scales to full-resolution satellite scenes beyond 64×64 patches.
