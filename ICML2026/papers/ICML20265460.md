## Problem

Brain functional networks are traditionally constructed using fixed, hand-crafted correlation coefficients (Pearson, distance correlation, HSIC) with no learnable component, which may discard critical information during construction and cap downstream performance regardless of how sophisticated the graph model is. The paper argues this mirrors the pre-representation-learning era in machine learning—hand-crafted features paired with learnable predictors—and proposes treating the brain network itself as a learnable, graph-structured representation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265460/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes row-wise z-score-normalized BOLD time series of N ROIs (e.g., 200×100 for ABIDE, 190×160 for ADHD-200) as input and produces both a learned N×N adjacency matrix (via TopK sparsification, row-wise normalization, and a nonlinear activation) and a disease class label. The adjacency matrix is not a fixed input to a downstream model but the learned representation itself, and the predictor is a simple MLP with pooling—no iterative message passing. The time series dimension D must be fixed, requiring truncation or padding for heterogeneous-length recordings.

## Method

The core mechanism is the Mapping of High-order Dependence Measure (HDM), which unifies linear and nonlinear correlations as inner products in a latent space and replaces the fixed projection (identity matrix for Pearson, difference matrix for dCor/HSIC) with a learnable square matrix O ∈ R^{D×D}, yielding Z = X̂O and A = σ(Norm(TopK(ZZᵀ))). This bilinear form with a learnable parameter captures high-order temporal dependencies (analogous to U/V-statistics) while remaining computationally tractable. The downstream predictor is a two-layer MLP with pooling, and training is end-to-end: cross-entropy on the classification label plus a denoising-autoencoder regularizer that reconstructs corrupted BOLD signals through a GCN using the learned adjacency A. Theoretical analysis proves universal approximation to any continuous U/V-statistic via multi-head inner products (m² channels reconstruct the full outer product), while showing a single inner-product channel is strictly limited in expressivity.

## Results

On the ADHD-200 dataset, BRep achieves the best overall performance with 84.53% AUC, 77.82% ACC, 78.11% SEN, and 73.30% SPE, outperforming the second-best BQN by 2.14% on ACC. On ABIDE, BRep achieves 73.58% ACC and 74.86% SEN (both best among all methods), though BQN retains a slight edge on AUC (79.85% vs. 77.64%) and SPE (72.03% vs. 68.12%). BRep consistently outperforms all GNN and Graph Transformer baselines across both datasets, demonstrating that learnable high-order connectivity construction with a simple MLP predictor surpasses complex message-passing architectures fed with fixed Pearson correlations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BRep's closest competitor is BQN (a quadratic NN using fixed Pearson correlations), which leads on ABIDE AUC (79.85% vs. 77.64%) and SPE (72.03% vs. 68.12%) but trails BRep on ABIDE ACC (+1.05%) and SEN (+1.60%), and on ADHD-200 AUC (84.53% vs. 83.34%), ACC (77.82% vs. 75.68%, a 2.14% gap), and SPE (73.30% vs. 71.63%). Against all six GNN baselines (GCN, GAT, BrainGNN, BrainGB, FBNetGen, A-GCL), BRep achieves the highest ACC on both datasets, with margins of 2.5–14.3% over the best GNN (A-GCL). Against all seven Graph Transformer baselines (SAN, Graphormer, GraphTrans, BrainNETTF, ContrastPool, ALTER, BioBGT), BRep outperforms the best GT (ALTER) by 4.34% on ADHD-200 ACC and 3.48% on ABIDE ACC. In the graph structure learning comparison (Table 4), BRep surpasses Pro-GNN by 3.51% on ADHD-200 ACC and 2.22% on ABIDE ACC. The paper further shows that adding HDM to existing architectures (GCN+HDM, BrainNETTF+HDM, BQN+HDM) yields consistent gains, confirming that the learnable high-order connectivity itself—not the downstream predictor—is the primary source of improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes brain network construction as a representation learning problem, drawing an explicit analogy to the ML community's shift from hand-crafted features to learned representations. It unifies classical linear (Pearson) and nonlinear (dCor, HSIC) correlations as inner products in latent spaces with fixed mappings, then generalizes them into a parametric, learnable high-order dependence measure (HDM) that approximates U/V-statistics. While prior end-to-end graph structure learning methods (Mahmood et al., 2021; Noman et al., 2025) treat the learned graph as an intermediate topology still requiring complex downstream GNNs or Graph Transformers, BRep conceptualizes the graph itself as the final semantic representation, reducing the predictor to a simple MLP. The theoretical analysis builds on universal approximation theorems to show that a single inner-product channel is insufficient for high-order dependencies, while multi-head inner products recover universality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a core assumption in brain network analysis—that performance gains come from increasingly sophisticated graph architectures (GNNs, Graph Transformers) applied to fixed Pearson-correlation networks—by showing that the fixed construction step is the actual bottleneck. The practical implication is significant: a simple MLP with a learned, high-order connectivity estimator outperforms complex graph models with hand-crafted correlations across four datasets, and the learned connectivity exhibits biologically interpretable modular structure. Open questions include extending beyond pairwise ROI interactions to hypergraph or tensor-based representations for multi-region synergistic effects, and handling variable-length BOLD sequences without the fixed-dimension constraint on the learnable matrix O.
