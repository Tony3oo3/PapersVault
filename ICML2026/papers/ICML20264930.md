## Problem

Existing dimensionality reduction methods for neuroscience data either ignore temporal structure (mapping approaches like PCA, VAEs, CEBRA), distorting or scrambling dynamics in latent space, or model temporal evolution via latent dynamical systems that struggle under non-repeatability and noise-dominated regimes. No prior method simultaneously learns a low-dimensional latent representation that preserves temporal relations AND produces identifiable, reproducible latent coordinates across training runs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264930/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes uniformly sampled time series across three modalities: smoothed neural spike counts (137 neurons × 100 timesteps), grayscale video frames (28×28 or 64×64), and audio spectrograms (64×64), with optional lag-h history windows for the dynamical flow. It produces a pair of learned vector fields (compressive and dynamical) defined over a continuous compression coordinate τ∈[0,1], yielding latent representations at every compression level, ordered latent coordinates (via nested dropout prefix masking), and reconstructed rollouts by integrating both flows as neural ODEs with a DOPRI5 solver. The latent space is identifiable up to a per-coordinate sign, making it reproducible across random seeds.

## Method

DCF learns two coupled flow fields via conditional flow matching: a compressive flow u_φ that transports data along a compression axis τ from a low-dimensional source to high-dimensional data space, and a dynamical flow v_θ that models temporal velocity at every compression level using a double linear bridge (interpolating first in τ, then in t). A deterministic encoder coupling x^(0) = b + LD^{1/2}·μ_ψ(x^(1)) defines the source distribution, where L (orthonormal via non-pivoted QR) sets subspace orientation, D (diagonal) controls per-coordinate scale, and μ_ψ is a learned nonlinear map with batch normalization. Nested dropout samples a prefix length K~Geom(p) each forward pass, zeroing encoder coordinates beyond K to enforce an ordered, soft low-rank source while breaking permutation invariance and ensuring identifiability up to sign. Training minimizes a combined loss of compressive flow matching, dynamical flow matching, and a masked alignment loss, with all three terms weighted equally; flows are parameterized by U-Net-style convolutional encoder-decoders for image/audio data and MLPs for neural data.

## Results

On the Neural Latents Benchmark '21 (monkey center-out reach), DCF achieves a median R² of 0.304 for linear cursor-velocity decoding from 3D latents, outperforming VAE (0.242), MARBLE (0.195), DMD (0.014), T-PHATE (−0.012), and CEBRA (0.202), though slightly below LFADS (0.311). For reconstruction of held-out firing rates, DCF achieves R² = 0.999, dramatically exceeding VAE (0.618) and LFADS (0.595). On the Musall mouse video (71,942 frames), DCF recovers four interpretable latent bands corresponding to distinct movement states, a structure that DMD and CEBRA fail to separate. On the rotating-ball toy dataset, DCF recovers a smooth closed-loop latent trajectory while most comparison models fail to identify the underlying low-dimensional manifold.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DCF outperforms five of six baselines on cursor-velocity decoding (Table 1): VAE (0.242), MARBLE (0.195), DMD (0.014), T-PHATE (−0.012), and CEBRA (0.202) all fall significantly below DCF's 0.304. LFADS is the only method that marginally exceeds DCF on this decoding task (0.311 vs. 0.304), but DCF overwhelmingly surpasses LFADS on reconstruction quality (R² = 0.999 vs. 0.595, Table S3). On the challenging Musall mouse video, DCF identifies four distinct latent bands and cleanly separates transient paw/controller movements as outliers, whereas DMD and CEBRA collapse these bands and fail to isolate outliers. On the rotating-ball simulation, most comparison models (VAE, MARBLE, DMD, T-PHATE, CEBRA) fail to recover the simple closed-loop latent structure that DCF captures. A key differentiator is cross-run stability: DCF's latent geometry is reproducible across five random seeds (identifiable up to sign), while LFADS and CEBRA show substantially lower seed-to-seed consistency on the monkey reach data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the flow-matching framework (Lipman et al., 2023) and the nested dropout mechanism (Rippel et al., 2014) to construct a dual flow-matching architecture with one compressive flow for dimension reduction and one dynamical flow for temporal evolution. The closest prior work is de Albuquerque & Pearson (2024), which used probability flow ODEs to map data into a low-dimensional latent space with identifiability but did not model temporal dynamics; DCF extends this by jointly learning a dynamical velocity field at every compression level and replacing the fixed source distribution with a learned encoder coupling. The paper also departs from standard latent flow/diffusion models (which rely on pre-trained front-end encoders) by making the dimension-reducing flow itself the primary inference mechanism, trained to minimize distortion of temporal dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dual-flow approach addresses a long-standing tension in neuroscience: mapping-based dimensionality reduction scrambles dynamics, while dynamical-systems approaches struggle under noise and non-repeatability. The identifiability-up-to-sign property (reproducible latent spaces across random seeds) is a practically significant advantage over VAEs, LFADS, and CEBRA, which show seed-to-seed variability. Key limitations include the need to manually set the effective latent dimension K_eff, inapplicability to discrete spike-count data without modification, and the fact that the model does not estimate intrinsic dimensionality. Open questions include adaptive learning of K_eff, extension to discrete flow matching for spike data, and whether the approach scales to much larger population recordings.
