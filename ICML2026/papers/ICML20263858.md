## Problem

Large reasoning models (LRMs) with long chain-of-thought reasoning unintentionally leak harmful information when given open-ended, sensitive-but-benign prompts, because current alignment methods enforce a binary refuse-or-respond boundary that fails to capture the nuanced safety boundary in ambiguous queries. This 'unintended elicitation' issue is widespread across LRMs and is exacerbated by their multi-step reasoning capabilities, which produce longer traces with more opportunities for harmful knowledge to surface.

## Input / Output

The method takes open-ended text prompts about sensitive topics (deliberately neutral in phrasing, not explicitly requesting harmful content) combined with a fixed safety principle system prompt that instructs the model to analyze social norms and applicable law before responding. The output is structured in two mandatory sections: a chain-of-thought reasoning trace under '### Analysis' and a final user-facing response under '### Final Response', with maximum prompt length of 1024 tokens and generated output up to 2048 tokens. The key design constraint is that the input prompts are open-ended and ambiguous, giving the model a wide answer space where it must autonomously determine the safe response boundary.

## Method

OpenSafeRL is a reinforcement learning framework (REINFORCE) that trains models to self-search for safe reasoning patterns in open-ended environments, rather than relying on binary refusal. The core mechanism is a lexicographic hierarchical reward function where safety acts as a hard binary gate (violation yields reward -1, nullifying all utility gains), followed by weighted faithfulness (β=0.3) and helpfulness (γ=0.2) scores, with GPT-4o serving as the reward model. A scalable data synthesis pipeline (inspired by Alpaca) generates 1.4k category-balanced open-ended risky prompts across 12 hazardous categories, combined with 1.2k harmful prompts from BeaverTails for RL training. A general safety principle system prompt guides exploration for sample efficiency, and the model learns context-aware safety boundaries that generalize to jailbreak robustness and over-refusal mitigation without sacrificing general capability.

## Results

On the main safety benchmark (Table 2), OpenSafeRL achieves an average jailbreak ASR of 10.7% on Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct and 11.2% on LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct, matching or slightly beating Circuit Breakers (11.8% and 9.6% respectively) while dramatically reducing over-refusal to 2.02% and 3.7% (vs. 31.07% and 34.82% for CB). On their own OpenLeakBench (Table 4), the method halves the harmfulness rate from 62% to 23.5% on Qwen-2.5-7B and from 37.5% to 14% on LLaMA-3.1-8B, while cutting average harmful tokens by ~50%. General capability is preserved: average scores on MMLU/BBH/GSM8K/MT-Bench remain within 0.5–1.0 points of the vanilla model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Circuit Breakers (CB, Zou et al. 2024), OpenSafeRL achieves comparable or slightly better average ASR (10.7% vs. 11.8% on Qwen; 11.2% vs. 9.6% on LLaMA) but dramatically lower over-refusal (2.02% vs. 31.07% on Qwen; 3.7% vs. 34.82% on LLaMA), indicating CB over-refuses benign queries. Against Gradient Ascent / Safe Unlearning (GA, Zhang et al. 2024), OpenSafeRL wins on both robustness (10.7% vs. 38.2% on Qwen) and over-refusal (2.02% vs. 70.35% on Qwen), showing GA's unlearning approach causes severe over-refusal. Against SFT with SafeChain data (Jiang et al. 2025), OpenSafeRL substantially outperforms on robustness (10.7% vs. 52.8% on Qwen) and over-refusal (2.02% vs. 17.93%), while SFT degrades capability more. Compared to GPT-4o as a reference (Table 14), the RL-aligned LLaMA-3-8B achieves a lower harmfulness rate (17.0% vs. 31.2%) and fewer harmful tokens (97.8 vs. 235.8), suggesting the method surpasses the teacher model's safety on open-ended adversarial prompts rather than merely distilling it.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant refusal-style alignment paradigm (SFT, DPO, RLHF) that trains models to binary-reject overtly harmful inputs, and from reasoning-based safety methods like Deliberative Alignment (Guan et al., 2024) and SafeChain (Jiang et al., 2025) that distill fixed safety CoT patterns. Its key conceptual departure is framing safety as a self-search problem in open-ended environments: rather than teaching a model a predetermined refusal path, OpenSafeRL uses REINFORCE with a lexicographic (safety-dominant) reward to let the model discover context-appropriate safety boundaries on its own. The hierarchical reward design—where a safety violation nullifies all utility gains—draws on constrained optimization ideas but is applied here specifically to the reasoning trace, penalizing unsafe intermediate states rather than just final outputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LRMs with long reasoning chains are deployed in high-stakes domains (healthcare, cybersecurity), the finding that extended CoT actually amplifies harmful knowledge leakage (up to 2× more harmful tokens vs. standard LLMs) is a critical warning that current alignment pipelines are insufficient. The practical accessibility of the method (~$20 API cost, 2.5-hour training on 8×A100) lowers the barrier for safety alignment, and the strong over-refusal reduction (2–3% vs. 30–70% for baselines) addresses a real deployment pain point. Open questions remain: whether the approach scales to frontier-scale models (tested only up to 14B), how it handles multi-turn adversarial interactions, and whether reliance on a GPT-4o reward model introduces subtle distributional biases that could erode with model family updates.
