## Problem

Current embodied AI systems treat privacy as a stage-local patch (e.g., face blurring in perception) rather than a life-cycle-level architectural constraint, yet privacy leakage is compositional across the instruction–perception–planning–interaction pipeline and often irreversible in high-frequency domestic deployments. The privacy-utility trade-off is non-linear and context-dependent, but existing fixed policies fail to adapt across environments, leaving a gap between abstract regulatory mandates (GDPR, CCPA, PIPL) and concrete, stage-aware system design.

## Input / Output

The system consumes RGB-D camera streams, LiDAR point clouds, and text navigation instructions, with a privacy level context (L1–L4) acting as a dynamic control signal that reconfigures the entire pipeline rather than merely filtering a single modality. Outputs include a navigation trajectory, a privacy-enforced system configuration (e.g., which modalities are active, whether computation runs in cloud or local TEE, data retention duration), and a quantitative characterization of the privacy-utility trade-off via success rate and SPL. Notably, at L4 the system may hard-cutoff visual feeds entirely and restrict execution to volatile memory, meaning the 'output' can be a deliberate refusal to navigate rather than a degraded trajectory.

## Method

SPINE introduces a four-level privacy classification matrix (L1 Public → L4 Restricted), where each level is defined by a tuple P_L = {S, I, C, Φ} specifying scenario context, permitted information flow, enforced control primitive, and dominant utility objective, with a highest-triggering-criterion escalation rule. A dynamic orchestration layer maps each level to concrete technical primitives across all four EAI stages—e.g., L3 activates local semantic sanitization, restricted field-of-view, and privacy cost maps in planning, while L4 enforces TEE-encapsulated instruction processing, source-level sensing cutoff, and in-memory-only execution with secure wipe. The paper validates this architecture through a controlled embodied-navigation probe (R2R-CE with ETPNav in Habitat, plus a real-world AGV on AgileX SCOUT MINI), using pixelation strength K as a tunable proxy for perception-stage privacy control. Two structural hypotheses are tested: H1 (Semantic Compensation) predicts that high-level instruction intent preserves baseline task success despite perceptual degradation, and H2 (Heuristic Decoupling) predicts that severe visual loss fractures perception–planning coupling, causing SPL to degrade disproportionately relative to SR.

## Results

On the R2R-CE benchmark (Habitat simulator, ETPNav), increasing pixelation strength from K=1 to K=8 reduces Success Rate (SR) from 0.660 to 0.460 (−30.3%) and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) from 0.600 to 0.340 (−43.3%) on Val-Seen. On Val-UnSeen, the SPL degradation (−37.2%) notably exceeds the SR drop (−29.4%), confirming that privacy-induced perceptual loss disproportionately fractures planning efficiency relative to task completion. The non-linear gap between SR and SPL losses supports the paper's H1 (Semantic Compensation) and H2 (Heuristic Decoupling) hypotheses, showing that high-level intent preserves baseline success while path-planning quality degrades sharply.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, SPINE does not benchmark against competing navigation or privacy methods; instead, it uses ETPNav as a fixed navigation backbone and varies the perception-stage privacy intervention (pixelation strength K) to isolate the privacy-utility trade-off. The 'baseline' is the vanilla K=1 setting (no privacy constraint), against which all stronger privacy levels are measured. The key finding is that the trade-off is non-linear: mild pixelation (K=4) causes modest degradation (SR −4.8%, SPL −9.8% on Val-Seen), while aggressive pixelation (K=8) triggers a sharp cliff (SR −30.3%, SPL −43.3%), and further increases (K=16, 32) yield diminishing additional loss. This non-linearity is the paper's central empirical claim—that privacy constraints do not degrade utility proportionally but can abruptly decouple perception from planning. Real-world AGV experiments qualitatively confirm that the L1→L4 classification correctly gates navigation access (e.g., L4 Bedroom entry is prohibited), but no quantitative real-world metrics are reported.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the dominant stage-local privacy paradigm in embodied AI—where perception-stage anonymization (Kim et al., 2019; Choi et al., 2025), privacy-aware motion planning (Shome et al., 2023), and interaction-stage disclosure policies (Dietrich et al., 2023) are treated as independent patches—by arguing that privacy leakage is compositional across the instruction-perception-planning-interaction pipeline. Rather than introducing a new algorithm, SPINE repurposes existing privacy-preserving primitives (TEE, FHE, ZKP, differential privacy, federated learning) as a unified, level-gated orchestration layer governed by a four-tier classification matrix (L1–L4). The empirical probe reuses the standard R2R-CE benchmark and ETPNav algorithm in Habitat, applying pixelation as a controllable proxy for perception-stage privacy strength, and complements it with a small-scale real-world AGV testbed, positioning the work as a conceptual framework validated through a narrow but falsifiable case study rather than a new navigation method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central practical insight—that privacy-utility degradation is non-linear, with path-efficiency (SPL) collapsing far more sharply than task success (SR) under perceptual sanitization—gives system designers a concrete warning that naive privacy patches can silently destabilize planning without obvious task-failure signals. However, the empirical evidence remains preliminary: a single navigation algorithm, one intervention type (pixelation), and a 4 m × 4 m real-world testbed limit generalizability, and the L1–L4 orchestration logic has not been stress-tested across diverse task types, multi-robot settings, or adversarial threat models. Key open questions include formal verification of cross-stage privacy invariants, real-time context-adaptive level switching under latency budgets, and bridging the regulatory-technical gap where GDPR-style mandates remain agnostic to the staged, closed-loop nature of embodied systems.
