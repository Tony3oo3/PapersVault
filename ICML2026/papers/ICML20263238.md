## Problem

Existing scaling laws for dense neural networks track error as a function of total parameter count, dataset size, and compute, but Mixture-of-Experts Transformers decouple total parameters from per-input active compute, making this accounting inadequate. A theory that cleanly separates the benefits of active per-token capacity from the statistical overhead induced by routing combinatorics has been missing, leaving it unclear which MoE scaling behaviors are certified by worst-case analysis and which arise from data-dependent routing structure or optimization dynamics.

## Input / Output

The method operates on causal decoder-only Transformer language models with MoE feed-forward layers, taking text token sequences (length 128 or 256) as input and producing next-token predictions. The theoretical framework takes as architectural inputs the number of experts M, active experts per token k, number of Transformer blocks L_T, and per-expert parameter budget, and produces closed-form generalization bounds and scaling exponents (α_N, α_D, α_C) as functions of the active parameter budget N_act, intrinsic data dimension d, and smoothness β. Empirically, the framework is validated on three text corpora (TinyStories, WikiText-103, OpenWebText) with estimated intrinsic dimensions of d≈23, 32, and 45 respectively, using GPT-2 hidden-state representations for dimension estimation.

## Method

The core technique conditions on a fixed top-k routing pattern, reducing the MoE Transformer to a deterministic active subnetwork, then derives a sup-norm covering-number bound via a parameter-to-function Lipschitz argument over the active parameter space. A union bound over all routing patterns adds a MoE-specific combinatorial overhead of order L_T·ℓ·k·log(eM/k), yielding a metric-entropy decomposition that separates active-parameter complexity from routing complexity. Under a d-dimensional manifold data model with C^β targets, this produces an approximation rate of N_act^{-2β/d} (matching dense-network minimax rates measured against active capacity) and a generalization bound where estimation scales as N_act/n plus the routing term. From these bounds, the paper derives neural scaling laws: data scaling exponent α_D = 2β/(2β+d), model scaling exponent α_N = 2β/d, and compute-optimal allocation N_act* ∝ C^{d/(2β+2d)}, all recovering dense-network exponents when measured against active parameters. Empirical validation on three corpora confirms the predicted exponents and reveals a two-regime routing behavior: a moderate regime (M/k ≤ 8) where loss increases with k·log(eM/k) as predicted, and a specialization-dominated regime at larger M/k where expert specialization yields gains beyond worst-case guarantees.

## Results

The paper's headline quantitative result is the close agreement between theoretical and empirical scaling exponents across three datasets. For WikiText-103 (d=32, β=1.0), the theoretical model-scaling exponent α_N=0.063 matches the empirical estimate 0.060, and the data-scaling exponent α_D=0.059 matches 0.058. For TinyStories (d=23, β=1.0), theory predicts α_N=0.087 vs. empirical 0.085, and α_D=0.080 vs. 0.081. The compute-optimal consistency relation α_D ≈ α_N/(1+α_N) holds to within 0.001–0.003 across all datasets, confirming that MoE models recover dense-network scaling exponents when measured against the active parameter budget N_act rather than total parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing MoE architectures or training methods; instead, it validates its theoretical predictions against empirical measurements and situates them relative to prior scaling-law frameworks. The empirical exponents closely match the intrinsic-dimension theory of Havrilla & Liao (2024) for dense Transformers, confirming that MoE models inherit dense-style scaling when model size is measured by active parameters N_act. Compared to empirical MoE scaling laws (JMSL, Ludziejewski et al. 2025; EL, Tian et al. 2025), the paper's parameter-free identities (α_D = α_N/(1+α_N), α_C = α_N·α_D/(α_N+α_D)) hold approximately in the routing-negligible regime, while deviations grow with expert pool size M, which the authors attribute to expert specialization effects outside the worst-case uniform bound. The routing ablation (Figure 1) reveals a two-regime behavior: for M/k ≤ 8, validation loss increases monotonically with the routing term k·log(eM/k) as predicted by theory, but for larger M/k, performance improves due to specialization, a regime the worst-case analysis does not capture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Havrilla & Liao (2024) framework for dense Transformer scaling on intrinsically low-dimensional data to the Mixture-of-Experts setting, introducing a clean decomposition where approximation and estimation rates are governed by the active per-token parameter budget N_act rather than total parameters, while routing contributes an additive combinatorial overhead of order L_T·ℓ·k·log(eM/k). The MoE architecture follows the standard sparsely-gated top-k design of Shazeer et al. (2017) and its descendants (Switch Transformers, GShard, GLaM), and the approximation construction lifts deep ReLU manifold-approximation results (Yarotsky, 2017) via partitions of unity. The empirical scaling analysis connects to the growing body of MoE-specific scaling law work (Krajewski et al., Ludziejewski et al., Tian et al.) but provides the first worst-case statistical guarantees that formally separate active capacity from routing multiplicity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled statistical reference point for MoE scaling: it certifies that dense-style exponents reappear when model size is measured in active parameters, and that routing combinatorics shift constants (via a log(eM/k) factor) rather than exponents in the worst-case regime. The empirical two-regime routing ablation—where moderate M/k degrades performance as predicted but large M/k improves it through expert specialization—highlights a key gap: the theory does not capture data-dependent routing structure or specialization gains, which are likely the dominant practical effects in deployed MoE systems. Open questions include replacing worst-case routing counts with effective routing entropy, deriving matching lower bounds, and incorporating optimization dynamics and load-balancing effects that the current uniform analysis cannot address.
