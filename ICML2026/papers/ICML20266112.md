## Problem

Imperfect-recall extensive-form games (IREFGs) model realistic sequential decision-making where players forget past information, but equilibrium computation in this setting is provably hard: solving two-player zero-sum IREFGs is NP-hard, and even deciding whether a behavioral Nash equilibrium exists is hard. While the connection between IREFGs and polynomial optimization over products of simplices is well-known, it has been used almost exclusively to prove hardness results. This paper asks what positive algorithmic guarantees the Moment-SOS hierarchy provides for IREFGs and identifies structural conditions under which convergence is finite or occurs at a single SDP level.

## Input / Output

The method takes an IREFG specified by its game tree, information-set partition, and terminal payoffs, which are converted in polynomial time into a polynomial utility function over behavioral strategy variables (one probability simplex per information set). In the single-player case, the output is an ex-ante optimal behavioral strategy with a global optimality certificate; in the multi-player case, it is either a behavioral Nash equilibrium or a certificate of non-existence. The SOS hierarchy produces a sequence of SDP relaxations at increasing polynomial degrees, and the flatness condition on moment matrices guarantees extraction of an optimal strategy once the relaxation is exact.

## Method

The paper applies the Lasserre Moment-SOS hierarchy to IREFGs via their polynomial-game representation. For single-player IREFGs, the hierarchy converges asymptotically to the ex-ante optimum, finitely under generic utility coefficients, and exactly at level ℓ+1 (where ℓ is the number of infosets) for non-absentminded games via a vertex-restricted formulation that exploits the multi-affineness of the utility. For multi-player IREFGs, the paper adapts the KKT-based approach of Nie & Tang (2024), concatenating each player's KKT stationarity conditions into a semi-algebraic superset of Nash equilibria and solving it with a select-verify-cut (SVC) loop of Moment-SOS subproblems. The paper further introduces SOS-concave and SOS-monotone IREFGs—subclasses where the negative block Hessian or symmetrized Jacobian is a sum-of-squares matrix polynomial—showing that in the single-player case the hierarchy converges at the first level, reducing equilibrium computation to a single SDP.

## Results

On randomly generated single-player IREFGs (3 actions per infoset, 50 instances per setting), SOS always recovers a certified global optimum at truncation degree D_SOS = D_u, while PGD with 100 random restarts reaches the global optimum only 70.7%–88.2% of the time depending on game size and polynomial degree. SOS runtime ranges from 0.02 s (ℓ=2, D_u=4) to 6.64 s (ℓ=2, D_u=10), whereas PGD completes in 0.05–0.25 s per run. Scalability is the main limitation: at fixed D_u=6, SOS runtime grows from 0.086 s (ℓ=2) to 1694.92 s (ℓ=6), and at fixed ℓ=2, increasing D_u from 4 to 16 inflates runtime from 0.018 s to 1674.72 s.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole empirical baseline is projected gradient descent (PGD) with 100 random restarts, drawn from recent work on local optima in imperfect-recall games (Tewolde et al., 2026). SOS wins decisively on solution quality: it achieves 100% global optimality with a certificate in every instance, whereas PGD's success rate drops to as low as ~71% (ℓ=4, D_u=4) and is highly instance-dependent (some polynomials are near-trivial at ~100%, others drop to ~1%). PGD wins on raw speed, completing in 0.05–0.25 s per run versus SOS's 0.02–6.64 s, but this advantage narrows or reverses for small games (e.g., ℓ=2, D_u=4: SOS 0.02 s vs. PGD 0.05 s). The paper notes that no other general-purpose baseline exists for certified global optima or behavioral Nash equilibria in IREFGs, so the comparison is inherently asymmetric: SOS provides a global certificate while PGD only guarantees convergence to a KKT-type stationary point. Overall, SOS is competitive in runtime for small-to-moderate instances and strictly superior in reliability, but its SDP size grows rapidly with both the number of infosets and polynomial degree, limiting practical applicability to modest game sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the IREFG-to-polynomial-game connection (Piccione & Rubinstein, 1997), which prior work had used almost exclusively to establish hardness results, as the foundation for positive algorithmic guarantees via the Moment-SOS hierarchy (Lasserre, 2001). For the multi-player case, it adapts the KKT-based select-verify-cut framework of Nie & Tang (2024) for polynomial games, extending it to the product-of-simplices structure inherent to behavioral strategies. The introduction of SOS-concave and SOS-monotone IREFGs draws directly on Helton & Nie's (2010) SOS-convex/SOS-monotone representations, translating them into the game-theoretic setting to obtain first-level (single-SDP) convergence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first general, provably convergent framework for computing behavioral equilibria in IREFGs, filling a gap left by the NP-hardness landscape: while prior results showed the problem is intractable in the worst case, this paper identifies structural conditions (non-absentmindedness, genericity, SOS-concavity/monotonicity) under which convergence is finite or even occurs at a single SDP level. The main limitation is scalability—experiments are confined to at most 9 infosets and degree 16, with runtimes growing super-exponentially—so practical applicability to the large-scale IREFGs motivating the area (poker abstractions, team games) remains open. Future directions include combining the polynomial formulation with treewidth-based Lasserre variants and incorporating scalable SDP solvers (low-rank, DSOS/SDSOS) to close this gap.
