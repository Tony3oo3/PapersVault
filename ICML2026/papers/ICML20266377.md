## Problem

Current inference-time privacy mechanisms for industrial computer vision—primarily post-hoc blurring and inpainting—rely on error-prone detection of sensitive regions and do not prevent the captured data from being repurposed for unauthorized inferences (function creep). The paper argues that what is fundamentally missing is an architectural mechanism to bound and quantify information retention at the point of capture, rather than attempting to conceal sensitive pixels after the fact.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266377/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes raw RGB camera frames (or video) at the edge device and produces a filtered, task-specific representation X̃ in an image-like format, designed for plug-and-play compatibility with existing downstream pipelines and pretrained models. Only X̃ is transmitted to a cloud or on-premise service, where a frozen task model M_frozen generates predictions (e.g., skeletal pose). The filter is trained solely with task labels Y_T, requiring no annotations of sensitive attributes such as identity or demographics.

## Method

The paper formalizes strict data minimization through the Information Bottleneck functional min_θ [H(Y_T | X̃) + βI(X̃; X)], which simultaneously enforces task utility and penalizes information retention beyond what the declared task requires. This provides an architecture-agnostic, information-theoretic definition of purpose limitation that subsumes existing approaches like adversarial training and disentangled representation learning as approximations of the same bound. The proposed architecture is modular: a neural-network privacy filter f_θ operates as a lightweight frontend inside a Trusted Execution Environment on the camera, transforming raw frames before they leave the device, while the downstream task model remains frozen and unmodified. Two key design requirements follow from the formulation: utility-only supervision (no sensitive-attribute labels needed) and holistic full-frame minimization (a global constraint on I(X̃; X) rather than region-specific redaction). The paper positions this as a theoretical framework and implementation blueprint rather than a new training algorithm, identifying concrete research challenges in TEE-constrained deployment, audit protocols, and quantifying residual task-sensitive attribute correlations.

## Results

This is a position paper that proposes a formal framework rather than reporting quantitative experimental results. The central contribution is the Information Bottleneck objective (Eq. 1) as an operational definition of purpose limitation, along with four system-level requirements (utility-only supervision, holistic full-frame minimization, task model preservation, and edge-native enforcement). No benchmarks, accuracy numbers, or ablation studies are presented; the paper's 'evidence' is a qualitative analysis of existing inference-time privacy mechanisms and a deployment blueprint.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not run quantitative comparisons but instead provides a structured qualitative critique of four categories of existing inference-time privacy mechanisms. Redactive obfuscation (blurring/inpainting) is argued to be fundamentally reactive, limited by detection reliability, and task-agnostic, degrading both sensitive and task-relevant information. Differential Privacy is characterized as protecting attributes within already-collected data rather than preventing over-collection at capture, and is positioned as complementary to (not a replacement for) data minimization. Cryptographic methods (HE, SMPC) and TEEs are acknowledged for confidentiality-in-use but criticized for being agnostic to what information is represented, offering weak barriers against first-party function creep. Optical privacy filters (e.g., PrivateEye, PrivHAR) are identified as the closest existing paradigm to the proposed approach, achieving physical omission at capture, but are limited by hardware inflexibility. The proposed IB-based strict data minimization is positioned as combining the strong function-creep protection of optical filters with the adaptability of digital methods, enforced via TEEs at the edge.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper repurposes the Information Bottleneck principle (Tishby et al., 2000) as a formal, system-level definition of GDPR data minimization for industrial vision, rather than treating it as merely a training-time regularization. It deliberately departs from the dominant inference-time privacy paradigms—redactive obfuscation, differential privacy, cryptographic confidentiality (HE/SMPC/TEEs), and optical privacy filters—arguing that all of them protect *access* to data rather than bounding *what is captured*, and thus leave function creep structurally unaddressed. The paper also situates its proposal within the Privacy by Design and contextual integrity literatures, and acknowledges that adversarial training, disentangled representation learning, and optical feature separation are empirical strategies that implicitly approximate the IB objective without making it an enforceable architectural constraint.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's main contribution is a research agenda: it identifies that no existing technical mechanism enforces purpose limitation at the architectural level, and outlines what would be needed—lightweight IB-optimized filters running inside edge TEEs, standardized continuous privacy audits that survive model and sensor drift, context-aware risk metrics that account for auxiliary side-information, and intelligible interfaces for non-expert stakeholders. Key open questions include quantifying residual semantic correlations between task signals and sensitive attributes (e.g., gait and identity), closing the hardware gap between constrained CPU enclaves and data-center confidential GPUs, and designing multi-task filter architectures that fit within TEE memory budgets. As a position paper with no experiments, its impact depends on whether the community adopts the IB-as-compliance framing and invests in the proposed implementation path.
