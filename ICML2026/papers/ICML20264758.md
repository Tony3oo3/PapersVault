## Problem

In shared representation learning across heterogeneous data sources, it is standard practice to pool all available sources when training a common feature extractor. This paper asks whether including all sources—even individually 'good' ones with comparable relevance and quality—can inadvertently impair subspace estimation, and if so, which subset of sources should be retained to achieve statistical optimality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264758/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes M distributed sources, each providing n_i local samples (x_ij, y_ij) generated under a shared low-dimensional subspace model, and produces a one-shot selected subpopulation S ⊆ [M] of source indices. In practice, the true local-head matrix A is inaccessible, so the empirical algorithm (Algorithm 2) operates on data-driven proxies constructed from split local averaging (Z-bar and Z-tilde matrices). The selected subpopulation is then used as a fixed filter before downstream subspace estimation (e.g., split-averaging, MoM, or DFHT), making this a pre-screening step rather than iterative client selection during training.

## Method

The core contribution is a source-screening principle grounded in the geometry of the local-head matrix A = [α_1*, …, α_M*]: a subpopulation S is 'admissible' if the condition number of Σ_{i∈S} α_i*(α_i*)^⊤ is Θ(1) and |S| = Θ(k·λ_min(AA^⊤)), and training exclusively on S achieves the minimax-optimal rate O(√(d/(Nλ_k))) even when discarding a large fraction of sources. Algorithm 1 (genie-aided) identifies such a subpopulation by iteratively sampling random column blocks, checking whether their Gram matrix is close to identity in the ‖·‖_{∞→1} norm, and pruning via Grothendieck factorization to extract well-conditioned blocks whose union forms S. Algorithm 2 replaces the inaccessible A with a data-driven proxy built from split local averaging, using stable rank as a consistent surrogate. The theory extends to nonlinear models by replacing linear local heads with effective heads α_i* = E[h_i(U)U], reducing the screening problem to the same matrix-geometric condition independent of the functional form of h_i.

## Results

On the ACSIncome dataset (d=10, partitioned by state), the proposed source-screening method achieves 74.2% binary classification accuracy at k=7, outperforming full-population training (72.8%) and the Power-of-Choice baseline (73.0%). On CelebA smile classification (d=192 via ViT-Tiny embeddings, partitioned by identity), the method reaches 90.5% accuracy at k=7, compared to 89.5% for full population and 89.8% for Power-of-Choice. In synthetic subspace estimation experiments, the empirical algorithm consistently reduces principal angle distance over full-population training, with the gap most pronounced in the clustered coefficient regime where source imbalance is severe.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's method is compared against three baselines: full-population training (no screening), random subsampling, and Power-of-Choice selection (Cho et al., 2020), all constrained to select the same number of sources. The proposed screening procedure wins on both real-world datasets across all tested values of k. On ACSIncome, Ours (k=7) achieves 74.2%, beating Power-of-Choice (73.0%) by 1.2 percentage points and full population (72.8%) by 1.4 points. On CelebA, the gap is 0.7 points over Power-of-Choice (90.5% vs. 89.8%) and 1.0 points over full population (90.5% vs. 89.5%). Random selection is consistently the weakest baseline, confirming that the gains are not merely from reducing data volume but from the structured selection criterion. In the synthetic subspace estimation setting, the method also outperforms a balanced-subsampling oracle that uses ground-truth cluster assignments, indicating the empirical algorithm captures informative structure beyond simple group balancing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the split-averaging estimator and minimax bounds of Niu et al. (2024) for shared linear subspace learning, and on the classic submatrix invertibility result of Bourgain & Tzafriri (1987) (via the algorithmic treatment of Tropp, 2009) to prove that a well-conditioned subset of sources suffices for optimal recovery. It departs from the standard assumption in multi-source representation learning (Tripuraneni et al., 2021; Collins et al., 2021; Duchi et al., 2022) that all sources should be pooled, showing instead that the geometry of the source diversity matrix—not the total sample count—governs statistical rates. The paper also distinguishes itself from federated client-selection literature by performing one-shot pre-screening for shared representation quality rather than iterative active selection for loss minimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core insight—that discarding a large fraction of 'good' sources can yield minimax-optimal subspace estimates—challenges the default practice of pooling all available data in multi-source and federated settings, and provides a principled criterion (admissible subpopulation via stable rank and condition number) for when and how to screen. However, the theory is developed under balanced sample sizes and standardized heads, the empirical algorithm requires knowledge of the latent dimension k, and real-world accuracy gains are modest (1–2% on ACSIncome and CelebA), leaving open whether the benefit persists in highly imbalanced, non-stationary, or truly large-scale federated deployments. Extending the guarantees to the fully empirical (non-genie) setting with heterogeneous sample sizes and to deeper nonlinear architectures remains a key open direction.
