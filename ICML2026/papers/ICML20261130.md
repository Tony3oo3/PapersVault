## Problem

Spectral graph neural networks based on the graph Fourier transform (GFT) are rarely used in practice because computing the GFT requires an O(n³) eigendecomposition and the resulting operations are fully global, lacking the local inductive biases that benefit most graph learning tasks. Conversely, local methods such as polynomial Laplacian filters and message passing struggle with long-range dependencies, requiring deep stacks that suffer from oversquashing. The paper asks whether the GFT can be decomposed into localized operations that preserve exact spectral semantics while enabling efficient, locality-aware spectral processing.

## Input / Output

The method takes a weighted undirected graph with node feature matrices and produces node-level class labels (transductive node classification) or graph-level predictions (inductive tasks such as peptide property prediction). Before any training, the graph undergoes a task-independent preprocessing stage: a hierarchical partition is found via spectral bisection and the inter-subgraph cut edges are sparsified using effective-resistance sampling. The resulting partition and Cauchy factorization fix the network architecture; only the spectral filter parameters (B-spline coefficients) are learned, and the full GFT basis is never explicitly materialized.

## Method

The key insight is that the GFT basis of any graph admits an exact factorization into a product of orthogonal Cauchy-like matrices (OCLMs), each corresponding to a rank-one Laplacian update (i.e., a bridge edge) between two subgraphs in a hierarchical partition. Starting from block-diagonal subgraph GFTs, successive Cauchy factors merge subgraph spectral representations level by level, yielding the global GFT in O(kn²) time (k = cut size) instead of O(n³). L2G-Net exploits this by inserting learnable spectral filters between Cauchy factors at each hierarchical level: local filters process subgraph spectra independently, Cauchy factors mix them across cuts, and a final global filter acts on the full graph representation. A greedy partitioning algorithm selects balanced cuts that minimize the factorization cost, and spectral sparsification bounds the cut size when no favorable partition exists, guaranteeing the Laplacian quadratic form is preserved within (1±ε). The architecture is graph-adaptive—different graphs yield different hierarchies—so the inductive bias encodes graph topology directly into the computation rather than relying on learned attention or positional encodings.

## Results

On the heterophilous transductive benchmarks, L2G-Net achieves 92.12% accuracy on Roman-empire, 53.39% on Amazon-ratings, 97.50% AUC on Minesweeper (best among all methods), and 85.57% AUC on Tolokers, matching or exceeding the strongest baselines (Polynormer, St-ChebNet) while using orders of magnitude fewer learnable parameters. On the City-Networks long-range transductive benchmark (100k–570k nodes), L2G-Net reaches 55.4% (Paris), 69.8% (Shanghai), 63.5% (LA), and 55.6% (London), outperforming ChebNet and GCN and matching Exphormer on Paris while remaining feasible where full eigendecomposition runs out of memory. The Cauchy factorization is 3–4× faster than dense eigendecomposition on the heterophilous suite and 3–4 orders of magnitude faster than projected O(n³) eigendecomposition on City-Networks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GCN (Kipf & Welling, ICLR 2017), L2G-Net wins decisively on all four heterophilous datasets (e.g., 92.12% vs. 73.69% on Roman-empire) and on all four City-Networks graphs (e.g., 55.4% vs. 53.2% on Paris), confirming that the local-to-global spectral inductive bias substantially outperforms standard message passing on non-local tasks. Against Polynormer (Deng et al., ICLR 2024), L2G-Net is competitive but slightly behind on Roman-empire (92.12% vs. 92.55%) and Amazon-ratings (53.39% vs. 54.81%), while matching or exceeding it on Minesweeper (97.50% vs. 97.46% AUC) and Tolokers (85.57% vs. 85.91% AUC); critically, L2G-Net achieves this with orders of magnitude fewer learnable parameters (Figure 5). Against ChebNet (Defferrard et al., NeurIPS 2016), L2G-Net outperforms it on all City-Networks graphs (e.g., 55.4% vs. 54.1% on Paris, 69.8% vs. 66.5% on Shanghai), demonstrating that the hierarchical spectral factorization captures long-range dependencies better than fixed-order polynomial filters. Against St-ChebNet (Hariri et al., NeurIPS 2026), L2G-Net wins on three of four heterophilous datasets (Roman-empire 92.12% vs. 92.03%, Amazon-ratings 53.39% vs. 53.15%, Minesweeper 97.50% vs. 95.71%) and is essentially tied on Tolokers (85.57% vs. 85.55%), while also offering a 3× runtime speedup over full eigendecomposition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the rank-one-update-to-Cauchy-matrix connection established in Fernández-Menduiña et al. (2025a) (Fast DCT+), which was restricted to path-graph Laplacians and required distinct eigenvalues, to arbitrary symmetric matrices and arbitrary graph partitions via the Hierarchical Graph Family (HGF) framework. It draws on the divide-and-conquer eigensolver tradition (Bunch et al., 1978; Cuppen, 1980; Gu and Eisenstat, 1995) but differs in that the input is a sparse graph whose hierarchical structure is given by the topology itself, yielding an exact factorization rather than an approximate HSS compression. For the GNN architecture, L2G-Net departs from both polynomial filters (ChebNet, BernNet) and graph transformers (Polynormer, GraphGPS) by inserting learnable spectral filters between Cauchy factors in a fixed, graph-determined hierarchy, combining locality and globality by construction rather than through attention or depth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The exact Cauchy factorization reduces the cost of computing the GFT basis from O(n³) to O(kn²), making exact spectral processing feasible at scales (569k nodes) where full eigendecomposition is intractable, and L2G-Net achieves competitive accuracy with orders of magnitude fewer learnable parameters than transformer-based baselines. Key limitations include the dependence on favorable graph structure (small cuts), the approximation error introduced by spectral sparsification when cuts are large, and the restriction to undirected graphs. Open questions include extending the framework to directed graphs (e.g., via Hermitian Laplacians), dynamic graphs, and whether the hierarchical inductive bias generalizes beyond the specific partitioning strategies explored here.
