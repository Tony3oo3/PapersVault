## Problem

Existing single-cell perturbation prediction methods rely on highly expressive neural architectures but treat model selection as a post hoc step, ignoring how the experimental measurement time gap fundamentally governs both the computational tractability of recovering latent cell-to-cell correspondences and the required model complexity. This paper identifies a critical time gap Δ that induces a phase transition: below Δ, the problem reduces to polynomial-time supervised learning; above Δ, even recovering the permutation matching becomes NP-hard in the worst case.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two unpaired cell-state matrices X^(0) ∈ R^{d×n} (pre-perturbation) and X^(t) ∈ R^{d×n} (post-perturbation at time t) with no known column-wise correspondence, operating on feature dimensions ranging from d=10 (targeted protein panels) to d=1000 (scRNA-seq genes). It outputs a linear transition map W^(t) ∈ R^{d×d} initialized at the identity, the recovered permutation matrix Π ∈ R^{n×n} encoding the latent coupling, and predicted post-perturbation states for new cells via x̂^(t) = W^(t) x^(0). The key constraint is that the input populations are explicitly unpaired—single-cell assays are destructive—so the method must jointly infer both the matching and the transition without any ground-truth pairing.

## Method

LAOT (Linear Alternating Optimal Transport) alternates between two polynomial-time subproblems: (1) fixing W and solving a linear assignment problem over the Birkhoff polytope to recover the permutation Π, and (2) fixing Π and solving a closed-form multivariate least-squares problem for W, initialized at the identity. The theoretical contribution establishes a Nyquist-like critical time gap Δ = √((1−δ)/(2nL²)) governed by the (2,δ)-restricted isometry property of the initial state matrix and the Lipschitz constant L of the transition dynamics, proving that permutation recovery is polynomial-time tractable for t < Δ and NP-hard for t > Δ (via reduction from PARTITION). A sharper bound Δ = √(1−δ)/(√2 · L·‖X^(0)‖_F) removes the n-dependence in the linear-transition special case. Empirically, LAOT with only d² parameters matches or outperforms nonlinear baselines (CellOT, scGen) on protein and scRNA-seq benchmarks within the trackable regime, while all methods collapse beyond the phase transition.

## Results

On the AP-1 protein perturbation benchmark (48h readout, d=10), LAOT achieves the lowest MMD² across all three melanoma cell lines (0.0006 on COLO858, 0.0007 on WM902B, 0.0011 on SKMEL19), outperforming CellOT (0.044–0.112) and scGen (0.017–0.142) by 1–2 orders of magnitude. On the 4i multiplexed imaging benchmark (8h exposure, d=48), LAOT achieves MMD² of 0.0063 (Imatinib), 0.0080 (Trametinib), and 0.0071 (Dexamethasone), beating CellOT and scGen on two of three drugs. On the high-dimensional SciPlex3 scRNA-seq benchmark (24h, 1000 genes), LAOT attains the lowest MMD² in all three drug conditions (0.0040, 0.0033, 0.0038), outperforming both nonlinear baselines despite using only a single d×d linear map with zero additional parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LAOT (a single linear map solved via alternating assignment + least squares) consistently outperforms both CellOT (input-convex neural network, 4 hidden layers × 64 units) and scGen (VAE with latent-space arithmetic) on the AP-1 protein benchmark, achieving 3–10× lower MMD² across all three cell lines. On the 4i imaging benchmark, LAOT wins on Imatinib (0.0063 vs. 0.0079 for Compact_CellOT) and Dexamethasone (0.0071 vs. 0.0075), while Compact_CellOT edges out LAOT on Trametinib (0.0076 vs. 0.0080); full-capacity CellOT and scGen trail by 4–10×. On SciPlex3 scRNA-seq (Table 2), LAOT achieves the lowest MMD² in all three drug conditions (Trametinib 0.0040, Givinostat 0.0033, Abexinostat 0.0038), beating Compact_CellOT by 15–30% and CellOT/scGen by 30–70%. The paper attributes the nonlinear baselines' underperformance to optimization instability in a nonconvex landscape with tens of thousands of parameters trained on only thousands of cells, whereas LAOT's convex subproblems converge deterministically (zero std across runs). The authors note that Compact_CellOT (reduced to 3×32 units) narrows the gap, supporting their hypothesis that model capacity—not architectural expressivity—is the key differentiator in the trackable regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the optimal-transport framework for single-cell perturbation prediction established by Schiebinger et al. (2019) and operationalized by CellOT (Bunne et al., 2023), which learns parametric transport maps via input-convex neural networks. It departs from this lineage by replacing the neural network with a minimal linear map (LAOT) and by importing the restricted isometry property from compressed sensing to characterize when the latent permutation coupling is recoverable in polynomial time. The NP-hardness result in the untrackable regime is a reduction from Pananjady et al. (2016) on linear regression with unknown permutation, transplanted into the biological perturbation setting. Conceptually, the paper reframes the problem from a model-centric view (pick the most expressive architecture) to a design-first view (choose the measurement time gap to control computational tractability).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, interpretable criterion (the critical time gap Δ) that experimental biologists can use to decide when a simple linear model suffices versus when the problem becomes computationally intractable, potentially saving significant modeling effort and improving reproducibility. Key limitations include the difficulty of verifying the RIP assumption on real biological data, the restriction to the balanced (permutation) setting that excludes cell proliferation/death, and the clear performance degradation under cross-cell-line transfer where the smoothness assumption breaks. Open questions include extending the analysis to unbalanced transport, estimating Δ a priori from biological priors (pathway activation times, protein half-lives), and determining whether the trackable-regime conclusion holds for rapid nonlinear shock responses even at short time gaps.
