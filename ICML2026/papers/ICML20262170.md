## Problem

LLM reasoning systems face a fundamental tension: strong verification (human inspection, domain-specific execution) establishes trust but is too costly to apply at every step, while weak verification (self-consistency, proxy rewards) scales effortlessly but is noisy and insufficient alone. No principled framework existed for orchestrating when to trust the cheap signal versus when to defer to the expensive one, leaving service providers without a mechanism to match oracle-level reliability at a fraction of the verification cost.

## Input / Output

At each time step the algorithm receives a problem prompt P_t, a model-generated response R_t, and a continuous weak verification score w_t ∈ [0,1] (e.g., from a proxy reward model or LLM-as-judge). It outputs a three-way decision—Accept, Reject, or query the Strong Verifier—based on two adaptive thresholds τ_R^t ≤ τ_A^t that partition the score range into a reject region, an uncertain region, and an accept region. The strong verifier g: P×R→{0,1} is only invoked in the uncertain region or during randomized exploration, and its binary output determines the final accept/reject when queried. The policy operates on an arbitrary, potentially non-stationary stream with no distributional assumptions.

## Method

The paper first proves (under a calibration assumption) that population-optimal weak-strong verification policies admit a two-threshold structure: reject below τ_low, accept above τ_high, and defer to strong verification in between, with the achievable tradeoff governed by the weak verifier's calibration and sharpness. Building on this, the Selective Strong Verification (SSV) algorithm maintains two online thresholds updated via importance-weighted gradient steps whenever the strong signal is observed, with a max/min projection enforcing τ_R ≤ τ_A. To obtain unbiased feedback without assuming the stream is i.i.d., the algorithm queries the strong verifier with small exploration probabilities q_A^t and q_R^t even in the accept and reject regions, dividing the update by q_t to correct for the random sampling. Theorem 5.1 establishes distribution-free, uniform-in-time control of both type-I and type-II errors via a Freedman martingale concentration bound, with the finite-sample slack decomposing into an intrinsic quantile-tracking term (1/(ηN)) and partial-feedback terms scaling with 1/q_min. Experiments on MATH (outcome-level) and Sudoku (step-by-step) confirm that SSV maintains target error rates while reducing strong-verification calls by up to 46% relative to the oracle at comparable accuracy.

## Results

On the Sudoku step-by-step task, SSV achieves 43.1% accuracy at α=β=0.01 with only 2.87 strong-verifier calls per puzzle, compared to the Strong-Only Oracle's 44.2% at 5.32 calls — a 46% reduction in verification cost for a 1.1-point accuracy drop. On the hardest MATH subset (Level 5), SSV reaches 60% accuracy with 2 strong calls per problem versus the Oracle's 2.8 calls for 63.5%. Across all settings, running-average Type-I and Type-II errors converge to their nominal targets (e.g., α=β=0.15), confirming the distribution-free finite-sample guarantees of Theorem 5.1. SSV also uses fewer weak-verifier calls (4.8–5.2 per puzzle) than the Weak-Only baseline (6.00), indicating that adaptive thresholding avoids redundant reasoning steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares SSV against two boundary baselines: the Strong-Only Oracle (strong verifier called on every query) and the Weak-Only Greedy (selects the highest weak-score candidate, never calling the strong verifier). On Sudoku, SSV at α=β=0.01 matches the Oracle's 44.2% accuracy within 1.1 points (43.1%) while halving the strong-verifier load (2.87 vs. 5.32 calls per puzzle). At the other extreme, SSV at α=β=0.30 (34.5% accuracy, 0.68 strong calls) still outperforms the Weak-Only Greedy (33.6%, 0 strong calls) while using fewer weak calls (4.84 vs. 6.00). On MATH Level 5, SSV reaches 60% accuracy with 2 strong calls versus the Oracle's 63.5% at 2.8 calls, and the Weak-Only baseline sits well below both. The key finding is that SSV traces a smooth Pareto frontier between the two extremes, with the slope governed by the weak verifier's sharpness: on easier tasks (MATH Level 2, Sudoku) the curve is steep, enabling near-Oracle accuracy at a fraction of the cost, while on harder tasks (MATH Level 5) the tradeoff is more linear.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself orthogonally to the large body of work on improving LLM reasoning (structured prompting, search, decoding strategies) and on improving weak verifiers (LLM-as-judge, process reward models), instead studying the orchestration layer above: when to trust a cheap internal signal versus deferring to an expensive external check. Algorithmically, it is an instance of learning-to-defer (L2D) where deferral means invoking strong verification, building on the selective prediction literature (Bartlett & Wegkamp, 2008; Mozannar & Sontag, 2021) and the online quantile-tracking machinery from adaptive conformal inference (Gibbs & Candès, 2021). The key departure is the combination of distribution-free online calibration, partial feedback (strong labels observed only when queried), and separate Type-I/Type-II error control within a single two-threshold policy, a combination the authors state has not been previously formulated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives service providers a principled, tunable knob (the target error levels α, β) for navigating the accuracy-versus-verification-cost frontier in deployed LLM reasoning systems, with distribution-free guarantees that require no assumptions on query distribution or verifier quality. The identification of weak-verifier sharpness (beyond calibration) as the key determinant of achievable cost savings provides actionable guidance for designing and selecting proxy verifiers. A notable limitation is that the policy conditions only on the scalar weak score rather than the full prompt-response context, restricting guarantees to marginal error control; extending to context-dependent thresholds with conditional error guarantees under partial feedback is left as future work. The 46% reduction in strong-verification calls at near-oracle accuracy on Sudoku, and the consistent error-rate convergence across non-stationary streams, suggest the approach is deployable but the evaluation is limited to two tasks and a small set of LLM backends.
