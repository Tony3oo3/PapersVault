## Problem

Standard in-context learning assumes that once a task is induced from a support set, its decision criterion remains fixed, yet real-world tasks often maintain stable high-level intent while their decision criteria shift with context (e.g., industrial tolerance levels, medical severity thresholds). No existing evaluation setting or benchmark supports assessing whether vision-language models can dynamically recalibrate their decision boundaries to context-conveyed criteria while keeping task semantics constant, leaving criterion adaptation entirely unmeasured.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance presents a query image (or 16-frame video sequence) alongside two paired support sets that share identical images but differ only in the binary label assigned to one criterion-sensitive exemplar within a triplet {always-abnormal, always-normal, criterion-sensitive}. The model must produce a yes/no answer for the same query under each support set, with correct behavior requiring divergent predictions on criterion-sensitive samples and consistent predictions on criterion-invariant ones. This dual-context pairing means performance is scored not as a single accuracy but as a joint pair of predictions evaluated against both sensitivity and invariance constraints simultaneously.

## Method

The paper formalizes CC-ICL by extending the standard ICL generative model to tasks with multiple valid criteria (|M| > 1), requiring the model to infer the active criterion from the support set via p_θ(M|T,S) before conditioning its prediction on that criterion. CC-Bench implements a dual-level data hierarchy (Category → Sub-category) across Industrial Inspection, Medical Diagnosis, and Video Surveillance, with a severity-graded labeling scheme defining two criteria: Criterion A (zero-tolerance, any deviation = abnormal) and Criterion B (functional integrity, only severe threats = abnormal). The key training contribution is Multi-Criterion Training (MCT), a LoRA-based fine-tuning strategy where each shared support triplet generates two training pairs with identical images but flipped labels on the criterion-sensitive sample, explicitly supervising the model to produce criterion-conditioned predictions for the same query. The paper introduces two complementary metrics—Criterion Sensitivity (CS) and Criterion Invariance (CI)—to disentangle adaptability from robustness, and reveals a prevalent rigid boundary bias where base models achieve moderate-to-high CI but consistently marginal CS (<20%), which MCT substantially mitigates in 7B-scale models without degrading general multimodal performance.

## Results

On CC-Bench, all base VLMs exhibit a severe rigid boundary bias: Criterion Sensitivity (CS) is consistently below 30% while Criterion Invariance (CI) reaches 30–90%, indicating models fail to adapt decisions to shifted criteria. After Multi-Criterion Training (MCT), Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct achieves CS of 68.32% (Industrial), 83.11% (Medical), and 45.71% (Video), with overall accuracy of 71.70%, 66.39%, and 63.81% respectively—surpassing GPT-4o (58.00%, 22.13%, 39.76%) and Gemini 2.5 Pro (57.00%, 32.41%, 47.62%) on overall accuracy. Average CS improvement across all four MCT-trained models is +26.91% to +66.13% depending on domain, while general multimodal performance (MMBench, MMStar) remains stable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The MCT-trained Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct substantially outperforms both proprietary baselines on overall accuracy: it exceeds GPT-4o by +13.70 pp (Industrial), +44.26 pp (Medical), and +24.05 pp (Video), and surpasses Gemini 2.5 Pro by +14.70 pp, +33.98 pp, and +16.19 pp respectively. Against the larger open-source Qwen2.5-VL-32B-Instruct, the MCT-trained 7B model leads by +32.10 pp (Industrial), +41.58 pp (Medical), and +42.62 pp (Video) in overall accuracy, demonstrating that criterion-aware training is more effective than simply scaling parameters. On Criterion Sensitivity specifically, the MCT-trained 7B model achieves 83.11% on Medical Diagnosis, far exceeding GPT-4o's 9.76% and Gemini 2.5 Pro's 3.38%, confirming that criterion adaptation is not an emergent property of scale or proprietary training. General multimodal benchmarks (MMBench: 87.73 vs. 87.57; MMStar: 60.20 vs. 59.73) confirm that MCT does not degrade pre-trained capabilities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant ICL framing that treats in-context learning purely as task induction (inferring a single, fixed decision rule from a support set). It repurposes the standard ICL formalism by identifying an underexplored axis—criterion adaptation under fixed task semantics—and formalizes it as CC-ICL, where the model must infer which of multiple valid decision criteria is active from context. On the evaluation side, it positions itself against both general multimodal benchmarks (where few-shot examples serve only format alignment) and ICL-specific benchmarks like VL-ICL and TrueMICL (which test task induction but hold criteria fixed), arguing that neither category can probe criterion-level adaptation. The MCT training strategy is a straightforward extension of standard LoRA fine-tuning with paired multi-criterion supervision rather than a novel architectural contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic diagnostic of criterion adaptation in VLMs, revealing that even GPT-4o and Gemini 2.5 Pro exhibit a severe rigid boundary bias (CS < 30% across domains), which has practical implications for any deployment where judgment thresholds are context-dependent (industrial QC, medical triage, surveillance). The CC-Bench and its CI/CS metrics offer the community a reusable evaluation tool, and the finding that a lightweight LoRA-based MCT (rank 4, 2 epochs, ~1.8K samples) closes most of the gap in 7B models without degrading general multimodal performance is practically actionable. Open questions include whether the binary two-criterion setup captures the continuous, multi-dimensional criterion shifts in real-world applications, whether the modality gap (poor static-to-video transfer) reflects a fundamental limitation or a data-coverage issue, and whether criterion adaptation generalizes beyond binary yes/no classification to open-ended generation tasks.
