## Problem

Most self-supervised learning methods enforce semantic consistency through continuous alignment (cosine similarity, cross-entropy over prototypes), which offers limited explicit control over how information is structured across representation dimensions and can yield entangled features. While discrete representations have been explored in reconstruction-based SSL (e.g., masked image modeling with fixed codebooks), it remained unclear how to define informative discrete targets for alignment-based SSL without ground-truth labels, or how to ensure each dimension carries meaningful, distinct information.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image and produces two stochastically augmented views (crops, color jitter, blur) that are processed by a ViT-B/16 backbone. The backbone outputs a continuous d-dimensional embedding, which is then passed through a three-layer MLP projection head to yield B=256 sigmoid probabilities parameterizing a 256-bit binary message. At inference time, only the continuous backbone embedding is used; the binary codes serve purely as a self-supervised pretext signal during training and are discarded.

## Method

BITS frames SSL as a discrete communication problem: a teacher network produces hard binary targets by thresholding its sigmoid outputs at 0.5, and a student network predicts these targets via element-wise binary cross-entropy, enforcing per-bit agreement rather than global vector similarity. A coding-rate regularization (negative log-determinant of the covariance of L2-normalized pre-binarization logits) is applied to encourage balanced, high-entropy utilization of the 256-bit channel, preventing redundancy across bits. The teacher is updated via EMA of the student, and the projection head is periodically reinitialized every 10 epochs to prevent the backbone from overfitting to a single coding scheme. The key distinction from prior work is that only the agreement mechanism is discretized—the backbone still learns continuous representations, and the fixed B-bit capacity acts as an explicit architectural constraint on information flow rather than a property of the learned embedding.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K, BITS-reset (binary agreement with periodic head reinitialization) achieves 50.64 mAP on FFPQ retrieval, 73.5% k-NN accuracy, and 77.8% linear probing accuracy, substantially outperforming both DINO (35.68 mAP, 72.39% k-NN, 76.3% LP) and SimDINO (38.62 mAP, 69.52% k-NN, 75.3% LP). The largest relative gains appear in retrieval, where BITS-reset improves mAP by 12.0 points over DINO and 12.0 points over SimDINO. Under severe domain shift (e.g., iNat2019), BITS-fixed achieves 53.2% linear probing accuracy versus 36.6% for DINO and 36.2% for SimDINO, and continued self-supervised fine-tuning further lifts BITS-fixed to 54.4%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DINO (softmax prototype agreement), BITS-reset wins on all three ImageNet-1K metrics: +15.0 mAP in retrieval (50.64 vs 35.68), +1.1 k-NN (73.5 vs 72.39), and +1.5 linear probing (77.8 vs 76.3). Against SimDINO (cosine agreement with coding-rate regularization), the gains are even larger: +12.0 mAP (50.64 vs 38.62), +4.0 k-NN (73.5 vs 69.52), and +2.5 linear probing (77.8 vs 75.3), indicating that discrete multi-label agreement is more effective than continuous cosine alignment even when both use coding-rate regularization. Against DINOv2 (reported in Appendix E), BITS-reset surpasses it in retrieval (50.64 vs 38.33 mAP) and linear probing (77.8 vs 76.8) but trails slightly in k-NN (73.5 vs 75.93), though DINOv2 uses a patch-level objective and longer training schedule. Under severe domain shift on iNat2019, BITS-fixed achieves 53.2% frozen linear probing versus 36.6% (DINO) and 36.2% (SimDINO), a gap of roughly 17 points; after 10-epoch self-supervised fine-tuning on the target domain, BITS-fixed reaches 54.4% while DINO degrades to 24.8%. BITS-reset underperforms BITS-fixed in the frozen fine-grained setting (e.g., 29.74% vs 53.2% on iNat2019), revealing a robustness-factorization trade-off, but recovers to competitive levels (49.01%) after a single head reinitialization during adaptation.

## Relation to Prior Work

BITS builds directly on the DINO/SimDINO teacher-student self-distillation framework, replacing the continuous agreement objective (softmax over prototypes or cosine similarity) with a multi-label binary cross-entropy loss over a fixed-capacity discrete channel, while retaining coding-rate regularization as an anti-collapse mechanism. Conceptually, it departs from prior discrete-representation SSL (BEiT, masked image modeling with codebooks) by applying discretization to the agreement mechanism rather than the reconstruction target, and from deep hashing by using binary codes as self-supervised pretext targets rather than final retrieval representations. The periodic head reinitialization is a novel component enabled by the bounded per-dimension gradients of BCE, which make the objective robust to abrupt teacher changes in a way that cosine-based objectives are not.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that discretizing the supervision signal (not the learned representation) is sufficient to induce more factorized, multi-label embeddings with substantially better retrieval performance, and that the resulting binary codes capture reusable cross-class semantic attributes (e.g., scene context, object isolation), offering an interpretable discrete language alongside continuous features. The domain-shift trade-off—where BITS-fixed excels in frozen transfer while BITS-reset benefits more from target-domain fine-tuning—suggests that factorized representations adapt more gracefully under distribution shift, a property with practical implications for domain adaptation pipelines. Key limitations include evaluation at ViT-B/16 scale only, a single pre-training dataset (ImageNet-1K), and the absence of comparison with DINOv2/v3 under a fully controlled protocol; open questions include whether the gains persist at larger scales, whether richer discrete alphabets (beyond binary) further improve structure, and how the approach extends to multimodal or temporal settings.
