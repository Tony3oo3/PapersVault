## Problem

Standard IRL methods recover only a deterministic point estimate of the reward function, discarding higher-order structure (variance, skewness, tail behavior) that is present in real-world reward signals such as stochastic contact forces or trial-to-trial dopamine fluctuations. Bayesian IRL captures parameter uncertainty but still drives policy inference through expected-return quantities, leaving the full reward-induced return distribution unexploited. The paper asks how to learn the complete reward distribution from offline expert demonstrations and derive a policy that is sensitive to the resulting return-distribution shape, not just its mean.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a fixed set of expert state-action trajectories (no online interaction), a user-specified prior over reward functions (e.g., standard normal or skew-normal), and a distortion function ξ defining the risk measure (default: CVaR at α=0.05). It outputs a parameterized conditional reward distribution q_φ(r|s,a) — instantiated as a skew-normal with learned location, scale, and skew per state-action pair — together with a risk-aware policy π_φ(a|s) and a quantile-regression critic that represents the full return distribution as N quantiles. The FSD-violation integral is estimated via Monte Carlo return sampling and empirical order statistics, so the reward network only needs to be differentiable in its parameters.

## Method

DistIRL replaces the MaxEntIRL mean-matching objective with a First-Order Stochastic Dominance (FSD) violation integral over the full return CDFs, which is equivalently expressed as an integral over quantile-function differences. For reward learning, this FSD violation is treated as an energy in an Energy-Based Model, and a variational family q_φ(r|s,a) is fit to the posterior via an ELBO that balances expected FSD violation against a KL prior; gradients are obtained through inverse-transform sampling of return trajectories. For policy learning, the intractable FSD-violation indicator is replaced by a Distortion Risk Measure M_ξ (e.g., CVaR), yielding a tractable objective max_φ ∫F_{Z^π}^{-1}(v) dξ̃(v) + H(π), solved by minimizing KL to a Boltzmann distribution of the DRM-optimal Q-values. The distributional critic is trained with Quantile Regression (Quantile Huber loss) in an off-policy evaluation loop, and the three components (reward, critic, policy) are updated alternately. The paper proves O(ε^{-2}) iteration complexity for reaching an ε-stationary point under bounded-reward, Lipschitz-reward-parameterization, and standard DRM regularity assumptions.

## Results

On risk-sensitive D4RL benchmarks (stochastic rewards), DistIRL achieves 3469±59 on HalfCheetah, 886±1 on Hopper, and 1526±148 on Walker2D, approaching expert performance (3540, 892, 1478) and substantially outperforming all offline IRL baselines. On the mouse spontaneous behavior dataset, S-DistIRL (skew-normal reward) achieves the highest Pearson correlation with recorded dopamine levels and the lowest Wasserstein-1 distance to the true dopamine distribution among all methods tested. On deterministic D4RL (medium-expert), DistIRL remains competitive, reaching 7779 on HalfCheetah and 3411 on Hopper, though Offline ML-IRL leads on HalfCheetah (11231) due to access to additional non-expert data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DistIRL decisively outperforms Offline ML-IRL across all three risk-sensitive D4RL tasks (e.g., 3469 vs. 826 on HalfCheetah, 886 vs. 192 on Hopper), which the authors attribute to Offline ML-IRL's transition model being pretrained under risk-neutral rewards and thus mismatched to the risk-averse expert data. ValueDICE, a model-free MaxEntIRL baseline optimizing expected risk-neutral returns, is also clearly beaten (1259 vs. 3469 on HalfCheetah; 260 vs. 886 on Hopper), confirming that expectation-level matching is insufficient when experts follow risk-averse behavior. Behavior Cloning is the strongest baseline, reaching 2828 on HalfCheetah and 1321 on Walker2D, but DistIRL still leads by 641 and 205 points respectively, and by a wide margin on Hopper (886 vs. 346). In the Gridworld and mouse experiments, BIRL (Chan & van der Schaar, 2021) recovers the reward mean reasonably but fails to capture reward variance or the full distributional shape of dopamine signals, whereas S-DistIRL achieves the highest mean-dopamine correlation and lowest W-1 distance, demonstrating that FSD-based distributional learning is necessary to recover higher-order reward structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

DistIRL extends the Maximum Entropy IRL framework (Ziebart et al., 2008) by replacing the expected-return matching objective with a First-Order Stochastic Dominance (FSD) violation criterion, and by treating the reward as a conditional distribution rather than a deterministic function. It draws on Distributional RL (C51, QR-DQN) for return-distribution estimation and on Distortion Risk Measures (CVaR, Wang-type) for risk-aware policy optimization, but repurposes these tools in an inverse (demonstration-driven) setting rather than a forward RL loop. The variational inference and energy-based model formulation connects to Bayesian IRL (Ramachandran & Amir, 2007; Chan & van der Schaar, 2021), but departs from BIRL by learning the full reward distribution shape (variance, skewness) rather than a posterior over parameters of a deterministic reward.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a genuine gap: mean-matching IRL is blind to higher-order reward structure (variance, skew, tail behavior) that directly affects policy robustness and safety. The neuroscience validation—recovering dopamine fluctuation distributions from behavior alone using a skew-normal reward model—is a compelling demonstration that internal motivational signal structure can be decoded without direct neural measurement, though the mean correlation (~0.3) remains modest. Open questions include sensitivity to the chosen DRM, modeling correlations across state-action reward distributions, and whether the FSD objective can be relaxed to targeted finite-moment matching when only specific moments are scientifically relevant. The method does not claim unique reward recovery and inherits the identifiability limitations of all IRL approaches.
