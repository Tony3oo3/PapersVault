## Problem

Standard MLLMs struggle to perform fine-grained dense prediction tasks (semantic segmentation, depth estimation, referring segmentation) without incorporating task-specific decoders, mask embedding tokens, or iterative inference passes. Existing solutions fragment the unified MLLM architecture—adding SAM-based decoders, retrieval mechanisms, or requiring hundreds of separate forward passes—complicating deployment and deviating from the generalist design principle. The paper asks whether a purely standard MLLM architecture can natively produce pixel-level dense predictions with no architectural additions.

## Input / Output

The model takes an RGB image (processed through SigLIP-2 with 2D RoPE and a 2×2 spatial-merge projector) plus a text instruction prompt that specifies the task and, for segmentation, the target category names in a shuffled order. Dense predictions are extracted from the LLM's vision-token hidden states: the model first autoregressively predicts category names as text, then uses those tokens' vocabulary IDs to index into the corresponding vision-token logits, averages multi-token category scores, reshapes to a spatial grid, bilinearly upsamples to pixel resolution, and applies argmax. The final mask is output as a Run-Length Encoding (RLE) string. For depth, the continuous range is discretized into 1000 bins (linear or log-uniform quantization) and the same argmax-over-bins procedure applies. For referring segmentation, a grounding-then-segmentation pipeline crops the target region with 1.2× padding and resizes the short edge to 1280 px before foreground/background classification.

## Method

DenseMLLM uses a standard 4B-parameter MLLM (SigLIP-2 encoder + 2×2-merge MLP projector + transformer LLM) with no external decoders. Its key training innovation is NTP-M (Next-Token Prediction for Multi-label), which extends the autoregressive objective to vision tokens by modeling each vocabulary position as an independent Bernoulli trial (sigmoid) rather than a single-label softmax, since a single vision token inherently encodes multiple semantic labels and tasks simultaneously. To handle the extreme class imbalance from the large vocabulary, NTP-M introduces relevant negative sampling: negatives are ranked by predicted probability and only the top-k most relevant negatives contribute to the loss, decoupled from the positive-sample average—distinct from OHEM which operates along the spatial dimension. Training follows a four-stage progressive recipe: (I) ~11.8T-token multimodal pre-training, (II) ~0.6T-token annealing focused on dense-prediction data, (III) 32K-context supervised fine-tuning, and (IV) DAPO-based reinforcement learning with a class-label IoU reward for segmentation. At inference, no extra tokens, retrieval steps, or repeated forward passes are needed, yielding a total runtime of ~1 s at 500×500 resolution.

## Results

DenseMLLM (4B, no task-specific additions) achieves 54.2 mIoU on ADE20K for semantic segmentation, 87.6 δ₁ on DDAD for depth estimation, and 80.7 cIoU on RefCOCO-val for referring expression segmentation, all without external decoders or extra inference passes. On general VL benchmarks, it matches or exceeds Qwen3-VL-4B on most of 15 benchmarks (e.g., 71.1 MMStar vs. 69.8, 2384 MME vs. 2309, 56.5 M-Verse vs. 46.8), confirming that dense prediction capability does not compromise general vision-language proficiency. The model also achieves 92.5 mIoU on VOC20 and 90.4 δ₁ on NYUv2, approaching specialist-model performance (e.g., 92.7 vs. 92.1 δ₁ on Cityscapes depth vs. SwinMTL).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On semantic segmentation, DenseMLLM (54.2 mIoU on ADE20K) outperforms all MLLM-based baselines without additions (VisionLLM-v2: 52.3, GiT: 47.8) and CLIP-based methods (SAN: 32.1, MaskCLIP: 12.3), but trails specialist models like Mask2Former (56.0) and BEIT-3 (62.8) which use task-specific decoders. For depth estimation, DenseMLLM (87.6 δ₁ on DDAD) substantially surpasses DepthLM (74.7) which requires ~1000 separate inference passes, and approaches the specialist UniDepth-v2 (88.2) within 0.6 points; on NYUv2 it reaches 90.4 vs. UniDepth-v2's 98.8. On referring expression segmentation, DenseMLLM (80.7 cIoU on RefCOCO-val) beats UFO (80.0, which requires mask retrieval), GLaMM (79.5, SAM decoder), UniPixel (80.5, SAM decoder), and VistaLLM (74.5, polygon-based) while using no extra architectural components. On general VL benchmarks (Table 2), DenseMLLM matches Qwen3-VL-4B on MMB (83.9) and slightly exceeds it on MMStar (71.1 vs. 69.8), MME (2384 vs. 2309), M-Verse (56.5 vs. 46.8), and A12D (85.6 vs. 84.1), while trailing on OCRBench (813 vs. 881) and POPE (86.4 vs. 89.3), confirming that dense prediction integration does not significantly degrade general capabilities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of equipping MLLMs with external task-specific decoders (GLaMM's SAM decoder, UFO's mask retrieval tokens, VisionLLM-v2's Deform-DETR) or iterative inference (DepthLM's per-point prediction) by demonstrating that a completely standard ViT-projector-LLM architecture can perform dense prediction natively. The key conceptual departure is in the training objective: rather than extending the architecture, the authors extend the Next-Token Prediction loss to vision tokens via a multi-label formulation (NTP-M) with relevant negative sampling, repurposing the existing unified vocabulary as a dense prediction interface. This positions the work as orthogonal to prior MLLM dense prediction efforts—same architecture, different supervision—making it a general technique applicable to any standard MLLM backbone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: dense prediction becomes a free capability of any standard MLLM without architectural fragmentation, extra inference passes, or specialized codebases, simplifying deployment in robotics and autonomous systems. However, the current framework is limited to semantic-level tasks; extending to instance segmentation, panoptic segmentation, optical flow, or surface normals requires new mechanisms for instance discrimination or geometric reasoning that the paper does not address. The long-tail open-world performance remains constrained by training data diversity, and the patch-size-induced downsampling introduces aliasing artifacts in high-overlap regions, leaving room for improvement in fine-grained scenarios.
