## Problem

Existing AI safety frameworks focus on model-level harms (toxicity, bias, discrimination) or existential risks (loss of control, catastrophic misuse), but neither captures how the widespread deployment of general-purpose AI degrades the institutional conditions on which democratic governance depends. The paper identifies this gap as a distinct class of 'sociopolitical risks' that emerge from aggregate deployment effects rather than individual model outputs, and that persist even when models are well-aligned.

## Input / Output

The analytical framework takes institutional processes—concretely, the input (citizen demands via voting, public comment, litigation), processing (legislative deliberation, judicial reasoning, regulatory analysis), and feedback (rulings, policy announcements, enforcement) channels of governance—as its unit of analysis. It produces a taxonomy of six failure modes (P1–P6) located at specific points in this governance loop, each classified by its relationship to alignment (alignment-independent, alignment-caused, or alignment-relevant), along with four concrete recommendations spanning evaluation methodology, institutional design, and procurement policy.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a mechanism-level analytical framework that conceptualizes democratic institutions as information-processing systems operating across three stages—input, processing, and feedback—and locates six specific failure modes within this loop: belief homogenization, belief reinforcement, congested bureaucracy, epistemic flood, unauditable authority, and normative centralization. Each failure mode is characterized by its affected institutional context, the mechanism through which AI alters institutional dynamics, and the conditions under which it emerges. The framework distinguishes three categories of alignment relationship: risks that persist regardless of alignment quality (driven by cost and scale), risks that are direct consequences of current alignment methods (e.g., RLHF narrowing output diversity, sycophantic optimization), and risks partially addressable through interpretability progress. Recommendations include Institutional Safety Levels (ISLs) as capability-triggered procedural safeguards, multi-agent simulation for system-level evaluation, governance-grade decision logging with provenance tracking, and pluralistic alignment reframed as a procurement diversification requirement.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments or results tables. Its core analytical contribution is a taxonomy of six sociopolitical failure modes (P1–P6) organized along the governance feedback loop (input, processing, feedback), each classified by its relationship to alignment: alignment-independent (P3 Congested Bureaucracy, P4 Epistemic Flood), alignment-caused (P1 Belief Homogenization, P2 Belief Reinforcement, P6 Normative Centralization), or alignment-relevant (P5 Unauditable Authority). The paper proposes four recommendations (R1–R4), including Institutional Safety Levels (ISLs) as capability-triggered procedural safeguards for public-sector AI deployment, multi-agent simulation benchmarks for sociopolitical effects, governance-grade decision logging, and pluralistic alignment as a procurement diversification requirement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare methods against quantitative baselines. Instead, it positions the sociopolitical risk framework analytically against three existing categories: (1) individual-level harms (toxicity, bias, discrimination), which it distinguishes by noting that model-level safeguards reduce per-instance harm but do not address aggregate institutional effects at scale; (2) existential risks (loss of control, catastrophic misuse, gradual disempowerment per Kulveit et al. 2025), which it connects to by identifying specific institutional mechanisms within the gradual-disempowerment arc and arguing that weakened governance institutions reduce society's capacity to coordinate against catastrophic risks; and (3) systemic/sociotechnical risk taxonomies (Critch & Russell 2023, Acemoglu 2024), which it complements by providing a mechanism-level account locating each failure in a specific stage of the governance loop and assessing what alignment can and cannot address. The paper's key differentiator is the explicit alignment taxonomy (independent, caused, relevant) applied to each failure mode, which prior frameworks do not provide.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes three largely separate literatures—model-level AI alignment (RLHF, constitutional AI), existential/gradual-disempowerment risk frameworks, and political-science governance theory (Easton's input–processing–feedback loop, Weberian rational-legal authority)—into a unified 'sociopolitical AI risk' category. It departs from prior AI-safety work by arguing that the unit of analysis must shift from individual model outputs to aggregate institutional effects, and from platform-governance research by showing that general-purpose AI introduces qualitatively new dynamics (a single model mediating across previously separate domains) rather than merely scaling existing algorithmic monoculture. The paper explicitly positions itself as complementary to, not a replacement for, model-level alignment, introducing a three-way taxonomy (alignment-independent, alignment-caused, alignment-relevant) that prior frameworks do not provide.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central practical contribution is the proposal of Institutional Safety Levels (ISLs) as capability-triggered procedural safeguards for public-sector AI deployment, analogous to existing frontier-model safety frameworks but applied at the institutional level. Key open questions include how to empirically validate sociopolitical effects (the authors acknowledge LLM-based multi-agent simulation inherits model biases and lacks reliable agent-to-agent theory of mind), how to enforce ISL thresholds when capability measurement remains unreliable, and how to balance personhood verification against privacy in public-participation channels. The mutual-dependency argument—that alignment without functioning oversight institutions optimizes for the wrong objectives, and institutional resilience without alignment lacks technical governance tools—reframes the AI-safety agenda in a way that could influence procurement policy and evaluation methodology beyond the research community.
