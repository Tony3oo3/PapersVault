## Problem

Existing detection-based defenses for LLMs achieve strong empirical accuracy but lack explicit, provable control over the false acceptance rate (FAR), a critical safety requirement in high-stakes deployments. This gap is compounded by two practical constraints: defenders rarely have access to labeled malicious calibration data, and real-world inputs arrive sequentially in a streaming fashion, ruling out offline density estimation methods.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261679/cropped_page7_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

OSCS consumes a benign-only calibration dataset, a sequential stream of test inputs, and real-valued detection scores produced by a pluggable scoring function (e.g., Mahalanobis distance or BadActs activation scores). At each time step it outputs a binary accept/reject decision and an estimated posterior benign probability, with the constraint that the cumulative FAR over all accepted inputs must not exceed a user-specified target α. The method requires only O(B) memory per step (where B is the number of grid points), making it suitable for streaming deployment with limited resources.

## Method

OSCS estimates the posterior benign probability L_t = π_c·f_c(w_t)/f_mix(w_t) via three online components: (1) standard KDE on the benign calibration set for f_c, (2) recursive kernel density estimation (RKDE) on a fixed grid to track the mixture density f_mix as scores arrive, and (3) a Storey-style threshold estimator for the unknown benign proportion π_c. Accept/reject decisions are made by maintaining running sums U_t (cumulative (1−L̂_t) over accepted samples) and R_t (count of accepted samples), rejecting whenever U_t/R_t would exceed α. The key theoretical result (Theorem 5.10) proves that FAR ≤ α + Δ, where the excess term Δ vanishes as calibration size, time horizon, grid resolution, and score discriminative power all grow, under mild smoothness and separation assumptions.

## Results

On backdoor detection (BERT-base, 20% poisoning), OSCS-md achieves FAR of 0.013–0.056 and OSCS-badacts achieves FAR of 0.009–0.062 across all 12 dataset-attack combinations, consistently meeting or nearly meeting the target α=0.05. On jailbreak detection (AdvBench, 20% jailbreak ratio), OSCS-ppl achieves FAR of 0.011–0.016 with Power 0.708–0.717, while OSCS-md achieves FAR of 0.044–0.051 with Power up to 0.990 across Mistral-7B, Qwen2-7B, and Llama-3.1-8B. In contrast, baselines such as STRIP show uncontrolled FAR of ~0.19–0.20 across nearly all settings, and SCM achieves low FAR only sporadically (e.g., 0.000 on Qwen2-7B but 0.168 on Llama-3.1-8B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

OSCS decisively outperforms all five baselines on the primary metric of FAR control (target α=0.05). STRIP shows no FAR control whatsoever, with FAR locked at ~0.19–0.20 across every dataset, attack, and model—indicating it simply applies a fixed threshold without any safety guarantee. BKI and RAP also fail to control FAR, with values ranging from 0.08 to 0.44 (BKI) and 0.13 to 0.34 (RAP). CUBE occasionally achieves low FAR (e.g., 0.001 on Yelp/BadNets with RoBERTa) but at the cost of very low Power (0.515–0.749), effectively rejecting most inputs. SCM, which is designed to control false rejection rate rather than FAR, shows highly inconsistent behavior: it achieves FAR=0.000 on Qwen2-7B jailbreak detection but FAR=0.168 on Llama-3.1-8B, confirming that its low FAR in some settings is coincidental. OSCS is the only method that consistently maintains FAR at or below 0.05 across all 12 backdoor scenarios (Tables 1–2) and all 6 jailbreak scenarios (Table 3), while retaining competitive Power (0.45–0.99 depending on scoring function and setting).

## Relation to Prior Work

OSCS sits at the intersection of detection-based LLM defenses (STRIP, CUBE, SCM, BadActs) and the statistical literature on false discovery rate control (Benjamini-Hochberg, Storey's π₀ estimator). It departs from prior FDR/lFDR methods by eliminating the need for malicious calibration samples, and from the closely related SAFER framework (Hu et al., 2026) by operating in a streaming, online setting rather than requiring offline access to the full test set. The core technical contribution is combining recursive kernel density estimation (RKDE) for the mixture distribution with Storey's proportion estimator to produce online posterior benignity estimates that drive a sequential accept/reject rule with provable FAR guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a genuine deployment gap: safety-critical LLM applications need provable bounds on how many malicious inputs slip through, yet prior defenses offer no such guarantee in realistic streaming settings without access to labeled attacks. The vanishing excess term in the FAR bound provides a principled path to tightening guarantees as data accumulates. Key open questions include robustness to adaptive adversaries who observe the OSCS mechanism, handling of non-stationary distribution shifts beyond the sliding-window extension, and the significant power cost (sometimes dropping to ~0.4–0.5) that explicit FAR control imposes relative to uncontrolled baselines.
