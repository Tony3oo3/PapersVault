## Problem

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) have become a standard mechanistic interpretability tool for autoregressive LLMs, but diffusion language models (DLMs) — an increasingly competitive alternative — lack any tailored interpretability interface. The fundamental difference in generation mechanism (iterative denoising with bidirectional attention over partially masked sequences) means the LLM-SAE recipe cannot be transferred plug-and-play, requiring new design choices for training data collection and inference-time intervention.

## Input / Output

The SAE is trained on residual-stream activations collected from DLM forward passes on partially masked sequences at random corruption levels t ∈ (0,1), with a DLM-specific position-selection choice: MASK-SAE uses only masked (to-be-predicted) positions while UNMASK-SAE uses only unmasked context positions. For diffusion-time steering, the method takes a chosen feature's decoder direction v_f and a steering strength α, injecting them into the residual stream at every denoising step via a position selector (all positions or only currently-masked positions). Outputs include sparse interpretable features with auto-generated explanations and interpretability scores, steered text continuations, pre-mask stability and post-decode drift metrics quantifying representation dynamics across decoding orders, and transfer scores (ΔL_DLM, explained variance) when base-trained SAEs are applied to instruction-tuned DLMs.

## Method

DLM-Scope trains Top-K SAEs (Gao et al., 2024) spliced into the residual stream at six layers (two shallow, two middle, two deep) of Dream-7B and LLaDA-8B, using the standard reconstruction + L1 sparsity objective but on activations sampled from denoising states under random corruption. The key architectural difference from LLM-SAEs is the explicit position-set selection S(x_t) ∈ {M(x_t), U(x_t)} that determines which token positions contribute training examples, isolating how diffusion corruption affects the learned dictionary. For inference-time steering, the feature direction is injected repeatedly across all K denoising steps (Eq. 7) rather than once in a single left-to-right pass, with two DLM-specific position selectors: ALL-TOKENS and UPDATE-TOKENS. A notable empirical finding is that in early layers, SAE insertion can reduce masked-token cross-entropy loss (negative ΔL_DLM), a phenomenon absent or markedly weaker in autoregressive LLMs where insertion always increases loss. The framework further enables decoding-order analysis by tracking Top-K feature-set Jaccard similarity (pre-mask stability) and post-decode drift across remasking strategies, and demonstrates that base-trained SAEs transfer nearly losslessly to instruction-tuned DLMs except at the deepest layer.

## Results

DLM-SAEs enable diffusion-time steering that outperforms single-pass LLM steering by 2–10× in deep layers. At the deepest layer (L27), Dream-Mask achieves a combined steering score S = 0.29 and Dream-Unmask S = 0.38, versus Qwen-2.5-7B's S = −0.02; LLaDA-Mask reaches S = 0.15 versus LLaMA-3-8B's S = 0.10. On GSM8K, confidence-based decoding orders substantially outperform random order (TOPK-MARGIN: 56%, ENTROPY: 59% vs. ORIGIN: 8%), and SAE feature dynamics (pre-mask stability, post-decode drift) correlate with this performance gap. Notably, SAE insertion in early DLM layers can reduce masked-token cross-entropy loss (negative Δ LM loss), a phenomenon absent or markedly weaker in autoregressive LLMs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DLM-SAEs against two autoregressive LLM baselines: Qwen-2.5-7B and LLaMA-3-8B, each with SAEs trained under the same Top-K architecture and sparsity budget. Against Qwen-2.5-7B, Dream SAEs achieve substantially higher steering scores across all layers, with the gap widening dramatically in deep layers (L27: Dream-Unmask S = 0.38 vs. Qwen S = −0.02; Dream-Mask S = 0.29 vs. Qwen S = −0.02). Against LLaMA-3-8B, LLaDA SAEs show a more pronounced advantage in shallow and middle layers where LLaMA's perplexity penalty is severe (e.g., L1: P = −7.89 for LLaMA vs. P = 0.00 for LLaDA-Mask), yielding steering scores of 0.13–0.15 for LLaDA versus −2.29 to −0.22 for LLaMA. In the deepest layer (L27), LLaDA-Mask (S = 0.15) still edges out LLaMA (S = 0.10), though the margin is smaller. Overall, DLM-SAEs consistently match or exceed LLM-SAE steering effectiveness, with the largest gains (2–10×) concentrated in deep layers where steerable semantic directions are most concentrated.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SAE-based mechanistic interpretability pipeline established for autoregressive LLMs (Anthropic's monosemanticity work, Top-K SAEs by Gao et al.) to diffusion language models, identifying two DLM-specific design axes absent in the LLM setting: which token positions supply training activations under diffusion corruption (MASK-SAE vs. UNMASK-SAE) and how steering is applied across iterative denoising steps (ALL-TOKENS vs. UPDATE-TOKENS). It also draws on the recent application of SAEs to text-to-image diffusion models (Surkov et al., 2025), where SAEs expose causal concept factors, but adapts the framework to the discrete-token, bidirectional-attention setting of DLMs. The paper positions itself as the first SAE interpretability interface for DLMs, filling a gap between the mature LLM-SAE literature and the rapidly growing but interpretability-poor DLM landscape.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By establishing that Top-K SAEs trained on DLM denoising activations yield faithful, interpretable features, this work provides the foundational tooling needed to audit, steer, and debug DLMs as they scale toward production use. The finding that SAE insertion can reduce cross-entropy loss in early DLM layers (a phenomenon absent in LLMs) opens a mechanistic question about how DLMs' bidirectional, iterative denoising differs from autoregressive processing in shallow representations. Key open questions include whether the negative ΔLM-loss regime has a clean mechanistic explanation, how these findings generalize beyond the two 7–8B DLMs tested, and whether SAE-based decoding-order signals can be turned into adaptive remasking policies that improve task accuracy.
