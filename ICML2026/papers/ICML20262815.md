## Problem

Benchmark scores for AI systems are difficult to interpret as measures of real-world task difficulty, and existing human-anchored frameworks like METR require expensive, benchmark-specific human completion time annotations that do not scale as new benchmarks proliferate. The paper asks whether human task completion time can be predicted from model performance data alone, without conducting new human studies for each benchmark.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262815/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sparse binary model-task success/failure matrix (~51% observed entries) spanning multiple benchmarks (METR, SWE-bench Verified, MLE-bench, GDPval, Cybench) along with human completion time annotations from METR's 170 tasks used solely for calibration. It produces per-task latent difficulty (b_i) and discrimination (a_i) parameters, per-model latent ability (θ_j), predicted human completion times (in minutes) for tasks lacking annotations via a log-linear mapping, and time-series task-length horizon forecasts partitioned by model release windows at 50% and 80% success thresholds.

## Method

BRIDGE fits a two-parameter logistic Item Response Theory (2PL IRT) model to the binary response matrix using MCMC with hierarchical priors, jointly estimating task difficulty, task discrimination, and model ability. The central insight is that IRT latent difficulty b is scale-invariant but sits on a log-odds scale: since model capability θ grows linearly with time (exponential scaling) and METR shows human task-time horizon h also grows exponentially, b must be linearly related to log(h). A log-linear calibration (log h = slope × b + intercept, R²=0.81) is fitted on METR's annotated tasks, anchoring the latent scale to human-interpretable minutes. This calibrated mapping is then applied to predict human completion times for out-of-distribution benchmarks (SWE-bench, Cybench, MLE-bench, GDPval) and to forecast frontier model task-length horizons over release windows using only model performance logs, independently reproducing METR's ~6-month doubling time.

## Results

On METR calibration tasks, the log-linear fit between IRT latent difficulty b and log human completion time achieves R² = 0.81, with each unit increase in b corresponding to a ~2.26× increase in human completion time. On Cybench, BRIDGE achieves R² = 0.45 correlation with human first-solve times and places 92.3% of tasks within a 0.5×–2× tolerance band. Frontier models released in 2025 achieve 50% success on tasks estimated to require ~1–2.5 hours of human effort, and the 50% solvable task-length horizon doubles approximately every 6 months, independently reproducing METR's exponential scaling findings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BRIDGE is compared against two baselines: a logit success-rate heuristic and LLM-based time estimators (Gemini 3 Pro and GPT-5.2). On Cybench, BRIDGE achieves the strongest correlation with human first-solve times (R² = 0.45) and 92.3% tolerance-band coverage, while the logit baseline substantially underestimates task duration and both LLMs consistently overestimate absolute completion times with markedly lower tolerance-band coverage. On SWE-bench Verified, BRIDGE produces monotonically increasing predictions that track the annotated bucket ordering and scale, achieving substantially better per-bucket classification accuracy and weighted macro F1 than both baselines; the logit heuristic systematically underestimates duration, while Gemini 3 Pro and GPT-5.2 yield compressed predictions with limited dynamic range, failing to separate longer-horizon buckets. BRIDGE thus outperforms both baselines across all evaluated benchmarks, demonstrating that the IRT-based latent scale captures task-level heterogeneity in discrimination and difficulty that neither raw success rates nor LLM judgment can recover.

## Relation to Prior Work

BRIDGE sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: psychometric IRT models adapted to LLM benchmarking (Lalor et al., 2019; Rodriguez et al., 2021; Hoffmann et al., 2025) and METR's human-anchored task-length evaluation (Kwa et al., 2026). Prior IRT-based LLM evaluation treated latent difficulty and ability as scale-invariant quantities useful for ranking but lacking human-interpretable meaning, while METR's framework required bespoke human time studies for each benchmark. BRIDGE's conceptual contribution is the log-linear calibration that anchors IRT's latent difficulty scale to human completion time, resolving the scale ambiguity of IRT and eliminating the need for new human studies. A concurrent effort by Epoch AI (Ho et al., 2025) applies IRT to benchmark-level difficulty estimation, but BRIDGE operates at individual task granularity and explicitly calibrates to human time rather than model-level ability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BRIDGE provides a scalable, model-performance-only pathway to human-interpretable AI capability tracking, which is practically important as the pace of new benchmarks outstrips the feasibility of repeated human studies. The independent reproduction of METR's exponential scaling (≈6-month doubling) using only model logs lends credibility to the framework and suggests it could become a standard tool for frontier capability forecasting. Open questions include how the log-linear calibration holds as task horizons extend into multi-day or multi-week regimes where inter-individual human variability grows, whether the binary success/failure abstraction loses signal for open-ended tasks (as with GDPval's LLM-as-judge), and how the framework extends to knowledge-intensive or human-AI collaborative settings where task difficulty is not purely procedural.
