## Problem

Interpretability research on LLMs frequently produces findings that do not generalise and causal interpretations that outrun the evidence, because claims about 'mechanisms,' 'features,' and 'circuits' are made without specifying what causal question is being answered or what evidence supports it. The same terminology can refer to different estimands and intervention classes across papers, making results hard to compare and deployment unreliable. The paper argues that a formal causal framework is needed to delimit what each method can and cannot establish.

## Input / Output

The framework operates as a diagnostic lens over existing interpretability studies rather than a trained model: it consumes method descriptions, intervention results (e.g., activation patching outputs, SAE reconstructions, steering vector effects), and the natural-language claims surrounding them, and produces a structured diagnosis consisting of a causal rung classification (L1 associational / L2 interventional / L3 counterfactual), an explicit estimand specification, a claim-evidence gap flag (rung mismatch or identification gap), and an identifiability assessment stating the equivalence class of the recovered representation. In the empirical pilot study, 50 papers yielding 186 individual claims were annotated to measure how often claim language exceeds the evidential rung of the reported method.

## Method

The core contribution is a unified causal diagnostic framework built on two pillars. First, Pearl's three-rung causal ladder is used as a taxonomy to classify every interpretability question (probe accuracy, circuit ablation, counterfactual 'what would have happened') and to expose rung mismatches where, e.g., associational evidence is narrated in counterfactual language. Second, causal representation learning (CRL) identifiability theory specifies the assumptions under which latent variables are recoverable from activations up to a well-characterised equivalence class (permutation, scaling, blockwise, or affine), turning vague 'does this feature exist?' questions into testable conditions on data variation and intervention design. The framework identifies two compounding failure modes—rung mismatch and identification gap—and illustrates them through three case studies (sufficiency vs. necessity in activation patching, proxy gaming in SAEs, and steering-vector entanglement), each showing the minimal additional evidence needed to close the gap. A pilot annotation study of 50 papers quantifies the prevalence of claim-evidence gaps, finding that roughly half of claims admit a stronger interpretation than the reported evidence licenses.

## Results

The paper's primary empirical contribution is a pilot study annotating 50 interpretability papers (186 claims), finding that roughly half of claims can admit a stronger causal interpretation than the reported evidence rung licenses. Annotator confidence drops monotonically with gap size (mean 4.9 for gap-free claims vs. 4.0 for two-rung gaps), though inter-annotator agreement on confidence is near-chance (α = 0.11). Three worked case studies (activation patching, SAE feature discovery, steering vectors) each formalise a specific estimand-evidence gap and identify the minimal additional evidence (e.g., L3 counterfactual conditioning, identifiability conditions) needed to close it.

**Table 2: Common estimand-evidence gaps in mechanistic interpretability.**

| Inferential Gap | Implied Claim | Actual Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Existence → Uniqueness | This is *the* circuit/factor causing a behaviour. | A circuit producing aligned output has been found, but the solution is generically non-unique; other circuits can implement equivalent I/O behaviour. |
| Correlation → Causation | Feature h_S^(l) causally mediates behaviour for concept c. | Without targeted interventions (interchange, causal scrubbing), only correlation is established; confounding may explain the association. |
| Decodability → Model Use | The model represents and computes with concept c. | A probe can decode c from h_S^(l), which does not imply the model's computations depend on c. |
| Local Sensitivity → Global Causal Role | The mechanism generalises beyond tested prompts. | Sensitivity holds for specific inputs; local attributions are input-dependent and may not transfer to held-out distributions. |
| Subspace → Direction | Concept c is encoded along a single direction. | A linear probe recovers the most predictive direction, but c may be best represented through a subspace; different datasets yield different valid projections. |
| Sufficiency → Necessity | This component is necessary for observed behaviour. | A pathway may be sufficient under specific interventions, but alternative pathways for the same behaviour may exist. |
| Low Loss → Identifiability | The canonical circuit/factor has been recovered. | The method identifies a solution only up to an equivalence class; without additional structural constraints, unsupervised methods cannot pin down a unique factorisation. |

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not benchmark a proposed method against quantitative baselines. Instead, it positions its causal-inference framework against two alternative views in §3. Against the pragmatic interpretability position (Nanda et al., 2025), the authors argue that proxy-task success is informative only when it satisfies an identifiability requirement—ruling out alternative internal explanations that also solve the proxy yet fail on the target—making identifiability a necessary complement rather than a competitor to pragmatism. Against the symmetry-based formalisation (Barbiero et al., 2026), the authors note that symmetry constraints specify desirable invariance principles but leave underspecified how violations translate into concrete failure modes, and that privileging human-interpretable symmetries risks a streetlight effect where model-relevant structure outside those symmetries is overlooked. The paper's diagnostic framework (rung mismatch + identification gap) is presented as complementary to both, providing a unified vocabulary for comparing claims across methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper unifies two previously separate literatures—Pearl's causal hierarchy (Pearl, 2009) and causal representation learning (Schölkopf et al., 2021)—as a formal diagnostic framework for mechanistic interpretability claims (circuits, SAEs, steering vectors, activation patching). It explicitly positions itself as not discovering new failure modes but providing a shared causal vocabulary (estimand, intervention class, equivalence class) that makes existing concerns about necessity/sufficiency (Heimersheim & Nanda, 2024), entanglement (Mueller et al., 2025), and evaluation rigor (Sharkey et al., 2025) precise and comparable. The paper also engages with and partially rebuts two concurrent alternative framings: pragmatic interpretability (Nanda et al., 2025) and symmetry-based interpretability (Barbiero et al., 2026), arguing that identifiability is a necessary complement to both.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's practical payoff is a diagnostic checklist and taxonomy (rung mismatch vs. identification gap) that lets practitioners verify whether their interpretability claims are supported by the evidence they actually collected, potentially reducing the gap between local success and reliable deployment. The pilot study (50 papers, 186 claims) finding that roughly half of claims admit a stronger causal reading than the method's evidence licenses is a useful signal, though the near-chance inter-annotator agreement on confidence (α=0.11) tempers its strength. Open questions the paper identifies—counterfactual semantics for safety verification, task-relative equivalence classes, compositional control of interventions, and transportability as a theory of edit generalisation—point toward a substantial research agenda at the CRL–interpretability intersection. As a position paper, its impact depends on whether the community adopts the causal vocabulary as a shared language; the framework is well-motivated but does not yet provide new methods or benchmarks to operationalise it at scale.
