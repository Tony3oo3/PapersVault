## Problem

Prior work on LLM personality relied heavily on self-report questionnaires and heuristic prompting with little behavioral validation, leaving it unclear whether LLMs' self-reported traits actually manifest in consistent downstream behavior. This paper systematically tests whether self-reported personality traits predict performance in real-world-inspired behavioral tasks and whether interventions like persona injection can control both self-reports and behavior, revealing a fundamental dissociation between the two.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261452/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation probes 12 instruction-tuned LLMs (6 small, 6 large across LLaMA, Qwen, Mistral, OLMo, GPT-4o, and Claude families) with BFI and SRQ questionnaires under 27 conditions per item (3 system prompts × 3 temperatures × 3 runs), alongside five behavioral tasks adapted from psychology (Columbia Card Task, IAT-based social bias, calibration/honesty, and Asch-style sycophancy). Outputs include per-item trait scores, behavioral task performance, and binary alignment indicators (whether mixed-effects coefficient signs match a priori human-expected directions), aggregated with clustered nonparametric bootstrap CIs over 2,000 replicates.

## Method

The paper structures its analysis around three research questions, using mixed-effects regression throughout. For RQ1 (origin), it fits binomial logistic regression to distinguish pre-trained from instruction-aligned models via six standardized trait scores, computes log run-to-run variance for stability, and regresses self-regulation on Big Five traits to assess coherence against human benchmarks. For RQ2 (manifestation), it fits mixed-effects models with self-reported traits as fixed effects predicting each behavioral task, then computes a per-trait-task alignment indicator (1 if the coefficient sign matches the human-expected direction, 0 otherwise) and aggregates these binary indicators with clustered bootstrap. For RQ3 (control), it uses separate binomial logistic regressions to predict persona condition (trait-specific vs. default) from either the six self-reported traits or a single behavioral measure, across three prompting strategies, revealing that personas shift self-reports strongly (e.g., agreeableness β≈3.95, p<.001) but produce negligible behavioral change (e.g., sycophancy β=0.03, p=0.67).

## Results

Instruction-tuning reduces run-to-run trait variance by 81–90% (≈86% averaged across Big Five, 89.5% for self-regulation), with aligned models showing ~+1.5 SD higher Openness, +0.5 SD higher Agreeableness, and −1 SD lower Neuroticism. Only ~24% of trait–task associations reach statistical significance, and among those, just 52% align with human-expected directions (chance = 50%); alignment proportions range from 45–62% across traits, with all 95% CIs overlapping the 50% chance line. Persona injection strongly shifts self-reports (agreeableness β = 3.95, p < .001; self-regulation β ≈ 2.2–2.9, p < .05) but has negligible behavioral effects (sycophancy β = 0.03, p = 0.67; risk-taking β ≈ −0.14 to 0.20, n.s.).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark a proposed method against competing baselines; instead it compares LLM trait–behavior patterns against human psychological expectations as the reference standard. Across 12 instruction-tuned models (LLaMA-3.2/3/3.1/3.3, Qwen2.5/3, Mistral-7B, OLMo-2, GPT-4o, Claude-3.7), alignment with human-expected trait–task directions hovers at chance (45–62%) for all small-to-medium models, with only Qwen-235B (82%) and GPT-4o (68%) showing modest above-chance alignment. Base (pre-trained) models show weaker and less coherent trait intercorrelations than instruction-tuned counterparts: e.g., Openness and Neuroticism show no significant association with self-regulation in base models (p = .068, .543), whereas aligned models show strong, human-consistent effects (all p < .001). For persona injection, self-reported traits reliably detect the injected persona (β ≈ 3.6–4.4 for agreeableness), but the corresponding behavioral tasks (sycophancy, risk-taking) fail to distinguish persona conditions from default (all n.s.), indicating that the 'baseline' of default prompting already produces behavior indistinguishable from persona-conditioned behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper integrates two previously separate lines of LLM personality research: self-report psychometrics (BFI, SRQ applied to LLMs, as in Pellert et al. 2024, Bhandari et al. 2025) and behavioral paradigms adapted from human psychology (Columbia Card Task, IAT, Asch conformity). Prior work treated these in isolation—questionnaire studies lacked behavioral validation, while intervention studies (persona prompting, activation steering) lacked grounding in established psychological theory. The key departure is the explicit testing of the *correspondence* between self-reported traits and downstream behavior across training stages (pre-training → SFT → DPO → RLHF), a multi-dimensional framework (origin, manifestation, control) that no prior study systematically addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-chance alignment between LLM self-reported traits and behavior (~24% significant associations, 52% directionally correct among those) challenges the widespread practice of using personality questionnaires as proxies for LLM behavioral reliability, with direct implications for alignment evaluation in high-stakes deployments. The finding that persona injection strongly shifts self-reports (β≈3.95) but leaves behavior essentially unchanged (sycophancy β=0.03, p=0.67) suggests current alignment methods shape linguistic plausibility rather than behavioral dispositions. Open questions include whether reinforcement learning from behavioral feedback (RLBF) or activation-level interventions can close the self-report–behavior gap, and whether the dissociation persists in reasoning models and agentic systems where behavioral consistency is more critical.
