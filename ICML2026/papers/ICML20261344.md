## Problem

Standard agent benchmarks evaluate trajectory-SFT agents solely by task success rate on the original interface, making it impossible to distinguish whether performance gains stem from semantic tool-use (understanding what tools do) or from memorizing interface-specific surface patterns (exact action names and invocation formats). Both mechanisms can produce identical scores, so benchmark scores alone are not identifiable evidence of environment-invariant capability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261344/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an existing agent benchmark environment (its interface specification, task prompts, action set, and environment dynamics) and produces minimally perturbed counterparts by rewriting only action names while keeping all other components identical. Outputs include task success rates under three interface conditions (original, synonym-perturbed, symbol-perturbed), the performance gap Δ, and the Interface Reliance (IR) score computed from a counterbalanced dual-interface protocol where both original and synonym aliases are simultaneously available and the agent freely chooses which to invoke.

## Method

PIPE (Perturb Interface Protocol for Evaluation) constructs perturbed environments by rewriting only the interface specification — specifically action names — while preserving task semantics, environment dynamics, observations, and intrinsic difficulty. Two perturbation families are used: synonym-based (semantically related alternatives verified by multi-annotator review) and symbol-based (meaningless tokens), with a strict policy that rejects original action names under perturbation to force genuine adaptation. The Interface Reliance (IR) metric quantifies preference for training-time interfaces by exposing the agent to both original and synonym aliases simultaneously, computing per-task preference ratios, and aggregating via geometric mean in log-space to avoid outlier dominance. Positional bias is controlled by counterbalancing the prompt ordering (original-first vs. synonym-first) and averaging the log-domain scores before exponentiating. Across 16 environments from AgentBench and AgentGym, PIPE reveals that trajectory-SFT agents exhibit environment-dependent, non-monotonic interface shortcutting that is invisible under standard evaluation, with the degree of reliance governed by the ratio of tool diversity to training supervision.

## Results

Under PIPE's minimal interface perturbations, trajectory-SFT agents exhibit severe performance degradation while non-TrajSFT agents remain largely stable. On AgentBenchPlus, AgentLM-14b drops from 61.2 to 4.44/3.08 on WEBSHOP (Δ = −57.44), and AgentLM-70b drops from 42 to 2/13.3 on DATABASE (Δ = −34.35), whereas GPT-4o-mini shows only Δ = −7 on WEBSHOP and even positive Δ on KNOWLEDGEGRAPH (+11.25). The Interface Reliance (IR) metric quantifies this: AgentLM-13b reaches IR = 15.50 on ALFWORLD (near-exclusive use of training-time action names), compared to IR ≈ 0.96–1.23 for non-TrajSFT agents. LLM-as-judge difficulty-control confirms perturbations do not increase intrinsic task difficulty (average scores shift by <0.1 on a 1–5 scale).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AgentLM (AgentTuning, Zeng et al.) is the most severely affected TrajSFT baseline: on WEBSHOP, AgentLM-14B collapses from 61.2 to 4.44/3.08 (Δ = −57.44), and AgentLM-70B on DATABASE drops from 42 to 2/13.3 (Δ = −34.35), while non-TrajSFT GPT-4o-mini degrades by only −7 on WEBSHOP. AgentFLAN (Chen et al.) is notably more robust than AgentLM, showing positive Δ on MIND2WEB (+1.98) and OPERATINGSYSTEM (+2.08), consistent with its stated design goal of disentangling format-following from reasoning, though it still degrades substantially on WEBSHOP (−39.3). API-based agents (GPT-3.5, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-5-mini) serve as non-TrajSFT controls and exhibit small or mixed Δ across environments, sometimes even improving under perturbation (e.g., GPT-3.5 gains +11.25 on KNOWLEDGEGRAPH), confirming that perturbations do not uniformly increase difficulty. On AgentGymPlus, newly fine-tuned Qwen3 and Gemma3 models (TrajSFT) show large drops on ALFWORLD (Gemma3-4B: 30.5→10.5/4.5, Δ = −23) and SCIWORLD (Gemma3-4B: 82.23→52.99/51.17, Δ = −30.15), while their non-TrajSFT counterparts remain near-stable (Δ within ±5). The IR metric further differentiates: TrajSFT agents reach IR values of 4–16 on sensitive environments (e.g., AgentLM-13B = 15.50 on ALFWORLD), whereas non-TrajSFT agents cluster around 1.0, confirming that interface shortcutting is a consequence of task-specific trajectory supervision rather than a property of the base model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of trajectory-SFT for LLM agents (AgentTuning/AgentLM, AgentFLAN, AgentBank) and agent evaluation benchmarks (AgentBench, AgentGym), but reframes the question from 'how to improve agent scores' to 'what do those scores actually measure.' It departs from prior work that uses interface brittleness to motivate new training paradigms (e.g., AgentRefine) by instead treating the brittleness as an evaluation diagnostic: the paper introduces PIPE (a minimal interface-rewrite protocol) and Interface Reliance (IR, a counterbalanced alias-preference metric) to decompose benchmark gains into semantic learning versus interface shortcutting. Conceptually, it connects to the broader shortcut-learning literature (spurious correlations, overfitting to surface features) but applies it specifically to the multi-turn tool-use setting where the 'shortcut' is memorization of action-name strings rather than visual or linguistic artifacts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that trajectory-SFT agents can lose 90%+ of their performance under a mere action-name rename (e.g., AgentLM-14b on WebShop: 61.2 → 4.44) directly challenges the common practice of interpreting benchmark success rates as evidence of transferable agent capability. PIPE and IR provide a lightweight, drop-in diagnostic that the agent community can adopt without retraining, making them likely to influence how future agent papers report and interpret results. Open questions include whether the 'loose vs. high-pressure' environment analysis generalizes beyond the 16 tested settings, whether more aggressive training-time interventions (curriculum tool introduction, interface augmentation) can fully eliminate interface reliance, and whether the findings extend to richer interface dimensions beyond action names (e.g., argument schemas, observation formats).
