## Problem

Existing multi-device Transformer inference methods (Tensor Parallelism, Sequence Parallelism, Block Parallelism) require high inter-device bandwidth (≥500 Mbps) to achieve meaningful speedups, yet communication accounts for 58.6–93.5% of total latency in bandwidth-constrained settings (≤100 Mbps) such as indoor Wi-Fi or edge deployments. This communication bottleneck renders prior approaches impractical for real-world wireless or resource-constrained environments where sustained throughput often falls below 300 Mbps.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard Transformer inputs—RGB images (patchified into token sequences for ViT) or text token sequences (for GPT-2/Llama-3-8B)—and partitions them evenly across N devices, with each device holding T/N tokens plus a replicated class token. Non-local tokens are transmitted as low-bit vector-quantized indices (10 bits per token with codebook size 1024, or G×10 bits for grouped VQ) rather than full-precision embeddings, reducing per-token communication from rD bits to log₂K bits. Outputs are class labels for vision tasks or next-token predictions for language tasks; for generative models, ASTRA accelerates the prefill stage while autoregressive decoding proceeds sequentially on a single device.

## Method

ASTRA builds on sequence parallelism and introduces Mixed-Precision Attention, where each device computes full-precision attention among its local tokens but approximates attention with non-local tokens using vector-quantized embeddings decoded from transmitted indices, achieving up to 2457.6× per-token compression. To preserve accuracy under this aggressive compression, ASTRA employs two key designs: (1) Noise-Augmented Vector Quantization (NAVQ), which injects Gaussian noise sampled from quantization residuals into quantized embeddings during training to improve distributional fidelity (proven via 2-Wasserstein distance reduction) and generalization; and (2) Distributed Class Tokens, which replicate the class token across all devices so each attends to full-precision local and quantized non-local tokens, then aggregate via mean pooling—provably reducing expected attention output error by a factor of 1/N. The VQ codebooks are jointly trained with the Transformer using a commitment loss, and the framework requires only lightweight fine-tuning (e.g., 52 GPU-hours for Llama-3-8B) rather than full retraining.

## Results

On ViT-Base (CIFAR-100/ImageNet-1K), ASTRA with 32 VQ groups achieves 91.64%/80.28% top-1 accuracy, within 0.89%/0.04% of the original 92.53%/80.32%, under a 76.8× communication compression. On GPT2-M, perplexity rises modestly (Wikipedia: 12.16→13.83; Wikitext-103: 14.8→16.84) at 102.4× compression. For Llama-3-8B prefill latency with 4 devices and 1024 tokens, ASTRA (G=1) achieves 1.563 s at 10 Mbps versus 4.578 s for single-device inference (2.93× speedup), while the fastest baseline (BP, N_b=4) only reaches 4.642 s (slower than single-device). Across all bandwidths from 10–500 Mbps, ASTRA delivers up to 2.64× end-to-end speedup over single-device and up to 15.25× over the best multi-device baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ASTRA dominates all three multi-device baselines at low bandwidths (≤100 Mbps), where communication is the bottleneck. Against Tensor Parallelism (Megatron-LM), ASTRA (G=1) is 275× faster at 10 Mbps (1.563 s vs. 430.952 s) and still 6.6× faster at 500 Mbps. Against Sequence Parallelism (Voltage), the gap is 18× at 10 Mbps (1.563 s vs. 28.256 s), narrowing to 1.3× at 500 Mbps. Against Block Parallelism (DeTransformer, N_b=4), the closest competitor, ASTRA is 3.0× faster at 10 Mbps (1.563 s vs. 4.642 s) and 1.13× faster at 100 Mbps; however, at 500 Mbps BP (N_b=4) marginally edges out ASTRA (1.485 s vs. 1.540 s), indicating that ASTRA's advantage is most pronounced in the bandwidth-constrained regime it targets. On the smaller ViT-Base/GPT2 models (Table 4), the speedup over BP+AG reaches 15.25× at 10 Mbps and 1.37× at 500 Mbps, confirming the same trend across model scales.

## Relation to Prior Work

ASTRA builds directly on the sequence parallelism paradigm introduced by Voltage (Hu & Li, 2024), which partitions input tokens across devices, and on the vector quantization machinery from VQVAE (Van Den Oord et al., 2017) and grouped VQ (Yang et al., 2023). The key departure from prior multi-device inference work (Megatron-LM's tensor parallelism, Voltage's sequence parallelism, DeTransformer's block parallelism) is the identification of inter-device communication as the dominant latency bottleneck under bandwidth ≤100 Mbps, and the introduction of Mixed-Precision Attention that transmits only low-bit VQ indices for non-local tokens rather than full-precision embeddings. Two accuracy-preserving mechanisms—Noise-Augmented Vector Quantization (inspired by Vicinal Risk Minimization) and Distributed Class Tokens (with a 1/N variance reduction guarantee)—are novel contributions not present in prior distributed inference or VQ literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ASTRA makes multi-device Transformer inference viable in realistic bandwidth-constrained environments (indoor Wi-Fi, edge clusters) where all prior methods perform worse than single-device inference, opening a practical deployment path for collaborative edge AI. Key limitations include the requirement for model fine-tuning (not plug-and-play), degraded zero-shot generalization due to VQ discretization, and acceleration of only the prefill stage for generative models rather than full autoregressive decoding. Open questions include extending the framework to decode-phase acceleration, developing codebook-sharing or dynamically composable codebooks to reduce storage overhead in heterogeneous deployments, and exploring hybrid compression strategies that better preserve out-of-distribution generalization.
