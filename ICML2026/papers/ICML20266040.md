## Problem

Existing biomedical VQA benchmarks conflate semantic plausibility with scientific correctness by relying on single-image questions and outcome-oriented scoring, failing to capture the evidence-constrained, multi-step nature of biomedical reasoning. This evaluation paradigm obscures whether models can derive conclusions through causal interpretation of experimental observations rather than probabilistic pattern matching, undermining trust in reported performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266040/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark instance provides a cluster of experimentally related pathology images (2+ per sample), an open-ended question that discloses experimental background and setup while deliberately withholding intermediate reasoning steps (observations, interpretations, sub-conclusions), and contextual text. The model must produce a structured reasoning trajectory—observation → interpretation → sub-conclusion per experimental branch—culminating in a final scientific conclusion, which is scored via the LCR metric: a harmonic mean of conclusion correctness and process fidelity with cascading binary penalties that nullify downstream scores when prerequisite nodes fail.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-stage benchmark construction framework: (1) Contextual Knowledge Extraction distills structured experimental metadata from biomedical literature; (2) Reasoning Path Construction employs a Multi-Expert Visual Integration module (four MLLMs with majority-voting consensus to suppress hallucination) followed by Logic Chain Generation that decomposes scientific reasoning into hierarchical micro-chains (setup → objective → observation → interpretation → sub-conclusion) with explicit evidence provenance annotation; (3) Question Synthesis generates open-ended questions via inverse formulation from pre-constructed logic chains, using information masking to withhold intermediate steps while disclosing experimental context. The evaluation metric, LCR score, is the harmonic mean of a conclusion score and a reasoning process score, where the process score applies cascading binary penalties across independent experimental branches—failure at the observation node nullifies all downstream interpretation and sub-conclusion scores—explicitly encoding causal dependency and penalizing hallucinated or logically incoherent reasoning even when the final answer appears correct. The resulting SORBE benchmark contains 3,215 high-quality multi-image QA pairs derived from the PathCap dataset, constructed in 32 hours on 8 GPUs.

## Results

On the proposed SORBE benchmark, all evaluated MLLMs show substantial performance degradation compared to standard medical VQA benchmarks. The best-performing model, Gemini-2.5-Flash, achieves only 42.0 LCR score on SORBE, while GPT-4o scores 26.0 and Claude Sonnet 4 scores 31.8. Among open-source models, Qwen3-VL-235B leads with 40.9, while domain-specific models like Fleming-VL-38B (17.8) and Hulu-Med-32B (22.2) perform at the bottom. The most striking finding is the dissociation between standard benchmark performance and SORBE: Fleming-VL-38B achieves 87.9 on OMVQA and 89.8 on SLAKE yet scores only 17.8 on SORBE, revealing that high scores on recognition-oriented benchmarks do not translate to evidence-driven scientific reasoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 10 MLLMs (3 proprietary, 7 open-source) on SORBE alongside 7 standard medical VQA benchmarks. Gemini-2.5-Flash outperforms all other models on SORBE (42.0 LCR), followed by Qwen3-VL-235B (40.9) and Claude Sonnet 4 (31.8), while GPT-4o trails at 26.0. Domain-specific biomedical models (Fleming-VL-38B at 17.8, Hulu-Med-32B at 22.2, Lingshu-32B at 22.6) do not outperform generalist models on SORBE, despite achieving top scores on standard benchmarks like OMVQA (87.9) and SLAKE (89.8). This reveals that SORBE probes fundamentally different capabilities—multi-image evidence-driven causal reasoning—than existing benchmarks focused on single-image recognition or description. The LCR metric further shows that even when models achieve moderate conclusion scores (e.g., GPT-4o at 52.3 S_conc), their process scores drop sharply (25.8 S_proc), indicating that correct final answers are frequently reached through flawed or hallucinated reasoning chains rather than valid evidence-grounded inference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant single-image, outcome-oriented paradigm in biomedical VQA benchmarks (VQA-RAD, PathVQA, SLAKE, PMC-VQA, MicroVQA) by introducing multi-image, evidence-constrained reasoning with explicit process evaluation. Conceptually, it extends the process reward model and chain-of-thought faithfulness literature (Uesato et al., Lightman et al., MME-CoT, M3CoTBench) into the biomedical domain, but replaces learned reward models with a structured, rule-based verification protocol (the LCR score) that enforces causal dependencies between observation, interpretation, and sub-conclusion nodes. The benchmark construction framework also builds on LLM-augmented data curation pipelines (PMC-VQA, MedXpertQA-MM) but introduces a multi-expert visual consensus mechanism and a strict observe-then-reason scaffold to mitigate hallucination and reasoning drift.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SORBE exposes a substantial and consistent performance gap: models scoring 60–90% on standard medical VQA benchmarks drop to 17–42% on SORBE, and domain-specific biomedical models (Hulu-Med, Fleming-VL) do not outperform generalist models, suggesting current systems lack genuine evidence-grounded causal reasoning rather than domain knowledge. The LCR metric, with its cascading penalty structure, provides a more faithful evaluation than free-form LLM-as-a-judge scoring, as confirmed by the ablation showing LCR yields consistently lower and more discriminative scores. Key limitations include reliance on the PathCap dataset (excluding experimental/statistical charts), pathology-specific scope, and dependence on LLM evaluators whose own reasoning biases may affect scoring. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to other biomedical subfields, whether the multi-expert consensus approach scales to larger image sets, and whether models can be specifically trained to improve process-level evidence alignment.
