## Problem

LLM inference serving systems (vLLM, SGLang) rely on generic heuristics inherited from classical distributed computing—FIFO scheduling, LRU eviction, round-robin or power-of-two-choices routing—that ignore the distinctive structure of LLM inference: dynamically growing KV cache memory, prefill-decode phase asymmetry, unknown output lengths, and continuous batching constraints. This leaves core decision problems (request routing, scheduling, cache eviction, load balancing, capacity planning) addressed through indirect heuristics rather than direct optimization, yielding unpredictable performance under workload shifts and no theoretical guarantees on robustness or fundamental limits.

## Input / Output

The decision problems addressed take as input online request arrivals with unknown decode lengths, per-worker KV cache states with monotonically growing memory footprints, heterogeneous GPU loads under barrier-synchronized all-to-all communication, and multimodal embedding caches with heterogeneous object sizes and content-dependent recomputation costs. The formulations produce routing assignments (which worker receives a request), scheduling decisions (which waiting request to admit), eviction policies (which cache entry to remove), token redistribution plans across expert replicas, and closed-form stability conditions specifying minimum fleet size for a given arrival rate—all with provable worst-case guarantees such as competitive ratios, optimal regret bounds, or stability thresholds.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a two-sided argument: LLM serving's structural characteristics (phase heterogeneity, growing memory, sticky assignments, unknown durations) transform classical optimization problems into novel variants without established solutions, while simultaneously creating new problem classes for the algorithms community. It demonstrates this through four concrete examples: LP-based per-batch token redistribution for MoE expert parallelism (LPLB, solved via embedded GPU interior-point solver in ~100 µs), online integer optimization for DP load balancing under barrier synchronization using a Balance-Future principle with short-horizon lookahead, queueing-theoretic stability analysis combined with combinatorial scheduling that establishes hindsight-optimal benchmarks via LP duals, and cost-aware caching (Least Expected Cost) achieving information-theoretically optimal regret. The key methodological insight, drawn from the airline revenue management precedent, is the formulation-to-insight-to-deployment pipeline: mathematical programs need not be solved at runtime but serve as analytical vehicles whose dual variables (e.g., bid prices from LP duals) and structural properties reveal design principles for fast, practical policies with inherited guarantees. The paper calls for cross-disciplinary engagement, arguing that algorithmic foundations provide worst-case robustness, fundamental limits for capacity planning, and optimality baselines that calibrate engineering effort—benefits heuristics validated on specific traces cannot offer.

## Results

As a position paper, this work does not present its own experimental results but cites quantitative evidence from referenced studies. Key numbers include: LPLB's embedded GPU LP solver achieves ~100 μs per solve (well within the 30–100 ms decode-step budget); Chen et al. (2026) prove an Ω(√(B log G)) reduction in long-run average imbalance over the default policy under adversarial arrivals; and Zhu et al. (2023) report up to 50× cost reduction, 4.3× FLOPs improvement, and 1.8× latency improvement for cost-aware caching over LRU on real LLM workloads. The airline revenue management precedent is cited as delivering $1.4 B in incremental revenue over three years via LP-derived bid-price policies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper with no direct method-vs-method experimental comparison; the `compares_to` list is empty. Instead, the paper contrasts optimization-based approaches against the heuristic baselines currently deployed in production systems. For MoE load balancing, LPLB (LP-based) is contrasted with auxiliary-loss heuristics (GShard, Switch Transformer) and reactive bias-adjustment methods, with the LP approach offering explicit objectives and provable near-optimality within its model. For DP request routing, the online integer optimization of Chen et al. (2026) is contrasted with vLLM's five routing policies (round-robin, power-of-two-choices, consistent hashing, cache-aware), showing a worst-case imbalance reduction that scales with cluster size. For caching, the Least Expected Cost (LEC) algorithm of Zhu et al. (2023) is contrasted with LRU eviction, demonstrating that cost-aware scoring exploits heterogeneous object sizes and content-dependent miss costs that LRU ignores. For scheduling, SJA and threshold-based policies are contrasted with FCFS, with the optimization framework providing hindsight-optimal benchmarks and competitive-ratio guarantees that FCFS cannot offer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not build on a single prior method but rather carves new conceptual ground by arguing that the algorithmic sub-problems embedded in LLM serving systems (vLLM, SGLang, Orca, DistServe) are structurally distinct from classical distributed computing and demand dedicated mathematical treatment. It repurposes frameworks from airline revenue management (LP duals yielding O(1) bid-price policies), online optimization (competitive-ratio analysis), and queueing theory (stability conditions) to argue they are directly applicable to LLM routing, scheduling, and capacity planning. The paper positions itself as a bridge between the ML systems community, which has driven architectural innovation (PagedAttention, continuous batching, prefill-decode disaggregation), and the operations research/algorithms community, whose tools remain underutilized for these novel problem structures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete gap: architectural innovations in LLM serving have outpaced the algorithmic foundations governing routing, scheduling, caching, and load balancing, which still rely on generic heuristics (FCFS, LRU, round-robin) that ignore LLM-specific structure like growing KV cache and unknown output lengths. Open questions include joint design of prediction and scheduling under uncertainty, multi-objective Pareto frontiers for production metrics, theoretical foundations for prefill-decode disaggregation, and scheduling for agentic workloads with branching and variable-length pauses. As a position paper, its evidence is illustrative rather than comprehensive, and the practical impact depends on whether the community adopts the proposed research agenda; however, the airline revenue management precedent and the concrete examples (LPLB's ~100μs LP solves, Ω(√(B log G)) competitive ratio gains) make the case compelling for a field deploying at gigawatt-hour scale.
