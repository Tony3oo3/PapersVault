## Problem

Pre-hoc fine-tuning prediction—estimating final performance before committing full training compute—lacks a principled theoretical account of what fundamentally limits predictability and how prediction uncertainty evolves with allocated computation. Existing predictors operate as black-box regressors that treat probing depth as a fixed hyperparameter, offering no structural explanation for why some tasks are predictable early while others require extended observation, and no normative criterion for when additional probing is worth its cost.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a task tuple (pretrained model, downstream dataset, stochastic optimizer) and a monotonic information set that grows with computation: zero-cost static descriptors (data-model compatibility signals) plus dynamic trajectory prefixes revealed by executing the optimizer up to budget c. Outputs are structured as a two-component risk decomposition (intrinsic floor + reducible optimization variance), a closed-form optimal probing depth under a linear cost model, a regime label from a two-dimensional phase space (intrinsic limit × decay exponent), and an effective power-law decay rate α estimated via offline calibration over repeated probes rather than a single deployment-time prediction.

## Method

The core contribution is a formal risk decomposition via the law of total variance that separates Bayes-optimal prediction risk into a computation-invariant intrinsic limit (static data-model mismatch) and a reducible optimization variance (excess uncertainty from observing only a finite trajectory prefix). Using stochastic approximation theory in a locally regular regime, the paper proves a necessary power-law lower bound V_opt(c) ≳ Kc^{-α} on how fast optimization-induced uncertainty can dissipate, where α is jointly determined by step-size schedule and metric sensitivity. This yields a budget-optimal probing equilibrium condition (marginal uncertainty reduction equals shadow-price-adjusted marginal cost) with a closed-form solution under linear cost, and a two-axis predictability phase diagram organizing tasks into Static-Sufficient, Dynamic-Critical, and Noise-Dominant regimes. The approach reframes probing not as feature extraction for a regressor but as a controlled posterior-contraction process governed by conditional mutual information I(R; X_d^(c) | X_s), with an information-theoretic interpretation linking variance reduction to multiplicative posterior shrinkage under Gaussian assumptions.

## Results

The paper validates its theoretical framework through structural (rather than accuracy-based) empirical analysis using N=1,500 independent fine-tuning runs per task. Key findings: (i) observable uncertainty exhibits approximately linear log-log decay consistent with the predicted power-law envelope V_opt(c) ≳ Kc^{-α} across all three regimes; (ii) the phase diagram (intrinsic floor vs. decay rate α) shows clear structural separation, with Static-Sufficient tasks (e.g., SST-2) clustering at low intrinsic ambiguity and high α, Dynamic-Critical tasks (e.g., GSM8K) at low intrinsic floor but small α, and Noise-Dominant tasks separated along the intrinsic-floor axis; (iii) marginal gains of probing decay monotonically in all regimes, with Static-Sufficient tasks exhausting gains almost immediately and Dynamic-Critical tasks retaining substantial marginal benefit over wide budget ranges.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare its framework against competing predictor methods or baselines in the traditional sense. It is a theoretical framework paper that validates structural predictions (power-law decay, regime separability, diminishing returns) rather than proposing a new predictor to benchmark against existing ones. The empirical section (Section 8) uses repeated-run protocols to estimate observable uncertainty proxies and confirm that the predicted qualitative behaviors—regime-dependent decay rates, clear phase-diagram separation, and monotone marginal-gain decay—hold across SST-2, GSM8K, and GLUE-style tasks. The paper explicitly positions itself as providing a 'mechanism-based view' for interpreting existing static-proxy and early-trajectory predictors, rather than introducing a new method to outperform them. The closest comparative discussion is qualitative: it explains why shallow probing succeeds on SST-2 (Static-Sufficient regime) but fails on GSM8K (Dynamic-Critical regime) as a regime mismatch rather than a methodological deficiency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the two-component uncertainty decomposition from Kendall & Gal (2017) — originally aleatoric vs. epistemic — into an intrinsic-limit vs. optimization-variance split specific to fine-tuning prediction, and grounds the reducible component in stochastic approximation theory to derive a power-law lower bound on uncertainty decay. It departs from the dominant empirical paradigm in pre-hoc prediction (proxy-based methods, trajectory extrapolation) by reframing probing not as feature extraction for a black-box regressor but as a controlled information-revelation process governed by a risk-cost tradeoff. The phase diagram synthesizes scaling-law insights (first-moment characterization) with second-moment uncertainty dynamics, filling a gap the authors identify in the descriptive-but-not-normative training-dynamics literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives practitioners a principled, regime-aware stopping rule for probing rather than a fixed step-count heuristic, and explains why the same predictor succeeds on SST-2 but fails on GSM8K as a structural regime mismatch rather than a methodological flaw. Key limitations include the reliance on a locally regular asymptotic regime (non-convex transients are excluded), the fact that α is an effective task-optimizer descriptor rather than a universal constant, and the observation that regimes can shift under different hyperparameters or model families. Open questions include adaptive stopping rules that update online, integration with concrete predictor architectures, and whether the power-law envelope holds under modern optimizers with aggressive preconditioning or mixed-precision training.
