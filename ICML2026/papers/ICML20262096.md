## Problem

Fully non-autoregressive (NAR) decoding in diffusion language models (dLLMs) suffers from incoherent generation, and the field has defaulted to semi-autoregressive block decoding as a workaround without understanding the underlying failure mechanisms. This paper identifies proximity bias—the tendency for confidence to accumulate on spatially adjacent tokens—and premature EOS dominance as the root causes, revealing that the entire generation trajectory is disproportionately determined by the very first unmasking decision.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (reasoning or planning question) and a fully masked token sequence of fixed length L=256 as input, and produces a generated text sequence as output. The planner consumes only the final-layer hidden representations (projected from 4096-dim to 128-dim) at P=32 randomly sampled candidate position sets, scoring each set with a 2-layer Transformer encoder. A progressive denoising schedule constrains early steps to unmask at least w=3 tokens (fewer than L/T), gradually increasing the unmasking budget via a power-law residual allocation, which increases separability of candidate positions at the critical first step.

## Method

The paper proposes two minimal interventions applied exclusively at the initial denoising step: (1) a lightweight 5M-parameter planner (2-layer Transformer encoder with learned positional embeddings and a position-wise scoring head) trained offline via binary cross-entropy on trajectory-level correctness labels, which selects the optimal initial unmasking position set from P=32 random candidates; and (2) EOS temperature annealing, which scales the EOS token logit by a linearly decaying factor λ_d = 3 − 2d/T before confidence ranking, suppressing premature termination in early high-uncertainty steps while preserving natural stopping later. The planner is trained in a one-time offline process where each prompt is decoded greedily from S=32 random initial positions, and only the 5M planner parameters receive gradients—the 8B diffusion backbone remains frozen. Because the intervention is confined to the first step's position selection, the method is orthogonal to and composable with any downstream token-selection heuristic (greedy, probability margin, temperature sampling), and generalizes plug-and-play to higher timestep budgets (T=64, 128) without retraining.

## Results

Under a constrained compute budget (T=32) in the fully non-autoregressive regime, the proposed method (Planner + EOS Temperature Annealing) achieves an average accuracy of 47.6 across GSM8K, MATH, Countdown, and Sudoku, outperforming the greedy Top-1 Probability baseline (44.8) by +2.8 points. The largest gain appears on GSM8K, where accuracy jumps from 46.6 to 56.8 (+10.2). When applied to the stronger Probability Margin baseline, the method further boosts average accuracy to 49.1. At higher budgets (T=128), the method reaches 52.8 average accuracy versus 37.7 for the Top-1 baseline, demonstrating that the planner generalizes across compute regimes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Top-1 Confidence baseline (Chang et al., 2022), the proposed Planner + EOS Annealing method improves average accuracy from 44.8 to 47.6 at T=32, with the largest single-task gain of +10.2 on GSM8K (46.6→56.8). Compared to the Probability Margin heuristic (Kim et al., 2025a), the method is orthogonal and additive: applying it to Prob Margin lifts average accuracy from 45.0 to 49.1, confirming compatibility with stronger position-selection heuristics. The Ancestral Sampling baseline (Austin et al., 2021), which selects positions uniformly at every step, collapses to 23.9 average accuracy, far below the proposed method's 47.6, highlighting that continuous positional randomness is destructive in the NAR regime. Temperature Sampling (Wang et al., 2025) at T=0.9 achieves 44.4 average, marginally below the greedy baseline, while the proposed method's targeted first-step intervention yields substantially larger gains. Finally, against Semi-Autoregressive decoding (Arriola et al., 2025), which suffers severe degradation under the same T=32 budget (27.0 average), the fully NAR method with the planner achieves 47.6, demonstrating that the proposed approach unlocks parallel decoding where semi-AR heuristics fail.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operates within the masked diffusion language model (MDLM) framework (Sahoo et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2024) and directly modifies the MaskGIT-style confidence-based position selection heuristic (Chang et al., 2022) that governs which tokens are unmasked at each denoising step. It departs from the prevailing semi-autoregressive decoding paradigm (Arriola et al., 2025; Nie et al., 2025) by arguing that the instability of fully non-autoregressive (NAR) decoding stems from a specific, identifiable failure mode—proximity bias—rather than an inherent incompatibility with parallel generation. The work is conceptually adjacent to Kim et al. (2026) on EOS overflow and Seo et al. (2025) on spatial incoherence, but distinguishes itself by requiring no backbone fine-tuning (unlike Rainbow Padding) and by confining its learned planner to a single initial step (unlike per-timestep planners in Peng et al., 2025 or Liu et al., 2025a).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By identifying proximity bias and the disproportionate importance of the first unmasking decision, the paper provides a mechanistic explanation for why the dLLM community has defaulted to semi-autoregressive workarounds, and shows that a 5M-parameter planner plus a single-logit EOS annealing can recover much of the lost performance without sacrificing parallelism. This opens a path toward truly parallel dLLM inference for reasoning tasks, which is critical for latency-sensitive deployment. However, the analysis is confined to 256-token sequences and structured reasoning/planning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, Countdown, Sudoku); whether proximity bias and the proposed fix generalize to open-ended generation, longer contexts, or diverse instruction-following tasks remains an open question. The planner is also task-specific in its training labels, raising questions about cross-task transfer without retraining.
