## Problem

Modern foundation models are evaluated on finite benchmark suites, yet capability claims implicitly concern behavior over much larger task families, and no principled theory exists for when a finite set of tests can meaningfully certify such family-level claims. The inferential leap from benchmark scores to capability guarantees remains unjustified, with no characterization of when finite evaluation can or cannot generalize.

## Input / Output

The evaluator receives black-box query access to a fixed stochastic model f and a known environment family P ⊂ Δ(Z), and may query f on any points in Z (including outside the supports of target distributions), possibly adaptively and with internal randomness. The output is a simultaneous estimate of the full risk profile {μ_p(f)}_{p∈P} with uniform high-probability (ε, δ) guarantees, where the required sample budget depends on an intrinsic overlap parameter of P rather than merely its cardinality m.

## Method

The paper formalizes capability evaluation as inference over task families and analyzes two sharply contrasting regimes. In the stochastic multi-environment setting, the key insight is that the χ²-radius V(P) — the infimum over proposal distributions q of the maximum χ² divergence across environments — governs sample complexity; the protocol draws i.i.d. samples from q, applies importance-weighted median-of-means estimators, and achieves O((1+V(P))log(m/δ)/ε²) samples, with matching lower bounds under a natural likelihood-ratio overlap condition and a necessary log(m)/ε² dependence even when V(P)=O(1). For infinite families, a total-variation cover combined with the finite-class protocol yields guarantees depending on the cover's χ²-radius and size, illustrated by a √k-dependent bound for length-k Bernoulli sources. In the combinatorial regime (e.g., Boolean circuits of bounded size s), the paper proves fundamental impossibility via Yao's minimax principle: no black-box protocol with T(n)=o(2^n/M(s)) queries can distinguish perfect performance from performance with additive error M(s), establishing a sharp information-theoretic boundary between certifiable and uncertifiable capabilities.

## Results

In the stochastic multi-environment regime, the paper establishes that the sample complexity for certifying the full risk profile over m environments is O((1+V(P)) log(m/δ) / ε²), where V(P) is the χ²-radius of the environment family, with a matching lower bound of Ω(V(P)/ε²) under a natural overlap condition. Without any structural overlap, a pessimistic Ω(m/ε²) lower bound is unavoidable, and even when V(P)=O(1), a Ω(log m / ε²) dependence is necessary for any adaptive protocol. In the combinatorial regime, the paper proves a fundamental impossibility: for Boolean circuits of size s, no black-box protocol using T(n)=o(2ⁿ/M(s)) queries can distinguish perfect performance from performance with additive error M(s), even when T(n) exceeds the circuit's description length.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or external methods to compare against. The key internal comparison is between the pessimistic Ω(m/ε²) lower bound (Proposition 3.1) and the improved O((1+V(P)) log(m/δ)/ε²) upper bound (Theorem 3.4): when environments overlap (small V(P)), the χ²-radius protocol achieves a substantial improvement over independent per-environment sampling, which would cost O(m log(m/δ)/ε²). The upper and lower bounds are near-tight in their dependence on V(P), with the gap being only the log(m/δ) factor, which Proposition 3.7 shows is unavoidable. The paper further contrasts the stochastic regime (where model-class-agnostic guarantees are possible via i.i.d. sampling) with the combinatorial regime (where Theorem 3.10 shows no such guarantees exist without explicit structure linking the model to the task family), establishing a sharp boundary between certifiable and uncertifiable capabilities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formalizing capability evaluation as an inference problem over task families, a framing the authors argue is absent from the benchmark-driven evaluation literature (GLUE, BIG-bench, HELM, Dynabench). Technically, the stochastic multi-environment results build on multi-mean estimation and importance sampling (Elvira et al., 2015; Demange-Chryst et al., 2023), but depart by providing sharp high-probability query-complexity characterizations governed by the χ²-radius rather than variance-reduction heuristics. The combinatorial impossibility results are philosophically aligned with backdoor-detection infeasibility (Pichler et al., 2024) and formal verification of neural networks (Katz et al., 2017), but target a different object: black-box certification of global correctness over structured task families. The paper does not build on a single prior theoretical framework but synthesizes tools from distribution testing, sequential analysis, and information theory under a new unifying problem statement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled boundary between what finite evaluation can and cannot certify, with the χ²-radius offering a concrete, computable quantity that determines multi-environment evaluation difficulty. The combinatorial impossibility results formally establish that benchmark-style black-box evaluation cannot certify global correctness for structured tasks (e.g., arithmetic), even when query counts exceed the model's description length—challenging the implicit assumption that thorough benchmarking validates capabilities. Key open questions include whether the likelihood-ratio condition in the χ²-radius lower bound can be removed, whether positive combinatorial guarantees exist for specific structured model classes (e.g., parity functions), and how to extend the framework to limited-access settings where the task family is only partially specified. Practically, the results suggest evaluation claims should explicitly state the structural assumptions on the task family under which they hold, and that evaluation should be viewed as a sequential decision problem rather than a single static test suite.
