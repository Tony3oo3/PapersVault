## Problem

Low-precision arithmetic (FP16, BF16, INT8) systematically distorts neural network confidence estimates because the softmax function exponentially amplifies logit-space quantization errors in an input-dependent manner, a phenomenon the authors term Precision Split. Existing calibration methods assume numerically exact forward propagation and none incorporates the hardware precision constant ε that governs the magnitude of this distortion, leaving a fundamental blind spot in safety-critical deployment where overconfidence is most dangerous.

## Input / Output

PACS takes a logit vector z(x) ∈ R^C produced by a network running in low precision, along with the target format's machine epsilon ε (e.g., 2^{-10} for FP16, 2^{-7} for BF16), and outputs temperature-scaled logits z^PACS(x) = z(x)/T(x) where T(x) is a per-sample scalar in [1, 1+λ]. Because T(x) is a positive scalar, the operation preserves all top-k predictions exactly, making it a drop-in inference-time postprocessing step requiring no calibration data, no retraining, and adding only three elementary operations (abs-max reduction, sigmoid, division) per sample with <0.04% wall-clock overhead.

## Method

The core mechanism identifies the dimensionless precision risk factor ε·||z(x)||_∞ as the quantity governing confidence distortion, derived from the fact that the quantize-then-stabilize (Q→S) computational path breaks softmax's shift invariance and compounds rounding errors from both the logits and their separately rounded maximum. PACS converts this risk into a smooth per-sample temperature T(x) = 1 + λ·σ((ε·||z(x)||_∞ − τ)/α) using a sigmoid gate, where τ sets the intervention threshold, α controls transition sharpness, and λ bounds maximum correction (default T_max = 2). A stop-gradient on T(x) ensures it functions purely as a numerical correction rather than a learnable coupling, preventing the optimization instabilities (NaN losses) observed when gradients flow through the temperature. The theoretical framework further proves that training-time gradient death is dominated by probability saturation (p_y rounding to 1.0 in FP16, accounting for 85% of zero-gradient cases) rather than numerical underflow, mandating logit-level intervention before softmax. Default parameters (τ=0.01, α=0.005, λ=1.0) are derived from the zero-gradient saturation threshold Z_sat ≈ 11 for FP16 rather than tuned, and the method generalizes across precision formats by adjusting only ε.

## Results

On ImageNet with ResNet-50 in FP16, PACS reduces Expected Calibration Error (ECE) from 5.82% (Standard FP16) to 1.92% while preserving top-1 accuracy at 76.13%, requiring no calibration data, no retraining, and adding only 0.04% inference overhead. PACS outperforms the strongest calibration baseline, Logit Normalization (LN, 2.05% ECE), by 6.3% relative improvement, and outperforms Parameterized Temperature Scaling (PTS, 2.08%) by 7.7%. Across architectures (Table 4), PACS achieves 1.78–2.15% ECE, with larger gains on Transformers (ViT-B/16: 2.15% vs. LN's 2.34%) than on CNNs (ResNet-50: 1.92% vs. LN's 2.05%), consistent with the theory that larger logit magnitudes amplify precision-induced distortion.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PACS (1.92% ECE) outperforms all eight baselines on ImageNet ResNet-50 FP16. Against post-hoc calibration methods that require held-out data, PACS beats Temperature Scaling (2.14%), PTS (2.08%), and LN (2.05%) by 10.3%, 7.7%, and 6.3% relative ECE reduction respectively, while needing no calibration data and no auxiliary network. Against training-time regularizers, PACS substantially outperforms Label Smoothing (3.21%), Mixup (3.87%), and Focal Loss (4.15%), all of which require full retraining. Unit Scaling, designed for gradient stability rather than calibration, barely improves ECE (5.65% vs. 5.82% baseline) despite full retraining, confirming that gradient health and output calibration are orthogonal concerns. PACS is also complementary: combining it with LN yields 1.75% ECE and with Label Smoothing yields 1.54% ECE, the best results in the table. On LLaMA-7B (BF16, C4), PACS achieves 7.8% ECE without any training, matching Soft-Prompt's 7.6% (which requires gradient-based tuning), and combining both reaches 6.5%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the temperature-scaling calibration lineage (Guo et al., 2017; Tomani et al., 2022) by deriving the temperature from a physical quantity—the product of the hardware precision constant ε and the input-specific logit norm—rather than learning it from validation data or treating it as a global hyperparameter. It departs from the mixed-precision training literature (Micikevicius et al., 2018; Blake et al., 2023), which targets gradient stability but ignores output calibration, and from post-hoc calibration methods (Wei et al., 2022; Tomani et al., 2022), which assume numerically exact logits. The key conceptual departure is reframing miscalibration under low precision as a deterministic, hardware-governed phenomenon ('Precision Split') rather than a statistical fitting problem, and showing that gradient health (Unit Scaling) and calibration quality (PACS) are orthogonal concerns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As FP16/BF16 training and INT8 inference become the industrial default, systematic overconfidence in deployed models poses real risks for safety-critical applications (autonomous driving, medical diagnosis). The pseudo-convergence finding—61.4% of samples contributing zero gradient by training's end despite healthy loss curves—suggests that standard AMP training may be silently degrading model quality in ways invisible to loss monitoring. Open questions include whether the continuous approximation holds under extreme quantization (4-bit, ternary), how the framework extends to non-softmax output layers (e.g., regression heads, diffusion models), and whether the 70/30 trunk/softmax split is architecture- and precision-universal or specific to the tested configurations.
