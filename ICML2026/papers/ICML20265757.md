## Problem

Standard evaluations reduce a learning algorithm's performance to a single aggregate score on an i.i.d. test set, making it impossible to see how far knowledge extracted from one training example actually transfers to others. Binary in-distribution vs. out-of-distribution comparisons further conflate memorization, local transfer, and broad generalization into a single pass/fail boundary. The paper asks: for each training example, how far does learning generalize, and does the answer depend on the learning paradigm?

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes 64 seed competitive programming problems (filtered from Codeforces for moderate difficulty and learnability) and constructs five paired variant levels per seed, yielding 256 transfer instances across D1–D4. Each level is defined by which shared attributes (implementation language, narrative, executable specification, algorithmic family) are progressively removed. The output is a pass@1 curve across the five distance levels per method, summarized by derived metrics: absolute and normalized gain at each level, Area Under Spectrum (AUS), and a Normalized Near-Far Gap (N-Fn) that quantifies whether gains concentrate near or far from the seed. A critical constraint is the matched-memorization protocol: gradient-based methods are compared only at checkpoints with equivalent D0 pass@1, isolating transfer efficiency from memorization capacity.

## Method

The Generalization Spectrum defines transfer distance through the nested removal of four shared attributes between a seed and its variant: D0 (exact recall, same problem in Python), D1 (implementation transfer, same problem in C++), D2 (context transfer, fully re-narrated problem preserving the executable specification), D3 (category-matched, different problem sharing the algorithmic family), and D4 (unpaired in-domain baseline). The D2 variants are synthesized via a four-stage cross-model pipeline (GPT-5.2 generation, Gemini-3-Pro consistency review, solution re-derivation, and automatic test-case verification) to ensure semantic equivalence without surface overlap. The key experimental protocol selects checkpoints at matched D0 levels before comparing D1–D4, preventing conflation of memorization with transfer. Under this protocol, the paper compares three canonical paradigms (ICL, SFT, GRPO) and within-family variants (RFT, SDFT, hint-assisted GRPO, ICL with pseudocode/key-insight/hints), revealing that RL converts memorization into near-transfer more efficiently than SFT and preserves a stronger far-transfer tail, while ICL's transfer is gated by demonstration-target correspondence and collapses at D3–D4.

## Results

At matched memorization (D0≈0.6), RL (GRPO) achieves pass@1 of 0.54/0.38/0.33/0.35 across D1–D4, outperforming reference SFT (0.41/0.31/0.19/0.22) at every transfer distance, with normalized near-transfer gains of 39.0% vs. 21.9% at D1 and 13.6% vs. 3.0% at D2. Paired ICL reaches 0.57/0.63 at D1/D2 (exceeding RL) but collapses to 0.17/0.10 at D3/D4, below the base model. The Area Under Spectrum (AUS) at D0≈0.6 is +0.15 for RL vs. +0.04 for SFT, and the Normalized Near-Far Gap (N-Fn) is +11.7% for RL vs. +16.3% for SFT, indicating RL's gains are more evenly distributed across distances.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against reference SFT (Ouyang et al., 2022), RL (GRPO, Shao et al., 2024) wins at every transfer distance at matched D0: at D0≈0.6, RL scores 0.54 vs. 0.41 at D1, 0.38 vs. 0.31 at D2, 0.33 vs. 0.19 at D3, and 0.35 vs. 0.22 at D4, with SFT's normalized gains turning negative at D3 (−7.1%) while RL remains positive (+12.3%). Paired ICL (Brown et al., 2020) dominates at near distances (D1: 0.57, D2: 0.63) but collapses below the base model at D3 (0.17) and D4 (0.10), revealing a hard correspondence bottleneck; random ICL is worse than the unadapted base model at all levels. RFT (Yuan et al., 2023) with self-generated targets preserves a smoother far-transfer tail (D3: 0.29, D4: 0.31 at D0≈0.6) compared to demonstration SFT (D3: 0.19, D4: 0.22), while DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) matches or slightly exceeds GRPO at D1 (0.57 vs. 0.54) but is comparable elsewhere. SDFT (Shenfeld et al., 2026) lifts local transfer above SFT (D1: 0.51, D2: 0.40) but drops below both SFT and the base model at D3 (0.13) and D4 (0.16), confirming that self-distillation compresses the generalization radius despite local gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the cognitive-science notion of transfer distance (Barnett & Ceci, 2002) into a concrete, per-sample evaluation protocol for LLM post-training, departing from the binary in-distribution/out-of-distribution comparisons that dominate prior generalization studies (Wang et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2022). It sits at the intersection of the ICL-vs-SFT-vs-RL comparison literature (Mosbach et al., 2023; Chu et al., 2025; Kirk et al., 2024) and competitive-programming benchmark construction (APPS, LiveCodeBench, Codeforces), but its core contribution is methodological: a matched-memorization protocol that isolates transfer efficiency from raw memorization, paired with a five-level distance axis (D0–D4) that grades how much shared structure is progressively removed. The framework is domain-agnostic in design but instantiated here on competitive programming, leveraging its executable correctness and continuously refreshed problem pool to mitigate contamination.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Generalization Spectrum provides a diagnostic lens that aggregate pass@1 scores cannot: it reveals *where* in the transfer-distance axis a method's gains concentrate or collapse, which is directly actionable for practitioners choosing between ICL, SFT, and RL or deciding whether a within-family variant (RFT, SDFT, hint-assisted GRPO) actually broadens generalization or merely lifts local performance. The negative results—that self-distillation and training-time hints can compress far transfer even while improving local transfer or optimization speed—offer a cautionary counterweight to the common assumption that richer learning signals uniformly improve generalization. Key limitations include restriction to a single domain (competitive programming), a primary model family (Qwen3-4B-Thinking), and 64 seeds; the authors acknowledge the need for validation in other reasoning and generative domains, and future work should explore combining methods with complementary spectral profiles and optimizing transfer efficiency rather than memorization alone.
