## Problem

Existing multi-bit LLM watermarking schemes enforce an all-or-nothing disclosure model: any verifier with the secret key can recover the entire embedded payload, making it impossible to expose coarse provenance information publicly while keeping sensitive metadata (user IDs, internal identifiers) restricted to authorized auditors. This forces providers to choose between full opacity and complete transparency, limiting practical deployment under emerging regulatory requirements for tiered content authentication.

## Input / Output

At each generation step, the method consumes the LLM's next-token probability distribution over the full vocabulary, a context window of h recent tokens (h=4 in experiments), per-level secret key tables, the multi-bit payload split into L segments, and a chunking schedule specifying the number of partitions at each routing stage. The output is a watermarked token sequence; during decoding, a verifier holding key tables for levels 1 through k recovers only the first k payload segments, while deeper segments remain statistically indistinguishable from random guessing (formally proven in Theorem 4.2).

## Method

HeRo embeds a multi-bit payload by recursively partitioning the vocabulary into a nested tree of contiguous chunks. At each routing stage ℓ, the current candidate set is split into K_ℓ chunks, their NTP probabilities are aggregated into a categorical distribution over chunks, and Gumbel-Max sampling (seeded by a PRF of the h-gram context and the level-ℓ key) selects one chunk, restricting the candidate set for the next stage. The final token is sampled from the last selected chunk using the deepest-level key. Because every sampling step follows the appropriate conditional probability, the marginal distribution of the output token equals the original NTP distribution (Theorem 4.1), preserving text quality. Selective disclosure arises naturally: without the level-ℓ key table, the pseudorandom variables are exchangeable across candidate message values, so per-token evidence is statistically indistinguishable and decoding reduces to chance-level accuracy (Theorem 4.2). The framework is instantiated with position allocation (MPAC-style) to distribute payload segments across generation steps, and a CUDA kernel with row-wise Philox seeds enables batched GPU generation with only ~4–5% overhead over the unwatermarked baseline.

## Results

On C4 with Llama2-7B and 200 generated tokens, HeRo¹(3) achieves 98.1% bit accuracy for a 24-bit payload (vs. 88.2% for MPAC, 74.8% for StealthInk, and 89.4% for BiMark) while maintaining a median PPL of 4.33, nearly identical to the unwatermarked baseline of 4.31. The two-level selective-disclosure variant HeRo²(2,2) achieves 92.5% bit accuracy at 24 bits with PPL 4.36, still outperforming all baselines. Decoding latency is 1.48 μs/token at batch size 64 for HeRo², roughly 33× faster than BiMark (133.23 μs/token) and 325× faster than MPAC (480.43 μs/token). Under 20% random replacement, HeRo¹(3) retains 75.2% bit accuracy versus 62.2% (MPAC), 55.2% (StealthInk), and 65.8% (BiMark).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HeRo consistently outperforms all three multi-bit baselines (MPAC, StealthInk, BiMark) on bit accuracy across every payload size (12–48 bits) on both C4 and OpenGen. At the 24-bit setting, HeRo¹(3) achieves 98.1% accuracy, exceeding MPAC(2,2.0) by 9.9 points, StealthInk(2) by 23.3 points, and BiMark(20) by 8.7 points. The selective-disclosure variant HeRo²(2,2) narrows the gap to 4.3 points over MPAC and 18.7 points over StealthInk, while uniquely supporting hierarchical access control. On text quality, MPAC shows the largest PPL degradation (4.76 vs. 4.31 baseline at 24 bits), whereas HeRo²(2,2) stays within 0.05 of the unwatermarked PPL. Under robustness attacks (20% random replacement), HeRo¹(3) retains 75.2% accuracy versus 62.2% for MPAC and 55.2% for StealthInk. In decoding latency, HeRo is an order of magnitude faster than BiMark and two orders faster than MPAC, with further speedups under batching that the baselines do not exploit.

## Relation to Prior Work

HeRo builds directly on the Gumbel-Max sampling framework and Aaronson score from Aaronson & Kirchner (2024) for statistically unbiased watermarking, and adopts the position-allocation mechanism from MPAC (Yoo et al., 2024) to distribute payload segments across generation steps. Its key departure from prior multi-bit methods (MPAC, StealthInk, BiMark) is the recursive hierarchical partitioning of the vocabulary into a nested tree, where each routing level is governed by a separate key table, enabling different verifiers to decode different payload layers. This contrasts with the all-or-nothing credential model implicit in all existing multi-bit schemes, and is distinct from designated-detector watermarking (Huang et al., 2024), which controls who can detect a watermark rather than what subset of metadata is revealed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Selective disclosure addresses a concrete deployment gap highlighted by the EU AI Act and California SB 942: providers need to let the public verify coarse provenance while keeping sensitive metadata (user IDs, session tokens) accessible only to authorized auditors. The paper's main open question is the formal characterization of the inherent trade-off between selective-disclosure granularity and detectability under a fixed token budget, which the authors identify but do not resolve. Practical limitations include evaluation on a single model family (Llama2-7B), reliance on static batching rather than continuous serving, and the observation that deeper hierarchies (8 levels) show meaningful accuracy degradation, suggesting the approach may not scale to very fine-grained access control without further optimization.
