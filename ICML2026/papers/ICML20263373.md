## Problem

The original LoRA gradient descent algorithm, despite its widespread use for fine-tuning large models, lacks a non-asymptotic convergence rate under minimal assumptions. The core obstacle is that even when the original loss is Lipschitz smooth, the reparametrized loss in terms of the adapter matrices A and B is not Lipschitz smooth, invalidating standard convergence analyses. Existing theoretical results either only characterize asymptotic behavior, analyze modified algorithms that differ from standard LoRA, or impose uniform boundedness on adapter norms that artificially restores Lipschitz smoothness.

## Input / Output

The method takes a frozen pretrained weight matrix W₀ ∈ R^{m×n}, a Lipschitz-smooth and lower-bounded loss function L, and LoRA adapter matrices A ∈ R^{r×n} and B ∈ R^{m×r} with rank r < min{m,n}, initialized with A random and B zero. It produces fine-tuned adapter matrices after T gradient descent steps, along with a guaranteed bound on the minimum gradient norm. In experiments, inputs are 32×32 CIFAR-10 RGB images (processed through ResNet-18 embeddings for logistic regression or directly for ResNet-18) and text prompts from the Alpaca dataset for TinyLlama-1.1B fine-tuning, with batch sizes of 512 or 16 respectively.

## Method

The proof proceeds in three steps. First, the adapter matrices are stacked into V = [B; Aᵀ] ∈ R^{(m+n)×r} so that BA = E₁VVᵀE₂ via extractor matrices, reformulating LoRA gradient descent as gradient descent on J(V) = L(E₁VVᵀE₂). Second, a modified descent lemma is derived for J(V), which contains higher-order terms (cubic and quartic in the step size, scaled by ||V₁||) reflecting the non-Lipschitz-smooth structure caused by the multiplicative V factor in ∇J(V) = 2Sym(E₁ᵀ∇L(E₁VVᵀE₂)E₂ᵀ)V. Third, an adaptive learning rate η_t = min{1/(4√2L(||V_t||² + ||∇L(E₁V_tV_tᵀE₂)||)), 1} is chosen to dominate these higher-order terms, guaranteeing one-step descent. The key difficulty is showing that the sum of learning rates diverges: since ||V_t||² can grow linearly in t (bounded by ||V₀||² + T/(4√2L) + 8(J(V₀)−L*)), the learning rate decays like 1/t, yielding a harmonic-series lower bound of Θ(log T) and the final O(1/log T) rate. If ||V_t|| is uniformly bounded, the rate recovers to the standard O(1/T).

## Results

The main theoretical result establishes that LoRA gradient descent achieves a minimum gradient norm convergence rate of O(1/log T) under only Lipschitz smoothness and lower-boundedness of the original loss, without requiring bounded adapter norms or Lipschitz smoothness of the reparametrized loss. If the adapter norms are uniformly bounded, the rate improves to the standard O(1/T) of gradient descent on Lipschitz smooth functions. Empirically, the derived adaptive learning rates (η^adapt, η^adapt2) and normalized learning rate (η^norm) produce faster and more stable convergence than constant learning rates of similar magnitude on logistic regression (CIFAR-10 embeddings), ResNet-18 (CIFAR-10), and TinyLlama-1.1B (Alpaca), with the advantage most pronounced when parameter norms are small.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing fine-tuning methods; instead, it compares learning rate schedules within the LoRA framework and LoRA against full-rank training. Adaptive (η^adapt, η^adapt2) and normalized (η^norm) learning rates consistently outperform constant learning rates of similar magnitude in both convergence speed and training stability across all three experimental settings. On logistic regression, ‖V_t‖ grows unbounded over 1000 epochs, empirically confirming the O(1/log T) regime predicted by theory, while on ResNet-18 the iterates converge to a bounded region after a finite number of steps. Comparing LoRA against full-rank gradient descent (Figures 7–8), LoRA exhibits measurably slower convergence, consistent with the theoretical slowdown introduced by the low-rank reparametrization. For the TinyLlama LLM experiments, adaptive learning rates provide clear benefits with small initialization (σ=1e-3) but behave nearly identically to constant rates with large initialization (σ=1/r), where ‖V_t‖ is already large and grows slowly relative to its magnitude.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the original LoRA algorithm (Hu et al., 2022) and provides the first non-asymptotic convergence analysis of its standard gradient descent implementation without requiring bounded adapter matrices or Lipschitz smoothness of the reparametrized loss. It departs from three existing lines of work: (i) asymptotic/infinite-regime analyses (NTK, infinite width), (ii) analyses of LoRA variants that preserve Lipschitz smoothness (RAC-LoRA, BernoulliLoRA, GaLore), and (iii) analyses that assume uniformly bounded adapter norms to artificially restore smoothness (Jiang et al., 2024; Ghiasvand et al., 2025). The key technical departure is the reformulation of the problem via the outer product VV^T (a Burer-Monteiro-style parametrization) combined with a modified descent lemma that accounts for the multiplicative V-dependence in the gradient, yielding a learning rate that must shrink with the parameter norm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(1/log T) convergence rate is substantially slower than the O(1/T) rate of standard gradient descent on Lipschitz smooth functions, revealing that the LoRA reparametrization introduces a fundamental slowdown mechanism (the 'position dependency' where convergence decelerates as iterates move away from the origin). This provides a rigorous explanation for why LoRA and full-rank fine-tuning can produce different solutions, and the derived adaptive learning rate strategies offer practical tools to mitigate the slowdown. Open questions include whether the O(1/log T) rate is tight, how it changes under convexity or strong convexity of the original loss, and whether the analysis extends to stochastic gradient descent (which requires a fourth-moment noise bound). The finding that LoRA creates a stationary point at the origin regardless of the original loss landscape also raises questions about whether LoRA can converge to suboptimal solutions in practice.
