## Problem

Protein binder design methods are evaluated using nonstandardized in silico protocols that vary in verifier choice, filtering thresholds, and computational budgets, making cross-study comparisons unreliable. The paper further reveals that structure prediction verifiers exhibit substantial and complementary biases, meaning reported success rates are highly sensitive to which verifier and filter configuration is used, yet this sensitivity has never been systematically quantified against wet-lab ground truth.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a target protein (sequence + cropped structure with hotspot annotations) and a set of generated binder backbones, then samples m=8 sequences per backbone via ProteinMPNN and evaluates each against a configurable structure-prediction verifier under a fixed 24-hour single-A100 GPU budget. Outputs are multi-level: per-sequence pass rate, a diversity-adjusted cluster count (FoldSeek/TM-score clustering of passing backbones), throughput-aware yield (expected successful backbones per 24 h), and a structural consistency rate measuring whether Protenix-Mini recapitulates the generated backbone after sequence design (RMSD < 2.5 Å).

## Method

ProtDBench formalizes evaluation as a modular, configurable pipeline (Algorithm 1) decoupled from the generative mechanism: a pluggable verifier assigns confidence vectors, a deterministic threshold-based filter maps them to binary pass/fail decisions, and success criteria aggregate at sequence, cluster, and throughput levels. The paper's first pillar retrospectively evaluates seven structure predictors (AF2-IG, ColabFold, Protenix, Protenix-Mini, Boltz-1, Boltz-2, Chai-1, ESMFold) on the Cao wet-lab dataset, quantifying per-verifier enrichment, union recall, and agreement curves to expose verifier-dependent bias and complementary coverage. The second pillar benchmarks seven open-source generative methods (five diffusion-based, two hallucination-based) across ten targets under a fixed AF2-IG-Easy filter, reporting throughput, per-sequence success, cluster diversity, and structural consistency. A key design choice is the throughput-aware metric N_24h = (24 h / T_tot) · N_pass, which decouples sampling quality from compute cost and reveals that high per-sequence success does not guarantee high practical yield.

## Results

Under a fixed 24-hour single-A100 budget, diffusion-based methods (PXDesign, BoltzGen, RFdiffusion-3) produce 100–1000× more successful binder backbones than hallucination-based methods (BindCraft, BoltzDesign-1) across 10 targets; PXDesign leads on 7/10 targets (e.g., 1247 on PDL1, 758 on TrkA) while BoltzGen leads on TrkA (804) and IR (606). On the Cao wet-lab dataset, Protenix and Protenix-Mini achieve the highest Top-1% ipTM success rates among all verifiers, and the union of Top-1% predictions across verifiers substantially improves recall over any single model, yet per-target agreement curves show recall dropping sharply as the agreement threshold k increases, revealing limited consensus. Per-sequence success rate and 24-hour throughput are decoupled: RFdiffusion-3 attains competitive per-sequence SR but its 33.9% generation-time share (vs. <12% for other diffusion methods) yields lower throughput, while BindCraft's high per-sequence SR is partly an artifact of its integrated iterative regeneration pipeline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among diffusion-based generators, PXDesign achieves the highest 24-hour throughput on 7 of 10 targets (e.g., 1247 on PDL1 vs. 549 for BoltzGen; 756 on IR vs. 606 for BoltzGen), while BoltzGen leads on TrkA (804 vs. 758) and IR (606 vs. 756 is actually PXDesign; BoltzGen leads on TrkA). RFdiffusion-3 is competitive on BHRF1 (751) and PDL1 (542) but falls behind PXDesign on most other targets, and its 33.9% generation-time share (vs. 7.5–13.4% for other diffusion methods) limits its throughput. Hallucination-based BindCraft and BoltzDesign-1 produce 5–50× fewer successful backbones per 24 h than the best diffusion methods, though the paper notes their high per-sequence SR is partly inflated by integrated iterative regeneration. On structural diversity (cluster pass rate at TM-score ≥ 0.6), hallucination-based methods exhibit higher rates than diffusion-based ones, suggesting broader design-space exploration. Among verifiers on the Cao dataset, Protenix and Protenix-Mini outperform AF2-IG, ColabFold, Boltz-1/2, Chai-1, and ESMFold on Top-1% ipTM success rates for most targets, and Protenix-Mini retains comparable enrichment at substantially lower inference cost; however, no single verifier dominates uniformly, and union recall across verifiers exceeds any individual model's recall, with per-target agreement curves showing recall dropping sharply as the number of agreeing verifiers k increases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not build on a single prior method but rather carves new ground by formalizing the evaluation of protein binder design as a configurable, standardized process independent of the generative mechanism. It repurposes existing structure prediction models (AF2-IG, Protenix, Boltz, Chai-1, ESMFold) as modular evaluation verifiers and existing generative methods (RFdiffusion-3, BoltzGen, PXDesign, BindCraft, etc.) as subjects under a fixed protocol. The key departure from prior work (e.g., AlphaProteo's ad-hoc filtering, Cao et al.'s energy-based designs) is the explicit treatment of verifier choice, filtering thresholds, and computational budget as first-class evaluation variables whose effects are systematically quantified rather than implicitly assumed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The field of de novo protein binder design has seen rapid methodological progress but lacks a common evaluation language, making cross-study comparisons unreliable. The verifier-bias finding—that different structure predictors recover largely distinct subsets of true binders under identical filters, with recall dropping sharply as agreement threshold increases—challenges the practice of reporting success under a single verifier and motivates multi-verifier or verifier-aware evaluation. Open questions include whether the observed bias patterns generalize from energy-based (Cao) designs to deep generative outputs, how to fairly evaluate hallucination-based methods that may exploit verifier score surfaces, and how to optimally allocate computational budget between generation and evaluation in production settings.
