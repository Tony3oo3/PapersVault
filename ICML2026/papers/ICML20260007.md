## Problem

Symmetric payoff perturbation, the standard technique for achieving last-iterate convergence in bilinear saddle-point problems, always yields only an approximate equilibrium for any fixed perturbation strength μ, requiring a decreasing μ schedule or careful tuning to recover equilibria of the original game. This paper asks whether a structural modification to the perturbation can eliminate this accuracy-speed trade-off while retaining fast convergence.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260007/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a game matrix A ∈ R^{m×n}, polytope strategy spaces X ⊆ R^m and Y ⊆ R^n, and a perturbation strength μ > 0. It outputs the minimax strategy x* and maximin strategy y* of the original (unperturbed) game. Notably, recovering both strategies requires running the algorithm twice with roles flipped, and the parameter-free variant (Algorithm 1) eliminates the need to know the game-dependent threshold α/‖x‖_max in advance, starting from an arbitrarily large μ_init and halving it until NashConv ≤ ε.

## Method

The core contribution is asymmetric perturbation: adding a strongly convex penalty (μ/2)‖x‖² to only player x's payoff while leaving player y's payoff linear. The key insight (Corollary 3.2) is that for μ below a game-dependent threshold, the minimax strategy of the perturbed game exactly coincides with that of the original game, because the unperturbed linearity of player y's objective prevents the perturbation from shifting the solution. The resulting algorithm AsymP-GDA performs alternating projected gradient updates where x uses the perturbed gradient (Ay + μx) and y uses the original gradient (A^⊤x), achieving a linear last-iterate convergence rate via a Hoffman-type error bound on the tangent residual rather than relying on strong convexity in both players. A parameter-free variant adaptively halves μ across episodes, solving only a bounded number of perturbed games independent of target accuracy ε, yielding O(ln(1/ε)) total iterations.

## Results

AsymP-GDA achieves a linear (exponentially fast) last-iterate convergence rate for bilinear saddle-point problems (Theorem 4.1), improving over the Õ(1/t) rate of symmetric perturbation approaches (Liu et al., 2023). The parameter-free variant (Theorem 4.3) requires only O(ln(1/ε)) total iterations to reach NashConv ≤ ε, regardless of the initial perturbation strength. In extensive-form games (Kuhn Poker, Leduc Poker, Liar's Dice, Goofspiel), AsymP-DGDA converges faster than all baselines (DMWU, DGDA, DOMWU, DOGDA, SymP-DGDA) and directly reaches equilibrium strategies, whereas SymP-DGDA converges only near equilibrium. Against CFR-based methods, AsymP-DGDA achieves lower NashConv on most games, with Leduc Poker as the sole exception.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against optimistic gradient methods (OGDA [Daskalakis et al., 2018]), AsymP-GDA converges faster and directly to the minimax equilibrium in normal-form games (BRPS, M-Ne), while OGDA exhibits slower convergence. In extensive-form games, AsymP-DGDA outperforms both Dilated OMWU [Lee et al., 2021] and Dilated OGDA [Farina et al., 2019] across all five games (Kuhn Poker, Leduc Poker, Liar's Dice, Goofspiel 4-card and 5-card), reaching lower NashConv in fewer strategy updates. Compared to standard Dilated MWU and Dilated GDA baselines, AsymP-DGDA also converges faster in all extensive-form games. Against CFR-based algorithms (CFR [Zinkevich et al., 2007], CFR+ [Tammelin, 2014], DCFR/LCFR [Brown & Sandholm, 2019]), AsymP-DGDA achieves lower NashConv on most games using last-iterate strategies, while CFR methods report average-iterate NashConv; Leduc Poker is the one game where CFR-based methods perform better. The key structural advantage over symmetric perturbation (SymP-GDA/SymP-DGDA) is that AsymP-GDA converges exactly to the original game's equilibrium for sufficiently small μ, while SymP-GDA converges to a point offset by O(μ) from the true equilibrium.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard symmetric perturbation framework (Facchinei & Pang, 2003; Liu et al., 2023), where both players' payoffs are regularized with strongly convex penalties, by perturbing only one player's payoff. The key theoretical ingredient is the near-linear growth bound on the bilinear objective from Wei et al. (2021), which the authors exploit to show that the asymmetric perturbation preserves the equilibrium exactly for sufficiently small μ. The approach is structurally analogous to Nesterov's smoothing (2005), which also adds a strongly convex term to only one side of a minimax formulation, but here the asymmetry is leveraged for equilibrium invariance rather than smoothness. For extensive-form games, the method builds on the dilated Euclidean regularizer of Hoda et al. (2010) and the sequence-form representation of von Stengel (1996).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The equilibrium invariance property (Corollary 3.2) is a clean structural result: a single-player perturbation recovers the exact original equilibrium, whereas the standard symmetric perturbation provably never does for any fixed μ > 0 (Theorem B.1). This eliminates the need for decreasing-μ schedules or continuation procedures that plague symmetric approaches, yielding a linear last-iterate rate with a fixed hyperparameter. However, the allowable range of μ depends on a game-dependent constant α that can be arbitrarily small (Appendix E), and recovering both players' strategies requires two separate runs. Extending the convergence guarantee to the dilated regularizer setting (AsymP-DGDA) and to non-bilinear games (e.g., zero-sum Markov games) remains open.
