## Problem

Current fair-AI research is designed for discrete, low-cardinality protected attributes like race or gender, and fails to address spatial fairness—the discrimination that arises when location (zip codes, neighborhoods) acts as a simultaneous proxy for multiple protected characteristics such as race, national origin, and source of income. This gap is consequential because location is routinely treated as a neutral technical attribute in high-stakes decision-making (mortgages, insurance, ride-hailing), despite its deep entanglement with protected groups due to historical practices like redlining. The paper argues that existing fair-AI methods are structurally ill-suited for spatial data due to challenges including high cardinality, spatial autocorrelation, and the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, this work does not propose a specific pipeline but delineates what spatial fairness systems must handle: location data at varying granularities (zip codes, census tracts, coordinates) combined with decision-making features and protected attribute labels. The critical input challenge is that spatial data is continuous, high-cardinality (thousands of geographic units), and MAUP-sensitive—the same underlying data yields different statistical results depending on aggregation level and boundary definitions. Outputs should include spatial fairness assessments robust across multiple zoning schemes, bias mitigation strategies validated against the underlying protected attributes (not merely location in isolation), and fairness metric evaluations that account for spatial autocorrelation and Tobler's First Law.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a structured diagnostic framework and set of research guidelines rather than a new algorithm. It identifies five unique challenges of spatial data that break standard fair-AI assumptions: high cardinality (making Fair-PCA's k+1 dimension requirement intractable for thousands of zip codes), complex distance metrics (road network vs. Euclidean vs. time-dependent travel time), spatial continuity (complicating discretization), MAUP (results shift with aggregation level or boundary redrawing), and spatial autocorrelation (violating the i.i.d. assumption underlying most fairness tests). It critiques the handful of existing spatial fairness definitions (Shaham et al. 2022, Sacharidis et al. 2023, Xie et al. 2022) for being legally unsound—evaluating fairness with respect to location in isolation rather than as a proxy for protected attributes—and for failing to demonstrate that their interventions actually reduce bias against the underlying protected groups. The paper proposes a joint protected attribute framework treating location as immutable alongside traditional protected attributes, a three-step validation protocol (spatial proxy analysis, multi-scale auditing, dependency clustering via Moran's I), domain-specific fairness definitions co-developed with legal and community stakeholders, exploration-exploitation trade-offs to break feedback loops of spatial neglect, and MAUP-robustness checks across multiple plausible zoning schemes.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not present quantitative experimental results. Instead, it provides a qualitative analysis of why existing fair-AI methods fail in spatial settings, identifying five unique challenges of spatial data (high cardinality, network distance complexity, spatial continuity, MAUP, and spatial autocorrelation) and three key limitations of current spatial fairness work (failure to demonstrate bias reduction, removal of user agency via fuzzy boundaries, and failure to build on existing interdisciplinary knowledge). The paper's main contribution is a set of guidelines for future spatial fairness research rather than empirical benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines. It critically reviews the handful of existing spatial fairness definitions and techniques (Shaham et al. 2022; Sacharidis et al. 2023; Xie et al. 2022) and identifies their shared shortcomings: they evaluate fairness with respect to location alone without accounting for legally protected attributes, they do not empirically verify that their spatial interventions reduce bias against the underlying protected groups (e.g., race), and they are not robust to the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem. The paper also contrasts these approaches with standard fair-AI methods (Fair-PCA, demographic parity, equalized odds, individual fairness) to argue that none are directly applicable to spatial data due to violations of independence assumptions, intractable dimensionality requirements, and sensitivity to arbitrary spatial partitioning choices.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new ground at the intersection of fair-AI, spatial statistics, and anti-discrimination law, arguing that the existing fair-AI toolkit (demographic parity, equalized odds, Fair-PCA, proxy removal) is structurally incompatible with spatial data due to high cardinality, MAUP, spatial autocorrelation, and continuity. It critiques the handful of prior spatial fairness definitions (Shaham et al. 2022; Sacharidis et al. 2023; Xie et al. 2022) for ignoring legally protected attributes and failing to demonstrate actual bias reduction. Rather than building on a single methodological lineage, the paper repurposes concepts from public policy (redlining, disparate impact doctrine), transport geography (network distance, MAUP), and spatial statistics (Tobler's First Law, spatial cross-validation) to argue that spatial fairness requires its own problem formulation and evaluation framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and legally grounded gap: decision-making systems in housing, insurance, and logistics that are 'fair' with respect to race or gender can still produce disparate impact through location as a proxy, and no existing fair-AI method is designed to detect or mitigate this. The five spatial-specific challenges (dimensionality, network distance, continuity, MAUP, spatial autocorrelation) collectively undermine the statistical assumptions (i.i.d., low-cardinality groups, discrete attributes) on which most fairness metrics and mitigation techniques rely, leaving the field without a workable framework. Key open questions include how to define joint protected-attribute fairness that is both legally compliant and computationally tractable at zip-code granularity, how to validate that spatial interventions actually reduce bias against the underlying protected groups, and how to maintain fairness robustness across alternative spatial partitions. As a position paper with no experiments, its impact depends on whether the community adopts its framing and guidelines as a research agenda.
