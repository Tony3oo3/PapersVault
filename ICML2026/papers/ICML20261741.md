## Problem

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models deployed with expert parallelism (EP) lack explicit load-balancing constraints during inference, unlike training where balance losses enforce even token distribution. This gap allows adversarial inputs to manipulate the router into concentrating tokens on a small shared subset of experts, turning the parallelism mechanism into a denial-of-service vector that inflates time-to-first-token (TTFT) and violates service-level agreements. No prior work had identified or weaponized this routing-level imbalance as a deployment-specific DoS attack.

## Input / Output

The attacker constructs prompts by repeating a single token from the model's vocabulary (optionally prefixed with a system prompt or instruction template), with prompt lengths ranging from 100 to 16,384 tokens. The method operates entirely in a black-box setting: the attacker has no access to model weights, router parameters, or the Expert-GPU mapping, and only needs to submit queries through a public API. Outputs include the latency amplification ratio (LAR) for both MoE kernel time and end-to-end TTFT, a per-layer routing bottleneck score quantifying device-level concentration, and projected SLA compliance degradation under Log-Normal TTFT distributions.

## Method

RepetitionCurse exploits the discrepancy between training-time balance losses and inference-time unconstrained routing: by repeating an identical token, the per-layer hidden states collapse toward a near-degenerate cluster, minimizing the embedding variance (Eq. 3) that the router implicitly relies on for load distribution. This gradient-free, model-agnostic strategy forces all tokens to route to the same top-k experts without requiring white-box access or expensive optimization. The paper derives a Theoretical Maximum Imbalance (TMI, Eq. 5) that characterizes two regimes—sparser models (k ≤ E_d) can achieve perfect single-GPU bottlenecks at high EP sizes, while denser models (k > E_d) are capped by physical expert placement. A vocabulary-wide coverage metric (Eq. 6) demonstrates that nearly any token in the vocabulary can serve as an effective trigger, and the attack is robust to prompt length and system-prompt variation. The authors further analyze practical constraints (unknown Expert-GPU mapping, inability to control which specific experts are targeted) and evaluate a vulnerability-aware greedy mapping (Algorithm 1) as a partial defense, showing it collapses for highly sparse models or large EP sizes.

## Results

Under 8-GPU expert parallelism, RepetitionCurse increases TTFT by up to 148% (LAR_ttft = 2.48) on Mixtral-8x7B and by up to 115% (LAR_ttft = 2.15) on Qwen3-30B-A3B at EP=32. The MoE kernel execution time amplification (LAR_moe) reaches 3.22× on Qwen3-30B-A3B at EP=32 with full attack batches (α=1). On commercial APIs, the attack inflates TTFT by 1.514× to 4.728× (95% CILB), with Kimi-Linear-Instruct showing the highest amplification at 4.728×. SLA compliance degrades from P99 to as low as P86.4 under BurstGPT workloads (σ=0.74) with a 2.48× LAR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between RepetitionCurse attack prompts and a balanced baseline of natural text (sampled from LongBench/ShareGPT), measured via LAR. RepetitionCurse consistently outperforms (i.e., causes greater degradation than) the balanced baseline across all 13 models and all EP sizes, with the gap widening as EP size increases. Against the EPLB (Expert Parallelism Load Balancer) defense from DeepSeek-AI, the paper shows that EPLB's periodic Expert-GPU remapping operates at coarse time scales (~10 min) and cannot prevent the attack within each relocation window; the default sequential mapping used by vLLM/SGLang remains vulnerable. The paper's proposed vulnerability-aware mapping (Algorithm 1) reduces bottleneck coverage by up to 36.7% for DeepSeek-V2-Lite at EP=16, but this defense collapses for highly sparse models (small k) or large EP sizes, where no mapping strategy is effective. On commercial APIs, RepetitionCurse achieves LAR_api of 1.514×–4.728× over normal prompts, confirming the attack's effectiveness in real black-box settings without any white-box access.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the existing line of LLM DoS research (Gao et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024; Li et al. 2025b), which focuses on exhausting system resources through excessive token generation, by instead targeting the MoE routing mechanism itself to cause resource *underutilization*. The attack vector exploits the well-known discrepancy between training-time balance losses and inference-time unconstrained routing in expert-parallel deployments (Du et al. 2022; Fedus et al. 2021), but no prior work had demonstrated that this imbalance could be weaponized in a black-box, model-agnostic manner. The RepetitionCurse method is a gradient-free, empirical approximation to a formalized embedding-variance minimization objective, drawing on the observation that repetitive tokens collapse hidden-state dispersion (Skean et al. 2025) without requiring white-box access.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that nearly all current MoE architectures (139 configurations surveyed) are susceptible to a trivially simple black-box attack, and that the vulnerability *worsens* with higher expert-parallelism sizes—the very direction industry is scaling for efficiency—exposes a fundamental trade-off between parallelization and robustness that has no current clean solution. The limited effectiveness of EPLB and the impracticality of perplexity-based filtering leave service providers without a general defense, making inference-time load-balancing strategies an urgent open problem. Open questions include whether architectural changes (e.g., capacity-aware routing at inference, He et al. 2025) can close the gap without sacrificing the efficiency gains that motivated MoE adoption, and whether the vulnerability persists under more aggressive dynamic rebalancing at finer time scales.
