## Problem

Existing private hypothesis selection algorithms face a fundamental trade-off: those achieving near-optimal statistical rates (e.g., the private Scheffé tournament or T-mechanism) require O(m²·n) time due to exhaustive pairwise comparisons, while faster near-linear-time algorithms sacrifice statistical optimality. The paper asks whether it is possible to simultaneously achieve near-optimal sample complexity O(log m / Δ_ε) and nearly linear O(m·n) computational complexity under ε-differential privacy.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes n i.i.d. samples from an unknown distribution and a finite hypothesis class H = {H_1, …, H_m} of publicly known distributions, and outputs the index of the best hypothesis under ε-DP. Rather than computing all m² pairwise scores, the algorithm uses query access to a score matrix M whose entries are clamped log-likelihood ratios scaled by 1/(2ε), with each entry having sensitivity at most 1. In the agnostic setting, the true distribution P need only satisfy ‖P − H_{i*}‖_TV ≤ η for some i*, and the output is an index i with D_ε(P, H_i) ≤ O(εη). As an application, the method also produces a private estimate θ̂ of a 1-dimensional statistic θ(P) over an η-cover of the distribution family.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage private tournament algorithm that avoids exhaustive pairwise comparisons. In the first stage, a random subset S of size Õ(√m) is sampled and the AboveThreshold mechanism privately identifies a strong hypothesis c̃ that outperforms all members of S, which by concentration implies c̃ eliminates all but Õ(√m) hypotheses. In the second stage, a carefully designed loss function is constructed: for hypotheses defeated by c̃ (M(i,c̃) ≥ 0.8Δ), the loss is simply M(i,c̃) requiring one query; for the remaining Õ(√m) hypotheses, the loss is the projected max over all other hypotheses. This loss has sensitivity 1 and closely approximates the original pairwise loss, so the exponential mechanism over it yields near-optimal statistical guarantees in O(m·n) time. For the agnostic setting, a BelowThreshold verification step is added: if any subsampled hypothesis is already good, it is returned directly; otherwise, the AboveThreshold step proceeds as in the simple case. A multi-round extension iteratively identifies T strong players, achieving query complexity Õ((T+1)·m^{1+1/(T+1)}), which for T = log log m gives near-linear complexity while maintaining the optimal O(log m / Δ_ε) sample complexity.

## Results

For simple hypothesis selection under ε-DP, the proposed algorithm (Theorem 4.1) achieves sample complexity O(log m / Δ_ε(H)) with running time O(m·n), matching the optimal sample complexity of the T-mechanism while reducing time from O(m²·n) to near-linear O(m·n). In the agnostic setting (Theorem 4.2), the algorithm requires O(T²·log(Tm/β)/(ε·η)) samples with expected running time O(T·n·m^(1+1/(T+1))·log m). For private universal estimation (Corollary 5.1), the method achieves near-optimal error ω_ε(O(log(m/β)·log²log m / n + εη; P, P)) with runtime O(n·m^(1+o(1))·log m), a polynomial speedup over the T-mechanism's O(n·m²).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to the Private Scheffé tournament (Bun et al., 2021), this paper improves both sample complexity (O(log m/Δ_ε) vs. O(log m/Δ_TV² + log m/(Δ_TV·ε))) and time complexity (O(m·n) vs. O(m²·n)), eliminating the quadratic pairwise-comparison bottleneck. Against the T-mechanism (Asi et al., 2024), the paper matches the sample complexity exactly at O(log m/Δ_ε) but reduces runtime from O(m²·n) to O(m·n), a quadratic speedup in the number of hypotheses. Versus Aliakbarpour et al. (2025), the paper achieves strictly better sample complexity (O(log m/Δ_ε) vs. Õ(log³m/(Δ_TV²·ε))) and comparable or better time (O(m·n) vs. Õ(m·n/Δ_TV)), since Δ_ε can be much smaller than Δ_TV·ε for well-separated hypotheses. The paper also establishes a matching lower bound (Theorem 4.3) showing Ω(log(m/β)/Δ_ε(H)) samples are necessary in the worst case, confirming the optimality of the achieved rate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two lines of work on private hypothesis selection: the statistically optimal but computationally expensive Scheffé-tournament and T-mechanism algorithms (Bun et al., 2021; Asi et al., 2024), which require O(m²·n) time due to exhaustive pairwise comparisons, and the computationally efficient but statistically sub-optimal algorithms of Aliakbarpour et al. (2025), which achieve near-linear time at the cost of a log³(m) sample complexity penalty. The paper's key departure is a two-stage 'sample-and-prune' design that uses the AboveThreshold mechanism to privately identify a small set of strong hypotheses, then applies the exponential mechanism to a carefully constructed loss function that avoids pairwise comparisons for most hypotheses. This framework is further generalized to a multi-round variant that achieves m^{1+o(1)} query complexity, and extended to the agnostic setting via an additional BelowThreshold verification step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a central open question in private hypothesis selection: whether near-optimal statistical rates and nearly linear computational complexity can be achieved simultaneously. The resulting algorithms have direct practical implications for differentially private universal statistical estimation in low-dimensional settings, where they improve runtime from O(n·m²) to O(n·m^{1+o(1)}) while preserving near-optimal accuracy. Limitations include the requirement that the privacy parameter ε be bounded by O(η) in the agnostic setting, and the multi-round algorithm's margin requirement scales with T², which may be restrictive in practice. Open questions include whether the sample-and-prune framework can be adapted to the local model of differential privacy or to continuous (infinite) hypothesis classes.
