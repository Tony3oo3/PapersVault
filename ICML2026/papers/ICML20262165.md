## Problem

On-policy preference learning methods like iterative DPO empirically outperform their offline counterparts, yet no prior theory explains why simply switching to on-policy sampling yields such large gains. Existing RLHF theory treats the base policy's coverage as a fixed quantity and focuses on designing exploration algorithms to circumvent it, leaving the intrinsic benefit of on-policy data generation unexplained.

## Input / Output

The method consumes prompts drawn from a fixed distribution, pairwise response pairs sampled from the current policy, and either binary preference labels (Bradley-Terry oracle) or continuous reward scores from a pre-trained reward model. It outputs a sequence of aligned policies, each a distribution over responses conditioned on prompts, converging to the KL-regularized reward-maximizing target. In the G-optimal design variant, a fixed offline joint distribution over (prompt, response) pairs is pre-computed and mixed with on-policy samples; in the reward distillation variant, the input additionally requires a queryable reward model whose pairwise differences serve as regression targets.

## Method

The paper's central insight is the coverage improvement principle: with sufficient batch size, each on-policy DPO update moves the policy into a local neighborhood of the target where feature-covariance coverage is uniformly better, making subsequent data increasingly informative and yielding exponential convergence in the number of iterations (sample complexity O(log(1/ε)·C*) versus O(ε⁻²·C*) for offline DPO). To remove the coverage dependence entirely, the authors construct a preferential G-optimal design—a joint distribution over prompts and responses that minimizes prediction variance for preference learning via a two-level Kiefer-Wolfowitz construction—enabling two-round convergence with a hybrid on-policy/offline sampler. For reward distillation, the paper identifies that REBEL's iterative regularization causes policy collapse, and proposes fixed-regularization and reward-calibration corrections that, combined with on-policy sampling, achieve noiseless O(1/n) convergence rates by treating each round as relative regression rather than stochastic preference classification.

## Results

On the general chat task (LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct base), on-policy DPO at iteration 3 achieves 45.44% AlpacaEval 2.0 LC Win-rate, outperforming off-policy DPO (41.26%) by over 4 points, while on-policy PDFR reaches 43.99% vs. 36.45% for off-policy PDFR (a ~7.5 point gain). On-policy RDRC (reward-calibrated REBEL) achieves 44.07% LC Win-rate at iteration 5, substantially exceeding off-policy REBEL (33.85%) and even surpassing on-policy REBEL (43.81% at iter 3, which degrades to 42.90% by iter 5). Alignment tax remains minimal: MMLU, GSM8K, ARC, Winogrande, TruthfulQA, and HellaSwag scores stay within 1–2 points of the base model across all on-policy methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against off-policy DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), on-policy DPO wins by +4.18 points on AlpacaEval LC Win-rate (45.44 vs. 41.26) and +2.36 on standard Win-rate (43.54 vs. 41.18) at the same 3-iteration/epoch budget, with monotonic gains across iterations while off-policy DPO saturates after epoch 1. Against off-policy PDFR (Yun et al., 2025), the on-policy version achieves a larger margin of +7.54 LC Win-rate points (43.99 vs. 36.45), suggesting the coverage improvement principle is especially beneficial for the softer preference-distillation objective. Against off-policy REBEL (Gao et al., 2024), on-policy RDRC (the reward-calibrated variant) achieves +10.22 LC Win-rate points (44.07 vs. 33.85), and critically, on-policy REBEL itself degrades after iteration 3 (43.81 → 42.90) confirming the predicted degeneracy, while RDRC continues to improve monotonically through iteration 5. All on-policy methods incur negligible alignment tax relative to the base model on MMLU, GSM8K, ARC, Winogrande, TruthfulQA, and HellaSwag, with shifts of at most ~1–2 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DPO framework (Rafailov et al., 2023) by providing the first theoretical analysis of how the sampling policy's coverage evolves during on-policy training, departing from prior work that treats coverage as a fixed quantity and designs exploration algorithms to circumvent it. The preferential G-optimal design in Section 4 applies the Kiefer-Wolfowitz equivalence (Kiefer & Wolfowitz, 1960) to preference learning in a novel way, while the reward distillation analysis in Section 5 modifies REBEL (Gao et al., 2024) and preference distillation (Yun et al., 2025) to prevent the degeneracy the authors identify in iterative reward regression. Unlike concurrent work on optimistic or active-learning variants of DPO (Xie et al., 2025; Foster et al., 2025; Cen et al., 2025), the paper shows that vanilla on-policy DPO already benefits from a 'coverage improvement principle' without any exploratory modifications.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the missing theoretical explanation for the well-known empirical superiority of on-policy over offline DPO, establishing an exponential separation in sample complexity (log(1/ε) vs 1/ε²) that sharpens prior lower bounds. The preferential G-optimal design offers a practical two-round algorithm that eliminates coverage dependence, and the identification of REBEL's degeneracy flaw (compounding tilt toward one-hot distributions) has direct implications for practitioners using reward distillation. Open questions include whether the d-factor in the G-optimal design bound can be removed, whether the full |X|-dependency can be avoided under structural assumptions on features, and whether the linear softmax and contextual bandit assumptions extend to the full MDP or general function class settings at scale.
