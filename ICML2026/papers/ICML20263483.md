## Problem

Automated design pipelines for LLM-based agentic systems (e.g., AFlow) optimize exclusively for task performance, leaving the resulting systems vulnerable when individual agents fail or are compromised. Prior safety work assumes weak threat models where attackers influence agents only indirectly, which is insufficient for worst-case robustness. This paper asks: can we automatically design agentic systems that remain safe even when a subset of agents is directly compromised by an adversary?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263483/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an environment specification (available tools, task descriptions, and safety/quality rubrics) plus an optional initial archive seeded with guardrail-based systems (e.g., Least-Privilege Tool Gating, Tool Filters). The Meta-Agent (GPT-5.1) and Meta-Adversary (Qwen3:32b) operate over a structured system representation—a tuple of agents (name, description, system message, assigned tools), a tool set, and a communication graph—rather than free-form Python functions. The output is a complete agentic system design that is evaluated on both clean quality and worst-case safety under attack, with the final system selected by maximizing a weighted sum of these scores.

## Method

MaMa formalizes safe agentic system design as a Stackelberg Security Game: the Meta-Agent (leader) commits to a system design, and the Meta-Adversary (follower) best-responds by selecting and overwriting ε agents to minimize safety. In each iteration, the Meta-Adversary performs LLM-based adversarial search over an archive of prior attacks, proposing new compromises, executing the corrupted system, and scoring results with a safety judge; it returns the top-k strongest attacks. The Meta-Agent then samples a reference system from its archive using a softmax-weighted rule balancing exploration and exploitation, analyzes the returned attacks and quality feedback, and applies a single targeted modification (adding/removing an agent, changing instructions, or altering the communication structure) to defend against the identified vulnerabilities. This iterative loop jointly optimizes task quality in clean settings and safety under worst-case adversarial compromise, yielding systems that generalize to stronger adversaries, different LLMs, and alternative attack modalities such as indirect prompt injection.

## Results

On the targeted attack evaluation (Table 4), MaMa achieves Attack Success Rates (ASR) of 0.17, 0.15, 0.05, and 0.25 across Travel Planning, Personal Assistant, Financial Article Writing, and Code Generation, substantially lower than Guardian Agents (0.52, 0.31, 0.06, 0.45) and LPTG+TF (0.56, 0.39, 0.09, 0.47). On the indirect tool injection attack (Table 5), MaMa achieves ASR of 0.007, 0.24, 0.00, and 0.10, outperforming all baselines except matching the best defense in Code Generation. The Meta-Adversary itself demonstrates the insufficiency of existing defenses: initial systems with LPTG+TF drop from safety scores of 4.3–4.9 (clean) to 1.5–3.2 under attack (Table 1). Systems designed by MaMa maintain quality comparable to or exceeding quality-only-optimized systems while achieving significantly higher safety under worst-case adversarial compromise.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MaMa outperforms all three baselines across nearly all environments and attack types. Against LPTG+TF (Least-Privilege Tool Gating + Tool Filters from Agent-Dojo), MaMa reduces targeted-attack ASR by 3–4× (e.g., 0.17 vs. 0.56 in Travel Planning) and indirect-injection ASR by 20–30× (e.g., 0.007 vs. 0.21 in Travel Planning). Against Guardian Agents (Nother et al., 2025), MaMa achieves roughly 3× lower ASR on targeted attacks (0.17 vs. 0.52 in Travel Planning; 0.15 vs. 0.31 in Personal Assistant) and 2–4× lower ASR on indirect injection. Against the Delimiters/Spotlighting defense (Hines et al., 2024), MaMa achieves the lowest ASR in Travel Planning (0.007 vs. 0.19) and Personal Assistant (0.24 vs. 0.46), though it slightly underperforms in Code Generation (0.10 vs. 0.08). The one environment where MaMa does not clearly dominate is Financial Article Writing, where all methods achieve near-zero ASR because tools are only called at episode end, making injection infeasible.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the automated agentic system design line (AFlow, AgentSquare, ScoreFlow) by introducing an adversarial second player, framing the design problem as a Stackelberg Security Game between a Meta-Agent (designer) and a Meta-Adversary (attacker). It departs from prior safety work on agentic systems (Guardian-Agents, LPTG+TF, Delimiters/Spotlighting), which applies fixed, manually specified defenses to handcrafted architectures, by instead letting the design process itself discover safety mechanisms through iterative adversarial feedback. The structured agentic system representation (agents, tools, communication graph) generalizes the Python-function abstraction used in AFlow to make the attack space (selecting subsets of agents to compromise) well-defined.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework that jointly optimizes task quality and adversarial safety during automated agentic system design, showing that safety-oriented design need not sacrifice performance and can even improve it. The transferability results (robustness to stronger adversaries, different LLMs, and indirect prompt injection) suggest the discovered defenses are structural rather than overfit to a specific threat. Key limitations include very high computational cost (2–5 days per environment), reliance on LLM-based judges for evaluation, and evaluation on only four to six relatively simple environments; open questions include zero-sum quality-safety tradeoffs, adversaries that manipulate tools or communication graphs, and scaling to complex real-world systems like computer-use agents.
