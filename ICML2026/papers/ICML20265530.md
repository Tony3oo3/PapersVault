## Problem

Mean-variance estimation (MVE) networks can learn aleatoric uncertainty but suffer from a non-convex, stationary-point-free loss landscape that causes overconfident or degenerate variance predictions, and they cannot estimate epistemic uncertainty. Bayesian neural networks (BNNs) capture epistemic uncertainty but are notoriously difficult to train, and jointly training them with an MVE head makes the optimization even more unstable. No existing method reliably disentangles aleatoric and epistemic uncertainties while maintaining accurate mean estimation across diverse problem settings.

## Input / Output

The method accepts heterogeneous inputs: tabular feature vectors (UCI regression), RGB images at 128×128 (APPA-REAL) or native resolution (image regression with ResNet-34), and time-series deformation sequences processed by a GRU (material plasticity). The output is a fully decomposed predictive distribution: a predictive mean, an aleatoric variance from the dedicated variance network, and an epistemic variance from the BNN posterior, which together form the total predictive variance. Notably, the variance network does not directly output σ²; instead it models the squared residual with a Gamma distribution and recovers the variance as the Gamma mean (α/λ).

## Method

VeBNN employs a cooperative sequential training strategy with three stages: (1) train a mean network under constant aleatoric variance (reducing to standard MSE), (2) train a separate variance estimation network on the fixed-mean squared residuals using a Gamma likelihood whose gradients contain no high-order terms, and (3) train a BNN with the aleatoric variance held fixed, using standard Bayesian inference (pSGLD, MC-Dropout, BBB, or deep ensembles). The key theoretical insight is that the joint MVE output-space loss has an indefinite Hessian and no finite critical point (Lemma A.1), so decoupling the mean and variance into separate convex sub-problems eliminates the degenerate optimization dynamics. The Gamma likelihood for the squared residual is provably well-behaved because the residual is asymptotically Gamma-distributed under Gaussian noise, and its gradient with respect to the shape and rate parameters avoids the vanishing-gradient pathology of the Gaussian NLL. The procedure can optionally iterate between steps 2 and 3, with the updated variance reweighting the mean training, and empirically converges within K=1–2 iterations.

## Results

On the 8 UCI regression datasets, VeBNN (pSGLD) achieves the best TLL on 4 of 8 datasets (Carbon: 4.04, Concrete: −2.87, Energy: −0.85, Power: −2.74), while VeBNN (MC-Dropout) leads on Boston (−2.42). On the APPA-REAL dataset with known aleatoric uncertainty, VeBNN (pSGLD) achieves the best RMSE (17.72), Wasserstein distance (13.50), Epistemic TLL (−8.80), and TIL (22.68) across all metrics. On the Material Plasticity Law Discovery dataset, VeBNN (pSGLD) consistently outperforms all jointly-trained MVE baselines across RMSE, WA, Epistemic TLL, TC, and TIL for all training set sizes (N=50 to 1000).

## Baselines & Comparisons

VeBNN consistently outperforms all jointly-trained MVE baselines across the majority of UCI and image regression datasets. The most dramatic improvement is over MVE (BBB), which suffers catastrophic failures (TLL as low as −980 on Superconduct) while VeBNN (BBB) recovers competitive performance (−3.60), demonstrating that cooperative training rescues even weak inference methods. Against MVE (β-NLL) and MVE (Natural), VeBNN wins on 6–7 of 8 UCI datasets, with the sole exception being Yacht where joint training achieves slightly better TLL at the cost of worse mean prediction and larger interval length. On the APPA-REAL dataset, VeBNN (pSGLD) beats MVE (pSGLD) by 0.85 in RMSE (17.72 vs. 18.57), 6.89 in WA (13.50 vs. 20.39), and 14.09 in Epistemic TLL (−8.80 vs. −22.89). On the Material Plasticity dataset, VeBNN (pSGLD) achieves lower WA than MVE (β-NLL=0.5) at all training sizes (e.g., 0.0067 vs. 0.0099 at N=1000) while matching ME (MSE) on RMSE, confirming that the cooperative strategy disentangles uncertainties without sacrificing mean accuracy. Evidential regression performs comparably to MVE (β-NLL) on UCI datasets but degrades significantly on the plasticity and APPA-REAL problems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Mean-Variance Estimation (MVE) networks (Nix & Weigend, 1994) and Bayesian neural networks, but departs from the standard joint-training paradigm by proposing a cooperative sequential strategy: train a mean network, then a variance network with a Gamma likelihood on squared residuals, then a BNN with the aleatoric variance fixed. It positions itself against the growing literature on MVE training pathologies (β-NLL, natural reparameterization, evidential regression) and against BNNs that typically ignore or assume homoscedastic noise. The theoretical contribution formalizes why joint MVE training is fundamentally problematic (non-convex output-space loss with no finite stationary point), providing a principled justification for the sequential decomposition that prior work had only addressed empirically or through ad-hoc loss modifications.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a straightforward, implementable recipe for uncertainty disentanglement that works across diverse architectures (MLP, GRU, ResNet) and inference methods (pSGLD, MC-Dropout, BBB, ensembles), and the Gamma likelihood for variance estimation cleanly resolves the gradient-imbalance problem without introducing tunable hyperparameters like β. Limitations include the Gaussian prior-likelihood assumption for the BNN, observed aleatoric uncertainty inflation under distribution shift (both aleatoric and epistemic rise together when only epistemic should), and the continued difficulty of BNN training. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-modal noise (sketched via MDNs), whether it integrates with imprecise probabilistic methods for broader epistemic coverage, and whether the cooperative strategy generalizes to classification or multi-output settings.
