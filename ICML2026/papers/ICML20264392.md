## Problem

Standard OR training recipes that fine-tune on complete written-out solutions improve easier benchmarks but sharply degrade harder ones when applied to Large Reasoning Models (LRMs), suggesting that externally provided solution patterns interfere with the model's own multi-step write-run-inspect-revise process. The paper asks how to adapt an LRM to optimization modeling without replacing its native solution trajectory with externally authored patterns, and whether local repair of the model's own mistakes can outperform full teacher-solution distillation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264392/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a natural language optimization problem and produces an iterative trajectory of text reasoning interleaved with executable Python/PuLP code blocks (up to 4 executions per rollout), where each code execution returns solver output that the model inspects before deciding whether to revise its formulation or code. The final output is a single boxed numerical objective value, evaluated via 8-sample pass@1 (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95) with correctness defined as relative error within a predefined threshold of the ground-truth optimum.

## Method

CALM (Corrective Adaptation with Lightweight Modification) implements a Reasoner-Intervener loop: the base LRM (Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507) first attempts the problem on its own, then an Intervener (Gemini-2.5-Pro) identifies the first concrete mistake from a seven-type error taxonomy (grouped into Code Utilization Distrust and Lack of OR Expertise), inserts a short corrective hint at that point, and lets the Reasoner continue from the revised context, repeating for up to 5 interventions. Across the curated dataset this process modifies fewer than 2.6% of generated tokens, preserving the model's own solution structure. The resulting corrected trajectories (112 problems, filtered for both numerical correctness and a clean solving process) are used for supervised fine-tuning, followed by reinforcement learning with GRPO and a binary correctness reward. Under a matched hard-benchmark control, this local-repair-then-RL pipeline outperforms direct distillation baselines that train on complete teacher-generated solutions from much stronger models, supporting the claim that local repair of the base model's own solution is more effective than full teacher-solution replacement.

## Results

STORM, a 4B optimization-modeling specialist built from Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507, achieves 68.9% macro-average pass@1 accuracy across five benchmarks (NL4Opt, MAMO-Easy, MAMO-Complex, IndustryOR, OptMath), a +11.8-point improvement over its base model (57.1%). This matches the 671B DeepSeek-R1-0528 (67.5%) and sets a new state-of-the-art on MAMO-Complex (70.3%). On out-of-domain math benchmarks (AIME 2025, AMC 2023, MATH500-Level5), RL with CALM averages 82.33% vs. 80.37% for RL without CALM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

STORM-4B (68.9% macro avg) outperforms the 671B DeepSeek-R1-0528 (67.5%) by 1.4 points and the 235B Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking (63.9%) by 5.0 points, despite being orders of magnitude smaller. Against the strongest learning-based baseline SIRL-Qwen2.5-7B (62.1%), STORM leads by 6.8 points; against GPT-4 (55.0%) and DeepSeek-V3 671B (61.0%), the margins are 13.9 and 7.9 points respectively. On individual benchmarks, STORM sets new SOTA on MAMO-Complex (70.3% vs. 69.1% for DeepSeek-R1) and NL4OPT (93.3% vs. 86.6%), but trails DeepSeek-R1 on OptMath (44.5% vs. 50.6%) and IndustryOR (50.0% vs. 52.5%). Under a matched hard-benchmark control (Table 4), CALM+RL also beats direct distillation from DeepSeek-R1 (54.9% vs. 47.4% Hard-3 avg) and Gemini-2.5-Pro (54.9% vs. 30.8%), confirming the gain comes from local repair rather than access to a stronger teacher.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the established line of OR modeling systems (ORLM, LLMOPT, SIRL, OptiMUS) that trained instruction-tuned models to produce complete solutions in a single pass. The key conceptual shift is recognizing that Large Reasoning Models already possess their own multi-step write-run-inspect-revise process, and that the standard recipe of fine-tuning on externally written complete solutions (e.g., OR-Instruct-3K) actively interferes with this native process on harder problems. CALM's local-repair principle—keeping the base model's own trajectory and inserting a short hint only at the first detected mistake—differs from both direct distillation and from prior hint-based adaptation work (START, CoRT) by being fully automated, dynamic, and process-level rather than relying on static hint libraries or manual annotation. The RL stage builds on GRPO (DeepSeek-Math) with a binary correctness reward and execution-output masking.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the standard SFT recipe for OR modeling (fine-tuning on complete solutions) helps easy problems but sharply degrades hard ones is a practical warning for anyone adapting LRMs to iterative, tool-using tasks: the training target's *process shape* matters, not just its final correctness. The matched hard-benchmark control showing that local repair of the base model's own solution outperforms full distillation from much stronger teachers (DeepSeek-R1-0528, Gemini-2.5-Pro) suggests a general principle for LRM post-training that could extend beyond optimization modeling. Limitations include the narrow scope to solver-based code execution (not general tool-using agents), reliance on a proprietary intervener for the main results (though a swap to DeepSeek-R1-0528 narrows the gap to 1.1 points), and the 4B base model being from the same family as one of the evaluated baselines. Open questions include whether the local-repair principle transfers to other iterative reasoning tasks, whether cheaper curation strategies can replace the Intervener, and whether the effect persists at larger model scales where the base model's native process is already stronger.
