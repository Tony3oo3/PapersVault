## Problem

Standard debate for scalable oversight forces AI agents into fixed adversarial positions, incentivizing persuasion over epistemic honesty, and demands that a weak judge adjudicate between sophisticated competing arguments. When the judge is significantly weaker than the consultants (the realistic capability-asymmetric setting), debate can underperform even simple direct answering. The paper asks whether a collaborative, mediation-inspired protocol can reduce the judge's burden by having the consultants resolve their own disagreements before the judge intervenes.

## Input / Output

The method takes a multiple-choice question and two consultant models that independently produce initial answers with chain-of-thought reasoning; only instances where the two consultants naturally disagree (with at least one correct) are retained for evaluation. Each turn, consultants output structured JSON containing their current answer, a 4–6 step reasoning trace, an explicit list of identified conflicts with per-conflict winner assignments, and a confidence level. The protocol terminates in one of three exit modes—Truth Consensus, Agreement Trap, or Disagreement Collapse—and the judge receives the full transcript to produce a final answer, acting as a verifier of consensus rather than an arbiter of competing claims.

## Method

Disagreement Resolution (DR) reframes the oversight interaction from adversarial debate to collaborative truth-seeking, drawing on human mediation principles (particularly Double Crux). At each turn, each consultant identifies a 'crux'—the specific disputed premise or inference step extracted from the mismatch between the two arguments—then updates a belief distribution over the two candidate answers and decides whether to retain its current answer or adopt the opponent's. This belief-revision mechanism allows convergence to consensus or isolation of a precise crux, structurally reducing the judge's task to verifying a simpler, focused claim rather than adjudicating a full adversarial transcript. The paper provides three theoretical results: (1) capability asymmetry is necessary for any consultancy to benefit the judge, (2) DR accuracy is dominated by the consultant pair's calibration and is comparatively insensitive to judge capability when consensus is reached with high probability, and (3) under reasonable calibration, DR provably outperforms Debate for sufficiently weak judges. Empirically, DR achieves 62.1% average judge accuracy versus 49.2% for Debate across GPQA, SuperGPQA, and HLE, with gains up to +27.5% for the weakest judge (Gemma-3-4B) paired with open-source consultants.

## Results

On three expert-level benchmarks (GPQA, SuperGPQA, HLE-MC), Disagreement Resolution (DR) achieves 62.1% overall judge accuracy compared to 49.2% for standard Debate, a +12.9% improvement. DR outperforms Debate in 10 of 12 evaluated conditions, with gains ranging from +3.6% to +27.5% depending on the consultant pair and judge model. The advantage is largest for the open-source GLM-4.6 vs. Kimi K2 Thinking pair (+21.3% average) and for the weakest judge (Gemma-3-4B), where DR gains reach +27.5% on GPQA. Both interactive protocols substantially outperform the static Double Consultancy baseline (19.6%) and the Naive Judge (35.4%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is DR versus standard Debate (following the multi-turn adversarial framework of Khan et al., 2024). DR wins in 10 of 12 conditions, with an overall average improvement of +12.9% (62.1% vs. 49.2%). The gap is most pronounced for the weaker Gemma-3-4B judge (+15.9% on GPQA, +12.7% on SuperGPQA for the GPT-4o–Claude pair; +27.5% on GPQA for the GLM–Kimi pair), confirming that DR's collaborative structure specifically scaffolds weak judges. DR loses to Debate in only two conditions: SuperGPQA with GPT-4o-mini judge (−3.6%) and HLE-MC with Gemma-3-4B judge (−6.8%) under the GPT-4o–Claude pair, suggesting that for very strong judges or extremely hard tasks, adversarial argumentation can still be informative. Against the static Double Consultancy baseline (independent consultant answers shown to the judge without interaction), both interactive protocols generally win, though Double Consultancy edges out DR on HLE-MC with the GPT-4o–Claude pair (56.8–58.0% vs. 38.6–44.3%), hinting that multi-turn tracking overhead can hurt on the hardest questions. The Naive Judge (no consultant help) achieves only 35.4% overall, confirming that the capability asymmetry setting is realistic and that interactive deliberation provides substantial value.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the adversarial debate paradigm for scalable oversight (Irving et al., 2018; Khan et al., 2024) by reframing the interaction as collaborative truth-seeking, drawing on human mediation and the Double Crux technique (Baumann, 2016). While prior debate work optimizes debaters for persuasiveness and relies on the judge to adjudicate between fixed positions, DR allows consultants to revise their answers and converge, shifting the judge from arbiter to verifier. The paper also positions itself against recent findings (Kenton et al., 2024) showing debate underperforms simple baselines in capability-asymmetric settings, arguing that the adversarial mechanism itself is the bottleneck rather than the judge's raw capability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a critical gap in scalable oversight: when the judge is genuinely weaker than the AI being supervised (the realistic deployment scenario), adversarial debate provides little signal. The finding that DR's advantage is largest for the weakest judges and actually reverses for stronger ones suggests mechanism design, not judge capability, should be the primary lever for oversight. Key limitations include evaluation only at inference time, no active judge interaction, and reliance on naturally occurring disagreements where ground truth is harder to guarantee. Open questions include whether DR can serve as a training-time signal, how an active judge could contribute beyond passive verification, and robustness against adversarial or collusive consultants.
