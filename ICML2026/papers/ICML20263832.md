## Problem

Existing benchmarks evaluate voice agents on either grounded task completion (text-only, turn-based settings like τ-bench) or conversational dynamics (synthetic scenarios without real tool calls like Full-Duplex-Bench), but none combine verifiable task completion, full-duplex interaction, and realistic audio conditions simultaneously. This leaves open whether audio-native models can reliably complete consequential multi-step tasks while managing real-time turn-taking, interruptions, and acoustic degradation.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes a continuous bidirectional audio stream discretized into 200ms ticks, where the user simulator generates speech via TTS with configurable personas, environmental noise, and channel degradation (G.711 μ-law 8kHz, Gilbert-Elliott frame drops), while the agent API processes audio indexed on audio timestamps rather than wall-clock time. Outputs include pass@1 (deterministic comparison of final database state against annotated goals, plus LLM-evaluated agent communications to handle spoken output variability) and four voice interaction quality dimensions: latency, responsiveness, interrupt rate, and selectivity. In the default configuration, the user simulator receives the agent's transcript directly rather than transcribing agent speech, isolating agent performance from ASR artifacts; an ASR-enabled mode provides a stricter end-to-end stress test.

## Method

The core mechanism is a tick-based full-duplex orchestrator that decouples simulation time from wall-clock time: by advancing a 200ms simulation clock independently, the user simulator can use the most capable LLM (GPT-4.1) for turn-taking decisions without real-time latency constraints, enabling reproducibility and fine-grained control over interruption timing, backchannel frequency, and yield behavior. The voice user simulator pipeline combines LLM-generated natural spoken language (with disfluencies, verbalized special characters, terse responses), TTS synthesis via ElevenLabs v3 with seven diverse personas, audio environment mixing (background noise at 15 dB SNR, burst noise via Poisson process, dynamic muffling, telephony compression), and LLM-driven turn-taking policies evaluated at 2-second intervals. Evaluation spans 278 tasks across retail, airline, and telecom domains inherited from τ²-bench, with a 3×3×2 design (3 models × 3 domains × 2 conditions: Clean vs. Realistic) plus single-factor ablations to isolate the impact of noise, accents, and turn-taking on task completion.

## Results

On the τ-Voice benchmark (278 tasks across Retail, Airline, and Telecom), GPT-5 (reasoning) achieves 85% pass@1 in text, while the best voice model (grok-voice) reaches only 51% under Clean conditions and 38% under Realistic conditions—retaining just 30–45% of text SOTA capability. Under Realistic conditions (noise, diverse accents, natural turn-taking), all three voice models drop a further 5–14 pp below their Clean scores. Qualitative error analysis of 91 failed simulations attributes 79–90% of failures to agent behavior rather than simulator artifacts, with authentication transcription errors and hallucinated completions as the dominant failure modes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks three audio-native voice models (gemini-live-2.5, gpt-realtime-1.5, grok-voice) against two text baselines: GPT-5 (reasoning, 85%) and GPT-4.1 (non-reasoning, 54%). Under Clean conditions, the best voice model (grok-voice, 51%) nearly matches GPT-4.1 (54%, a 3 pp gap) but still trails GPT-5 by 34 pp; under Realistic conditions the gap widens to 46 pp from GPT-5 and 16 pp from GPT-4.1. Among voice models, grok-voice leads overall (51% Clean, 38% Realistic) and dominates Telecom (58% vs. 20–28%), while gpt-realtime-1.5 achieves the single best per-domain score in Retail (71% Clean) and the best voice interaction quality on latency (0.90 s) and responsiveness (100%) but the worst selectivity (6%). gemini-live-2.5 is the most robust to acoustic degradation, losing only 17% of its Clean performance under Realistic conditions versus 24–28% for the others. No voice model masters both task completion and conversational dynamics simultaneously, and all three fall well below even the non-reasoning text baseline under realistic conditions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends τ²-bench (Barres et al., 2025) and its predecessor τ-bench (Yao et al., 2025) from text-only, turn-based evaluation to full-duplex voice interaction, inheriting the task infrastructure (domains, tools, databases, policies) while adding a tick-based audio orchestrator, a controllable voice user simulator, and voice-specific interaction metrics. It positions itself against Full-Duplex-Bench (Lin et al., 2025, 2026), which evaluates turn-taking and interruptions but on synthetic scripted scenarios without real tool calls or verifiable database state, and against speech-understanding benchmarks (VoiceBench, VocalBench, Audio MultiChallenge) that test acoustic robustness in isolation from grounded multi-turn task solving. The key conceptual departure is combining all three dimensions—verifiable task completion, full-duplex interaction, and realistic audio—into a single benchmark, which the authors argue no prior work achieves.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a substantial and previously unmeasurable voice-text gap (voice agents retain only 30–45% of text SOTA capability under realistic conditions), with the dominant source being the modality shift itself rather than acoustic degradation. The model-specific accent vulnerability (grok-voice loses 38% of Clean performance while gemini-live-2.5 is nearly unaffected) raises concrete accessibility concerns for deployment. Key limitations include TTS-synthesized (not recorded) speech, English-only evaluation, a simulator that is more patient than real users, and a default configuration that bypasses ASR on the agent side—meaning scores are upper bounds on real-world performance. Open questions include whether cascaded ASR→LLM→TTS baselines would narrow the gap, how findings generalize to non-English languages and recorded speech, and whether the observed trade-offs between responsiveness and selectivity are fundamental to current architectures or addressable with better training.
