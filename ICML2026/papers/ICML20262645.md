## Problem

Existing agent safety benchmarks evaluate either single-turn tool-using tasks or multi-turn conversations without tools, leaving the intersection of multi-turn interactions and real tool use unexplored. This gap is critical because distributing harmful intent across multiple turns substantially increases attack success rates (ASR rises by 16% on average across models), exposing a widening capability-safety gap that current safety training—focused on semantically harmful text—fails to address.

## Input / Output

For the benchmark, single-turn harmful tasks (365 tasks across five MCP tools: Filesystem, Playwright, PostgreSQL, Notion, Terminal) are transformed into multi-turn attack sequences averaging 3.19 turns via a taxonomy-guided LLM decomposition. For the ToolShield defense, the input is a newly introduced tool's function documentation; the agent generates a safety tree, synthesizes single- and multi-turn test cases, executes them in a sandboxed environment, and distills a static safety experience list (typically 2.5K–6K tokens) that is injected into the agent's context at deployment. The final output is a per-task classification (complete, reject, or failed) assessed by an LLM judge validated at 95.15% agreement with rule-based rubrics.

## Method

The paper contributes two components. First, a Multi-Turn Attack Taxonomy (MAT) that systematically transforms single-turn harms into multi-turn sequences along three orthogonal dimensions—Format (Addition vs. Decomposition), Method (Mapping, Wrapping, Composition, Identity), and Target (Data Files vs. Environment States)—yielding 8 subcategories that preserve the original harmful objective while obscuring intent across turns. Second, ToolShield, a training-free, tool-agnostic self-exploration defense operating in four stages: (1) the agent analyzes tool documentation to build a safety tree and generate single- and multi-turn test cases covering identified harm scenarios; (2) test cases are executed in a sandbox to capture full execution trajectories; (3) trajectories are summarized into safety experiences capturing both successful refusals and failure blind spots; (4) experiences are incrementally added, updated, or skipped in a growing list. The key insight is that the same reasoning capability enabling tool use can be redirected to recognize tool misuse: by exploring a tool's functional risks before deployment, the agent converts its own mistakes into explicit safety guidance, reducing ASR by 30% on average without retraining or tool-specific heuristics.

## Results

On MT-AgentRisk (365 multi-turn tasks across 5 tools), multi-turn attacks increase ASR by 16% on average across six models, ranging from +8.8% (Deepseek-v3.2) to +27.1% (Claude-4.5-Sonnet). ToolShield, the proposed training-free defense, reduces multi-turn ASR by 30% on average: Claude-4.5-Sonnet drops from 72% to 22% (−49.9 pp), Qwen3-Coder by −24%, and Seed-1.6 by −38%. On a 100-task subset, ToolShield achieves 15% ASR for Claude-4.5-Sonnet and 57% for Gemini-3-Flash, while introducing zero false positives on 170 benign tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ToolShield substantially outperforms LlamaFirewall (Chennabasappa et al., 2025), which only reduces ASR by ~3 pp (88%→85% for Claude-4.5-Sonnet, 81%→79% for Gemini-3-Flash), confirming that external prompt/execution guardrails struggle to detect compositional harm from real-world tools. Against the LLM Guardrail baseline (Chen et al., 2025b, ShieldAgent-style trajectory monitoring), ToolShield achieves comparable ASR (15% vs. 16% on Claude) but at roughly half the inference-time cost ($0.66 vs. $1.22); combining both yields the lowest ASR at 7%. A Taxonomy Defense baseline (injecting the MAT description with seed examples) improves safety awareness but remains less effective than ToolShield because it provides only abstract pattern-level guidance without execution-grounded context. An ablation removing simulated execution (ToolShield w/o simulation) shows that agents cannot reliably identify harmful patterns without observing actual execution traces, validating the necessity of the sandboxed execution stage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two previously separate research threads: multi-turn jailbreak safety (MHJ, SafeDialBench, Haicosystem) which studies distributed harm in text-only dialogues, and tool-using agent safety (SafeArena, OpenAgentSafety, MCP-Safety) which evaluates single-turn harmful tasks with real tools. The key conceptual departure is the Multi-Turn Attack Taxonomy (MAT), which formalizes how single-turn harms decompose into multi-turn sequences along format (Addition vs. Decomposition), method (Mapping/Wrapping vs. Composition/Identity), and target (Data vs. Environment States) dimensions. For defense, ToolShield departs from both external guardrail systems (LlamaFirewall, Llama Guard) that filter inputs without improving the agent's own safety awareness, and RL-based alignment that requires retraining per tool, instead leveraging the agent's own reasoning capabilities for sandboxed self-exploration in a training-free, tool-agnostic pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first benchmark to jointly evaluate multi-turn and tool-use safety, exposing a critical blind spot: current safety training targets semantically harmful text but ignores functional risks that emerge only when tools are chained across turns. The finding that the most capable open-source model (DeepSeek-v3.2) is the least safe (88.57% ASR) while GPT-5.2's advantage stems from dedicated safety alignment rather than capability scaling has broad implications for how the field thinks about the capability-safety relationship. Limitations include coverage of only five tools, black-box evaluation without mechanistic analysis, and reliance on the agent's own (potentially weak) capabilities for experience generation. Open questions include whether the taxonomy generalizes to cybersecurity or long-horizon dozens-of-turns settings, and whether white-box defenses leveraging internal representations could complement the experience-based approach.
