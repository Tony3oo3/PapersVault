## Problem

Deep neural network models of sensory cortex have relied on supervised training with curated, balanced datasets (e.g., LibriSpeech, ImageNet), which are biologically implausible and statistically unlike the noisy, long-tailed, high-contrast environments the brain actually experiences. It remained unclear whether the training data distribution—rather than architecture or objective—was the dominant factor in neural predictivity, and whether models trained on real-world data could generalize to out-of-distribution stimuli never encountered during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260639/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

For audition, 165 two-second natural sounds (158 everyday sounds + 7 foreign speech clips) are resampled to 16 kHz mono and passed through pretrained models, with activations extracted at 14 layers (one Conv1d + 12 Transformer blocks + final hidden state) and globally average-pooled over time to yield fixed-length 768-dim vectors per stimulus. For vision, 80 grayscale object images (~9° visual angle) or 27 color triad images are fed through ResNeXt backbones. These model feature vectors are then mapped to fMRI voxel responses via ridge-regression encoding models (yielding noise-corrected r²), compared via Representational Dissimilarity Matrices (Spearman ρ), or scored on the Brain-Score benchmark.

## Method

The paper conducts a systematic empirical benchmark of 19 audio models (5 unsupervised HuBERT/Wav2Vec2 variants + 14 supervised CNN/Transformer models) and 4 visual ResNeXt models, isolating the effect of training data distribution by holding architecture and parameter count constant. The key unsupervised models (HuBERTspeech, Wav2Vec2speech) are pretrained on WenetSpeech—10,000+ hours of diverse, noisy Mandarin speech from YouTube and podcasts spanning 10 domains (drama, interviews, sports, etc.)—while control models use curated LibriSpeech (960 h) or small FSD50k+AudioSet (153 h). For vision, ResNeXt-50-32x4d models are self-supervised on SAYCam infant head-cam video (415+ hours, 6–32 months) using a temporal classification objective, then evaluated on adult fMRI responses to curated object images. The central finding is that real-world data distribution drives superior brain predictivity over dataset size or architecture, and that these models exhibit striking OOD generalization: a Mandarin-only model predicts English-driven auditory cortex responses, and an infant-video model predicts adult visual cortex responses to ImageNet-style photographs, matching or exceeding in-distribution DINO baselines.

## Results

On the NH2015 auditory fMRI dataset, HuBERTspeech (unsupervised, WenetSpeech) achieves r² = 0.773, outperforming the previous supervised SOTA (CochResNet50-MultiTask, r² = 0.729) by 6%, while Wav2Vec2speech reaches r² = 0.743 (+1.9%). On the B2021 RSA metric, the top two unsupervised real-world models (0.488, 0.473) surpass the previous SOTA (0.461) by 5.9% and 2.6%. In vision, camSAY models (trained on infant head-cam video) achieve Brain-Scores of 0.438–0.393 on the Coggan & Tong dataset, exceeding the previous leader (swin-small, 0.267) by ≥47%, and outperform the best ImageNet-pretrained ResNeXt-101-64x4d (0.21) by 18.6% on the Bracci et al. dataset despite evaluating on OOD stimuli.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HuBERTspeech and Wav2Vec2speech (unsupervised, WenetSpeech) decisively outperform all 14 supervised baselines (CochResNet50-MultiTask, AST, VGGish, S2T, Wav2Vec2FT, CochCNN9 variants) on both NH2015 and B2021, winning by 6% (r²) and 5.9% (RSA) over the previous SOTA. Against same-architecture controls, HuBERTcore (153 h, real-world FSD50K+AudioSet) beats HuBERTLS (960 h, curated LibriSpeech) by 15.5% on B2021 RSA, demonstrating that data distribution matters more than dataset size. Fine-tuning Wav2Vec2 on LibriSpeech (Wav2Vec2FT) yields no improvement or even degrades performance relative to the pretrained-only version, confirming that supervised fine-tuning is counterproductive for brain prediction. In vision, camSAY-ResNeXt-50-32x4d (0.249–0.259 Brain-Score) outperforms the same architecture pretrained on ImageNet (0.169) by 47–53%, and even larger ImageNet models (ResNeXt-101-64x4d, 0.21) by 18.6%, isolating data distribution as the driving factor. Remarkably, camSAY models evaluated on OOD stimuli (curated object images) achieve Brain-Scores (0.249–0.259) comparable to DINOv2/DINOv3 models (0.251–0.262) evaluated on their in-distribution web images, despite using an older architecture and far less data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Tuckute et al. (2023) benchmark of 19 audio models for auditory cortex prediction by adding five unsupervised models (HuBERT and Wav2Vec2 variants) trained on real-world data, and extends the Orhan et al. (2020) Baby Vision models by evaluating them against ImageNet-pretrained and DINO baselines on Brain-Score. Conceptually, it shifts the explanatory focus from architecture and learning objective—dominant themes in prior DNN-brain alignment work—to training data distribution as the primary driver of neural predictivity. It departs from prior findings (Li et al. 2023; Zhuang et al. 2021) that unsupervised models could at best close the gap with supervised ones, and from the assumption that curated, domain-matched data (LibriSpeech, ImageNet) is optimal for brain modeling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that real-world data distribution outweighs architecture, parameter count, and dataset size in explaining brain responses provides a concrete, actionable principle for neuroAI model design: train on ecologically valid, noisy, long-tailed data rather than balanced curated corpora. The cross-language OOD generalization (Mandarin-trained model predicting English brain responses) and cross-domain visual generalization (infant egocentric video predicting adult object-image responses) suggest that unsupervised learning on naturalistic statistics captures invariant sensory representations analogous to biological learning. Key limitations include reliance on predictive/representational alignment metrics that do not establish mechanistic correspondence, evaluation on only two auditory fMRI datasets, and the open question of whether matching training language to the listener's native language would further improve predictivity.
