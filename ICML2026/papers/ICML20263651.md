## Problem

Conditional flow matching models can generate samples for unseen conditions, but the theoretical characterization of their out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization remains incomplete. Standard Lipschitz-based analyses are geometrically incompatible with the snowflake-universal structure of Wasserstein space, making them vacuous for complex distributions. This paper asks: what geometric factors determine whether a conditional flow model generalizes to unseen conditions, and can OOD error be decomposed into interpretable, actionable components?

## Input / Output

The method takes a condition variable (discrete class label, continuous attribute, or scalar drift parameter) and Gaussian noise as input, producing a generated sample in the data space (28×28 grayscale for MNIST, 32×32 RGB for CIFAR-10, 64×64 RGB for CelebA, or 2D vectors for synthetic data). The key diagnostic output is a three-term OOD error decomposition—Interpolation Sparsity, Geometric Distortion, and In-Distribution Fit—computed via latent-space Wasserstein distances and local Jacobian sensitivity probes, rather than a single scalar loss. For high-dimensional images, all transport distances are computed in the VAE latent space as a proxy for the true pixel-space Wasserstein geometry.

## Method

The paper formulates conditional generation as a learned map f: C → (P₂(R^d), W₂) from a condition manifold to the Wasserstein space, and derives a generalization bound for unseen conditions by treating the target distribution as a Wasserstein barycenter of nearby training conditions. Because P₂ is snowflake-universal and admits no bi-Lipschitz embedding from Euclidean condition spaces, the analysis replaces standard Lipschitz assumptions with a coarse embedding (quasi-isometric with additive slack), which is shown to be a mathematical necessity rather than a convenience. Applying Grönwall's inequality to the continuity equation and decomposing the instantaneous velocity error via a triangle inequality over barycentric weights yields the bound W₂(ŝ, μ_c̄) ≤ K·ΣwᵢW₂(μ_cᵢ, μ_c̄) + δ + C·ε_train, where the three terms correspond to interpolation sparsity (data coverage), geometric distortion (irreducible snowflake cost), and in-distribution fit (model approximation error). The framework is validated on a controlled linear-drift manifold where exact W₂ distances are computable, and then applied as a diagnostic tool on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and CelebA to identify whether OOD failure is topological (sparsity-dominated) or geometric (distortion-dominated).

## Results

On the controlled Linear Drift manifold (two moons in R², trained on c∈[0,4], extrapolated to c∈(4,7]), the Robust model (spectral normalization + VAE bottleneck, Fourier scale s=1.0) strictly respects the theoretical upper bound with monotonic linear error growth, while the Baseline (high-frequency Fourier, s=15.0) oscillates and frequently exceeds the bound. The empirical expansion constant K_emp ≈ 10 confirms the predicted linear scaling of OOD error with geometric drift. On CelebA, the geometric distortion proxy δ̂ correlates with the isolated geometric residual at R² = 0.89, validating the diagnostic decomposition. Dataset-specific failure modes are identified: MNIST exhibits purely topological failure (T₂≈0, high T₁), CIFAR-10 shows coupled topological-geometric failure, and CelebA is dominated by geometric distortion (high T₂, low T₁).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead contrasts two configurations of the same conditional flow matching model on the controlled drift experiment. The Baseline uses high-frequency Fourier condition embeddings (scale s=15.0) without regularization, simulating overfitting and high geometric sensitivity; the Robust model applies spectral normalization to all linear layers, a VAE bottleneck (β=0.01) on the condition encoder, and low-scale Fourier features (s=1.0). The Robust model's error tracks the irreducible intrinsic data drift (Term 1) with minimal geometric distortion (Term 2), whereas the Baseline's error is dominated by geometric distortion and violates the theoretical bound. This demonstrates that the performance gap is driven entirely by stabilizing the conditional map f: C→P₂ (reducing L_c), not by altering the data distribution. On high-dimensional image datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10, CelebA), the framework serves as a diagnostic rather than a competitive benchmark, identifying whether failure is topological (sparsity) or geometric (distortion) without proposing a single superior architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the conditional flow matching framework (Lipman et al., 2023) and the Otto Riemannian structure on Wasserstein space (Otto, 2001), but departs from the dominant line of work that modifies training objectives to enforce optimal-transport structure (Pooladian et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2024). Instead, it accepts the trained vector field as given and provides a post-hoc geometric diagnostic. The key theoretical departure is replacing the standard bi-Lipschitz continuity assumption on the conditional map c ↦ μ_c with a coarse embedding (Pritchard & Weighill, 2024), motivated by the snowflake universality of P₂ (Andoni et al., 2018) which renders bi-Lipschitz embeddings mathematically impossible. The Wasserstein barycenter (McCann, 1997) serves as the geometric reference for unseen conditions, connecting this work to the broader literature on Fréchet regression in Wasserstein space (Bigot & Klein, 2018; Chen et al., 2023) while explicitly analyzing regimes where those methods' smoothness assumptions break down.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three-term decomposition (Interpolation Sparsity, Geometric Distortion, In-Distribution Fit) provides practitioners with an actionable diagnostic: it identifies whether OOD failure stems from insufficient data coverage, intrinsic geometric instability of the learned map, or underfitting—each pointing to a different intervention. However, the framework has notable limitations: the barycentric reference may misrepresent truly discontinuous or strongly multimodal conditionals, high-dimensional diagnostics rely on latent-space proxies whose validity depends on encoder quality, and the absolute bound constants are loose. Open questions include whether the coarse embedding assumption holds for p ≤ 2 Wasserstein spaces (currently unresolved in the metric geometry literature), whether the latent-space proxy approach transfers to architectures without explicit VAE encoders, and whether the linear-drift prediction for extrapolation error holds beyond the controlled 2D setting validated here.
