## Problem

Existing retrieval-augmented agents treat external memory as a static or append-only corpus, so noise, redundancy, and obsolete entries accumulate over time, causing 'memory saturation' where more experience actually degrades performance. No prior method provides a principled optimization procedure for non-parametric memory under an explicit capacity budget, leaving a gap between static RAG and parametric fine-tuning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262459/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the system takes a text query (e.g., a MATH problem, HumanEval prompt, or ToolBench task) and retrieves a small set of distilled natural-language rules (k ≤ 16) from the experience library via a two-stage hybrid retriever (metadata hard-filtering + dense embedding recall), then conditions a frozen LLM on those rules to produce a reasoning trajectory and final answer. The 'learned' artifact is the library itself: a compact set of ~27–30 condition→strategy strings (down from hundreds of raw experiences), which is the object being optimized rather than any model weights.

## Method

TSGD casts the experience library Φ as a discrete, non-differentiable parameter and performs 'textual gradient descent' via a six-agent pipeline (initializer, retrieval, solver, validator, optimizer, regularizer). On each failure, the optimizer agent reflects on the error and emits a textual gradient—a discrete ADD, EDIT, or DELETE operation with associated content—while a dual-verification step (local repair check + global non-regression check on a content-aware validation subset) gates acceptance to prevent overfitting to individual samples. A periodic regularizer enforces the capacity budget B through hierarchical clustering, semantic merging, and global pruning, compressing the library from hundreds of entries to ~27–30 high-utility rules. The paper provides a Rademacher-complexity generalization bound scaling as √(B log|V*|/m), a monotonic-improvement convergence proof for the dual-verified update sequence, and an idealized O(K log K) sample-complexity model for compositional skill acquisition, grounding the design in an Information Bottleneck view where the optimizer maximizes I(Y;Φ) and the regularizer minimizes I(Q;Φ).

## Results

On MATH Level 5 (in-distribution, Mean@32), TSGD achieves 68.0% overall accuracy with Grok-4.1, a +18.7 pp improvement over the zero-shot LLM baseline (49.30%) and +15 pp over TF-GRPO (53.0%). On out-of-distribution AIME 2024, TSGD reaches 55.0% versus 41.46% for zero-shot and 43.0% for TF-GRPO. With GPT-4o mini, TSGD similarly lifts MATH Level 5 from 54.60% to 68.0% and AIME 2024 from 15.21% to 30.0%. The optimized library compresses to only 27 entries (from 533 initial seeds), a ~20× reduction, while maintaining or improving accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against RAG (Lewis et al., 2020), TSGD wins decisively: 68.0% vs. 50.0% on MATH Level 5 overall (+18 pp) and 55.0% vs. 39.0% on AIME 2024 (+16 pp) with Grok-4.1, while also using a 27-entry library versus RAG's 177-entry corpus. Against TF-GRPO (Cai et al., 2025a), TSGD leads by 15 pp on MATH Level 5 (68.0 vs. 53.0) and 12 pp on AIME 2024 (55.0 vs. 43.0), indicating that discrete experience optimization outperforms training-free policy optimization for this task. Against FLEX (Cai et al., 2025b), TSGD matches Pass@16 on Qwen-2.5-72B (79.2 vs. 79.2) and slightly exceeds it on Llama-3.1-70B (50.0 vs. 49.1), but does so with a 6.7× smaller library (27 vs. 180 entries on Qwen; 22 vs. 222 on Llama), highlighting the compression advantage of the capacity-budgeted optimization. Across all three baselines, TSGD's gains are most pronounced on Geometry (+15.8 pp over zero-shot) and Intermediate Algebra (+18.3 pp), where domain-specific heuristics are most critical.

## Relation to Prior Work

TSGD builds directly on the RAG paradigm (Lewis et al., 2020) for the retrieval mechanism and on textual-gradient optimizers like TextGrad (Yuksekgonul et al., 2025) for the idea of using LLM-generated feedback as a surrogate gradient, but departs from both by treating a structured, capacity-bounded experience library as the optimization variable rather than a static corpus or a prompt. It also extends the editable-memory line (AgentKB, ReasoningBank, FLEX) by adding formal dual verification (local repair + global non-regression) and periodic regularization, plus the first Rademacher-based generalization bound and monotonic-convergence guarantee for discrete memory optimization. The multi-agent decomposition (initializer, retriever, solver, optimizer, validator, regularizer) draws on Reflexion-style self-correction but subordinates it to an explicit acceptance criterion that prior systems lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a 20× compression of the experience library (533→27 entries) can *improve* accuracy, directly addressing the 'memory saturation' failure mode that plagues append-only RAG and agent-memory systems in sustained deployment. The theoretical contributions (generalization bound scaling as √(B/m), monotonic convergence under dual verification) give the first formal justification for why capacity-bounded memory optimization should generalize, which prior heuristic systems could not provide. Key limitations include reliance on proprietary backbones for headline results (open-source gains are smaller), an idealized sample-complexity model, and evaluation concentrated on mathematical reasoning; open questions include whether the framework transfers to truly non-stationary or multi-modal settings and whether the dual-verification overhead remains acceptable at scale.
