## Problem

As make-on-demand chemistry expands accessible molecular libraries to billions or trillions of compounds, surrogate inference over the full candidate set becomes a computational bottleneck comparable to the expensive oracle evaluations themselves, violating the core assumption of standard Bayesian optimization that inference cost is negligible. This paper addresses how to perform surrogate-guided candidate selection at ultra-large library scale without requiring exhaustive scoring of every molecule at each iteration.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a finite molecular library (up to ~10^8 compounds in scaling experiments), precomputed molecular feature embeddings (T5Chem language-model embeddings or physicochemical descriptors), an expensive oracle (docking scores), and a user-chosen number of partitions K. At each of T=20 sequential rounds, it outputs a batch of B=5,000 candidates for oracle evaluation, with the surrogate retrained online on all accumulated observations. The key operational constraint is that surrogate inference per round is restricted to the single bandit-selected partition (~N/K molecules) rather than the full library, yielding approximately K-fold fewer inferences than standard BO while keeping batch size and round count identical across methods.

## Method

BOBA decomposes the library into K persistent subspaces via k-means clustering on molecular embeddings, then treats each subspace as an arm in a multi-armed bandit. At each round, UCB1 selects a partition based on empirical mean reward (average docking score of the last evaluated batch) plus an optimism bonus for under-sampled arms; within the selected partition, a Bayesian neural network surrogate with a last-layer Laplace approximation computes UCB acquisition scores, and the top-B candidates are selected for expensive evaluation. The paper demonstrates that optimism-under-uncertainty allocation (UCB1) is essential—epsilon-greedy and softmax degrade sharply as K grows—and that chemically structured partitions (k-means on T5Chem embeddings) substantially outperform random or unrefined-descriptor partitions. A cost-regret analysis identifies the optimal partition count as K* ∝ N^(2/3) T^(1/3), balancing the inference cost term NT/K against the bandit regret term √(KT), and scaling experiments up to 10^8 molecules confirm that BOBA's relative performance versus full-library BO improves as library size grows.

## Results

On the ENAMINE-5M library docked against CKB, BOBA with UCB1 recovers nearly the same number of top-100 molecules as full-library BO while performing approximately K-fold fewer surrogate inferences (e.g., ~5× fewer at K=5, ~100× fewer at K=100). In the scaling experiment on ZINC-AmpC, BOBA's normalized AUC (top-1000 retrieval curve, relative to full-library BO) does not deteriorate as library size grows from ~10⁵ to ~10⁸ molecules, and for several partition granularities approaches the full-library BO value at the largest scale. UCB1 remains competitive with full-inference BO even at K=100 partitions, whereas ε-greedy and softmax show substantial degradation as K increases. T5Chem-embedding-based partitions consistently outperform unrefined physicochemical descriptor partitions across all values of K.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against full-library BO (Garnett, 2023), BOBA with UCB1 matches or nearly matches the number of top-100 molecules recovered while reducing surrogate inference cost by a factor of K (the number of partitions). At the largest tested scale (~10⁸ molecules on ZINC-AmpC), BOBA's normalized AUC approaches that of full-library BO for moderate K values, confirming that the inference savings come at negligible performance cost. Against the random subsampling baseline (Wang-Henderson et al., 2023), BOBA substantially outperforms across all settings: both methods reduce inference cost to the same degree, but BOBA's persistent K-means partitions combined with UCB1 bandit feedback concentrate evaluations in empirically promising regions, whereas random subsampling repeatedly reallocates computation to arbitrary regions of chemical space. The paper also ablates partition structure (K-means vs. random assignment) and feature space (T5Chem vs. physicochemical descriptors), finding that structured clustering in T5Chem embedding space is essential for competitive performance, with random partitions causing substantial degradation even under identical inference budgets.

## Relation to Prior Work

BOBA combines two well-established tools—Bayesian optimization (Garnett, 2023) for within-partition candidate selection and UCB1 multi-armed bandits (Auer et al., 2002) for cross-partition allocation—into a hierarchical framework that explicitly treats surrogate inference cost as a first-class constraint alongside evaluation cost. This departs from standard active-learning/BO pipelines, which assume inference over the full library is negligible, and from synthon-based hierarchical search methods (Klarich et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025) that rely on a fixed combinatorial precursor hierarchy rather than learned molecular-structure-aware partitioning. The key conceptual contribution is reframing ultra-large-library screening as a dual-constraint problem where the number of partitions K serves as a tunable knob between inference savings and bandit allocation difficulty.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck that will only intensify as make-on-demand libraries grow to trillions of compounds: even a single forward pass of a surrogate over the full library becomes infeasible. The demonstrated tunable tradeoff (K* ∝ N^{2/3} T^{1/3}) and the empirical finding that BOBA's relative performance does not degrade—and in some cases approaches full-library BO—at 10^8 molecules provide a concrete path to scaling virtual screening. Key limitations include the requirement for explicit library enumeration and full-library featurization/clustering (a one-time but non-trivial cost), the restriction to stationary partition rewards, and the fact that the synthon-space extension (which would eliminate the enumeration requirement) is left to future work. Open questions include whether non-stationary (rotting) bandits improve performance as top candidates are depleted, and whether task-specific fine-tuned representations can further close the gap to full-library BO.
