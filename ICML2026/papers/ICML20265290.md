## Problem

LLM-based end-to-end GPU kernel generation faces a fundamental tension: pure top-down decomposition guarantees correctness but fragments critical cross-operator fusion opportunities, while pure bottom-up (flat) approaches expose maximal fusion but suffer from exponential search space, context overflow, and prohibitive sampling costs. No existing framework simultaneously achieves scalable exploration and peak performance for full-model tensor graph optimization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265290/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

LEGO takes a PyTorch model's source code (nn.Module hierarchy) as input, using the module tree as a structural prior for decomposition rather than flattening the graph. It produces an optimized execution plan structured as a recursive AND-OR FusionTree, where each OR node retains the minimal-latency plan among competing strategies (baseline, direct codegen, split, full-fusion, partial-fusion), and terminal AND nodes contain concrete Triton kernel source or vendor library calls. Correctness is enforced at three levels: static type checking, per-submodule numerical validation (atol=1e-2, rtol=1e-2), and end-to-end token-level verification for deployment-critical models.

## Method

LEGO formulates tensor graph optimization as a search over a recursive AND-OR FusionTree, synergizing two complementary flows: Top-Down Construction decomposes the graph along nn.Module boundaries (exploiting structural isomorphism as a strong prior to prune the partitioning space from O(2^N) to a tractable subspace), while Bottom-Up Mutation speculatively fuses verified sub-plans to recover cross-boundary data locality. Each OR node executes a Phased Exploration Protocol—benchmarking a verified baseline, attempting direct Triton codegen, performing source-guided recursive split, then speculatively fusing children via full-fusion or LLM-proposed partial-fusion—retaining only the minimal-latency plan. Sibling sub-regions are explored in parallel by independent agents, decoupling exploration time from model size (bounded by tree depth, max 4). A global memoization table keyed by isomorphism signatures (operator topology, shapes, dtypes) prunes redundant exploration across structurally identical blocks, and a serialized GPU profiling service ensures interference-free latency measurements for the Bellman-style cost propagation.

## Results

LEGO achieves 2.18×–13.48× end-to-end speedups over PyTorch Eager on RTX 6000 Ada and 2.84×–9.26× on H100 across five diverse architectures (ResNet18, Qwen3-8B, SD3-MMDiT, Mamba2-1.3B, mHC-MoE). The largest gains appear on emerging operators: mHC-MoE reaches 13.48× (RTX 6000 Ada) and 9.26× (H100), while SD3-MMDiT reaches 4.09× and 6.13× respectively. Compared to a monolithic LLM baseline (pass@10 × feedback@10), LEGO reduces exploration time by up to 2.47× and token consumption by up to 7.02×.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LEGO outperforms TorchInductor (both Default and Max-Autotune) on all five workloads across both GPUs, with the largest margins on mHC-MoE (13.48× vs. 0.91× Max-AT on RTX 6000 Ada) and SD3-MMDiT (4.09× vs. 1.84×). Among the three open-source LLM agents, LEGO is the only system that produces valid, faster kernels on all five workloads; KernelAgent (KernelFalcon) fails on 3–4 of 5 workloads and regresses below Eager where it does succeed, CudaForge achieves a real speedup only on ResNet18 (1.75× on RTX 6000 Ada, 1.42× on H100), and AKG fails on most workloads. Against the manually tuned TileLang DSL compiler, LEGO is within 0.1–0.5× on four of five workloads (e.g., 13.48× vs. 13.01× on mHC-MoE) but trails slightly on Qwen3-8B (2.21× vs. 2.25×) due to TileLang's hand-tuned GQA attention. In the HuggingFace Transformers integration, LEGO-optimized Qwen3-8B achieves 15–21% lower time-to-first-token than vLLM across all prompt lengths (e.g., 524 ms vs. 618 ms at P=4096), and LEGO-synthesized attention kernels reach near-parity with FlashAttention-2 and FlashInfer at batch sizes ≥ 8 within the vLLM engine.

## Relation to Prior Work

LEGO carves new ground at the intersection of three research threads: (1) tensor compilers (TorchInductor, XLA) that flatten graphs and apply heuristic fusion patterns, (2) LLM-driven kernel synthesis systems (KernelFalcon, CudaForge, AKG) that operate on single operators or small subgraphs, and (3) structure-aware graph search (TASO, DyPARS) that exploits architectural regularity. Rather than building on a single prior system, LEGO repurposes the AND-OR tree formalism from classical AI planning and the Bellman optimality principle from dynamic programming, instantiating them with LLM-parameterized actions (Split, Generate, Fuse) and source-code nn.Module boundaries as a structural prior. The key departure from prior LLM-based agents is the explicit bi-directional search: top-down decomposition for correctness and parallelism, coupled with bottom-up speculative fusion to recover cross-boundary locality that strict hierarchical partitioning would sever.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LEGO addresses a practical bottleneck—end-to-end GPU kernel optimization for novel architectures where no vendor kernel exists—by showing that LLM-driven synthesis can match manually-engineered TileLang kernels while reducing exploration cost by up to 7× in tokens and 2.47× in wall-clock time. The ablation revealing that removing bottom-up fusion collapses mHC-MoE speedup from 11.29× to 1.20× validates the core design hypothesis that structural decomposition alone is insufficient for peak performance. Key limitations include heavy dependence on frontier-model reasoning quality (DeepSeek-V3 fails on complex workloads), single-GPU evaluation, and a sampling budget of K=1 for partial fusion that may under-explore the mutation space. Open questions include heterogeneous model orchestration for cost reduction, multi-stage co-optimization beyond single-pass recomposition, and extension to multi-GPU or non-NVIDIA accelerators.
