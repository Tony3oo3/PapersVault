## Problem

Aligning LLMs to multiple, potentially conflicting reward functions is fundamentally difficult: a single model trained on a weighted average of rewards produces mediocre outputs across all criteria, losing the diversity needed for inference-time alignment methods like best-of-N to select high-reward responses. Inference-time alignment solves this by applying different reward functions at test time, but is computationally expensive (requiring multiple forward passes) and performs poorly if the base model was not trained with inference-time alignment in mind. The paper asks: can we train a single base model that is specifically optimized to be efficiently adapted to diverse preferences via inference-time alignment under a limited computational budget?

## Input / Output

The method takes a context/prompt, a set of m reward functions {r_i}, and a corresponding set of inference-time alignment algorithms {T_i} (e.g., BoN with N samples or soft BoN with temperature τ), along with an aggregation function g (weighted sum, min, or smooth min) combining the per-task objectives. Training samples M=8 responses per context from the current policy to estimate the functional derivative. The output is a meta-aligned base model (policy π) that, when combined with any of the inference-time alignment algorithms at test time, produces responses optimized for the corresponding reward criterion — effectively pushing the Pareto frontier forward compared to standard alignment.

## Method

IAMA formulates the two-stage alignment (train base model, then apply inference-time alignment T_i) as a non-linear optimization problem in the space of probability measures, where the reward term R[π] = E_{y~T_i[π]}[r_i(y)] is non-linear in π due to the measure transformation T_i, unlike the standard linear expected reward. To solve this, the paper proposes non-linear GRPO: at each iteration, it computes the first-order variation (functional derivative) ∂R/∂π[π_t](y) of the non-linear objective, approximates it empirically from M sampled responses, and uses it as a drop-in modified reward in a standard GRPO update — making the implementation a minimal change to existing GRPO code. For BoN, the functional derivative is -∫_{r(y)}^{r_max} N·(C[π](r))^{N-1} dr (computable in O(M log M) via sorting); for soft BoN, it involves exponential-tilting terms computable in O(M). The paper proves that BoN-type objectives are concave and L_f·r_max-smooth relative to KL divergence in the measure space, yielding linear (exponential) convergence of non-linear GRPO to the unique optimum, with a dimension-independent O(1/M) approximation error bound via the DKW inequality.

## Results

On the HH-RLHF helpfulness/harmlessness task (Alpaca 7B, BoN N=4, target KL=0.1), IAMA via non-linear GRPO pushes the Pareto frontier forward compared to standard GRPO alignment when BoN sampling is applied at inference time, while without BoN sampling the two approaches perform comparably. On the length reward task (Mistral 7B, UltraFeedback), the IAMA model produces a bimodal response-length distribution covering both short (~50 tokens) and long (~150 tokens) targets, whereas the standard GRPO baseline yields only a unimodal distribution concentrated around ~100 tokens. The method generalizes to BoN with N=8 and N=16 (exceeding the M=8 training samples), with stronger polarization as N increases. On the Mistral 7B HH-RLHF experiment, IAMA similarly advances the Pareto frontier over the standard GRPO baseline with BoN(N=4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares IAMA (non-linear GRPO) against two baselines. Against standard GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), IAMA wins on the Pareto frontier for helpfulness vs. harmlessness when BoN(N=4) or BoN(N=8) sampling is used at inference time, producing diverse responses that allow the inference-time selector to find high-reward outputs for each criterion; however, without BoN sampling, IAMA does not outperform standard GRPO since the latter directly optimizes the weighted sum of expected rewards. Against InfAlign (Balashankar et al., 2025), IAMA is compared in the same HH-RLHF setting (Appendix J.4); InfAlign is restricted to a single reward function, BoN-type algorithms, and win-rate maximization, whereas IAMA handles multiple rewards and general inference-time alignment algorithms, and the paper reports that IAMA achieves superior Pareto frontier coverage. The key practical advantage is that IAMA requires only a single base model (no model soup or multiple checkpoints) while enabling per-preference alignment at inference time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) to handle non-linear objectives in the space of probability measures by replacing the reward function with a functional derivative, and generalizes InfAlign (Balashankar et al., 2025) from a single-reward, BoN-only, win-rate setting to multiple rewards and general inference-time alignment algorithms. Conceptually, IAMA is positioned as a generalization of MAML (Finn et al., 2017) where 'adaptation' occurs via inference-time alignment functionals in measure space rather than gradient steps in parameter space. The optimization framework draws on mirror descent with KL divergence as Bregman divergence in measure spaces (Aubin-Frankowski et al., 2022), but the key departure is handling the non-linearity introduced by inference-time alignment operators, which standard TRPO/PPO/GRPO cannot address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, implementable framework for multi-objective LLM alignment that preserves response diversity for inference-time selection, addressing a practical limitation of naive weighted-sum RLHF that collapses to mediocre outputs. The convexity and linear convergence guarantees for BoN-type objectives give theoretical grounding that is absent in prior inference-aware alignment work. Limitations include experiments restricted to 7B-scale models, convexity guarantees only for BoN-type (not soft BoN or self-consistency) objectives, and the need for empirical approximation of functional derivatives whose error bounds, while dimension-independent, still depend on sample count M. Open questions include how the approach scales to larger models, whether the framework extends cleanly to non-concave objectives like Worst-of-N, and how it interacts with more complex inference-time alignment procedures beyond reward-based selection.
