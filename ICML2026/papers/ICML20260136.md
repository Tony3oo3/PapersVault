## Problem

Despite being trained identically, the deterministic DDIM sampler produces significantly more hallucinations (mode interpolations) than the stochastic DDPM sampler, yet no rigorous theoretical characterization of the reverse dynamics mechanism behind this gap existed. Prior work attributed mode interpolation to score smoothing by neural networks or provided only empirical observations, leaving the fundamental question of why removing the noise term in the reverse process causes hallucinations unanswered.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260136/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The theoretical analysis takes an N-mode Gaussian mixture as the target distribution and studies the exact reverse PF ODE (DDIM) and VP SDE (DDPM) starting from standard Gaussian noise at time T, producing a terminal sample at time 0 whose location relative to the mode line segments determines whether it is a hallucination. Empirically, they use 25-mode Gaussian mixtures in 2D (extended to 64D) and a 3-triangle 64×64 image dataset, classifying a sample as a hallucination if it lies within 5σ of a line segment joining two modes but more than 5σ from any mode center.

## Method

The core mechanism is a two-phase convergence: after a critical time τ, trajectories rapidly collapse onto the ε-tube around the nearest i,j-mode segment (Theorem 4.2), after which the dynamics reduce to 1D parallel motion along that segment. The midpoint of the segment is a hyperbolic unstable equilibrium, but the repulsion rate is too slow to reach either mode within the remaining time budget, so DDIM trajectories become trapped and produce mode interpolations (Prop. 4.7). In contrast, DDPM's noise term provides a stochastic escape: by reducing the problem to a 1D SDE along the bisector normal and combining Dubins-Schwarz time-change with optional stopping, they prove the probability of terminating in the midpoint neighborhood is exponentially small (Prop. 5.1). They further rule out DDIM step-skipping as the cause of the gap and show that appending a few DDPM steps to a DDIM trajectory significantly reduces hallucination rate, offering a practical hybrid sampler.

## Results

The paper proves two core theoretical results: (1) after a critical time τ, DDIM trajectories converge to the ε-tube around the nearest i,j-mode segment and become trapped in a ϑ-neighborhood of the midpoint, yielding mode interpolation hallucinations (Prop. 4.7); and (2) DDPM's noise term makes the terminal midpoint event exponentially unlikely, with probability bounded by exp(−π²∫η/16(1+∫K)²ϑ²) + 2exp(−λ_rep·ϑ²/2η_max) (Prop. 5.1). Empirically, on a 25-mode 2D Gaussian mixture with 100,000 samples, DDPM exhibits a significantly lower mode interpolation hallucination rate than DDIM, and this gap persists across all DDIM step counts (from 50 to 1000 steps), ruling out discretization error as the cause. Adding just a few DDPM steps after DDIM reaches the midpoint neighborhood (τ₃=9) measurably reduces DDIM's hallucination rate, validating the theoretical escape mechanism.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two diffusion sampling strategies trained on identical models: DDIM (deterministic PF ODE, Eq. 3) and DDPM (stochastic VP SDE, Eq. 2). DDPM consistently achieves a lower mode interpolation hallucination rate than DDIM across all experimental settings, including the 25-mode Gaussian mixture (Figures 1a, 3, 5) and the 3-triangle image dataset (Figure E.10). Critically, the hallucination rate gap is not explained by DDIM's step-skipping: even when both samplers use the same number of discretization steps, DDIM still hallucinates more (Figure 3). The gap is also not attributable to score approximation, since the theoretical results hold under the exact score function. The paper further shows that the gap is structural rather than a matter of step count: the deterministic dynamics of DDIM create a hyperbolic saddle at the midpoint that traps trajectories, while DDPM's stochastic noise provides an escape mechanism that is robust even under score error (Prop. C.6). On the 3-triangle counting task, DDIM also produces more counting hallucinations than DDPM, consistent with the mode interpolation mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DDPM (Ho et al., 2020) and DDIM (Song et al., 2021a) samplers within the unified score-based generative modeling framework (Song et al., 2021b), and adopts the mode-interpolation definition of hallucination from Aithal et al. (2024). It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) whereas Aithal et al. attributed mode interpolation to neural-network score smoothing, this paper proves the mechanism arises even under the exact score, isolating the sampler's dynamics as the root cause; and (2) while recent ODE-vs-SDE comparisons (Deveney et al., 2025; Beyler & Bach, 2025) study statistical distances between sampling distributions, this paper provides the first trajectory-level geometric analysis of where and why hallucinations arise in the reverse process. The paper positions itself as the first to mathematically characterize the hallucination mechanism difference between DDIM and DDPM.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean mechanistic explanation for a well-known empirical observation (DDIM hallucinates more than DDPM) and yields an immediately actionable recommendation: adding a few stochastic DDPM steps at the end of a DDIM schedule significantly reduces hallucination rate while preserving most of DDIM's speed advantage. Limitations include the restriction to well-separated Gaussian mixtures in the main theoretical analysis and the 2D primary experiments, though higher-dimensional and image experiments (3-triangle dataset) provide supporting evidence. Open questions include extending the analysis to latent diffusion for images, characterizing the full effect of score estimation error on the trapping mechanism, and designing adaptive samplers that detect when trajectories enter the midpoint neighborhood and inject noise accordingly.
