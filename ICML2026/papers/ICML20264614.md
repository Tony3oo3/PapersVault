## Problem

Existing anchor-video-based camera control methods render training anchors from estimated 3D point clouds along camera trajectories, but inaccuracies in point cloud and pose estimation produce misaligned anchors that force the model to simultaneously repair visible regions and inpaint invisible ones. This misalignment necessitates large-scale multi-view datasets, extensive backbone fine-tuning, and high compute budgets, while limiting generalization to dynamic in-the-wild videos.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264614/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

For I2V, the method takes a single RGB image, a user-specified camera trajectory, and a text prompt, producing a 49-frame video at 480×720 with the desired camera motion. For V2V, it takes an input video and a target trajectory, using DepthCrafter to build dynamic 4D point clouds for anchor rendering. At test time, anchor videos are constructed via standard point-cloud rendering (DAv2 depth + unprojection), with optional GroundedSAM-based masking of dynamic objects to allow free motion in those regions while the background drives camera control.

## Method

EPiC constructs training anchor videos by masking source frames based on first-frame visibility estimated via RAFT optical flow, preserving only pixels traceable to the first frame and masking newly revealed regions—this yields perfectly aligned anchors without any camera pose or point cloud estimation. A lightweight Anchor-ControlNet (26M parameters, <1% of the 5B backbone) with a reduced hidden dimension of 256 is injected into the first 8 DiT layers of the frozen CogVideoX-5B-I2V model, taking the anchor video latent concatenated with the noisy latent as input. A visibility-aware output masking mechanism multiplies the ControlNet output by a downsampled binary visibility mask before adding it to the base model's features, so the ControlNet only copies visible content while the frozen base model synthesizes occluded regions. Synthetic dashed-ray artifacts are injected into training anchors to bridge the gap with flying-pixel artifacts present in real point-cloud renderings at test time. The entire system trains on just 5K in-the-wild videos for 500 steps (~15 GPU hours) with the backbone frozen, yet generalizes zero-shot to V2V camera control.

## Results

On RealEstate10K, EPiC achieves the best camera control accuracy with Rotation Error 0.40±0.11, Translation Error 0.86±0.18, and CamMC 1.17±0.23, while also attaining the highest total VBench quality score of 82.63. On MiraData, EPiC similarly leads with RotErr 0.66±0.22, TransErr 1.78±0.67, and CamMC 2.10±0.60, and the best total quality score of 82.89. For zero-shot V2V on Kubric-4D, EPiC (trained only on I2V data) achieves PSNR 19.65 and SSIM 0.60, competitive with methods specifically trained for V2V. Notably, EPiC achieves these results with only 26M trainable parameters, 5K training videos, and 500 training steps—over an order of magnitude less than all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EPiC outperforms all five I2V baselines on every camera control metric (RotErr, TransErr, CamMC) on both RE10K and MIRA, with the largest margins over CameraCtrl (e.g., 0.40 vs. 1.12 RotErr on RE10K) and AC3D (0.40 vs. 0.86). Against the closest competitor Gen3C, EPiC reduces RotErr from 0.45 to 0.40 on RE10K and from 0.81 to 0.66 on MIRA, while also showing lower variance across seeds. ViewCrafter and FloVD, which use anchor-video and flow-map conditioning respectively, trail EPiC by 0.10–0.36 in RotErr on RE10K. For V2V on Kubric-4D, EPiC (PSNR 19.65, SSIM 0.60) is within 0.07 PSNR of GCD (19.72) and TrajectoryCrafter (19.61) despite being trained exclusively on I2V data, demonstrating strong zero-shot transfer. In qualitative comparisons, EPiC avoids the content inconsistencies of ViewCrafter, the messy invisible-region generation of Gen3C, and the trajectory-following failures of AC3D and FloVD, while also resisting erroneous anchor-video signals that mislead TrajectoryCrafter and Gen3C in V2V.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the anchor-video-based camera control paradigm established by ViewCrafter, Gen3C, and TrajectoryCrafter, which render anchor videos from estimated 3D point clouds to guide diffusion models. It departs from this lineage in two fundamental ways: (1) it replaces point-cloud rendering with visibility-based masking of source videos (using optical flow) to construct perfectly aligned training anchors without any camera pose or depth estimation, and (2) it replaces full backbone fine-tuning with a 26M-parameter Anchor-ControlNet that applies visibility-aware output masking, delegating invisible-region synthesis entirely to the frozen base model. The approach also contrasts with camera-parameter-based methods (CameraCtrl, AC3D) and flow-based methods (FloVD) by providing explicit 3D geometric guidance while remaining lightweight.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dramatic efficiency gain—5K videos, 500 training steps, 26M parameters, and 15 GPU hours versus 632K videos, 150K steps, and billions of parameters in prior anchor-video methods—makes precise camera control accessible to much smaller research groups and enables rapid iteration. The conceptual reframing that the learning task reduces to 'copying visible regions' rather than 'repairing misaligned 3D content' provides a clear design principle that could generalize to other structured-conditioning problems in video generation. Key limitations remain: test-time inference still depends on depth estimation quality for point-cloud rendering, and training-time visibility masks can degrade under fast motion or heavy occlusion. Open questions include whether the approach extends to longer videos, more complex multi-object dynamics, or fully 4D-consistent generation without per-frame depth estimation.
