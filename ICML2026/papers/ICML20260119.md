## Problem

In LLM RL post-training, the dominant paradigm discards each generated rollout after a single gradient update, treating fresh on-policy data as essential for performance. This 'generate-then-discard' approach makes inference compute the dominant cost (often >80% of GPU hours), yet experience replay—a foundational technique in classical RL for sample efficiency—has been largely unexplored in this setting due to the assumption that off-policy staleness outweighs any computational savings.

## Input / Output

The method takes a base LLM (Qwen2.5-7B or Qwen3-0.6B), training prompts from OpenR1-Math-220k, and rollouts generated by inference workers, and produces a fine-tuned LLM evaluated on MATH or OpenR1-Math-220k. The key operational detail is that rollouts are stored in a FIFO replay buffer of size N and sampled uniformly (with replacement) by trainer GPUs across multiple gradient steps rather than being consumed once. The primary evaluation metric is accuracy per unit of active GPU-seconds (compute), not per gradient step, with the compute ratio γ = (1+W/T)/(1+μ) quantifying the savings relative to the no-buffer baseline.

## Method

The paper inserts a replay buffer into an asynchronous RL pipeline where W inference workers continuously generate rollouts into a shared FIFO buffer while T trainer GPUs sample batches from it without removal, decoupling rollout production from consumption. The theoretical contribution is a bias-variance decomposition of the gradient estimator under replay, yielding a convergence bound whose key terms are the staleness horizon N/R, the replay ratio B/R, and the rollout-to-training compute cost ratio μ. Theorem 4.5 then optimizes over buffer design to show that the optimal staleness horizon and replay ratio grow with μ, formally establishing that strict on-policy sampling is suboptimal when generation is expensive. Empirically, a buffer of size 84 with (W,T)=(6,2) on Qwen2.5-7B saves up to 40% of compute while matching or exceeding on-policy accuracy, and the buffer additionally stabilizes training and improves pass@k by preserving output diversity through increased training-distribution diversity from older samples.

## Results

Training Qwen2.5-7B on OpenR1-Math-220k with a replay buffer of size 84 and (W,T)=(6,2) achieves equal or higher MATH accuracy than the on-policy GRPO baseline while reducing compute by ~35% (γ=0.65). More aggressive configurations like (4,4) reduce compute by up to 68% (γ=0.32), though with some accuracy trade-off at very high replay ratios. The buffer also stabilizes training (preventing crashes observed in the baseline) and improves pass@k for k>1, indicating better preservation of output diversity. A Pareto frontier analysis across learning rates and buffer sizes (Figure 4) confirms that the best buffer configurations consistently dominate the best non-buffer configurations at every compute budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole baseline is strictly on-policy GRPO (no replay buffer), which generates rollouts, uses them for a single gradient update, and discards them. The replay buffer method wins decisively on compute efficiency: at (W,T)=(6,2), each gradient step costs only 0.65× the compute of the baseline, and at (4,4) it costs only 0.32×. On accuracy, the buffer configurations match or exceed the on-policy baseline at the same compute budget (Figure 1, Figure 4), contradicting the prevailing assumption that off-policy data degrades performance. The buffer also provides an unanticipated stability benefit: the on-policy baseline exhibits training crashes and instability, while buffer configurations train more smoothly and sometimes reach higher peak accuracy. On output diversity (pass@k), the buffer method outperforms the baseline for k>1, suggesting that reusing older rollouts regularizes the training distribution and prevents the entropy collapse common in RL fine-tuning. The Pareto frontier sweep (Figure 4) confirms that no non-buffer configuration (across all tested learning rates) can match the best buffer configuration at any compute level.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the experience replay mechanism from classical deep RL (DQN, Mnih et al. 2015) to the LLM post-training setting, embedding it within the asynchronous inference-worker/trainer pipeline popularized by Noukhovitch et al. (2024). It modifies GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as the base policy-gradient algorithm, adding a FIFO replay buffer and uniform sampling. Unlike recent LLM replay-buffer works (Wang et al. 2025, Bartoldson et al. 2025, Lu et al. 2025) that use buffers to improve exploration or final accuracy via specialized losses, this paper's sole motivation is compute efficiency: amortizing the cost of rollout generation by reusing trajectories, with a theoretical framework (bias-variance decomposition under staleness) that characterizes the optimal buffer size and replay ratio as a function of the inference-to-training cost ratio μ.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Given that inference (rollout generation) can consume over 80% of post-training GPU hours, a 40% compute reduction from a simple buffer is practically transformative for any team running RL fine-tuning at scale. The theoretical characterization of the staleness–diversity–cost trade-off gives practitioners principled guidance for buffer hyperparameters rather than pure trial-and-error. Key open questions include whether these gains persist at frontier model scale (the paper tests up to 7B), whether more sophisticated off-policy corrections or prioritized sampling can push the Pareto frontier further, and how the findings interact with other emerging efficiency techniques like speculative decoding or distillation-based rollout generation.
