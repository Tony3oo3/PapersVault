## Problem

Existing layer-skipping techniques for vision-language models rely on ad-hoc heuristics or hyperparameter sweeps to decide which layers to bypass, lacking principled criteria that guarantee minimal performance loss. No unified theoretical framework connects commonly measured redundancy signals (e.g., cosine distance between adjacent layer activations) to formal guarantees on downstream task performance, leaving practitioners without a systematic way to determine when skipping is safe across different models and tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261479/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a pre-trained VLM (e.g., LLaVA 1.5 7B/13B, LLaVA-NeXT, Qwen 2.5 VL) and a small label-free calibration subset (as few as 1k samples) to compute per-layer metrics: average adjacent-layer cosine distance, probability of t-proximal states, and Visual Attention Ratio (VAR) for the answer token. It outputs two indices — a Late Entry layer L (where vision tokens are inserted) and an Early Exit layer j (where vision tokens are removed) — which together define a layer-skipped forward pass that processes vision tokens only in the middle layers. No ground-truth labels or downstream task evaluation are required to determine these indices.

## Method

The paper defines four notions of redundancy — geometric (expected cosine distance), proximal (high-probability closeness), functional (bounded change in optimal task estimators), and informational (low conditional entropy) — and proves implication chains connecting them: proximal implies geometric (Proposition 1), geometric implies functional under Lipschitz continuity of the conditional expectation (Theorems 1–2), and informational implies functional under a Markov chain assumption via Pinsker's inequality (Theorem 5). Layer skipping is unified into two paradigms: Late Entry (withholding vision tokens from early layers, justified by geometric/proximal redundancy of vision activations) and Early Exit (removing vision tokens from late layers, justified by low cross-modal attention measured by VAR). A label-free algorithm (Algorithm 1) scans all layers on a calibration subset, finds the largest prefix satisfying redundancy thresholds for Late Entry and the smallest suffix satisfying redundancy plus VAR thresholds for Early Exit, and a Hoeffding-based generalization bound (Theorem 7) guarantees that a small subset suffices. Empirically, the framework confirms that early and late vision tokens are redundant across LLaVA, LLaVA-NeXT, Qwen 2.5 VL, and DeepSeek-VL, and that skipping layers where conditions are violated causes significant degradation while skipping where they hold preserves performance.

## Results

The paper validates its theoretical redundancy framework by showing that layer skipping at layers meeting geometric/proximal redundancy conditions causes minimal performance degradation, while skipping at non-redundant layers causes significant drops. For Late Entry on General VQA (LLaVA 1.5 7B), skipping the first 4 vision-token layers (cosine distance 0.025, P[D<0.05]=0.972) preserves accuracy at 0.553 vs. 0.564 baseline, whereas skipping 8 layers (cosine distance 0.073, P[D<0.05]=0.267) drops accuracy to 0.370. For Early Exit, skipping the last 4 layers (VAR=2.44) maintains accuracy at 0.581 vs. 0.582 baseline, while skipping at layer 20 (VAR=4.33) still holds at 0.582. Across all four models (LLaVA 1.5 7B/13B, LLaVA NeXT 7B, Qwen 2.5 VL) and four task categories, the pattern holds: redundancy conditions predict viable skipping layers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new layer-skipping method to benchmark against existing ones; instead, it validates its theoretical redundancy conditions against the no-skip full-model baseline. The key comparison is between skipping at layers where redundancy conditions are met (low cosine distance, high proximity probability, low VAR) versus layers where they are not. When conditions are met (e.g., skipping first 4 vision layers in LLaVA 1.5 7B), accuracy degrades by only 1–2% (0.564→0.553 on General VQA). When conditions are violated (e.g., skipping 8 or 12 layers), accuracy drops by 20–35% (0.564→0.370 or 0.261). The paper also unifies existing methods (DeepInsert, VTW, LayerSkip, SAINT, γ-MoD) under its Early Exit / Late Entry taxonomy, showing they all implicitly rely on the same redundancy signals the framework makes explicit. A notable task-dependent finding is that Captioning tasks show higher VAR in late layers than discriminative VQA, making early exit less viable for captioning (CIDEr drops from 0.819 to 0.720 at layer 12 vs. accuracy dropping only 1.1% for VQA at the same skip point).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides a theoretical unification of the growing family of VLM layer-skipping and token-pruning methods (LayerSkip, AdaSkip, FlexiDepth, DeepInsert, γ-MoD, Skip-Vision, VTW, ST³), which have been largely empirical and heuristic-driven. It draws on information-theoretic tools—distance-based Fano's inequality, conditional entropy bounds, and partial information decomposition—to formalize four notions of redundancy (geometric, proximal, functional, informational) and prove implication relationships among them. The Visual Attention Ratio (VAR) from Jiang et al. (2025) is adopted as the operational cross-modal attention metric, and the Lipschitz-continuity assumptions on layer functions echo those in Skip-Vision (Zeng et al., 2025). The key departure from prior work is the shift from ad-hoc per-method heuristics to a single framework with provable conditions that are computable without downstream task labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By grounding layer-skipping in verifiable redundancy conditions, the paper gives practitioners a principled, label-free procedure (Algorithm 1) for identifying skippable layers using only a small calibration subset, reducing the trial-and-error that has characterized the field. The finding that redundancy is task-dependent—captioning resists early exit while discriminative VQA does not—adds a useful nuance for deployment decisions. Open questions remain: why these redundancies arise (pretraining artifact vs. intentional architecture), whether the Lipschitz assumptions hold universally across scales, and whether the framework extends to video-language or audio-language settings. The generalization bound (Theorem 7) is reassuring but relies on bounded per-sample redundancy, which may be loose in practice.
