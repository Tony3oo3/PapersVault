## Problem

Previous theoretical analyses of neural network simplicity biases have largely assumed (quasi-)isotropic inputs, ignoring two defining properties of natural images: approximate translation-invariance and power-law power spectra. This paper asks how the separation between Fourier amplitudes (encoding pairwise correlations) and phases (encoding higher-order correlations) shapes the sample complexity of learning, and whether power-law spectra can accelerate the recovery of phase information even when the spectra themselves carry no discriminative signal between classes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263871/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework operates on high-dimensional binary classification problems with inputs x ∈ R^N drawn from a Fourier data model where the baseline distribution P_0 is a zero-mean Gaussian with circulant (translation-invariant) covariance, and the planted distribution P shares the same mean and covariance but differs only in fourth-order cumulants via a phase modification at a single frequency k_0. Experimentally, the method consumes grayscale texture patches (8×8, 16×16, 32×32 from ALOT), RGB images from ImageNet and CIFAR-100, with amplitude manipulations (flattening to unity or matching across classes) applied in Fourier space before inverse DFT. Outputs include class predictions, test loss/accuracy curves under different amplitude conditions, and rigorous sample complexity bounds (cubic n ≫ N³ log²N for isotropic inputs vs. quasi-linear for power-law spectra).

## Method

The core contribution is a Fourier data model that surgically decouples amplitude and phase statistics for translation-invariant inputs: the planted distribution modifies only the phase at one frequency k_0 via ψ = φ + ε sin(φ) + U (where U is a discrete corrector preserving translation-invariance), leaving the covariance unchanged while introducing non-trivial fourth-order cumulants. Theoretically, the paper extends the information exponent framework of Ben Arous et al. to general non-isotropic (circulant) covariances, proving that isotropic inputs yield k* = 4 and hence cubic sample complexity for online SGD, while a power-law spectrum with extensive eigenvalue λ_{k0} ≈ √N reduces the effective information exponent to k* = 2, enabling quasi-linear recovery. The key mechanism is that the extensive eigenvalue creates an extensive signal-to-noise ratio λ_{k0}² ≈ N in the population loss that couples the phase signal to the quadratic form σ_Σ², allowing SGD to first recover the shared principal subspace quickly and then converge to the phase-carrying mode. Empirically, two-layer fully connected classifiers and ResNet-18 networks trained on ALOT textures, ImageNet, and CIFAR-100 confirm that flattening Fourier amplitudes slows learning onset while matching amplitudes across classes (removing amplitude-based discrimination) still accelerates phase learning relative to flat spectra.

## Results

The paper establishes that for isotropic (flat-spectrum) translation-invariant inputs, online SGD requires a cubic sample complexity of n ≫ N³ log²N steps to weakly recover phase information, and provably fails for n ≪ N³ (Theorem 3.1). In contrast, for inputs with power-law decaying Fourier amplitudes (as in natural images), the effective signal-to-noise ratio λ_{k₀}² ≈ N reduces the information exponent from k* = 4 to k* = 2, yielding quasi-linear sample complexity for phase recovery (Conjecture 5.3). Experimentally, on ALOT textures, ImageNet (ResNet-18), and CIFAR-100, flattening all Fourier amplitudes to one causes a clear slow-down in the onset of learning, while matching amplitudes across classes (so only phases differ) produces a significant speed-up compared to the flat case, confirming the theoretical prediction across architectures, datasets, and optimizers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without standard method-vs-baseline comparisons; the `compares_to` list is empty. The key internal comparisons are between different input covariance structures: (1) isotropic vs. power-law spectra, where the latter dramatically accelerates phase learning from cubic to quasi-linear sample complexity; (2) original vs. amplitude-flattened vs. amplitude-matched data, where the amplitude-matched setting (shared power-law spectrum, differing only in phases) shows the fastest learning onset, while the flattened setting (all amplitudes = 1) shows the slowest, matching the theoretical prediction that flat spectra correspond to the hard k* = 4 regime. The paper also contrasts its findings with prior work: unlike Mousavi-Hosseini et al. (2023) where anisotropic covariances impede SGD, here the non-trivial eigenstructure actively speeds up recovery of the relevant directions. The speed-up is notable because the power-law spectrum itself is shared across classes and provides no discriminative information—it purely acts as an effective signal-to-noise amplifier for the phase-encoded signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the information-exponent framework of Ben Arous et al. (2021, 2022, 2025b) from isotropic Gaussian inputs to non-isotropic, translation-invariant inputs with circulant covariance, which is the key technical novelty. It also builds on the distributional simplicity bias literature (Ingrosso & Goldt 2022; Bardone & Goldt 2024) that showed networks learn pairwise statistics before higher-order correlations, but reframes this through a Fourier lens where amplitudes encode pairwise correlations and phases encode higher-order correlations. The paper departs from prior power-law spectrum analyses (Paquette et al. 2024; Braun et al. 2025) by placing the power-law in the spectrum of non-Gaussian inputs rather than in a Gaussian additive model, and by studying SGD in the feature-learning regime rather than the kernel regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central insight—that power-law Fourier spectra, which carry no discriminative information between classes, dramatically accelerate learning of the phase information that does discriminate—provides a mechanistic explanation for why gradient-based networks learn natural images efficiently despite the inherent hardness of extracting higher-order statistics. This suggests natural images occupy a 'favourable regime' where spectral structure acts as an implicit curriculum. Open questions include deriving information-theoretic thresholds, extending the analysis to wavelet bases and other texture models, and making the non-isotropic conjectures (5.2, 5.3) fully rigorous by computing the diffusive terms. The theoretical results for the non-isotropic case remain conjectural, limiting the strength of the formal guarantees.
