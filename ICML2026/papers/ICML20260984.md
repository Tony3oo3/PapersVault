## Problem

Meta-learning for Training-data Selection (MTS), which learns per-sample weights via bi-level optimization to align training with validation distributions, often underperforms naive training in practice. The paper identifies two root causes: an extremely low gradient signal-to-noise ratio (GSNR) in the selection network that destabilizes optimization, and the absence of input features that meaningfully correlate with data quality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260984/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The selection network consumes a hand-crafted feature vector per training sample (KNN cosine similarities in training and validation sets, distances to class centroids, forgetting-event counts, gradient norms, and learnable class/source embeddings) rather than raw pixels, and outputs a scalar weight in (0,∞) via a sigmoid-activated 3-layer MLP. The main ResNet-50 (or ViT-Base) classifier takes standard RGB images and produces class predictions. A small validation set (same distribution as test) is required during training to compute the hypergradient for the selection network.

## Method

The paper provides a theoretical analysis showing that MTS induces a winner-takes-most dynamic: the normalized weight vector p converges toward a one-hot vector at the dominant index, collapsing the effective batch size and making the selection-network gradient hypersensitive to a single sample, which degrades GSNR. The key remedy is increasing the batch size N (to 1024), which raises the normalization scalar S and dilutes the influence of any single dominant weight. Complementing this, the authors design a multi-scale feature set—local KNN geometry, global class-centroid distances, optimization-dynamics signals (forgetting events, gradient/error norms from a reference training run), and categorical embeddings for class label and data source (real vs. synthetic)—that encodes distributional and generalization-relevant information unavailable from raw pixels alone. The selection network is trained with a one-step bi-level hypergradient update using AdamW, while the main classifier uses SGD with momentum, both at equal update frequency.

## Results

Across four benchmarks (Waterbirds, CelebA, Texture, and PACS with 4 domain pairs), the proposed method achieves an average accuracy of 73.75%, improving over No Selection (68.26%) by 5.49% and over the strongest baseline, hard selection with Aux-Clf(Val) (70.86%), by 2.89%. The largest individual gains are on Waterbirds (+11.68% over No Selection) and PACS-Photo (+11.08%). Ablations confirm that reducing batch size from 1024 to 64 degrades average accuracy by 2.79%, and replacing the proposed features with raw images drops accuracy by 5.55%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all seven baselines on average. Training-dynamics heuristics (Forgetting, GraNd) consistently underperform No Selection by 0.53–1.37%, and CLIP Similarity yields only marginal changes (−0.17% to +0.02%), indicating that simple per-sample scores lack sufficient signal for effective selection. Aux-Clf trained on the training domain causes substantial drops (−3.63% to −5.39%), while Aux-Clf(Val) trained on the validation domain is the strongest baseline at +2.60% (hard) / +1.86% (soft), still trailing the proposed method by 2.89% / 3.63%. NormSim shows dataset-specific behavior, improving on photorealistic benchmarks but degrading by up to 8.96% on PACS-Sketch, and Importance Weighting yields a mean drop of 0.91% due to high-variance density-ratio estimates. MetaWeightNet, the closest prior MTS method, achieves only +0.62% over No Selection, corroborating the paper's argument that naive MTS with small batches and a single loss feature underperforms; the proposed method's gains stem from combining a large batch size (N=1024) with informative distributional and training-dynamics features.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Meta-learning for Training-data Selection (MTS) framework introduced by Ren et al. (2018) and Shu et al. (2019), which use bi-level optimization to learn per-sample data weights. Rather than proposing a new selection paradigm, the paper diagnoses why existing MTS implementations underperform by providing a theoretical analysis of the gradient signal-to-noise ratio (GSNR) in the selection network, showing that winner-takes-most weight dynamics cause a spiky distribution that degrades GSNR. It departs from prior work by identifying batch size as the critical remedy and by replacing the single training-loss feature used in MetaWeightNet with a richer set of distributional and optimization-dynamics features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical characterization of why MTS fails (Theorem 3.2 on one-hot convergence, Theorem 3.6 on GSNR upper bounds) gives practitioners a principled understanding of the optimization landscape rather than relying on trial-and-error. The practical takeaway—that a simple increase in batch size from 64 to 1024 is essential for MTS to work—is actionable and well-supported across four benchmarks and two architectures. Limitations include the memory cost of large batches (mitigated by gradient accumulation, left as future work), the dependence on a validation set whose distribution matches the target, and the simplifying assumption of constant alignment values in the convergence proof. Open questions include whether the GSNR analysis extends to multi-step inner optimization or to settings where the selection network is much larger than the classifier.
