## Problem

Existing causal discovery methods that handle cyclic graphs, nonlinearity, or interventional data typically impose restrictive assumptions such as linearity, Gaussian noise, known intervention targets, or surgical (hard) interventions. No prior framework simultaneously accommodates directed cycles, nonlinear mechanisms, non-Gaussian exogenous noise, and soft interventions with unknown targets, leaving a significant gap for realistic experimental settings like gene regulatory networks probed via CRISPR perturbations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264513/cropped_page6_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

SCOUT consumes K interventional experiments (each with N_k tabular samples) where the intervention type (shift, scale, or noisy-function) and the set of intervened nodes are unknown. It outputs a directed cyclic adjacency matrix M^G ∈ {0,1}^{d×d} (learned via Gumbel-softmax with L1 sparsity regularization) and an intervention target matrix T^I ∈ {0,1}^{K×d} (also Gumbel-softmax parameterized), jointly identifying both the causal graph structure and which nodes were perturbed in each experiment.

## Method

SCOUT models the causal mechanism f as a contractive neural network whose input is masked by the learned adjacency matrix (Hadamard product), ensuring invertibility of the forward map X ↦ (id−f)(X) via spectral normalization and the Banach fixed-point theorem. A second normalizing flow layer—piecewise rational quadratic neural spline flows—transforms the non-Gaussian exogenous noise ε into a standard Gaussian, enabling tractable density evaluation under arbitrary noise distributions. The log-determinant of the Jacobian is computed via an unbiased power-series estimator (truncated at a random Poisson cutoff) combined with the Hutchinson trace estimator. Training maximizes a regularized log-likelihood score over the neural network parameters θ, the Gumbel-softmax graph parameters φ, and the Gumbel-softmax target parameters ψ, with L1 penalties on both the adjacency and target matrices. The paper proves that exact maximization of this score recovers the I*-Markov equivalence class of the ground-truth graph and the true intervention family, extending the directed global Markov property to the interventional setting via σ-separation on an augmented graph with context variables.

## Results

On synthetic cyclic graphs (d=10, 10 single-node interventions, 1000 samples each), SCOUT achieves near-perfect graph recovery (AUPRC ≈ 1.000) across all noise and intervention settings except Noisy Function + Gaussian noise, where it matches baselines. For intervention target recovery, SCOUT attains AUPRC 1.000±0.000 for both shift and scale interventions under all three noise distributions (Gaussian, Exponential, Gumbel), while BACKSHIFT degrades to 0.859–0.962 for shift and 1.000 for scale. On the real-world Perturb-CITE-seq dataset (61 genes, 3 cell conditions), SCOUT achieves the lowest interventional NLL across all conditions. On SERGIO (close-to-real-world, d=10), SCOUT reaches AUPRC 0.978±0.025 for target recovery versus 0.432±0.032 for BACKSHIFT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SCOUT is compared against four main baselines: NODAGSFlow (nonlinear cyclic, but assumes surgical interventions with known targets and Gaussian noise), LLC (linear cyclic, known targets), BACKSHIFT (linear cyclic, shift interventions only, unknown targets), and in the appendix, DCDI, UT-IGSP, and BACADI (all DAG-only). SCOUT outperforms NODAGSFlow and LLC in graph recovery across all settings because it handles soft interventions, unknown targets, and non-Gaussian noise simultaneously. Against BACKSHIFT, SCOUT wins decisively on target recovery for shift interventions (e.g., 1.000 vs. 0.859 AUPRC under Gumbel noise) and for noisy-function interventions (0.607 vs. 0.423 under Gumbel), while matching it on scale interventions. In the DAG setting (Appendix C.1), SCOUT also outperforms DCDI (0.157 vs. 1.000 AUPRC for shift+Gaussian) and BACADI (0.887 vs. 1.000), though UT-IGSP achieves comparable target recovery. On the real-world Perturb-CITE-seq dataset, SCOUT achieves the lowest interventional NLL across all three cell conditions, confirming that modeling non-Gaussian noise improves predictive accuracy over Gaussian-only baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

SCOUT extends the NODAGS-Flow framework (Sethuraman et al., 2023), which first introduced a differentiable, likelihood-based approach to nonlinear cyclic causal discovery using contractive residual flows, by adding three critical capabilities: soft (non-surgical) interventions, non-Gaussian exogenous noise via neural spline flows, and unknown intervention target identification via Gumbel-softmax. The joint causal modeling formulation with context variables follows Mooij et al. (2016), and the consistency proof builds on the σ-separation and Markov equivalence machinery from Bongers et al. (2021) and the KL-divergence score decomposition from Brouillard et al. (2020). Prior methods addressed subsets of these challenges in isolation—BackShift handles linear cyclic models with shift interventions and unknown targets, LLC handles linear cyclic models with known targets, and DCDI/UT-IGSP/BaCaDI handle DAGs with unknown targets—but none simultaneously accommodate all four challenges (cycles, nonlinearity, non-Gaussian noise, soft interventions with unknown targets).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: real biological and physical systems exhibit feedback loops, non-Gaussian noise, and soft perturbations with partially known targets, yet prior methods required restrictive assumptions (linearity, Gaussianity, known targets, or hard interventions) that fail in these settings. The consistency theorem (Theorem 3.1) provides the first identifiability guarantee for cyclic causal discovery under soft interventions with unknown targets, which is a meaningful theoretical contribution. Key limitations include the contractivity requirement on causal mechanisms (which may not hold in practice, as acknowledged in the SERGIO experiments), the assumption of no confounders, and computational constraints that limited the real-world evaluation to 61 genes. Open directions include handling confounders, measurement noise, and scaling to genome-scale networks.
