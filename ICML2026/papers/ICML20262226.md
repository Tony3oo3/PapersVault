## Problem

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) lack dedicated post-hoc interpretability methods that attribute model predictions to individual training points and physics constraints. Existing analyses focus on training dynamics and failure modes but cannot reveal how specific collocation points or loss components (PDE residual, boundary conditions, initial condition) shape a converged model's predictions, leaving a gap for training-data-centric diagnostics that go beyond residual inspection.

## Input / Output

The method operates post-hoc on a converged PINN, consuming its parameters θ₀, the full training set (PDE collocation points, boundary/initial condition points), test points, and the composite loss function. It produces an n×m influence matrix (training points × test points) computed via low-rank Arnoldi-approximated inverse-Hessian vector products, from which it derives point-to-region maps, per-loss-component fractional contributions r_{L_i} ∈ [0,1] summing to 1, cancellation scores κ ∈ [0,1], and scalar temporal/regional indicators. The influence formula Inf = −∇_θ f(z;θ₀)ᵀ H⁻¹ ∇_θ L(x;θ₀) is evaluated with f being any differentiable quantity (prediction, loss, physical observable), not restricted to the loss itself.

## Method

PINNFLUENCE generalizes influence functions to PINNs in three key ways: (1) extending the theoretical guarantee from strict convex minima to non-degenerate stationary points (saddle points) via the implicit function theorem, which is critical since PINN optimization typically converges to saddle points in ill-conditioned landscapes; (2) allowing the target function f to be any differentiable quantity rather than the loss, enabling attribution of predictions and physical observables to training data; and (3) exploiting linearity in the loss argument to decompose total influence into per-component fractions for the PDE residual, each boundary condition, and the initial condition. The inverse Hessian is approximated via 200 Arnoldi iterations retaining the top-50 eigenvectors with damping λ=10⁻³, yielding a low-rank surrogate RRᵀ ≈ H⁻¹ that reduces per-pair cost to O(p·k). On top of these point-wise scores, the framework constructs normalized diagnostic indicators—most notably a temporal influence indicator η that quantifies whether predictions are disproportionately shaped by past-time training points—enabling systematic comparison between well-trained and poorly-trained models across diverse PDEs.

## Results

Across five time-dependent PDEs (Heat, Allen-Cahn, Burgers', Drift-Diffusion, Wave), the temporal influence indicator (η) distinguishes well-trained from poorly-trained PINNs: well-trained models yield η values close to the data-distribution baseline t̄ (e.g., 0.46±0.04 for Drift-Diffusion vs. t̄=0.46), while poorly-trained models show strongly skewed values (e.g., 0.21±0.06 for Drift-Diffusion, 0.11±0.02 for Wave). In PBRF validation, influence functions achieve Spearman rank correlations of 0.08–0.77 with actual fine-tuning effects, substantially exceeding the Grad-Dot baseline (−0.08 to 0.15). Loss-component decomposition reveals that poorly-trained models maintain elevated IC influence fractions (r̄_Lic) that plateau after t≈0.4, whereas well-trained models show IC influence decaying toward zero over time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole quantitative baseline is Grad-Dot (Charpiat et al., 2019), a gradient-only attribution method equivalent to setting the inverse Hessian to the identity. In PBRF validation (Table 6), influence functions consistently outperform Grad-Dot across all seven PDE configurations: IF correlations range from 0.08 to 0.77, while Grad-Dot stays within −0.08 to 0.15, confirming that the inverse-Hessian factor carries meaningful information beyond raw gradient dot products. The largest gap appears on Burgers' well-trained (0.77 vs. −0.08) and Allen-Cahn poorly-trained (0.69 vs. −0.01). In leave-one-out retraining validation (Table 5), both methods show near-zero Spearman correlations (|ρ| < 0.05), confirming that IFs approximate local fine-tuning (PBRF) rather than exact retraining, consistent with prior findings (Bae et al., 2022). The paper also qualitatively contrasts PINNFLUENCE with training-time diagnostics (gradient alignment, PINNACLE, causal PINN) in Table 10, emphasizing that PINNFLUENCE operates post-hoc at per-point × per-prediction × per-loss granularity, a resolution those methods do not provide.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes influence functions (Koh & Liang, 2017) from strict convex minima to non-degenerate stationary points and from loss-to-loss attribution to arbitrary differentiable quantities (predictions, physical observables), enabling application to PINNs' composite physics-informed objectives. It positions itself as a post-hoc, per-point diagnostic that complements rather than competes with training-time interventions such as gradient alignment analysis, adaptive collocation selection (PINNACLE), and causality-enforcing training schemes. The approach is distinct from architecture-based interpretability (KAN, GINN-KAN) and from mechanistic interpretability methods (sparse autoencoders, linear probing) applied to neural operators, as it operates on already-trained PINNs without architectural constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PINNFLUENCE provides the first training-data-centric interpretability framework for PINNs, enabling practitioners to attribute predictions to individual collocation points and physics constraints—a capability absent from residual-based analysis. The counterintuitive finding that well-trained PINNs exhibit temporally symmetric (not causally ordered) influence, while poorly-trained models show the 'expected' causal alignment, offers a concrete diagnostic signal for training failure. Limitations include the first-order approximation under ill-conditioned Hessians (condition numbers up to 10^33), non-negligible computational cost, and the need for domain knowledge to construct problem-specific indicators. Open questions include whether influence-based resampling and loss-reweighting can actively improve PINN training, and whether the diagnostic patterns generalize to higher-dimensional or strongly nonlinear PDEs.
