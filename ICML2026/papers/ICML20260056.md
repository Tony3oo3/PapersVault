## Problem

Existing AI safety benchmarks test whether models refuse facially unlawful requests, but real-world corporate law violations typically involve routine actions—editing documents, trading stock, requesting payment—that become illegal only due to situational context such as a pending investigation or bankruptcy filing. This paper asks whether frontier AI models can recognize this context-dependent illegality and refuses to perform the action, and whether they do so more reliably than humans.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260056/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a single-turn task instruction (e.g., 'edit the meeting minutes to remove my name') with a set of corporate artifacts (emails, memos, board minutes) whose content varies across four treatment levels that progressively introduce contextual legal triggers. In agentic mode, models additionally receive tool access (read_inbox, list_documents, read_document, edit_document, send_email, plus one scenario-specific action tool) via the Inspect framework. The output is a per-sample compliance classification (Complied / Refused / Warned but Complied) produced by an LLM-as-judge (Claude Sonnet 4.5, temperature 0.0), aggregated into compliance rates by treatment and construct, with over-refusal (T0 refusals) and under-refusal (T1–T3 compliance) reported separately.

## Method

The paper introduces a 4 (treatment) × 4 (construct) factorial benchmark spanning four U.S. federal corporate-law domains—SEC obstruction (18 U.S.C. §1519), insider trading (§10(b)/Rule 10b-5), bankruptcy automatic stay (11 U.S.C. §362), and IPO gun-jumping (Securities Act §5(c))—where the identical action is legal at T0 and progressively more clearly illegal at T1–T3 as contextual signals accumulate. Eleven frontier models are evaluated in both chat and agentic modes, with 3 naming variations × 4 legal-phrasing variations × 5 samples (temperature 1.0) yielding 960 samples per model per mode for robustness. A human baseline (N = 303 Prolific participants) uses between-subjects treatment assignment and within-subjects construct with Latin-square counterbalancing in an HTML corporate-desktop simulation. Responses are classified via a validated LLM-as-judge (99.1% agreement on 215 manual reviews), and the Grok-4.1-fast reasoning/non-reasoning comparison isolates the effect of chain-of-thought from agentic scaffolding.

## Results

Across 11 frontier models evaluated in chat and agentic settings on four corporate law constructs, the best-performing models (Gemini-3-pro, GPT-5.2-pro in agent mode) achieve near-zero compliance on illegal actions (T1–T3: 0–16%) while maintaining ~100% compliance on legal baseline actions, clearly outperforming the human baseline (T1=90%, T2=73%, T3=61%). In contrast, context-blind models (Grok-4.1-fast without reasoning, DeepSeek-chat) maintain 75–100% compliance even at T3, performing worse than humans. Enabling reasoning (Grok-4.1-fast R vs. NR) reduces T3 chat compliance from 95% to 29%, and agentic framing generally yields steeper treatment gradients than static chat prompts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates six model families (Claude Haiku/Opus 4.5, GPT-5.2-pro/5-mini, Gemini-3-pro/flash, Grok-4.1-fast R/NR + Grok-3-mini, DeepSeek-reasoner/chat) against a human baseline of 303 Prolific participants. Gemini-3-pro is the strongest performer, achieving 0% compliance at T2–T3 across all constructs in both modes while maintaining 98–100% baseline compliance, outperforming humans by 61–88 percentage points on illegal actions. GPT-5.2-pro excels in agent mode (T3: 1%) but is weaker in chat (T3: 40%), still beating humans. Grok-4.1-fast with reasoning substantially outperforms its non-reasoning counterpart (T3 chat: 29% vs. 95%), isolating a large reasoning effect. Claude Haiku and Opus 4.5 exhibit severe over-refusal in chat (0–32% baseline compliance), rendering their low illegal-action compliance uninformative; however, Claude Haiku recovers in agent mode (74% baseline, 1% T3). DeepSeek-chat and Grok-4.1-fast (NR) are classified as context-blind in chat, with T3 compliance of 75% and 95% respectively—worse than the 61% human rate—though both improve in agent mode.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Aka (2025), which tested AI recognition of context-dependent illegality on a single corporate law construct, to four constructs with systematic factorial variation, multiple frontier models, and a human baseline. It positions itself at the intersection of three literatures—safety/refusal benchmarks (HarmBench, OR-Bench, CASE-Bench), legal reasoning evaluation (LegalBench), and agentic risk assessment (AgentHarm, ToolEmu)—arguing that all three test facially unlawful or overtly harmful requests, whereas real corporate violations often arise from routine actions made illegal by situational context. The paper deliberately withholds explicit legal materials from models to test contextual recognition rather than rule-following, distinguishing its construct from work like Doyle & Tucker (2025) that supplies practice guides.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic empirical evidence on whether frontier AI agents can detect context-dependent illegality in corporate settings, a precondition for responsible deployment of AI in compliance-sensitive functions. Key open questions include whether findings generalize to expert human baselines (the current baseline is crowdsourced Prolific participants), cross-jurisdictional legal systems, and multi-turn interactions with user pushback. The near-universal failure on Insider Trading and the large reasoning effect (66 pp drop in T3 compliance for Grok 4.1-fast) suggest that current safety training conflates topic-level caution with genuine legal contextual reasoning, pointing to a concrete target for improved alignment.
