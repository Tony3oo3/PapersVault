## Problem

Standard conjoint analysis reports Average Marginal Component Effects (AMCEs) that average each attribute's effect over a fixed distribution of other attributes, ignoring strategic interdependence between competing candidates. This paper replaces marginal effect estimation with offline policy optimization over exponentially large factorial profile spaces, learning interpretable stochastic policies that account for attribute interactions and, in an adversarial extension, simultaneous strategic competition between two parties.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261873/cropped_page8_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes forced-choice conjoint data (binary selection indicators from paired-profile comparisons), institutional parameters specifying primary rules and electorate composition, and the experimental logging distribution. It produces a factored stochastic policy as independent per-attribute categorical probabilities (e.g., P(priority=economy)=0.4, P(priority=immigration)=0.3), along with the expected vote share under that policy, asymptotic standard errors for both the policy probabilities and the value via the Delta method, and a strategic-divergence measure quantifying how differently the two parties' optimal strategies weight each profile.

## Method

The core mechanism is a two-step estimator: first fit a Bernoulli GLM (or Bayesian neural network) to the forced-choice outcome with main effects and pairwise interactions, then optimize a factored product-of-Categoricals policy against the fitted outcome model. In the average case with two-way interactions and L2 regularization, the optimizer admits a closed-form solution as a linear system (Proposition 3.1); for richer models or boundary violations, a logit-parameterized gradient ascent is used. The adversarial extension formulates a restricted minimax game where both parties simultaneously choose factored policies, with institutional structure (primaries inducing nominee distributions, then a general election) embedded in the payoff. Uncertainty is propagated end-to-end via the Delta method, using either unrolled backpropagation through the optimization trace or implicit differentiation of the stationarity conditions. Under KL regularization, the factored policy is shown to be the mean-field variational approximation to the full-simplex Gibbs optimizer, with an explicit approximation bound in terms of interaction strength.

## Results

In Monte Carlo simulations (n ∈ {500, 1500, 3500, 10000}, K ∈ {5, 10, 20}), the average-case optimal stochastic intervention shows negligible bias and rapidly declining RMSE for both π̂* and Q̂(π̂*), with 95% coverage approaching nominal levels as n grows. In the adversarial simulation (n ∈ {1000, 5000, 10000}, p_R ∈ {0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 0.65, 0.8}), estimation error is driven primarily by sample size with only modest sensitivity to partisan composition; coverage falls below 95% at n=1000 but recovers at larger n. On the Ono & Burden (2019) U.S. presidential conjoint, the adversarial restricted-equilibrium vote share falls within the historical two-party range since 1976 and closely matches the 2016 result, whereas the average-case (uniform-opponent) optimizer produces vote shares outside that historical range.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the standard AMCE-based policy, which selects per-factor maximizers (i.e., picks the best level for each attribute independently, ignoring interactions). The proposed factored stochastic policy substantially outperforms this AMCE baseline in expected outcomes on synthetic data (Fig. 4), because it accounts for two-way interactions that the marginal AMCE approach misses. On the real presidential conjoint, the non-adversarial (average-case) optimizer—closest in spirit to AMCE analysis—produces restricted-equilibrium vote shares that fall outside the historical two-party range since 1976, while the adversarial minimax optimizer produces vote shares that align with the 2016 election result and sit within the historical band (Fig. 2). The paper also compares a Bernoulli GLM with pairwise interactions against a Bayesian Transformer outcome model (Table 2): the GLM is most efficient and best calibrated when the linear approximation is correct or close, while the Transformer can reduce RMSE under stronger nonlinear misspecification but exhibits imperfect coverage. Overall, the adversarial formulation is the key differentiator: it is what brings predicted vote shares into the empirically plausible range, a property the averaging/AMCE-style baseline lacks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant conjoint-analysis paradigm of estimating Average Marginal Component Effects (AMCEs) by reframing the problem as offline policy optimization over a combinatorial action space, building on the factorial-design and logit-outcome-model foundations of Hainmueller et al. (2014) and the two-way interaction parameterization of Egami & Imai (2019). The uncertainty-propagation machinery draws directly on implicit differentiation (Lorraine et al., 2020) and the Delta method, while the adversarial extension connects to classical zero-sum game theory (Nemirovski, 2004; Littman, 1994) and the variational/mean-field literature (Wainwright & Jordan, 2008) for bounding the cost of the product-of-Categoricals restriction. The data-driven respondent clustering in the application leverages the heterogeneous-effects framework of Goplerud et al. (2025), and the broader policy-learning-from-experimental-data lineage (Athey & Wager, 2021; Kitagawa & Tetenov, 2018) provides the conceptual template that the paper specializes to conjoint factorial designs with strategic interaction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The shift from marginal-effect estimation to joint stochastic-policy learning addresses a real limitation of AMCEs: they cannot capture interaction-driven strategic positioning, and the adversarial extension with institutional structure (primaries → general election) yields restricted-equilibrium vote shares that align with historical U.S. presidential outcomes, whereas the standard averaging approach does not. The closed-form two-way optimizer and the mean-field variational bound on the factored-policy approximation provide theoretically grounded, interpretable tools for a setting where the action space grows exponentially. Open questions include whether the factorized policy class remains adequate under higher-order interactions or strongly nonlinear outcome surfaces, whether the institutional pushforward assumptions (affineness for bilinear payoffs) hold in more complex multi-candidate or multi-round settings, and how the two-step estimator's inference behaves under post-selection or sample-splitting corrections.
