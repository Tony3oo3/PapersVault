## Problem

Deriving scaling laws for language models requires evaluating thousands of checkpoints across tens of thousands of questions (or models × questions × samples for test-time), making it prohibitively expensive and often forcing studies to small scales where unintuitive behaviors emerge. Traditional approaches treat each model-benchmark pair in isolation with O(M×N) parameters, preventing transfer of learned structure across benchmarks. The paper asks whether Item Response Theory can factorize the scaling estimation problem to enable both query efficiency and cross-benchmark generalizability.

## Input / Output

The method consumes empirical probability responses—token-level pCorrectChoice in pre-training and per-attempt pass@1 averaged over repeated sampling in test-time—along with the scaling variable (pre-training FLOP or sample count k) and a one-time calibration set of item parameters. It produces a factorized representation of M latent ability parameters θ and N item parameters (difficulty z, discrimination d), from which scaling curves, pass@k predictions, and data-mixture rankings are derived. A key structural constraint is that θ must scale linearly with log FLOP in the pre-training setting, and the calibrated item parameters are frozen and reused for all subsequent adaptive testing and cross-benchmark transfer.

## Method

IRSL embeds the scaling law within the IRT framework by factorizing the M×N response matrix into M model-specific ability parameters θ and N question-specific item parameters (z, d), reducing parameter complexity from O(M×N) to O(M+N). The key technical contribution is Beta-IRT, which replaces the standard Bernoulli loss with a Beta loss to model the empirical probability responses that LMs uniquely provide (token probabilities, pass@1 rates), achieving reliable calibration with as few as 2 test takers versus 64+ for Binary-IRT. For pre-training, a two-stage procedure first maps FLOP → θ (empirically linear in log FLOP) and then θ → benchmark performance via the IRT logistic σ(d_j·(θ_i − z_j)); for test-time, pass@k is derived by substituting the IRT-predicted pass@1 into the standard 1−(1−p)^k formula. Adaptive testing then estimates a new model's θ using only ~50 questions (a 99.9% reduction from 37,682), and the estimated θ generalizes across benchmarks sharing the same measurement objective, enabling performance forecasting on unseen question sets without additional queries.

## Results

On pre-training downstream scaling (6,612 checkpoints, 37,682 questions, 10 benchmarks), Beta-IRT IRSL achieves comparable Decision Accuracy to Traditional pCorrectChoice on high-quality benchmarks (ARC Challenge, ARC Easy, MMLU) and superior accuracy on lower-quality benchmarks (CommonsenseQA, OpenBookQA, PIQA, SocialIQA), using only 50 questions per benchmark (a 99.9% query reduction). Beta-IRT calibration achieves near-perfect parameter recovery (RMSE < 0.05, ρ > 0.999) with as few as 2 test takers, requiring 30–60× fewer than Binary-IRT. On test-time scaling (12 LMs, 120 questions, 4 benchmarks, up to 2,500 samples), IRSL consistently outperforms traditional scaling: the performance gap (Traditional MAE − IRSL MAE) is predominantly positive across all 100 random train-test splits, confirming lower MAE for IRSL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the traditional two-step scaling law from Bhagia et al. (2024), which fits a power-law loss-to-FLOP relationship followed by a sigmoid mapping to benchmark performance. On high-quality benchmarks (ARC Challenge, ARC Easy, MMLU), Beta-IRT IRSL ties with Traditional pCorrectChoice in Decision Accuracy, while both outperform Traditional Acc and Binary-IRT variants. On lower-quality benchmarks (CommonsenseQA, OpenBookQA, PIQA, SocialIQA), Beta-IRT IRSL clearly outperforms all traditional and Binary-IRT baselines, demonstrating greater robustness under limited query budgets. On extremely noisy benchmarks (BoolQ, HellaSwag, WinoGrande), Beta-IRT fails to capture a predictive trend, attributed to benchmark homogeneity (e.g., BoolQ: σ_z = 0.19, σ_d = 0.14) that limits IRT's discriminative power. For test-time scaling, IRSL consistently achieves lower MAE than traditional pass@k estimation across all four benchmarks (AIME 2024, AIME 2025, Global MMLU Lite, MMLU Pro), with the gap distribution concentrated to the right of zero over 100 splits. Additionally, the estimated latent ability θ transfers reliably across difficulty levels and benchmarks (e.g., ARC Easy → ARC Challenge, AIME 2024 → AIME 2025), a capability the traditional baseline does not offer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper integrates Item Response Theory (IRT) into the scaling law estimation framework, building on the 1PL/2PL IRT models (Rasch, 1993; Lord, 1952) and the Beta regression literature (Ferrari & Cribari-Neto, 2004; Chen et al., 2019). It departs from prior IRT-based LM evaluation work (Truong et al., 2025; Hofmann et al., 2025; Kipnis et al., 2025), which uses Binary-IRT for efficient benchmarking, by (a) embedding IRT within the scaling law fitting procedure rather than treating it as a standalone evaluation tool, and (b) replacing Bernoulli loss with Beta loss to exploit the empirical probability responses (token probabilities, pass@1) that LMs uniquely provide. The traditional two-step scaling law fitting (Bhagia et al., 2024) is replaced by a single IRT-based factorization that reduces parameter complexity from O(M×N) to O(M+N).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 99.9% reduction in required queries (50 questions vs. 37,682) for reliable scaling law estimation has direct practical impact on pre-training data selection and test-time compute allocation, where evaluation cost is the primary bottleneck. The generalizability of latent ability θ across benchmarks sharing a measurement objective (e.g., ARC Easy → ARC Challenge, AIME 2024 → AIME 2025) opens a path toward amortized evaluation across related tasks. Key limitations include the requirement for pre-calibrated item difficulties (limiting applicability to new benchmarks), failure on homogeneous benchmarks (BoolQ, HellaSwag), and the preliminary scale of test-time experiments (12 LMs, 120 questions). Open questions include whether shared latent abilities can be fitted across benchmarks simultaneously, whether alternative probabilistic models (Beta-Binomial, zero-inflated) improve robustness, and whether the approach extends to other scaling paradigms beyond pre-training and repeated sampling.
