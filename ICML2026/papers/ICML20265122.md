## Problem

Autofocus for spike cameras is difficult because their sparse binary measurements lack reliable instantaneous spatial gradients, and sensor noise or illumination drift breaks the unimodal response assumptions underlying conventional focus measures. The only prior spike-camera autofocus method (spike dispersion) degrades under multi-peak or irregular responses in dynamic or long-sweep scenarios, leaving no robust, sensor-observable focus cue.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265122/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a raw spike-camera binary sequence S_t ∈ {0,1}^{H×W} sampled over a focus sweep, partitioned into B non-overlapping blocks of dt consecutive frames, along with a set of candidate spectral radii R. It outputs a single predicted focus block index b̂ (a scalar in {0,…,B−1}) identifying the best-focused state. No intensity image reconstruction or explicit edge map is required at any stage; all computation operates directly on accumulated spike blocks and their 2D FFT power spectra.

## Method

CEN exploits the observation that defocus acts as a low-pass filter, so during a focus sweep spectral energy migrates outward toward higher frequencies near focus and recedes under renewed defocus. For each accumulated block, it removes the DC component, computes an FFT-shifted power spectrum, and measures a bounded spectral centroid (the center of mass of radial frequency energy within radius r), yielding one scalar per block per candidate radius. A structure-consistency score—combining peak prominence, local curvature, width-to-length ratio, plateau penalty, and edge-bias penalty—selects the frequency bound whose response curve most faithfully reflects the expected migration pattern, without assuming unimodality. Finally, focus is localized by a weighted near-maximum centroid aggregation over blocks within a tolerance τ of the peak, avoiding brittle single-point argmax decisions. The entire pipeline is training-free, uses fixed hyperparameters across scenes, and requires no image reconstruction or spatial-domain sharpness estimation.

## Results

On the SAD real spike-camera dataset (Table 2), CEN achieves a mean absolute focus error of 2.80 blocks (0.3% relative error) across four scenes, outperforming the next-best direct baseline SD (6.67 blocks, 0.7%) and all spatial/temporal/frequency-domain baselines by a wide margin. On synthetic CVIA focus sweeps, CEN achieves zero error under moderate light and 1.30 cm mean error under low light, versus 0.24 cm and 4.60 cm for the best baselines (HF/MFDCT/SD). CEN also attains the highest ground-truth ratio (0.980) and lowest GT rank (15.73) in response discriminability, and maintains the lowest error under both static (3.14) and dynamic (2.50) motion as well as across all five illumination variation patterns.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CEN decisively outperforms all spatial-domain baselines (GRAD: 224.67, LAP: 283.47) and temporal-domain baselines (COUNT: 522.60) on mean absolute error, with the gap most pronounced on complex-texture scenes like Lily and Fan. Against the closest direct competitor, Spike Dispersion (SD, Su et al. CVPR 2024), CEN reduces mean error from 6.67 to 2.80 blocks, with the largest gains on dynamic scenes (2.50 vs. 9.50) and the Lily scene (2.50 vs. 12.00); SD remains competitive on the regular-pattern USAF target (7.67 vs. 3.44). The high-frequency energy baseline (HF) is slightly better than CEN on USAF (3.33 vs. 3.44) but degrades severely on Fan (88.50 vs. 2.00) due to burst-like spike activity. Among reconstruction-based pipelines (TFP/TFI + GRAD/LAP/RDF/DRDF), CEN achieves 2.80 mean error versus 28.20–386.13 for count-normalized reconstruction and 65.47–140.67 for TFI reconstruction, confirming that avoiding the intermediate image-reconstruction step preserves focus-relevant spectral structure. Overall, CEN wins or ties on 3 of 4 scenes and achieves the best mean across all evaluated conditions (static/dynamic, five illumination patterns, moderate/low light).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in spike-camera autofocus by identifying a frequency-domain observable (spectral energy migration) that prior work had not exploited. The only prior spike-camera autofocus method is Spike Dispersion (SD, Su et al. 2024), which operates in the temporal domain; all other baselines (GRAD, LAP, MFDCT, HF) are repurposed from conventional frame-based imaging. The spectral-centroid statistic itself is a classical signal-processing tool (analogous to audio pitch estimation), but its application as a focus cue on accumulated spike blocks, combined with the structure-consistent response identification for multi-peak handling, is novel. The paper explicitly positions itself as departing from both spatial-domain sharpness measures and reconstruction-based pipelines, arguing that the frequency-domain migration cue is directly sensor-observable without intermediate image reconstruction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Spike cameras are gaining traction in neuromorphic vision, and reliable autofocus is a prerequisite for practical deployment in robotics and intelligent sensing. The paper demonstrates that a simple bounded spectral centroid, computed via one 2D FFT per block, outperforms both temporal (SD) and spatial (GRAD, LAP) baselines across diverse scenes, motion types, and illumination patterns, suggesting that frequency-domain statistics are inherently more stable for sparse binary measurements. Key open questions include: (i) whether the O(N log N) FFT cost is acceptable under strict microsecond/microjoule edge constraints, (ii) how the method behaves in multi-depth scenes where spectral responses from different layers superpose, and (iii) whether adaptive block sizing or online (non-sweep) focus control can be integrated. The authors acknowledge that extreme motion-dominated or zero-illumination scenarios remain unsolved.
