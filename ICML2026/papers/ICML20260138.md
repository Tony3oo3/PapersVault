## Problem

Retrieval-augmented agents are evaluated on isolated, independently sampled queries, but deployed systems share a single embedding space serving heterogeneous user populations simultaneously. This paper identifies that geometric crowding in this shared space induces a fundamental, population-dependent failure mode: dense majority goals statistically saturate the retrieval neighborhood of semantically adjacent minority goals, and standard accuracy-driven learning dynamics amplify this static fragility into irreversible representational marginalization.

## Input / Output

The framework operates at the population level rather than per-query: it consumes a continuous user goal density field ρ_G over a d-dimensional embedding space, a document embedding set, and a shortlist budget L, and produces a retrieval success probability as a function of local density, a critical phase transition threshold N_c = L/I_min, and (in the dynamic setting) the temporal evolution of document embeddings toward a thresholded steady state ρ_D*. The unusual aspect is that the key diagnostic output is a system-level early-warning threshold rather than a per-query relevance score, and the 'input' is a density distribution rather than discrete query-document pairs.

## Method

The paper develops a two-part mean-field framework grounded in stochastic geometry and optimal transport. In the static analysis, document locations are modeled as a Poisson point process with intensity proportional to the goal density, yielding an exponential success probability P_success ≈ exp(−λ_D(x)V_d(ε)) and a sharp phase transition (Theorem 2.2) where minority retrieval collapses super-polynomially once majority density exceeds N_c = L/I_min. In the dynamic analysis, learning is modeled as a Wasserstein gradient flow of a free energy functional combining an interaction potential V[ρ_D] = ∫ρ_G(x)e^{−Cρ_D(x)}dx (derived as the thermodynamic limit of a standard recall-maximization objective) and entropic regularization, yielding a nonlinear Fokker-Planck/McKean-Vlasov equation. The central result (Theorem 3.1) proves that the steady state is a thresholded equilibrium ρ_D*(x) = max(0, (1/C)ln(ρ_G(x)/τ)), where minority goals below the critical density τ receive strictly zero document allocation, demonstrating that locally optimal accuracy-driven updates produce globally exclusive majority service.

## Results

The paper demonstrates a sharp phase transition in minority retrieval success across four modalities: on QQP, Recall@1 drops below 0.6 at N_maj ≈ 200,000 (collapse onset at N_maj > 25,000); on CIFAR-100, Recall@1 falls from near-perfect to below 0.3 at N_maj ≈ 12,000; on Wikipedia Movie Plots (stylistic retrieval), Recall@1 drops below 0.2 at N_maj ≈ 2,000; and on 20 Newsgroups, Recall@1 degrades to ~0.2 at N_maj ≈ 15,000. In the dynamic setting, the system exhibits a metastable regime (t < 1,500 steps) where minority Recall@1 remains above 0.8, followed by an irreversible collapse below 0.1 within 500 steps. Increasing the retrieval budget (k ∈ {1,2,5,10}) provides only modest improvement, and a realistic Cross-Encoder reranker fails to recover minority performance even when shortlist size L > 100, confirming that geometric crowding induces discriminative confusion robust to budget expansion.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that validates its own predictions rather than benchmarking against competing retrieval methods. The key empirical comparisons are: (1) Theoretical prediction (Eq. 1, Poisson point process) vs. empirical simulation — the empirical success probability closely tracks the theoretical curve, confirming the phase transition. (2) Uniform PPP baseline vs. clustered Gaussian Mixture (G=10, d=64) — clustering accelerates collapse dramatically, dropping the critical threshold from N_maj ≈ 200,000 to N_maj ≈ 5,000, confirming the PPP assumption is conservative. (3) Oracle reranker vs. realistic Cross-Encoder (MS-MARCO) on 20 Newsgroups — the Oracle benefits from larger shortlists (L), but the Cross-Encoder stagnates or degrades even as L > 100, because the embedding space becomes saturated with majority 'hard negatives' that create discriminative confusion. (4) Varying k ∈ {1,2,5,10} — higher k values provide only modest improvements, indicating the geometric crowding effect is not easily mitigated by expanding the retrieval budget. No external retrieval baselines (e.g., BM25, DPR, ColBERT) are compared, as the paper's contribution is identifying a fundamental geometric failure mode rather than proposing a new retrieval algorithm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes mean-field theory from neural network analysis (Mei et al., 2018) and McKean-Vlasov formulations for SGD dynamics (Mignacco et al., 2020) to a new domain: population-level retrieval in shared embedding spaces. It departs from the dominant RAG evaluation paradigm (i.i.d. query-by-query assessment) and from classical IR fairness analyses that attribute bias to training data or algorithmic design, instead showing that geometric crowding alone—via Poisson point process interference and Wasserstein gradient flow—produces endogenous marginalization. The connection to model collapse (Shumailov et al., 2023) is explicitly drawn but mechanistically distinct: here the failure arises from spatial resource competition in a fixed embedding manifold rather than from recursive data generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes RAG evaluation from an isolated-query metric to a collective population dynamic, providing a concrete critical threshold N_c that system operators could monitor as an early-warning signal for retrieval collapse. The dynamic model's prediction that standard accuracy-driven fine-tuning irreversibly erases minority content is a strong claim with practical urgency for deployed agents, though it is supported by a single simulation on one dataset. Key open questions include whether density-aware regularization or metadata pre-filtering can prevent the phase transition at scale, whether the thermodynamic-limit assumptions hold at realistic index sizes, and how the results extend to multi-document relevance and soft-relevance settings beyond the one-to-one correspondence assumption.
