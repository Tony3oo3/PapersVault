## Problem

RLVR can yield large reasoning gains from very few training instances, but its performance is extremely sensitive to which instances are chosen, making data selection a central bottleneck. Existing selection pipelines rely on training-time optimization signals (gradients, reward trajectories) and/or access to verifiable rewards or ground-truth answers over large candidate pools, which is costly and often infeasible in specialized domains like medical QA. No prior method can identify high-impact RLVR training instances from a large unlabeled pool without any training, labels, or reward evaluation at selection time.

## Input / Output

The method takes a large pool of unlabeled reasoning instances (e.g., 10.2K medical questions or 350 math problems), a base LLM, and a selection budget B (as low as 0.1% of the pool). For each candidate, it performs exactly one deterministic (T=0) greedy rollout and extracts only anchor-level hidden states at the start and end of the chain-of-thought span, requiring no ground-truth answers or reward computation. The output is a compact subset of B instances that are then annotated and used for downstream GRPO-based RLVR training; the selection itself is one-shot with no iterative select-label-train loop.

## Method

SHIFT computes a reasoning-induced representation shift (RIRS) for each candidate by extracting layer-averaged hidden states at the start and end of a single greedy CoT rollout and taking their difference Δ(x) = e(x) − s(x) ∈ R^D. The L2 norm of Δ(x), log-transformed for stability, serves as a training-free utility proxy motivated by implicit weight-transfer theory in transformer blocks (Dherin et al., 2025), where context-induced representation change bounds the magnitude of internal adaptation. For diversity, each instance is mapped to a normalized 2D feature φ(x) = [s(x); Δ(x)] / ‖[s(x); Δ(x)]‖₂ that captures both the instance-conditioned starting representation and the reasoning-induced transformation. Selection proceeds via a quality-weighted farthest-first CoreSet: the highest-utility instance seeds the set, and subsequent instances are added by maximizing q̃(x)·d(x,S), jointly prioritizing high RIRS magnitude and complementarity to already-selected examples. This single greedy pass scales linearly with pool size and requires only one decoding pass per candidate, in contrast to self-consistency baselines that need R stochastic rollouts.

## Results

On MATH-500 (in-domain) at a 2% selection budget, SHIFT achieves 62.67 Pass@1, outperforming Random (53.73), CoreSet (47.33), and Q-PPL (60.00), and even exceeding the full-data RLVR reference (66.00) on the OOD AMC benchmark (38.55 vs. 33.73). On MEDQA at 0.1% budget, SHIFT reaches 50.35 Pass@1 (vs. 45.28 Random, 43.91 CoreSet) and nearly matches full-data performance on MedXpertQA-U (12.22 vs. 12.39). At 4% on MATH-500, SHIFT improves to 64.67, narrowing the gap to full-data (66.00) while using only a small fraction of the pool.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SHIFT outperforms all training-free baselines on MATH-500 at 2%: it beats CoreSet (47.33) by +15.34, Cluster (44.67) by +18.00, Q-PPL (60.00) by +2.67, and Random (53.73) by +8.94. On the OOD AMC benchmark at 2%, SHIFT (38.55) surpasses every baseline including CoT Similarity (37.35) and SC-Entropy (31.33), and exceeds the full-data reference (33.73). On MEDQA at 0.1%, SHIFT (50.35) leads CoreSet (43.91) by +6.44, Random (45.28) by +5.07, and Q-PPL (43.99) by +6.36, while also achieving the best transfer scores on MedXpertQA-R (9.62 vs. 6.02 CoreSet) and MedXpertQA-U (12.22 vs. 7.64 CoreSet). At 4% on MATH-500, SHIFT (64.67) still leads in-domain but Cluster (40.96) edges it out on AMC (36.14), suggesting a utility-coverage tradeoff at larger budgets. On medical QA at 0.2%, SHIFT achieves the best MedXpertQA-R score (11.12) and ties for best MedXpertQA-U (12.22), while SC-Entropy leads on PUBMEDQA (72.40 vs. 70.80).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the farthest-first CoreSet selection procedure (Sener & Savarese, 2017) by embedding it in an RIRS-augmented feature space and adding a quality-weighting term derived from hidden-state dynamics. The RIRS construction follows Liang et al. (2025), who showed that start-to-end hidden-state deltas capture non-trivial structure of transformer computation, and is theoretically motivated by the implicit weight-transfer view of in-context learning in Dherin et al. (2025). The key departure from prior RLVR data selection (Wang et al., 2025c) and active learning is the removal of all training-time and reward-based signals: selection is performed entirely from a single inference-time rollout on an unlabeled pool, before any RL training begins.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SHIFT makes RLVR data selection feasible in annotation-scarce, specialized domains (e.g., medical QA) where obtaining verifiable rewards over a large candidate pool is prohibitively expensive, and where running proxy fine-tuning merely to rank instances defeats the purpose of data efficiency. The demonstrated correlation between pre-RL RIRS and single-instance RLVR gain (Spearman ρ=0.818) provides empirical grounding for the proxy, though the pilot study uses only 10 instances. Open questions include whether the layer-averaged RIRS is optimal (vs. layer-specific or multi-anchor dynamics), whether the approach generalizes beyond 8B-scale models, and how robust the selection is to degenerate or truncated rollouts. The theory-metric gap—block-level implicit-update theory versus trajectory-level surrogate—remains a conceptual limitation that future work should tighten.
