## Problem

Synthetic data has become a default response to privacy concerns in ML, but its use has quietly shifted from a claim of residual inference risk under stated assumptions to an appearance-based property inferred from data generation itself. As a result, privacy assurance in privacy-sensitive settings is often implicit, unverifiable, and unevenly distributed, with heightened exposure for rare and minority records, yet no shared standard requires explicit threat models or falsifiable privacy claims when synthetic data is invoked as privacy evidence.

## Input / Output

The audit consumes accepted paper PDFs from ICML, NeurIPS, and ACL (2024–2025; roughly 18,000 papers total) and applies a conservative, rule-based screening pipeline explicitly designed to undercount rather than overcount appearance-based claims. It produces tiered synthetic-data usage classifications (explicit construction vs. indirect/pseudo-labeled forms, with simulation-only papers excluded), privacy-framing categories (no privacy language, privacy language without verification, privacy language paired with explicit verification), and a proposed Minimum Privacy Claim Standard specifying three conditions: threat model, scope, and evidence.

## Method

The paper combines a descriptive empirical audit with a conceptual re-grounding argument. The audit applies a multi-stage screening pipeline to published PDFs: detecting explicit or indirect synthetic data usage, identifying privacy-related language, and checking for references to formal mechanisms (differential privacy, adversarial audits, bounded guarantees) or empirical verification (inference attacks, disaggregated risk reporting). The conceptual contribution maps three classes of existing tools—explicit risk bounding (DP), empirical inference evaluation (TAPAS, membership/attribute inference attacks), and risk heterogeneity analysis (disaggregated reporting, correlation inference)—to the specific failure modes identified (structural false negatives, tail-risk redistribution, institutional non-falsifiability), then distills them into a Minimum Privacy Claim Standard requiring that any privacy claim grounded in synthetic data specify a concrete threat model, clarify which risks are addressed versus unaddressed, and provide at least one evidentiary mechanism. The paper further documents how industry vendor claims, legal classification practices, and conference author guidelines collectively normalize the appearance-based framing, and engages three alternative views (pragmatic risk reduction, negative-evidence sufficiency, disclosure-only norms) to delineate the boundary of its argument.

## Results

The empirical audit of ICML, NeurIPS, and ACL (2024–2025) shows that synthetic data usage is consistently present across all venues and years, with explicit synthetic data construction (Tier 3) and indirect forms (Tier 2) both appearing at non-trivial rates. Among papers that use synthetic data, a substantial fraction invoke privacy-related language (e.g., reduced exposure, safer data sharing) without pairing those claims with any explicit verification mechanism. In ICML 2025 specifically, only a small minority of synthetic-using papers pair privacy statements with formal guarantees or adversarial evaluation, while at NeurIPS the near-universal presence of privacy language is largely attributable to mandatory ethics and reproducibility statements rather than to privacy being a central scientific claim of the work.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it contrasts two paradigms for privacy assurance in synthetic data: appearance-based claims (privacy inferred from non-identity, novelty, or absence of verbatim replicas) versus evidence-based claims (privacy grounded in explicit threat models, bounded inference risk, or formal guarantees such as differential privacy). The audit reveals that the vast majority of synthetic-using papers in ICML, NeurIPS, and ACL (2024–2025) fall into the appearance-based category, with only a small minority providing any form of adversarial evaluation or formal privacy mechanism. The paper further contrasts industry practice (where verification is limited to inexpensive checks like verbatim-reproduction tests or organizational certifications such as SOC 2/ISO 27001) with regulatory guidance from the EDPB and EDPS, which explicitly rejects blanket anonymity claims and requires case-by-case assessment of residual inference risk. Across the three tool categories the paper identifies—explicit risk bounding (e.g., DP), empirical inference evaluation (e.g., TAPAS, membership inference attacks), and risk heterogeneity analysis (e.g., disaggregated tail-risk reporting)—none is routinely applied in the audited corpus, leaving the gap between claimed and verified privacy unaddressed in current ML research norms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not extend a specific technical method but rather repositions the well-established adversarial-risk framework from differential privacy (Dwork et al., 2014; Clifton & Tassa, 2013) as the normative standard for evaluating synthetic data releases. It draws on the membership inference and extraction attack literature (Hayes et al., 2019; Carlini et al., 2021; Nasr et al., 2025) as evidence that appearance-based audits systematically miss leakage, and on synthetic data auditing tools like TAPAS (Houssiau et al., 2022) and DP-based generators (Abadi et al., 2016) as the existing toolkit that should be invoked. The paper's novel contribution is a descriptive empirical audit of ICML/NeurIPS/ACL 2024–2025 papers showing that synthetic data is routinely deployed in privacy-sensitive contexts without explicit threat models or falsifiable claims, coupled with a proposed Minimum Privacy Claim Standard that repurposes existing verification tools into a lightweight disclosure norm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a structural governance gap: ML venues, vendors, and increasingly legislatures treat synthetic data generation as sufficient privacy evidence, while regulatory bodies (EDPB, EDPS) explicitly reject this presumption, creating a compliance blind spot that disproportionately exposes rare and minority records. The Minimum Privacy Claim Standard is deliberately lightweight—requiring only that authors state a threat model, scope, and at least one evidentiary mechanism—making it feasible to adopt in review without new infrastructure. Open questions include how to operationalize the standard across heterogeneous data types and use cases, whether it will be adopted by venue policies, and how to handle the tension between the utility-preserving properties that make synthetic data attractive and the verification burden that rigorous privacy claims impose. A key limitation is that the audit is descriptive and rule-based; it documents framing patterns but does not measure actual inference risk in the audited papers.
