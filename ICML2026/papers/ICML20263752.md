## Problem

Existing prompt sensitivity metrics for LLMs only compare final outputs (e.g., accuracy or consistency), revealing consequences but not the internal mechanisms behind sensitivity. This coarse-grained view cannot explain why semantically irrelevant prompt changes destabilize model behavior, nor can it detect latent instability that remains invisible when the final prediction happens to stay the same.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263752/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a multiple-choice question (question words Q and option words M) as the input variable set, a pair of semantically equivalent prompt templates (T and T̂, differing only in formatting like letter case or separators), and a target LLM. Words in the prompt template are treated as fixed background context rather than input variables. The output is a per-sample decomposition of the LLM's log-odds score into interaction effects I_S for every subset S of input words, a classification of each salient interaction as stable or unstable (opposite sign, same sign with large magnitude shift, or same sign with similar magnitude), and an aggregate IPS score computed as the symmetric mean absolute percentage error of normalized interaction effects across all samples and template pairs.

## Method

The paper decomposes an LLM's scalar output score v(x) into a sum of AND-OR interaction effects via a surrogate logical model φ(x), guaranteed exact by the Universal Matching Property (Theorem 3.1) and sparse by the sparsity property (only a small fraction of 2^n interactions carry salient effects). The proposed IPS metric (Eq. 3) quantifies prompt sensitivity by computing the symmetric mean absolute percentage change in normalized interaction effects between two semantically equivalent prompt templates, averaged over all salient interactions (threshold τ=0.1), all samples, and all template pairs. Applied to 50 open-source LLMs across 6 families on ARC and MMLU, the metric reveals that supervised fine-tuning, larger model scale, dense (vs. MoE) architecture, and few-shot learning all reduce prompt sensitivity. The key mechanistic finding is that all four factors reduce sensitivity primarily by stabilizing low-order interactions (involving ≤ n/3 input variables), while high-order interactions remain highly sensitive regardless of these factors—indicating that LLMs can easily learn stable simple patterns but struggle to stabilize complex multi-variable relationships.

## Results

Across 50 open-source LLMs evaluated on ARC and MMLU, the proposed IPS metric ranges from 1.268 (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, least sensitive) to 1.752 (Mistral-7B-v0.3, most sensitive). The IPS score exhibits a significant negative correlation with output consistency, confirming alignment with traditional metrics while offering finer-grained resolution. When the LLM's final output changes between two semantically equivalent prompts, approximately 50% of salient interactions reverse sign and only 2.7–6.8% remain stable; strikingly, even when the output is unchanged, 60–80% of salient interactions are still unstable. The IPS ranking is robust to the salience threshold τ, with an average Spearman rank correlation of 0.9905 and Pearson correlation of 0.9957 across 16 threshold values (0.05–0.20).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external prompt-sensitivity baselines; instead, it validates the IPS metric against the standard output-consistency metric, showing a significant negative correlation (Figure 4b) that confirms IPS captures the same phenomenon at a finer granularity. Internally, the paper compares four model configurations: (1) instruct/chat models consistently show lower IPS than their base counterparts across Llama, Mistral, Qwen, OLMo, and InternLM families; (2) within each model series, IPS decreases monotonically as parameter count increases (e.g., Qwen2.5 from 0.5B to 72B); (3) dense models outperform MoE models in stability within the same family, an effect that persists even when active parameter counts are matched (Table 7, Appendix I); (4) moving from 0-shot to 1-shot yields the largest single drop in IPS, with diminishing returns for 2-shot and 3-shot. The key mechanistic finding is that all four factors reduce sensitivity predominantly at the low-order interaction level, while high-order interactions remain highly sensitive across all configurations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the game-theoretic interaction framework (Li & Zhang, 2023; Ren et al., 2024a) — originally developed to explain DNN inference logic via sparse AND-OR interaction patterns — as a fine-grained diagnostic tool for prompt sensitivity in LLMs. Prior prompt sensitivity work (Sclar et al., 2024; Chatterjee et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024) relies exclusively on output-level metrics (accuracy shifts, output consistency), which the authors argue reveal only consequences rather than causes. The paper's IPS metric and order-level decomposition are novel applications of the interaction framework to this domain, while the masking strategy follows Cheng et al. (2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The IPS metric offers a per-sample, interaction-level diagnostic that exposes latent instability invisible to output-level metrics, giving practitioners a more granular tool for auditing LLM robustness. The counterintuitive finding that all four robustness factors (SFT, scale, dense architecture, few-shot) stabilize already-robust low-order interactions while leaving inherently unstable high-order interactions largely untouched opens a clear research direction: designing training objectives that specifically target high-order interaction stability. Limitations include the O(2^n) computational cost of exhaustive interaction computation (mitigated but not eliminated by sparsity), the reliance on a specific masking token per model, and the fact that the mechanistic explanation for why low-order interactions are easier to stabilize remains open.
