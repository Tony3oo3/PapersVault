## Problem

End-to-end neural operators generalize poorly under parameter shifts and cannot reach the 10⁻⁵ relative-residual regime needed for high-fidelity sparse solves, while existing hybrid neural-iterative schemes suffer from a stagnation problem: residuals undergo substantial distribution shift across iterations, causing per-iteration residual reduction to plateau after only a few correction steps. No prior neural solver performs fully iterative, residual-aware subspace corrections that remain effective as the residual landscape evolves.

## Input / Output

At each solver iteration, the method consumes the current residual vector (L2-normalized per graph), the sparse matrix A as a directed graph with scalar edge attributes A_ij, and physical node coordinates encoded via a learnable positional MLP. The solver runs iteratively with early stopping (target relative residual or stagnation detection at ≤5% per-step reduction), and the active projection subspace is bounded by a sliding-window hyperparameter K_max to control memory and dense linear-algebra cost. The final output is the solution vector x, with the residual updated in-place at each step.

## Method

G-RANS represents the residual on the matrix graph and uses a Residual-Aware Basis Generator (RBG), instantiated as Poly-GAT (a multi-scale GATv2 backbone), to emit blocks of correction directions from multiple propagation depths, capturing multi-hop polynomial features of the operator conditioned on the current residual. A differentiable numerical projection (QR orthonormalization followed by least-squares minimization) then computes the residual-minimizing update within the predicted subspace, with block re-orthogonalization and a sliding window maintaining a historical orthonormal basis across iterations. Training is fully self-supervised via a three-stage progressive bootstrap curriculum: residuals generated along the solver's own trajectory are recycled as fixed inputs at successive stages, inducing a native multi-stage residual distribution that mitigates stagnation. A key theoretical result shows that under a Linear Identity Configuration (identity activations, edge-coupled attention weights set to A_ij·I, raw residual as node feature), the Poly-GAT architecture exactly recovers the Krylov subspace, making each G-RANS update mathematically equivalent to one cycle of GMRES(L+1).

## Results

On n≈1000 sparse FEM systems across four PDE families (Poisson, advection-diffusion, Helmholtz, reaction-diffusion), G-RANS consistently reduces the relative residual to the 10⁻⁵ regime (e.g., 2.80×10⁻⁵ on Poisson, 4.00×10⁻⁵ on Helmholtz) within ~23–25 neural iterations. Within this accuracy regime, G-RANS achieves 2–4× wall-clock speedups over GMRES (e.g., 3.03×–3.53× on Poisson), 2.2×–2.8× over FGMRES, and 2.6×–3.5× over MINRES (symmetric cases). Under ±70% coefficient perturbation, relative residuals degrade only modestly (e.g., 4.50×10⁻⁵ on Poisson vs. 2.80×10⁻⁵ at ±10%), and cross-equation transfer (e.g., React.-Diff.→Poisson) reaches 2.1×10⁻⁵, outperforming same-equation training in some cases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against classical Krylov solvers, G-RANS is consistently faster within the 10⁻⁵ relative-residual regime: it achieves 2.98×–3.60× speedup over GMRES, 2.21×–2.79× over FGMRES, and 2.59×–3.45× over MINRES (symmetric cases only), with the advantage growing at larger n (up to ~5× at n≈2000) and under stronger coefficient shifts. Against end-to-end neural operators, G-RANS is orders of magnitude more accurate: on n≈1000 systems at ±10% variation, GNO plateaus at relative residuals of 8.4×10⁻²–1.15×10⁻¹ and FNO at 5.4×10⁻²–2.27×10⁻¹, while G-RANS reaches 2.4×10⁻⁵–4.8×10⁻⁵, a gap of roughly three orders of magnitude. G-RANS also uses far fewer parameters (56,260) than GNO (483,937) or FNO (601,825). Under Jacobi preconditioning, G-RANS still maintains 2.0×–2.3× speedups over GMRES and FGMRES on the more challenging Helmholtz and reaction-diffusion systems. The ablation (sub-NSS, removing both multi-scale and multi-stage components) stagnates at ~3×10⁻² relative residual after ~9 iterations, confirming that both the residual-aware multi-scale basis and the progressive bootstrap curriculum are essential for reaching the 10⁻⁵ regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

G-RANS positions itself at the intersection of graph neural networks and Krylov subspace methods, departing from prior neural approaches to sparse linear systems that either learn fixed initializations/warm starts (NeurKItt, NOWS) or learned preconditioners (GNP, NP-KSG) while still relying on classical iterations for final accuracy. Architecturally, it builds on GATv2 (Brody et al., 2022) and GAT (Veličković et al., 2018), repurposing the attention mechanism as a residual-conditioned, multi-scale basis generator (Poly-GAT) that replaces the deterministic Arnoldi process. The key conceptual departure is the theoretical result showing that under a Linear Identity Configuration, G-RANS exactly recovers GMRES (Saad & Schultz, 1986), establishing it as a neural generalization of the Krylov framework rather than a separate paradigm. The progressive bootstrap training curriculum is a novel self-supervised strategy specifically designed to counter the residual distribution shift that causes stagnation in prior neural iterative schemes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

G-RANS is the first neural solver to achieve 10⁻⁵ relative residuals on both nonsymmetric and indefinite sparse systems without SPD assumptions, closing a significant accuracy gap over end-to-end neural operators (which plateau at ~10⁻²) while delivering 2–4× wall-clock speedups over GMRES/FGMRES/MINRES in that accuracy regime. The strong cross-equation generalization (e.g., React.-Diff. → Poisson achieving 2.1×10⁻⁵, better than Poisson-trained) and robustness to ±70% coefficient shifts suggest the learned subspace captures operator-agnostic structure. Limitations include evaluation restricted to 2nd-order elliptic PDEs at n ≤ ~10⁴, compressed speedups (~1.15×) at 3D n~10⁴, and the theoretical guarantee only applies under the LIC rather than the trained non-linear regime. Open questions include whether the framework extends to higher-order PDEs, non-elliptic systems, or much larger-scale problems, and whether the GMRES-order polynomial basis can be surpassed by richer learned subspaces.
