## Problem

Evolutionary sequence design methods are sample-efficient but apply random mutations that ignore sequential dependencies, scaling poorly for long sequences. RL and generative model approaches capture sequence structure but require large training datasets and often exceed 10² oracle evaluations to converge. No existing method combines the sample efficiency of evolutionary search with the ability to exploit sequential structure under tightly constrained oracle budgets (B ∈ {20, 50, 100}).

## Input / Output

The method takes an initial dataset of biological sequences (proteins of 20 amino acids with lengths 20–5388, or DNA 8-mers over 4 nucleotides) paired with continuous property values, a black-box oracle (analytical expression, PSIPRED, or ESM-2-based model), and a per-iteration budget B. Over 10 active-learning iterations it produces B new sequences per round, each evaluated by the oracle, along with position-wise attribution scores and identified critical/non-critical motifs. The oracle is assumed expensive (wet-lab or high-fidelity simulation), and the method must operate without any pre-trained generative model or large labeled dataset.

## Method

IDEAS integrates an explainable model (COLOR, a compositional linear-operation architecture with ~10K–44K parameters) into an evolutionary loop. At each iteration, COLOR is trained on the accumulated dataset to produce position-wise attribution scores φᵢ; contiguous sub-segments (motifs of size m) are scored by averaging φᵢ over their span, and the top-B sequences' motifs are aggregated into a shared motif library M. Mutations are then interpretable: a starting position j is sampled from a softmax over negative motif-level scores (τ₁ controls exploitation vs. random position selection), and a replacement motif s is sampled from a softmax over positive motif attribution scores (τ₂ controls exploitation vs. random motif selection). Each top sequence receives two mutations—an exploitative one (τ₁=τ₂=1) and an exploratory one (τ₁=τ₂=1000)—replacing low-attribution regions with high-attribution motifs from the library. The framework is XAI-agnostic: any faithful attribution method (DeepLift, Integrated Gradients, GradientSHAP, etc.) can be swapped in as a plug-and-play component without architectural changes.

## Results

IDEAS achieves the best AUC (area under the property-vs-iteration curve) across all eight datasets at B=50, with headline values of 1639.84 on Aliphatic Index, 37.36 on GRAVY Index, 5.83 on α-helix ratio, and 8.90 on Thermostability. Across all datasets and oracle budgets (B ∈ {20, 50, 100}), IDEAS outperforms all seven baselines by an average of 19% in AUC. The restricted variant IDEAS-X (exploitative mutations only) achieves within 2% of IDEAS's AUC but 41% lower diversity, confirming that exploratory mutations contribute to diversity without sacrificing optimization speed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IDEAS wins on all eight datasets against every baseline. Against the strongest evolutionary baseline AdaLead, IDEAS improves AUC by 65% on Aliphatic Index (1639.84 vs. 996.55), 39% on GRAVY Index (37.36 vs. 26.90), 22% on AAV (4.37 vs. 3.59), and 38% on Thermostability (8.90 vs. 6.46), with smaller margins on α-helix (5.83 vs. 5.71) and TF1 (4.72 vs. 4.67). Against CMA-ES, IDEAS leads by 138% on Aliphatic Index and 79% on GRAVY Index, though CMA-ES is competitive on TF2 (4.65 vs. 5.82) and TF3 (4.00 vs. 4.84). The RL baselines DynaPPO and GFN-AL are the weakest performers on most datasets (e.g., DynaPPO scores only 188.91 on Aliphatic Index vs. IDEAS's 1639.84) and could not be run on AAV or Thermostability due to prohibitive runtime. The generative baselines Cbas and Dbas trail IDEAS by 266% and 222% on Aliphatic Index respectively, and by 250%+ on GRAVY Index. Bayesian Optimization (BO) is consistently outperformed, with IDEAS achieving 3.0× higher AUC on Aliphatic Index and 2.4× on GRAVY Index.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the COLOR explainable model (Pandey et al., 2025), modifying it to produce directional (signed) attribution scores rather than magnitude-only scores. Conceptually, it departs from prior XAI-in-biology work (Seitz et al., 2024/2025; De Almeida et al., 2022) that used attribution scores for post-hoc biological insight, by repurposing them as an active design signal that guides which positions to mutate and which motifs to insert. It also bridges two previously separate lineages: evolutionary sequence design (AdaLead, CMA-ES), which is sample-efficient but ignores sequential structure, and RL/generative approaches (DynaPPO, GFN-AL, Dbas, Cbas), which capture structure but require large training sets or many oracle evaluations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

IDEAS addresses a practical bottleneck in biotech: wet-lab oracle evaluations are expensive and limited, making sample efficiency critical. The 19% average AUC improvement over seven baselines across eight tasks and three budgets, combined with 16–50× speed advantages over RL baselines, makes it a practical tool for constrained design cycles. The plug-and-play XAI integration (any faithful attribution method works within 2%) and robustness to attribution noise (66% PCC drop → only 8% AUC loss) lower the barrier to adoption. Key limitations include lower diversity and novelty than RL/generative methods due to the locality of mutations, and the authors identify integrating attribution-guided signals within learned generative frameworks as the natural next step.
