## Problem

Large Reasoning Models achieve high final-answer accuracy, but their internal reasoning strategies remain poorly understood, and prior analyses either focus solely on accuracy or use flat graph representations that entangle structural dependencies with behavioral transitions. The paper asks: how can we represent an LLM's reasoning trace to enable comprehensive, quantitative comparison of exploration, exploitation, verification, overthinking, and forgetting across models, tasks, and training regimes?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265723/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a raw chain-of-thought reasoning trace and the original problem statement as input. ReJump-Extractor (powered by Gemini 2.5 Pro) first produces a tree-layer JSON where each node encodes a partial solution with 'problem', 'parent', and 'result' fields, then a jump-layer JSON list of transitions with 'from', 'to', and 'category' (calc/verify/backtrack) fields. Downstream, the representation yields six per-instance behavior metrics, task-level aggregates, and pairwise tree/jump similarity scores; it also drives Best-of-N response selection and prompt selection without requiring ground-truth labels.

## Method

ReJump decomposes a reasoning trace into two layers: a tree of partial-solution nodes connected by prerequisite edges (structure), and a jump sequence recording the chronological visitation order with each transition labeled as calculation, verification, or backtracking (action). This separation makes behaviors like backtracking vs. verification vs. forgetting directly definable, unlike flat graph encodings where a backward edge is ambiguous. Six metrics—solution count, jump distance, success rate, verification rate, overthinking rate, and forgetting rate—are computed from the two layers, and two similarity metrics (Zhang-Shasha tree edit distance for structure, Jensen-Shannon divergence on 3×3 action-transition matrices for behavior) enable cross-model comparison. An LLM agent performs the extraction in two prompted passes (tree then jump), achieving >0.94 similarity with human annotations on Game of 24 and >80% per-trace accuracy on MATH-500. The representation is then used both diagnostically (comparing LRMs, distilled vs. teacher models, RL checkpoints, in-context examples) and prescriptively (ReJump-guided BoN selection and prompt selection yield +6.8–9.1% pass@1 gains on Game of 24).

## Results

ReJump-guided Best-of-N selection improves pass@1 on Game of 24 by +6.8% to +9.1% over majority vote: QwQ-32B goes from 0.76 (MV) to 0.82 (BoN w.ReJump), and Phi-4-reasoning-plus from 0.77 to 0.84. ReJump-Extractor achieves 0.943 tree similarity and 0.940 jump similarity with ground-truth annotations on Game of 24, and 80–81% pass@1 accuracy in human evaluation on MATH-500. Across the five LRMs evaluated, models with comparable final accuracy (e.g., DeepSeek-R1 and Grok 3 Mini Beta) exhibit substantially different reasoning profiles in exploration (d_jump), success rate, and verification rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For the application experiments on Game of 24, BoN w.ReJump outperforms all four baselines (Majority Vote, Longest Trace, Most Solutions, and Weighted MV w.ReJump) on both QwQ-32B and Phi-4-reasoning-plus, with pass@1 gains of +4 to +7 percentage points over MV. Weighted MV w.ReJump is the strongest baseline, already improving over MV by +3–4 points, but BoN w.ReJump adds a further +2 points by selecting the single highest-exploration trace rather than averaging. In the broader model comparison (Sec. 5.1), DeepSeek-R1 and Grok 3 Mini Beta achieve the highest pass@1 but through opposite strategies: Grok uses shorter jumps with higher per-attempt success, while DeepSeek-R1 explores more broadly with longer jumps. Distilled models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen) move closer to their teacher in both tree similarity (0.715→0.728 on MATH-500) and jump similarity (0.771→0.878), confirming inheritance of reasoning structure. In-context reasoning examples increase jump similarity to DeepSeek-R1 but do not consistently improve accuracy or tree similarity, indicating they teach fine-grained actions rather than problem decomposition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Tree-of-Thought framework (Yao et al., 2023) for its tree structure but departs from prior graph-based reasoning analyses (Minegishi et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a) by explicitly separating the structural hierarchy of partial solutions (tree layer) from the model's traversal behavior (jump layer). Where prior flat-graph representations entangle structural dependencies with behavioral transitions—making it ambiguous whether a backward edge indicates verification, backtracking, or forgetting—ReJump's two-layer decomposition makes each behavior directly definable and measurable. The paper also situates itself within the growing literature on overthinking/underthinking (Chen et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), thought calibration (Wu et al., 2025), and RL effects on reasoning diversity (Yue et al., 2025; Dang et al., 2025), providing a unified representation under which all these phenomena can be quantified and compared.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ReJump offers a practical, interpretable framework for diagnosing *why* models reason differently beyond final accuracy, which is increasingly important as LRMs proliferate with opaque training pipelines. The finding that RL progressively aligns reasoning behavior with task-specific demands (more exploration for search-heavy tasks, more exploitation for proof-like tasks) provides a mechanistic lens for understanding how training shapes reasoning, with implications for curriculum design and model selection. Key limitations include the reliance on a separate capable LLM (Gemini 2.5 Pro) for extraction, which is costly at scale; similarity metrics that capture only structural and transition-level information while missing semantic content and temporal ordering; and the need for manual granularity decisions when applying ReJump to new tasks. Open questions include whether the representation generalizes to non-mathematical or multi-modal reasoning, whether a small fine-tuned extractor can match the quality of a frontier LLM, and whether the six metrics form a complete behavioral taxonomy or miss important dimensions.
