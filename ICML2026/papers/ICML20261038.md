## Problem

Existing LLM decoding strategies suffer from a fundamental mismatch between computation and task difficulty: sampling methods commit to a single path with fixed hyperparameters, while search-based methods (beam search, best-of-n) expend a uniform budget regardless of where the model is actually uncertain. No prior approach adaptively allocates search width based on the model's own per-step uncertainty, and no theoretical guarantees exist for why entropy-proportional branching should outperform fixed-width search under a matched compute budget.

## Input / Output

EDEN takes a text prompt and a language model (open-source with full logits, or closed-source exposing only top-k logprobs, k ≤ 20) and produces a single highest-scoring decoded text sequence of at most T tokens. The method consumes the model's next-token probability distribution at every generation step to compute entropy, and for closed-API settings it tolerates entropy estimation error up to 0.5/B_max, requiring only sublinear samples in vocabulary size. The output is scored by a length-penalized cumulative log-likelihood (Score_α = s(y)/t^α), with branch-and-bound pruning ensuring the returned sequence is optimal within the explored search space.

## Method

At each decoding step, EDEN estimates the Shannon entropy H_t of the next-token distribution and converts it into a branching factor via the monotone rule B_t = max(1, ⌊B_max · H_t / log|V|⌋), expanding more candidates at high-entropy (uncertain) steps and following a near-greedy path at low-entropy steps. The search is a branch-and-bound beam search that maintains admissible upper and lower bounds on the length-normalized log-likelihood score, pruning any partial sequence whose upper bound falls below the best lower bound seen so far. The key theoretical result (Theorem 3.4) proves that under sub-Gaussian estimation noise and a distributional Lipschitz continuity assumption, any branching factor monotone in entropy achieves strictly lower cumulative regret than any fixed branching factor when entropies vary across steps, with explicit regret rates of O(T·exp(−cmΔ²)) for constant budget and sublinear/bounded regret under logarithmic budget scaling. For closed-source models, the paper derives sublinear sample-complexity bounds Θ(|V|/(ε log|V|)) for entropy estimation sufficient to preserve correct branching decisions.

## Results

On Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, EDEN achieves the best average Friedman rank (1.25, p=0.012) across four benchmarks: 80.5% on GSM8K, 32.8% on MATH-500, 31.3% on HumanEval, and 7.4% on SciBench. Critically, EDEN matches or exceeds beam search (width 3) accuracy while using 50–50% fewer expansions (e.g., 598 vs. 1200 on GSM8K, 840 vs. 1200 on MATH-500). A Bayesian hierarchical model assigns EDEN a 75% posterior probability of being the best method overall, with ≥96% pairwise dominance over all sampling-based baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EDEN outperforms all four sampling-based baselines (Top-k, Top-p/Nucleus, Top-H, Min-p) by 2–11 percentage points across all four datasets, with the largest gaps on MATH-500 (e.g., 32.8% vs. 23.0% for Top-k) and SciBench (7.4% vs. 4.5% for Top-H). Against best-of-n and majority voting, EDEN wins on every dataset, with majority voting particularly weak on HumanEval (19.6% vs. EDEN's 31.3%). Compared to Diverse Beam Search (width 3), EDEN achieves higher accuracy on all four benchmarks (e.g., 80.5% vs. 77.5% on GSM8K) while using roughly half the expansions. The closest competitor is standard Beam Search (width 3): EDEN slightly trails on GSM8K (80.5% vs. 81.5%) but exceeds it on MATH-500 (32.8% vs. 29.2%), HumanEval (31.3% vs. 28.8%), and SciBench (7.4% vs. 6.9%), all while using 598–1018 expansions versus beam search's fixed 1200, yielding a 77% Bayesian posterior probability of superiority over beam search.

## Relation to Prior Work

EDEN extends the adaptive beam search lineage (Yu-Hsiang et al., 2018) by replacing fixed or heuristically-chosen beam widths with an entropy-driven branching factor, and incorporates the branch-and-bound pruning framework of Rush et al. (2013) for admissible score bounds. It departs from a growing body of entropy-based decoding work (Simonds 2025, Li et al. 2025/2026, Entropix 2024, HARP 2025) that uses entropy for binary decisions—whether to branch, switch models, or insert chain-of-thought—by instead treating entropy as a continuous signal that monotonically modulates the branching factor, backed by explicit regret bounds and sample-complexity guarantees rather than ad hoc thresholds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EDEN offers a principled, plug-and-play decoding strategy that provably outperforms fixed-width search under a shared compute budget, with the practical advantage of working under closed-API constraints via top-k logprobs. Key limitations include evaluation only up to 7B parameters, heterogeneous branching complicating GPU parallelism, and reliance on the model's output distribution carrying meaningful uncertainty signal (a concern for poorly calibrated or undertrained models). Open directions include integrating process reward models, extending to higher-level abstractions like tool calling, and validating at larger model scales where high-entropy reasoning forks are known to persist.
