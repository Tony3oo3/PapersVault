## Problem

While prior work has observed empirical performance improvements when using quantum-processor-generated distributions as latent spaces in generative models, the conditions under which these improvements occur and whether they are genuinely connected to quantum properties remain unclear. The paper addresses two gaps: the absence of a theoretical framework linking the computational complexity of the latent distribution to achievable data distributions, and the lack of rigorous apples-to-apples benchmarking against relevant classical baselines that isolate quantum interference as the causal factor.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260156/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The latent input is a discrete photon-count vector of length L (e.g., [1,1,0,1,0,0] for 3 photons in 6 channels) produced by a boson sampling circuit, where N identical photons are distributed across L output channels. The quantum and photonic variants share the same circuit topology but differ in whether photons are indistinguishable (multi-photon interference) or distinguishable (independent single-photon routing), isolating quantum interference as the sole variable. The generator maps these latent vectors to molecular graphs (QM9) or discrete data points (synthetic quantum dataset), with the generator architecture constrained to non-decreasing layer widths and LeakyReLU activations to satisfy invertibility conditions from the theory.

## Method

The paper establishes a theoretical result (Theorem 1) showing that if a generator network is invertible and Lipschitz-continuous, the pushforward of a quantum latent distribution (in complexity class Q) cannot be efficiently sampled classically, meaning quantum latents expand the set of reachable data distributions beyond what classical latents can achieve. This is formalized using a GAN-induced Wasserstein distance metric, yielding a corollary that no classical latent distribution can approximate certain quantum pushforward distributions within any fixed epsilon. Empirically, the authors benchmark four latent distributions (quantum, photonic, Bernoulli, Gaussian) in a MolGAN-based GAN on QM9 and a synthetic boson-sampling dataset, using 20 seeds per configuration and re-sampling optical circuits per seed to avoid circuit-specific effects. The quantum-vs-photonic comparison isolates multi-photon interference as the performance driver, and results are validated on a real ORCA PT-2 photonic processor (16 photons, 32 channels) to confirm transferability from simulation to hardware.

## Results

On QM9 with d_z=16, the quantum latent distribution achieves the best FCD (1.160±0.06), generating 2522±65 valid and 1331±37 novel molecules out of 10k attempts, outperforming all classical baselines on every metric. The advantage narrows at larger latent sizes: at d_z=48, the quantum latent still leads on #Valid (1528±65) and #Novel (856±40) but Bernoulli slightly edges it on FCD (1.671 vs 1.696). On the synthetic quantum dataset, the quantum latent achieves the lowest L1 distance to nearest integers (0.036±0.001) versus 0.041 for distinguishable photons and 0.061–0.065 for Gaussian/Bernoulli. A real ORCA PT-2 photonic processor (16 photons, 32 channels) closely matches the simulated quantum latent (FCD 1.410 vs 1.414 at d_z=32), confirming transferability to hardware.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four latent distributions: quantum (indistinguishable photons in a boson sampler), photonic (distinguishable photons, isolating multi-photon interference as the sole difference), Bernoulli (random bits), and Gaussian (standard). At d_z=16 on QM9, the quantum latent dominates all three baselines on every metric, with the largest gap over Bernoulli (FCD 1.160 vs 1.822; #Valid 2522 vs 1244) and a clear margin over photonic (FCD 1.160 vs 1.333; #Valid 2522 vs 1954). At d_z=32, quantum and photonic are nearly tied on FCD (1.536 vs 1.533) but quantum still leads on #Valid (+158) and #Novel (+32); Gaussian and Bernoulli fall further behind. At d_z=48, Bernoulli slightly beats quantum on FCD (1.671 vs 1.696) but quantum retains the lead on #Valid (+72 over Bernoulli, +221 over photonic) and #Novel (+40 over Bernoulli, +110 over photonic). The real ORCA PT-2 processor (Table 4) reproduces the simulated quantum results almost exactly (FCD 1.410 vs 1.414), and both outperform all classical baselines, confirming that the advantage is not an artifact of idealized simulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GAN-induced distance formalism from Hu et al. (2023) to establish complexity-theoretic conditions under which quantum latent distributions enable generative models to produce data distributions inaccessible to classical latents. It builds on MolGAN (De Cao & Kipf, 2018) as the base architecture for molecular generation and on prior quantum-GAN feasibility studies (Li et al., 2021; Kao et al., 2023; Rudolph et al., 2022) that demonstrated empirical improvements but lacked theoretical grounding or controlled classical baselines. The key departure from prior work is the isolation of multi-photon quantum interference as the specific mechanism (via a distinguishable-photon control) and the provision of a rigorous apples-to-apples benchmarking protocol across multiple latent types, circuit architectures, and a real photonic processor.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical justification for why quantum latent distributions can improve generative model performance, moving the field beyond empirical demonstrations. However, the practical impact is currently limited: the advantage is modest, narrows with larger latent dimensions, is dataset-dependent (negative results on CIFAR-10/StyleGAN and under different QM9 hyperparameters), and is confined to GANs at small scale (≤48 dimensions). Open questions include whether the advantage persists in modern architectures (diffusion, flow matching) at scale, whether joint training of the quantum circuit with the network amplifies the effect, and whether classical approximations of boson sampling statistics (Villalonga et al., 2021; Oh et al., 2024) can replicate the benefit without hardware.
