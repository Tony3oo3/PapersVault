## Problem

Crowdsourced label aggregation methods (Majority Vote, Bayesian) are widely used but their fairness properties remain largely unexplored, with no convergence guarantees for the demographic parity gap of the aggregated consensus. The only existing fair aggregation method (Li et al., 2020) lacks convergence proofs and makes questionable modeling choices such as assuming Gaussian noise for inherently discrete labels. This paper bridges both the theoretical gap (no sharp fairness bounds) and the practical gap (no principled post-processing that enforces strict ε-fairness on arbitrary aggregation rules).

## Input / Output

The method consumes R binary noisy labels per task, optional task features X, and a binary sensitive feature A (e.g., gender, race), along with estimated posterior probabilities Φ̂_k(w,a) from any upstream aggregation rule (Majority Vote, Bayesian, or Dawid-Skene). Unlike the continuous-input framework of Denis et al. (2024), the input space W = {0,1}^R × X is discrete and finite, which the theory explicitly handles. The output is a randomized classifier φ*_{β*}(w,a) ∈ [0,1] that produces an ε-fair predicted label, where randomization at the decision boundary (parameters ω_a) is essential for existence of a solution in the discrete setting.

## Method

The paper contributes two theoretical results and one algorithm. First, it derives the first sharp non-asymptotic bound showing the demographic parity gap of both Majority Vote and Bayesian aggregation converges exponentially to that of the ground-truth, under interpretable conditions that partition annotators into competent (p_r ≥ 1/2+ε), adversarial (p_r ≤ 1/2−ε'), and middle groups. Second, in the small-crowd regime, it upper-bounds the Majority Vote fairness gap by the sum of individual annotator fairness gaps scaled by a factor involving the Poisson binomial variance, revealing bias amplification when biases are aligned. The FairCrowd algorithm then solves the constrained problem min_{φ∈G_ε} R(φ) via a Lagrangian/min-max formulation, yielding a closed-form solution: a Bayes-optimal classifier with a shifted decision boundary parameterized by β* (found by minimizing a convex function L(β)+ε|β|) and randomization weights ω_a (found by grid search to satisfy the exact fairness constraint). This generalizes Denis et al. (2024) from continuous to discrete inputs and applies as a plug-in post-processing step to any aggregation rule.

## Results

The paper evaluates F1-score against the Global Demographic Parity (DP) gap across ε ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2} (and dataset-specific ranges) on three real datasets and one synthetic dataset. FairCrowd (FC) consistently outperforms Post_TD across all aggregation backends (Majority Vote, Bayes, Dawid-Skene), with the largest gains at small ε values where Post_TD discards probability information. FC applied to Majority Vote (FC_Maj) matches or exceeds the in-processing FairTD baseline on Crowd Judgement and the synthetic dataset, while FairTD fails to terminate within 30 hours on Jigsaw and Hate Speech (vs. <2 s for FC and Post_TD). On the highly imbalanced Measuring Hate Speech dataset, FC remains stable across resampled test sets despite larger variability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is Li et al. (2020), which provides two variants: FairTD (in-processing, using a linear mixed-effects model to estimate per-annotator bias and accuracy) and Post_TD (post-processing via label flipping à la Kamiran & Calders). FairCrowd outperforms Post_TD on all datasets and all aggregation methods, with the advantage most pronounced at small ε (e.g., ε = 0.01 on Jigsaw), because Post_TD operates only on hard predictions and discards the posterior probability estimates produced by the aggregation step. FC_Maj (FairCrowd + Majority Vote) matches or beats FairTD on Crowd Judgement and the synthetic dataset, suggesting that FairTD's learned aggregation component offers no advantage over a simple majority vote once a principled fairness post-processing is applied. On Jigsaw and Hate Speech, FairTD does not terminate within 30 hours, rendering it inapplicable, while FC and Post_TD complete in under 2 seconds. On the imbalanced Hate Speech dataset, FC and Post_TD are roughly competitive, though FC shows slightly more variability across resampled test sets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines: the ε-fair post-processing framework of Denis et al. (2024), which the authors generalize from continuous to discrete inputs (critical for binary crowdsourced labels), and the convergence analysis of Gao et al. (2016) for crowdsourced aggregation, which they extend from accuracy to fairness guarantees. It departs from Li et al. (2020), the only prior work directly enforcing demographic parity in truth discovery, by replacing their Gaussian-noise assumption and unconverged iterative procedure with a principled min-max formulation that admits closed-form solutions and provable convergence. The practical algorithm FairCrowd is a post-processing wrapper applicable to any aggregation rule (Majority Vote, Bayesian, Dawid-Skene), distinguishing it from Li et al.'s in-processing approach that must be co-designed with a specific aggregation model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first non-asymptotic theoretical guarantees linking annotator quality to the fairness gap of aggregated labels, filling a gap explicitly identified by Lazier et al. (2023) as lacking convergence guarantees. The finding that Majority Vote amplifies rather than filters bias in the small-crowd regime (scaling as O(√R) toward maximum bias) challenges the common intuition that larger crowds automatically improve fairness. A key open question the authors acknowledge is the interaction between sensitive features and task inputs when estimating confusion matrices, which they leave for future work due to identifiability challenges. The practical impact is moderate: FairCrowd is a lightweight post-processing step that works with existing pipelines, but its scope is limited to a single binary sensitive feature and does not address multi-feature or intersectional fairness.
