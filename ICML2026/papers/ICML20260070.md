## Problem

Learning-rate warm-up is a near-universal heuristic in modern deep learning, yet no theoretical framework explains why it improves convergence. Existing empirical explanations (reducing gradient variance, improving stability, enabling larger peak LR) are fragmented and point to secondary benefits rather than a core mechanism. The paper asks whether an intrinsic property of neural network loss landscapes justifies warm-up and identifies one: an early curvature-reduction phase in which local sharpness decays as the loss decreases.

## Input / Output

At each iteration k, the schedule consumes the current stochastic mini-batch loss f_{S_k}(w_k), a single tunable threshold C (swept over a small set like {3, 3.5, 4}), and a base LR schedule (WSD or cosine annealing with no warm-up). It outputs an effective per-iteration learning rate η_k / max{1, f_{S_k}(w_k)/C}, which is nonlinear in the loss trajectory rather than linear in time. Because C is the only free parameter, the schedule requires far less hyperparameter tuning than standard linear warm-up, which needs both a warm-up fraction and a peak LR.

## Method

The paper introduces (H0, H1)-smoothness, a condition bounding the Hessian spectral norm by an affine function of loss suboptimality: ‖∇²f(w)‖ ≤ H0 + H1(f(w) − f*). This generalizes (L0, L1)-smoothness (which bounds curvature by gradient norm) and enjoys closure under finite sums and affine transformations, properties the (L0, L1) class lacks. The key insight is that during the early sharpness-reduction phase of training, curvature and suboptimality both decay, so a curvature-adaptive step size η_k = 1/(10H0 + 20H1(f(w_k)−f*)) naturally increases over time, producing a warm-up-like schedule. The authors prove the condition holds for deep linear networks under balancedness, two-layer networks with cross-entropy loss under L2 regularization, and single-attention-layer transformers, and empirically verify it on 70M–410M parameter language models and vision models. Convergence analysis shows GD with this adaptive schedule achieves O(H0·dist²/ε + H1·dist²) iterations under the Aiming condition and O(H1/μ·(f(w0)−f*) + H0/μ·log(1/ε)) under μ-PL, both strictly faster than the corresponding fixed-step lower bounds when the initial suboptimality is large. The practical one-parameter surrogate η_k / max{1, f_{S_k}(w_k)/C} matches tuned linear warm-up on FineWeb language modeling and ImageNet-32 classification while improving over no warm-up.

## Results

On FineWeb language modeling (70M, 160M, 410M parameters), the proposed one-parameter (H₀,H₁) warm-up schedule matches the validation perplexity of a tuned linear warm-up across all three model sizes, while both warm-up variants clearly outperform training with no warm-up. On ImageNet-32 with ViT-Tiny, the (H₀,H₁) warm-up similarly matches tuned linear warm-up in test accuracy and both beat the no-warm-up baseline. The (H₀,H₁) schedule requires tuning only a single threshold C (swept over {3.5, 4, 4.5} for language models and {3, 3.5, 4} for ViT), whereas linear warm-up requires tuning the warm-up fraction. Results are reported as mean of 3 runs with min-max shaded regions; the paper explicitly notes it targets validation of the theoretical schedule rather than state-of-the-art performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three learning-rate schedules: (1) no warm-up, (2) tuned linear warm-up (standard practice), and (3) the proposed (H₀,H₁)-driven warm-up of the form ηₖ / max{1, f_{Sₖ}(wₖ)/C}. On FineWeb language modeling (70M, 160M, 410M), the (H₀,H₁) warm-up matches tuned linear warm-up in validation perplexity at all model sizes, and both substantially outperform no warm-up, confirming the theoretical prediction that an increasing step-size is beneficial in the early sharpness-reduction phase. On ImageNet-32 with ViT-Tiny, the same pattern holds: (H₀,H₁) warm-up matches linear warm-up in test accuracy and both beat no warm-up. A key practical distinction is that the (H₀,H₁) schedule produces a nonlinear, loss-driven LR ramp (visible in the effective-LR panels of Figures 4 and 5), unlike the linear ramp, yet achieves comparable final performance with a single tunable parameter C rather than a warm-up fraction. The paper does not report a case where the (H₀,H₁) schedule clearly outperforms a well-tuned linear warm-up; its advantage is theoretical grounding and reduced hyperparameter search (one parameter vs. warm-up length + peak LR).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the (L0,L1)-smoothness framework of Zhang et al. (2020b) by replacing the gradient-norm dependence with loss suboptimality, yielding the (H0,H1)-smoothness condition. The motivation is a concrete failure mode: during early training, gradient norms can increase, causing (L0,L1)-smoothness to prescribe decreasing step-sizes—directly contradicting the empirical warm-up practice. The condition also inherits useful closure properties (finite sums, affine transformations) that the (L0,L1) class provably lacks. The convergence analysis builds on the Aiming condition (Liu et al., 2023) and Polyak-Lojasiewicz structure, while the curvature-reduction phase it formalizes was previously observed empirically by Kalra et al. (2023) and Kalra & Barkeshli (2024) but left without a convergence-theoretic explanation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first convergence-theoretic justification for learning-rate warm-up, filling a gap where prior analyses covered only the decay phase. The practical one-parameter schedule (ηk / max{1, f(wk)/C}) matches tuned linear warm-up on language and vision models, suggesting a principled alternative to hand-tuned warm-up fractions. Key limitations: the theory applies only to the early sharpness-reduction phase (roughly 20% of loss reduction), the constants H0 and H1 are unknown in practice (requiring the surrogate threshold C), and the stochastic analysis uses a non-standard convergence metric. Open questions include characterizing the duration of the warm-up phase, extending to practical optimizers (Adam, Muon), and generalizing beyond Euclidean geometry.
