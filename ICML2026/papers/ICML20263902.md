## Problem

Prior work showed a single attention head can implement the MMSE estimator for Gaussian channels, but left open which generative models admit exact Bayes-aligned attention representation and what statistical geometry the learned similarity actually encodes. This paper asks two questions: for which observation models can one head exactly reproduce the Bayes posterior, and when does a single shared quadratic query-key metric suffice versus requiring multiple heads to capture varying curvature?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a prompt of n i.i.d. labeled pairs (x_t, y_t) from a fixed context θ—where x_t is a discrete class label and y_t is a noisy observation with sufficient statistic T(y_t) ∈ R^k—plus a single fresh query observation y_{n+1}. It outputs a posterior distribution over the finite class set X (computed as a softmax over class-specific logits) and the corresponding posterior mean E[g(x)|y_{n+1}, θ]. The structural constraint is that the observation model must be a regular minimal exponential family with finite latent classes, and the query is a single observation rather than a sequence, making this an in-context estimation (ICE) task rather than function learning.

## Method

The core insight is that softmax attention's normalized scores structurally match Bayes' rule: for exponential-family likelihoods, the log-likelihood is linear in the sufficient statistic T(y), yielding the same bilinear query-key form as dot-product attention, with log-prior-minus-log-partition as class biases (Theorem 1). The geometric contribution identifies the Bayes-aligned score as a negative Bregman divergence D_{A*}(T(y), μ_θ(x)) in mean/sufficient-statistic space and proves a sharp boundary: a single globally shared quadratic metric is Bayes-correct on an open region if and only if the dual potential A* is quadratic there, making the Gaussian/Mahalanobis case the unique exception (Corollary 1). When curvature varies, the paper constructs a multi-head local-curvature atlas where each head uses the Hessian ∇²A*(μ_h) at a ρ-net point, with explicit logit error bounds and head-count scaling governed by the metric entropy of the task-relevant mean-parameter region (Propositions 3–4). The ICE analysis proceeds as a regime ladder from fixed-context representability through oracle dictionary asymptotics, non-oracle plug-in consistency, and finite-n posterior control via a softmax stability lemma, culminating in an optimizer-agnostic converse showing that near-optimal population cross-entropy forces approximate alignment of the learned key subspace with the Bayes log-odds subspace (Corollary 2).

## Results

In the Poisson Topic Scale ICE experiment (d=4096, C=8 classes, scale s∈[8,512]), the fixed-scale H=8 curvature atlas substantially outperforms the single-head quadratic surrogate in the low-shot regime: at n=1 per class, accuracy rises from 0.428 to 0.684 (~45% error reduction), and at n=2 from 0.667 to 0.855 (~56% error reduction). Both learned surrogates approach near-ceiling accuracy by n=16–32, while plug-in Bayes reaches 0.938 at n=1 and 0.996 at n=8. In the Gaussian diagnostic, the learned SPD metric W tracks the Bayes precision Σ_z^{-1} up to scale with sorted-eigenvalue correlation 0.960±0.005 and scaled relative Frobenius error 0.355±0.005 across five seeds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary empirical comparison is between a single-head global quadratic surrogate and a fixed-scale H=8 multi-head curvature atlas, both evaluated against plug-in Bayes and oracle Bayes upper bounds on a Poisson ICE task. The atlas wins decisively in the low-shot regime (n≤4), reducing error by 45–56% relative to the single-head model, confirming the theoretical prediction that curvature heterogeneity (dual Hessian varying by a factor of 64× across scales) necessitates multiple local quadratic charts. At n≥16 the gap narrows to ≤1 percentage point as both surrogates approach the plug-in Bayes ceiling. Neither learned surrogate matches plug-in Bayes at n=1–2 (0.684 vs. 0.938 for the atlas), reflecting the fundamental limitation that Poisson Bayes logits depend on log λ (nonlinear in the mean), so no quadratic/linear-key surrogate can be globally exact. In the Gaussian diagnostic, the single-head model is sufficient (as predicted by Corollary 1) and recovers the Bayes precision metric up to scale, with no atlas needed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the Gaussian-channel in-context estimation result of Kunde et al. (2025)—where a single attention head implements the MMSE estimator with the inverse noise covariance as the query-key metric—to arbitrary regular exponential-family observation models. The geometric backbone (Bregman divergences, Fisher information, convex duality) is standard exponential-family information geometry (Amari & Nagaoka, 2000; Wainwright & Jordan, 2008), but the paper's contribution is attention-specific: it identifies exactly when the Bayes-aligned attention score admits a single globally shared quadratic metric (Corollary 1) and constructs a multi-head local-curvature atlas with explicit approximation and head-count bounds when curvature varies. The Bayesian ICL literature (Xie et al., 2022; Ahn et al., 2023; von Oswald et al., 2023) provides the interpretive framing, while the plug-in ICE consistency and finite-sample stability results extend the oracle representability theorem to realistic finite-prompt settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The sharp boundary (one shared quadratic metric is globally Bayes-correct iff the dual potential A* is quadratic) gives practitioners a clean diagnostic: in near-constant-curvature regimes (e.g., Gaussian) a single learned metric is justified, while in curvature-heterogeneous regimes (e.g., Poisson with varying scale) multiple heads or non-quadratic scoring are structurally necessary. The multi-head curvature atlas with covering-number-based head-count scaling provides a quantitative design principle for how many local charts are needed. Key limitations include the restriction to finite discrete latent classes, the absence of optimization guarantees (the atlas is an existence result, not a training guarantee), and the fact that all experiments are synthetic single-seed diagnostics rather than evidence that large trained transformers actually recover Fisher geometry. Open questions include continuous latent spaces, deep-stack composition of atlas approximations, and whether standard gradient-based training can discover the optimal chart centers and routing map.
