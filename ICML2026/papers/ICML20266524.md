## Problem

As LLM answer engines replace ranked search results as the default information-seeking interface, Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) has emerged as a commercial practice that manipulates the evidence pool and generation process of these engines. However, existing governance frameworks and academic evaluation methods were designed for the SEO paradigm of ranked lists and do not address the distinct risks GEO introduces: concentrated influence through opaque retrieval, undisclosed commercial influence embedded in synthesized answers, and systematic blind spots between offline academic benchmarks and deployed system dynamics.

## Input / Output

The GEO pipeline consumes a user query q, a pre-indexed web corpus D (crawled from external search engines), and injected optimized content consisting of retrieval booster messages b_i (designed to maximize semantic similarity with paraphrased user queries under a length constraint ℓ(b_i) ≤ L) and ranking shifter messages c_i (conditioned on b_i, designed to alter how the LLM describes and ranks the target topic t once included in the top-k context C(q)). The output is the LLM-generated answer, where the optimization objective is to shift the utility U(q, t; C(q)) — measuring the target topic's ranking position, visibility, or citation frequency within the final synthesized response — rather than merely improving position in an intermediate ranked list.

## Method

The paper formalizes GEO as a joint optimization problem over two stages: a retrieval booster objective J_boost(b_i) = E_{q~Π(t)}[Sim(q, b_i)] that maximizes expected query-content similarity to ensure the optimized content enters the top-k retrieval set, and a ranking shifter objective J_shift(c_i | b_i) = E_{q~Π(t)}[U(q, t; C(q))] that maximizes the target topic's exposure within the LLM's generated answer. This decomposition, following PoisonedRAG, is embedded in a three-block pipeline (LLM generation, search/retrieval flow, and GEO content distribution) that the authors use as an analytical lens to systematically compare academic frameworks (which assume content is already in the retrieval set and optimize only the shifter on static corpora) against industry systems (which jointly optimize both booster and shifter, distribute content across high-authority external platforms, and evaluate on live deployed engines). The paper then identifies three risk clusters — concentrated influence from low contestability and top-k boundary sensitivity, undisclosed commercial influence that collapses the advertising disclosure boundary, and academic-industry blind spots from visibility and evaluation asymmetries — and proposes corresponding governance actions including answer-level disclosure standards, black-box auditing protocols, and deployment-aligned longitudinal metrics.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative contribution is a sensitivity experiment across 30 information-seeking query pairs and seven deployed OpenAI/Gemini models. For all three Gemini models, 100% of query pairs produced different cited-domain sets after paraphrasing, with Gemini-3-flash showing a mean Jaccard distance of 0.869 (near-complete domain turnover). In contrast, GPT-4o showed only 13.3% change and a mean J_d of 0.133, while GPT-5.4 reached 83.3% change and J_d = 0.686. The average word difference between original and paraphrased queries was only 13%, yet citation behavior shifted substantially, demonstrating that the retrieval boundary is highly sensitive to minor input variation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it compares academic and industry GEO practices along multiple dimensions. Academically, five representative frameworks (Aggarwal et al., Kumar & Lakkaraju, Nazary et al., Pfrommer et al., Nestaas et al.) are contrasted: all assume optimized content is already in the retrieval context, use offline evaluation (except Nestaas et al.), and focus on the content owner's website. Industry GEO systems (ProFound, Goodie, AirOps, AthenaHQ) jointly optimize retrieval boosters and ranking shifters, distribute content across external high-authority platforms, and evaluate on live systems using visibility, citation frequency, and ranking metrics. The sensitivity experiment further reveals a stark cross-model gap: Gemini models exhibit near-total citation instability (J_d ≈ 0.68–0.87, 100% change) while GPT-4o is relatively stable (J_d = 0.133, 13.3% change), suggesting that pipeline updates can shift citation behavior at scale in ways that are difficult to predict and that academic offline benchmarks would miss entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Aggarwal et al. (2024), which first introduced GEO as a task, and on PoisonedRAG (Zou et al., 2025), whose decomposition of adversarial content into retrieval-boosting and ranking-shifting components is adopted as the formal backbone. It unifies five prior academic GEO studies (Aggarwal, Kumar & Lakkaraju, Nazary, Pfrommer, Nestaas) under a single three-block pipeline (LLM, Search Flow, GEO) and, critically, departs from the purely technical framing by introducing a governance and risk-analysis layer—contestability, disclosure, and auditing—that none of the prior GEO papers address. The academic-industry comparison is novel in scope: no prior work systematically contrasts offline academic benchmarks with deployed commercial GEO services (AirOps, ProFound, Goodie, AthenaHQ).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GEO is transitioning from an academic curiosity to a multi-million-dollar commercial market with documented real-world incidents (Microsoft's 2026 hidden-prompt report, OECD's 2026 China poisoning case), yet existing governance frameworks (FTC disclosure rules, EU AI Act) were designed for ranked-list search, not synthesized LLM answers. The paper's three risk clusters—concentrated influence via low contestability, covert commercial influence embedded in evidence, and academic-industry evaluation blind spots—identify concrete gaps that regulators and platform designers have not yet addressed. Key open questions include: how to implement high-precision answer-level commercial disclosure without over-labeling, how to make black-box auditing of deployed engines feasible at scale, and whether the small-scale sensitivity test (30 queries, 7 models) generalizes to the full query distribution. As a position paper, it provides a roadmap rather than a solution, and its practical impact depends on whether platform providers and regulators adopt the proposed auditing and disclosure standards.
