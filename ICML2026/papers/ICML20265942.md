## Problem

Multilingual LLM safety is severely imbalanced: high-resource languages maintain robust defenses while non-high-resource (NHR) languages remain highly vulnerable to jailbreak attacks. Prior work has documented this disparity at the behavioral level but has not identified the underlying neural mechanisms governing cross-lingual safety transfer, leaving mitigation strategies to 'blind' tuning paradigms that suffer from limited efficacy or catastrophic forgetting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265942/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained instruction-tuned LLM (e.g., Qwen3-8B, Llama3.1-8B-it, Gemma2-9B-it) and a parallel safety dataset of 802 English jailbreak queries with safe refusals, machine-translated into target NHR languages to provide cross-lingual semantic anchors. A binary gradient mask is applied during training that restricts updates to specific rows/columns in the attention projection matrices (W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O) corresponding to English MS-Neurons, freezing all other parameters. The output is a safety-aligned model updating only ~0.5–0.6% of total parameters, which produces consistent safe refusals across HR and NHR languages while preserving general reasoning capabilities.

## Method

The paper first identifies monolingual safety neurons (MS-Neurons) via contrastive activation analysis: neurons whose deactivation causes the largest representational shift on jailbreak prompts but not on benign prompts within attention projection layers, with a 3% top-percentile threshold. Cross-lingual shared safety neurons (SS-Neurons) are then defined as the intersection of each NHR language's MS-Neurons with English MS-Neurons, and their causal role is verified through targeted masking experiments showing disproportionate safety collapse. The core intervention—SS-Neuron expansion—trains the model on the parallel safety dataset while applying a binary gradient mask that updates only the English MS-Neuron superset, forcing NHR inputs to recruit a broader array of English safety neurons and physically widening the cross-lingual safety bridge. This surgical approach updates less than half the parameters of LoRA, outperforms SOTA baselines (MPO, DPO, SimPO, SmoothLLM) on both MultiJail and AdvBench-x, and demonstrates zero-shot transfer to unseen NHR languages via leave-one-out experiments.

## Results

On MultiJail, the SS-Neuron expansion strategy reduces average ASR to 2.78% (Gemma2-9B-it) and 3.41% (Llama3.1-8B-it), substantially outperforming the strongest training-based baseline MPO (7.22% and 5.08%). On AdvBench-x, it achieves 0.82% and 0.29% average ASR respectively. The method updates only 0.51–0.57% of total parameters while matching or surpassing full fine-tuning (100% parameters) on both benchmarks across all three model architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The SS-Neuron expansion strategy outperforms all eight baselines on both MultiJail and AdvBench-x across both model architectures. Against the strongest multilingual baseline MPO, it reduces MultiJail ASR from 7.22%→2.78% (Gemma2-9B-it) and 5.08%→3.41% (Llama3.1-8B-it), and AdvBench-x ASR from 1.59%→0.82% and 2.54%→0.29% respectively. Among preference-optimization methods (DPO, KTO, R-DPO, SimPO), the gap is even larger: e.g., on Llama3.1-8B-it MultiJail, Ours achieves 3.41% versus 12.06% (DPO), 15.95% (KTO), 25.64% (R-DPO), and 22.14% (SimPO). Inference-time defenses show mixed results: SmoothLLM is competitive on Gemma2 (5.23% MultiJail) but degrades on Llama3.1 (25.32%), while Self Defense is strong on AdvBench-x (1.32%) yet collapses on Llama3.1 MultiJail (35.08%). LoRA, used as a parameter-efficiency reference in Table 7, actually worsens MultiJail ASR on Gemma2 (10.93%→33.06%) and Llama3.1 (31.47%→33.06%), whereas Ours reduces it to 1.59% and 2.22% with fewer trainable parameters (0.57% vs. 1.15% for LoRA). Full fine-tuning (100% parameters) achieves comparable or slightly better ASR but at 100× the parameter cost and with significant utility degradation on MGSM/MMMLU, which Ours avoids by updating only safety-critical neurons.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior multilingual safety work (Shen et al. 2024b; Deng et al. 2023; Yong et al. 2023), which identified the HR/NHR safety gap at the behavioral level, by providing the first neuron-level causal account of cross-lingual safety. It repurposes the neuron-probing and causal-intervention framework from factual-recall and language-specific representation studies (Meng et al. 2022a,b; Tang et al. 2024) and redirects it toward safety-refusal circuits, then converts the resulting mechanistic insight into a targeted gradient-masking training strategy. Unlike blind parameter-efficient methods (LoRA, DPO, MPO) that update parameters without mechanistic guidance, the SS-Neuron expansion strategy uses the identified neuronal subset as an explicit optimization target, bridging mechanistic interpretability and alignment practice in a way neither community had previously attempted.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a <0.3% neuronal subset (SS-Neurons) acts as the primary cross-lingual safety bottleneck reframes multilingual alignment from a data-coverage problem to a representational-routing problem, with direct implications for efficient and targeted safety interventions. The practical method—updating <0.6% of parameters via gradient masking on identified neurons—outperforms full fine-tuning and LoRA while preserving general capabilities, offering a template for mechanistically-guided alignment beyond safety. Key open questions include whether the English-anchored SS-Neuron framework imposes Anglocentric safety norms on culturally diverse contexts, whether the identified neurons are robust to adversarial targeting (the paper itself formalizes a neuron-masking attack), and whether the sparse-neuron paradigm generalizes to non-safety capabilities like reasoning or knowledge retrieval.
