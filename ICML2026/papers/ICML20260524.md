## Problem

Minimum MMD points enjoy fast numerical integration guarantees, but the non-convexity of the MMD objective means existing algorithms can only recover local minima (stationary points), not global minima. This raises the question of why stationary MMD points still exhibit strong integration performance in practice, and whether they can be computed with provable guarantees.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260524/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a target distribution μ on ℝ^d, a positive semi-definite kernel k, a number of particles n, and initial particle positions, and produces a stationary MMD point set {x_i}_{i=1}^n used to approximate integrals of functions in the associated RKHS. Unlike kernel thinning or herding, the points are not constrained to a pre-existing candidate set; instead they are freely optimised in ℝ^d. The expectation E_{Y~μ}[∇₁k(·,Y)] must be computable in closed form, via Stein kernels, or as an empirical average when μ is a dataset.

## Method

The paper proves two main results. First, a super-convergence theorem showing that stationary MMD points exactly integrate a linear subspace F_n of the RKHS that expands with n, so the integration error is bounded by |f|_n · MMD(μ, μ_n) where |f|_n → 0, yielding o(MMD) convergence for integrands in span({1} ∪ H_X). Second, a noisy MMD particle descent algorithm that injects Gaussian noise (scale β_t) into the discretised Wasserstein gradient flow update, provably converging to stationary MMD points. The key technical contribution is a non-asymptotic finite-particle error bound (Theorem 4.1) that is polynomial or logarithmic in the number of iterations T rather than exponential, achieved by tracking the joint particle distribution and exploiting a gradient dominance (Polyak-Łojasiewicz) condition on the noise-injected dynamics.

## Results

On a 2D 10-component Gaussian mixture, stationary MMD points achieve an empirical integration error rate of −1.39 for f₁ ∈ H, exceeding the MMD decay rate of −1.35 and confirming the predicted super-convergence (Theorem 3.4). For f₂ ∉ H, performance is comparable to support points but still outperforms IID, QMC, kernel herding, and kernel thinning. Computationally, generating n=100 stationary MMD points takes ~20 seconds versus >3000 seconds for kernel thinning with oversampling factor g=6. In high-dimensional Gaussian mixtures (d=10, d=50), the method consistently outperforms i.i.d. sampling across all tested sample sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Stationary MMD points consistently outperform all five baselines (IID, QMC, kernel thinning, kernel herding, support points) on integration error for f₁ ∈ H across the 2D Gaussian mixture, House8L (d=8), and Elevators (d=18) experiments. Against kernel thinning (KT), the method achieves comparable or better integration error while being ~150× faster at n=100 (20 s vs. >3000 s), since KT requires selecting from a candidate pool of size 2^g·n². Against support points (SP), stationary MMD points are competitive on f₂ (an unbounded integrand outside H) but clearly superior on f₁, which lies in the RKHS. Against QMC and kernel herding, the advantage is most pronounced in multimodal settings where these methods struggle to allocate points across all modes. In high-dimensional experiments (d=10, d=50), the only baseline reported is i.i.d. sampling, which is uniformly outperformed, though the gap narrows with increasing dimension.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the MMD gradient flow framework of Arbel et al. (NeurIPS 2019) for particle-based distribution approximation, incorporating the Gaussian noise injection scheme from Arbel et al. (NeurIPS 2018) to escape local minima. The convergence analysis draws on the finite-particle propagation-of-chaos techniques from Banerjee et al. (ICLR 2025) for Stein variational gradient descent. The key conceptual departure is from prior work on minimum MMD points (e.g., support points, kernel thinning, kernel herding), which study the globally optimal configuration that is intractable to compute; instead, this paper studies the weaker notion of stationary MMD points and proves they are both computable and sufficient for fast integration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper resolves a long-standing gap between theory and practice in MMD-based numerical integration: theoretical guarantees were stated for minimum MMD points, but all practical algorithms can only find stationary (local) minima. The super-convergence result (integration error is o(MMD)) provides the first explanation for why these local-minima point sets empirically outperform the MMD bound. Open questions include the practical cost of verifying Assumption 5 (the Polyak-Lojasiewicz-type noise condition) at each iteration, the O(n²T) computational complexity, and whether the connected-support assumption (Assumption 2) can be further relaxed for highly disconnected or low-dimensional manifolds. The method is directly applicable to coreset selection and efficient attention mechanisms where MMD-based compression is already deployed.
