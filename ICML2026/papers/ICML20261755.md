## Problem

Using synthetic data from a stronger teacher model to fine-tune a reasoning student model (e.g., Qwen3-8B) often causes catastrophic forgetting and performance drops rather than improvements, because the teacher's stylistic patterns (transitional phrases, discourse markers, formatting conventions) diverge substantially from the student's learned distribution. This distributional mismatch is especially harmful for reasoning models that have already undergone extensive post-training, where even stylistic tokens like 'wait' or 'okay' play a functional role in the reasoning process.

## Input / Output

TESSY takes a text prompt (e.g., a programming contest question), a student model, a teacher model, two boundary predictors, and a per-iteration span length k (set to 20 tokens) as inputs. It produces a complete synthetic SFT sequence structured as alternating student-generated style spans and teacher-generated capability spans within the thinking section, followed by a final answer generated entirely by the student model. When teacher and student vocabularies differ, the last word of each span is discarded to prevent subword mismatch artifacts.

## Method

TESSY decomposes the SFT objective into capability-token loss and style-token loss, then constructs synthetic data where capability tokens (code, formulas, reasoning steps) are sampled from the teacher distribution while style tokens (transitional phrases, tone markers) follow the student distribution. The generation proceeds in an alternating loop: the student model generates a span of k tokens, a style-boundary predictor (a token-level binary classifier on Qwen3-0.6B-Base) truncates it at the first capability token, then the teacher model generates the next span, truncated by a capability-boundary predictor at the first style token. This generate-then-rollback strategy is repeated until the thinking section is complete, after which the student model generates the final answer. Boundary predictors are trained on 100K automatically annotated segments where the teacher model labels all style spans, and the entire pipeline is implemented on vLLM with prefix caching for efficient model switching.

## Results

On in-domain code generation, TESSY improves Qwen3-8B by 7.78% on LiveCodeBench-V5 (55.09→62.87), 5.85% on LiveCodeBench-V6 (49.58→55.43), 11.34% on LiveCodeBench-Pro (25.35→36.69), and 6.68% on OJBench (18.75→25.43). On out-of-domain math, TESSY gains 3.75% on AIME-2024 (76.67→80.42) and 0.93% on AIME-2025 (69.17→70.10) while maintaining comparable performance on GPQA-Diamond and OlympiadBench. In contrast, the widely used Teacher-Only approach causes performance drops of up to 10.02% on OJBench and 13.77% on LiveCodeBench-V5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TESSY outperforms all six baselines on every in-domain code generation benchmark. Against Teacher-Only (Kim & Rush, 2016), which generates all SFT data from GPT-OSS-120B, TESSY gains 21.55% on OJBench (25.43 vs. 8.73) and 14.59% on LiveCodeBench-Pro (36.69 vs. 22.10), while Teacher-Only actually degrades the student below its original performance. Against Self-Distillation (Yang et al., ACL 2024), where the student rewrites teacher references, TESSY leads by 11.21% on OJBench (25.43 vs. 14.22) and 17.43% on LiveCodeBench-Pro (36.69 vs. 19.26); Self-Distillation also causes the largest drop on LiveCodeBench-V5 (51.50 vs. 55.09 baseline). Against Teacher-Mix (Lin et al., 2020), which blends teacher- and student-generated samples 1:1, TESSY improves by 13.58% on OJBench (25.43 vs. 11.85) and 14.74% on LiveCodeBench-Pro (36.69 vs. 21.95). The only baseline that avoids degradation on in-domain tasks is Reject-Sampling, but it yields only a marginal 0.71% gain on LiveCodeBench-Pro and still drops on OJBench, whereas TESSY delivers consistent double-digit improvements across all four code benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

TESSY builds on the alternating-generation paradigm introduced by Speculative Knowledge Distillation (Xu et al., ICLR 2025) and AdaSwitch (Peng et al., 2025), but repurposes it for offline SFT data synthesis rather than online training. The key conceptual departure is the explicit decomposition of model outputs into 'capability tokens' (task-relevant reasoning/code) and 'style tokens' (discourse markers, transitional phrases), with boundary predictors enforcing the split. This positions TESSY against self-distillation (Yang et al., 2024), teacher-only distillation (Kim & Rush, 2016), and teacher-student mixing (Lin et al., 2020), all of which the paper shows fail for reasoning models due to stylistic distribution mismatch rather than insufficient capability transfer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear diagnosis—stylistic divergence, not capability gap, is the primary cause of SFT failure in reasoning models—and a practical, open-source solution that consistently improves performance across multiple teacher-student pairs and benchmarks. A notable limitation is that TESSY's synthesized data has a lower generation-quality ceiling than unrestricted teacher-only outputs, suggesting the framework trades some capability upper bound for distributional safety. Open questions include whether the style/capability boundary can be identified more precisely without learned predictors, whether the approach extends to multimodal or multi-turn settings, and whether the findings generalize beyond code generation to broader reasoning tasks.
