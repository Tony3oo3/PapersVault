## Problem

Standard Transformer-based PDE solvers partition the spatial domain into uniform patches, distributing computation evenly regardless of local solution complexity. This wastes the token budget on smooth regions while under-resolving areas with sharp gradients, discontinuities, or multiscale structures, creating a fundamental efficiency-accuracy trade-off that uniform patchification cannot resolve.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266048/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a sequence of T PDE state frames, each an H×W×C spatial field (all normalized to 128×128 with up to 4 channels, padded and masked for cross-dataset consistency), and produces the next-step solution field at the same resolution. During autoregressive rollout, the refinement indicator is recomputed from the model's own predictions at each step, so no ground-truth future labels are needed at inference. The output is a full-resolution field obtained by fusing a dense coarse prediction with a sparse fine prediction via a lightweight CNN.

## Method

MeshTok replaces uniform patchification with an AMR-inspired adaptive tokenization: an activity-based indicator (combining gradient magnitude and Laplacian energy) selects the top-k fraction of coarse patches for refinement, splitting each into a 2×2 sub-grid of fine tokens. A coarse-fine dual encoder produces tokens at both resolutions, and a merge operator replaces selected coarse tokens with their four fine counterparts, yielding a heterogeneous multi-scale sequence processed by a shared Transformer with block-causal temporal attention. A geometry-aware FiLM positional encoding maps each token's continuous spatial coordinates and scale level (coarse/fine) to per-channel affine parameters, enabling consistent cross-scale interactions. Decoding routes tokens back to coarse and fine groups, producing a full-resolution coarse field and a sparse fine field that are fused by a CNN to inject fine-scale corrections only in refined regions. The paper also provides a theoretical analysis showing that refinement strictly enlarges the realizable function class and that the activity-based indicator approximates a budgeted best-approximation optimum.

## Results

On the multi-dataset pretraining benchmark (Table 1), MeshTok (31.43M params) achieves the lowest relative ℓ₂ error across all five evaluated PDE tasks: 2.095 on Gray-Scott, 1.056 on Allen-Cahn, 1.187 on CNS(1.0, 0.01), 0.249 on CNS(0.1, 0.01), and 0.276 on SWE. The closest competitor, BCAT (29.19M params), trails by 0.25–0.17 points on the harder fluid-dynamics tasks (SWE: 0.276 vs. 0.443; CNS(1.0,0.01): 1.187 vs. 1.339). In the compute-matched comparison (Table 4), MeshTok achieves 0.972 relative error at ~2.8×10⁹ MACs, outperforming width- and depth-inflated coarse baselines (1.029–1.065) and reduced full-refinement baselines (1.063–1.078) under the same MAC budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MeshTok outperforms all seven baselines on every task in the main pretraining table. Against the closest Transformer-based competitor BCAT, MeshTok reduces relative ℓ₂ error by 0.25 on Gray-Scott (2.095 vs. 2.345), 0.25 on Allen-Cahn (1.056 vs. 1.310), 0.15 on CNS(1.0, 0.01) (1.187 vs. 1.339), 0.005 on CNS(0.1, 0.01) (0.249 vs. 0.254), and 0.17 on SWE (0.276 vs. 0.443). Against the larger MoE-POT (59.15M params), MeshTok (31.43M) achieves substantially lower errors on all tasks, e.g., 1.187 vs. 4.801 on CNS(1.0, 0.01) and 0.276 vs. 0.757 on SWE, indicating that adaptive tokenization is more effective than simply scaling model width. Neural-operator baselines (FNO, DeepONet) are far behind on fluid-dynamics tasks: FNO scores 1.098 on SWE versus MeshTok's 0.276, and DeepONet fails entirely on Allen-Cahn (error > 50%). In the downstream fine-tuning setting (Table 2), MeshTok also achieves the lowest epoch-5 error on three of four unseen PDE families (Shear flow 3.334, Burgers 0.252, Reaction-Diffusion 8.106), while BCAT-from-scratch remains best on Black-Scholes-Barenblatt (0.123 vs. 0.142). In the compute-matched comparison (Table 4), MeshTok's 0.972 error beats both width-inflated coarse baselines (1.029–1.065) and depth-reduced full-refinement baselines (1.063–1.078) at comparable MACs, confirming the gain is not merely from extra parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

MeshTok repurposes the classical adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) paradigm from numerical analysis as a token-level strategy for Transformer-based PDE models, rather than modifying the underlying simulation grid. It builds directly on the block-causal attention architecture of BCAT and the multi-physics pretraining framework of recent PDE foundation models (MoE-POT, DPOT, MPP), but departs from their uniform patchification by introducing an activity-guided, two-level coarse-to-fine token hierarchy with FiLM-based geometry-aware positional encoding. The closest prior work is AMR-Transformer (Xu et al., 2025), which also couples AMR with a Transformer for fluid simulation, but MeshTok frames the contribution as a general tokenization principle applicable to any structured-grid PDE family and provides a formal representation-theoretic analysis (token-collision lower bound, refinement-induced class inclusion) that the prior work lacks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that adaptive token density is a practical and theoretically grounded inductive bias for PDE Transformers, with the compute-matched comparison (Table 4) showing the gain is not merely from extra FLOPs but from better allocation of a fixed budget. Key limitations include restriction to structured Cartesian grids, a two-level hierarchy, and a noted reduction in marginal refinement benefit as model scale grows, leaving open whether multi-level or jointly-optimized refinement policies can close the remaining gap to full refinement. The preliminary 3D MHD results (still ~30% relative error) suggest the approach has not yet been validated in higher-dimensional settings, and extension to unstructured meshes or irregular geometries would require fundamentally different backbones and encodings.
