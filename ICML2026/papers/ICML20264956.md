## Problem

Conventional spatial mixup strategies for point clouds (coordinate interpolation, patch replacement) generate physically unrealistic samples with sharp geometric discontinuities that distort global topology and hinder robust feature learning. While spectral methods offer a natural way to preserve geometric structure, the Spherical Harmonic Transform's potential for cross-sample mixing—enabled by its universal, instance-independent basis—remains largely unexplored for data augmentation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264956/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes pairs of point clouds (1,024 points each) with their class labels during training. Each point cloud is normalized to the unit sphere, projected into the SHT coefficient space up to a randomly sampled maximum degree L ∈ [15, 70], hierarchically mixed, and reconstructed back to 3D coordinates via the Inverse SHT. The output is an augmented point cloud paired with either a soft label (PSMix-S, weighted by spectral energy distribution) or a hard label (PSMix-H, assigned to the dominant spectral contributor), making PSMix-H directly composable with existing spatial mixup methods as an orthogonal plug-in.

## Method

PSMix shifts the mixing paradigm from spatial to spectral domain using the Spherical Harmonic Transform, which provides a universal canonical basis (unlike instance-specific GFT) enabling mathematically valid linear combination of coefficients from disparate objects. The core mechanism is a rotation-aware hierarchical mixing: source coefficients are first rotated via Wigner D-matrices (parameterized by adversarially optimized Euler angles), then mixed with target coefficients using a frequency-adaptive ratio λ_l = σ(l − τ) that assigns low-frequency components (global shape) to the rotated source and high-frequency components (local details) to the target. An adversarial rotation optimization strategy treats the base rotation R₀ as a learnable variable updated via a minimax objective—maximizing classification loss to find challenging orientations while minimizing it to improve the network—stabilized by Gaussian sampling around R₀ with linearly annealed variance. The framework is applied stochastically (Bernoulli gate ρ) alongside clean samples, and the PSMix-H variant's hard-label design allows seamless integration with spatial augmentations like PointMixup and PointCutMix for a joint spatial-spectral augmentation pipeline.

## Results

On ModelNet40-C, PSMix-S achieves 78.21% mean accuracy with DGCNN and 79.10% with PointNeXt, outperforming the best spatial mixing baseline (PointCutMix-R at 77.30% / PointMixup at 75.84%) by 0.91% and 3.26% respectively. On the more challenging ScanObjectNN-C benchmark, PSMix-S reaches 62.19% (DGCNN) and 57.47% (PointNeXt), surpassing the next-best method AdaptPoint by 4.71% and 2.00%. The joint spatial-spectral strategy (PSMix-H as a plug-in on top of spatial methods) yields additional gains of 3.5–4.8% mean accuracy over spatial-only baselines, with the largest improvements on transformation corruptions (up to +25.7% for RSMix+PSMix-H on PointNeXt).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PSMix-S outperforms all seven spatial/generative baselines on both ModelNet40-C and ScanObjectNN-C across both DGCNN and PointNeXt backbones. On ModelNet40-C with DGCNN, PSMix-S (78.21%) beats the strongest spatial method PointCutMix-R (77.30%) by 0.91% and PointMixup (74.48%) by 3.73%; with PointNeXt, the margin widens to 3.26% over PointMixup (75.84%) and 5.11% over PointCutMix-R (73.99%). On ScanObjectNN-C, PSMix-S leads AdaptPoint (the best prior method) by 4.71% (DGCNN: 62.19% vs 57.48%) and 2.00% (PointNeXt: 57.47% vs 55.47%). The gains are most pronounced on transformation corruptions (ROT, RBF, FFD), where PSMix-S achieves 73.50–87.32% versus 28–55% for spatial methods on PointNeXt. As a plug-in, PSMix-H adds 3.5–4.8% mean accuracy on top of spatial methods (e.g., PointCutMix-K+PSMix-H reaches 81.80% on DGCNN vs 76.97% standalone), confirming orthogonality. PGD and AdaptPoint show extreme variance across corruption types (e.g., PGD drops to 6.73% on BG noise with PointNeXt), whereas PSMix maintains more consistent performance across all 15 corruption categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Spherical Harmonic Transform (SHT) and Wigner D-matrices—tools previously applied to shape retrieval and SO(3)-equivariant learning—for the data augmentation task, arguing that SHT's universal canonical basis on the unit sphere resolves the instance-specific basis mismatch that makes Graph Fourier Transform ill-posed for cross-sample mixing. It departs from the dominant spatial mixup lineage (PointMixup, RSMix, PointCutMix, PointPatchMix) by shifting the mixing operation from coordinate interpolation to frequency-adaptive coefficient fusion, and introduces an adversarial minimax objective over rotation parameters that goes beyond standard data augmentation. The hierarchical mixing strategy (low-frequency structure from one sample, high-frequency detail from another) is a novel application of multi-resolution spectral decomposition to the augmentation setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PSMix addresses a concrete geometric-integrity failure mode of spatial mixup and demonstrates that spectral-domain augmentation is an orthogonal complement rather than a replacement, yielding consistent additive gains when stacked with existing spatial methods. The adversarial rotation optimization component elevates the contribution beyond a static augmentation recipe toward a training strategy that actively seeks challenging orientations. Limitations include the periodic overhead of adversarial updates (every 5 epochs), the unit-sphere normalization assumption that may not suit all point cloud distributions, and the fact that the patch-wise variant (PSMix-P) is only preliminarily validated on object-level benchmarks. Open questions include whether the spectral mixing paradigm extends to dense scene-level point clouds, whether the adversarial rotation can be made more efficient, and whether the approach transfers to other 3D tasks beyond classification.
