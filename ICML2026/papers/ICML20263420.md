## Problem

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) are widely used for interpretability of neural network activations, but different training runs on the same data produce different concept dictionaries and sparse codes, undermining their reliability. The paper identifies three architectural and training issues that cause this instability—unidirectional features, poor dictionary conditioning, and insufficient encoder expressiveness—and shows theoretically that even a perfectly stable dictionary does not guarantee identifiable sparse codes.

## Input / Output

The SAE consumes 768-dimensional intermediate activations from pretrained models (Pythia-160M layer 12 on The Pile, or DINOv2-Base patch tokens on ImageNet-1k) and produces a k-sparse code (k=40) over a 4096-concept dictionary, along with a reconstructed activation. Both inputs and dictionary atoms are normalized to unit norm, and the pre- and post-bias terms are tied. The key output for identifiability analysis is the pair of sparse codes from independently trained SAEs, compared via intersection-over-union on sparsity patterns and ℓ₂ error after dictionary alignment.

## Method

The paper proposes the identifiable SAE (iSAE), which modifies the standard TopK SAE in three ways: (1) replacing TopK with AbsTopK to allow negative concept loadings and eliminate redundant opposing atoms; (2) adding an aRIP regularization term that penalizes deviation of the Gram submatrix eigenvalues from 1 on observed sparse code supports, making the restricted isometry property measurable and optimizable; and (3) for the iSAE-ME variant, replacing the near-linear encoder with a 5-step iterative encoder inspired by LISTA. Theoretically, the paper proves that when the learned dictionary satisfies aRIP on unions of observed supports, and the code distribution has sufficient richness and diversity, both the dictionary and sparse codes are near-identifiable up to signed permutations, with error bounds that vanish as reconstruction error and aRIP level approach zero.

## Results

On synthetic i.i.d. data, iSAE-ME achieves near-perfect identifiability (SAE IoU 0.997, ℓ₂ 0.020, DCS 0.999) and near-zero reconstruction error (MSE 0.001), compared to TopK's essentially unidentified solution (IoU 0.113, ℓ₂ 0.882, DCS 0.606, MSE 0.447). On real Pythia-160M activations, iSAE-ME attains the lowest MSE (0.148 vs. 0.166 for TopK), while AbsTopK achieves the highest dictionary stability (DCS 0.873) and best SAE code IoU (0.631). On DINOv2, iSAE-ME again achieves the best reconstruction (MSE 0.191 vs. 0.217 for TopK), though TopK retains the highest DCS (0.836). On SAEBench, iSAE-ME achieves the best cross-entropy loss (3.906) while TopK leads on sparse probing (0.909) and spurious correlation removal (0.381).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the TopK SAE baseline (Gao et al., 2024), iSAE-ME achieves substantially lower reconstruction error across all four settings (e.g., MSE 0.001 vs. 0.447 on synthetic i.i.d., 0.148 vs. 0.166 on Pythia-160M), and dramatically better code identifiability on synthetic data (IoU 0.997 vs. 0.113). However, on real-world activations, TopK retains the highest dictionary cosine similarity (DCS 0.813 on Pythia, 0.836 on DINOv2), and the SAE encoder of TopK actually outperforms the oracle solver on code IoU, suggesting the near-linear encoder is not the bottleneck in these settings. Against the AbsTopK baseline (Zhu et al., 2025), iSAE-ME wins on reconstruction in every setting but loses on dictionary stability (DCS 0.797 vs. 0.873 on Pythia) and SAE code IoU (0.375 vs. 0.631). The aRIP-regularized iSAE (without multistep encoding) consistently improves the aRIP measurement R_S over both baselines (e.g., 0.114 vs. 0.183 on Pythia) and improves oracle code identifiability, but these gains do not fully transfer to the amortized SAE encoder on real data. Overall, the paper demonstrates that no single variant dominates all metrics on real activations, with the multistep encoder trading identifiability for reconstruction quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies sparse autoencoders (SAEs) with classical dictionary learning theory, drawing on the restricted isometry property (RIP) from compressed sensing (Candes et al., 2005) and the multistep encoder architecture from LISTA (Gregor & LeCun, 2010). It builds directly on the standard TopK SAE training recipe (Gao et al., 2024) and the AbsTopK bidirectional activation (Zhu et al., 2025), but departs from the SAE literature's focus on reconstruction error by introducing a formal statistical identifiability criterion and a data-driven relaxation of RIP (aRIP) that is both measurable and optimizable. The paper also contrasts with prior SAE stability work (Song et al., 2025; Fel et al., 2025) by showing that dictionary stability alone is insufficient for code stability, a gap those works did not address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As SAEs become the primary tool for mechanistic interpretability, steering, and oversight of large language models, run-to-run identifiability is a 'bare minimum' requirement that the paper argues has been overlooked. The impossibility result (Theorem 3.2) and the aRIP-based identifiability guarantee (Theorem 3.6) provide the first theoretical characterization of when SAE codes are stable, bridging a gap between scalable SAE training and the identifiability guarantees of classical dictionary learning. Key open questions remain: the multistep encoder that achieves near-perfect identifiability on synthetic data paradoxically worsens it on real activations, suggesting optimization dynamics are the dominant remaining barrier; and the gap between synthetic and real-world settings limits the transferability of the theoretical guarantees.
