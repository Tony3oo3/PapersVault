## Problem

Existing urban MLLMs rely almost exclusively on satellite imagery, limiting them to macroscopic tasks like land-use classification while missing fine-grained street-level details such as building facades and local context. No prior MLLM jointly learns from both satellite and street-view imagery, and the two main obstacles are the absence of large-scale paired cross-view urban data with textual annotations and the architectural inability of standard MLLMs to fuse complementary visual knowledge across views.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265885/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model accepts one satellite image (512×512, zoom level 15) paired with 3–6 street-view images (512×512) plus a free-form text query, and can also handle single-view inputs by duplicating the image through the cross-view perceiver. Outputs span a wide range of structured and free-form responses: scene classification labels (single or multi-label), object and spatial-relationship reasoning answers, geo-localization coordinates, landmark names, and numerical indicator predictions (population density, nightlight intensity, median income, poverty ratio, beauty/wealth scores) evaluated with accuracy-at-margin-2 or F1.

## Method

UrbanMLLM is built on VILA-1.5 and introduces two key designs. First, a cross-view perceiver module performs bidirectional cross-attention between satellite and street-view visual features (SVI→SI and SI→SVI), followed by a learned gating mechanism that adaptively combines fused and original features before a two-layer MLP adapter aligns them to the LLM's text space. Second, an interleaved pre-training paradigm organizes cross-view urban content into coherent documents—each pairing a satellite image with its geolocated street-view images and LLM-generated captions—so the model implicitly learns cross-view relationships via in-context learning. Training proceeds in two stages: one epoch of interleaved pre-training on 4.5 million matched cross-view pairs covering the entire United States, followed by one epoch of supervised fine-tuning on ~700K instruction-tuning QA pairs spanning 13 urban tasks.

## Results

On the satellite imagery benchmark (Table 2), UrbanMLLM-8B achieves 0.787 on single-scene classification, 0.800 on object reasoning, 0.871 on geo-localization, and 0.872 on population density prediction, outperforming the strongest open-source baseline InternVL-2-40B (0.664/0.672/0.756/0.632) and GPT-4o (0.680/0.691/0.745/0.484). On street-view tasks (Table 3), UrbanMLLM-8B reaches 0.963 on spatial relationship reasoning and 0.827 on beauty-level prediction, surpassing Gemini-3.1-Pro (0.777/0.845) and GPT-4o (0.709/0.824). On cross-view tasks (Table 4), UrbanMLLM-8B achieves 0.761 on median income prediction and 0.345 on spatial relationship reasoning, yielding an average 3.3% improvement over GPT-4o across four cross-view tasks. The model also generalizes to the external CityBench benchmark without additional fine-tuning, outperforming the reported state-of-the-art on CityInfer, LocInfer, and Population tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UrbanMLLM-8B consistently outperforms all ten baselines across the majority of tasks. Against the strongest general-purpose MLLMs, it surpasses GPT-4o by 10.7 points on satellite scene classification (0.787 vs. 0.680), 12.6 points on population prediction (0.872 vs. 0.484), and 12.6 points on geo-localization (0.871 vs. 0.745); it also beats Gemini-3.1-Pro on most satellite tasks except geo-localization (0.871 vs. 0.867) and nightlight prediction (0.798 vs. 0.562). Among open-source MLLMs, UrbanMLLM-8B leads InternVL-2-40B by 12.3 points on single-scene classification and 24.0 points on population prediction, and outperforms LLaVA-N-34B by 21.3 points on multi-scene classification. On street-view tasks, UrbanMLLM-8B achieves 0.963 on spatial relationship reasoning, exceeding Gemini-3.1-Pro (0.777) and GPT-4o (0.709) by 18.6 and 25.4 points respectively. On cross-view indicator prediction, UrbanMLLM-8B leads GPT-4o on median income (0.761 vs. 0.684) and population (0.543 vs. 0.499), though GPT-4o retains an edge on poverty ratio (0.848 vs. 0.668), which the authors attribute to potential data overlap in GPT-4o's training. The CLIP-based baselines (RemoteCLIP, OpenAICLIP) are limited to indicator prediction only, where UrbanMLLM-8B still achieves 0.872/0.798 versus their 0.782/0.708, and the domain-specific satellite models GeoChat and LHRS-Bot are substantially outperformed across all shared tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

UrbanMLLM builds directly on VILA-1.5 as its base MLLM architecture (visual encoder, LLM backbone, visual adapter) and adds a cross-view perceiver module that applies cross-attention between satellite and street-view image embeddings — a mechanism standard in vision transformers but, to the authors' knowledge, not previously applied to unify aerial and ground-level urban imagery. The interleaved pre-training paradigm draws on MMC4-style multi-image documents and VILA's interleaved pre-training, but repurposes them for geospatially matched cross-view urban profiles. The paper departs from the dominant line of urban MLLMs (GeoChat, SkysenseGPT, LHRS-Bot, EarthGPT) that rely exclusively on satellite/remote-sensing imagery, and from CLIP-based urban models (RemoteCLIP, UrbanCLIP, UrbanVLP) that handle only prediction tasks, by being the first to jointly learn from both views within a single generative MLLM for perception, reasoning, and prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first large-scale cross-view urban dataset (4.5M paired satellite–street-view images with geotags and captions) and a 13-task benchmark that unifies satellite, street-view, and cross-view evaluation, filling a clear gap in urban MLLM research. However, several limitations temper its impact: the dataset and primary evaluation are U.S.-only, a portion of SFT supervision comes from detectors (GroundingDINO) or MLLM-generated labels rather than human annotation, and the single-view fallback (duplicating the image) does not truly test cross-view fusion. Open questions include whether the cross-view perceiver generalizes to non-U.S. city layouts, whether the auto-generated labels introduce systematic biases in indicator prediction, and whether the modest ablation gains (≈3% from the perceiver) justify the added architectural complexity at scale.
