## Problem

Hoeffding Trees and their ensemble variants (e.g., Adaptive Random Forests) select splits using fixed-sample concentration inequalities, yet the algorithm actually commits to a split at a data-dependent stopping time. This optional-stopping mismatch invalidates all existing statistical guarantees, allowing the probability of a false split to inflate to one. The paper asks how to design a splitting criterion that remains valid under arbitrary data-dependent stopping and non-stationary, dependent data streams.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequential stream of (X_t, Y_t) pairs where X_t is a d-dimensional feature vector and Y_t is a class label or regression target; for regression, losses are adaptively rescaled to [0,1] at each step. At each node, a finite candidate class of splits (feature index + threshold) is maintained, and the algorithm outputs a growing decision tree whose structure (splits, leaf regions, empirical class distributions) is updated online. The tree serves as a drop-in replacement for the Hoeffding Tree base learner, so it can be deployed standalone or inside Adaptive Random Forests without modifying the surrounding ensemble logic.

## Method

The paper reframes split selection as an online model-comparison problem: an incumbent (the current unsplit leaf) is tested against a challenger (the leaf with a candidate split applied) in shadow mode, with both predictors updated identically on every observation. For the strong null (challenger never outperforms incumbent), the test is a betting game where wealth is updated multiplicatively using the parameter-free Universal Portfolio over a discrete grid of betting fractions; for the weak null (no average advantage), an empirical Bernstein confidence sequence on the running mean loss difference is used. Under the null, the wealth process is a nonnegative supermartingale, so Ville's inequality yields an anytime-valid threshold: the split is committed only when wealth exceeds 1/α^{v,c}. Global false-split control over the entire tree lifetime follows from a union bound over the countable family of tests, with per-test levels allocated so their sum is at most α. Under stationary i.i.d. data, the paper further proves that the deployed tree's expected loss is monotonically non-increasing at every step and strictly improves at each committed split.

## Results

Across 12 streaming datasets (6 regression, 6 classification), the proposed Anytime-Valid Tree (AVT_B) outperforms the standard Hoeffding Tree (HT) on the majority of datasets, with abalone being the main exception. The ensemble variant AVF_B achieves the strongest overall performance, consistently outperforming the Adaptive Random Forest (ARF) baseline while producing substantially smaller trees (fewer nodes and less depth). Inference costs remain comparable to HT (standard tree traversal), while update costs increase due to maintaining shadow challenger statistics and Universal Portfolio wealth, though they stay within a few milliseconds per instance on most datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two baselines: the Hoeffding Tree (HT, Domingos & Hulten 2000) as a single-tree method and Adaptive Random Forests (ARF, Gomes et al. 2018) as the ensemble counterpart. AVT_B (the proposed single tree) outperforms HT on the majority of the 12 datasets in prequential accuracy/MAE, with abalone being the principal exception where HT attains better aggregate performance; however, AVT_B grows deeper trees than HT since a single tree must capture more heterogeneity without ensembling. AVF_B (the proposed ensemble) consistently outperforms ARF across nearly all datasets, with gains most pronounced on streams exhibiting consistent drops or increased volatility over time. Importantly, AVF_B also produces the smallest trees on average (fewer nodes and less depth) even compared to single-tree methods, while AVT_B tends to be deeper than HT. Computationally, inference times are comparable across all methods (standard tree traversal), but update times for AVT_B and AVF_B are higher due to maintaining shadow challenger statistics and Universal Portfolio wealth, though they remain within practical bounds (a few milliseconds per instance on most datasets).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper corrects a fundamental statistical flaw in Hoeffding Trees and all their variants (including McDiarmid-based and linearized-measure corrections): fixed-sample concentration bounds are invalid under the data-dependent stopping rules that online tree growth inherently employs. The fix draws on the anytime-valid inference / testing-by-betting framework (Shafer & Vovk, 2001; Orabona & Jun, 2023), using the Universal Portfolio as a parameter-free betting strategy for the strong null and empirical Bernstein confidence sequences (Howard et al., 2021) for the weak null. The resulting Anytime-Valid Tree (AVT) is a drop-in replacement for the HT base learner within Adaptive Random Forests (Gomes et al., 2018), the de facto standard ensemble for data streams, and inherits the strong/weak null distinction from Choe & Ramdas (2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Because Hoeffding Trees underpin virtually all bagging-based streaming ensembles (ARF, SRP, KNL), the identification that their split guarantees are vacuous under optional stopping is a significant correction for the streaming ML community. The anytime-valid replacement provides guarantees under arbitrary non-stationary and dependent streams—precisely the regime where these methods are deployed—while empirically producing smaller trees with equal or better accuracy. Open questions include the computational overhead of maintaining shadow-challenger wealth for many candidate splits (update times increase by roughly an order of magnitude), the practical tuning of the global alpha allocation across adaptively created tests, and whether the monotonicity guarantee extends cleanly to the strong-null (betting) variant without additional persistence assumptions.
