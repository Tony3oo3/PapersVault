## Problem

Current evaluation methods for video generative models rely on subjective human/VLM judgments or pixel-level trajectory matching, neither of which can rigorously verify whether a model encodes underlying physical laws. Trajectory matching in particular penalizes physically valid but visually divergent realizations (e.g., different initial velocities), while VLM evaluators hallucinate violations in clean physics. The paper asks: can we quantitatively measure whether generated videos respect Newtonian conservation laws and equations of motion, independent of pixel alignment?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264484/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes real-world videos of 9 controlled Newtonian experiments (falling, projectile, rolling, sliding, pendulum, double pendulum, bouncing, collision, spring; ~130 recordings total) as conditioning for VGMs via single-frame, multi-frame, or keyframe-interpolation prompts, optionally augmented with COSMOS-Transfer style variants for visual diversity. From both real and generated videos, SAM-2 extracts per-frame object masks whose centroids form 2D trajectories; velocity and acceleration are derived via central differences combined with sliding-window linear regression and Savitzky-Golay smoothing. The pipeline outputs three scores per experiment: a Dynamical score (0–1, PINN fit quality), a Physical Invariance score (0–1, normalized variance of conserved quantities), and a discard rate (fraction of generations failing permanence, consistency, or motion thresholds). No camera calibration is required because the PINN-based score is scale-invariant.

## Method

Morpheus evaluates physical plausibility at the level of governing equations and conserved invariants rather than pixel-aligned trajectories. For the Dynamical score, a small PINN (two hidden layers, 20 neurons, tanh) is trained on each extracted trajectory with a composite loss L_data + λ·L_physics, where L_physics encodes the experiment-specific ODE (e.g., ÿ+g=0 for free fall, θ̈+(g/l)sinθ=0 for a pendulum); the score is max(1−NMSE, 0), making it scale-invariant. For the Physical Invariance score, experiment-specific conserved quantities (total energy, momentum-to-mass ratio, oscillation period, etc.) are computed from the trajectory and their temporal standard deviation is normalized to (0,1), with 1 indicating perfect conservation. A three-stage automatic discard filter (object disappearance, count inconsistency, negligible motion) removes unusable generations before scoring. The key design choice is evaluating functional adherence to physical laws rather than trajectory overlap, which the paper validates via a real-vs-real stress test showing that Physics-IQ trajectory matching rejects perfectly valid physics (Spatiotemporal IoU as low as 0.010 for projectile) while Morpheus scores near-perfectly on the same recordings.

## Results

On the Morpheus benchmark (130 real-world Newtonian experiments), real-world videos achieve Dynamical Scores of 0.96–0.99 and Physical Invariance Scores ≥ 0.90 with zero discard rate, establishing the upper bound. The best video generative model, Wan2.1 with keyframe interpolation, reaches only a combined score of 0.66 with a 16% discard rate, revealing a substantial gap between visual fidelity and physical fidelity. Multi-frame conditioning (COSMOS-Predict2) improves to 0.59 over single-frame (~0.50), while PyramidalFlow and LTX-Video suffer discard rates exceeding 45% due to the 'stillness trap' (objects failing to move). Even the strongest closed-source models (Veo3, Kling-2.5) fail to consistently encode conservation laws, particularly on constrained dynamics like pendulums and collisions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among video generative models, open-source Wan2.1 with keyframe conditioning (0.66) outperforms all closed-source models (Veo3, Kling-2.5 at ~0.50 single-frame), suggesting physical reasoning depends on conditioning strategy rather than model scale or proprietary data. COSMOS-Predict2 in multi-frame mode (0.59) is competitive and benefits most from prompt upsampling (+27.9% single-frame), while CogVideoX and LTX-Video are weakest, with LTX-Video and PyramidalFlow suffering >45% discard rates from generating static videos. Against VLM-based evaluation (VideoPHY2-AUTOEVAL), Morpheus proves far more reliable: the VLM assigns only PC=3.71 to clean real-world videos (barely above 3.53 for generations), hallucinates 0% JPC pass on real projectiles, bouncing balls, and collisions, and even rates generated sliding books (82.8% rule adherence) above real ones (40.0%). Against trajectory-matching (Physics-IQ), a real-vs-real stress test shows Spatiotemporal IoU of only 0.010 for projectile and 0.021 for collision between two valid recordings, confirming that trajectory matching rejects physically correct but slightly divergent realizations—precisely the limitation Morpheus overcomes by scoring adherence to governing ODEs and conserved invariants instead.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), originally developed for solving PDEs on fully observable scientific data (Raissi et al., 2017), to evaluate noisy 2D trajectories extracted from generated videos. It departs from prior physical-plausibility benchmarks (VideoConPhysics, PhyGenBench, Physics-IQ, VAMP) that rely on VLM judgments, pixel-level trajectory matching, or object-tracking metrics, by instead scoring adherence to governing ODEs and conservation laws (energy, momentum, period). The evaluation pipeline also builds on standard video understanding tools (SAM-2 for segmentation, Depth Anything for camera-geometry validation, DEVA for object-count consistency) but combines them in a novel physics-informed scoring framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even the strongest VGMs (Veo3, Kling, Wan2.1) achieve at most ~0.66 combined physical score versus ~0.96–0.99 for real recordings undermines the 'world model' narrative for current video generators and provides a concrete, reproducible benchmark for tracking progress. The demonstration that a state-of-the-art VLM evaluator (VideoPHY2-AUTOEVAL) hallucinates violations in clean real-world physics and fails to separate real from generated videos (23.6% vs. 13.9% JPC) is a cautionary result for the field's reliance on VLM-based physical assessment. Limitations include restriction to Newtonian rigid-body mechanics, short fixed-camera clips, and 2D trajectory analysis; extending to fluid dynamics, 3D scenes with camera motion, and longer horizons remains open, as does whether physics-informed training objectives could close the gap.
