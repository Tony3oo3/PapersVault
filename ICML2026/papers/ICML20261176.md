## Problem

Looped transformers improve reasoning by performing multiple latent iterations per token, but the paper identifies a 'latent overthinking' phenomenon: most tokens are already correctly predicted after the first pass, yet uniform additional iterations can flip correct predictions into errors. This means existing looped architectures waste computation on easy tokens while actively degrading their accuracy, creating a gap between the potential of selective iteration (up to 7.3% oracle gain) and the uniform-depth approach actually deployed.

## Input / Output

The model takes a standard text prompt and autoregressively generates text tokens, but internally each output token may undergo 1–4 latent iterations before verbalization, with a per-token neural decider deciding whether to continue iterating or emit the token. Training uses a balanced 100K-sample subset of Open-R1 (math, QA, code) with sequences up to 8,192 tokens (4,096 for 4B models), fine-tuning from Qwen3-Base checkpoints. At inference, the continuation threshold is set to 0.9, resulting in roughly 7% of tokens receiving a second iteration and the rest verbalizing after a single forward pass.

## Method

TaH introduces three architectural components for selective latent iteration: (1) duo-causal attention, which extends the causal mask to a 2D (token position × iteration depth) grid so that deeper iterations can attend to shallower-depth KV states of all prior tokens while preserving full sequence-level parallelism; (2) depth-aware LoRA adapters applied only at iterations d > 1, shifting the deeper-iteration objective from general next-token prediction to focused hard-token refinement while preserving strong first-pass predictions; and (3) a lightweight MLP iteration decider that concatenates shallow, middle, and final hidden states to predict a per-token continuation probability. Training proceeds in two stages under a static oracle policy (top-1 mismatch of a reference SFT model): Stage 1 fine-tunes the backbone with next-token loss only at the oracle-assigned depth, and Stage 2 freezes the backbone and trains the decider via weighted binary cross-entropy to imitate the oracle's continue/stop decisions, decoupling the circular dependency between iteration policy and prediction quality.

## Results

Across nine reasoning benchmarks (math, QA, coding), TaH achieves average accuracy gains of +3.0% (0.6B) and +3.8% (1.7B) over the Standard Qwen3 baseline at identical parameter counts, while skipping latent iterations on 93% of tokens. With <3% additional parameters (TaH+), gains increase to +5.3% and +6.2% respectively. On the hardest benchmark AIME25, TaH-1.7B reaches 13.8% vs. 10.8% for Standard and 10.8% for Ouro. TaH also matches Standard's inference cost with only 4–5% additional FLOPs, compared to 2.19–2.27× overhead for AlwaysThink.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Standard Qwen3 baseline (same parameter count), TaH wins on 8 of 9 benchmarks at 1.7B (losing only GPQA by 0.1%), with an average gain of +3.8%; TaH+ extends this to +6.2%. Compared to Ouro (the closest looped-transformer competitor, same training data), TaH achieves +3.8% (0.6B) and +4.4% (1.7B) average gains while iterating on only 7% of tokens versus Ouro's 100%, and TaH+ reaches +6.1–6.8%. Against AlwaysThink (always-iterate variant of the same architecture), TaH gains +7.2% (0.6B) and +11.4% (1.7B), confirming that selective iteration avoids the latent overthinking problem. SoftThink (latent optimization via continuous concept space) is outperformed by TaH by +4.4% (0.6B) and +4.6% (1.7B) on average. Ponder-1.4B, a pre-trained looped model fine-tuned on the same data, collapses to 1.9% average accuracy on MATH500/GSM8K-500 versus TaH's 55%, indicating that TaH's fine-tuning-from-pretrained approach is far more effective than training looped models from scratch.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the looped transformer family (Saunshi et al., 2025; Zhu et al./Ouro, 2025; Zeng et al./Ponder, 2025) by identifying a critical failure mode—latent overthinking—where uniform iteration across all tokens degrades accuracy by flipping correct first-pass predictions into errors. Rather than scaling iteration depth uniformly, TaH introduces selective iteration as a new design principle: a lightweight neural decider triggers latent passes only on tokens likely to be mispredicted, while depth-aware LoRA adapters shift the objective from general next-token prediction to focused hard-token refinement. The duo-causal attention mechanism, which extends causality to a 2D (token position × iteration depth) grid, is a novel architectural contribution that enables cross-depth information flow without sacrificing sequence-level parallelism, distinguishing TaH from prior dynamic-recursion approaches that either sacrifice parallelism or restrict attention to a single depth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The latent overthinking finding challenges the implicit assumption in looped transformers that more computation per token is always beneficial, opening a direction where selective, sparse iteration outperforms uniform iteration at a fraction of the cost. Practically, TaH achieves 3.8–6.2% accuracy gains over strong baselines while incurring only 4–8% additional FLOPs (vs. 2× for always-iterate), making it a viable drop-in enhancement for parameter-constrained models. Limitations include evaluation restricted to 0.6B–4B models and a single 100K-sample training set (Open-R1), leaving open whether the approach scales to larger models or benefits from broader pretraining data. The 83% decider accuracy (vs. oracle) suggests room for improvement in the learned iteration policy, and the current implementation's Python-level control flow adds non-trivial latency overhead that system-level optimization could reduce.
