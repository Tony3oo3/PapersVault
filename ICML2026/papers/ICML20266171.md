## Problem

Deep neural networks have strong empirical performance in high-dimensional regression, but their theoretical guarantees for functional (infinite-dimensional) covariates remain largely unexplored. Existing DNN convergence-rate results assume fixed-dimensional, compactly-supported inputs, whereas functional data are inherently infinite-dimensional and unbounded, making those results inapplicable. This paper asks whether DNN estimators can attain minimax-optimal polynomial convergence rates in functional linear and generalized linear regression, and characterizes the fundamental limits for fully nonparametric functional regression.

## Input / Output

The method takes a functional covariate X(t) ∈ L²[0,1] and a scalar response Y, and produces a scalar prediction (regression) or class probability (classification). The functional input is consumed via its Karhunen-Loève expansion, truncated to K_n principal component scores forming a K_n-dimensional vector; critically, K_n grows with sample size n (typically K_n ∝ n^{1/(a+2b)}), so the network input dimension is not fixed but increases with data. This growing-dimensionality setting is the key departure from standard DNN theory and is what necessitates the new analytical techniques.

## Method

The estimator is a fully connected ReLU network trained by empirical risk minimization (least squares for the linear model, negative log-likelihood for the generalized linear model) on the first K_n FPCA scores of each functional covariate. The main theoretical contribution extends the bias-variance decomposition of Schmidt-Hieber (2020) to the growing-dimensionality, unbounded-input setting by introducing a truncation step on the principal component scores and carefully controlling the additional truncation bias. For functional GLMs, the paper uses Bregman divergence as the prediction loss and adapts the change-of-measure technique of Dou et al. (2012) to bound the nonlinear link-function contribution. Under polynomial eigenvalue decay (λ_k ∝ k^{-a}) and slope-function smoothness (b_k ∝ k^{-b}), the DNN estimator achieves the minimax-optimal prediction rate n^{-(a+2b-1)/(a+2b)} up to logarithmic factors, matching classical FPCA-based methods while requiring weaker technical assumptions. For fully nonparametric functional regression, a Le Cam two-point lower bound via small-ball probabilities shows that no polynomial rate is achievable without additional structural assumptions.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution establishes that DNN estimators achieve the minimax-optimal polynomial prediction error rate Õ_P(n^((1−a−2b)/(a+2b))) for both functional linear regression (Theorem 3.4) and functional generalized linear models (Theorem 3.7), matching the classical FPCA-based rates of Cai & Hall (2006) up to a logarithmic factor. For fully nonparametric functional regression, Theorem 3.8 proves a lower bound of c(log n)^(−p(a−1)), confirming that no polynomial rate is achievable without additional structure. In the empirical studies on UK electricity and NHANES data, DNNs exhibit a characteristic U-shaped error curve across input resolutions, with optimal performance at intermediate dimensions (3–6 hour bins or 90% FVE for FPCA on UK data; 20 bins or 90% FVE on NHANES), while linear models degrade substantially in nonlinear settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not include a formal head-to-head comparison table against named competing methods, but makes several important comparisons. Against classical FPCA-based estimators (Cai & Hall 2006; Hall & Horowitz 2007), the DNN estimator achieves the same polynomial convergence exponent but with an additional log n factor, while operating under substantially weaker assumptions (no fourth-moment orthogonality condition on scores, and a relaxed smoothness requirement b > 1/2 instead of b ≥ a+2). In the simulation studies, DNNs consistently approximate the underlying regression function across all six test cases (linear, quadratic, interaction, and logistic), whereas Generalized Linear Models perform well only in the linear cases (Cases 1, 4, 6) and degrade substantially in the nonlinear ones (Cases 2, 3, 5). Between the two preprocessing strategies, FPCA provides more efficient representations than binning at comparable effective dimensions due to its parsimony, though both reveal the same resolution-versus-learnability trade-off that underlies the theoretical bias-variance balance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two mature theoretical traditions: functional data analysis (FDA), where FPCA-based estimators for functional linear and GLM regression have well-established minimax rates (Hall & Horowitz, 2007; Cai & Hall, 2006; Dou et al., 2012), and DNN regression theory, where convergence rates are known for fixed-dimensional, compactly supported covariates (Schmidt-Hieber, 2020; Kohler & Langer, 2021; Suzuki, 2019). The key technical departure is that functional covariates violate both the fixed-dimension and compact-domain assumptions underlying existing DNN theory: the principal component scores are unbounded and the effective dimension K_n grows with sample size. The paper extends the bias-variance decomposition and covering-number framework of Schmidt-Hieber (2020) and Suzuki (2019) by introducing a truncation step for the scores and carefully controlling the resulting approximation bias, while also extending the change-of-measure technique of Dou et al. (2012) to the DNN estimator for functional GLMs. For the fully nonparametric case, the lower bound connects to the small-ball probability literature (Li & Linde, 1999; Mas, 2012) that has long shown polynomial rates are unattainable in infinite-dimensional regression.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first convergence-rate analysis for DNN regression with functional covariates, establishing that DNNs match classical FPCA minimax rates (up to logarithmic factors) under strictly weaker regularity assumptions — notably dropping the fourth-moment zero condition and relaxing the slope-function smoothness requirement. The nonparametric lower bound (Theorem 3.8) and the detailed discussion of why standard DNN dimension-reduction tools (manifold structure, effective Minkowski dimension) cannot yield polynomial rates in the functional setting are important negative results that clarify the fundamental limits of the problem. The central open question — whether any DNN architecture or training procedure can achieve polynomial rates for fully nonparametric functional regression — remains unresolved, and the paper's framework provides a concrete starting point. Practically, the U-shaped error curves observed in the data applications (UK electricity, NHANES) offer actionable guidance on the resolution-versus-learnability trade-off when choosing FPCA truncation levels or binning resolutions.
