## Problem

Token-based transformer world models for visual RL suffer from temporal inconsistency in long-horizon rollouts—objects duplicate, disappear, or transmute—because they treat next-frame prediction purely as independent token generation without modeling the persistence of tokens across time. This forces the model to relearn persistent structure at every step, making identity preservation fragile and compounding errors over imagined trajectories.

## Input / Output

The method operates on discrete tokens produced by either a nearest-neighbor patch-lookup tokenizer (e.g., 7×7×3 patches mapped to a 4096-entry codebook) or a learned VQ-VAE tokenizer, so it is tokenizer-agnostic. At decoding time it consumes three inputs: the previous frame's L tokens, the transformer's per-position categorical predictions over the codebook, and the 2D spatial coordinates of each token position. The output is a finalized next-frame of L tokens where each position is either an exact copy of a previous-frame token or a sample from the transformer's predicted distribution, determined by a binary assignment matrix.

## Method

ITC inserts an optimal transport decoding step between the transformer's next-token predictions and the final next-state tokens, leaving the transformer architecture and training loss completely unchanged. It constructs a bipartite graph with 2L source vertices (previous-frame tokens and wildcard generation slots) and L destination vertices (next-frame positions), scoring edges via an affinity matrix that combines token similarity (dot product of the transformer's predicted distribution with the previous token's one-hot encoding) and a spatial displacement penalty capped at a fixed radius. A Sinkhorn solver with entropic regularization produces a soft transport plan, which is then converted to a strict one-to-one binary assignment via a greedy column-wise argmax binarization procedure adapted from Puzzle Mix. The resulting latent correspondence variables determine whether each next-frame token is copied from the previous frame or sampled from the transformer, enabling selective reuse of persistent structure while still allowing new content to appear. The method also adopts 3D RoPE (spatial + temporal rotary position embeddings) to better encode the multi-dimensional structure of the token grid.

## Results

On Craftax-classic after 1M environment steps, ITC achieves a return of 72.46% and a score of 35.60%, surpassing the previous state-of-the-art (Dedieu et al., 2025) of 67.42% return and 27.91% score. On Atari 100K, ITC (built on Simulus) achieves an interquartile mean (IQM) of 1.092 and optimality gap of 0.376, improving over Simulus (0.990 / 0.412). On MinAtar, ITC outperforms the previous model-based state of the art in all 4 games (e.g., 50.04 vs. 44.81 on Asterix, 99.53 vs. 93.92 on Breakout). On Craftax, ITC reaches 7.09% return and 2.40% score, exceeding both Dedieu et al. (5.44% / 1.53%) and Simulus (6.59% / —).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ITC is built directly on top of the Dedieu et al. (2025) backbone (patch tokenizer + GPT-2 transformer) and adds only an optimal-transport decoding step, so the Craftax-classic comparison isolates the decoding contribution: ITC improves return from 68.55% to 72.46% and score from 27.24% to 35.60% over the same backbone with ITC's hyperparameters. Against earlier transformer world models (IRIS, Δ-IRIS, DART) and the non-transformer DreamerV3, ITC leads by a wide margin on Craftax-classic (72.46% vs. 25.0%, 35.0%, 55.45%, and 53.2% return respectively). On Atari 100K, ITC is applied to the Simulus (VQ-VAE tokenizer) backbone and surpasses Simulus in IQM (1.092 vs. 0.990) and optimality gap (0.376 vs. 0.412), while also beating STORM (0.561 IQM) and Diamond (0.641 IQM). On MinAtar, ITC outperforms both the model-based Dedieu et al. (2025) baseline in all 4 games and the model-free Artificial Dopamine (AD) agent in all 4 games. On the harder Craftax environment, ITC (7.09% return) exceeds both Dedieu et al. (5.44%) and Simulus (6.59%), confirming that the optimal-transport decoding step generalizes to more complex dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of token-based transformer world models (IRIS, Δ-IRIS, DART, Dedieu et al., Simulus) by reframing next-frame prediction from a pure token-generation problem into a structured assignment problem with latent token correspondence variables. The core mechanism borrows optimal transport (specifically the Sinkhorn algorithm) from the OT literature and applies it as a plug-and-play decoding step between the transformer's next-token predictions and the final state tokens, drawing on the binarization procedure from Puzzle Mix (Kim et al., 2020). It also adopts 3D VideoRoPE positional encoding (Wei et al., 2025) to better encode spatiotemporal structure, and demonstrates compatibility with both nearest-neighbor patch-lookup tokenizers (Dedieu et al.) and learned VQ-VAE tokenizers (Simulus/Cohen et al.), positioning ITC as a tokenizer-agnostic decoding layer rather than a new architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ITC addresses a fundamental and widely observed failure mode in world models—temporal inconsistency (object duplication, disappearance, transmutation) that compounds over long imagination rollouts—by explicitly modeling which tokens persist across frames. The plug-and-play nature (no architecture or training changes, only 2.8% overhead) makes it immediately adoptable on top of existing backbones, and the consistent SOTA across four diverse benchmarks (Craftax-classic, Craftax, MinAtar, Atari 100K) with two different tokenizer families strengthens the generality claim. Open questions include whether the fixed distance cap and the hybrid center/edge strategy for Craftax generalize to 3D or non-grid environments, and whether the approach extends to continuous-latent world models where discrete token assignment is less natural.
