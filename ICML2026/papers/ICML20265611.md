## Problem

Naive concatenation of heterogeneous multispectral sequences (NMR, IR, MS) for molecular structure elucidation exhibits a counterintuitive full-modality collapse: adding more spectral modalities causes substantial performance degradation rather than improvement, because long, low-density modalities (IR, MS) numerically dominate attention and gradient updates, overwhelming short, high-density constraints (NMR). This multispectral imbalance—driven by orders-of-magnitude differences in sequence length, token autocorrelation, and per-token semantic density—induces negative transfer and gradient conflicts that standard early-fusion architectures cannot resolve.

## Input / Output

The method consumes three spectral modalities with modality-specific tokenization: NMR (¹H + ¹³C) is discretized into symbolic tokens representing 0.1 ppm chemical shift bins and multiplicities (max length 256), IR is binned into 1024 frequency intervals with local binning compression (window=4, stride=4), and MS undergoes top-100 peak filtering by relative intensity. Each token carries an explicit modality tag. The output is an autoregressively generated SMILES string decoded via beam search (beam width 10), evaluated by Top-K accuracy where the ground-truth SMILES must appear among the top K candidates.

## Method

MM-Spectrum replaces the dense FFN in a Transformer encoder with a structured sparse MoE layer featuring three key mechanisms. First, modality-aware routing injects learnable tag embeddings and modality biases into the router input (h̃_i = h_i + e_{m(i)} + b_{m(i)}), decoupling spectral identity from token content to stabilize early specialization without hard-coding expert assignments. Second, the expert pool is partitioned into shared (redundancy), modality-specific (unique constraints), and interaction (cross-modal synergy) subspaces guided by a Partial Information Decomposition inductive bias, enforced by lightweight consistency and separability regularizers. Third, heterogeneous Heavy/Light experts with varying hidden dimensions are paired with a computation-cost penalty (L_cost = (1/B)Σ p_{i,e}·c_e) that induces Pareto-efficient capacity allocation. Training follows a stability-to-specialization curriculum that anneals balancing/entropy regularizers and router temperature from high to low, transitioning through coverage, alignment, and specialization phases.

## Results

On the full tri-modality (NMR+MS+IR) setting of the Alberts et al. (2024) benchmark, MM-Spectrum achieves 76.04% Top-1, 87.83% Top-5, and 90.26% Top-10 SMILES elucidation accuracy, improving over the Dense Concatenation baseline (44.29% / 59.39% / 62.04%) by +31.75 / +28.44 / +28.22 percentage points. Critically, the baseline's full-modality performance (44.29%) is *worse* than its NMR-only performance (69.83%), confirming the multispectral collapse phenomenon that MM-Spectrum resolves. On the large-scale MMST dataset (~6M samples), MM-Spectrum reaches 87.35% Top-1 versus 65.71% for the dense baseline (+21.64 pp), and on experimental SDBS spectra it lifts Top-1 from 14.10% (full-modality baseline) to 26.67% (from scratch) or 59.52% (pre-train & fine-tune).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MM-Spectrum decisively outperforms all five compared baselines under the full tri-modality setting. Against the primary Dense Concatenation baseline (Alberts et al., 2024), it gains +31.75 pp in Top-1 (76.04% vs. 44.29%), reversing the full-modality collapse where the baseline underperforms even NMR-only (69.83%). Among alternative fusion strategies (Table 11), Gradient Blending (Wang et al., 2020) is the strongest non-MoE competitor at 46.78% Top-1, yet MM-Spectrum still leads by +29.26 pp. PCGrad (Yu et al., 2020) and OGM-GE (Wu et al., 2022) yield only marginal gains over the dense baseline (44.73% and 44.87% respectively), while Contrastive Alignment (Liang et al., 2024) actually degrades performance to 39.01%, confirming that representation-level alignment alone cannot counteract the severe spectral scale and redundancy imbalance. The gap to all baselines exceeds 29 pp in Top-1, indicating that the improvement is architectural (structured MoE with modality-aware routing) rather than attributable to optimization-level gradient surgery or contrastive pre-training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sparse Mixture-of-Experts lineage (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022; Du et al., 2022) by identifying that standard MoE's homogeneity assumption breaks down under severe multispectral imbalance, where long low-density sequences (IR/MS) numerically dominate short high-density constraints (NMR). It departs from prior multimodal spectroscopy work (Alberts et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024) that relies on naive early-fusion concatenation or contrastive alignment, reframing multispectral fusion as a structured allocation problem guided by Partial Information Decomposition (Williams & Beer, 2010) and multi-gate routing (Ma et al., 2018). The curriculum scheduling mechanism (Bengio et al., 2009) is repurposed to anneal MoE balancing constraints from broad coverage to specialized routing, a departure from the fixed auxiliary losses used in Switch Transformers and GLaM.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates a counterintuitive and practically critical failure mode: adding spectral modalities to a naive concatenation baseline causes catastrophic performance collapse (44.29% vs. 69.83% NMR-only), a phenomenon that would mislead practitioners into discarding complementary evidence. The structured MoE solution reverses this with a +31.75% Top-1 gain, validated across three datasets and robust to missing modalities, suggesting the design principles (modality-aware routing, PID-structured experts, heterogeneous capacities) could transfer to other severely imbalanced multimodal settings. Open questions include whether the PID-inspired expert partition is genuinely necessary beyond providing additional parameters, whether the approach scales to more than three modalities or to 3D structure prediction, and whether the curriculum schedule is critical or merely a training trick.
