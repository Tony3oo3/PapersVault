## Problem

Subsampled natural gradient (SNG) is a workhorse optimizer for high-precision scientific machine learning, but standard convergence analyses rely on a two-independent-mini-batch proxy that decouples the stochastic gradient from the stochastic preconditioner. This proxy fails to provide meaningful convergence rate characterizations in realistic small-sample settings (k ≪ n), where the preconditioner captures only a tiny subset of parameter space and is unlikely to be informative for an independent gradient estimate.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a parametric optimization problem min_θ L(v_θ) where v_θ = Jθ is a linear ansatz, taking as input the current parameters θ_t, the function-space gradient r, the model Jacobian J ∈ R^{m×n}, and a sample size k < n. Each iteration draws a single mini-batch S of k rows and produces an updated parameter vector θ_{t+1} = θ_t − η J_S^{+(λ)} r_S, requiring only inversion of a k×k kernel matrix. The theoretical output is a linear convergence rate bound expressed in terms of the sketch-and-project constant α (minimum eigenvalue of the expected projector) and a second-moment quantity γ, with the optimal rate scaling as α/γ.

## Method

The paper reframes SNG as a regularized randomized block Kaczmarz (sketch-and-project) step applied to the natural gradient subproblem, rather than as a stochastic preconditioner for the deterministic natural gradient. The key technical insight is that under squared volume sampling (SVS), the expected SNG direction J_S^{+(λ)} r_S equals a preconditioned gradient descent step W̃ J^⊤ r even in the presence of coupling between gradient and preconditioner (Lemma 4.1), where W̃ = (J^⊤J)^{−1/2} P̄ (J^⊤J)^{−1/2} combines the expected projector with the Fisher information. This yields global convergence for any single mini-batch size (Theorem 4.2) and, for the linear least-quadratics model problem, an explicit linear rate (1 − η·λ_min(H)·α)^t (Theorem 5.1) where α can scale superlinearly in k under fast spectral decay of J. The paper further shows that the SPRING momentum algorithm arises exactly from Nesterov-accelerated sketch-and-project methods (Theorem 6.1), explaining why its acceleration benefit is greatest at small sample sizes where α is small.

## Results

The paper establishes that under squared volume sampling (SVS), the expected SNG direction equals a preconditioned gradient descent step without decoupling gradient and preconditioner (Lemma 4.1), yielding global convergence for any mini-batch size (Theorem 4.2). The key convergence rate for the linear least-quadratics model is E‖θ_t − θ*‖²_{W̃⁺} ≤ (1 − η·λ_min(H)·α)^t ‖θ_0 − θ*‖²_{W̃⁺} (Theorem 5.1), where the optimal rate scales as α/γ. Empirically, α grows superlinearly (even exponentially) in sample size k when the Jacobian J exhibits fast spectral decay, while γ exerts only a mild damping effect (Figure 3). SPRING achieves an accelerated rate of O((1 − √(α/β))^t) and provides substantial speedup for small k, with the gap shrinking as k increases (Figure 5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is conceptual: the sketch-and-project framework (this work) versus the standard stochastic preconditioning framework (two independent mini-batches) used in prior analyses (Ren & Goldfarb 2019; Martens 2020; Yang et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2024). Numerically (Figure 2), the SVS-SNG proxy closely tracks the realistic single-mini-batch SNG algorithm across varying step sizes and regularization, while the two-batch proxy exhibits drastically different behavior, confirming that prior analyses fail at realistic small sample sizes (k=10, n=7801). Relative to SGD, SNG's advantage is that the sketch-and-project constant α can scale superlinearly (or even exponentially) in k when J has fast spectral decay, whereas SGD's linear convergence rate scales at most linearly in k; this is confirmed empirically in Figure 4 where SNG's rate constant grows superlinearly for a quadratically decaying spectrum but only linearly for a flat spectrum. For the SPRING comparison (Goldshlager et al. 2024, Figure 5), SPRING provides substantial acceleration over SNG at small sample sizes (consistent with the √(α/β) vs. α rate improvement), but the gap narrows as k increases since larger k already yields larger α and thus less room for acceleration. The paper does not claim SPRING improves the dependence on the Hessian condition number κ(H).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard stochastic-preconditioning analysis of subsampled natural gradient (SNG), which relies on a two-independent-mini-batch proxy to decouple the stochastic gradient from the stochastic preconditioner (Ren & Goldfarb 2019; Martens 2020; Yang et al. 2020). Instead, it reframes SNG as a regularized randomized block Kaczmarz method within the sketch-and-project framework (Gower & Richtárik 2015; Needell & Tropp 2014), using squared volume sampling (SVS) as a theoretical device to compute exact expectations without decoupling. The key SVS expectation identity is inherited from Dereziński & Yang (2024), and the accelerated sketch-and-project formulation used to explain SPRING draws on Gower et al. (2018) and Dereziński et al. (2025b). The paper thus unifies previously separate threads—SNG for scientific ML, randomized Kaczmarz methods, and Nesterov-accelerated sketch-and-project—under a single analytical lens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first convergence-rate characterization of SNG in the small-sample regime (k ≪ n), which is the practically relevant setting for scientific ML problems like neural network wavefunctions and PINNs. The finding that the optimal rate scales as α/γ, where α captures the sketch-and-project constant that can grow superlinearly with k under spectral decay, provides a concrete explanation for why SNG outperforms SGD in these settings. Key open questions include: tight bounds on the second-moment quantity γ (only preliminary characterizations are given), extending the analysis beyond the linear least-quadratics model problem to general nonlinear settings, verifying Conjecture 6.2 for SPRING in the LLQ setting, and identifying algorithms that improve the condition-number dependence. Practically, the paper suggests that SVS-inspired sampling (e.g., introducing negative correlations within mini-batches) could yield new practical algorithms, though direct SVS implementation remains infeasible at scale.
