## Problem

While prior work showed that cryptographically undetectable backdoors can be planted in single-hidden-layer networks, no construction achieved statistical undetectability (undetectable even by unbounded adversaries) in the white-box setting for full DNNs, nor did it quantify the backdoor's strength relative to what an adversary could achieve without it. This paper asks whether a malicious trainer can embed a backdoor into a large class of deep feedforward networks that is statistically indistinguishable from honest training while granting the trainer exponentially greater power to generate invariance-based adversarial examples.

## Input / Output

The model consumes discrete integer vectors (e.g., 28×28 grayscale image pixels in [0,255]) and produces a real-valued embedding in R^l. The backdoor vector z ∈ {±1}^n is sampled jointly with the first-layer matrix during training and is the only difference between the honest and backdoored training algorithms. Given any input x, the backdoor owner computes x' = x + z to obtain a colliding pair whose model outputs are unusually close, while no efficient adversary can produce any pair with comparable collision quality.

## Method

The backdoor is planted by rejection-sampling the first-layer m×n Gaussian matrix A conditioned on ‖Az‖_∞ ≤ κ√n for a randomly chosen z ∈ {±1}^n, where κ is set so that A remains statistically close to i.i.d. Gaussian. The key analytical challenge is bounding the total variation distance between the planted and null distributions, which reduces to showing that the number of ±1 solutions N(A) to ‖Az‖_∞ ≤ κ√n concentrates in its second moment when m = o(n); this is achieved via a correlation-based moment calculation over ρ-correlated Gaussians and a binary-entropy tail bound. The resulting Rényi divergence is O(m/n · log(n/m) + e^{-Ω(m)}), yielding a TV distance of Õ(√(m/n)). The backdoor extends to full DNNs by substituting A into the first (frozen, compressing) layer while keeping all subsequent layers bi-Lipschitz, so that the Lipschitz distortion β_upper bounds how much the collision quality degrades through the network. Under standard lattice-hardness assumptions (SBP/NBP), no polynomial-time adversary given only A can find any z' with ‖Az'‖_∞ remotely as small as ‖Az‖_∞, giving the backdoor strength Õ(2^{n/m}/(√(nm)·β_upper)).

## Results

The paper proves that backdoors of strength at least $\tilde{\Omega}(2^{n/m}/(\sqrt{nm}\cdot\beta_{\text{upper}}))$ can be planted in DNNs with a frozen compressing Gaussian first layer, while the total variation distance between honest and backdoored model descriptions is only $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{m/n})$. In the proof-of-concept experiment on Fashion-MNIST (256×784 first layer), the planted backdoor achieves $\|\mathbf{Az}\| \approx 10^{-10}$, while the best of three tested collision-finding algorithms achieves $\|\mathbf{Az}\| \geq 0.03$, yielding an empirical backdoor strength of roughly $10^9$. The embedding model reaches ~89% test accuracy with a linear classifier (dropping to ~86.5% after input scaling to accommodate the backdoor), and the D'Agostino-Pearson normality test confirms the backdoored matrix is statistically indistinguishable from i.i.d. Gaussian (all p-values > 0.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the planted backdoor against three analytic collision-finding algorithms from Bogdanov et al. (2025): Algorithm A (minimum column norm), Algorithm B (Cool), and Algorithm C (KernelRound). Across all tested parameter settings (n=100, m=10/20/30), the planted solution achieves $\|\mathbf{Az}\| \approx 10^{-10}$ while the best algorithm (B, Cool) achieves only $\|\mathbf{Az}\| \geq 0.03$, a gap of roughly $10^9$ in backdoor strength. The LLL lattice-reduction algorithm is also tested and fails to recover the planted solution for $\kappa \geq 10^{-m/3}$ when $n=(10/3)m$. In contrast to prior work by Goldwasser et al. (2022), whose CLWE-based backdoor has strength less than 1 (i.e., an adversary can outperform the backdoor holder), this paper's backdoor strength is provably exponentially large. Compared to Kalavasis et al. (2024), which relies on indistinguishability obfuscation and requires the honest model to contain obfuscated circuits, this construction uses only standard lattice-based assumptions and applies to natural DNN architectures with bi-Lipschitz activations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the undetectable backdoor line initiated by Goldwasser et al. (FOCS 2022) in three key ways: from one hidden layer to full DNNs, from sensitivity-based to invariance-based adversarial examples, and from computational to statistical undetectability. The security of the construction rests on the computational hardness of the symmetric binary perceptron / number balancing problem over Gaussian matrices, building directly on Vafa & Vaikuntanathan (STOC 2025) and Bogdanov et al. (2025). Unlike Kalavasis et al. (2024), which relies on indistinguishability obfuscation and requires obfuscated circuits in the honest model, this work achieves white-box undetectability using only the natural structure of a frozen Gaussian compressing first layer, with no contrived cryptographic components in the network itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result establishes a fundamental, provable power asymmetry between model trainers and users in MLaaS and foundation-model settings: the trainer can generate invariance-based adversarial examples of exponentially greater strength than any efficient adversary, while the tampering is statistically undetectable even given full model weights. The conceptual insight that the Johnson-Lindenstrauss transform—a standard, natural dimensionality-reduction tool—carries lattice-based cryptographic hardness within it challenges the assumption that cryptographic security requires deliberate algebraic design. Open questions include whether the conservative theoretical bound (which requires statistical indistinguishability) can be tightened to match the empirically observed near-unlimited backdoor strength, whether other forms of model control are possible, and whether the results extend beyond the specific architectural constraints (frozen Gaussian first layer, bi-Lipschitz remaining layers, discrete inputs). The practical mitigation is straightforward—secure randomness generation—but the finding that controlling the first layer's randomness suffices for a powerful backdoor is a significant trust implication for outsourced training.
