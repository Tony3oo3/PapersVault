## Problem

Computer architecture, which requires reasoning about multi-objective trade-offs across compute, memory, and interconnect design spaces, remains entirely absent from LLM evaluation benchmarks. Existing systems benchmarks target code generation, RTL synthesis, or software engineering artifacts but do not assess the conceptual and analytical reasoning—recalling principles, analyzing workloads, designing under constraints, and implementing solutions—that underpins architectural decision-making. This gap prevents systematic measurement of whether LLM progress translates into capabilities useful for agentic architecture design.

## Input / Output

QUARCH v1.0 comprises 2,671 expert-validated QA pairs in two formats: 1,547 multiple-choice questions (MCQs) with four answer options and 1,124 free-response questions (FRQs), spanning 2,123 text-only and 548 multimodal items that include datapath diagrams, circuit schematics, pipeline timing charts, roofline plots, and specification tables. Each question carries relevant context describing the scenario, constraints, and figures. Evaluation produces per-generation accuracy (n=3 samples per question, estimating pass@k=1) disaggregated by four skills (Recall, Analyze, Design, Implement), 13 architecture topics, and input modality, with FRQs graded by an LLM-as-a-judge validated against human domain-expert consensus.

## Method

The benchmark is constructed via a three-pronged strategy: (1) synthetic generation of cloze-style MCQs from a curated corpus of technical manuals, publications, and online resources, filtered by LLM-as-a-judge and verified by three independent human experts; (2) expert crowdsourcing and time-boxed competitions through a web portal that targets advanced analysis, design, and implementation skills difficult to generate synthetically; and (3) automated parsing of university architecture exams using LlamaParse for diagram extraction and LLM-based segmentation into context/question/solution fields. A formalized skills framework (Recall, Analyze, Design, Implement) enables fine-grained competency tracking, with all QAs manually annotated by domain experts for primary and secondary skill labels. For FRQ evaluation, LLM-as-a-judge (Claude-3.7-Sonnet-Thinking) is validated against 11 human experts across 925 responses, achieving 89.41% agreement comparable to the 90.92% human-human inter-rater rate. A knowledge-transfer case study fine-tunes Gemma-3-27B-IT and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct on 676 SFT samples distilled from 45 memory-related QUARCH FRQs (including 135 GPT-5.2 explanations) and evaluates on a realistic memory hierarchy design task using Accelergy simulation, showing up to 1.99× more area-efficient solutions and 40% more viable designs.

## Results

On QUARCH v1.0 (2,671 expert-validated QAs), frontier models achieve 84–91% on QUARCH-RECALL but only 33.7–73.2% on QUARCH-REASONING, exposing a large gap between domain knowledge retrieval and higher-order architectural reasoning. GPT-5.2 (reasoning) leads with 73.2% on reasoning QAs, followed by GEMINI-3-FLASH (71.9%) and GEMINI-3-PRO (71.0%), while LLAMA-4-MAVERICK and MISTRAL-MEDIUM-3.1 trail at ~34%. In the fine-tuning case study, SFT on QUARCH memory-related QAs improves GEMMA-3-27B-IT's success rate from 30% to 70% and LLAMA-3.3-70B's from 55% to 95% on a memory hierarchy design task, with the best fine-tuned designs achieving up to 1.99× more area-efficient solutions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, QUARCH evaluates 10 frontier models (plus 20 additional models in the appendix) rather than proposing a single method against baselines. The most striking comparison is between reasoning and non-reasoning variants: GPT-5.2 outperforms GPT-5 (Non-Reasoning) by 24.8 percentage points on reasoning QAs (73.2% vs. 48.4%) but only by 2 points on recall (88.4% vs. 86.4%), confirming that test-time deliberation specifically benefits higher-order architectural skills. Among multimodal models, GEMINI-3-FLASH and GEMINI-3-PRO form a tight second tier (71.9% and 71.0% on reasoning), while CLAUDE-SONNET-4 (48.1%) and CLAUDE-3.7-SONNET-THINKING (51.8%) lag significantly. At the bottom, LLAMA-4-MAVERICK (33.7%) and MISTRAL-MEDIUM-3.1 (33.8%) show that strong recall (~85%) does not translate to reasoning ability. In the knowledge-transfer case study, fine-tuned GEMMA-3-27B-IT surpasses its base model by 40 percentage points in constraint-satisfaction rate (70% vs. 30%), and fine-tuned LLAMA-3.3-70B reaches 95% vs. 55%, demonstrating that QUARCH-derived knowledge directly improves downstream architectural design.

## Relation to Prior Work

QUARCH carves new ground by targeting conceptual and analytical reasoning about computer architecture design decisions, a gap the authors identify between software-oriented benchmarks (SWE-bench, CodeMMLU, CRUXEval) that evaluate code artifacts and hardware-oriented benchmarks (VerilogEval, CVDP, MG-Verilog) that evaluate RTL/EDA tool interaction. The four-skill decomposition (Recall, Analyze, Design, Implement) draws on educational competency frameworks (ABET, ACM Bloom's taxonomy for computing) but is novel in its application to LLM evaluation. For evaluation methodology, the paper builds on the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023) and validates it against human expert grading, while the knowledge transfer case study leverages supervised fine-tuning and distillation techniques from prior work (Lampinen et al., 2022; Muennighoff et al., 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QUARCH fills a critical gap by providing the first dedicated, expert-validated benchmark for architectural reasoning, enabling fine-grained tracking of LLM progress in a domain that underpins all computing systems design. The pronounced recall-to-reasoning gap (84–91% vs. 34–73%) and the demonstration that fine-tuning on a small QUARCH subset yields up to 1.99× more area-efficient memory designs suggest that architectural reasoning is a distinct, trainable capability rather than an emergent byproduct of general knowledge. Open questions include whether the skills framework captures all relevant architectural competencies, how the benchmark will evolve as models improve, and whether the gated evaluation protocol can sustain long-term validity against contamination. The benchmark's extensibility via a public submission portal and its modular skills taxonomy position it as a growing resource rather than a static snapshot.
