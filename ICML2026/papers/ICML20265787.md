## Problem

Existing certified robustness methods for regression (α-smoothing, RS-Reg) rely on probability-of-inclusion criteria that reduce the smoothed function's behavior to a binary inside/outside decision, discarding local geometric information and yielding loose certificates. Additionally, these methods anchor certification to a fixed target (ground-truth label or base-model output) rather than the smoothed prediction g(x) actually deployed at inference, creating a theoretical misalignment. This paper addresses both limitations by deriving prediction-centered certificates that exploit higher-order statistics (variance and gradient) of the smoothed regressor.

## Input / Output

The method takes a base regression model (E2CNN for 28×28 grayscale MNIST rotation, MiVOLO-v2 for 64×64 RGB UTKFace age estimation) and, at test time, estimates the variance C and gradient norm ‖G‖₂ of the base function under isotropic Gaussian smoothing noise via U-statistics on n Monte Carlo samples. It outputs a certified ℓ₂ radius R (in normalized pixel space, where radius r corresponds to 255r in 8-bit coordinates) guaranteeing |g(z+δ)−g(z)|≤ε for all perturbations with ‖δ‖₂≤R, with the certificate centered on the smoothed prediction g(z) itself. The radius is computed by bisection search over a monotonically increasing worst-case shift function.

## Method

The core contribution is a variational formulation of the worst-case base function: given empirically observable constraints (variance ≤ C, gradient = G, and optionally pointwise bounds [−M,M]), the paper solves a calculus-of-variations problem using Lagrange multipliers to analytically characterize the function f* that maximizes the smoothed output shift. A key structural result is that the worst-case perturbation aligns with the gradient direction G, reducing the d-dimensional search to a one-dimensional problem depending only on ‖G‖₂. In the unbounded setting this yields a closed-form worst-case shift Δ_max(R) = √(C−σ²‖G‖²)·√(e^{R²/σ²}−1−R²/σ²) + R‖G‖₂; in the bounded setting, a dimensionality-reduction theorem shows the multivariate optimization is exactly equivalent to optimizing a univariate clipped affine function of the likelihood ratio. The approach generalizes the Neyman-Pearson lemma from classification to regression by replacing the 0/1 indicator with a real-valued worst-case function, and requires tuning only the smoothing scale σ (rather than both σ and a trimming rate α as in α-smoothing).

## Results

On MNIST rotation (ε=10°), the proposed (E,C,G)+M method achieves a mean certified ℓ₂ radius of 0.210 pixels (median 0.208) at σ=0.75, certifying all 100 test samples, compared to 0.120 pixels for α-smoothing (which abstains on 6/100 samples) and 0.090 pixels for the (E,C)+M variant without gradient. On UTKFace age estimation (ε=6 years), (E,C,G)+M achieves a mean radius of 2.152 versus 0.817 for α-smoothing (which returns zero radius on 59/100 images). On synthetic functions, the (C,G) certificate consistently yields larger mean certified radii than α-smoothing across all three test functions and both tolerance levels (e.g., 0.351 vs 0.233 on the Slice function at ε=0.2). Tightness analysis shows a mean 3.41× gap between certified and PGD-optimized radii, with 98% of samples empirically sound.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole external baseline is α-smoothing (Rekavandi et al., 2024), which certifies regression robustness via probability-of-inclusion over a trimmed output region. The proposed (E,C,G)+M method decisively outperforms α-smoothing on every practical metric: on MNIST rotation it achieves a 1.76× larger mean certified radius (0.210 vs 0.120 pixels) and certifies 100/100 samples versus 94/100 for α-smoothing; on UTKFace the mean radius is 2.63× larger (2.152 vs 0.817) and α-smoothing returns zero radius on 59/100 images while (E,C,G)+M is nonzero on all. A critical practical advantage is robustness to noise-level selection: at σ≥0.5, α-smoothing certifies zero radius on 100% of MNIST test points, whereas (E,C,G)+M maintains mean radii of 0.156 and 0.210 pixels at σ=0.5 and 0.75 respectively. On synthetic functions (Table 1), the (C,G) certificate beats α-smoothing in all six function/tolerance combinations, with the largest gap on the Sandwich function (0.241 vs 0.102 at ε=0.2, a 2.36× improvement). The paper also shows that α-smoothing requires tuning both σ and a trimming rate α, while the proposed method tunes only σ, simplifying deployment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the randomized smoothing certification framework from Cohen et al. (2019) from classification to regression, but makes a deliberate methodological departure: rather than relying on the Neyman-Pearson lemma and probability-of-inclusion criteria (as in α-smoothing and RS-Reg), it formulates the worst-case base function as a calculus-of-variations problem constrained by variance and gradient statistics. This shift from binary 'inside/outside region' counting to geometric constraints on the smoothed landscape is the paper's core conceptual contribution, generalizing the likelihood-ratio structure of the Neyman-Pearson lemma to continuous-valued functions via Euler-Lagrange equations. The prediction-centered certificate also resolves an anchor inconsistency in prior regression certificates, where the certified target (ground-truth label or base-model output) differed from the actually deployed smoothed prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, test-time-computable certificate for continuous regression that is tighter than existing probability-mass-based methods, which is relevant for safety-critical applications like autonomous navigation and medical imaging where bounding prediction deviation is essential. Key limitations include reliance on asymptotic (not finite-sample) statistical guarantees for variance and gradient estimation, and the failure to exploit output periodicity in the MNIST angle task, making certificates conservative near wrap-around boundaries. Open questions include whether the dimensionality-reduction theorem extends to multi-output regression, how certificates scale in very high-dimensional input spaces (subject to the known σ/√d curse), and whether finite-sample validity can be achieved without strong distributional assumptions.
