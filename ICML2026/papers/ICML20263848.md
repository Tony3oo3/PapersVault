## Problem

Real-time ATE estimation from streaming observational data faces two coupled challenges: strict memory budgets that preclude storing the full data history, and non-stationary distributional shifts in both the treatment assignment mechanism and the outcome-generating process. Existing online causal inference methods rely on parametric propensity score or outcome regression models that become fragile under such shifts, while offline covariate-balancing methods require full dataset access and cannot operate under bounded memory.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequential stream of (x, a, y) triples where x ∈ R^p are covariates, a ∈ {0,1} is the binary treatment indicator, and y is the observed outcome. At each time step τ it outputs a weighted ATE estimate computed from the maintained coreset, with the critical constraint that only O(d log n / ε²) feature-outcome pairs are stored (rather than all n observations), where d is the random feature dimension and ε controls the coreset approximation error. The coreset is mergeable, so treated and control summaries can be maintained independently and combined on demand for the balancing step.

## Method

FCore maps covariates into a d-dimensional random Fourier feature space to approximate a rich integral feature class, then compresses the stream into mergeable feature coresets using a discrepancy-based greedy signing rule within a merge-and-reduce pipeline (bucket size m = ⌈2/ε²⌉). The greedy signing assigns each new point a sign that cancels the current residual feature sum, yielding a deterministic, dimension-free residual bound of √(2m) per halving step—avoiding the Rademacher-complexity factors that arise from random signing. On the maintained coresets, FCore directly learns balancing weights by minimizing a convex quadratic objective that matches the weighted treated and control feature-mean embeddings to the coreset-induced mixture, bypassing parametric propensity score estimation entirely. The balancing weights are optimized via softmax-parameterized Adam with warm-starting across time steps, and the final ATE is computed as the difference of weighted outcome sums over the treated and control coresets.

## Results

The paper evaluates FCore using Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of ATE estimates across four datasets. On the synthetic Heterogeneous outcome shift dataset, FCore achieves consistently lower MAE than all model-based baselines, while on the Kang-Schafer treatment shift dataset it maintains near-zero MAE across all coreset retention ratios. On the real-world IHDP dataset, FCore outperforms G-comp, IPTW, and AIPTW at every retention ratio, remaining accurate even under aggressive compression (ρ=0.16). On the COVID-19 vaccine safety dataset, FCore recovers a negative ATE of approximately −2% for both headache and fatigue outcomes while using only 0.5%–1% of the full data stream.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares FCore against three online causal inference baselines from Luo et al. (2024): G-comp (outcome regression), IPTW (inverse propensity score weighting), and AIPTW (doubly robust estimator combining both). FCore wins on all four evaluated datasets. Under heterogeneous outcome shift, the model-based baselines exhibit substantial and growing MAE due to outcome model misspecification as functional complexity increases across batches, while FCore maintains low and stable error. Under Kang-Schafer treatment shift, IPTW and AIPTW suffer large errors and high variance as the propensity intercept drifts, whereas FCore stays near-zero MAE because it bypasses parametric propensity score estimation entirely. On IHDP, FCore achieves lower MAE than all three baselines at every coreset retention ratio tested, with the gap widening under aggressive compression. On the COVID-19 dataset, FCore matches the previously reported ground-truth ATE (≈−2%) using only 0.5%–1% of the data, demonstrating that the coreset compression does not degrade estimation quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three existing threads: discrepancy-based coreset construction (Karnin & Liberty, 2019) for data compression, random feature representations (Rahimi & Recht, 2007) for approximating integral function classes, and covariate balancing for ATE estimation (Kong et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2024). It departs from the model-based online estimators of Luo et al. (2024), which sequentially update parametric propensity score and outcome regression models, by replacing both with a model-agnostic balancing objective over a nonparametric function space. The key conceptual move is repurposing the merge-and-reduce discrepancy pipeline—previously applied to generic approximation tasks like KDE—into a task-aware, mergeable summary specifically designed to preserve the feature statistics needed for weighted ATE estimation under streaming memory constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: real-time causal inference (e.g., vaccine safety surveillance) where data arrive continuously, memory is bounded, and distributional shifts invalidate parametric nuisance models. The clean separation of error into sampling, coreset, noise, and balancing components, each with explicit bounds, gives practitioners a transparent understanding of where accuracy is lost and how to trade off memory (via ε) against estimation error. Open questions include whether the RKHS-ball specialization in the analysis is too restrictive for highly non-smooth outcome functions, whether task-informed feature constructions (beyond generic RFFs) can tighten the d^{-1/4} approximation rate, and how the method behaves when both treatment and outcome shifts occur simultaneously at high frequency.
