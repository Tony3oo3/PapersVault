## Problem

Existing multimodal reasoning benchmarks lack explicit categorization of logical reasoning types, predominantly covering only inductive and deductive reasoning while neglecting abductive reasoning. Additionally, many benchmark questions conflate reasoning with simple visual perception or require complex domain knowledge, leading to inaccurate assessment of a model's true logical reasoning capability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265657/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question pairs one or more RGB images with a text prompt, presented in three formats: multiple-choice (58.5%), free-form (31.6%), and rule-based puzzles such as Sudoku or Number Bridge (9.9%). Evaluation produces accuracy scores decomposed along two axes—reasoning type (deductive, inductive, abductive) and capability (calculation, planning & exploring, pattern analysis, spatial & temporal, causal chain analysis)—plus an overall average. Answer extraction and free-form judgment are performed by GPT-4o-mini, while rule-based questions are verified via dedicated scripts that convert model outputs into structured formats (e.g., bridge matrices) for deterministic checking.

## Method

MME-Reasoning is constructed through a multi-source collection pipeline (textbooks, logic puzzle books, synthetic generation, sampling from PuzzleVQA and MM-IQ, and self-designed spatial/temporal questions) followed by manual curation that filters out questions solvable by perception alone or requiring knowledge beyond K12, yielding 1,188 questions balanced across the three reasoning types. Each question is annotated with reasoning type, difficulty (easy/medium/hard), capability labels, and question format using a multi-annotator protocol with majority-vote resolution. The evaluation protocol is format-specific: MCQ answers are matched directly, free-form answers are judged for consistency by GPT-4o, and rule-based answers are parsed into intermediate representations and checked against predefined correctness scripts. The empirical study evaluates 30+ SoTA MLLMs across thinking, chat, and rule-based RL categories, and further analyzes token efficiency, difficulty scaling, MCTS-based test-time compute, CoT prompting effects, and human expert baselines to characterize where and why current models fail.

## Results

On MME-Reasoning (1,188 questions covering inductive, deductive, and abductive reasoning), the best-performing model Gemini-2.5-Pro-Thinking achieves only 60.2% average accuracy, followed by Seed1.5-VL-Thinking (59.9%) and o4-mini (57.5%). Human experts score 83.4%, revealing a substantial gap. Abductive reasoning is the weakest dimension across all models: closed-source models show a 5.38-point deductive-over-abductive gap, widening to 9.81 points for open-source models. Rule-based RL models (e.g., R1-VL, Vision-R1, MM-Eureka) do not consistently outperform their base chat models, with most 7B-scale RL models showing performance degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Thinking models consistently outperform their chat counterparts: Seed1.5-VL-Thinking (59.9%) beats Seed1.5-VL (47.5%) by 12.4 points, and Claude-4-Sonnet-Thinking (51.2%) surpasses Claude-3.7-Sonnet (33.3%) by 17.9 points. Among closed-source thinking models, Gemini-2.5-Pro-T leads at 60.2%, narrowly ahead of Seed1.5-VL-T (59.9%) and o4-mini (57.5%), while o1 (45.7%) trails significantly. Open-source models lag substantially: the best open-source thinking model (Bee-8B at 38.2%) scores 22 points below the best closed-source model, and the best open-source chat model (Qwen2.5-VL-72B at 34.1%) is 26 points behind. Rule-based RL models (R1-VL, Vision-R1, MM-Eureka, R1-Onevision) generally underperform their base chat models—for example, Vision-R1-7B (24.0%) scores below Qwen2.5-VL-7B (26.8%)—suggesting that RL trained primarily on math does not transfer to comprehensive logical reasoning. All models show a consistent abductive reasoning deficit: even the top model Gemini-2.5-Pro-T scores only 62.8% on abductive vs. 64.0% on deductive, while open-source models like Molmo-72B drop to 14.3% abductive vs. 23.1% deductive.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in multimodal reasoning evaluation by being the first benchmark to systematically cover all three classical reasoning types (deductive, inductive, and abductive) following Peirce's taxonomy, whereas prior benchmarks (MathVista, LogicVista, VisualPuzzles, VisuLogic, MM-IQ) focus almost exclusively on inductive and deductive reasoning. It also departs from prior work by explicitly separating reasoning from perception and domain knowledge (requiring K12-level knowledge at most), and by introducing rule-based evaluation formats (Sudoku, Number Bridge) alongside MCQ and free-form questions. The empirical analysis builds on the recent wave of 'thinking mode' and rule-based RL (GRPO) models, positioning MME-Reasoning as a stress test for these approaches beyond their math-centric training distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest MLLMs (Gemini-2.5-Pro-Thinking at 60.2%) fall far short of human expert performance (83.4%), and that abductive reasoning is a consistent and significant bottleneck across all models (5–10 point gap vs. deductive). The finding that rule-based RL models trained primarily on math do not consistently outperform their base models on comprehensive reasoning challenges the assumption that scaling RL on narrow task distributions generalizes to broader reasoning. Open questions include how to specifically target abductive reasoning improvement, whether test-time compute scaling (MCTS, CoT prompting) can close the gap, and whether the multimodal alignment process itself degrades the long-chain reasoning capabilities that text-only LLMs have acquired.
