## Problem

Prior training-data extraction attacks on diffusion models require access to the training set, substantial computational resources (e.g., 175M generated images), or prompts extracted directly from training captions, simulating an explicit adversary. This paper asks whether template-memorized images—recurring layouts and visual structures from e-commerce product photos—can be reconstructed through seemingly benign, naturally occurring prompts that a naive user might type, without any access to training data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265501/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes simple text prompts formed by combining e-commerce product collocations (e.g., 'Unisex T-Shirt', 'Shower Curtain') with generic visual descriptors (e.g., 'Floral', 'Galaxy', 'blue'), producing phrases like 'Floral Unisex t-shirts'. For each prompt, 25–50 images are generated with distinct seeds (0–49) from a target diffusion model. The pipeline outputs identified template groups (a many-to-many mapping between collocations and image templates), flagged near-duplicate cliques, and traced source images linked to specific e-commerce listings.

## Method

The attack operates in three stages, all in a black-box setting with no training-data access. First, ~108 product collocations are scraped from print-on-demand e-commerce websites (Redbubble, Zazzle, etc.) via Common Crawl snapshots, then combined with six visual descriptors to form prompts. Second, category-specific segmentation models (MaskFormer for household items, SegFormer for clothing) estimate an editable-region mask, and a clique search over the fixed region using CLIP cosine similarity > 0.95 identifies near-duplicate groups; cliques of size ≥ 2 are flagged as suspected template memorization. Third, source images are traced via Google Lens, targeted e-commerce searches, and manual visual inspection. The key insight is that e-commerce product images share fixed template layouts with only a small editable print region, so even generic prompts can trigger reconstruction of the memorized template structure—requiring only 11,400 generated images versus orders of magnitude more in prior work.

## Results

The attack identifies 67 template-memorized image templates from 108 e-commerce collocations, generating only 11,400 images total—orders of magnitude fewer than prior work (175M for Carlini et al., 9,000 for Somepalli et al.). Of the 108 collocations tested, 43 yielded at least one template, with a relatively uniform dispersion (constant marginal returns). The BE-PRSS detector (Chen et al., 2025) achieves only 0.595 accuracy, 0.520 precision, and 0.722 recall on TMI detection, while its mitigation reduces TMI-producing collocations from 28 to 22 (≈21% reduction). In a real-world prompt validation, 8/20 Midjourney prompts and 7/15 DiffusionDB prompts containing the identified collocations produced TMIs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is resource efficiency: it identifies 67 template-memorized images using only 108 prompts and 100 variations (≈11,400 total images), versus 350,000 prompts / 175M images for Carlini et al. (2023), 9,000 prompts for Somepalli et al. (2023a), and 30,000 prompts for Webster (2023)—all of which require direct access to the training set, whereas this method operates in a black-box setting with no training data access. Against the BE-PRSS detection and mitigation method of Chen et al. (2025), the paper shows that template-level memorization evades existing detection: BE-PRSS reaches only 0.595 accuracy and 0.520 precision on TMI classification, and its mitigation reduces TMI-producing collocations from 28 to 22, a modest ~21% drop accompanied by utility degradation. The paper also demonstrates that the same collocations successfully extract TMIs from newer models (SD 3.5, Flux Schnell, Midjourney v6.1), though to a lesser extent than SD 1.4, indicating the vulnerability persists across model generations. A user study with 70 participants confirms that the identified copying (including interpolations and perturbations) is visually apparent to human observers, validating annotations that automated metrics (SSCD, ℓ₂) frequently miss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the established line of training-data extraction attacks on diffusion models (Carlini et al. 2023, Somepalli et al. 2023, Webster 2023), which rely on training-set access, verbatim prompts, or large-scale generation, by targeting a distinct sub-phenomenon: template-memorized images reconstructed through benign, naturally occurring e-commerce collocations. It repurposes existing tools—CLIP-based clique search, segmentation masks (MaskFormer, SegFormer), and near-duplicate detection—into a black-box pipeline that requires no training data and generates orders of magnitude fewer images. The paper also positions itself against concurrent work on local memorization (Chen et al. 2025, BE-PRSS) and foreground-background memorization (Di et al. 2026), showing that template-level coupling follows a many-to-many structure not captured by either.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that everyday prompts like 'blue Unisex T-Shirt' can reconstruct images containing identifiable real people—without any adversarial intent—exposes a gap between current threat models and actual deployment risk, and raises urgent questions about consent and privacy in generative AI. The newly documented phenomena (interpolation across multiple sources, localized perturbations, cross-category template leakage) demonstrate that standard full-image similarity metrics and existing detectors (BE-PRSS accuracy ~0.60) are fundamentally ill-suited to template-level memorization, leaving a significant open problem in scalable detection and mitigation. Limitations include reliance on manual visual inspection for source tracing, a primary focus on SD 1.4 with only qualitative evidence on SOTA models, and the inability to distinguish genuine leakage from legitimate template reuse in e-commerce data without controlled experiments.
