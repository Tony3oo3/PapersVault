## Problem

LLMs struggle to self-correct because they cannot reliably localize errors within their own unstructured chain-of-thought reasoning; prior work showed models can correct when given the exact error location but fail to find it themselves. This paper asks whether imposing discrete structure on reasoning—generating one semantically complete thought at a time—makes self-localization tractable, and whether targeted backtracking-and-resampling from the last correct step outperforms holistic regeneration.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text reasoning question (math, science, or commonsense) and produces a structured reasoning trace where each thought is a self-contained reasoning step delimited by a </thought> token, terminated by a \boxed{answer}. Unlike standard CoT which emits a continuous token stream, the model is prompted iteratively at each state to generate only the next thought, seeing the growing prefix of prior thoughts in-context. This produces a tree of reasoning paths where backtracking to any thought boundary is well-defined, enabling resampling from a validated prefix rather than regeneration from scratch.

## Method

Thought-ICS frames LLM inference as a Thought-MDP where actions are complete thoughts (token sequences) rather than single tokens, with the model itself determining thought boundaries via stop sequences. The correction loop has three stages: (1) verification (oracle or self) gates whether correction proceeds, (2) self-localization prompts the model to identify the first erroneous thought by step number, and (3) resampling backtracks to the state before that thought and regenerates an alternative continuation using the same thought-by-thought policy. The key insight is that discrete thought boundaries create natural decision points for error attribution, yielding clean prefixes (free of prior errors) that make resampling 2–4× more likely to produce a correct answer. For fully autonomous operation, Thought-ICS-A adds a confidence safeguard that resets to the initial answer when verification and localization disagree or maximum iterations are exhausted, mitigating the specificity collapse that causes naive self-correction to break more answers than it fixes.

## Results

Under oracle verification (model told when wrong but not where), Thought-ICS achieves 20–40% self-correction lift over unstructured chain-of-thought baselines across eight models (3B–120B) and six reasoning benchmarks. In the fully autonomous setting (no oracle), Thought-ICS-A with a confidence safeguard achieves net positive correction lift, outperforming Self-Refine and CoVe on all models except LLaMA-3B. On the largest model (GPT-OSS-120B), Thought-ICS-A reaches 65.5% average accuracy vs. 61.9% for CoVe and 55.7% for Self-Refine (55.3% for raw CoT). Structured thought-by-thought generation also improves initial accuracy by +6.5% on average before any correction is applied.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Thought-ICS-A outperforms Self-Refine on every model except LLaMA-3B, with the largest margin on GPT-OSS-20B (69.0% vs. 52.1%, +16.9 pp) and GPT-OSS-120B (65.5% vs. 55.7%, +9.8 pp). Against CoVe, Thought-ICS-A wins on all models except LLaMA-3B, with the most substantial gains on GPT-OSS-20B (+17.1 pp) and Qwen-14B (+5.7 pp). On LLaMA-3B, all self-correction methods (including both baselines) underperform raw CoT, indicating that the model lacks sufficient capability for effective self-verification. The key structural advantage is that Thought-ICS backtracks to a verified-correct prefix and resamples only the erroneous suffix, whereas Self-Refine regenerates from scratch each iteration and CoVe performs a single verification-then-regenerate pass. Under oracle verification (the paper's core scientific result), Thought-ICS also outperforms its unstructured counterpart Token-ICS by 19.1 percentage points in average accuracy (67.2% vs. 48.1%) while using only 1.21× the correction tokens.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the self-correction literature (Self-Refine, CoVe, Reflexion) by reframing the correction loop around a structural hypothesis: that the granularity at which reasoning is generated determines the model's ability to localize errors. It builds directly on Tyen et al. (2024), who showed that LLMs can correct when given oracle error locations but cannot self-localize, by proposing that the failure is not a capability deficit but a representational one — continuous token streams lack the discrete boundaries needed for step-level attribution. The neurocognitive framing (anterior cingulate cortex monitoring at decision points) is motivational rather than mechanistic, and the method itself (prompting for discrete 'thoughts' with stop sequences) is a lightweight prompting intervention rather than a new architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding — that structuring generation into discrete thoughts yields 20–40% correction lift under oracle verification and outperforms Self-Refine/CoVe in the autonomous setting — is practically useful but bounded: the gains depend on an oracle verifier that is unavailable in deployment, and in the fully autonomous regime the confidence-safeguard (resetting to the initial answer on low-confidence exits) effectively caps the method at 'don't make it worse' for many problems. The specificity-collapse result (self-verification degrades over iterations, breaking more than it fixes) is a sobering reminder that the verification bottleneck remains the dominant open problem in autonomous self-correction. For practitioners, the main takeaway is that thought-level structure is a cheap, training-free lever for improving correction reliability, but the ceiling is set by the model's self-verification accuracy, which no prompting strategy in this paper resolves.
