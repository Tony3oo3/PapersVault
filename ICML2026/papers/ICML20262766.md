## Problem

RLVR training with GRPO reduces generation diversity by over-incentivizing positive rewards, while NSR's uniform negative upweighting introduces gradient conflict from semantic overlap between positive and negative trajectories (Lazy Likelihood Displacement). The open question is how to selectively suppress erroneous reasoning components without penalizing the valid token distributions shared with correct trajectories, thereby improving both Pass@1 and Pass@k simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262766/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes text prompts and produces reasoning responses (mathematical solutions, code, agent actions, or function calls) via a policy model (Qwen3 1.7B–32B). During training, each prompt yields a group of G sampled trajectories with binary verifier rewards; the algorithm additionally extracts penultimate-layer hidden states (d-dimensional vectors) for all tokens, applies LayerNorm and group-wise centering, and operates under a 4096–8192 token response budget depending on the task domain.

## Method

ResRL decouples positive and negative gradient updates by projecting negative-token hidden representations onto a rank-k positive subspace (constructed via truncated SVD on sampled, centered positive tokens) and using the projection residual as a token-wise reweighting signal. Theoretically, the paper proves that the output-head gradient inner product factorizes into logit-space and representation-space terms (Lemma 1), and that the orthogonal-complement energy e(x) monotonically upper-bounds representation alignment (Theorem 1), providing a conservative single-forward proxy for gradient interference. In practice, negative tokens with low residual energy (highly aligned with the positive subspace) receive downweighted penalties, while tokens deviating into the orthogonal complement receive stronger suppression; quantile-based normalization across the group ensures robustness to scale variation. The final objective modifies GRPO's clipped policy gradient by replacing group-level advantages with token-wise coefficients: a small positive scaling λ_pos for positive trajectories and a residual-modulated weight ω_{i,t} ∈ [ξ, 1] for negative trajectories, with a length-scaled reward discount as a verbosity safeguard.

## Results

On mathematical reasoning (Avg@16), ResRL achieves 57.0% on Qwen3-4B, surpassing NSR (52.1%) by 9.4% and FlowRL (53.6%) by 3.4%; on Qwen3-8B it reaches 64.7%, improving over NSR (61.9%) by 4.5%. On CodeForces, ResRL attains a rating of 1469.5 (78.9th percentile), exceeding NSR (1340.9) by 9.6%. On ALFWorld, ResRL achieves 86.7% overall success rate, outperforming EMPG (78.5%) by 10.4%, and on BFCL multi-turn it reaches 41.25% OA, beating ResT (40.13%) by 2.8%. Averaged over AIME24/25 and AMC23 at k=128, ResRL improves Pass@128 by 7.0% over NSR on Qwen3-4B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ResRL consistently outperforms GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) across all model scales on math (e.g., 57.0 vs 53.1 Avg@16 on Qwen3-4B), code (1469.5 vs 1267.9 CodeForces rating), and agent tasks (86.7% vs 74.8% ALFWorld). Against DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), ResRL leads by a wide margin on every benchmark (e.g., 57.0 vs 46.2 on Qwen3-4B math). FlowRL (Zhu et al., 2025a) is the strongest diversity-oriented baseline; ResRL surpasses it by 3.4% Avg@16 on Qwen3-4B and 2.7% on Qwen3-8B, with particularly large gains on AIME24/25 (27.7%/27.8% relative improvement on 4B). NSR (Zhu et al., 2025b) is the closest competitor: ResRL wins on Qwen3-1.7B (48.6 vs 47.5) and Qwen3-4B (57.0 vs 52.1) but trails on AIME24 for Qwen3-8B (50.8 vs 55.4), though it still leads in aggregate (64.7 vs 61.9). On agent tasks, ResRL beats PPO (Ouyang et al., 2022) by 7.8% and EMPG (Wang et al., 2025) by 10.4% on ALFWorld overall success, and also surpasses ReAct (Yao et al., 2022b), GPT-4o, and Gemini-2.5-Pro. On BFCL function calling, ResRL (8B) exceeds ResT (Lin et al., 2025a) by 2.8% on multi-turn OA and ToolACE (Liu et al., 2025) by 2.3%, and notably outperforms the much larger Qwen3-235B-A22B (Yang et al., 2025a) on multi-turn (41.25 vs 40.12).

## Relation to Prior Work

ResRL sits at the intersection of three threads in RLVR research: GRPO's clipped policy-gradient objective (Shao et al., 2024), NSR's paradigm of upweighting negative-sample gradients to preserve diversity (Zhu et al., 2025b), and the LLD analysis showing that negative gradients can destructively interfere with positive ones at the output head (Deng et al., 2025b). The key departure from NSR is geometric: rather than applying a uniform penalty to all negative tokens, ResRL projects negative hidden states onto a low-rank positive subspace and reweights each token by its orthogonal-complement residual, so that shared semantic directions are protected while error-specific components are suppressed. The theoretical contribution formalizes this by decomposing the head-gradient inner product into logit and representation factors (Lemma 1) and showing that the projection residual provides a monotonic upper bound on representation alignment (Theorem 1), yielding a single-forward proxy that avoids expensive token-wise gradient estimation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and practically important tension in RLVR: methods that improve Pass@1 (e.g., GRPO) tend to collapse diversity, while diversity-preserving methods like NSR can inadvertently suppress shared valid reasoning steps, limiting Pass@1 gains. ResRL's projection-residual mechanism offers a principled, computationally lightweight resolution (O((M+T₋)dk) per group) that simultaneously improves both metrics across twelve benchmarks and three model scales. Open questions include whether the low-rank positive-subspace assumption holds for all task domains and model architectures, whether the theoretical bounds are tight enough to guide hyperparameter selection in practice, and whether the approach extends naturally to multi-turn or tool-augmented settings where the positive/negative semantic overlap structure may differ. The finding that the KL penalty can be removed entirely without destabilizing training (Figure 6) is a useful practical insight for long-horizon RLVR training.
