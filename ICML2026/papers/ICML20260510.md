## Problem

In RLVR training with GRPO, a substantial subset of hard examples remains unlearnable even when correct rollouts are observed, contradicting the assumption that positive reward signals are sufficient for learning. The paper asks why these examples resist learning and whether the cause lies in optimization mechanics (rollout scarcity, clipping, KL penalties) or in something more fundamental about the model's internal representations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260510/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes math problem prompts (MATH levels 1–5 or DeepScaleR) and a policy model (Qwen2.5-0.5B/3B, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct), sampling k=8 rollouts per example under GRPO with dynamic sampling. Outputs include a per-example learnability classification (easy/learnable/unlearnable, where unlearnable means pass@1 < 0.1 over 32 samples at convergence despite observing correct rollouts), example-level gradient cosine similarity computed via LoRA-parameter gradients over 1000 rollouts per example, and GPT-5-mini reasoning quality scores (0–5) on correct-answer rollouts. The working definition of unlearnability is operational rather than categorical, and intersections across three independent trainings are taken to reduce noise.

## Method

The paper is an empirical diagnostic study rather than a new training algorithm. It first rules out three optimization-side hypotheses through targeted interventions: oversampling with experience replay to control positive rollout count, ablating PPO clipping and KL penalty, and measuring within-prompt gradient interference between correct and incorrect rollouts—none resolve the unlearnability gap. The core analytical contribution is cross-example gradient similarity: per-example GRPO gradients (averaged over tokens and rollouts, computed on LoRA parameters) are compared via cosine similarity, revealing that unlearnable examples are consistent gradient outliers whose learning signals do not transfer from the broader training distribution. Complementary reasoning-quality scoring by GPT-5-mini shows that even correct-answer rollouts on unlearnable examples contain incoherent or erroneous intermediate steps, indicating the model exploits ungeneralizable shortcuts rather than genuine reasoning. The paper further demonstrates that data augmentation (GPT-5-generated similar problems and subproblems) and curriculum learning fail to improve gradient similarity or reasoning quality, while mid-training (OctoThinker) substantially increases gradient alignment, pointing to a fundamental representation limitation that RL post-training cannot repair.

## Results

Across all three model/dataset settings, approximately half of the hard examples that observe at least one correct rollout during training remain unlearnable (pass@1 < 0.1 at convergence). Specifically, Qwen2.5-0.5B on MATH Easy shows 30.2% unlearnable vs. 25.6% learnable; Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct on MATH Hard shows 21.9% unlearnable vs. 31.6% learnable; and Qwen2.5-3B on DeepScaleR shows 16.7% unlearnable vs. 14.2% learnable. Unlearnable examples exhibit substantially lower cross-example gradient similarity (cosine similarity with the rest of the training distribution) and lower reasoning quality scores (GPT-5-mini rated 0–5) compared to both learnable and easy examples, and neither data augmentation nor curriculum learning closes this gap during RL training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical diagnostic paper rather than a method comparison, so the 'baselines' are standard RLVR interventions tested against the unlearnability phenomenon. All tested interventions fail to resolve unlearnability: oversampling with replay (k=8, k_pos=1) slows learnable examples but leaves the unlearnable gap intact; removing the KL penalty and using clip-higher (DAPO-style) produce no improvement; SFT on distilled correct responses and RL with k=64 rollouts on unlearnable examples alone also fail to close the gap. Data augmentation (GPT-5-generated similar problems and subproblems) shows that even semantically near-identical problems have low gradient similarity to the originals, and training on augmented data does not transfer to the unlearnable examples. The one intervention that does help is mid-training: OctoThinker-3B-Hybrid-Base and OctoThinker-3B-Long-Base (mid-trained from Llama-3B on 20B tokens) both exhibit consistently higher gradient similarity for difficult examples compared to Llama-3.2-3B-Base, suggesting that reshaping representations before RL is the effective remedy rather than any RL-stage technique.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the GRPO training framework (Shao et al., 2024) with DAPO-style dynamic sampling (Yu et al., 2025) as its experimental backbone, and positions itself against the growing body of work analyzing what LLMs actually learn during RLVR (Yue et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026). Its closest prior work is Sun et al. (2025a), which studied extremely difficult examples with zero initial pass@k and showed learnability is possible with fine-grained rewards; this paper deliberately challenges that result by showing that even with positive reward signals present, a substantial subset of examples remains unlearnable. The gradient-similarity analysis methodology draws on cross-example gradient comparison techniques, while the mid-training comparison connects to OctoThinker (Wang et al., 2025) as evidence that representation quality prior to RL is the critical bottleneck.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that roughly 30–50% of hard examples with correct rollouts are fundamentally unlearnable under standard RLVR challenges a core assumption in the field—that positive reward is sufficient for learning—and shifts the bottleneck from the RL stage to the pre-RL representation stage. Practically, this suggests that RL training pipelines should invest more in mid-training quality and data curation rather than trying to fix unlearnable examples through RL-side interventions (oversampling, curriculum, augmentation), all of which the paper shows fail. Key open questions include: what specific representation properties make an example unlearnable, whether the phenomenon persists at frontier model scale, and whether non-mathematical reasoning domains exhibit the same gradient-outlier structure. The limitation to 0.5B–3B models in mathematical reasoning means generalizability to larger models and broader task families remains untested.
