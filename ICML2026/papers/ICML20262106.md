## Problem

Current large reasoning models trained with RL post-training (e.g., GRPO) rely exclusively on answer-based verification, providing no signal for the model to develop meta-awareness about its own knowledge boundaries, optimal thinking duration, or relevant problem-solving concepts. Existing meta-cognitive approaches require external verifiers, curated datasets, or human-designed reasoning pipelines, limiting scalability. This paper asks whether meta-awareness can be verified and reinforced using only self-generated predictive signals aligned against empirical rollout statistics.

## Input / Output

The model receives a single problem statement and produces two parallel outputs: a chain-of-thought solution (verified against ground truth via rule-based scoring) and a structured meta-prediction in JSON format containing a predicted pass-rate (integer 0–8, normalized by dividing by 8), a predicted solution length (integer between 128 and the maximum response length), and a list of mathematical notions (strings). The meta-prediction is verified against dynamic ground truth extracted from the solution rollouts: actual Pass@1 over G rollouts, the min/max token lengths among correct responses, and the differential notion frequency between correct and incorrect rollouts. Notions already present in the problem statement are excluded to prevent reward hacking.

## Method

MAPR augments the GRPO/DAPO policy gradient objective with a parallel self-predictive trajectory: the model simultaneously generates G solution rollouts and M meta-rollouts, where meta-rollouts predict difficulty, length, and notions that are then scored against the empirical statistics of the solution rollouts. The composite meta reward averages three components: an exponential-decay difficulty reward (0.01^|p−p̂|), a binary length reward (1 if predicted length falls within the correct-response length range), and a notion reward (fraction of predicted notions appearing more frequently in correct than incorrect rollouts). MAPR-efficient further leverages these predictions for training acceleration via predictive gating (skipping prompts whose predicted pass-rate has low variance and mean near 0 or 1, before any solution rollout) and early length cutoff (terminating rollouts that exceed predicted length scaled by a margin), reducing wall-clock training time by over 1.28× while improving final accuracy.

## Results

On Qwen3-8B (the primary model), MAPR achieves 55.71% average Pass@1 across six mathematical benchmarks, outperforming the GRPO baseline (51.23%) by 8.74%. The largest gains appear on harder problems: AIME'25 improves by 28.17% (22.19→28.44) and AIME'24 by 19.72% (28.54→34.17). On Qwen3-4B, the average gain is even larger at 16.45%, with an 83.18% relative improvement on AIME'25. MAPR-efficient further accelerates training by 1.28× to reach the same GRPO baseline performance while achieving higher final accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MAPR is compared against two baselines: GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and DAPO (Yu et al., 2025). Against GRPO, MAPR wins on all six mathematical benchmarks for all three Qwen3 model sizes (4B, 8B, 14B), with the largest relative gains on harder problems (AIME, Olympiad, Minerva) and smaller but consistent gains on easier ones (MATH500, AMC'23). The only exception is Minerva on Qwen3-4B, where MAPR slightly underperforms (41.12 vs 42.27, −2.72%). Against DAPO, MAPR+DAPO outperforms DAPO on all six benchmarks in both Pass@1 (55.01 vs 53.33, +3.15%) and Pass@8 (73.49 vs 69.23, +6.15%), while also eliminating DAPO's redundant zero-variance sampling phase for additional efficiency. The paper also demonstrates consistent gains when applied to Llama 3.1 8B Instruct and Gemma 2 9B IT, confirming the method is not model-family-specific.

## Relation to Prior Work

MAPR builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) as the underlying policy-gradient framework, adding a parallel meta-prediction path whose outputs are verified against empirical rollout statistics rather than external labels. It departs from prior meta-cognition work (e.g., Meta-R1, ReMA, DAST) that relies on external verifiers, curated datasets, or multi-agent architectures by using self-generated pass-rate, length, and notion statistics as dynamic ground truth, thereby eliminating external supervision. Unlike inference-time efficiency methods (L1, Thinkless, S-GRPO) that constrain output length or trigger early stopping at test time, MAPR targets training-time efficiency through predictive gating and early cutoff during RL post-training, while simultaneously improving final accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in the 1.28× training speedup with higher final accuracy, which directly reduces the compute cost of RL post-training for reasoning models—a bottleneck that scales with model size and benchmark difficulty. The paired analysis showing that meta-awareness calibration (not merely additional training steps) drives the accuracy gains provides a mechanistic explanation that could guide future meta-cognitive training designs. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond mathematical reasoning to open-ended generation tasks, whether the notion feed-in redundancy finding holds at larger scales, and whether the static online gating strategy remains effective as the model's knowledge boundary shifts substantially during long training runs.
