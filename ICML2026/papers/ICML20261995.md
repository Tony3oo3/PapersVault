## Problem

Estimating the global average treatment effect (GATE) from a single randomized trajectory in dynamic systems with temporal carryover and nonstationarity is challenging: naive difference-in-means estimators incur large bias from intertemporal interference, stationary off-policy evaluation methods (e.g., difference-in-Q's) fail without stationarity or multiple trajectories, and switchback designs require complex implementation and tuning. No existing approach achieves a favorable bias-variance tradeoff under both carryover effects and nonstationarity within the standard Bernoulli A/B test design.

## Input / Output

The estimator consumes only the observed treatment assignments Z_t and outcomes Y_t from a single Bernoulli-randomized trajectory (no state X_t access required), along with a user-specified truncation size k. It outputs a GATE point estimate, a consistent nonstationary HAC asymptotic variance estimate, and corresponding confidence intervals. The truncation size k can be selected post-experiment via a stability-based heuristic (Algorithm 1) that compares successive TPG estimates against their estimated standard errors, requiring no prior knowledge of the state space or multiple independent trajectories.

## Method

The Truncated Policy Gradient (TPG) estimator replaces each instantaneous outcome Y_t in the difference-in-means estimator with a k-step accumulated reward sum, yielding an unbiased estimator of a hypothetical truncated policy gradient — the gradient of a policy that plays Bernoulli(1/2) for the first t−k steps and then plays all-treatment or all-control for the final k steps. By a first-order Taylor approximation, this truncated policy gradient approximates the GATE, with bias decomposing into a Taylor error of order O(k²) (rather than O(T²) for the untruncated policy gradient) and a mixing bias that decays geometrically as γ^k under a uniform mixing-time assumption. The variance is bounded via strong α-mixing coefficients derived from the contraction property of the transition kernels, yielding O((k+1)³M²/T) rather than O(T²M²). A nonstationary HAC variance estimator is constructed using time-averaged blocks B_t − B̄ and Cesàro-L² mean stability, enabling valid inference and data-driven k-selection from a single trajectory without requiring knowledge of E[B_t].

## Results

In the nonstationary patient-arrival queueing simulator (Case Study I), the TPG estimator with k=4 or 5 (selected by the post-experiment heuristic) closely approximates the true GATE, while the DM and stationary DQ estimators exhibit substantial bias and LSTD-OPE yields a mean of −2.39 (far outside the plotted range). In the NYC ride-sharing simulator (Case Study II), TPG with k=2 or k=3 already produces estimates very close to the GATE, consistently reducing bias across switchback interval lengths (10–60 min) with only modest variance increase. The nonstationary HAC variance estimator achieves near-nominal 95% CI coverage (94.6%) at the selected k=5 in Case Study I, and the k-selection algorithm is robust to the confidence-level hyperparameter α (RMSE ranges from 0.039 to 0.050 across 70%–95% levels).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the DM (difference-in-means) estimator, TPG substantially reduces bias in both case studies: in the ride-sharing setting, DM is highly sensitive to interval length (large bias at 10–30 min intervals), while TPG consistently corrects this bias across all intervals. Against the stationary DQ estimator (Farias et al., 2022), TPG wins decisively: DQ exhibits substantial bias in the patient-arrival setting and fails to learn stable Q-values in the ride-sharing setting, yielding high bias and variance. Against LSTD-OPE (also Farias et al., 2022), TPG is far superior, as LSTD-OPE produces a mean estimate of −2.39 in Case Study I, completely outside the relevant range. Against the bias-corrected (BC) switchback estimator (Hu & Wager, 2022), TPG is competitive: BC with a 20-minute burn-in performs well in Case Study I, but in Case Study II BC offers little improvement over DM under 1-hour intervals and slightly overestimates with small burn-in; moreover, BC requires a more complex switchback design with prior knowledge of interval length, whereas TPG operates as a simple post-processing step on standard Bernoulli A/B test data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the policy-gradient interpretation of the difference-in-Q's (DQ) estimator (Farias et al., 2022) from stationary to nonstationary Markovian settings, and adopts the uniform mixing-time assumption from Hu & Wager (2022) on switchback experiments. The key conceptual departure is truncation: rather than estimating the full-horizon policy gradient (which incurs O(T²) bias and variance under nonstationarity), the TPG estimator uses only k-step reward sums, yielding a first-order Taylor approximation whose error scales as O(k²) instead of O(T²). This positions TPG as a middle ground between the naive difference-in-means estimator (Horvitz & Thompson, 1952) and the more complex switchback or OPE-based designs, operating within the standard Bernoulli A/B test framework without requiring state observations or multiple trajectories.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, low-bias estimator for GATE in the increasingly common setting of nonstationary dynamic systems (ride-sharing, digital health, marketplaces) where existing methods either assume stationarity or require elaborate experimental designs. The nonstationary CLT and consistent HAC variance estimator enable valid confidence intervals from a single trajectory, which is the realistic data regime for most platform experiments. Open questions include whether the mixing-time assumption (Assumption 2.1) holds in practice for highly persistent systems, whether the approach extends to continuous or multi-action settings, and whether the heuristic k-selection can be made theoretically optimal. The finite-state-space and bounded-reward assumptions also limit direct applicability to some real-world systems.
