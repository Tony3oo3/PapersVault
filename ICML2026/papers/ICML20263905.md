## Problem

Graph neural networks rely on trainable neighborhood aggregations (learned convolutions, attention) as their core mechanism, yet it remains unclear whether this learned component is actually necessary for strong performance. The paper asks whether fixed, non-learnable aggregation operators can match GNNs, and if so, what this implies about the true source of GNN performance on standard benchmarks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263905/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a graph with node feature vectors and an adjacency matrix, then precomputes a fixed tabular feature matrix by applying a set of reducers (mean, sum, max, min, std) recursively over neighborhoods at K hops and concatenating all results with the original features, yielding an input of dimensionality |x_v|·(1+|R|·K) per node. Only the downstream MLP is trained end-to-end; the aggregation pipeline itself is entirely training-free and can be cached. The output is a standard node class label (or ROC-AUC score for binary tasks like Minesweeper and Questions).

## Method

Fixed Aggregation Features (FAFs) replace all learned message-passing layers with a training-free preprocessing step: at each hop k, a set of fixed reducers r ∈ {mean, sum, max, min, std} is applied to the previous hop's neighborhood representations (Eq. 1), and all hop-reducer combinations are concatenated with the original node features into a single tabular vector (Eq. 2). A well-tuned MLP (2–5 layers, with normalization and dropout) is then trained on this tabular representation using the same hyperparameter grid as the GNN baselines. Theoretically, the paper grounds the approach in Kolmogorov-Arnold representations, proving that a lossless fixed univariate aggregation exists (Theorem 4.2), and showing that sum/mean are injective at 1-hop for orthogonal features (Theorem 4.1), while information loss grows at higher hops. Empirically, FAFs with a 4-reducer set (mean, sum, max, min) match or beat GCN, GAT, and GraphSAGE on 12 of 14 standard node-classification benchmarks, with the only notable gaps on Minesweeper and Roman-Empire where deep GNNs with residual connections exploit longer-range signal.

## Results

Across 14 node-classification benchmarks, FAFs (fixed multi-hop aggregations fed to a well-tuned MLP) match or outperform classic GNNs (GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE) on 12 of 14 datasets. FAFs achieve the best test accuracy on 5 datasets (e.g., Amazon-Computer 94.01%, Amazon-Photo 96.54%, PubMed 80.96%, Questions 78.69%, Squirrel 44.59%) and are within 1% on another 5. The two clear exceptions are Roman-Empire (78.11% vs. 91.05% for GCN) and Minesweeper (90.00% vs. 97.72% for GraphSAGE), where GNNs with 10–15 layers and linear residual connections maintain a substantial advantage. Notably, mean aggregation alone is often among the top-performing single reducers, and the best FAFs typically use only 2–4 hops.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FAFs are compared against three classic message-passing GNNs—GCN, GAT, and GraphSAGE—using the same hyperparameter tuning protocol as Luo et al. (2024). FAFs achieve the best test accuracy on 5 of 14 datasets (Amazon-Computer, Amazon-Photo, PubMed, Questions, Squirrel), with margins of 0.1–3.5% over the strongest GNN baseline. On 5 additional datasets (Amazon-Ratings, Chameleon, Coauthor-CS, Coauthor-Physics, WikiCS), FAFs are within 1% of the best GNN, effectively tying. The two clear losses are Roman-Empire (78.11% vs. 91.05% for GCN, a 12.9-point gap) and Minesweeper (90.00% vs. 97.72% for GraphSAGE, a 7.7-point gap), where GNNs rely on 10–15 layers with linear residual connections to capture long-range signal that fixed 2–4-hop aggregations cannot recover. The paper attributes these gaps to information loss at higher hops in non-injective reducers and the absence of residual connections in the FAF pipeline, noting that the remaining gap aligns with the gains from parameterized residuals reported by Luo et al. (2024).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work showing that GNNs can be simplified (SGC, SIGN, APPNP, partially frozen GCNs) by pushing the simplification to its logical extreme: fixing the aggregation entirely and training only a downstream MLP. Theoretically, it builds on Schmidt-Hieber's (2021) Kolmogorov-Arnold construction to prove that a lossless, fixed, univariate neighborhood aggregation exists, and generalizes Xu et al.'s (2019) GIN injectivity lemma from one-hot to orthogonal features to show sum/mean preserve 1-hop information. Unlike PNA, which learns weighted combinations of reducers, or SIGN, which uses only linear diffusions, FAFs concatenate all hops with nonlinear reducers (max, min, std) and rely on a well-tuned MLP to extract signal, positioning itself as a tabular-learning approach to graph data rather than a graph architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that training-free aggregations rival learned GNNs on 12/14 standard benchmarks challenges a core assumption in the field and provides a strong, interpretable baseline that should be included in future graph-learning evaluations. The two failures (Minesweeper, Roman-Empire) are well-diagnosed as requiring long-range, hop-specific interactions that simple fixed reducers cannot capture, pointing to a concrete need for harder benchmarks. Open questions include designing aggregation functions that balance injectivity with practical learnability, whether the results extend to inductive settings and other tasks (link prediction, graph classification), and whether the tabular view can be leveraged with tabular foundation models for broader graph learning.
