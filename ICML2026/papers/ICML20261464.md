## Problem

Policy-gradient training often becomes unstable or slows down as the policy approaches optimality, yet prior analyses typically assume uniformly bounded gradient variance or noise-to-signal ratio (NSR). This paper asks: how exactly does the REINFORCE estimator's variance vary across policy parameters, and can it be characterized without Monte Carlo approximation? The answer is that the NSR is highly non-uniform and can diverge near optima, providing a concrete mechanism for the oscillations and policy collapse observed in practice.

## Input / Output

The method takes system dynamics (matrices A, B for linear systems; polynomial maps for nonlinear), reward matrices (Q_s, Q_a), policy parameters (gain matrix K and log-standard-deviation vector ℓ), initial-state covariance Σ_0, horizon T, and discount factor γ. It produces exact closed-form or numerically evaluable expressions for the REINFORCE estimator's Frobenius variance and NSR, rather than Monte Carlo estimates. A key practical detail is that the paper demonstrates Monte Carlo estimation of the NSR is unreliable even with large rollout budgets, motivating the exact moment-evaluation approach.

## Method

The core technique is to express the REINFORCE estimator as a polynomial in jointly Gaussian variables (initial state and policy noise), then compute its moments exactly via Isserlis/Wick theorem. For one-step LQG, this yields closed-form NSR expressions showing the ratio scales as Θ(σ_0²/σ²) when initial-state covariance dominates policy covariance. For multi-step LQG, the T-step system is lifted to a single-step system in a higher-dimensional space (stacked states, actions, and noises), after which the same Gaussian moment framework applies, with bounds revealing exponential growth in horizon when the closed-loop matrix F is unstable. For polynomial dynamics with polynomial feedback, the estimator remains a polynomial in Gaussians so moments are still exactly computable, though complexity grows combinatorially. For general nonlinear systems with expressive (e.g., neural) policies, the paper derives upper bounds on variance in terms of the return's fourth conditional moment and the policy Jacobian's fourth moment, capturing the O(σ⁻²) scaling with policy covariance.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative result is that for one-step LQG with isotropic covariances, the NSR scales as Θ(σ₀²/σ²) when the initial-state covariance dominates the policy covariance (σ₀ ≫ σ), with the REINFORCE variance growing as Θ(σ₀⁶/σ²). For multi-step systems, the variance bound grows exponentially with horizon T as O(ρ(F)^(2T)) when the closed-loop dynamics are unstable (ρ(F) > 1), verified numerically across four 2D systems. Empirically, on a double-integrator with T=30, GD converges smoothly to the deterministic optimum while SGD and Adam oscillate once NSR exceeds a threshold; on a quadratic polynomial system, a single noisy gradient step near the optimum triggers full policy collapse (log-std → −∞). Monte Carlo estimation with a 10-minute rollout budget per iteration fails to accurately track the true NSR along optimization trajectories, highlighting the necessity of the exact moment-evaluation framework.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the REINFORCE estimator against GPOMDP (Baxter & Bartlett, 2001) in Appendix B (Figure 8), finding that the NSR landscape for GPOMDP is qualitatively similar to REINFORCE—both exhibit the same trend of increasing NSR near optimality—though GPOMDP generally has lower absolute variance due to its reward-to-go structure. The paper also contrasts exact Gaussian-moment computation of the NSR against Monte Carlo estimation (Figure 9), showing that even with 10 minutes of rollouts per iteration, the empirical estimate fails to capture the true NSR trend along optimization trajectories, underscoring the practical value of the closed-form characterizations. Across optimizers (GD, SGD, Adam) on both linear and polynomial systems, GD consistently approaches the optimum while SGD and Adam get stuck in high-NSR regions or oscillate in both parameter space and objective value; the effect is more pronounced with smaller batch sizes and larger learning rates, where policy collapse becomes observable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on policy-gradient estimator variance (Sutton et al. 1999 for the REINFORCE estimator; Zhao et al. 2011 and Preiss et al. 2019 for O(σ⁻²) variance bounds in LQG) by providing exact, closed-form or moment-evaluation characterizations of the noise-to-signal ratio (NSR) rather than merely upper bounds. It departs from prior work in two key ways: (i) it shows the NSR is highly non-uniform across policy parameters and can diverge near optimality, rather than being uniformly bounded as classical stochastic-optimization analyses assume; and (ii) it extends the analysis beyond LQG to polynomial dynamics and generic nonlinear systems, and connects the NSR landscape directly to optimization trajectories (GD, SGD, Adam). The NSR framing itself draws on the strong growth condition from stochastic optimization (Bottou et al. 2018; Cevher & Vu 2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, mechanistic explanation for a long-observed but poorly understood phenomenon: policy-gradient training slows down or becomes unstable as the policy approaches optimality. The finding that the optimal policy is deterministic (Σ→0) and that this drives the NSR to infinity gives practitioners a principled reason why entropy regularization, covariance floors, or larger batch sizes help near the optimum. Key open questions remain: a formal characterization of how the NSR landscape governs instability (beyond the escape-from-ball argument), extension to non-Gaussian policy distributions, and understanding the interaction between NSR and adaptive exploration mechanisms. The exact results are limited to structured systems (linear, polynomial), and the nonlinear case yields only upper bounds, so the full generality of the non-uniformity claim is not yet established.
