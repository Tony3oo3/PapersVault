## Problem

Traditional image forgery localization relies on physical sensor noise and compression artifacts that are absent in diffusion-generated content, while MLLM-based semantic approaches fail because modern diffusion models produce semantically consistent edits with no exploitable contradictions. The paper identifies that the diffusion process itself imposes a spectral bias that suppresses local high-frequency variance, creating a measurable statistical energy gap between authentic and generated regions that neither physical cues nor semantic reasoning can capture.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image at arbitrary resolution and first computes a Local Adjacency Discrepancy (LAD) map using a 3×3 neighborhood with a tanh-saturated pairwise intensity-difference operator (sensitivity threshold τ=0.004). The output is a pixel-level binary forgery mask at the original image resolution (produced by SAM's mask decoder) plus a scalar image-level authenticity score in [0,1] from a global classification head. The intermediate coarse mask from LAD-Net serves as a dense spatial prompt fed into SAM's prompt encoder, enabling prompt-guided refinement without requiring user-provided points or boxes.

## Method

FLAME is a coarse-to-fine framework grounded in a Gibbs Random Field analysis proving that diffusion's spectral bias creates an interior energy gap (generated regions have lower local pairwise energy than authentic regions due to suppressed high-frequency noise) and a boundary spike (hard latent compositing at the mask edge disrupts local covariance). The LAD map quantifies this by averaging tanh-normalized squared intensity differences over a 3×3 neighborhood, producing a 'Naturalness Response Field' where authentic regions appear high-energy and forged regions as low-energy voids bounded by intensity spikes. A lightweight LAD-Net (dilated convolutions with Instance Normalization and SE blocks) extracts multi-scale texture features from the LAD map to produce a global detection score and a coarse localization mask. This coarse mask is then injected as a dense spatial prompt into a frozen SAM, whose semantic features are modulated by a parameter-efficient residual Feature Adapter that fuses texture and semantic representations via channel-wise concatenation and a bottleneck MLP, enabling pixel-precise boundary delineation. Training uses a hybrid loss (Dice + Focal + IoU + detection BCE) with a two-stage protocol: base training on MagicBrush and SID, followed by fine-tuning on 500 EditStream-generated samples with 20% replay to prevent catastrophic forgetting.

## Results

On the primary pixel-level localization task, FLAME (base) achieves an average IoU of 0.358 and F1 of 0.459 across five OOD benchmarks (CoCoGLIDE, AutoSplice, NanoBanana, Qwen-Image-Edit, Flux Kontext), substantially outperforming the strongest baseline TruFor (IoU 0.247, F1 0.324). After fine-tuning via EditStream (FLAME-F), the average OOD IoU rises to 0.460 and F1 to 0.565, with the largest gains on the target Qwen-Image-Edit dataset (IoU 0.482 vs. 0.321 for base). For image-level detection, FLAME-F reaches an average AP of 0.755, compared to 0.635 for the best baseline (TruFor).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLAME outperforms all seven baselines on every dataset and metric. The closest competitor is TruFor (CVPR 2023), which achieves an average OOD IoU of 0.247 and F1 of 0.324, still trailing FLAME by 0.111 IoU and 0.135 F1; TruFor's advantage on AutoSplice (IoU 0.364) is the only dataset where a baseline comes within 0.14 of FLAME. SAFIRE (AAAI 2025) and Mesorch (AAAI 2025) perform competitively on CoCoGLIDE (IoU 0.394 and 0.382) but collapse on the newer EditStream-generated datasets (NanoBanana, Qwen-Image, Flux Kontext), where FLAME leads by 0.10–0.20 IoU. SIDA (CVPR 2025) and FakeShield (ICLR 2025), both MLLM-based approaches, show the weakest OOD generalization (average IoU 0.191 and 0.131 respectively), confirming the paper's argument that semantic-level reasoning is insufficient for pixel-level localization of diffusion artifacts. AdaIFL (ECCV 2024) and SparseViT (AAAI 2025) are the weakest baselines overall (average IoU ≤ 0.133), struggling particularly on the most recent generative models. FLAME-F further widens the gap, achieving 0.460 average OOD IoU versus TruFor's 0.247, a 86% relative improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

FLAME departs from two dominant IFL paradigms: physical-signal-based methods (TruFor, Noiseprint) that rely on sensor noise and compression artifacts absent in synthetic data, and MLLM-based semantic reasoning approaches (SIDA, FakeShield) that the authors argue fall into a 'semantic illusion trap' where modern diffusion models produce semantically coherent edits. Instead, it grounds forgery localization in the spectral bias of diffusion models, formalized through Gibbs Random Field energy modeling, and introduces the LAD map as a content-agnostic forensic fingerprint. For the refinement stage, it adapts SAM 3 (Carion et al., 2025) with a parameter-efficient feature adapter that fuses low-level texture cues with high-level semantic priors, and complements this with EditStream, an autonomous data synthesis pipeline that extends prior MLLM-driven generation protocols (Sun et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025) with a self-updating model scouting agent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FLAME provides a theoretically grounded, architecture-agnostic forensic signal that addresses the critical gap between traditional physical forensics and the rapidly evolving landscape of diffusion-based editing, with the LAD map offering a lightweight, interpretable alternative to heavy MLLM-based pipelines. EditStream's autonomous integration of emerging models directly tackles the temporal obsolescence problem that plagues static forensic benchmarks, creating a practical mechanism for continuous defense updates. However, the method's reliance on local high-frequency statistics means it degrades under JPEG compression and noise injection, and struggles with object removal where inpainted backgrounds are spectrally similar to authentic context. Open questions include whether the energy gap persists as future generative models explicitly model sensor noise, and how to defend against detector-aware anti-forensic perturbations that deliberately alter local statistics.
