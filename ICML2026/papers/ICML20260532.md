## Problem

Standard split conformal prediction guarantees only marginal coverage, while practitioners in high-stakes settings need reliable conditional coverage for specific instances. The paper identifies that the standard pinball loss used in quantile regression within conformal procedures is misaligned with the conditional-coverage objective: it underweights regions where the conditional score CDF is steep at the target quantile, precisely where small quantile errors induce large coverage deviations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260532/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained black-box point predictor μ̂(x), a calibration dataset D_cal (split internally into three disjoint subsets of 40%/40%/20%), and a test covariate x_test. It outputs a prediction set C_α(x_test) = {y : S(x_test, y) ≤ γ̂ + q̂_τ(x_test)}, which for the ℓ∞ residual score on scalar labels reduces to the interval [μ̂(x_test) ± (q̂_τ(x_test) + γ̂)]. The nonconformity score is the ℓ∞ norm of residuals, generalizing to multi-dimensional labels without modification.

## Method

CPCP derives a density-weighted pinball loss as a sharp Taylor-based surrogate for the Mean Squared Coverage Error (MSCE): the leading term of the squared coverage deviation scales as f_{S|X}(q_τ(x))²·ε_q(x)², so multiplying the standard pinball loss by the conditional density at the true quantile aligns the optimization objective with conditional coverage. The density weight is estimated via a central finite difference of two auxiliary quantiles at levels τ±δ, implemented through a three-headed neural network (shared MLP backbone + main head + two Softplus-activated auxiliary heads) that structurally prevents quantile crossing. Training proceeds in three stages: (1) joint pinball-loss training of all three heads on D_cal,1, (2) freezing the backbone and fine-tuning the main head on D_cal,2 with the estimated density weights (plus clipping and loss-mixing for stability), and (3) Rectified Conformal Prediction conformalization on D_cal,3 to restore exact marginal validity. The paper provides a non-asymptotic excess-risk bound of O(ℜ_n(𝒢)^{2/3}) for the weighted objective, with the rate governed by a local Hölder norm-equivalence assumption on the hypothesis class.

## Results

Across all eight real-world regression benchmarks (ranging from 13 to 98 features and 1 to 49 output dimensions), CPCP (Clip+Mix) consistently achieves the best conditional-coverage diagnostics. On the hardest dataset (WEC Perth, 49-dim output), CPCP (Clip+Mix) reaches WSC ≈ 0.88 vs. 0.54 for Split CP and ≈ 0.81 for RCP, while reducing MSCE from 0.035 (Split) to ≈ 0.0005. On easier datasets (e.g., Bike Sharing), CPCP (Clip+Mix) achieves WSC ≈ 0.88 and MSCE ≈ 0.0006, compared to 0.65 and 0.012 for Split. The method maintains moderate prediction-set volume (mean per-dimension log volume) rather than simply inflating intervals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CPCP (Clip+Mix) outperforms all five primary baselines (Split, PLCP, Gaussian Scoring, CQR, RCP) on every conditional-coverage metric across all eight datasets. The most dramatic gains are over Split CP, which suffers severe localized undercoverage (WSC as low as 0.54 on WEC Perth vs. 0.88 for CPCP). Against the closest competitor RCP, CPCP improves WSC by 3–6 percentage points on harder datasets while reducing MSCE by roughly 2–3×, confirming that the density-weighted pinball objective (not merely multitask quantile training) drives the improvement—RCP-MultiHead, which co-trains three quantile heads without reweighting, performs comparably to plain RCP. The ALD-based baselines (CQR-ALD, RCP-ALD) partially recover the heteroscedastic weighting but remain inferior to CPCP's data-driven finite-difference weights, particularly on high-dimensional outputs. CPCP (Clip+Mix) further narrows the gap to the best achievable WSC (≈0.89) by stabilizing the inverse-weight optimization, while maintaining prediction-set volumes comparable to RCP rather than inflating intervals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Rectified Conformal Prediction (RCP; Plassier et al., 2025a), which transforms raw nonconformity scores into rectified versions to improve conditional coverage, and on the MSCE–pinball-loss connection established by Kiyani et al. (2024). The key departure is identifying that the standard pinball-loss upper bound on MSCE is loose because it implicitly assumes a uniform Lipschitz constant on the conditional CDF; via Taylor expansion, the authors derive a sharper surrogate—a density-weighted pinball loss where weights are the conditional score density at the true quantile. This positions CPCP as a refinement of the quantile-regression component within the RCP framework, rather than a new conformalization procedure, and contrasts with group-conditional or localized approaches that relax the exact conditional guarantee.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The density-weighted pinball loss offers a simple, model-agnostic modification to an already-standard component of conformal prediction pipelines, with exact non-asymptotic excess-risk guarantees that are directly relevant to the finite-sample regime where conformal prediction is most valuable. The ablation showing that multitask quantile training alone (RCP-MultiHead) does not improve over RCP, while the density reweighting does, cleanly isolates the mechanism. Open questions include whether higher-order finite-difference schemes (which the theory suggests could improve the rate) yield practical gains, how the method behaves under severe distribution shift, and whether the O(n^{-1/3}) excess-risk rate can be tightened with stronger regularity assumptions on the score distribution.
