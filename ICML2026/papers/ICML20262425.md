## Problem

Existing theoretical analyses of in-context learning focus on single-task settings where all examples originate from the same underlying function, while classical continual learning theory analyzes training-time dynamics driven by parameter updates. This leaves a critical gap: when a pretrained Transformer processes a sequence of heterogeneous tasks within a single prompt via shared attention, how does it implicitly perform continual learning, and what are the fundamental limits on generalization and forgetting? The paper asks whether attention-based aggregation during inference inherently induces inter-task interference, systematic bias, and order-dependent forgetting even in the complete absence of parameter updates.

## Input / Output

The method takes a concatenated multi-task prompt structured as an embedding matrix P ∈ R^{D×(T(M+1)+1)}, where T tasks each contribute M in-context (x,y) pairs plus one query token, all processed by a single frozen attention layer with no parameter updates. It produces a scalar regression prediction ŷ_{t,q} for each task's query, along with two theoretical error quantities: a generalization error G_t = E[(ŷ_{t,q} − y_{t,q})²] measuring per-task prediction accuracy at the time the task is encountered, and a forgetting error F_t = E[(Ŷ_{t,q} − ŷ_{t,q})²] measuring how much the final prediction (after all T tasks are processed) deviates from the intermediate prediction at task t. The input covariance Λ and training prompt length N are treated as structural parameters that shape the learned attention weights.

## Method

The paper develops the first theoretical framework for in-context continual learning by analyzing masked linear self-attention (MLSA), where a causal mask ensures information flows only forward in the sequence, encoding task order structure. They derive an explicit prediction formula showing that task t's prediction aggregates all historical task information with a task-dependent weight β_t = 1/(t(M+1)), enabling order-sensitive generalization that standard linear self-attention cannot capture. The central result is a bias-variance-interference decomposition of prediction error: the variance term (scaling as M/(t²(M+1)²)) captures finite-sample estimation noise, the bias term (reducing to ‖(1/t)Σw_s − w_t‖₂² in the large-training-data regime) quantifies systematic task misalignment, and the interference term captures cross-task mean interactions that determine positive versus negative transfer. For forgetting, they prove it arises structurally from attention reweighting—past tasks receive negative coefficients c_t while future tasks receive positive coefficients d—rather than from information loss, and that mean-misalignment-induced forgetting persists asymptotically regardless of context length. The framework is validated empirically on GPT-2 (12-layer and 3-layer) for linear, sparse linear, and two-layer ReLU regression tasks, and on Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct for real NLP classification (SST-2 and AG News), confirming the predicted non-monotonic context-length effects, similarity-dependent transfer, and order-dependent forgetting.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with heterogeneous NLP tasks (SST-2 and AG News), the paper quantifies both negative transfer and forgetting: Task B accuracy drops from 0.736 (baseline) to 0.580 at M=1 (Δ=−0.156), recovering to 0.684 at M=19, while Task A suffers catastrophic forgetting, dropping from 0.934 to 0.472 at M=1 (Δ=−0.462) with persistent degradation even at M=19 (0.536, Δ=−0.386). On non-linear tasks (sparse linear regression and 2-layer ReLU networks), increasing context length M causes Task 5 normalized MSE to jump to 0.9905 at M=19, confirming that extended historical context from heterogeneous tasks induces systematic bias and provable negative transfer. The theoretical bias-variance-interference decomposition predicts that variance-induced interference decays as O(1/M) while mean-induced interference persists asymptotically, which is validated by the observed plateau in forgetting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not compare against competing methods in the standard benchmark sense; instead, it validates its derived error bounds against empirical observations. The key empirical validation is that the theoretical prediction of non-monotonic error in context length (variance reduction vs. bias from task misalignment) matches the observed U-shaped MSE curves in GPT-2 experiments, with a peak at intermediate M before recovery. The paper positions itself against prior single-task ICL theory (Bai et al. 2023, Ahn et al. 2023, Von Oswald et al. 2023), showing that those frameworks recover as the t=1 special case of its multi-task generalization formula. Against the empirical ICCL paradigm of Kang et al. (2025), which demonstrates that ICL can emulate continual learning, this paper provides the missing theoretical explanation for when and why in-context generalization succeeds or fails, and how inter-task interference leads to forgetting. The Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct results serve as a real-world validation that the theoretical interference mechanism (order-dependent reweighting, irreducible mean misalignment) manifests in modern instruction-tuned LLMs without any parameter updates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate theoretical lines: (1) ICL analysis via simplified Transformers (linear/masked linear self-attention) following Zhang et al. 2024a, Bai et al. 2023, and Ahn et al. 2023, and (2) continual learning theory focused on training-time parameter updates (Lin et al. 2023, Evron et al. 2022, Ding et al. 2024). It departs from both by analyzing multi-task sequential prompts processed through a single shared attention layer without any parameter updates, using the gradient-flow convergence results of Zhang et al. 2024a to obtain closed-form parameter limits and then extending the error analysis to the multi-task setting. The key conceptual departure is treating the attention mechanism itself as the source of continual-learning dynamics (interference, forgetting) rather than parameter drift.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The bias-variance-interference decomposition provides the first theoretical explanation for why LLMs exhibit order-dependent forgetting and negative transfer in heterogeneous multi-task prompts, and gives concrete, actionable guidance for prompt design (e.g., context length thresholds, task ordering, similarity grouping). A key limitation is that the analysis is restricted to single-layer linear/masked linear self-attention with linear regression targets, so whether the quantitative predictions transfer to deep, multi-head, non-linear architectures remains an open question. The real-LLM validation on Qwen2.5-1.5B is encouraging but limited to two classification tasks, leaving open whether the interference structure holds for generative or reasoning tasks. Future work could explore attention modifications (e.g., task-aware masking, learned gating) that provably reduce the interference terms identified here.
