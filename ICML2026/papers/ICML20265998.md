## Problem

Existing embedding-based defenses for LLM multi-agent systems (e.g., G-Safeguard, GUARDIAN) assume that malicious and benign message embeddings remain clearly separable, but this assumption breaks under adaptive attacks that craft near-benign messages. The paper identifies this failure mode theoretically—showing that well-separated training data leads to overly broad benign acceptance regions—and asks what complementary signals can defend MAS when embedding separability collapses.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265998/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes per-round agent messages (text), the token-level logits or probabilities produced by the generating LLM (via vLLM for open-weight models or API-returned probabilities for GPT-4o-mini), and the directed communication topology. It produces a scalar confidence score per message computed from top-k token entropy, which is then used to either hard-prune messages below a threshold δ or attach as metadata for soft down-weighting during neighbor aggregation. The final system output is the majority-vote group decision after R communication rounds.

## Method

The paper first proves (Theorem 4.1) that any L-Lipschitz scoring function with benign margin γ must accept all embeddings within distance γ/L of the benign support, guaranteeing evasion for near-benign attack embeddings. It then instantiates three adaptive attacks—Slow Drift (smooth per-round embedding trajectory), Benign Wrapper (long benign prefix + short malicious payload), and Chaos Seeding (inducing benign disagreement to widen the embedding spread)—that systematically reduce the separability rate Sep(c). For defense, the core mechanism aggregates token-level entropies via a top-k operator into a single uncertainty score, inverted to a confidence score C(m). Two strategies exploit this signal: confidence pruning blocks messages with C < δ before they reach neighbors, and confidence down-weighting packs C as metadata into the message so receiving agents are instructed to discount uncertain content during reasoning. Experiments across LLaMA-3.1-8B, Qwen3-4B, and GPT-4o-mini on MMLU, GSM8K, and BBH show that confidence-guided filtering recovers substantially more accuracy under near-benign attacks than embedding-based baselines, and that both embedding and confidence signals decay over rounds faster in denser topologies, motivating early intervention.

## Results

Under near-benign attacks (averaged over Slow Drift, Benign Wrapper, and Chaos Seeding), the confidence-guided defense achieves 52.7% Acc-Avg on MMLU, 77.7% on GSM8K, and 56.0% on BBH with LLaMA-3.1-8B, substantially outperforming G-Safeguard (39.3/66.7/23.3) and GUARDIAN (32.6/46.2/20.2). The advantage generalizes across Qwen3-4B (74.9/90.5/87.3) and GPT-4o-mini (71.9/89.6/70.0). In the clean (no-attack) setting, all methods perform comparably, with the confidence-guided method matching or slightly exceeding baselines (e.g., 68.0 vs. 68.0/67.0 on MMLU with LLaMA-3.1-8B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two embedding-based graph defenses: G-Safeguard (GNN-based anomaly detection on the utterance graph) and GUARDIAN (temporal attributed graph with encoder-decoder reconstruction). The confidence-guided method outperforms both baselines on every task and model under near-benign attack. The largest gaps appear on BBH with LLaMA-3.1-8B, where the confidence-guided defense reaches 56.0% Acc-Avg versus 23.3% (G-Safeguard) and 20.2% (GUARDIAN)—a gap of over 30 points. On GSM8K with LLaMA-3.1-8B, the advantage is 77.7% vs. 66.7% (G-Safeguard) and 46.2% (GUARDIAN). Under the obvious (non-adaptive) attack, G-Safeguard performs reasonably (42.4% on MMLU), but the confidence-guided method still leads at 62.4%, confirming that the advantage is not limited to the hardest attack setting. In the clean setting, all methods are within 1–3 points of each other, indicating the confidence-guided defense does not sacrifice normal MAS performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a critical analysis of the dominant embedding-based MAS defense paradigm (G-Safeguard, GUARDIAN), showing via a Lipschitz-margin theorem that these methods learn an overly broad benign acceptance region when trained on well-separated data, creating a guaranteed evasion zone. It repurposes token-level entropy-based confidence scoring—previously used for selective prediction and test-time reasoning (Wang et al., 2025a; Geng et al., 2024)—as a complementary defense signal in the multi-agent communication setting, where it had not been applied. The three near-benign attacks (Slow Drift, Benign Wrapper, Chaos Seeding) extend the MAS attack literature by explicitly optimizing for embedding-space proximity to benign messages rather than merely injecting overtly malicious content.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a structural weakness in the most widely adopted class of MAS defenses and provides a practical, training-free alternative (confidence-guided pruning/down-weighting) that generalizes across open-weight and API models. The finding that both embedding and confidence signals decay over communication rounds—and that topology density accelerates this decay—has direct implications for system design, suggesting that MAS deployments need early-round intervention and topology-aware monitoring. Open questions include whether richer internal signals (logit margins, self-consistency) can extend the effective defense window, and whether the confidence approach degrades under more sophisticated adaptive attacks that also manipulate token-level uncertainty.
