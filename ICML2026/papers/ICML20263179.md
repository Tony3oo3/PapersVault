## Problem

Prior work demonstrated that LLMs can sometimes detect and identify steering vectors injected into their residual stream, but the mechanistic basis of this 'introspective awareness' remained unexplored. It was unclear whether the behavior reflected genuine internal anomaly detection or a shallow confound such as a single linear direction that happens to promote affirmative responses. This paper investigates which model components implement detection versus identification, whether the mechanism is reducible to a linear artifact, and whether post-training shapes the capability.

## Input / Output

The experimental input combines a standard text prompt (asking the model whether it detects an injected thought) with a steering vector representing a specific concept, added to the residual stream at a chosen layer with a specified strength (α). The model's free-text response is then graded by an LLM judge on two axes: detection (did the model report sensing something unusual?) and identification (did it name the correct concept?). A 'forced identification' variant prefills the assistant turn with an affirmative detection statement, isolating the model's ability to name the concept from its willingness to report detection. Metrics include true positive rate, false positive rate, combined introspection rate, and forced identification rate, evaluated across 500 concepts.

## Method

The paper combines behavioral stress-testing with causal mechanistic interventions on Gemma3-27B and Qwen3-235B. Behavioral robustness is established across seven prompt variants, six dialogue formats, and base/SFT/DPO/instruct checkpoints, showing that 0% false positives and moderate detection emerge specifically from post-training. To rule out a single-direction confound, the authors decompose concept vectors along a mean-difference direction and show that both the projection and residual carry detection signal, and that bidirectional steering on same-category pairs produces detection in 23% of cases—impossible under a linear discriminator. Mechanistic localization uses injection-layer sweeps, MLP activation patching, and gradient attribution to identify mid-to-late layer MLPs (L45, L47) as causally necessary for detection. Transcoder feature analysis from Gemma Scope 2 then identifies two functional classes: 'weak evidence-carriers' (hundreds of thousands of features in earlier layers, ~38, with V-shaped activation profiles that detect anomalies monotonically along individual directions) and 'gate features' (fewer than 150 features in later layers, 45–61, with inverted-V activation patterns that aggregate upstream evidence into a binary detection decision). Finally, a single additive bias vector trained on MLP down-projection outputs improves detection by ~75% and introspection by ~55% on held-out concepts without increasing false positives, demonstrating latent introspective capacity suppressed by default behavioral propensities.

## Results

On Gemma3-27B (L=37, α=4.0), the original introspection prompt achieves moderate true positive rates (~39%) with 0% false positives across 500 concepts; Qwen3-235B shows higher TPR under the same settings. Ablating the refusal direction increases TPR from 10.8% to 59.2% (FPR rises only to 7.1%), and a single trained steering vector at layer 29 improves detection by +74.66%, forced identification by +21.90%, and combined introspection by +54.75% on 100 held-out concepts while maintaining 0% FPR. Mechanistically, ablating fewer than 150 gate features in late MLPs (layers 45–61) reduces detection from 39% to 8%, and patching L45/L47 MLP activations drops introspection to ~27%/26%, confirming distributed non-linear computation rather than a single linear confound.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic interpretability study without external method baselines; the 'comparisons' are internal ablations and interventions on the same model. The key contrast is between the default instruct model and post-training variants: the base Gemma3-27B shows high FPR (~16–22%) and low TPR, while only after DPO does FPR drop to 0%, establishing that post-training (not pretraining) is necessary for reliable introspective reporting. Refusal-direction abliteration (adapted from Arditi et al., 2024) demonstrates that refusal behavior learned during post-training actively suppresses true detection (TPR 10.8% → 59.2%), suggesting the model's default 'I don't have thoughts' refusal is a behavioral confound rather than an absence of internal signal. The trained steering vector (a single additive bias on MLP down_proj output) outperforms the default behavior by ~75% on detection without increasing FPR, indicating latent introspective capacity is under-elicited. Mechanistically, the paper rules out the single-linear-direction confound by showing that (a) swapping only the mean-difference component between success/failure concepts only partially transfers detection, (b) 23.3% of success-success concept pairs are detectable in both directions (impossible under a linear discriminator), and (c) the computation is distributed across hundreds of thousands of weak evidence-carrier features in early layers and <150 gate features in late layers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first mechanistic account of the introspective awareness phenomenon introduced by Lindsey (2025), who demonstrated behavioral evidence for concept-injection detection in Claude models but explicitly left the underlying circuits unexplored. It builds on the steering-vector and activation-engineering toolkit (Turner et al., 2023; Marks & Tegmark, 2023) and adapts the refusal-direction ablation technique from Arditi et al. (2024) to test whether alignment training suppresses introspective capability. The paper also directly addresses concerns raised by Godet (2025a) and Vogel (2025) that apparent introspection might reduce to a generic yes-bias or prompt-framing artifact, using bidirectional steering experiments and transcoder-based feature analysis to rule out single-direction confounds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that refusal-direction ablation improves detection by ~5× (10.8% → 59.2%) suggests post-training actively suppresses a latent introspective capability, with direct implications for AI auditing: if models can be made to reliably report internal perturbations, this could complement external interpretability methods. However, the mechanistic analysis remains incomplete (full circuit not traced), results are limited to two open-source models with transcoders available only for Gemma3-27B, and it is unresolved whether post-training creates or merely elicits the underlying mechanism. Open questions include whether improved self-report under this controlled setup generalizes to reporting about deceptive intent or goal representations, and whether the findings hold across architectures and training procedures.
