## Problem

Existing AI-text detectors rely on surface-level textual features and cannot distinguish fully AI-generated peer reviews from human reviews that have been linguistically polished by an LLM. The paper argues that authorship should be attributed from the ideas and judgments a review expresses, not merely from its writing style, and that different AI models converge on similar claims when reviewing the same paper while human reviewers introduce more unique and diverse ones.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260992/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a target peer review and k=3 AI-generated reference reviews of the same paper (matched by evaluation score, and from different models if the target is AI-generated) as input. Each review is first segmented by an LLM into structured claims across five categories, of which only three (evaluation, constructive input, clarification dialogue) are used for semantic comparison. The output is a three-class label (human-written, LLM-refined, AI-generated) with per-class confidence scores, enabling optional confidence-thresholding to flag uncertain predictions for manual review.

## Method

Sem-Detect combines claim-level semantic analysis with textual features in a supervised LightGBM classifier. The key mechanism is best-match cosine similarity: each target claim is compared against all claims in the AI reference reviews using Qwen3-0.6B embeddings, and five aggregate semantic features (proportion above threshold, mean above-threshold similarity, mean best-match similarity, intra-review diversity, log claim count) are computed alongside four textual features (perplexity, entropy, top-k token percentage, Fast-DetectGPT score). The claim-level granularity is critical—LLM-based extraction groups semantically coherent units that sentence-level chunking would fragment, and this finer granularity outperforms full-review embedding (as in Anchor) for isolating the convergence pattern that distinguishes AI from human authorship. Training uses 20,000+ reviews from 800 ICLR/NeurIPS papers, with four LLMs generating both fully AI reviews and LLM-refined variants of authentic human reviews, and hyperparameters are selected via randomized search optimizing macro-F1.

## Results

On the binary human-vs-AI detection task over 20,000+ peer reviews from ICLR and NeurIPS (2021–2022), Sem-Detect achieves an AUC of 0.999 and a TPR@0.1% FPR of 0.760, a 25.5% relative improvement over the strongest baseline (EditLens, 0.606). In the three-class setting (human / LLM-refined / AI-generated), Sem-Detect correctly identifies 91.18% of AI reviews and 91.61% of LLM-refined reviews, with fewer than 3.5% of LLM-refined human reviews misclassified as fully AI-generated and only 0.66% hard human-to-AI errors. Under distribution shift (unseen models and prompts), AI precision remains high (0.96–0.97) while recall drops to 0.65, reflecting a conservative failure mode that avoids false accusations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Sem-Detect outperforms all eight baselines on every reported metric. The gap is most pronounced at low FPR thresholds critical for deployment: at TPR@0.1% FPR, Sem-Detect (0.760) beats EditLens (0.606) by 25.5% relative, Anchor (0.541) by 40%, and RADAR (0.153) by nearly 5×. General-purpose zero-shot detectors (LogRank, MAGE, Fast-DetectGPT, Binoculars) are essentially non-functional at low FPR, with TPR@0.1% FPR ≤ 0.021, confirming that surface-level textual signals are insufficient for peer-review detection. Among domain-specific methods, the TF Model (Kumar et al.) relies on token-frequency patterns that degrade under LLM refinement (AUC drops to 0.674 in the collapsed binary), while Anchor (Yu et al.) operates at full-review granularity and cannot separate LLM-refined from fully AI-generated reviews. EditLens (Thai et al.) is the closest competitor in AUC (0.998 vs. 0.999) but lags substantially in TPR@0.1% FPR and, in the ICLR 2026 comparison, classifies 24% of reviews as AI-generated versus Sem-Detect's 5%, reflecting a more aggressive decision boundary. In the three-class setting, only Sem-Detect provides full three-way predictions; RADAR and Anchor, while competitive in collapsed binary AUC (0.966 and 0.980), cannot distinguish LLM-refined from human-written reviews.

## Relation to Prior Work

Sem-Detect synthesizes ideas from three distinct threads: general-purpose AI-text detectors (Fast-DetectGPT, RADAR, Binoculars) that extract surface-level textual features; domain-specific peer-review methods (Anchor, TF Model) that condition on the manuscript under review; and the retrieval literature (Dense X Retrieval, LumberChunker) showing that claim-level granularity outperforms document-level representations for similarity comparison. Its key conceptual departure is operating at the claim level rather than the full-review level, and introducing a three-class taxonomy (human / LLM-refined / AI-generated) that prior binary detectors cannot express. The method repurposes the manuscript-conditioned comparison idea from Anchor but replaces whole-review cosine similarity with per-claim best-match similarity against multiple AI-generated references, exploiting the observation that different LLMs converge on similar evaluative claims while humans do not.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three-class distinction is practically critical given the policy shift from ICML 2025's blanket LLM ban to ICML 2026's allowance of LLM-assisted editing: venues need to detect fully AI-generated reviews without penalizing reviewers who legitimately use LLMs for clarity. The released dataset of 20,000+ reviews spanning three authorship classes is a valuable resource for the community. Key limitations include the assumption that AI models will always converge on similar claims (which may erode as models produce more diverse, novel insights), the requirement to generate reference reviews at inference time, and the fact that cross-domain validation was limited to a still-deep-learning-focused medical imaging venue. The paper itself raises the open philosophical question of whether idea origin remains meaningful if AI-generated insights become indistinguishable from or superior to human ones.
