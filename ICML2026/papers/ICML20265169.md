## Problem

Multi-turn, multi-agent LLM game evaluations suffer from high run-to-run variance (compounding early deviations amplified by agent coupling) and prompt sensitivity that destabilizes comparative rankings. Existing automatic prompt optimization methods (TextGrad, MIPRO, GEPA) are designed for static tasks, update prompts from a local batch without persistent memory, and therefore produce run-dependent, high-variance results in interactive settings where different tournaments surface different decisive states and rare failure modes.

## Input / Output

The method takes a base LLM (GPT-4o-mini or Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct) and a text-based two-player game environment (from TextArena/SPIN-Bench) as input, and produces an optimized inference-time context c = (q, M) — a system prompt q plus a subsampled set M of structured insights drawn from a persistent memory bank — that is injected at inference time without any weight updates. During play, the conditioned LLM outputs a complete turn-level action (a full model response such as a card play, negotiation proposal, or board placement). The optimization loop consumes 2,000 self-play games per task across 5 generations of 8 candidates × 50 games each, and the final context is evaluated against three held-out opponent models over 50 games per opponent.

## Method

MEMO is a weight-free self-play framework that couples two components across optimization generations. The exploration component maintains a population of N=8 candidate contexts evaluated in self-play tournaments against a fixed baseline agent, using TrueSkill Bayesian skill ratings with a lower-confidence bound (S(c) = μ_c − κσ_c) for conservative selection; new candidates are generated via random style-guided proposals and memory-augmented prompt edits. The retention component extracts typed strategic insights (rule clarifications, legality constraints, strategy priors) from sampled completed trajectories via a reflection prompt, then merges them into a persistent memory bank using database-style add/remove/edit operations that grow, refine, and self-correct over generations. A prioritized replay buffer stores trajectory prefixes with inverse-frequency scoring to revisit rare, decisive states, balancing exploration with fresh play via a gating parameter β. The key architectural insight is the cross-generation coupling: the memory bank supplies reusable priors that condition both the population initialization (fraction π=0.75) and the memory-augmented proposal operator, while structured tournament exploration provides the repeated evaluation needed to filter which insights are retained — ablations show neither component alone achieves the full gain (tournament+memory yields +24.3 pp over baseline, replay adds a further +2.1 pp).

## Results

Across five text-based games (Simple Negotiation, Two Dollar, Kuhn Poker, Briscola, Simple Tak), MEMO raises mean win rate from 24.9% to 49.5% for GPT-4o-mini and from 20.9% to 45.0% for Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct, using only 2,000 self-play games per task. Run-to-run stability improves dramatically: mean RSE drops from 31.1% to 6.4% (GPT-4o-mini) and from 20.9% to 7.0% (Qwen-2.5-7B). MEMO also uses ~91K output tokens on average, roughly one-quarter of MIPRO (354K) and 20% fewer than GEPA (114K), while achieving the highest win rates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MEMO outperforms all static prompting baselines (CoT, ToT) by 14–25 points in mean win rate for GPT-4o-mini, and by 19–20 points for Qwen-2.5-7B. Against prompt-optimization methods, MEMO leads TextGrad by +14.9 pp, MIPRO by +12.8 pp, and GEPA by +17.5 pp (GPT-4o-mini), with similar margins for Qwen-2.5-7B. Against the RL baseline UnstableBaseline (Qwen-2.5-7B), MEMO matches the 45.0% mean win rate but achieves a dramatically lower RSE (7.0% vs. 43.3%), indicating far more stable evaluation; moreover, MEMO uses 19× fewer environment interactions (2,000 vs. 38,000 games). SPIRAL (RL) reports results on only two of five games (Kuhn Poker 56.7%, Simple Tak 32.7%), so a full comparison is not possible, though MEMO exceeds SPIRAL on Simple Tak (36.9% vs. 32.7%) while trailing on Kuhn Poker (60.0% vs. 56.7% is actually MEMO winning). The paper notes that RL remains more effective in perfect-information settings (e.g., UnstableBaseline reaches 47.3% on Simple Tak vs. MEMO's 36.9%), while MEMO dominates in negotiation and imperfect-information games.

## Relation to Prior Work

MEMO sits at the intersection of three threads: (1) automatic prompt optimization (TextGrad, MIPRO, GEPA, DSPy), which the authors note is largely developed for static tasks and lacks persistent memory across optimization rounds; (2) self-play and evolutionary LLM methods (SPIRAL, Absolute Zero, GEPA's evolutionary search), which typically require weight updates or external search at play time; and (3) experience-based LLM agents (ExpeL, VOYAGER), which accumulate insights but operate in single-agent settings. MEMO's conceptual departure is treating inference-time context as an optimizable agentic object in multi-turn, multi-agent, path-dependent games, coupling a persistent memory bank (retention) with tournament-style prompt evolution under TrueSkill and prioritized replay (exploration), all without parameter updates. The cross-generation coupling of memory and exploration is the key architectural novelty, as ablations show neither component alone recovers the full gain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that substantial performance headroom in multi-agent LLM games (roughly doubling win rates) can be unlocked through context optimization alone, using 19× fewer environment interactions than RL baselines, which is practically significant for settings where weight updates are infeasible or expensive. The stability improvements (RSE dropping from ~31% to ~6%) address a real reproducibility concern in multi-agent LLM evaluation that the field has acknowledged but not systematically resolved. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger game spaces or more than two agents, whether learned contexts transfer reliably across model families (the paper shows mixed results: effective for Gemini but not Grok), and how these findings generalize beyond simplified text-based games to high-stakes interactive applications. The boundary condition that RL remains superior in perfect-information games while MEMO excels in imperfect-information and negotiation settings suggests the two paradigms are complementary rather than competing.
