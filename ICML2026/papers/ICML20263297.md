## Problem

Natural-language negation in KG queries is both scope-sensitive (constraining only a specific subgoal or branch) and evidence-dependent (targeting particular witness paths rather than final answer entities), yet existing LLM-to-KG pipelines leave these attachments implicit, resorting to post-hoc filtering or overly broad enforcement that fails to respect where and when an exclusion should apply. The paper identifies that no answer-only post-filter can recover witness-level semantics once evidence is projected away, and that global negation after disjunctive merging causes scope drift across branches.

## Input / Output

The LLM specifier takes a natural-language query and a KG, producing a structured EEC request: anchor entity IDs, a grouped composition schema (disjunction within groups, conjunction across groups), a scope map assigning each atomic subgoal a stable scope ID, and scope-indexed banned-entity sets. The MATLOGIC executor consumes this request over a retrieved K-hop entity-centric subgraph (sparse adjacency matrix) and returns an answer entity set plus a compact witness trace recording one supporting atomic item per group and predecessor pointers sufficient to reconstruct a concrete K-hop witness for each answer, enabling audit against the enforced exclusions.

## Method

The paper introduces the Executable Exchange Contract (EEC), an interface that makes exclusion scope and enforcement timing explicit as structured metadata exchanged between an LLM specifier and a matrix-based executor (MATLOGIC). MATLOGIC executes under a canonical P→N→C/D schedule: (P) sparse-matrix K-hop propagation produces witness-carrying intermediate evidence per atomic subgoal; (N) each banned-entity set is compiled into a scope-local reachability mask (blocking all pairs whose K-hop witness traverses a banned entity via pre(z)⊗suf(z) aggregation) and applied to the effective closure before any merge; (C/D) intermediate results are composed by union within groups and intersection across groups, then projected to answer IDs. The paper proves two non-commutativities—masking does not commute with projection when the predicate is not output-determined, and does not commute with disjunctive merge when the out-of-scope branch is not safe—establishing P→N→C/D as the unique semantics-preserving enforcement boundary. Contract-aligned diagnostics then attribute failures to specifier (scope/binding compilation) versus executor (enforcement), showing the bottleneck is primarily in the LLM's compilation of scope-bound constraints.

## Results

On FB15k-237, LLM-MATLOGIC achieves the best MRR across most standard CQA patterns, with particularly large gains on composition-heavy queries: 58.2 on 2p and 57.1 on 2i, versus the best prompting baseline MFC at 35.2 and 42.4 respectively. On the five negation-aware patterns, LLM-MATLOGIC leads on 2in (11.4), 3in (12.7), and pni (10.9) MRR, outperforming LARK (7.5/4.8/3.6) and BetaE (5.9/8.2/2.8). On end-to-end NL-KGQA, LLM-MATLOGIC attains 63.5 Hits@1 on CWQ and 76.7 on WebQSP using only 3.0 LLM calls per question, compared to ToG's 51.8/72.2 with 14.3/10.7 calls and MFC's 62.8/78.9 with 9.7/7.5 calls.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LLM-MATLOGIC substantially outperforms all embedding-based CQA baselines (GQE, Query2Box, BetaE, HQE, HypE, CQD, CQD-Hybrid) across nearly all nine standard patterns, with the largest margins on multi-hop paths (2p: 58.2 vs. HypE's 35.2) and intersections (2i: 57.1 vs. CQD-Hybrid's 34.4). Against the strongest prompting baselines, LLM-MATLOGIC beats MFC on 7 of 9 patterns (e.g., 2p: 58.2 vs. 35.2; 2i: 57.1 vs. 42.4) and LARK on 7 of 9, though it trails LARK on pi (24.2 vs. 42.8) and MFC on pi (24.2 vs. 37.7) and 2u (39.6 vs. 48.2). On negation-aware patterns, LLM-MATLOGIC leads on 2in, 3in, and pni but loses to LARK on inp (15.1 vs. 23.3) and pin (7.5 vs. 18.7), which the authors attribute to specifier-side compilation errors. On NL-KGQA, LLM-MATLOGIC beats ToG on both CWQ (63.5 vs. 51.8) and WebQSP (76.7 vs. 72.2) with roughly 5× fewer LLM calls, roughly matches MFC on CWQ (63.5 vs. 62.8) at 3× lower call cost, but trails MFC on WebQSP (76.7 vs. 78.9). It also outperforms DiFaR² on WebQSP (76.7 vs. 65.3) but falls short of the fine-tuned DecAF on both CWQ (63.5 vs. 70.4) and WebQSP (76.7 vs. 82.1), which requires no LLM calls at inference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of complex query answering (CQA) over knowledge graphs and LLM-based KGQA by introducing an explicit interface-level contract (EEC) for handling scoped negation, rather than treating it as an implicit property of query structure or a post-hoc filtering step. On the CQA side, prior methods (GQE, Query2Box, BetaE) assume negation scope is already fixed by the logical query; on the KGQA side, systems like ToG and MFC perform iterative LLM reasoning but leave exclusion attachment and enforcement timing implicit. The MATLOGIC executor repurposes sparse-matrix reachability propagation (a standard graph algorithm) with a novel scope-local masking mechanism, and the P→N→C/D schedule is proven to be the canonical enforcement boundary via non-commutativity arguments (Propositions D.1–D.2), giving the approach a theoretical grounding absent in prior prompting-based or embedding-based pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's key practical insight is that the bottleneck for exclusion-rich KG queries lies in the specifier (LLM compilation of scope and banned sets), not the executor, as shown by the large gap between oracle and generated constraints (avg. MRR 0.199→0.369). This attribution is actionable: it directs future effort toward better scope-binding prompts or learned compilers rather than more sophisticated graph execution. However, absolute performance on negation-aware patterns remains low (single-digit to low-teens MRR), the exclusion mechanism is limited to entity-level banned sets (not arbitrary relational predicates), and the approach depends on a single-pass GPT-3.5 call for specification, raising open questions about robustness to linguistically ambiguous negation and scalability to richer exclusion semantics in production KG systems.
