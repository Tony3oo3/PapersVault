## Problem

Evolution Strategies (ES) are powerful black-box optimisers that handle non-differentiable objectives and parallelise naturally, but naive ES becomes prohibitively expensive at billion-parameter scale because full-rank random perturbations yield batched matrix multiplications with very low arithmetic intensity, limiting prior work to small models and populations of at most ~1,440. This paper asks how to make ES practical for large-scale pretraining and fine-tuning of foundation models.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a parameterised model (matrices of shape m×n per layer), a scalar fitness function f(M) that need not be differentiable, and training data for fitness evaluation. It outputs updated parameter matrices via stochastic gradient ascent on the ES objective. A distinctive feature is that EGGROLL can operate entirely in integer datatypes (e.g., int8) at both training and inference time, and population sizes up to 2^20 ≈ 1 million are supported on a single GPU, with only scalar fitness values communicated between workers.

## Method

EGGROLL replaces full-rank Gaussian perturbations E ∈ R^{m×n} with low-rank factorisations E = (1/√r)AB^T where A ∈ R^{m×r}, B ∈ R^{n×r}, and r ≪ min(m,n), reducing per-layer perturbation storage from mn to (m+n)r. The key hardware insight is that the forward pass decomposes as x(μ+σE)^T = xμ^T + (σ/√r)(xB_i)A_i^T, preserving the high-arithmetic-intensity GEMM of standard batched inference while adding only cheap per-perturbation work (e.g., vector-dot products when r=1), achieving up to 91% of pure batch-inference throughput. A counter-based deterministic RNG reconstructs perturbations on demand so they need not persist in memory, and the overall parameter update is a weighted sum of N rank-r matrices, giving rank min(Nr, m, n) — full rank in practice. Theoretically, the paper proves O(1/r) convergence of the low-rank update to the true Gaussian ES gradient via Edgeworth expansion, and shows that in high dimensions (d=mn) both ES and EGGROLL linearise under the critical noise scaling σ_d = o(d^{-1/2}), recovering first-order gradient updates even for discontinuous objectives.

## Results

EGGROLL achieves up to 91% of pure batch inference throughput, a ~100× speedup over naive ES for billion-parameter models at large population sizes. On the countdown reasoning task with RWKV-7 1.5B, EGGROLL reaches 35% validation accuracy versus 23% for GRPO under identical hardware and wall-clock time. For the 14B RWKV-7 model fine-tuned on DeepScaleR, EGGROLL improves AIME24 accuracy from 13% to 30%, AIME25 from 7% to 33%, and HMMT25 from 11% to 13%. In tabula rasa RL across 16 environments, EGGROLL outperforms OpenES on 7/16, ties on 7/16, and underperforms on 2/16, while delivering substantial wall-clock speedups.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), EGGROLL clearly wins on the countdown task (35% vs. 23% validation accuracy on RWKV-7 1.5B) and on GSM8K fine-tuning with the 7B RWKV model, where EGGROLL allows 8192 parallel generations versus GRPO's 256 under the same GPU budget. On the 14B DeepScaleR regime, GRPO is infeasible due to Adam's memory overhead, so EGGROLL is the only viable method, improving AIME24 from 13% to 30%. Against OpenES (Salimans et al., 2017), EGGROLL is broadly competitive across 16 tabula rasa RL environments (7 wins, 7 ties, 2 losses) while delivering substantial wall-clock speedups from its batched low-rank structure. For language model pretraining, EGGROLL-trained int8 RNN (EGG) outperforms a same-size fp32 Transformer trained with backprop SGD (3.40 vs. 3.58 bits/byte), though at ~180× more GPU-hours due to the 2^20 population size. Overall, EGGROLL wins or ties on most comparisons while being significantly faster, with the main caveat that pretraining requires substantially more compute than the backprop baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

EGGROLL repurposes LoRA's low-rank factorization (Hu et al., 2022) from gradient-based fine-tuning to evolutionary strategies, and builds its parallel population loop directly on Salimans et al.'s (2017) OpenES algorithm. Unlike prior low-rank evolutionary approaches (Choromanski et al., 2019; Garbus & Pollack, 2025) that restrict the search space to a low-rank manifold, EGGROLL uses low-rank perturbations purely as a hardware-efficient evaluation mechanism while the population-averaged update remains full-rank. The paper also positions against zeroth-order LLM fine-tuning methods (MeZO, Malladi et al. 2023; ESSA, Korotyshova et al. 2025) and GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), arguing that the population sizes those methods use (~100–256 perturbations) are too small for pretraining, whereas EGGROLL's batched low-rank evaluation enables populations exceeding one million.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By making ES practical at billion-parameter scale with populations of 2^20 on a single GPU, EGGROLL opens the door to training non-differentiable, low-precision, or neurosymbolic systems end-to-end—tasks where backpropagation is infeasible or ill-suited. The int8 RNN outperforming a fp32 Transformer (3.40 vs. 3.58 bits/byte) is a striking demonstration, though it required ~180× more GPU-hours, so the compute-for-quality tradeoff remains an open question. The O(1/r) convergence rate (faster than the standard O(1/√r) CLT rate, due to symmetry eliminating odd cumulants) and the three-regime high-dimensional analysis provide rigorous grounding, but whether these guarantees hold under the non-stationary, non-convex landscapes of real LLM training remains to be tested. A key open question is whether the population-size advantage translates into better optimization in data-limited regimes, and whether the method extends to architectures beyond recurrent models (e.g., transformers with KV-cache constraints).
