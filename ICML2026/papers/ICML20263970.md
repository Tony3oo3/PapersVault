## Problem

While LLMs are increasingly treated as participants in psycholinguistic paradigms to generate semantic norms, it remains unclear what a model's own discrete behavioral outputs reveal about its own internal hidden-state geometry. Unlike in human cognition, both behavior and internal representations are simultaneously observable in LLMs, yet no prior work has used representational similarity analysis to directly compare a model's behavior-derived semantic geometry against its own layerwise hidden-state similarity under a matched vocabulary and experimental protocol.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263970/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes eight instruction-tuned decoder-only transformers (7B–14B parameters) and probes them over a fixed 5,000-word high-frequency English noun vocabulary (SUBTLEX-US ∩ C4). Behavioral data are collected via two paradigms—forced choice (select 2 of 16 candidates, 313 trials per cue) and free association (generate 5 single-word associates, 126 stochastic runs per cue)—yielding 17.5M+ total trials. Hidden states are extracted at every layer under four contextual strategies (Averaged over 50 C4 sentences, Meaning prompt, Task-FC prompt, Task-FA prompt), mean-centered to counter anisotropy, and converted to cosine-similarity matrices. Outputs include PPMI-weighted behavioral similarity matrices, layerwise hidden-state similarity matrices, RSA Pearson correlations, NN@k overlap scores, held-out-words ridge R² values, and a publicly released association dataset.

## Method

The core design is a within-model paired comparison: for each of eight models, the same 5,000-word vocabulary is used to build both a behavioral cue-response count matrix (aggregated across trials, reweighted by PPMI to suppress globally frequent responses, then converted to a cosine-similarity geometry) and a layerwise hidden-state similarity matrix (extracted under four contextual strategies and mean-centered). Alignment is quantified three ways: (i) RSA via Pearson correlation between upper-triangular entries of hidden-state and reference similarity matrices sampled over 500k word pairs; (ii) nearest-neighbor overlap (NN@k) at k ∈ {5,…,200}; and (iii) a held-out-words ridge regression that predicts a target model's hidden-state similarity from five scalar predictors (FastText, BERT, cross-model consensus, raw-count FC, raw-count FA) under an 80/20 vocabulary split to prevent leakage. The cross-model consensus baseline averages hidden-state cosine similarities across all layers of all other models, isolating model-specific from shared structure. The key finding is that forced-choice behavior—whose fixed candidate sets produce a dense cue-response matrix—aligns substantially more with hidden-state geometry than free association, and that FC similarity predicts unseen hidden-state similarities beyond lexical and cross-model baselines, indicating recoverable model-specific structure from discrete outputs alone.

## Results

Averaged across eight instruction-tuned models, all layers, and four extraction strategies, forced-choice (FC) behavioral similarity achieves a mean RSA Pearson correlation of r = .43 with hidden-state geometry, substantially outperforming free association (FA, r = .18), FastText (r = .20), BERT (r = .11), and approaching cross-model consensus (r = .74). In held-out-words ridge regression, the full model (behavioral + FastText + BERT + cross-model consensus) reaches mean test R² = .587 versus .569 for the lexical/consensus baseline, with FC contributing +.022 incremental R² and FA only +.002. Peak performance is Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct under the Meaning strategy (R² = .844). Nearest-neighbor overlap (NN@k) confirms the ranking: FC rises from .20 (k=5) to .29 (k=200), while FA peaks at .18 (k=5) and cross-model consensus reaches .56 (k=200).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FC behavioral similarity (r = .43) more than doubles FastText (r = .20) and nearly quadruples BERT (r = .11) on RSA alignment with hidden-state geometry, and it also outperforms FA (r = .18) by a factor of ~2.4. In nearest-neighbor overlap, FC (.20→.29 across k) consistently exceeds FastText (.15→.21) and BERT (.16→.19) at every neighborhood size, while FA peaks early (.18 at k=5) and does not improve with larger k. In the held-out-words ridge regression, FastText and BERT are included in the baseline alongside cross-model consensus; adding FC on top of this baseline still yields a +.022 R² gain, demonstrating that FC carries model-specific structure beyond what these lexical embeddings capture. FA's incremental contribution is negligible (+.002), suggesting its open-vocabulary responses largely reflect the same shared cross-model geometry already captured by the consensus baseline. Overall, the behavioral FC protocol recovers roughly 58% of cross-model consensus's alignment (r = .43 vs. .74) using only discrete text outputs, with no access to logits, hidden states, or other-model weights.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA), previously used to relate LLM activations to brain signals or to compare models to each other, to a new pairing: an LLM's own behavior-derived semantic geometry versus its own layerwise hidden-state geometry under a matched vocabulary. It draws on the psycholinguistic tradition of word-association paradigms (De Deyne et al., 2019; Günther et al., 2019) and recent work treating LLMs as participants in semantic norms tasks (Abramski et al., 2024; Suresh et al., 2023), but deliberately operates in a weaker access regime than extraction/cloning attacks (Carlini et al., 2024; Gharami et al., 2025) by using only discrete text outputs with no logits or hidden-state access. The cross-model consensus baseline is motivated by the emerging platonic/universal representation hypothesis (Huh et al., 2024; Kaushik et al., 2025), which the paper uses as a reference to separate shared from model-specific structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a purely black-box behavioral protocol (forced-choice selections over a fixed vocabulary) recovers a nontrivial projection of a model's internal semantic geometry—roughly 58% of what cross-model consensus captures—without any access to logits, hidden states, or weights, which has direct implications for auditing closed-source models. The FC-over-FA gap and its explanation via matrix density provide concrete experimental-design guidance for future behavioral interpretability work. Key limitations include restriction to high-frequency English nouns, correlational (not causal) analysis, and dependence on candidate-set construction; open directions include causal interventions on hidden states, other psycholinguistic paradigms (rankings, triadic comparisons), and multilingual/other-POS generalization.
