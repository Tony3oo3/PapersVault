## Problem

Standard policy learning minimizes overall population risk but can assign treatment to large fractions of patients who receive no benefit, wasting resources and exposing them to unnecessary harm. The proportion of treated patients with no benefit (treatment risk) is generally not point-identifiable from observational or trial data due to unmeasured confounders or selection bias, making it unclear how to learn policies with certified control over this risk. This paper addresses the gap of learning interpretable treatment allocation policies that provably control treatment risk under partial identifiability from finite samples.

## Input / Output

The method consumes finite samples (X, A, L) of tabular patient covariates, binary treatment assignments, and binary health-loss outcomes, drawn from either an observational study or a randomized trial. It also requires user-specified hyperparameters: a treatment risk tolerance τ, a degree of odds miscalibration Γ ≥ 1 bounding unmeasured confounding or selection bias, and a confidence level 1−α. The output is a treatment allocation policy π(X) restricted to a class of interpretable fast-and-frugal decision trees, accompanied by a finite-sample certification that the treatment risk T(π) ≤ τ with probability at least 1−α across all miscalibration levels up to Γ.

## Method

The method derives upper bounds on both population and treatment risks using importance weights parameterized by the miscalibration degree Γ, which are tight when Γ=1 and widen as unmeasured confounding or selection bias increases. It then applies sampling splitting: the first split learns a nominal policy by minimizing the empirical bound on population risk subject to a treatment-risk constraint over the policy class (fast-and-frugal decision trees grown greedily with the constraint checked at each split), while the second split constructs a distribution-free upper confidence bound on the treatment risk via the Bentkus inequality. The key calibration step selects an empirical tolerance τ_n that is the smallest value whose corresponding policy's confidence bound stays below τ, ensuring the final certified policy satisfies the risk-control guarantee with probability ≥ 1−α. This calibration mechanism is analogous to conformal prediction's split-conformal procedure but adapted to the partially identified, constrained policy-learning setting.

## Results

On synthetic data with 1000 repeated experiments, the certified policy π(X;τ_n) controls the treatment risk T(π) ≤ τ with probability ≥ 90% (1−α) across all tested tolerances τ when the miscalibration degree Γ is correctly specified. When unmeasured confounding is present and the method incorrectly assumes Γ=1, risk control fails entirely; specifying Γ=2 restores valid control at the cost of higher population risk. On the STAR dataset (4218 students, 100 random splits), valid coverage is achieved for all τ values under Γ=1, with the trade-off between treatment and population risks clearly visible. The International Stroke Trial (IST) dataset yields analogous results, confirming the method's applicability to real-world RCT data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external competing methods but instead contrasts its certified policy π(X;τ_n) against two internal baselines: (1) the nominal policy π(X;τ) from Eq. (12), which minimizes the empirical risk bound subject to a constraint but provides no finite-sample guarantee, and (2) standard empirical risk minimization (π(X;1) with Γ=1). The nominal policy cannot certify risk control with any given probability, while the certified policy achieves the (1−α) guarantee. The critical comparison is between certified policies using Γ=1 versus Γ=2 under true unmeasured confounding: the Γ=1 policy fails to control treatment risk (Figure 4a), whereas the Γ=2 policy restores valid control, albeit with a tighter constraint and consequently higher population risk (Figure 4b). This demonstrates that the sensitivity parameter Γ is essential for valid inference under partial identifiability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) constrained policy learning (Wang et al. 2018; Doubleday et al. 2022), which assumes point identifiability of risks; (2) partial identifiability in causal inference (Manski 2003; Kallus & Zhou 2021), which provides bounds but not finite-sample certification; and (3) distribution-free risk-controlling prediction sets (Bates et al. 2021), whose proof technique is adapted here for policy learning. The key departure is combining Tan's (2006) odds-miscalibration sensitivity framework with a conformal-style sampling-splitting procedure to yield a *certified* finite-sample guarantee on treatment risk under partial identifiability, while using fast-and-frugal decision trees (Zhang et al. 2015) for interpretability. Unlike Kallus (2022), who defines harm via unobservable counterfactuals, this paper targets the observable proportion of treated patients who receive no benefit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method addresses a growing clinical concern—overtreatment and low-value care—by providing a principled, certifiable way to limit the fraction of treated patients who gain no benefit, even when unmeasured confounding or trial-selection bias prevents point identification of risks. The finite-sample guarantee (not merely asymptotic) and the use of interpretable fast-and-frugal trees make the approach more deployable in high-stakes clinical settings than black-box alternatives. Open questions include whether average risk control suffices when subgroups defined by sensitive features exhibit heterogeneous risk profiles (the authors note stratification may be needed), how to calibrate Γ in practice beyond the omitted-covariate benchmarking heuristic, and whether the conservatism of upper-bound-based control can be tightened without sacrificing validity.
