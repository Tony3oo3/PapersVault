## Problem

Prior multi-reward RL work has defaulted to applying GRPO by summing all reward components and normalizing the aggregate, without examining whether this is appropriate. The paper shows that this naive approach causes distinct reward combinations (e.g., a rollout earning both rewards vs. only one) to collapse into identical normalized advantage values, reducing the resolution of the training signal and leading to suboptimal convergence or outright training failure.

## Input / Output

Given a question with G sampled rollouts, each rollout carries n individual scalar reward scores (e.g., correctness ∈ [−3,3], format ∈ {0,1}, length ∈ {0,1}). GDPO produces a single normalized advantage scalar per rollout that is fed into the policy gradient objective. A key design constraint is that the final advantage magnitude must remain numerically stable regardless of how many reward dimensions are added, which is enforced by a post-aggregation batch-wise normalization step over all rollouts in the current training batch.

## Method

GDPO replaces GRPO's single group-wise normalization over the summed reward with a two-stage scheme: (1) each of the n rewards is independently normalized within the group of G rollouts (mean/std per reward), and the resulting per-reward advantages are summed; (2) the summed advantages are then batch-wise normalized across all rollouts in the training batch to prevent the numerical scale from growing with reward count. This decoupling preserves distinctions that GRPO erases—for example, reward combinations (0,2) and (0,1) receive different advantage magnitudes under GDPO but identical ones under GRPO—yielding substantially more distinct advantage groups as either the number of rollouts or rewards increases. The method is plug-and-play: it can replace the advantage estimator in any policy optimization objective (GRPO, DAPO, CISPO) without modifying the clipping or loss structure, and the paper validates both stages independently, showing that removing the batch-wise normalization step occasionally causes convergence failure.

## Results

On the tool-calling task (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct), GDPO achieves 32.81% average accuracy and 80.66% format correctness on BFCL-v3, outperforming GRPO (30.18% / 76.33%). On math reasoning (DeepSeek-R1-1.5B), GDPO reaches 29.4% AIME accuracy vs. GRPO's 23.1%, while reducing the length-exceeding ratio from 10.8% to 6.5%. For the 7B model, GDPO gains 2.9% on AIME (53.1% vs. 50.2%) and cuts the exceed ratio from 2.1% to 0.2%. In the three-reward coding setting (DeepSeek-R1-7B), GDPO maintains comparable pass rates while reducing bug ratios by 1.5–1.4 points and length-exceeding ratios by 2.7–3.5 points over GRPO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GDPO is compared primarily against GRPO (Guo et al., 2025), the standard group-relative advantage estimator. GDPO wins on nearly every metric: on tool calling it gains +2.6% avg accuracy and +4.3% format correctness (1.5B); on math it gains up to +6.3% AIME accuracy (1.5B) and +2.9% (7B) while halving or more the length-exceeding ratio; on coding it reduces bug ratios by 1.4–1.5 points at comparable pass rates. Against DAPO (Yu et al., 2025a), replacing the GRPO advantage with GDPO inside the DAPO objective improves average tool-calling accuracy from 30.30% to 32.19% and format correctness from 75.19% to 81.24%. Against CISPO (Chen et al., 2025a), the same substitution lifts accuracy from 27.82% to 30.74% and format from 77.48% to 79.15%. The one notable exception is DeepSeek-R1-7B on MATH accuracy, where GRPO (94.1%) marginally edges GDPO (93.9%), though GDPO still dominates on the length-exceeding metric (0.1% vs. 0.5%).

## Relation to Prior Work

GDPO builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025), the group-relative policy optimization method that has become the default for LLM RL training, and extends it to the multi-reward setting where prior work (ToolRL, DLER, L1) has applied GRPO naively by summing rewards before normalization. The paper positions itself against GRPO variants like DAPO and CISPO as a drop-in advantage estimator replacement, and distinguishes itself from BNPO (Xiao et al., 2025) by targeting a specific multi-reward collapse phenomenon rather than general variance reduction. It also connects to the broader multi-reward RLHF literature (Safe RLHF, ALARM) but focuses specifically on the normalization mechanism rather than reward design or hierarchical reward structures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As multi-reward RL becomes the standard approach for aligning LLMs to diverse human preferences (accuracy, format, length, safety, etc.), the identified reward collapse in GRPO represents a practical pitfall that could silently degrade training quality across many ongoing projects. The fix is minimal (decouple per-reward normalization) and broadly compatible with existing policy optimization objectives, making it easy to adopt. Limitations include evaluation on relatively small models (1.5B–7B), a fixed set of two-to-three rewards, and reliance on a specific conditioned length reward design for the math/coding tasks; open questions include behavior at very large reward counts, interaction with other normalization techniques, and whether the batch-wise normalization step remains necessary at scale.
