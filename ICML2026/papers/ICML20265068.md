## Problem

In LLM-based multi-agent systems with fixed agent roles and interaction structures, prompts serve as the primary behavioral policy, yet optimizing them remains difficult because outcome-level feedback conflates the effects of many interdependent interaction steps over long, noisy trajectories. Existing prompt optimization methods either rely on scalar outcome scores (insufficient for diagnosing which agent or instruction caused failure) or treat prompts as monolithic text (preventing the minimal, localized edits that expert practitioners actually use). No prior method jointly leverages interaction trajectories as diagnostic evidence and translates that evidence into targeted, unit-level prompt refinements.

## Input / Output

TRUCE consumes natural-language agent prompts (decomposed into semantically coherent policy units), full multi-agent interaction trajectories (partitioned into sub-trajectories using the system's native execution boundaries such as communication rounds or task-stage blocks), and a task-level outcome evaluation score. It produces structured policy-editing suggestions—each an (operation, natural-language instruction) tuple targeting a specific policy unit or introducing a new one—aggregated across multiple tasks by frequency support before being applied as minimal localized edits to the prompt representation. Agent roles and interaction topology remain unchanged throughout optimization.

## Method

TRUCE performs verbalized credit assignment grounded in a structural analogy to reinforcement learning: prompts are policies, trajectories are rollouts, and task evaluations are rewards, but all operations occur in natural-language space rather than through numeric gradients. The method partitions each trajectory into protocol-level sub-trajectories, then uses an LLM to generate a verbalized credit signal for each sub-trajectory by jointly reasoning over the interaction segment and the global outcome evaluation. These credit signals are mapped to structured policy-editing suggestions (ADD, MODIFY, DELETE) over the unit-based prompt representation, where each policy unit is an interpretable behavioral rule. Suggestions are aggregated across tasks by measuring their frequency of appearance (support), retaining only the top-k most consistently supported edits to suppress spurious, task-specific updates. This iterative loop—execute, attribute, suggest, aggregate, apply—enables stable, minimal prompt refinement without global rewrites, and a theoretical analysis in the appendix provides high-probability convergence guarantees to an ε-local optimum under a stylized abstraction of the optimization procedure.

## Results

On MultiAgentBench (GPT-4o-mini + GPT-4o), TRUCE achieves an average Task Score of 69.00 and Coordination Score of 88.27, outperforming the best baseline (Reflexion) by +5.6 TS and +7.5 CS. On the Qwen3 setting, TRUCE reaches 76.61 TS and 85.96 CS, again leading all baselines. On the CODESIM programming benchmark, TRUCE attains 80.80% average Pass@1 (GPT-4o) and 83.50% (Qwen3), improving over the expert-designed original prompts by +0.57 and +0.97 points respectively, while all other baselines show little to no gain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRUCE consistently outperforms all five baselines on MultiAgentBench. Against Reflexion, TRUCE gains +5.6 TS and +7.5 CS on average (GPT-4o setting), with the largest CS improvement in Coding (+22.5). Against DsPy (MIPROv2), TRUCE leads by +8.3 TS on average, with a particularly large gap in Bargaining (+27.3 TS), where DsPy's scalar-feedback approach degrades severely. Against TextGrad, TRUCE achieves +6.3 TS and +8.1 CS on average, with the most pronounced advantage in Coding CS (+17.9), reflecting the benefit of unit-level edits over global prompt rewrites. Against the two multi-agent-specific baselines, TRUCE beats MAPRO by +2.2 TS and +3.5 CS on Research, and by +5.3 TS and +24.2 CS on Coding; it also outperforms HiveMind by +0.4 TS and +8.1 CS on Research, and by +0.3 TS and +22.1 CS on Coding. On the CODESIM programming benchmark, TRUCE is the only method that improves over the expert-designed original prompts (80.80 vs. 80.23 Pass@1 with GPT-4o), while Reflexion, DsPy, and TextGrad all show equal or negative gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

TRUCE synthesizes three research threads that have been studied largely in isolation: RL-style credit assignment (value functions, advantage estimation), LLM prompt optimization (from black-box search like DsPy/MIPROv2 to textual-gradient methods like TextGrad), and multi-agent prompt refinement (MAPRO, HiveMind). It departs from prior prompt optimizers by rejecting the monolithic-prompt paradigm—where the entire prompt is rewritten based on a scalar score or a global textual gradient—and instead introduces a unit-based prompt representation paired with trajectory-level verbalized credit signals. The structural analogy to RL (policy → prompt, rollout → trajectory, credit assignment → sub-trajectory attribution, policy update → unit-level edit) is the paper's conceptual backbone, but the instantiation is entirely in natural language space with no differentiable parameters, distinguishing it from both classical RL and gradient-based prompt tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

In practical deployments where agent roles and interaction topologies are frozen (proprietary LLMs, regulated workflows), prompts are the only tunable lever, yet current optimization methods either ignore trajectory evidence or produce destabilizing global rewrites. TRUCE's unit-level, evidence-grounded edits mirror how human experts actually refine multi-agent prompts, and the efficiency gains (more milestones per round and per token) suggest that better credit assignment yields not just higher scores but faster task completion. Open questions include scalability to very large agent populations, sensitivity to the quality of the LLM performing credit attribution, and whether the approach transfers to settings where the interaction structure itself must co-evolve with prompts. The theoretical analysis is a stylized abstraction (Monte Carlo neighborhood search) rather than a tight bound on the actual LLM-driven procedure, so convergence guarantees remain aspirational.
