## Problem

Prior work on LLM confidence estimation assumes models have privileged self-knowledge about their own correctness, framing calibration as an introspection problem. This paper shows that assumption is empirically unsupported: a model predicting its own correctness performs no better than an unrelated model doing the same task. The missing ingredient is historical performance information, which the paper demonstrates is the primary driver of reliable cross-model correctness prediction.

## Input / Output

The correctness model receives a triple: the original query prompt, the target model's full free-form response (averaging ~198 tokens on MMLU, exceeding 1000 tokens for math reasoning), and the explicit name of the generating model. It outputs a calibrated P(is_correct) extracted as the logit probability of the token 'yes' after a fixed correctness-elicitation prompt, yielding a continuous confidence score alongside a binary yes/no label. The model name conditioning is a deliberate design choice that distinguishes this from reward models or judges, which are trained model-agnostically.

## Method

The Generalized Correctness Model (GCM) fine-tunes a single Qwen3-8B via LoRA (rank 32, batch size 16) on the concatenation of correctness datasets from 8 heterogeneous target models (spanning Qwen2.5, Llama3, Gemma3, and Qwen3 families at 3B–72B), using cross-entropy loss on the yes/no token with the generating model's name as a conditioning signal. The key insight is that aggregating historical correctness patterns across models teaches transferable prediction strategies—particularly world-knowledge-based answer verification and question-difficulty matching—that outperform both single-model SCMs and the self-emitted confidences of much larger models. Post-hoc spline calibration (2 or 4 knots) on a 5% held-out split further reduces ECE to ≤.02 without retraining. The paper also ablates conditioning factors (full response vs. answer-only vs. answerless) to decompose contributions from phrasing, parametric knowledge, and question-type matching, and explores training-free history injection via semantic ICL retrieval (top-5 from a vector database) as an alternative when fine-tuning is infeasible.

## Results

On MMLU, the GCM (Qwen3-8B) achieves 82.0% accuracy and 0.890 AUROC when predicting Llama3.1-8B's correctness, and 83.6% accuracy and 0.865 AUROC for Gemma3-27B, outperforming the target-specific SCM by 2.8–4.0% accuracy and 0.033–0.054 AUROC while maintaining ECE ≤ 0.029. With post-hoc spline calibration, the GCM further reduces ECE to 0.016–0.020 without sacrificing accuracy or AUROC. On the Spider text-to-SQL benchmark, the GCM similarly outperforms the SCM by 3.9–2.5% accuracy and 0.053–0.028 AUROC. The GCM also beats Llama3-70B's self-emitted P(True) confidences by 2.4% accuracy and 0.265 AUROC on MMLU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against P(True) (Kadavath et al., 2022), the GCM wins decisively on every metric: it improves accuracy by 7.9–8.1% and AUROC by 0.083–0.158 while reducing ECE from ~0.20 to ~0.02–0.03, showing that raw logit-based self-confidence is far less reliable than a history-trained external predictor. Against Verbal Confidence (Tian et al., 2023), the GCM gains 5.6–3.9% accuracy and 0.085–0.127 AUROC over the verbalized baseline, with ECE reduced by roughly 0.13–0.14. The GCM also outperforms the target-specific SCM (the Kapoor et al., 2024-style single-model fine-tuning approach) by 2.8–4.0% accuracy and 0.033–0.054 AUROC on MMLU, and by 2.5–3.9% accuracy on Spider, demonstrating that aggregating correctness histories across 8 models yields a more generalizable predictor than training one model per target. Even when the GCM is transferred to an OOD dataset (TriviaQA), post-hoc calibration with only 5% of target data brings its ECE from 0.105 down to 0.031, matching the SCM's calibration while retaining a 0.001 AUROC advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the P(True) logit-based confidence elicitation of Kadavath et al. (2022) and the fine-tuning-on-correctness-labels paradigm of Kapoor et al. (2024), but fundamentally departs from the 'self-knowledge' framing that dominates the confidence calibration literature. Where prior work treats confidence as a model introspecting on its own outputs, this paper reframes correctness prediction as a cross-model, history-encoding problem: a single small LLM (Qwen3-8B) is fine-tuned via LoRA on the aggregated correctness histories of eight different target models, conditioning on model identity and performance patterns. The GCM also incorporates retrieval-augmented in-context learning (semantic retrieval of k=5 similar historical examples) and post-hoc spline calibration as complementary, training-free ways to inject history, distinguishing it from reward models and LLM judges that deliberately ignore model identity to remain unbiased.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that self-knowledge provides no measurable advantage for correctness prediction challenges a core assumption in the LLM confidence literature and redirects the field toward history-based, cross-model approaches. Practically, a single 8B GCM outperforms the self-emitted confidences of 70B+ models in both accuracy and calibration (ECE ≤ .03), offering a lightweight, inference-efficient (prefill-only, ~0.125s/example) calibration layer for multi-model deployments. Key limitations include weaker cross-dataset generalization (ECE jumps to .105 on TriviaQA without post-hoc calibration), evaluation restricted to multiple-choice QA and text-to-SQL (no open-ended generation), and reliance on a judge model for ground-truth labeling. Open questions include whether the approach extends to reasoning models with long chain-of-thought outputs, whether the history-encoding strategy transfers to non-English or domain-specific settings, and whether the GCM's advantage persists as target models become more homogeneous in capability.
