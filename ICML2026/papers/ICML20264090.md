## Problem

Existing scientific benchmarks evaluate static question-answering and fail to capture the interactive, tool-mediated nature of real scientific workflows, while general tool-use benchmarks lack the breadth of domain-specific scientific tools. This paper asks whether LLM agents can orchestrate thousands of typed scientific tools across multiple disciplines in long-horizon, multi-step workflows, and identifies that even frontier models (e.g., GPT-5 at 41.3% SR) degrade sharply as interaction horizons extend, with weaker models falling into repetitive tool-calling loops.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a scientific problem (text plus optional multimodal inputs such as molecular structures, spectra, or phase diagrams) alongside a tool registry of 1,780 typed functions with scientific type signatures (SMILES, ProteinStructure, Vector3D, Matrix, etc.). Interaction proceeds via a ReAct-style loop where each tool call returns fine-grained feedback—typed outputs, execution status, or error diagnostics—and intermediate artifacts accumulate in a sandboxed filesystem. The final output is a strict JSON answer to a sequence of sub-questions, scored by hierarchical accuracy with 0.05 numeric tolerance and an LLM-based semantic fallback for textual fields.

## Method

SciAgentGym formalizes the environment as E=(S,A,T,O) with 1,780 tools organized by domain and function, a sandboxed filesystem, scientific databases, and a Python interpreter, all enforcing typed input/output signatures for automatic validation. SciAgentBench constructs 259 tasks (1,134 sub-questions) through a four-stage pipeline: aggregating ~5,000 candidates from five source benchmarks, filtering via four frontier LLMs (retaining tasks with <50% ensemble accuracy), verifying solvability via closed-loop execution in the environment, and expert validation of multi-step reasoning requirements. The key training contribution, SciForge, models the tool space as a dependency graph G_d=(V_d,E_d) where edges encode type-level output-to-input compatibility, then samples executable program graphs via backward construction with strict argument-binding constraints and stage-aware ε-greedy predecessor selection that prioritizes logical workflow order (query→computation→analysis→visualization). Forward execution in the environment yields golden traces, and execution errors are deliberately retained as error-recovery training data (interleaving failed attempts with corrected retries). Each verified trace is converted to a natural-language question via LLM with information control that maps intermediate numeric outputs to qualitative descriptors, preventing solution leakage while preserving reasoning context.

## Results

On SciAgentBench (259 tasks, 1,134 sub-questions), GPT-5 achieves the highest overall Success Rate of 41.3% with tools (SPL 0.24), while the average across all 18 evaluated models is 28.3%. Performance degrades sharply with difficulty: the average drops from 47.4% on L1 (≤3 steps) to 16.4% on L3 (≥8 steps), a 65.4% relative decline. The paper's fine-tuned SciAgent-8B reaches 30.1% SR with tools, outperforming its 235B backbone Qwen3-VL-235B-Inst (23.9%) by +6.7 points, and SciAgent-4B improves over Qwen3-VL-4B-Inst by +5.5 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The headline comparison is SciAgent-8B (30.1% SR) versus its 29× larger backbone Qwen3-VL-235B-Inst (23.9%), a +6.7-point gain that the paper attributes to execution-grounded trajectory fine-tuning via SciForge. SciAgent-8B also surpasses Qwen3-VL-235B-Think (28.0%) and Qwen3-VL-32B-Think (27.9%), but remains below the top closed-source models: GPT-5 (41.3%), Grok-4-1 (40.3%), and Claude-Sonnet-4 (35.9%). Among open-source models, SciAgent-8B edges out GLM-4.6V (30.9% vs. 30.1% is actually GLM slightly ahead) and clearly beats all Qwen3-VL variants ≤32B. On the L3 long-horizon subset, SciAgent-8B achieves 20.3%, substantially above Qwen3-VL-235B-Inst (4.6%) and Qwen3-VL-8B-Inst (7.0%), though still trailing GPT-5 (34.6%) and Grok-4-1 (28.6%). The ablation in Table 4 further shows that training on non-scientific tools causes negative transfer (−4.9 points), confirming that the gains are specific to scientific tool-use rather than generic tool-calling ability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two established lines of work: interactive agent environments (AgentBench, AgentGym, DiscoveryWorld, MedAgentGym) and scientific reasoning benchmarks (GPQA, ScienceQA, SciBench). Prior scientific benchmarks are static QA tasks, while general tool-use benchmarks (ToolBench, BFCL, τ-Bench) lack domain-specific scientific toolkits. SciAgentGym departs from both by providing a stateful, multi-disciplinary environment with 1,780 typed scientific tools and a ReAct-style interaction loop, while SciForge introduces a novel data synthesis paradigm that models the tool action space as a dependency graph and generates training trajectories through actual environment execution rather than LLM-only generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a critical and quantified bottleneck: even GPT-5 achieves only 41.3% overall success, with a 65% relative degradation from L1 to L3 difficulty, suggesting that current frontier models lack fundamental understanding of logical dependencies between scientific tools. The finding that an 8B model fine-tuned on execution-grounded trajectories outperforms a 235B model by 6.7 points, with positive cross-domain transfer, points toward a data-quality-over-scale paradigm for scientific agent training. Open questions include whether the dependency-graph synthesis approach generalizes beyond the four covered disciplines, whether the L3 performance ceiling can be broken with longer training or RL, and whether the error-recovery trajectory mechanism transfers to non-scientific tool-use domains.
