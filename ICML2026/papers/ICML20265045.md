## Problem

Standard on-policy RL for LLM reasoning operates on fixed prompt distributions and bounded rollout lengths, creating a distribution shift when test-time budgets exceed training budgets. This causes premature termination within the training budget and repetitive, verbose generation when the model continues beyond it, fundamentally limiting extrapolation. The paper asks how to train models that can consistently improve their reasoning to test-time budgets an order of magnitude longer than those seen during training.

## Input / Output

At each RC turn, the model receives the original problem prompt concatenated with the previous turn's compressed summary (empty string for turn 1) under a reasoning system instruction, and generates a trace of at most H_R tokens (16k default); it then generates a summary of at most H_S tokens (2048) under a separate summarization instruction. The summary encodes high-level strategic information (strategies employed, conclusions reached) rather than verbatim content. After T turns, the final output is the last reasoning trace, with total effective budget T × H_R decoupled from any single autoregressive generation length.

## Method

RC replaces standard autoregressive decoding with an iterative generate-summarize loop that exploits summarization-generation asymmetry: instruction-following LLMs are better at reasoning conditioned on a summary of a prior attempt than at generating correct solutions from scratch. Training uses GRPO to optimize summary-conditioned generation at each turn independently, assigning outcome-based rewards per turn to avoid cross-iteration credit assignment. A summary replay buffer stores generated summaries across epochs, enabling off-policy training on later-turn summaries without generating prohibitively long on-policy trajectories and extending the effective training horizon by T_train per epoch. The key architectural insight is that by bounding each autoregressive generation to H_R (matching the training budget), RC keeps conditional distributions within the training support even as the effective reasoning horizon grows to T × H_R, directly addressing the distribution shift that limits standard RL extrapolation.

## Results

RCT-4B (a 4B model trained with RC at a 16k-token budget) achieves 66.3% on HMMT 2025, 58.0% on IMO-AnswerBench, and 34.1% on FrontierScience at 192k–256k test-time tokens, substantially outperforming the untrained base model (39.8%, 40.9%, 23.3% respectively). On HMMT 2025, RC training improves the 4B model from 40% accuracy at 16k tokens to 70% at 512k tokens. The model also generalizes to the out-of-domain FrontierScience benchmark despite being trained exclusively on math problems, and outperforms larger models such as Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B on IMO-AnswerBench.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Qwen3-4B-Thinking model (a specialized reasoning model trained for long-horizon autoregressive decoding), RCT-4B wins on HMMT 2025 (66.3 vs. 62.5), IMO-AnswerBench (58.0 vs. 53.8), and FrontierScience (34.1 vs. 25.7), but trails on AIME 2025 (74.9 vs. 81.3). Against Polaris-4B (a Qwen3-4B model trained for extrapolation via YaRN context extension), RCT-4B wins on HMMT (66.3 vs. 60.2), IMO (58.0 vs. 47.7), and FS (34.1 vs. 23.6), again losing only on AIME (74.9 vs. 79.4). Against trained iterative decoding baselines (Self-Refine and Self-Verify, both trained with a similar RL procedure but without summarization), RCT-4B outperforms on all four benchmarks, with the largest gaps on IMO (58.0 vs. 52.2/52.3) and AIME (74.9 vs. 60.4/61.2). The paper emphasizes that HMMT 2025 (Nov), IMO, and FS were released after the base model's training cutoff, minimizing contamination risk, and RCT-4B outperforms all autoregressive baselines on these three benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two main lines of work on test-time compute scaling. First, prior extrapolation methods (Setlur et al. 2025, An et al., Luo et al. 2025) modify training curricula or reward structures to encourage in-context exploration within autoregressive generation, but remain coupled to the training distribution and saturate at 3–4× the training budget. Second, iterative decoding methods (self-refinement, self-verification, RSA, DSM Agent) improve performance at fixed budgets but do not enable extrapolation beyond training horizons. RC repurposes the iterative decoding paradigm for a fundamentally different purpose—decoupling effective reasoning horizon from training budget—by exploiting the summarization-generation asymmetry in instruction-following LLMs, and pairs this with a short-horizon RL training objective (GRPO on summary-conditioned rollouts) plus a summary replay buffer for off-policy learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RC provides a practical recipe for training small models (4B) to leverage test-time budgets an order of magnitude larger than their training budget, outperforming much larger specialized reasoning models on math and science benchmarks. The finding that instruction-following models outperform specialized reasoning models for RC is a useful design principle, and the compatibility with existing test-time scaffolds (RSA, DSM Agent) suggests broad applicability. Key limitations include: RC does not optimize summary generation (credit assignment is difficult), uses only myopic per-turn rewards (no incentive for far-sighted exploration), requires summarization-generation asymmetry (absent in some reasoning-specialized models), and is less effective for search-heavy problems where summarization risks discarding critical state. Open questions include how to train summarization effectively, how to incentivize multi-turn reasoning contributions, and whether the approach extends to domains without the 'cliquey' reasoning graph structure that makes summarization beneficial.
