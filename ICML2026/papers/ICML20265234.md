## Problem

Existing time series forecasting methods exhibit a systematic 'recent-data bias'—an excessive reliance on the most recent observations that renders forecasts highly vulnerable to perturbations (noise, anomalies, missing values) in recent data. The paper provides both empirical evidence (gradient-based importance scoring across diverse architectures) and a theoretical explanation (exponential gradient decay under VAR(1) dynamics) for why this bias arises, and shows that existing robustness approaches (seasonal-trend decomposition, anomaly detection-imputation pipelines) fail to adequately address it.

## Input / Output

The method takes a historical multivariate time series X ∈ R^{H×C} (H=96 time points, C channels), along with historical and future timestamps, and produces a future F-step forecast Ŷ ∈ R^{F×C} for F ∈ {96, 192, 336, 720}. A key design choice is the channel-independent strategy: a single shared dual-layer MLP backbone processes all channels identically. During training, BARS randomly omits a fraction (1−ρ) of time points and projects the remaining samples onto a fixed 358-dimensional Fourier basis (179 sine/cosine pairs spanning minute-to-year frequencies); during inference, all historical points are used without sampling.

## Method

TameR decomposes forecasting into a dual-space problem: a Learnable Periodicity Extraction (LPE) module captures the periodic component via a per-channel learnable cycle table (R^{W×C}) aligned to timestamps, while a Basis-Aligned Randomized Sampling (BARS) module handles the residual in a Fourier basis domain. BARS randomly samples time points during training and solves a regularized least-squares projection onto 179 trigonometric basis functions, which theoretically disperses local perturbations across global coefficients (Proposition C.4) and attenuates per-point gradient influence (Proposition C.3). A two-stage training protocol first learns accurate periodic cycles with a simple linear residual model, then jointly fine-tunes LPE and BARS with a dual-layer MLP backbone. The final forecast is the sum of the LPE periodic prediction and the BARS residual reconstruction, trained end-to-end with MAE loss for anomaly robustness.

## Results

Under perturbations in recent data (single anomalous point), TameR achieves an average rank of 1.33 and a 1st Count of 31 out of 64 (8 datasets × 2 metrics × 4 prediction lengths), substantially outperforming the next-best baseline TimesNet (Avg Rank 2.20, 1st Count 9). On unperturbed data, TameR maintains competitive accuracy with an Avg Rank of 1.82 and 1st Count of 16, using only a dual-layer MLP backbone. TameR consistently achieves the best or second-best robustness across all 8 datasets except Exchange, where its basis-function reconstruction is slightly disadvantaged by the dataset's pronounced distribution shift.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TameR decisively outperforms all 14 baselines on robustness to recent-data perturbations, winning 31 of 64 dataset-metric combinations (Table 1). The closest competitor is TimesNet (Avg Rank 2.20), which shows minimal MSE degradation under perturbation but still trails TameR by 0.017–0.038 MSE on ETT datasets and 0.021–0.027 on Weather. Linear-based models (DLinear, TimeMixer, CycleNet) and attention-based models (PatchTST, iTransformer, Leddam) all suffer large accuracy drops when the last data point is perturbed, confirming the recent-data bias. RobustTSF, specifically designed for anomaly-robust forecasting, paradoxically performs worst (Avg Rank 6.36) because its detection-imputation pipeline is not optimized for perturbations in the most recent historical points. On clean data (Table 2), TameR remains competitive (Avg Rank 1.82) but is slightly outperformed by Leddam (2.07) and CycleNet (2.52) on high-correlation datasets like Solar and Traffic, where its channel-independent strategy is less advantageous. The sole dataset where TameR loses on robustness is Exchange, where pronounced distribution shifting challenges its basis-function reconstruction approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by identifying 'recent-data bias' as a universal, previously unexamined limitation across mainstream TSF architectures, supported by both empirical gradient-based importance scoring and a theoretical proof via VAR(1) optimization dynamics. Methodologically, it repurposes ideas from CycleNet (learnable periodic cycles) and basis-expansion forecasting (N-BEATS, FiLM, DAM) into a novel dual-space framework, but departs from prior robustness work (RobustTSF, TSFA pipelines, randomized smoothing) by targeting perturbations specifically in recent data rather than anomalies anywhere in the sequence or adversarial perturbations over the full input. The BARS strategy is distinct from standard dropout in that it operates on raw temporal indices and projects into a fixed trigonometric basis domain, avoiding the imputation artifacts of time-domain padding or interpolation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The systematic demonstration that recent-data bias is a universal property of diverse TSF architectures (attention, linear, convolution, frequency-domain) provides a concrete diagnostic for practitioners and a clear target for future robustness research. TameR's practical advantage—achieving robustness with a simple dual-layer MLP backbone—suggests that architectural complexity is not the primary driver of robustness, which could reshape how the community approaches robust TSF design. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to highly non-stationary or distribution-shifting series (where it underperforms, e.g., Exchange), whether the fixed 179-frequency basis is optimal across all domains, and whether the channel-independent strategy can be relaxed to capture inter-variable dependencies without reintroducing recent-data bias.
