## Problem

TabPFN-style tabular foundation models (e.g., TabPFN-2.5) are designed and benchmarked for inputs with up to roughly 2,000 features, leaving HDLSS domains such as gene expression (m ≫ 2,000, n small) outside their operating range. The paper asks how to make small frozen tabular foundation models effective in truly high-dimensional, low-sample regimes without retraining or architecturally modifying the backbone.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261426/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a tabular matrix X ∈ R^(n×m) with m ≫ n and class labels, clusters samples into k groups, and constructs per-cluster feature dissimilarity graphs. It outputs class predictions from a frozen TabPFN-2.5 head that operates on M compressed scalar meta-feature tokens (Z(x) ∈ R^M, M ≪ m), where M is set by a PCA cumulative-variance intrinsic-dimension rule (clipped to [32, min(512, m)]). The TabPFN-2.5 head is never backpropagated through; only GO-LR and NSC hyperparameters are tuned via Optuna (150 trials/dataset).

## Method

GOTabPFN is a two-stage front-end that converts HDLSS tables into compact tokens for a frozen TabPFN-2.5 head. First, GO-LR (Graph-guided Ordering with Local Refinement) builds cluster-wise weighted feature graphs from pairwise dissimilarities, initializes each local ordering with a nearest-neighbor TSP-path heuristic, then refines via adjacent-swap descent under a weighted Minimum Linear Arrangement (MinLA) dispersion objective; the paper proves this objective is exactly weighted MinLA (NP-hard) and strictly generalizes TSP-path. Local orders are aggregated into a single global permutation Π* by weighted mean-rank integration. Second, NSC (Neuro-Inspired Subunit Compression) segments the Π*-ordered feature axis into M contiguous subunits (via uniform, equal-mass, or largest-jump cutpoint rules) and pools each segment onto its first principal direction (SegPCA) to yield one scalar meta-feature per subunit, inspired by dendritic subunit integration in cortical neurons. The resulting M-token sequence is passed to the frozen TabPFN-2.5 head, which performs in-context prediction without any gradient updates to the backbone.

## Results

On 8 HDLSS biomedical benchmarks (5×5 nested CV), GOTabPFN achieves the best accuracy on all 8/8 datasets with an average rank of 1.00 ± 0.00. Headline accuracies include 97.54% on Arcene, 97.44% on Lung, 96.46% on Prostate, and 93.82% on GLI-85. Across 8 additional cross-domain high-dimensional datasets (Table 2), GOTabPFN attains the best average rank (1.25 ± 0.66) and the top result on 7/8 tasks. On Colon, the full pipeline runs in 31.4 s with 115.6 MB peak GPU memory, confirming practical efficiency for HDLSS settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GOTabPFN outperforms all 8 compared baselines on every one of the 8 HDLSS datasets, achieving an average rank of 1.00. The largest absolute gains are over the best original foundation-model head on GLI-85 (+4.16 pp, 89.66→93.82), Arcene (+2.60 pp, 88.00→90.60), and SMK (+2.24 pp, 71.99→74.23), where feature redundancy and noise make ordering-aware compression most impactful. On near-saturated datasets (ALLAML, Prostate, TOX), gains shrink to 0.06–0.38 pp, reflecting limited headroom. Among the strongest TabPFN-family competitors—TabPFN-Wide, TuneTables, BETA (TabPFN Unleashed), and TabDPT—GOTabPFN consistently leads, with the gap widening on harder/noisier datasets. Classical baselines (Real-MLP, ProtoGate) and in-context-learning models (TabICL, TANDEM) are also surpassed across all benchmarks. On the broader cross-domain suite (Table 2), GOTabPFN maintains its lead (7/8 top results) over TabPFN-Wide, TANDEM, TabDPT, and MLP, confirming that the GO-LR + NSC interface generalizes beyond biomedical HDLSS to image-derived, text-like, and sensor data.

## Relation to Prior Work

GOTabPFN sits at the intersection of three prior threads: (1) tabular foundation models (TabPFN, TabPFN-2.5, TabPFN-Wide, TuneTables, BETA) that perform in-context prediction but are designed for moderate feature counts (~2,000); (2) feature-ordering/seriation work for tabular data (TabSeq, DynaTab, Mambular, ROTATOR-LLM) that showed column order matters for sequence-sensitive backbones; and (3) classical combinatorial optimization (MinLA, TSP-path, seriation). The paper's key departure is coupling a MinLA-grounded ordering (GO-LR) with a neuro-inspired subunit compression (NSC) as a frozen front-end to an unmodified TabPFN-2.5 head, rather than retraining the backbone or performing feature selection à la ProtoGate. The theoretical contribution formalizes the ordering step as weighted MinLA (NP-hard) and shows it strictly generalizes TSP-path, while the practical solver is a nearest-neighbor TSP-path heuristic followed by local adjacent-swap refinement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, theory-grounded recipe for making small tabular foundation models usable in genuinely high-dimensional (m ≫ n) biomedical and genomics settings without architectural changes or retraining, which is a real gap since TabPFN-2.5 is benchmarked up to ~2,000 features. The finding that a simple greedy+refinement ordering outperforms stronger metaheuristics (SA, GA, ACO, Christofides) on downstream accuracy—despite those achieving lower TSP surrogate cost—is a useful practical insight: optimizing the MinLA dispersion objective directly matters more than optimizing a TSP proxy. Limitations include the frozen backbone's 10-class and 50K-sample caps, increased runtime from graph construction, and evaluation limited to 8 HDLSS + 8 cross-domain datasets; whether the ordering+compression interface transfers to other frozen backbones or to high-sample regimes remains open.
