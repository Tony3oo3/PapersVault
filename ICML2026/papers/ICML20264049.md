## Problem

Existing scientific literature retrieval agents rely on keyword or embedding-based matching that captures only content-level similarity, failing to model the relational structure of the scientific network such as corroborating versus conflicting studies, technological lineages, and collective credit allocation. No prior benchmark systematically evaluates whether retrieval systems can understand these multi-level scholarly relations, leaving a fundamental gap between what scientific practice demands and what current retrieval paradigms deliver.

## Input / Output

Each query is a natural-language question tied to one of 2,640 fine-grained subfields across 7 disciplines (e.g., 'What is the most influential citation path from Attention Is All You Need to An Image is Worth 16×16 Words?'), and the system must operate over a 269-million-paper citation network with full-text access via arXiv PDFs and OpenAlex metadata. Outputs are task-specific: ranked lists of 50 papers with percentile-rank scientometric scores (ego-centric), per-paper binary citation/sentiment/co-mention labels verified through GROBID paragraph-level parsing (pair-wise), or ordered citation path sequences scored on consistency, connectivity, and rationality (path-wise). The downstream application produces structured Markdown literature review reports evaluated on relevance, completeness, depth, logical consistency, and usefulness.

## Method

SciNet constructs 8,940 relation-aware tasks across three levels of relational abstraction grounded in established scientometrics and graph algorithms. Ego-centric tasks use Uzzi's co-citation Z-score for novelty and Funk & Owen-Smith's disruption index (N_i − N_j)/(N_i + N_j) as objective ground truth; pair-wise tasks parse citation contexts with GROBID and classify sentiment via GPT-5 (validated at 98% human agreement on 200 samples); path-wise tasks identify maximum cumulative-citation paths via BFS on the citation network, reflecting collective credit allocation. The evaluation protocol benchmarks eight retrieval methods spanning embedding models (SciBERT, Qwen3-Embed), agentic systems (PaSa, PaperQA2, GPT-4o-search), and deep research agents (o3/o4-mini-deep-research), revealing that even the best systems achieve below 20% recall on relation-aware tasks. A downstream literature review experiment further demonstrates that relation-aware retrieval yields a 25.3% improvement in review quality over search-only baselines.

## Results

Across all three relation-aware task categories, even the strongest Deep Research agents (o3-deep-research) achieve recall@50 of only 1.47% on novelty and 4.71% on disruption in ego-centric tasks, meaning over 95% of truly groundbreaking papers are missed. On pair-wise tasks, Cite-Acc peaks at 62.73% for o3-deep-research, but Cite-Sentiment drops to 13.68% and CoMention-Paragraph to 17.09%. On path-wise tasks, Consistency reaches 63.28% for o3-deep-research, yet Connectivity is capped at 14.54%, revealing that even top-tier models cannot maintain explicit citational chains. In the downstream literature review experiment, the Deep Research system achieves an average human preference rank of 2.20 versus 3.00 for the Base LLM, representing a 25.3% improvement in review quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 8 retrieval models across three paradigms, with a clear performance hierarchy: Deep Research agents > web-search agentic models > specialized agentic frameworks > embedding models. SciBERT is the weakest baseline, scoring near-zero on all pair-wise metrics (2.13% Cite-Acc, 0% on all others) and achieving 0% recall@50 on ego-centric tasks. Qwen3-Embedding improves modestly (11.34% Cite-Acc, 22.93% CoMention-Acc) but remains far below agentic systems. Among agentic models, gpt-4o-search substantially outperforms PaSa (48.65% vs. 19.54% Cite-Acc) and PaperQA2 (48.65% vs. 8.43% Cite-Acc), demonstrating the value of web search tools. o3-deep-research achieves the best CoMention-Acc (75.68%) and CoMention-Paragraph (17.09%), but its Cite-Sentiment (13.68%) is actually lower than o4-mini-deep-research (17.83%), indicating that even the strongest system struggles with fine-grained sentiment reasoning. No model exceeds 20% on the most demanding relational metrics (Cite-Sentiment, CoMention-Paragraph, Connectivity), confirming that relation-aware retrieval remains fundamentally unsolved across all current paradigms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two established scientometric indicators—Uzzi et al.'s co-citation Z-score for novelty and Funk & Owen-Smith's disruption index—to construct ground-truth labels for a new class of retrieval tasks. It departs sharply from prior scientific retrieval benchmarks (LitSearch, PASA, STARK), which focus on semantic topical matching or entity-centric multi-hop queries over structured knowledge bases. SciNet instead frames retrieval as a problem of understanding the relational topology of the citation network itself, introducing three levels of abstraction (ego-centric, pair-wise, path-wise) that have no direct analogue in existing benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even state-of-the-art Deep Research agents achieve below 5% recall on identifying truly novel or disruptive papers exposes a fundamental gap between current retrieval paradigms and the relational reasoning that scientific practice demands. The 25.3% downstream improvement in literature review quality when agents are empowered with relational ground truth suggests practical stakes beyond benchmarking. Open questions include how to capture implicit (non-citation-based) scientific influence, how to handle the Matthew Effect bias in citation networks, and whether the current standardized query format adequately reflects the linguistic diversity of real researcher queries.
