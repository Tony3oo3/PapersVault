## Problem

Small language model agents match large ones on simple tasks but fail to scale with task complexity, yet existing routers either underperform the largest agent or fail to reduce cost on agentic workflows where long trajectories decouple task inputs from downstream success. The paper asks how task complexity mediates the small-vs-large agent gap and how to route heterogeneous agents per-task to maximize workload handled by cheap agents without degrading performance on complex tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260064/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes agentic task descriptions (deep search or coding), short strategic plans generated by each candidate agent (not full solutions), and a growing auction memory of past strategies and outcomes. Output is a per-task routing decision selecting one agent, its winning strategy, and the final task solution produced by executing that strategy in the Agent Research Environment. Unusually, the routing signal is the agents' own strategic plans scored by a cost-value objective rather than the task description itself, and the auction memory enables iterative refinement where only agents cheaper than the provisional winner produce revised bids.

## Method

SALE (Strategy Auctions for Workload Efficiency) is a test-time auction framework where heterogeneous agents bid with strategic plans scored by a cost-value mechanism. Cost is estimated from strategy length times per-token price, while value combines normalized generation entropy with a 0-5 Likert jury score from all agents (self-and-peer). A min-max MILP optimization over assignment variables and scoring weights selects the agent with the lowest cost-minus-value. After each auction, all strategies and outcomes are stored in a shared memory bank; agents cheaper than the provisional winner retrieve k similar past tasks via cosine similarity and produce refined bids through contrastive prompting (showing winning vs. losing strategy pairs). The final assignment updates only if a refined bid achieves a lower cost-minus-value, coupling per-task routing with continual test-time self-improvement without training a separate router. The auction overhead is under 1% of total inference cost.

## Results

On the full HST-BENCH test set, SALE achieves 67.3% pass@1 at $0.21/Mt on deep search and 61.1% pass@1 at $0.27/Mt on coding, exceeding the best single agent (Qwen3-32B: 63.8% at $0.36/Mt and 58.4% at $0.36/Mt) by +3.5 and +2.7 pass@1 points respectively, while reducing cost by 42% and 25%. SALE reduces reliance on the largest agent by 52% on average and lowers overall inference cost by 35%, with the auction overhead accounting for under 1% of total inference tokens. Gains are consistent across all five complexity bins, with SALE outperforming the best single agent at every level (e.g., +3.8 pass@1 at 36% lower cost on the most complex deep search tasks).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SALE dominates all four baselines on the performance-cost Pareto frontier. WTP (Hu et al., 2024) achieves modest cost savings on deep search (62.4% at $0.32/Mt vs. SALE's 67.3% at $0.21/Mt) but collapses on complex coding tasks, dropping to 6.3% pass@1 at τ(t)≤60 versus SALE's 26.1%. CARROT (Somerstep et al., 2025) reduces cost by 22% on deep search and 14% on coding relative to the best single agent but still underperforms SALE by 6.0 and 4.0 pass@1 points respectively on the full set. TO-Router (Stripelis et al., 2024) effectively defaults to the strongest agent, yielding metrics nearly identical to the 32B baseline (63.0% at $0.33/Mt on deep search; 58.4% at $0.36/Mt on coding) and failing to reduce cost. FrugalGPT (Chen et al., 2024), the non-predictive cascade, matches the best agent on simple tasks but degrades sharply with complexity (12.5% at τ(t)≤60 on deep search) while incurring the highest cost ($0.51–0.61/Mt), exposing the fundamental mismatch of sequential execution for long agentic trajectories.

## Relation to Prior Work

SALE repurposes auction-theoretic mechanism design (Che 1993; Hong et al. 2016) and virtual-agent-economy ideas (Tomasev et al. 2025; Dutting et al. 2024) for the concrete problem of routing agentic tasks across heterogeneous LLMs, departing from prior predictive routers (WTP, CARROT, TO-Router) that learn a separate classifier from task descriptions and from non-predictive cascades (FrugalGPT) that execute full trajectories. It also extends the task-complexity scaling analysis of Kwa et al. (2025) and Sinha et al. (2026) from isolated model behavior to system-level allocation, and adapts memory-driven agent improvement (Cao et al. 2026; Wang et al. 2025e) by storing bidding strategies and auction outcomes rather than execution traces. The cost-value scoring draws on entropy-based reasoning quality signals (Chen et al. 2026; Wang et al. 2025b) and LLM-jury evaluation (Verga et al. 2024; Mousavi et al. 2023), but the combination of strategic-plan bidding, MILP-based assignment, and contrastive memory refinement is new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic, complexity-conditioned evidence that small agents' advantage erodes sharply on realistic agentic workloads, and demonstrates that a lightweight, training-free coordination mechanism can simultaneously improve accuracy and cut cost relative to any single model or existing router. Practically, the 52% reduction in reliance on the largest agent and 35% cost savings are meaningful for deployment economics, and HST-BENCH offers a reusable benchmark for complexity-dependent routing research. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to frontier-scale models (70B+), cross-family agent pools, and task-distribution shift where auction memory may contain stale strategies; the linear memory growth and single-family evaluation (Qwen3 4B–32B) limit immediate extrapolation. More broadly, the work motivates a systems-level view where gains come from market-inspired coordination among heterogeneous agents rather than from ever-larger individual models.
