## Problem

Trajectory inference methods are evaluated via held-out marginal distributions, but marginals do not determine how states at different times are coupled — distinct trajectory models can achieve similar marginal scores while inducing very different path distributions. This paper addresses the absence of a tractable estimator for the KL divergence between probability measures on function space, which would enable direct, path-level comparison of inferred trajectory distributions rather than their marginal projections.

## Input / Output

The method consumes sampled trajectories from two distributions ν^A and ν^B (functions in H = L²(𝕋; ℝ^d)) together with a trace-class noise covariance operator C whose Cameron-Martin space must contain the data support. It outputs forward and reverse KL divergence estimates as scalars, computed by Monte Carlo averaging over samples from ν^A, the noise measure μ_0, and uniform time points — no ODE integration of the full generative dynamics is required. Velocity fields are represented in a truncated Fourier basis, and the Cameron-Martin norm in the integrand is evaluated after projection onto this basis.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution is a velocity-only representation of the KL divergence between trajectory distributions: under FFM's linear interpolation, KL(ν^A‖ν^B) equals ∫₀¹ (t/(1-t))‖v_t^A − v_t^B‖²_{H_{μ_0}} dμ_t^A dt, derived through three lemmas that establish absolute continuity, express KL via the weak continuity equation with log-density test functions, and link the logarithmic score mismatch to the velocity mismatch via Gaussian conditioning. Practically, a single MINO (Mesh-Informed Neural Operator) network with a binary conditioning flag jointly approximates both velocity fields, trained with a conditional flow-matching objective that linearly combines Hilbert and Cameron-Martin norm losses. A subtraction-based parameterization enforces v(x,1)=x to cancel the t/(1-t) singularity at the boundary, ensuring the velocity difference vanishes where the integrand is most sensitive. The FKL estimate is then obtained by Monte Carlo evaluation of the integrand at sampled (x_t, t) pairs, making the procedure scalable to realistic snapshot counts and trajectory resolutions without simulating full generative dynamics.

## Results

On the Petal dataset (τ=0.75), MSBM and TIGON achieve nearly identical W2 scores (0.160 vs. 0.155), yet FKL strongly separates them: MSBM yields KL(ν^A‖ν^B)=9.641 and KL(ν^B‖ν^A)=17.055, while TIGON yields 96.144 and 35.505, correctly reflecting MSBM's superior alignment with true dynamics. Across all three synthetic datasets, FKL consistently ranks SBIRR best on Lotka-Volterra and Repressilator, and MSBM best on Petal, whereas marginal-metric Critical Difference plots produce inconsistent or contradictory rankings. On real scRNA-seq data (hESC), TIGON is ranked best by all five marginal metrics at τ=0.25, but FKL reveals a fundamental mismatch: TIGON generates smooth deterministic trajectories while the reference (SBIRR) paths are stochastic, yielding FKL values of 293.102 (forward) and 161.731 (reverse) versus far lower values for stochastic methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates six trajectory inference methods (vSB, SBIRR, MSBM, MFL, eAM, TIGON) using both standard marginal metrics (EMD, W2, SWD, MWD, MMD) and the proposed FKL. On Petal, marginal metrics rank TIGON and MSBM nearly identically (W2: 0.155 vs. 0.160), but FKL assigns TIGON a forward divergence ~10× higher than MSBM (96.144 vs. 9.641), exposing that TIGON's deterministic ODE dynamics are fundamentally misaligned with the stochastic ground truth. On Repressilator, marginal-metric CD plots rank TIGON above SBIRR, yet FKL places SBIRR clearly ahead (23.519 vs. 54.515 forward), consistent with visual inspection showing TIGON produces a smooth spiral rather than the true three-gene oscillation. On real hESC data, TIGON dominates all five marginal metrics at τ=0.25 (e.g., W2=0.990 vs. 1.026 for vSB), but FKL assigns it the worst score (293.102 forward) because its smooth trajectories conflict with the stochastic reference. Forward KL (mode-coverage) consistently favors MFL on real datasets due to its radiating off-support trajectories, while reverse KL (mode-seeking) favors MSBM, revealing an asymmetry invisible to marginal metrics. Overall, FKL resolves counterintuitive marginal-metric rankings and provides a single coherent trajectory-level criterion across all seven datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Functional Flow Matching (FFM) (Kerrigan et al., 2024), which provides the linear-interpolation velocity-field structure that makes the KL derivation tractable, and on the subtraction-based boundary parameterization of Hu et al. (2025) to cancel the t→1 singularity. Conceptually, it sits at the intersection of infinite-dimensional generative modeling (Kerrigan et al., 2023; Pidstrigach et al., 2024; Lim et al., 2024; Park et al., 2024) and information-measure estimation (Pinski et al., 2015a,b; Belghazi et al., 2018; Franzese et al., 2024), but departs from prior work by deriving a velocity-only representation of KL that is both theoretically grounded in the Cameron-Martin framework and practically estimable via Monte Carlo without ODE integration. In the trajectory inference literature, where evaluation has been exclusively marginal-based (W2, EMD, MMD), this paper introduces the first path-space divergence metric that is both analytically justified and computationally scalable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the standard evaluation protocol in trajectory inference—comparing held-out marginal distributions—is fundamentally non-identifiable and can produce rankings that contradict visual and dynamical ground truth, a finding with direct implications for how the single-cell genomics community benchmarks new methods. The forward/reverse KL asymmetry (mode coverage vs. mode seeking) provides a diagnostic lens that marginal metrics cannot offer, revealing that methods like MFL and MSBM optimize for qualitatively different properties. Key limitations include restriction to mass-conserving dynamics (unbalanced settings remain open) and the need for a reference path measure on real data (here proxied by SBIRR), which introduces a circularity concern. The practical adoption of FKL will depend on whether the community accepts the additional computational cost of training two neural-operator velocity fields over the simplicity of marginal distances.
