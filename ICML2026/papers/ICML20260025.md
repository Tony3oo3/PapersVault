## Problem

Dynamic Sparse Training (DST) methods converge significantly slower than dense training, often requiring up to five times more epochs to reach comparable accuracy, yet the role of normalization layers in this slowdown had never been studied. This paper identifies Batch Normalization as a previously unrecognized source of training instability in sparse networks: because BN rescales gradients by the inverse of each neuron's pre-activation standard deviation, heterogeneous fan-in in sparse layers induces non-uniform gradient scaling that rotates the descent direction at every mask update.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the standard backpropagated gradients (already distorted by BN's 1/σ_i rescaling) together with the binary sparsity mask that determines each neuron's active fan-in. It produces a corrected gradient vector in which each neuron's gradient component is multiplied by √(1−s_i) and the whole vector is normalized by √(1−s_avg) to preserve global magnitude. The correction is a diagonal preconditioner, so it adds negligible computational overhead and can be applied on top of any existing optimizer (SGD, HAM, etc.) without modifying the network architecture or the DST pruning/regrowth schedule.

## Method

The key theoretical result shows that for a neuron with sparsity s_i, BN amplifies its pre-activation gradient by a factor (1−s_i)^(−1/2) relative to the dense case, because the pre-activation variance shrinks to (1−s_i)·σ_i² while the BN backward pass divides by σ_i′. This neuron-dependent multiplicative distortion is non-uniform across a sparse layer, so it rotates the gradient direction rather than merely rescaling it; in DST, the mask update step changes {s_i} abruptly, causing a discontinuous jump in descent direction. SparseOpt corrects this with a sparsity-aware diagonal preconditioner D whose entries are √(1−s_i), yielding the update w^{t+1} = w^t − (η/√(1−s_avg))·D·∇L(w^t), which restores uniform gradient scaling across neurons while approximately preserving the global gradient norm. For dense layers (s_i = 0 ∀i) the preconditioner reduces to the identity, recovering standard SGD. The paper further proves via a two-neuron invariant analysis that the correction is orthogonal to the HAM optimizer's implicit-bias mechanism, and empirically demonstrates that using the original (uncorrected) gradients for RigL's regrowth criterion while using corrected gradients for weight updates yields the best mask exploration (ITOP rate) and final accuracy.

## Results

On ImageNet with ResNet50, SparseOpt consistently improves top-1 accuracy over the standard SGD baseline across all sparsity levels (90%, 95%, 97%) and training budgets (90, 180, 270 epochs) for both RigL and SET. The gains are most pronounced at higher sparsity and shorter schedules: at 97% sparsity with 90 epochs, RigL achieves 71.15% vs. 69.94% baseline (+1.21%), and SET achieves 71.43% vs. 70.12% (+1.31%). On CIFAR-100 with ResNet20, SparseOpt (with HAM) reaches 54.06% top-1 at 97% sparsity after 100 epochs, compared to 52.53% for the baseline (+1.53%). The accuracy gap narrows with longer training, confirming that SparseOpt primarily accelerates convergence rather than changing the final asymptotic accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against standard SGD with momentum (the default optimizer for DST). SparseOpt wins consistently across all sparsity levels, both DST methods (RigL and SET), and all training budgets on ImageNet; the largest margins appear at 97% sparsity with 90 epochs (e.g., +1.21% for RigL, +1.31% for SET). On CIFAR-100, the same pattern holds with even larger early-training gaps (e.g., +1.53% at 97% sparsity, 100 epochs for RigL). The paper also compares against the HAM optimizer (Jacobs et al., 2026), the current state-of-the-art sparse optimizer, and shows that SparseOpt is complementary: combining both (SparseOpt + HAM) yields further gains over either alone (e.g., 61.64% vs. 60.80% at 90% sparsity, 100 epochs on CIFAR-100 with RigL). The authors note that with sufficiently long training (270+ epochs on ImageNet), both methods converge to similar final accuracy, confirming that SparseOpt's benefit is primarily in convergence speed rather than a change in the optimization landscape's global optimum.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the preconditioned gradient descent framework (AdaGrad, RMSProp, Shampoo) to the specific context of Dynamic Sparse Training, building directly on DST methods like RigL (Evci et al., 2020) and SET (Mocanu et al., 2018) that periodically update sparse masks during training. While sparsity-aware initialization (Evci et al., 2022) already accounted for heterogeneous fan-in at initialization, no prior work analyzed how Batch Normalization's backward pass interacts with neuron-wise sparsity to distort gradient direction. The proposed SparseOpt is positioned as complementary to existing sparse optimizers like HAM (Jacobs et al., 2026), with a formal proof (Lemma A.3) showing the two mechanisms are orthogonal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DST has long been known to converge up to 5× slower than dense training, but the root cause was unclear; identifying BN as a source of gradient-direction instability provides a concrete, fixable explanation. The correction is a simple diagonal preconditioner with negligible overhead, making it easy to adopt in existing DST pipelines. Key open questions include whether the analysis extends to other normalization layers (preliminary results suggest Layer Normalization has a similar effect), transformer architectures, and LLM sparse training. The paper also highlights an unresolved tension: corrected gradients improve weight updates but original gradients are better for mask exploration in RigL, suggesting the interplay between optimization and topology search is not yet fully understood.
