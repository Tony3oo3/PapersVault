## Problem

Existing analyses of gradient descent's implicit bias characterize either norm minimization (from gradient flow at vanishing learning rates) or sharpness minimization (from the Edge of Stability at large learning rates) in isolation, yet neither bias alone explains the strong generalization of trained networks. The paper asks how these two forms of implicit regularization interact as the learning rate varies, and whether a single bias can account for good generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263236/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes standard image datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10) and a sweep of learning rates as input, training full-batch GD to a fixed loss threshold ε rather than a fixed number of epochs, with identical small-scale initializations across all learning rates. The output is a family of final model parameters indexed by η, from which the authors extract parameter norms (ℓ₁, ℓ₂, nuclear), worst-case loss sharpness (operator norm of the Hessian), and test error to characterize the phase transition and generalization landscape.

## Method

The paper combines systematic empirical experiments with a theoretical analysis of a simplified regression model. Empirically, they train FCNs, CNNs, ResNets, and Vision Transformers across multiple datasets, activations, and loss functions, identifying a data- and model-dependent critical learning rate η_c ≈ 2/s_GF at which a sharp phase transition occurs: below η_c both norm and sharpness remain nearly constant (flow-aligned regime), while above η_c sharpness decreases hyperbolically toward 2/η while parameter norm increases (EoS regime). Theoretically, they analyze a two-layer diagonal linear network with shared weights trained on a single data point with square loss, proving via Riemannian geometry on the interpolating manifold that the ℓ₁-norm minimizer and the sharpness minimizer are geometrically distinct sets (Proposition 3.2). They further show that neither minimizer achieves optimal generalization, and that the learning rate interpolates between the two biases, with best test error occurring at intermediate learning rates where norm and sharpness are balanced, yielding a U-shaped generalization curve.

## Results

The paper empirically demonstrates a sharp phase transition at a critical learning rate η_c ≈ 2/s_GF, below which both parameter norm and loss sharpness remain nearly constant (flow-aligned regime), and above which sharpness decreases hyperbolically (~2/η) while the ℓ₁-norm increases approximately linearly (EoS regime). In the toy diagonal linear network model, the authors prove that the ℓ₁-norm minimizer and the sharpness minimizer on the interpolating manifold are geometrically distinct, and neither achieves the minimum expected generalization error. U-shaped test error curves are observed in several settings (e.g., MNIST-5k with MSE loss), with optimal generalization occurring at intermediate learning rates where norm and sharpness biases are balanced, rather than at either extreme.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare a proposed method against external baselines; instead, it contrasts two implicit biases of gradient descent. The norm-minimization bias (induced by vanishing learning rate / gradient flow, per Chou et al. 2023) and the sharpness-minimization bias (induced by large learning rate / EoS, per Ahn et al. 2022) are shown to be in conflict: in the EoS regime, increasing η drives sharpness down while simultaneously driving parameter norm up. In the theoretical toy model, the risk of the ℓ₁-norm minimizer satisfies R(M_ℓ₁) < R(M_S_L), yet both are suboptimal compared to a carefully tuned intermediate learning rate that lands near the true risk minimizer. Empirically, minimal-norm solutions in the flow-aligned regime never achieve optimal test error, and in settings like MNIST-5k with MSE loss, excessively large learning rates (deep EoS) also degrade generalization, producing a U-shaped curve. The learning rate thus acts as a regularization hyperparameter that interpolates between the two biases, and best generalization requires balancing them rather than optimizing either in isolation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-studied lines of implicit bias research: the norm-minimization bias of gradient flow (Chou et al., 2023; Woodworth et al., 2020) and the sharpness-minimization bias of the Edge of Stability regime (Cohen et al., 2021; Ahn et al., 2022). Rather than analyzing either bias in isolation, the paper's central contribution is studying their interaction, showing that the learning rate acts as a continuous knob interpolating between the two regimes with a sharp phase transition at a critical learning rate η_c = 2/s_GF. The theoretical analysis builds on the Riemannian geometry of the solution manifold and the dynamic stability results of Ahn et al. (2022), while the empirical program extends the EoS experimental framework of Cohen et al. (2021) to systematically vary learning rate and track both norm and sharpness simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that neither the norm minimizer nor the sharpness minimizer achieves optimal generalization — with the best test error occurring at intermediate learning rates where both biases are balanced — challenges the prevailing narrative that a single implicit bias explains deep learning generalization. This reframes the learning rate as a regularization hyperparameter controlling generalization capacity, with practical implications for hyperparameter tuning. However, the theoretical analysis is restricted to a single-data-point diagonal linear network, and the U-shaped generalization curve is not universal across all experimental settings, leaving open whether the trade-off structure persists for more complex architectures, stochastic optimizers, and additional implicit biases (e.g., label noise, momentum).
