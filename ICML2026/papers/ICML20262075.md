## Problem

Common approaches to reporting LLM benchmark results—such as computing a simple average score with a standard error across all items and trials—can produce statistically invalid confidence intervals or conflate two distinct estimands (benchmark accuracy vs. generalized accuracy) without making the underlying assumptions explicit. The paper formalizes a statistical model of the evaluation setting and demonstrates that regression-free estimators, while assumption-light, fail to account for the hierarchical clustering of trials within items, leading to under-confident or over-confident uncertainty estimates depending on which accuracy notion is targeted.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a three-dimensional array of binary correctness outcomes indexed by LLM (m), item (n), and trial (t), as produced by running each LLM multiple times on each benchmark item. It outputs a suite of statistics: generalized accuracy estimated by Monte Carlo integration over the fitted item-difficulty distribution, benchmark accuracy as the mean of per-item success probabilities, per-LLM capability fixed effects (β), per-item difficulty random effects (γ), the random-effects variance (σ²) and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for variance decomposition, and effective samples per trial. All estimates come with standard errors and confidence intervals derived from the GLMM fit.

## Method

The paper specifies a generalized linear mixed model where each trial outcome Y_{i,j,k} is Bernoulli with success probability π_{i,j} = sigmoid(β_i − γ_j), with β_i as a fixed effect for LLM i's latent capability and γ_j as a random effect for item j's difficulty drawn from N(0, σ²). This is structurally identical to the one-parameter Item Response Theory (Rasch) model, but with LLMs treated as a fixed finite population rather than a random sample. Generalized accuracy is obtained by marginalizing over the item-difficulty distribution via Monte Carlo integration, while benchmark accuracy is the average of sigmoid(β̂_i − γ̂_j) across the n selected items. The key advantage over regression-free grand-mean estimators is partial pooling across LLMs: the GLMM borrows strength from the full LLM pool to stabilize item-difficulty estimates and produce narrower, more accurate confidence intervals, especially when the number of items is modest. The model also yields an automatic variance decomposition into inter-cluster variance (σ², reflecting spread in item difficulties) and intra-cluster variance (captured by ICC, reflecting LLM nondeterminism on the same item), which regression-free methods cannot provide without additional ad hoc calculations.

## Results

In simulated settings, well-specified GLMMs produce generalized accuracy estimates with RMSE comparable to regression-free methods (both varying inversely with number of items and trials), while achieving ~95% long-run coverage. GLMM confidence intervals are on average narrower than regression-free CIs, with the largest relative difference at smaller n (fewer than ~200 items); beyond that, the gap becomes small in both relative and absolute terms. On real BIG-Bench Hard data (22 LLMs, 5 trials/item), variance decomposition reveals substantial heterogeneity: Movie Recommendation has σ²=16.0, ICC=0.83, and only 0.23 effective samples per trial, whereas Web of Lies has σ²=0.47, ICC=0.12, and 0.67 effective samples per trial. On GPQA Diamond, Chemistry items (n=93) show a mean random effect of +0.59 versus Biology (−0.37) and Physics (−0.55), and a Date Understanding item with all 22 LLMs failing was flagged as ambiguously worded.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the regression-free grand-mean estimator with cluster-aware standard error proposed by Miller (2024), which computes generalized accuracy as the mean of per-item scores with SE = std(z̄_i)/√n. In well-specified simulations, GLMM and regression-free methods achieve nearly identical RMSE and both reach ~95% nominal coverage, so neither is clearly more accurate in point estimation. However, GLMM confidence intervals are consistently narrower on average, with the advantage most pronounced at small n (e.g., 40 items) and diminishing beyond ~200 items where both CIs become very tight. Under model misspecification, GLMM RMSE remains roughly comparable to regression-free, though long-run coverage drops into the 80–90% range, whereas regression-free methods maintain near-target coverage across all settings. On real GPQA Diamond data (198 items, 22 LLMs, 8 trials), the same pattern holds: GLMM CIs are narrower than regression-free CIs, and the paper notes that some LLM pairs (e.g., Llama 3 40B vs. Phi-4) appear significantly different under benchmark accuracy but not under generalized accuracy at α=0.01. The GLMM also provides statistics unavailable from the regression-free approach—item difficulty estimates, LLM capability scores, and variance decomposition (σ², ICC, effective samples per trial)—which the paper demonstrates are practically useful for diagnosing benchmark composition and LLM behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Rasch model (a one-parameter Item Response Theory model) as a generalized linear mixed model for LLM benchmark analysis, positioning it between regression-free estimators (Miller, 2024) and hierarchical Bayesian GLMs (Luettgau et al., 2025). It draws explicitly on generalizability theory (Cronbach et al., 1972) and Classical Test Theory to formalize the distinction between benchmark accuracy and generalized accuracy, while noting that multi-parameter IRT models (Polo et al., 2024; Song et al., 2025) offer more flexibility at the cost of a less favorable observations-to-parameters ratio in the LLM setting. The key departure from prior work is treating LLMs as fixed effects (a finite, non-randomly-selected population) rather than random draws, and providing a frequentist GLMM treatment rather than a Bayesian one.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides evaluators with a principled, implementable statistical framework that yields narrower confidence intervals, item difficulty estimates, and variance decompositions (ICC, σ²) that are not available from standard regression-free approaches. Practically, this means evaluators can diagnose whether a benchmark's variance is driven by item heterogeneity or LLM nondeterminism, and whether additional trials or additional items would be more informative. Open questions include the unidimensionality assumption (a single scalar capability per LLM), the independence of item selection, and the behavior of these methods on very small benchmarks (<50 items) where Bayesian approaches may be preferable. The paper also highlights that human-annotated difficulty labels correlate only weakly with LLM-estimated difficulty, raising questions about benchmark construction practices.
