## Problem

Observing Hebbian or anti-Hebbian structure in synaptic weight updates is classically taken as evidence for local, correlation-based plasticity mechanisms in the brain, and as indirect evidence against global error-driven optimization. This paper tackles the identifiability gap: whether such signatures uniquely imply an underlying Hebbian computation, or whether they can emerge as byproducts of regularized and noisy optimization dynamics that have nothing to do with Hebbian learning per se.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on a single layer's weight matrix W ∈ R^{n_b × n_a} and computes the Hebbian alignment as the Frobenius inner product (or cosine similarity) between the expected learning signal g(x,θ) and the population Hebbian update H̄(W) = E_x[h_b(x)h_a(x)^T], where h_b = Wh_a(x). The key controlled variables are the L2 weight decay strength γ and the injected noise variance σ², which together determine whether the alignment is positive (Hebbian) or negative (anti-Hebbian). The output is a scalar alignment metric tracked over training steps, plus a predicted phase boundary at γ ∝ σ² separating the two regimes.

## Method

The core theoretical result is a near-stationarity argument: when the full update ΔW = g(x,θ) − γW is small in expectation, the learning signal must satisfy E_x[g(x,θ)] ≈ γW, and its inner product with the Hebbian direction reduces to γ·E_x[‖h_b(x)‖²] > 0, guaranteeing positive Hebbian alignment that strengthens monotonically with γ. This derivation is rule-agnostic—it holds for SGD, Adam, DFA, or even a non-learning RandomNN construction—because the weight decay term always aligns with the anti-Hebbian direction, forcing the learning signal to compensate in the Hebbian direction. A complementary analysis on linear regression shows that additive Gaussian noise ε ~ N(0, σ²I) injected into weights produces a negative alignment term −σ²‖x‖⁴ that competes with the positive γ term, yielding a sharp phase boundary at γ ∝ σ². Empirically, the authors validate these predictions across CIFAR-10 classification (MLPs with varying depth, width, activation functions, and batch sizes) and student-teacher regression (MLPs and transformers), measuring alignment at convergence and throughout training, and confirming the quadratic phase boundary in a noise-vs-weight-decay sweep.

## Results

The headline metric is the population cosine similarity (Hebbian alignment) between the learning signal and the Hebbian update at convergence. Across all models and learning rules in Table 1, alignment increases monotonically with L2 weight decay γ: for the Regression MLP with SGD, alignment rises from −0.10 (γ=0) to 0.59 (γ=5×10⁻³); for the Transformer with Adam, it reaches 0.99 at γ=5×10⁻⁴. Even a non-learning RandomNN baseline shows alignment growing from 0.00 to 0.50 (MLP) and 0.09 (Transformer), confirming the effect is not tied to actual learning. In the noise–weight-decay tradeoff (Figure 4), the phase boundary between Hebbian and anti-Hebbian regimes follows the predicted scaling γ ∝ σ².

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two key baselines to demonstrate the universality and specificity of the emergent Hebbian signature. First, Direct Feedback Alignment (DFA; Nøkland, 2016) is tested as an alternative learning rule: in the Regression MLP, DFA achieves Hebbian alignment of 0.45 at γ=0 and 0.87 at γ=5×10⁻³, confirming that the alignment phenomenon is not specific to backpropagation/SGD but extends to biologically plausible local rules. In the Transformer, DFA shows a weaker but still positive trend (0.08→0.12), suggesting the effect is most pronounced in shallower architectures. Second, classical Hebbian learning (Oja's rule and normalized Hebbian; Oja, 1982) is trained on the same data and its updates are compared to the SGD gradient: at convergence, the alignment between Hebbian-rule updates and the supervised gradient approaches zero (Figure 18), demonstrating that the emergent Hebbian signature observed in regularized SGD is a consequence of the optimization dynamics and weight decay, not of explicit PCA-style feature extraction. Thus, the paper's central claim—that weight decay generically projects diverse learning signals into Hebbian-like directions—is supported by the fact that even a non-learning RandomNN baseline exhibits the same monotonic alignment trend, while genuine Hebbian learning does not reproduce the SGD dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the missing mechanistic explanation for a phenomenon first noted by Ziyin et al. (2025a), who observed that weight decay could cause alignment with the Hebbian rule but could not explain why. The analytical derivation builds on the equal-norm/concentration condition from Ziyin et al. (2025b) and extends the classical Hebbian learning literature (Oja, BCM, STDP) by showing that Hebbian signatures can emerge as a byproduct of regularized optimization rather than requiring explicit Hebbian computation. It also departs from the Xie & Seung (2003) line of work on gradient-Hebbian equivalence by showing the alignment is not a mathematical equivalence but a statistical, near-stationarity phenomenon that holds for arbitrary update rules, including non-learning constructions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a widely held identifiability assumption in computational neuroscience: that observing Hebbian or anti-Hebbian plasticity in synaptic updates uniquely implies an underlying Hebbian computation. The simple phase boundary prediction (γ ∝ σ²) provides a concrete, testable framework for distinguishing mechanistic from emergent Hebbian plasticity in biological experiments. Key limitations include testing only on small models (≤50M parameters), where the stationarity condition may not hold in larger networks, and the authors acknowledge that some layers exhibit anti-Hebbian alignment even at high weight decay without a clear theoretical explanation. Open questions include how to design neurophysiological experiments that separate the two routes, and whether the effect persists at the scale of modern large-scale models.
