## Problem

Existing theoretical analyses of in-context learning (ICL) have not fully exploited the connection between ICL and Bayesian inference, leaving two critical gaps: they fail to jointly couple the number of pretraining prompts N with the per-prompt context length p, and they do not accommodate heterogeneous mixtures of task types that characterize real LLM pretraining. This paper develops a finite-sample statistical theory that fills both gaps by providing a unified risk decomposition and non-asymptotic bounds under a meta-learning framework with task mixtures.

## Input / Output

The model receives a prompt P^k consisting of k exchangeable input-output pairs (x_i, y_i) with x_i ∈ R^{d_feat} and y_i ∈ R, followed by a query point x_{k+1} ∈ R^{d_feat}, and produces a single scalar regression prediction M(P^k) ∈ R. The context pairs are drawn i.i.d. from a task-specific distribution conditional on a task function f sampled from one of T finite regression families (e.g., linear or basis-function regression), with sub-Gaussian observation noise. The risk is averaged over all sequence lengths k = 1, …, p, reflecting the sequential nature of in-context prediction where the model must predict each next output as the context grows.

## Method

The core contribution is a model-agnostic risk identity (Proposition 3.1) that decomposes ICL risk into a model-dependent Bayes Gap and a model-independent Posterior Variance, then provides non-asymptotic bounds for each. For the Bayes Gap, the paper analyzes a uniform-attention (mean-pooling) Transformer whose feature encoder maps each example to the probability simplex Δ^{m-1} and whose decoder reads out a prediction from the mean-pooled features and query; the bound couples N and p via a rate of m/(pN) derived through sequential learning theory and optimal-transport-based approximation of the Bayes predictor. For the Posterior Variance, Theorem 3.3 shows that the posterior over the task index concentrates exponentially fast in k, so the irreducible error is upper-bounded by the minimax risk of the true task family plus a vanishing task-identification term. Theorem 3.4 further establishes that under input-distribution shift, only the Bayes Gap incurs an OOD penalty proportional to the prompt-level Wasserstein distance, while the Posterior Variance remains intrinsic to the target domain.

## Results

The key empirical validation is the power-law fit to pooled Bayes-gap values: the joint model a + b(pN)^(-β) + c/N achieves R² = 0.912, while N-only (R² = 0.030) and p-only (R² = 0.016) surrogates fit poorly, confirming the coupled pN dependence predicted by Theorem 3.2. With N = 1,280,000 pretraining prompts and p = 15, the Bayes Gap drops below 10⁻², and the Transformer's inference-time MSE closely tracks the Bayes (mixture) predictor. The gap between the Transformer and the Bayes (oracle) predictor (which knows the true task family) essentially vanishes after only k ≈ 3–4 in-context examples, validating the exponential task-type identification rate in Theorem 3.3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper, so comparisons are against Bayes-optimal reference predictors rather than competing ICL methods. The Transformer is compared against two baselines: Bayes (mixture), the optimal predictor under the mixture prior over task families, and Bayes (oracle), which assumes the true task family is known. With sufficient pretraining, the Transformer tracks Bayes (mixture) almost exactly (Bayes Gap < 10⁻²), and its error curve rapidly converges to Bayes (oracle) after ~3–4 in-context examples, demonstrating that task-type identification error vanishes quickly. In the harder three-family experiment (Table 2), the same qualitative pattern persists: at σ = 0.1, wrong-family mass drops from 0.067 at k=3 to 0.0010 at k=5, while at σ = 0.5 it only drops from 0.093 to 0.0054, showing that higher noise slows identification but the Transformer still approaches the oracle. The joint pN surrogate (R² = 0.912) dramatically outperforms the N-only (R² = 0.030) and p-only (R² = 0.016) alternatives, confirming that both pretraining breadth (N) and per-prompt context length (p) are jointly necessary for the Bayes Gap to shrink.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ICL-as-Bayesian-inference line (Xie et al., 2022; Panwar et al., 2024; Arora et al., 2025) from empirical and information-theoretic observations to a concrete finite-sample statistical theory, and generalizes the nonparametric ICL bounds of Kim et al. (2024a) from single task families to heterogeneous task mixtures. It departs from prior algorithm-specific analyses (Akyurek et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2023; von Oswald et al., 2023) by not assuming the Transformer implements any particular procedure (ridge regression, gradient descent), instead showing that pretraining drives the model toward the Bayes-optimal in-context predictor. Technically, it combines sequential learning theory (Rakhlin et al., 2010) for the within-prompt dimension with optimal transport (Peyre & Cuturi, 2019) for the approximation argument, yielding a coupled p-N rate that earlier nonasymptotic ICL theories had not captured.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean Bayes Gap + Posterior Variance decomposition gives practitioners a principled way to separate what more pretraining (N, p) can fix from what only additional in-context examples can reduce, and the exponential task-posterior concentration (Theorem 3.3) explains why roughly three examples suffice for task identification in mixtures. The OOD result (Theorem 3.4) that only the Bayes Gap degrades under input-distribution shift, quantified via Wasserstein distance, offers a targeted mitigation strategy (control Lipschitz constants, increase p and N) rather than a blanket retraining prescription. The main open question is extending the Bayes Gap bound from uniform-attention to standard softmax attention, where prompt-dependent attention weights make the hypothesis class data-dependent; the authors sketch a route via sequential covering numbers but leave it unresolved.
