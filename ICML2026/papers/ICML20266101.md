## Problem

Modern language models are trained on compressed views (e.g., BPE tokens) produced by a fixed external compressor, which hard-wires that compressor into the model's interface and introduces well-documented artifacts such as prompt boundary failures, retokenization drift, and under-trained tokens. The paper asks whether the efficiency benefits of compressed training can be retained while decoupling the model from any specific compressor at inference time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266101/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the model consumes and produces raw UTF-8 byte sequences exclusively, with no tokenizer or compressor in the loop. During training, each sample is presented either as raw bytes or as a compressed symbol sequence (from a BPE tokenizer, a neural arithmetic coder, or gzip) with probability r=0.9 for the compressed form; both formats are wrapped in sentinel tokens (⟨comp⟩/⟨/comp⟩, ⟨raw⟩/⟨/raw⟩) and packed into fixed-length contexts over a shared vocabulary that includes 256 raw byte values plus compressed symbols. The compressed representations are discarded entirely after training, so the deployed model has a universal byte-level interface.

## Method

Proxy compression is a mixed-representation training scheme in which a single autoregressive transformer (EvaByte architecture) is jointly trained on raw byte sequences and compressed views produced by an external proxy compressor, using standard next-symbol prediction with no architectural modifications. A Bernoulli mixing schedule (r=0.9) determines per-sample format, and an optional in-context translation pairing phase during warmup concatenates both views of the same sample to explicitly encourage cross-representation alignment. The paper instantiates three proxy compressor families: tokenizer-based (OpenCoder BPE, ~2.9× effective compression), neural (a 40M byte-level LM driving arithmetic coding with entropy-based segmentation for parallelism, ~2.6×), and generic gzip (~2.5×). The central empirical finding is strong cross-representation transfer: models seeing only 10% raw bytes during training substantially outperform pure byte-level baselines under fixed compute, and at scale (7B–14B) match or surpass tokenizer-based models while retaining byte-level robustness. Structured compressors (tokenizer and neural) transfer effectively, whereas gzip's unstable, semantically unstructured outputs fail to provide useful training signal.

## Results

On HumanEval-Plus at 14B parameters, Proxy(Tokenizer) achieves 30.5% pass@1 and Proxy(Neural) achieves 29.9%, both surpassing the tokenizer-based baseline (29.3%) and substantially outperforming the byte-level baseline (24.4%). On MBPP-Plus at 14B, Proxy(Tokenizer) reaches 49.5% and Proxy(Neural) reaches 49.2%, again exceeding the tokenizer baseline (48.1%) and byte-level baseline (42.1%). The advantage of proxy compression over byte-level baselines grows with model scale: at 0.5B the gap is small or negative, but by 14B proxy models lead by 5–7 points on HumanEval-Plus. On robustness (ReCode, 7B), Proxy(Neural) attains the highest robust pass@1 (19.8) while matching the tokenizer baseline on standard pass@1 (30.5 vs. 32.9), compared to 14.9 for the tokenizer baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the tokenizer-based baseline (OpenCoder BPE, 96,640 vocab), proxy compression models trail at small scales (0.5B–4B) but close and reverse the gap at 7B–14B: at 14B, Proxy(Tokenizer) leads by 1.2 points on HumanEval-Plus (30.5 vs. 29.3) and 1.4 points on MBPP-Plus (49.5 vs. 48.1), while Proxy(Neural) leads by 0.6 and 1.1 points respectively. Against the pure byte-level baseline, proxy models consistently outperform at every scale above 1.5B, with the gap widening from ~2 points at 1.5B to ~6 points at 14B on HumanEval-Plus (30.5 vs. 24.4), demonstrating that 90% compressed training data transfers effectively to raw-byte inference. On robustness (ReCode, 7B), Proxy(Tokenizer) matches the tokenizer baseline on standard pass@1 (32.9) while improving robust pass@1 from 14.9 to 19.1, and Proxy(Neural) achieves the highest robust pass@1 (19.8) at a modest 2.4-point drop in standard pass@1 (30.5 vs. 32.9). Under longer training (320B symbols, Table 2), the 7B Proxy(Neural) model exactly matches the tokenizer baseline (21.3/36.0) while Proxy(Tokenizer) leads on HumanEval-Plus (22.0 vs. 21.3), confirming the trend persists beyond the 100B-symbol budget.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of byte-level language modeling (ByT5, EvaByte) and tokenizer-based training (OpenCoder, standard BPE pipelines), introducing a mixed-representation training scheme that treats external compressors as a training-time proxy rather than a permanent interface. The neural proxy compressor directly builds on Lester et al. (2024)'s arithmetic coding with equal-information windows, while the conceptual framing draws on the 'language modeling is compression' perspective (Deletang et al., 2024). The key departure from prior work is that the compressor is discarded at inference: unlike tokenizer-based models that are permanently coupled to a fixed vocabulary, or byte-level models that forgo compression entirely, proxy compression uses compression purely as a training-time efficiency mechanism while retaining a universal raw-byte interface.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Proxy compression offers a practical path to decoupling training efficiency from inference interface, potentially allowing practitioners to gain ~3× training data throughput while retaining the robustness advantages of byte-level models (e.g., resistance to prompt-boundary artifacts and formatting perturbations). The finding that transfer quality improves with model scale—small models show weak or negative transfer while 14B models match or surpass tokenizer baselines—suggests the approach becomes more valuable precisely at the scales where training cost is most prohibitive. Key open questions include whether the technique generalizes beyond code to multilingual natural language, how to characterize the trade-off between compression aggressiveness and transfer fidelity, and whether proxy compression can be integrated into architecture design rather than remaining a data-preprocessing step. The gzip failure also raises the broader question of what structural properties a representation must have to serve as an effective training proxy.
