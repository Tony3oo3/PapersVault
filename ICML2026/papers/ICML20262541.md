## Problem

Existing alignment methods for tool-integrated LLM agents optimize primarily for task accuracy while neglecting process-level objectives such as tool-use efficiency. Fixed-weight scalarization is fundamentally limited: static weights misallocate learning effort as objectives saturate at different rates, and linear scalarization can only recover Pareto-optimal solutions on convex portions of the trade-off curve. The paper addresses how to jointly optimize multiple conflicting objectives (accuracy and efficiency) in online RL for LLM agents without relying on static reward compositions.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a text query (a math problem or open-domain question) and interacts with an external tool—either a Python interpreter for mathematical reasoning or a dense-passage retrieval system (E5 over 2018 Wikipedia) for multi-hop QA. At episode completion, the agent produces a final answer and a sequence of tool calls, and is scored on a vector reward (r_task, r_tool) where r_tool = exp(−α|N_call − N_optimal|) and N_optimal is dynamically tracked as the minimum tool count among successful trajectories in the current batch, making the efficiency target non-stationary but monotonically non-increasing over training.

## Method

ParetoPO is a two-stage multi-objective RL framework built on GRPO. Stage 1 (Hypervolume-Guided Dynamic Scalarization) maintains a Pareto archive of non-dominated outcome vectors and computes a smoothed hypervolume gain ΔHV̄_t at each step; this gain modulates a meta-level reward r_pareto = 0.5 + 1.5·tanh(ΔHV̄_t) that multiplicatively rescales the scalarized reward, creating a time-varying effective objective that implicitly reweights trade-offs and encourages exploration of under-represented Pareto regions. Stage 2 (Pareto Ranking Policy Optimization) replaces scalarized advantages with dominance-aware ones: trajectories within each rollout group are sorted via non-dominated sorting into Pareto ranks, and each trajectory's advantage is set to A_base,ρ = N_rank − ρ + 1 plus a within-rank refinement β·(r̂_w − 0.5) that injects user preference without violating rank ordering. Theoretically, the paper proves Stage 1 asymptotically covers the supported convex hull of achievable returns (Proposition 3.1) and Stage 2 converges to Pareto-ascent stationary points where no common first-order improvement direction exists across all objectives (Theorem 3.6), with bounded second-moment guarantees on the rank-based gradient estimator.

## Results

On mathematical reasoning (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B), ParetoPO achieves 80.0% EM with only 0.9 average tool calls on MATH500, 30.0% EM / 0.8 calls on AIME2024, and 70.0% EM / 0.8 calls on AMC23 — simultaneously the highest accuracy and lowest tool usage across all five benchmarks. On multi-hop QA (Qwen2.5-3B Base), ParetoPO reaches 48.0% EM with 0.9 search calls on Natural Questions and 42.2% EM with 1.2 calls on HotpotQA. The 7B variants show consistent gains: 84.6% EM / 1.2 calls on MATH500 and 50.1% EM / 0.9 calls on NQ, all statistically significant (p < 0.05) over the best baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ParetoPO dominates all five baselines on both accuracy and tool efficiency simultaneously, a pattern the authors attribute to baselines not lying on a strong Pareto frontier. ToRL-GRPO (Li et al., 2025) maximizes task accuracy but uses 2.1–2.7 tool calls on math benchmarks, whereas ParetoPO achieves higher EM (e.g., 80.0 vs. 77.8 on MATH500) with fewer calls (0.9). OTC-GRPO (Wang et al., 2025) reduces tool usage to ~1.1–1.3 calls but sacrifices 4–6 EM points relative to ParetoPO, indicating its fixed penalty over-corrects. MO-GRPO (Ichihara et al., 2025) shows inconsistent trade-offs, trailing ParetoPO by 8.8 EM on MATH500 while using 2.0 calls. On multi-hop QA, Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025) achieves 40.4% EM with 1.4 search calls on NQ, while ParetoPO reaches 48.0% EM with 0.9 calls — a 7.6-point accuracy gain at 36% fewer tool invocations. The Qwen2.5-Math-Ins-TIR baseline (no RL) is outperformed by 6.2 EM on MATH500 with fewer tool calls (0.9 vs. 1.3), confirming that the multi-objective RL training itself drives the gains beyond mere tool access.

## Relation to Prior Work

ParetoPO extends the multi-objective RL literature (Lu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a) by replacing fixed-weight scalarization with a two-stage framework: hypervolume-guided dynamic scalarization for global exploration, followed by Pareto-ranking-based advantage computation for local refinement. It builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as the base policy optimizer and on the meta-level hypervolume reward idea from Lu et al. (2025), but adds smoothing and a dominance-aware ranking mechanism that prior scalarization-only methods lack. The paper departs from gradient-based multi-objective approaches (Li et al., 2025a; He & Maghsudi, 2025) by avoiding per-objective gradient computation, instead operating on discrete Pareto ranks within small rollout groups, which is more tractable for LLM-scale training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in tool-using LLM agents: most alignment methods optimize accuracy alone, leaving tool-call efficiency uncontrolled, which directly impacts inference cost and reliability. The theoretical guarantees (supported-hull coverage in Stage 1, Pareto-ascent stationarity in Stage 2, bounded second moments) provide a principled foundation that goes beyond heuristic reward mixing. Limitations include evaluation on only two objectives (accuracy and tool efficiency), small model scales (1.5B–7B), and a local approximation of optimal tool-call count (N_optimal) that may not generalize to tasks with highly variable optimal call counts. Open questions include whether the two-stage design extends to three or more conflicting objectives, whether it scales to larger models, and whether the Pareto-ranking mechanism remains effective when the number of nondominated trajectories grows with batch size.
