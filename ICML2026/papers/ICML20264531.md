## Problem

Disentangling high-dimensional transformer embeddings into monosemantic features requires solving the NP-hard sparse encoding subproblem, yet the dominant approach—sparse autoencoders (SAEs)—uses a simple linear encoder whose solution quality relative to the global optimum is unclear. It remains unknown whether more principled alternating-optimization (AO) methods, such as KSVD, could find better dictionaries, and whether such methods can even be scaled to the millions-of-samples, thousands-of-dimensions regime required for modern transformer models.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a data matrix Y ∈ R^{d×n} of transformer or vision-model activations (e.g., 2.6M samples of d=2304 Gemma-2-2B layer-12 embeddings, or 1.3M d=384/768 DINOv2 CLS tokens) and produces an overcomplete dictionary D ∈ R^{d×m} (m=4096 or 16384, with unit-norm columns) plus a sparse coefficient matrix X ∈ R^{m×n} with at most k nonzeros per column (k ∈ {16,20,32,40,64,80}). Unlike SAEs, DB-KSVD produces bidirectional (signed) sparse codes, with roughly 50% negative coefficients, and the Matryoshka variant imposes a hierarchical grouping of dictionary elements into exponentially-sized subgroups that are fit sequentially to residuals.

## Method

DB-KSVD adapts the classic KSVD alternating-optimization loop (sparse encoding via Matching Pursuit, then dictionary update via SVD of per-element error matrices) with three scaling modifications. First, Parallel Matching Pursuit precomputes D^T D and D^T Y so that each of the k greedy iterations per sample reduces to an argmax and a vector addition, eliminating all matrix-vector products during encoding. Second, Inner Batching partitions the m sequential dictionary updates into shuffled batches of size w (matching available CPU workers), where each worker updates a local copy and results are synchronized via all-to-all; the per-element SVD is replaced by a Lanczos iteration on the d×d Gram matrix E_Ωj E_Ωj^T, removing the dependence on n from the eigenvalue step. Third, Outer Batching mini-batches the data samples (analogous to SGD) to handle out-of-core problem sizes, with batch sizes one to two orders of magnitude larger than d. A Matryoshka structuring extension partitions dictionary elements into exponentially-sized groups and fits each group to the residual of the previous group, encouraging incoherence and improving autointerpretability and absorption metrics.

## Results

On the SAEBench benchmark (Gemma-2-2B, m=4096), DB-KSVD outperforms the Standard ReLU SAE in all six metrics except autointerpretability and one instance of sparse probing, and is generally competitive with MatryoshkaBatchTopK SAE. On DINOv2-B (k=32), DB-KSVD achieves 0.817 variance explained vs. 0.794 for SAE, 0.802 autointerpretability vs. 0.797, but 0.893 sparse probing vs. 0.932 for SAE. The Matryoshka structuring variant improves autointerpretability and absorption at a slight cost to loss recovered and spurious correlation removal. DB-KSVD runs a single iteration in 5.5 s (CPU) vs. an estimated 65,000 s for the baseline KSVD implementation, a ~10,000× speedup.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On SAEBench (Gemma-2-2B, m=4096), DB-KSVD beats the Standard ReLU SAE (Cunningham et al., 2023) on all metrics except autointerpretability and one sparse probing instance, and is broadly competitive with the MatryoshkaBatchTopK SAE (Bussmann et al., 2025) except on autointerpretability. On DINOv2, DB-KSVD consistently outperforms TopK-SAE (Gao et al., 2024) on variance explained (e.g., 0.875 vs. 0.812 for DINOv2-S at k=32) and autointerpretability (0.817 vs. 0.723), with the gap widening for the smaller DINOv2-S model, suggesting SAEs struggle more at lower dimensions. However, SAE consistently wins on sparse probing (e.g., 0.932 vs. 0.893 for DINOv2-B at k=32), with the gap shrinking as k_sp increases from 1 to 5. The Matryoshka structuring adaptation improves autointerpretability and absorption for both DB-KSVD and SAE baselines, hinting at a shared underlying phenomenon related to dictionary coherence. Overall, the matching performance of two fundamentally different optimization approaches (alternating optimization vs. gradient descent) suggests both are near the practical limits of the dictionary learning problem at these scales.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the classic KSVD alternating optimization algorithm (Aharon et al., 2006) to the problem of disentangling transformer embeddings, a task previously dominated by sparse autoencoders trained via gradient descent. It incorporates the Matryoshka hierarchical encoding structure from recent SAE work (Bussmann et al., 2025) as an inductive bias to encourage dictionary incoherence. The key conceptual departure is replacing the SAE's simple linear encoder with a proper (albeit approximate) sparse coding step via Matching Pursuit, and replacing gradient-based dictionary updates with SVD-based closed-form updates, while introducing batching and parallelization to scale to millions of samples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a fundamentally different optimization paradigm (alternating minimization with closed-form block updates) matches SAE performance across six SAEBench metrics and both text and vision modalities provides strong evidence that SAEs are not exploiting artifacts of gradient descent but genuinely finding strong solutions to the dictionary learning problem. This suggests the field may be approaching practical limits of the sparse linear superposition model at current problem sizes, and that new representational assumptions may be needed for further progress. Open questions include whether the superposition hypothesis fully captures transformer internals, whether bidirectional (non-nonnegative) codes are equally interpretable, and whether the quadratic sample complexity implied by information-theoretic bounds is truly binding in practice.
