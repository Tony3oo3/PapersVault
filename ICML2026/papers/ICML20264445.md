## Problem

Existing long-form knowledge editing methods like AnyEdit rely on fixed-size sliding windows to segment text, which blindly cuts through semantic dependencies (e.g., splitting a function definition or separating a mathematical condition from its conclusion). This structural misalignment causes high inter-segment key correlation, leading to destructive crosstalk during the mass-editing solver and degrading editing quality as knowledge complexity increases.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264445/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a question and a long-form answer text as input, along with a pretrained LLM (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Llama-2-7B, or Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct). It computes token-level surprisal over the answer conditioned on the question, selects the top-k local peaks (where k = ceil(L/40)) as segmentation boundaries, and produces an updated W_out weight matrix via the MEMIT closed-form least-squares update. The editing is applied at a single target layer, with perturbations optimized per-segment and aggregated in one batch update.

## Method

AnyEdit++ replaces AnyEdit's fixed-window chunking with Bayes-Chunk, an adaptive segmentation that identifies boundaries at local maxima of token-level information surprisal (−log P(y_t | y_<t)), which empirically align with natural semantic transitions where the model's hidden state undergoes significant reorganization. Given these boundaries, the method anchors each segment's edit at the token immediately preceding the high-surprisal boundary, then runs the standard AnyEdit autoregressive pipeline: per-segment perturbation optimization (maximizing segment generation likelihood) followed by a single MEMIT batch weight update. Two theoretical results underpin the design: (1) a crosstalk bound showing that interference is proportional to pairwise cosine similarity of anchor keys, which surprisal-based boundaries minimize by enforcing structural independence; and (2) a causal locality principle proving that the immediate predecessor state provides strictly superior control gain over distant history, because vertical residual-stream propagation acts as a quasi-isometry while horizontal attention propagation introduces a damping bottleneck.

## Results

On the EditEverything benchmark (7 domains), AnyEdit++ achieves the highest average BLEU and BERT Score across all three LLMs: 75.00/94.50 on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, 50.13/87.66 on Llama-2-7B, and 85.33/96.29 on Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct. The largest gains over AnyEdit appear in logically structured domains: Code BLEU improves by ~20% on Llama-2-7B (70.50 vs. 50.78) and by 5–8% on the other two models. On the traditional UnKE and CounterFact benchmarks, AnyEdit++ reaches 86.13 BLEU / 97.16 BERT Score, outperforming AnyEdit (82.65/96.87) by ~3.5 BLEU points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AnyEdit++ consistently outperforms all four baselines across every model and dataset. Against MEMIT and AlphaEdit (single-point locate-and-edit methods), the gains are dramatic: AnyEdit++ exceeds MEMIT by 32+ BLEU points on Llama-3.1-8B and by 23+ points on Llama-2-7B, confirming that single-token editing is fundamentally insufficient for long-form knowledge. Against AnyEdit (the direct predecessor using fixed-window chunking), AnyEdit++ wins on every metric: +2.36 BLEU on Llama-3.1-8B, +7.83 BLEU on Llama-2-7B, and +3.52 BLEU on Qwen-2.5-7B, with the largest relative gains in Code (~20% on Llama-2-7B) and Math, indicating that surprisal-guided boundaries are most beneficial for logically dense content. On the QwQ-Edit dataset (ultra-long CoT math), AnyEdit++ maintains a consistent BLEU and BERT Score advantage over AnyEdit across all length and logical-density bins. Finally, when Bayes-Chunk is plugged into the fine-tuning-based FT-UKE method (Xiong et al., 2025), it further improves both BLEU and BERT Score, demonstrating that the segmentation strategy generalizes beyond the locate-and-edit paradigm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends AnyEdit (Jiang et al., 2025), which introduced an autoregressive divide-and-conquer paradigm for editing arbitrarily long knowledge by sequentially optimizing perturbations at segment boundaries. The key departure is replacing AnyEdit's fixed-size sliding window with Bayes-Chunk, a surprisal-guided adaptive segmentation that aligns boundaries with peaks in the model's information content. The underlying editing mechanics (vector optimization, MEMIT-style weight updates) are inherited unchanged from the ROME/MEMIT lineage, and the paper adds a theoretical layer—proving crosstalk bounds via anchor-key orthogonality and a causal locality principle for gradient propagation—that prior long-form editing work lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature of Bayes-Chunk (demonstrated by improving FT-UKE without architectural changes) makes it a practical drop-in for any long-form editing pipeline, and the theoretical grounding (structural independence, causal locality) gives practitioners principled criteria for choosing segmentation strategies. However, the empirical gains over AnyEdit are modest (1–3% BLEU, 0.3–1.3% BERT Score), and the method's advantage is most pronounced on highly structured, logically dense content (math, code), leaving open whether surprisal-based boundaries generalize equally well to more diffuse or creative knowledge types. Scalability to very long documents (hundreds of segments) and the computational cost of computing full surprisal curves remain practical concerns the paper does not fully address.
