## Problem

ML fairness research has largely operationalized unfairness as discrimination along individual sensitive attributes (race, sex, age), treating contextual environments as noise to be normalized rather than signal to be audited. This framing fails to capture structural injustice instantiated through social determinants—context-level variables like neighborhood deprivation, school funding, and healthcare access—that shape outcomes independently of demographic group membership and can produce different levels of disadvantage within the same demographic group. The paper argues that sensitive-attribute-centered mitigation strategies can themselves introduce new forms of structural injustice, and that auditing structural injustice must precede mitigation.

## Input / Output

The analyses consume tabular data at two distinct granularities: individual-level records (sensitive attributes, age, income, screening dates) and context-level aggregates (Area Deprivation Index scores per PUMA, neighborhood resource indices, institutional policy exposure) that are shared across all individuals in the same geographic or institutional unit. Outputs are not single fairness scores but multi-layered audit results: disparity measures computed conditional on social-determinant strata (e.g., income distributions for the same demographic group across ADI levels), theoretical characterizations of when sensitive-attribute-centered mitigation generates new injustice (via stochastic dominance conditions on CDFs), and semi-synthetic policy simulations quantifying early-detection gains from structural improvements. The breast cancer analysis links ~54,000 mammography records from ~45,000 patients at OSF HealthCare with SEER age-specific incidence rates for the simulation component.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a formal framework for quantifying structural injustice via social determinants, operationalized through a three-criteria definition (context-level definition, social-structural content, exogenous stratification) that cleanly separates social determinants from sensitive attributes and their proxies. Theoretically, it constructs a stylized college admissions model with region-specific demographic compositions and stochastic dominance of academic preparedness distributions, proving (Theorem 4.5) that quota-based affirmative action imposes stricter score thresholds on non-URM applicants from disadvantaged regions than on URM applicants from advantaged regions, and that this unintended harm paradoxically decreases as structural injustice worsens but increases as conditions improve. Empirically, it links U.S. Census PUMS microdata with the updated Area Deprivation Index to show that identical demographic groups (e.g., African American women, White women) exhibit substantial outcome heterogeneity across ADI strata, and runs a semi-synthetic breast cancer screening simulation (100,000 particles per region, 10,000 screening slots, 500 runs) demonstrating that adopting rich-region screening patterns in the poor region yields non-trivial gains in early detections (e.g., 1,367±33 to 1,461±36 per 10,000 screenings). The paper concludes with three actionable pillars: privacy-aware data governance for social determinant variables, dynamic context-adaptable fairness metrics (e.g., Social Determinant Parity), and multi-layer causal models that explicitly encode bidirectional individual-community influences.

## Results

On U.S. Census PUMS data, median annual income for African American women drops from $38,000 in low-ADI PUMAs to $23,800 in mid-ADI and $18,800 in high-ADI PUMAs, demonstrating that social determinants (area deprivation) shift outcome distributions even within a fixed intersectional demographic group. In the OSF HealthCare breast cancer screening analysis (~54,000 records, ~45,000 patients), age at first-ever screening for White women differs by over 3 years in the mean and nearly 5 years in the median between rich (ADI 0–25) and poor (ADI 75–100) regions under a uniform screening guideline. A semi-synthetic simulation (100,000 particles per region, SEER incidence rates) shows that adopting the rich-region screening pattern in the poor region increases early detections from 1,367 ± 33 to 1,461 ± 36 per 10,000 screenings allocated to the poor region. Theorem 4.5 establishes a counterintuitive paradox: quota-based (sensitive-attribute-centered) mitigation is *less* likely to disadvantage non-URM applicants from poor regions when structural injustice is more severe, but *more* likely to generate new structural injustice as regional conditions improve.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper and does not benchmark a proposed method against competing baselines in the standard sense. Instead, it argues that three existing technical paradigms are structurally inadequate for capturing structural injustice: (i) standard benchmarks and causal fairness graphs that drop context-level variables (e.g., address, neighborhood deprivation), (ii) sensitive-attribute-centered fairness metrics (including intersectional variants) that cannot capture shifting contextual influences, and (iii) individual-level causal models that cannot represent community-level aggregate variables or their bidirectional influence on individuals. The empirical evidence is used to demonstrate the *limitations* of these paradigms rather than to outperform them: the census analysis shows that fixing race and sex still leaves large income gaps explained by ADI, and the breast cancer screening data shows that a uniform (attribute-blind) guideline still produces multi-year disparities in screening age across regions. The theoretical result (Theorem 4.5) further shows that the dominant mitigation strategy in the literature (quota-based affirmative action) can introduce new structural injustice, particularly as conditions improve, which no sensitive-attribute-only metric would flag.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper extends the algorithmic fairness literature by arguing that the field's dominant paradigm—auditing discrimination along sensitive attributes (race, sex, age)—is fundamentally inadequate for capturing structural injustice instantiated through context-level social determinants. It builds on cross-disciplinary measurement tools (the Area Deprivation Index from Kind et al., 2014; the Child Opportunity Index; the Index of Concentration at the Extremes) and on causal fairness frameworks (Kusner et al., 2017; Kilbertus et al., 2017; Nabi & Shpitser, 2018; Chiappa, 2019), but departs from them by insisting that community-level variables, individual-level causal graphs, and sensitive-attribute-centered metrics cannot represent the bidirectional, aggregate structure of social determinants. The paper also draws on political philosophy (Young, 1990; Powers & Faden, 2019) and the social drivers of health literature to ground its formal Definition 2.2, which cleanly separates social determinants from sensitive attributes and their proxies via three criteria (context-level definition, social-structural content, exogenous stratification).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central practical claim—that auditing structural injustice through social determinants must precede mitigation, and that sensitive-attribute-centered interventions can introduce new forms of structural injustice—reframes the fairness research agenda from 'remove bias' to 'identify and intervene on structural levers.' The three proposed pillars (privacy-aware data governance, dynamic context-adaptable metrics like Social Determinant Parity, and multi-layer causal models) are concrete but remain largely aspirational; no existing benchmark, library, or causal framework yet operationalizes them. Key open questions include how to handle the no-interference violation inherent in community-level interventions, how to design longitudinal fairness metrics that track shifting structural conditions, and how to prevent social-determinant variables from being weaponized as proxy-laundering mechanisms. The empirical analyses (census PUMS, OSF HealthCare breast cancer screening) are descriptive and supportive but do not yet demonstrate end-to-end mitigation via social determinants.
