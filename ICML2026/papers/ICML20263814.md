## Problem

Multiple-choice question answering (MCQA) is the dominant format for benchmarking LLM reasoning, but it exposes models to answer options that can be exploited to inflate apparent reasoning ability. The paper asks: how much of an LLM's MCQA performance reflects genuine problem-solving versus exploitation of the option set, and does this differ systematically between reasoning and non-reasoning models?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263814/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline feeds LLMs question stems, multiple-choice options, or both, under controlled timing of option revelation (before, after, or never shown relative to chain-of-thought generation). Outputs include per-configuration accuracy, a chance-corrected exploitation score (additive gap in percentage points), and—on a subset of math/code tasks—executable Python traces that are run on 200 counterfactual inputs to produce SHA-256 behavior hashes capturing the model's underlying input-output function rather than its surface-level option selection.

## Method

The paper constructs a design space of one-stage and two-stage evaluation configurations (MC-CoT, QMC-CoT, Q-CoT, Q-CoT-MC-1T, Q-CoT-MCNA-CoT) that systematically vary whether and when options are visible to the model, isolating MC-only exploitation from QMC-based exploitation. Exploitation is quantified as the excess accuracy over a chance-corrected free-text baseline, and a 'None-of-the-Above' (NOTA) intervention is used to disrupt elimination-based shortcuts. For diagnostic probing, models are prompted to emit Python code as their reasoning trace; this code is executed in a sandbox on 200 deterministically generated probe inputs, and the resulting output vectors are hashed to produce behavior signatures that reveal whether different models converge on the same underlying computational error. The study spans 27 LLMs (reasoning and non-reasoning) across 15 benchmarks, comparing MMLU to MMLU-Pro to test whether harder distractors reduce exploitability.

## Results

Across 27 LLMs and 15 benchmarks, models above ~50B parameters score 30–40 percentage points higher on QMC-CoT (options before reasoning) than Q-CoT (free-text), with reasoning models showing the largest gaps. On MC-only exploitation (options without the question), Qwen3-32B achieves 13 points above random guessing, and all reasoning models outperform the best non-reasoning model. Introducing a NOTA option reduces the reasoning-model advantage from 12.63 pp (MC-CoT) to 5.29 pp (MCNA-CoT), and Q-CoT-MCNA-CoT drops accuracy by 2–15 pp relative to QMC-CoT, confirming a second-pass option-exploitation shortcut. Behavior hashing reveals that models within the same family are 2.7× more likely to share an error pattern, yet ~94.7% of models share at least one incorrect behavior with another model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare proposed methods against baselines; instead it compares evaluation formats to isolate exploitation. The central comparison is QMC-CoT vs. Q-CoT: nearly every model (even after boosting Q-CoT with a random-guessing term Q-CoT+k) scores higher when options precede reasoning, confirming that the gain is not merely 'picking the closest option' (observed in only ~23% of reasoning-model cases). A second comparison, MC-CoT vs. MCNA-CoT, shows that NOTA collapses above-chance option-only performance toward random guessing for most datasets, though ARC, HellaSwag, and PIQA remain highly exploitable. A third comparison, QMC-CoT vs. Q-CoT-MCNA-CoT, demonstrates that removing the correct answer from the option set disrupts the second-pass shortcut, with smaller reasoning models suffering larger drops (up to 15 pp). Finally, MMLU vs. MMLU-Pro (4 vs. 10 options) shows that harder distractors do not reliably curb exploitation: reasoning models exploit MMLU-Pro more easily than non-reasoning models exploit MMLU, indicating that improved reasoning ability translates into improved test-exploitation ability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the growing body of work on MCQA exploitation (Balepur et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024; Turner & Kurzeja, 2025) by systematically varying both CoT timing and option design across 27 LLMs and 15 benchmarks, rather than proposing yet another fix. It builds on the NOTA intervention literature (Raman et al., 2024; 2025; Tam et al., 2025) and the Open-LLM-Leaderboard's MCQA-to-FTQA conversion (Myrzakhan et al., 2024), but reframes the question from 'how do we fix MCQA?' to 'what is MCQA actually measuring?' The executable CoT behavior-hashing diagnostic is a novel contribution that departs from prior MCQA-as-diagnostic work (Haladyna et al., 2002; Liu et al., 2025) by probing underlying computational errors rather than surface-level option selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since MCQA still dominates LLM leaderboards (66% of Llama 3.1 tasks, 3/4 in o1-preview's blog), the finding that reasoning models—designed to always emit CoT—cannot be evaluated in a 'decoupled' format means the most widely reported scores for the most important models may systematically overstate free-text reasoning ability by 30–40 percentage points. The near-zero correlation (r=0.090) between MCQA selection patterns and executable-reasoning error patterns undermines the common practice of inferring a model's misconception from which distractor it picks. Open questions include whether exploitation gaps will narrow as models improve, how to design evaluation protocols that are fair to reasoning models' architectural constraint of always reasoning over context, and whether behavior hashing generalizes beyond math and code tasks to broader reasoning domains.
