## Problem

Mixed-precision arithmetic is standard in large-scale machine learning but its impact on MCMC-based scientific computing remains largely unexplored. In neural quantum state (NQS) variational Monte Carlo, the Metropolis-Hastings sampling step dominates computational cost, yet no theoretical framework existed to quantify how reduced-precision evaluation of the wavefunction biases the sampled distribution or to determine when such bias is negligible. This paper derives general analytical bounds on the stationary-distribution bias induced by finite-precision MH MCMC and validates them empirically in the NQS/VMC setting.

## Input / Output

The method takes spin configurations (binary vectors of length N on a lattice), sparse Hamiltonian matrix elements, and neural network parameters θ as input. The neural network (RBM or ResCNN) outputs log ψ_θ(x) for each configuration, from which the sampling distribution p_θ(x) = |ψ_θ(x)|² is derived. The output is the ground-state energy estimate, optimized wavefunction parameters, and a quantified sampling speedup. A key structural detail is that the method maintains two model copies: a double-precision copy for gradient computation and stochastic reconfiguration updates, and a reduced-precision copy used exclusively for generating MCMC samples, with parameters downcast after each update step.

## Method

The core contribution is twofold: (i) a theoretical framework bounding the total-variation bias between the exact and perturbed stationary distributions of MH MCMC under additive log-density noise, and (ii) a practical mixed-precision VMC optimization scheme. Theoretically, the paper combines a Doeblin minorization condition (giving a contraction rate r) with a case analysis of the MH acceptance function to show that the stationary-distribution bias scales as O(σ²/(1−r)) for small Gaussian noise σ, where r encodes the chain's mixing speed. The key insight is that rapid mixing (r ≪ 1) makes the sampler robust to numerical perturbations, and the bound depends on system size only through σ and r. Practically, the mixed-precision scheme keeps all gradient and preconditioning computations in double precision while performing only the wavefunction evaluations during sampling in f16, bf16, or f32. Empirically, on the 2D transverse-field Ising model at criticality and the 1D Heisenberg model using RBM and ResCNN architectures, this yields up to 3.5× sampling speedup on an NVIDIA H100 GPU with no measurable degradation in the converged ground-state energy.

## Results

For the 2D TFIM at the critical point (L=10, N=100) optimized with a ResCNN, mixed-precision sampling yields total training speedups of 2.057× (f32), 2.296× (f16), and 2.257× (bf16) relative to double precision, while the relative energy error versus training step is nearly identical across all data types. For RBM sampling alone, speedups scale with both the number of samples and parameter density, reaching up to 3.5× on an NVIDIA H100 GPU. Throughout training, the Monte Carlo gradient noise dominates the low-precision error bound for f32 and f16, confirming that the optimization dynamics are unaffected; for bf16 this separation is not observed, yet the final accuracy remains comparable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against competing NQS architectures or alternative MCMC samplers; the sole baseline is the double-precision (f64) VMC reference. Against this reference, f32 and f16 sampling produce statistically indistinguishable energy trajectories and gradient estimates, with the derived TV-distance bound (Eq. 14) remaining below the Monte Carlo noise floor throughout training. bf16 sampling also yields comparable final energies, but the low-precision error bound is no longer strictly dominated by MC noise, indicating a narrower safety margin. In the noisy-RBM warmup experiment (Fig. 1b), the empirical relative error in expectation values tracks the theoretical KL/TV bounds closely for small σ, and the acceptance-rate analysis (Fig. 1c) confirms that the derived |Δα| bound is tight in the small-noise regime and correctly predicts the onset of ergodicity loss at large σ. No external method is benchmarked; the contribution is the theoretical framework plus the demonstration that existing NQS workflows can be accelerated without accuracy loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: (i) the theory of perturbed Markov chains, where prior results (Roberts et al. 1998; Johndrow et al. 2015; Hoffman 2020) have studied ergodicity robustness or bounded kernel-level perturbations abstractly, and (ii) NQS-based VMC (Carleo & Troyer 2017; Sorella 2005), where MCMC sampling is the dominant computational bottleneck. The key departure is that rather than bounding error via abstract kernel norms or focusing on pathological ergodicity loss, the authors derive explicit TV-distance bounds that separate the noise level σ from the chain's contraction rate r, yielding a practical criterion for when reduced-precision sampling is safe. In the NQS context specifically, Chen & Heyl (2024) mention lower-precision wavefunction evaluation but leave its impact unquantified; this paper provides the first systematic theoretical and empirical treatment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for the quantum simulation community: up to 3.5× sampling speedups on modern GPUs with no measurable degradation in ground-state accuracy, validated across multiple Hamiltonians (TFIM, Heisenberg), architectures (RBM, ResCNN), and phases. The theoretical framework (Theorem 3.3) generalizes beyond NQS to any MCMC-based inference where the target log-density is evaluated in reduced precision, including Bayesian neural networks and energy-based models. Key open questions include extending the analysis to non-local proposals (e.g., cluster updates), handling the preconditioning step in low precision (where S-matrix condition number amplifies rounding errors), and whether the Gaussian noise assumption holds for deeper architectures. The paper also highlights that bf16, despite its wider dynamic range, can be less reliable than f16 for sampling because its larger σ violates the MC-noise-dominance condition.
