## Problem

Existing multimodal manipulation detection methods are tightly coupled to specific manipulation types, requiring large-scale type-specific data and specialized model designs that fail to generalize to emerging forgery patterns. The paper identifies that the fundamental weakness of current MLLMs is not a lack of world knowledge but an inability to form clear conceptual boundaries in feature space, preventing them from explicitly detecting the intrinsic conflicts that define manipulated misinformation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264081/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a news image and its accompanying caption as a paired input. At inference, it is prompted with the simple instruction 'Is the news real or fake?' and produces a binary real/fake label followed by a structured conflict explanation of the form 'Fake. Because C1 from S1 conflicts with C2 from S2,' where each conflict factor names the specific contradictory concept and each conflict source is one of {Image, Caption, World Knowledge}. During training, the CAC corpus provides explicit supervision in the structured format <Image, Text, {C1, C2}, {S1, S2}>.

## Method

CORE is a three-stage training framework built on open-source MLLMs (Qwen2.5-VL-3B, Gemma3-4B) using LoRA. First, the Conflict Attribution Corpus (CAC, 19,532 samples) is constructed via a multi-MLLM pipeline that generates and cross-validates fine-grained conflict factor/source annotations from SAMM base data augmented with Google Search background knowledge. Second, Modality Bridging Pre-Training (MBPT) trains a lightweight cross-attention Cross-modal Aligner on 50k FineHARD samples using a SigLIP-style contrastive loss plus an object-occurrence VQA loss, enabling text-described visual conflicts to be grounded back into the visual feature space. Third, Conflict Perception Training (CPT) applies a conflict-aware contrastive loss that explicitly pushes the two conflict factor representations apart in the embedding space (maximizing their distance), combined with a language modeling loss that forces the model to articulate the conflict reasoning. This reshapes the MLLM's conceptual boundaries so that semantically conflicting concepts (e.g., 'U.S. President' vs. 'Football Award') become clearly separable, enabling rapid few-shot or zero-shot adaptation to unseen manipulation types.

## Results

On the four multimodal manipulation detection benchmarks (DGM4, MDSM, MMFakeBench, NewsCLIPpings), CORE_Qwen and CORE_Gemma achieve state-of-the-art accuracy with as few as 100 training samples, surpassing the best baselines by an average of 9.94% and 10.50% respectively. At 750 samples, CORE_Gemma reaches 82.0% on MDSM and 79.4% on MMFakeBench, while CORE_Qwen achieves 74.5% and 79.4% respectively. In zero-shot cross-manipulation settings, CORE outperforms the second-best method by over 14% on MDSM, and in cross-dataset zero-shot evaluation, CORE_Qwen improves over the best baseline by an average of 11.4%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CORE decisively outperforms all baselines across every dataset and sample budget. Among specialized non-MLLM detectors, HAMMER (56.9% DGM4-750) and RamDG (57.9%) are the strongest, yet CORE_Qwen exceeds them by 7.5–8.5 points; FKA-Owl and AMD lag further behind at 52.4% and 40.8% on DGM4-750, respectively. Among untrained MLLM baselines, Gemma3-4B is the best (60.8% DGM4-750, 63.0% MDSM-750), still trailing CORE_Gemma by 7.6 and 19.0 points. Large zero-shot MLLMs (Qwen3VL-235B, Gemma3-27B, LLaMA3.2-90B, SeedVL-1.5) plateau at 48–62% accuracy regardless of sample size, confirming that scale alone does not confer conflict-perception ability. Notably, CORE with only 100 samples (e.g., 69.0% on MDSM) already surpasses HAMMER and RamDG trained on 750 samples (62.2% and 61.7%), demonstrating that the conflict-oriented training paradigm is far more data-efficient than manipulation-specific designs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in multimodal manipulation detection—designing specialized models and training pipelines for specific forgery types (HAMMER, RamDG, FKA-Owl, AMD)—by reframing the problem around a single underlying signal: intrinsic conflict between modalities or with world knowledge. It repurposes off-the-shelf MLLMs (Qwen2.5VL, Gemma3) as backbones, leveraging their pre-trained world knowledge, but identifies and addresses a specific gap: these models conflate semantically distinct concepts in feature space despite high knowledge-accuracy scores. The technical toolkit draws on SigLIP-style contrastive losses, cross-attention alignment, and LoRA fine-tuning, but applies them in a novel two-stage pipeline (Modality Bridging Pre-Training + Conflict Perception Training) supervised by the newly constructed Conflict Attribution Corpus, which provides fine-grained conflict-factor and conflict-source annotations absent from prior datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper tackles the practical 'arms race' problem where new manipulation types emerge faster than detection methods can be retrained, and demonstrates that a conflict-oriented paradigm can adapt to unseen manipulations with as few as 100 samples or even zero-shot. The CAC dataset (19.5k samples with structured conflict annotations) is a useful community resource for studying conflict reasoning in multimodal settings. Limitations include reliance on SAMM for base manipulation annotations (potentially biasing the conflict taxonomy), the constraint to exactly two conflict factors per sample, and evaluation primarily on 3–4B parameter MLLMs; whether the approach scales to more subtle or multi-layered manipulations where conflicts are not cleanly binary remains an open question.
