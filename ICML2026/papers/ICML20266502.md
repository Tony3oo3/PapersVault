## Problem

AI capability is scaling far faster than safety, as evidenced by leading models consistently scoring higher on capability than safety benchmarks. Existing paradigms—post-hoc alignment ('Make AI Safe') and static intrinsic safety ('Make Safe AI')—are brittle, reactive, and unable to address unforeseen risks in open-ended, non-stationary environments. The paper argues that safety must be reconceived as a dynamic, coevolving capability rather than a one-time certification or add-on constraint.

## Input / Output

The R²AI system consumes user prompts and environmental signals through a two-stage pipeline: the Fast Safe Model performs low-latency input filtering and output sanitization, escalating ambiguous or high-risk cases to the Slow Safe Model for reflective reasoning. The Safety Wind Tunnel injects controllable adversarial attack scenarios (prompt injection, jailbreaks, multi-turn goal redirection) at multiple granularities, while the External Environment supplies real-world deployment feedback. Outputs include safety-filtered responses, verifier feedback signals dispatched at both short- and long-term timescales for credit assignment, and updated parameters for both safe models and the Attacker.

## Method

R²AI operationalizes safe-by-coevolution through four interacting components: a Fast Safe Model (System 1, low-latency reactive defense), a Slow Safe Model (System 2, deliberative safety reasoning), a Safety Wind Tunnel (controllable Attacker + Verifier for adversarial stress-testing), and the External Environment (real-world deployment feedback). The Fast-Slow interaction is formalized as a cooperative Stackelberg game where the Slow Model (leader) learns long-horizon safety policies and the Fast Model (follower) optimizes reactive filtering, with the Slow Model taking precedence on disagreement. The Safety Wind Tunnel runs a closed-loop adversarial coevolution where the Attacker escalates as defenses improve, and verification failures are decomposed into short- and long-term credit signals. A three-step inductive process—near-term safety guarantee, safe iterative step, and continual safety by induction—ensures the system remains within a defined safety margin across upgrades, supported by a formal proposition (Hypotheses 3.1–3.2) proving safety preservation by induction. A Reset-and-Recover mechanism, grounded in a Temporal Swiss Cheese Model, halts progression upon red-line violations and reconstructs a verifiable checkpoint from trusted historical model states to restart the coevolutionary loop.

## Results

This is a position paper that proposes the safe-by-coevolution principle and the R²AI framework; it contains no empirical experiments, quantitative benchmarks, or main results tables. The paper's core 'results' are theoretical: Hypothesis 3.1 (Near-Term Safety Guarantee) and Hypothesis 3.2 (Safe Iterative Step) together yield Proposition 3.3 (Continual Safety via Induction), which proves by mathematical induction that a system initialized within a safety margin M and updated via a safety-preserving coevolutionary operator C remains safe for all t ≥ t₀. The paper also introduces a five-level hierarchy (L1 Alignment through L5 Verifiable Reflection) and a conceptual contrast between 'Make AI Safe' and 'Make Safe AI' paradigms, but provides no numerical evaluations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, R²AI does not run empirical comparisons against named baselines. Instead, it contrasts two existing safety paradigms conceptually: 'Make AI Safe' (post-hoc alignment via RLHF, RLAIF, red-teaming, guardrails) versus 'Make Safe AI' (intrinsic safety by construction). The paper argues that 'Make AI Safe' methods are reactive, brittle, and fail under irreversible risks, while 'Make Safe AI' approaches (formal guarantees, Scientist AI, Tool AI) struggle in open-ended, non-stationary environments. R²AI positions itself as a synthesis: it retains the proactive, built-in safety philosophy of 'Make Safe AI' but adds the dynamic, adversarial coevolutionary mechanism (fast-slow dual models, safety wind tunnel, reset-and-recover) that the authors argue is missing from both existing paradigms. No head-to-head quantitative comparison is provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes ideas from biological immunity, dual-process cognition (Kahneman's System 1/2), Stackelberg game theory, and adversarial training into a unified 'safe-by-coevolution' framework (R²AI), deliberately positioning itself against both the reactive 'Make AI Safe' paradigm (RLHF, RLAIF, guardrails, red-teaming) and existing 'Make Safe AI' approaches (formal guarantees, Scientist AI, Tool AI) that the authors argue are insufficient for open-ended, non-stationary environments. It extends the AI-45° Law (Yang et al., 2024) and the causal ladder of trustworthy AI into a five-level safety spectrum (L1–L5) and formalizes the coevolutionary process via an induction argument over a safety margin. The fast-slow dual-system architecture draws on dual-process theory and the cooperative Stackelberg game, while the Safety Wind Tunnel repurposes adversarial training and automatic red-teaming as a continually evolving internal challenger rather than a one-time evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a coherent conceptual roadmap for scaling AI safety toward AGI/ASI by reframing safety as an evolving capability rather than a one-time certification, which is timely given the widening capability-safety gap documented for frontier models. However, as a position paper it provides no implementation, no empirical evaluation, and no concrete algorithmic instantiation of R²AI; the central Hypothesis 3.2 (existence of a safety-preserving coevolutionary mechanism) remains an unproven assumption, and the definition of the safety margin M is left deliberately abstract. Key open questions include: how to rigorously specify and verify M in open-ended settings, whether the reset-and-recover mechanism can be triggered reliably before irreversible harm, and whether the fast-slow distillation loop can maintain safety guarantees under distributional shift at the scale of ASI. For practitioners, the five-level taxonomy and the resistance/resilience evaluation dimensions (attack success rate, recovery speed) offer useful structuring, but the framework's practical viability awaits concrete implementation and stress-testing.
