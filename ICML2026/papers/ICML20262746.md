## Problem

Classical instrumental variable (IV) estimators require at least as many instruments as treatment dimensions, a condition that fails for high-dimensional treatments like clinical billing codes or images. The standard workaround of applying unsupervised dimension reduction (PCA, autoencoders) before IV estimation is fundamentally flawed: because the representation is learned without knowledge of the instrument, it may discard the instrument-driven variation in the treatment, introducing omitted treatment bias that invalidates downstream causal estimates.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262746/cropped_page2_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes triples (Z, X, Y) where X is a high-dimensional treatment (e.g., 815-dim billing codes or 28×28 MNIST images), Z is a low-dimensional instrument vector (e.g., 475-dim physician assignment), and Y is a scalar outcome. It produces a low-dimensional representation D̃ of X (with dimension r ≤ dim(Z) to satisfy the IV order condition), an outcome-improving direction u identified via IV regression in representation space, and a perturbed treatment X_{αu} decoded back to the original high-dimensional space. The bottleneck dimension and the split between instrument-driven (D̃) and instrument-independent (Ṽ) components serve as tunable hyperparameters.

## Method

The paper proposes two variants of Instrument-Guided Representation Learning. In the linear setting (LIRR), it regresses X on Z, takes the SVD of the resulting coefficient matrix C = BA, and uses the left singular vectors as the encoder B̂, so that D̃ = B̂ᵀX isolates the instrument-relevant subspace; a subsequent 2SLS on D̃ with Z as instrument recovers the causal direction. In the nonlinear setting (IRAE), an autoencoder is trained with a composite loss combining reconstruction, a linear prediction penalty λ‖e_D(X) − AZ − c‖² that forces the D-component to be linearly driven by Z, and HSIC/d-HSIC independence regularizers ensuring the residual δ ≈ U is independent of Z, that Z is independent of the V-component, and that D̃, Ṽ, and δ are mutually independent. The key theoretical result is that under bounded completeness and joint independence of (Z, U, V), the optimal encoder recovers D̃ = PD for an invertible matrix P, guaranteeing that the IV moment restriction in representation space has a unique solution and that the identified intervention direction yields a provably positive expected outcome improvement for sufficiently small step size α.

## Results

On the semi-synthetic pneumonia dataset (N=50,000), LIRR achieves 34.64±11.21, 34.74±11.25, and 34.99±11.20 hours of saved recovery time at confounding strengths γ=1.0, 2.0, and 3.0 respectively, maintaining stable performance as unobserved confounding increases. On the nonlinear MNIST benchmark, the full IRAE model achieves 1.10±0.46 average test improvement at α=1.0, compared to −0.47±0.04 for a vanilla autoencoder. LIRR outperforms PCA+2SLS by ~1.7 hours and dramatically exceeds OLS, LASSO, PLS, and DeepIV under moderate-to-high confounding (γ≥2.0), where those methods degrade to near-zero or negative improvement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LIRR consistently outperforms all baselines at γ≥2.0: it saves ~34.7 hours versus PCA+2SLS (~33.0), OLS (3.84), PLS (6.67), LASSO (0.00), and DeepIV (0.86). At the lowest confounding (γ=1.0), PLS actually achieves the highest single-point improvement (40.95), but this is an artifact of PLS aligning with confounding directions that happen to correlate with the outcome when confounding is weak; PLS collapses to 3.22 at γ=3.0. DeepIV, despite its flexible nonparametric architecture, underperforms because its complex model is poorly suited to the linear DGP and requires substantially more data to generalize. On the nonlinear MNIST task, the full IRAE (with all independence regularizers) achieves 1.10±0.46 improvement at α=1.0, outperforming the ablated IRAE[2] (0.92±0.47) and dramatically exceeding Vanilla AE (−0.47±0.04), confirming that instrument regularization is essential for capturing the relevant latent structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the identifiable VAE (iVAE) framework by repurposing the instrument Z as auxiliary information that guides representation learning, but critically departs from iVAE by not requiring Z as an encoder input at inference time (since Z is unavailable during intervention). It also builds on the Rep4Ex line of work, which shares a similar structural equation model but targets interventions on the instrument rather than on the latent treatment space. The paper positions itself against both the classical two-step pipeline (unsupervised PCA/AE followed by 2SLS) and direct non-parametric IV methods like DeepIV, arguing that the former introduces omitted treatment bias while the latter requires completeness in the full high-dimensional treatment space. The linear LIRR algorithm connects to the classical instrumented PCA literature (Rao, Sabatier, Kelly) but generalizes it with a formal identification theorem and an explicit intervention procedure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first theoretical framework showing that instrument-guided representation learning can resolve the order condition violation in high-dimensional IV settings, with clean identification guarantees in both linear and nonlinear regimes. The practical relevance is significant for domains like clinical treatment pathways where treatments are thousands of billing codes but instruments are low-dimensional. Key open questions include the strength of the joint independence assumption (Z ⊥ U ⊥ V), which the authors acknowledge is violated in realistic clinical settings (e.g., senior physicians managing severe cases), and the lack of sensitivity analysis for such violations. The empirical gains over PCA+2SLS are moderate, and the MNIST benchmark, while illustrative, is a simplified toy problem that may not capture the complexity of real high-dimensional treatments.
