## Problem

Unlearning research for LLMs is predominantly English-centric, leaving two critical gaps: (1) how unlearning in one language transfers to other languages in multilingual models, and (2) whether unlearning truly erases knowledge or merely suppresses its expression, and where in the model's layers this suppression is localized. Understanding both is essential for safe multilingual deployment, since cross-lingual leakage could allow adversaries to recover supposedly forgotten content by querying in a different language.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes multilingual TOFU question-answer pairs (200 fictitious authors × 20 QA pairs each, translated into EN, CH, DE, RU, TU via Gemini) and produces three types of outputs: a 5×5 cross-lingual unlearning transfer matrix reporting NLI-score changes per (fine-tune, unlearn, query) language triple; layer-wise cosine similarity curves comparing hidden representations across model variants (base, fine-tuned, unlearned) and languages; and per-layer steering vectors (ℓ₂-normalized activation differences) that, when injected at inference time, yield NLI-score recovery gains on the forget set. Evaluation uses xlm-roberta-large-xnli for multilingual semantic equivalence scoring, validated against native-speaker annotations (89% average agreement).

## Method

The paper extends TOFU to five languages and systematically permutes fine-tuning (L_FT), unlearning (L_unl), and query (L_Q) languages to map cross-lingual transfer patterns, finding that transfer strength is governed by language family, shared script, and pretraining coverage. Unlearning is implemented via DPO (with GA and NPO as ablations), where the forget objective pushes the model toward refusal responses while a KL-regularized retain term preserves non-target knowledge. The key mechanistic finding comes from layer-wise cosine similarity and PCA analyses: unlearning preserves cross-lingual alignment in early and middle layers (the shared 'concept' space) while altering only the later decoding layers, indicating suppression rather than erasure. Exploiting this structure, the authors extract per-layer steering vectors as activation differences between the fine-tuned model and an auxiliary unlearned model (trained on a shuffled retain set to avoid data leakage), then subtract scaled versions of these vectors from hidden states during inference, recovering 50% (Qwen) and 90% (Gemma) of unlearned knowledge across languages without retraining or access to the original answers.

## Results

On the multilingual TOFU benchmark (5 languages: EN, CH, DE, RU, TU), unlearning transfer is highly asymmetric: unlearning in the fine-tuning language (e.g., EN) drops the forget-set NLI score by −90±3 in EN but only −4 to −9 in other languages, while unlearning in a weaker language (e.g., TU) still reduces the high-performing EN score by −10±0. Cross-lingual prompting gains correlate strongly with unlearning transfer (Pearson r = 0.50, p < 0.05; Spearman ρ = 0.60, p < 0.01), confirming that shared internal representations mediate transfer. Layer-wise cosine similarity shows unlearning preserves cross-lingual alignment in early and middle layers, with divergence only in later decoding layers. Inference-time steering with a single extracted vector recovers 50% of unlearned knowledge in Qwen2.5-7B and 90% in Gemma2-9B, far exceeding a random Gaussian baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three unlearning objectives—DPO (primary), Gradient Ascent (GA; Jang et al., 2023), and Negative Preference Optimization (NPO; Zhang et al., 2024)—and finds that all three exhibit qualitatively identical cross-lingual transfer patterns (stronger transfer between typologically similar or script-sharing languages, asymmetric transfer from high- to low-coverage languages), indicating the findings are not an artifact of a specific unlearning algorithm. The novel steering-vector recovery experiment is compared against a random Gaussian baseline with matched norm and scale: the extracted unlearning direction recovers 50% (Qwen) and 90% (Gemma) of the lost NLI score, while the random baseline yields only small, inconsistent gains, confirming the vectors capture a structured suppression direction rather than generic noise. The paper does not frame this as a method-vs-baseline competition; rather, it demonstrates that all current parameter-update unlearning methods (DPO, GA, NPO) behave as language-agnostic suppression in late layers and are therefore equally vulnerable to cross-lingual leakage and inference-time reversal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the TOFU unlearning benchmark (Maini et al., 2024) from English to five languages and uses DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) as the primary unlearning objective, while adapting the contrastive activation addition technique (Rimsky et al., 2024) to construct per-layer steering vectors that reverse unlearning at inference time. It departs from prior unlearning work, which is predominantly monolingual and English-centric, by systematically varying fine-tuning, unlearning, and query languages to characterize cross-lingual transfer patterns. While isolated cross-lingual unlearning effects have been noted (Lu & Koehn, 2025; Hwang et al., 2025; Choi et al., 2024), this paper provides the first comprehensive, multi-factor study linking transfer strength to language family, script, and pretraining coverage. The mechanistic analysis builds on prior evidence that unlearning behaves as suppression rather than erasure (Shi et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025) but localizes this effect layer-wise in a multilingual setting for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a single English-extracted steering vector recovers up to 90% of unlearned knowledge (Gemma) across languages fundamentally challenges the robustness of current unlearning guarantees in multilingual deployments, with direct implications for privacy compliance and adversarial attack surfaces. The paper demonstrates that single-language evaluation can substantially overestimate unlearning robustness, making multilingual assessment a necessary component of any unlearning pipeline. Limitations include the use of TOFU (simple fact retrieval, 1% forget fraction), two model families, and five languages, leaving open whether findings generalize to pretraining-acquired knowledge, larger unlearning scales, or more complex reasoning tasks. Future work should investigate whether stronger unlearning methods (e.g., those targeting early/middle layers) can resist steering-based recovery, and whether the observed suppression structure persists at scale.
