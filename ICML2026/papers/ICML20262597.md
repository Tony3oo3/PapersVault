## Problem

Tool-augmented LLM agents introduce a previously unexplored attack surface: under strict query-only access (no model or tool modification, no gradient access), an adversary can stealthily rewrite input prompts to inflate the number of tool-calling steps while preserving task semantics, imposing substantial computational overhead on the platform. This Denial-of-Efficiency (DoE) vulnerability is distinct from prior agent-safety work that relies on backdoor poisoning, internal component modification, or explicitly malicious prompt injection.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an original user prompt (text and/or RGB image) together with the task-specific enabled tool subset, and produces a single rewritten prompt that is semantically equivalent but induces longer tool-calling trajectories. The rewrite is constrained to preserve the answer format (e.g., all multiple-choice options remain intact) and must not explicitly name any tool. At deployment, the rewriter also draws on a pre-constructed offline policy bank and a few-shot history context of past successful rewrites to guide the single-step transformation.

## Method

STA is a training-free, multi-agent framework with three LLM roles: a Prompt Rewriter that generates candidate rewrites, a Quality Judge that scores and critiques each attempt, and a Policy Inductor that distills reusable rewriting strategies. In the offline phase, the framework runs a 15-round rewrite-execute-judge loop over a small probe set (1% of data), scoring each rewrite with a two-dimensional reward combining a step-induction bonus (normalized tool-call count, range [0,5]) and a semantic-preservation penalty (embedding cosine similarity mapped to [-5,0]); successful entries are stored in per-probe and global top-k history buffers. The Policy Inductor then abstracts these successes into a bank of J structured policies, each specifying a name, description, applicability conditions, and generic rewrite rules. At deployment, the Prompt Rewriter selects the most suitable policy for the current query and performs a single-step rewrite, which is directly submitted to the victim agent—requiring no further iteration and strictly respecting the query-only threat model.

## Results

STA consistently increases tool-calling steps across all 6 victim models, with Δ Steps ranging from 0.88 (Qwen3-VL-2B, high-budget) to 3.57 (Qwen2-VL-7B, high-budget) under the low-budget (K_max=15) and high-budget (K_max=40) settings. Semantic preservation is maintained with |Similarity| below 1 in most cases (e.g., 0.70±0.20 for Qwen2-VL-7B low-budget), confirming stealth. The proportion of samples reaching the tool-calling cap (Δ Cap Hits) increases by up to 26.54% (Qwen2-VL-7B, low-budget), indicating substantial computational overhead. Across 4 agentic frameworks, attack rewards range from 1.35 (AutoGen, GPT-4o-mini) to 2.99 (OctoTools, Qwen2-VL-7B), while post-attack task accuracy drops by only 1–3 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper introduces a new attack surface (Denial-of-Efficiency) and does not compare against prior attack methods; the baseline is the original unmodified agent behavior. STA outperforms the original setting on every model–budget combination, with Qwen2-VL-7B showing the largest step inflation (3.33–3.57 additional tool calls) and GPT-4.1-nano the smallest (1.03–1.16). Across agentic frameworks, attack effectiveness scales with framework capability: OctoTools yields the highest attack reward (2.99 for Qwen2-VL-7B) versus AutoGen (2.17), while post-attack accuracy drops remain modest (e.g., 47.27%→45.58% on OctoTools vs. 40.92%→39.28% on AutoGen for Qwen2-VL-7B). The attack is largely budget-agnostic, with consistent Δ Steps across K_max=15 and K_max=40, though Cap Hits naturally decrease at higher budgets. VLM-based STA components (e.g., Qwen3-VL-4B) produce stronger attacks than LLM-only variants, and increasing the probe fraction from 1% to 10% with more policies (8→16) further improves both effectiveness and cross-budget resilience.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new attack surface—Denial-of-Efficiency (DoE)—that is distinct from prior agent-safety work on backdoor/poisoning attacks (AgentPoison, BadAgent), explicit prompt injection, and memory-extraction attacks, all of which either repurpose known vulnerabilities or require internal modification. Methodologically, STA repurposes the iterative prompt-rewriting paradigm from adversarial NLP and combines it with a multi-agent LLM collaboration loop (Rewriter–Judge–Inductor) and an offline policy-distillation step, drawing on reward-design conventions from summarization evaluation (Fabbri et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2023). The target systems (OctoTools, AutoGen, LangChain, GPT-Functions) represent the current training-free, plug-and-play tool-augmented agentic paradigm, which the authors position as the natural and underexplored substrate for this new attack vector.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that a query-only adversary can reliably inflate tool-calling steps across six models, four frameworks, and thirteen datasets while preserving task semantics, the paper exposes a practical cost-amplification vulnerability that agent platform operators must now consider in their threat models. The absence of a stealthiness-vs-strength trade-off (Table 4) and the cross-model transferability of the policy bank suggest that simple rate-limiting or per-query anomaly detection may be insufficient, pointing toward the need for cost-aware architectural safeguards. Open questions include whether the attack generalizes to streaming or multi-turn agentic settings, whether the offline policy-construction phase (even at 1% probe data) is a meaningful operational constraint, and whether the observed step inflation translates into measurable latency or monetary cost in production deployments.
