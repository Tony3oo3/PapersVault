## Problem

Multi-fidelity regression in extreme data imbalance (HF < 5% of total) is limited by GP surrogates' cubic scaling, overfitting to sparse HF observations, and reliance on nested input designs. A deeper structural gap is that standard autoregressive couplings condition HF corrections only on LF mean predictions, ignoring the heteroscedastic and non-Gaussian error structure that arises where LF models are least reliable. The paper asks whether frozen tabular foundation models can perform training-free MF regression while exploiting full distributional summaries for cross-fidelity transfer.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two frozen TFM forward passes with no parameter updates: first, the aggregated low-fidelity dataset (all fidelities 1 through T−1, with fidelity index encoded as an integer categorical column) serves as in-context context to produce a predictive distribution at each HF input; second, the residual TFM receives an augmented feature vector concatenating the original input x, the LF predictive mean, variance, and nine decile quantiles (0.1–0.9) as in-context context. The output is a point prediction (LF mean + residual mean) and an additive predictive variance (LF variance + residual variance), with no training loop, kernel fitting, or hyperparameter search required at inference time.

## Method

FIRE decomposes MF regression into two zero-shot in-context learning stages using a frozen TFM (TabPFNv2.5). In the first stage, a base TFM performs ICL on the bi-level aggregated LF dataset (all lower fidelities pooled with a fidelity token) to yield a full predictive distribution summarized by mean, variance, and decile quantiles. In the second stage, a residual TFM performs ICL on an augmented dataset where each sample's input is [x, μ_LF, σ²_LF, {q_τ}] and the target is the HF residual r = y_HF − μ_LF; this distribution-conditioned residual learning is the core novelty, enabling the model to adapt correction magnitude to local LF uncertainty and capture non-Gaussian residual shape. The final prediction adds the two means and the two variances. Theoretically, the paper proves Bayes risk monotonicity (adding variance/quantiles cannot increase risk), shows mean-only conditioning is misspecified under heteroscedastic discrepancies, and establishes that quantile conditioning strictly reduces risk when residuals are non-Gaussian.

## Results

Across 31 multi-fidelity benchmarks (synthetic, HPO, and physics-based), FIRE achieves the highest Elo rating and lowest average rank for both accuracy (NRMSE) and uncertainty (NLL), outperforming all seven GP-based and deep learning baselines. FIRE maintains a ~1.8 average-rank lead over the strongest GP baseline (NARGP, rank ≈ 3.5) and achieves a >60% pairwise win rate against every competing method. In the extreme data-scarce regime (2–5% HF-to-LF ratio), FIRE's advantage is largest, while it also dominates the Pareto frontier by being simultaneously the fastest and most accurate method. Ablation shows that removing variance or quantile features from the residual conditioning drops performance by ~250 Elo points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FIRE outperforms all seven main baselines (AR(1), ResGP, NARGP, ContinuAR, MisoKG, MFBNN, MFRNP) on both NRMSE and NLL across all 31 problems, with the largest gaps in the 2–5% HF-data regime and on real-world physics/HPO tasks. Against the strongest GP baseline NARGP, FIRE holds a ~1.8 average-rank advantage and a >60% pairwise win rate; NARGP itself degrades sharply below 5% HF ratio while FIRE remains stable. Deep learning baselines MFRNP and MFBNN are computationally expensive (requiring 1000 training iterations) and lose to FIRE on both accuracy and runtime, with FIRE dominating the Pareto frontier. On the LCBench HPO subset, FIRE also beats the specialized multi-fidelity PFNs FT-PFN and LC-PFN, which are restricted to <10 input dimensions. The ablation in Appendix A.2 confirms that the distribution-conditioned residual framework itself (not just the TFM) drives gains: even FIRE(GP) with a standard GP backbone outperforms all traditional GP MF methods, though FIRE(TFM) still leads by a clear margin.

## Relation to Prior Work

FIRE builds on the classical Kennedy & O'Hagan autoregressive multi-fidelity decomposition (f^(t) = ρ_t·f^(t-1) + δ_t) but replaces the GP surrogates at each fidelity level with frozen tabular foundation models performing zero-shot in-context learning. The key conceptual departure is the distribution-conditioned residual: rather than conditioning the high-fidelity correction on the low-fidelity mean alone (as in AR(1), ResGP, NARGP), FIRE augments the residual input with the base model's predictive variance and a set of quantiles, drawing on heteroscedastic GP theory and Bayesian stacking literature. The bi-level fidelity aggregation (collapsing all lower fidelities into a single dataset with a fidelity-index token) is a practical simplification over recursive co-kriging, validated by ablation showing no accuracy loss but a 10× runtime reduction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FIRE addresses a practical pain point in MF regression: the extreme data-imbalance regime (2–5% HF data) where GP kernels overfit and deep surrogates require extensive per-problem training. The training-free paradigm eliminates this overhead entirely, and the 31-benchmark suite spanning synthetic, HPO, and physics-based domains provides a more comprehensive evaluation than prior MF studies. Limitations include the TFM's context window (bounding problem size), quadratic attention cost, and dependence on pre-training quality; the paper also notes that FIRE-GP achieves better calibration than FIRE-TabPFN, suggesting the TFM's predictive distributions are not yet perfectly calibrated. Open questions include extension to multi-fidelity optimization and whether the distribution-conditioning framework transfers to non-tabular (e.g., spatial PDE) settings.
