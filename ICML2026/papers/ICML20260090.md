## Problem

Despite decades of research on online learning in games, the number of iterations required for FTRL (and its canonical instance MWU) to converge to a Nash equilibrium in potential games remained wide open. While asymptotic convergence was known for MWU under restrictive conditions, no non-asymptotic rates existed for the general FTRL family, and it was unclear whether FTRL could serve as an efficient first-order optimizer in constrained optimization. This paper resolves the question by establishing both exponential upper and lower bounds, and a doubly exponential lower bound for fictitious play.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes as input a potential game specified by its payoff matrix (multilinear potential function), a learning rate sequence η^(t) (including vanishing rates 1/t^α for α∈[0,1)), a permutation-invariant 1-strongly convex regularizer, and an initial strategy. The outputs are iteration-count bounds: an upper bound of exp(O_ε(1/ε²)) for alternating ε-lazy no-regret dynamics, a lower bound of 2^Ω(m log m) for FTRL in m×m two-player potential games, and a doubly exponential lower bound of (C·2ⁿ)! for fictitious play in n-player binary-action games. The lower bound construction uses a spiral-structured payoff matrix with an extra initialization row/column, and the multi-player construction embeds a snake-in-the-box path on the n-dimensional hypercube.

## Method

The upper bound is proved by bounding the total number of ε-lazy updates (at most Φ_range/ε) and then deriving a recursion T_{k+1} ≤ T_k(1 + O_ε(1/ε)) for the time between consecutive updates, yielding the exponential bound. For the FTRL lower bound, the authors construct a spiral potential game (extending Panageas et al.'s matrix with a negative-entry initialization gadget) and partition the trajectory into periods where FTRL concentrates on successive action profiles; they track cumulative utility gaps to show FTRL cannot skip periods and that period lengths satisfy the recurrence T_k + T_{k-1} ≥ (1/2)Σ(ℓ−2)T_ℓ, forcing 2^Ω(m log m) total rounds even under vanishing learning rates. The doubly exponential fictitious play bound connects the two-player spiral mechanism to the snake-in-the-box problem: an exponential-length snake on the n-cube is embedded into a binary-action n-player potential game where exactly one player has a positive best-response gap at each step, and the resulting recurrence T_k ≥ (k−1)T_{k−1} + T_{k−2} + T_{k−3} − ΣκT_κ yields T_k ≥ (k−1)!, giving (C·2ⁿ)! iterations.

## Results

The paper establishes three main theoretical bounds. First, an exponential upper bound: alternating ε-lazy no-regret dynamics converge to a 2ε-Nash equilibrium in at most exp(O_ε(1/ε²)) iterations (Theorem 1.1). Second, a matching exponential lower bound: in two-player m×m potential games, FTRL with any permutation-invariant regularizer and learning rate η^(t) = 1/t^α (α ∈ [0,1)) can require 2^Ω(m log m) iterations to reach an ε-Nash equilibrium for ε = 1/poly(m) (Theorem 1.2). Third, a doubly exponential lower bound for fictitious play: in an n-player binary-action potential game, FP requires 2^Ω(2^n) iterations to reach an ε-Nash equilibrium for ε = 1/2^n (Theorem 1.3). The upper and lower bounds match up to factors in the exponent.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines. The key comparison is against prior theoretical lower bounds. For fictitious play in potential games, the paper's doubly exponential bound of 2^Ω(2^n) iterations (Theorem 1.3) improves exponentially over the prior best lower bound of 2^Ω(n) by Panageas et al. (2023) and Brandt et al. (2013). For FTRL in two-player potential games, the 2^Ω(m log m) lower bound (Theorem 1.2) is the first non-trivial lower bound for the general FTRL class (not just MWU or FP), and it holds even under vanishing learning rates. The paper also notes that its upper bound of exp(O(1/ε²)) matches the lower bound up to factors in the exponent, establishing tightness. The construction for the two-player lower bound adapts the recursive matrix from Panageas et al. (2023), while the multi-player construction introduces a novel connection to the snake-in-the-box problem from coding theory (Evdokimov, 1969).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the spiral-matrix construction of Panageas et al. (2023) (originally for fictitious play) to the FTRL setting, where the analysis is significantly more complex due to FTRL's propensity to mix actions under vanishing learning rates. It extends the asymptotic convergence results for MWU in potential games (Kleinberg et al., 2009; Palaiopanos et al., 2017) to the full FTRL class with non-asymptotic rates, and leverages the FTRL–MD equivalence (McMahan, 2011) to transfer lower bounds to mirror descent variants. The multi-player result draws on the snake-in-the-box problem from coding theory (Kautz, 1958; Evdokimov, 1969), connecting a graph-theoretic path constraint to the unilateral-deviation structure of potential games.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The exponential lower bound for FTRL (and by equivalence, MWU) in two-player potential games establishes a fundamental barrier: the most celebrated online optimization algorithm is a poor first-order optimizer in constrained settings, being exponentially slower than gradient descent. The doubly exponential lower bound for fictitious play in n-player games is an exponential strengthening over all prior results and reveals that the foundational learning algorithm in games can be catastrophically slow. A key open question the authors identify is whether doubly exponential lower bounds extend to FTRL with small learning rates, where hiding the path is harder due to mixing. The positive potential property (FTRL never decreases the potential) provides structural understanding but does not rescue convergence speed.
