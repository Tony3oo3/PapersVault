## Problem

Decontextualized general health-AI benchmarks (e.g., MedQA, PubMedQA) are widely treated as evidence that a model is ready for clinical deployment, yet high benchmark performance is neither necessary nor sufficient for clinical utility. Many models topping leaderboards fail in real-world workflows (IBM Watson for Oncology, Epic's sepsis model), while others with modest scores (ambient AI scribes) deliver meaningful clinical benefit. The paper asks what role benchmarks should legitimately play in deployment decisions and what evaluation instruments should replace them.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the analytical input is the current landscape of clinical AI benchmarks, leaderboard practices, and documented deployment failures (IBM Watson, Epic sepsis, GPT-4 triage studies, ambient scribe RCTs). The output is a structured argument identifying specific failure modes of benchmark-centric evaluation and a proposed recalibrated framework: benchmarks as candidate-selection triage tools, with deployment claims requiring prospective studies, RCTs, target trial emulation, and continuous post-deployment monitoring. The paper spans text-based QA benchmarks, multimodal EHR+imaging evaluations, and interactive/simulation-based benchmarks as its analytical subjects.

## Method

The paper organizes its argument into two complementary layers. First, it catalogs task-level mismatches between benchmarks and clinical practice: dataset label errors and contamination, the static single-snapshot format that misses iterative differential diagnosis, missing modalities (physical exam, behavioral cues, interactive patient engagement), single-metric aggregation that hides fairness/safety trade-offs, and the conflation of local utility with generalizability. Second, it identifies inherent limitations that no benchmark redesign can resolve: many benchmark tasks are unrealizable as clinical tasks (no single correct answer for a specific patient), construct validity claims about 'medical reasoning' rest on untested causal chains, and benchmark-centrism creates misaligned incentives that reward shortcut learning over robustness. The proposed solution is a narrower evidentiary role for benchmarks (screening candidates under controlled conditions) paired with context-specific prospective evaluation matched to risk, workflow integration, and patient-centric endpoints, supported by policy changes in funding, publication, and regulatory practice.

## Results

This is a position paper with no experimental results or main results table. The authors support their argument with cited statistics from prior work: a frontier LLM undertriaged 52% of true emergency scenarios and overtriaged 65% of nonurgent cases (Ramaswamy et al., 2026); 4% of MedQA questions contain insufficient information and 3% have label errors (Griot et al., 2025); only 2% of ML-based clinical studies were prospective (Ben-Israel et al., 2020); and recasting MedQA into a sequential diagnostic format (AgentClinic) can reduce diagnostic accuracy to below one-tenth of the original static accuracy (Schmidgall et al., 2026). The central claim is qualitative: benchmark performance is neither necessary nor sufficient for clinical utility.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method or run head-to-head comparisons against baselines. Instead, it contrasts two categories of systems to illustrate its thesis. On one side, models with strong benchmark performance that failed in practice: IBM Watson for Oncology (strong expert-recommendation benchmarks but unsafe/irrelevant clinical suggestions), Epic's sepsis model (promising benchmark performance but missed two-thirds of sepsis cases in external validation), and GPT-4 (near-human USMLE scores but accuracy drops under cognitive biases and reinforcement of racial/gender stereotypes). On the other side, models with modest or absent benchmark presence that delivered clinical value: ambient AI scribe systems (improved documentation efficiency and reduced clinician burden in RCTs) and localized triage models (reduced ED overcrowding at specific hospitals). The paper also contrasts static exam-style benchmarks (MedQA, PubMedQA) with newer interactive/multimodal benchmarks (AgentClinic, EHRXQA, MedHELM, HealthBench, CLIBench), arguing the latter narrow but do not close the gap to deployment readiness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes a growing body of evidence on the gap between benchmark performance and clinical utility, drawing on documented deployment failures (IBM Watson for Oncology, Epic's sepsis model, GPT-4 triage undertriage) and recent calls for prospective evaluation (Azad et al. 2026, Agrawal et al. 2025, Wiens et al. 2019). It builds on validity-theoretic frameworks (Salaudeen et al. 2025a; Jacobs & Wallach 2021) to formalize why benchmark scores support construct-level inferences but not criterion-level deployment claims. The paper departs from the prevailing benchmark-centric evaluation culture by explicitly narrowing the evidentiary role of benchmarks to candidate screening, and by contrasting 'Good on Paper, Bad in Practice' models with 'Quiet Workhorses' that deliver local utility despite modest leaderboard scores.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central practical implication is a reallocation of research and regulatory resources: away from proliferating general-purpose benchmarks and toward prospective, context-specific studies (RCTs, target trial emulation, post-deployment monitoring) that measure patient-centric and workflow-level endpoints. Open questions remain about how to design reproducible prospective evaluations at scale, how to balance the reproducibility benefits of benchmarks with the clinical relevance of deployment studies, and how to create regulatory and funding incentives that reward context-aware evaluation over leaderboard performance. The argument is strongest for LLM-based clinical tools entering deployment now, but the underlying logic applies to all clinical AI, including imaging and predictive models.
