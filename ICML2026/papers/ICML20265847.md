## Problem

In multi-tenant multi-LoRA serving, a discrete residency budget K limits how many adapters can be simultaneously device-resident. When the active adapter working set exceeds K, adapter misses become structurally unavoidable, and under continuous batching these misses on the critical path cause head-of-line blocking that amplifies tail latency into a congestion collapse rather than smooth degradation. Existing in-engine mitigations (eviction, prefetch) are reactive and cannot prevent on-demand misses near the K boundary, leaving a gap for a proactive mechanism that enforces feasibility before dispatch.

## Input / Output

CLIMB consumes an online stream of requests, each tagged with a LoRA adapter identity and a class label (VIP for latency-critical, BG for throughput-oriented), along with a fixed per-run residency budget K (e.g., vLLM's max_loras). It maintains per-adapter ingress FIFO queues and tracks an in-flight set of adapters with dispatched-but-incomplete requests. The output is a per-dispatch-eligibility decision: which adapters' queue heads are admitted into the engine, constrained so that the effective active set (admitted ∪ in-flight) never exceeds K. The engine's internal continuous-batching and token-scheduling logic is left entirely unchanged; CLIMB acts solely as an ingress gate.

## Method

CLIMB is a minimal feasibility-first ingress controller that enforces a hard invariant |A_t ∪ I_t| ≤ K at every dispatch opportunity, where A_t is the admitted adapter set and I_t is the in-flight set. When VIP demand saturates the budget (|V_t| ≥ K), CLIMB pauses all new BG admissions and dispatches only from VIP queue heads; otherwise it admits all VIP adapters and fills remaining slots via round-robin over BG adapters. The key insight is that by gating eligibility at ingress rather than reacting inside the engine, unavoidable infeasibility is externalized as explicit pre-dispatch queueing rather than amplified engine-side stalls on the continuous-batching critical path. A simplified queueing model (effective load ρ_eff = λ(s₀ + p_miss·τ)) yields an interpretable knee law predicting where the cliff boundary lies, and a proposition formalizes that feasibility-first admission guarantees admitted traffic operates near the fast regime with p_miss ≈ 0.

## Results

On the cliff-inducing workload (W=8, K=4), CLIMB reduces VIP TTFT p99 from 38.71 s (GLOBALFIFO) to 13.06 s at matched throughput (10.66 rps), by collapsing the engine component from 38.43 s to 0.13 s and shifting the residual tail into explicit ingress queueing (12.97 s). At the safe anchor (W=8, K=8), both policies converge to 0.13 s VIP TTFT p99, confirming CLIMB is near-transparent when feasibility is not at risk. The TTFT decomposition shows the cliff is engine-dominated under GLOBALFIFO (engine p99 ≈ 38 s vs. queue p99 ≈ 2 s) but queue-dominated under CLIMB (queue p99 ≈ 13 s vs. engine p99 ≈ 0.13 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

At the cliff point (W=8, K=4), CLIMB reduces VIP TTFT p99 from 38.71 s (GLOBALFIFO) to 13.69 s at identical throughput (10.66 rps), a ~65% reduction, by keeping the engine component at the safe-anchor level (0.13 s) and externalizing delay into ingress queueing. LRUGATE (locality-driven gating) is ineffective, actually worsening VIP p99 to 40.96 s while shifting delay to the queue component, confirming that reactive in-engine heuristics cannot prevent feasibility violations. BGCAP (static BG distinct-cap) achieves a much lower VIP p99 (3.77 s) but at the cost of BG p99 exploding to 118.4 s and throughput dropping to 6.89 rps (−35%), making it a throughput-confounded improvement. LOCKGATE (lock the active set) achieves the lowest VIP p99 (0.05 s) but throughput collapses to 3.99 rps (−62%), representing the most aggressive throttling. At the safe anchor (K=8), CLIMB is near-transparent, matching GLOBALFIFO and LRUGATE at 0.13 s VIP p99 and 10.66 rps, while BGCAP and LOCKGATE retain their characteristic throughput/latency distortions regardless of feasibility.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the multi-LoRA serving infrastructure established by S-LoRA, Punica, and dLoRA, which handle dynamic adapter loading and multi-adapter execution within the engine, and on continuous-batching engines like vLLM/PagedAttention. It departs from these systems by identifying a previously uncharacterized failure mode—the discrete 'residency cliff' where exceeding the adapter budget by even one adapter triggers nonlinear tail-latency blow-up via continuous-batching cadence coupling—and by shifting the control point from in-engine eviction/prefetch to a minimal ingress admission gate. The theoretical diagnostic (knee law) draws on classical queueing theory (M/G/1 saturation) but applies it to a discrete feasibility constraint rather than continuous load, and the admission-control framing connects to SEDA-style tail-SLO serving, though the discrete adapter-residency constraint is a new ingredient not present in prior admission-control work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, mechanistic explanation for why multi-LoRA deployments can exhibit catastrophic tail latency without throughput loss, and a minimal, engine-agnostic fix (ingress gating) that practitioners can layer on top of existing vLLM/S-LoRA stacks without modifying the engine. The quantified trade-off—VIP p99 reduced from ~39s to ~13s at matched throughput, at the cost of BG backlogged_wait increasing from ~3ms to ~5s—makes the design space explicit for operators choosing between VIP protection and BG liveness. Limitations include evaluation on a single GPU (RTX 5090) with a single model (Qwen2.5-7B), a fixed K per run, and workloads specifically designed to induce the cliff; open questions include whether transition-aware admission (accounting for adapter-set turnover costs) or workload-aware K provisioning can further tighten the knee, and whether the mechanism generalizes to other adapter types or multi-GPU settings.
