## Problem

LLM-as-a-judge evaluation of subjective tasks suffers from a reproducibility crisis: the same evaluator contradicts itself when re-judging identical samples because free-form protocols (e.g., Chain-of-Thought) unpredictably shift their implicit criteria, a phenomenon the authors term dimension drift. Existing remedies are unsatisfactory — multi-judge ensembles are costly and still drift at the individual level, while fixed checklists eliminate drift but are too rigid to capture task-specific nuances, leaving an unresolved stability-adaptivity dilemma.

## Input / Output

The method takes an evaluation instance I = (S, M), where S is task-specific context (dialogue history, role profile, scenario description) and M is the model response under evaluation, and produces a scalar subjective quality score (e.g., 1–5 Likert) derived from structured answers to a fixed set of k=5 binary or small-categorical questions. The rubric construction phase additionally consumes unlabeled evaluation instances for candidate question generation and m simulated response fillings to estimate joint answer-rating distributions; all questions are constrained to discrete answer spaces (binary preferred) to keep probability estimation tractable and suppress micro-level drift.

## Method

The paper reformulates subjective evaluation as a Bayesian Experimental Design problem: each evaluation question is an experiment whose utility is the Expected Information Gain (EIG) — the expected reduction in Shannon entropy of the latent rating distribution p(y|I) upon observing the answer. The pipeline proceeds in five stages: (1) generate a diverse candidate question pool Q via high-temperature LLM proposals on unlabeled instances, (2) estimate the joint distribution p̂(y,a|q,I) by simulating LLM answers and conditional ratings across m instances, (3) compute EIG(q) for each candidate, (4) select the top-k questions maximizing total EIG to form a fixed rubric Q*, and (5) prompt the LLM-as-a-judge to answer only Q* and aggregate answers into a reproducible score. This 'generate-then-score' design unifies stability (a pre-committed, repeatable rubric that eliminates dimension drift) with adaptivity (questions chosen to be maximally informative for the specific task domain), resolving the trade-off that plagues both free-form CoT judging and static checklists.

## Results

On six subjective-evaluation benchmarks, the EIG framework achieves the highest average self-consistency (κ_self = 0.7786) and the strongest human alignment (p_ohuman = 0.4719 QWK), outperforming all baselines on both metrics across every dataset. Compared to the most unstable baseline (LLM+CoT, κ_self = 0.6132), EIG improves self-consistency by +7.6% in Cohen's κ; compared to the most rigid baseline (CheckEval, p_ohuman = 0.3118), it improves human alignment by +51% in relative QWK. The method also achieves EM_dim = 1.0 (zero dimension drift) on all tasks, whereas CoT shows pronounced drift on complex benchmarks like CharacterEval.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PoLL (Verga et al., 2024), the multi-judge ensemble baseline, EIG wins on both metrics: κ_self 0.7786 vs. 0.7375 (+5.6%) and p_ohuman 0.4719 vs. 0.3739 (+26% relative), while also avoiding PoLL's linearly growing amortized cost. Against CheckEval (Pereira et al., 2024), the fixed-checklist baseline, EIG achieves higher self-consistency (0.7786 vs. 0.7472) and substantially higher human alignment (0.4719 vs. 0.3118, +51% relative), demonstrating that information-theoretic question selection captures task-specific nuances that rigid checklists miss. The free-form LLM+CoT baseline is the least stable (κ_self = 0.6132) due to dimension drift, while LLM Direct sits in between on consistency but lags EIG on human alignment (0.3610 vs. 0.4719). Paired bootstrap significance tests confirm that EIG's human-alignment gains over every baseline are statistically significant (all 95% CIs exclude zero) across all six benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing Expected Information Gain (EIG) from Bayesian Experimental Design and active learning into the context of LLM-as-a-judge rubric construction. It departs from two existing extremes: free-form protocols like G-Eval/CoT that are flexible but suffer from 'dimension drift' (unpredictable shifting of implicit criteria across repeated evaluations), and fixed checklists like CheckEval that are stable but brittle and task-agnostic. Rather than building on a single prior method, the paper identifies the stability-adaptivity trade-off as a fundamental gap and proposes an information-theoretic optimization objective (maximizing EIG over candidate questions) as a principled middle ground, drawing on the BED literature (Felder & Brent, Frazier) for its formalism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The reproducibility crisis in LLM evaluation—where the same judge contradicts itself on identical inputs—undermines the comparability of research results across the community, making this a practically urgent problem. The EIG framework offers a clean, interpretable mechanism for selecting evaluation dimensions that is both task-adaptive and repeatable, and the Love-EQ dataset (1,039 annotated romantic-chat instances, Fleiss' κ = 0.73) adds a new resource for emotional-intelligence evaluation. Key limitations include dependence on the underlying LLM for question generation and simulation (evaluator biases can propagate into the rubric), the one-time construction cost (~80 equivalent evaluations for break-even), and the fact that the information-theoretic objective optimizes entropy reduction rather than directly modeling human judgment structure. Open questions include whether the framework extends to non-text modalities, whether the EIG criterion captures all relevant aspects of 'informative' evaluation, and how the approach behaves when the evaluator model is significantly weaker than the generator model.
