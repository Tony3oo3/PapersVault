## Problem

Instance-wise feature selection methods that jointly train a selector and predictor suffer from information leakage: when unselected features are replaced with a fixed ablation value (e.g., zero), the predictor can exploit the ablation pattern itself as an information channel, causing genuinely important features to be omitted without degrading prediction accuracy. Additionally, the discrete nature of feature subset selection is non-differentiable, forcing existing methods to rely on Gumbel-Softmax relaxations, reinforcement learning, or disjoint training, each with significant drawbacks in speed, expressiveness, or end-to-end optimizability.

## Input / Output

The method takes a d-dimensional feature vector x and, at each training step, draws replacement values x̂ from the product of the marginal feature distributions (sampled with replacement from the training data columns). It produces a continuous importance mask m ∈ [0,1]^d (one scalar per feature) and a predicted class label ŷ via softmax. The number of important features is adaptive per instance: a feature is deemed important when its mask value exceeds 0.5, so no fixed k needs to be specified in advance.

## Method

Hide&Seek consists of two fully connected feed-forward networks: a 'Hide' selector that maps x to a continuous mask m ∈ [0,1]^d via a sigmoid output layer, and a 'Seek' predictor that maps the masked input z to ŷ. The key reformulation replaces discrete feature removal with a continuous convex combination: z_j = m_j·x_j + (1−m_j)·x̂_j, where x̂_j is a random draw from the marginal distribution of feature j. This makes the entire pipeline differentiable and, because the replacement values are distributional rather than fixed, prevents the predictor from learning the ablation pattern as an information channel. Training uses a parsimony-weight annealing schedule λ_t = (t/T)²·λ_max that quadratically ramps up the L1 sparsity penalty on the mask, allowing the model to prioritize prediction accuracy early and sparsity later. The full loss is cross-entropy plus (λ_t/d)‖m‖₁, optimized end-to-end with Adam over 500 epochs without batching.

## Results

On the six synthetic IWFS benchmarks (Syn1–Syn6), Hide&Seek achieves median TPR of 97–100% and FDR of 0–4%, outperforming all seven baselines on both metrics. On the switch-feature datasets (Syn4–6), Hide&Seek attains 100% median switch accuracy, compared to ~51% for INVASE and ~58% for L2X, while REAL-x also reaches 100% due to its disjoint training. Training time is 5 seconds (vs. 1 h 18 m 52 s for INVASE and 1 m 16 s for REAL-x), and Hide&Seek uses ~3 k parameters compared to ~100 k for INVASE and ~57 k for REAL-x. On the correlated Credit Default data, Hide&Seek achieves F1 scores of 67–100 across Syn1–6, exceeding INVASE (57–100) and REAL-x (52–86).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Hide&Seek outperforms all seven baselines on both TPR and FDR across all six synthetic datasets. Against the closest selector-predictor competitor INVASE, Hide&Seek matches TPR on Syn1–2 (100 vs. 100) but achieves substantially lower FDR on Syn6 (4 vs. 9) and, critically, identifies the switch feature X₁₁ in 100% of instances versus INVASE's ~51%, which the authors show accounts for nearly all of INVASE's TPR gap. REAL-x matches Hide&Seek's switch accuracy (100%) thanks to its disjoint predictor training, but suffers higher FDR (up to 15 on Syn4) and is 15× slower to train. L2X is decisively outperformed on both metrics (e.g., Syn4: 99/4 vs. 44/65) and also exhibits ~58% switch accuracy, confirming the information-leakage failure mode. SHAP and LIME, which require a fixed number k of top features, trail Hide&Seek by 20–80 points in TPR on the switch-feature datasets. On the correlated Credit Default data, Hide&Seek leads INVASE and REAL-x on F1 in 5 of 6 settings (e.g., Syn6: 71 vs. 62 vs. 52), and on the TCGA breast-cancer task it ranks ESR1 and CCNB2 as the top two genes, matching the biologically established findings of Covert et al. (2021).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the selector-predictor framework for instance-wise feature selection established by L2X (Chen et al., 2018), INVASE (Yoon et al., 2018), and REAL-x (Jethani et al., 2021), which all jointly train a selector to identify important features and a predictor to make predictions from those features. The key departure is replacing discrete feature removal with a continuous mask that blends each feature with a stochastic replacement drawn from its marginal distribution, enabling end-to-end differentiability without the Gumbel-Softmax relaxation (L2X), actor-critic RL (INVASE), or decoupled training (REAL-x). The paper also generalizes the information-leakage analysis of Jethani et al. (2021) from specific tree-structured and one-hot-encoding scenarios to a broader class of 'switch features' whose partition, rather than exact value, determines the response, proving the standard KL-based training objective is an ill-posed proxy in this setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The proof that the standard ablation-based loss can systematically misidentify genuinely important features (with a lower bound on misidentification rate equal to the probability of the largest partition cell) is a cautionary result for the broader XAI community: it shows that a seemingly natural training objective can encode predictions in the explanation rather than in the selected features. Practically, Hide&Seek offers a fast (5-second training), parameter-efficient (~3k parameters vs. 57k–100k for competitors) alternative that avoids the information-leakage failure mode while maintaining joint training. Open questions include whether the continuous mask relaxation can be tightened toward true binary selection, whether marginal sampling suffices in highly structured (non-Gaussian) feature spaces, and how the method scales to very high-dimensional settings (the 100-feature experiment shows degradation, though ensembling helps).
