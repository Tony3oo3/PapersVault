## Problem

Multi-agent system (MAS) explainability research currently operates without explicit specifications for what should be explained, to whom, and against what behavioral baseline, rendering methods non-comparable, non-reproducible, and disconnected from deployment governance requirements. A systematic analysis of 2,381 MAS papers from top ML venues (2021–2025) reveals that 65% omit stakeholder specifications, 76% lack quantitative evaluation bounds, and 99% ignore auditability requirements, while only 2% specify accountability chains for failure attribution.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes two structured specifications as input: a Research Contract (defining explanandum, stakeholder, intervention unit, evaluation bounds, adversarial context, and auditability) and an Agent Contract (defining deontic norms—obligations, permissions, prohibitions—along with violation predicates and accountability rules). The system's actual behavior traces (agent actions, state logs, communication records) are then evaluated against the Agent Contract's expected-behavior baseline B_expected, and the resulting discrepancy Δ is explained per the Research Contract. Output is a contrastive, norm-grounded explanation identifying which specific norms were violated by which agents, with primary/secondary accountability attribution and log-traceable evidence, rather than a free-form post-hoc description.

## Method

The paper proposes a two-contract framework that is method-agnostic and architecture-agnostic across LLM-based, learning-based, and hybrid MAS. The Research Contract C_R = (E, S, I, B, X, G) formalizes six explanation requirements (explanandum, stakeholder, intervention unit, evaluation-bound tuples, adversarial context, governance), while the Agent Contract C_A = (N, ≻, V, A) encodes expected behavior as a finite norm set with deontic types (O/P/F), a priority ordering for conflict resolution, observable violation predicates mapping system states to {violated, satisfied, unknown}, and accountability rules mapping violation patterns to responsible parties. The key mechanism is their interaction: C_A defines B_expected, the deployed system produces B_actual, and the norm-violation set Δ becomes the explanation target governed by C_R—making explanations inherently contrastive (deviation from expectation), grounded (referencing defined norms), and actionable (identifying specific violated obligations). The framework is validated empirically through corpus-level coding of 2,381 papers and retroactive contract analysis of two representative MAS XAI methods (TriQPAN and CEMA), plus three illustrative case studies spanning warehouse robotics, autonomous vehicle fleets, and LLM agent instruction compliance.

## Results

Across 2,381 MAS-related papers from NeurIPS, ICML, and ICLR (2021–2025), the corpus analysis reveals severe specification gaps: only 34.6% specify a target stakeholder, 24.4% provide quantitative evaluation bounds, and a mere 0.8% address auditability requirements. For the Agent Contract, the gaps are even more acute—only 11.2% define prohibitions, 20.0% specify violation criteria, and just 2.0% establish accountability chains. Temporal trends show limited improvement: Stakeholder specification rises from 25.6% (2021) to 45.9% (2025), but Evaluation Bounds remain nearly flat (21.8%→22.8%) and Auditability stays near zero (0.0%→1.7%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new XAI method to benchmark against baselines; instead, its empirical contribution is the corpus-level specification-gap analysis. The closest analogue to a method comparison is the retroactive contract coding of two existing MAS explainability methods—Rodriguez et al.'s TriQPAN (requirements-based traceability) and Gyevnar et al.'s CEMA (causal counterfactual explanation). TriQPAN scores higher on specification completeness (Specified on Explanandum, Stakeholder, Intervention Unit, and Obligations; Partial on Evaluation Bounds, Permissions, Auditability, and Violation Criteria), while CEMA is Specified only on Explanandum and Partial on Intervention Unit and Permissions, with all other elements Not specified. Both methods leave Prohibitions, Adversarial Context, and Accountability Chain entirely unspecified, confirming the corpus-level finding that elements tied to methodological novelty are specified more often than deployment-critical governance elements. The paper's argument is that neither method, despite technical strength, produces norm-contrastive or auditable explanations without an explicit Agent Contract baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two largely separate literatures: the 25-year tradition of normative multi-agent systems (deontic logic, electronic institutions, commitment theory from Singh, Esteva, Boella & van der Torre) and the MAS XAI methods literature (causal counterfactuals, Shapley-based credit assignment, requirements-based traceability). It repurposes Design by Contract (Meyer, 1992) as the organizing paradigm, arguing that MAS XAI needs the same 'specify the what before the how' discipline that software engineering adopted decades ago. The documentation-standards lineage (Model Cards, Datasheets) provides the template mechanism, while Constitutional AI and IRON supply the tiered-norm and runtime-revision patterns for LLM-based agents. The key departure from prior MAS XAI work is that no existing method or survey proposes what specifications should precede method development; this paper fills that meta-level gap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The empirical finding that 99.2% of 2,381 MAS papers omit auditability and 98% omit accountability chains is striking and directly relevant as the EU AI Act's transparency obligations take effect in August 2026. The framework's method-agnostic design means it doesn't constrain algorithmic innovation but enables fair comparison and deployment validation. Key open questions include whether the community will actually adopt contract templates (the paper acknowledges this depends on venue review norms shifting), how contracts handle genuinely emergent multi-agent behaviors that no individual norm captures, and whether the coding rubric generalizes beyond the three venues analyzed. The case studies are illustrative rather than experimental, so the claim that contracts 'transform vague accounts into verifiable explanations' remains a design argument rather than an empirically validated one.
