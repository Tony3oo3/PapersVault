## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks for science evaluate knowledge recall, literature review, code reproduction, or hypothesis generation, but none directly test whether models can predict the outcomes of empirical experiments—a capability that could reshape resource allocation in scientific research. SciPredict addresses two questions: can LLMs predict experimental outcomes with sufficient accuracy, and can they reliably distinguish trustworthy predictions from unreliable ones to guide real experimental decisions?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263992/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each task provides an experimental setup description, specific measurements taken, a prediction question, and optionally expert-curated background knowledge (atomic bullet points from prior literature). Models must produce a prediction in one of three formats—multiple-choice (3-4 options, 12.35% multi-select), free-form (judged by an LLM against 1-10 expert-written binary rubric criteria), or numerical (checked against an acceptable range reflecting measurement precision)—along with three self-assessed reliability scores (confidence, difficulty, feasibility) each on a 1-5 scale. The multi-format design intentionally probes recognition versus generation, and the reliability scores enable calibration analysis independent of raw accuracy.

## Method

SciPredict is a 405-task benchmark derived exclusively from empirical studies published after March 31, 2025 (postdating model training cutoffs), spanning 33 sub-fields across physics (25%), biology (50%), and chemistry (25%), with 40% MCQ, 32% free-form, and 28% numerical questions. Curation cost $336k and 7,380 expert hours, with multi-stage quality control ensuring unambiguous setups, scientifically sound distractors, and realistic numerical ranges. Evaluation tests 15 SOTA LLMs and a matched human expert baseline under five background-knowledge conditions (none, expert-curated, self-generated, combined, and filtered-to-unknown-only), with free-form answers scored by Gemini-3-Pro against rubrics (validated against GPT-5.2 with no significant differences). The key analytical contribution is a calibration framework that correlates self-reported confidence/difficulty/feasibility against measured accuracy, revealing that models maintain ~20% accuracy regardless of their self-assessments while human experts show accuracy rising from ~5% to ~80% as rated feasibility increases. A 16-type, 5-category error taxonomy (applied via LLM judge) further decomposes failures into factual/extraction errors (avg. 80.1%) and logical reasoning flaws (avg. 87.4%) rather than comprehension issues.

## Results

SOTA LLMs achieve prediction accuracy of 14–26% on SciPredict, with human expert baselines at approximately 20%. Providing expert-curated background knowledge improves accuracy by ~3% on average (1.2–5.8% depending on model), while self-generated background knowledge typically degrades performance. Models exhibit severe miscalibration: their accuracy remains near ~20% regardless of self-reported confidence or feasibility, whereas human experts show strong calibration with accuracy rising from ~5% on infeasible tasks to ~80% on feasible ones. Model failures are dominated by factual/extraction errors (avg. 80.1%) and logical/reasoning flaws (avg. 87.4%), with information fabrication (54.0%) and factual contradiction (52.3%) being the most prevalent fine-grained error types.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 15 SOTA LLMs against a matched human expert baseline (domain experts assigned by subfield expertise). Frontier models (Claude Opus 4.5, GPT-5.2, Gemini 3 Pro) achieve 22–26% accuracy, modestly exceeding the ~20% human expert baseline, while smaller models (Llama 3.3 70B at ~14%) fall below it. However, the critical distinction is in calibration: human experts show a strong monotonic relationship between rated feasibility and actual accuracy (rising from ~5% to ~80%), while all models maintain a flat ~20% accuracy regardless of their self-assessed confidence, difficulty, or feasibility scores. On the background-knowledge ablation, expert-curated BK consistently helps all models (+~3%), but self-generated BK hurts most models relative to no background at all, and combining self-generated with expert BK (SABK) rarely improves over expert BK alone. Performance on SciPredict correlates moderately with the HLE benchmark (Pearson r ≈ 0.46), indicating that general hard-reasoning ability explains some but not all variance in experimental outcome prediction. Chemistry is the hardest domain for both models and humans, while Biology (50% of tasks) and Physics (25%) show higher accuracies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a distinct niche from the growing family of LLM-for-science benchmarks. Prior work has evaluated LLMs on reproducing ML experiments (PaperBench, MLE-Bench), generating scientific hypotheses (Yang et al., Ke et al.), performing literature reviews (Lab-Bench), or predicting outcomes in behavioral/social sciences (Cui et al., Saynova et al.). SciPredict departs from all of these by targeting quantitative prediction of empirical outcomes in hard sciences (physics, biology, chemistry) with a post-March-2025 data cutoff to prevent leakage, and by introducing calibration assessment (confidence, difficulty, feasibility) as a first-class evaluation dimension alongside raw accuracy. The benchmark's construction pipeline—expert curation of background knowledge, multi-stage quality control, and three question formats (MCQ, free-form, numerical)—repurposes ideas from rubric-based evaluation and range-based numerical scoring but applies them in a unified experimental-outcome-prediction setting that had no prior standardized benchmark.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that frontier LLMs achieve only 14–26% accuracy (comparable to ~20% human experts) and, critically, show no meaningful correlation between self-reported confidence/feasibility and actual accuracy, while humans calibrate from ~5% to ~80%—challenges the near-term viability of LLMs as experimental-planning tools. The discovery that self-generated background knowledge frequently degrades performance (and that even combining it with expert-curated knowledge rarely helps) reveals a fundamental gap in models' metacognitive ability to identify what prior knowledge is relevant. Open questions include whether calibration can be improved through training or prompting, whether the ~3% gain from expert background knowledge can be amplified, and whether the benchmark's 405-task scale and three-domain scope adequately capture the full landscape of experimental reasoning. The $336k curation cost and 7,380 expert hours also raise practical questions about scalability and reproducibility of similar benchmarks.
