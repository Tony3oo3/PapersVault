## Problem

For neural network training objectives that are not globally L-smooth, it remains an open question how to select the largest step size that still guarantees convergence of gradient descent. Existing definitions of 'large' step sizes are incoherent across the literature, and it is unclear whether a given fixed step size will ever reach the edge of stability (EoS) or how early this occurs. The paper addresses this by providing a unifying, local definition of large step sizes and a first-order algorithm that finds them without second-order information.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard supervised classification data (5000-image subsamples from CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, SVHN, or EMNIST) and a parameterized neural network (MLP, CNN, ResNet-34, VGG-11, DenseNet-121, or WideResNet-50-2) with a linear last layer including a bias term. At each iteration it produces a single adaptive scalar step size via the equality line search, which is then applied to the full-batch gradient. The step size is not fixed a priori but is recomputed every iteration by alternating halving and doubling until the nonmonotone Armijo-type condition is met approximately as an equality, and the NLS-ub variant additionally caps the step size below the number of classes K to avoid globally-flat saddle regions.

## Method

The paper introduces a local segment smoothness assumption (Definition 2.1) that requires only Lipschitz or Hölder continuity of the gradient along the anti-gradient segment, and defines 'large' step sizes (Definition 2.3) as those exceeding 2/l(w_k, η_{w_k}) at least every P iterations. The core algorithm is an equality line search (Algorithm 2) that combines backtracking and extrapolation: if the nonmonotone descent condition (using the Zhang & Hager reference value) is initially violated, the step is halved; if it is initially satisfied, the step is doubled, iterating until the condition holds approximately as an equality. This guarantees Lipschitz-awareness (η_k ≥ 2δ(1−c)/l), Hölder-awareness for any ν ∈ [0,1], and asymptotic sharpness-awareness (η_k ≥ 2(1−c)δ/(|λ₁(H(w*))|+ε)) without ever computing the Hessian. A key empirical finding is that these step sizes drive GD to the EoS immediately (sharpness × step size > 2 throughout training), but can also cause the network to get stuck at globally-flat saddle points where sharpness equals its provable minimum 2/K; the NLS-ub variant prevents this by capping step sizes below K, allowing GD to traverse slightly sharper valleys and achieve better training loss and test accuracy.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative finding is that NLS and PoNLS drive the sharpness (largest Hessian eigenvalue) to the global minimum of 2/K (e.g., 0.2 for K=10 on SVHN, 0.02 for K=100 on CIFAR-100, 2/26≈0.077 for K=26 on EMNIST) while maintaining the product sharpness×step size above the EoS threshold of 2 from the very first iteration. The NLS-ub variant, which caps step sizes below K, avoids hitting exactly 2/K and instead hovers slightly above it, achieving lower training loss and higher test accuracy than both NLS and CDAT on resnet34/SVHN. In 'unsuccessfully-flat' runs (e.g., PoNLS on EMNIST×CNN), the training loss remains stuck at the random-guessing level (~0.09 for K=10) throughout all iterations, while 'successfully-flat' runs achieve substantially lower loss and better generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SAM (Foret et al., 2020), NLS and PoNLS clearly outperform in minimizing sharpness, driving it to the global minimum of 2/K, whereas SAM's sharpness reduction is less aggressive and its EoS is evaluated differently (Long & Bartlett, 2024). Against CDAT (Roulet et al., 2024), NLS and PoNLS also achieve lower sharpness, and the NLS-ub variant frequently surpasses CDAT in both training loss and test accuracy on resnet34/SVHN (Figure 3), though CDAT consistently maintains sharpness×step size above 2. Against PoNLS (the deterministic version of PoNoS from Galli et al., 2023), NLS shows very similar behavior—both operate at the EoS from the start and both can hit the globally-flat 2/K region—but NLS-ub (the upper-bounded variant) is the key differentiator, as it avoids the saddle-point trap that causes PoNLS to get stuck at random-guessing accuracy in some configurations. Overall, the paper's method wins on sharpness minimization against all three baselines, and NLS-ub wins on test accuracy against CDAT, while the comparison with SAM is less direct due to different EoS formulations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes nonmonotone line searches (Grippo et al., 1986; Zhang & Hager, 2004) as a mechanism for automatically finding the largest converging step sizes in gradient descent, a problem previously addressed only through fixed step size grids (Sadrtdinov et al., 2024) or second-order curvature information (CDAT, Roulet et al., 2024). The theoretical analysis builds on Nesterov's universal gradient method (2015) to extend Lipschitz-awareness to Hölder-awareness, and on the self-stabilization argument (Damian et al., 2022) to motivate the NLS-ub upper bound on step sizes. The paper positions itself as the first in the deep learning literature to use extrapolation passes in line searches to push step sizes to the convergence boundary, providing a unifying definition of 'large' that requires only local segment smoothness rather than global Lipschitz continuity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper answers a longstanding open question (Goodfellow et al., 2016) about selecting the largest converging learning rates, and provides a practical first-order algorithm (NLS-ub) that operates at the edge of stability from the start of training. The key open question it raises is why GD with large step sizes so efficiently finds globally-flat saddle points, and whether this behavior persists at scale or with stochastic optimization. Limitations include experiments on only 5000 training examples, primary use of MSE loss (cross-entropy results are in the appendix), and the absence of a convergence proof for the stochastic setting. The finding that the global minimum of sharpness is harmful rather than beneficial challenges the core assumption behind SAM and related methods, but the causal mechanism remains unexplained.
