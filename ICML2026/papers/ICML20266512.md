## Problem

Experiments with AI agents inherit the researcher degrees of freedom that drove the replication crisis in human subjects research, but amplify them: the combinatorial space of prompt × model × decoding × parsing configurations is vast, and the near-zero marginal cost of running additional configurations removes the natural friction that once constrained specification search. Unlike traditional ML benchmarking (fixed test sets, standardized metrics) or human subjects research (high per-trial costs), AI agent experiments lack analogous safeguards, leaving a methodological gap where undisclosed specification search can produce an illusion of robustness across models and conditions.

## Input / Output

The template requires verbatim prompt text (not paraphrased summaries), exact model checkpoint hashes or API version dates with access timestamps, and complete generation parameters, along with a mandatory disclosure of pilot testing history including the number of prompt iterations explored and whether preliminary results informed final hypotheses. The output is a time-stamped preregistration document that classifies each analysis as confirmatory or exploratory, pre-specifies the full robustness space (all models, prompt variants, and parameter ranges to be tested with an aggregation rule), and produces a specification curve reporting results across the entire pre-registered configuration space rather than a single privileged path.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a preregistration template that extends traditional preregistration logic to the AI agent experimental pipeline, adding structured fields for each category of researcher degrees of freedom in a taxonomy spanning agent selection, scaffolding, prompt engineering, experimental design, response processing, and analysis. Key provisions include requiring exact model identifiers and version checkpoints (not just model names), verbatim prompt specifications, pre-specification of exclusion criteria for refusals and malformed responses, and a staged preregistration option for adaptive designs with explicit decision rules (e.g., 'if pilot accuracy < 20%, increase context by 500 tokens'). The paper empirically demonstrates the problem through a 2,430-specification simulation of anchoring effects in LLMs, where the specification curve ranges from strongly negative to >1.0 anchoring indices, showing that a researcher could report 'robust human-like anchoring,' 'no anchoring,' or 'reverse anchoring' depending on which defensible path through the specification space they select. The template also embeds transparency commitments (raw output archiving, code sharing) and an attestation section confirming data collection has not yet begun, addressing the temptation of post-hoc registration enabled by cheap re-runs.

## Results

The paper's central empirical demonstration is a simulation of anchoring effects in LLMs across 2,430 experimental specifications (varying model family, system prompt, anchor distance, delivery method, question content, and outlier handling). The resulting specification curve shows anchoring indices ranging from strongly negative (reverse anchoring) through zero (no effect) to values exceeding 1.0 (stronger than typical human anchoring effects), demonstrating that a researcher's choice of specification path alone can flip the conclusion from 'robust human-like anchoring' to 'no anchoring' to 'reverse anchoring.' The paper also contextualizes this by citing the Open Science Collaboration's finding that only 36% of replications were significant despite 97% of originals reporting significance, with replication effects roughly half the magnitude of originals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper and does not compare computational methods or models against baselines in the traditional sense. Instead, it draws methodological parallels across three research paradigms: human subjects behavioral experiments (high evaluation flexibility, high marginal cost), traditional ML benchmarking (low cost, constrained by fixed test sets and standardized metrics), and experiments with AI agents (low cost, high flexibility). The paper argues that AI agent experiments occupy a uniquely vulnerable region of the cost-flexibility tradeoff space, where the near-zero marginal cost of additional API calls (seconds and pennies) combined with a vast specification space (prompt × model × temperature × seed × parsing) creates strong incentives for invisible specification search. The proposed preregistration template is positioned as analogous to ClinicalTrials.gov registration for clinical trials and OSF Registries for behavioral science, rather than as a method to be benchmarked against alternatives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the preregistration framework from behavioral sciences (Simmons et al., 2011; Gelman & Loken, 2013; Open Science Collaboration, 2015) and clinical trial registration into the emerging paradigm of LLM-as-participant behavioral experiments (Horton et al., 2023; Aher et al., 2023; Binz & Schulz, 2023). It draws on specification curve analysis (Simonsohn et al., 2020) and multiverse analysis (Steegen et al., 2016) as methodological tools, while distinguishing its argument from ML reproducibility norms (Pineau et al., 2021; Henderson et al., 2018) by emphasizing that the experiment itself is the evaluation, leaving no held-out test set to protect against overfitting. The paper carves new ground by systematically cataloging AI-specific degrees of freedom (prompt wording, model selection, decoding parameters, retry policies) and proposing a tailored preregistration template that addresses these novel choice surfaces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As AI agents assume autonomous roles in negotiation, trading, and platform moderation, the behavioral experiments studying them will increasingly inform real-world policy, making methodological credibility urgent. The paper's anchoring simulation (2,430 specifications yielding conclusions from reverse anchoring to super-human anchoring) concretely demonstrates that without preregistration, the field risks accumulating a literature of specification-optimized findings that appear robust but are not. Open questions include whether venues and funding agencies will actually enforce preregistration, how to handle the tension between the exploratory nature of early AI research and confirmatory rigor, and whether the template can be made low-friction enough for widespread adoption. The paper acknowledges that preregistration can be gamed (post-hoc registration after extensive exploration) and that it may constrain serendipitous discovery, though it argues these costs are outweighed by the credibility gains.
