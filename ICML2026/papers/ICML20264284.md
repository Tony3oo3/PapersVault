## Problem

Existing diffusion inversion methods reduce local approximation errors via fixed-point iterations but largely overlook how the spacing and distribution of diffusion timesteps in the noise scheduler affects overall reconstruction fidelity. This paper reveals that the inversion error scale is strongly dependent on timestep size and position, exhibiting a parabolic trend with larger errors concentrated at both small and large timesteps, and that uniformly spaced timesteps are suboptimal for accurate latent recovery.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a set of uniformly spaced inversion timesteps (from any standard scheduler such as 'leading', 'linspace', or 'trailing'), the precomputed noise schedule parameters {α_t}, and a window width d for local search. It outputs a rescheduled set of K timesteps that minimize the accumulated inversion error coefficient. The dynamic programming solver runs offline in ~9 seconds on CPU for T=50 timesteps, so the actual inversion pipeline incurs no additional runtime overhead and requires no extra model parameters.

## Method

The paper first derives a closed-form expression showing that the inversion error from a timestep jump Δt factors into a scaling coefficient c_α(t, Δt) = √α_t · Δψ(α_{t-1}, Δt-1) — which depends only on the noise schedule — multiplied by the standard single-step fixed-point residual Δε_θ. Building on this, TRDI applies a two-stage coarse-to-fine rescheduling: (1) a global power-law rescaling t_k = t_1 + (t_K − t_1)·((k−1)/(K−1))^γ that stretches timesteps toward the high-error early region (γ=1.05 for SDXL, γ=0.90 for SDXL Turbo), and (2) a windowed dynamic programming pass that, within a sliding window of width 2d+1 around each step, finds the locally optimal transition minimizing the accumulated c_α cost. The local DP avoids a full global optimization (which would be biased since Δε_θ is unknown) while still adaptively concentrating finer steps in high-error regions and coarser steps in low-error regions.

## Results

On MSCOCO reconstruction with SD v1.5 (50 steps), the proposed timestep rescheduling improves all four baselines: DDIM gains +0.14 dB PSNR and −6.12 LPIPS (193.97→187.85), ReNoise gains +0.32 dB PSNR and −8.97 LPIPS (166.27→157.30), NPI gains +0.26 dB PSNR and −6.60 LPIPS, and GNRI gains +0.18 dB PSNR and −5.69 LPIPS (147.02→141.33). On PIE-Bench editing with SDXL, the method yields up to 24.31% structure distance improvement (DDIM) and up to 16.13% background preservation gain (SDXL Turbo DDIM), while maintaining comparable CLIP similarity. On the few-step SDXL Turbo (4 steps), improvements are even more pronounced, with GNRI achieving a 35.67% structure distance gain and 30.58% background preservation gain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method is evaluated as a plug-in enhancement on top of four existing inversion baselines: DDIM, ReNoise, NPI (Negative Prompt Inversion), and GNRI. Across all four baselines and both tasks (reconstruction and editing), the proposed rescheduling consistently improves every metric without adding parameters or inference cost. The largest relative gains appear on the weakest baseline (DDIM): on SDXL editing, DDIM's structure distance improves by 24.31% and background MSE drops by 5.99%. ReNoise, already the strongest reconstruction baseline, still benefits by 1.43% PSNR and 5.39% LPIPS reduction on MSCOCO. NPI shows moderate gains (1.25% PSNR, 3.63% LPIPS on reconstruction; 18.77% structure distance on SDXL editing). GNRI, the most advanced fixed-point baseline, sees smaller but consistent improvements (0.81% PSNR, 3.87% LPIPS on reconstruction; 4.62% structure distance on SDXL editing). On the extreme few-step setting (SDXL Turbo, 4 steps), gains amplify dramatically—GNRI achieves 35.67% structure distance and 30.58% background preservation improvement—confirming that the method is most impactful when the uniform timestep schedule is most suboptimal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the DDIM deterministic inversion framework (Song et al., 2021) and the fixed-point iteration approaches that refine local inversion errors (Meiri et al., 2023; Garibi et al., 2024; Samuel et al., 2025). Its key conceptual departure is shifting focus from per-step error correction to the global timestep scheduling: while prior inversion work treats timesteps as a fixed input and optimizes within each step, and while timestep scheduling has been studied for diffusion sampling (Xue et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024) and training (Wang et al., 2025), its role in inversion had been largely overlooked. The paper positions its method as a scheduler-level retrofit that is orthogonal to and composable with existing inversion formulations (DDIM, ReNoise, NPI, GNRI), rather than a replacement for them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value is clear: a parameter-free, zero-overhead rescheduling that consistently improves all tested inversion baselines across three model architectures (SD v1.5, SDXL, SDXL Turbo) makes it an easy drop-in enhancement for any diffusion inversion pipeline. However, the improvements, while consistent, are modest on most metrics (1–5% on PSNR/SSIM, 3–16% on LPIPS), and the theoretical analysis relies on a surrogate error coefficient rather than the exact inversion error, which the authors acknowledge may be less reliable under model mismatch or semantic editing conflicts. Open questions include whether the parabolic error pattern generalizes to stochastic inversion methods, alternative noise schedules, or video diffusion, and whether the local dynamic programming can be replaced by a principled global optimization once the exact error term is better characterized.
