## Problem

Implicit Neural Representations (INRs) based on vanilla ReLU MLPs are widely believed to be architecturally incapable of representing high-frequency content, driving the field toward architectural fixes like Fourier features and periodic activations. This paper challenges that view, arguing the low-frequency bias is not an intrinsic architectural limitation but a symptom of stable rank degradation in weight updates during training. The INR community has treated optimization as an implicit afterthought, almost universally defaulting to Adam, despite evidence from adjacent domains that the optimizer fundamentally shapes what a network can learn.

## Input / Output

The INR is a continuous function queried at arbitrary coordinates: 2D spatial coordinates (e.g., 768×512 for Kodak images) map to RGB values, 1D temporal coordinates map to audio amplitude, and 3D coordinates plus viewing direction map to volume density and radiance for NeRF. For inverse problems, the input is an undersampled measurement (100 CT projections from a 435×326 thoracic X-ray, or a 4×-downsampled DIV2K image), and the network is trained to be consistent with that measurement before being evaluated on the full-resolution grid. All experiments use small MLPs (4–8 layers, 32–300 hidden units), making the optimization-level gains particularly striking given the limited model capacity.

## Method

The paper introduces stable rank as a unifying lens: since the weight gradient is an outer product of upstream gradients and input activations, the effective dimensionality of each update is upper-bounded by the rank of the activations, which monotonically degrades with depth. Architectural interventions (Fourier features, SIREN, batch normalization) are reinterpreted as input- or activation-level rank-increasing strategies, but they do not prevent rank collapse in the weight updates themselves. The core contribution is an optimizer-level intervention: applying Muon, which maps each hidden-layer weight update to its Frobenius-nearest semi-orthogonal matrix via Newton-Schulz iteration, producing high-rank, scale-invariant updates that distribute changes evenly across all singular directions. In practice, Muon is applied only to hidden-layer weight matrices while Adam handles biases, input/output layers, and coordinate encodings, with a joint learning-rate grid search (81 configurations) to ensure fairness. This yields up to +9 dB PSNR over the same architecture trained with Adam, bringing a plain ReLU MLP to parity with Fourier-feature networks and improving every tested architecture across images, audio, shapes, CT, super-resolution, and NeRF.

## Results

On the Kodak image overfitting benchmark (24 images, 768×512), Muon consistently outperforms Adam across all seven INR architectures. The largest gains appear for ReLU-based models: ReLU MLP improves from 22.83 to 30.04 PSNR (+7.2 dB) and ReLU FFN from 31.21 to 40.34 PSNR (+9.1 dB), while WIRE jumps from 32.43 to 42.99 PSNR (+10.6 dB). For architectures already strong with Adam (SIREN: 39.33→41.43, FINER: 39.87→42.59), gains are smaller but still consistent (+2–3 dB). The headline finding is that a plain ReLU MLP trained with Muon (30.04 PSNR) nearly matches a ReLU FFN trained with Adam (31.21 PSNR), closing a gap previously attributed to architectural expressiveness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is Adam vs. Muon on identical architectures, with Muon winning on every architecture and every metric (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) across all tasks. The most striking result is that Muon-optimized ReLU MLP (30.04 PSNR) approaches Adam-optimized ReLU FFN (31.21 PSNR), effectively neutralizing the need for Fourier feature encoding for basic image fidelity. For already-expressive architectures like SIREN (39.33→41.43) and FINER (39.87→42.59), Muon still wins but with smaller margins (+2–3 dB), suggesting these architectures already maintain higher stable rank through their periodic activations. WIRE shows the largest absolute gain (+10.6 dB) but also the highest variance under Adam (±9.56), indicating Muon stabilizes training for complex-valued wavelet representations. The authors also benchmark against the Lion sign-based optimizer, which does not consistently preserve stable rank and trails Muon in both rank and reconstruction quality, confirming that the gains come specifically from orthogonalized updates rather than merely switching away from Adam. On inverse problems (sparse-view CT, SISR, NeRF), Muon again dominates: e.g., ReLU MLP CT reconstruction improves from 25.86 to 32.93 PSNR, and NeRF Hotdog from 28.69 to 33.17 PSNR.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant INR literature that attributes spectral bias to architectural limitations (requiring Fourier features, SIREN, WIRE, or FINER activations) and instead reframes it as an optimization problem of stable rank degradation during training. It builds on the stable rank analysis from deep learning theory (Daneshmand et al., 2020; Feng et al., 2022) and the orthogonalizing optimizer lineage (Carlson et al., 2015; Tuddenham et al., 2022; Jordan et al., 2024), applying them to INRs for the first time in a systematic way. The paper also positions itself against curvature-aware preconditioner work (Chng et al., 2025; Saratchandran et al., 2023) by showing that orthogonalized updates specifically target the rank collapse mechanism rather than merely accelerating convergence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a vanilla ReLU MLP trained with Muon reaches near-parity with a Fourier Feature Network trained with Adam (+7–8 dB PSNR) challenges a long-standing assumption in the INR community and suggests that optimization is a third, previously underexplored axis of INR design alongside input and activation-level interventions. The stable rank framework provides a unifying lens that explains why diverse architectural modifications (Fourier features, SIREN, batch normalization) all work by raising the effective rank of activations or updates. Key open questions remain: the framework does not predict an optimal target rank (maximal rank may hurt ill-posed inverse problems), Muon introduces additional learning-rate tuning complexity, and whether softer or adaptive forms of orthogonalization can better balance expressivity with implicit regularization for reconstruction tasks.
