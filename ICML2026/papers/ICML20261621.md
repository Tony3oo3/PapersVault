## Problem

Deployed vision-language models (VLMs) rely on undocumented vision preprocessing pipelines—patch size, resizing strategy, target resolution—that materially affect model behavior yet are treated as private deployment secrets. No prior work showed that these architectural parameters could be systematically extracted through standard black-box API queries alone, without gradients, timing, or hardware side channels. This paper asks whether the structural granularity of ViT patchification itself creates a detectable, exploitable signature in model outputs.

## Input / Output

The stealing attack inputs synthetic N×N grid images (typically N=6) with variable cell size D in pixels, rendered from a binary cube palette {0,255}³, optionally zero-padded to a canvas of size L×L with content anchored top-left, paired with a fixed text prompt asking the model to report the grid dimension. Each cell size is evaluated over M=200 i.i.d. color samples repeated 6 times, yielding ~4.8×10⁴ total API queries (~$10–50 at reduced sample counts). The output is a triple: the patch size P (in tokenizer-input pixels), the preprocessing class (dynamic vs. fixed resolution), and the fixed target resolution S when applicable. The downstream adversarial attack takes standard RGB images (NIPS 2017 dataset, 1000 images) and produces ℓ∞-bounded perturbations (ε=8/255) that selectively fool one target VLM while remaining benign to a non-target.

## Method

The core mechanism is Patch-Size Matching (PSM): when the effective cell size D_eff equals an integer multiple kP of the patch size, every P×P patch falls entirely inside a single constant-color grid cell, so boundary cues are systematically absent from patch tokens and the model's accuracy collapses. This produces periodic accuracy valleys whose period encodes P. The three-stage black-box attack first sweeps D on unpadded images to detect dynamic-resolution pipelines (stable periodic valleys directly reveal P) versus fixed-resolution ones (smooth, aperiodic curves). For fixed-resolution models, Stage 2 zero-pads the grid to multiple canvas sizes L, exploiting the linear rescaling D_eff=(S/L)·D to recover the ratio S/P from the period T(L)=PL/S at each L. Stage 3 disambiguates candidate resolutions via a paired consistency test: the true target S produces answers more consistent with the model's own output on the un-resized image (fewer extra resampling steps), selected by a one-sided z-test over non-tie wins. Period identification is made objective via a Welch-style t-statistic with permutation p-values. The recovered (S, P) then enables a contrastive adversarial objective that hides perturbation energy in frequency bands preserved by the target's pipeline but aliased away by the non-target's, achieving model-specific attack success while sparing the other model.

## Results

On the NIPS 2017 Adversarial Challenge dataset (1,000 images, ε=8/255), the model-targeted attack achieves 86.5% ASR on GPT-4o while suppressing Claude 3.7 to 4.5% ASR, outperforming the model-agnostic baseline (78.5% / 13.5%) by +8.0% on the target and −9.0% on the non-target. The stealing attack reliably recovers patch size P and preprocessing type across all tested models: P=16 for Qwen3-VL 30B and GPT-4.1-mini (dynamic), P=14 for Qwen2.5-VL 72B and Claude 3.7/4.5 (dynamic), and P=16 with fixed target resolution S=512 for GPT-4o. The consistency check (Stage 3) identifies S=512 for GPT-4o with z-statistics ≥10.2 against all alternative candidates at α=10⁻³.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its model-targeted attack against two prior black-box VLM attack methods: SSA-CWA (Dong et al., 2023) and AnyAttack (Zhang et al., 2025). The targeted-to-GPT-4o attack achieves 86.5% ASR on GPT-4o, substantially outperforming SSA-CWA (42.3%) and AnyAttack (36.3%) by +44.2 and +50.2 percentage points respectively. On the non-target (Claude 3.7), the targeted attack suppresses ASR to 4.5%, well below both SSA-CWA (9.1%) and AnyAttack (7.8%), demonstrating selective model manipulation. The model-agnostic baseline (Eq. 13) already achieves 78.5% on GPT-4o but cannot distinguish between models (13.5% on Claude 3.7), whereas the targeted variant simultaneously boosts the target and suppresses the non-target. The key advantage over prior methods is the use of stolen preprocessing parameters (S=512, P=16 for GPT-4o; dynamic, P=14 for Claude 3.7) to craft perturbations that exploit pipeline-specific frequency responses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the property-inference and model-stealing literature (Wang & Gong 2018; Duddu et al. 2018; Tramer et al. 2016) by targeting a previously unexploited component: the vision tokenizer's patch size and preprocessing pipeline. Unlike prior property-inference work that relies on timing side channels or query-response distributions, here the side channel is purely task-level behavioral — periodic accuracy collapses induced by ViT patchification alignment (Patch-Size Matching). The adversarial-attack component builds directly on Hu et al. 2025's transfer-attack framework for black-box VLMs, but the key departure is using stolen preprocessing parameters to construct a contrastive, model-targeted objective rather than a generic ensemble-averaged perturbation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that 'implementation details' in the vision frontend (patch size, resize resolution, dynamic vs. fixed pipeline) are extractable for roughly $10–50 in API calls, reframing them as security-relevant secrets rather than benign deployment choices. This opens a new attack surface: preprocessing-aware transfer attacks and surgical model-targeted manipulations that remain invisible to other VLMs. Open questions include whether randomized or stochastic preprocessing, convolutional patch division, or query-rate controls can effectively mitigate the side channel, and whether the approach generalizes to other architectural choices (e.g., patch overlap, positional encoding granularity) or to non-ViT vision encoders.
