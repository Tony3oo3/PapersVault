## Problem

In adaptive network recruitment (e.g., respondent-driven sampling for HIV outreach), allocating limited resources to individuals stochastically generates new recruitment opportunities, making the set of future decision-makers endogenous to current allocation choices. Existing frameworks for budgeted stochastic control (stochastic knapsack, bandits with knapsacks, prophet inequalities) assume a fixed or exogenously evolving action set, and the resulting multi-round Bellman recursion over distribution-valued, variable-size frontiers is computationally intractable. This paper asks: how can one plan optimally across multiple rounds when allocation decisions simultaneously consume budget, yield immediate reward, and reshape the distribution of future opportunities?

## Input / Output

At each round the algorithm receives a realized frontier of n individuals, each characterized by an arrival distribution over ℕ (the number of recruits they can generate, learned from covariates via count-data models), together with the remaining budget r and a population-level distribution P governing future recruits. The output is a two-level decision: a scalar round budget s ∈ {0,…,r} governing intertemporal trade-offs, and a per-individual allocation vector k ∈ ℕⁿ (summing to at most s) that distributes resources within the current frontier. The process iterates until the budget is exhausted or the frontier empties, with newly recruited individuals forming the next round's frontier.

## Method

The core insight is a clean separation: within each round, greedy allocation by marginal survival probabilities is provably optimal (Theorem 4.2), reducing the intra-round problem to a deterministic rule; the remaining decision is the scalar round budget s. To handle the intractable multi-round Bellman recursion—where the next frontier size is random and the state is a high-dimensional distribution-valued object—the paper introduces a population-level surrogate value function U_P(r, n) that depends only on remaining budget and frontier size, exploiting the exchangeability of i.i.d.-drawn future individuals. Under this surrogate, even allocation is optimal (Proposition 6.1), and the exact transition distribution of the next frontier size is computed via truncated probability generating functions, yielding a dynamic program with O(b²) space and O(b⁵ log b) time for total budget b. A multi-round robustness theorem (Theorem 7.2) then decomposes suboptimality under noisy distributional estimates into three interpretable terms: frontier-level estimation error, population-model misspecification error, and intrinsic surrogate approximation error from population heterogeneity.

## Results

The paper evaluates on the ICPSR 22140 HIV network with maximum budget b=200, discount factors γ∈{0.5, 0.7, 0.9}, and initial frontier sizes n∈{5, 10, 15}, reporting mean accumulated discounted reward over 30 independent runs. Results are presented as plots (Figures 3–4) rather than a numerical table, showing that π^our outperforms even the best-tuned constant-allocation baselines (Const(k)) for most settings across both simulated and realized referral dynamics. Both constant and greedy baselines exhibit a concave trend in their tuning parameter (k or α), confirming that suboptimal fixed choices degrade performance. The authors note that in some disease networks (Chlamydia, Gonorrhea) at high discount factor γ=0.9, greedy-based methods outperform their surrogate, suggesting limitations under significant model misspecification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two families of baselines: constant-allocation policies Const(k) for k∈{2,3,5,10} (the standard RDS operational guideline) and greedy scheduling policies Greedy(α)/GreedyRemainder(α) for α∈{0.1,0.2,0.5,1.0} that fix the within-round allocation to the single-round-optimal greedy rule but vary the intertemporal budget schedule. For most configurations on the HIV network (both simulated and realized referrals), π^our achieves the highest accumulated discounted reward, outperforming even the best-tuned Const(k) and Greedy(α) variants. The constant baselines (Heckathorn 1997; Goel & Salganik 2009) are the most directly comparable operational strategies, and π^our's advantage is attributed to its ability to adapt the round budget dynamically rather than committing to a single fixed k. However, the authors acknowledge that in Chlamydia and Gonorrhea networks at γ=0.9 (Appendix D), greedy-based methods outperform the surrogate, indicating that the population-level approximation can be suboptimal under high long-term planning requirements combined with model misspecification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from classical budgeted allocation frameworks (stochastic knapsack, bandits with knapsacks, prophet inequalities) by introducing endogenous state evolution: allocation decisions stochastically generate the next decision frontier, creating a variable-size, distribution-valued state space akin to branching MDPs. The single-round greedy optimality result builds on the discrete concavity structure of survival-probability decompositions, while the multi-round planning leverages probability generating functions—a standard tool in branching process theory—to compute a population-level surrogate value function exactly. The approach connects to approximate dynamic programming but distinguishes itself by using a structured, exactly computable surrogate rather than learned or Monte Carlo approximations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, polynomial-time planning algorithm for adaptive network recruitment with interpretable robustness guarantees, directly relevant to public-health applications like HIV respondent-driven sampling. A key limitation acknowledged by the authors is that the population-level surrogate can underperform greedy baselines under significant model misspecification combined with high discount factors (e.g., Chlamydia/Gonorrhea networks at γ=0.9), suggesting the abstraction loses decision-relevant heterogeneity in some regimes. Open questions include designing surrogates that retain more frontier-composition information, handling strategic participant behavior, and extending to richer operational constraints.
