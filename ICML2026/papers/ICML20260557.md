## Problem

Fully 4-bit (NVFP4) training of LLMs remains significantly worse than BF16 training, and prior FP4 recipes either retain a subset of Transformer blocks in BF16 or suffer from two under-addressed optimization failures: weight oscillation (quantized weights repeatedly flipping between adjacent bins as learning rate decays) and structural activation outliers that exceed FP4's representable range. The paper identifies that the performance gap cannot be explained by quantization error alone but is driven by these optimization pathologies.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard text tokens from the OLMo-2-Mix-1124 corpus and produces a fully NVFP4-quantized LLM (70M–370M parameters) where all linear-layer activations, weights, and gradients are stored in E2M1 with per-16-element E4M3 scales. A small structural exception is made: the top 10% of activation channels by accumulated L2-norm are retained in FP8 in both forward and backward passes, so the final model is FP4-weighted but uses a thin FP8 path for outlier channels during training.

## Method

TetraJet-v2 combines three mechanisms. (1) An unbiased double-block NVFP4 linear layer: a 1×128 outer FP32 scale handles the E4M3 range limit, a 1×16 inner E4M3 scale provides fine-grained quantization, and stochastic rounding with forward/backward alignment of the quantized activation X̂ guarantees unbiased gradient estimates. (2) OsciReset: periodically (every 200 steps after a start threshold) computes OsciRisk = dist_Q/dist_M per weight element over a 50-step window; elements exceeding a threshold (τ=8) are reset to their quantization-bin center, eliminating oscillation without freezing parameters. (3) OutControl: statically selects the top-p% outlier channels via a brief calibration window and computes their products in FP8, while applying Random Hadamard Transform (block size 16) to both dX and dW matrix multiplications in the backward pass to further suppress residual outliers. Together these reduce the BF16 performance gap by 51.3% on average and yield 1.67× end-to-end speedup over FP8 on RTX 5090.

## Results

On OLMo-2 pre-training (70M/150M/370M parameters, 52B/107B/212B tokens), TetraJet-v2-full achieves validation perplexity of 47.75/34.95/25.11, reducing the gap to BF16 (45.27/33.49/23.70) by an average of 51.3% compared to prior FP4 methods. On downstream tasks for the 370M model, TetraJet-v2-full attains the highest average accuracy (43.60%) among all FP4 methods, approaching BF16 (44.13%). The method also achieves a 1.67× end-to-end speedup over FP8 (TransformerEngine) on a full Transformer layer, with 1.83× speedup on linear layers alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TetraJet-v2-full consistently outperforms all five compared FP4 baselines across model sizes. Against the two primary baselines in the main table, it beats Quartet (Castro et al., 2025) by 1.01/1.94/1.05 in validation PPL at 70M/150M/370M, and NVIDIA's NVFP4 recipe (NVIDIA et al., 2025) by 3.19/1.78/1.09, respectively. In the expanded comparison (Table 12), the advantage is even more pronounced: Wang et al. (2025) achieves Val PPL of 97.22/82.96 (70M/150M), far worse than TetraJet-v2-full's 47.75/34.95, while Tseng et al. (2025, MXFP4) and Cook et al. (2026, NVFP4) score 51.17/37.18 and 50.99/36.65, still trailing TetraJet-v2-full by 3.4–4.2 PPL points. On downstream tasks (370M), TetraJet-v2-full leads all FP4 methods in average accuracy (43.60% vs. 42.79% for Quartet and 42.77% for NVIDIA), with notable gains on BoolQ (59.23% vs. 56.54%/56.48%) and CQA (37.18% vs. 34.23%/34.72%). The method also generalizes to the Muon optimizer, maintaining its lead over the NVIDIA baseline (38.73 vs. 40.67 Val PPL at 150M).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends TetraJet (Chen et al., 2025b), which addressed MXFP4 training for Vision Transformers, to the NVFP4 LLM pre-training setting, and builds on the Random Hadamard Transformation approach from Tseng et al. (2025) for backward-pass outlier control. It departs from NVIDIA's NVFP4 pre-training recipe by enforcing a strict fully-4-bit configuration (NVIDIA kept a subset of Transformer blocks in BF16) and by ensuring unbiased gradient estimation through stochastic rounding and forward/backward activation alignment. A key conceptual departure is from prior oscillation-suppression methods (Nagel et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025b), which targeted QAT or ViT fine-tuning; the paper demonstrates these do not transfer to LLM pre-training and instead proposes OsciReset, a simpler reset-to-bin-center strategy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Fully FP4 training on Blackwell hardware is a major efficiency target (3× compute over MXFP8, 2× lower memory), and this paper provides the first systematic diagnosis of why prior FP4 recipes fall short—identifying weight oscillation and outlier sensitivity as optimization bottlenecks orthogonal to raw quantization error. The 1.67× end-to-end speedup over FP8 with a 51.3% reduction in the BF16 gap is practically significant for large-scale pre-training cost. However, experiments are limited to 370M parameters and 212B tokens, and the absolute gap to BF16 remains non-trivial (e.g., 25.11 vs. 23.70 validation PPL at 370M), leaving open whether OsciReset and OutControl scale to multi-billion-parameter models or MoE architectures. The paper also raises the interesting but underexplored question of whether a late-stage precision switch (FP6×FP4) could close the remaining gap at acceptable hardware cost.
