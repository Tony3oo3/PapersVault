## Problem

Standard Adaptive Self-Consistency (ASC) methods for LLM inference use prior-free stopping rules that ignore the shape of the answer-frequency distribution (peaked for easy queries, flat for hard queries), leading to unnecessary over-sampling. While a Bayesian stopping rule that incorporates this prior information is conceptually natural, the exact posterior requires iterating over all K! answer permutations, making it computationally intractable for real-time inference. The paper asks: can we design a tractable posterior approximation that provably recovers the optimal stopping rate?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequential stream of LLM-sampled answers (only identity/equality is observed, not answer content), represented as a count-of-counts multiset, along with an answer-frequency prior π (either fully known or drawn from a candidate set Π^M with hyper-prior weights). It outputs a stopping time n* and the identified mode answer. A key constraint is that the observation model is anonymous: the algorithm sees only group sizes of identical answers, not which latent label each group corresponds to, which is what makes the posterior combinatorially complex.

## Method

The core contribution is an L-aggregated posterior approximation that tracks only the top (L−1) most frequent answer counts explicitly while aggregating all remaining answers into a single 'other' bucket, reducing posterior computation from O(K!) to O(K^L · n̄²). The stopping rule is a simple posterior-threshold test: halt when P(H₁|C_n^L) ≥ 1−δ, where H₁ is the hypothesis that the most-frequently observed answer is the true mode. The key theoretical result is that L=3 (tracking the top-2 counts plus an aggregate) achieves the same asymptotic expected stopping time as the exact L=K posterior, i.e., E[n*]/log(1/δ) → 1/[(p₁−p₂)log(p₁/p₂)], while L=2 is strictly suboptimal. For uncertain priors, the posterior is extended with a hyper-prior-weighted sum over candidate distributions, and L≥3 still strictly dominates prior-free ASC baselines, though L=3 no longer matches the exact posterior rate when the true prior is confounded with another candidate.

## Results

On synthetic data (K=8, π=(0.5,0.2,0.1,0.1,0.05,0.03,0.01,0.01)), L=3 achieves near-identical stopping times to the exact posterior (e.g., 18.07 vs. 18.13 generations at 1−δ=0.99) while running in 14.2 ms vs. 29.8 ms for the exact method. On real-world FEVAL-TTC (CommonsenseQA), the L=3 uncertain-prior method reduces sampling cost by over 80% at 1−δ=0.95 (1.00 vs. 5.28 generations for Qwen-2.5-72B) while maintaining comparable answer accuracy (86.3% vs. 87.2%). At higher confidence (1−δ=0.99), the method still saves ~22% samples (6.23 vs. 8.04 generations) with matching answer accuracy (88.1%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is Adaptive Self-Consistency (ASC) from Aggarwal et al. (2023), which uses a Beta-posterior with an uninformative prior. The proposed L-aggregated Bayesian method uniformly outperforms ASC on sample efficiency: at 1−δ=0.95 on CommonsenseQA, it requires only 1.00 generations (Qwen, GPT-4o) or 4.58 (LLaMA) versus 5.28, 5.10, and 7.55 for ASC respectively — an 81% reduction for the first two models. On answer accuracy, the method is within 0.2–1.8 percentage points of ASC, and in some configurations (e.g., LLaMA at 1−δ=0.95) it actually matches or exceeds ASC. In the synthetic experiment (Table 1), ASC is also poorly calibrated: it over-samples at high confidence (44.07 generations at 1−δ=0.99 vs. 18.13 for the exact method) while under-sampling at low confidence (only 1.00 generation at 1−δ=0.7, achieving just 50.4% mode accuracy vs. 76.0% for L=3). The paper's key finding is that L=3 replicates the sample efficiency of the intractable exact posterior (L=K) starting at 1−δ=0.8, while running in 14.2 ms versus 29.8 ms for the exact method.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Adaptive Self-Consistency (ASC) by Aggarwal et al. (2023), which uses Beta-posterior updates with an uninformative prior to stop sampling once a dominant answer emerges, by introducing informative Bayesian priors over the answer-frequency distribution. The theoretical backbone follows Jain et al. (2022) on PAC mode estimation, but the key departure is formalizing optimal stopping under an informative prior for mode identification—a problem the authors note was previously vacuous because a naive prior would 'give away the mode.' The L-aggregated posterior approximation is a new computational tool that reduces the intractable O(K!) exact posterior to O(K^L), enabling real-time deployment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: the method achieves 50–80%+ reductions in LLM sampling cost while maintaining or even improving answer accuracy, which directly lowers inference costs for test-time scaling. The clean theoretical result that L=3 (tracking only the top-2 answer counts) achieves asymptotic optimality gives practitioners a simple, well-justified implementation choice. Open questions include the cold-start problem (no historical data for prior estimation), robustness to biased priors, and the intriguing observation that early stopping can sometimes recover the true answer via runner-up candidates when the mode is wrong—understanding when this happens is left for future work.
