## Problem

Spectral GNNs for heterophilous graphs suffer from three coupled pathologies: hub domination (high-degree nodes overwhelm low-degree spoke clusters during aggregation), suboptimal polynomial bases that assume continuous orthogonality invalid on discrete graph spectra (causing frequency leakage), and progressive oversmoothing/oversquashing with depth. Existing fixes—signed message passing, chunked aggregation, or higher-order polynomials—either introduce sign-cancellation across layers, mix signals across unrelated graph regions, or remain computationally intractable at scale.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sparse graph adjacency matrix and a node feature matrix, then recursively coarsens the graph via k-means++ clustering on encoder embeddings at a user-specified ratio R (typically 0.4–0.5) until a threshold size is reached. For node classification, the output is a per-node embedding formed by additive skip-connected unpooling that sums the base-level features with filtered summaries from every hierarchical level; for graph classification, the single coarsest supernode embedding serves as the graph representation. The entire pipeline runs in near-linear time in the number of edges, avoiding the O(n³) eigendecomposition of prior spectral pooling methods.

## Method

HMH first learns an adaptive signed adjacency at every propagation layer by combining a learned feature-affinity score (MLP on concatenated embeddings) with a structural-similarity score (Jaccard overlap of two-hop neighborhoods), mapped through softmax to [0,1] and then to [-1,1] edge weights—positive for similar nodes (low-pass averaging) and negative for dissimilar nodes (high-pass sharpening). This layer-wise recomputation breaks the fixed-matrix sign-flipping pathology of standard SMP. The encoder embeddings then drive a soft k-means++ coarsening hierarchy; at each level, a sparse orthonormal Haar basis is assembled from a global scaling vector, inter-cluster contrast wavelets, and strictly local intra-cluster wavelets, all supported only within their respective clusters. Diagonal learnable gain matrices in this Haar domain selectively amplify heterophilous (high-frequency) channels while attenuating hub-dominated low-frequency components. Finally, skip-connected unpooling reintegrates filtered signals from all levels back to the original nodes, and the logarithmic path-length reduction from coarsening provably prevents exponential gradient decay (oversquashing) regardless of depth.

## Results

On node classification, HMH achieves up to 98.60% on Coauthor-Physics, 96.80% on Coauthor-CS, and 87.40% on Minesweeper (ROC-AUC), outperforming all spectral and heterophilous baselines on most heterophilic benchmarks. On the large-scale OGBN-Arxiv (169K nodes), HMH reaches 73.30% accuracy, surpassing OptBasisGNN (72.27%) and TFE-GNN (71.34%). For graph classification, HMH achieves 94.50% on MUTAG and 80.7% on NCI109, exceeding the best pooling baselines by up to 4.8 percentage points. The paper also demonstrates that HMH maintains >98% accuracy on the Tree-Neighbors-Match oversquashing benchmark at depth 10, where GIN and ChebNet drop to random-guess (50%) by depth 8.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HMH outperforms all 15+ baselines on 9 of 11 node-classification datasets, with the largest margins on heterophilic benchmarks: +0.55% over TFE-GNN on Minesweeper (87.40 vs 86.85), +3.56% over SLOG on Tolokers (84.56 vs 81.00), +1.36% over GPR-GNN on Roman-empire (76.10 vs 74.74), and +1.03% over OptBasisGNN on OGBN-Arxiv (73.30 vs 72.27). On homophilic graphs, HMH leads on CS (96.80 vs 96.10 TFE-GNN) and Physics (98.60 vs 97.85 TFE-GNN) but trails TFE-GNN on Citeseer (81.50 vs 82.83) and SLOG on Chameleon (41.20 vs 43.02). Among spectral polynomial baselines (ChebNet II, BernNet, JacobiConv, OptBasisGNN), HMH consistently surpasses them by 2–15 points on heterophilic datasets, confirming the advantage of localized Haar bases over global polynomial filters. For graph classification, HMH achieves the best MUTAG score (94.50% vs 89.7% Mincut-Pool) and NCI109 (80.7% vs 77.13% DiffPool), though it is slightly behind MaxCut-Pool on NCI1 (80.9% vs 83.2%) and PROTEINS (76.8% vs 77.1%). The hub-domination stratified analysis (Table 3) shows HMH narrows the Spoke-to-Hub accuracy gap by up to 10.8% on Penn94 compared to GCN, while improving all degree cohorts simultaneously on Squirrel (+9.1% on Spokes). On the oversquashing benchmark (Tree-Neighbors-Match), HMH sustains >98% accuracy at tree depth 10, whereas GIN and ChebNet collapse to 50% (random) by depth 8.

## Relation to Prior Work

HMH sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) spectral GNNs that approximate the graph Laplacian eigenbasis via polynomials (ChebNet, GPR-GNN, BernNet, JacobiConv, OptBasisGNN), (2) signed message passing introduced by GPR-GNN (Chien et al., 2021) and extended by Chunked Message Passing (Pei et al., 2024) to handle heterophily, and (3) hierarchical graph pooling (DiffPool, SAGPool, EigenPool, Haar-based pooling). The key departure is replacing global, dense polynomial bases with sparse, orthonormal Haar wavelet bases constructed bottom-up from a learned graph hierarchy, and replacing fixed label-based signed adjacency with layer-wise adaptive feature/structure-driven signed weights. This combination—adaptive signing + localized wavelet filtering + multi-scale skip-connection fusion—is novel in the GNN literature, though each component draws on established signal-processing and graph-coarsening ideas.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides rigorous theoretical proofs (Theorems 4.1, 5.3–5.5) that hub domination, oversmoothing, and oversquashing are inherent pathologies of standard SMP/CMA and polynomial spectral filters, and that the Haar-based hierarchical construction provably mitigates all three. This is valuable because prior work largely addressed these issues empirically without formal guarantees. The near-linear scalability makes the approach practical for large graphs (demonstrated on OGBN-Arxiv with ~170K nodes). However, the method is limited to static, undirected, single-relation graphs, and the performance gains over strong baselines (3% on node classification, 7% on graph classification) are moderate rather than transformative. Open questions include extending the framework to dynamic graphs, multi-relational/directed settings, and whether the k-means++ coarsening step can be made fully differentiable for end-to-end training.
