## Problem

Modern time-series forecasting models have grown architecturally complex, yet it remains unclear whether reported SOTA gains stem from genuine modeling advances or from improved data views and favorable evaluation configurations. Existing benchmarks treat models as monolithic black boxes (the Attribution Gap) and rely on fragile point estimates that fall into either the 'Fairness Trap' or the 'Best Trap' (the Benchmarking Crisis), making performance attribution and reproducibility fundamentally unreliable.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes multivariate time series X ∈ R^{T×N} and a specified architectural component variant (the Evaluated Object, e.g., a particular Embedding or Encoder choice) at one of five pipeline stages. Rather than producing a single forecast, it outputs three distributional statistics—marginalized performance μ̂, stability σ̂, and peak potential L_best—computed over K=600 stratified paired Evaluation Conditions spanning six datasets, four look-back lengths, four horizons, multiple hidden dimensions, encoder depths, and learning rates. All components are assessed under the identical paired EC set, ensuring variance is attributable solely to the module under investigation.

## Method

CombinationTS decomposes the forecasting pipeline f = T_out^{-1} ∘ D ∘ Φ ∘ E ∘ T_in into five orthogonal, interchangeable modules and evaluates each under a shared probabilistic Evaluation Condition Space Ω grounded in Evaluatology. A stratified Monte Carlo protocol samples K=600 paired conditions (dataset, look-back, horizon, encoder layers, latent dim, learning rate) so that every Evaluated Object is assessed under exactly the same stochastic environment, eliminating confounding from random sampling. Performance is summarized via marginalized mean μ̂, standard deviation σ̂, and best-case L_best, enabling a principled effectiveness-stability trade-off analysis. The key empirical finding—the 'Identity Paradox'—shows that once the Embedding (data view) is well-designed (e.g., Patch-wise or Variate-wise), a parameter-free Identity Encoder achieves statistically significant lower MSE than Transformer or MLP encoders (Mann-Whitney U, p<0.0001), shifting the burden of proof onto architectural complexity. Additional experiments reveal that explicit periodic inductive biases (CycleNet-style) yield consistent gains, naive decomposition without cross-component interaction degrades performance, and spectral (Fourier/Wavelet) encoders improve μ over attention without reducing σ.

## Results

The headline finding is the 'Identity Paradox': on ETT datasets, the parameter-free Identity encoder achieves substantially lower MSE than both Transformer and MLP encoders (e.g., μ=0.4392 vs. 0.5095/0.4960 on ETTh1; μ=0.2621 vs. 0.2909/0.2784 on ETTm2), with roughly half the instability (σ=0.0424 vs. 0.1004/0.0938 on ETTh1). Mann-Whitney U tests confirm the advantage is statistically significant (p<0.0001 vs. Transformer, p=0.0115 vs. MLP across all datasets combined). Among Input Transformations, the Cycle prior (CycleNet) yields the most consistent gains in both μ and σ over the RevIN baseline, while naive Trend-Seasonal and Multi-Scale decomposition frequently fail to beat the baseline. Spectral processing (SimpleTM) improves μ over iTransformer (e.g., 0.4324 vs. 0.4401 on ETTh1) but provides no significant reduction in σ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against RevIN (Kim et al., 2021) as the normalization baseline, the Cycle input transformation (CycleNet, Lin et al., 2024) consistently outperforms it in both μ and σ across all six datasets (e.g., μ=0.3891 vs. 0.3975 on ETTh1; μ=0.1999 vs. 0.2061 on Electricity), confirming that explicit periodic inductive biases are more efficient than implicit learning. Naive Trend-Seasonal decomposition (DLinear, Zeng et al., 2023) and Multi-Scale downsampling (TimeMixer, Wang et al., 2024a) frequently fail to beat the RevIN baseline without cross-component interaction; TimeMixer's full architecture (with mixing) achieves lower L_best (e.g., 0.3610 on ETTh1) but this is not directly comparable under the paired EC protocol. For the encoder comparison, the Identity encoder outperforms iTransformer (Liu et al., 2024) on four of six datasets in μ (ETTh1: 0.4392 vs. 0.4401; ETTm2: 0.2621 vs. 0.2870; ETTm1: 0.3721 vs. 0.3911; Weather: 0.2348 vs. 0.2412) with substantially lower σ, while iTransformer retains a slight edge on Electricity (0.1776 vs. 0.1888). SimpleTM (Chen et al., 2025a) outperforms iTransformer in μ across all datasets (e.g., 0.4324 vs. 0.4401 on ETTh1; 0.2697 vs. 0.2870 on ETTm2) but shows comparable or slightly higher σ, indicating spectral gains stem from better signal representation rather than robustness. On the non-stationary Exchange-Rate dataset, PatchLinear (Patch-wise + Identity) achieves the lowest average MSE (0.357), outperforming both iTransformer (0.360) and PatchTST (Nie et al., 2023) (0.367), confirming the Identity Paradox generalizes beyond periodic benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

CombinationTS builds on the Evaluatology framework (Zhan et al., 2024; 2025) for probabilistic, effect-attribution-based evaluation and applies it to time-series forecasting for the first time. It departs from prior TSF benchmarking tools (TSLib, BasicTS, TFB, TAB) that standardize implementation interfaces but treat models as monolithic black boxes, by decomposing architectures into five orthogonal modules (Input Transformation, Embedding, Encoder, Decoder, Output Transformation) and evaluating them under a shared stratified Monte Carlo condition space. The paper also responds directly to the 'reproducibility crisis' findings of Brigato et al. (2026) and Chen et al. (2025b), which showed that SOTA claims are statistically fragile, by replacing point-estimate leaderboard comparisons with distributional statistics (μ, σ) over paired evaluation conditions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Identity Paradox—that a parameter-free encoder matches or beats Transformers when the embedding is well-designed—establishes a new burden of proof for architectural complexity in TSF, forcing future work to demonstrate consistent gains in both marginalized performance and stability over strong modular baselines rather than relying on fragile leaderboard peaks. The probabilistic evaluation protocol (stratified paired MC sampling over a shared condition space) provides a reusable methodology that could be adopted across the broader ML evaluation community. Key limitations include coverage of only six standard benchmarks, a fixed set of modular alternatives, and the assumption of clean boundaries between pipeline stages; open questions include how data-specific characteristics (periodicity, non-stationarity, cross-variate dependency) interact with modular choices and whether automated composition strategies can select optimal module combinations per dataset.
