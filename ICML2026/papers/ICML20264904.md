## Problem

Action chunking improves temporal consistency at moderate frequencies but breaks down at high frequencies (e.g., 60 Hz), where policies trained directly in action space produce imprecise, jittery trajectories due to the high temporal information density and fine-grained spatial variation. Additionally, asynchronous inference—necessary for real-time execution—introduces discontinuities at chunk boundaries that cause visible stalls and rollback in robot motion.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264904/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy consumes RGB images, proprioceptive state, and (for VLA variants) language instructions, and outputs a 48-step high-frequency action chunk at 60 Hz covering ~0.8 s, where each step is a 10-dimensional vector (Cartesian xyz, roll-pitch-yaw orientation, gripper width). Internally, the VAE compresses each 48-step chunk into a 12-step continuous latent (temporal downsampling ratio f=4, latent dimension d=10), so the policy predicts 12 latent vectors that are decoded back into 48 high-frequency actions. The VAE reconstruction error is sub-millimeter (≤0.50 mm per axis) across all three real-world tasks.

## Method

The approach has two components. First, a VAE (1D-conv encoder with 2 layers at kernel 5/stride 2, MLP decoder with 2 layers, diagonal Gaussian latent with KL weight β=1e-6) is trained to compress 48-step high-frequency action chunks into 12-step continuous latents; the policy is then trained to predict these latents conditioned on observations, shifting the learning target from fine-grained per-step commands to coherent short-horizon motion patterns on a regularized latent manifold. Second, Reuse-then-Refine (RTR) is a training-free execution strategy for asynchronous inference: during the inference window, previously executed actions are concatenated with the non-outdated portion of the newly predicted chunk, and the resulting temporally misaligned sequence is passed through the frozen VAE encoder-decoder to produce a refined chunk that transitions seamlessly from the prior chunk. RTR adds only ~2 ms of overhead and is specifically designed for latent-space policies, where the VAE's continuity properties smooth boundary discontinuities—unlike RT-C, which degrades continuity when applied in latent space.

## Results

Under synchronous real-robot execution (Table 1), latent-space training consistently reduces jerk by 3–10× across all three policies and tasks. The improvement is most dramatic for OFT, where success rate jumps from 28% to 74% on Peel Cucumber and from 74% to 100% on Write Board, while jerk drops from 5.238 to 0.558 (Write Board). Under asynchronous inference with RTR (Table 3), OFT's jerk on Peel Cucumber drops from 10.005 (Original) to 1.540 (Latent+RTR), and PI0.5's success rate on Peel Cucumber improves from 72% to 80%. On the LIBERO simulation benchmark, latent versions match or slightly exceed original success rates (e.g., PI0.5-Latent: 90.65% avg vs. 89.85% for PI0.5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper applies its latent-space VAE compression to three base policies (DP, OFT, PI0.5) and compares each against its original action-space counterpart. The improvement is most pronounced for OFT, whose discrete action tokenization causes severe quantization errors at 60 Hz: latent training raises Peel Cucumber success from 28% to 74% and reduces Write Board jerk by ~9× (5.238→0.558). For DP and PI0.5, which already handle high-frequency actions more gracefully, latent training still yields 3–5× jerk reduction and fewer safety-threshold exceedances. Against RT-C (Black et al., 2025), the proposed RTR strategy achieves substantially lower boundary gaps under asynchronous inference (e.g., PI0.5: 4.069 mm vs. 4.640 mm for RT-C; OFT: 4.867 mm vs. 15.022 mm for Original), and critically, applying RT-C in latent space actually degrades continuity (8.478 mm boundary gap vs. 6.842 mm for Latent alone), whereas RTR consistently improves it. On the LIBERO simulation benchmark, ACT-Latent achieves 83.8% average success vs. 82.3% for ACT, and PI0.5-Latent reaches 90.65% vs. 89.85%, confirming that the latent representation does not sacrifice task-level generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the action-chunking paradigm established by Diffusion Policy, OpenVLA-OFT, and PI0.5 to high-frequency (60 Hz) regimes where direct action-space learning degrades, by compressing action chunks into a continuous VAE latent space before policy training. The VAE-based compression draws on the latent-space philosophy from image/video generation (latent diffusion), while the Reuse-then-Refine (RTR) execution strategy departs from RT-C, which is tailored to flow-matching/diffusion models in action space and is shown to be ineffective—or even harmful—when naively transferred to latent space. Prior latent-action work (VQ-VLA, LatentVLA, RDP) uses discrete tokens or serves different purposes; this paper is the first to use a continuous VAE specifically to make high-frequency action chunking tractable across diverse policy architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results show that a simple VAE (f=4 downsampling) plus a training-free refinement step can dramatically improve high-frequency control—e.g., OFT's success rate on Peel Cucumber jumps from 28% to 74% under synchronous execution—making smooth, continuous robot execution practical without architectural changes to the base policy. The finding that RT-C degrades continuity in latent space is a useful caution against assuming that execution strategies transfer across representations. Open questions include whether the approach scales to even higher frequencies (90–120 Hz), whether RTR can be adapted for action-space policies, and how it generalizes beyond the three contact-rich tasks and single-arm setup evaluated here.
