## Problem

Instance stability (sensitivity to removing a single training sample) is a well-established lens for understanding generalization and the benefits of bagging, but no analogous measure exists for sensitivity to removing a single feature—the axis on which feature bagging actually operates. The paper asks whether feature bagging provably improves stability along this feature axis, and if so, how the stability gains depend on subsampling aggressiveness and ensemble size.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263158/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a dataset D = (X, y) with n instances and d features, a base algorithm A₀, instance and feature subsampling ratios p and q, and a number of bagging rounds B. It produces two instability scalars—instance instability (II) and the newly defined feature instability (FI)—each computed as the average squared prediction change after removing one instance or one feature, respectively, under a shared-randomness (or affected-axis resampling) coupling. In the linear-model setting the output is an exact asymptotic decomposition into variance and bias terms with closed-form limits; in the model-free setting the output is an upper bound of the form rad²(W)·q/((d−1)(1−q)) times a recursion-accumulation factor.

## Method

The paper introduces Feature Instability (FI), the leave-one-feature-out analogue of classical Instance Instability, and analyzes how feature bagging reduces it in two complementary settings. In a Gaussian linear regression model under proportional asymptotics (d/n→γ), the bagged least-squares estimator is decomposed into B sketched sub-estimators, and the expected instability is exactly characterized as a weighted sum of same-submodel (1/B-weighted) and cross-submodel ((B−1)/B-weighted) terms, yielding closed-form limits that reveal feature removal has a distinct 1/d scaling invisible from II alone. In a model-free Hilbert-space setting, a one-step Lipschitz-type condition on the expected transition map of a recursive stochastic process yields an FI bound for infinite feature bagging that preserves the q/((d−1)(1−q)) factor from the one-step case, with a recursion-accumulation factor M_T capturing how perturbations propagate through data-dependent feature subsampling. This recursive framework is instantiated for random forward selection (where the bound is O(T²/(d−1)·q/(1−q)) for T≲q⁻¹) and for a simplified dyadic random forest (where the bound involves A_T = Σ(1+√2)^(T−t)), showing that more aggressive feature subsampling and a modest number of bagging rounds (B = O(d)) suffice to approach the infinite-bagging stability level.

## Results

Theoretically, Theorem 3.1 gives exact asymptotic limits showing that bagging reduces both II and FI: the 1/B-weighted same-submodel terms vanish as B grows, leaving only O(1/n) cross terms, and more aggressive subsampling (smaller p or q) yields stronger large-B stability. Proposition 4.1/4.4 establish that infinite feature bagging is φ-feature stable with φ² ≥ M²/(d−1)·q/(1−q), a direct feature-axis dual of the instance-bagging bound. Empirically, FI captures generalization-relevant variation complementary to II: at ν=0 (weak-signal regime), R²(FI→gap)=0.546 vs. R²(II→gap)=0.231, with joint R²=0.675; PMI(FI; gap | II)=0.498 confirms FI's unique contribution. Across all 96 synthetic and 64 real-data estimator/residualizer groups, the conditional contribution of FI after accounting for II is positive (bootstrap positive rate = 1.0), and noise-control checks confirm the signal is not spurious (319/320 positive PMI differences).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external competing methods; instead it compares bagged vs. non-bagged variants and FI vs. II as complementary stability measures. Feature bagging is provably more stable than its non-bagged counterpart: for random forward selection (Theorem 4.6), bagging wins whenever T ≲ (1−q)/q, and for dyadic random forests (Theorem 4.7), bagging wins for shallow trees with sufficiently small q. On the empirical side, FI and II are complementary rather than redundant: at ν=1 their correlation is moderate (I(FI;II)=0.558) and both contribute roughly equally to explaining the generalization gap, while at ν=0 FI dominates (R²=0.546 vs. 0.231) but the two measures become more correlated (I(FI;II)=0.748), reducing each one's independent contribution. Noise-control experiments (replacing FI with Gaussian or permuted noise) confirm the observed conditional signal is genuine: the FI advantage over noise is positive in all 320 ΔR² entries and 319/320 PMI entries. More aggressive feature subsampling (smaller q) consistently yields larger stability gains in both the linear-model theory (Table 1) and the random forward selection experiments (Figure 3).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the algorithmic stability framework of Bousquet & Elisseeff (2002) and the bagging-stability guarantees of Soloff et al. (2024a) from the instance axis to the feature axis, introducing feature instability (FI) as a formal leave-one-feature-out analogue of instance instability (II). The linear-model analysis builds directly on the sketched least-squares formulation of Wu & Sun (2025) and the double-descent literature (Hastie et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023), while the model-free recursive results are motivated by the randomization-as-regularization perspective of Mentch & Zhou (2020) and the tree-ensemble analysis of Curth et al. (2024). The key departure is that prior stability theory for bagging addresses only instance subsampling; this paper is the first to formalize and analyze stability under feature subsampling, including the data-dependent recursive structure of random forests.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical justification for why feature bagging stabilizes models along the feature axis, complementing the well-understood instance-axis story. The empirical finding that FI carries generalization-relevant information complementary to II (validated across 160+ estimator/residualizer/dataset configurations with noise controls) suggests that feature-level sensitivity is a distinct and useful diagnostic for model reliability. Open questions include tightening the recursive bounds (currently quadratic in T for forward selection, exponential for forests), extending the linear-model analysis beyond Gaussian assumptions, and investigating whether FI-based stability guarantees yield practical generalization bounds analogous to the classical II-based ones. The model-free results, while clean, are conditional and may be too conservative for deep or highly correlated feature settings.
