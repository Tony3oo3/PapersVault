## Problem

Text embedding research is structurally anchored to surface-level semantics: training data (STS, NLI, IR), evaluation benchmarks (MTEB, BEIR), and contrastive objectives all reward lexical overlap and topical similarity while systematically leaving implicit meaning—pragmatic inference, speaker stance, and sociocultural context—unmodeled. The paper argues this is not an incidental gap but a structural mismatch between what embeddings are optimized for and what real-world language interpretation requires, and provides empirical evidence that even state-of-the-art models achieve only marginal gains over a Bag-of-Tokens baseline on tasks probing these deeper layers.

## Input / Output

The pilot study takes existing NLP datasets (PUB, P-Stance, IHS, SBIC, Political Bias) that were not originally designed for embedding evaluation and reformulates them into three settings: classification, pairwise classification following the MTEB protocol, and zero-shot similarity where models select labels based on embedding proximity to label descriptions. Four model families (encoder-only, LLM-based, multimodal, proprietary) are evaluated without any additional tuning or prompt engineering, with accuracy reported per dataset and averaged across the three semantic tiers (utterance, speaker, society).

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a position argument grounded in a three-tier linguistic framework (utterance-level pragmatics, speaker-level stance, society-level sociocultural context) validated by a pilot empirical study. The study evaluates 14 representative embedding models across 7 datasets spanning all three tiers, using standard MTEB-aligned protocols with a Bag-of-Tokens lexical baseline and a random predictor for reference. The key finding is a 'performance divide': while modern embeddings achieve substantial gains over Bag-of-Tokens on surface-meaning benchmarks, their improvements nearly collapse on implicit semantics tasks, with encoder-only models sometimes approaching random performance. The paper then outlines a three-part research agenda: curating linguistically diverse training data with explicit implicit-semantic supervision (e.g., contrastive pairs where surface meaning is similar but implied meaning differs), designing benchmarks that directly probe pragmatic, attitudinal, and social understanding, and reframing implicit semantics as a first-class modeling objective in embedding training objectives.

## Results

Across seven implicit-semantics datasets spanning utterance-level pragmatics (P-IMP, P-PRE, P-R&D), speaker-level stance (P-Stance), and society-level social meaning (IHS, SBIC, Pol. Bias), the best-performing model (GTE-Qwen) achieves only 75.2% average accuracy, a mere 13.0-point gain over the Bag-of-Tokens lexical baseline (62.2%). Encoder-only models show the weakest performance, with the best (Stella) reaching only 69.8%, just 7.6 points above Bag-of-Tokens. LLM-based embeddings (GTE-Qwen 75.2%, E5-Mistral 74.9%, Linq-Mistral 73.5%) and proprietary OpenAI-Large (75.0%) perform substantially better, yet all remain far from ceiling, with several subtasks (e.g., P-R&D, Pol. Bias) still within 10–20 points of the lexical baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All evaluated models are compared against a Bag-of-Tokens lexical baseline (62.2% avg) and a random predictor (44.8% avg). Among encoder-only models, Stella (69.8%) and GIST-Large (69.3%) lead but still trail the best LLM-based models by 5–6 points; S-BERT (64.8%) is the weakest, barely 2.6 points above Bag-of-Tokens. LLM-based embeddings dominate: GTE-Qwen (75.2%) edges out OpenAI-Large (75.0%) and E5-Mistral (74.9%), while Linq-Mistral (73.5%) excels specifically on utterance-level pragmatics (P-IMP 80.3%, P-PRE 87.7%) but lags on society-level tasks. E5-Mistral stands out on Pol. Bias (71.5%) and P-Stance (81.1%), whereas OpenAI-Large leads on P-Stance (83.7%) and IHS (67.1%). The multimodal Jasper encoder (72.9%) sits between the best encoder-only and LLM-based models, suggesting that cross-modal pretraining provides some implicit-semantic benefit. Critically, even the top performers show only 13 points of gain over Bag-of-Tokens, and on the hardest subtask (P-R&D), the best model (Linq-Mistral, 70.4%) still trails the lexical baseline's P-PRE score (75.3%), underscoring the paper's central claim that current embeddings remain anchored in surface-level semantics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not build on a specific prior method but instead critiques the entire text embedding pipeline—training data, benchmarks, and modeling objectives—as structurally misaligned with linguistic theory. It draws on decades of pragmatics, stance, and sociolinguistics research (Grice, Du Bois, Silverstein, Kiesling) to formalize a three-tier framework (utterance/speaker/society) that prior embedding work has not explicitly targeted. Empirically, it repurposes existing NLP datasets (PUB, P-Stance, IHS, SBIC, Political Bias) into embedding-evaluation settings, bridging the gap between linguistic evaluation and the MTEB/BEIR benchmark ecosystem. The paper positions itself as a paradigm-shift call rather than a method contribution, arguing that instruction-following embeddings and LLM-based embedders represent promising but insufficient steps toward implicit-semantics-aware representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The pilot study's finding that encoder-only embeddings barely exceed Bag-of-Tokens on implicit semantics tasks—and that MTEB leaders do not transfer to these settings—exposes a structural blind spot in how the field defines and measures 'semantic understanding.' Key open questions include: whether a single static vector can adequately encode dynamic, context-dependent pragmatic meaning; how to construct training supervision that distinguishes surface-similar but implicitly-different texts at scale; and whether new benchmarks can avoid the data-leakage and score-inflation problems that plague MTEB. The paper's limitations (pilot-scale evaluation, reformulated datasets not originally designed for embedding tasks, no ablation isolating which linguistic tier is hardest) mean the empirical case, while suggestive, is not yet definitive.
