## Problem

The classical aleatoric/epistemic decomposition of predictive uncertainty is structurally insufficient for safety-critical tasks like OOD detection: epistemic uncertainty from finite ensembles is bounded by log(M) beyond the best single component, so it cannot reliably distinguish distributional novelty from within-domain ambiguity. The paper argues that a third, first-class source of uncertainty—domain uncertainty over whether the input x is well-supported by the training distribution—is entirely absent from the conditional predictive distribution p(y|x,D), and that only the joint p(x,y|D) provides a coherent representation of all three sources.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on RGB images (32×32 CIFAR-10) and produces the joint probability p(x,y|D) = p(x|D)·p(y|x,D) as the complete uncertainty representation. In experiments, the conditional component is approximated by a 5-model Deep Ensemble of simple CNNs, while the domain component p(x|D) is treated as an oracle (true input density) to demonstrate upper-bound gains. Evaluation uses mixed test sets (80% CIFAR-10 ID + 20% SVHN OOD) and corruption-based covariate shift (CIFAR-10C), reporting Risk-Coverage curves, entropy bounds, and AU/EU coupling diagnostics rather than a single scalar score.

## Method

The paper is a position paper combining theoretical analysis with empirical validation. Theoretically, it derives LogSumExp-based entropy bounds showing that for a finite M-component ensemble, predictive entropy is sandwiched within a log(M)-wide interval around the negative max log-likelihood, proving that epistemic uncertainty is capacity-limited and cannot express ignorance about missing hypotheses. It then formalizes domain uncertainty as p(x|D) and shows via joint entropy decomposition that H[X,Y|D] = H[X|D] + H[Y|X|D], with the joint entropy bounded by log(KM) when both components are ensembles. Empirically, 5-model Deep Ensembles trained on CIFAR-10 are evaluated on mixed ID/OOD and corruption-shift test sets, demonstrating that (i) AU outperforms EU for OOD ranking, (ii) EU grows monotonically with AU (logarithmic coupling), and (iii) weighting the predictive distribution with the true input density substantially improves Risk-Coverage AUC, quantifying the potential gain from accurate p(x|D) modeling.

## Results

Using a 5-model Deep Ensemble trained on CIFAR-10 (~68% accuracy) and evaluated on a mixed test set of 80% CIFAR-10 (ID) and 20% SVHN (OOD), the paper demonstrates that predictive entropy is tightly bounded by the ensemble size (log M slack), and that epistemic uncertainty increases monotonically with aleatoric uncertainty in an approximately logarithmic relationship. In Risk-Coverage (RC) curve experiments, ordering samples by epistemic uncertainty yields the weakest OOD detection performance, while aleatoric uncertainty and Maximum Softmax Probability (MSP) both outperform EU. The oracle joint probability p(x)·p(y|x) substantially improves the area under the RC curve over any individual uncertainty measure, confirming the paper's central claim that the joint distribution captures uncertainty that conditional predictive entropy alone cannot.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against existing OOD detectors; instead, it compares uncertainty *measures* as ranking signals for OOD detection via Risk-Coverage curves. Against the popular assumption that epistemic uncertainty should best capture distributional shift, the results show AU consistently outperforms EU on the mixed CIFAR-10/SVHN test set, a finding corroborated by prior benchmarks (Mucsányi et al., 2024; Schweighofer et al., 2025). MSP, despite its simplicity, also beats EU, narrowing the gap to the oracle joint p(x)·p(y|x). The oracle joint distribution, which weights the predictive distribution by the true input density, achieves the best RC performance, establishing a clear upper bound that no conditional-only uncertainty measure can reach. Under corruption-based covariate shift (CIFAR-10-C Gaussian noise at severity 1 and 5, with 20% and 80% corrupted fractions), the same AU > EU ordering persists, and the entropy bounds remain tight, reinforcing that the limitation is structural rather than dataset-specific.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper builds on the classical information-theoretic decomposition of predictive uncertainty into aleatoric and epistemic components (Kendall & Gal, 2017; Depeweg et al., 2018) and the growing body of evidence questioning that dichotomy's operational validity (Mucsanyi et al., 2024; de Jong et al., 2026; Wimmer et al., 2023). It repurposes the joint distribution idea from Bishop (1993) and hybrid generative-discriminative models (Nalisnick et al., 2019a; Zhang et al., 2020) into a principled argument that domain uncertainty p(x|D) must be a first-class component alongside the conditional p(y|x,D). The paper departs from the OOD detection literature, which typically treats distribution shift as a separate detection problem (Lee et al., 2018; Hendrycks et al., 2019), by arguing it should be integrated into a unified uncertainty framework following Brando et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical explanation—via LogSumExp entropy bounds—for why ensemble-based epistemic uncertainty is fundamentally capacity-limited (bounded by log M) and cannot reliably distinguish OOD from uncertain ID inputs, grounding a concern raised empirically by multiple prior benchmarks. The three-axis framing (domain, epistemic, aleatoric) offers a more coherent vocabulary for safety-critical UQ, but the practical path forward remains open: accurately estimating p(x|D) in high-dimensional spaces is acknowledged as extremely difficult, and the paper does not propose a concrete method to do so. Open questions include whether the joint framework can be operationalized with modern generative models, how to handle the coupling between AU and EU that the paper documents, and whether the three-axis decomposition yields actionable decision rules beyond binary OOD rejection.
