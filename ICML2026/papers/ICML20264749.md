## Problem

Existing LLM-based multi-agent systems for competition-level code generation rely on fixed or statically optimized interaction topologies that neither adapt density to task difficulty nor refine the topology within an instance using execution feedback. This leads to redundant communication and unnecessary token overhead on easier problems while failing to provide sufficient coordination on harder ones. The paper asks how to automatically generate task-specific interaction topologies that scale density with difficulty and evolve in response to execution feedback.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264749/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The orchestrator receives a programming problem statement and, in subsequent turns, the full execution feedback (error type, sandbox logs, and the previous turn's topology trace) as conditioning context. It emits a variable-length YAML token sequence that is deterministically decoded into a strict layered DAG specifying agent roles, intra-layer parallelism, and cross-layer dependencies. The final output is the generated code solution validated against test cases, with the topology itself (as YAML) serving as an intermediate structured artifact that can be inspected and reused across turns.

## Method

AgentConductor centers on a 3B-parameter LLM orchestrator (Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct) trained in two stages: SFT on 4,500 GPT-4o-generated topologies to instill structural priors over the YAML-encoded layered DAG format, followed by GRPO-based reinforcement learning with a multi-objective reward combining YAML validity penalties, graded code-execution rewards, and a difficulty-aware graph density score S_complex. The density function normalizes node count, edge density, and graph depth into [0,1] with difficulty-dependent upper bounds (4/7/10 nodes for easy/medium/hard), and a theoretical derivation in Appendix C.2 proves that minimizing S_complex corresponds to minimizing average per-agent token cost. During inference, the orchestrator performs up to two turns of topology generation: after a failed execution, it conditions on the error feedback and prior topology to regenerate a refined DAG, enabling iterative evolution without retraining. The approach achieves 58.8% pass@1 on APPS with 13% lower topology density and 68% lower token cost than the strongest baseline.

## Results

AgentConductor (3B) achieves pass@1 of 58.8% on APPS, 46.3% on LiveCodeBench (v4), and 38.8% on CodeContests, with a contest-level average of 48.0%. On basic code generation, it reaches 97.5% on HumanEval and 95.1% on MBPP (96.3% average), yielding an overall average of 72.1%. In terms of cost efficiency, AgentConductor uses 277,600 prompt tokens and 79,800 completion tokens on APPS—roughly 13% lower topology density and 68% lower token cost than the strongest baseline—while achieving the highest S_complex score (5.2, indicating the sparsest topology).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AgentConductor outperforms all 10 baselines across every dataset. Against the strongest classical multi-agent system, MetaGPT (43.2% contest avg.), AgentConductor leads by 4.8 pp on contest-level average and by 7.5 pp on APPS specifically (58.8% vs. 51.3%). Among workflow-optimization methods, FlowReasoner (40.2%) and Chain-of-Agents 32B (40.3%) are the closest competitors on contest-level tasks, but AgentConductor surpasses them by 7.8 pp and 7.7 pp respectively on the contest average; notably, AgentConductor uses a 3B backbone versus Chain-of-Agents' 32B. On basic code generation, the gap narrows: AgentConductor edges out FlowReasoner by 0.2 pp on HumanEval (97.5% vs. 97.3%) and leads MetaGPT by 2.8 pp on MBPP (95.1% vs. 92.3%). Topology-optimization baselines (GPTSwarm, AgentPrune, MacNet, G-Designer) trail by 10–14 pp on contest-level averages, with G-Designer the weakest at 34.3%. In cross-domain transfer (GAIA, HLE, PopQA), AgentConductor (3B) also outperforms Chain-of-Agents (7B) by 3.0 pp on GAIA average and 4.6 pp on HLE, confirming generality beyond code generation.

## Relation to Prior Work

AgentConductor synthesizes three research threads that had previously been pursued in isolation: (1) topology-optimization methods for multi-agent systems (GPTSwarm, AgentPrune, MacNet, G-Designer) that prune or generate interaction graphs but keep them static within an instance; (2) workflow-centric RL approaches (FlowReasoner, Chain-of-Agents) that optimize multi-agent pipelines end-to-end but restrict communication to chain or tree structures; and (3) agentic RL with environment feedback (Search-R1, RAGEN) that supports multi-turn interaction but without explicit topology control. The paper's conceptual departure is to train a small LLM orchestrator via GRPO to emit a difficulty-calibrated, layered-DAG topology in YAML that evolves across turns in response to execution feedback, thereby unifying topology generation, difficulty-aware sparsity, and iterative refinement in a single policy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a 3B orchestrator achieves state-of-the-art pass@1 on competition-level benchmarks while consuming 68% fewer tokens than the strongest baseline, suggesting that adaptive topology density is a more efficient use of multi-agent compute than fixed or uniformly sparse graphs. The difficulty-aware density bounds (4/7/10 nodes for easy/medium/hard) provide a principled, interpretable knob for cost-accuracy trade-offs that practitioners can tune. Open questions include whether the two-turn limit is sufficient for the hardest problems, whether the approach scales to more than six agent roles without retraining, and whether the cross-domain transfer results (GAIA, HLE, PopQA) generalize to domains with richer tool-use or multi-modal inputs. The reliance on GPT-4o-mini as the execution backbone for individual agents also raises questions about cost at scale.
