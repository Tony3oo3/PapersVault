## Problem

Existing audio QA benchmarks focus on short segments, single-turn questions, or non-medical environmental sounds, leaving a significant gap in evaluating reasoning over real clinical audio where diagnostically meaningful cues (respiratory sounds, vocal strain, hesitations, prosodic markers) are embedded within long, noisy conversations. Medical audio is additionally difficult to collect at scale due to privacy regulations and the high cost of domain-expert annotation, causing prior benchmarks to underrepresent complex clinical scenarios.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes heterogeneous medical audio ranging from isolated physiological recordings (heart murmurs, lung crackles, cough paroxysms) to 3+ minute multi-speaker clinical conversations and speech-plus-sound mixes, paired with text-based MCQ prompts (10 options, 3 difficulty levels), open-ended clinical questions, or vocalized questions appended directly to the audio waveform. Outputs are MCQ letter selections, concise open-ended text answers scored by an LLM judge on correctness/relevance/completeness/clarity, or sequential multi-turn answers with explicit answer-conditioned dependencies across 3 turns.

## Method

MedMosaic is constructed via a synthetic QA generation pipeline that uses Gemini-3-flash to produce 46,701 adversarial question-answer pairs across seven categories (sound-only, speech-only, speech+sound, long-form, multi-turn, voice-based, and open-ended), with distractors deliberately designed to be lexically similar and clinically plausible yet subtly incorrect, forcing reliance on audio evidence rather than parametric medical knowledge. For speech+sound and voice-based categories, a two-stage audio synthesis pipeline first uses Qwen 2.5-14B Instruct to insert 35 contextually appropriate acoustic tags (22 medically relevant, 13 paralinguistic) into raw transcripts, then renders the enriched text via ElevenLabs v3 TTS using 151 curated voices to produce naturalistic clinical audio with embedded coughs, wheezes, pain vocalizations, and prosodic cues. Multi-turn sequences enforce answer-conditioned dependencies where incorrect earlier answers introduce ambiguity, and long-form questions require multi-hop temporal reasoning over evidence distributed across minutes of dialogue without restating the relevant cues. Clinical validity is confirmed through stratified SME validation (72.4% unmodified acceptance), and the benchmark is evaluated on 13 audio/multimodal models using robust string-matching for MCQs and GPT-5.1 as a four-criterion judge for open-ended responses.

## Results

On MedMosaic (46,701 QA pairs across 10 categories), Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves the highest weighted-average accuracy at 68.1%, followed by Gemini-2.5-Flash at 60.5% and Qwen-2.5-Omni-7B at 42.8%. Performance ranges from 20.8% (R1-AQA) to 68.1% (Gemini-2.5-Pro), with the largest gaps appearing in sound-only categories where GPT-4o-Audio scores near chance (2.1–4.1%) due to rejecting a large fraction of physiological audio inputs. Notably, Gemini-2.5-Flash outperforms Gemini-2.5-Pro on Long-Form MCQ (73.6% vs. 67.4%) and Multi-Turn QA (78.3% vs. 76.7%), suggesting the larger model favors shorter-horizon reasoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini-2.5-Pro leads on 8 of 10 categories, with the largest margins over GPT-4o-Audio in sound-only tasks (e.g., 67.0% vs. 2.1% on Cough, 61.7% vs. 3.2% on Heart), where GPT-4o-Audio rejects 1,729 of 9,729 heart sound inputs. Gemini-2.5-Flash narrowly beats Pro on Long-Form MCQ (73.6% vs. 67.4%) and Multi-Turn (78.3% vs. 76.7%), while Pro dominates open-ended and voice-based QA (79.2% vs. 74.8% on OE Speech; 70.3% vs. 52.7% on Voice QA). Among open-source models, Qwen-2.5-Omni-7B (42.8%) and Gemma-3n-8B (42.1%) are the strongest, but both trail Gemini-2.5-Flash by ~18 points. GPT-4o-Audio shows a stark speech-vs-sound asymmetry: 57.8% on MCQ Speech but 2.1–4.1% on all sound categories, attributed to its inability to process physiological audio. Audio Flamingo 3 (24.1%) and R1-AQA (20.8%) perform near chance on most categories, with AF3's weakness in Long-Form (8.1%) linked to its training data lacking doctor-patient dialogue.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the audio question-answering benchmarking paradigm (MMAU, MMAR, AudioBench) into the medical domain, where prior work is limited to CaReAQA (short physiological sound segments) and text-only medical QA (MeDiaQA). It departs from existing audio benchmarks by integrating physiological sounds (heart, lung, cough) with clinical conversations, multi-turn answer-conditioned dependencies, long-form temporal reasoning, and voice-embedded questions into a single unified evaluation. Methodologically, it repurposes LLM-based synthetic QA generation (Gemini-3-flash) combined with TTS (ElevenLabs v3) and acoustic tag insertion (Qwen 2.5-14B) to scale benchmark construction while controlling reasoning difficulty, a pipeline not previously applied to medical audio.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest model (Gemini-2.5-Pro) plateaus at 68.1% weighted accuracy, and exposes stark modality biases—GPT-4o-Audio scores near zero on physiological sounds while handling speech at 57.8%—highlighting that current audio-language models lack balanced cross-modal medical reasoning. The SME-validated synthetic generation pipeline (72.4% acceptance) offers a scalable template for constructing domain-specific benchmarks, though the gap between synthetic and real clinical audio remains an open concern. Key open questions include whether models can genuinely reason over subtle physiological acoustic cues (murmur timing, cough typology) rather than pattern-matching, and how performance generalizes beyond English-language, synthetic-voice settings to real, multilingual clinical recordings.
