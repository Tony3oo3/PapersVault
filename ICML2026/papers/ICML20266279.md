## Problem

Pre-trained VLMs like CLIP suffer severe accuracy degradation under adversarial perturbations, and existing test-time adaptation methods (TPT, R-TPT) rely on sample-level confidence heuristics that treat augmented views as isolated points. This sample-centric approach fails to distinguish confident adversarial mispredictions from true semantic consistency, because it overlooks the distributional structure preserved across augmented views under attack.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single test image (potentially adversarially perturbed) and a set of class-name text prompts as input. At inference, it generates N=64 augmented views (random crops, flips, resizes) of the image and M=4 learnable text prompts per class, all encoded by a frozen CLIP backbone. The output is a single class label, and the method additionally maintains a dynamic cache of pseudo-labeled visual features accumulated across the test stream, which is updated online without any labeled data or parameter updates to the CLIP encoder.

## Method

RITA models the adversarial image as a discrete distribution over N augmented visual features and each class as a distribution over M textual prototypes, then aligns them via entropy-regularized optimal transport (solved with Sinkhorn) rather than pointwise cosine similarity. A dynamic cache mechanism selects low-entropy views from the test stream, aligns them to the textual space via an Orthogonal Procrustes problem, and constructs a second OT-based distribution matching term that captures historical semantic consensus. The final prediction minimizes the sum of the global prompt-alignment OT cost and a weighted cache-consensus OT cost. Theoretically, the paper shows that the 2-Wasserstein alignment decomposes into a centroid term (equivalent to TPT's mean-pooling) plus a Bures-metric structural variance penalty, explaining why distribution-level alignment is inherently more robust to adversarial distortion of individual embeddings.

## Results

On eight fine-grained classification benchmarks under PGD (ε=1.0), RITA achieves 58.6% average clean accuracy and 49.7% average robust accuracy with ViT-B/32, and 62.0% / 52.7% with ViT-B/16. This represents a 45.0% and 50.9% absolute improvement in robust accuracy over vanilla CLIP (4.7% and 1.8%), and a 1.8% and 2.2% gain over the strongest baseline R-TPT. On ImageNet (ViT-B/16), RITA reaches 55.1% robust accuracy versus 0.6% for CLIP and 54.4% for R-TPT, with an OOD average robust accuracy of 46.2% across four ImageNet variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RITA outperforms all test-time adaptation baselines on average robust accuracy: it beats R-TPT (the strongest prior method) by 1.8% (ViT-B/32) and 2.2% (ViT-B/16), MTA by 10.9% and 11.8%, TPT by 11.9% and 13.4%, and C-TPT by 15.1% and 16.9%. Against the zero-shot CLIP baseline, RITA improves robust accuracy by 45.0% and 50.9% on the two ViT backbones, while also achieving the highest clean accuracy (58.6% and 62.0%), confirming no clean-accuracy trade-off. When stacked on top of adversarially fine-tuned models, RITA consistently adds robustness gains: +7.9% over TeCoA, +6.5% over PMG, and +7.7% over FARE in average robust accuracy (Table 2). The Ensemble baseline (simple view averaging) is the closest competitor on clean accuracy but trails RITA by 1.4% and 1.1% on robust accuracy, validating that distribution-level OT alignment provides a meaningful advantage over naive aggregation.

## Relation to Prior Work

RITA extends the test-time prompt tuning paradigm introduced by TPT (Shu et al., 2022) and its adversarial-robustness variants (R-TPT, C-TPT, MTA) by replacing their sample-level entropy-based view filtering with distribution-level alignment via entropy-regularized optimal transport (Cuturi, 2013). The key conceptual departure is modeling augmented visual features and multi-prompt textual prototypes as discrete distributions and measuring their correspondence through a transport plan, rather than treating each augmented view as an isolated point. The dynamic cache mechanism, which progressively accumulates reliable views from the test stream and aligns them via Orthogonal Procrustes before a second OT computation, has no direct analogue in prior test-time adaptation work. The theoretical decomposition (Theorem 3.1) formalizes why OT is strictly stronger than the centroid-matching objective implicit in TPT: it additionally penalizes structural covariance mismatch via the Bures metric.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RITA offers a principled, plug-and-play test-time defense that requires no retraining, no labeled data, and no model parameter updates, making it directly deployable on frozen CLIP-family backbones and composable with existing adversarially fine-tuned weights (TeCoA, PMG, FARE). The distribution-level framing provides a clearer geometric explanation for why sample-level confidence heuristics fail under attack—adversarial outliers corrupt the holistic embedding while the augmented-view distribution retains semantic structure. However, the method is limited to image classification; the authors explicitly note that extension to generative tasks such as image captioning remains open. The cache mechanism also introduces a mild dependency on test-stream ordering, and the modest 1.8–2.2% gains over R-TPT, while consistent, suggest the distributional structure exploited by OT may be partially captured by simpler ensemble strategies in some regimes.
