## Problem

Prior neurosymbolic abductive reasoning methods (LLM-Tres, ARGOS) assume a monolithic commonsense: that there exists a single set of missing assumptions every human would agree is true. In ambiguous or fluid domains, different individuals hold defensible but incompatible commonsense beliefs, making this assumption fail and rendering any associated probability uninterpretable. The paper asks how to formally define and compute the truth of a query when commonsense is heterogeneous across a population of reasoners, and how to do so efficiently without exhaustively enumerating all belief combinations.

## Input / Output

The method takes a textual abductive reasoning problem — a set of premises S and a True/False query c — and produces a binary True/False verdict via majority vote over multiple sampled reasoning paths. The LLM first translates the text into first-order logic (premises and query proposition) via few-shot prompting with four annotated examples; a SAT solver then verifies entailment at each step. The final answer is a Monte Carlo estimate of the 'abductive probability' (fraction of sampled commonsense vectors that entail the query), thresholded at 0.5. No training is involved; the entire pipeline is inference-only.

## Method

PACS (Probabilistic Abductive CommonSense) frames abductive reasoning as sampling over a population of reasoners, each with a distinct commonsense belief vector L, and defines the query's truth as the majority vote across these samples. At each step, an LLM proposes candidate commonsense propositions (translated to logic via few-shot prompting), and a SAT solver computes a model-count-based score — the product of the number of satisfying assignments and the count of remaining unconstrained variables — that serves as a tractable surrogate for expected remaining steps to resolution. The ordering of which proposition to sample next is formulated as a Markov Decision Process, and the derived greedy score guides a beam-search-like procedure that samples n thoughts per node, retains the top m, and early-stops any path the moment the query becomes entailed or refuted. All completed paths are retained (not just the best), and the final answer is a majority vote across them, yielding a Monte Carlo estimate of the abductive probability. This design explicitly accommodates heterogeneous commonsense by preserving contradictory paths rather than collapsing to a single 'correct' proof.

## Results

Using Llama 3-Instruct 8B, PACS achieves 82% accuracy on FOLIO, 82% on CosmosQA, 80% on QUAIL, and 88% on Disamb-QA, outperforming all baselines on three of four datasets. Scaling to Llama 3.3 70B, PACS reaches 88% (FOLIO), 91% (CosmosQA), 84% (QUAIL), and 93% (Disamb-QA). Notably, PACS with the 8B non-thinking model matches the accuracy of Self-Consistency with the much larger GPT-OSS-120B thinking model (82% FOLIO, 80% QUAIL), while the 70B PACS variant surpasses it by 6 and 4 points respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PACS outperforms all six baselines on FOLIO, QUAIL, and Disamb-QA with the 8B model, with the largest margins over LLM-Tres (+19 to +32 points) and LoT (+13 to +24 points). Against the strongest neurosymbolic baseline ARGOS, PACS gains +4 points on FOLIO, +7 on QUAIL, and +8 on Disamb-QA, while tying on CosmosQA (82%). Compared to the heuristic beam-search If-Beam, PACS improves by +8 points on FOLIO, +6 on QUAIL, and +16 on Disamb-QA, again tying on CosmosQA. Against the neural baselines COT and SC-20, PACS leads by 11–22 points and 8–14 points respectively across datasets. The paper further shows that PACS with Llama 3 8B (82% FOLIO, 80% QUAIL) matches Self-Consistency with the 120B GPT-OSS thinking model, while PACS with Llama 3.3 70B exceeds it by 6 and 4 points, suggesting that principled search algorithms can compensate for smaller model capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the abductive neurosymbolic reasoning line initiated by LLM-Tres and the authors' own ARGOS, which both assume a monolithic, universally-agreed commonsense set. PACS departs by explicitly modeling commonsense as heterogeneous across a population of reasoners, replacing the single-proof search with a Monte-Carlo aggregation over diverse sampled belief vectors. The search procedure itself is grounded in a Markov Decision Process formulation that yields a model-count-based scoring function, connecting the neurosymbolic setting to the tree-search and planning literature (MCTS, beam search) but with a principled, derived heuristic rather than an ad-hoc novelty/informativeness score.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The probabilistic framing—asking 'would most people say this is true?' rather than 'is this true?'—offers a more realistic and interpretable account of commonsense reasoning than the confidence-score proxies used in prior neurosymbolic methods. The comparison showing PACS with a smaller non-thinking Llama 3 8B matching a 120B thinking model suggests that principled step-level search may be more effective than scaling model size for structured reasoning, though this claim rests on a single comparison table. Key open questions include replacing the uniform model of solution distributions with an LLM-learned one, and addressing the translation-error bottleneck that currently limits performance on less structured datasets like CosmosQA.
