## Problem

Existing meta-learning methods are structurally limited to fixed feature dimensions, fixed label spaces, and single modalities, so they fail when the test domain, modality, or number of classes differs from training. Additionally, the meta-learning literature uses terms like 'universal' and 'general-purpose' inconsistently without formal definitions, making it impossible to compare the true generalization scope of different approaches.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a support set of N labeled (feature, label) pairs per class and an unlabeled query sample, where features are first encoded by a modality-specific pretrained backbone (ViT-H for images, DistilBERT for text, wav2vec 2.0 for audio) and then projected into a fixed-dimensional common latent space. The number of classes K at test time can exceed those seen during training (up to 20×), and the feature modality can be entirely different from the training modality. Output is a single class label prediction per query, produced in one forward pass with no test-time optimization.

## Method

TAIL is a non-causal (bidirectional) transformer that processes all support tokens and query tokens jointly in a single sequence to directly produce query predictions, operating as a demonstration-conditioned inference function. Three mechanisms enable practical universality: (i) modality-specific encoder outputs are projected into a shared latent space via randomly sampled extended permutation matrices, which acts as data augmentation and prevents overfitting to any particular encoder's coordinate structure; (ii) a global learnable dictionary of M label embeddings is accessed per episode through a uniformly random injective mapping, so all embeddings receive unbiased gradient updates and the model extrapolates to label spaces larger than any training task; (iii) multiple query samples are processed inline in one sequence, reducing inference and training cost by orders of magnitude compared to prior transformer meta-learners. The non-causal architecture preserves permutation invariance to demonstration order and equivariance to label re-indexing, properties that causal variants (SNAIL, GPICL) lack. Meta-training is performed on a large-scale multi-domain dataset (ImageNet, Meta-Album, MedIMeta) with episodes sampled at varying shot counts and class numbers.

## Results

On standard in-domain few-shot benchmarks, TAIL achieves state-of-the-art accuracy across all four datasets in both 1-shot and 5-shot settings: 99.63% on miniImageNet (5-shot) and 98.79% (1-shot), 98.67% on tieredImageNet (5-shot), 94.55% on CIFAR-FS (5-shot), and 89.78% on Pascal VOC (5-shot). Most strikingly, TAIL generalizes to unseen modalities without retraining, achieving 89.62% on text classification (IMDB, 5-shot) and 55.33% on audio classification (GTZAN, 5-shot) despite being trained exclusively on images, while GPICL degrades to near-random performance (~50% and ~20% respectively). TAIL also handles label space extrapolation, maintaining competitive accuracy up to 70-way classification when trained only on ≤5-way tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TAIL outperforms all four baselines on every in-domain benchmark in both 1-shot and 5-shot settings. Against the closest transformer-based competitor GPICL, TAIL gains +0.19% on miniImageNet (5-shot) and a more substantial +1.15% in the 1-shot setting (98.79% vs. 97.64%), with the gap widening on CIFAR-FS (+3.35% 1-shot) and Pascal VOC (+3.02% 1-shot). Against CAML, the advantage is larger in the 1-shot regime: +6.42% on CIFAR-FS and +3.72% on Pascal VOC. Against SNAIL, TAIL leads by +7.98% on CIFAR-FS (1-shot) and +3.79% on Pascal VOC (1-shot). The most dramatic separation appears in cross-modal evaluation: TAIL trained only on images achieves 89.62% on IMDB text (5-shot) and 55.33% on GTZAN audio (5-shot), while GPICL collapses to 50.88% and 20.03% respectively—essentially random chance—confirming that TAIL's random projection and non-causal architecture are critical for cross-modality transfer.

## Relation to Prior Work

TAIL extends the line of transformer-based algorithm-implicit meta-learners (SNAIL, GPICL, CAML) by replacing their causal architectures with a non-causal transformer and introducing three key mechanisms: random extended-permutation projections for cross-modal feature alignment, a randomized global label embedding dictionary for variable and extrapolated label spaces, and inline multi-query processing for computational efficiency. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior work by introducing a formal distinction between algorithm-explicit and algorithm-implicit learning paradigms and by defining 'practical universality' as a rigorous criterion requiring generalization across varying feature domains, label spaces, and data distributions—addressing what the authors identify as a terminological gap in the meta-learning literature. The approach also draws on the in-context learning literature (Brown et al., 2020; Oswald et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025), positioning TAIL as a meta-learner whose implicit algorithm emerges from large-scale meta-training rather than being explicitly specified.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to generalize from image-only training to text and audio classification without retraining or architectural changes represents a meaningful step toward truly universal few-shot learners, with practical implications for reducing the cost of deploying meta-learners across new domains. The computational efficiency gains (orders of magnitude faster than GPICL/CAML at larger label sets, and faster than Linear Probing for K<70 since no test-time training is needed) make transformer-based meta-learning viable at scale. However, the practical universality demonstrated is currently limited to classification tasks, and performance still depends on strong pretrained encoders (ViT-H), leaving open whether the algorithm-implicit paradigm can match encoder-only baselines in zero-shot or extremely low-data regimes. Extending the framework to regression, structured prediction, and reinforcement learning remains future work.
