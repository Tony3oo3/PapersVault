## Problem

Existing cultural evaluation benchmarks for LLMs reduce cultural knowledge to discrete factual probes (e.g., 'What is the currency of Japan?'), capturing only microstructural properties while ignoring whether models internalize broader cultural structure, pluralism, and relational patterns across countries. This paper asks whether LLMs capture the macrostructure of cultural knowledge—how cultural items cluster, how much consensus exists across countries, and how diverse the underlying patterns are—and whether such macrostructural alignment predicts a model's ability to serve users from unfamiliar cultural backgrounds.

## Input / Output

The method consumes LLM log-probabilities extracted over exhaustive candidate item lists (ranging from 21 religions to >3,700 food items) for 170 countries across 9 cultural domains, with multi-token items handled by summing next-token log-probabilities before a softmax yields a country-level distribution. These distributions are stacked into n×t matrices and converted into 170×170 country-country cosine-similarity adjacency matrices. The outputs are per-domain Effective Rank and Spectral Gap Ratio scores, binarized relative to cross-model medians to assign one of four macrostructural regime labels (LH, HL, HH, LL), aggregated into a Variational Awareness score via regime agreement with human-collected ground truth, plus downstream Appropriateness and Interaction scores from a recipe-recommendation simulation.

## Method

The core contribution is a spectral-analysis framework that shifts cultural evaluation from pointwise factual accuracy to domain-level macrostructural modeling. For each cultural domain, country-level probability distributions are aggregated into an adjacency matrix A = D⁻¹HHᵀD⁻¹ encoding pairwise cosine similarity between countries' distributions. Two spectral metrics are then computed from A's eigenvalue spectrum: the entropy-based Effective Rank (ER), which quantifies the number of significant latent dimensions (i.e., cultural pluralism), and the Spectral Gap Ratio (SR = λ₁/λ₂), which captures the degree of cross-country consensus. Together, ER and SR induce four interpretable macrostructural regimes (low/high × low/high) corresponding to distinct cultural configurations such as near-universal consensus (house numbers), fragmented country-specific systems (currency), clustered diversity (food), or few dominant clusters (religion). Variational Awareness is operationalized as the fraction of domains where a model's regime assignment matches human-collected ground truth, yielding a discrete, domain-agnostic measure that does not require culture-specific answer annotations. A simulation-based recipe-recommendation task with LLM-simulated users validates that macrostructural alignment strongly predicts downstream meta-cultural competency (Spearman ρ = 0.94 for Appropriateness), outperforming rankings from existing microstructural benchmarks.

## Results

On the macrostructural evaluation (macro-F1 against human-grounded ER-SR regime labels across 9 domains × 170 countries), Llama-3.1-8B-it achieves the highest score of 0.374, followed closely by Llama-3.1-70B-it (0.363) and Aya-8B (0.363), while GPT-2 scores only 0.050. Critically, Llama-70B does not significantly outperform Llama-8B, and Gemma-2-2B-it (0.349) performs comparably to Gemma-2-9B-it (0.333), indicating macrostructural competence plateaus around 2B parameters in instruction-tuned models. In the downstream recipe-recommendation simulation, macrostructural alignment strongly predicts meta-cultural performance: Spearman rank correlations between macro-F1 ordering and APR/INT scores are 0.942 and 0.828 respectively (both p < 0.05).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its macrostructural rankings against five recent microstructural cultural benchmarks (CulturalBench, CDEval, BLEnd, NormAd, WorldCuisines) that overlap in models and domains. A key divergence emerges: microstructural benchmarks consistently rank Gemma-2 variants above both Llama and Aya, and report Llama-70B as significantly outperforming smaller models, whereas the macrostructural analysis shows Gemma-2 ranking below Llama variants (p < 0.05 in the simulation) and Llama-70B performing no better than Llama-8B. In the simulation, Llama-3.1-8B-it and Llama-3.1-70B-it achieve the highest APR and INT scores, while Aya-8B—ranked above Gemma in macrostructural evaluation but below in microstructural benchmarks—outperforms Gemma variants in the downstream task, confirming that macrostructural alignment better predicts meta-cultural competency. Pairwise t-tests on bootstrapped macro-F1 distributions reveal that only GPT-2 is significantly different from other models (p < 0.05), in contrast to microstructural benchmarks which show clear, significant ordering among all models. All eight models systematically misclassify food domains as HL (high diversity, low consensus) rather than the human-expected HH regime, a failure not captured by any of the five microstructural baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the cultural evaluation of LLMs from the dominant microstructural paradigm (matching outputs to ground-truth facts, as in CulturalBench, CDEval, BLEnd, NormAd, and WorldCuisines) to a macrostructural paradigm grounded in Cultural Consensus Theory and spectral analysis. It builds directly on the Effective Rank formulation of Roy & Vetterli (2007) and the cultural domain formalization of Adilazuarda et al. (2024), while extending the meta-cultural competency framework of Saha et al. (2025) by proposing a measurable proxy (regime agreement on ER/SR) for variational awareness. The key conceptual departure is treating cultural knowledge as a network whose global eigenstructure—rather than pointwise factual accuracy—reveals whether a model internalizes pluralism and cross-cultural relational patterns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that instruction-tuned models plateau in macrostructural competence around 2B parameters (Llama-70B ≈ Llama-8B, Gemma-2-9B ≈ Gemma-2-2B) challenges the assumption that scaling improves cultural reasoning, and the systematic failure of all eight models to capture the high-diversity/high-consensus (HH) regime for food domains suggests a structural blind spot in current training paradigms. The framework's practical value is demonstrated by its strong correlation with downstream recipe-recommendation performance, but the evidence base is narrow: eight open-weight models, nine English-language domains, and a single downstream task. Open questions include whether larger closed-source models break the observed asymptote, how the framework extends to multilingual settings, and whether integrated micro-macro evaluation can guide training toward genuinely pluralistic cultural representations.
