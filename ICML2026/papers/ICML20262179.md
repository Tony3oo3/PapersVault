## Problem

Existing geometric transfer learning methods for molecular property regression (e.g., GATE) align latent spaces by matching infinitesimal distances via local perturbations, which limits coverage to local regions, introduces ambiguity in defining 'infinitesimal' under high curvature, and requires a shared embedding layer across tasks. Moreover, most transfer learning research in molecular science targets classification, leaving regression-based transfer underexplored despite its prevalence in practical applications.

## Input / Output

The method consumes molecular graphs encoded by a DMPNN encoder into task-specific latent vectors, which are then mapped through SiLU-activated transfer/inverse-transfer MLPs into a shared locally flat coordinate frame. The output is a scalar regression value for a target molecular property. A key constraint is that the transfer modules must use fully smooth (C²) activations to support second-order derivative computation for curvature, and the Jacobians are derived analytically in closed form rather than via automatic differentiation to avoid prohibitive memory costs (~85.5× overhead for second-order terms).

## Method

GEAR connects two task-specific models (source and target) through directed transfer and inverse-transfer MLP modules that map each task's latent space into a common locally flat frame, where the induced Riemannian metric is computed from the Jacobian of the coordinate transformation. The core alignment mechanism matches the Ricci scalar curvature of the source and target latent spaces, computed analytically from closed-form expressions for the metric, its first and second derivatives, Christoffel symbols, and the Riemann tensor of a two-layer SiLU MLP. The total training objective combines six loss terms: standard regression, autoencoder reconstruction of the inverse transfer, embedding consistency in the flat frame, cross-task mapping prediction, metric flatness regularization (via K-loop diffeomorphism iteration), and the curvature-matching loss. This design eliminates the need for a shared embedding layer, captures global geometric structure rather than local perturbations, and allows each task's encoder to be independently architected.

## Results

Across 23 molecular property regression task pairs (from OCHEM, PubChem, and CCCB databases), GEAR achieves the lowest RMSE in 18/23 pairs under random split and 17/23 under scaffold split, and ranks within the top two in 22/23 pairs for both schemes. On average, GEAR improves RMSE by 14.4% over GATE (random split) and 8.3% over GATE (scaffold split), and by 22.8% over MTL (random) and 21.4% over GSP-KD (scaffold). The detailed per-task RMSE values are reported in Appendix Tables 8–11, with Figure 2 in the main text summarizing the comparison visually.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GEAR is compared against six baselines: single-task learning (STL), multi-task learning (MTL), GSP-KD knowledge distillation (Joshi et al., 2022), two transfer-learning variants (retraining all layers vs. head only), and GATE (Ko et al., 2023b). GEAR outperforms GATE—the closest geometric baseline—by 14.4% average RMSE under random split and 8.3% under scaffold split, and achieves a 17.5% lower minimum validation loss in the curvature-loss ablation (Figure 3). Against GSP-KD, GEAR gains 21.4% average RMSE improvement under scaffold split; against MTL, the gain is 22.8% under random split. GEAR wins the most individual task pairs (18/23 random, 17/23 scaffold) and is top-2 in 22/23 for both splits, indicating consistent superiority rather than isolated wins. The scaffold-split results (which simulate out-of-distribution generalization) show smaller but still significant margins, confirming robustness beyond in-distribution settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

GEAR extends the GATE framework (Ko et al., 2023b), which aligned source and target latent spaces by matching distances between local perturbation points. The key departure is replacing this perturbation-based local alignment with direct Ricci curvature matching, which captures global geometric structure and eliminates the need for a shared embedding layer across tasks. This sits within a broader line of work applying Riemannian differential geometry to deep learning (Bronstein et al., 2017) and geometric transfer learning for molecular property prediction (Ko et al., 2024; Yim et al., 2024), but shifts the geometric signal from first-order (distance) to second-order (curvature) invariants.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The curvature-matching formulation addresses three concrete limitations of prior geometric TL methods—limited local coverage, ambiguous 'infinitesimal' scale, and the shared-embedding constraint—making the framework more flexible for heterogeneous task pairs. However, the method is currently restricted to two-task settings, uses only the scalar curvature (a lossy summary that cannot capture anisotropic tensorial structure), and requires smooth activations throughout the transfer modules. Open questions include whether scaling to many tasks is feasible without quadratic growth in pairwise constraints, and whether richer geometric objectives (Ricci tensor, full Riemann tensor) would yield further gains at acceptable computational cost.
