## Problem

Existing detoxification methods for LLMs rely on model retraining, gradient access, learned auxiliary classifiers or reward models, or architecture-specific intervention points, making them costly, non-portable, or inapplicable to black-box deployments. No prior work applies zeroth-order optimization directly to per-input detoxification at test time, leaving a gap for a fully training-free, model-agnostic procedure that requires only forward evaluations and a toxicity scorer.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text prompt (tokenized into an embedding matrix X ∈ R^{T×d}), a black-box toxicity scoring function h: Y → [0,1] (e.g., the Perspective API), and a language model f accessed only through forward passes. It outputs a perturbed embedding matrix whose decoded tokens are identical to the original prompt (the shift is entirely sub-lexical, within each token's embedding region) and a detoxified completion generated from those modified embeddings. The procedure terminates early once the completion's toxicity score falls below a threshold τ = 0.5, typically after fewer than 4 iterations.

## Method

TIDE (Test-time Iterative Detoxification via Embeddings) minimizes the composite black-box objective Φ(X) = h(f(X)) by iteratively updating the prompt embedding matrix using a zeroth-order finite-difference gradient estimator derived from Nesterov & Spokoiny (2017). At each iteration, N independent Gaussian perturbation matrices U_i are sampled, the toxicity of completions from X + μU_i is evaluated, and a Monte Carlo average of scaled directional differences yields an unbiased estimate of ∇Φ_μ(X), the gradient of the Gaussian-smoothed objective. The embedding is then updated via normalized gradient descent (g_k / ‖g_k‖₂) with a fixed step size η, a cosine-similarity projection to prevent drift from the original embedding, and early stopping when toxicity drops below τ. The entire procedure requires no training, no model parameters, no auxiliary modules, and no gradient access—only forward evaluations batched through an inference stack like vLLM—making it fully model-agnostic and deployable as a drop-in test-time wrapper.

## Results

On the challenging subset of RealToxicityPrompts (1199 prompts) with GPT-2 Large, TIDE reduces average maximum toxicity from 0.591 (base) to 0.156 and the toxic rate from 0.639 to 0.003, at a fluency perplexity of 5.53 (vs. 4.13 base). Across all four models (Llama 3.1-8B, Qwen3-4B, Gemma 2-2B, GPT-2 Large) and three benchmarks (AttaQ, BOLD, RTP-challenging), TIDE consistently achieves the lowest toxic rate (≤0.003) at comparable or lower perplexity than all baselines. The method requires on average only 1.0–3.2 optimization iterations (early stopping at toxicity < 0.5), translating to roughly 9 seconds of additional wall-clock time per prompt on Llama 3.1-8B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TIDE achieves the lowest toxic rate (0.003) by a wide margin over all baselines on GPT-2/RTP-challenging, compared to 0.046 for RAD (β=100), 0.062 for DeStein, and 0.250 for GeDi. Against DeStein, which attains the lowest max toxicity (0.116), TIDE trades a small increase in max toxicity (0.156) for a substantially lower perplexity (5.53 vs. 8.72) and a toxic rate roughly 20× lower. Compared to RAD (β=100), TIDE has slightly higher max toxicity (0.156 vs. 0.134) but lower perplexity (5.53 vs. 7.21) and a toxic rate an order of magnitude lower (0.003 vs. 0.046). SASA is essentially ineffective at the low temperature (0.1) used here: its toxicity and perplexity are nearly identical to the base model across all β values, because the peaked token distribution prevents its margin-based adjustments from shifting probability mass. DExperts and Toxification Reversal provide only modest toxicity reductions (max toxicity 0.527 and 0.334, respectively) with limited perplexity cost. Across the non-GPT models (Llama 3.1-8B, Qwen3-4B, Gemma 2-2B), where only RAD and SASA are portable, TIDE again dominates on toxic rate (0.000–0.001) while matching or beating RAD on perplexity at comparable toxicity levels.

## Relation to Prior Work

TIDE applies the Nesterov–Spokoiny (2017) Gaussian-smoothed finite-difference estimator—a tool previously used in LLMs for train-time fine-tuning or dataset-level soft-prompt optimization—to a fundamentally different regime: per-input, test-time control of a single generation. Conceptually, it sits at the intersection of two threads: (i) embedding-space steering (Han et al., 2024; Leong et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024), which treats input embeddings as control variables but typically relies on a pre-learned or heuristically estimated direction, and (ii) decoding-time detoxification (RAD, SASA, GeDi, DExperts), which reshapes the token distribution using auxiliary reward or classifier models. TIDE departs from both by computing a per-input descent direction directly from the toxicity objective via zeroth-order queries, requiring no learned components, no gradient access, and no modification of the decoding procedure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that TIDE offers a drop-in, training-free detoxification layer compatible with any model exposing input embeddings and any black-box toxicity scorer, with no architecture-specific hooks or auxiliary checkpoints. The paper's claim that embedding-space detoxification is 'largely resolved' by a near-optimal descent direction is bold and, if correct, redirects future effort toward better objectives or complementary safety signals rather than alternative steering heuristics. Key open questions include: whether the oracle-access advantage (optimizing the same metric used for evaluation) persists under adversarial or distribution-shifted toxicity measures, whether the ~9-second per-prompt overhead is acceptable in latency-sensitive deployments, and whether the approach extends cleanly to multi-turn or instruction-following settings where the 'prompt' is a long conversation history rather than a short open-ended seed.
