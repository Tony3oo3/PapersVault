## Problem

Global AI governance is fragmented across incompatible jurisdiction-specific regimes (EU AI Act, China's algorithm registration, US NIST AI RMF, ASEAN guidance), creating a standardization vacuum where no shared technical language exists for communicating AI risk across borders. The paper argues that laws alone are insufficient to operationalize compliance consistently, and that an ISO-like interoperability protocol layer is needed to enable machine-readable, cross-jurisdictional risk communication without harmonizing legal thresholds.

## Input / Output

The manifest schema consumes structured AI system metadata (model fingerprint, version, deployment regions), standardized evaluation metrics (fairness scores like Equalized Odds and Disparate Impact, inference energy in kWh, robustness AUC), training data lineage (sources, geographic scope, licensing, integrity hashes), and deployment context (intended use, prohibited uses, human-in-the-loop status). It produces a versioned, cryptographically signed JSON or YAML artifact organized into fixed blocks (data_provenance, fairness, energy, privacy_security, monitoring, conformity, attestations) with an explicit regulatory crosswalk mapping fields to EU AI Act risk tiers and NIST AI RMF functions, making the same manifest interpretable under multiple legal regimes.

## Method

The paper proposes a modular, versioned machine-readable schema for AI Risk Manifests (termed 'AI nutrition labels') that standardizes three core risk dimensions—bias/fairness, energy consumption, and data provenance—using ISO-style must/should language and structured JSON/YAML formats. A key design distinction separates integrity (guaranteed by cryptographic attestations via SigStore/Rekor-style signing) from truthfulness (requiring independent third-party re-verification against hashed dataset artifacts), shifting the trust model from narrative transparency to evidence-backed, machine-checkable assurance. The schema incorporates a regulatory alignment crosswalk so a single manifest functions as a reusable compliance artifact across jurisdictions without overriding local legal thresholds. To address Goodhart's Law and adversarial compliance, the design mandates disclosure of metric selection rationale, supports continuous post-market monitoring with certificate revocation, and enables probabilistic third-party spot checks via API-accessible telemetry. Diffusion is modeled on the SBOM precedent: procurement-driven adoption through public-sector mandates and cloud provider integration, followed by open-source tooling commoditization and eventual ISO/IEC ratification, rather than requiring prior international treaty negotiation.

## Results

This is a position paper without empirical experiments or quantitative benchmarks. The paper's primary contribution is a proposed machine-readable AI Risk Manifest schema (illustrated in JSON/YAML) encoding three unified risk dimensions: bias (e.g., equalized_odds_score = 0.92, disparate_impact_ratio = 0.87 in the worked example), energy consumption (e.g., training_co2_tons = 75, inference_kwh_per_1k = 0.05), and data provenance. The paper argues that such manifests would reduce redundant compliance costs for SMEs and enable cross-jurisdictional interoperability, but provides no measured performance data or ablation studies to validate these claims empirically.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare its proposed method against empirical baselines. Instead, it draws conceptual analogies to existing standardization efforts: the GDPR–ISO 27001 coupling (showing how legal mandates were operationalized through technical standards), the Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) as a procurement-driven diffusion precedent, and the ATLAS framework for ML lifecycle provenance. The paper contrasts four regulatory regimes (EU AI Act, China's algorithm governance, US NIST AI RMF, ASEAN guidance) to illustrate the 'standardization vacuum' its proposal addresses, but does not benchmark its schema against any competing manifest format (e.g., Model Cards, AI FactSheets, or the UK ATRS) in terms of coverage, expressiveness, or compliance cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GDPR–ISO 27001 coupling model (law defines 'what,' standards operationalize 'how') to the AI domain, and draws a direct structural parallel to the Software Bill of Materials (SBOM), whose procurement-driven diffusion via US Executive Order 14028 the authors adopt as their preferred adoption mechanism. It goes beyond existing human-readable documentation efforts—Model Cards, IBM AI FactSheets, the ATLAS framework, and the UK ATRS—by proposing a unified, machine-readable, versioned schema that integrates fairness metrics (from AI Fairness 360, Fairlearn), energy reporting (building on CarbonTracker), and data provenance (via W3C PROV) into a single interoperable artifact. The key departure from prior work is the explicit framing of AI risk manifests as a technical interoperability layer complementary to, rather than subordinate to, jurisdiction-specific law, with cryptographic attestation (Sigstore-style) as a verification primitive.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and growing pain point—the 'standardization vacuum' where the same AI system may be classified as low-risk in one jurisdiction and high-risk in another—by proposing a shared technical substrate that reduces redundant compliance costs, particularly for SMEs. Open questions remain substantial: the truthfulness gap (cryptographic signatures guarantee integrity but not that evaluations were conducted honestly), Goodhart's Law gaming of manifest metrics, the political feasibility of multi-stakeholder standardization amid geopolitical competition, and the 'cold start' problem of achieving initial adoption without a binding treaty. As a position paper, its impact depends on whether the proposed schema gains traction in procurement and certification workflows; the six-stage diffusion pathway is plausible but untested.
