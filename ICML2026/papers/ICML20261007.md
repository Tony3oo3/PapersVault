## Problem

Prior work on transformer state-tracking limitations has focused almost exclusively on out-of-distribution failures such as length extrapolation, leaving open the question of how much in-distribution training data transformers actually require compared to recurrent models. This paper asks whether the inductive bias inherent in recurrent architectures—forcing step-by-step state updates into a single hidden vector—translates into measurable data-efficiency advantages even when training and evaluation distributions match, and whether transformers can share learned state-tracking mechanisms across different sequence lengths.

## Input / Output

The model receives a sequence of n tokens, where each token is either an integer from Z_m (modular addition task) or a permutation from S_5 (permutation composition task), with multi-digit values encoded as single tokens. The output format varies across three supervision regimes: a single final group element (outcome supervision), an autoregressive sequence of partial sums appended after the input (chain-of-thought), or a per-token aligned partial sum produced simultaneously with each input token (aligned chain-of-thought). Sequence lengths are drawn from one of three distributions—fixed at L, uniform over {2,...,L}, or short-to-long—allowing independent control of length and state-space size.

## Method

The paper conducts a large-scale empirical study (over 200,000 training runs) comparing a 6-layer GPT-2 transformer, single-layer LSTM, Dense-SSM (bilinear RNN), and Mamba across modular addition (m up to 100) and permutation composition (S_5) tasks. Data efficiency is quantified via a minimal sample size N* found through a hybrid binary-geometric search over training set sizes, with success defined as validation loss below 10^{-4} across a 15-configuration hyperparameter grid. The key analytical contribution is the Sharing Factor κ, defined as the ratio of cumulative samples needed by independent per-length models to the samples needed by a single jointly-trained model, which reveals whether a model amortizes learning across lengths. The central finding is that recurrent models exhibit κ≫1 (effective weight sharing and amortized learning) in their preferred supervision formats, while transformers show κ≤1 (length-specific isolated solutions or even destructive interference), and this sharing factor correlates strongly with OOD length-generalization ability.

## Results

On modular addition (m=5, uniform length distribution, L=20), the 6-layer GPT-2 transformer requires 1.7K samples under CoT but 2M under ACoT, while the single-layer LSTM needs only 80 samples under ACoT and the Dense-SSM needs 56 (at m=10). Under outcome supervision (no intermediate labels), the transformer fails to converge for m≥10 at L≥10, whereas the Dense-SSM succeeds up to m=20, L=30 with as few as 1.1K samples. The sharing factor κ (ratio of sum of per-length N* to joint N*) is ≈1 or <1 for transformers across all formats (as low as 0.28 under CoT), indicating length-specific solutions, while recurrent models achieve κ≫1 under their preferred formats, confirming effective amortized learning across sequence lengths.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the GPT-2-based transformer (Radford et al., 2019), both the single-layer LSTM (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997) and the Dense-SSM (Ebrahimi & Memisevic, 2025) require orders of magnitude fewer samples: at m=10, L=20, uniform, the LSTM ACoT needs 247 samples versus 3.4K for the transformer CoT (14×), and the Dense-SSM ACoT needs only 56 (61×). The transformer's data efficiency degrades sharply under ACoT (2M samples at m=5, L=20) while the LSTM and Dense-SSM improve with longer sequences, confirming the induction-bias hypothesis. Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2024) exhibits slightly better cross-length sharing than the transformer under outcome supervision and ACoT but remains far below the LSTM and Dense-SSM, and shows destructive interference (κ<1) under CoT. The Llama 3 (Grattafiori et al., 2024) and Pythia (Biderman et al., 2023) transformer variants reproduce the same low-sharing-factor pattern as the GPT-2 model, indicating the result is not architecture-specific. Overall, recurrent models with an induction bias (LSTM, Dense-SSM) dominate transformers in in-distribution data efficiency for state tracking, with the gap widening as state-space size and sequence length increase.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work demonstrating transformer failures in state-tracking (Anil et al., 2022; Dziri et al., 2024; Deletang et al., 2023) by shifting the lens from out-of-distribution length generalization to in-distribution data efficiency. It builds on the algebraic formulation of state tracking as group composition (Marchetti et al., 2026; Merrill et al., 2024) and the recent finding that dense, input-dependent transition matrices in SSMs can recover state-tracking capability (Fan et al., 2024; Ebrahimi & Memisevic, 2025). The key conceptual departure is the introduction of the 'induction bias' framing and the sharing factor metric, which formalize the hypothesis that recurrent architectures' step-by-step state updates enable amortized learning across sequence lengths in a way that transformers' parallel attention mechanism does not.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that transformers exhibit destructive interference (κ < 1) when trained on mixed sequence lengths—meaning a single model is less data-efficient than separate per-length models—challenges the assumption that attention-based architectures should naturally benefit from multi-length training. The orders-of-magnitude data efficiency gap between transformers and RNNs on even simple in-distribution tasks has direct implications for the feasibility of end-to-end learning in agentic scenarios (robotics, GUI control) and may help explain 'context rot' at large context lengths. Key limitations include the reliance on synthetic group tasks that isolate state tracking but omit language-level complexity, and the computational cost (200K+ training runs) that constrains the breadth of architectures and task families evaluated. Whether these patterns transfer to realistic sequential prediction domains remains an important open question.
