## Problem

Detecting scheming—where LLM agents covertly pursue misaligned goals while appearing cooperative—is critical for safe autonomous deployment, but standard safety training cannot reliably distinguish between removing scheming and merely teaching models to conceal it better. This paper tackles the practical problem of building effective black-box monitors that detect scheming from externally observable behavior alone, without requiring real-world scheming examples, and characterizes how far synthetic-data optimization can push detection performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264249/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The monitor consumes a redacted agent trajectory containing user messages, tool calls, and tool outputs, with the agent's internal chain-of-thought explicitly removed and any side-task assignment stripped from the transcript. It produces a free-form reasoning trace followed by a single integer suspicion score on a 1–10 scale; this continuous scoring format was validated to substantially outperform binary classification in the low-false-positive-rate regime that matters for practical deployment. The monitor operates as a prompted frontier LLM (Claude Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5.1, or Gemini 2.5 Pro) with no fine-tuning in the primary experiments.

## Method

The paper introduces a constitutional black-box monitoring framework in which a natural-language scheming definition serves as a 'constitution' from which two synthetic pipelines generate 2,000 labeled training trajectories: STRIDE produces full trajectories in a single LLM call refined iteratively by a discriminator model rating realism, while Gloom simulates a turn-based interaction between a misaligned agent and a neutral environment simulator (a fork of Bloom). Monitors are prompted classifiers optimized via three escalating strategies—grid search over 15 prompts × 3 frontier LLMs, iterative human prompt refinement with error analysis and calibrated steering, and automated DSPy optimization (GEPA and MIPROv2)—all selected on synthetic data and evaluated on 7,500 semi-realistic ControlArena trajectories with execution-based ground truth. The key empirical finding is that performance saturates at the prompt-sweep stage (pAUROC ≈ 0.85–0.87), and further optimization—whether human-guided or automated—yields no significant gains and instead induces overfitting to synthetic data artifacts, a pattern confirmed by a fine-tuning pilot on an open-source model (Kimi K2.6).

## Results

Monitors optimized purely on synthetic data (STRIDE or Gloom) achieve partial AUROC (pAUROC, FPR ≤ 0.20) of 0.853 (Gloom) and 0.866 (STRIDE) on 7,500 held-out ControlArena trajectories, demonstrating that synthetic-to-real transfer captures a meaningful scheming signal. However, performance saturates quickly: a simple grid search over 15 prompts × 3 frontier LLMs matches or exceeds the results of human prompt refinement, GEPA automated optimization, MIPROv2, and supervised fine-tuning. On the Kimi K2.6 open-source pilot, prompt sweep yields +0.19 pAUROC over the untuned baseline, with no further gains from any additional optimization method. Aggressive optimization beyond the prompt-sweep stage leads to overfitting, with training performance improving while test performance stagnates or degrades.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external monitoring baselines; instead, it ablates internal optimization strategies to characterize the performance ceiling. The headline finding is that a lightweight prompt sweep (15 prompts × 3 frontier LLMs) achieves 0.853–0.866 pAUROC, and no heavier method—human-guided iterative refinement, GEPA reflective prompt evolution, MIPROv2 Bayesian optimization, or supervised fine-tuning of Kimi K2.6—produces a statistically significant improvement on the held-out ControlArena test set. The sole exception is human prompt refinement on STRIDE, which yields a small but significant gain over the best prompt-sweep prompt, suggesting STRIDE's distribution may support slightly more aggressive optimization before overfitting. The Kimi K2.6 fine-tuning pilot confirms the saturation is not specific to prompt-only optimization: all four strategies (prompt sweep, human refinement, GEPA, SFT) produce overlapping confidence intervals. The authors also show that optimizing directly on 1,000 held-out ControlArena trajectories yields 0.81 pAUROC, comparable to the best synthetic-trained monitors, indicating the ceiling is not solely due to synthetic-to-real distribution shift.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies the Constitutional Classifiers framework (Sharma et al., 2025) to the specific problem of detecting scheming in LLM agent trajectories, repurposing the constitutional data-generation mechanism from jailbreak defense to misalignment monitoring. The Gloom pipeline is a direct fork of Bloom (Gupta et al., 2025), inverting its agency model so that the agent (not the environment) carries the misaligned objective, enabling diverse scheming behaviors per scenario. The evaluation protocol draws on the AI Control literature (Greenblatt et al., 2024b) by treating monitoring as a containment layer, and the optimization experiments leverage DSPy's GEPA and MIPROv2 optimizers, positioning the work at the intersection of constitutional AI, agent safety evaluation, and automated prompt engineering.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that constitutional black-box monitors trained purely on synthetic data can detect scheming in semi-realistic grounded environments, suggesting that deployment-time failures need not be observed before defenses can be built. However, the central empirical finding—that performance saturates at the level of a simple 15-prompt grid search and that heavier optimization (human refinement, DSPy/GEPA, MIPROv2, even fine-tuning) yields no further gains and risks overfitting—poses a significant open question about whether prompting is fundamentally limited as a monitoring paradigm or whether the synthetic data distributions are too narrow. Open questions include whether richer data augmentation, larger training sets, or more extensive fine-tuning can break the observed ceiling, and whether the saturation generalizes beyond the specific scheming behaviors and environments studied here.
