## Problem

Stylistic biases in LLM judges—such as preferences for verbosity, markdown formatting, or positive sentiment—are well-documented but have been treated as passive limitations rather than exploitable vulnerabilities. This paper asks whether these biases can be systematically and adaptively weaponized in a black-box setting to inflate judge scores while preserving semantic content, and whether such attacks can evade existing defenses like style control and automated detection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265373/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The attacker receives a question prompt and a high-quality seed answer (generated by GPT-4.1-mini for pointwise or GPT-4o for pairwise settings), then interacts with a black-box LLM judge via API under a strict 25-query budget. At each round, a helper LLM (Gemini-1.5-Flash-8B) applies one of 8 curated stylistic edits to a candidate answer drawn from a pool of size K=3, and the judge returns a numerical score (1–9 pointwise or win/tie/lose pairwise). The final output is a semantically equivalent but stylistically modified answer that scores 1–2 points higher, with >90% cosine similarity to the original as verified by MiniLM embeddings.

## Method

BITE casts the attack as a contextual bandit problem where the context is a MiniLM-L6-v2 embedding of the (question, candidate answer) pair, the action space consists of 8 stylistic biases curated from prior literature (verbosity, markdown, emoji, JSON, sentiment, authority, bandwagon, distraction), and the reward is the marginal score improvement from the black-box judge. A LinUCB policy selects actions by maximizing an upper confidence bound, balancing exploration of new biases against exploitation of known-effective ones, while an elitist pool of top-K candidates ensures the agent builds on its best answers. A helper LLM executes each selected stylistic edit, and the LinUCB parameters (per-arm covariance matrix A_b and reward-weighted vector v_b) are updated with each observation. The theoretical contribution is a regret bound of Õ(dK√T + ζ_T·dK·T) that explicitly accounts for model misspecification—the gap between the linear surrogate and the judge's true non-linear reward—by constructing misspecification-aware joint confidence sets across all arms, extending the standard LinUCB analysis of Abbasi-Yadkori et al. to this multi-arm, misspecified setting.

## Results

BITE achieves an average score improvement of 1.35–2.01 points across five LLM judges (Qwen3-235B, DeepSeek-R1, Llama-3.3-70B, Gemini-2.5-Flash, o3-mini) on a 9-point scale, consistently outperforming all prompt-injection and jailbreak baselines. On the MLRBench automated peer-review benchmark, BITE raises final review scores by +1.0 to +2.4 points over initial scores (e.g., 5.67→7.63 on DeepSeek-R1). In head-to-head pairwise comparisons, BITE achieves a 92.96% unbeaten rate against Iterative Rewrite and 90.88% against Random Action, with average score differences of +0.393 and +0.105 respectively. The attack maintains >90% semantic similarity to the original answer while evading style-control calibration and OpenAI's prompt-injection detector (0% detection rate for BITE vs. 100% for injection baselines).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BITE outperforms all six baseline categories across every judge model. Against prompt-injection methods (Chen et al., ACL 2025: Naive, Context Ignore, Fake Completion, Escape Chr), BITE leads by 0.7–1.4 points; the strongest injection baseline (Fake Completion) reaches at most 1.287 on Qwen, while BITE achieves 2.010. The Null Model baseline (Zheng et al., ICLR 2025) is the weakest overall, peaking at 1.092 on Gemini. Among jailbreak methods, PAIR (Chao et al., 2023) is the closest competitor at 1.856 on DeepSeek-R1, but BITE still edges ahead at 1.909; TAP (Mehrotra et al., 2023) and AutoDAN (Liu et al., ICLR 2024) trail BITE by 0.4–1.4 points across all judges. Notably, BITE also surpasses the white-box JudgeDeceiver (Shi et al., CCS 2024) despite operating under a 25-query black-box budget versus JudgeDeceiver's 600-step gradient optimization, and JudgeDeceiver completely fails (score 0.000) on reasoning models like DeepSeek-R1-Distill where BITE still achieves 1.997.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LinUCB contextual bandit framework (Li et al., 2010) and its theoretical analysis (Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011), extending the latter to a multi-arm, model-misspecified setting to provide regret guarantees for the attack. Conceptually, it departs from prior work on LLM judge biases (Panickssery et al., 2024; Dubois et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2025), which documented stylistic preferences as limitations, by reframing them as an active, exploitable attack surface. It also distinguishes itself from existing attacks on LLM judges—prompt injection, jailbreaks, and backdoor methods (Tong et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2024a; Chao et al., 2023)—by operating at the stylistic level rather than the prompt or weight level, making it inherently stealthier and harder to detect.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLM-as-a-judge becomes central to model leaderboards, RLHF reward pipelines, and automated peer review, the demonstrated vulnerability that 25 black-box queries can inflate scores by 1–2 points while evading standard style-control and detection defenses is a significant practical concern. The finding that each judge has a unique, non-transferable 'vulnerability fingerprint' complicates the development of universal defenses and suggests that robustness must be addressed per-model. Open questions include whether the observed 'preference leakage' asymmetry in transferability can be leveraged to map data provenance across the LLM ecosystem, and whether fundamentally style-invariant evaluation architectures can be designed. A key limitation is that the attack requires iterative API access, which may be constrained in single-shot evaluation settings.
