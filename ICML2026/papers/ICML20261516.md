## Problem

Standard multi-resolution hash encodings (I-NGP) are only C0-continuous: first derivatives are piecewise constant and second derivatives vanish within cells, making them fundamentally incapable of providing the analytic spatial derivatives that physics-informed neural networks require for PDE residuals. Existing workarounds—finite-difference stencils or autograd through discontinuous hash lookups—introduce O(ε²) truncation error, instability, or prohibitive multi-pass computational cost, creating an accuracy ceiling around 10⁻⁵ that prevents convergence on high-frequency or sharp-transport PDEs.

## Input / Output

The method consumes spatial coordinates (x, y, z) and time t as query points, with PDE source terms and boundary/initial conditions specified analytically or via collocation sampling. The output is the PDE solution u(x,t) together with its full analytic differential operators (gradient, Hessian, Laplacian) computed in a single forward pass—no finite-difference offsets or repeated evaluations needed. In the SDF learning setting, the output additionally includes smooth curvature derived from the analytic Hessian; in the image reconstruction setting, the network is trained from gradient-only supervision and outputs a scalar field whose gradient matches the target.

## Method

Hermite-NGP replaces d-linear interpolation in multi-resolution hash encoding with bicubic Hermite interpolation, storing 2^d coefficients per grid vertex (function value plus all mixed partial derivatives up to first order in each dimension) in separate hash tables grouped by derivative type. This yields a globally C1-continuous field with well-defined, non-zero second derivatives throughout each cell, enabling fully analytic computation of gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians by differentiating the Hermite basis functions directly—no autograd or finite differences. These analytic encoding derivatives propagate through a SIREN MLP via closed-form recursive chain-rule formulas (exploiting σ''=−ω²σ), so the full Laplacian of the network output is obtained in one forward pass. A coarse-to-fine curriculum training strategy, inspired by multigrid V-cycles, progressively activates finer hash resolution levels to stabilize optimization, and the grouped table structure allows non-uniform capacity allocation across derivative types (e.g., smaller tables for mixed derivatives) to optimize the memory-accuracy tradeoff.

## Results

Hermite-NGP achieves relative L² errors as low as 1.81×10⁻⁵ on Helmholtz 2D (a=10, large model), 6.09×10⁻⁵ on Helmholtz 3D (a=3), and 8.49×10⁻⁵ on Convection 1+1D (c=30), representing 10–20× improvements over the strongest baselines. It is the only method that converges on the high-frequency Helmholtz 2D (a=100) setting (L²=4.59×10⁻²) and on Taylor-Green vortex (L²=7.71×10⁻⁵, 9× better than PIG). On 3D complex-geometry tasks, it achieves 3× lower L² error than PIG on Poisson with mesh boundaries (avg 0.0043 vs 0.0131) and 2.4× lower gradient MAE than NeuralAngelo on SDF learning (avg 0.0456 vs 0.1099). Per-epoch training costs are 1.8–3.6 ms for models up to 16.8M parameters on a single GPU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Hermite-NGP outperforms all 10 baselines on every benchmark where both methods are evaluated. Against the closest PINN-based competitor PirateNet, it achieves 7× lower error on Helmholtz 2D (a=10: 1.81e-05 vs 3.57e-04), 10× on Convection (8.49e-05 vs 8.54e-04), and 14× on Helmholtz 3D (a=3: 6.09e-05 vs 8.40e-04); PirateNet fails entirely on Taylor-Green and Helmholtz 2D a=100. The hash-encoding baseline INGP-FD (finite-difference Laplacian) is beaten by 33× on Convection (8.49e-05 vs 7.02e-01) and 8800× on Taylor-Green (7.71e-05 vs 6.80e-01), confirming that FD-based derivatives introduce a fundamental accuracy ceiling. PIG (Gaussian-based) is the strongest 3D baseline but is still 9× worse on Taylor-Green (7.71e-05 vs 7.27e-04) and 3× worse on Poisson 3D (0.0043 vs 0.0131 avg). On SDF learning, NeuralAngelo's FD-based curvature is 2.4× noisier in gradient MAE (0.1099 vs 0.0456 avg). The additional baselines ∂∞-Grid (6.07e-03) and SIREN (6.67e-02) on Helmholtz 2D a=10 are 300–3700× worse than Hermite-NGP's 1.81e-05, and Hermite-NGP also wins on image reconstruction PSNR (32.56 dB vs ∂∞-Grid and SIREN). SPINN and PIXEL, while competitive on some 2D tasks, fail or degrade severely on high-frequency and 3D settings, and vanilla PINN fails on most Helmholtz configurations entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends multi-resolution hash encoding (I-NGP) by replacing d-linear interpolation with Hermite interpolation and explicitly storing mixed partial derivatives at hash grid vertices, drawing a direct conceptual bridge from gradient-augmented level set methods in computational physics (Nave et al., 2010; Jiang et al., 2015) to neural PDE solving. It departs from prior NGP-based PDE approaches (INGP-FD, NeuralAngelo) that resort to finite differences or autograd through discontinuous hash tables—both of which introduce truncation error or instability—by treating derivative information as a first-class optimization target rather than a post-hoc computation. The SIREN activation is retained for its closed-form second-derivative property, and the coarse-to-fine training strategy borrows from multigrid V-cycles in classical PDE solvers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method eliminates the fundamental accuracy ceiling (~10⁻⁵) imposed by finite-difference Laplacians in hash-encoding PDE solvers, enabling relative L² errors down to 10⁻⁵ with per-epoch costs of 3.5 ms—making high-precision neural PDE solving practical for complex geometries without body-fitted meshes. Key limitations include storage overhead scaling as 2^d in higher dimensions, dependence on SIREN activations for closed-form second derivatives, and restriction to strong-form residuals. Open questions include whether quintic Hermite interpolation can extend to third-order PDEs, whether weak-form (variational) formulations are compatible, and whether the approach generalizes to other activation families without losing the analytic derivative advantage.
