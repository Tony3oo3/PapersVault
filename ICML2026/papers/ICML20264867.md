## Problem

Existing multidisciplinary benchmarks focus on understanding and reasoning tasks, while current text-to-image generation benchmarks emphasize world-knowledge illustration with loose diagnostic criteria. No benchmark rigorously evaluates whether a model can integrate understanding, reasoning, and generation to solve exam-style drawing problems with strict semantic correctness requirements, as encountered in real college-level exams (AP, A-level, IB).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264867/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample provides a complex exam-style text prompt (average 74.8 words, range 24–173) with precise drawing constraints, and the model must produce a single generated image. Each prompt is paired with a ground-truth reference image and 3–14 fine-grained scoring points (average 6.9) phrased as yes/no visual questions (e.g., 'Does the molecule contain exactly 8 carbon atoms?'). The 1,000 samples span 10 subjects organized under a four-level taxonomy (10/40/132/236 categories) aligned with ISCED-F codes, covering image types including chemical structures, geometric shapes, diagrams, maps, plots, sheet music, and trees/graphs.

## Method

GenExam introduces a two-dimensional evaluation protocol tailored to multidisciplinary images. Semantic correctness (0–1) is computed by having GPT-5 answer each scoring-point question via visual question answering, with the ground-truth image provided as reference; the score is the weighted sum of correctly answered points. Visual plausibility comprises three sub-dimensions—spelling, logical consistency, and readability—each rated 0/1/2 by the MLLM judge. Two final scores are derived: a strict score (fraction of images where all scoring points are correct and all plausibility scores equal 2) and a relaxed score (weighted average with weights 0.7/0.1/0.1/0.1). Data curation combines web-collected exam images with existing datasets (MMMU, ScienceQA, TextbookQA, MAVIS, ChEBI, HLE, R-Bench), filtered by GPT-5 on text richness, domain, complexity, and knowledge density, then refined by three PhD annotators who cross-validate prompts and scoring points.

## Results

On GenExam (1,000 samples, 10 subjects), Nano Banana Pro achieves the highest strict score of 70.2% and relaxed score of 93.0%, while GPT-Image-1.5 reaches 42.5% strict and 81.5% relaxed. All other closed-source models (GPT-Image-1, Seedream 4.5, FLUX.2 max, Imagen-4-Ultra) score below 15% strict. Open-source T2I models (FLUX.2 dev: 2.4%, Qwen-Image-2512: 1.5%) and unified MLLMs (BAGEL, Show-o2, BLIP3o, Janus-Pro, Emu3) all score 0% strict, with relaxed scores ranging from 6.7% to 42.3%, revealing a massive gap between closed-source and open-source models on multidisciplinary drawing exams.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Nano Banana Pro dominates all 10 subjects, achieving strict scores above 55% in every discipline (e.g., 97.6% in History, 88.3% in Economics) and near-perfect relaxed scores (91–99.9%). GPT-Image-1.5 is the second-best model but trails Nano Banana Pro by 27.7 points in strict score, with its weakest subject being Music (29.2% strict). Among other closed-source models, GPT-Image-1 (13.1%), Seedream 4.5 (12.3%), and FLUX.2 max (8.6%) cluster in the 8–13% strict range, while Imagen-4-Ultra and Seedream 4.0 score 7.8%. The gap to open-source models is stark: FLUX.2 dev (2.4% strict, 42.3% relaxed) and Qwen-Image-2512 (1.5% strict, 35.3% relaxed) are the strongest open-source T2I models but still fall 40+ points behind GPT-Image-1.5 in strict score. All unified MLLMs (BAGEL, Show-o2, BLIP3o, Janus-Pro, Emu3) achieve 0% strict score, with relaxed scores between 6.7% and 12.9%, indicating they cannot produce a single fully correct image across the entire benchmark. The paper's error analysis attributes open-source failures primarily to rendering and logical consistency issues (spelling errors, mislabeled components, redundant axes) rather than lack of domain knowledge.

## Relation to Prior Work

GenExam carves new ground at the intersection of two previously separate lines of work: multidisciplinary exam benchmarks (MMMU, HLE, R-Bench) that test understanding and reasoning, and text-to-image generation benchmarks (GenEval, MMMG, SridBench) that test visual concept illustration. The authors explicitly position their contribution as absent from both: prior exam benchmarks never evaluate generation, and prior T2I benchmarks never impose the rigor of exam-style scoring with fine-grained, per-sample scoring points. The evaluation methodology repurposes MLLM-as-a-judge (GPT-5) but transforms it from a single-instruction scorer into a multi-question diagnostic protocol inspired by human exam marking, while the four-level taxonomy is grounded in ISCED-F for academic rigor.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a stark and perhaps underappreciated gap: even the strongest closed-source model (Nano Banana Pro) fails ~30% of exam problems, while all open-source models score below 5% strict, suggesting that current T2I systems are far from expert-level multidisciplinary visual reasoning. A key open question the paper surfaces is why unified MLLMs (BAGEL, Show-o2) underperform dedicated T2I models despite possessing multimodal understanding—implying that the bottleneck is not knowledge but the translation of knowledge into precise visual execution. The reliance on GPT-5 as the sole primary evaluator, while validated against human raters, introduces a single-point-of-failure dependency that limits the benchmark's independence as the field evolves.
