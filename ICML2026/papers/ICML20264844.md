## Problem

Classical Bayesian adaptive querying and computerized adaptive testing face a tension between expressiveness and tractability: IRT-based methods rely on low-dimensional parametric latent traits that are restrictive for heterogeneous, high-dimensional response patterns, while flexible Bayesian models require expensive nested Monte Carlo or variational posterior approximations that are impractical for real-time interactive settings. This gap is especially acute in cold-start regimes where neither user history nor item calibration data is available, precisely when strong structured priors are most needed.

## Input / Output

The method takes a bank of m categorical questions (K response categories), a pre-computed dictionary of n LLM-elicited persona-question response distributions (μ_{θ,x} ∈ Δ^{K-1} for each persona θ and question x), a prior weight vector p(θ) over personas, and a feasible question set with a query budget T. It produces a sequence of adaptively selected questions, a posterior distribution over persona membership after each observation, and a finite-mixture predictive distribution over each target question's response. Notably, all item-level response distributions are obtained entirely offline from an LLM (GPT-5-mini) via direct probability elicitation, so no observed responses to items are required at deployment time; the only data-dependent component is the persona prior, learned via EM from training users.

## Method

The core contribution is a persona-induced latent variable model where user heterogeneity is captured by a discrete latent variable θ ∈ {1,…,n} representing membership in a finite dictionary of AI personas, with each persona-question pair yielding a categorical response distribution obtained by prompting an LLM. Under the conditional-independence assumption, Bayesian inference admits closed-form posterior updates over the finite persona set (p(θ|Y_{I_t}) ∝ p(θ)∏_{i∈I_t} μ_{θ,i,Y_i}) and finite-mixture posterior predictions (p(Y_x=k|Y_{I_t}) = Σ_θ μ_{θ,x,k} p(θ|Y_{I_t})), eliminating the need for nested Monte Carlo or variational approximations that bottleneck standard Bayesian experimental design. This tractability enables direct implementation of greedy one-step-lookahead adaptive querying and non-adaptive optimal design with efficient expected-uncertainty computations at each step. The persona prior p(θ) is learned via empirical Bayes (EM over the marginal likelihood of training users), downweighting rarely-matched personas and mitigating misspecification when applying a synthetic persona dictionary to a real population. The key architectural insight is that LLM persona outputs function as explicit components of a Bayesian prior rather than heuristic simulation tools, combining the expressiveness of LLM-generated response distributions with the computational simplicity of discrete latent variable models.

## Results

On real WorldValuesBench users (17,692 held-out), the greedy persona-based policy achieves the lowest log loss at small budgets (1.019 at T=5, 1.001 at T=15), outperforming the best CAT baseline (MGRM at 1.044 and 1.022 respectively). At larger budgets (T≥20), the non-adaptive persona design overtakes greedy, reaching 0.977 log loss at T=20, while the best CAT method (MGRM) plateaus at 1.011. On synthetic users with a well-specified prior, the gap widens substantially: greedy achieves 1.089 log loss at T=5 versus 1.164 for MGRM, and 1.006 at T=86 versus 1.141 for MGRM. Brier score and ordinal MSE show the same qualitative ordering across all budgets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four CAT/IRT baselines (GRM, GPCM, MGRM, MGPCM) against persona-based querying policies on WorldValuesBench. Persona-based methods outperform all CAT baselines across every budget and every metric (log loss, Brier score, ordinal MSE). The largest gaps are against the unidimensional GPCM baseline (e.g., 1.001 vs. 1.104 log loss at T=15 for greedy vs. CAT-GPCM), while the closest competitor is the multidimensional MGRM (1.001 vs. 1.022 at T=15). Notably, the non-adaptive persona design achieves its best advantage at moderate budgets (T=20: 0.977 vs. 1.015 for MGRM), reflecting a robustness benefit under model misspecification. At T=86 (all questions asked), persona methods converge to 0.998 log loss, still beating the best CAT (MGRM at 1.006). On synthetic data with a correctly specified prior, the advantage is even more pronounced, with greedy reaching 1.006 vs. 1.141 for MGRM at T=86, confirming that the gains on real data are not solely due to CAT's structural limitations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes LLM-generated persona response distributions as an explicit finite-mixture Bayesian prior, bridging three previously separate threads: classical Bayesian experimental design (Lindley, 1956; Rainforth et al., 2024), computerized adaptive testing / item response theory (Wainer et al., 2000; Van der Linden & Glas, 2010), and LLMs as human-behavior simulators (Argyle et al., 2023; Aher et al., 2023; Horton, 2023). It departs from neural BED methods (Foster et al., 2021; Ivanova et al., 2021) by retaining closed-form posterior updates rather than learned surrogates, and from LLM-based adaptive elicitation work (Piriyakulkij et al., 2023; Handa et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025) by maintaining an explicit discrete latent variable with interpretable persona semantics rather than relying on predictive uncertainty from a neural sequence model. Structurally, the model is a latent class analysis (Goodman, 1974) whose mixture components are specified offline by an LLM rather than estimated from task-specific data, which is the key departure enabling cold-start deployment without per-item calibration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in eliminating the item-calibration bottleneck of classical CAT: new questions can be incorporated immediately via LLM prompting, making the framework deployable in cold-start item regimes where IRT simply cannot operate. The observed efficiency-robustness tradeoff—where greedy adaptive querying is overtaken by non-adaptive design under model misspecification at larger budgets—offers a useful caution for practitioners deploying adaptive systems with imperfect priors. Key limitations include dependence on persona dictionary quality and LLM fidelity, restriction to categorical responses, and the fact that the empirical Bayes prior fitting, while lightweight, still requires a training split. Open directions include online dictionary refinement, extension to continuous or free-text responses, and longer-horizon RL-style policies.
