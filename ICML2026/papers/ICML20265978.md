## Problem

Despite the promise of LLMs for recommender systems, no robust scaling laws have been established for their continual pre-training (CPT) in the recommendation domain, forcing researchers into costly trial-and-error. The authors argue this failure is not algorithmic but stems from the pathologically poor quality of raw user interaction logs—riddled with position bias, popularity bias, exposure bias, and noise—which prevents predictable power-law behavior even in large models (e.g., PLUM's 3B model failing to outperform its 900M counterpart).

## Input / Output

The model consumes user interaction histories (UIH) where each item is encoded as a sequence of 8 discrete semantic tokens (e.g., REC5824 REC2334 REC4570 REC3722 REC7479 REC3968 REC2388 REC6054) generated by a pre-trained Sparse Autoencoder applied to item text descriptions. The output is the next item in the sequence, also represented as semantic tokens. All training data is synthetic: UIH sequences are generated via random walks over a collaborative-filtering graph rather than drawn from real user logs, and the evaluation test sets are held-out real interactions filtered to the shared item vocabulary.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-layer synthetic data curriculum that purifies recommendation signals before CPT. Layer 1 establishes semantic grounding (item-text alignment) and collaborative filtering knowledge (association rules mined from co-occurrence statistics, expressed as templated natural-language statements with lift values). Layer 2 generates position-debiased user interaction histories by constructing an item-item graph from CF co-occurrence weights and simulating user journeys via Node2Vec 2nd-order biased random walks, which have no intrinsic concept of rank or presentation order, eliminating positional artifacts by construction. Qwen3 models (0.6B–8B) are then continually pre-trained on 163B tokens with a 50/50 mix of general-domain text (SmolLM-Corpus) and the synthetic recommendation curriculum (9% item-text, 30% CF, 11% UIH). This yields the first robust power-law scaling (L(D) = L∞ + A·D^−α) across recommendation domains, with UIH showing the strongest exponent (α ≈ 0.45–0.59), and reveals an asymmetric cross-domain transfer where CF data reduces UIH asymptotic loss by 31% but the reverse does not hold.

## Results

Across model scales from 0.6B to 8B parameters trained on 163B tokens, the paper establishes the first robust power-law scaling for recommendation CPT: L(D) = L∞ + A·D^(−α). User Interaction History (UIH) exhibits the strongest scaling exponent (α = 0.45–0.59, L∞ ≈ 0.64–0.66), followed by Collaborative Filtering (α = 0.28–0.39, L∞ ≈ 0.33–0.36), Item-Text alignment (α = 0.13–0.21, L∞ ≈ 0.86–1.07), and General domain near saturation (α ≈ 0.02–0.03). In the TSTR validation, standard sequential models (SASRec, GRU4Rec, NARM, STAMP) trained exclusively on synthetic data outperform those trained on real data by up to +130% on Recall@100, confirming the synthetic curriculum captures more generalizable preference patterns.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against other LLM-based recommendation systems (e.g., PLUM, OneRec, LUM) in a head-to-head comparison; instead, its primary comparison is data-centric: Train-on-Synthetic-Test-on-Real (TSTR) versus Train-on-Real-Test-on-Real (TRTR). Across all four sequential models (SASRec, GRU4Rec, NARM, STAMP) and all cutoff points (Recall@10, @100, @1000), TSTR consistently and substantially outperforms TRTR, with SASRec showing the largest gap (+130% on Recall@100). This demonstrates that the synthetic curriculum is a superior training resource rather than merely a high-fidelity substitute. A secondary comparison is across model scales: the 8B model achieves the lowest General-domain perplexity (0.79) but UIH L∞ plateaus at ~0.66 from 0.6B onward, indicating that even 0.6B parameters suffice for the recommendation domains in this dataset. The ablation further shows that adding CF data to UIH reduces asymptotic UIH loss by 31% (L∞ from 0.95 to 0.66), while the reverse transfer (UIH→CF) does not hold, validating the layered curriculum design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the model-centric and distillation-centric approaches to LLM recommendation scaling (PLUM, LUM, SUAN, OneRec) by arguing the bottleneck is data quality, not architecture. It repurposes Node2Vec random walks—originally a graph feature-learning method—as a data generation engine, constructing a two-layer 'pedagogical curriculum' (item-text alignment + CF association rules, then position-debiased synthetic interaction histories) that purifies collaborative signals from positional and presentation-order artifacts. The synthetic continued pretraining literature (Yang et al., 2024; Allen-Zhu & Li, 2024a) provides the theoretical motivation that diverse, grounded synthetic data outperforms repetition of source documents, and the Chinchilla framework supplies the scaling-law formalism, but no prior work had applied either to the recommendation CPT setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By establishing the first robust power-law scaling for LLM CPT in recommendation (across 0.6B–8B parameters, 163B tokens, 7 evaluation domains, and two model families), this paper gives practitioners a quantitative roadmap for data curation and compute allocation that was previously absent. The asymmetric cross-domain transfer finding (CF data reduces UIH asymptotic loss by 31%, but not vice versa) provides actionable guidance for data mixing ratios. Key open questions include whether the framework generalizes to truly massive catalogs (hundreds of millions of items), whether popularity bias in graph edge weights can be further mitigated, and whether the inverse-scaling phenomenon on OOD items (smaller over-trained models outperforming larger near-optimal ones) persists under different training regimes or post-training stages.
