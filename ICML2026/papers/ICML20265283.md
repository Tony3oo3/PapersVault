## Problem

Existing misfit-based analyses of weak-to-strong generalization (W2SG) rely on convexity assumptions on the student hypothesis class, leave the residual error term implicit, ignore the statistical dependence between teacher and student induced by training, and fail to explain why increasing student capacity is necessary for W2SG. This paper removes these restrictive assumptions and provides an explicit analytic characterization of the residual, identifying a concrete sufficient condition under which the student provably outperforms the teacher.

## Input / Output

The method consumes soft pseudo-labels generated by a weak teacher model over text data samples, along with a pre-trained strong student model (GPT-2 or Qwen variants), and produces a fine-tuned strong student model whose expected population risk is bounded by the teacher's risk minus the expected misfit plus an explicit residual. The theoretical output is a two-sided risk inequality (forward and reverse) where the residual is expressed as a product of the student's distance from its posterior mean teacher and the student's own risk, making the condition for W2SG directly interpretable.

## Method

The core technical contribution replaces the generalized Pythagorean theorem (used in prior misfit analyses) with a Bregman bias-variance decomposition, which eliminates the need for convexity and realizability assumptions on the student hypothesis class. By taking expectations jointly over data and model parameters, the analysis captures the teacher-student statistical dependence and yields an explicit residual term that vanishes when the student converges to its 'posterior mean teacher' (the teacher's posterior conditioned on the student). For squared loss, an overparameterized ridge regression example proves that increasing student size drives the residual to zero while the misfit remains positive, guaranteeing W2SG. For cross-entropy, the theory shows that lower student predictive entropy promotes W2SG and that reverse cross-entropy (RCE) is more robust to teacher uncertainty than standard CE, motivating a confidence-adaptive loss (CACE) that switches between RCE and CE based on teacher confidence.

## Results

On the CAI-Harmless reward modeling task, the proposed CACE loss achieves the highest test accuracy across all six teacher-student pairs, reaching 96.75% (Qwen-1.8B→Qwen-7B) and 96.58% (Qwen-7B→Qwen-14B), outperforming standard CE by up to 1.12 points. On HH-RLHF, CACE achieves 69.78% (Qwen-7B→Qwen-14B) and 69.20% (Qwen-1.8B→Qwen-7B), with the largest gain over CE being +3.17 points (Qwen-1.8B→Qwen-14B: 69.20 vs. 66.03). The symmetric cross-entropy (SL) baseline is competitive, occasionally matching or exceeding CACE on HH-RLHF with the largest model pairs (e.g., 70.85% vs. 69.78% for Qwen-7B→Qwen-14B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CACE against four baselines: standard CE, RCE, the AUX confidence-regularized loss from Burns et al. (2024), and the Symmetric Cross Entropy (SL) from Wang et al. (2019). CACE consistently outperforms AUX across all 12 teacher-student configurations on both datasets, with gains of 0.2–3.2 points, confirming that confidence-adaptive switching is more effective than the fixed warm-up regularization in AUX. Against SL, CACE wins on all six CAI-Harmless settings (by 0.12–0.45 points) and on two of six HH-RLHF settings (notably Qwen-1.8B→Qwen-14B: 69.20 vs. 68.65), but SL edges out CACE on the remaining four HH-RLHF pairs, most dramatically on Qwen-7B→Qwen-14B (70.85 vs. 69.78, a 1.07-point gap). Both CACE and SL substantially outperform plain CE and RCE, validating the paper's theoretical claim that combining forward and reverse cross-entropy better exploits teacher confidence signals. The paper also demonstrates that RCE alone is far more robust than CE under high teacher uncertainty (label smoothing with α=0.001), where CE accuracy drops sharply while RCE remains stable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the misfit-based W2SG analysis initiated by Charikar et al. (2024) and Mulgund & Pabbaraju (2025), which relied on the generalized Pythagorean theorem and required convexity of the student hypothesis class. The key departure is replacing the Pythagorean argument with a Bregman bias-variance decomposition (Pfau, 2013; Adlam et al., 2022) and analyzing expected population risk jointly over data and model parameters, which removes convexity, realizability, and sequential consistency assumptions while yielding an explicit residual term. The paper also builds on the empirical W2SG framework of Burns et al. (2024) by formalizing the role of the 'posterior mean teacher' and introducing reverse cross-entropy (RCE) and confidence-adaptive CE (CACE) as practical training objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first clean sufficient condition for W2SG—student convergence to its posterior mean teacher—and shows concretely (via overparameterized ridge regression) that increasing student capacity drives the residual to zero while the misfit remains strictly positive, explaining why larger students are needed in practice. The practical finding that RCE is robust to teacher uncertainty while CE is not, and that combining them via CACE consistently improves performance, offers actionable guidance for W2S training pipelines. However, the sharpest quantitative results are confined to ridge regression; extending the analysis to non-convex deep networks (where double descent under CE lacks formal characterization) remains open, and the paper acknowledges that pre-training's role in shaping representations is not captured by the current theory.
