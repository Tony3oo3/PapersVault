## Problem

While prior work has identified task-specific neurons (e.g., for multilingual processing or code generation), it remains unclear whether LLMs contain a tiny set of neurons that is persistently engaged across diverse capability dimensions and whose removal causes global capability collapse. This paper asks whether such a cross-task, structurally indispensable neuron subset exists and, if so, what its properties and functional role are.

## Input / Output

For detection, the method consumes only four short prompts (one each from MMLU, MATH500, EvalPlus, and MGSM) to record per-neuron activations during generation, then outputs an extremely sparse keystone set (typically <0.2% of all neurons; e.g., 30 out of 1,245,184 in Llama-3.1-8B). For the fine-tuning application, the input is 10k instruction-response pairs (OpenMath Instruct 2 or WildGuard), and the output is a model where only keystone-associated weights are updated while all other parameters remain frozen, producing text generations that improve on the target task while preserving other capabilities.

## Method

The identification proceeds in two stages: (1) multi-prompt activation analysis records |a_i(t)| for every neuron across four capability-dimension prompts, sorts neurons within each layer-module block, and takes the intersection of top-ρ sets to form a candidate pool; (2) α-controlled masking progressively deactivates the top-α fraction of this ranked list and sweeps α to locate the sparse frontier where a handful of neurons already triggers complete benchmark collapse. The paper then characterizes these keystone neurons through multiplicative rescaling (showing they are tightly calibrated and far more sensitive than random neurons), activation patching (showing precise token-wise values matter, not just a constant), and depth-wise distribution analysis (showing they are largely established during pretraining). For fine-tuning, gradients of all non-keystone neurons are zeroed after back-propagation so that only keystone-associated weights receive updates, yielding math and safety gains comparable to or exceeding full-parameter SFT while substantially reducing weight drift and preserving non-target capabilities.

## Results

Across all evaluated models (0.6B–30B, dense and MoE), deactivating the identified keystone neurons (<0.2% of all neurons) causes complete capability collapse: all four benchmark accuracies (MMLU, MATH500, MGSM, EvalPlus) drop to 0, while perplexity on C4/WikiText-2 increases by 1–3 orders of magnitude (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct C4 perplexity goes from 11.78 to 522). In contrast, ablating the same number of random neurons preserves accuracy near base levels. For fine-tuning, Keystone-only SFT on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct achieves 51.20% MATH500 and 6.67% AIME-2024, outperforming Full FT (31.40% and 0.00%) while preserving MMLU (69.8 vs. 62.5) and EvalPlus (0.550 vs. 0.322). On WildGuard safety, Keystone-only reaches 82.82% vs. 75.65% for Full FT on the 8B model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between Keystone-only fine-tuning (updating only parameters associated with the identified keystone neuron set) and standard Full-parameter fine-tuning, both using identical data (10k OpenMath Instruct 2 pairs) and optimization hyperparameters. On the 8B model, Full FT causes severe math regression (MATH500 drops from 49.60% to 31.40%) and substantial non-math degradation (MMLU 70.9→62.5, EvalPlus 0.532→0.322), whereas Keystone-only improves math (MATH500 49.60→51.20, AIME 3.33→6.67) while keeping non-math scores near base. On the 1B model, both methods improve over base, but Keystone-only achieves larger gains on every math benchmark (e.g., GSM8K 50.27 vs. 45.03) and the strongest non-math scores (MMLU 44.8 vs. 41.4). On the WildGuard safety task, Keystone-only outperforms Full FT by 7.17 points (8B) and 4.18 points (1B). Weight-drift analysis confirms the mechanism: Keystone-only induces ~4 orders of magnitude less parameter change (3–5×10⁻⁸ vs. 2–3×10⁻⁴), acting as an implicit regularizer that prevents the representation drift and negative transfer observed in full-parameter updates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neuron-identification literature from task-specific units (language-specific neurons, knowledge neurons, task-specific neurons) to a fundamentally different question: whether a tiny, cross-task-active subset is necessary for *all* capabilities simultaneously. Prior work on modularity in LLMs (Qiu et al., Bricken et al., Pochinkov) and on specialized neurons (Tang et al., Song et al., Dai et al.) established that individual capabilities map to identifiable subcomponents, but none had isolated a compact, prompt-stable backbone whose joint removal collapses the entire model. The fine-tuning component repurposes the lottery-ticket and PEFT intuition—restricting updates to a sparse, pre-identified subset—but applies it to a structurally defined (activation-based) rather than randomly discovered subnetwork.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical upshot is that a handful of neurons (as few as 30 in an 8B model) can serve as a minimal intervention surface for both interpretability (ablation, scaling sensitivity) and parameter-efficient fine-tuning that outperforms full-parameter SFT while preserving unrelated capabilities. Key limitations include coverage of only 0.5B–30B open-weight models, a fixed set of four capability axes, and the open question of whether the same ultra-sparse backbone persists at frontier scale or in proprietary systems. Future work on interaction with quantization, compression, and broader capability taxonomies is explicitly flagged as needed to confirm generality.
