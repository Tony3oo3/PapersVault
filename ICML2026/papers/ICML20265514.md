## Problem

Transformers frequently learn brittle heuristics rather than generalizable algorithms, but it remains unclear when and why this happens even on tasks with known algorithmic solutions. This paper asks two questions: (Q1) whether a given-depth Transformer can represent the correct algorithm on a given graph instance, and (Q2) whether gradient descent actually leads to that algorithm or instead to a shortcut supported by the training distribution. Using graph connectivity as a testbed, the paper shows that the answer to (Q2) depends critically on whether the training data aligns with the model's architectural capacity.

## Input / Output

The model receives a self-loop augmented adjacency matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{n×n} (the standard adjacency matrix plus the identity, so diagonal entries are always 1) and produces an n×n real-valued score matrix where positive entries indicate predicted connectivity between the corresponding vertex pair. Correctness is binary: the model is 'perfect' on a graph if and only if every positive score corresponds to a truly connected pair and every zero/negative score corresponds to a disconnected pair. Training uses Erdős-Rényi graphs ER(n,p), while evaluation includes out-of-distribution families such as 2Chain (two isolated chains) and 2Clique (two isolated cliques).

## Method

The paper analyzes the Disentangled Transformer, a simplified architecture where MLPs are removed and each attention layer appends its output as a new coordinate slice rather than adding to the residual stream, making the computation tractable for analysis. The key theoretical result is an exact capacity bound: an L-layer model can solve connectivity on all graphs with diameter ≤ 3^L (via a matrix-powering algorithm) but provably fails on some graph of diameter 3^L + 1 under nonnegative weights. Under the permutation symmetry of the task, trained weights decompose into two channels: an I_n-channel that implements the local matrix-powering algorithm and a J_n-channel that computes global degree-based heuristics. Training dynamics analysis shows that when the training distribution is dominated by within-capacity graphs (diameter ≤ 3^L), the algorithmic channel suffices and gradient descent suppresses the heuristic channel; when beyond-capacity graphs are prevalent, the heuristic channel is promoted as a compensatory mechanism. The practical prescription—restricting training to within-capacity graphs—transfers empirically to standard Transformers, improving OOD generalization.

## Results

A 2-layer Disentangled Transformer achieves ≥99% accuracy on node pairs with shortest-path distance ≤ 3² = 9, but predictions drop sharply beyond this threshold (Fig. 2); a 3-layer model extends this to diameter 27. Under standard unrestricted ER(n=20, p=0.08) training, a 2-layer standard Transformer reaches near-perfect in-distribution exact-match accuracy but drops to ~0% on OOD 2Chain and 2Clique graphs. Restricting training to within-capacity graphs (diam ≤ 9) causes the same 2-layer standard Transformer to generalize to both OOD families (Fig. 6, Fig. 10). The algorithmic I_n-channel energy share rises to ~100% under within-capacity training, while the heuristic J_n-channel is suppressed; the model tolerates a small fraction (ρ < ~10%) of beyond-capacity graphs before OOD performance collapses.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it contrasts two training regimes for the same architecture. Unrestricted Erdős-Rényi training (the implicit baseline) yields near-perfect in-distribution accuracy but catastrophic OOD failure (~0% exact-match on 2Chain and 2Clique for both standard and Disentangled Transformers). Within-capacity restricted training (diam ≤ 3^L) is the proposed intervention: for standard 2-layer Transformers, it recovers OOD generalization on both 2Chain and 2Clique (Fig. 6, Fig. 10), and for Disentangled Transformers it drives the algorithmic channel to ~100% energy share while suppressing the degree-based heuristic (Fig. 4). The paper also shows that removing at-capacity graphs (diam < 3^L) or including beyond-capacity graphs (diam > 3^L) both degrade OOD performance, identifying the exact 3^L cutoff as critical (Fig. 11–12). A stratified-sampling experiment (Fig. 5) further shows the model is robust to up to ~10% beyond-capacity noise before a phase transition to heuristic-dominated behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Disentangled Transformer architecture (Friedman et al., 2023; Nichani et al., 2024) as a tractable proxy for standard Transformers, and refines the expressivity bound of Merrill & Sabharwal (2025) from an asymptotic O(exp(L)) to the exact 3^L diameter threshold. While prior work established that log-depth Transformers can represent the matrix-powering algorithm for connectivity, this paper is the first to analyze what gradient descent actually selects, showing that the training distribution—specifically whether it contains beyond-capacity graphs—determines whether the model learns the algorithmic solution or a degree-based heuristic. The permutation-equivariant parameterization (W = A⊗I + B⊗J) provides a clean algebraic decomposition into algorithmic and heuristic channels that prior expressivity analyses did not exploit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, actionable prescription: restricting training data to graphs within the model's 3^L capacity threshold suppresses heuristic learning and promotes the true algorithmic solution, improving OOD generalization. This reframes the data curation problem for algorithmic tasks from 'more diverse data is better' to 'data must align with architectural capacity.' Key limitations include the nonnegativity assumption in the capacity upper bound, the population-level (not finite-sample) training dynamics analysis, and the fact that theoretical results are for Disentangled Transformers while transfer to standard Transformers is empirical only. Open questions include whether the capacity-based data restriction generalizes to other algorithmic tasks (sorting, arithmetic, pathfinding) and whether the 3^L threshold holds without the nonnegativity constraint in practice.
