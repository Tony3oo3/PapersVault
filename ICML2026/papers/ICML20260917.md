## Problem

Existing MoE compression methods make pruning decisions at the expert level—either removing whole experts or assigning uniform prune ratios—yet the paper shows that information within even 'high-importance' experts is highly concentrated in a small fraction of channels, making expert-level granularity too coarse to capture internal redundancy and leading to misallocated pruning budgets. No prior work provides an accurate, scalable metric that quantifies per-expert channel-level redundancy across hundreds of experts in modern MoEs.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained MoE model (e.g., Qwen3-30B-A3B with 48 layers × 128 experts) and a calibration set of ~3M tokens (C4 for general tasks, GSM8K or OpenCodeReasoning for math/code) to compute per-channel saliency scores and expert importance priors. It produces a structurally pruned MoE with heterogeneous per-expert channel budgets, where each expert's retained channel count is aligned to a hardware block size (64 or 128) for BitsAndBytes NF4 4-bit quantization compatibility. If an expert's budget falls below a minimum threshold, the expert is entirely trimmed and the router dimension is shrunk accordingly.

## Method

The framework has three components. First, Attribution-based Loss Approximation (ALA) estimates expert importance via a first-order Taylor expansion of the loss with respect to expert outputs, computing ΔL ≈ −(∂L/∂z_ℓ,e)ᵀ z_ℓ,e in a single backward pass per layer, reducing calibration cost by 14–26× over exhaustive expert ablation. Second, Coverage-Maximized Budget Allocation (CBA) reformulates the pruning objective from uniform ratio allocation to maximizing cumulative channel-score coverage under a global channel budget: it precomputes per-expert prefix sums of sorted channel scores, then binary-searches a single scaling factor α over the ALA-derived importance prior to find the largest coverage vector ρ(α) = min(αφ, 1) whose minimal channel count N(ρ) meets the budget, applied hierarchically at both inter-layer and intra-layer levels. Third, Alignment-Aware Redistribution (AAR) converts the optimal per-expert budgets into hardware-compatible dimensions using Hamilton's largest-remainder apportionment: channels are rounded down to the nearest block multiple, and the released quota is redistributed as whole blocks to experts with the largest fractional remainders, ensuring divisibility by the GEMM block size without wasting capacity on padding.

## Results

On Qwen3-30B-A3B, the proposed OursQ (P25% + Q4b) achieves 5.27× storage reduction (61.06 GB → 11.64 GB) while matching the unpruned baseline average accuracy (75.66 vs. 75.64) across ARC-C, ARC-E, HellaSwag, PIQA, BoolQ, WinoGrande, and MMLU. Under aggressive 50% channel pruning without quantization, the method reaches 95.0 on MATH500 (vs. 92.8 baseline) and 70.81 average on general tasks. On Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B, OursQ achieves 5.12× compression (28.67 GB → 5.60 GB) with a 69.07 average that surpasses the 68.32 baseline. On DeepSeek-MoE-16B, OursQ delivers 5.22× compression (32.7 GB → 6.26 GB) with 63.78 average, approaching the 65.62 baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method consistently outperforms all six baselines at matched or comparable storage budgets. Against Wanda (unstructured pruning), OursQ achieves 69.07 vs. 68.02 average on Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B at similar storage (5.60 vs. 7.10 GB), while on Qwen3-30B-A3B Wanda's unstructured P25% collapses to 35.76 average versus OursQ's 75.66. Against MoNE (expert replacement), the gap is dramatic: OursQ scores 69.07 vs. 62.17 on Qwen1.5 and 63.78 vs. 33.94 on DeepSeek-MoE-16B at comparable storage. Against He et al. (holistic MoE compression), OursQ leads by 7.11 points on Qwen1.5 (69.07 vs. 61.96) and 4.08 on DeepSeek-V2-Lite (67.61 vs. 63.97). Against EAC-MoE (joint pruning + quantization at 3.03-bit), OursQ achieves 69.07 vs. 61.77 on Qwen1.5 despite using 4-bit (higher precision) at slightly larger storage (5.60 vs. 4.35 GB). Against C-Prune (cluster-driven expert pruning), OursQ leads by 5.32 points on Qwen1.5 (69.07 vs. 63.75). Against PuzzleMoE (expert merging), OursQ achieves 75.66 vs. 71.9 on Qwen3-30B-A3B at substantially lower storage (11.64 vs. 46.56 GB for M25%). Notably, OursQ surpasses the unpruned baseline on Qwen1.5 (69.07 vs. 68.32) and matches it on Qwen3 (75.66 vs. 75.64) after lightweight DoRA fine-tuning, while under 50% pruning it reaches 95.0 on MATH500 (vs. 92.8 baseline) for Qwen3-30B-A3B.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant expert-level pruning paradigm (MoNE, C-Prune, He et al.) by operating at the channel level within each expert, motivated by the observation that intra-expert information is highly concentrated in a small fraction of channels. It repurposes attribution-based loss approximation (a first-order Taylor expansion of the loss w.r.t. expert outputs) to replace costly per-expert ablation, and introduces a coverage-maximization objective that reformulates prune-ratio allocation as maximizing cumulative channel-score coverage under a global budget. The alignment-aware redistribution step borrows Hamilton's largest-remainder apportionment from political science to satisfy low-bit GEMM block-size constraints, a connection not previously drawn in the MoE compression literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: over 5× storage reduction with under 1% accuracy drop on knowledge benchmarks, and in some cases the pruned model surpasses the original on reasoning tasks (e.g., MATH500: 95.0 vs. 92.8), suggesting that aggressive channel pruning removes noise that degrades complex reasoning. The finding that router statistics are poorly correlated with true expert contribution challenges a widely-used heuristic in MoE compression. Open questions include whether the coverage-maximization objective generalizes to MoEs with hundreds or thousands of experts, how it interacts with dynamic or learned routing, and whether the lightweight DoRA fine-tuning step can be eliminated for truly training-free deployment.
