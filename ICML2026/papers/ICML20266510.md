## Problem

The field increasingly uses human cognitive benchmarks (Rosch's typicality norms, THINGS database) to evaluate whether LLMs develop 'human-like' concept representations, but these benchmarks derive from narrow Western populations and assume stable, universal cognitive structures that neither LLMs nor humans actually possess. This position paper argues the entire evaluation paradigm rests on a chain of unsupported assumptions, producing contradictory findings across studies and motivating potentially harmful alignment interventions based on phantom failures.

## Input / Output

The paper critiques an evaluation paradigm in which LLMs (text-in, text-out systems trained on global multilingual data) are administered cognitive benchmarks—categorization tasks, similarity judgments, and concept organization assessments—originally designed for human participants with preestablished 'human norms.' The output of the paper itself is a structured four-part critique identifying which foundational assumptions fail, engagement with four alternative justifications for the status quo, and stakeholder-specific recommendations (for evaluation designers, researchers, alignment teams, and reviewers) rather than a new benchmark or model.

## Method

The paper structures its argument as a 'house of cards' with four interdependent assumptions, each refuted by targeted evidence: (1) LLM outputs reflect stable internal structures—refuted by prompt sensitivity research showing 13–85% performance gaps from option reordering and up to 76 accuracy-point shifts from formatting alone; (2) human cognition provides a universal standard—refuted by five decades of cross-cultural research documenting systematic variation in categorization, reasoning, and neural processing; (3) adherence to or deviation from norms yields meaningful information—refuted by contradictory conclusions across studies using the same Rosch and THINGS benchmarks; (4) diagnosed failures warrant alignment correction—refuted by evidence of safety tax, alignment-forgetting trade-offs, and culturally biased alignment effects. The paper then engages four alternative views (cognitive universals, population-specific framing, training-data fixes, statistical averaging) and closes with concrete recommendations for each stakeholder group, arguing that evaluation should characterize emergent patterns across diverse linguistic and cultural conditions rather than measuring deviation from a single population's norms.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not report empirical results from its own experiments. The key quantitative evidence it cites to support its argument includes: Pezeshkpour & Hruschka (2024) showing 13%–85% performance gaps from mere option reordering across benchmarks; Sclar et al. (2024) documenting up to 76 accuracy-point differences from formatting changes alone; and contradictory conclusions across studies using the same THINGS database (Du et al. 2025 find human-like representations emerge naturally, while Studdiford et al. 2025 find it depends on architecture, and Hrytsyna & Alves 2025 find alignment depends on input description richness).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new method or compare against baselines in the conventional sense. Instead, it critiques the evaluation framework itself by highlighting that studies using the same cognitive benchmarks (Rosch's typicality norms, THINGS database) reach incompatible conclusions: Shani et al. (2026) find LLMs 'fall short on fine-grained semantic distinctions,' Du et al. (2025) conclude human-like representations emerge naturally, Studdiford et al. (2025) find the result is architecture-dependent, and Hrytsyna & Alves (2025) show alignment varies with input description richness. The paper argues these contradictions reveal a broken evaluation framework rather than genuine insights about LLM cognition, and calls for culturally pluralistic evaluation approaches designed for globally-trained systems rather than tests measuring alignment with a single population's cognitive norms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not build on a single prior method but rather synthesizes three distinct literatures to critique a growing evaluation paradigm: (1) cross-cultural cognitive psychology (Chiu 1972, Nisbett et al. 2001, Gutchess et al. 2010) documenting systematic variation in categorization and reasoning across populations; (2) prompt-sensitivity research (Pezeshkpour & Hruschka 2024, Sclar et al. 2024, Zhuo et al. 2024) showing LLM outputs shift dramatically with superficial input changes; and (3) alignment-tax studies (Huang et al. 2025, Lin et al. 2024, Ryan et al. 2024) documenting unintended consequences of alignment procedures. It departs from recent LLM evaluation work (Shani et al. 2026, Du et al. 2025, Studdiford et al. 2025, Hrytsyna & Alves 2025) that applies Rosch's typicality norms and the THINGS database to LLMs, arguing these tools were designed for narrow Western populations and assume stable internal structures that LLMs do not possess.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a rapidly growing trend of borrowing cognitive-psychology benchmarks to assess LLM 'human-likeness,' arguing the framework is structurally incapable of measuring what it claims due to prompt sensitivity, cultural non-universality, and contradictory cross-study findings. Its most consequential implication is for alignment teams: if 'failures' are diagnosed using culturally specific norms, corrective interventions may inscribe bias and degrade capabilities (the 'safety tax' and 'alignment-forgetting trade-off'). A key open question the paper leaves unresolved is what evaluation framework *should* replace the current one for globally-trained, multilingual LLMs; the authors acknowledge they offer no fully developed alternative, only a call for culturally pluralistic, prompt-robust evaluation designed around what LLMs actually are rather than what humans are.
