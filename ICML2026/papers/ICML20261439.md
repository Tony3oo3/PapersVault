## Problem

When deploying a single predictor across multiple subpopulations, existing robust optimization criteria (minimax regret, group DRO) compare absolute loss or regret across groups, implicitly assuming equal potential predictability. This leads to models that extract vastly unequal fractions of available signal when groups differ in inherent predictability. The paper asks how to formulate group fairness so that the criterion is comparable across groups with different task difficulties, and provides a game-theoretic answer grounded in cooperative bargaining theory.

## Input / Output

The method takes a feature vector x and a group label g for each sample, along with a pre-specified baseline predictor f_0 (e.g., the unconditional mean in regression or the majority class in classification) and a function class F. It produces a single group-fair predictor f_RI that maximizes the worst-case relative improvement ρ_g(f) = (R_g(f_0) − R_g(f)) / (R_g(f_0) − R_g(f_g*)) across all groups, where f_g* is the group-optimal predictor. The output is a predictor (not a per-group model), and the key constraint is that f_0 must belong to F for the no-harm guarantee to hold.

## Method

The core contribution is a new fairness criterion—relative improvement—that measures each group's risk reduction as a fraction of its total achievable improvement from a baseline, and maximizing its worst case: f_RI = argmax_f min_g ρ_g(f). The paper proves this optimization corresponds exactly to the Kalai-Smorodinsky bargaining solution, inheriting its axiomatic properties (Pareto optimality, symmetry, scale invariance, individual monotonicity) that absolute-scale methods like minimax regret lack. For m > 2 groups, a leximin refinement resolves non-uniqueness while preserving these axioms. The paper establishes geometric well-posedness (compactness and convexity of the risk set under mild regularity conditions), a no-harm guarantee (no group is worse than baseline), and an O(1/√n) finite-sample convergence rate for the empirical estimator. The framework unifies existing methods—group DRO, maximin explained variance, minimax regret—as different classical bargaining solutions (Rawlsian, Egalitarian, Equal Loss), enabling axiomatic comparison through a single Table 1.

## Results

On the North Dakota race partition (age feature, gap ratio ≈ 0.10), minimax regret yields per-group relative improvements of (−74%, 82%), leaving the white group worse than baseline, while maximin relative improvement achieves (57%, 57%), equalizing proportional gains. Across 400 ACS configurations (50 states × 2 group partitions × 4 features), the oracle gap ratio ranges from 0.04 to 3.48 (median 0.80); only 27% are near-symmetric, with 26% exceeding a factor-of-two asymmetry. The empirical estimator achieves an O(1/√n) convergence rate for the worst-group relative improvement, matching rates established for minimax regret.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares maximin relative improvement (MMRI) against three absolute-scale baselines: minimax regret (MMR, Agarwal & Zhang 2022), group DRO (Sagawa et al. 2019), and maximin explained variance (MMV, Meinshausen & Buhlmann 2015). In the North Dakota race/age example, MMR allocates nearly all model capacity to the non-white group (82% improvement) while degrading the white group below baseline (−74%), whereas MMRI equalizes both at 57%. Axiomatically, MMRI is the only method satisfying full Pareto optimality, symmetry, scale invariance, and individual monotonicity simultaneously; MMR and MMV satisfy only weak Pareto optimality and lack scale invariance, while group DRO (Rawlsian) also lacks scale invariance. The Nash solution satisfies IIA but violates individual monotonicity, meaning richer function classes can harm some groups. The paper positions these as complementary: the axiomatic framework serves as a decision guide where practitioners select the criterion matching their normative priorities, with MMRI being the natural choice when groups differ substantially in inherent predictability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies existing group fairness criteria—minimax regret (Agarwal & Zhang, 2022), group DRO (Sagawa et al., 2019), and maximin explained variance (Meinshausen & Buhlmann, 2015)—under a single cooperative bargaining framework, showing each corresponds to a distinct classical bargaining solution (equal-loss, Rawlsian, and egalitarian, respectively). The novel contribution is the relative improvement criterion, which the paper proves to be exactly the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution (Kalai & Smorodinsky, 1975; Moulin, 1984; Imai, 1983), inheriting its axiomatic properties of scale invariance and individual monotonicity that absolute-scale methods lack. The paper departs from prior robust-optimization approaches by normalizing risk reduction by group-specific potential improvement, and by establishing well-posedness conditions (Theorem 3.4) that formally bridge learning problems to bargaining theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The axiomatic framework (Table 1) gives practitioners a principled decision guide: when groups differ substantially in inherent predictability—as the ACS experiments confirm for 73% of real configurations—scale invariance makes relative improvement the natural choice, while the no-harm guarantee (Theorem 4.1) ensures no group is degraded below baseline, a property minimax regret provably violates. Open questions include the algorithmic complexity of computing leximin solutions as the number of groups grows, the behavior in overparameterized regimes where strict convexity and standard complexity controls fail, and extension to settings like LLM evaluation across heterogeneous benchmarks. The framework's scope beyond fairness to general multi-objective optimization is noted but left unexplored.
