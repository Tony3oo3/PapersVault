## Problem

Existing multilingual policy optimization methods either confine each training question to a single response language or assume a fixed dominant language (typically English) provides superior supervision. This assumption fails when knowledge is unevenly distributed across languages, causing models to overlook underrepresented knowledge embedded in their parameters. The paper asks how to adaptively select which languages to explore during RL training so that the most informative cross-lingual knowledge is surfaced for each question.

## Input / Output

The method takes a multilingual training question along with automatically assigned topic (e.g., regional knowledge, reasoning, safety) and region labels, and produces a multilingual rollout group of K responses in router-selected languages. A fixed quota K_on of rollouts is reserved for the input language to preserve on-policy learning, while the remaining K−K_on rollouts are sampled from the router's language distribution. Each rollout is scored by a cross-lingual calibrated similarity reward gated by a language-consistency indicator, and the final gated rewards drive a GRPO policy update.

## Method

LRPO introduces a trainable language router parameterized by a topic-by-language matrix A and a region-by-language matrix B, which converts topic and region logits into a language distribution via temperature-scaled softmax. Language selection is formulated as a contextual multi-armed bandit: the router is updated every M policy steps using exponential moving averages of aggregated per-(topic, region, language) rewards, with ε-greedy sampling and simulated annealing on both ε and the softmax temperature to balance exploration of underutilized languages against exploitation of informative ones. To make quality signals comparable across languages, LRPO performs offline estimation of per-language-pair similarity distributions (from semantically equivalent, naturally mismatched, and hard contrastive pairs) and applies either mean-based or quantile-based calibration to raw cross-lingual similarity scores during training. A language-consistency gate zeros out the reward for any rollout that does not follow its assigned target language, ensuring the policy is incentivized to expose knowledge encoded in diverse languages. The overall training loop alternates between multilingual rollout generation, GRPO policy updates with calibrated gated rewards, and periodic router adaptation.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-1.5b-it, LRPO achieves an overall average score of 32.15 across five multilingual benchmarks, improving +3.51 over the vanilla instruction-tuned model (28.64) and +1.73 over GRPO (30.42). The largest gains appear on open-ended generation tasks: mGSM-v2 improves from 24.87 (vanilla) to 38.25, and CARE-pro from 35.73 to 52.51. Across all three model families (LLaMA3.2-1b, Qwen2.5-1.5b, Gemma3-4b), LRPO achieves the best overall score, with seen-language average improvements of +5.08 over the base model and +2.85 over GRPO on Qwen2.5-1.5b.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LRPO outperforms all five baselines on Qwen2.5-1.5b-it, the model where gains are most pronounced: it beats GRPO (30.42) by +1.73, DPO (29.33) by +2.82, and the multilingual-specific methods MAPO (28.40), LIDR (27.58), and MPO (28.38) by 3.75–4.57 points. On LLaMA3.2-1b-it and Gemma3-4b-it, LRPO edges out GRPO by +0.11 and +0.22 respectively, while the other baselines cluster within 0.5 points of the vanilla model. The multilingual baselines (MAPO, LIDR, MPO) that assume English as a dominant supervision language consistently underperform even the monolingual GRPO, suggesting that fixed English-dominant routing is suboptimal. Gains are concentrated on open-ended generation benchmarks (CARE, CARE-pro, mGSM-v2), where cross-lingual knowledge exploitation is most impactful, while multiple-choice tasks (Global-MMLU-Lite, Include-Lite) show only marginal differences across methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as its policy optimization backbone, extending it from single-language rollouts to multilingual rollout groups where language is an explicit, trainable variable. It departs from prior multilingual policy optimization methods (MAPO, LIDR, MPO) that assume a fixed dominant language—typically English—provides higher-quality supervision, instead formulating language selection as a contextual multi-armed bandit problem with topic- and region-conditioned routing. The cross-lingual reward calibration (mean-based and quantile-based) addresses a practical gap not handled by prior work: raw semantic similarity scores are not directly comparable across language pairs, which would otherwise distort preference signals within a multilingual rollout group.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that no single language is universally optimal for eliciting knowledge from a multilingual LLM, the paper challenges the English-centric assumption embedded in most current alignment pipelines and offers a practical, low-overhead mechanism (bandit-based routing) to exploit complementary cross-lingual knowledge during RL training. The introduced CARE-pro benchmark fills a notable gap in evaluating fine-grained, insider regional knowledge and cross-cultural information seeking. However, gains are modest (+2.85 over GRPO on Qwen2.5-1.5b seen languages) and not statistically significant on the weakest model (LLaMA3.2-1b), and the router's expressiveness is limited to two additive matrices (topic×language, region×language), leaving open whether more sophisticated routing or interaction with larger models would yield proportionally larger benefits.
