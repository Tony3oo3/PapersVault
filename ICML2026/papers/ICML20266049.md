## Problem

The debate over emergent capabilities in language models is polarized between advocates who claim capabilities unlock at specific scales and skeptics who attribute breakthroughs to metric thresholding artifacts. Both camps typically analyze single training runs or a few-seed averages at each scale, leaving the full distribution of performance across random seeds uncharacterized. This paper asks whether the apparent discontinuities in scaling curves are instead produced by continuous changes in a bimodal performance distribution that shifts gradually with scale.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266049/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes decoder-only Transformer checkpoints (trained from scratch on synthetic tasks, or partially reinitialized Qwen2.5-0.5B/1.5B for LM experiments) and varies three factors: random seed (200–250 seeds per scale for synthetic tasks, 60–80 for LM tasks), model architecture (independently scaling width or depth), and training data composition (MMLU/C4 mix ratios, hierarchical/linear sentence proportions). It produces the full empirical performance distribution across seeds at each scale, characterized via histograms, KDE, Wasserstein-L2 distances between consecutive scale distributions, per-seed breakthroughness and linearity scores, and continuous-metric equivalents (max per-token loss, NLL) to rule out thresholding artifacts.

## Method

The core contribution is a systematic empirical protocol that trains hundreds of independent seeds at each model scale and characterizes the resulting multimodal performance distribution rather than reporting a single curve. On synthetic length-generalization tasks (counting, reverse-order addition) and LM tasks (MMLU multiple-choice QA via continued pretraining of reinitialized Qwen2.5, and question-formation grammatical generalization), the paper shows that individual seeds sample from a bimodal distribution whose two modes correspond to 'failure' and 'success' clusters; a seed's scaling curve appears emergent when it switches from the low to the high mode between consecutive scales, and linear when it remains within one gradually shifting mode. The paper decomposes the mixture into success probability and conditional mean of successful runs, demonstrating both change continuously even when the overall mode jumps abruptly. To confirm bimodality is not a thresholding artifact, it evaluates continuous metrics (maximum per-token loss for synthetic tasks, NLL on the correct answer token for MMLU) and applies Hartigan's Dip Test for statistical significance. For LM experiments, partial reinitialization of the final attention layer and LM head of pretrained Qwen2.5 models simulates independent training runs at a fraction of the cost of full pretraining, with batch size identified as a sensitivity factor for observing bimodality.

## Results

Across synthetic length generalization tasks (counting and addition), the paper demonstrates that performance across 200–250 random seeds forms bimodal distributions at intermediate scales, with the mode showing sharp jumps while the mean and success probability evolve continuously. On MMLU with Qwen2.5-0.5B, bimodality emerges at 7.5% MMLU training data (one cluster near 0% accuracy, another near 25% random baseline), while Qwen2.5-1.5B shows the same bimodality at just 0.08% MMLU. On the Question Formation task, 3-layer models produce a unimodal distribution around 70% OOD accuracy, while 6- and 8-layer models exhibit clear bimodality with seeds clustering at 0% (linear rule) or 100% (hierarchical rule). Bimodality persists under continuous metrics (NLL loss, max per-token loss), with Hartigan's Dip Test confirming statistical significance (p < 0.001) for MMLU NLL distributions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods; instead, it contrasts experimental conditions to characterize the source of apparent emergence. The key comparison is between thresholded metrics (exact-match accuracy) and continuous metrics (NLL loss, max per-token loss): both exhibit bimodal distributions, refuting the 'mirage' hypothesis that breakthroughs are purely thresholding artifacts (Schaeffer et al., 2024). A second comparison is between single-seed scaling curves and full seed-population distributions: single seeds can appear either emergent or linear depending on which mode they sample, while the population distribution shifts continuously. Third, the paper compares Qwen2.5-0.5B and Qwen2.5-1.5B on MMLU, showing that the larger model recovers the capability at much lower data mixture ratios (0.08% vs. 7.5%), consistent with standard scaling laws. Finally, on the Question Formation task, varying data composition (0–100% hierarchical-inducing sentences) shifts the probability of learning the hierarchical rule but does not eliminate the competing linear-rule solution even at 100% hierarchical data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of the scaling laws literature (Kaplan et al., 2020), the emergent capabilities debate (Wei et al., 2022 vs. Schaeffer et al., 2024), and recent work documenting random-seed variation in out-of-distribution performance (Zhou et al., 2024a,b; Qin et al., 2024; Juneja et al., 2023). It builds on the synthetic length generalization tasks of Zhou et al. (2024a,b) and the question formation setup of McCoy et al. (2018) and Qin et al. (2024), but departs from both the emergence camp (which treats specific scales as deterministic capacity thresholds) and the mirage camp (which claims continuous metrics eliminate apparent breakthroughs) by showing that the relevant object of study is the full performance distribution across seeds, not a single run or a summary statistic. The breakthroughness and linearity metrics from Srivastava et al. (2023) are used to characterize individual scaling curves, but the paper's core contribution is reframing emergence as a stochastic property of a bimodal distribution rather than a deterministic phase transition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that bimodality persists under continuous metrics (NLL loss, max per-token loss) directly challenges the widely-cited resolution that 'just use loss instead of accuracy' eliminates emergence artifacts, with statistical significance confirmed via Hartigan's Dip Test across multiple task domains. Practically, this means that predicting a model's capability at a given scale requires estimating the probability of a successful run, not just the expected performance—a significant shift for resource allocation and scaling extrapolation. Key open questions include: a formal theory for when and why bimodality arises (the paper explicitly notes this gap), the sensitivity of bimodality to hyperparameters like batch size and learning rate, and whether the reinitialization-based LLM experiments faithfully capture from-scratch pretraining dynamics. The paper also leaves unresolved the mechanistic origin of the clusters—whether they reflect competing circuits, optimization basins, or data-order effects.
