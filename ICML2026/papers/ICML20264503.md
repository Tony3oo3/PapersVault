## Problem

Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) suffer from 'overthinking'—performing redundant reflection and exploration even on simple or already-solved problems—yet existing mitigation methods either rely on static, pre-reasoning difficulty estimates or require task-specific training. The paper identifies that problem difficulty actually evolves dynamically throughout the reasoning trajectory and is linearly encoded in step-level hidden embeddings, but no prior method exploits this latent signal for adaptive, training-free reasoning control.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264503/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the LRM processes a text question autoregressively; at each step boundary (delimited by \n\n within the <think>... block), the hidden state from a pre-selected layer is extracted as a step embedding. A lightweight ridge regressor—fitted once offline on 600 MATH training samples and frozen thereafter—maps each step embedding to a normalized difficulty score in [0,1]. The output is the standard reasoning trace and final answer, but with per-step logit biases applied to a fixed set of reflection-keyword tokens, so the model's generation is steered without any parameter modification.

## Method

DyCon has two components. First, it explicitly models evolving difficulty by fitting a ridge regressor (with L2 regularization) that maps step-level hidden embeddings to a log-transformed, min-max-normalized remaining reasoning length, using the remaining length as a continuous difficulty proxy; the optimal layer and regularization strength are auto-selected by maximizing validation R². Second, during inference, at each step boundary the regressor predicts a difficulty score d̂_s, and a difficulty-conditioned logit bias δ_{s,i} = (1−d̂_s)·{√m_{t,i} if d̂_s≥τ, else m_{t,i}} is subtracted from the logits of reflection-keyword tokens (where m_{t,i} is the positive margin of each token's logit over the vocabulary mean). This design ensures that low-difficulty steps receive stronger suppression of reflection triggers (encouraging early convergence) while high-difficulty steps receive gentler suppression (preserving deep exploration), all without modifying the LRM's parameters.

## Results

Across four model backbones (4B–32B) and twelve benchmarks, DyCon achieves up to 40.6% token reduction (QwQ-32B on MMLU-algebra) and up to 6.7% absolute Pass@1 gain (QwQ-32B on AIME25: 66.7% vs. 60.0% baseline) while maintaining or improving accuracy on all math benchmarks. On non-mathematical benchmarks (Table 2), the method yields up to 52.5% token reduction (TriviaQA, DeepSeek-R1-Qwen-7B) and up to 8.6% accuracy gain (GPQA-Diamond, DeepSeek-R1-Qwen-7B: 47.0% vs. 38.4%). The regressor is fitted on only 600 MATH training samples and transfers without adaptation to coding, science, and commonsense tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DyCon consistently outperforms or matches all 11 baselines on accuracy while reducing token usage. Against the strongest logit-suppression baselines (NoWait, DEER, SEAL), DyCon achieves equal or higher Pass@1 on AIME24/25 with 5–17% fewer tokens (e.g., QwQ-32B AIME25: DyCon 66.7% vs. NoWait 56.7%, DEER 50.0%, SEAL 56.7%). Aggressive compression methods (NoThinking, ThinkPilot) reduce tokens dramatically but collapse accuracy on hard problems (e.g., ThinkPilot drops to 10.0% on AIME25 for R1-Qwen-7B vs. DyCon's 36.7%), whereas DyCon preserves accuracy by scaling suppression with estimated difficulty. On non-math benchmarks (Table 2), DyCon improves GPQA-Diamond by +8.6% (R1-Qwen-7B) and +2.0% (Qwen3-4B) while cutting tokens by 26–33%, and reduces TriviaQA tokens by up to 52.2% with no accuracy loss. The method also generalizes to the LLaMA family (Table 4: R1-Llama-8B AIME24 53.3% vs. baseline 40.0%, with 18% fewer tokens), confirming cross-architecture effectiveness. Ablations (Table 3) confirm that replacing the dynamic difficulty estimator with a static coefficient drops AIME24 accuracy by 16.7 points, validating the core evolving-difficulty mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

DyCon builds directly on the logit-suppression mechanism introduced by NoWait (Wang et al., 2025a), which reduces probabilities of reflection keywords to terminate reasoning, but departs from it by making the suppression difficulty-aware and step-level rather than uniform. Conceptually, it extends the line of work on overthinking mitigation (TrimR, FlashThink, DEER, Dynasor-CoT) by replacing static or externally-computed difficulty signals with a training-free linear probe over the LRM's own step-level hidden states, grounded in the empirical observation that evolving difficulty is linearly encoded in these representations. Unlike SFT/RL-based approaches (AdaCoT, OThink-R1) that require curated training data, DyCon requires only 600 MATH samples to fit a ridge regressor, positioning it as a lightweight, model-agnostic control layer on top of any pretrained LRM.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that step-level hidden states linearly encode dynamically evolving difficulty (R² ≈ 0.8) provides a clean, interpretable signal that could inform broader interpretability research on how LRMs track their own reasoning progress. Practically, the training-free nature and cross-domain generalization (fitted on MATH, effective on code, science, and commonsense benchmarks) make it an immediately deployable efficiency tool for reasoning model serving. Key limitations include reliance on remaining generation length as a difficulty proxy (which conflates difficulty with verbosity), the single-layer linear assumption, and evaluation limited to text-only reasoning; extending to multi-modal settings and verifying robustness under adversarial or out-of-distribution inputs remain open.
