## Problem

Test-time scaling (TTS) has been predominantly applied to verifiable domains like mathematics and coding, where response correctness is easily judged. Extending TTS to preference alignment is challenging because response quality is holistic and not reducible to a simple verifiable answer, and existing test-time alignment methods either impose fixed computational overhead or require additional trained reward models. The paper asks how to effectively score and select model outputs for open-ended preference alignment tasks at inference time without any additional training.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (question), a free-form preference text x_p (e.g., 'I dislike gamification'), and a base LLM, and produces a selected aligned response along with a scalar REAR score per candidate. Computing the REAR score requires two forward passes per response: one under the full prompt (question + preference) and one under the question-only prompt, yielding token-level log-probabilities that are combined as a weighted sum. Unlike outcome-based reward models that only score complete responses, the REAR score is applicable to response segments, enabling its use in step-by-step tree search algorithms like DVTS.

## Method

The core insight is to decompose the implicit reward of a pretrained LLM into a question-related component r_0 and a preference-related component r_p, then introduce a tunable coefficient λ to rescale the preference component at test time (REAR = r_0 + λ·r_p). Using the maximum entropy RL framework, the paper proves that this realignment reward reduces to a linear combination of token-level policy log-probabilities: (1−λ)β log π(a|s) + λβ log π(a|s⊕x_p) plus potential-based shaping terms that telescope over a trajectory. This yields a computable sequence-level score S_REAR that requires only the base model's own log-probabilities—no external reward model or additional training. The score is integrated into two TTS algorithms: best-of-N sampling (rank N independently generated candidates by S_REAR) and DVTS tree search (select branches at segment boundaries by REAR scores while promoting diversity). The coefficient λ=20 serves as a robust default across tasks and model families, with λ>1 emphasizing preference adherence and λ<1 prioritizing question answering.

## Results

On the PrefEval benchmark (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct), DVTS with REAR achieves 77.7 on Explicit Preference, 78.6 on Implicit Choice, and 19.1 on Implicit Preference, while BoN with REAR achieves 74.1, 78.2, and 16.2 respectively. Aggregated across all five preference-alignment metrics, DVTS with REAR improves over greedy decoding by 11.6%, over BoN with GenRM by 8.3%, over Amulet by 10.8%, and over LA by 9.3%. On mathematical reasoning (MATH500, AIME24, AIME25, AMC23), REAR-guided BoN consistently outperforms majority voting across both Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507. On MMHal-Bench (Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct), BoN with REAR achieves 84.20 overall score and 21.87% hallucination rate, improving over greedy (78.99 / 28.12%) and BoN with GenRM (80.21 / 23.96%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

REAR-guided methods (both BoN and DVTS) outperform all baselines on the majority of preference-alignment benchmarks. Against BoN with GenRM (Zhang et al. 2025a; Mahan et al. 2024), DVTS with REAR leads by 8.7 points on PrefEval Explicit Preference (77.7 vs. 69.0) and by 6.2 points on Implicit Preference (19.1 vs. 12.9), while BoN with REAR leads by 5.1 and 3.3 points respectively; the gap narrows on Multifaceted Bench (76.8 vs. 76.1) and Ping-Pong (3.03 vs. 3.01). Against Amulet (Zhang et al. 2025c), REAR wins on every benchmark, with the largest margins on PrefEval Implicit Preference (19.1 vs. 13.1 for DVTS) and Implicit Choice (78.6 vs. 70.4). Against Linear Alignment (Gao et al. 2024), REAR dominates on PrefEval Explicit Preference (77.7 vs. 64.2) and Implicit Preference (19.1 vs. 12.8), though LA is competitive on Implicit Choice (78.0 vs. 78.6 for DVTS). On mathematical reasoning, REAR-guided BoN outperforms majority voting (Wang et al. 2023) across MATH500, AIME24, AIME25, and AMC23 for both Qwen models tested. Against the external reward model Skywork-Reward-Llama-8B (Liu et al. 2024a), REAR-guided BoN outperforms on 3 of 4 preference tasks, and REAR additionally enables DVTS (which external RMs cannot support due to lack of segment-level scores). REAR is also 1.4× faster than BoN with GenRM and 1.7× faster than BoN with an external RM, since it requires only a single non-autoregressive forward pass per response.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the maximum entropy RL framework (Haarnoja et al., 2018) and the RLHF objective (Ouyang et al., 2022) to derive a computable reward from the base model's own log-probabilities, and adapts the DVTS tree-search algorithm (Beeching et al., 2025) for preference-guided branch selection. It departs from decoding-time alignment methods (DExperts, Contrastive Decoding, Drift) that modify per-token distributions, and from test-time alignment methods (Amulet, Linear Alignment) that impose fixed per-step overhead, by instead defining a trajectory-level scoring function that is natively compatible with best-of-N sampling and tree search. The key conceptual departure is framing preference alignment as a 'realignment' problem via reward decomposition into question-related and preference-related components, rather than treating the base model as already aligned and merely needing a verifier.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

REAR provides a training-free, single-model mechanism for test-time preference alignment that avoids loading separate reward models, making it deployment-friendly and 1.4–1.7× faster than GenRM/external-RM baselines. The generalization to mathematical reasoning and visual hallucination tasks—by simply specifying a task-relevant preference text—suggests the reward decomposition is a broadly applicable scoring primitive rather than a preference-specific hack. Open questions include how the method behaves under substantially different preference distributions (the λ=20 default may not transfer), whether the performance ceiling on hard subsets like PrefEval implicit preference (≈19) reflects a fundamental limitation of log-probability-based scoring, and how to select λ without a validation sweep in production settings.
