## Problem

Existing confidence estimation methods for LLM reasoning are either restricted to the final answer (providing only a coarse reliability signal) or require internal model access such as token-level logits, limiting them to open-source models. This paper addresses the gap of assigning fine-grained, step-level confidence scores to individual reasoning steps in a fully black-box setting, using only the generated reasoning traces and final-answer correctness labels.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262801/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes N=20 sampled reasoning trajectories per question (parsed into directed reasoning graphs where each step is a node-edge pair of intermediate result and logical operation) along with binary final-answer correctness labels, and produces a confidence score c_ij ∈ [0,1] for every step in every trajectory. Notably, confidence here is defined as correctness-oriented reliability (a step's contribution to a correct answer via consensus alignment) rather than model certainty (token probability), and the framework requires no step-level human annotations—only the naturally available final-answer labels from evaluation.

## Method

The core insight is that correct reasoning traces, despite surface variability in step order and expression, converge on shared logical invariants (consensus anchors), and steps deviating from this consensus are likely erroneous. This is formalized under the Information Bottleneck principle (min I(T_i;Z) − β I(Z;Y)), yielding two complementary instantiations: NIBS, a training-free non-parametric method that scores each step by its semantic similarity (cosine or NLI) to steps in correct trajectories, and GIBS, a learned graph-based model that represents trajectories as directed graphs, constructs a consensus graph via Maximum Common Subgraph aggregation over correct solutions, and trains a BERT + 2-layer GCN encoder to predict a soft selection mask via a loss combining KL-divergence sparsity (compression) and cross-entropy alignment with the consensus mask (relevance). At inference, GIBS outputs per-step confidence directly from a single forward pass, bypassing expensive MCS computation.

## Results

GIBS achieves the highest AUROC in 7 out of 9 dataset–model configurations across GSM8K, MoreHopQA, and Math. On MoreHopQA with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, GIBS reaches 0.8084 AUROC and 0.8357 AUCPR, outperforming the strongest NIBS variant (NLI-Max) by 14.2 AUROC points. On GSM8K with Phi-4-Reasoning, GIBS attains 0.7892 AUROC versus 0.6600 for NLI-Max. Using step-level confidence to guide self-correction improves correction success rate by up to 13.5% over answer-level feedback alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GIBS outperforms all six baselines across every dataset–model configuration. Among white-box methods, P(true) [Kadavath] and LECO [Yao] are consistently the weakest, with AUROC values frequently below 0.50 (e.g., LECO scores 0.2760 on MoreHopQA with Llama3.1-8B), indicating they provide near-random or anti-correlated step-level signals. SL(norm) [Lin 2024] and Token Entropy [Kuhn] perform moderately but remain 10–20 AUROC points below GIBS in most settings; Entropy is the closest white-box competitor on MoreHopQA with DeepSeek (0.6103 vs. GIBS 0.8084). Among the adapted black-box baselines, PARC [Mukherjee] (AUROC 0.589–0.637) outperforms ROSCOE [Golovneva] (AUROC 0.408–0.524) by leveraging premise dependencies, yet both fall 15–25 AUROC points short of GIBS, confirming that local premise-step verification is insufficient without global consensus alignment. The paper's own NIBS variants (particularly NLI-Max and Cos-Mean) serve as strong training-free baselines, with NLI-Max reaching 0.7450 AUROC on GSM8K/DeepSeek, but GIBS still leads by 5–14 points, demonstrating the added value of learned structural subgraph selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends confidence estimation (CE) from the final-answer level to individual reasoning steps, and further restricts the setting to black-box LLMs where token-level probabilities are unavailable. It formalizes the problem through the Information Bottleneck principle, using Maximum Common Subgraph (McCreesh et al., 2017) to construct consensus anchors from correct trajectories. The two instantiations—NIBS (a training-free, non-parametric consensus-alignment method) and GIBS (a graph neural network with a differentiable IB mask)—depart from prior step-level verification work (process reward models, LLM-as-judge) by requiring neither human step annotations nor internal model access, and from prior graph-of-thought representations by using graph structure for confidence attribution rather than for generating alternative reasoning paths.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value is demonstrated by the 13.5% improvement in self-correction success rate when step-level confidence guides regeneration, showing that localizing errors is more actionable than merely flagging wrong answers. Key limitations include the need for N=20 sampled trajectories per question, reliance on structured graph parsing (though parsing success is >93%), and the assumption that at least some correct solutions exist in the sample to form consensus anchors. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very long reasoning chains (hundreds of steps), how it behaves when no correct solution appears in the sample (the label-free self-consistency variant degrades significantly for weaker models), and whether the graph-based formulation is necessary for domains where reasoning is inherently linear.
