## Problem

Transformers show inconsistent success on AI planning tasks, yet the theoretical conditions under which they can length-generalize (verify longer plans than seen in training) remain largely unexplored. A key technical barrier is that existing length-generalization frameworks like C-RASP assume a fixed token vocabulary, while planning with variable numbers of objects causes the effective alphabet to grow at test time, making prior tools inapplicable to the most practically relevant setting.

## Input / Output

The model receives a single tokenized sequence of the form `<init> I <plan> π <goal> G <verdict> V`, where I and G are sets of ground propositions (predicate names followed by object arguments) and π is a sequence of grounded actions (action schema names followed by object arguments), all separated by custom delimiters. The model is trained to predict the final verdict token ('correct' or 'incorrect') via standard causal language modeling. In the variable-universe setting, the number of distinct object identifiers in O can exceed anything seen during training, so the effective input alphabet is unbounded at test time; plan length is measured in number of actions (11–100 for training, 101–200 for OOD evaluation).

## Method

The paper introduces C*-RASP, a formal language extending C-RASP with a Match Predicate that compares object identifiers via local positional offsets rather than fixed vocabulary lookups, enabling reasoning over a countably growing alphabet of object names. They define a Symbolic Limit Transformer—a transformer whose internal product functions satisfy translation invariance (interactions depend only on relative differences in both position and object ID) and locality (interactions vanish beyond a finite bandwidth)—and prove that expressibility in C*-RASP is necessary and sufficient for length generalization under an idealized learning model with a complexity regularizer. Theoretically, they show plan verification is in C*-RASP for delete-free and well-formed domains (positive results via counting-based programs) but not in C*-RASP for general STRIPS or conditional-effect domains (negative results via reduction to FlipFlop and PARITY). Empirically, they train GPT-2 with absolute positional encodings on three custom domains (Heavy Grippers, Colors, Lights Out) in multiple structural variants, confirming that generalization to longer plans and more objects succeeds precisely for well-formed and delete-free formulations and fails for STRIPS and conditional-effect ones.

## Results

The paper evaluates GPT-2 (with APE) trained on plans of 11–100 actions and tested on 101–200 actions across three domains (Heavy Grippers, Colors, Lights Out) in four formulations. Well-formed and delete-free variants achieve high OOD accuracy even at longer plan lengths and with more objects, while the STRIPS variant of Colors shows a clear accuracy drop as plan length increases, and the conditional-effects variant of Lights Out performs at chance level. In controlled OOD evaluations (Figure 6), well-formed and delete-free variants generalize along both the object-count and plan-length axes, whereas STRIPS Colors declines when either dimension increases. The well-formed Lights Out variant generalizes perfectly despite an exponential action space, confirming the theoretical prediction that structural formulation—not problem size—drives learnability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external transformer baselines; instead, it contrasts different formalizations of the same planning domains to isolate the effect of structural properties on length generalization. The key comparison is between well-formed/delete-free formulations (which are in C*-RASP) and STRIPS/conditional-effects formulations (which are not). Well-formed and delete-free variants consistently achieve high OOD accuracy across all three domains, while STRIPS Colors degrades with plan length and conditional-effects Lights Out collapses to chance level. The controlled experiments in Figure 6 further show that the well-formed and delete-free variants generalize when either the number of objects or the plan length is increased independently, whereas STRIPS Colors fails under both conditions. The Lights Out comparison is particularly striking: the same underlying game, when compiled from conditional effects into a well-formed formulation with an exponential action space, generalizes perfectly, demonstrating that the formulation choice—not the intrinsic difficulty of the task—determines transformer learnability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the C-RASP/C-RASP[Pos] length-generalization framework (Yang & Chiang, 2024; Huang et al., 2025b) by introducing C*-RASP, which handles the previously intractable setting where the effective vocabulary grows at test time. It also extends the Limit Transformer learning model of Huang et al. (2025b) to a Symbolic Limit Transformer that accounts for growing alphabets, using a new match predicate to compare object identities without memorizing them. The application to AI planning departs from the almost exclusively empirical analysis of LLM planning failures in the literature (Valmeekam et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2025a) by providing formal language-theoretic characterizations of when plan verification is learnable, connecting planning domain structure (well-formedness, delete-freeness, conditional effects) to expressiveness in C*-RASP.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical explanation for why transformers succeed or fail on planning tasks, identifying that the structural formulation of a domain (not merely its size) is the decisive factor for learnability. The C*-RASP framework has implications well beyond planning, opening the door to analyzing any task where the test-time vocabulary grows (e.g., index hints, variable-length entity sets). Key open questions include extending the theory to plan generation (PSPACE-complete vs. P for verification), Rotary Positional Encodings, and scratchpad/chain-of-thought settings, as well as whether the idealized learning model's predictions hold under realistic training dynamics.
