## Problem

Despite widespread use of verbal confidence prompting to extract uncertainty estimates from black-box LLMs, the internal mechanism by which models generate these scores remains unknown. Two specific questions are unresolved: whether confidence is computed just-in-time when explicitly requested or automatically during answer generation and cached for later retrieval, and whether verbal confidence merely reflects token log-probabilities or a richer evaluation of answer quality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262248/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a question, a previously generated answer (from a separate Phase 0 run), and confidence-classification instructions, and outputs a single confidence class (e.g., 'Almost certain') or numeric score (0–9 or 0–100) without chain-of-thought. The key analytical output is residual stream activations at specific token positions—particularly the post-answer-newline (PANL) token and the confidence-colon (CC) token—extracted across all 62 layers of Gemma 3 27B, with control positions (PANL+1, first-confidence-colon, answer-colon) used to isolate confidence-specific effects from generic content signals.

## Method

The paper employs a convergent suite of six mechanistic interpretability experiments—activation steering, corrupt-then-restore patching, mean-ablation noising, cross-confidence activation swap, attention blocking, and linear probing with variance partitioning—to trace when and where confidence is computed. Steering and patching reveal that confidence representations emerge at PANL at earlier layers (21–25) before appearing at CC at later layers (30–35), while control positions and the answer-generation position show no causal effect. Attention blocking pinpoints the sequential information flow: confidence is gathered from answer tokens via PANL's attention at layers 22–28, then retrieved by CC at layers 30–36, ruling out just-in-time computation at CC. Variance partitioning against six log-probability baselines shows PANL activations explain 38% of verbal confidence variance beyond token probabilities (versus only 10% for the combined log-prob baseline), indicating a second-order evaluation of question-answer fit rather than a simple generation-fluency readout.

## Results

Across six convergent experimental paradigms on Gemma 3 27B (ECE=0.12, AUROC=0.71 on TriviaQA), the paper establishes that verbal confidence is computed via cached retrieval: confidence representations emerge at the post-answer-newline (PANL) token at layers 21–25, are then retrieved at the confidence-colon (CC) token at layers 30–35. Attention blocking confirms the sequential pathway answer tokens → PANL (layers 22–28, ~20% token change rate) → CC (layers 30–36, ~21% token change rate), while blocking CC's attention to question/answer tokens produces only ~10% change (indistinguishable from the PANL+1 control), ruling out just-in-time computation. Critically, variance partitioning shows that PANL activations at layer 40 explain 38% of verbal confidence variance, whereas a combined six-index token log-probability baseline explains only 10% (r=0.32, R²=0.10), indicating verbal confidence reflects a richer evaluation of question–answer fit rather than a simple fluency readout. Results generalize to Qwen 2.5 7B, Magistral Small 24B (reasoning model), and the BigMath and MMLU datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare competing methods in the traditional benchmarking sense; instead, it tests two competing hypotheses about when and what verbal confidence represents. On the 'when' question, the cached retrieval hypothesis is strongly supported over just-in-time (JIT) computation: all six experimental paradigms show confidence representations at PANL emerging at earlier layers than at CC, and attention blocking of CC→Q+A produces effects indistinguishable from the PANL+1 control (~10% token change), confirming CC does not compute confidence de novo from question/answer tokens. On the 'what' question, the second-order (richer evaluation) account is supported over the first-order (log-probability readout) account: a combined six-index log-probability baseline (including length-normalized mean, min, max, variance, first-token, and last-token log-probs) explains only 10% of verbal confidence variance (R²=0.10), while PANL residual activations at layer 40 explain 38%. The answer-colon (AC) position—whose residual stream directly produces the first answer token—shows null causal effects across steering, patching, and noising, and only weak decodable signal (R²~0.2 vs. R²~0.75 at PANL), confirming that verbal confidence is not a readout of the same signals that drive answer generation. Control positions (PANL+1, first-confidence-colon) consistently show no causal effects, ruling out generic out-of-distribution artifacts (confirmed by OOD analysis showing intervention shifts within natural pairwise variability).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability toolkit (activation steering, patching, noising, swap, attention blocking, linear probing) to a new computational target: the internal generation of verbal confidence. It is most directly inspired by Geva et al.'s (2023) cache-and-retrieve mechanism for factual recall, which the authors explicitly parallel, and builds on the causal intervention methods of Meng et al. (2022), Turner et al. (2023), Geiger et al. (2021), and Wang et al. (2023). It departs from Stolfo et al. (2024b), who identified confidence-regulation neurons at the output level but did not trace the upstream computation, and from prior work showing confidence information is decodable from hidden states (Azaria & Mitchell, 2023; Burns et al., 2022), by providing causal evidence for *when* and *how* confidence is computed rather than merely that it is present.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that verbal confidence reflects a second-order evaluation of question-answer fit—rather than a first-order readout of token log-probabilities—has direct implications for LLM reliability: it suggests models possess a form of self-evaluation that could support error detection, and that calibration failures may stem from biases in this distinct computation rather than from generation fluency. The cached retrieval mechanism implies confidence is formed before the model knows it will be asked, opening the door to interventions at the post-answer position for improving calibration. Open questions include whether this constitutes genuine introspection, how the mechanism operates in more complex multi-step reasoning, and whether the overlapping circuits the authors acknowledge (consistent with Lindsey et al., 2025) can be fully disentangled.
