## Problem

Frontier LLMs still hallucinate on simple factoid questions with clear ground truth, and the paper argues this persists because most factuality gains have come from expanding the knowledge boundary (encoding more facts) rather than improving awareness of that boundary (distinguishing known from unknown). The authors conjecture a fundamental discrimination gap—models lack the internal separability to perfectly distinguish correct from incorrect answers—creating an unavoidable tradeoff where eliminating hallucinations requires suppressing valid information (a 'utility tax'). This paper reframes hallucination as confident error rather than mere error, revealing a third path beyond the answer-or-abstain dichotomy: faithfully expressing uncertainty.

## Input / Output

The input is a fact-seeking query (e.g., about rare long-tail entities) processed by a parametric LLM without external tools, combined with the model's intrinsic confidence signal, which is operationalized as the fraction of repeated samples that do not contradict a candidate assertion. The output is a text response in which each factual assertion carries linguistic uncertainty (hedges, qualifiers, epistemic markers) calibrated to that assertion's intrinsic confidence level. Faithfulness is evaluated assertion-by-assertion via the cMFG metric (conditional mean faithful generation), which averages alignment between decisiveness and intrinsic confidence across equal-width confidence bins, rather than relying on aggregate calibration metrics like ECE.

## Method

This position paper proposes 'faithful uncertainty'—aligning a model's linguistic uncertainty with its intrinsic uncertainty—as a complementary objective to knowledge expansion, grounded in the theoretical observation that mapping internal parameters to an output string is a fully observable, closed-loop problem (unlike mapping to external truth). The key reframing is that hallucination equals confident error, so an error wrapped in appropriate hedging becomes a 'useful hypothesis' rather than a trust-eroding hallucination, dissolving the utility-factuality tradeoff. The paper supports this with empirical evidence that AUROC for separating correct from incorrect answers clusters at 0.70–0.85 across methods, making the utility tax unavoidable under the strict factuality objective, and identifies concrete open problems including the bootstrapping paradox for SFT, preserving uncertainty signals through post-training, and disentangling sources of uncertainty (aleatoric vs. epistemic vs. normative). For agentic systems, the paper extends metacognition into a control layer: faithful uncertainty governs when to invoke tools, how to weigh retrieved evidence against internal priors, and when to halt, addressing the storage-vs-control distinction that tools solve the former but not the latter.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that AUROC for separating correct from incorrect answers using model confidence signals clusters in the 0.70–0.85 range across realistic factual QA tasks (e.g., Farquhar et al. report 0.79 average across 30 model×task combinations; Savage et al. find GPT-4 tops out at 0.79 in medical QA; Kang et al. find GPT-4o-mini reaches only 0.68–0.72 on biography generation). At the literature-average AUROC of 0.71, reducing the hallucination rate from 25% to a 5% target requires discarding 52% of valid answers; even at the 0.85 ceiling the utility tax remains ~28%, and only becomes negligible (<5%) at AUROC ≥ 0.95, far above any currently reported method. The SimpleQA Verified visualization (Fig. 3) confirms that frontier models hug the low-refusal diagonal, while the ideal high-factuality/high-utility region remains unpopulated.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper and does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; the 'compares_to' list is empty. Instead, it synthesizes evidence from multiple prior works to characterize the discrimination gap. The paper contrasts calibration-based approaches (which achieve good aggregate alignment, e.g., smECE ≈ 0.014 in the Nakkiran et al. reliability diagram) with the discrimination needed for instance-level hallucination elimination, showing that well-calibrated signals still impose a prohibitive utility tax. It also contrasts the 'expand knowledge boundary' strategy (scale, data, training recipes) with the 'improve awareness of boundary' strategy, arguing the former has driven most factuality gains while the latter remains fundamentally limited. The paper positions faithful uncertainty as complementary to, not a replacement for, continued knowledge expansion, and notes that reasoning models (e.g., OpenAI o1) paradoxically increase hallucination rates while better expressing confidence, suggesting the metacognitive signal is distinct from the factual one.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper builds directly on Yona et al. (2024), which first defined faithful uncertainty as the alignment between a model's intrinsic confidence and its verbalized linguistic uncertainty, and on Ghafouri et al. (2024), which extended the concept to epistemic integrity. The paper departs from the dominant calibration-focused uncertainty quantification literature (Kadavath et al., Tian et al., Nakkiran et al.) by arguing that calibration is insufficient and that the true bottleneck is a discrimination gap—the inability to separate correct from incorrect answers at the instance level. It also synthesizes evidence from truthfulness probing, confident hallucination studies, and the counterintuitive finding that extended reasoning worsens hallucinations to build a unified case that the answer-or-abstain dichotomy is the wrong framing, and extends the metacognitive objective to the agentic tool-use setting where it becomes a control layer rather than merely an expression mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central reframing—hallucination as confident error rather than any error—dissolves the apparent utility-factuality tradeoff and provides a concrete, tractable research objective (faithful uncertainty) that does not require solving the hard problem of knowing when the model is wrong, only when it is uncertain. Key open problems include the bootstrapping paradox (static SFT labels vs. dynamic uncertainty), preserving calibrated uncertainty signals through post-training alignment, disentangling sources of uncertainty for linguistically precise hedges, and developing causal evaluation frameworks that prevent models from mimicking hedging style without genuine introspection. For agentic systems, the paper identifies a critical gap: current search agents lack the metacognitive awareness to decide when to invoke tools, creating systematic overuse and sycophancy to retrieved evidence—problems that will become more acute as agents operate in open-ended environments.
