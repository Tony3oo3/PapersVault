## Problem

Inference-time scaling via Sequential Monte Carlo relies on reward models to guide trajectory selection, yet all deployed systems use only approximate reward models. Despite strong empirical success, there was no theoretical characterization of when approximate rewards suffice, no particle or time complexity guarantees for SMC in this setting, and no understanding of how reward model quality governs the computational cost of reasoning.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a text prompt s_0, an autoregressive base language model π_ref, and an approximate prefix-level reward model V̂ as input, operating at sentence-level granularity where each reasoning step appends a full sentence or segment (rather than a single token). The theoretical contributions produce explicit particle counts N and time complexity bounds as functions of horizon T, target TV accuracy δ_TV, reward model ratio bound L, and Bellman error ε. The algorithms output reasoning trajectories s_{1:T} sampled from the reward-tilted target distribution π_ref(s_{1:T}|s_0)·φ(s_{1:T}).

## Method

The paper models inference-time reasoning as reward-tilted sampling and fits a Feynman-Kac model where the incremental potential G_t equals the ratio of consecutive reward model values V̂(s_0,s_{1:t})/V̂(s_0,s_{1:t-1}) under the naive proposal π_p = π_ref. The central insight is that the Bellman error ε (one-step consistency of V̂ with the optimal twist) is the key quantity: exponential-in-T lower bounds (via Yao's min-max principle) show sub-exponential complexity requires ε = O(1/T), while matching upper bounds show SMC with N = O(L^6 T (1+ε)^{6T}/δ_TV) particles achieves polynomial time in this regime. They further prove that single-particle guided SMC alone cannot reach arbitrary TV accuracy unless ε = 0, and propose a Metropolis-Hastings correction over a finite resampling pool that achieves geometric TV contraction with only O(log(δ_TV^{-1})) correction steps, yielding total time Õ(LT^3 log(δ^{-1}) log(δ_TV^{-1})).

## Results

The paper establishes that the Bellman error ε of an approximate reward model is the key quantity governing inference-time scaling efficiency. Without a small Bellman error, any sampler requires exponential time Ω(L^(2T/3)) (Theorem 4.1) or Ω((1+ε)^(2T/3)) (Corollary 4.2). In the critical regime ε = O(1/T), the SMC sampler with naive proposal achieves polynomial time O(L⁶T²/δ_TV) (Corollary 5.2), and the SP-gSMC+MH method achieves Õ(LT³ log(δ⁻¹) log(δ_TV⁻¹)) (Theorem 6.1), matching the complexity of VGB (Rohatgi et al., 2025) while requiring no special knowledge of L(1+ε) for hyperparameter selection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is VGB (Rohatgi et al., 2025), a rejection-sampling-based backtracking algorithm for imperfect process verifiers. The paper's SP-gSMC+MH method achieves the same asymptotic time complexity Õ(LT³ log(δ⁻¹) log(δ_TV⁻¹)) as VGB, but with a practical advantage: VGB's rejection sampling (Algorithm 3) requires a priori knowledge of the constant L(1+ε) to set its threshold hyperparameter M, whereas SP-gSMC+MH uses a resampling-pool construction that avoids this requirement entirely. The SMC (Naive) method is strictly worse in its dependence on δ_TV (polynomial δ_TV⁻¹ vs. logarithmic log(δ_TV⁻¹)), reflecting the fundamental difference between particle-count scaling and Markov-chain mixing as the accuracy knob. The paper also shows that single-particle guided SMC without any correction cannot achieve arbitrary TV accuracy unless the reward model is perfect (Theorem 4.3), establishing that some form of correction (MH or resampling) is necessary for sub-exponential guarantees.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the twisted Sequential Monte Carlo framework of Zhao et al. (2024), which formalized inference-time reasoning as reward-tilted sampling via a Feynman-Kac flow and introduced the optimal twist (lookahead value function) as the ideal guidance signal. It also draws on Rohatgi et al. (2025) for rejection-sampling-based value-guided decoding and connects to a broader family of reward-guided generation methods (FUDGE, SIXO, Soft-RL, value-based decoding in diffusion). The key departure is that all prior work treats the reward model as a black box or assumes it is exact; this paper is the first to identify the Bellman error of the approximate reward model as the single governing quantity for SMC-based inference-time scaling, and to prove matching (up to constants) exponential lower and polynomial upper bounds conditioned on that error.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that Bellman error O(1/T) suffices to convert exponential reasoning complexity into polynomial complexity provides a concrete, actionable criterion for reward model design: practitioners now know the precision threshold their process verifier must meet for a given reasoning horizon. The information-theoretic lower bound (Corollary 4.2) shows this threshold is tight—no algorithm can escape the (1+ε)^(2T/3) dependence without a smaller Bellman error—so improving the reward model is not optional but fundamental. Open questions include whether the uniform Bellman error assumption can be relaxed to on-policy or moment-based versions (sketched in Remark 3.4), whether the global Bellman error variant (Appendix H) yields tighter bounds, and whether the polynomial guarantees survive in the token-level (rather than sentence-level) setting where the branching factor is much larger.
