## Problem

Stochastic graph augmentations (e.g., DropEdge) regularize GNN training against overfitting but do not improve long-range information flow, while rewiring methods mitigate over-squashing without serving as regularizers. Moreover, training on perturbed graphs while inferring on the original graph introduces a train-inference distribution shift unless an explicit correction is applied. The paper asks whether a single augmentation can simultaneously regularize training and, when needed, enrich connectivity for long-range communication.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a standard undirected graph (binary adjacency matrix and node feature matrix) and produces node or graph class labels, depending on the task. During training, the adjacency is stochastically perturbed each epoch by independently dropping edges with probability p and adding non-edges with probability q; at inference, RADE-OF uses the original graph with standard aggregation, while RADE-OFS uses a modified aggregation that adds the expected contribution of all non-edges. The framework is backbone-agnostic and applies to any weighted-sum message-passing architecture (GCN, GIN, GAT).

## Method

RADE samples a perturbed adjacency each training epoch by independently dropping existing edges (prob. p) and adding non-edges (prob. q), then applies an expectation-preserving aggregation correction so that the training-time message aggregation matches the chosen inference-time aggregation in expectation. Two variants are provided: RADE-OF rescales existing-edge messages and centers added non-edge messages so the expected training aggregation equals the original input-graph aggregation (pure regularization); RADE-OFS rescales existing-edge messages but leaves non-edge messages uncentered, then modifies the inference-time aggregation to include the expected non-edge contribution, thereby creating extra communication paths that mitigate over-squashing. A mini-batch GradNorm rule adaptively updates p and q after each batch by matching the gradient norm of the RADE variance-based regularization proxy to that of the supervised loss, with a density-normalized penalty on q to prevent excessive densification, rendering the method hyperparameter-free in practice.

## Results

On node classification (GCN backbone), RADE-OF achieves the best accuracy on 7 of 8 datasets, with gains over the GCN baseline ranging from +0.06% (Physics, 96.58 vs 96.52) to +0.98% (Flickr, 52.25 vs 51.89 micro-F1). On graph classification, RADE-OFS is best on all six datasets, with the largest improvements on MUTAG (+2.24% over GCN, 86.24 vs 84.00) and peptides-func (+1.83% AP, 56.97 vs 55.14), confirming its advantage when long-range communication is critical. RADE-OF consistently outperforms all non-RADE baselines (Dropout, DropEdge, DropNode, DropMessage) on node classification, with the largest margin of +0.43% on Computer (90.22 vs 89.79 DropEdge).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RADE-OF outperforms all four augmentation baselines (Dropout, DropEdge, DropNode, DropMessage) on 7 of 8 node-classification datasets, with the most consistent advantage over DropEdge (e.g., +0.80% on Computer, +0.71% on ogbn-arxiv) and DropNode (e.g., +0.62% on Computer, +0.54% on ogbn-arxiv). Dropout is the closest competitor on several datasets (Cora, CiteSeer, ogbn-arxiv) but still trails RADE-OF by 0.1–0.3%. On graph classification, RADE-OFS dominates all baselines on every dataset, with particularly large margins over DropEdge on MUTAG (+1.74%) and peptides-func (+1.68%), while RADE-OF is second-best on most graph datasets. The SGC (linearized GCN) comparison in the ablation table confirms RADE-OF-Lin also improves over the unregularized linear baseline on all datasets, though the nonlinear RADE-OF is consistently stronger. Overall, RADE-OF is the more reliable default for node classification, while RADE-OFS excels on graph-level tasks where over-squashing is more pronounced.

## Relation to Prior Work

RADE unifies two previously separate lines of graph augmentation: stochastic deletion-based regularizers (DropEdge, DropNode, DropMessage) that combat overfitting, and connectivity-enriching rewiring methods (virtual nodes, diffusion rewiring, spectral rewiring) that mitigate over-squashing. Its key conceptual departure is treating random edge *addition* as a first-class stochastic regularizer alongside deletion, with expectation-preserving aggregation corrections that ensure train-inference alignment—a property that prior deletion-only methods (e.g., DropEdge) lack by default. The adaptive rate selection borrows the GradNorm gradient-matching principle from multi-task learning and applies it to balance the implicit regularization strength against the supervised loss, making the augmentation hyperparameter-free in practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RADE offers a simple, drop-in augmentation that simultaneously regularizes training and (in the OFS variant) creates additional long-range communication paths at inference, addressing two GNN pathologies in one mechanism without dataset-specific rate tuning. The expectation-preserving correction framework is a useful general principle: it shows that any weighted-sum aggregator can be made alignment-safe under stochastic topology perturbation, and the ablations confirm that omitting this correction causes substantial degradation (up to −2.90% on Flickr). Limitations include modest absolute gains on node classification (≤0.43%), reliance on second-order delta-method approximations for GCN/GAT coefficient expectations, and the variance-regularization analysis (Eq. 5) being derived under a linear MPNN assumption that the nonlinear backbones only approximately satisfy. Open questions include whether the adaptive GradNorm rates converge stably on very large or heterogeneous graphs, and whether the non-interchangeability of add and drop persists under more complex (non-Bernoulli) perturbation distributions.
