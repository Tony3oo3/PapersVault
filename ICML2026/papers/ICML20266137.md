## Problem

Existing neural PDE solvers for freeform 3D geometries are constrained by a 'single-node bottleneck': their learned geometry-encoding mechanisms consume GPU memory proportional to the full mesh, capping processable resolution at the VRAM of one compute unit (typically ~10M nodes). This prevents high-fidelity industrial simulations on meshes exceeding 10 million nodes, where inter-node communication overhead further degrades training efficiency.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a 3D mesh represented as a point cloud (surface nodes for geometry, volume nodes for PDE evaluation) along with a pre-computed sparse segmentation matrix S ∈ R^{K×N} (K geometry tokens, N mesh vertices) generated by a deterministic dihedral-angle-based graph decomposition in under 15 seconds per sample on CPU. Physics parameters (Mach number, angle of attack, material properties, boundary conditions) are incorporated as additional input features. The output is a full-field PDE solution (velocity, pressure, temperature, stress, or displacement) at queried volume or surface nodes, plus task-specific scalar QoIs such as drag coefficient, lift coefficient, or maximum von Mises stress.

## Method

PGD-NO decouples geometry encoding from solution querying by replacing learned feature extraction with a deterministic iterative decomposition algorithm: the mesh graph is partitioned at sharp dihedral angles using a decaying threshold schedule, small fragments are merged via h-hop BFS expansion, and the result is a sparse segmentation matrix S that maps N nodes to K geometry tokens. The architecture projects point features into a high-dimensional space, aggregates them into token space via the matrix product SX, then applies M successive Geometric Token Layers where cross-attention (or a transposed segmentation mapping in variant v3) propagates refined token features back to individual nodes, followed by a final MLP. Because volume nodes serve only as query points and do not participate in surface feature encoding, the volume point cloud can be partitioned across GPUs independently, shifting the memory bottleneck from O(8N_v) to O(3N_s) and enabling linear scalability to 100M+ nodes without inter-node communication. Three architectural variants trade off expressiveness (multi-head attention on tokens) against efficiency (direct transposed mapping), and stochastic spatial sampling during training further reduces peak memory for very large meshes.

## Results

PGD-NO achieves the lowest L² relative error across all five 3D industrial benchmarks. On Heat Sink, PGD-NO-v1 reaches 2.47% field error (vs. 3.09% for Transolver++), and on CFD-VOL it achieves 12.2% (vs. 15.8%), representing relative improvements of ~20% and ~23% over the strongest baseline. For critical engineering quantities of interest, PGD-NO also leads: 3.35% on chip temperature (Heat Sink), 41.7% on max stress (JEB), 16.4% on drag coefficient (DrivAerNet++), 1.31% on lift coefficient (Aircraft), and 13.2% on mid-plane velocity (CFD-VOL). At 60M volume nodes, PGD-NO-v1 maintains 12.5% error while Transolver++ cannot process the mesh at all due to memory exhaustion.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PGD-NO consistently outperforms all nine baselines across the five benchmarks. Against the strongest competitor Transolver++ (ICML 2025), PGD-NO-v1 reduces field error by 20% on Heat Sink (2.47% vs. 3.09%), 8% on JEB (39.4% vs. 40.3%), 5% on DrivAerNet++ (18.2% vs. 19.2%), 6% on Aircraft (4.77% vs. 4.98%), and 23% on CFD-VOL (12.2% vs. 15.8%). The gap is largest on Heat Sink, where the geometry decomposes cleanly into flat facets, and on CFD-VOL, where the external-domain fluid prediction benefits most from precomputed surface tokens. Graph-based methods (GNO, MeshGraphNet) and grid-based methods (GINO, Galerkin) fail entirely on CFD-VOL due to memory exhaustion, while point-based methods (GNOT, Transolver, AB-UPT) handle it but with 2–12% higher error. PGD-NO-v2 (with additional MHA on tokens) wins on JEB's organic geometries, while PGD-NO-v3 (transposed segmentation, no long-range attention) is best on the smooth Aircraft surface, confirming that the optimal variant depends on geometric complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

PGD-NO departs from the dominant paradigm in neural PDE solvers where geometry encoding is a learned, differentiable process (e.g., Transolver++'s Gumbel-Softmax token assignment, GINO's grid mapping). Instead, it repurposes classical mesh segmentation techniques—dihedral-angle detection, iterative graph partitioning, and proximity-based merging—as a deterministic pre-computation step that produces 'geometry tokens' before any neural computation begins. This draws on the geometry decomposition literature (convex decomposition, spectral clustering) that has long been used in CAD and manufacturing but has not previously been integrated into end-to-end neural operator architectures. The learned component is reduced to a lightweight attention-based decoder that queries these fixed tokens, effectively inverting the standard branch-trunk or encoder-decoder split.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: by shifting the memory bottleneck from volume nodes (O(8N_v)) to surface nodes (O(3N_s)), PGD-NO eliminates the single-node VRAM constraint that has capped all prior neural PDE solvers, enabling 60M-node meshes on standard hardware without inter-node communication. However, the accuracy gains over Transolver++ are modest (1–3% L2 relative error), and the method shows a clear performance plateau on irregular, smooth geometries (JEB dataset) where sharp-edge-based decomposition is less effective. Open questions include whether combining deterministic tokens with local learnable refinements can close this gap, whether the approach generalizes to time-dependent or multi-physics problems, and whether the precomputed-token paradigm can serve as a geometry-agnostic backbone for scientific foundation models across diverse meshing formats.
