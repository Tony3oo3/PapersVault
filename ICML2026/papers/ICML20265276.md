## Problem

Most equivariant machine learning interatomic potentials are built on spherical tensors, yet Cartesian coordinates and tensorial physical properties are naturally expressed in Cartesian space. The absence of direct analogues of the Wigner-3j symbol and Generalized Clebsch-Gordan coefficients in Cartesian space has prevented controlled architecture-matched comparisons between spherical and Cartesian models and blocked the ability to assign independent learnable weights to individual irreducible components in Cartesian formulations.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a point cloud of atoms within a fixed cutoff radius (6 Å), encoding angular information via Cartesian harmonics of interatomic unit vectors and radial information via zeroth-order spherical Bessel functions of interatomic distances, with element types passed through one-hot linear embeddings. Outputs include per-atom energies, forces, stress tensors, and higher-order tensorial response properties (dipole moments, polarizability, elastic tensors, etc.), with the readout summing scalar (ℓ=0) node features across layers through an MLP head.

## Method

The paper introduces the Cartesian-3j symbol and Cartesian Generalized Clebsch-Gordan Coefficients by contracting Wigner-3j symbols with path matrices that map between Cartesian and spherical bases, enabling irreducible Cartesian tensor products (ICTP) and contractions (ICTC) with the same computational structure as their spherical counterparts. A key architectural insight is that Cartesian models can avoid Cartesian-3j symbols at the edge level entirely—relying only on reducible Cartesian tensor products/contractions (RCTP/RCTC) between node features and Cartesian harmonics—and apply the ICTD projection matrices only at the node level, reducing the number of arrays involved from three to two per edge interaction. Leveraging this framework, the authors build Cartesian counterparts of NequIP, Allegro, and MACE for controlled comparison (showing comparable accuracy but worse compute/memory scaling), then design TACE-v1-OAM-M (L_max=2, ℓ_max=3, 5 layers, correlation order 2, gated SiLU interactions, ℓ₁≤ℓ₂ path restriction) trained on OMat24+sAlex+MPtrj, achieving competitive Matbench Discovery performance (F1=0.889, κ SRME=0.173) with 18.8M parameters.

## Results

On Matbench Discovery, TACE-v1-OAM-M (18.8M params) achieves an F1 score of 0.889 and a thermal conductivity κ SRME of 0.173, placing it competitively among state-of-the-art uMLIPs but below the top performers (eSEN-30M-OAM: F1=0.925, κ=0.170; NequIP-OAM-XL: F1=0.906, κ=0.125). In the controlled 3BPA comparison, Cartesian versions of NequIP, Allegro, and MACE match their spherical counterparts in accuracy (e.g., cNequIP 64(64)-2-2-4: E=6.5 meV vs. NequIP 5.0 meV) but incur 1.5–3× higher memory and up to 3× slower training. For molecular dynamics speed, TACE-torch reaches 508 ps/day at 192 atoms (FP64), comparable to NequIP-AOTI (543 ps/day) and well above MatRIS-torch (43 ps/day), though it OOMs at 2304 atoms without operator fusion.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TACE-v1-OAM-M (F1=0.889, κ=0.173) trails the top spherical-basis models: eSEN-30M-OAM leads on F1 (0.925 vs. 0.889) while matching on κ (0.170 vs. 0.173), EquFlash wins on both metrics (F1=0.919, κ=0.158), and NequIP-OAM-XL achieves the best κ (0.125) with a higher F1 (0.906). However, TACE-v1-OAM-M outperforms several smaller or older baselines on both metrics: GRACE-2L-OAM (F1=0.880, κ=0.294), DPA-3.1-3M-FT (F1=0.884, κ=0.470), MACE-MPA-0 (F1=0.852, κ=0.412), and MatterSim v1 5M (F1=0.862, κ=0.575). On κ alone, TACE beats MatRIS-10M-OAM (0.218), NequIP-OAM-L (0.166 is slightly better), ORB v3 (0.210), SevenNet-MF-ompa (0.317), and Allegro-OAM-L (0.319), but loses to GRACE-2L-OAM-L (0.169). In the controlled 3BPA architecture-matched comparison, Cartesian NequIP/Allegro/MACE achieve near-identical accuracy to their spherical versions (within ~1 meV energy RMSE) but with 1.5–3× memory overhead and up to 3× slower training, confirming that Cartesianization alone does not yield efficiency gains without dedicated architectural redesign.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the irreducible Cartesian tensor decomposition theory of Shao et al. (2025) and the TACE framework (Xu et al., 2026) by introducing the missing algebraic tools—Cartesian-3j symbols and Cartesian Generalized Clebsch-Gordan Coefficients—that are direct analogues of Wigner-3j and GCGC in spherical space. Prior Cartesian MLIPs (HotPP, CACE, CAMP, TensorNet, ICT potential, CarNet) either used reducible tensors, were limited to low rank, or lacked the precomputation machinery available in spherical methods; ICT potential explicitly concluded that Cartesian product bases could not be precomputed, a claim this paper overturns. By extending e3nn into cartnn and constructing Cartesian counterparts of NequIP, MACE, and Allegro under fixed architectures, the paper enables the first controlled ST-vs-ICT comparison, which no prior work could perform.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that irreducible Cartesian formulations are theoretically sound and achieve accuracy comparable to spherical counterparts, but the exponential dimensional growth of Cartesian tensors makes naive basis substitution impractical—dedicated architectural choices (avoiding edge-level Cartesian-3j, using ICTC for contraction, restricting to highest-weight components) are essential. The finding that nonlinearity substantially improves performance on large training sets (OAM) yet degrades extrapolation on 3BPA is a useful practical caveat for model design. Key open questions include developing operator-fusion kernels for Cartesian tensors, sparse storage strategies for high-rank tensors, and hybrid Cartesian-spherical architectures that could exploit the complementary strengths of each basis; without these, Cartesian models remain at a compute and memory disadvantage relative to spherical methods with libraries like oeq and cueq.
