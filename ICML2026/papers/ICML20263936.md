## Problem

When organizations delegate text generation to LLM providers via pay-for-performance contracts, noisy evaluation inflates expected payments, yet more accurate evaluation methods are expensive. This paper addresses the cost-benefit trade-off between coarse (cheap but noisy) and refined (costly but accurate) performance evaluation by introducing adaptive contracts, where the decision to acquire a refined evaluation depends on the initial coarse signal—a setting that exhibits novel economic properties absent from classic contract design, including cases where no contract can extract any principal utility despite positive first-best value.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a tuple (q⁰, q¹, …, q^ℓ, c, d, r) specifying signal distributions per action, conditional outcome distributions per signal, action costs, per-signal inspection costs, and a reward matrix over (signal, outcome) pairs. It outputs an optimal adaptive contract (p, s, t): a binary (or probabilistic) inspection policy p indicating which signals to refine, a payment vector s for uninspected signals, and a payment matrix t for inspected signal-outcome pairs, along with the principal's expected utility. A key structural constraint is limited liability (all payments non-negative), and the contract must satisfy incentive compatibility so the agent's best response is the targeted action.

## Method

The paper formulates adaptive contract optimization as a mixed-integer QCQP and identifies two tractable families: (i) constant-many actions, where a sparsity argument bounds the number of inspected signals by n−1, reducing the problem to polynomially many LP solves; and (ii) the Independent Signal-Outcome Property (ISOP), where a dual-LP analysis shows the optimal contract pays for at most one signal and one outcome, enabling polynomial-time enumeration over single-signal inspection policies. In the general unstructured case, the paper proves NP-hardness of approximation via reduction from Independent Set, and NP-hardness even under Symmetric-ISOP when MLRP is violated. For randomized inspection, the paper shows committed mixed inspection (CoMI) admits no Stackelberg equilibrium, then restores equilibrium via two practical restrictions—disallowing commitment to inspection probabilities (UMI) and requiring inspected payments not exceed uninspected payments (CoNI)—and proves randomized inspection can strictly improve principal utility under either restriction.

## Results

On AlpacaEval 2.0 (question-answering), the optimal adaptive contract (inspecting short responses, paying $0.47 for favorable outputs and $0.03 for long outputs) incentivizes GPT-4o and yields a principal expected utility of 1.00, which is 14% higher than the closest non-adaptive contract baseline. On SWE-Bench (code generation), the optimal information-design trade-off is a suite of 3 initial tests with an extended suite of 17 tests, and the optimal inspection policy transitions through three distinct regimes (inspect on full success → inspect on full failure → no inspection) as inspection cost increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares adaptive contracts against non-adaptive contract baselines (standard pay-for-performance contracts without selective inspection) on AlpacaEval 2.0. The adaptive contract achieves a 14% improvement in principal utility over the best non-adaptive baseline, primarily by selectively inspecting only short responses (where the coarse length signal is most informative) rather than uniformly inspecting or not inspecting. On SWE-Bench, the comparison is between different inspection policies as a function of inspection cost, showing that the adaptive policy (inspecting conditionally on initial test results) dominates both always-inspect and never-inspect strategies across the cost range. The paper also demonstrates that randomized inspection variants (UMI, CoNI, UNI) can strictly improve over deterministic contracts in some instances (Theorem 5.6), though the CoMI variant fails to admit a Stackelberg equilibrium. No direct comparison against existing LLM pricing schemes (e.g., per-token billing) is provided numerically, though the paper positions adaptive contracts as superior to current opaque pricing in the introduction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classic principal-agent contract design framework (Dutting et al., 2019; Dye, 1986) by introducing adaptive, two-tiered performance evaluation, where the principal can selectively acquire a costly refined signal after observing a coarse one. It builds directly on Saig et al. (2024), which applied contract design to LLM delegation but assumed a single non-adaptive evaluation, and on Dye (1986), which studied costly inspection in a stylized one-dimensional model with restrictive assumptions (strict risk-aversion, monotonicity between quality and inspection costs) that do not hold in the LLM setting. The paper also positions itself relative to action-inspection contracts (Fallah & Jordan, 2024; Ezra et al., 2026), where the principal pays to observe the agent's chosen model rather than a refined quality signal, and to information-design-in-contracts work (Babichenko et al., 2024; Castiglioni & Chen, 2025), but distinguishes its contribution as the designer (not the forecaster) controlling adaptive information acquisition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic treatment of when and how adaptive evaluation contracts are computationally tractable (constant actions, independent evaluations) versus NP-hard, giving practitioners clear structural guidance for designing LLM delegation contracts. The empirical demonstration on AlpacaEval and SWE-Bench shows a 14% utility gain over non-adaptive baselines, validating the framework's practical relevance. Key open questions remain: whether limited correlation (beyond full ISOP) suffices for tractability, whether the CoNI variant admits a polynomial-time algorithm, how results extend to multi-level inspection trees, and how to handle the common-prior assumption in dynamic LLM markets where model capabilities shift over time.
