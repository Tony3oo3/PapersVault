## Problem

Foundation models produce unreliable outputs, and heuristic uncertainty scores cannot perfectly separate correct from incorrect predictions, leaving users without task-specific risk guarantees on accepted answers. Existing risk-control methods for selective prediction (e.g., COIN, Trust or Escalate) rely on worst-case upper confidence bounds (Hoeffding, Clopper-Pearson) that are overly conservative, accepting far fewer valid samples than necessary. The paper asks whether a tighter, still statistically valid finite-sample guarantee can be obtained for the error rate among accepted point predictions, and whether such a guarantee extends to multi-model routing systems.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pretrained model's point prediction and a scalar uncertainty score (predictive entropy for closed-ended tasks; semantic entropy, graph-based scores, or length-normalized self-entropy for open-ended tasks) computed on a held-out calibration set of (uncertainty, error-label) pairs. It outputs a single calibrated threshold (single-model) or a jointly calibrated threshold pair (two-model routing) that, at test time, maps each new input to an accept, route, or abstain decision. The calibration-test split is 50/50 by default, and the threshold is fixed after calibration; no model parameters are updated.

## Method

LEC reframes selective prediction as a decision problem governed by a linear expectation constraint E[Z − αS] ≤ 0, where Z is the joint selection-and-error indicator and S is the selection indicator, so that the constraint directly bounds the ratio of expected accepted errors to expected accepted predictions (the selection-conditioned error rate). Under exchangeability, a finite-sample sufficient condition is derived: the sum of (err_j − α) over the k calibration points with uncertainty below the candidate threshold must be ≤ −1, a leave-one-out correction analogous to conformal risk control. The calibrated threshold is the largest value satisfying this condition, maximizing retention subject to the risk constraint. For two-model routing, the same linear constraint is imposed at the system level on the combined selection and accepted-error indicators, and the threshold pair is chosen by joint optimization to maximize total accepted samples while preserving the system-level guarantee. This expectation-level formulation avoids the worst-case tail conservatism of UCB-based methods, yielding tighter risk control and substantially higher power (e.g., +9% on TriviaQA) while maintaining valid finite-sample guarantees.

## Results

Across eight LLMs on CommonsenseQA and TriviaQA, LEC consistently keeps the empirical selection-conditioned error rate at or below the target risk level (e.g., 0.0497 at α=0.05 on OpenChat-3.5/CommonsenseQA) while operating closer to the target than UCB-based baselines. On TriviaQA at α=0.05, LEC achieves power of 0.7193 with Qwen2.5-14B, retaining 9.5% more admissible samples than UCB-CLP (0.6240), while UCB-HFD yields no feasible threshold. In two-model routing, LEC-Routing at α=0.05 accepts 33.9% of samples (vs. 20.3% for the primary model alone) and admits 1610 correct samples on CommonsenseQA, exceeding both individual models (965 and 1579).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LEC is compared against two UCB-based baselines: UCB-CLP (Clopper-Pearson upper confidence bound, representing COIN-style risk control) and UCB-HFD (Hoeffding-based UCB). LEC wins on both statistical tightness and power across all eight LLMs and all risk levels tested. On TriviaQA at α=0.05, LEC achieves 0.7193 power with Qwen2.5-14B versus 0.6240 for UCB-CLP (+9.5% relative), while UCB-HFD fails to produce any feasible threshold. On CommonsenseQA at α=0.05, LEC reaches 0.6850 power with OpenChat-3.5 versus 0.6556 (UCB-CLP) and 0.4887 (UCB-HFD). In the two-model routing setting, LEC-Routing outperforms UCB-CLP-Routing and UCB-HFD-Routing on system-level risk tightness and sample retention: at α=0.05 on CommonsenseQA, LEC-Routing accepts 33.9% of samples (1610 correct) versus 20.3% for the primary model alone, while UCB-based routing remains more conservative with higher reliance on the secondary model. The advantage is most pronounced at low risk levels where UCB-HFD frequently becomes infeasible, and persists under alternative alignment criteria (bi-entailment, LLM-as-a-Judge) and black-box uncertainty estimators.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the exchangeability-based leave-one-out calibration argument from conformal risk control (Angelopoulos et al., 2024) but departs fundamentally from the UCB-based risk control paradigm used by COIN (Wang et al., 2026) and Trust-or-Escalate (Jung et al., 2025), which enforce worst-case tail bounds via Hoeffding or Clopper-Pearson intervals. Rather than bounding the empirical error rate with high probability, LEC directly constrains the expectation of a linear functional of selection and error indicators, yielding a tighter feasible region. The paper also extends the selective prediction setting from a single model to a two-model routing system, where joint threshold calibration over system-level indicators is required to preserve the statistical guarantee—a formulation not present in prior UCB-based routing work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LEC provides a cleaner and less conservative mechanism for risk-constrained selective prediction that is directly applicable to increasingly common LLM routing pipelines, where practitioners need system-level guarantees without per-model over-conservatism. The finding that naive per-model calibration fails to achieve valid system-level guarantees in routing highlights a subtle but important design consideration for multi-model deployments. Open questions include characterizing when routing yields maximal benefit over single-model deployment, extending the framework to task-specific ratio-form risk metrics beyond error rate, and scaling joint threshold search to deeper routing architectures. The marginal (rather than conditional) guarantee and exchangeability assumption remain standard limitations of the distribution-free calibration literature.
