## Problem

Current reward model (RM) evaluation methods measure only static preference perception accuracy on clean, in-distribution test sets, obscuring critical vulnerabilities that emerge under real-world perturbations such as user typos, format changes, or language shifts. The paper introduces 'suitability'—conditional reliability of an RM under specific perturbations—and asks whether one can statistically infer that an RM exhibits systematic vulnerabilities in a given real-world scenario, rather than merely scoring its accuracy on fixed samples.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a reward model, a dataset of N preference pairs (prompt x, preferred response y_w, rejected response y_l), a perturbation function P that generates a paired perturbed dataset D', a permutation count B, and an ordered significance level set α (default {0.05, 0.01, 0.001}). It outputs a suitability risk report r_S that pairs a paired-sample Cohen's d effect size with tiered significance markers (*, **, ***) derived from a count-based permutation p-value, plus the raw p-value and effect size. The paired structure is essential: each original sample and its perturbed counterpart share the same prompt, enabling sign-flipping permutation tests that eliminate inter-sample confounding.

## Method

Reward Auditor formulates suitability inference as a one-sided paired hypothesis test (H₀: E[M−M′]=0 vs. H₁: E[M−M′]>0) on the distribution of preference perception confidence differences ΔM_i = M_i − M_i'. It constructs the null distribution via random sign-flipping of the paired differences (s ~ U{−1,1}^N) and computes a count-based permutation p-value p̂=(c+1)/(B+1), which avoids the discreteness problem of finite permutations and requires no parametric distributional assumptions. Practical significance is captured by paired Cohen's d, and the two are combined into a single suitability risk report r_S = ê^{I*(p̂,α)} where the exponent is a tiered significance marker. For auditing across 10 perturbation scenarios (5 controlled prompt-side, 5 stylized response-side), a Group-aware Benjamini-Hochberg procedure with adaptive weights controls the false discovery rate while preserving statistical power. The paper empirically validates the non-parametric choice by showing that preference confidence difference distributions frequently violate normality (D'Agostino K² test) and that the permutation test's p-values correlate strongly (ρ≥0.85) with the Wilcoxon benchmark across all perturbations, unlike the paired t-test.

## Results

Auditing 26 RMs across 10 perturbation scenarios on the RM Bench Chat subset, 21 out of 26 RMs fail to maintain suitability under both controlled and stylized perturbations. Stylized perturbations (especially Synonym Transformation and Language Conversion) expose the most severe and widespread vulnerabilities, with effect sizes reaching up to 0.906 (SOLAR-10.7B under SP). In the downstream transferability study, the Spearman rank correlation between aggregated RM suitability risk and perturbed policy model win rate reaches −0.881, confirming that quantified suitability risk directly predicts alignment performance degradation. The paired permutation test maintains ρ ≥ 0.85 (p < 0.001) correlation with the Wilcoxon benchmark across all 10 perturbation conditions, while the paired t-test drops to ρ = 0.36 (EF) and ρ = −0.02 (SLC).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed paired permutation test against two baselines: the parametric paired t-test and the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test, using Spearman correlation of p-values as the agreement metric across 10 perturbation conditions. The paired permutation test decisively outperforms the t-test in robustness: it maintains ρ ≥ 0.85 (all p < 0.001) correlation with the Wilcoxon benchmark under every perturbation, while the t-test's correlation collapses to ρ = 0.36 (p = 0.115) under EF and ρ = −0.02 (p = 0.937) under SLC, indicating the t-test produces unreliable inferences on the non-normal confidence difference distributions (confirmed by D'Agostino K² tests showing widespread normality violations). The permutation test also proves robust to the choice of test statistic: p-values from mean-, t-test-, and trimmed-mean-based statistics all correlate at ρ ≥ 0.95 (p < 0.005), whereas the median-based statistic shows weaker agreement (ρ ≈ 0.79). In the downstream alignment transferability experiment, RMs with low suitability risk (as measured by the permutation-test-based framework) produce policy models whose win rates are nearly unaffected by perturbation, while high-risk RMs yield significantly degraded policy performance (Spearman ρ = −0.881), validating the framework's predictive utility over simple accuracy scoring.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant RM evaluation paradigm (RewardBench, PPE, RMB, RM-Bench, RewardBench 2) that measures static preference-perception accuracy on clean test sets, reframing the question from 'how accurate is the RM?' to 'can we statistically infer systematic vulnerabilities under real-world perturbations?' Methodologically, it builds on the Bradley-Terry model for confidence scoring, paired permutation tests (Good, 2013; Ojala & Garriga, 2010) for distribution-free inference, the count-based p-value correction of Phipson & Smyth (2010), and the structure-adaptive Benjamini-Hochberg procedure of Li & Barber (2019) for multiplicity control across perturbation groups. Conceptually, it adapts the 'Suitability Filter' framework of Pouget et al. (2025) from general classifier evaluation to the specific statistical structure of reward modeling, where paired original-perturbed samples enable high-power sign-flipping tests.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the first statistically rigorous tool for certifying (or rejecting) RM robustness before deployment, with the -0.881 Spearman correlation between suitability risk and downstream PPO alignment performance offering strong validation that the metric predicts real-world failure. Key limitations include reliance on continuous reward scores (incompatible with discrete LLM-as-a-judge outputs), text-only scope, and single-perturbation-at-a-time auditing that may miss compounding multi-perturbation effects. Open questions include extension to multimodal reward modeling, agentic/embodied settings with long-horizon trajectory perturbations, and whether the group-aware BH procedure's power gains hold when perturbation groups are highly correlated in practice.
