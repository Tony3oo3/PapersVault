## Problem

Video generative models cannot generalize to unconventional or creative camera motions (e.g., dolly zooms, search-and-focus pans, explosive shakes) because collecting sufficient real training videos with such rare movements is infeasible. This creates a fundamental paradox: generative models are data-hungry, yet the most artistically valuable camera motions are precisely those with the least available training data.

## Input / Output

At inference, the method accepts a text prompt describing scene content plus either a camera-motion text instruction (text-based variant) or an explicit 3D camera trajectory (trajectory-based variant), and produces a 5-second, 49-frame video at 720×480 resolution with the specified camera motion. Training data consists of synthetic low-poly 3D scenes rendered in Unity (~5 s per video) using only basic geometries (spheres, cubes, cylinders) and publicly available background/floor textures, requiring no manual scene design. A key detail is that the appearance LoRA trained on synthetic data is discarded at inference, so the output retains the visual prior of the base model rather than the virtual rendering style.

## Method

VIVIDCAM employs a two-stage dual-adaptation training scheme to disentangle camera motion from synthetic appearance. In Stage 1, an appearance LoRA is trained on static synthetic videos (no camera motion) to absorb the virtual rendering style; in Stage 2, a camera LoRA (text-based) or trajectory encoder (trajectory-based) is fine-tuned on synthetic videos with camera motion while the appearance LoRA remains frozen, so the motion module learns only motion. Two additional mechanisms strengthen disentanglement: a temporal derivative consistency loss (L_flow) that aligns frame-to-frame differences between predicted and ground-truth videos, providing appearance-invariant supervision, and style-aligned prompts ('In this low-poly <VIRTUAL> scene') appended during training to explicitly anchor the virtual appearance. At inference, the appearance LoRA is dropped, leaving only the camera-control module superimposed on the frozen base model (CogVideoX-5B or AC3D), which eliminates synthetic artifacts while preserving the learned motion patterns.

## Results

On automated camera pose precision (Table 2), VIVIDCAM-COG achieves the lowest TransErr on simple (0.1704) and composed (0.2208) motions, while VIVIDCAM-AC3D achieves the lowest TransErr (0.3376) and RotErr (0.3619) on complex motions. In the human study (Table 3), VIVIDCAM-COG attains the highest Action Correctness on simple (0.86) and composed (0.93) motions, and VIVIDCAM-AC3D leads on complex motions (0.81), all while maintaining Realism scores comparable to baselines (0.65–0.78). FVD values remain close to the vanilla base models (e.g., 1866 vs. 1488 for CogVideoX on complex motions), confirming that the synthetic-data training does not substantially degrade visual quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VIVIDCAM-COG (text-based) outperforms CameraCtrl, CogVideoX, and AC3D on TransErr for both simple and composed motions, with the largest margin over CameraCtrl (0.1704 vs. 0.3578 on simple TransErr). VIVIDCAM-AC3D (trajectory-based) dominates on complex motions, reducing TransErr from 0.4271 (AC3D) and 0.5753 (CameraCtrl) to 0.3376, and RotErr from 0.5864/0.7042 to 0.3619. In the human study, VIVIDCAM-COG achieves the best Action Correctness on simple (0.86) and composed (0.93) motions, while VIVIDCAM-AC3D leads on complex motions (0.81 vs. 0.68 for AC3D and 0.62 for CameraCtrl). CogVideoX retains the best RotErr on simple motions (0.0297), but this reflects the trivially small rotation in those motions; its TransErr (0.3113) and composed-motion Action Correctness (0.53) are substantially worse. FVD scores for both VIVIDCAM variants remain within ~10–20% of their respective base models, confirming that the disentanglement strategy preserves visual realism despite training exclusively on low-poly synthetic videos.

## Relation to Prior Work

VIVIDCAM extends the dual-adaptation (LoRA) training scheme from AnimateDiff, which was originally designed to bridge minor appearance gaps between image and video datasets, to the much more challenging setting of disentangling camera motion from the drastic visual domain gap between low-poly synthetic and realistic videos. For camera control, it builds on trajectory-based methods (AC3D, CameraCtrl) that condition on Plücker embeddings, and on text-based control via CogVideoX, but departs from prior synthetic-data approaches (Fu et al., Shuai et al.) that require high-quality, manually curated 3D scenes by showing that basic geometries in low-poly Unity scenes are sufficient. The temporal derivative consistency loss and style-aligned prompt are novel additions that go beyond what AnimateDiff's dual-adaptation provides.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: generating training data for arbitrary camera motions becomes a matter of writing a few lines of Unity code rather than collecting or curating large annotated real-video datasets, dramatically lowering the barrier to training camera-control models for unconventional motions. The finding that adding realistic data (RealEstate10K) does not improve and slightly degrades performance suggests the synthetic-data paradigm may be self-sufficient for motion learning. Limitations include the 49-frame temporal horizon inherited from the base model, inherited failures on highly dynamic scenes, and the need for two separate training stages; open questions include whether the approach scales to longer videos, other control modalities beyond camera, and whether the low-poly requirement can be further relaxed or made fully automated.
