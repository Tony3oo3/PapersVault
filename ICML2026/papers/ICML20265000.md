## Problem

Vision Transformers introduce structured, architecture-induced artifacts (patch-grid patterns from tokenization, attention-routing effects) that make gradient-based pixel-level attributions unstable and visually misleading. Existing methods either produce noisy pixel-level maps dominated by these artifacts or retreat to coarse patch-level explanations that lack fine-grained visual evidence, leaving a gap in obtaining stable, high-resolution, class-consistent attributions for ViTs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265000/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

DAVE takes a 224×224 RGB image and a target class label as input and produces a pixel-level attribution map at the model input resolution. It operates on frozen pretrained ViTs (no fine-tuning) and approximates the distribution-aware effective transformation via Monte Carlo sampling over small spatial transformations (rotations up to ±20°, translations up to 10% of image extent, horizontal flips) and Gaussian perturbations, requiring 50 samples by default. The final attribution is the element-wise product of the averaged, filtered effective transformation matrix with the input image, yielding a per-pixel importance score for the target class.

## Method

DAVE decomposes the ViT input gradient into an effective transformation (the direct, input-conditioned linear mapping from input to output) and an operator-variation term (the derivative of the input-dependent operator itself), then discards the latter to eliminate high-frequency instability. It then applies a Reynolds-inspired equivariant averaging over a compact group of small spatial transformations to suppress architecture-induced artifacts (e.g., patch-grid patterns) while preserving locally equivariant, class-relevant signal. A final low-pass filter, implemented as expectation under Gaussian perturbations (equivalent to convolution with a Gaussian kernel), further stabilizes the attribution. Practically, the effective transformation is computed via a single modified forward-backward pass per Monte Carlo sample, where operator dependencies (attention weights, GELU multipliers, LayerNorm statistics) are detached so that backpropagation yields only the effective transformation rather than the full Jacobian.

## Results

On ImageNet-1k, DAVE achieves the best GridPG localization across all four conventional ViT backbones: 60.19% (ViT-B/16), 63.52% (DeiT-B/16), 65.76% (DeiT-III-B/16), and 51.33% (DINO-B/16), with the largest gain of +11.92 pp over the best baseline on DeiT-III-B/16. On EnergyPG, DAVE leads on DeiT-B/16 (82.23%), DeiT-III-B/16 (82.43%), and DINO-B/16 (83.38%), though it trails C-LRP by 2.22 pp on ViT-B/16. In pixel-deletion faithfulness, DAVE produces the flattest curves (highest AUC) across all models, and in user studies with 120 participants, DAVE is preferred in 70–77% of pairwise comparisons and achieves 67.5% class-identification accuracy in the HIVE task versus 52.8% for the next-best method (AttnLRP).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DAVE outperforms all six gradient- and attention-based baselines on GridPG across every model, with the most substantial margins on DeiT-B/16 (+8.05 pp over AttnLRP) and DeiT-III-B/16 (+11.92 pp over AttnLRP). On EnergyPG, DAVE leads on DeiT-B/16 (+2.61 pp over Chefer-LRP), DeiT-III-B/16 (+0.49 pp over Chefer-LRP), and DINO-B/16 (+1.12 pp over LeGrad), but trails Chefer-LRP by 2.22 pp on ViT-B/16 (78.60 vs. 80.82). Among the baselines, Chefer-LRP and LeGrad are the strongest competitors on EnergyPG, while AttnLRP is the closest on GridPG for ViT-B/16 (58.40 vs. DAVE's 60.19). On B-cos models (Table 2), DAVE improves over the inherent B-cos explanations by +4.33 pp GridPG and +9.14 pp EnergyPG on B-cos-ViT, and by +0.77 pp and +4.02 pp on B-cos-ViT-C, confirming that DAVE adds value even on inherently interpretable architectures. In the DINOv2-with-registers setting (Appendix C.1), DAVE achieves 62.94% GridPG, a +24.97 pp margin over the best standard baseline (AttnLRP at 37.97%), while PCA-based patch-token baselines score at chance (25%). In user studies, DAVE is preferred over every baseline in 70–77% of pairwise trials and achieves 67.5% HIVE class-identification accuracy, well above AttnLRP (52.8%), IntGrad (52.4%), SmoothGrad (52.0%), LeGrad (47.3%), and Chefer-LRP (46.3%).

## Relation to Prior Work

DAVE builds on the B-cos network framework (Bohle et al., 2022, 2024), which treats ViT layers as input-dependent linear operators, and extends this by decomposing the full input gradient into an 'effective transformation' (the direct input-output mapping) and an 'operator variation' term (sensitivity of the operator itself to input changes). It departs from prior ViT-specific attribution methods (AttnLRP, Chefer-LRP, LeGrad) that address attention artifacts through redistribution rules or layer-wise gradient summation, by instead isolating a locally equivariant component of the transformation via a Reynolds-inspired averaging operator from representation theory. The low-pass stabilization step is analogous in form to SmoothGrad but applied to the filtered effective transformation rather than raw gradients, and the method is explicitly designed for ViT architecture rather than being architecture-agnostic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DAVE addresses a persistent practical gap: producing stable, pixel-level (not patch-level) attributions for ViTs that are both faithful and interpretable to humans, which prior methods have struggled with due to patch-grid artifacts and attention-induced noise. The mathematical decomposition provides a principled explanation for *why* standard gradient-based methods produce structured artifacts in ViTs (operator variation amplifies high-frequency components), rather than merely patching symptoms. Limitations include the Monte Carlo sampling overhead (though modest at ~2 GB for 50 steps), the assumption that attribution should be locally equivariant under small spatial transformations (which may not hold for all tasks), and evaluation limited to ImageNet-1k classification. Open questions include whether the equivariant averaging generalizes to video, 3D, or non-spatial token sequences, and whether the effective-transformation decomposition can inform training-time regularization for more interpretable models.
