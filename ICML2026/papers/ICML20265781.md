## Problem

In exemplar-free class-incremental learning, using a frozen text-to-image generator to synthesize old-class replay data introduces a structural asymmetry: old classes are exclusively synthetic while new classes are exclusively real. This asymmetry causes a 'Domain Shortcut' where the model exploits real-vs-synthetic domain cues instead of semantic class distinctions, leading real old-class test samples to be systematically misclassified as the current new task. Existing generative replay methods either require costly generator retraining or fail to address this domain-induced feature bias.

## Input / Output

At each incremental stage, the model consumes three data streams: real new-class images, synthetic old-class images generated by a frozen T2I model (Qwen-Image) conditioned on VLM-extracted attribute captions, and synthetic new-class images used for domain subspace estimation. The backbone is a ResNet-32 (32×32 or 64×64 inputs) or ResNet-18 (224×224 inputs), and the output is a standard class label over all seen classes. A key constraint is that no real old-class data or exemplars are stored; only text prompts in a Prompt Bank are retained across stages.

## Method

DREAM (Domain-Regularized Exemplar-free Alignment Model) eliminates the Domain Shortcut through two mechanisms. First, Feature Domain Rectification (FDR) estimates the shared domain subspace via a hierarchical SVD: per-class residuals between real samples and synthetic class centroids are decomposed (Micro-SVD), then aggregated across new classes (Macro-SVD) to yield a low-rank basis U_k; a soft orthogonal projection P = I − γU_kU_k^T (γ=0.8) suppresses domain directions while preserving entangled semantic information. Second, Real-Anchored Prototype Consolidation (RAPC) enforces cosine-similarity alignment of both synthetic and real features toward EMA-updated real-data prototypes, further closing residual domain gaps and tightening intra-class compactness. The theoretical foundation rests on two corollaries: the frozen generator's artifacts are class-invariant (shared subspace), and the domain bias is low-rank while semantic variance is isotropic, justifying SVD-based excision. Training combines cross-entropy on all three data streams, rectified feature distillation from the previous-stage teacher, and the prototype loss, with the projection matrix and prototypes refreshed once per epoch via a ping-pong caching mechanism.

## Results

DREAM achieves the best Task-Agnostic Average Incremental Accuracy (A_avg) among all exemplar-free methods across four benchmarks. On CIFAR-100 (T=10), DREAM reaches 69.73%, surpassing the strongest exemplar-free baseline SEED (62.04%) by 7.69% and the best generative replay method DiffClass (68.05%) by 1.68%. On ImageNet-Subset (T=20), DREAM attains 74.43%, outperforming SEED (62.26%) by 12.17% and R-DFCIL (49.99%) by 24.44%. On CIFAR-10 (T=5), DREAM achieves 88.98%, exceeding SEED (74.03%) by 14.95% and approaching the exemplar-based T-CIL+DER (74.93%) while using no stored exemplars.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DREAM consistently outperforms all exemplar-free baselines across every dataset and task split. Against the strongest exemplar-free method SEED, DREAM gains +14.95% on CIFAR-10 (T=5), +7.69% on CIFAR-100 (T=10), +12.17% on ImageNet-Subset (T=20), and +11.32% on Tiny-ImageNet (T=20). Among generative replay methods, DREAM surpasses DiffClass (the strongest prior generative replay) by +1.78% on CIFAR-100 (T=5), +1.68% on CIFAR-100 (T=10), and +1.92% on ImageNet-Subset (T=5), and outperforms R-DFCIL by margins of 10–24% depending on dataset and task count. Notably, DREAM's CIFAR-100 (T=10) result of 69.73% is within 0.25% of the exemplar-based T-CIL+DER (69.98%), demonstrating that training-free generative replay with domain rectification can match methods that store real exemplars. The ablation (Table 2) confirms that raw generative replay without DREAM drops to 33.27% on CIFAR-100 (T=10), a 36.46% gap, underscoring that the Domain Shortcut is the dominant failure mode rather than data quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of exemplar-free class-incremental learning (EFCIL) and generative replay by introducing a training-free paradigm: instead of training GANs or diffusion models per stage (as in Shin et al. 2017, Gao & Liu 2023, Meng et al. 2024), it uses a frozen text-to-image model (Qwen-Image) to synthesize old-class data from VLM-extracted prompts. The key conceptual departure is the identification of the 'Domain Shortcut'—a failure mode where the structural asymmetry (old classes = synthetic only, new classes = real only) causes the model to exploit domain-discriminative features over semantic class cues. The solution borrows geometric tools from subspace learning (SVD, orthogonal projection) and domain adaptation, but repurposes them specifically to excise the shared domain subspace estimated from paired real/synthetic new-class data, a setting not addressed by prior DA or CIL orthogonality methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of Domain Shortcut as the primary bottleneck (rather than synthetic data quality) reframes how the community should think about generative replay in CIL, and the 36-point improvement from a 5-dimensional soft projection on CIFAR-100 T=10 demonstrates that a lightweight geometric fix can unlock most of the performance lost to domain bias. The training-free approach also eliminates generator retraining costs and privacy concerns associated with storing exemplars, making it practically attractive for deployment. Limitations include reliance on the low-rank, class-shared assumption for domain artifacts (validated but not proven for all generators), sensitivity to the soft-projection strength γ, and evaluation restricted to image classification on four datasets. Open questions include whether the rectification generalizes to video or multimodal CIL, whether it composes with other EFCIL techniques (e.g., model expansion), and whether the domain subspace can be estimated from fewer samples in data-scarce regimes.
