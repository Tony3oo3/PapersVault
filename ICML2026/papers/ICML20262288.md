## Problem

Prior interpretability efforts on MoE expert routing have consistently failed to recover clear, human-understandable specialisation patterns, largely because they assume each expert handles a single coherent domain (the Domain Specialisation Hypothesis). This paper identifies why that assumption is structurally untenable—real data contains far more fine-grained categories than any MoE layer has experts—and proposes and tests the Superposed Specialisation Hypothesis (SSH): experts specialise in disjoint unions of fine-grained features spanning multiple unrelated micro-domains, mirroring the superposition phenomenon in neural network representations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes residual stream activations (pre-routing) from MoE layers, decomposed via trained Top-K or BatchTopK SAEs into sparse latent feature vectors, alongside token contexts used for explanation generation and evaluation. It produces a two-level explanation hierarchy: individual SAE feature descriptions (generated via AutoInterp on decile-sampled activating examples) aggregated into a single natural-language paragraph per expert. Evaluation is made more discriminative by constructing hard negatives—semantically similar contexts that do not route to the target expert but contain tokens routed to a feature-sharing expert—rather than random negatives.

## Method

RouterInterp operates in three stages: (1) selecting the top-n SAE latents most predictive of each expert's routing via the ρ-usefulness metric (the difference in expected feature activation conditional on the expert being in the router's top-k), (2) generating concise natural-language explanations for each selected feature using the AutoInterp pipeline with decile-sampled activating examples, and (3) prompting an LLM to synthesise all n feature explanations into a single coherent expert-level paragraph. The SAE basis is specifically motivated by the fact that MoE routing is a linear operation (W_r · x), so only linearly accessible information can drive routing, making SAE features the natural decomposition. The paper provides two theoretical arguments for SSH—interference minimisation (dissimilar domains can share an expert with low co-activation, enabling computation in superposition) and load-balancing pressure (small batch sizes force the router to distribute tokens from few macro-domains across all experts)—and empirical evidence that each expert's top routing-predictive latents occupy ~7–8 semantically disjoint clusters rather than one coherent domain, with SAE-based classifiers achieving 0.73–0.74 macro-F1 in routing prediction versus 0.30–0.63 for token-level and sparse-probe baselines.

## Results

SAE-based classifiers predict MoE expert routing with 0.740 macro-F1 on OLMoE-1B-7B (layer 11) and 0.729 on gpt-oss-20b (layer 12), substantially outperforming token-level n-gram baselines (0.564/0.295 unigram, 0.633/0.355 bigram) and sparse linear probes on neuron (0.666/0.586) or PCA (0.680/0.536) bases at matched sparsity. For explanation quality, RouterInterp achieves a 0.61 explanation score (macro-F1) on gpt-oss-20b layer 20, outperforming bigram-based AutoInterp (0.40) by ~57% and Expert Activations AutoInterp (0.56) at four of five layers. Empirical evidence for the Superposed Specialisation Hypothesis shows mean G(E_i) of 7.8/10 on gpt-oss-20b and 7.1/10 on OLMoE-1B-7B, far from the DSH prediction of G=1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The SAE predictor dominates all baselines on routing prediction: it beats the bigram baseline by 0.107 (OLMoE) and 0.374 (gpt-oss) macro-F1, and outperforms the best sparse linear probe (neuron basis) by 0.074 and 0.143 respectively, confirming the gain is specific to the learned SAE basis rather than any sparse readout of the residual stream. For explanation quality on gpt-oss-20b, RouterInterp (s=128) outperforms Bigram AutoInterp at every layer (e.g., 0.61 vs. 0.40 at layer 20, a ~57% relative improvement) and beats the stronger Expert Activations AutoInterp baseline at four of five layers (0.59 vs. 0.51 at layer 12; the sole exception is layer 16 where the SAE has the highest FVU). The SAE-basis advantage is robust across depth and sparsity: at s=128, the SAE predictor achieves strictly higher macro-F1 than the best neuron or PCA probe at four of five gpt-oss layers. Causal intervention experiments further confirm that top-ρ SAE features are not merely correlated with routing but causally drive it: positive steering increases expert activation by +13.5 pp and negative steering decreases it by −40.9 pp, with gaps of +12.3 pp and −29.4 pp over random-feature controls.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SAE-based interpretability pipeline (Bricken et al., 2023; Paulo et al., 2025) to the previously under-studied problem of MoE expert routing, using ρ-usefulness (Ilyas et al., 2019) for feature selection and AutoInterp for explanation generation. It departs from prior MoE interpretability efforts (Jiang et al., 2024; Lewis et al., 2021; Zoph et al., 2022) that relied on token co-occurrence statistics and found no clear domain patterns, by reframing the question through the Superposed Specialisation Hypothesis: experts specialise in disjoint collections of fine-grained features rather than a single coherent domain. The conceptual link to the superposition literature (Elhage et al., 2022) is extended from polysemantic neurons to polysemantic experts, and the load-balancing argument provides a training-dynamics explanation for why SSH should emerge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The SSH finding challenges the widely-held narrative that MoE experts specialise in interpretable domains, with implications for how we design, debug, and trust MoE-based frontier systems. RouterInterp provides a practical, scalable pipeline for generating routing explanations, but its performance is bounded by SAE reconstruction quality (FVU) and evaluation is limited to two models in the 1B–20B range. Open questions include whether experts cluster by functional similarity rather than input similarity, how routing specialisation evolves across training checkpoints, and whether the method generalises to frontier-scale architectures with shared experts and different routing schemes.
