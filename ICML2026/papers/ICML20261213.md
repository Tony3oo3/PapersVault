## Problem

Neural-network variational Monte Carlo methods for excited states scale superlinearly (up to O(N_s^4)) with the number of states due to two compounding issues: estimating pairwise wave-function overlaps requires proportionally more Monte Carlo samples, and representing many states demands either separate networks or prohibitively large architectures. No prior method could jointly generalize across molecular structures, chemical compositions, and electronic states within a single model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261213/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes nuclear positions R, nuclear charges Z, and a target state index s as input, producing antisymmetric many-electron wave functions for all N_s states simultaneously from a single shared network. The state index s acts only as a lightweight selector into a shared orbital backbone rather than indexing separate networks, so adding states incurs near-zero additional forward-pass cost. Outputs include excited-state energies, potential energy surfaces across multiple geometries, and the wave functions themselves, which can be evaluated at arbitrary nuclear configurations for generalization.

## Method

The paper introduces two complementary contributions. First, Multi-State Importance Sampling (MSIS) pools samples from all states into a mixture distribution ρ_mix to estimate pairwise overlaps, reducing the estimator variance from O(N_s/N_b) to O(1/N_b) and bounding the integrand to eliminate heavy-tailed outliers near wave-function nodes; normalizer ratios are estimated via iterative multi-distribution bridge sampling. Second, the Excited Pfaffian architecture generalizes Hartree-Fock's orbital selector concept into the Neural Pfaffian framework: a single shared backbone produces orbitals Φ, and each state s is distinguished only by a lightweight skew-symmetric antisymmetrizer A_sk, exploiting the identity Pf(Φ A Φ^T) to encode orthogonal determinants without duplicating the network. A spin-state snapping loss dynamically snaps wave functions to the nearest S(S+1) eigenvalue without requiring a priori spin knowledge, and a pretraining scheme propagates Hartree-Fock orbitals across a structure DAG via basis projection and Procrustes alignment to provide smooth, consistent initialization.

## Results

On the carbon dimer PES, Excited Pfaffian computes 12 states (vs. 8 for prior neural VMC methods) using only 800M samples, matching the SHCI reference baseline with 40× fewer samples than NES (32B) and 4× fewer than Schätzle et al. (3.2B). On beryllium, the method recovers all 33 states (ground + 32 excited) with errors < 0.5 mHa against NIST, the first neural-network VMC computation of the full excitation spectrum. Across second-row atoms (Li–Ne), all 10 low-lying states are captured with chemical accuracy (< 1.6 mHa vs. NIST), and on 12 molecules the method matches QUEST theoretical best estimates while finding states missed by prior approaches. The computational scaling is near-constant at O(N_s^0.14) versus O(N_s^4) for NES, yielding >200× per-step speedups on C₂ and >300× less total compute for the 33-state Be calculation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NES (Pfau et al., 2024), Excited Pfaffian matches accuracy on the C₂ PES while using 40× fewer samples (800M vs. 32B) and 200× less compute, while modeling 12 states instead of 8; for the 33-state beryllium calculation, the compute reduction exceeds 300×. Compared to Szabo et al. (2024), the joint multi-state training reduces total samples by 20–80× on atoms and ~15× on molecules, and additionally discovers excited states on CH₂O and HNO that prior per-spin-sector methods miss. Against Schätzle et al. (2025) on the C₂ PES, Excited Pfaffian uses 4× fewer samples and tracks the SHCI reference more faithfully at stretched geometries (>1.6 Å), where Schätzle's method exhibits state-tracking failures in both singlet and triplet sectors. Versus the SHCI reference (Holmes et al., 2017), Excited Pfaffian achieves comparable accuracy across all 12 C₂ states, with only a single state swap near 1.6 Å reflecting a narrow avoided crossing. Against NIST experimental data, all 33 Be states are recovered with < 0.5 mHa error and all 10 atomic states for Li–Ne with < 1.6 mHa. Finally, compared to a ground-state-only Neural Pfaffian (Gao & Günnemann, 2024) on C₂ near the 1.3 Å crossing, Excited Pfaffian correctly tracks individual electronic states through the crossing and yields the true ground-state energy at all geometries, whereas the ground-state-only model becomes trapped on the wrong adiabatic branch.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Neural Pfaffian architecture (Gao & Günnemann, 2024) to excited states by sharing orbital parameters across states and varying only a lightweight antisymmetrizer matrix, directly inspired by Hartree-Fock orbital selectors. It integrates with the generalized wave function framework (Gao & Günnemann, 2023a) to jointly generalize across molecular structures and electronic states for the first time. The Multi-State Importance Sampling estimator draws on the balance heuristic from Monte Carlo rendering (Veach & Guibas, 1995) to pool samples across states, departing from the standard practice of scaling batch size linearly with the number of states (Entwistle et al., 2023; Szabo et al., 2024). The spin-state snapping loss builds on the S₊ penalty of Li et al. (2024b) but removes the need for a priori knowledge of target spin sectors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-constant scaling O(N_s^0.14) versus O(N_s^4) for NES makes previously infeasible computations tractable, demonstrated by the first neural-network computation of all 32 excited states of beryllium and 12-state carbon dimer PES at 200× lower cost. The first cross-molecule generalization for excited states opens the door to photochemical simulations and excited-state force fields, though accuracy degrades for structurally distinct compounds and state continuity remains challenging near conical intersections. Open questions include whether the pretraining scheme can guarantee state tracking across large geometric changes, whether the spin-snapping loss can be made more robust against early convergence to wrong spin sectors, and whether lossless generalization across chemically diverse compounds is achievable.
