## Problem

Existing inference-time steering methods for LLMs (ITI, SteerConf, CCPS) optimize proxies for correctness—such as verbalized confidence or perturbation stability—rather than correctness itself, and none demonstrates transferability to out-of-distribution benchmarks. Meanwhile, miscalibration persists across post-training pipelines (RLHF, DPO), and retraining to fix it is expensive. CORAL addresses both gaps by directly predicting the correctness gap from internal activations and showing that the learned steering direction generalizes without retraining.

## Input / Output

For each MCQA question with n options, CORAL performs n forward passes through a frozen LLM, extracting mean-pooled hidden states over answer tokens at a single chosen layer (typically layers 17–21 for 30-layer models), z-score normalized to 4096-dimensional vectors. The probe outputs a bounded residual prediction in [-1,1] per option, which is centered to zero-sum and applied as an additive correction to the base softmax probabilities, renormalized to a valid distribution. The entire inference overhead is ~20 ms per question (0.3% of the forward pass) on a single RTX 2080 Ti.

## Method

CORAL trains a 4-layer MLP (1024→512→256→128, ReLU, dropout 0.2, tanh output) with weight decay to predict residual correctness r_j = 1−p_j (correct) or −p_j (incorrect) from frozen residual stream activations, using a loss that combines MSE with an L2 output penalty to directly minimize the Brier score. At inference, the probe's per-option predictions are centered to ensure zero-sum, then scaled by γ (optimal at 1.0) and added to base probabilities before renormalization. The key insight, validated by SAE ablations showing negligible per-feature causal effects, is that correctness signals are distributed across many activation dimensions and require supervised nonlinear aggregation rather than sparse feature selection. Probes trained on only 8.4k–10k questions from CommonsenseQA/RACE or MMLU transfer without retraining to four held-out benchmarks (ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, Math-MC, OpenBookQA), averaging 14% accuracy and 49% ECE improvements.

## Results

CORAL achieves consistent gains across three 7B models (Mistral-7B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, DeepSeek-7B-Chat) on in-distribution benchmarks (CommonsenseQA, MMLU, RACE), improving accuracy by ~10% and ECE by ~50% on average. The largest single-benchmark gain is on RACE with DeepSeek-7B-Chat, where CORAL reaches 73.06% accuracy (vs. 55.67% for the next-best method) and 2.62% ECE (vs. 3.00% for CCPS). On transfer to four held-out benchmarks (HellaSwag, OpenBookQA, ARC-Challenge, Math-MC), CORAL averages 14% accuracy improvements and 49% ECE reductions without any retraining. The optimal steering strength γ=1.0 is consistent across all model families, and middle layers (17–21) contain the strongest correctness signals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CORAL outperforms all four baselines on accuracy across nearly every model–benchmark combination. Against SteerConf, CORAL gains 13–34 percentage points in accuracy (e.g., 74.93% vs. 40.83% on MMLU/Mistral), as SteerConf's prompt-based confidence elicitation fails to improve factual correctness. Against Few-Shot prompting, CORAL improves accuracy by 10–17 points (e.g., 73.06% vs. 55.67% on RACE/DeepSeek) while also reducing ECE. Against ITI, CORAL wins on both accuracy and calibration in most settings; ITI's attention-head steering sometimes matches accuracy but produces substantially worse ECE (e.g., 16.56% vs. 2.62% on RACE/DeepSeek). Against CCPS, CORAL achieves higher accuracy in most cases (e.g., 74.93% vs. 60.56% on MMLU/Mistral) and, critically, transfers far more effectively to held-out benchmarks—CCPS underperforms even Few-Shot prompting on most transfer tasks, while CORAL maintains its gains. The one setting where CORAL does not dominate is OpenBookQA NLL for Mistral, where ITI achieves a slightly lower NLL (60.50 vs. 69.57), though CORAL still leads on accuracy, ECE, cwECE, and Brier.

## Relation to Prior Work

CORAL synthesizes three research threads—activation probing (Alain & Bengio, 2017; Geva et al., 2021), inference-time steering (ITI, Li et al., 2023; contrastive activation addition, Rimsky et al., 2024), and LLM calibration (Guo et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2021)—into a unified framework that directly targets correctness rather than behavioral proxies. It departs from the sparse-feature steering paradigm (SAE-based methods like CorrSteer) by demonstrating that correctness signals are distributed and require supervised MLP aggregation, aligning with recent mechanistic findings (Lindsey et al., 2025; Conmy et al., 2023). The residual correctness formulation (r = y − p) is a novel training target that unifies accuracy and calibration under the Brier score, distinguishing CORAL from prior methods that optimize confidence elicitation (SteerConf) or perturbation stability (CCPS) as indirect proxies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CORAL offers a practically deployable calibration tool: 5 hours of single-GPU training, 0.3% inference overhead, and transfer to unseen benchmarks without retraining, making it attractive for MCQA deployment where retraining is infeasible. The SAE ablation providing a clean negative result for sparse-feature steering on correctness is useful for the interpretability community, reinforcing that distributed signals need supervised aggregation. However, the evaluation is confined to MCQA, leaving open whether the learned correctness subspace generalizes to free-form generation, multi-hop retrieval, or non-English settings—key questions for broader adoption. The paper also raises safety concerns: the same transferable steering vectors could be repurposed to push models toward misinformation.
