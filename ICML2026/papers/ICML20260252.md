## Problem

Existing LLM-based scientific equation discovery methods treat LLMs as static hypothesis generators whose parameters remain fixed regardless of the target system, and provide only coarse scalar feedback (e.g., MSE) that reveals which hypotheses succeed but not why specific mathematical components drive success. This leads to inefficient exploration of vast combinatorial hypothesis spaces and over-reliance on the LLM's internal priors rather than data-driven adaptation to the observed system.

## Input / Output

The method takes a scientific dataset of input-output pairs (x_i, y_i), a task description, and an open-source LLM backbone (1B–8B parameter models). Equations are represented as executable Python function skeletons with placeholder parameters, which are optimized via BFGS on the training split before scoring. The output is a discovered symbolic equation (Python program) evaluated by normalized MSE on held-out in-domain and out-of-domain test splits. A distinctive aspect is that intermediate hypotheses are annotated with term-level ablation contribution scores as inline comments, which are then fed back as structured in-context examples to the LLM in subsequent iterations.

## Method

DecAEvolve unifies three mechanisms in a feedback-driven loop. First, **Decomposition** parses each candidate equation into an AST, splits at additive nodes into atomic symbolic terms, and computes single-term and pairwise contribution scores via structural ablation (removing terms, re-optimizing remaining parameters with BFGS, and measuring the score delta); these contributions are serialized as inline comments in the program. Second, **Adaptation** employs GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) for per-system test-time fine-tuning using LoRA adapters, where the reward is exp(−MSE) computed on the observed data, implicitly distilling the data distribution into model parameters without exposing raw data. Third, **Evolutionary search** uses a multi-island buffer where decomposition-annotated programs serve as in-context few-shot examples, guiding the adapted LLM to generate increasingly refined hypotheses. The key architectural insight is that decomposition feedback simultaneously shapes both the evolutionary prompt construction and the GRPO training prompts, creating a tight coupling between fine-grained structural understanding and policy adaptation that neither mechanism achieves alone.

## Results

On the four primary benchmarks (Nonlinear Oscillator 1 & 2, Bacterial Growth, Stress-Strain), DecAEvolve with Qwen2.5-7B achieves the best NMSE across all settings: 1.25e-6 (ID) / 1.51e-5 (OOD) on Oscillator 1, 8.06e-7 (ID) / 1.64e-5 (OOD) on Oscillator 2, 0.0007 (ID) / 0.0012 (OOD) on E. coli growth, and 0.0198 (ID) / 0.0322 (OOD) on Stress-Strain. Compared to the same-backbone LLM-SR baseline, DecAEvolve reduces NMSE by up to an order of magnitude (e.g., 1.25e-6 vs. 1.33e-5 on Oscillator 1 ID, a ~10× improvement). On the 10-problem LLM-SRBench Chemistry subset, DecAEvolve (Llama-3.1-8B) achieves the best OOD NMSE on 9/10 datasets and best ID NMSE on 8/10, improving over LLM-SR by one to several orders of magnitude.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DecAEvolve consistently outperforms all non-LLM symbolic regression baselines (PySR, uDSR, SINDy, DSR, E2E, NeSymReS) across nearly all dataset splits, with the sole exception being SINDy on Oscillator 2 (NMSE 4.6e-16 ID), where SINDy's linear-in-basis-functions assumption matches the problem structure perfectly. Against the primary LLM-based baseline LLM-SR, DecAEvolve wins on every dataset and backbone combination tested: with Llama-3.1-8B, it improves Oscillator 1 ID NMSE from 1.36e-5 to 1.37e-6 (~10×), E. coli OOD from 0.0240 to 0.0045 (~5×), and Stress-Strain OOD from 0.0761 to 0.0322 (~2.4×). On the LLM-SRBench Chemistry evaluation, DecAEvolve (Llama-3.1-8B) dominates both SGA and LaSR by orders of magnitude on most problems (e.g., CRK12: 3.3e-10 ID vs. 3.2e-5 for LLM-SR and 6.5e-3 for SGA), with the single exception of CRK22 where the highly nonlinear coupled kinetics exceed the n=1000 search budget. Notably, DecAEvolve with small open-source models (1B–3B) achieves results competitive with or better than LLM-SR using much larger closed-source models (Mixtral, GPT-3.5), demonstrating that test-time adaptation can bridge the capability gap between model scales.

## Relation to Prior Work

DecAEvolve extends LLM-SR (Shojaee et al., 2025a), which reframed symbolic regression as LLM-guided program synthesis with multi-island evolutionary search, by addressing two limitations the authors identify in all prior LLM-based SR methods: static hypothesis generation (fixed model parameters regardless of the target system) and coarse scalar-only feedback. The key conceptual departure is the integration of test-time RL adaptation via GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) — repurposed here not for global reasoning fine-tuning but for per-system alignment of the LLM's generation policy — combined with AST-based term-level ablation that provides interpretable directional feedback about which symbolic components drive accuracy. This positions DecAEvolve as a hybrid of evolutionary search, reinforcement learning, and symbolic decomposition, rather than a pure prompt-engineering or evolutionary approach.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication that 1B–8B open-source models with targeted test-time adaptation can match or exceed much larger closed-source models (GPT-3.5-turbo, Mixtral) on equation discovery is significant for democratizing scientific discovery tools without requiring frontier-scale compute. However, the framework introduces non-trivial overhead (~39% from decomposition re-optimization plus GRPO fine-tuning cost), making it best suited for settings where solution quality and generalization outweigh wall-clock time. Open questions include whether the decomposition mechanism generalizes to highly correlated or non-additive equation structures, whether the GRPO adaptation transfers across related scientific systems, and how the approach scales to the 100+ problem LLM-SRBench suite where per-problem training becomes prohibitive.
