## Problem

A common intuition in ML suggests that reweighting training data toward a uniform distribution over skills would help models learn rare long-tail knowledge more efficiently. However, for compositional reasoning tasks (multi-step arithmetic, state tracking, multi-hop QA), this intuition is wrong: training under a power-law distribution consistently outperforms uniform training, even when test accuracy is measured under uniform distribution. The paper asks why this asymmetry helps and provides both a theoretical separation and a mechanistic explanation.

## Input / Output

The model receives a sequence of k atomic 'skills' (e.g., arithmetic operations like +3 or ×4, group elements from S_5, or relational hops in a QA graph) and must output the composed result (integer, group element, or entity) directly without chain-of-thought. The critical design choice is the training distribution over these k skills: either uniform or power-law (p_j ∝ j^{-α}), while the test distribution is always uniform. A 0.6B-parameter Qwen3-style transformer is trained from scratch, so no prior numerical or algebraic knowledge is present at initialization.

## Method

The paper introduces a minimalist k-multiplicative composition task where d skills each carry a hidden scalar w_i* ∈ {−1,+1} and the target is the product of k independently sampled skills' hidden scalars. A correlational statistical query (CSQ) lower bound proves that under uniform distribution, any gradient-based learner requires d^{Ω(k)} samples or runtime, while under a power-law distribution (α > 1), minibatch gradient descent recovers the ground truth in Õ(d^{2α}) samples and Õ(d^α) time. The key mechanism is that power-law sampling makes the initial population gradient norm Θ(1) (via the weighted similarity A(0) = Σ p_i w_i w_i*), creating a steeper loss landscape that enables escape from the flat initialization region, whereas uniform sampling yields an initial gradient of only d^{−Ω(k)}. This triggers a three-stage learning dynamic: (I) power-law enables escape from the flat region, (II) high-frequency 'head' skills are learned first and their growth in A(t) amplifies the gradient signal for rare 'tail' skills, and (III) final convergence is slowed by the low sampling probability of tail skills. The theory is validated empirically on S_5 state tracking (where uniform training fails entirely without curriculum or CoT, but power-law training succeeds), multi-hop QA, and synthetic grade-school math, with loss landscape visualizations and per-skill-bin gradient norm analyses confirming the predicted stage-wise dynamics.

## Results

The paper establishes a theoretical separation: under uniform distribution, learning k-multiplicative composition requires d^Ω(k) samples (CSQ lower bound), while under power-law (α>1), gradient descent achieves the task with only Õ(d^{2α}) samples and Õ(d^α/η · log(1/ε)) time. Empirically, on S_5 state tracking (k=4), power-law (α=1.5) enables near-perfect learning without curriculum or CoT, whereas uniform distribution fails entirely. On multi-step arithmetic (k=4, 0.6B Qwen3-style model), switching from uniform to power-law (α=1.0) yields significant test accuracy improvement. On multi-hop QA and synthetic iGSM, power-law training consistently converges faster with fewer samples across all entity counts and hop numbers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between power-law and uniform training distributions on identical architectures and tasks. On S_5 state tracking (k=4), power-law (α=1.5) achieves near-perfect test accuracy while uniform distribution cannot learn the task at all without intermediate supervision (curriculum or CoT) — a qualitative but decisive separation. On multi-step arithmetic (k=4), power-law (α=1.0) consistently outperforms uniform sampling in both training speed and final test accuracy measured under uniform test distribution. On multi-hop QA, power-law training learns faster across all combinations of entity count |E| and hop number k, reducing the exponential data requirement observed under uniform sampling (Yao et al., 2025a). On synthetic iGSM, power-law outperforms uniform regardless of whether modular arithmetic is used. The paper also shows that power-law is compatible with explicit curriculum (k=1→4), further reducing loss plateaus compared to uniform+curriculum, and that the benefit is robust to random skill ordering (ruling out easy-to-hard ordering as the sole explanation).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the CSQ lower bound framework of Szörényi (2009) and the parity/PolyNet model of Barak et al. (2022) to introduce a k-multiplicative composition task, then shows that the uniform-distribution assumption underlying prior hardness results for compositional reasoning (parity, k-hop state tracking) is the critical ingredient that can be broken. It generalizes earlier results showing that biased distributions help learn parity (Daniely & Malach, 2020; Cornacchia & Mossel, 2023) and symmetry-breaking for single-index models (Cornacchia et al., 2025) to the compositional setting, where the natural power-law (Zipf) distribution of skills provides the asymmetry. The empirical tasks draw on established benchmarks: S5 state tracking (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023b), multi-hop QA (Yao et al., 2025a), and synthetic grade-school math (Ye et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the widely-held intuition that reweighting training data toward uniformity improves long-tail skill acquisition, showing instead that the asymmetry of power-law is precisely what enables efficient compositional learning — a simple data-distribution change that requires no architectural modification, CoT, or curriculum. The theoretical separation (d^Ω(k) vs. d^O(1) sample complexity) is clean and the mechanistic three-stage learning picture (escape flat region → head skills bootstrap tail → long-tail convergence) is verified across multiple task families. Key limitations include the reliance on synthetic/algorithmic tasks and small (0.6B) models in pretraining settings; whether the effect transfers to real-world skill composition in large-scale LLM training, and the optimal granularity and exponent of the asymmetry, remain open questions.
