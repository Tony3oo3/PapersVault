## Problem

Existing quantum boosting algorithms either scale worse in the weak-learner strength γ than their classical counterparts or require maintaining an explicit m-dimensional weight vector at each iteration. No prior boosting method, classical or quantum, had adopted a lazy projection strategy to reduce per-iteration cost, and none achieved a runtime dependence on 1/ε better than 1/ε.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a labeled training set S = {(x_i, y_i)}_{i=1}^m stored in a quantum read-only classical memory (QCROM), a γ-weak learner oracle W (quantum or classical) with runtime W, and parameters γ ∈ (0,1/2) and ε ∈ (0,1). It outputs a strong hypothesis H(x) = MAJ(h_1(x), …, h_T(x)) with empirical error < ε and generalization error ≤ ε for sufficiently large m. Notably, the m-dimensional weight vector is never explicitly stored; only the names of the T weak hypotheses and the O(T/K) projection constants are kept in classical memory, with individual weight entries computed on the fly in O(t) time at iteration t.

## Method

QuantumBoost quantizes Kale's SmoothBoost by combining two innovations: (1) a quantum subroutine that computes approximate Bregman projections onto the set of high-density measures Γ_ε using amplitude-estimation-based mean estimation, achieving Õ(t/(√ε·ζ)) runtime per projection instead of the classical O(t/(ε·ζ²)); and (2) a lazy projection strategy that enforces the high-density constraint only every K = 1/γ iterations rather than every iteration, reducing the number of expensive projection calls from T to T/K. The weight vector M^t is maintained implicitly through the recursive structure of multiplicative updates and projection constants, enabling quantum state preparation of weak-learner examples via amplitude amplification in Õ(1/√ε) operations per example. Convergence in T = O(log(1/ε)/γ²) iterations is proven by bounding the accumulated relative-entropy error from approximate projections (with precision ζ = γ/4) against the per-iteration progress from the multiplicative update rule, yielding an overall runtime of Õ(W/(√ε·γ⁴)) with no explicit dependence on m or the VC-dimension d.

## Results

QuantumBoost achieves an overall runtime of Õ(W/(√ε·γ⁴)) with T = O(log(1/ε)/γ²) iterations, where W is the weak learner's runtime. This is the first boosting algorithm (classical or quantum) to improve the runtime dependence on 1/ε to Õ(1/√ε), while matching AdaBoost's best-known γ⁴ scaling. The algorithm removes all explicit dependence on the number of training examples m and the VC-dimension d, which all prior boosting methods required.

## Baselines & Comparisons

QuantumBoost strictly dominates all five prior boosting algorithms on the ε-dependence: it achieves 1/√ε versus 1/ε for AdaBoost and Kale's SmoothBoost, 1/ε^2.5 for Quantum SmoothBoost, and 1/ε² for classical SmoothBoost. On the γ-dependence, QuantumBoost matches AdaBoost's best-known γ⁴ scaling, improving over Quantum AdaBoost (γ^11), Kale's SmoothBoost (γ⁶), and Quantum SmoothBoost (γ⁴ but with worse ε). On iteration count, QuantumBoost uses O(log(1/ε)/γ²) iterations, matching AdaBoost and Kale's SmoothBoost but improving over SmoothBoost and Quantum SmoothBoost which require 1/(εγ²) iterations. Crucially, QuantumBoost is the only method with no explicit dependence on m or d, whereas all baselines carry at least a √d or d factor. The lazy projection strategy (projecting every K = 1/γ iterations rather than every iteration) is what enables the improved ε-scaling without sacrificing the iteration count.

## Relation to Prior Work

QuantumBoost quantizes Kale's SmoothBoost (2007) and incorporates Barak, Hardt, and Kale's (2009) implicit representation of approximate Bregman projections, which avoids explicitly verifying smoothness at each iteration. It also reuses the quantum state-preparation subroutine from Izdebski and de Wolf (2023) to generate quantum examples for the weak learner. The key departure from all prior boosting work—classical or quantum—is the adoption of a lazy projection strategy (projecting onto the high-density set only every K iterations rather than every iteration), a technique borrowed from convex optimization that had not previously been applied in a boosting context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QuantumBoost is the first boosting algorithm (classical or quantum) to achieve a Õ(1/√ε) runtime dependence on the target error while matching AdaBoost's 1/γ² iteration count and removing the explicit m-dependence, making it the most efficient known boosting method in terms of runtime scaling. The lazy projection idea, while natural in convex optimization, required a non-trivial proof technique (switching from KL-divergence to relative entropy analysis) to establish convergence, and its transfer to boosting opens a new design axis for future boosters. Open questions include whether the 1/γ⁴ dependence can be improved, whether the algorithm extends to agnostic learning, and whether the sample complexity gap (an extra 1/ε factor over the optimal Õ(d/(γ²ε))) can be closed.
