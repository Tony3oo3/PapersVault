## Problem

Existing distribution shift adjustment methods for language models (importance weighting, doubly robust estimation) account only for shifts over variables observable in the text representation h(X), ignoring unobservable components such as external annotator attributes or information lost when compressing raw text into fixed-dimensional embeddings. This unobserved component introduces omitted variable bias that makes standard doubly robust evaluation overly optimistic and yields models that remain brittle despite adjustment for observable shift. The paper asks how to bound and mitigate this OVB when the omitted variables are fundamentally unidentifiable from the data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a labeled source dataset D_P = {(X_i, Y_i)}, an unlabeled target dataset D_Q = {X_j}, a language model f, and user-specified sensitivity parameters (C_Y, C_D, ρ) that characterize the strength of omitted variables. Texts are reduced to a representation h(X) ∈ R^d (e.g., LIWC features, transformer embeddings), and the nuisance models g and α are learned only over h(X), not the full text or external covariates Z. The method produces three outputs: a worst-case generalization performance bound, a model optimized to minimize that bound, and—when target labels are available—an empirical upper bound on the sensitivity parameters that quantifies how much explanatory power the omitted variables carry for a given model.

## Method

The core contribution is a DRO-based worst-case generalization bound that extends Chernozhukov et al.'s (2024) nonparametric OVB bounds to language model evaluation. The OVB of the short doubly robust objective is bounded as |L_DR_s − L_DR|² ≤ ρ² C_Y² C_D² σ² ν², where σ² (fidelity) and ν² (overlap) are identifiable from data, while C_Y, C_D, and ρ are sensitivity parameters characterizing the explanatory power and confounding of omitted variables. These parameters define an uncertainty set of plausible target distributions, and the worst-case bound L_DR + ρ^max C_Y^max C_D^max σν can be used for robust evaluation, direct optimization, or (with target labels) inference of omitted-variable strength. For GLM log-likelihood losses (regression, binary/multiclass classification, text generation), the paper restructures the doubly robust objective so the outcome model predicts the label Y rather than the loss ℓ itself, eliminating the need to retrain a loss-predictor at every optimization step and making the approach computationally tractable for standard NLP tasks.

## Results

On MATH-Perturb, the standard doubly robust (DR) objective yields overly optimistic performance estimates relative to true test performance for both MATH-P-Simple and MATH-P-Hard, with the gap being larger for MATH-P-Hard where underlying logic changes. On the semi-synthetic Amazon dataset (100 iterations with randomly omitted features), the worst-case generalization bound increasingly outperforms the DR baseline as the true product of sensitivity parameters (ρC_Y C_D) grows. On real-world datasets, the performance gain over DR peaks at ρC_Y C_D ≈ 0.8 for EmoBank and ρC_Y C_D ≈ 0.06 for Hate Speech, after which overly pessimistic bounds degrade performance. On Wild-Time (2012→2013–2018), the worst-case objective maintains a consistent advantage over DR as temporal drift accumulates, while both DR and unadjusted baselines show worsening error over time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: the standard doubly robust (DR) objective that adjusts only for observed distribution shift, and an unadjusted model with no distribution shift correction. The worst-case generalization bound consistently outperforms DR on all five datasets when sensitivity parameters are set at or near their benchmarked values, with the margin growing as the true strength of omitted variables increases (Amazon) or as temporal drift accumulates (Wild-Time). On MATH-Perturb, the DR objective is uniformly optimistic relative to true test performance, while the worst-case bound intersects true performance at plausible sensitivity parameter values, providing a more accurate evaluation. On EmoBank, the method shows the largest gains over DR (peak at ρC_Y C_D ≈ 0.8), reflecting strong unobserved writer-intent effects, whereas on Hate Speech the gains are modest (peak at ρC_Y C_D ≈ 0.06), suggesting most Reddit–Gab distribution shift is already captured in the text representations. The unadjusted baseline performs worst across all settings, confirming that some distribution shift adjustment is necessary, but the worst-case bound provides a meaningful improvement over DR specifically when unobserved variables are present.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the nonparametric OVB bounds of Chernozhukov et al. (2024) from causal parameter estimation to the generalization performance of language models under distribution shift, and embeds those bounds within a distributionally robust optimization framework (Duchi & Namkoong, 2021) to produce a worst-case objective. It departs from the standard doubly robust estimation literature (Robins et al., 1994) by explicitly acknowledging that the 'long' covariate set (full text plus external variables) is inaccessible to language models, and by introducing sensitivity parameters that quantify the resulting bias. The GLM log-likelihood restructuring is a novel technical contribution that makes the bound tractable for core NLP tasks (regression, classification, text generation) by allowing the nuisance model to predict labels rather than model-dependent losses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, theoretically grounded way to account for unobservable distribution shift in language model evaluation and training—something standard importance-weighting and doubly robust methods silently ignore. The three demonstrated uses (robust evaluation, robust optimization, and interpretability of omitted-variable strength) are practically relevant, especially the ability to quantify how much a black-box LLM like GPT-4.1 is affected by unobserved shift. Key limitations include the need for user-specified or benchmarked sensitivity parameters (with no fully automatic calibration), the worst-case nature of the bound which can be overly pessimistic (as shown by the inverted-U performance curves), and the fact that LLM experiments are evaluation-only rather than involving actual LLM training. Open questions include whether the GLM formulation covers all important generative tasks, how to scale the optimization to full LLM fine-tuning, and whether sensitivity parameters can be learned end-to-end.
