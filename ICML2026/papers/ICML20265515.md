## Problem

Current Context Engineering (CE) methods rely on manually crafted agentic harnesses—rigid generation-reflection-curation workflows and predefined context schemas—that impose structural inductive biases (brevity or verbosity) and restrict optimization to a narrow, intuition-bound design subspace. No single fixed harness is universally optimal across tasks, yet prior methods cannot discover task-appropriate strategies that lie beyond human-designed heuristics.

## Input / Output

The method takes a task specification (description, data format, evaluation criteria) and training rollouts (query–prediction–evaluation tuples) as input, and produces a context function instantiated as a collection of files and code in a workspace directory. The context function maps each query to its context via a composition of dynamic operators (retrieval, filtering, composition) applied to static components (knowledge bases, decision rules, examples), with no predefined schema constraining the representation. At inference time, a one-shot retrieval function (query → context string) is the interface used for evaluation, though the underlying artifacts are fully programmatic.

## Method

MCE formalizes CE as a bi-level optimization problem that co-evolves CE skills (how to represent and optimize context) and context artifacts (what context is learned). At the meta-level, an agent performs agentic crossover: it reasons over the task specification, inspects the full history of prior skills, their learned artifacts, and per-example evaluations stored in a filesystem, then synthesizes a new skill as a folder containing methodology, executable code, context templates, and dynamic operators. At the base-level, a fully agentic agent executes the skill using coding toolkits and file-system access to learn from training rollouts and update context as flexible files and code, maintaining a global view of accumulated context rather than performing instance-by-instance additive curation. The dual-level loop is orchestrated by a simple (1+1)-Evolution Strategy where each iteration generates one offspring context and retains the better of it and the current best. This design replaces heuristic scaffolding with a generic design space, allowing the system to reconstruct prior pipelines, discover novel CE architectures, and dynamically adjust context verbosity, autonomy, and granularity per task.

## Results

MCE achieves 89.1% average relative improvement over the base model (DeepSeek-V3.1) across five benchmarks in the offline setting, and 74.1% in the online setting, ranking first on all five benchmarks in both settings. Compared to the prior SOTA (ACE), MCE delivers 18.4% and 33.0% higher average relative gain in offline and online settings respectively, with per-benchmark gains ranging from 5.6% to 53.8% (mean 16.9%). MCE also demonstrates superior context efficiency (75% accuracy at 20K tokens vs. ACE's 70% at 79K tokens on FiNER), 13.6× faster training, and 4.8× fewer rollouts to reach 95% training accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MCE outperforms all four baselines on every benchmark in both offline and online settings. Against ACE (Zhang et al., ICLR 2026), the closest competitor, MCE achieves 89.1% vs. 70.7% average relative gain offline (e.g., 75.0% vs. 71.0% on FiNER, 89.2% vs. 79.2% on Symptom2Disease) and 74.1% vs. 41.1% online, with the largest gap on Symptom2Disease (76.4% vs. 62.3% online, where ACE's reflection overhead actually degrades performance below the base model). Against GEPA (Agrawal et al., 2025), MCE wins on all five offline benchmarks (e.g., 75.0% vs. 66.0% on FiNER, 0.80 vs. 0.76 F1 on Aegis2.0), though GEPA's brevity bias gives it a relative edge on the lightweight-model Aegis2.0 task. Against MIPROv2 (Opsahl-Ong et al., EMNLP 2024), MCE leads by a wide margin on all benchmarks (89.1% vs. 48.6% average relative gain offline). Against Dynamic Cheatsheet (Suzgun et al., 2025), MCE dominates in the online setting (74.1% vs. 35.8% average relative gain), with DC even degrading below the base model on Aegis2.0 (0.53 vs. 0.54 base F1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: agentic context engineering (ACE, GEPA, Dynamic Cheatsheet) and LLM-driven evolutionary computation (AlphaEvolve, EvoFlow, REEVO). It departs from prior CE methods by treating the engineering workflow itself as an evolvable object rather than a fixed scaffold, introducing 'agent skills'—folders of instructions, code, and templates—as a new level of abstraction for evolutionary search. The bi-level formulation (meta-level evolves skills via agentic crossover; base-level executes skills to produce context as files and code) repurposes the (1+1)-ES from evolutionary computation at this skill level, while the base-level's fully agentic, file-system-mediated optimization draws on the emerging paradigm of coding-toolkit agents (Claude Agent SDK, DeepAgents).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MCE's practical impact is substantial: a 13.6× training speedup over ACE and 4.8× fewer rollouts to reach comparable accuracy make iterative CE far more tractable, while the strong-to-weak context transferability (4–7% lower degradation) suggests learned contexts can serve as a lightweight alternative to domain-specific fine-tuning. The paper's key limitation is that MCE may offer little advantage on reasoning-intensive tasks where existing iterative trial-and-error harnesses are already well-suited, and it struggles with very long, complex trajectories requiring fine-grained credit assignment. Open questions include whether skill evolution generalizes beyond CE to other agentic capabilities, whether co-evolving context-utilization skills alongside context-learning skills yields further gains, and how skill composition and progressive disclosure scale across domains.
