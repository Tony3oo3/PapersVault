## Problem

Existing zeroth-order optimizers for LLM fine-tuning (e.g., MeZO, HIZOO, LOZO) rely on hand-crafted, static perturbation strategies such as standard normal sampling that are not adaptable to model-specific curvature structure. The paper identifies that learning-to-learn (L2L) techniques, which have shown promise on small-scale models, have not been scaled to LLMs due to prohibitive memory costs of per-parameter auxiliary networks, and that the foundation-model era—where a small set of base LLMs is repeatedly fine-tuned—makes a 'train once, reuse widely' learned optimizer both feasible and highly desirable.

## Input / Output

At deployment, the method takes the LLM's current parameters (compressed to per-block mean and variance statistics), the previous step's perturbation variance, and the two previous loss values as inputs to lightweight per-block PertNNs, which output a block-diagonal variance matrix Σ_t. This matrix is normalized so that ||Σ_t||_F² = d (the total parameter count), ensuring the effective learning rate remains controlled by a single scalar η. The final output is the updated LLM parameters after a two-point ZO gradient step, with the only additional memory being the PertNN weights (<2 MB for OPT-30B in FP16).

## Method

ZO Fine-tuner replaces the fixed N(0,I) perturbation of MeZO with a learned block-diagonal variance matrix Σ_t, where each parameter block (e.g., an attention Q/K/V matrix or projection layer) shares a single perturbation variance predicted by a compact two-layer MLP (PertNN) with 64 hidden units. The reparameterization trick (sampling z~N(0,I) and computing u=Σz) makes the perturbation differentiable, enabling gradient-based L2L training where the meta-objective minimizes the post-update loss along a first-order fine-tuning trajectory. A normalization step fixes the total variance budget to d, decoupling relative block-wise variance from the overall step size. The L2L training procedure includes periodic re-initialization of model parameters to prevent overfitting to the low-loss region, and the entire training is a one-time cost per base model. The design is theoretically motivated by Theorem 3.1, which shows that under a block-diagonal Hessian assumption, adaptive per-block variances yield a tighter convergence bound than MeZO's uniform variance.

## Results

Across 28 task-model combinations (4 LLMs × 7 datasets), ZO Fine-tuner achieves the lowest converged training loss in 82.1% of settings and the highest accuracy/F1 in 75.0%, with an average accuracy improvement of 2.5% over MeZO. The method is trained once on the COPA dataset and reused as-is across all other tasks and models, demonstrating strong out-of-distribution transfer. On LLaMA-3.1-8B, ZO Fine-tuner achieves the best loss on 6 of 7 datasets (e.g., 0.18 on SST-2 vs. 0.25–0.36 for baselines; 0.26 on CB vs. 0.47–0.61), while on OPT-30B it leads on 5 of 7 (e.g., 0.56 on SQuAD vs. 0.59–0.96). Memory overhead is negligible: peak GPU memory is 62 GB for OPT-30B vs. 61 GB for MeZO, compared to 316 GB for first-order Adam.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ZO Fine-tuner is compared against four zeroth-order baselines: MeZO, MeZO-Adam/AdamU, HIZOO, and LOZO. Against MeZO (the simplest baseline), ZO Fine-tuner wins on 82.1% of 28 task-model pairs in loss and 75.0% in accuracy, with the largest gaps on SST-2 (e.g., 0.14 vs. 0.29 loss on LLaMA-1B) and CB (0.26 vs. 0.54 on LLaMA-8B). Against MeZO-AdamU, which adds momentum-based adaptation, ZO Fine-tuner still dominates on most settings, particularly on LLaMA-3.1-8B where it achieves 0.18 loss on SST-2 versus 0.36 for MeZO-AdamU. Against HIZOO, which incorporates Hessian information, ZO Fine-tuner wins on the majority of pairs but HIZOO occasionally matches or slightly edges it on accuracy (e.g., HIZOO 0.93 vs. ZO Fine-tuner 0.91 on COPA with LLaMA-8B). Against LOZO, which exploits low-rank gradient structure, ZO Fine-tuner is consistently superior in loss across nearly all settings, with LOZO showing the largest degradation on OPT-30B (e.g., 2.83 loss on DROP vs. 1.59 for ZO Fine-tuner). Notably, ZO Fine-tuner requires only a learning rate as a tunable hyperparameter, whereas HIZOO, LOZO, and MeZO-AdamU each require 2–4 additional hyperparameters (smooth constant, rank, interval, momentum coefficients), making it more practical to deploy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: zeroth-order (ZO) optimization for LLM fine-tuning, initiated by MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023) and extended by hand-crafted variants like HIZOO, LOZO, and ZO-AdamU; and the learning-to-learn (L2L) paradigm (Andrychowicz et al., 2016), which uses auxiliary networks to adaptively guide optimization. The key departure is that prior ZO methods rely on static, manually designed perturbation distributions, while prior L2L work was confined to small-scale models with limited cross-architecture transferability. By exploiting the approximately block-diagonal Hessian structure observed in Transformers (Zhang et al., 2024b), the authors make L2L tractable at LLM scale through a compact per-block PertNN, and leverage the LLM supply-chain structure (few base models, many derivatives) to enable a 'train once, reuse widely' workflow that neither prior line of work supported.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a single learned optimizer trained on one dataset (COPA) transfers to 28 task-model combinations with negligible memory overhead (<2 MB for OPT-30B) and <3.4% time overhead, offering a near-inference-memory fine-tuning path with consistently better convergence than all hand-designed ZO baselines. The 'train once, reuse widely' paradigm is particularly appealing for model developers who could ship a pretrained finetuner alongside each base model. Open questions include whether richer (non-diagonal) covariance structures would further improve performance, how the method interacts with PEFT techniques like LoRA or quantization, and whether the block-wise approximation remains sufficient as model architectures evolve. The method remains within the two-forward-pass ZO regime, so it does not address the fundamental accuracy gap with first-order training.
