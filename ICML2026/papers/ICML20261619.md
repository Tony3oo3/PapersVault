## Problem

While the Rashomon set (a collection of near-optimal models) has been studied for interpretability and fairness, its impact on adversarial robustness, privacy, and stability remains largely unexplored. The paper asks: what are the positive and negative trustworthiness consequences of having a large, diverse Rashomon set, and how do these properties behave qualitatively differently at the set level versus the single-model level?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261619/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a labeled tabular dataset, a hypothesis space (sparse decision trees, rule lists, or linear models), and a Rashomon tolerance ε (typically 1–3% accuracy drop) to define the set of near-optimal models. The output is not a single model but the Rashomon set itself treated as a policy object, accompanied by theoretical bounds on adversarial risk, mutual information leakage, and set-level stability under distribution shift. Empirically, the Rashomon set is constructed via TreeFARMS for sparse trees, and privacy is measured through DRAFT reconstruction error rather than direct mutual information computation.

## Method

The paper establishes a robustness-privacy trade-off governed by model diversity within the Rashomon set. On the robustness side, it proves that Hamming distance (for discrete models) or angular distance (for linear models) between a candidate model and the attacked ERM model bounds the adversarial risk gap, showing that diverse near-optimal models fail differently and enable reactive robustness without retraining. On the privacy side, it proves that the expected TV/KL divergence between the true data distribution and an ensemble of m sampled Rashomon models decreases as m grows, meaning more released models yield more distributional leakage even when each model is individually sparse and private. Stability is shown via Pinsker's inequality (distribution shift) and a K/n perturbation bound (dataset modification), proving the Rashomon set remains nearly invariant under small shifts. Empirically, white-box attacks on the optimal tree are enumerated and other Rashomon members' responses measured, while DRAFT reconstruction error tracks leakage as trees are sequentially disclosed.

## Results

The paper establishes a robustness-privacy trade-off governed by Rashomon set diversity: on COMPAS, the trade-off slope is approximately −2 (high-efficiency regime where robustness gains come at minimal privacy cost), while on FICO it is approximately −1 (diminishing returns). Under a 6% dataset modification, more than 80% of models in the pre-computed Rashomon set remain valid, and under 2% perturbation the sets are nearly identical. For information leakage, the 'farthest' tree-selection strategy (maximizing diversity) yields lower DRAFT reconstruction error than the 'closest' strategy, confirming that greater diversity accelerates leakage. Trees with larger Hamming distance from the optimal tree consistently achieve higher adversarial accuracy, validating the reactive robustness mechanism.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark a proposed method against competing algorithms; instead, it compares trustworthiness properties at two levels of abstraction: a single sparse model versus the full Rashomon set. At the single-model level, sparse decision trees and rule lists are shown to be private (mutual information bounded by O(l_f log d)) and algorithmically stable, yet inherently vulnerable to adversarial perturbations (Theorem 4.2 shows a guaranteed error increase of (n_+ − n̄_+)/n under L_0 attacks with budget η = l + I − 1). At the Rashomon-set level, the same diversity that causes vulnerability in isolation enables reactive robustness: models far from the attacked model in Hamming distance (discrete) or angular distance (linear) maintain high adversarial accuracy. For privacy, the paper compares three tree-selection strategies (random, closest-to-optimal, farthest-from-optimal) using DRAFT reconstruction error as the leakage metric; the farthest strategy produces the lowest reconstruction error (most leakage), while the closest strategy preserves the most privacy. The trade-off is further validated on multi-layer perceptrons, confirming the phenomenon extends beyond interpretable architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Rashomon set formalism of Semenova et al. (2022) and the broader Rashomon Effect literature (Breiman 2001; Marx et al. 2020), but shifts the analytical lens from individual-model properties (interpretability, fairness, simplicity) to set-level trustworthiness. It departs from Hsu et al. (2026), which evaluates robustness, fairness, and stability of individual models within a Rashomon set, by studying the Rashomon set as a whole: reactive robustness via model rotation, set-level stability under distribution shift, and aggregate information leakage from releasing multiple near-optimal models. The robustness-privacy trade-off is thus analyzed at the set level rather than the single-model or training-procedure level that dominates prior trustworthy-ML work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes the Rashomon set from a theoretical curiosity into a natural governance object, with concrete implications for institutions that already generate many near-optimal models during development. The central open question it raises is how to operationalize disclosure: how many and which near-optimal models to release to balance transparency, robustness, and privacy, a problem the paper sketches but does not fully solve. Limitations include a focus on decision trees and linear models (with MLP experiments as a sanity check), white-box attack settings, and the fact that the stability results assume small shifts; extending the framework to adaptive multi-model attacks and deeper architectures remains open.
