## Problem

Optimization-based post-training quantization (e.g., GPTQ) degrades severely at 2–3 bits because fixed quantization grids enforce shape invariance: the relative spacing between quantization levels is identical across all groups, differing only by a per-group scale. This rigidity restricts the feasible set for the Hessian-induced nearest-point projection, so the solver cannot find low-error solutions even though the optimization objective itself is sound. The paper identifies this grid rigidity—not the objective—as the fundamental bottleneck for extreme low-bit quantization.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-trained LLM weights and 1024 C4 calibration samples (used to compute the approximate Hessian H = XX^T). The output is a quantized weight matrix decomposed into k binary bit-plane matrices (one per non-bias bit) and per-group scalar coefficient vectors (one bias plus k scales per group of g columns). Because each bit-plane carries its own coefficient, BPDQ uses larger group sizes (g=128 for 4-bit, g∈{64,128} for 2/3-bit) than GPTQ/AWQ (g=64/32) to offset the extra coefficient storage, yielding comparable bits-per-weight. At inference, the binary bit-planes enable a Look-Up Table (LUT) kernel for efficient per-token decoding.

## Method

BPDQ replaces the fixed shape-invariant grid with a variable grid constructed via bit-plane decomposition: for 2-bit, each group's quantized values are {0, c₁, c₂, c₁+c₂} where c₁ and c₂ are independent per-group coefficients, expanding the feasible set from a 1-D ray to a 2-D plane in level-space. Initialization applies round-to-nearest 8-bit quantization, retains the k most-significant bit-planes, and fits scalar coefficients via a closed-form weighted least-squares solution under the Hessian-induced geometry (using the local Cholesky factor U_loc). The method then iterates (10 rounds) between column-wise bit-plane updates—exact enumeration over 2^k candidate bit vectors minimizing local reconstruction error with GPTQ-style error propagation—and group-wise coefficient refitting via the same closed-form WLS. A delta correction (ΔE·U_loc = Ŵ_old − Ŵ_new) is applied after each refit to preserve error-propagation consistency in the Hessian-induced geometry. The paper proves that the variable grid strictly contains the fixed-grid feasible set (Proposition 1) and that every step of the iterative procedure is consistent with the Hessian-induced optimality condition (Appendix B).

## Results

On Qwen2.5-72B at 2-bit (W2-G64), BPDQ achieves 87.72% GSM8K and 51.20% MATH500, dramatically outperforming GPTQ (63.46% / 28.40%) and AWQ (0.00% / 0.00%). At the extreme W2-G256 setting, BPDQ compresses the 72B model to 22.69 GB (single RTX 3090) while retaining 83.85% GSM8K (92.32% of the 16-bit baseline's 90.83%). At 4-bit, BPDQ matches or slightly exceeds GPTQ and AWQ across all benchmarks (e.g., 92.65% GSM8K vs. 90.52% GPTQ, 91.28% AWQ on Qwen2.5-72B). On Qwen3-32B at 2-bit, BPDQ-W2-G64 reaches 80.89% GSM8K versus 5.91% (GPTQ) and 3.18% (AWQ), confirming the variable-grid advantage generalizes across model families.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GPTQ (the closest method, sharing the same Hessian-based optimization framework), BPDQ achieves a decisive win at 2-bit: 87.72% vs. 63.46% GSM8K on Qwen2.5-72B (W2-G64), and 80.89% vs. 5.91% on Qwen3-32B, while remaining competitive at 4-bit (92.65% vs. 90.52% GSM8K). Against AWQ, the gap is even more extreme at 2-bit (87.72% vs. 0.00% GSM8K on 72B), though AWQ is competitive at 3–4 bits by leveraging outlier preservation. Against AnyBCQ (another bit-plane method), BPDQ substantially outperforms at 2-bit (44.50% vs. 9.63% GSM8K on Qwen2.5-7B) and at 3-bit (76.42% vs. 26.61%), while AnyBCQ degrades severely even at 4-bit (29.26% vs. 78.24% GSM8K). Against VPTQ (vector quantization), VPTQ achieves slightly higher accuracy (67.63% vs. 44.50% GSM8K at 2-bit on 7B) but incurs ~40× the quantization time (4×160 min on 4 GPUs vs. 40 min on 1 GPU), making BPDQ the practical choice for deployment. On LLaMA2-7B (Table 7), BPDQ is competitive with VPTQ, QuIP#, and AQLM at 3-bit (8.19% vs. 8.36% GSM8K) and slightly trails at 2-bit (1.30% vs. 1.63%), confirming that a residual fidelity gap versus VQ remains but is narrow.

## Relation to Prior Work

BPDQ extends the optimization-based PTQ lineage (OBD/OBS → OBC → GPTQ) by retaining GPTQ's Hessian-induced geometry, Cholesky-based error propagation, and output-aligned objective, while replacing the fixed shape-invariant quantization grid with a variable grid constructed from bit-plane decomposition and per-group scalar coefficients. Conceptually, it bridges two previously separate threads: the rigorous second-order optimization framework of GPTQ and the hardware-friendly bit-plane arithmetic of methods like AnyBCQ, which lacked a principled output-aligned objective. The theoretical framing connects to the recent geometric interpretation of GPTQ as Babai's nearest-plane algorithm on a Hessian-induced lattice, with the key departure being that the lattice structure itself becomes group-adaptive rather than globally rigid.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: BPDQ enables 2-bit inference of a 72B model on a single consumer GPU (RTX 3090, 22.69 GB) with 83.85% GSM8K accuracy, a deployment scenario previously inaccessible to optimization-based PTQ. The diagnostic insight—that the low-bit failure of GPTQ stems from grid rigidity rather than the optimization objective—redirects future work toward grid design rather than objective redesign. However, a fidelity gap versus vector quantization (VPTQ) persists, quantization time is ~3× GPTQ due to 10 iterations, and the 2-bit results, while dramatically better than GPTQ/AWQ, still show meaningful degradation from 16-bit baselines. Open questions include whether rotation techniques or enhanced sequential solvers (Qronos) can close the remaining gap, and whether the bit-plane structure's natural fit for FPGA/ASIC deployment can be exploited for energy-efficient edge inference.
