## Problem

Offline preference-based RL (PbRL) typically learns a reward model from scarce pairwise preferences and then performs offline RL, but this two-stage pipeline is vulnerable to reward over-optimization and poor generalization under limited feedback. Meanwhile, reward-free representation learning (RFRL) has shown that compact, task-agnostic representations can be learned purely from unlabeled data, yet the connection between RFRL and PbRL has remained unexplored. This paper asks whether RFRL-pretrained representations can directly serve as features for preference learning, eliminating the need for an explicit reward or preference model.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two inputs: (1) a large static offline dataset of reward-free trajectories (5M transitions per environment from ExORL RND data), and (2) a small set of pairwise preference labels (typically 2,000 ternary labels over trajectory segments of length 20–200 steps). The output is a task-conditioned policy π(s, z*) recovered as argmax_a F_θ(s,a,z*)ᵀz* and a low-dimensional latent task specification vector z* (a few hundred dimensions) that encodes the preference-aligned task direction. Notably, no scalar reward function or preference scoring network is ever trained; the entire preference signal is absorbed into the contrastive geometry of the FB latent space.

## Method

FB-PbRL is a two-stage framework built on Forward-Backward (FB) successor-measure representations. In stage 1, F_θ and B_ω are pre-trained on reward-free offline data using the standard FB measure loss and orthonormality regularization, learning a latent space that spans all reward functions. The key theoretical insight is that under linear reward parameterization r_ψ(s,a) = B_ω(s,a)ᵀψ and the orthonormality condition H_B ≈ I, the Bradley-Terry preference loss is exactly equivalent to a SimCLR contrastive loss over segment-level backward embeddings z_σ = (1/k)Σ B_ω(s_t,a_t). In stage 2, the task specification z* is found by minimizing this contrastive objective (aligning z* with preferred segment embeddings and repelling from non-preferred ones), which is convex in the low-dimensional z*. A preference-guided fine-tuning (PG-FT) step then jointly adapts F_θ, B_ω, and z* so the latent geometry becomes reward-aligned and z* stays in-distribution, after which the policy is updated via the standard FB actor objective L_π = −E[F_θ(s,π(s),z*)ᵀz*].

## Results

Under the PbRL Protocol (2,000 preference pairs) on DMC, FB-PbRL (Ours-FT) achieves domain-average returns of 621.7 (Cheetah), 762.9 (Walker), 846.9 (Quadruped), and 570.8 (PointMass), outperforming the best offline PbRL baseline (LiRE at 313.4, OPRL at 253.8, 631.1, and 337.5 respectively) by 2–3×. Under the Zero-Shot RFRL Protocol, Ours-FT uses only preference labels while baselines access ground-truth rewards, yet still achieves the best average on Cheetah (645.4 vs. PSM 626.0) and Quadruped (826.3 vs. PSM 618.7). On real human-preference manipulation data, FB-PbRL scores 89.0 on Adroit Pen-cloned and 71.2 on MetaWorld Button-Press-Topdown, matching or exceeding DPPO and substantially outperforming LiRE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FB-PbRL (Ours-FT) decisively outperforms all five offline PbRL baselines (DPPO, OPPO, OPRL, CLARIFY, LiRE) on DMC, with domain-average margins of 2–3× over the strongest competitor in each domain (e.g., 762.9 vs. OPRL's 253.8 on Walker; 846.9 vs. OPRL's 631.1 on Quadruped). Against zero-shot RFRL baselines that receive ground-truth rewards at test time, Ours-FT (using only preferences) still wins on Cheetah (645.4 vs. PSM 626.0) and Quadruped (826.3 vs. PSM 618.7), trails FB slightly on Walker (699.4 vs. 719.9), and is outperformed by RLDP on PointMass (501.1 vs. 671.1) due to sparse coverage in that dataset. On real human-preference manipulation, FB-PbRL matches DPPO on Adroit Pen-human (70.2 vs. 76.3) and surpasses it on Pen-cloned (89.0 vs. 75.1), while LiRE collapses to 4.5–14.4 on Adroit due to its reliance on repeated trajectory segments. The test-time-only variant (Ours-T) already exceeds all PbRL baselines on most tasks, confirming that the RFRL pre-trained representations are the primary source of the performance gain rather than the fine-tuning stage alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously disconnected lines of research: reward-free representation learning (RFRL), particularly the Forward-Backward (FB) decomposition framework of Touati & Ollivier (2021) and Touati et al. (2023), and offline preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL) built on Bradley-Terry reward modeling. The key conceptual departure is showing that the standard BT preference loss, when expressed in the FB latent space under linear reward parameterization and orthonormality of the backward embedding, reduces exactly to a SimCLR-style contrastive objective (Chen et al., 2020). This eliminates the need for an explicit reward or preference model, replacing the conventional two-stage 'learn reward → run offline RL' pipeline with 'pre-train FB representations → contrastive search and fine-tune on preferences.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By connecting RFRL to PbRL, this work opens a principled path toward feedback-efficient RL that does not require hand-crafted rewards or expensive per-step reward labels, potentially lowering the barrier to deploying RL in domains where only pairwise human comparisons are feasible (robotics, healthcare, scientific experimentation). The convexity of the test-time task-specification optimization and the theoretical guarantee (Proposition 1) provide practical and analytical advantages over training high-capacity reward networks. Key limitations include dependence on offline data coverage (visible in PointMass Bottom Right failures) and the current restriction to FB-based representations, though the authors sketch extensions to HILP and online settings. Open questions include whether the SimCLR connection generalizes to non-linear reward parameterizations, how the method scales to very large preference datasets, and whether the fine-tuning stage can be replaced by purely test-time search in richer environments.
