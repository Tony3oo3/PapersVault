## Problem

Multi-step prompt injection attacks on LLM agents present a fundamental detection challenge: malicious intent emerges only after the full workflow completes, while each individual action (reading a page, extracting data, transmitting it) remains legitimate in isolation. Existing defenses—input sanitization, output validation, instruction hierarchy—operate on individual actions or content patterns and cannot capture the sequential causal structure that makes a sequence of benign-looking actions collectively harmful. This paper reframes prompt-injection defense as a causal inference problem over agent execution graphs.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a logged agent trajectory as a sequence of typed events (navigate, click, type, read, tool-call) with origin domains, parent action identifiers, and observed content, along with the user task specification and a policy configuration specifying blocked domains. The trajectory is parsed into a directed acyclic graph with three typed edge types (data dependency, trust transfer, state enablement) and per-node trust labels (trusted/untrusted/sensitive) computed automatically without manual annotation. Beyond a binary attack/benign label and confidence score, the system outputs an intervention decision (BLOCK/WARN/ALLOW), the complete attack chain as a causal subgraph recovered via bidirectional slicing, ranked bottleneck nodes for targeted intervention, and counterfactual metrics (Probability of Necessity and Average Treatment Effect) quantifying the causal dependence of the harmful outcome on the injection.

## Method

CausalTrace constructs a Structural Causal Model from the agent trajectory, inferring three edge types—data dependency (information flow), trust transfer (external content influencing agent behavior), and state enablement (precondition establishment)—then applies Pearl's do-calculus to compute the Probability of Necessity, answering whether the harmful outcome would have occurred had the injection been blocked via graph mutilation and counterfactual propagation. An ensemble of five complementary detectors (LLM semantic classifier, GNN on the causal graph, random forest on 34 graph-derived features, d-separation-based mechanism integrity test, and probabilistic causal inference) votes on the final classification, with the LLM serving only as a Stage-1 router that exits early on high-confidence single-step cases and triggers full causal analysis for multi-step or uncertain trajectories. Taint propagation tracks untrusted content through data and trust edges to sensitive sinks, while integrity watermarking independently verifies that sensitive actions preserve trusted provenance lineage, providing defense-in-depth against adaptive adversaries. Bidirectional slicing recovers the complete attack chain as the intersection of the forward slice from the injection source and the backward slice from the malicious outcome, and a task-conditioned counterfactual (via a task alignment score) disambiguates user-authorized cross-domain actions from genuine attacks, eliminating false positives on legitimate workflows that share surface-level features with injections.

## Results

On a held-out benchmark of real attack trajectories (InjecAgent, WASP, pajaMAS), CausalTrace achieves 95.3% F1 (96.1% precision, 94.5% recall), substantially outperforming non-causal baselines that rely on action sequences (81.2%), action frequencies (74.3%), or domain features (65.1%). On the 1,477-trajectory out-of-distribution evaluation, CausalTrace reaches 92.6% F1 with per-benchmark recall of 85.8% (BrowserART), 86.8% (SafeArena), 100% (WASP), and 84.2% (pajaMAS). The combined in-distribution + OOD F1 is 98.0% (95% CI [97.7%, 98.3%]), with only 3 false positives across 27,398 benign trajectories (FPR = 0.011%). Attack chain recovery achieves 91.4% edge recall, 94.8% injection detection, and 87.2% bottleneck identification at 0.15 ms slice computation time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CausalTrace outperforms all eight baselines by a wide margin on the primary held-out evaluation. The strongest non-causal baselines (action-sequence-based classifiers, corresponding to PromptGuard, NeMo Guardrails, LLM-Guard, and Rebuff which operate on content/sequence patterns) achieve only 81.2% F1, a 14.1-point gap behind CausalTrace's 95.3%. Weaker content-pattern baselines (KeywordFilter, ActionWhitelist) and domain-feature methods score 74.3% and 65.1% F1 respectively. The graph-based baselines GCN and GAT, trained on the same causal trajectory graphs, are subsumed within the 'causal graph methods' group that achieves 95.3% F1, indicating that the full CausalTrace ensemble (combining taint, watermark, counterfactual, and GNN signals) provides the strongest single-system performance. On the OOD evaluation, CausalTrace maintains 92.6% F1 with zero false positives on 250 benign SafeArena trajectories, while the 6.8-point drop from in-distribution (99.4%) to OOD reflects genuine domain shift rather than baseline-specific weakness. The paper notes that the Attack Detection Classifier alone (operating on 34 causal graph features) achieves 99.1% F1, confirming that the causal graph representation is the primary discriminative signal rather than any individual detector.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts Pearl's structural causal model and do-calculus framework (originally developed for general causal reasoning) to the specific problem of LLM agent trajectory analysis, combining it with dynamic taint analysis and provenance tracking borrowed from program security (DyTAN, Flume, SLEUTH). It departs from existing prompt-injection defenses—input sanitization, instruction hierarchy, and content-based classifiers—which operate on individual actions or surface patterns—by reframing detection as a graph-reachability and counterfactual-dependence problem over typed causal edges (data dependency, trust transfer, state enablement). The bidirectional slicing technique for attack-chain recovery is directly adapted from program-analysis provenance systems, while the integrity watermarking mechanism is a novel addition that tracks trusted provenance orthogonally to taint propagation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap: existing agent-security benchmarks record binary attack outcomes without tracking *how* injections propagate to harm, and content-based defenses cannot capture multi-step attacks where individual actions are legitimate in isolation. The 1M+ trajectory CausalBench benchmark and the interpretable attack-chain recovery (with bottleneck identification) provide practical tooling for post-hoc forensic audit of agent sessions. Key limitations include the post-hoc-only design (no real-time interception), reliance on complete trajectory logging, and the assumption of additive invertible noise in the probabilistic causal module. Open questions include extending to streaming trajectories for real-time intervention, formal robustness guarantees against structure-aware adversaries who mimic benign causal topology, and whether the causal-inference components provide meaningful defense-in-depth beyond what graph-topology features alone achieve.
