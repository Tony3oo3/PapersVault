## Problem

Atomistic Boltzmann Generators (BGs) enable asymptotically unbiased equilibrium sampling via importance reweighting, but scale poorly to larger molecular systems due to high-dimensional Jacobian computation and diminishing proposal-target overlap. Coarse-grained surrogates (Boltzmann Emulators) reduce dimensionality but lack a reweighting mechanism, producing biased estimates when trained on short or biased trajectories. The paper addresses how to perform equilibrium sampling in reduced-dimensional CG space while retaining the importance-sampling correction that guarantees asymptotic correctness.

## Input / Output

The method consumes atomistic MD trajectories (unbiased or biased via well-tempered metadynamics/umbrella sampling) along with a predefined CG mapping (Heavy Atom or Core Beta) that projects configurations onto a lower-dimensional bead space. At inference, a Gaussian prior is transformed through the trained flow to produce CG configurations, which are then reweighted using the learned PMF to yield equilibrium samples. Outputs include reweighted CG samples with importance weights, from which dihedral free energy profiles, Ramachandran distributions, and normalized effective sample size (ESS) are computed; a 1% weight-clipping threshold is applied to mitigate weight degeneracy from out-of-domain extrapolation.

## Method

CG-BGs combine two components operating in CG coordinate space: a continuous normalizing flow trained via Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) to generate a proposal density q_θ(R) over CG configurations, and a learned potential of mean force U_η(R) (parameterized with MACE equivariant GNNs) that serves as the target energy for importance reweighting. The key insight is that the PMF can be learned from rapidly converged biased trajectories using Enhanced Sampling Force Matching (ESFM), which exploits the invariance of the fiber distribution p(r|R) under CG-only biasing (Proposition 2), so the force-matching regression target is unchanged regardless of the bias. At inference, importance weights w(R) ∝ exp(−βU_η(R))/q_θ(R) correct the flow proposal to the target Boltzmann distribution p(R), enabling unbiased estimation of equilibrium observables without requiring long unbiased MD simulations. This distinguishes CG-BGs from Boltzmann Emulators (which omit reweighting) and from atomistic BGs (which operate in full atomistic space and are limited by implicit solvent approximations for the target energy).

## Results

On alanine dipeptide (Heavy Atom mapping), CG-BGs achieve a JS divergence of 0.0048 and PMF error of 0.2005 after reweighting, with an ESS of 0.5112, substantially outperforming implicit solvent baselines GB(OBC1) (JS=0.0157, PMF=0.3709) and GB(OBC2) (JS=0.0182, PMF=0.4028). For alanine tripeptide, CG-BGs (Heavy Atom) reach JS=0.0056 and PMF=0.1957, while GB(OBC2) degrades to JS=0.0932 and PMF=1.0274. For alanine hexapeptide (Core Beta), CG-BGs achieve JS=0.0100 and PMF=0.3646 versus GB(OBC2) at JS=0.1652 and PMF=1.8401, demonstrating that the accuracy gap widens with system size. Atomistic BGs (TarFlow, ECNF++) trained on implicit solvent data perform far worse (JS=0.0548–0.1451, PMF=1.27–21.40), confirming that CG-BGs are not limited by solvent approximation error.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CG-BGs consistently outperform both implicit solvent baselines (GB OBC1/OBC2) across all peptide systems, with the advantage growing with system size: on alanine dipeptide the JS divergence is ~3× lower (0.0048 vs. 0.0157–0.0182), while on hexapeptide it is ~16× lower (0.0100 vs. 0.1652). Against atomistic BGs (TarFlow and ECNF++) trained on implicit solvent data, CG-BGs achieve dramatically better PMF error (0.20 vs. 1.27 for TarFlow, 21.40 for ECNF++) and higher ESS (0.51 vs. 0.0034 for TarFlow), because atomistic BGs are fundamentally capped by the implicit solvent approximation. In terms of computational cost (Table 5), CG-BGs with Core Beta mapping reduce total training+inference time from 2.80 h (all-atom) to 0.47 h for dipeptide, and from 12.52–16.83 h (ECNF++/SBG) to 0.47 h, a 27–36× speedup. The Core Beta mapping also yields higher ESS (0.5528) than Heavy Atom (0.5112) due to lower-dimensional proposal space, though at a small accuracy cost in PMF error. Overall, CG-BGs win on all accuracy metrics against every baseline while also being substantially cheaper to train and run at inference.

## Relation to Prior Work

CG-BGs sit at the intersection of two established but previously disconnected lines of work: Boltzmann Generators (Noé et al., 2019), which combine exact-likelihood normalizing flows with importance sampling for equilibrium estimation, and bottom-up coarse-graining via force matching (Noid et al., 2008; Chen et al., 2026), which learns effective potentials in reduced coordinate spaces. The key conceptual departure is that prior BGs operate in full atomistic space (limiting them to small systems), while Boltzmann Emulators (Lewis et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2024) scale to larger systems but omit reweighting and thus inherit training-data bias. CG-BGs resolve this tension by performing both generative modeling and importance reweighting in CG coordinates, using a learned PMF as the target energy, with the PMF itself trained via enhanced sampling force matching (ESFM) from rapidly converged biased trajectories rather than requiring long unbiased simulations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a practical pathway to equilibrium sampling of larger molecular systems by reducing the effective dimensionality of both generation and reweighting, with demonstrated computational savings of roughly 6× in inference time for the Core Beta mapping versus all-atom. The simulation-free PMF evaluation capability—reusing a single set of flow-generated samples to compare multiple candidate CG potentials via reweighting—offers a new validation tool that avoids repeated MD simulations. Key limitations include reliance on predefined collective variables (nontrivial for complex systems), testing only on small peptides up to 72 atoms, and the inherent approximation bias from the learned PMF. Open questions include scaling to larger/complex systems, automatic CV discovery, and whether energy-based or neural-sampler formulations could replace the flow+reweighting pipeline.
