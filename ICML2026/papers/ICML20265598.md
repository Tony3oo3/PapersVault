## Problem

Decisive error recognition in multi-agent systems—pinpointing the exact agent and step that first triggered a cascading failure—remains unsolved because existing LLM-as-a-judge methods achieve ≤10% step-level accuracy, fine-tuning approaches require prohibitively expensive and ambiguous annotations, and both fail to adapt to continuously emerging error types in dynamic deployments. The paper identifies that over 80% of MAS failure trajectories share ≥0.8 semantic similarity in error logs, yet naive in-context reuse of full trajectories degrades accuracy due to extreme length (17% exceed 32K tokens) and low signal-to-noise ratio.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a failed MAS trajectory (a sequence of agent-step-result tuples, often 60K+ tokens) and a cache of compact error schemata retrieved via BGE-M3 cosine similarity, with the trajectory's own schema masked to prevent leakage. It outputs the decisive error as a triple (agent*, step*, reason), where step* is the earliest step whose correction would flip the trajectory outcome from failure to success. Schemas are truncated to a fixed per-turn character budget (500–2000 chars) before embedding, making the approach robust to context-length limits.

## Method

CORRECT is a training-free, three-phase framework: (1) Offline, failure trajectories are semantically clustered (K selected via silhouette-coefficient saturation), and an LLM distills one compact error schema per cluster encoding error signatures, context analysis, and detection heuristics; (2) Online, the top-k most similar schemas are retrieved and instantiated in the LLM prompt alongside the target trajectory to guide step-level error localization; (3) Dynamically, the cache self-maintains via schema expansion (adding new schemas when user-confirmed errors fall below a similarity threshold δ to all cached entries) and schema distillation (regenerating and replaying candidates for schemas exceeding a hotness threshold θ_hot to retain only the most discriminative version). This schema-retrieval mechanism replaces both full-trajectory ICL and parameter updates, enabling subsecond adaptation to new error patterns at near-zero inference overhead (<3.5% latency increase).

## Results

On the WHO&WHEN benchmark, CORRECT with GPT-5 achieves 17.2% exact-step accuracy (Acc@0) and 32.3% tolerant accuracy (Acc@1) on the Human-Crafted subset, and 38.1% Acc@0 / 58.8% Acc@1 on the Algorithm-Generated subset — a +19.8 point gain over the best LLM-as-a-Judge baseline on Algorithm-Generated Acc@0. Across seven diverse MAS tasks in CORRECT-Error, CORRECT delivers average improvements of 20.1%, 27.6%, and 28.7% at Acc@1, Acc@3, and Acc@5 respectively (GPT-4o-mini subset), with the largest single-task gain of +46.9 points on Math500. All improvements are achieved with zero training overhead and <3.5% latency increase.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CORRECT consistently outperforms the LLM-as-a-Judge baselines from Zhang et al. (2025) across all model families: with GPT-5, it improves Acc@0 from 8.6% to 17.2% (+8.6 pts) on Human-Crafted and from 18.3% to 38.1% (+19.8 pts) on Algorithm-Generated, while the best zero-shot judge (Gemini-2.5-Flash) reaches only 31.8% Acc@0 on Algorithm-Generated. Against MIPRO (Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024), which optimizes prompts via Bayesian search, CORRECT with Qwen-2.5-7B achieves 12.1% vs. 8.6% Acc@0 on Human-Crafted and 19.8% vs. 15.1% on Algorithm-Generated. Fine-tuning (Qwen-2.5-7B SFT) and Naive ICL both underperform CORRECT by 8.6 and 6.9 points respectively on Human-Crafted Acc@0, confirming that raw trajectory insertion and supervised learning fail to capture structural error patterns. In cross-domain transfer (Appendix A.13), CORRECT also beats the baselines from Cemri et al. (2025) on error-category detection (16.6% vs. 15.7% recall on ProgramDev+ChatDev; 15.8% vs. 11.9% on AG2+GSM8K) and from Zhu et al. (2025) on AgentErrorBench (46% vs. 36% on GAIA, 35% vs. 22% on ALFWorld, 24% vs. 16% on WebShop with GPT-5-nano).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Zhang et al. (2025), which formalized the decisive error concept and introduced the WHO&WHEN benchmark with LLM-as-a-judge baselines (all-at-once, step-by-step, binary search). CORRECT departs from both the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm (which the authors show degrades to ~3.5% step-level accuracy on long trajectories) and fine-tuning approaches (which are data-hungry and hurt generalization) by reframing error recognition as a knowledge-transfer problem: distilling past failures into compact, reusable error schemata and retrieving them at inference time. The design is conceptually adjacent to retrieval-augmented generation and in-context learning, but explicitly addresses why naive ICL fails (32K+ token trajectories, low signal-to-noise) by replacing raw trajectories with condensed structural summaries. The CORRECT-Error dataset extends the error-injection methodology with a bootstrap pipeline seeded by human annotations, scaling from ~200 trajectories to 2,000+.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CORRECT provides the first training-free, near-zero-overhead framework for decisive error localization in MAS, making it practical for real-time deployment where new error types emerge continuously. The CORRECT-Error benchmark (2,000+ trajectories across 7 task domains) addresses a critical evaluation gap, with human validation showing synthetic errors are near-indistinguishable from real ones. Key open questions remain: the subjectivity of decisive error labels (annotator disagreement >50% in WHO&WHEN), whether semantic similarity is the optimal retrieval signal versus structured/causal matching, and how to integrate schemata with broader skill systems in multi-agent frameworks. The finding that LLMs achieve only ~21% accuracy in recognizing their own injected errors underscores the fundamental need for external detection mechanisms.
