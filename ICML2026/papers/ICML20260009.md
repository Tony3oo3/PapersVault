## Problem

Standard PINN training minimizes an MSE loss that implicitly assumes PDE residuals follow a zero-mean Gaussian with fixed global variance. The paper shows both theoretically and empirically that residuals from nonlinear PDE operators are systematically heavy-tailed and heterogeneous, causing a small number of large residuals to dominate gradients and destabilize optimization. No prior work had formally modeled the residual distribution in PINNs or corrected this statistical mis-specification at the likelihood level.

## Input / Output

The method takes spatial-temporal collocation points (x, t) and a PDE differential operator as input to a neural surrogate u_θ, producing the PDE solution u(x, t) as output. Unusually, the Student-t loss is applied exclusively to interior PDE residuals, while boundary and initial conditions are enforced with standard MSE, reflecting the assumption that non-Gaussian errors arise from interior interfaces and multiscale structure rather than prescribed constraints. The EM procedure is unrolled into a finite sequence of alternating E- and M-steps per outer iteration, with the M-step for θ implemented as an inner loop of mini-batch Adam updates, making the method drop-in compatible with existing PINN solvers.

## Method

The paper recasts PINN training as likelihood-based inference over constraint residuals, showing that MSE corresponds to MLE under a Gaussian model, then replaces it with a Student-t residual likelihood that captures heavy-tailed behavior. The key mechanism is a Gaussian-Gamma hierarchical representation of the Student-t, which yields a closed-form E-step computing adaptive weights w_i = (ν+1)/(ν+λr_i²) that downweight large residuals, and an M-step that reduces to a weighted MSE objective for network parameters θ, a closed-form update for precision λ, and one or two Newton iterations for degrees of freedom ν. A bounded-score proposition guarantees that individual residuals contribute uniformly bounded terms to stochastic gradients (unlike MSE where contributions grow linearly with |r|), and a quadratic majorization theorem establishes that each EM iteration constructs a tight upper bound on the marginal objective, ensuring monotone descent. The approach uses only ~3,300 parameters in a 3-layer MLP yet achieves up to an order-of-magnitude reduction in relative ℓ₂ error on classical PINN failure modes such as Allen-Cahn and Wave equations.

## Results

On four established PINN failure-mode benchmarks (Wave, Reaction, Convection, Allen-Cahn), t-PINN achieves the best rRMSE and rMAE across all problems using only 3,297 parameters (vs. 0.29M–0.53M for baselines). Headline improvements over the second-best method are 76.5% on Wave (rRMSE 0.0019 vs. 0.0081), 64.1% on Reaction (0.0061 vs. 0.0170), 18.1% on Convection (0.0059 vs. 0.0072), and 82.2% on Allen-Cahn (0.0097 vs. 0.0545). Cross-architecture experiments (Table 2) confirm consistent gains: e.g., ~5.6× reduction in relative ℓ₂ error on Wave and ~7.8× on Allen-Cahn with a standard MLP backbone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

t-PINN outperforms all six baselines on every benchmark for both rRMSE and rMAE. The closest competitor is PINN-FP64 (double-precision training), which leads on Wave (0.0081 rRMSE) and Convection (0.0072 rRMSE) among baselines; t-PINN improves on these by 76.5% and 18.1% respectively. On Reaction, RoPINNs achieves the best baseline rRMSE (0.0170) and PINNMamba the best rMAE (0.0092); t-PINN beats both by 64.1% and 77.5%. On Allen-Cahn, PINN-FP64 is the strongest baseline (0.0545 rRMSE) and t-PINN reduces error by 82.2%. Architecturally, PINNsFormer and PINNMamba use 0.29M–0.45M parameters and still trail t-PINN (3,297 params) by 1–2 orders of magnitude on Allen-Cahn and Wave. The cross-architecture study (Table 2) further shows that t-PINN's gains persist across MLP, Modified MLP, and PirateNet backbones, confirming the improvement is attributable to the training procedure rather than model capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws a direct line from classical robust regression (Student-t likelihood, Gaussian-Gamma scale-mixture, EM/IRLS) to PINN training, a connection that prior PINN work had not formalized. While Daw et al. (2023) identified heavy-tailed residuals via kurtosis as a diagnostic to motivate adaptive sampling, and Wang et al. (2022a) proposed alternative L^p objectives, neither treated the residual distribution as a parametric model to be jointly inferred with network parameters. The paper's key departure is recasting the PINN loss as a likelihood over residuals and deriving a principled EM procedure from a Student-t hierarchical model, making the weighting mechanism a consequence of posterior inference rather than a heuristic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The probabilistic reframing (MSE = Gaussian MLE) gives practitioners a clear diagnostic for when standard PINN training is mis-specified, and the EM procedure is a drop-in replacement that preserves existing solver infrastructure. The bounded score function and convergence guarantee address a practical concern: heavy-tailed residuals can destabilize SGD, and the Student-t loss provably prevents this. Open questions include whether the approach extends to inverse problems and sparse-observation settings where PINNs may be most competitive, whether nonparametric or implicit residual distributions can replace the parametric Student-t assumption, and whether the initialization sensitivity (Table 6) can be mitigated with adaptive priors.
