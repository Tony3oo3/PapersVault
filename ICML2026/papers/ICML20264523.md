## Problem

Modern vision-language models are trained on a tiny, biased subset of the combinatorial space of visual concepts yet are expected to recognize familiar parts in novel, unseen combinations. While empirical work documents widespread compositional failures, no prior work has characterized what geometric structure representations must necessarily possess for compositional generalization to succeed. This paper asks: assuming a model does generalize compositionally, what are the non-negotiable, model-agnostic constraints on its embedding geometry?

## Input / Output

The framework takes RGB images (and, in the CLIP-style instantiation, text prompts) as input and produces a single embedding vector z ∈ R^d per image. A linear readout h(z) = Wz + b then yields k × n per-concept logits, from which argmax gives the predicted value for each of the k concepts. The analysis is scoped to linear readouts: if a non-linear head is used, all layers before the final affine layer are absorbed into the encoder, so the geometric constraints apply to the effective embedding on which the linear probe operates.

## Method

The paper formalizes three desiderata—divisibility (every concept combination has a nonempty decision region), transferability (a readout trained on any valid subset correctly classifies the full grid), and stability (per-concept posteriors are invariant across valid retrainings)—and proves that under gradient descent with cross-entropy loss, these conditions force the embedding to decompose as z_c = Σ_i u_{i,c_i} with cross-concept difference vectors (u_{i,1} − u_{i,0}) orthogonal to (u_{j,1} − u_{j,0}) for i ≠ j. The proof leverages the implicit bias of GD on separable data (convergence to the max-margin/SVM solution) together with stability to show every grid point is a support vector, making concept flips produce additive shifts along mutually orthogonal directions. A tight lower bound d ≥ k on embedding dimension is also established. Empirically, per-concept factors are recovered by averaging embeddings that share a concept value, and a whitened R² score on the probe span quantifies the degree of linear factorization; across CLIP, SigLIP, and DINO families on PUG-Animal, dSprites, MPI3D, and ImageNet-AO, the degree of factorization and cross-concept orthogonality positively correlates with compositional generalization accuracy on held-out combinations.

## Results

Across CLIP, SigLIP, and DINO model families evaluated on PUG-Animal, dSprites, MPI3D, and ImageNet-AO, pretrained encoders achieve whitened R² (linear factorization score) of 0.4–0.6, consistently above the random baseline of 0.12–0.42, indicating partial but imperfect additive per-concept decomposition. Cross-concept factor orthogonality is substantially stronger in pretrained models (pairwise cosine ≈ 0.09–0.12) than in a randomly initialized OpenCLIP ViT-L/14 (≈ 0.32), while within-concept direction similarity remains high (≈ 0.53–0.55). Critically, projected R² positively correlates with compositional generalization accuracy on held-out concept combinations across all model families and train fractions (|S|/N from 0.001 to 0.999), with the random baseline occupying the low-R²/low-accuracy corner.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method but instead surveys existing pretrained encoders against the necessary geometric conditions derived theoretically. All six model families (OpenAI CLIP, OpenCLIP, SigLIP/SigLIP2, MetaCLIP/MetaCLIP2, DINOv1–v3) exhibit partial linear factorization (R² ≈ 0.4–0.6) and partial cross-concept orthogonality (cosine ≈ 0.09–0.12), substantially outperforming the randomly initialized OpenCLIP ViT-L/14 baseline (R² near random, cross-concept cosine ≈ 0.32). The key comparative finding is that the degree of factorization (R²) predicts compositional generalization accuracy: models with higher R² consistently achieve higher accuracy on unseen concept combinations, and this correlation holds across all train fractions and datasets. Factor geometry is remarkably similar across model families (CLIP, OpenCLIP, SigLIP, SigLIP2), supporting the Platonic Representation Hypothesis. No single model family dominates; rather, the gap from perfect factorization (R² = 1.0) is what limits compositional performance, and this gap is comparable in scale across all pretrained models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant sufficient-conditions framing in compositional generalization (Wiedemer et al., 2023; Mahajan et al., 2025; Uselis et al., 2025) by asking the complementary necessary-conditions question: what must be true of embeddings *if* a model already generalizes compositionally? It builds on the Linear Representation Hypothesis (Mikolov et al., 2013; Park et al., 2023; Elhage et al., 2022) and empirical observations of linear subspaces in VLMs (Trager et al., 2023), but reframes these as *necessary consequences* of compositional generalization under standard GD+CE training rather than mere empirical regularities. The concurrent work of Fel et al. (2025) on Minkowski-sum geometry in DINOv2 provides complementary evidence of structured embedding spaces, while disentangled representation learning (Eastwood & Williams, 2018; Dittadi et al., 2022) addresses the related but distinct question of what training objectives should target.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete geometric target (linear factorization with cross-concept orthogonality, minimum dimension d ≥ k) that future model architectures and training objectives can aim for, and the R²-accuracy correlation offers a practical diagnostic for assessing compositional capability directly in representation space. The gap between current models' partial factorization (R² ≈ 0.4–0.6) and the ideal (R² = 1.0) may explain persistent failures on compositional benchmarks and predicts the geometry models may converge to at scale. Key limitations include the worst-case stability assumption (relaxing to average-case is open), the fixed-encoder assumption (in practice the encoder is trained once), and the binary-concept-value restriction in the main theorem, all of which constrain how directly the theory maps to real training pipelines.
