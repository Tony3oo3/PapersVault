## Problem

RL for multi-constraint instruction following suffers from a fundamental reward sparsity problem: weak models rarely generate responses satisfying all constraints simultaneously, yielding near-zero binary rewards, while aggregated constraint-level rewards introduce ambiguity where different responses receive identical scores despite violating different subsets of constraints. This paper asks how to convert failed attempts into informative training signals so that RL can learn efficiently from partial constraint satisfaction without requiring the model to already produce perfect responses.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text instruction decomposed into a task description and a set of atomic constraints (hard constraints verified by deterministic rules, soft constraints evaluated by an LLM judge) and produces a text response that should satisfy all constraints. Training uses the HIR-16K dataset of 16K queries, each paired with at least 5 decomposable constraints (76K hard and 47K soft constraints total). During training, m=6 responses are sampled per prompt, and k=2 failed responses are selected for hindsight replay, with the rewritten pseudo-instruction retaining only the subset of constraints the response actually satisfies.

## Method

HiR employs a select-then-rewrite replay strategy: from each sampling group, k failed responses are selected using a curriculum-based score that combines response entropy (diversity) and constraint integrity (fraction of satisfied constraints), with the integrity weight λ increasing exponentially over training steps to shift from exploration to exploitation. Selected responses are replayed under 'hindsight' pseudo-instructions constructed by removing unmet constraints, converting failures into successes with positive binary rewards. The model is then trained on both original and replayed samples using a PPO-style objective (Reinforce++ or PPO) with only binary reward signals. Theoretically, the training objective decomposes into dual-preference learning: a response-level term aligning winning vs. losing responses under the original instruction, and an instruction-level term that contrasts the same response under the hindsight pseudo-instruction versus the original instruction, implicitly teaching the model to identify which specific constraints are unmet.

## Results

HiR achieves the best instruction-level accuracy (ILA) across all seven instruction-following benchmarks for every model backbone tested. For Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, HiR reaches 86.3 on IFEval, 40.5 on IFBench, 73.2 on CFBench, 60.8 on InfoBench, 61.5 on ComplexBench, 80.6 on MulDimIF, and 80.4 on FollowBench—surpassing frontier models like DeepSeek-V3.1 (IFEval 86.1, IFBench 34.7, MulDimIF 68.3) on multiple benchmarks. For the weaker Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, HiR yields the largest absolute gains (e.g., +12.4 on IFEval, +49.1 on MulDimIF over the base model), demonstrating that the hindsight replay mechanism is most beneficial when the initial model struggles to produce fully constraint-satisfying responses.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HiR consistently outperforms all six baselines (SFT, DPO, RPO, RL-IR, RL-CR, and HeRL/TreeRPO) across all seven instruction-following benchmarks and all three model backbones. The largest margins are over RL-CR (constraint-level accuracy reward), the strongest RL baseline: HiR gains +4.5 on IFEval, +3.9 on IFBench, and +5.9 on MulDimIF for Qwen2.5-7B, and +0.5, +3.6, and +1.6 respectively for Qwen3-4B. DPO and RPO (preference-optimization baselines) are notably weaker and sometimes degrade performance (e.g., DPO drops Qwen2.5-7B IFEval from 72.6 to 66.9), while SFT shows inconsistent gains and even hurts CFBench/ComplexBench scores. Against the more recent HeRL and TreeRPO methods (Table 9), HiR still leads on all three reported benchmarks (IFEval 86.3 vs 86.1, IFBench 40.5 vs 38.8/37.8, MulDimIF 80.6 vs 80.2/79.8) while using fewer rollouts (m=6+k=2 vs m=8). Notably, Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 + HiR surpasses DeepSeek-V3.1 on IFEval (86.3 vs 86.1), IFBench (40.5 vs 34.7), InfoBench (60.8 vs 58.4), and MulDimIF (80.6 vs 68.3), demonstrating that a 4B model trained with HiR can match or exceed much larger frontier models on instruction following.

## Relation to Prior Work

HiR adapts the Hindsight Experience Replay (HER) paradigm from Andrychowicz et al. (2017) to the LLM instruction-following setting, where atomic constraints serve as 'hindsight goals' and the subset of satisfied constraints becomes the 'achieved state.' Within the broader RLVR framework for LLMs, it departs from prior approaches that either use sparse binary instruction-level rewards (RL-IR) or ambiguous aggregated constraint-level rewards (RL-CR) by converting failed responses into positive samples through constraint removal, thereby enabling learning with only a binary reward signal. The optimization builds on Reinforce++ (Hu et al., 2025), and the theoretical analysis reframes the training objective as dual-preference learning at both the response level (winning vs. losing) and the instruction level (hindsight pseudo-instruction vs. original instruction), a perspective not present in prior RL-for-LLM work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The sparse-reward bottleneck in RL for multi-constraint instruction following is a practical barrier that limits sample efficiency, particularly for smaller models; HiR's select-then-rewrite strategy directly addresses this by extracting learning signal from failures, enabling small LLMs (3B–7B) to approach frontier-model performance on several benchmarks with fewer rollouts. Key limitations include the assumption that constraints are independently decomposable (complex nested or dependent constraints may break the rewrite), reliance on LLM-as-a-judge for soft constraints, and the fact that the theoretically unbiased importance-weighted variant actually underperforms the simpler objective, suggesting the selection bias is functionally beneficial but not fully understood. Open directions include extending to constraint-dependency structures, multi-modal and agentic settings, and developing lighter-weight reward models to reduce evaluation cost.
