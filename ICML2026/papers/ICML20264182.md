## Problem

Current agentic benchmarks test explicit instruction-following where ground truth is fully specified in task descriptions, but real-world user communication is inherently underspecified—speakers rely on shared context and unstated constraints they expect listeners to infer. This paper identifies a distinct capability gap: the ability to identify, reason about, and satisfy implicit requirements spanning accessibility needs, privacy boundaries, catastrophic risks, and contextual constraints that no existing benchmark systematically measures.

## Input / Output

The Primary Agent receives a natural-language user prompt and entity descriptions (names, available actions, parameters) but is deliberately withheld execution rules and implicit requirements. It produces a turn-based action trajectory (entity, action, arguments, rationale) over up to 50 steps. The Evaluator Model (GPT-5.2-high) then maps the trajectory and final world state to binary pass/fail judgments per rubric criterion, aggregated into Scenario Pass Rate (all criteria met) and Normalized Scenario Score (fraction of criteria met per scenario). Rubrics reference concrete state variables (e.g., `backup_verified == true`) rather than open-ended quality assessments, making evaluation deterministic state verification.

## Method

The paper introduces two coupled contributions: the Implicit Intelligence framework (four categories: Implicit Reasoning, Catastrophic Risk, Privacy & Security, Accessibility) and Agent-as-a-World (AaW), a simulation paradigm where interactive worlds are defined in single human-readable YAML files and simulated by a fixed LLM (Claude Opus 4.5) acting as a deterministic rule executor with no interpretive discretion. Each YAML specifies entities with state and actions, hidden execution rules visible only to the World Model, and a binary evaluation rubric. Scenarios are constructed via a hybrid pipeline: an iterative Plan-Attempt-Refine loop synthetically generates and hardens scenarios until frontier models fail, followed by two-expert human validation enforcing logical consistency, shared expectations, and a difficulty gate (at least one model must fail ≤70% while another passes 100%). The key design choice is that the World Model's role is strictly constrained to executing pre-specified 'returns' fields, eliminating simulation bias and ensuring identical action sequences produce identical environments regardless of which agent generated them.

## Results

Across 205 scenarios and 16 models, the best-performing model (GPT-5.2-pro) achieves only 48.3% Scenario Pass Rate (SPR) and 72.7% Normalized Scenario Score (NSS), failing to satisfy all implicit requirements in over half of scenarios. The best open-weight model (DeepSeek V3p1) reaches 27.3% SPR, trailing the top frontier model by 21 percentage points. Category-level analysis shows Claude Opus 4.5 leads on Catastrophic Risk (50.0% SPR) while GPT-5.2-pro leads on Implicit Reasoning (51.4%) and Privacy & Security (47.8%), indicating no single model dominates all implicit-intelligence dimensions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper introduces a new benchmark rather than proposing a method to compare against prior baselines, so the key comparisons are across model families and within them. Frontier models substantially outperform open-weight models: the best open model (DeepSeek V3p1, 27.3% SPR) trails GPT-5.2-pro (48.3%) by 21 pp, and all open-weight models fall below 30% SPR. Within the GPT family, progression is notably non-monotonic—GPT-5 (44.9%) outperforms both its predecessor GPT-4.1 (18.5%) and its successors GPT-5.1 (20.5%) and GPT-5.2 (33.7%), suggesting implicit intelligence does not scale automatically with model iterations. Similarly, DeepSeek's reasoning-focused R1 (22.4%) underperforms their general-purpose V3p1 (27.3%). Category-level strengths are distributed: Claude Opus 4.5 leads Catastrophic Risk (50.0%), GPT-5.2-pro leads Implicit Reasoning (51.4%) and Privacy (47.8%), and GPT-5 leads Accessibility (48.5%), indicating no single model generalizes across all four implicit-requirement types.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant agent-benchmark paradigm (SWEbench, WebArena, GAIA, Tau-bench) where ground truth is fully specified in task descriptions, by introducing evaluation of unstated contextual requirements that agents must infer through environmental exploration. The Agent-as-a-World harness draws on the LLM-as-simulator line (SimuRA, Li et al. 2025b) and the declarative-specification tradition of TextWorld/ALFWorld, but repurposes both for evaluation rather than training, and shifts the evaluation target from stated-goal completion to implicit-requirement satisfaction. The authors explicitly distinguish their use of 'implicit' (unstated user expectations) from the latent-computation sense in the implicit-reasoning survey literature (Li et al. 2025a), and complement safety/alignment frameworks (MI9, AURA) by making alignment failures directly measurable in interactive scenarios.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 48.3% ceiling for the best model (GPT-5.2-pro) across 205 scenarios quantifies a substantial and previously unmeasured gap between explicit instruction-following and contextual reasoning, suggesting that current agent training objectives systematically underweight implicit requirement inference. The non-monotonic model progression (GPT-5 outperforming GPT-5.1 and GPT-5.2) challenges the assumption that capability improvements transfer uniformly across task types. Key open questions include whether findings generalize beyond the controlled iOS setting to real-device or web environments, whether the small entity lists (3–5) artificially cue hidden requirements, and how to evaluate the complementary failure of over-inference (agents taking excessive actions beyond task scope).
