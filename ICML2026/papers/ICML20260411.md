## Problem

Existing model routers treat routing as a closed-set classification task over a fixed list of models, which makes them unable to incorporate newly released models without full retraining and unable to expose the accuracy-cost trade-off to users. This paper asks whether routing can be reframed as a pre-hoc estimation problem—predicting how correct and how expensive each candidate model will be before execution—so that the system generalizes to unseen models and gives users a continuous control knob over the performance-cost frontier.

## Input / Output

Given a text query and a candidate model pool, SCOPE first retrieves the top-K most semantically similar anchor questions (from a fixed set of 250) via dense retrieval, then serializes each candidate model's historical correctness and token cost on those anchors into a structured prompt. The estimator (a 4B reasoning LLM) outputs, per candidate model, a chain-of-thought rationale, a binary correctness prediction, and a continuous token-cost estimate. The final routing decision is produced by maximizing a calibrated utility function parameterized by a user-specified trade-off coefficient α ∈ [0,1], where α=1 prioritizes accuracy and α=0 prioritizes cost; a budget constraint can also be solved for α automatically per workload.

## Method

SCOPE replaces model-name memorization with behavioral fingerprinting: each model is characterized by its ground-truth correctness and token usage on 250 anchor questions, and for a new query the top-K retrieved anchors form an in-context evidence slice that conditions the predictor. The predictor is a Qwen3-4B model trained in two stages—first, SFT via hindsight distillation (a teacher model generates concise rationales justifying known outcomes, compressing reasoning from ~2355 to ~239 tokens), then GRPO with a gated composite reward that combines a sparse correctness-matching signal and an adaptive plateau-with-decay token-cost reward. At decision time, a log-normalized utility function U = α·ŷ + (1−α)·(1−c̃)^γ_dyn is computed per candidate, and an anchor-based calibration prior (weighted average of actual anchor performance, scaled by a dynamic weight w_cal that increases with α) is blended with the real-time prediction to produce the final routing score. This separation of prediction from decision-making is what enables both training-free generalization to unseen models (only a single forward pass over the 250 anchors is needed to build a new fingerprint) and continuous user control over the accuracy-cost Pareto frontier.

## Results

On the Test Set (seen models), SCOPE (α=1.0) achieves 75.00% average accuracy and 84.33% Performance Gap Recovered (PGR) at a cost of $0.63, outperforming the strongest supervised baseline (Graph Router at 73.02% accuracy, $0.72) and xRouter (57.94%, $1.01). On the OOD Set (unseen models), SCOPE (α=1.0) reaches 50.80% accuracy and 59.32% PGR at $3.08, substantially exceeding all baselines (best: Graph Router at 42.00%, $1.37). In the cost-focused regime (α=0.0), SCOPE maintains 53.57% accuracy at just $0.03 on the Test Set, while on the OOD Set it achieves 39.60% at $0.75. The paper reports up to +25.7% accuracy improvement over the strongest individual model (Claude-Sonnet-4.5) and up to 95.1% cost reduction depending on the α setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SCOPE decisively outperforms all four baseline families on both the Test and OOD sets. Against the supervised baselines from RouterBench (MLP and kNN routers, Hu et al., 2024), SCOPE (α=1.0) gains +30.95 and +39.29 percentage points in accuracy on the Test Set (75.00% vs. 44.05% and 35.71%), while on the OOD Set the gap widens to +9.60 and +10.40 points (50.80% vs. 41.20% and 40.40%), with the supervised routers also incurring 3–4× higher costs ($11.52–$11.79 vs. $3.08). The SVM Router (Feng et al., 2025b) performs worst among learned baselines, achieving only 30.16% on the Test Set and 40.40% on OOD, far below SCOPE. Graph Router (Feng et al., 2025a) is the strongest supervised competitor at 73.02% on the Test Set, but SCOPE still edges it out by +1.98 points at a lower cost ($0.63 vs. $0.72); on the OOD Set the advantage grows to +8.80 points (50.80% vs. 42.00%). xRouter (Qian et al., 2025), an LLM-based RL router, reaches 57.94% on the Test Set at $1.01, which SCOPE surpasses by +17.06 points at 38% lower cost; on the OOD Set xRouter degrades to 30.57% (its fixed candidate pool cannot accommodate unseen models), while SCOPE maintains 50.80% without any retraining.

## Relation to Prior Work

SCOPE sits at the intersection of two routing paradigms: lightweight predictive routers (RTR, CARGO, OmniRouter) that expose continuous signals but require multi-stage pipelines, and LLM-based routers (xRouter, Router-R1) that internalize routing policies via RL but are locked to a fixed candidate set. The paper departs from both by reframing routing as a retrieval-augmented estimation problem: instead of memorizing model identities, it retrieves behavioral fingerprints from a small anchor set and uses a GRPO-trained reasoning model to predict correctness and token cost ex-ante. The training pipeline composes hindsight distillation (Liu et al., 2023) for concise CoT initialization with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) for outcome-level reward optimization, while the decision layer borrows the utility-maximization framing from multi-objective optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: as model pools grow and pricing varies by two orders of magnitude, a router that adapts to new models without retraining (38× compute reduction vs. baseline retraining) and exposes a continuous accuracy-cost knob is directly deployable. The OOD generalization result—where retrained baselines collapse to near-random but SCOPE maintains 50.8% accuracy on unseen frontier models—is the strongest evidence that behavioral fingerprinting captures transferable model characteristics. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-turn agentic routing (acknowledged as future work), how performance scales beyond 11 candidates, and whether the 4B predictor's ~0.5s latency remains acceptable in latency-critical pipelines. The OOD accuracy gap (50.8% vs. 75% in-distribution) also suggests the fingerprinting mechanism has limits under extreme distribution shift.
