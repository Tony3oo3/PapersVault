## Problem

Current AI and platform regulation targets discrete algorithmic components—recommender models, ranking systems, user profiling—on the assumption that fixing these in isolation will improve discovery diversity and cultural representation. The paper argues this algorithm-centric framing is structurally misaligned with how discovery actually emerges, which depends on interactions among models, interfaces, user behavior, economic incentives, and cultural norms, creating blind spots where formal compliance coexists with unchanged cultural outcomes.

## Input / Output

The CEDC framework consumes regulatory texts (25+ measures across 10 jurisdictions), platform curation system descriptions, user engagement data, and cultural production context as qualitative inputs. It produces a circuit-level diagnostic map that assigns each regulation to one or more of six components (marked as primary or secondary), identifies which components are systematically under-regulated, and surfaces component-level intervention targets. The output is not a computational artifact but a structured analytical table and narrative diagnosis, with the key finding that regulation concentrates on Facilitators and Interpreters while Engagements, Experiences, and Ripples remain largely unaddressed.

## Method

The core contribution is the Cultural Expressions Discovery Circuit (CEDC), a six-component framework—Engagements, Mediations, Facilitators, Interpreters, Experiences, and Ripples—derived through an interdisciplinary desk review of over 100 works spanning computer science, law, sociology, anthropology, economics, and media studies. Rather than modeling discovery as a linear pipeline, the CEDC explicitly encodes non-linear, cyclical interactions between components (e.g., Facilitators ↔ Ripples feedback loops, Interpreters ↔ Experiences mutual reinforcement), treating discovery as an emergent property of the full circuit. The framework is then applied as a diagnostic lens: each of 25+ regulatory measures is mapped onto the circuit to reveal structural gaps, and three specific failure modes of algorithm-centric regulation are identified—targeting only algorithmic components, formalizing cultural objectives through exclusively technical proxies, and applying modular engineering thinking to an inherently interconnected ecosystem. The paper concludes with concrete research and regulatory recommendations organized by circuit component, illustrated through a music streaming case study.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments; its primary empirical contribution is a qualitative mapping of 24 regulatory measures across six jurisdictions (EU, UK, US, CA, CN, JP, IN, SG, KR, BR) onto the six CEDC components. The key finding is a pronounced structural imbalance: regulation concentrates overwhelmingly on Facilitators (algorithmic curation/ranking) and Interpreters (user profiling/data practices), while Engagements, Experiences, and Ripples receive minimal or no direct coverage. For example, only 2 of 24 measures directly target Ripples (downstream cultural/economic effects), and none directly target Experiences (user trust, agency, and intent). The authors argue this component-level focus creates structural blind spots that prevent algorithm-centric regulation from achieving outcome-oriented goals like discovery diversity and cultural exposure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper and does not propose a new method to be benchmarked against baselines; there are no quantitative comparisons. The paper's analytical contribution is the CEDC framework itself, which is used as a diagnostic lens to evaluate existing regulatory instruments rather than to compare competing technical approaches. The closest analogue to a 'comparison' is the mapping in Table 1, which reveals that no single jurisdiction's regulatory portfolio covers all six CEDC components, and that the most commonly targeted components (Facilitators and Interpreters) are precisely those where the authors argue intervention is least effective for achieving discovery-diversity outcomes. The paper positions the CEDC against the dominant algorithm-centric regulatory paradigm (e.g., DSA transparency obligations, AI Act risk categorization) and argues for complementary circuit-level interventions such as Canada's Online Streaming Act funding mechanism, which targets Ripples and Engagements rather than algorithmic components.

## Relation to Prior Work

The CEDC is explicitly named after and structurally inspired by Du Gay et al.'s Culture Circuit, but repurposes it as a diagnostic governance tool for algorithmic content discovery rather than a general cultural-studies model. Conceptually, it synthesizes the socio-technical assemblage tradition (Ananny, Gillespie, Dolata et al.), the recommender-systems literature on popularity bias and exposure diversity, and the growing body of platform-regulation scholarship (DSA, DMA, AI Act) into a single six-component circuit. The paper departs from prior work by arguing that the fragmentation across ML, HCI, media studies, and policy has left a structural gap: no existing framework maps how discovery outcomes emerge from *interactions* among components, which is precisely what the CEDC formalizes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CEDC provides a shared vocabulary and diagnostic map that can bridge technical research (feedback-aware evaluation, multi-stakeholder optimization) and regulatory design (longitudinal monitoring, enabling instruments beyond compliance), and it has already been adopted within a European Commission study on cultural discoverability. Key open questions include how to operationalize 'discovery' as a stable, measurable objective across the ML lifecycle, how to design circuit-level regulatory instruments that are precise rather than omnibus, and whether the music-anchored framework transfers to other cultural domains (visual arts, literature, heritage). The paper's main limitation is its qualitative, literature-synthesis methodology: the circuit is a conceptual model without empirical validation of its component interactions or predictive power.
