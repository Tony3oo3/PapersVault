## Problem

Multi-Output Gaussian Processes (MOGPs) incur cubic computational cost in the number of outputs P, making them intractable for high-dimensional settings such as climate modelling (10,000+ spatial locations) and spatial transcriptomics (5,000 genes). Existing scalable alternatives impose restrictive structural assumptions—low-rank coregionalisation or sum-of-separable kernels—that limit the model's capacity to capture intricate inter-output dependencies. The paper asks whether one can scale MOGPs to massive output dimensions while retaining full expressiveness in the cross-output covariance structure.

## Input / Output

The model consumes input features x (e.g., time indices, spatial coordinates, or robot joint states) together with a per-output latent variable h_p of dimensionality D_H; the concatenated pair (x_n, h_p) is the actual input to the embedding network. Outputs are full predictive distributions per output dimension—predictive mean and variance under a Gaussian likelihood, or a mixture-of-Gaussians approximation under non-Gaussian likelihoods such as the Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial used for spatial transcriptomics counts. Mini-batching is performed jointly over inputs (N_b) and outputs (P_b), enabling training on datasets with over 21 million observations (4,352 spatial locations × 5,000 genes).

## Method

T-LVMOGP constructs a flexible multi-output deep kernel by mapping each (x_n, h_p) pair through a small residual-connected neural network (RCNN) with spectral normalisation on every weight matrix, producing an embedding x̃_{n,p} in a learned space where a stationary ARD-RBF base kernel defines all cross-output covariances. This reformulates the multi-output problem as a single scalar GP in the embedding space, allowing inducing points Z to be placed directly in that space and the standard SVGP variational machinery (with optional tighter bounds from Titsias 2025) to be reused without modification. Spectral normalisation with a per-layer upper bound (SN-UB) enforces Lipschitz continuity of the embedding map, preventing feature collapse and overconfident predictions while preserving enough expressiveness to model non-separable output correlations. Training proceeds via stochastic variational inference with mini-batches sampled over both inputs and outputs, and the outer expectation over the per-output latent variable q(h_p) is handled with the reparametrisation trick. The paper proves that LV-MOGP and GS-LVMOGP kernels are special cases of T-LVMOGP obtained by restricting Φ_θ to blockwise linear scalings, confirming the proposed kernel as a strict generalisation.

## Results

On the ERA5 climate dataset (3,395 outputs), T-LVMOGP achieves MSE 0.002±0.000 and NLL −1.564±0.024, outperforming the next-best baseline SV-LMC (MSE 0.012, NLL 0.758) by over 6× in MSE. On SARCOS (7 outputs, 48,933 inputs), T-LVMOGP reaches MSE 0.022±0.000 and NLL −0.485±0.009 with a training time of 5.26 s/epoch, the fastest among all compared MOGP baselines. On the Copernicus Marine output-extrapolation task (10,873 outputs), T-LVMOGP achieves MSE 0.029 and NLL −0.439, substantially outperforming GS-LVMOGP (best Q=3: MSE 0.035, NLL 4.975). On the zero-inflated spatial transcriptomics dataset (5,000 genes × 4,352 spots), T-LVMOGP attains MSE 9.189, beating GS-LVMOGP (best Q=3: MSE 11.024) while matching its NLL of 0.674.

## Baselines & Comparisons

T-LVMOGP consistently outperforms all six baselines on MSE across every dataset. On ERA5, it reduces MSE from 0.012 (SV-LMC) and 0.014 (GS-LVMOGP) to 0.002, a 6–7× improvement, while also achieving the best NLL (−1.564 vs. −0.699 for GS-LVMOGP). On SARCOS, T-LVMOGP matches G-MOGP's MSE (0.022 vs. 0.023) but trains 11% faster (5.26 vs. 5.89 s/epoch) and achieves a slightly better NLL (−0.485 vs. −0.483). SV-LMC is the fastest baseline on ERA5 (0.570 s/epoch) but is outperformed by T-LVMOGP on both accuracy metrics by a wide margin. On the Copernicus Marine output-extrapolation task, T-LVMOGP beats GS-LVMOGP (Q=3) by 17% in MSE (0.029 vs. 0.035) and by a factor of ~11 in NLL (−0.439 vs. 4.975), while also training 41% faster. On spatial transcriptomics with the ZINB likelihood, T-LVMOGP improves MSE by 17% over GS-LVMOGP (Q=3) while matching its NLL, at a comparable training time (73.0 vs. 78.2 s/epoch).

## Relation to Prior Work

T-LVMOGP unifies and generalises the LV-MOGP line of work (Dai et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2025) by replacing their separable or sum-of-separable kernel structures with a Lipschitz-regularised neural network that maps (input, output-latent) pairs into a shared embedding space, where a standard base kernel defines all cross-output covariances. The paper formally shows in Appendix D that both LV-MOGP (Q=1) and GS-LVMOGP (Q>1) are special cases recoverable by restricting the embedding to blockwise linear scaling, positioning T-LVMOGP as a strict generalisation. The inference machinery is inherited from SVGP (Titsias, 2009; Hensman et al., 2013) with inducing points placed in the learned embedding space, and the Lipschitz regularisation via residual connections and spectral normalisation (Miyato et al., 2018; Van Amersfoort et al., 2021) addresses the overfitting and feature-collapse pathologies identified in deep kernel learning (Ober et al., 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine scalability bottleneck: standard MOGPs incur cubic cost in the number of outputs P, making them intractable for climate modelling (10,000+ spatial locations) and spatial transcriptomics (5,000 genes). By reformulating the multi-output problem as a single-output GP in a learned embedding space, T-LVMOGP inherits all existing single-output GP scalability tools (SVGP, tighter bounds, KISS-GP) without structural kernel constraints. Key open questions include the factorised variational posterior over latent variables, which limits capture of posterior output couplings, and whether structured or amortised variational distributions could close this gap. The paper also notes that tighter variational bounds (Titsias, 2025; Bui et al., 2025) integrate naturally but their benefit is less pronounced for non-Gaussian likelihoods.
