## Problem

Prior theoretical work on OOD generalization has derived only upper bounds on target risk under various distributional assumptions, leaving the fundamental minimax rate of the target excess risk completely uncharacterized. This paper closes that gap by establishing the first lower bound for OOD generalization error and showing that, under mild conditions, the simple ERM algorithm achieves a nearly optimal rate without requiring knowledge of distributional parameters.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes n_i labeled samples from each of K source distributions P_i (with no access to any target data) and outputs a binary classifier from a hypothesis class H of VC dimension d. Crucially, the target distribution Q is entirely unknown at training time, and the algorithm does not require explicit knowledge of the noise parameters β_i, transfer exponents ρ_i, or irreducible gaps α_i that govern the theoretical bounds. The analysis is distribution-agnostic over a general input space X with binary labels Y = {−1, +1} under 0-1 loss.

## Method

The lower bound is constructed via an extension of Fano's inequality applied to a family of distribution tuples indexed by binary strings over a shattered set of size d, with a Varshamov-Gilbert packing ensuring sufficient Hamming separation; this yields a minimax rate of Ω(min_i {(d/n_i)^(1/((2−β_i)ρ_i)) + α_i}). For the upper bound, the key insight is that under the RCS assumption (a common optimal hypothesis exists across all source and target domains) and its empirical counterpart, simply minimizing the average empirical risk over all K source domains (Algorithm 5) matches the lower bound up to logarithmic factors, without needing to identify which source domain is best. The analysis characterizes noise via the Bernstein Class Condition and source-to-target transfer via the transfer exponent, both of which are distribution-dependent but need not be estimated in practice. This framework yields a no-free-lunch theorem for OOD generalization and provides a conditional theoretical explanation for why ERM is competitive with purpose-built OOD algorithms in DomainBed.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments. The main quantitative result is a matching pair of minimax bounds: the lower bound (Theorem 4.2) shows that for any algorithm, the worst-case target excess risk is Ω(min_i{(d/n_i)^(1/((2−β_i)ρ_i)) + α_i}), while the upper bound (Theorem 5.12) shows that under the RCS and empirical-RCS conditions (with α_i = 0), simple ERM (minimizing average source empirical risk) achieves Õ(min_i{(d/n_i)^(1/((2−β_i)ρ_i))}). This establishes that ERM is nearly optimal up to a logarithmic factor in this regime, providing a theoretical explanation for the DomainBed observation that ERM is competitive with purpose-built OOD generalization algorithms.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not conduct empirical comparisons against baseline algorithms; all results are theoretical. Instead, it compares the theoretical guarantees of five algorithmic strategies: (1) the Oracle algorithm (Algorithm 1), which knows all distributional parameters and achieves the optimal rate Õ(s_K); (2) AD-ERM (Algorithm 2), which picks an arbitrary domain and only achieves a suboptimal max{·} bound; (3) R-ERM (Algorithm 3), which randomizes over domains and improves to an average bound; (4) C-ERM (Algorithm 4), which requires knowing i* = argmin ρ_i and achieves a nearly optimal min{·} rate; and (5) the final ERM algorithm (Algorithm 5), which requires no distributional parameters and achieves the same nearly optimal min{·} rate under RCS/empirical-RCS conditions. The key theoretical 'comparison' is between the lower bound (Theorem 4.2) and the ERM upper bound (Theorem 5.12), which together prove near-optimality. The paper also positions its results relative to the DomainBed empirical finding (Gulrajani & Lopez-Paz, 2021) that ERM matches or outperforms specialized OOD methods, offering a conditional theoretical justification for this phenomenon.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the transfer-exponent framework and RCS assumption from Hanneke & Kpotufe (2019) and Hanneke et al. (2023) from single-source transfer learning to the multi-source OOD generalization setting, where no target data is available. While prior OOD generalization theory (IRM, DRO, domain-adversarial methods) focused exclusively on deriving upper bounds for target risk under various distributional shift assumptions, this work is the first to establish matching lower bounds via a minimax/Fano's inequality argument, placing OOD generalization on equal footing with classical PAC learning theory. The paper also departs from the common practice of designing algorithms to minimize specific discrepancy measures (MMD, H-divergence, CORAL) by showing that under RCS conditions, no such optimization is needed—simple ERM suffices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-optimality of ERM under RCS/empirical-RCS conditions provides the first rigorous theoretical explanation for the widely observed DomainBed phenomenon where carefully designed OOD algorithms fail to beat simple ERM, reframing the question from 'how to design better OOD algorithms' to 'when do the conditions for ERM optimality hold?' The no-free-lunch theorem establishes a fundamental impossibility: without structural assumptions on the target distribution, no algorithm can guarantee good OOD generalization. Key limitations include the restrictive RCS assumption (a single hypothesis must be simultaneously optimal across all source and target domains), the binary classification and VC-dimension framework, and the conditional nature of the upper bound (requiring both population-level RCS and empirical RCS). Open questions include whether a hierarchical Bayesian relaxation of the single-optimum assumption is possible, whether results extend to multi-class or regression settings, and how to practically verify the RCS conditions.
