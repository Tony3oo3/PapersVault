## Problem

The ML community commonly treats ground truth datasets as neutral, objective measurements naturally given by the world, obscuring the contingent decisions, human labor, and technological choices that shape them. This framing leads to inappropriate 'off-label' use of datasets, overestimated model reliability, and a lack of transparency about where and when models can be safely deployed. The paper argues that acknowledging ground truths as human constructions is necessary for improving reliability, accountability, and interdisciplinary collaboration.

## Input / Output

The paper examines ground truth datasets across multiple modalities and domains—handwritten digits (MNIST), natural images (ImageNet), medical imaging (MRI, brain segmentation, lung scans), planetary sensor data (Mars terrain, impact craters), text (NLP sentiment), and synthetic data—as case studies of construction choices and their consequences. It produces a conceptual framework organized around four 'construction sites' (direct measurement, expert interpretation, crowd consensus, synthetic data) along with a set of concrete recommendations for documentation, evaluation, and ML education practices.

## Method

Rather than proposing a new algorithm, the paper develops an analytical framework that unboxes the 'black box' of ground truth construction by identifying the specific decisions at each stage: what sources are legitimate, how observations are interpreted, what values are recorded, and who validates attributions. It introduces the concept of 'situated reliability' to replace the assumption of universal ground truth validity, arguing that reliability is necessarily bounded by the context of data collection, expert judgment, and sensor range. The paper distinguishes between 'making sense' (implying a pre-existing truth to be captured) and 'constructing sense' (acknowledging the joint, communicative work of ML and domain experts), and calls for formulating multiple ground truths for the same problem to expose the impact of construction choices. It draws on STS, CSCW, and critical data studies to ground its arguments, and proposes concrete actions including a standard vocabulary for ground truth limitations, augmented dataset documentation (extending Datasheets for Datasets), sensitivity analyses à la Recht et al., and curriculum changes in ML education programs.

## Results

This is a position paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative results tables. The closest quantitative illustrations are cited from prior work: a 47% performance drop when an MRI model trained on undocumented open-access ground truths was applied to newly collected data (Shimron et al., 2022), and a Mars terrain classifier achieving 74% accuracy on 14 classes versus 92% on 5 coarser classes (Barrett et al., 2022), demonstrating how ground truth granularity choices directly alter reported accuracy. The paper's contribution is conceptual: proposing 'situated reliability' as a framework and calling for standardized documentation of ground truth construction choices.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a method to be benchmarked against baselines, so no direct performance comparisons are made. Instead, it contrasts two conceptual framings of ground truth: (1) the prevailing view of ground truths as neutral, objective, pre-existing measurements, versus (2) the authors' view of ground truths as contingent, situated human constructions. The paper draws on illustrative case studies (MNIST resolution choice, ImageNet crowd-sourcing, mushroom dataset label collapse, Mars crater cataloging) to argue that the 'objective truth' framing obscures consequential design decisions. It positions its recommendations (multiple ground truths, standard vocabulary for limitations, situated reliability) against the current practice of treating a single dataset as universally valid, citing Recht et al. (2019) as evidence that sampling strategy alone can shift ImageNet classifier accuracies substantially.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes a dispersed body of work from Science and Technology Studies (Jaton's ground-truthing analyses, Gitelman's 'raw data is an oxymoron'), Computer-Supported Collaborative Work (Suchman, Collins, Goodwin's professional vision), and recent ML-critical studies (Kang's Ground Truth Tracings, Henriksen & Bechmann's truth practices in healthcare AI, Muller et al.'s social life of labels, Plank's human label variation) into a unified argument that ground truths are contingent human constructions. It departs from the dominant ML framing that treats reference datasets as neutral, pre-existing measurements, and builds on Gebru et al.'s Datasheets for Datasets and Recht et al.'s ImageNet sensitivity analysis as partial precedents for documenting construction choices. The paper does not introduce a new empirical method but repurposes ethnographic and STS concepts (situatedness, professional vision, truth practices) to reframe a core ML assumption.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central contribution is the concept of 'situated reliability'—the idea that a ground truth's validity is bounded by its construction context—and a concrete call for standard vocabulary, multiple ground truths per task, and expanded documentation practices. Open questions remain: how to operationalize sensitivity analyses across the field without prohibitive cost, whether the ML community will adopt the proposed checklists and review criteria, and how to balance the tension between acknowledging contingency and maintaining practical benchmarks. The paper's limitations are inherent to its position-paper format: it offers no empirical validation of whether the proposed practices actually improve model reliability or reduce misuse, and the cost of constructing multiple ground truths may be prohibitive for many research groups.
