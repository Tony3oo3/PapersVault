## Problem

The community lacks a unified, IR-level benchmark that tests whether LLMs can not only understand compiler intermediate representations but also rewrite them under compiler-grade semantic constraints with measurable performance impact. Existing IR-level evaluations are highly heterogeneous—each work designs its own tasks, metrics, and correctness assumptions—making side-by-side comparison of models on compiler IR nearly impossible and obscuring where models are reliably helpful versus risky.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265363/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

All four tracks consume LLVM IR (function-level or module-level) wrapped in explicit <IR> tags, with track-specific auxiliary context: a natural-language analysis question (Analysis), a verifier error message or none (Repair), a named optimization pass or a before/after IR pair (Refactor), or a target-architecture note (Transform). Outputs are structured as JSON for Analysis (wrapped in <CIR_JSON>), complete IR fragments for Repair/Refactor/Transform (wrapped in <IR_OUT>), or a single pass-name label for Refactor-Reverse. The layered evaluation pipeline then applies the LLVM verifier as a syntactic gate, Alive2 for formal equivalence on function-level candidates, checksum-based harness execution as a fallback for module-level or inconclusive cases, and finally llvm-mca static cost plus real-execution timing against the -O3 baseline.

## Method

CIRBench organizes 800 curated LLVM IR instances into four compiler-oriented tracks—Analysis (100), Repair (200), Refactor (200), and Transform (300)—each stratified into Easy/Medium/Hard by instruction count, basic-block count, and control/data-flow complexity. The central technical contribution is a layered, correctness-aware evaluation protocol that mirrors production compiler practice: candidates must first pass the LLVM verifier (Valid@k), then survive semantic equivalence checking via Alive2 when it returns a definite verdict or via deterministic checksum-based harness tests otherwise (Equiv@k), with performance measured only for equivalence-clean candidates. For Transform, two evaluation modes are provided: Direct (measuring the model's IR as-is) and Copilot (running the model's output through the standard -O3 pipeline), capturing both standalone and cooperative LLM-compiler integration. The benchmark is evaluated on six mainstream LLMs (GPT-5, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Grok 4 Fast, Qwen3-Max, DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp) using fixed prompt templates, nucleus sampling with k∈{1,5} candidates, and 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, revealing that while models can solve subsets of Analysis and Repair, their median Transform speedup remains below the -O3 baseline despite occasional up-to-4.96× individual wins.

## Results

On the Analysis track, canonicalized EM spans 41% (DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp) to 70% (GPT-5), with F1 from 66.4% to 84.0%. On Repair-Hint, GPT-5 achieves the highest Equiv@1 of 85.5% and Equiv@5 of 92.0%, while on Refactor-Normal all models score near-zero EM@1 and Equiv@1 remains below 25%. On Transform, every model's median dynamic speedup in Direct mode falls below the -O3 baseline (range 0.66×–0.96×), yet individual kernels reach up to 4.96× speedup (DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp); in Copilot mode, medians shift toward or slightly above parity (0.92×–1.23×). GPT-5 stands out on Transform with Equiv@5 of 92.7%, exceeding the next-best model by 15–50 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CIRBench is a benchmark paper, so the primary comparison is across six mainstream LLMs (GPT-5, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Grok 4 Fast, Qwen3-Max-Preview, DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp) and against the LLVM -O3 compiler as the performance baseline on Transform. GPT-5 leads on most correctness axes: it achieves the highest Repair-Hint Equiv@1 (85.5%), the highest Transform Equiv@5 (92.7%, a 15–50 pp margin over the next model), and the best Analysis EM (70%). Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Grok 4 Fast form a second tier, strong on Repair but weaker on Refactor and Transform equivalence. Qwen3-Max-Preview and DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp show the sharpest drop from understanding (Analysis/Repair) to rewriting (Refactor/Transform). Against the -O3 compiler baseline, all six models underperform on median dynamic speedup in Direct mode (0.66×–0.96×), meaning more than half of equivalence-clean candidates are slower than the compiler; in Copilot mode (where -O3 post-processes model output), medians recover to 0.92×–1.23×, with Gemini 2.5 Flash and DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp slightly exceeding parity. The persistent Valid–Equiv gap across all tracks (e.g., GPT-5 Transform Valid@1 = 80.6% vs. Equiv@1 = 73.3%) confirms that passing the LLVM verifier is far from sufficient for semantic correctness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by unifying four previously fragmented IR-level LLM tasks (analysis, repair, refactoring, and transformation) into a single benchmark with a layered correctness protocol (LLVM verifier → Alive2 equivalence → performance measurement). Prior IR-level work such as IR-Optset, LLM-Vectorizer, and various prompting studies each cover only a subset of these tracks with bespoke metrics, while compiler-ML work (MLGO, Souper, equality saturation) focuses on learning policies or local rewrites without evaluating general-purpose LLMs. CIRBench repurposes existing tools (Alive2, llvm-mca, LLVM's built-in analyses) into a standardized evaluation harness, positioning itself as the missing shared yardstick that Table 1 makes explicit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides the first reproducible, layered-correctness protocol for LLMs at the compiler IR boundary, which is critical because 'passes the LLVM verifier' is demonstrably far from semantic safety (the persistent Valid-Equiv gap). The finding that median dynamic speedup is below the -O3 baseline (0.85×) despite a 4.96× maximum tempers the field's optimism about LLMs as standalone optimizers and reframes them as speculative rewrite generators requiring strong filters. Open questions include whether graph- or alias-aware representations can close the fine-grained reasoning gap, how to achieve pass-level controllability, and whether cooperative (Copilot) settings can reliably convert the positive tail into consistent gains.
