## Problem

Empirical data mixing scaling laws can predict domain losses under different mixture weights, but they function as black boxes with no theoretical grounding: it is unclear whether they generalize to unseen scales, how many parameters are truly needed, or why domain losses interact in the observed non-trivial ways. This paper provides a unified theoretical framework that explains the mechanics driving these interactions from first principles.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a K-dimensional mixture weight vector h on the simplex, model size N, training token budget D, and target importance weights w, and produces per-domain loss predictions L_i(h|N,D) plus the optimal mixture h*. Loss prediction for any given mixture is obtained by solving a convex capacity-allocation program (Eq. 1), while the optimal mixture is found via a bi-level optimization solved with Online Mirror Descent using a closed-form gradient. The full model requires only 3K or 5K free parameters (c_i, b_i, E_i, and optionally A_i, a_i), substantially fewer than empirical baselines that need K(2K+1) or K(K+2) parameters.

## Method

The framework extends the single-domain Quantization Model and Projected Linear Regression Model to K domains under a 'Shared Head, Disjoint Tail' spectral assumption: domains share high-frequency fundamental skills but have orthogonal, domain-specific power-law tails. Two mechanisms govern domain interaction: (1) Capacity Competition, where a finite model capacity N is allocated across domains via a convex resource-constraint problem, coupling all domain losses through a shared Lagrange multiplier; and (2) Noise Reduction, where one-pass SGD introduces a data-dependent noise term A_i(Dh_i)^{-a_i} that depends only on the domain's own weight, breaking the symmetry that would otherwise make the optimal mixture equal the target distribution. The resulting loss formula (Eq. 3) combines the capacity-allocation term with the noise term, and the optimal mixture is found by solving the bi-level problem via Online Mirror Descent with an analytically derived gradient (Proposition 3.2). Empirically, the model achieves lower MRE than all empirical baselines, identifies superior training mixtures, and successfully extrapolates to unseen scales using parameters fitted exclusively on small-scale proxies.

## Results

On 64 1B-parameter models trained on K=17 Pile domains, the Extended Linear Regression Model (Eq. 3) achieves the lowest fitting error with MRE 1.533% and MAE 0.034, using only 5K free parameters. The simpler Extended Quantization Model (Eq. 1) achieves MRE 2.064% and MAE 0.051 with just 3K parameters. For optimal mixture prediction, the paper's method consistently yields the lowest test perplexity across 200M (4-domain), 122M (7-domain), and 310M (7-domain) settings. In cross-scale extrapolation, the model fitted exclusively on 122M/10B proxy losses matches the state-of-the-art Additive Law (fitted on data spanning 412M–1.4B parameters and 4B–46B tokens, including the target 1B/30B scale) at the unseen 1B/30B target.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's Extended Linear Regression Model (Eq. 3) outperforms all four empirical baselines on fitting accuracy: it achieves MRE 1.533% vs. Additive (2.209%), Exponential (6.990%), BiMix (2.963%), and RegMix (6.480%), while using fewer free parameters (5K) than Additive (K(2K+1)) and RegMix (K²). On optimal mixture prediction (Figure 2), the paper's method consistently achieves the lowest test perplexity across all three model scales (200M, 122M, 310M), beating Additive, Exponential, BiMix, and RegMix. In the 4-domain extrapolation (200M/8B → 700M/16B), the paper's extrapolated mixture strictly outperforms both the static small-scale mixture and the Additive Law's prediction. In the 7-domain extrapolation (122M/10B → 1B/30B), the paper's method—fitted only on small-scale proxy losses—matches the final test loss of the Additive Law, which was fitted on a much wider range of scales explicitly including the target 1B/30B configuration. The simpler Extended Quantization Model (Eq. 1) also beats all baselines on MRE (2.064%) with only 3K parameters, though it cannot predict non-trivial optimal mixtures due to its symmetry limitation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends two single-domain theoretical frameworks for neural scaling laws—the Quantization Model (Michaud et al., 2023) and the Projected Linear Regression Model (Lin et al., 2024a; Bordelon et al., 2024)—to the multi-domain data mixing setting, introducing a 'Shared Head, Disjoint Tail' structural assumption to bridge the gap. It positions itself as a theoretical explanation for the empirical data mixing laws proposed by Shukor et al. (2026), Ye et al. (2025), Ge et al. (2025), and Liu et al. (2025a), identifying two specific mechanisms (Capacity Competition and Noise Reduction) that explain the non-trivial domain interactions those empirical forms capture without justification. The bi-level optimization formulation and Online Mirror Descent solver for finding optimal mixtures build on the convex optimization structure inherited from the single-domain capacity allocation problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical framework that explains *why* data mixing scaling laws take their observed functional forms, moving the field from empirical curve-fitting toward principled prediction. The cross-scale extrapolation result—matching a state-of-the-art empirical baseline (fitted on data including the target scale) using only small-scale proxy losses—is practically significant for reducing the cost of finding optimal pretraining mixtures. Key limitations include the disjoint-tail idealization (though stress-tested up to 40% overlap), the reliance on non-convex fitting procedures, and the focus on loss prediction rather than downstream task performance. Open questions include extending the framework to unseen domains, explicitly modeling domain overlap, and developing convex or analytical fitting methods.
