## Problem

As Transformer context windows expand from 100K to 10M tokens, it remains unclear how much additional precision is gained by feeding more tokens into an already-trained model. This paper introduces token sample complexity — the rate at which attention computed on n tokens converges to its infinite-token (mean-field) limit — and provides the first quantitative, non-asymptotic convergence bounds for this regime, revealing that convergence is sub-parametric (slower than 1/√n) and governed by an attention horizon parameter.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a fixed sequence of n i.i.d. token embeddings drawn from a centered sub-Gaussian distribution ν with covariance matrix Σ, together with fixed attention parameters Q, K, V (so A = KᵀQ). It produces high-probability upper bounds on the uniform error of the attention map over Mahalanobis balls and on moment errors of the push-forward distribution, with the rate exponent β = 1/(2(1+32H²)) determined by the attention horizon H = ‖Σ^{1/2}AΣ^{1/2}‖₂. In the hardmax limit (H→∞), the bound tightens to an exact logarithmic rate σ/√(ln n) derived from Gaussian extreme value theory.

## Method

The paper formalizes self-attention as a continuous operator on probability measures and then bounds the gap between the finite-n empirical attention map and its mean-field limit using empirical process tools. The key technical challenge is that the softmax kernel e^{⟨Ax,y⟩} is not uniformly Lipschitz, so standard uniform CLTs do not apply; instead, the authors use symmetrization, Rademacher complexity, and Dudley's entropy integral with a duality argument to handle the vector-valued process. For moment convergence, they split the error into a compact-region term (growing like exp(cR²) via uniform convergence) and a tail term (decaying like exp(−cR²) via sub-Gaussian concentration), then optimize the radius R ~ √(ln n) to balance them, yielding the sub-parametric rate n^{−β} with β < 1/2. The attention horizon H quantifies the interaction between token anisotropy (Σ) and attention geometry (A), and empirically governs the observed slowdown across BigBird and BERT layers on real text.

## Results

The paper establishes that attention convergence is sub-parametric: the moment convergence rate is O(n^{-β}) with β = 1/(2(1+32H²)) < 1/2, where H = ‖Σ^{1/2}AΣ^{1/2}‖₂ is the attention horizon. In the hardmax regime (H→∞), convergence slows further to σ/√(ln n). On the arxiv-classification task, replacing dense attention with n i.i.d. sampled keys yields sub-parametric downstream convergence (exponents β = 0.27–0.46 across RMSE, pooling, logit, and disagreement metrics); using only 25% of keys (n=1101) recovers 84.5% accuracy versus 86.4% dense, and doubling keys to 2124 adds only +0.7%. Gaussian synthetic experiments confirm strong agreement between empirical rates and the theoretical prediction β_th = 1/(2(1+H²)) across a wide range of horizons.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without direct method-vs-method baselines; the primary comparison is between theoretical convergence predictions and empirical observations. On Gaussian synthetic data, the fitted empirical exponents closely match the theoretical prediction β_th = 1/(2(1+H²)) across horizons, validating the bound's tightness in this setting. On real BigBird and BERT models across seven text distributions (Wikipedia EN/DE/ZH, CC-News), convergence rates are consistently sub-parametric (β < 0.5) and deteriorate systematically as the attention horizon H increases, confirming the theory's qualitative predictions. Window-based (non-i.i.d.) key sampling also exhibits the same horizon-dependent slowdown, indicating the result is not an artifact of i.i.d. subsampling. The downstream classification experiment shows that high-horizon query groups converge more slowly in both error and accuracy recovery, demonstrating that the attention-level slowdown propagates through the full network. The paper also contrasts its sub-Gaussian analysis with the compact-support setting, showing that the latter yields parametric 1/√n rates only with constants exponential in the support radius, making them impractical for large supports.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the mean-field formulation of attention as an operator on probability measures, initiated by De Bie et al. (2019), Pevny & Kovarik (2019), and Sander et al. (2022), and extended by Geshkovski et al. (2023, 2025) who proved the Transformer PDE is the n→∞ limit of an interacting particle system. However, prior work established only the existence of the limit, not the rate at which finite-n attention approaches it. The paper departs from this line by introducing 'token sample complexity'—a distinct notion from classical PAC sample complexity—and applying empirical process tools (Dudley's entropy integral, Rademacher complexity, symmetrization) to derive the first non-asymptotic, parameter-dependent convergence rates. An independent concurrent work by Boursier & Boyer (2025) also studies token sample complexity but focuses on the Jacobian of the attention map under Gaussian inputs, whereas this paper handles the self-attention setting (shared query/key/value tokens) and derives sub-parametric rates governed by the attention horizon H.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As context windows expand toward 10M tokens, the paper provides a quantitative answer to the practical question of how much benefit additional tokens actually provide: convergence is sub-parametric (n^{-β}, β < 1/2), meaning diminishing returns set in much earlier than the classical 1/√n rate would suggest. The attention horizon H = ‖Σ^{1/2}AΣ^{1/2}‖₂ gives a concrete, computable diagnostic for when sparse attention suffices, and the end-to-end experiment showing that 25% of keys recovers most dense-model accuracy offers a direct inference-time guideline. Key open questions remain: the tightness of the upper bound in the Gaussian regime (experiments suggest it is tight but this is unproven), the extension to non-i.i.d. structured sampling (e.g., windowed attention), and identifying distribution classes with provably tight rates. The sub-Gaussian assumption is also necessary—Appendix C.9 shows heavy-tailed distributions can prevent convergence entirely.
