## Problem

Existing distribution closeness testing (DCT) methods rely on total variation over discrete finite-support distributions, limiting their use to complex data such as images. While MMD is a natural kernel-based extension to continuous domains, the paper shows that distribution pairs with identical MMD values can exhibit different finite-sample distinguishability depending on their RKHS norms, making MMD alone an uninformative closeness measure for DCT.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two i.i.d. samples of size m drawn from unknown distributions P and Q, a bounded shift-invariant kernel κ(x,x')=Ψ(x−x') with Ψ(0)=K, and a closeness parameter ε typically estimated from a reference distribution pair whose distance is deemed acceptable. It outputs a binary reject/fail-to-reject decision at significance level α, the empirical NAMMD discrepancy value, and p-values. The kernel must be fixed across all distribution pairs being compared to ensure a unified closeness measurement, and ε is a plug-in tolerance rather than a free hyperparameter.

## Method

The core contribution is Norm-Adaptive MMD (NAMMD), which rescales MMD² by the RKHS norms of the two distributions: NAMMD = MMD² / (4K − ‖μ_P‖² − ‖μ_Q‖²), yielding a value in [0,1] that is larger for pairs with greater RKHS norms even at fixed MMD. The test threshold is derived analytically from the asymptotic Gaussian distribution of the NAMMD estimator under the least-favorable boundary case NAMMD=ε, with a plug-in variance estimator built from U-statistic components (ζ₁, ζ₂) following Sutherland (2019). The paper proves asymptotic type-I error control, a sample-complexity bound of O(1/(NAMMD−ε)²), and a conditional power theorem showing that NAMMD-based DCT rejects whenever MMD-based DCT rejects (under a norm condition on the reference pair) and additionally rejects in cases where MMD-based DCT fails. The approach is compatible with multiple-kernel fusion and deep kernels, and extends to unequal sample sizes without modification to the denominator.

## Results

On the primary DCT comparison (Table 2), NAMMD-based DCT achieves higher average test power than MMD-based DCT across all four tolerance levels: 0.976 vs 0.973 (ε=0.1), 0.955 vs 0.928 (ε=0.3), 0.951 vs 0.924 (ε=0.5), and 0.984 vs 0.970 (ε=0.7), with the largest gap (≈2.7 pp) at ε=0.3. Against Canonne's total-variation-based DCT on discrete distributions (Table 1), NAMMD achieves 0.934 vs 0.800 average test power at ε'=0.1 and 0.976 vs 0.931 at ε'=0.5. In practical ImageNet variant experiments, NAMMD-based DCT correctly ranks distributional closeness consistent with accuracy-gap ordering and outperforms MMD-based DCT in test power (Figure 3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MMD-based DCT (Gretton et al., 2012), NAMMD consistently achieves higher test power on all five datasets and all four ε values in Table 2, with the most pronounced gains at intermediate tolerances (ε=0.3: +2.7 pp average; ε=0.5: +2.7 pp average). Against Canonne's total-variation DCT (COLT 2022) on discrete distributions, NAMMD wins on 4 of 5 datasets at ε'=0.1 (e.g., 0.934 vs 0.800 average) and at ε'=0.3, but loses on 2-D blob at ε'=0.7 where Canonne's method captures fine-grained structure with lower variance. In the two-sample testing comparison (Figure 2), NAMMDFuse (NAMMD adapted with the multi-kernel fusion scheme of Biggs et al., 2023) achieves test power that is either higher or comparable to MMDFuse, MMD-D (Liu et al., 2020), MMDAgg (Schrab et al., 2022), AutoTST (Kübler et al., 2022), ME_MaBiD (Zhou et al., 2023), and ACTT (Domingo-Enrich et al., 2023) across sample sizes. In the practical ImageNet variant experiments, NAMMD-based DCT outperforms MMD-based DCT in distinguishing closeness levels among ImageNet-A, ImageNet-V2, ImageNet-R, and ImageNet-Sketch, and its conclusions align with the ground-truth accuracy-gap ranking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends distribution closeness testing (DCT) from the discrete, total-variation-based setting (Batu et al., Canonne et al.) to continuous and complex data by introducing a kernel-based discrepancy. It builds directly on MMD (Gretton et al., 2012) as the underlying distance, but identifies a specific limitation: distribution pairs with identical MMD values can exhibit different finite-sample distinguishability depending on their RKHS norms. The proposed NAMMD rescales MMD by these norms, and the test threshold is calibrated using the asymptotic variance estimator from Sutherland (2019). The paper also positions itself against the broader TST literature (MMD-Fuse, MMD-D, AutoTST, etc.) by showing that DCT's composite null hypothesis invalidates permutation-based calibration, necessitating the analytic asymptotic approach.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value lies in enabling label-free assessment of whether a distribution shift is 'close enough' for a model to handle without adaptation, which is directly relevant to deployment monitoring and domain adaptation decisions. The norm-adaptive scaling is a simple, interpretable fix for a real gap in MMD's informativeness for DCT. Open questions remain: kernel selection criteria tailored to DCT (rather than TST), extension to non-i.i.d. data, and whether the norm condition in Theorem 9 holds broadly in practice. The method's reliance on a reference pair to set ε also limits its applicability when no natural reference exists.
