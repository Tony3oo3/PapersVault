## Problem

While unified multimodal models can now natively generate interleaved images, it remains unclear whether explicit visual mental imagery actually improves multi-step visual reasoning or whether observed failures stem from generation errors, interpretation errors, or fundamental reasoning deficits. The field lacks a controlled, procedural framework to disentangle these factors across the full spectrum from implicit text-only reasoning to explicit image and video generation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265699/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each task instance is a single procedurally generated puzzle image plus a text prompt specifying rules; restricting input to one image ensures compatibility across all model families, including video models that accept only an initial frame. Outputs vary by family: MLLMs and latent reasoning models emit text action sequences or option labels, UMMs produce interleaved text with generated state-update images, and video models produce solution videos. Scoring is fully automated—simulation-based for planning tasks (RUSH HOUR, SLIDING PUZZLE, HINGE FOLDING), exact-match for recognition tasks (FORM BOARD, PAPER FOLD), and a lenient frame-by-frame auto-rater for video outputs that recovers object trajectories and implied actions.

## Method

MENTISOCULI comprises five procedurally generated tasks (FORM BOARD, HINGE FOLDING, PAPER FOLD, RUSH HOUR, SLIDING PUZZLE) designed around six desiderata—visual nature, high information density, sequential manipulation, procedural generation with ground-truth visual CoT, stratified difficulty (1–5 minimum steps), and generative feasibility—yielding 30 samples per level per task. The evaluation spans the full spectrum of machine mental imagery: MLLMs (Gemini 2.5/3, GPT-5.1, Qwen3-VL), latent visual reasoning models (Mirage and LatentSketchpad fine-tuned on RUSH HOUR), UMMs (Gemini 2.5-I, Gemini 3-I, Emu 3.5), and video models (Veo 3.1, Wan 2.6). A key analytical contribution is the oracle visual CoT experiment, which substitutes ground-truth intermediate states for self-generated imagery to isolate generation errors from interpretation errors, complemented by a per-puzzle coupling analysis comparing text-derived versus image-derived actions to quantify how poorly the two channels align. The paper further tests whether standard reasoning enhancements (ICL, prompt optimization, increased reasoning budget, tool use) transfer to visual reasoning, and includes a human psychophysics experiment on RUSH HOUR to contextualize model performance and reveal that models do not adaptively increase reasoning effort with difficulty, unlike humans.

## Results

Across all five MENTISOCULI tasks, performance degrades monotonically with difficulty, and by Level 5 all evaluated models operate at or below chance performance. Gemini 3 (Pro) is the strongest MLLM, followed by GPT-5.1 and Qwen3-VL, but even Gemini 3 falls below chance at Level 5 on most tasks. Critically, UMMs (Gemini 3-I, Gemini 2.5-I) frequently perform *below* their corresponding text-only MLLM counterparts, and video models (Veo 3.1, Wan 2.6) never exceed chance. On RUSH HOUR, humans achieve >60% accuracy at Level 5, while Gemini 3 performs comparably to humans constrained to 5–10 seconds of response time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four model families across a spectrum from implicit to explicit visual reasoning. Gemini 3 (MLLM) outperforms Gemini 3-I (UMM) on RUSH HOUR and most other tasks, demonstrating that interleaved image generation currently provides no consistent benefit over text-only reasoning; the same holds for Gemini 2.5 vs. Gemini 2.5-I. Latent visual reasoning models (Mirage, LatentSketchpad, fine-tuned on 200 samples/level) show modest gains over MLLMs at Levels 2–3 on RUSH HOUR but drop to near-chance at Level 5, and text-only fine-tuning matches or exceeds the latent variants on most levels (Table 1). Video models Veo 3.1 and Wan 2.6 never exceed chance under lenient scoring, though Veo 3.1 matches Gemini 2.5-I on lower levels. When oracle (ground-truth) visual chain-of-thought is provided, UMMs improve on FORM BOARD but still fail to reliably exceed chance on planning tasks like RUSH HOUR and SLIDING PUZZLE, revealing a deeper interpretation failure beyond generation errors. GPT-5.1 and Gemini 3, when given a lossless text transcription of RUSH HOUR, perform on par with humans, confirming that the bottleneck is visual understanding and manipulation rather than raw reasoning capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against a spectrum of visual reasoning approaches—from implicit MLLM reasoning through latent visual tokens (Mirage, LatentSketchpad) to explicit interleaved image generation in UMMs and natively visual video models—and argues that prior benchmarks (Zebra-CoT, MIRA, STARE, VisFactor) evaluate reasoning *about* visual information rather than reasoning *with* self-maintained mental imagery. It builds directly on VisFactor's FORM BOARD and PAPER FOLD generators, de Oliveira et al.'s sliding puzzle framework, and Wiedemer et al.'s video-model auto-rater, but departs by imposing six explicit desiderata (visual nature, high information density, sequential manipulation, procedural generation, stratified difficulty, generative feasibility) that prior benchmarks collectively fail to satisfy. The conceptual framing draws on cognitive science literature on mental imagery (Richardson, Nanay) to define a rigorous task category the authors claim is absent from existing VLM/MLLM evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that explicit visual thoughts (latent, interleaved, or video-based) do not improve multi-step reasoning, and that UMMs fail to leverage even ground-truth visualizations—challenges a popular direction in the field and identifies a concrete 'interpretation error' as a distinct bottleneck beyond generation quality. The procedural, stratified design ensures the benchmark's longevity against data contamination, and the human reference data (showing models perform like humans given only 5–10 seconds) provides a clear ceiling. Open questions include whether dedicated training on multi-step visual reasoning data can close the interpretation gap, whether the decoupling between image and text channels in UMMs is architectural or a training-data artifact, and whether the 60,000× cost premium of video-based reasoning can ever be justified by performance gains.
