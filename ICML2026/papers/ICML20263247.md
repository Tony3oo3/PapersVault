## Problem

All neural operators represent inputs as raw coordinate-value pairs, ignoring the differential structure that defines the underlying PDE, forcing networks to learn differentiation implicitly from finite, noisy samples. This paper asks whether providing derivative features aligned with PDE structure improves learning, and—more surprisingly—discovers that the optimal derivative order is strictly less than the PDE order due to a spectral bias-variance tradeoff that no prior equation-matching approach addresses.

## Input / Output

The method consumes spatial coordinates and input function values (initial or boundary conditions) sampled on a regular grid, then augments them with k learnable fractional derivative features D^{β_k}u computed via Grünwald-Letnikov finite differences, each with its own learnable order β_k and scale s_k. The output is the PDE solution function evaluated at spatial grid points. The regular-grid requirement is specific to the GL stencil formulation; the derivative orders and scales are optimized jointly with backbone weights, so the input dimensionality grows by k channels per spatial point.

## Method

∂-NO (delNO) is an architecture-agnostic preprocessing augmentation that wraps any neural operator backbone (FNO, TFNO, LocalNO, Transolver, CNO) with learnable fractional derivative features whose orders β_k are optimized end-to-end via gradient descent. The theoretical foundation rests on three results: Picard iteration on mild solutions shows derivatives of the input appear explicitly in the solution operator; providing these features improves approximation bounds from O(W^(-(s-m)/d)) to O(W^(-s/d)); and a spectral bias-variance analysis yields a closed-form optimal order β* = m − δ* where δ* depends on noise level, sample size, and network capacity cutoff. The Grünwald-Letnikov finite-difference formulation is used instead of the Weyl spectral derivative to avoid Gibbs ringing and ensure differentiability with respect to β, with the h^{-β} scaling factor omitted for training stability. The β parameters use a 10× higher learning rate than backbone weights because gradients through them propagate back through the full network and attenuate accordingly.

## Results

Across 5 PDE benchmarks (Burgers smooth/shock, KdV, Darcy, NS2D) and 4 backbones (FNO, TFNO, LocalNO, Transolver), ∂-NO improves relative L² error in every tested cell (clean σ=0 and noisy σ=0.05). Gains range from 1.5% (FNO on NS2D clean) to 75.6% (Transolver on Burgers ν=0.001 clean). On the CNO benchmark suite (7 PDEs), ∂-CNO improves CNO in all 14/14 settings under both L² and L¹ metrics, with peak gains of 64.7% on Poisson (noisy) and 55.4% on Wave (noisy). Learned derivative orders β* consistently fall below the PDE order m, confirming the theoretical bias-variance prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

∂-NO is an architecture-agnostic augmentation, so it is compared against each backbone's vanilla version rather than against competing methods. Against FNO (Li et al., 2020), ∂-FNO reduces relative L² error by 15.1% on smooth Burgers (clean) up to 26.5% on near-shock Burgers (clean), with consistent 1.5–24% gains across all other datasets. Against Transolver (Wu et al., 2024), gains are largest: 75.6% on near-shock Burgers (clean, 18.99%→4.63%) and 46.2% (noisy), reflecting Transolver's greater sensitivity to input representation. Against LocalNO (Liu-Schiaffini et al., 2024), improvements are more modest (0.5–15.8%), consistent with LocalNO's already-localized differential kernels partially capturing derivative information. Against CNO (Raonic et al., 2023), ∂-CNO wins in all 14/14 settings (7 PDEs × 2 noise levels × 2 metrics), with peak gains of 64.7% on Poisson (noisy L²) and 55.4% on Wave (noisy L¹), while gains are smallest on Darcy and Airfoil (≤5%) where the solution operator is less derivative-dominated. The paper also includes a matched-parameter control (Appendix L.2) confirming that gains come from the derivative inductive bias rather than added capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of neural operators and fractional calculus by introducing learnable fractional derivative features as a preprocessing augmentation, distinct from the three established uses of fractional calculus in deep learning (fractional PINNs, fractional gradient methods, fractional architectures). It departs from equation-aware emulators (Zhu et al., 2025) by requiring only the PDE order m rather than full equation structure, and from physics-informed approaches (PINNs, PINO) by providing derivatives as input features rather than loss terms. The theoretical contribution builds on Picard iteration for mild solutions and Sobolev-space approximation theory to prove both that derivative features improve approximation bounds and that the optimal derivative order is strictly below the PDE order.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding that β* < m challenges the natural assumption that derivative features should match the known PDE order, revealing that at any practically achievable data scale, the statistically optimal physics is sub-physical due to a spectral bias-variance tradeoff. This has broad implications for how domain knowledge is incorporated into ML systems: inductive biases should be parameterized rather than prescribed. Limitations include the regular-grid assumption for the Grünwald-Letnikov stencil, the requirement s > m+d/2 for sharp theoretical predictions (degrading near shocks), and the fact that the theorem characterizes population-optimal rather than optimizer-converged values. Open questions include extensions to irregular geometries, temporal fractional derivatives, and formal convergence guarantees for the learned β.
