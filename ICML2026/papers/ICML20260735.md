## Problem

Amortised samplers (e.g., diffusion models trained to sample from unnormalised densities) suffer from mode collapse and training instability on multi-modal targets, while Monte Carlo methods like SMC are anytime but require many steps at inference. Prior work has noted individual connections between SMC, maximum-entropy RL, and hierarchical variational inference, but no unified framework has been used to make these methods mutually beneficial: SMC has not been used as a behaviour policy to train amortised samplers, nor have learned sampler components been used as SMC proposals and intermediate targets.

## Input / Output

The method requires only pointwise queries to the unnormalised density R(x) (and optionally its gradient ∇log R(x) in the gradient-based setting), with no access to the normalising constant Z. It produces a trained amortised diffusion sampler (or prepend/append model for discrete spaces) that generates samples from π(x) = R(x)/Z in a single fixed-length rollout. The training procedure additionally learns intermediate flow functions F_n^φ that serve as unnormalised intermediate targets for SMC, and the sampler is evaluated using the amortised model alone (without SMC at inference).

## Method

The paper establishes a formal dictionary between hierarchical variational inference, maximum-entropy RL, and SMC/AIS, showing that learned policies and flow functions correspond exactly to SMC proposal kernels and twist functions. Exploiting this, it trains the amortised sampler off-policy using SMC (with the learned sampler as proposal and learned flows as intermediate targets) as the behaviour policy, creating a mutually beneficial cycle where SMC explores high-density regions the sampler has not yet covered. The policy is trained with the trajectory balance (TB) loss while the flows are trained with a chunk-based subtrajectory balance (SubTB) loss, and an importance-weighted experience replay buffer stores historical SMC samples weighted by their AIS normalising-constant estimates. An adaptive inverse-temperature tempering scheme (selected via binary search to maintain a minimum effective sample size) stabilises training by reducing importance-weight variance, and adaptive resampling triggers only when ESS drops below a threshold.

## Results

In the gradient-based setting (Table 2), the full method (TB/SubTB+SMC+IW-Buf) achieves the best MMD on 4 of 5 targets: Funnel (0.050), Robot4 (0.103), GMM40 d=50 (0.035), and ManyWell d=64 (0.043), and the best Sinkhorn on Funnel (115.54), Robot4 (0.39), GMM40 d=50 (3579.17), and MoS d=50 (2017.86). The most dramatic improvement is on Robot4, where Sinkhorn drops from 28.62 (SMC-HMC) and 11.24 (SCLD) to 0.39. In the gradient-free setting (Table 1), the method reduces EUBO on GMM40 d=5 from 3156.7 (on-policy TB) to 2.3, and Sinkhorn from 3110.2 to 83.3, demonstrating near-perfect mode coverage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DDS (Vargas et al., 2023), the method achieves substantially lower MMD on Robot4 (0.103 vs 1.381) and GMM40 d=50 (0.035 vs 0.050), and lower Sinkhorn on Funnel (115.54 vs 130.59) and GMM40 (3579 vs 6883); DDS retains a slight MMD edge on MoS (0.245 vs 0.299) and a marginal Sinkhorn edge on ManyWell (66.41 vs 68.57). Against LV (Richter et al., 2020/2024), the method wins on MMD for Robot4 (0.103 vs 0.422), Funnel (0.050 vs 0.109), and ManyWell (0.043 vs 0.260), and on Sinkhorn for Robot4 (0.39 vs 1.71) and GMM40 (3579 vs 3952). Against SCLD (Chen et al., 2025), the method dominates on Robot4 (Sinkhorn 0.39 vs 11.24) and GMM40 (3579 vs 7477), but SCLD achieves a lower MMD on MoS (0.130 vs 0.299) and a slightly lower Sinkhorn on ManyWell (67.27 vs 68.57). Against PIS (Zhang & Chen, 2022), the method wins decisively on Robot4 (Sinkhorn 0.39 vs 3106.73) and GMM40 (3579 vs 16426). Against CMCD (Vargas et al., 2024), the method achieves lower Sinkhorn on Funnel (115.54 vs 124.89/139.07) and GMM40 (3579 vs 22132/4259), though CMCD-LV is competitive on MoS (1945.71 vs 2017.86). Overall, the SMC+IW-Buf combination is the most robust, avoiding the training instabilities (×) that affect SMC alone on GMM40 d=50 and Robot4.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously treated-in-separation perspectives—hierarchical variational inference (HVI), maximum-entropy reinforcement learning (MaxEnt RL), and sequential Monte Carlo with annealed importance sampling (SMC/AIS)—into a single mathematical framework, showing that learnt sampling policies and flow functions correspond exactly to SMC proposal kernels and intermediate target densities. It builds directly on the GFlowNet off-policy training objectives (trajectory balance, subtrajectory balance) and the diffusion sampler literature, but departs from prior off-policy training approaches (e.g., Langevin-guided exploration in Sendera et al. 2024, adaptive teachers in Kim et al. 2025b) by using SMC itself as the behaviour policy, creating a mutually reinforcing cycle where the amortised sampler provides proposals to SMC and SMC provides training trajectories to the sampler. The importance-weighted experience replay buffer is derived from the AIS weight structure rather than from RL-specific prioritisation heuristics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified HVI–MaxEnt RL–SMC dictionary enables principled transfer of techniques between these communities (e.g., adaptive resampling and ESS-based tempering from SMC into RL training, and off-policy RL objectives into SMC kernel learning), and the resulting algorithms show substantial improvements in mode coverage over on-policy baselines on multi-modal targets. However, the experiments are confined to relatively low-dimensional benchmarks (d ≤ 64) and the paper acknowledges that scaling to high-dimensional Bayesian posteriors, data-derived densities, and discrete biological sequence design remains open. The framework's generality (no specific loss form required, works in continuous and discrete spaces) positions it as a potential foundation for future work combining amortised sampling with Monte Carlo correctors, twisted SMC objectives, and applications to molecular simulation and Bayesian inference.
