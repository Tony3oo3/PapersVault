## Problem

Differentiable optimization layers require computing hypergradients by implicitly differentiating KKT conditions, which involves expensive Hessian factorization and large linear solves that become prohibitive at scale. Existing first-order alternatives (e.g., penalty-based bilevel methods) handle inequality constraints only with O(1/ε) oracle complexity, far slower than the O(log(1/ε)) achievable for equality-only problems. The paper asks whether one can compute ε-approximate hypergradients for general convex-constrained problems using only first-order information at near-constant cost.

## Input / Output

The method takes a convex optimization problem specified in CVXPY (objective, inequality/equality constraints) parameterized by neural network outputs, plus an upper-level loss f(x, y). The forward pass returns the optimization solution y*; the backward pass returns an ε-approximate hypergradient computed from two black-box solver calls (original and perturbed) followed by a finite difference. A key design constraint is solver-agnosticism: the upper-level gradient ∇_y f is detached and injected as a linear term c^T y into the perturbed lower-level objective, so no solver-specific backward pass is needed and any convex solver (GUROBI, MOSEK, SCS) can be swapped in without modification.

## Method

The core contribution is an active-set Lagrangian reformulation that converts the differentiable optimization into a bilevel problem whose lower level has only linear equality constraints. Specifically, at a reference point x̄, the active inequality constraints are identified, linearized, and combined with the original equalities into a 'ghost' problem P2 whose hypergradient matches the original at x̄ (Theorem 4.5). A finite-difference oracle then perturbs the ghost lower-level objective by δf(x,y), solves both the unperturbed and perturbed problems with any black-box solver, and assembles the hypergradient from the difference of their Lagrangian gradients (Equation 4), yielding O(log(1/ε)) oracle calls for an ε-approximate hypergradient. The solver-agnostic trick replaces f with the linear surrogate c^T y (c = detach(∇_y f)) so the perturbation is just an added linear term, preserving the hypergradient while keeping the problem within the solver's supported class. Under LICQ and active-set identification, the overall convergence complexity is Õ(δ^{-1}ε^{-3}) oracle calls to reach a (δ,ε)-Goldstein stationary point, matching the best known rate for nonsmooth nonconvex optimization.

## Results

Across three benchmark tasks (synthetic DFL, Sudoku, SOCP), FFOCP and FFOQP match the training loss convergence of exact-gradient methods (CvxpyLayer, QPTH) while achieving substantially lower backward-pass runtime and near-constant peak memory. FFOCP outperforms CvxpyLayer, LPGD, and BPQP on both forward and backward time for convex problems, and FFOQP outperforms QPTH and dQP on QP tasks. As the decision-variable dimension d_y grows to 1000, CvxpyLayer runs out of memory while FFOCP maintains stable, sublinear time and memory scaling. The theoretical guarantee is an O(log(1/ε)) first-order oracle cost per hypergradient evaluation, yielding an overall Õ(δ⁻¹ε⁻³) oracle complexity to reach a (δ,ε)-Goldstein stationary point.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FFOCP dominates CvxpyLayer on backward time (especially on Sudoku, where CvxpyLayer's dense ill-conditioned KKT factorization becomes 'extremely slow and unstable') and on memory (near-constant peak vs. out-of-memory at d_y=1000); it also avoids CvxpyLayer's heavy canonicalization overhead in the forward pass. Against LPGD, FFOCP is faster in both forward and backward passes, and LPGD required a 100× tighter solver tolerance (10⁻¹² vs. 10⁻⁴) to converge on the synthetic and Sudoku tasks. FFOCP also beats BPQP and dQP, which both inflate problem dimension by transforming the original convex program into a QP before solving. For QP-specific tasks, FFOQP outperforms QPTH on the synthetic DFL task (where QPTH has a much higher forward time) and matches QPTH on Sudoku, while also outperforming dQP. The one area where QPTH retains an edge is backward time on QP tasks, since its KKT factorization is computed during the forward pass; however, FFOQP's specialized variant narrows this gap considerably.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Kornowski et al. (2024), which established O(log(1/ε)) first-order hypergradient approximation for bilevel problems with linear equality constraints. The key departure is an active-set Lagrangian reduction that converts general convex inequality constraints into linear equalities locally, extending the near-constant-time guarantee from the equality-only setting to general convex constraints. On the differentiable optimization side, the paper departs from the dominant KKT-implicit-differentiation paradigm (CvxpyLayer, QPTH/OptNet) and the optimization-based approaches (LPGD, BPQP, dQP) by treating the lower-level solver as a pure black box, making the method solver-agnostic in a way no prior differentiable optimization layer achieves.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The solver-agnostic property is practically significant: it allows practitioners to plug in any state-of-the-art solver (GUROBI, MOSEK, SCS) without implementing solver-specific backward passes, which most existing differentiable optimization layers do not support. The O(log(1/ε)) hypergradient rate for general convex constraints closes a gap left by penalty-based approaches that require O(1/ε) iterations. Open questions include the active-set identification assumption (which the paper acknowledges is non-trivial and provides a weaker guarantee without it in Appendix F), the lack of GPU support for batched solves, and whether the additional smoothness/boundedness assumptions for general convex constraints (Assumption D.1) hold in practice for large-scale problems.
