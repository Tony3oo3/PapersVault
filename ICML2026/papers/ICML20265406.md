## Problem

Existing robustness certification frameworks for vision-language models are limited to pixel-level Lp perturbations or a small set of hand-designed geometric transforms, and cannot capture real-world semantic variations such as shape, style, or scene changes. While latent-space certification methods (e.g., ApproxLine, GCERT) can model richer semantic shifts, they require substantial training data for each variation, limiting practical use. This paper is the first to certify VLM robustness under open-vocabulary semantic-level variations without requiring any additional data per variation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single image, a pair of text prompts specifying the source and target semantics (e.g., 'a photo of a round gyoza' and 'a photo of a triangular gyoza'), the set of class label prompts, and a target extent anchored either by the target prompt embedding (text-specified) or by a reference image (image-specified). It produces a complete labeled partition of the scalar extent range [φ_a, φ_a'] into maximal prediction-invariant subintervals, each annotated with its predicted class, along with a prediction invariance probability measuring the fraction of the extent range where the original prediction is preserved. The output is a deterministic, closed-form certificate rather than a probabilistic guarantee.

## Method

The key insight is that in a VLM's shared embedding space, a semantic variation between two semantics (a, a') is confined to a two-dimensional subspace (the semantic plane) spanned by their text-embedding proxies. The image embedding z is decomposed into an in-plane component z_∥ and an orthogonal component z_⊥; the semantic transformation γ(φ) rotates z_∥ by angle φ within the plane while holding z_⊥ fixed, thereby varying only the target semantic strength while preserving all other semantics. Because the VLM classifier induces Voronoi decision regions with pairwise bisector boundaries, substituting γ(φ) into each pairwise margin yields a closed-form trigonometric equation A cos φ + B sin φ + C = 0 whose roots give all possible class-flip extents. Sorting these roots partitions the extent range into prediction-invariant intervals, producing a complete certificate. The framework further derives certificate bounds under a bounded cross-modal misalignment budget δ, localizing residual uncertainty to explicit margin-tolerance sets.

## Results

The paper evaluates semantic robustness certification using mean absolute discrepancy (lower is better) between the certified transformation and a visual reference transformation. On synthetic semantic variations (Table 1), the image-specified method (I-Spec) achieves 2.3–6.7% discrepancy versus 8.4–19.3% for ExactLine across Oxford Pets, Flowers102, and Food101. On real-world semantic variations (Table 2), I-Spec achieves 0.0–11.0% discrepancy versus 0.0–32.8% for ExactLine across eight datasets (DTD, FGVCAircraft, Caltech101, StanfordCars, Flowers102, Food101, UCF101). The text-specified method (T-Spec) also consistently outperforms ExactLine, with the largest gaps on StanfordCars (e.g., 6.2% vs. 32.8% for Color) and UCF101 (4.0% vs. 30.1% for Viewpoint).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is ExactLine (Sotoudeh & Thakur, NeurIPS 2019), a complete certification method that certifies predictions along a linear interpolation path between two endpoint images. The proposed method (both T-Spec and I-Spec) consistently outperforms ExactLine on mean absolute discrepancy across all eight real-world datasets and all semantic types tested. The advantage is most dramatic on complex semantic variations: on StanfordCars Color, I-Spec achieves 6.2% vs. ExactLine's 32.8% (a 5.3× reduction), and on UCF101 Viewpoint, I-Spec achieves 4.0% vs. 30.1%. On simpler variations like Flowers102 Shape and Background, both methods reach 0.0% discrepancy, indicating that linear interpolation suffices when the semantic change is straightforward. The text-specified variant (T-Spec) occasionally underperforms I-Spec (e.g., FGVCAircraft Background: 14.9% vs. 11.0%), but still beats ExactLine in most cases. The paper attributes ExactLine's weakness to linear interpolation in input space introducing unintended semantic changes for complex variations like viewpoint and shape, whereas the proposed embedding-space transformation isolates the target semantic direction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the robustness certification literature from pixel-level (L_p balls, randomized smoothing) and geometric (rotation, translation) transformations to semantic-level variations in VLM embedding spaces. It departs from prior semantic certification methods (ApproxLine, GCERT) that require training generative models with substantial in-domain data for each variation, and from ExactLine which requires two endpoint images, by instead leveraging the open-vocabulary structure of VLMs: text prompts serve as semantic proxies that define a 2D subspace (the 'semantic plane') in which the transformation is a simple rotation. The closed-form Voronoi decision boundary of the VLM classifier then enables exact, data-free certification of prediction-invariant intervals along the semantic extent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a practical, training-free tool for VLM practitioners to audit where predictions are stable under specified semantic drift (e.g., shape, color, style), which is directly relevant for high-stakes deployment. Key limitations include dependence on the quality of the language proxy (bounded by the cross-modal misalignment budget δ, which can be conservative when the modality gap is large) and the difficulty of isolating a single semantic factor in real-world images. Open questions include integrating modality-gap correction into the certification, building stronger controlled semantic-variation benchmarks, and extending the analysis beyond the 2D plane assumption to multi-attribute variations.
