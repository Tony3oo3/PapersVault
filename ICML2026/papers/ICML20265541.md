## Problem

Conventional LVLMs process all videos through a single fixed pipeline (uniform frame count and resolution), ignoring that different user queries demand fundamentally different visual evidence—high resolution for fine-grained recognition versus dense temporal coverage for temporal reasoning. No existing dataset jointly annotates query sensitivity to both frame count and spatial resolution, and no adaptive gating strategy handles the mix of continuous neural modules and discrete video-processing stages within a single vision tower.

## Input / Output

The system takes a video (sampled into up to 120 frames), a text query, and video duration metadata (serialized as seconds plus a duration bin). The 0.1B Inductor consumes the query and duration to predict one of 8 task-regime labels; a deterministic scheduler then selects a frame count and pooled token grid from {27×27, 18×18, 14×14, 11×11} under a fixed 20.5K visual-token budget, using duration thresholds at 30 s and 180 s and minimum sampling intervals of 0.5 s / 1 s / 2 s. The final output is a free-form text answer from the Qwen2-7B LLM, with the selected frame count, resolution, and projector expert route logged for auditability.

## Method

TAM couples a compact SmolLM2-135M Inductor (trained on the newly constructed TA-116K dataset of 116K annotated queries) with a hybrid-gated MoE vision tower built on SigLIP-400M and Qwen2-7B. The vision encoder uses soft-gated sparse MoE (top-2-of-4 experts via Co-Upcycling of the original MLP layers) for stable routing over complex visual features, while the projector uses hard-gated resolution-specific experts (one per pooled grid size) for deterministic specialization. Training proceeds in three stages: (1) Inductor classification on TA-116K, (2) LLM-only adaptation to variable frame counts and resolutions (empirically shown to outperform the conventional projector-first order), and (3) joint MoE-VE and MoE-Projector training with auxiliary load-balancing losses. The key empirical finding is that MoE capacity and dynamic frame/resolution (DFR) are synergistic: each alone yields marginal or negative gains, but their combination produces a +12.1% average improvement, indicating that sparse experts specialize effectively only when fed task-appropriate visual inputs.

## Results

TallVA-8B-A7B (8B total / 7B active) achieves the best score on 9 out of 10 benchmarks among comparable-LLM models, with headline results of 75.9 on EgoSchema, 84.0 on NExT-QA, 65.6 on Video-MME, and 53.4 on MLVU-Test. The MoE-ViT + Dynamic Frame Number & Resolution (DFR) combination yields a +12.1% average improvement over the uniform-budget baseline, whereas either component alone gives only −2.7% (MoE alone) or +2.8% (DFR alone). End-to-end FLOPs (1.198×10¹⁷) remain comparable to dense 7B video LVLMs such as Qwen2-VL-7B (1.173×10¹⁷).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against its direct baseline LLaVA-Video-7B, TallVA wins on 9 of 10 benchmarks, with the largest gains on EgoSchema (+18.6, 75.9 vs 57.3) and MLVU-Test (+8.6, 53.4 vs 44.8), while losing slightly on MLVU-vol (68.5 vs 70.8). Versus Qwen2-VL-7B, TallVA leads on 8 of 9 reported benchmarks (losing only on MVBench by 1.5 points and MLVU-vol by 0.9), with the widest margin on EgoSchema (+9.2) and PerceptionTest (+6.8). Against InternVL2-8B, TallVA dominates across all 9 shared benchmarks, most notably on MLVU-Test (+15.0, 53.4 vs 38.4) and Video-MME (+7.0). Compared to LLaVA-OneVision-7B, TallVA wins on all 10 benchmarks, with EgoSchema (+15.8) and PerceptionTest (+12.0) showing the largest gaps. Notably, TallVA also surpasses the much larger LLaVA-OneVision-72B on 8 of 10 benchmarks (losing only on Video-MME by 0.6 and LongVideoBench by 3.6), demonstrating that task-aware visual budgeting can partially compensate for a smaller LLM backbone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLaVA-Video architecture (SigLIP-400M + Qwen2-7B) by replacing its fixed-pipeline vision tower with a hybrid-gated MoE vision tower, using Co-Upcycling (He et al., 2024) to initialize experts from the pretrained SigLIP checkpoint and ST-MoE auxiliary losses (Zoph et al., 2022) for load balancing. It departs from prior vision-side MoE work (CLIP-MoE, V-MoE, CuMo) that expanded expert capacity without query-aware routing, and from task-aware frame-selection methods (Koala, Frame-Voyager, DynFocus) that operate within a fixed visual budget. The key conceptual departure is making the frame-count and resolution budget an explicit, query-conditioned decision that simultaneously determines the projector expert route, rather than treating budgeting and expert routing as independent problems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding that MoE-ViT and Dynamic Frame/Resolution are synergistic (neither alone suffices; together +12.1% vs. −2.7% and +2.8% individually) provides a concrete design principle for future video LVLMs: sparse visual experts require task-structured inputs to specialize meaningfully. The 0.1B Inductor and rule-based scheduler offer a low-cost, interpretable routing layer that can be frozen during LVLM training and extended to new task types with ~800 samples of LoRA fine-tuning. Limitations include evaluation at 7B scale only, reliance on hard-coded routing rules rather than learned end-to-end budgeting, and persistent hallucination on high-difficulty queries. Open questions include whether learned differentiable routers can replace the rule-based scheduler, how TAM interacts with content-level token pruning or key-frame discovery, and whether the synergy finding generalizes to longer-context (128K+) settings.
