## Problem

As AI systems enter institutional workflows, workers must decide whether to delegate execution to AI and how much effort to invest in verifying outputs, while institutions evaluate workers using outcome-based standards that may misalign with workers' private costs. Existing work studies task allocation, reliance behavior, or auditing in isolation, but no unified framework explains how worker-chosen delegation and verification jointly shape institutional worker quality. This paper identifies a structural mechanism—rational over-delegation—by which AI can degrade institutional quality for some workers even when baseline task success improves and no behavioral biases are present.

## Input / Output

The model takes two worker ability parameters (verification reliability α and execution efficiency β), task profile parameters (worker and institutional benefit/loss values, cost discount ξ), AI characteristics (success probability p_a, execution cost C_a), and the worker's baseline success probability p_w. It produces the optimal delegation-verification action (d*, s*), the resulting institutional worker quality Q(W), a workflow regime classification (manual work, pure delegation, or verified delegation), and a compliance gain/loss determination relative to a qualification threshold τ. A key structural constraint is that both worker and institutional utilities are affine in the delegation probability d, forcing the optimal delegation decision to be binary and creating sharp phase transitions in the (α, β) ability space.

## Method

The paper models worker-AI interaction as a rational optimization problem where the worker chooses (d, s) ∈ [0,1]² to maximize a utility function combining task success probability p(d,s) and cost, while institutional worker quality is defined by evaluating a distinct institution-centered utility at the worker's optimal action. The task success probability decomposes into three pathways (direct worker execution, direct AI execution, and correction after detected AI error), with detection governed by a concave function φ(s) parameterized by α and verification cost governed by a convex function C_v(s). Because utility is affine in d, the optimal delegation decision is binary, and the paper characterizes the ability space using continuous separatrix functions ψ₀, ψ₁, ψ, and ψ_τ that partition it into manual-work, pure-delegation, and verified-delegation regimes with sharp transitions. The main theoretical results (Theorems 3.1, 3.5, 3.6) show that AI induces a verification amplification phenomenon: workers with high α experience compliance gains while workers with low α rationally over-delegate and suffer compliance losses, all arising endogenously from rational optimization. The framework is validated through a data-to-parameter mapping using the Collab-CXR radiology dataset, where clinician observations are mapped to model parameters to identify regime membership and evaluate intervention policies.

## Results

The paper's main results are theoretical characterizations rather than empirical benchmarks. In the illustrative parameterization (Figure 1), the manual-to-delegation threshold is t = 0.72, and a quality-degradation region emerges below the intersection point (α, β) = (0.39, 0.82) of the separatrices ψ and ψ₀. In the Collab-CXR calibration (Section 5), a single clinician is mapped to α = 0.108, β = 0.342, placing them in the manual-work regime with Q(W) = Q₀(W) = 133.77; reaching a qualification threshold τ = 150 requires increasing α to 0.335, β to 0.479, or AI accuracy p_a from 0.368 to 0.432. The key structural finding is that worker utility is affine in delegation probability d, making d* binary and producing sharp phase transitions between manual work, pure delegation, and verified delegation as (α, β) cross threshold curves.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines to compare against; the 'compares_to' field is empty. The paper's internal comparison is between the pre-AI baseline (d,s) = (0,0) and the AI-assisted optimal action (d*, s*), showing that worker quality Q(W) can exceed, equal, or fall below the pre-AI quality Q₀(W) depending on the worker's verification reliability α relative to a threshold ψ(β). The paper contrasts its framework with prior work on learning-to-defer, human-AI reliance, and economic monitoring models, but does not run head-to-head numerical comparisons. The Collab-CXR exercise (Section 5) is explicitly framed as a data-to-parameter mapping for illustration rather than an empirical validation, and the paper states it 'does not estimate causal effects or capture dynamic learning.' The closest analogue to a baseline comparison is the compliance gain/loss analysis (Theorem 3.6), which shows that workers with α above ψ_τ(β) are upgraded while those below are downgraded relative to a qualification threshold τ, with the boundary being monotonically decreasing in β.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by unifying three previously separate literatures—human-AI task allocation (learning-to-defer, selective prediction), empirical over-reliance/oversight-decay studies, and economic monitoring/auditing models—into a single decision-theoretic framework where a rational worker jointly chooses delegation probability and verification effort, and institutional quality is evaluated at the resulting optimum. Prior routing and deferral methods assume fixed human accuracy and exogenous effort; economic monitoring models place verification control with the principal rather than the worker; and human-AI integration frameworks (Celis et al., 2025; Kleinberg et al., 2018) characterize optimal decisions given signals but do not model effort costs or institutional misalignment. The paper's departure is making both delegation and verification endogenous worker choices under a principal-agent misalignment, then characterizing the resulting phase transitions in the (α, β) ability space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that AI can degrade institutional worker quality through purely rational over-delegation, with no behavioral bias or miscalibration required, and that this degradation emerges via sharp phase transitions rather than gradual decay—challenges the common assumption that AI assistance uniformly improves outcomes. The verification amplification phenomenon (AI disproportionately benefits high-verification workers while harming low-verification ones) has direct implications for how institutions should design oversight protocols, qualification thresholds, and incentive structures in AI-assisted workflows. Key limitations include the static single-task, single-worker setting, exogenous verification reliability, and the absence of dynamic learning or deskilling effects; open questions include whether these phase transitions persist in multi-worker, multi-round, or dynamically evolving settings, and how to empirically calibrate the model's parameters at scale.
