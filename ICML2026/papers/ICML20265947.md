## Problem

Existing inference-time CoT interventions for LRM safety apply static, unconditional safety prompts at fixed positions, creating an inherent trade-off: early insertion causes over-refusal on benign queries, while mid-reasoning insertion misses the timesteps where reasoning actually becomes unsafe. The paper identifies that a model's safety state is not static but evolves dynamically throughout CoT generation, and that no prior method can determine both whether and where to intervene based on the model's real-time internal state.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a standard text query and, during CoT generation, periodically (every n tokens) spawns two lightweight parallel forward passes from the current context: one appending a comma-terminated safety sentence and one appending length-matched blank tokens. From the top-layer hidden states of these branches it derives a per-step safety score (cosine similarity), and conditionally injects a safety guidance prompt into the generation context when the score falls below a threshold α (default −0.2). The final output is the model's steered CoT and response, with interventions triggered in only ~18.5% of cases, keeping overhead under 11% runtime.

## Method

SafeCompass is a plug-and-play, data-free inference-time framework that dynamically steers CoT reasoning by monitoring the model's internal safety state. At each monitoring checkpoint, it constructs two parallel branches from the current context: a safety branch appending a comma-terminated safety sentence (e.g., 'I will prioritize safety guidelines during my inference process,') and a control branch appending an equal-length sequence of blank tokens. The top-layer hidden states of the final token in each branch are differenced and normalized to yield a context-dependent safety direction d_safety, which captures the latent safety semantics at that specific reasoning position. The safety score A_t is then computed as the cosine similarity between the current hidden state h_t and d_safety. When A_t drops below threshold α, a safety guidance prompt is injected into the generation context, steering the reasoning trajectory back toward safety. This mechanism is entirely training-free, requires no pre-collected data, and generalizes across model families (Qwen3, DeepSeek-R1) and scales (8B–32B), reducing average attack success rate by up to 10× over the best baseline while preserving general reasoning performance.

## Results

SafeCompass achieves the lowest average Attack Success Rate (ASR) across all four evaluated models, reducing it to 0.020 on Qwen3-8B (vs. 0.520 no-defense baseline, a 26× reduction) and 0.012 on Qwen3-32B (vs. 0.499 baseline). On DeepSeek models, the average ASR drops to 0.094 (DeepSeek-8B) and 0.123 (DeepSeek-32B), representing 4.5× and 3.7× reductions respectively. Over-refusal remains negligible (0.01 on Or-Bench, 0.00 on Xstest for Qwen3-8B), and general reasoning is preserved (GSM8K 0.910, MMLU-Pro 0.719, Alpaca 0.837). Interventions are triggered in only 18.54% of cases, with a maximum runtime overhead of 11%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SafeCompass outperforms all six baselines on average ASR across every model. On Qwen3-8B, it achieves 0.020 avg ASR versus the best baseline SafeDecoding (0.231, an 11.6× improvement) and SafePath-ZS (0.266, 13.3× improvement); on Qwen3-32B the gap widens to 0.012 vs. SP-ZS's 0.138 (11.5×). On DeepSeek models the advantage narrows but remains consistent: 0.094 vs. SD's 0.196 on DeepSeek-8B (2.1×) and 0.123 vs. SD's 0.181 on DeepSeek-32B (1.5×). Against Jailbreak Antidote (JA), which uses a pre-computed static safety direction, SafeCompass is substantially better (0.020 vs. 0.394 on Qwen3-8B), confirming the benefit of dynamic on-the-fly direction extraction. On over-refusal (Or-Bench / Xstest), SafeCompass matches or beats all baselines (0.01/0.00 on Qwen3-8B), while methods like RPO (0.09/0.10) and SafeDecoding (0.09/0.11) show 9–11× higher over-refusal. On general capability (GSM8K, MMLU-Pro, Alpaca), SafeCompass is within 0.5% of the no-defense baseline, whereas JA degrades MMLU-Pro by up to 28% (0.439 vs. 0.719 on Qwen3-8B) and SD drops GSM8K by 12% (0.795 vs. 0.915).

## Relation to Prior Work

SafeCompass sits at the intersection of two prior lines of work: (1) contrastive activation steering, where Rimsky et al. (2024) and Jailbreak Antidote (Shen et al., 2025) showed that hidden-state differences between safe and unsafe behaviors can be used to nudge model outputs, and (2) CoT intervention methods like SafeChain (Jiang et al., 2025a) and SafePath (Jeung et al., 2025) that inject fixed safety guidance tokens at predetermined positions in the reasoning chain. The key departure is making both the safety direction and the intervention timing dynamic: rather than relying on a pre-computed static vector (as in Jailbreak Antidote) or a fixed insertion point (as in SafePath), SafeCompass extracts a context-dependent safety direction on-the-fly via lightweight contrastive branches and triggers intervention only when a real-time safety score drops below threshold. This transforms CoT intervention from a uniform, unconditional operation into a targeted, state-aware one.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play, data-free nature of SafeCompass (no training, no pre-collected safety data, ~10% overhead) makes it immediately deployable across model families, which is practically significant as LRMs enter safety-critical applications. The 10× ASR reduction over the best baseline while maintaining near-zero over-refusal addresses a trade-off that static methods fundamentally cannot resolve, as demonstrated by the paper's own preliminary experiments. Open questions include whether the monitoring mechanism itself can be adversarially evaded (the authors acknowledge this risk), whether top-layer states are optimal versus layer-specific safety signals, and whether direct hidden-state manipulation (rather than token insertion) could further reduce the intervention footprint. The reliance on a fixed threshold α and interval n, while shown to be robust, leaves room for adaptive mechanisms that could tighten the safety-utility frontier further.
