## Problem

Riemannian Flow Matching enables simulation-free training of generative models on manifolds, but sampling still requires numerically integrating a learned probability-flow ODE over many steps. MeanFlow solved this in Euclidean space by learning a long-range average-velocity field for one-step generation, yet extending it to manifolds is non-trivial: instantaneous velocities live in point-dependent tangent spaces, so even defining an 'average velocity' requires mapping vectors to a common space, and naively reusing Euclidean identities breaks geometric consistency.

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a single draw from a base distribution (e.g., uniform on the manifold) and optionally a class label, then produces one manifold-valued sample in a single network evaluation (1 NFE). During training, the network receives a geodesically interpolated point x_t (computed via Exp/Log maps between a data point x_1 and a noise point x_0), along with the reference time r and current time t, and outputs an average-velocity vector in the tangent space T_{x_t}M. The log-map representation keeps all computations in a single Euclidean vector space, avoiding coordinate-dependent Christoffel symbols.

## Method

RMF defines the average velocity over [r,t] by parallel-transporting instantaneous velocities along the trajectory to the tangent space at the current state, then derives a Riemannian MeanFlow identity (Proposition 3.1) that expresses this average velocity as the instantaneous velocity minus a covariant-derivative correction term. To make the identity computable without trajectory simulation, the method works in a log-map tangent representation and approximates the covariant derivative via Jacobian-vector products (JVPs) of the network, avoiding explicit Christoffel symbols. The resulting loss decomposes into two terms (a velocity-matching term and a derivative-coupling term) whose gradients empirically conflict; RMF-MT mitigates this by casting the objective as a two-task problem and applying PCGrad to project away conflicting gradient components. Conditional generation is supported via classifier-free guidance, combining conditional and unconditional predictions in the common tangent space.

## Results

On the Spherical Earth dataset (MMD ↓ at 1 NFE), RMF achieves the best score on Volcano (0.092) and RMF-MT achieves the best on Flood (0.048), while the strongest baseline G-LSD leads on Earthquake (0.032) and Fire (0.027). On the 7D RNA torus dataset, RMF-MT attains the best MMD (0.07), outperforming all baselines including G-LSD (0.08). On SO(3), RMF-MT achieves the best MMD on Fisher (0.039) and Line (0.035), and on SE(3) grasping, RMF achieves the highest 1-step success rate (65%) versus G-LSD (60%) and RFM (3.2%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RMF/RMF-MT consistently outperforms RFM (Chen & Lipman, 2024) by a wide margin across all manifolds (e.g., 0.092 vs. 0.351 MMD on Volcano), confirming that one-step average-velocity generation substantially reduces sampling cost without sacrificing quality. Against Riemannian Consistency Training (RCT; Cheng et al., 2025), RMF-MT wins on 4 of 5 torus subsets and all 4 SO(3) datasets, while RCT retains an edge only on the large General protein set. The strongest baseline, G-LSD (Davis et al., 2026), is competitive: it beats RMF-MT on Earthquake (0.032 vs. 0.035) and Fire (0.027 vs. 0.032) on the sphere, and on Cone/Swiss Roll on SO(3), but RMF-MT matches or exceeds it on the remaining categories and on the 7D RNA torus (0.07 vs. 0.08). On SE(3) grasping, RMF achieves the best 1-step success rate (65%) over G-LSD (60%) and RFM (3.2%), though RMF-MT underperforms RMF here, suggesting distribution fidelity does not always translate to task success. On the FANTOM5 promoter DNA benchmark, RMF matches Fisher FM and Eulerian RMF (Woo et al., 2026) at MSE 0.030 with 1 NFE versus 100 NFE for Dirichlet FM and Fisher FM, while achieving a k-mer correlation of 0.94. Among MeanFlow variants, α-Flow (Zhang et al., 2026) yields only marginal gains over RMF (e.g., 0.061 vs. 0.068 on Flood), and Improved MeanFlow (Geng et al., 2026) provides a modest improvement but still trails RMF-MT on most categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Euclidean MeanFlow identity (Geng et al., 2025) to Riemannian manifolds by defining average velocity through parallel transport of instantaneous velocities along geodesic trajectories, yielding an intrinsic Riemannian MeanFlow identity involving covariant derivatives. It builds on the geodesic interpolation framework of Riemannian Flow Matching (Chen & Lipman, 2024) for simulation-free training, but departs from the multi-step ODE integration paradigm by enabling one-step sampling. The paper also distinguishes itself from concurrent work (Woo et al., 2026) that derives three equivalent flow-map-based objectives, and from GFM (Davis et al., 2026) which extends Flow Map Matching to manifolds, by instead parameterizing long-range dynamics through a geometry-aware average-velocity field and applying PCGrad to the decomposed loss rather than manual weighting schedules.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

One-step generation on manifolds is practically important for scientific and engineering tasks (protein torsion angles, 3D rotations, robotic grasping) where multi-step ODE integration is costly. The paper demonstrates favorable scaling to 128-dimensional hyperspheres and competitive performance across diverse manifolds, but is limited to settings with closed-form Exp/Log maps; extending to manifolds without such structure remains open. An interesting negative finding on SE(3) shows that better distribution fitting (RMF-MT) does not necessarily improve downstream task performance (grasping success), highlighting a gap between distributional fidelity and functional utility. The curvature-dependent approximation error (Proposition B.2) also suggests that highly curved regions or long-range one-step transport may require few-step fallbacks.
