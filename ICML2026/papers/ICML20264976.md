## Problem

Existing privacy defenses for LLM agents—prompting (fixed instructions at initialization) and guarding (post-hoc blocking of proposed actions)—are static and passive, unable to support contextual, proactive privacy decisions during multi-step agent execution. Moreover, all such defenses are brittle against strategically optimized adversarial attacks that exploit long-tailed risk patterns, and no prior work explores whether privacy defenses can be systematically strengthened by learning from their own failure trajectories.

## Input / Output

The instructor model receives the agent's full context buffer C_{<t} (system prompt, conversation history, and all prior tool-call/result pairs) at each step where a non-empty tool-call result has just arrived, and produces a single natural-language privacy guidance string h_t that is appended to the buffer as a user message before the agent formulates its next tool call. During training, the input is a truncated failure trajectory (all states before the first detected privacy leakage), and the reward is computed from the appropriate disclosure score (AD) of the single agent action produced after inserting the generated guidance.

## Method

CDI introduces a lightweight instructor model (e.g., Qwen3-4B) that intervenes after tool-call results are obtained, generating step-specific, context-aware privacy guidance that proactively steers the agent's subsequent action—offloading privacy reasoning from the agent itself. The key training contribution is an experience-driven optimization framework: failure trajectories collected from adversarial attacks are truncated at the first privacy-leakage point, and the preceding context is used as a frozen RL environment where the instructor generates a guidance, the agent produces one action, and the resulting AD score serves as the GRPO reward. A staged training strategy (400 steps of PP warm-up followed by 200 steps of AD optimization) addresses the cold-start instability of the noisy AD signal. The instructor is fine-tuned via LoRA (rank 32) on a single A6000 GPU, and the resulting defense generalizes across agent backbones and unseen scenarios because it reinforces intrinsic privacy reasoning rather than memorizing scenario-specific norms.

## Results

On the 100 unseen test configurations, optimized CDI achieves 94.2% Privacy Preservation (PP), 80.6% Helpfulness Score (HS), and 86.5% Appropriate Disclosure (AD), substantially outperforming the no-defense baseline (36.8% PP, 66.3% AD) and both optimized prompting (85.1% PP, 83.8% AD) and guarding (83.6% PP, 74.8% AD). Under a new round of adversarial attacks, optimized CDI retains 79.5% PP and 82.4% AD, while optimized prompting drops to 55.1% PP and optimized guarding to 50.3% PP. Even without optimization, CDI already delivers the strongest protection (75.9% PP, 82.8% AD before attack) compared to prompting (48.1% PP) and guarding (47.0% PP).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the PrivacyLens prompting baseline (Shao et al., 2025), CDI wins decisively on all metrics: before optimization, CDI achieves 75.9% PP vs. 48.1% for prompting, and after optimization the gap widens under adversarial attack (79.5% vs. 55.1% PP). Against the Progent guarding baseline (Shi et al., 2025), CDI is superior on both privacy and helpfulness; optimized guarding severely degrades helpfulness (69.0% HS on test) due to its block-without-rewrite design, while CDI maintains 80.6% HS. Against the stronger PrivacyChecker prompting variant (Wang et al., 2025), which uses a detailed handcrafted system prompt, CDI still outperforms on adversarial robustness (79.5% vs. 51.6% PP) and on test AD (86.5% vs. 82.8%), confirming that the advantage is not an artifact of weak baseline implementation. The paper also shows that safety-aligned model variants (Qwen3-4B-SafeRL, gpt-oss-safeguard-20B) do not meaningfully improve over their base counterparts as guard/instructor models, further underscoring the value of the experience-driven RL optimization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Zhang & Yang (2025), which established a multi-agent privacy simulation framework and a search-based adversarial attack algorithm for LLM agents. It formalizes two existing defense paradigms—prompting (from PrivacyLens, Shao et al. 2025) and guarding (from Progent, Shi et al. 2025)—as distinct intervention points in a canonical ReAct/MCP agent loop, then introduces CDI as a third, previously unexplored intervention point (after tool-call results, before the next action). The experience-driven optimization framework repurposes GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and LoRA fine-tuning to train a lightweight instructor model on failure trajectories, extending the prompt-optimization approach of Zhang & Yang (2025) from static text search to gradient-based model training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLM agents increasingly mediate access to personal data (health records, financial info, schedules), the paper demonstrates that static defenses are fundamentally brittle against computationally optimized adversarial attacks, motivating a shift toward learned, contextualized guidance. The finding that a 4B instructor model can substantially improve privacy for much larger agent backbones has direct practical implications for deployment cost. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to privacy-utility trade-off scenarios (where some disclosure is beneficial), whether it scales to more complex multi-party settings, and whether the RL training signal from simulated failures transfers to real-world attack distributions.
