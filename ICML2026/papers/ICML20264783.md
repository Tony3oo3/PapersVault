## Problem

Existing latent action models compress all scene dynamics into a single monolithic latent action, making it exponentially harder to learn as the number of independently acting entities grows. This limits their applicability to complex multi-entity scenarios such as crowded intersections, multi-agent games, or soccer matches where many entities move simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264783/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes action-free video frames (128×128 for MultiGrid, 224×224 for Procgen and nuPlan) with no action labels. It produces autoregressive T-step video predictions, K per-entity latent actions drawn from a shared prior, and K factorized state slots. For controllable generation, a user specifies which entity to control and samples latent actions from the prior for that entity while others follow their inferred actions.

## Method

FLAM trains in two stages: first a VQ-VAE with FSQ quantization is pretrained to extract patch-level features, then a factored latent action model is trained end-to-end on those features. The LAM decomposes each frame into K slots via slot attention augmented with causal temporal attention for cross-frame consistency, then applies a shared inverse dynamics model (spatio-temporal transformer) to infer a per-slot latent action and a shared forward dynamics model to predict each slot's next-frame value. All factors share the same latent action space (unit-normal prior with VAE-style KL regularization), reducing the learning problem from |A|^K joint combinations to |A| per-factor actions. A cross-attention aggregator maps predicted slots back to the feature space conditioned on the current frame, and the entire pipeline is optimized jointly with a next-frame prediction loss, encouraging action-driven rather than appearance-driven factorization.

## Results

FLAM achieves the best average prediction quality across all four metrics on both simulation and real-world datasets: PSNR 34.9, SSIM 0.890, LPIPS 0.051, and FVD 419.6, compared to the next-best baseline AdaWorld (PSNR 24.7, SSIM 0.862, LPIPS 0.096, FVD 749.5). On the real-world nuPlan autonomous driving dataset, FLAM improves PSNR from 18.1 (AdaWorld) to 19.7 and reduces FVD from 2309 to 1946. Notably, FLAM also outperforms the World Model baseline (which uses ground-truth actions) on Procgen environments, where only the player's action is provided and inferred latent actions help capture stochastic motion of other entities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLAM outperforms all four baselines on average across every metric. Against the vanilla latent action models, FLAM improves PSNR by 10.2 over AdaWorld (34.9 vs. 24.7) and 6.8 over Genie (34.9 vs. 28.1), with FVD roughly halved (419.6 vs. 749.5 and 717.5). Against the object-centric baselines, FLAM shows the largest gains: PlaySlot achieves PSNR 29.1 and FVD 858.0 on average, while SlotFormer degrades severely (PSNR 19.9, FVD 1597.7), particularly on complex visual datasets where their reconstruction-based slot assignments become temporally inconsistent. On the simple MultiGrid domain, FLAM ties with PlaySlot and the World Model baseline (PSNR 56.5), but on more complex Procgen environments and the real-world nuPlan dataset, FLAM's advantage grows substantially. FLAM also surpasses the World Model baseline (which uses ground-truth actions) on Bigfish (PSNR 30.8 vs. 23.9) and Leaper (PSNR 38.1 vs. 24.7), attributed to inferred latent actions capturing stochastic entity motion that monolithic action tokens miss.

## Relation to Prior Work

FLAM extends the latent action model (LAM) framework introduced by LAPO and Genie, which learn a single scene-level latent action via joint inverse and forward dynamics training, by decomposing both the state and action spaces into K independent factors. It repurposes Slot Attention as a factorizer but departs from prior object-centric work (PlaySlot, SlotFormer) by jointly optimizing slot extraction and dynamics learning end-to-end, so that factors are organized by action independence rather than visual similarity. The spatio-temporal IDM/FDM architecture is directly adopted from Genie, while the VQ-VAE/FSQ encoder follows standard tokenization practice. Conceptually, FLAM bridges factored MDP theory (where state and action spaces are explicitly decomposed) with the action-free video setting where the factorization must be learned.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FLAM addresses a fundamental scaling bottleneck in LAMs: the latent action space must encode |A|^K joint combinations for K entities, making monolithic models intractable in multi-entity scenes. The factorized formulation reduces this to |A| per-factor actions in a shared space, enabling per-entity controllability in video generation and sample-efficient policy learning from unlabeled video. Key limitations include training a separate VQ-VAE per dataset (no universal encoder) and relying on a transformer aggregator with a frozen decoder rather than a generative model like diffusion. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large entity counts, whether pretrained tokenizers can be shared across domains, and whether more expressive decoders (diffusion, flow-matching) would further close the gap to the reconstruction upper bound.
