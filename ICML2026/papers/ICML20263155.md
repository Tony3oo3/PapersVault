## Problem

Multimodal large language models increasingly lose access to early visual evidence and intermediate reasoning constraints as autoregressive generation lengthens, because all prior approaches (CoT prompting, RAG, post-training) operate within the same single growing-context paradigm where attention to image tokens decays as O(L_v/n_t). The paper identifies that no existing mechanism provides a structured, state-dependent way to selectively revisit either visual evidence or reasoning state on demand without repeatedly restating them in text.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263155/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard RGB image and a text prompt (or chat dialogue) as input, processed by a frozen MLLM backbone (Qwen2.5-VL-7B or InternVL3-8B). During autoregressive decoding, at delimiter-eligible steps (punctuation or newline boundaries), the system may inject 4, 8, or 16 compact memory tokens drawn from either a visual or reasoning latent bank into the active context before predicting the next token. The final output is a standard generated text answer with reasoning chain, but with a controllable per-sample cap (N_max=5) on total routed injections to bound overhead.

## Method

DLMR adds three lightweight components on top of a frozen MLLM backbone: (i) two input-agnostic latent memory banks Z^(v) and Z^(r) that disentangle reusable visual evidence from evolving reasoning constraints; (ii) a memory injector implemented as a LoRA-augmented copy of the backbone that contextualizes selected latents into step-specific tokens by concatenating them with current context embeddings and extracting the last k hidden states; and (iii) a discrete router (a linear classification head over the final-layer hidden state) that at each eligible step selects the memory source s∈{v,r} and injection budget k∈{4,8,16}. Training proceeds in three stages with the backbone frozen throughout: Stage 1 aligns latent banks to teacher representations via a two-teacher cosine loss with cross-branch hinge and separation terms; Stage 2 trains the injector and latents via SFT or GRPO on multimodal reasoning data with mixed injection schedules; Stage 3 trains the router via GRPO with a cost-aware objective that rewards smaller budgets only when the answer is correct, plus a KL penalty to a reference policy.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-VL-7B, DLMR (RL) achieves 56.45% average on four reasoning benchmarks (MMMU, MathVerse, MathVision, MathVista) and 71.07% on three general benchmarks, outperforming the strongest baseline Visual-RFT (50.76% / 68.38%) by +5.69 / +2.69 points. On InternVL-3-8B, DLMR (RL) reaches 63.08% reasoning and 79.01% general, beating Visual-RFT (55.02% / 76.04%) by +8.06 / +2.97 points. The largest single-benchmark gain is on MathVision, where DLMR (RL) scores 39.34% (Qwen) and 38.56% (InternVL) versus 29.12% and 35.43% for Visual-RFT respectively. DLMR also reduces average generated tokens by ~9.7% compared to Visual-RFT while improving accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DLMR consistently outperforms all four baseline paradigms across both backbones. Against CCoT (compressed chain-of-thought prompting), DLMR (RL) gains +9.15 points on reasoning average for Qwen2.5-VL-7B (56.45 vs. 47.30) and +12.26 for InternVL-3-8B (63.08 vs. 50.82), showing that prompt-level compression cannot substitute for structured memory reuse. Against GRPO (RL post-training without memory), DLMR (RL) improves reasoning by +6.16 (Qwen) and +8.75 (InternVL), indicating that the dual-latent memory mechanism adds value beyond standard RL fine-tuning. Against Visual-RFT (visual reinforcement fine-tuning), the closest competitor, DLMR (RL) leads by +5.69 on Qwen reasoning (56.45 vs. 50.76) and +8.06 on InternVL reasoning (63.08 vs. 55.02), with the gap widening on the most long-horizon benchmark MathVision (+10.22 and +3.13 respectively). Against RCTS-RAG (retrieval-augmented tree search), DLMR (RL) gains +7.26 (Qwen) and +11.43 (InternVL) on reasoning, demonstrating that explicit latent memory routing is more effective than external retrieval for preserving intermediate constraints. On general VQA benchmarks, DLMR (SFT) achieves the best scores (71.45 / 79.25), while DLMR (RL) trades a small general drop for substantially stronger reasoning, suggesting SFT stabilizes broad visual grounding and RL further sharpens multi-step constraint maintenance.

## Relation to Prior Work

DLMR sits at the intersection of latent-memory-augmented LLMs (Vismem, Dual Memory Networks, JanusVLN) and multimodal chain-of-thought reasoning (Visual CoT, Visual-RFT, RCTS-RAG), but departs from both by explicitly disentangling memory into two specialized latent buffers (visual evidence vs. reasoning constraints) and coupling them with a discrete, state-dependent router that controls what to retrieve and how much to inject. Where prior memory systems treat the latent space as a single undifferentiated source and prior CoT/RAG methods remain bound to a monolithic growing context, DLMR introduces a structured, budget-aware reuse interface inspired by cognitive-science working-memory models. The three-stage training recipe (self-supervised latent alignment → LoRA injector SFT/GRPO → GRPO router) is a novel progressive curriculum that keeps the backbone frozen throughout.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DLMR addresses a concrete and well-documented failure mode of MLLMs—progressive loss of visual grounding over long generations—with a parameter-efficient mechanism that is backbone-agnostic and transfers to text-only reasoning, suggesting the dual-memory-plus-router pattern is a general-purpose long-horizon decoding tool rather than a vision-specific hack. The finding that selective latent revisitation can simultaneously improve accuracy and reduce generated token count (9.7% fewer tokens than Visual-RFT) points toward a practical efficiency lever for deployment. Open questions include whether the two-memory decomposition is optimal (could three or more specialized buffers help?), how the router behaves under truly out-of-distribution task types, and whether the approach scales to multi-image or video inputs where visual evidence is far richer.
