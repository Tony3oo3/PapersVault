## Problem

Despite iForest being one of the most widely used unsupervised anomaly detectors, its empirical success lacks a theoretical explanation. Prior analyses were either too coarse to distinguish which points receive longer or shorter paths (e.g., Pelletier 2024 showed only Θ(log n) scaling) or were not specific to iForest's random construction mechanism. This paper asks: what is the inductive bias of iForest, i.e., under what conditions does it detect anomalies and to what extent?

## Input / Output

The method takes an unlabeled dataset of n d-dimensional points and outputs the expected path length from root to leaf across isolation trees as the anomaly score (shorter path = more anomalous). The paper derives a closed-form expression for this expected path length in 1D (Theorem 3.5) and extends it to multi-D via a projected iForest that averages one-dimensional path lengths over coordinate projections. In the infinite-sample limit, the score decomposes into a log-density term, a log-centrality term (measuring distance to support boundaries), and a sample-size constant, revealing that iForest scores combine local density with global position information unlike k-NN.

## Method

The key insight is modeling the iTree growth process as a random walk on the state space of intervals (x_ℓ, x_r) containing the target point, where transition probabilities are proportional to the gap between consecutive data points. The path length of a point equals the hitting time of the absorbing state (x_i, x_i), which yields a closed-form sum over pairwise distances (Theorem 3.5). Asymptotic analysis as n→∞ via Riemann integral approximation and order-statistics properties shows the path length decomposes into 2ln p(x) + ln[(x−a)(b−x)] + 2ln n + O(1), proving iForest reflects both density and centrality. Fixed-dataset case studies then derive exact detection thresholds for marginal single, central single, and marginal clustered anomalies, showing iForest is more conservative but more parameter-adaptive than k-NN, whose thresholds depend on the hyperparameter k.

## Results

The paper derives a closed-form expected path length function for iForest (Theorem 3.5) and shows that in the infinite-sample limit, the path length decomposes into a density term 2 ln p(x), a centrality term ln[(x−a)(b−x)], and a constant 2 ln n + 2 + 2γ (Theorem 4.1). For fixed-dataset analysis, Table 2 summarizes the detection thresholds: iForest requires a gap of U·κ for marginal single anomalies, Θ(√(n₀κ)) for central single anomalies, and Θ(n₁²κ) for marginal clustered anomalies, all independent of any hyperparameter. Empirically, 99.7% of features across 933,440 OpenML binary classification datasets satisfy the mild density-ratio assumption (κ ≥ √(n+3)), and the mean-squared error between empirical and theoretical path lengths converges to zero as the number of trees grows from 100 to 1000.

**Table 2: Decision thresholds of iForest and k-NN for different types of anomalies.**

| Anomaly Type | iForest Threshold | k-NN Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Marginal single | U·κ | Θ(kδ) |
| Central single | Θ(√(n₀κ)) | Θ(kδ) |
| Marginal clustered | Θ(n₁²κ) | Θ(kδ) |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the standard k-NN anomaly detector (average L₁ distance to k nearest neighbors). iForest is more conservative than k-NN across all three anomaly types: its detection thresholds are uniformly larger, meaning it requires anomalies to be more extreme before flagging them. However, iForest is strictly more parameter-adaptive: its thresholds depend only on data-distribution quantities (U, L, κ, n₀, n₁), whereas every k-NN threshold scales with the hyperparameter k, so a poorly chosen k can cause missed detections or false positives. For marginal single anomalies, iForest's threshold U·κ is a fixed data property, while k-NN's Θ(kδ) grows linearly with k. For central single anomalies, iForest's Θ(√(n₀κ)) threshold is large (explaining its known insensitivity to central anomalies), but it remains independent of any tuning parameter, unlike k-NN's Θ(kδ). The empirical visualizations (Figure 7) confirm that k-NN can fail to detect rightmost clustered anomalies when k is too small, while iForest correctly identifies all marginal clustered anomalies without any parameter tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the original iForest algorithm (Liu et al., 2012) by modeling its stochastic tree-growth process as a random walk, yielding the first closed-form expression for the expected path length function. It departs from prior theoretical work on anomaly detection (convergence, PAC bounds, learnability) that was not specific to iForest, and from random forest consistency analyses (Scornet et al., Gao & Zhou) that target supervised learning. The closest prior result is Pelletier (2024), who showed iForest path length is Θ(log n) in the infinite-sample limit; this paper refines that coarse characterization by identifying the density and centrality components that determine which points receive longer or shorter paths.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical explanation of why iForest works as well as it does in practice, revealing that its scoring criterion combines local density with global centrality within the data support—a property absent from k-NN and density-based detectors. The finding that iForest is inherently less sensitive to central anomalies (requiring Θ(√n₀κ) separation vs. Θ(kδ) for k-NN) provides practitioners with a principled understanding of when iForest will underperform. Limitations include the primary analysis being one-dimensional (the multi-dimensional extension uses a projected variant rather than the full iForest), and the fixed-dataset results rely on Assumption 5.2 (κ ≥ √(n+3)), though the authors verify it holds for 99.7% of OpenML features. Open questions include extending the random walk framework to the full multi-dimensional iForest and to the broader family of isolation-based methods.
