## Problem

Discriminative time-series forecasting produces over-smoothed, unimodal predictions that suppress stochastic volatility, while first-order generative flow matching methods ignore system inertia, causing phase-space ambiguities (intersecting trajectories) and amplifying high-frequency noise. No existing generative framework enforces kinematic consistency—treating momentum and acceleration as first-class dynamics—when forecasting complex, high-volatility temporal systems.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multivariate historical window x^H (length T_H) structurally partitioned into endogenous observations (for position/velocity initialization) and exogenous forcing variables e (e.g., temperature deltas, precipitation, time embeddings) that condition the acceleration field as driving forces rather than generic context. It produces a future sequence x^F (length T_F) either as a deterministic point prediction (σ=0) or a probabilistic ensemble of 100 Monte Carlo trajectories (σ>0), generated by integrating a second-order ODE with K=20 symplectic Euler steps. The internal state is 2D per time step (position + velocity), doubling the state dimension relative to first-order methods.

## Method

KineFlow lifts conditional flow matching to a phase-space formulation where the generative process evolves a coupled (position X, velocity V) state via a learned Neural Acceleration Field A_θ, making the trajectory a double integral that structurally attenuates high-frequency noise (ω^{-2} spectral decay vs. ω^{-1} for first-order flows). The architecture comprises three components: (1) a Kinematic Phase-Space Prior that initializes position from a frozen deterministic backbone and velocity from finite-difference momentum; (2) a Neural Acceleration Field built from 1D convolutions, AdaGN residual blocks conditioned on integration time and exogenous forcing, and multi-head self-attention for long-range coherence; (3) a symplectic Euler integrator for inference. Training regresses the implied constant acceleration along a linear optimal-transport path in the augmented phase space, and the phase-space lifting resolves trajectory intersections that are topologically impossible for first-order ODE vector fields.

## Results

On standard benchmarks (Electricity, Weather, Traffic, ETTm1), KineFlow achieves an average MSE of 0.269, CRPS of 0.293, and V-Err of 0.197, outperforming the best discriminative baseline TimeXer (MSE 0.315, CRPS 0.363) by 15% in MSE and 19% in CRPS, and the best generative baseline TSFlow (MSE 0.607, CRPS 0.332) by 56% in MSE and 12% in CRPS. On the high-volatility AEMO electricity dataset, KineFlow achieves MSE 0.400 (36% below TimeXer's 0.628), CRPS 0.459 (8% below TSFlow's 0.498), and V-Err 0.146 (22% below the best baseline 0.187). In cross-domain transfer (VIC→SA, TAS→NSW), KineFlow outperforms the strongest foundation model Sundial by 14% and 8% in MSE respectively, while using 140× fewer parameters (0.90M vs. 128M).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the strongest discriminative baseline TimeXer, KineFlow reduces average MSE by 15% (0.269 vs. 0.315), CRPS by 19% (0.293 vs. 0.363), and V-Err by 12% (0.197 vs. 0.225) across four standard benchmarks, with the largest gains on high-volatility datasets (Weather: 22.5% MSE reduction; Traffic: 22.2%). Against the best generative baseline TSFlow, KineFlow achieves a 56% MSE reduction (0.269 vs. 0.607) and 12% CRPS improvement (0.293 vs. 0.332), indicating that second-order phase-space dynamics substantially outperform first-order flow matching for both point accuracy and probabilistic calibration. On the AEMO electricity dataset, KineFlow's 36% MSE advantage over TimeXer (0.400 vs. 0.628) and 22% V-Err reduction (0.146 vs. 0.187) demonstrate superior capture of sharp demand peaks and momentum shifts. In cross-domain transfer, KineFlow (0.90M params, T_H=24) outperforms all six foundation models (up to 128M params, T_H=1000), beating Sundial by 14% MSE on VIC→SA and 8% on TAS→NSW. The input-fairness ablation (Table 6) confirms that simply concatenating exogenous features into TimeXer does not close the gap (0.373 vs. 0.302 average MSE), attributing KineFlow's advantage to its structural acceleration-field integration rather than additional input channels.

## Relation to Prior Work

KineFlow extends Conditional Flow Matching (Lipman et al., 2023) from first-order velocity fields to second-order acceleration fields in an augmented phase space, drawing on the augmented Neural ODE lineage (Dupont et al., 2019; Norcliffe et al., 2020) and latent position-momentum models (Yildiz et al., 2019). Unlike recent high-order flow matching extensions (Cao et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025) that add higher derivatives without strict physical coupling, KineFlow enforces a rigid kinematic constraint (position update = velocity) so that the network only learns the acceleration component, making the double integration a structural low-pass filter rather than a loose regularization. The framework also repurposes the AdaGN conditioning mechanism from diffusion model literature (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021) to inject exogenous variables as driving forces rather than generic context, and uses a frozen deterministic backbone (Autoformer) to construct an informative phase-space prior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a 0.9M-parameter second-order model can match or exceed 128M-parameter foundation models on cross-domain transfer, suggesting that structured kinematic inductive bias can substitute for scale in time-series forecasting — a potentially important direction for resource-constrained deployment. The theoretical results (ω^{-2} spectral decay, O(Δτ²) inertial buffering) provide clean guarantees that first-order generative models lack, and the 20-step inference cost (vs. 500 for diffusion) makes probabilistic forecasting practical in latency-sensitive settings. Open questions include whether the kinematic bias generalizes to non-physical time series (e.g., financial returns with no true momentum), how the method scales to very long horizons (T_F > 720), and whether the frozen-backbone design is optimal or whether end-to-end joint training could further improve performance.
