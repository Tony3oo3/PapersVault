## Problem

Existing LLM quantization methods are constrained to discrete integer bit-widths (2, 3, 4-bit), creating a 'deployment gap' where models cannot be optimally fitted to specific hardware memory budgets. For instance, a 70B model on a 24GB GPU faces a choice between an oversized 3-bit quantization and a lossy 2-bit one, with the intermediate memory capacity effectively wasted. This paper addresses whether quantization rate can be made a continuous, tunable parameter rather than a fixed architectural constant.

## Input / Output

The method consumes full-precision weight matrices and a small calibration set (4096 samples × 2048 tokens from RedPajama) to produce binary codes in a lifted D-dimensional space plus a learned mapping matrix M. The effective bit-width is set by the ratio D/d (e.g., 24/10 = 2.4-bit), making it a continuous structural parameter rather than a discrete choice. At inference, decoding reduces to a single fused linear projection T* = MT⁻¹ followed by a 1-bit–float GEMM, requiring no lookup tables or specialized codebook kernels.

## Method

LiftQuant represents each d-dimensional weight vector as a linear projection of a 1-bit hypercubic lattice from a higher-dimensional lifted space R^D, where the effective bit-width equals D/d. A mapping matrix M is optimized to minimize quantization error on an i.i.d. Gaussian source (leveraging the CLT guarantee that high-dimensional lattice projections converge to Gaussians), with a generalized-inverse heuristic to accelerate the otherwise exponential nearest-neighbor search. A layer-wise whitening transform T = diag(s₁)(P₁⊗P₂)diag(s₂) reshapes real LLM weights into the i.i.d. Gaussian distribution expected by the lattice, combining AWQ-style importance scaling, Hadamard-based decorrelation, and per-channel isotropy refinement. At inference, M and T⁻¹ are fused into a single matrix T*, so the entire decoding path is o = diag(s)·W_q·(T*·aᵀ)—a low-complexity linear transform plus a 1-bit uniform quantizer—enabling arbitrary fractional bit-widths through a single unified, hardware-friendly operator.

## Results

On Llama-3-70B at 2-bit, LiftQuant (LQ-32/16) achieves a WikiText-2 perplexity of 4.69, outperforming QTIP (4.97) and QuIP# (5.77). The headline result is the fractional 2.4-bit configuration (LQ-24/10), which drops WikiText-2 PPL to 4.10 on Llama-3-70B—surpassing all 2-bit baselines by a large margin (best 2-bit: QTIP at 4.97). At 3-bit on Llama-2-70B, LiftQuant (LQ-24/8) achieves 3.35 W2 PPL, beating QuIP# (3.56) and EQAT-g128 (3.61). Inference throughput on Llama-2-70B reaches 31.3 TK/s (LQ-32/16), exceeding QTIP's 24.5 TK/s at 2-bit.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against UQ baselines, LiftQuant dominates across all models: on Llama-2-70B at 2-bit, it achieves 4.08 W2 PPL versus GPTQ (NaN), Quarot (5.60), and EQAT-g64 (4.52). Among VQ methods, LiftQuant at 2-bit slightly trails QTIP on smaller models (e.g., Llama-2-7B: 6.52 vs 6.28) but reverses the gap on Llama-3-70B (4.69 vs 4.97), which the authors attribute to its fully differentiable fine-tuning pipeline. The fractional 2.4-bit configuration (LQ-24/10) is the decisive advantage: it outperforms every 2-bit baseline by 0.5–1.0 PPL on Llama-3-70B (4.10 vs QTIP's 4.97), a gap no integer-bit method can close. At 3-bit, LiftQuant (LQ-24/8) matches or beats QuIP# and VPTQ on all tested models (e.g., Llama-2-70B: 3.35 vs 3.56). In inference throughput, LiftQuant (31.3 TK/s) exceeds QTIP (24.5 TK/s) at 2-bit but remains below AWQ (36.1 TK/s), reflecting the additional linear projection cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

LiftQuant sits at the intersection of vector quantization (AQLM, VPTQ, QuIP#, QTIP) and lattice quantization, but departs from both by replacing learned codebooks or fixed lattice structures with a 'lift-then-project' mechanism grounded in the Central Limit Theorem: a simple 1-bit hypercubic lattice in a higher-dimensional space, when linearly projected, naturally generates a Gaussian-like codebook in the target space. The whitening transform draws on AWQ-style activation-aware scaling, Hadamard rotations from QuIP#/Quarot, and isotropy refinement from FlatQuant, but the key conceptual departure is decoupling the effective bit-width (D/d ratio) from the coding format, turning quantization rate into a continuous structural parameter rather than a discrete architectural choice. This contrasts with prior fractional-bit approaches like Q-Palette, which assemble heterogeneous quantizer libraries, or group-size modulation in EfficientQAT, which offers only a few discrete 'gears.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: LiftQuant enables a 70B model at 2.4 bits to fit a 24GB GPU with performance far exceeding any 2-bit alternative, directly addressing the 'deployment gap' that wastes hardware memory between integer bit-widths. However, the approach has clear limitations: nearest-neighbor search complexity grows exponentially with D−d (capped at ~20 for practicality), the high-bit regime (≥4-bit) requires impractically small block sizes, and the method's advantage over QTIP at strictly 2-bit is marginal (within 0.1 bit). Open questions include whether the continuous bit-width advantage persists under activation quantization or multi-GPU inference, whether the '2× scaling + quantization' heuristic generalizes, and whether the search overhead can be further reduced for real-time deployment.
