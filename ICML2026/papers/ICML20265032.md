## Problem

Label Distribution (LD), a probability simplex representation for label ambiguity, suffers from three inherent limitations: it cannot faithfully recover raw data (e.g., two different colors share the same LD), it counterintuitively distorts inter-sample order relations, and it cannot represent out-of-distribution samples or negative label correlations. The paper introduces Generalized Label Distribution (GLD) as a unified, bijective representation that resolves all three issues while remaining losslessly convertible to every existing label form (LD, LL, TL, LR, SL).

## Input / Output

The method takes a feature vector x ∈ R^m (tabular features or image descriptors) and produces a GLD vector g ∈ [-1,1]^c, where each component is a 'relative degree' indicating the signed association strength between the sample and a label. Unlike LD which is constrained to the probability simplex (all values ≥ 0, summing to 1), GLD values can be negative, enabling explicit OOD detection (all-negative g) and negative correlation capture. The mapping η(q; a, b) = 2(q−a)⊘(b−a) − 1 is bijective, so the original raw data q ∈ ∏[a_j, b_j] is perfectly recoverable from g, and g can be losslessly projected down to LD, LL, TL, LR, or SL via simple thresholding, sorting, or argmax operations.

## Method

The core contribution is a bijective normalization of raw label data into [-1,1]^c (GLD), combined with three learning strategies: (1) GLD-SVR, which applies arctanh/tanh reparameterization to SVR for bounded regression; (2) GLD-kNN, which averages neighbor GLDs; and (3) GLD-BFGS, a specialized linear model f(x) = tanh(Wx) trained with a squared Mahalanobis distance loss ℓ = (1/n)Σ e_i^T Σ^{-1} e_i, where the covariance Σ is iteratively estimated via Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage with an annealing schedule. The Mahalanobis loss is theoretically justified as the negative log-likelihood under a multivariate Gaussian noise model, and the paper provides Rademacher complexity generalization bounds (Theorem 3.9) and variance-reduction guarantees showing GLD-kNN dominates LD-kNN (Theorem 3.6). The framework also subsumes existing LDL methods (LD-DF, LD-LRR, LD-Delta) by replacing their LD-specific losses with the Mahalanobis form, extending their applicability to GLD.

## Results

On the artificial dataset (artf), GLD-DF achieves the best average rank (1.14/12) with a subset accuracy of 96.45% (vs. 37.10% for LD-DF), inter-sample Kendall's tau of 0.9648 (vs. 0.5103), and OOD error of 2.45% (vs. 16.35%). On the real-world jura dataset, GLD-DF reaches 69.42% subset accuracy and 15.91% OOD error compared to LD-DF's 3.26% and 59.05%, respectively. On wq, GLD-Delta attains the best average rank (2.29) with 11.87% subset accuracy versus LD-Delta's 2.23%. Across all datasets, GLD methods match or exceed LD counterparts on LD prediction metrics (Clark, μ_KLD) while delivering substantial gains on LL prediction, inter-sample ranking, and OOD classification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Geng (2016) baselines (LD-SVR, LD-kNN, LD-BFGS), GLD counterparts achieve comparable or slightly better LD prediction (Clark, μ_KLD) but dramatically outperform on LL prediction (e.g., GLD-SVR: Ham. 0.0143 vs. LD-SVR: 0.2338; S.Acc. 95.70% vs. 40.85% on artf), inter-sample ranking (Ken.' 0.9502 vs. 0.5083), and OOD detection (3.35% vs. 12.70% error). Against González et al. (2021) LD-DF, GLD-DF dominates on artf with 96.45% vs. 37.10% subset accuracy and 2.45% vs. 16.35% OOD error, and on jura with 69.42% vs. 3.26% subset accuracy and 15.91% vs. 59.05% OOD error. Against Jia et al. (2023) LD-LRR, GLD-LRR matches on Clark/μ_KLD but improves S.Acc. from 36.85% to 80.40% and Ken.' from 0.5062 to 0.9589 on artf. Against Li et al. (2025) LD-Delta, GLD-Delta achieves 90.90% vs. 38.95% S.Acc. and 6.60% vs. 10.75% OOD error on artf, while on wq GLD-Delta attains the best average rank (2.29) versus LD-Delta (5.29). The consistent pattern is that GLD methods preserve LD prediction quality while unlocking substantial gains in tasks that LD fundamentally cannot support (LL recovery, order preservation, OOD detection).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Label Distribution Learning (LDL, Geng 2016) and the broader family of label ambiguity representations (multi-label, label ranking, ternary labels), but departs by proving that LD has fundamental theoretical limitations: it cannot faithfully recover raw data, distorts inter-sample order, and cannot handle OOD samples. The authors introduce GLD as a bijective, order-preserving generalization of LD and adapt existing LDL algorithms (SVR, kNN, BFGS, decomposition-fusion, label-ranking loss, Delta loss) to the new representation. The learning framework is inspired by multi-target regression and the problem-transformation/algorithm-adaptation taxonomy from Zhang & Zhou and Geng.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rigorous theoretical foundation (mutual information bounds, Rademacher complexity, variance comparisons) showing GLD strictly dominates LD in expressiveness, which could reshape how the LDL community formulates and evaluates label ambiguity tasks. However, the practical scope is currently narrow: all experiments use simple models (linear, kNN, SVR) on small tabular datasets, and the authors explicitly note that continuous label spaces and deep learning integration remain unexplored. The framework's value will depend on whether GLD-based losses (squared Mahalanobis distance with Ledoit-Wolf covariance) prove useful in deeper architectures and larger-scale settings.
