key_contribution: Proposes Group Cognition Learning (GCL), a two-stage governed collaboration paradigm with four specialized agents (Routing, Auditing, Public-Factor, Aggregation) that explicitly regulates cross-modal interaction via marginal-predictive-gain auditing and contribution-aware consensus, achieving state-of-the-art results on CMU-MOSI, CMU-MOSEI, and MIntRec while mitigating modality dominance and spurious coupling.
limitations: The protocol is formally designed for exactly three modalities (language, acoustic, visual) and the paper does not analyze scalability to more than three modalities or to streaming/online settings. The Impact Statement acknowledges that selective gating trained on biased data could inadvertently suppress minority modalities or underrepresented demographic features, but no empirical bias audit is conducted.

surprise_factor: The most notable finding is the ablation showing that forcing full cross-modal exchange (MAE 0.721) performs *worse* than language-only (MAE 0.714), confirming that unregulated fusion injects more noise than signal—a result that challenges the implicit assumption in many fusion architectures that more connectivity is always beneficial. ## Problem

Centralized multimodal fusion suffers from modality dominance (gradients concentrate on the easiest predictive pathway, under-training weaker modalities) and spurious modality coupling (models overfit to incidental cross-modal correlations). Existing interventions—optimization rebalancing, routing-style designs, and disentanglement—still lack an explicit governance mechanism that audits which cross-modal interactions should be admitted and which dependencies should be suppressed at the process level.

## Input / Output

Each sample provides a text sequence, an audio signal, and video frames, which are independently encoded by modality-specific encoders into fixed-length representations before entering the governed interaction protocol. For sentiment analysis (MOSI/MOSEI), the output is a continuous intensity score in [-3, 3]; for intent recognition (MIntRec), it is one of 20 discrete class labels. The protocol operates entirely on the encoded representations, so the same two-stage architecture transfers across modality compositions (e.g., audio-visual on CREMA-D/UCF101, image-text on Food101) by swapping only the front-end encoders.

## Method

GCL replaces static fusion with a two-stage, four-agent governed interaction protocol. In Stage 1 (Selective Interaction), a Routing Agent proposes directed routes between modalities via lightweight MLPs over global context, and an Auditing Agent computes a sample-wise admission gate by estimating marginal predictive gain (teacher gain from a local task head during training, a learned predictor at inference) and normalizing it with the routing logits through a temperature-scaled sigmoid. In Stage 2 (Consensus Formation), a Public-Factor Agent distills a permutation-invariant shared semantic anchor from the refined channels, and an Aggregation Agent conditions per-modality proposal networks and gating networks on this anchor to produce a contribution-aware weighted consensus. Training jointly optimizes the task loss, local unimodal supervision (stabilizing the gain signal), a public-factor auxiliary loss, a gain-alignment loss that opens gates only when teacher gain is positive, and a contrastive redundancy penalty that enforces orthogonality among specialized channels to suppress spurious co-adaptation.

## Results

On CMU-MOSI, GCL achieves MAE 0.685, Pearson Corr 0.812, Acc-2 86.79%, Acc-7 49.06%, and F1 86.40%, surpassing the strongest baseline TSDA (MAE 0.695, Acc-7 48.6%) by 0.010 MAE and 0.46 Acc-7. On the larger CMU-MOSEI benchmark, GCL reaches MAE 0.520 and Acc-7 55.36%, improving over TSDA (MAE 0.529, Acc-7 54.9%) and EMOE (MAE 0.536, Acc-7 54.1%). On MIntRec (multimodal intent recognition), GCL attains 72.74% accuracy and 70.95% F1, outperforming EMOE (72.58%/70.73%) and TSDA (72.59%/70.68%). Generalization experiments on CREMA-D, UCF101, AVE, and Food101 confirm consistent gains of 0.5–1.0% over the strongest baseline ARL across all four tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GCL outperforms all 21 baselines across every reported metric on both sentiment benchmarks. On MOSI, the margin over the closest competitor TSDA is 0.010 MAE (0.685 vs. 0.695) and 0.46 Acc-7 (49.06 vs. 48.60), while over EMOE the gap widens to 0.025 MAE and 1.36 Acc-7. On MOSEI, GCL leads TSDA by 0.009 MAE and 0.46 Acc-7, and DLF by 0.016 MAE and 1.46 Acc-7. On MIntRec, GCL edges out EMOE by 0.16% accuracy and 0.22% F1, and TSDA by 0.15% accuracy and 0.27% F1. In the generalization study (Table 4), GCL beats ARL by 0.85% Acc on CREMA-D, 0.73% on UCF101, 0.75% on AVE, and 0.20% on Food101, confirming that the governed interaction protocol transfers across modality compositions (audio-visual, image-text) without task-specific redesign. Notably, GCL also achieves the best efficiency: 117.56M parameters and 20.06 s/epoch, reducing parameters by 54% versus ConFEDE (256.98M) and training time by 25% versus EMOE (26.80 s/epoch).

## Relation to Prior Work

GCL synthesizes ideas from three distinct lines of multimodal learning—disentanglement (MISA, FDMER, ConFEDE) for shared/private factor separation, routing/expert designs (EMOE, D2R) for adaptive collaboration, and contrastive objectives for redundancy control—into a unified two-stage protocol with explicit agent roles. Its key conceptual departure is reframing cross-modal interaction as a *governed process* rather than an implicit byproduct of end-to-end optimization: the Auditing Agent's marginal-predictive-gain signal and the Public-Factor Agent's explicit shared anchor are not present in prior fusion or disentanglement methods, which optimize interaction only through the final task loss. The paper does not build on a single predecessor but carves new ground by making the interaction topology sample-adaptive and audit-compliant, contrasting with both dense fusion (TFN, MulT) and static decomposition (MISA, FDMER).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The governance framing offers a principled answer to two persistent failure modes—modality dominance and spurious coupling—that prior optimization interventions (CGGM, MCIS) and disentanglement methods address only indirectly. The ablation showing that forced full exchange is *worse* than language-only fusion is a useful diagnostic for practitioners: indiscriminate connectivity is actively harmful, not merely suboptimal. Open questions include whether the teacher-gain auditing signal scales to more than three modalities or to streaming/online settings, whether the lightweight MLP-based routing generalizes to very high-dimensional encoders, and whether the protocol's interpretability advantage translates to real-world deployment decisions. The paper also raises ethical concerns about repurposing the selective gating to suppress minority signals, an underexplored dimension for governed multimodal systems.
