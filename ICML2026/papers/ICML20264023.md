## Problem

Large Reasoning Models generate long Chain-of-Thought trajectories whose internal dynamics remain poorly understood, making it difficult to diagnose why a reasoning path succeeds or fails. Existing steering approaches target isolated behaviors or use local heuristics without a global hierarchical view of how cognitive modes evolve over a trajectory, leaving a gap between interpretability (identifying steerable behaviors) and actionable control (deciding when and where to intervene to maximize correctness).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264023/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt and the target model's hidden representations at sentence boundaries (detected via punctuation tokens) during autoregressive CoT generation. It produces a steered CoT trajectory with sparse interventions—orthogonal activation vectors injected at a single selected layer—leading to a final answer. Interventions are gated by a confidence-aware policy that skips steering when the model is already on a high-value trajectory, typically requiring only a handful of interventions per problem (e.g., ~0.5 on MATH-500 for GPT-L under Q-Value steering).

## Method

The paper models CoT reasoning as a Finite State Machine over six abstract cognitive states (init, deduce, augment, uncertain, backtrack, closure), with sentences as the atomic unit of transition. It constructs a Transition Advantage Matrix R by subtracting outcome-conditioned transition probabilities (incorrect minus correct), then treats the FSM as a deterministic MDP and runs Q-value iteration (γ=0.9, 100 steps) to compute long-horizon transition utilities. At inference time, a triplet-loss-trained state encoder and two lightweight MLP classifiers predict the current and next state at each sentence boundary; a Q-gap threshold and confidence gate decide whether to intervene. When intervention is triggered, a precomputed contrastive difference-of-means steering vector for the target transition is injected orthogonally into the hidden state, preserving content-aligned components while biasing the next-state distribution. This planning-aware, sparse intervention strategy achieves significant accuracy gains (e.g., +13% on AIME25 for gpt-oss-20b) with roughly 25× fewer interventions than greedy or weighted baselines.

## Results

Q-Value steering achieves the largest gains on the hardest benchmark, AIME25, where GPT-L improves from 43.30% (default) to 56.67% accuracy with only 55.20 average interventions, and QWEN reaches 86.67% (vs. 83.33% default). On MATH-500, GPT-L peaks at 83.20% accuracy with a remarkable 0.48 interventions per sample—roughly 25× fewer than greedy steering (12.17). Across all four benchmarks and models, Q-Value steering matches or exceeds the best accuracy while consistently requiring the fewest steering interventions, and greedy steering occasionally degrades performance (e.g., QWEN on AIME25 drops from 83.33% to 76.67%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three steering policies (Greedy, Weighted, Q-Value) against a no-steering default baseline. Q-Value steering wins or ties on accuracy in 13 of 16 model–dataset combinations, with the largest margin on AIME25 (e.g., +13.37 pp for GPT-L over default, +13.34 pp over greedy). Greedy steering is the weakest: it degrades accuracy in 4 of 16 settings (notably QWEN on AIME25: 83.33%→76.67%, GPT-M on MATH-500: 86.40%→85.40%), confirming that myopic local-optimal transitions can push reasoning into suboptimal paths. Weighted steering is competitive on accuracy but consistently requires more interventions (e.g., 83.87 vs. 55.20 on AIME25/GPT-L) and often inflates CoT token counts. The standout efficiency result is Q-Value steering on MATH-500/GPT-L, which achieves the highest accuracy (83.20%) with only 0.48 interventions per sample—approximately 25× fewer than greedy (12.17) and 27× fewer than weighted (12.81)—demonstrating that long-horizon planning identifies a small number of high-leverage decision points rather than biasing every transition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the activation-steering line of work (Turner et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025a; Venhoff et al., 2025) from local, single-behavior interventions to global trajectory planning over a structured state space. Prior approaches treat overthinking or specific reasoning behaviors as isolated phenomena to suppress or modulate, whereas this paper introduces a six-state FSM abstraction grounded in classical cognitive science (Polya, Schoenfeld) and formulates reasoning control as a deterministic MDP solved via Q-value iteration. The key conceptual departure is shifting from 'steer this one behavior' to 'plan the sequence of cognitive transitions that maximizes long-horizon correctness,' enabling sparse, policy-driven interventions at sentence boundaries rather than dense per-token adjustments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides an interpretable, training-free mechanism for auditing and controlling LRM reasoning strategies, with practical implications for reliability in high-stakes deployment (the authors note the FSM offers a layer of transparency for verifying not just answers but the structural validity of the reasoning path). Key open questions include whether the six-state taxonomy generalizes beyond math/science to open-ended or creative tasks, whether the Markov assumption is sufficient (second-order experiments show negligible gains, but the analysis is limited), and whether steering can substitute for genuine knowledge gaps or merely reshapes trajectory structure. The correlation-vs-causation caveat on the Transition Advantage Matrix is important: the matrix identifies statistically associated transitions but does not prove that forcing them yields correctness, particularly when the model lacks underlying domain competence.
