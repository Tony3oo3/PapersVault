## Problem

In-context learning (ICL) enables large language models to perform tabular prediction by conditioning on labeled examples at inference time, but the resulting context-conditioned decision rule is dynamic and black-box, raising fundamental questions about whether algorithmic recourse is well-defined, stable, and computable in this setting. No prior work has studied recourse under ICL, leaving a critical gap between recent advances in tabular ICL and the absence of actionable guidance for adversely affected individuals.

## Input / Output

Given a tabular query instance receiving an unfavorable prediction, a set of m labeled context demonstrations, a black-box ICL model (TabPFN, TabICL, or general LLMs such as GPT-4o, Qwen3, LLaMA), and feature-level feasibility constraints (immutability, monotonicity), ASR-ICL produces a sparse recourse instance that modifies only a small subset of actionable features to flip the prediction to the desired target class. The method operates under a fixed query budget (default 150 model evaluations) and enforces constraints via projection onto the feasible set, supporting both binary and multi-class tabular tasks without task-specific modifications.

## Method

ASR-ICL is a zeroth-order optimization framework that adaptively concentrates recourse search on a small subset of influential features (k ≪ d) rather than optimizing over the full feature space. At each iteration, it samples a temporary feature subspace according to a learned importance distribution, solves the low-dimensional recourse problem using RACOS (a derivative-free optimizer supporting mixed continuous-categorical spaces), and updates feature importance scores via exponential moving average based on the achieved objective value. Theoretically, the paper establishes that recourse under a single-layer linear self-attention ICL model is well-defined and bounded, with the optimal perturbation converging to the classical linear-model solution as context size grows, and derives a high-probability upper bound that tightens with more test-time demonstrations. The framework naturally extends to multi-class settings by reducing recourse success to a binary indicator of whether the target class is achieved.

## Results

On binary classification tasks (Australian Credit, COMPAS, Diabetes), ASR-ICL with TabPFN-2.5 achieves perfect validity (1.00) across all three datasets while maintaining average recourse costs of 3.83, 2.76, and 2.78 respectively—roughly 2–3× lower than DiCE (8.96, 7.51, 5.02) and FACE (6.18, 6.51, 5.13) at the same validity level. On multi-class tasks (Corporate Rating, Student Performance), ASR-ICL with TabPFN-2.5 reaches 0.98 and 1.00 validity with costs of 4.79 and 3.63. ASR-ICL also reduces the average query budget by 2–3× compared to a full-space zeroth-order baseline (e.g., 27 queries vs. 144 on Australian Credit with TabPFN-2.5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ASR-ICL is compared against three classical recourse baselines designed for fixed trained models: DiCE (optimization-based diverse counterfactuals), AR (constrained linear optimization), and FACE (graph-based manifold search). On validity, ASR-ICL matches or exceeds DiCE and FACE across all three binary datasets when paired with TabPFN-2.5 (1.00 vs. 1.00), while AR lags significantly (0.72–0.94). On average recourse cost, ASR-ICL substantially outperforms both DiCE and FACE: e.g., on Australian Credit, ASR-ICL achieves cost 3.83 versus 8.96 (DiCE) and 6.18 (FACE) at the same 1.00 validity, a 2.3× and 1.6× reduction respectively. AR produces the lowest cost (1.76 on Australian Credit) but at the expense of validity (0.82), making it less reliable in practice. The key distinction is that ASR-ICL operates in a fully black-box, context-conditioned setting where no gradients or model internals are available, whereas all three baselines assume access to a fixed trained classifier. GPT-4o shows the weakest ASR-ICL performance (validity 0.71–0.99), suggesting that noisier in-context decision rules increase recourse difficulty.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two largely separate research threads: algorithmic recourse for tabular data (Wachter et al., Ustun et al., Poyiadzi et al., Mothilal et al.), which universally assumes a fixed trained decision function, and in-context learning for tabular prediction (TabPFN, TabICL, LLM-based approaches), which focuses on accuracy but ignores recourse. The theoretical analysis builds directly on Zhang et al. (2024) for the closed-form ICL predictor under linear self-attention, and on Wachter et al. (2017) for the recourse objective formulation. The key conceptual departure is that ICL induces a context-conditioned, dynamic decision rule that violates the fixed-model assumption underlying all prior recourse methods, making the problem structurally different rather than merely a new application of existing algorithms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As ICL-based tabular predictors (TabPFN, TabICL, LLMs) become the default for high-stakes decision systems, the absence of recourse in this setting is a practical gap with direct regulatory implications (GDPR right to explanation). The convergence theorem showing ICL recourse approaches classical linear-model recourse as context size grows provides a principled justification for why larger context windows improve recourse quality, and the dimensionality-dependent bound motivates the adaptive subspace design. Open questions include whether the theoretical guarantees extend beyond the single-layer linear self-attention model to deeper or non-linear ICL architectures, how recourse stability behaves under adversarial or strategically chosen contexts, and whether the zeroth-order approach scales to very high-dimensional tabular spaces (d > 100) without further structural assumptions.
