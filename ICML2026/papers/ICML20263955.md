## Problem

Existing Nash regret bounds for linear bandits (Sawarni et al., 2023) suffer from a suboptimal d^{5/4} dependence on ambient dimension, caused by reliance on reward-estimate-dependent confidence intervals that require restrictive multiplicative concentration inequalities. Additionally, the broader problem of minimizing p-means regret—a unifying fairness-utility framework that generalizes Nash regret—had remained entirely open in the linear bandit setting.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a compact, unit-norm-bounded action set X ⊂ R^d, a time horizon T, a fairness parameter p ∈ R, and a sub-Gaussian noise parameter σ, and sequentially selects arms while observing σ-sub-Gaussian rewards with non-negative means. The output is an arm selection sequence over T rounds accompanied by order-optimal regret guarantees: Õ(d/√T) for Nash regret (p=0) and p ≥ 0, and Õ(d/√T · d^{|p|/2} · max{1,|p|}) for p < 0. A key structural constraint is that expected rewards must be non-negative for the geometric/power mean objectives to be well-defined, though realized rewards may be negative (sub-Gaussian).

## Method

FAIRLINBANDIT is a two-phase meta-algorithm whose core innovation is a data-adaptive stopping rule for Phase I that enables the use of standard additive UCB confidence intervals in Phase II, bypassing the reward-dependent Nash confidence bounds that caused the d^{5/4} bottleneck. Phase I interleaves D-optimal design pulls (maximizing information about θ*) with John ellipsoid distribution pulls (preserving welfare by keeping expected rewards bounded away from zero), running in geometrically increasing epochs until the stopping condition—based on the current parameter estimate and its confidence width—is satisfied. Phase II then initializes any optimistic linear bandit algorithm (LINUCB or LINPE) with the accumulated sufficient statistics, exploiting the well-conditioned design matrix from Phase I to ensure UCB widths remain below the optimal reward. The data-adaptive termination guarantees Phase I lasts Θ̃(d²/⟨θ*,x*⟩²) rounds, which is precisely what makes the UCB-based analysis tight enough to recover the order-optimal d/√T rate. The framework is plug-and-play: any optimistic linear bandit algorithm satisfying arm-independent confidence width and per-round regret bounds can be substituted into Phase II, yielding a generic reduction from Nash/p-means regret to average regret minimization.

## Results

The paper achieves an order-optimal Nash regret bound of Õ(σd/√T) for linear bandits, improving upon the previous state-of-the-art Õ(σd^{5/4}/√T) of LINNASH (Sawarni et al., 2023). For p-means regret, the bound is Õ(σd/√T) for p ≥ 0 and Õ(σd/√T · d^{|p|/2} · max(1,|p|)) for p < 0, establishing the first p-means regret guarantees in the linear bandit setting. Experimentally, on MSLR-WEB10K and Yahoo! LTRC (d=10, T=10^8), both FairLinPE and FairLinUCB converge to lower Nash regret than LINNASH, with LINNASH exhibiting instability at T=10^7. In runtime, FairLinPE is the fastest (272.87s at T=10^6) while FairLinUCB is the slowest (744.62s at T=10^6), reflecting a speed–convergence tradeoff.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is LINNASH (Sawarni et al., 2023), the prior state-of-the-art for Nash regret in linear bandits. On both MSLR-WEB10K and Yahoo! LTRC at T=10^8, both FairLinPE and FairLinUCB achieve lower Nash regret than LINNASH throughout the learning curve, with the gap widening over time (Figures 1a, 1b). At the shorter horizon T=10^7, LINNASH exhibits visibly unstable regret (Figure 1c), attributed to its reliance on reward-dependent confidence intervals that remain too wide to eliminate suboptimal arms effectively. In the p-means regret comparison (Figures 1d–f), FairLinUCB consistently outperforms FairLinPE across all tested values of p (0.5, −0.5, −1.5), while both substantially outperform what LINNASH would achieve (which is not directly plotted for p≠0 since LINNASH only targets Nash regret). Computationally, FairLinPE is roughly 3× faster than LINNASH at T=10^6 (272.87s vs. 864.61s) and about 1.07× faster at T=10^8 (3943.80s vs. 4213.14s), whereas FairLinUCB is slower than both due to its per-round matrix updates over the full arm set.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the two-phase LINNASH algorithm of Sawarni et al. (2023) for Nash regret in linear bandits, which achieved a suboptimal Õ(d^{5/4}/√T) bound due to its reliance on reward-estimate-dependent (Nash confidence bound) intervals. The key departure is a data-adaptive stopping rule for the exploration phase that enables switching to standard UCB-style confidence intervals, yielding the order-optimal Õ(d/√T) bound. The paper also extends the p-means regret framework of Krishna et al. (2025) and Barman et al. (2023) from finite-armed to linear bandits, and broadens the reward model from sub-Poisson to sub-Gaussian. The reduction framework further generalizes the approach to any optimistic linear bandit algorithm, connecting to the broader UCB/PE literature (Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011; Chu et al., 2011).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This resolves an explicitly open problem from Sawarni et al. (2023) and provides the first p-means regret guarantees in linear bandits, establishing a unified fairness-utility trade-off framework for infinite-armed settings. The 'no free lunch' result showing that regret degrades as p decreases (with the bound becoming vacuous unless T ≥ Ω(p²d^{|p|})) quantifies an intrinsic fairness-performance cost that practitioners in clinical trials or resource allocation should account for. The reduction framework is a useful modular tool, though its practical impact is currently limited to the linear bandit setting. Open questions include whether the p < -1 regret bound is tight and whether the framework extends to non-linear (e.g., logistic) reward models.
