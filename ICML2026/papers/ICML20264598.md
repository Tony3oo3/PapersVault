## Problem

Standard dimension reduction methods for single-cell transcriptomics (PCA, t-SNE, UMAP, VAEs) rely on Euclidean or log-transformed metrics that fail to preserve global geometric relationships between cell types, distorting long-range biological distances. The compositional nature of gene expression (counts sum to a fixed library size) and the stochastic sampling process of scRNA-seq are not explicitly accounted for, leading to embeddings that capture local neighborhoods but misrepresent the large-scale organization of the transcriptomic manifold.

## Input / Output

The method takes an L1-normalized single-cell RNA-seq count matrix (cells × genes) where each row is a probability vector on the (G−1)-simplex, and produces a low-dimensional cell-by-factor matrix (cells × K) whose rows also lie on a (K−1)-simplex, plus a gene-to-factor relation matrix (genes × K) with column-stochastic structure. The embedding is explicitly hyperspherical rather than Euclidean, and the number of factors K is selected by monitoring MDS loss saturation alongside inter-factor Pearson correlation to avoid redundant sub-factor splitting. The gene-to-factor matrix directly yields interpretable functional gene group assignments without manual feature engineering.

## Method

InfoGlobe models RNA counts as Multinomial samples and applies a square-root transformation to map the probability simplex onto a Fisher-Rao hypersphere, where geodesic (angular) distances between cells provide a principled long-range similarity measure. Extending Chentsov's theorem, the paper proves that coarse-graining genes into functional groups via a Markov embedding preserves the Fisher-Rao metric, making the low-dimensional factor manifold isometric to the high-dimensional gene manifold. The core optimization is a constrained matrix factorization X = AZ (both factors on multinomial simplices) trained with a combined loss: a per-cell Fisher-Rao reconstruction term aligning observed and reconstructed gene distributions, plus a global MDS-style term matching all pairwise Fisher-Rao distances between the original and embedded spaces. This geometry-preserving objective simultaneously recovers interpretable gene programs and a cell embedding that faithfully preserves both local and global manifold structure, enabling downstream tasks like trajectory inference, batch correction via ANOVA-identified batch factors, and spatial domain segmentation.

## Results

On simulated scRNA-seq data, InfoGlobe achieves a Spearman rank correlation of 0.88 for global distance preservation, substantially outperforming the best baseline (IRVAE at 0.51) and all VAE-based methods (scPHERE 0.69, scVI 0.36, FlatVI 0.45). For local structure, InfoGlobe scores 0.83 Trustworthiness and 0.84 Continuity versus 0.73–0.75 for the strongest baselines. On Leiden clustering, InfoGlobe embeddings yield ARI 0.70 and FMI 0.84, exceeding the best baselines (FlatVI ARI 0.58; scVI/IRVAE FMI 0.80). On the breast cancer Visium spatial segmentation task, SpaGCN with InfoGlobe embeddings improves ARI from 0.48 (PCA) to 0.56, surpassing SEDR (0.37) and matching or exceeding BayesSpace (0.51).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the four VAE-based dimension reduction methods (scPHERE, scVI, FlatVI, IRVAE), InfoGlobe wins on all three structure-preservation metrics, with the most dramatic gap on global Spearman correlation (0.88 vs. 0.36–0.51), confirming that Fisher-Rao geodesic supervision is critical for long-range geometry. Against the two Euclidean factorization methods (NMF, GBCD), InfoGlobe correctly separates qualitative (on/off) from quantitative (varying-strength) gene programs in simulation, whereas NMF and GBCD conflate distinct quantitative factors and introduce spurious gene–factor associations; InfoGlobe also maintains stable factor representations when cell-type proportions change, while NMF and GBCD embeddings are profoundly altered. On spatial domain segmentation, SpaGCN with InfoGlobe embeddings (ARI 0.56) outperforms SpaGCN with PCA (0.48) and SEDR (0.37), and slightly exceeds BayesSpace on ARI (0.56 vs. 0.51) while being marginally lower on NMI/AMI (0.62/0.61 vs. 0.63/0.62), indicating a trade-off where InfoGlobe better captures fine-grained boundary distinctions (DCIS/LCIS vs. IDC, tumor edge vs. healthy) at a small cost in overall mutual information.

## Relation to Prior Work

InfoGlobe carves new ground by grounding single-cell dimension reduction in the Fisher-Rao information geometry of Multinomial distributions, rather than in the Euclidean or decoder-induced pullback metrics used by VAE-based methods (scVI, scPHERE, FlatVI, IRVAE) or the variance-driven reconstruction objectives of NMF/GBCD. The key theoretical departure is an extension of Chentsov's theorem showing that the Fisher-Rao metric is isometric between gene-level and gene-group-level Multinomial manifolds, which justifies a geometry-preserving matrix factorization where both the gene-to-factor matrix and the cell embedding live on probability simplices. Unlike prior manifold-learning approaches that stitch local Euclidean neighborhoods to approximate geodesics, InfoGlobe uses the analytically tractable great-circle distance on the square-root-transformed hypersphere as an explicit global supervision signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The isometry result provides a principled, distribution-aware foundation for dimension reduction in scRNA-seq that simultaneously yields interpretable gene-program factors, preserves long-range cell-type relationships, and enables transparent batch correction without explicit alignment—addressing limitations that are common but rarely jointly resolved by existing methods. A key limitation acknowledged by the authors is the assumption of a globally constant gene-to-factor mapping, which may not hold in contexts with cell-type-specific co-expression rewiring; relaxing this assumption is identified as future work. The framework's reliance on pairwise Fisher-Rao distances for the MDS loss also raises scalability questions for atlas-scale datasets with millions of cells, though the running-time benchmark (Appendix F) suggests near-linear scaling in cell count for moderate gene numbers.
