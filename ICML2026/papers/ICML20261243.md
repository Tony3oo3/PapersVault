## Problem

Most multimodal fusion object detection methods adopt predefined, stage-aligned fusion architectures that restrict cross-modal interactions to corresponding backbone stages, failing to exploit semantic complementarity across hierarchical levels. Non-aligned fusion architectures enable richer cross-stage information flow but face exponentially growing combinatorial complexity (≈3.5×10¹⁰ configurations for 6 stages and 8 operators), making manual design infeasible. Existing NAS approaches for fusion architecture search suffer from a critical trade-off: differentiable search is efficient but prone to local optima, while evolutionary search explores globally but is computationally expensive and slow to converge.

## Input / Output

The method takes paired visible and infrared images as input, each processed through a YOLO11n backbone that produces stage-wise feature maps at 6 candidate fusion points (5 backbone stages plus input level). The search output is a discrete fusion architecture specifying which stages activate fusion, which of 8 candidate RGB-T operators (e.g., ICAFusion, CDC-YOLO, Fusion Mamba) are selected at each active stage, and how cross-stage fused features propagate. The instantiated architecture is then fully trained to produce a standard object detector outputting bounding boxes and class labels, evaluated via mAP@0.5 and mAP@0.5:0.95.

## Method

MFH-NAS introduces a Gated Fusion Strategy (GFS) selector that parameterizes each fusion stage with softmax-weighted operator coefficients (α) and a sigmoid activation gate (β), making the discrete fusion-operator and stage-activation choices differentiable. The search alternates between two stages in a closed loop: Stage I performs DARTS-style differentiable optimization over the supernet to rapidly learn local operator preferences and gate tendencies, while Stage II applies regularized evolution (selection, crossover, mutation) over top-K discrete candidates to explore global fusion topologies and escape local optima. The best evolved architecture from each round feeds back a correction (Δα, Δβ) to reinitialize the next differentiable round, balancing exploitation and exploration over T alternating cycles. After convergence, the final architecture is obtained by retaining GFS modules with sigmoid(β)>0.5 and selecting the highest-weight operator at each retained location, then training the instantiated network from scratch.

## Results

On M3FD, MFH-NAS achieves mAP@0.5 of 0.882 and mAP@0.5:0.95 of 0.598, outperforming the best single-modality YOLO11 baseline (0.794 VIS / 0.774 IR) by 8.8 percentage points in mAP@0.5 and exceeding the next-best NAS method (SMoA, 0.567) by 3.1 points in mAP@0.5:0.95. On LLVIP, MFH-NAS reaches 0.961 mAP@0.5 and 0.661 mAP@0.5:0.95, and on RGBT-Tiny it attains 0.487 mAP@0.5 and 0.247 mAP@0.5:0.95, both the best among all compared methods. The total search cost is 2.1 GPU-days, moderate relative to DC-NAS (2.8) and BM-NAS (0.9).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MFH-NAS achieves the best mAP@0.5 and mAP@0.5:0.95 on all three benchmarks. On M3FD, it surpasses the strongest NAS baseline BM-NAS (0.877) by 0.5 pp in mAP@0.5 and SMoA (0.567) by 3.1 pp in mAP@0.5:0.95; the gap over handcrafted fusion methods is even larger (e.g., +4.5 pp over Fusion Mamba's original 0.834). On LLVIP, gains are smaller due to the already-saturated performance of all methods (>0.95 mAP@0.5), with MFH-NAS edging out BM-NAS (0.959) by 0.2 pp and ParZC (0.660) by 0.001 in mAP@0.5:0.95. On RGBT-Tiny, MFH-NAS leads DC-NAS (0.474) by 1.3 pp in mAP@0.5 and SMoA (0.243) by 0.4 pp in mAP@0.5:0.95. In the single-fusion-method ablation (Table 1), MFH-NAS's average mAP@0.5 of 0.842 across 12 fusion operators exceeds the handcrafted average (0.826) and all four NAS baselines (DC-NAS 0.837, BM-NAS 0.837, EG-NAS 0.840, SMoA 0.841). The search cost of 2.1 GPU-days is higher than BM-NAS (0.9) and SMoA (1.1) but lower than DC-NAS (2.8), reflecting the overhead of the alternating differentiable–evolutionary loop.

## Relation to Prior Work

MFH-NAS sits at the intersection of two NAS paradigms: differentiable search (DARTS-style continuous relaxation, Liu et al. 2018) and evolutionary search (Regularized Evolution, Real et al. 2019), which prior multimodal fusion NAS methods (BM-NAS, DC-NAS, EG-NAS, SMoA) have used in isolation. The key departure is the explicit modeling of cross-stage, non-aligned fusion connectivity as a searchable dimension alongside fusion-operator selection, whereas most prior RGB-T fusion work fixes stage-aligned fusion and only designs the fusion module. The Gated Fusion Strategy selector, which jointly learns whether to fuse and which operator to apply at each stage, extends the search space beyond what bilevel or divide-and-conquer NAS formulations have explored for multimodal detection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that jointly searching fusion operators and cross-stage topology yields consistent gains over both handcrafted stage-aligned designs and single-paradigm NAS baselines across three benchmarks, validating that the fusion architecture itself is a meaningful design degree of freedom. However, the cross-dataset transfer experiments reveal that discovered architectures are strongly dataset-specific (e.g., an M3FD-searched architecture drops from 0.882 to 0.755 mAP@0.5 on RGBT-Tiny), limiting the practical reusability of a single searched architecture. Open questions include whether the hybrid search scales to more than two modalities or to larger backbones, and whether the 2.1 GPU-day search cost is acceptable for iterative deployment in safety-critical settings like autonomous driving.
