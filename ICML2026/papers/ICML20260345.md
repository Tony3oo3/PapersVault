## Problem

Curriculum-style post-training (e.g., depth-increasing or hint-decreasing schedules) has been empirically shown to improve LLM reasoning sample efficiency, yet no principled theory explains when, why, or in what sense curricula help compared to direct post-training. Existing curriculum learning theory is confined to train-from-scratch settings with problem-specific difficulty notions, leaving the post-training regime—where a strong pretrained base model already assigns non-trivial probability to correct reasoning trajectories—largely unaddressed.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (a reasoning problem such as parity, Countdown, or MATH) and a pretrained transformer base model (e.g., Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct) as input, and produces an autoregressive chain-of-thought trajectory terminated by an EOS token, from which the final answer is read off the pre-EOS state. The theoretical analysis additionally outputs sample-complexity and oracle-query-complexity bounds: the key quantity is the coverage coefficient ‖π⋆/π_ref‖∞, which inversely controls the base model's success rate and thus the number of samples needed to observe an informative correct CoT.

## Method

The paper introduces a 2-States Conditioned Autoregressive Reasoning Tree (2S-ART) that models CoT generation as a state-conditioned autoregressive process over a tree, where each step selects a legal index and updates a reasoning state via a two-state map Φℓ. Under a Probabilistic 2S-ART (PART) base model that assigns uniform probability to legal children, the success probability of depth-ℓ tasks decays as Θ(d^{-(ℓ+1)}), making the coverage coefficient grow exponentially with depth. The central result (Corollary 1) shows that if a curriculum of L stages keeps each stage's sample complexity at most the L/p-th root of the target's complexity (p ≪ L), the total curriculum cost is polynomial while direct post-training is exponential. Two concrete curricula—depth-increasing (truncate CoTs to progressively longer prefixes) and hint-decreasing (provide progressively shorter correct prefixes)—are shown to satisfy this condition, yielding Θ((k⋆+1)d²) sample complexity for RL fine-tuning and test-time scaling versus Ω(d^{2k⋆+2}) without curriculum (Theorems 2–3). A transformer with attention-based index selection and FFN-based atomic operations (e.g., XOR for parity) is proven to faithfully replicate the PART base model (Theorem 1), grounding the theory in a concrete architecture.

## Results

Theoretically, curriculum post-training reduces RL fine-tuning sample complexity from Ω̃(d^{2k*+2}) (no-curriculum) to Õ((k*+1)d²), and test-time scaling oracle-query complexity from Ω̃(d^{2k*}) to Õ((k*+1)d²), an exponential-to-polynomial gap. On the synthetic parity task (d=8, k*=3), curriculum fine-tuning converges at n≈76 samples versus n≈1.6M for no-curriculum, and curriculum test-time scaling converges at n≈1,410 oracle queries versus n≈251,410. On large-scale benchmarks (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct), both curriculum strategies outperform no-curriculum GRPO: on Countdown, Hint-Decreasing achieves 42.6% mean Pass@K vs. 36.0% for no-curriculum; on MATH, Depth-Increasing reaches 38.2% vs. 36.3%; and on Blocksworld, Hint-Decreasing leads at 9.2% vs. 8.3%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four methods: the unmodified base model (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct), no-curriculum GRPO fine-tuning, Depth-Increasing Curriculum, and Hint-Decreasing Curriculum. On Countdown, both curriculum strategies clearly beat no-curriculum GRPO (40.5% and 42.6% vs. 36.0% mean Pass@K), with Hint-Decreasing showing the largest gains on harder levels (Level 5: 9.4% vs. 3.8%). On MATH, Depth-Increasing edges out no-curriculum (38.2% vs. 36.3% average), while Hint-Decreasing is slightly weaker overall (33.8%) but competitive at large K and on the hardest level (15.4% vs. 14.8%). On Blocksworld, gains are smaller and more curriculum-dependent: Hint-Decreasing leads (9.2% vs. 8.3% no-curriculum) while Depth-Increasing is nearly tied (8.4%). In the synthetic parity experiments, the gap is dramatic: curriculum fine-tuning converges ~20,000× faster in sample complexity (n≈76 vs. n≈1.6M), and curriculum test-time scaling converges ~178× faster in oracle queries (n≈1,410 vs. n≈251,410), directly validating the theoretical exponential-to-polynomial reduction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the curriculum learning literature from pre-training (Bengio et al., 2009; Weinshall et al., 2018; Abbe et al., 2023b; Cornacchia & Mossel, 2023) to the post-training regime, where a strong pretrained base model already assigns non-trivial probability to reasoning trajectories. It builds on the coverage coefficient framework of Foster et al. (2025) and rejection sampling analysis of Block & Polyanskiy (2023) to formalize task difficulty as the rarity of correct CoTs under the base policy, and on transformer CoT theory (Kim & Suzuki, 2025; Wen et al., 2025a; Nichani et al., 2024) to instantiate the analysis in a concrete architecture. The key departure from prior work is that all existing curriculum theory targets train-from-scratch settings with problem-specific difficulty notions, whereas this paper provides a general, base-model-relative characterization (Cor. 1) that applies to post-training starting from a pretrained transformer, and introduces the 2S-ART abstraction to unify parity, Countdown, Markov-chain, and causal-graph reasoning under one framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first principled theoretical explanation for why curriculum post-training improves sample efficiency in LLM reasoning, providing a clean necessary-and-sufficient condition (cumulative stage difficulty vs. final difficulty) and an exponential-to-polynomial sample complexity guarantee for both RL fine-tuning and test-time scaling. The 2S-ART framework offers a reusable abstraction for future theoretical work on structured reasoning. Limitations include the analysis being restricted to a specific transformer reparameterization with a single trainable matrix, the d² per-stage rate not being minimax-tight, and large-scale experiments (Table 1) showing only modest gains on Blocksworld, suggesting the theory's conditions may not always hold in practice. Open questions include extending the framework to diffusion language models (discussed in the conclusion), relaxing the uniform-probability-on-children assumption, and understanding whether the exponential gap persists under more sophisticated optimization that exploits cross-sample structure.
