## Problem

Momentum methods (Heavy-Ball, Nesterov) are ubiquitous in deep learning and widely believed to reduce gradient noise and accelerate convergence, yet their behavior under distribution shift—where past gradients become systematically stale—remains theoretically understudied. This paper asks: when does momentum provably help versus hurt for strongly convex, smooth objectives whose minimizer drifts over time, and is the resulting tracking penalty an analytical artifact or an information-theoretic barrier?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263934/cropped_page6_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes noisy first-order gradient samples drawn from a time-varying conditional distribution Π_{t+1}|F_t, parameterized by a step size γ and a momentum coefficient β ∈ [0,1) (with a two-parameter formulation β₁+β₂=β, β₁β₂=0 that unifies Heavy-Ball and Nesterov). Outputs are structured as a three-term tracking-error decomposition—exponentially decaying initialization transient, irreducible noise floor, and drift-induced lag—each carrying explicit (1−β)⁻¹ or (1−β)⁻² amplification factors for momentum methods, alongside minimax dynamic-regret lower bounds under an L_{p,q} gradient-variation budget.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a unified Lyapunov analysis that recasts SGDM as a 2d-dimensional augmented-state linear recursion (via a mode-splitting transform), yielding finite-time expectation and high-probability tracking bounds with explicit momentum penalties that diverge as β→1. High-probability guarantees are obtained through conditional Ψ₂-Orlicz norms and an optional-stopping argument for weighted martingale sums, producing time-resolved drift functionals that exponentially forget older perturbations and reveal a drift-noise coupling absent in prior uniform-drift analyses. The paper complements these upper bounds with minimax lower bounds via a Fano-inequality testing reduction over a J-block packing of localized bump losses, decomposing the minimax risk into a statistical (information-limited) term and an inertia-limited term that enforces an unavoidable lag window of length Ω(κ/(1−β)) per regime change. In the uniformly spread drift regime, the tuned upper bound matches the minimax rate, confirming the momentum penalty is information-theoretic rather than an artifact of the analysis.

## Results

On a drifting strongly convex quadratic (d=100), the headline finding is that momentum methods degrade catastrophically as β→1 under drift: at γ=0.10, β=0.99, σ²=0.8, Heavy-Ball tracking error reaches 401.37 while SGD remains at 4.11 (≈98× worse). Conversely, at low momentum and small step size (β=0.5, γ=0.01, σ²=0.1), HB/NAG outperform SGD (0.34/0.35 vs. 1.04), confirming the noise-suppression benefit in the information-limited regime. The theoretical contribution is an explicit (1−β)⁻¹ noise-floor inflation and (1−β)⁻² initialization amplification in the tracking bound, plus a minimax lower bound proving an unavoidable inertia window of length Ω(κ/(1−β)) for any SGDM(β) policy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three first-order methods—vanilla SGD (Robbins & Monro, 1951), Polyak Heavy-Ball (Polyak, 1963), and Nesterov acceleration (Nesterov, 1983)—on drifting quadratics, linear/logistic regression, and a teacher-student MLP. In the low-momentum, small-step-size regime (β=0.5, γ=0.01), HB and NAG beat SGD by roughly 3× on tracking error (0.34 vs. 1.04), consistent with the information-limited regime where temporal averaging suppresses noise. However, as β increases toward 1, the advantage reverses dramatically: at γ=0.10, β=0.99, σ²=0.8, HB's tracking error explodes to 401.37 (≈98× SGD's 4.11) and NAG to 38.32 (≈9× SGD), while SGD remains nearly flat across all β values. The degradation is amplified by larger step sizes, higher noise, and ill-conditioning (κ=1000 vs. κ=10), matching the theoretical (1−β)⁻¹ and (1−β)⁻² penalties. The minimax lower bound confirms this is not an artifact of the analysis: any SGDM(β) policy must incur an inertia window of Ω(κ/(1−β)) steps after a regime shift, making the momentum penalty information-theoretically unavoidable in drift-dominated settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the nonstationary stochastic optimization tracking literature (Cutler et al., 2021; Besbes et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2019) by incorporating momentum methods (Heavy-Ball and Nesterov) into the analysis, which prior tracking results largely omitted. It builds on the steady-state equivalence results of Yuan et al. (2016) and Kovachki & Stuart (2021) showing momentum ≈ rescaled SGD in stationary settings, but departs by proving that under distribution shift this equivalence breaks down: momentum incurs explicit (1−β)⁻¹ and (1−β)⁻² amplification penalties. The minimax lower bound via Fano's inequality and a J-block hard-instance construction is a new technical contribution not present in prior dynamic-regret work (Jadbabaie et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2016), which did not isolate an algorithm-specific inertia penalty.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first information-theoretic justification for the empirical observation that momentum degrades under drift, showing the penalty is unavoidable rather than a tuning artifact. The drift-noise coupling and time-resolved high-probability bounds directly motivate practical restart/windowing heuristics for adaptive systems. Key open questions include extending the analysis to adaptive optimizers (Adam, AdamW) whose EMA moments introduce analogous stale-history effects, recovering Nesterov's √κ acceleration in drifting settings, and analyzing time-varying β schedules. The scope is limited to strongly convex, L-smooth objectives with fixed β, so the results do not immediately transfer to the nonconvex, ill-conditioned landscapes typical of deep learning.
