## Problem

GRPO is the dominant RL algorithm for LLM reasoning training, yet it is assumed to operate solely with outcome-level rewards, while process reward models (PRMs) are known to enable finer-grained credit assignment. The paper proves that GRPO covertly implements a non-trivial Monte-Carlo-based PRM whenever trajectories within a group share overlapping prefixes, and then identifies a structural flaw in this hidden PRM: the frequency of a shared prefix compounds its advantage signal, simultaneously hindering exploration of high-reward steps and exploitation of high-reward trajectories under different conditions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (reasoning query) and samples a group of k trajectories (text completions) from the policy; the implicit PRM structure is defined entirely by the prefix-overlap tree B(G) induced by these sampled trajectories, with no additional input required. The output is a reasoning trajectory ending in a \boxed{} answer, and the training signal is derived from outcome-level rewards (e.g., exact-match correctness) that are decomposed into step-level rewards via Monte Carlo averaging over prefix-sharing trajectory subsets. The λ-GRPO modification adds a per-token scaling factor |λ^(i,t)|^{-1} computed from the B(G) tree, introducing negligible overhead (~10 seconds per training run).

## Method

The paper first proves (Theorem 1) that the standard GRPO loss with outcome rewards is algebraically equivalent to a PRM-aware RL objective where step-level rewards are Monte Carlo estimates: for each process set λ (a subset of trajectories sharing a common prefix), the step reward is the mean outcome reward of trajectories in λ, and the token-level advantage is normalized by the group-wide reward standard deviation. The key structural insight is that the GRPO loss, when re-expressed over the B(G) prefix-overlap tree, scales each process set's contribution by its cardinality |λ|, which amplifies both positive and negative advantages in proportion to how many trajectories share that prefix. λ-GRPO corrects this by dividing each token's loss term by |λ^(i,t)|, the size of the process set containing that token, so that every process set contributes equally regardless of how many trajectories share it. Empirically, λ-GRPO outperforms standard GRPO on 15/20 benchmark cells across AIME24, MATH-500, AMC23, Minerva, and OlympiadBench, and reaches peak validation accuracy in roughly half the training steps.

## Results

On five reasoning benchmarks (AIME24, MATH-500, AMC23, Minerva, OlympiadBench), λ-GRPO outperforms standard GRPO on 15 out of 20 individual benchmark cells across Qwen-1.5B and Llama-1B models. The strongest result is Qwen with β=0.04, where λ-GRPO achieves 0.5739 average accuracy versus 0.5394 for GRPO and 0.5175 for the base model. All four λ-GRPO models reach peak validation accuracy in fewer than half the training steps of their GRPO counterparts, representing a >10% validation accuracy improvement and an approximate 2× training speedup.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is standard GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), the original group-relative policy optimization algorithm with outcome-level rewards. λ-GRPO wins on 15 of 20 individual benchmark cells and on average for 3 of 4 model configurations; the single exception is Llama with β=0.04, where λ-GRPO's average (0.0973) falls slightly below GRPO's (0.0996), though λ-GRPO still wins on 3 of 5 individual tasks in that setting. The largest gains appear on AIME24 (e.g., Qwen β=0.04: 0.4000 vs. 0.3000) and AMC23 (Qwen β=0.0: 0.7500 vs. 0.6250), while MATH-500 shows the most mixed results with GRPO occasionally matching or exceeding λ-GRPO. In all four configurations, λ-GRPO also surpasses the untrained base model on average, whereas standard GRPO fails to improve over base on Qwen β=0.0 (0.4837 vs. 0.5175). Additionally, λ-GRPO reaches peak validation accuracy in roughly half the training steps of GRPO, indicating faster convergence alongside higher final performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and its DAPO token-level variant (Yu et al., 2025), proving that the standard GRPO objective with outcome-level rewards is mathematically equivalent to a PRM-aware RL objective equipped with a Monte-Carlo-based PRM derived from shared trajectory prefixes. This reframes the relationship between GRPO and the broader PRM literature (Lightman et al., 2024; Kazemnejad et al., 2025; Hou et al., 2025), which has typically required either explicit step-level annotation or algorithmic modifications to incorporate finer-grained rewards. The proposed λ-GRPO modification departs from prior GRPO+PRM adaptations (Shao et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025b) by exploiting the implicit PRM structure already present in vanilla GRPO rather than introducing an external reward signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical equivalence provides a clean lens for understanding GRPO's credit assignment dynamics and identifies a concrete flaw (frequency-scaled advantage) that hinders both exploration and exploitation under imbalanced process steps. The λ-GRPO fix is computationally negligible and yields consistent improvements over standard GRPO, suggesting practitioners can gain step-level credit assignment benefits without the cost of training or deploying explicit PRMs. However, experiments are limited to 1B–1.5B models on a single training dataset, and the anti-exploitation effect is only partially addressed, leaving open whether the implicit PRM structure and the λ-GRPO correction scale to larger models and more diverse reasoning tasks.
