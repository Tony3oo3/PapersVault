## Problem

Existing representational similarity metrics (CKA, RSA, mKNN, etc.) are systematically confounded by network scale: wider models produce spuriously higher similarity due to dimensionality-driven null baselines, and deeper models inflate scores when the maximum over many layer pairs is reported. This undermines the comparative use of these metrics and casts doubt on the Platonic Representation Hypothesis, which claims that scaling drives cross-modal representational convergence. No prior calibration was both metric-agnostic and simultaneously addressed both the width and depth confounders.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pair of row-aligned embedding matrices (X ∈ R^{n×d_x}, Y ∈ R^{n×d_y}) and a black-box similarity function, plus K permutation draws (e.g., 200) and a significance level α (e.g., 0.05). For the aggregation-aware variant, it additionally takes layer-wise representations from two models of depths L_A and L_B and an aggregation operator T (e.g., max, top-k). The output is a calibrated score in [0,1] that is max-preserving (perfect alignment remains 1) with a principled zero point, alongside an add-one permutation p-value and the empirical null critical value τ_α; when the metric is unbounded, the unnormalized excess [s − τ_α]_+ is returned instead.

## Method

The core contribution is a unified permutation-based null-calibration framework that transforms any similarity metric into a calibrated effect size. For scalar comparisons (addressing the width confounder), K null scores are generated by permuting sample correspondences; a critical threshold τ_α is extracted as the (1−α)-quantile of the combined observed-plus-null multiset, and the calibrated score rescales the excess over τ_α to [0,1]. For selection-based summaries (addressing the depth confounder), the same permutation is applied consistently across all layers of one model, and the null distribution of the final aggregate statistic (e.g., max over all L_A×L_B layer pairs) is computed, recovering the classical maxT procedure as a special case. Theoretically, the paper proves that spectral metrics (CKA, RV) have null baselines scaling as O(d/n) via random matrix theory, while neighborhood metrics (mKNN) scale only as O(k/n), and that max-aggregation inflates by O(σ√(log M)) under sub-Gaussian tails. The framework provides finite-sample Type-I error control under an exchangeability assumption and is invariant to monotone transformations of the raw score for the p-value, while the calibrated score deliberately breaks monotone invariance to correct scale-dependent baselines.

## Results

After null-calibration, the previously reported global spectral convergence (CKA) largely disappears: the Pearson correlation between CKA scores and language-model capability drops from 0.86 to 0.45, and Procrustes distance correlation falls from 0.89 to 0.39. In contrast, local neighborhood metrics retain strong cross-modal alignment even after calibration—mKNN correlation stays near 0.85 and CKNNA near 0.87. Under the null hypothesis, calibrated scores collapse to zero across all (n, d/n) configurations, with Type-I error controlled at or below the nominal α=0.05. The same pattern extends to video-language alignment, where calibrated CKA shows no scaling trend but mKNN retains a clear trend for VideoMAE-Large/Huge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed null-calibration framework is the only method that simultaneously achieves metric-agnostic debiasing, preserves a bounded [0,1] score range, and handles aggregation-aware calibration (e.g., max over layer pairs). Murphy et al. (2024) and Chun et al. (2025) provide analytical debiasing for CKA specifically but do not generalize to other metrics, do not preserve a bounded upper bound, and cannot correct the depth confounder from layer-pair selection. Cui et al. (2022) and Cai et al. (2019) address input-structure confounding in RSA/CKA via regression or Bayesian methods but only partially remove the null baseline and are not aggregation-aware. Diedrichsen et al. (2021) offer a whitened unbiased RSA variant that is partially bounded but still metric-specific and not aggregation-aware. Smilde et al. (2009) correct the RV coefficient analytically but only for that single metric. The paper empirically verifies that its permutation-based calibration closely matches the analytical CKA bias correction of Murphy et al. (2024), confirming equivalence in the CKA case while extending to all metric families (spectral, geometric, neighborhood) without metric-specific derivations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al., 2024) and the rich literature of representational similarity metrics (CKA, CCA, RSA, mKNN), but departs from prior debiasing efforts (Murphy et al. 2024 for CKA, Diedrichsen et al. 2021 for RSA) by proposing a unified, metric-agnostic permutation-based calibration that also handles the aggregation step (max over layer pairs) via a generalization of the maxT procedure (Westfall & Young, 1993). Where prior work identified metric-specific finite-sample biases, this paper identifies two structural confounders—width (O(d/n) null baselines from random matrix theory) and depth (selection inflation from multiple comparisons)—and shows they jointly explain the apparent scaling trends in representational convergence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that global spectral convergence largely vanishes after calibration while local neighborhood alignment persists reframes the Platonic Representation Hypothesis into the weaker Aristotelian one, with direct implications for how the neuro-AI and multimodal alignment literatures should interpret published similarity scores. The calibration framework is simple, general, and provides finite-sample Type-I error guarantees, making it a practical tool for any representational similarity study. Key open questions include why representations converge in local neighborhoods but not global geometry, and whether the exchangeability assumption (required for valid permutation tests) holds for grouped or structured data. The paper also notes that representational similarity has no ground-truth scale, so the framework reports presence/absence of calibrated evidence rather than proving convergence.
