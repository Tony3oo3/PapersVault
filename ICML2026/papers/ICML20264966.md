## Problem

Integrating standard Galerkin/FEM assembly into Python-based automatic differentiation frameworks (e.g., PyTorch) introduces severe computational bottlenecks: scatter-add loops over 10⁵–10⁷ elements fragment the computation graph into O(Ek²) autograd nodes, causing GPU underutilization, Python interpreter overhead, and prohibitive backpropagation costs. This limits the practical efficiency of both numerical PDE solvers and physics-informed learning methods (PINNs, VPINNs, Deep Ritz) on large or unstructured meshes.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes unstructured mesh geometry (triangular/tetrahedral elements with arbitrary domain shapes), spatially-varying PDE coefficients ρ, source/forcing terms, boundary conditions (Dirichlet, Neumann, Robin), and initial conditions for time-dependent problems. Outputs are FEM coefficient vectors on the mesh (rather than pointwise values), assembled sparse stiffness matrices and load vectors, learned solution operators mapping inputs to full spatio-temporal trajectories, and optimized material density fields for topology design. The method natively handles mixed boundary conditions and dynamic mesh topologies without recompilation.

## Method

TENSORGALERKIN reformulates Galerkin assembly as a two-stage Map-Reduce pipeline co-designed inside PyTorch's autograd. Stage I (Batch-Map) lifts the element index to a batch dimension and computes all local stiffness matrices and load vectors via a single dense tensor contraction (torch.einsum), fusing loops over elements, basis functions, and quadrature points into one GPU kernel. Stage II (Sparse-Reduce) replaces imperative scatter-add with precomputed binary routing matrices and a deterministic sparse matrix multiplication (SpMM), aggregating local contributions into the global sparse system. This architecture compresses the backward computation graph from O(Ek²) nodes to O(1), independent of mesh size. The framework powers three downstream applications: TENSORMESH (a GPU-native FEM solver), TENSORPILS (physics-informed operator learning that computes spatial derivatives analytically via shape-function gradients rather than AD, using a GNN backbone to predict FEM coefficients), and TENSOROPT (end-to-end differentiable PDE-constrained optimization where adjoint gradients propagate through the same Map-Reduce pipeline via a differentiable sparse solver layer).

## Results

As a neural PDE solver, TENSORPILS achieves 0.56% relative L² error on the K=2 checkerboard Poisson problem, outperforming Deep Ritz (3.34%), PINN (0.90%), and VPINN (11.58%) while training 2× faster (117.8 it/s vs. 58.7 it/s for Deep Ritz). For physics-informed operator learning, TENSORPILS achieves 0.085±0.010 relative L² error on the wave equation (in-distribution) and 0.083±0.013 on the Allen-Cahn equation (out-of-distribution), beating the fully data-driven baseline (0.230±0.017 and 0.152±0.080) and PI-DeepONet (0.626±0.033 and 8.536±6.306) by at least an order of magnitude. As a numerical PDE solver, TENSORMESH on GPU achieves a 3× speedup over FEniCS on 3D Poisson and >10× over its own CPU version on 3D elasticity at 10⁶ DoFs. For PDE-constrained topology optimization, TENSOROPT completes a 51-iteration SIMP compliance minimization in 8.35 s versus 31.13 s for JAX-FEM (3.7× speedup) with <0.33% compliance difference.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FEniCS, Firedrake, MFEM, scikit-fem, and torch-fem (all CPU FEM solvers), TENSORMESH on GPU achieves the shortest runtimes across 3D Poisson and elasticity benchmarks, with a 3× speedup over FEniCS (the best CPU baseline) on Poisson and >10× over its own CPU variant on elasticity at 10⁶ DoFs, while matching or improving residual accuracy. Against JAX-FEM (the primary GPU FEM baseline), TENSORMESH is orders of magnitude faster on solve-time scaling, and TENSOROPT achieves a 3.7× end-to-end speedup on the 2D cantilever topology optimization task while converging to topologically identical designs (<0.33% compliance difference). As a neural PDE solver, TENSORPILS outperforms PINN (which requires two AD passes for the Laplacian) by 38% in error at K=2 and 2.7× in training speed; it beats VPINN by 95% in error at K=2; and it surpasses Deep Ritz by 50% in error while training 2× faster. For physics-informed operator learning, TENSORPILS dominates PI-DeepONet by at least an order of magnitude in relative L² error on both wave and Allen-Cahn equations, and surprisingly outperforms the fully data-driven AGN baseline in the out-of-distribution regime (0.090 vs. 0.230 on wave; 0.083 vs. 0.152 on Allen-Cahn), demonstrating that physics-informed training enables better extrapolation than supervised learning with 16 training samples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reformulates the classical Galerkin/FEM assembly procedure (Brenner & Scott) as a two-stage tensorized Map-Reduce operation—batched einsum for local physics and sparse matrix multiplication for global topology—co-designed within PyTorch's autograd to achieve an O(1) backward graph regardless of mesh size. It departs from AD-reliant physics-informed methods (PINNs, VPINNs, Deep Ritz) by computing spatial derivatives analytically via pre-computed shape function gradients rather than multiple backpropagation passes, and from JIT-compiled frameworks like JAX-FEM by leveraging PyTorch's eager execution to handle dynamic meshes without recompilation. The differentiable sparse solver TORCH-SLA (Chi & Wen, 2026) provides the O(1)-graph adjoint solve that keeps the PDE-constrained optimization loop within the same autograd pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(1)-graph property is the key practical contribution: it eliminates the Python-loop and autograd-fragmentation overheads that have made GPU-accelerated FEM within Python AD frameworks intractable at scale, enabling 3–50× speedups over CPU FEM stacks and order-of-magnitude gains over JAX-FEM. The unified solve-learn-optimize framework (TENSORMESH/TENSORPILS/TENSOROPT) lowers the barrier for differentiable PDE workflows in the PyTorch ecosystem. However, the framework is restricted to PDEs with a variational (Galerkin) structure, does not yet cover discontinuous Galerkin or Petrov-Galerkin methods, and time-dependent problems are treated only briefly via Method of Lines—extending to non-variational and fully nonlinear PDEs remains open.
