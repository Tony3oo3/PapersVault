## Problem

Existing tool-augmented agent benchmarks rely almost exclusively on final-answer accuracy, masking critical differences in reasoning quality such as redundant tool calls, hallucinated facts, and inability to recover from tool failures. Comparing an agent's trajectory against a single ground-truth path is infeasible because multiple valid trajectories exist for the same query, and annotating all of them is prohibitively expensive. The paper asks whether an LLM-based evaluator can assess trajectory quality across multiple dimensions without any ground-truth trajectory reference.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262146/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

TRACE consumes a complete ReAct-style trajectory (an ordered sequence of thought–action–input–observation tuples), the original user query, and any associated input files (images, documents). It produces three scalar scores: an efficiency ratio in [0,1] (necessary evidence / total evidence), a hallucination rate in [0,1] (fraction of steps whose thought is not grounded in prior evidence), and an adaptivity score in [0,1] (fraction of tool-failure events followed by a sensible alternative action). Efficiency is computed only on trajectories that reach the correct final answer, while hallucination and adaptivity are evaluated regardless of final correctness.

## Method

The core mechanism is an evidence bank E that incrementally stores each step's (action, input, observation) tuple as the agent progresses. For efficiency, an LLM evaluator examines the final answer alongside the complete evidence bank and identifies the minimal subset E_min of evidence logically necessary to derive that answer; the efficiency score is |E_min|/|E_n|. For hallucination, each thought th_t is checked against the evidence bank state E_{t-1} via an IsGrounded LLM call that flags any specific verifiable claim not supported by prior observations. For adaptivity, when an observation signals a tool failure, the LLM evaluator judges whether the next step's thought acknowledges the failure and the action selects a sensible alternative. The key design insight is that structuring trajectory information into discrete evidence tuples (rather than feeding the raw unstructured dialog) enables accurate multi-dimensional evaluation even with small open-source models like Llama-3.1-8B, which outperform naive LLM-as-a-Judge baselines by up to 19 points on efficiency.

## Results

On the Meta-GTA meta-evaluation dataset, TRACE consistently outperforms the naive LLM-as-a-Judge baseline across all models and metrics. The largest gains appear with smaller open-source models: Llama-3.1-8B improves from 55.67% to 70.46% on efficiency (+14.79) and from 44.61% to 64.05% on Meta-m&m's efficiency (+19.44). Against PIPA's state consistency metric, TRACE achieves 94.64% vs. 84.46% (Claude-Sonnet-4) on all Meta-GTA trajectories, and shows substantially lower variance across multiple valid trajectories (98.68±1.26 vs. 77.31±9.96). When applied to evaluate 9 real LLM agents on GTA, TRACE reveals that models with near-identical overall accuracy (e.g., Qwen-72B at 0.5202, GPT-4.1 at 0.5281, o3-mini at 0.5263) differ markedly in efficiency, hallucination rate, and adaptivity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRACE is compared against two baselines. First, against the naive LLM-as-a-Judge approach (evaluating the full unstructured dialog without an evidence bank), TRACE wins on nearly all metrics across all five evaluator models, with the most dramatic gains for smaller models (e.g., +14.79 on efficiency for Llama-3.1-8B on Meta-GTA, +19.44 on Meta-m&m's). Second, against PIPA's state consistency metric (the closest existing trajectory evaluation method), TRACE achieves higher accuracy on all Meta-GTA trajectories (e.g., 94.64 vs. 84.46 with Claude-Sonnet-4; 70.46 vs. 41.20 with Llama-3.1-8B) and substantially lower variance when multiple valid trajectories exist for the same query (98.68±1.26 vs. 77.31±9.96 with Claude-Sonnet-4), demonstrating that the evidence bank makes evaluation robust to trajectory ordering while PIPA's accumulated-state approach degrades with multiple input files.

## Relation to Prior Work

TRACE builds directly on the ReAct framework's trajectory structure (thought, action, input, observation) and the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm, but departs from both by introducing an evidence bank that structurally accumulates tool outputs step-by-step rather than feeding the full unstructured dialog to an evaluator. It extends the line of trajectory-level evaluation initiated by PIPA (state consistency) and ToolBEHonest (hallucination detection), which each target a single dimension, into a unified multi-dimensional, reference-free assessment covering efficiency, hallucination, and adaptivity simultaneously. The meta-evaluation datasets (Meta-GTA, Meta-m&m's) augment existing benchmarks that rely on single ground-truth trajectories, addressing the known limitation that multiple valid solution paths exist for the same query.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a practical, cost-effective way to diagnose *why* an agent fails or succeeds beyond a binary accuracy score, which is critical as tool-augmented agents move toward production deployment where reasoning quality (not just answer correctness) determines reliability. The finding that small open-source models (Llama-3.1-8B) achieve competitive evaluation accuracy with TRACE (vs. 55.67% for naive LLM-as-a-Judge) significantly lowers the barrier to trajectory evaluation at scale. Open questions include whether the evidence bank approach generalizes to very long trajectories (beyond 10 steps), to non-ReAct architectures beyond the limited Magentic-One demonstration, and whether the three chosen dimensions (efficiency, hallucination, adaptivity) capture the full space of reasoning quality or miss important aspects like planning coherence or information integration.
