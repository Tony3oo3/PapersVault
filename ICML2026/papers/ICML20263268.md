## Problem

Existing multi-modal brain network analysis methods treat phenotypic information as auxiliary features fused only at the classifier level, implicitly assuming that connectome representation learning should proceed identically regardless of phenotype context. Additionally, these methods rely on local neighborhood aggregation and fail to explicitly model the long-range inter-network coupling and cross-hemispheric synchronization that underpin clinically relevant brain function.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263268/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes three input streams per subject: a sparse fMRI functional connectome graph (N ROIs, adjacency from thresholded Pearson correlation, node features from ROI time-series statistics), a structured phenotype vector (age, site, clinical scale scores, z-score normalized with missingness indicators), and a free-form clinical narrative with diagnosis keywords and total scores stripped out. Beyond the classification label, the model produces two interpretable outputs: a network-level long-range routing matrix quantifying multi-hop signal propagation intensity between functional networks, and prompt-to-ROI attention scores that identify which brain regions are queried under the given phenotype context.

## Method

PhenoBrain injects phenotype information at the mechanism level through three coupled components. First, a Phenotype-Conditioned Long-Range Router (PCLR) builds a multi-hop communication kernel by adding a low-rank, phenotype-modulated adjustment (ΔF = U·diag(W·z_pheno)·Vᵀ) to the base FC matrix, then performing K-hop propagation with softmax-weighted hop coefficients to capture individual-specific long-range interactions. Second, a Phenotype Prompt Generator compresses textual phenotype token embeddings into M compact prompt tokens that act as semantic probes within the Graph Transformer. Third, a FiLM-style modulation applies phenotype-derived affine parameters (γ, β) to the attention logits at every Transformer layer and head, so that ROI-to-ROI and prompt-to-ROI attention patterns are individually conditioned. Training combines supervised cross-entropy with an InfoNCE alignment loss between the brain embedding and the global phenotype vector to encourage cross-modal consistency and reduce shortcut reliance.

## Results

On the ABIDE dataset (ASD vs. healthy control), PhenoBrain achieves 76.35% accuracy and 83.46% AUC, improving over the strongest baseline MGDR by 3.40% in ACC and 3.46% in AUC. On the PIXAR dataset (children vs. adults), PhenoBrain reaches 91.67% ACC and 92.59% AUC, surpassing MGDR by 1.56% (relative) in ACC and 1.05% (relative) in AUC. PhenoBrain also attains the best sensitivity on PIXAR (93.33%), exceeding the previous best (SPromptGL, 90.07%) by 3.62%, and the best specificity on both datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PhenoBrain outperforms all nine baselines on ACC, AUC, and SPE across both datasets. Against the strongest baseline MGDR (MICCAI 2024), PhenoBrain gains +2.51% ACC and +2.79% AUC on ABIDE, and +1.41% ACC and +0.96% AUC on PIXAR. The sole exception is SEN on ABIDE, where MGDR (88.64%) exceeds PhenoBrain (83.84%) by 4.80%, attributed to MGDR's tendency toward high-recall decision boundaries for the positive class. On PIXAR, PhenoBrain surpasses even the best SEN baseline SPromptGL (90.07%) by 3.26 points. Classic GCN-based methods (InceptionGCN, PopGCN, LSTMGCN, EV-GCN, Multi-GCN) trail PhenoBrain by 8–19% in ACC on ABIDE and 3–13% on PIXAR, confirming that phenotype-conditioned long-range routing and prompt-guided attention provide substantial gains over purely local graph aggregation. The long-range communication analysis (Figure 3) further shows that while GCN baselines peak at 2–3 hops and degrade with oversmoothing, PhenoBrain maintains stable or improving performance as hop count increases, validating its multi-hop routing design.

## Relation to Prior Work

PhenoBrain departs from two existing lines of work: (1) multi-imaging-modality brain network methods (MMGL, CroGen, RTGNN, Cross-GNN) that fuse different scan types but ignore clinical phenotype, and (2) recent phenotype-aware methods (MGDR, SPromptGL) that incorporate phenotypic data only at the classifier or late-fusion stage. The key conceptual departure is injecting phenotype as a conditional variable that reshapes the representation-learning mechanism itself—via a low-rank phenotype-modulated routing kernel (PCLR) and FiLM-style attention modulation—rather than concatenating phenotype embeddings at the decision layer. The multi-hop routing component draws on long-range graph propagation ideas, while the prompt-token interface borrows from the NLP prompt paradigm applied to graph Transformers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The mechanism-level fusion framing offers a principled alternative to the prevailing late-fusion paradigm in brain network analysis, and the interpretability outputs (phenotype-specific routing matrices, prompt-to-ROI attention) provide clinically grounded evidence rather than black-box embeddings. However, the textual phenotypes are expert-constructed narratives rather than naturally occurring clinical notes, limiting generalizability; the datasets (ABIDE, PIXAR) are relatively small and the performance gains over strong baselines like MGDR are moderate (~3% ACC). Open questions include whether the phenotype-conditioned routing captures genuinely distinct neurobiological mechanisms or merely fits dataset-specific noise, and whether the approach scales to larger, more heterogeneous clinical cohorts with naturally generated text.
