## Problem

PPI++ is widely used to combine gold-standard labels with noisy pseudo-labels for mean estimation, and prior work guarantees its asymptotic variance is never worse than the classical estimator regardless of pseudo-label quality. However, this guarantee is purely asymptotic, while the highest-value use of PPI++ is precisely when labeled samples are scarce. The paper asks: in finite samples, when does PPI++ actually help versus hurt, and can we characterize this threshold exactly?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263209/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes n labeled pairs (Y, F) and N unlabeled pseudo-labels F, with N taken to be arbitrarily large so that the pseudo-label mean and variance are known exactly. The output is an exact finite-sample variance decomposition of the Cross-fit PPI++ estimator into four interpretable components (classical variance, efficiency gain, efficiency loss, and a cross-fold term), valid for any joint distribution of Y and F with bounded fourth moments. For Gaussian labels, this reduces to a simple correlation threshold of 1/√(cn−2) below which PPI++ is provably worse than the classical estimator.

## Method

The core contribution is an exact non-asymptotic variance expression (Theorem 4.1) for the Cross-fit PPI++ estimator under sample splitting, decomposed into: the classical variance σ²ᵧ/n, an efficiency gain term −σ²_fy/(nσ²_f) capturing the benefit of pseudo-label correlation, an efficiency loss term (1/(nσ²_f))·E[(σ̂_fy−σ_fy)²] capturing the cost of estimating the covariance from finite labeled data, and a cross-fold term of order O(1/n²). Lemma 4.1 then characterizes the covariance estimation error for general distributions, yielding a 'no free lunch' condition: PPI++ outperforms the classical estimator if and only if the pseudo-label correlation exceeds a threshold that shrinks as O(1/√n). The paper further shows that the simpler Single-Sample PPI++ (without cross-fitting) is biased in finite samples and that standard plug-in variance estimators for it are always narrower than the classical variance, leading to under-coverage in confidence intervals.

## Results

The paper's headline theoretical result is a 'no free lunch' condition: for Gaussian labels, Cross-fit PPI++ outperforms the classical estimator if and only if the pseudo/true-label correlation exceeds 1/√(n/2 − 2), while Single-Sample PPI++ requires correlation above 1/√(n − 2). When pseudo-labels are independent of true labels, Cross-fit PPI++ variance is inflated by a factor of (1 + 2/(n−2)) relative to the classical estimator. On the AlphaFold dataset (correlation = 0.81), the original pseudo-labels only reduce MSE relative to classical at n ≥ 20 (Cross-fit) and n ≥ 10 (Single-Sample), and Single-Sample PPI++ at n = 20 achieves only 87% nominal coverage, confirming the theoretical prediction of over-optimistic variance estimates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three estimators: the Classical sample mean, PPI (fixed λ=1), and PPI++ (data-driven λ), in both Single-Sample and Cross-fit variants. The central finding is that PPI++ does NOT uniformly dominate Classical: it wins only when the pseudo-label correlation exceeds a sample-size-dependent threshold, and loses (i.e., has higher variance) when the correlation is below that threshold. In the independent pseudo-label case, both PPI++ variants are strictly worse than Classical by a multiplicative O(1/n) factor. Cross-fit PPI++ is unbiased and achieves correct asymptotic coverage, while Single-Sample PPI++ is biased in finite samples (bias = (2σ_yf μ_f − σ_yf²)/(nσ_f²)) and its plug-in variance estimator is always smaller than the classical variance estimate, leading to under-coverage (e.g., 87% at n=20 on AlphaFold). PPI (λ=1) can be worse than both PPI++ and Classical when pseudo-labels are noisy, since it does not discount the pseudo-label contribution. The paper positions its contribution as demystifying the asymptotic 'free lunch' claim of Angelopoulos et al. (2023b) by showing the finite-sample regime where that guarantee breaks down.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides an exact non-asymptotic variance analysis of PPI++ (Angelopoulos et al., 2023b), the power-tuned variant of Prediction-Powered Inference (Angelopoulos et al., 2023a), which prior work had only characterized asymptotically. The analysis draws on the control variate literature (Lavenberg & Welch, 1981; Nelson, 1990) and the survey sampling GREG estimator (Cassel et al., 1976), where analogous variance-inflation effects from estimating the optimal coefficient on the same data are well known, but had not been made exact and distribution-free for the PPI++ setting. The paper departs from the asymptotic 'free lunch' narrative of Angelopoulos et al. (2023b) by showing that the finite-sample guarantee fails precisely in the small-n regime where PPI++ is most needed, and it performs the exact finite-sample variance calculation that Tille (2020) had deemed intractable for the analogous GREG estimator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As PPI and PPI++ become routine tools in LLM-as-judge evaluations, clinical trials, and AI system benchmarking, the 'no free lunch' result warns practitioners that using PPI++ with weakly correlated pseudo-labels can produce worse estimates and over-confident (narrower) confidence intervals than simply averaging the gold-standard labels. The paper's practical framework—mapping assumed pseudo-label quality and sample size to a recommended estimator—gives applied researchers a transparent decision procedure rather than a blanket 'always use PPI++' recommendation. Open questions include extending the exact finite-sample characterization to the general M-estimation setting (noted but not fully resolved), to finite unlabeled sample sizes N, and to adaptive or data-driven choices of the pseudo-label model itself.
