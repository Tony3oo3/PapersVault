## Problem

Existing dimensionality reduction methods (PCA, t-SNE, UMAP, MDS, AE) all preserve some invariant—variance, pairwise point-to-point similarity, topology, or reconstruction—but implicitly assume a single generative source, overlooking that real-world data typically arises from a mixture of k distinct distributions. For downstream tasks like classification, preserving the affinity between each data point and its underlying distribution is more critical than preserving pairwise point-to-point similarities, yet no prior method explicitly targets this point-distribution similarity structure.

## Input / Output

The method takes a data matrix X ∈ R^{n×d}, a user-specified number of distributions k, a target embedding dimension p (p ≪ d), and a continuous positive-definite kernel κ. It first partitions the data into k subsets via the linear-time KBC clustering algorithm, then computes empirical kernel mean embeddings for each subset. The output is a low-dimensional embedding matrix Z ∈ R^{n×p} where each row z_i is positioned relative to k distribution centers {z̄_c} such that Euclidean distances ‖z_i − z̄_c‖₂ approximate the RKHS distances ‖φ(x_i) − μ_c‖_{H_κ}. An optional post-processing step rigidly translates each subset so its empirical mean coincides with its target center.

## Method

Ψ-DR (PSIDR) preserves a novel point-distribution similarity invariant by solving a two-stage decoupled procedure. Stage 1 embeds the k distributions: it computes the k×k MMD distance matrix from empirical kernel mean embeddings and applies classical MDS (closed-form eigendecomposition) to obtain low-dimensional centers {z̄_c} in R^p. Stage 2 locates each data point via multilateration: the point-distribution inner product ⟨φ(x_i), μ̂_c⟩ is converted to a target distance d_ic = √(2(1−⟨φ(x_i), μ̂_c⟩)), and each z_i is found by solving a linearized least-squares system (A z_i = b_i) derived from squaring the distance equations to the k fixed anchors. This yields a linear-time algorithm with overall complexity O(k³ + np²k + nkd), since k and p are typically much smaller than n. The paper provides a full theoretical analysis proving embedding consistency (Theorem 5.7), finite-sample error bounds scaling as O(k²/n_min) (Theorem 5.8), and point-to-distribution distance preservation in R^p (Theorem 5.9).

## Results

On 10 benchmark datasets, Ψ-DR achieves the best score on 28 out of 40 evaluation metrics (CH, DB, ACC, NMI), compared to t-SNE (9 wins) and UMAP (4 wins). On MNIST, Ψ-DR attains a Calinski-Harabasz index of 52,306 (vs. UMAP 46,927, t-SNE 30,448) and a Davies-Bouldin index of 0.96 (vs. UMAP 1.46, t-SNE 1.48), while also achieving 0.814 NMI (vs. UMAP 0.717, t-SNE 0.671). On the large-scale Rcv1 dataset (804K points, 47K dimensions), Ψ-DR completes in 382 seconds, roughly 93× faster than t-SNE (35,638 s) and 7.6× faster than PaCMAP (2,911 s), confirming its linear-time complexity in both n and d.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against classical baselines, Ψ-DR dominates on intrinsic clustering quality (CH and DB) across nearly all datasets, e.g., achieving CH=37,146 on Banknote vs. UMAP's 1,369 and t-SNE's 792, and DB=0.05 vs. UMAP's 0.84. t-SNE is the strongest competitor on classification accuracy (ACC), winning 9 of 10 datasets (e.g., 0.996 on Pendigits vs. Ψ-DR's 0.906), but Ψ-DR surpasses it on NMI in 8 of 10 cases, indicating better cluster-label alignment. UMAP is competitive on ACC (winning 4 datasets) but is consistently outperformed by Ψ-DR on CH, DB, and NMI. MDS and AE are clearly inferior, with MDS producing extreme DB values (e.g., 191.87 on MNIST) and AE showing poor NMI on several datasets. Against state-of-the-art methods (Table 7), Ψ-DR wins 18 of 40 metrics versus PaCMAP (9) and LocalMAP (12); PaCMAP is the closest competitor on ACC (e.g., 0.999 on Skin vs. Ψ-DR's 0.996), while Ψ-DR leads decisively on CH (e.g., 1,279,417 on Skin vs. PaCMAP's 47,859) and NMI (0.828 vs. 0.022 on Skin). In terms of runtime, Ψ-DR is second only to PCA, completing the 804K-point Rcv1 dataset in 382 s versus 35,638 s for t-SNE and 2,911 s for PaCMAP, confirming its linear scalability in both sample size and dimensionality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in dimensionality reduction—preserving pairwise point-to-point similarity (t-SNE, UMAP, MDS) or global variance (PCA)—by introducing a point-to-distribution similarity invariant grounded in Kernel Mean Embedding (KME) within RKHS. The algorithmic pipeline repurposes two classical tools in a novel decoupled fashion: classical MDS embeds the k distribution centers in the MMD metric, and closed-form multilateration then places each data point relative to those anchors. The clustering step relies on KBC, a recent linear-time kernel-bounded clustering method, to obtain the k subsets that approximate the underlying mixture components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The linear-time complexity O(nd) is a significant practical advantage over the quadratic (or worse) scaling of t-SNE, UMAP, and MDS, making the method viable for datasets with hundreds of thousands of points and tens of thousands of dimensions (e.g., Rcv1, single-cell RNA-seq). The theoretical analysis provides consistency guarantees and finite-sample error bounds, which are rare among non-linear DR methods. A key limitation is that performance is bottlenecked by the least-represented distribution (n_min), so highly imbalanced data degrades quality. Open questions include whether the invariant generalizes beyond mixture-of-Gaussians-like structures and how the method behaves when the number of true components k is misspecified.
