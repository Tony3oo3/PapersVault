## Problem

Standard language model pretraining entangles syntactic competence with semantic plausibility and world knowledge, making it unclear whether syntax can be learned more efficiently if semantic cues are actively suppressed. This paper asks whether a tiny LM can acquire full syntactic competence while scoring at chance on world-knowledge and plausibility probes, and whether such disentanglement yields practical gains in data/parameter efficiency and post-hoc controllability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264989/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The SAMBAL pipeline takes parsed natural-language sentences (from the 10M-token baby-cosmo-fine corpus) and produces grammatical nonsense: open-class tokens are replaced with syntactically-compatible but semantically-unrelated lemmas while closed-class items, agreement morphology, and complement structure are frozen. The resulting corpus is used to pretrain 5M–30M-parameter GPT-BERT hybrid models via a combined causal/masked objective. At evaluation, the models output next-token logit margins over minimal-pair sentences (BLiMP, SyntaxGym, custom swap and conflict probes), perplexity on domain text (LoTR, PubMed), and InfoNCE alignment scores over hidden states.

## Method

SAMBAL performs constrained relexicalization: for each replaceable open-class token, it computes a syntactic context key from the dependency parse (POS, morphosyntactic features, dependency role, subcategorization frame) and samples a replacement lemma from a bucketed lexicon (WordNet/VerbNet) that preserves c-selection (complement type, valency, raising/control/ECM signatures) while ablating s-selection (semantic type restrictions and plausibility). A corpus-conditioned back-off chain gates candidates by observed frequency in matching syntactic contexts, and a round-trip UD reparse rejects outputs that violate structural invariants. Tiny GPT-BERT models are then pretrained identically to baselines but on the fully relexicalized corpus. The key finding is that this yields near-parity on syntactic benchmarks (BLiMP 79.3% vs. 79.2%) while collapsing world-knowledge and plausibility scores to chance, and that the resulting content-neutral base adapts rapidly to new domains via LoRA with far less data than training from scratch.

## Results

On syntactic benchmarks, SAMBAL matches the standard-pretraining baseline on BLiMP (79.3% vs. 79.2% long-regime; 71.2% vs. 71.2% short-regime) and on SyntaxGym excluding reflexive suites (79.8% vs. 78.6% long), while collapsing on reflexive suites due to a near-threshold data-coverage issue. On semantic suppression, SAMBAL scores at chance on EWoK (50.8% vs. 54.3% baseline) and shows near-zero plausibility margin on the swap probe (ΔK = 0.05 vs. 7.62 baseline), while strongly preferring grammatical sentences (97% syntax accuracy, ΔG = 5.73 vs. 55%, 0.65 baseline). On the grammar-plausibility conflict probe, SAMBAL achieves 100% accuracy versus 10.2% for the baseline. In the low-budget regime, a 5M-parameter SAMBAL model matches a 30M-parameter baseline on BLiMP at 10% data (60.6 vs. 59.6).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is a standard GPT-BERT model (30M params) trained on the same 10M-word baby-cosmo-fine corpus without relexicalization. SAMBAL ties the baseline on BLiMP in both training regimes and slightly exceeds it on SyntaxGym excluding reflexives (79.8% vs. 78.6% long), but falls well short on full SyntaxGym (66.0% vs. 70.47%) due to a reflexive-suite collapse that targeted augmentation largely repairs. On semantic probes, SAMBAL is deliberately worse: it drops to chance on EWoK (50.8% vs. 54.3%) and shows no plausibility preference on the swap probe (55% vs. 100% baseline accuracy), confirming suppression of world knowledge and selectional semantics. On the adversarial grammar-plausibility conflict set, SAMBAL achieves 100% accuracy while the baseline selects the plausible-but-ungrammatical option 89.8% of the time. On efficiency, SAMBAL's advantage is concentrated in small-model/low-data settings: a 5M SAMBAL model at 10% data (60.6) slightly exceeds a 30M baseline (59.6), but the gap vanishes at larger budgets. On LoTR domain adaptation via LoRA, the baseline fine-tuned model achieves lower perplexity (29.2 vs. 36.3), though both substantially outperform a from-scratch LoTR model (55.5), and both lose ~7 BLiMP points after fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GPT-BERT hybrid causal/masked architecture (Charpentier & Samuel, 2024) and the BabyLM small-scale pretraining framework (Hu et al., 2024), but departs from prior work in a key direction: where earlier relexicalization studies (Gulordava et al., 2018; Arps et al., 2024) remove meaning only at evaluation time to probe whether syntax is decodable, SAMBAL removes semantic content throughout training to test whether syntax is still learnable. It also inverts the data-centric syntax-improvement line (Mueller & Linzen, 2023; Hu et al., 2025), which adds structural bias to the training signal, by instead ablating semantic plausibility to isolate syntactic learning. The modular specialization via LoRA connects to the broader model-editing and parameter-efficient adaptation literature (Hu et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2023), but applies it to a content-neutral syntactic base rather than a fully pretrained model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic evidence that a tiny LM can acquire near-full syntactic competence while suppressing world knowledge and semantic plausibility, with a striking 6× parameter-efficiency gain in low-resource regimes (5M SAMBAL matches 30M baseline). This suggests a practical path toward modular language models: a syntax-competent, knowledge-free base that can be rapidly specialized to new domains via lightweight adapters, avoiding the difficulty of editing parametrically stored knowledge. Key open questions include whether the efficiency gains persist at larger scales, whether residual leakage channels (function-word distributions, preserved gender/humanness features) can be further closed, and whether the approach portably extends beyond high-resource English where parsing and lexicon coverage are strong.
