## Problem

Parameter Sharing in MARL suffers from gradient conflicts among agents on shared weights, limiting behavioral diversity and degrading performance. Existing subnetwork-partitioning methods (e.g., GradPS) rely on scalar conflict metrics that conflate directional and scale components of weight updates, inadvertently discarding shared directional features when mitigating conflicts. The paper identifies that agents largely agree on directional updates but substantially disagree on scale updates, a distinction invisible to standard cosine-similarity-based conflict detection.

## Input / Output

Each agent's policy network receives its local observation, a one-hot agent ID, and the last executed action. The Riemannian backbone processes the observation through L2-normalized weight vectors, while a shared scale generator (MLP→GRU→MLP, hidden size 64) produces per-agent multiplicative scale coefficients γ and shift vectors β. The final per-agent representation feeds into a QMIX/VDN/QPLEX mixing network to produce individual Q-values, from which a joint action is selected. The scale generator parameters are shared across all agents and layers, so parameter overhead is fixed and independent of agent count.

## Method

HPS structurally decouples direction and scale in parameter sharing via two components. First, the Riemannian Backbone constrains shared weight vectors onto the unit hypersphere S^{d-1} via L2 normalization in the forward pass; in the backward pass, the chain rule through normalization yields a projection operator P⊥ = (I − wwᵀ) that mathematically eliminates all radial (scale) gradient components, guaranteeing the backbone evolves solely on tangential (directional) consensus. Second, an Agent-Specific Scale Generator, conditioned on agent ID and local observation, outputs per-agent scale γ and shift β vectors applied via Featurewise Linear Modulation: h_final = σ((1+γ) ⊙ h_dir + β). This re-routes scale adaptation to a separate, agent-differentiated pathway while the backbone remains insulated from scale conflicts. The paper provides a formal proof (Eq. 12) that P⊥ g_parallel = 0, establishing provable geometric isolation of the two optimization objectives.

## Results

On the Predator-Prey (Stag Hunt) benchmark, HPS achieves 69.77±0.16 (small), 117.41±0.41 (medium), and 174.64±1.56 (large) average reward, outperforming the strongest baselines GradPS (172.88) and FuPS (174.48) on the large scenario while maintaining the lowest variance. On SMACv1 and SMACv2 (reported in Figures 3–4), HPS consistently outperforms state-of-the-art parameter-sharing methods across most scenarios, with advantages becoming more pronounced as agent count and heterogeneity increase (e.g., 27m_vs_30m, MMM2, and 10-agent SMACv2 maps). On VMAS continuous-control tasks, HPS improves over QMIX, VDN, and QPLEX baselines across Balance, Sampling, and Joint Passage Size scenarios. Ablation studies confirm that both the Riemannian Backbone and the Agent-Specific Scale Generator contribute, with the Scale Generator being the more critical component for resolving scale conflicts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HPS outperforms all eight compared baselines on the majority of evaluated scenarios. Against the strongest subnetwork-based methods, HPS beats GradPS (Qin et al., ICML 2024) by ~1.8 reward on Stag Hunt large (174.64 vs. 172.88) and by a wider margin on SMACv1/v2 heterogeneous maps where GradPS's scalar conflict metric over-branches neurons. HPS substantially outperforms SNP (Kim & Sung, 2023) and Kaleidoscope (Li et al., 2024), which suffer severe performance degradation on larger or more heterogeneous scenarios (e.g., SNP drops to 100.43 vs. HPS's 174.64 on Stag Hunt large; Kaleidoscope drops to 75.53). Against role/subtask decomposition methods LDSA (Yang et al., 2022) and LSVQ (Zhou et al., 2024), HPS achieves comparable or slightly better results while using fewer parameters (233% vs. 533% and 714% of FuPS on 27m_vs_30m). In the additional MAPPO-based comparison (Figure 13), HPS also surpasses the MAPPO baseline, confirming the method's generality beyond QMIX. The paper notes that in highly homogeneous, low-conflict scenarios (e.g., Zerg 5x5 in SMACv2), simple FuPS can match HPS, suggesting the geometric decoupling provides the largest gains when agent heterogeneity and scale conflicts are most pronounced.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of MARL parameter-sharing work that treats gradient conflicts as a monolithic phenomenon resolved by branching or masking neurons (GradPS, Kaleidoscope, SNP) or by explicit role/subtask decomposition (LDSA, LSVQ). Instead, it introduces a geometric decomposition of gradients into radial (scale) and tangential (direction) components, drawing on neurobiological principles of feature selectivity vs. response intensity, to show that conflicts are predominantly scale-based. The architectural solution—constraining shared weights to a unit hypersphere (Riemannian backbone) and re-introducing per-agent scale via a FiLM-style generator—repurposes ideas from Riemannian optimization and conditional feature modulation in a way that prior MARL subnetwork methods did not.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The geometric diagnosis provides a principled explanation for why scalar conflict metrics (cosine similarity) used by GradPS and similar methods are insufficient: they conflate direction and scale, leading to unnecessary network over-segmentation. The HPS architecture offers a compact, scalable alternative to parameter isolation, with parameter overhead that actually decreases relative to branching methods as agent count grows. However, the paper acknowledges that in environments demanding strong sharing, the Riemannian constraint may slightly impair performance, and directional conflicts (though rarer) remain unaddressed. The pragmatic workaround of applying scale modulation to GRU outputs rather than constraining GRU weights internally also leaves open whether full Riemannian treatment of recurrent layers is feasible.
