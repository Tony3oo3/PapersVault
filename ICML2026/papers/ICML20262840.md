## Problem

No prior work has evaluated adaptive LoRA merging methods on user-contributed LoRAs found 'in the wild' on model repositories like Hugging Face Hub; past studies relied on bespoke, controlled LoRA pools trained on curated datasets. This paper asks whether adaptive merging of nearly 1,000 heterogeneous, user-contributed LoRAs can meaningfully improve target-task performance in realistic data-constrained settings, and whether it can outperform the simple baseline of training a new LoRA on the same target-task data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pool of 958 user-contributed LoRAs (with varying ranks from 4 to 512, heterogeneous target-module combinations, and unknown training hyperparameters) alongside a small target-task dataset of 100 samples (80 train, 20 validation) and the base model weights (Llama 3.1 8B-Instruct). Unlike prior work, the recycled LoRAs have no access to their original training data, forcing approximations like Quasi-FIM for task-similarity selection. The output is a single merged model whose per-module LoRA updates are weighted by learned coefficients, foldable into the base weights for standard inference.

## Method

The paper formalizes a unified design space for adaptive merging along four axes—LoRA selection (random, evaluation-based, cosine, Quasi-FIM), coefficient granularity (model, layer, sublayer, module), coefficient activation (softmax, linear, Leaky ReLU), and tuning strategy (gradient-based, gradient-free, joint)—and identifies the best configuration through extensive ablation: evaluation-based top-k selection, module-level granularity, Leaky ReLU with zero initialization, and gradient-based tuning. The merged update at each target module is a weighted sum of the selected LoRAs' low-rank products, with per-module learnable coefficients optimized on the target-task training set. A key empirical finding is that once the target-task LoRA is included in the pool, the identity of the other recycled LoRAs becomes nearly irrelevant—randomly initialized LoRAs with matched standard deviations perform comparably to carefully selected ones—suggesting the adaptive merging gain is primarily a structural regularization effect rather than genuine cross-task knowledge transfer. Positive transfer is confirmed only when the pool contains highly relevant 'in-house' LoRAs trained on closely related tasks, explaining why prior controlled evaluations overstate practical gains.

## Results

Across 62 downstream tasks using Llama 3.1 8B-Instruct, the proposed adaptive merging method ("Ours") achieves 0.675 average accuracy with the target-task LoRA in the pool, the highest among all methods, compared to 0.657 for the target-task LoRA alone and 0.467 for zero-shot prompting. Without the target-task LoRA, π-Tuning (0.668) and the proposed method (0.652) lead among adaptive methods, while non-adaptive methods (TSV: 0.488, TIES: 0.467, Simple Averaging: 0.496) remain close to the prompting baseline. The key finding is that adaptive merging provides only a modest ~1.8% absolute gain over the target-task LoRA baseline, and that randomly initialized LoRAs perform comparably to carefully selected recycled LoRAs when the target-task LoRA is present.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method (Ours, 0.675) outperforms all seven baselines when the target-task LoRA is included: AdaMerging (0.669, +0.6%), π-Tuning (0.668, +0.7%), LoraHub (0.663, +1.2%), Arrow (0.613, +6.2%), Simple Averaging (0.536, +13.9%), TSV (0.522, +15.3%), and TIES (0.466, +20.9%). However, the most critical comparison is against the target-task LoRA baseline (0.657), where the gain is only +1.8% absolute, and the paper shows this gain is largely a regularization effect rather than positive transfer, since randomly initialized LoRAs achieve 0.672. Without the target-task LoRA, the proposed method (0.652) ties AdaMerging (0.652) but trails π-Tuning (0.668) by 1.6%, while LoraHub (0.563) and all non-adaptive methods fall well behind. The paper further demonstrates that when highly relevant in-house LoRAs are used instead of recycled ones, adaptive merging does show stronger positive transfer, suggesting prior work's gains depend on controlled task similarity that is rare in realistic heterogeneous pools.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the adaptive merging literature (AdaMerging, LoraHub, π-Tuning) by formalizing a unified design space over selection strategy, coefficient granularity, activation function, and tuning method, then evaluating these methods on a pool of ~1,000 user-contributed LoRAs from Hugging Face Hub rather than the bespoke, controlled LoRA collections used in prior work. The proposed method ('Ours') is closest to AdaMerging's per-module learnable coefficient formulation, differing primarily in using Leaky ReLU activation with zero initialization and evaluation-based LoRA selection. The key conceptual departure is the evaluation setting: prior work trains its own LoRAs on curated tasks, while this paper recycles heterogeneous, user-contributed modules with unknown training data, hyperparameters, and ranks, making it the first large-scale in-the-wild evaluation of adaptive merging.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that adaptive merging's gains over a simple target-task LoRA largely vanish, and that randomly initialized LoRAs perform comparably to carefully selected ones when the target-task LoRA is in the pool—challenges the positive-transfer narrative underpinning much of the LoRA recycling literature. This suggests the field's reported successes may depend on close task relevance that is rare in realistic heterogeneous pools, and that the observed benefits are closer to a structural regularization effect than genuine cross-task knowledge transfer. Open questions include whether fundamentally different selection or discovery mechanisms could identify the rare highly-relevant LoRAs in large public pools, and whether the regularization benefit can be captured more directly without the overhead of merging. The paper also highlights infrastructure gaps: sparse metadata, inconsistent licensing, and heterogeneous training configurations in public LoRA repositories hinder systematic recycling.
