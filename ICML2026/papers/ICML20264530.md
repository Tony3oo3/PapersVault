## Problem

Long-term fairness algorithms assume labels are fully observed, but in high-stakes settings like lending or hiring, labels (e.g., ability to repay) are only revealed after a positive decision—a property called selective labels. The paper formally shows that evaluating disparity only on the accepted population provides no guarantee over the true population disparity, and that existing long-term fairness RL methods silently fail under this partial-observation regime.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular individual features x_t (10-dim for lending, 13-dim for recidivism, 126-dim for school admission), a binary sensitive attribute z, and the subset of labels observed only for previously accepted individuals. It outputs a binary accept/reject action at each step, a learned policy network π, a label predictor φ (a linear model with sigmoid output), and an observed disparity estimate Δ̃_t computed via imputation. A key structural constraint is that the predictor is trained exclusively on accepted data using self-normalized inverse propensity weights whose denominators aggregate acceptance probabilities across all K training policies, preventing the variance explosion typical of single-policy IPW.

## Method

SELLF builds on PPO with two fairness-specific modifications derived from a theoretical decomposition (Theorem 3.2) linking observed disparity Δ̃_t to true disparity Δ_t through group rejection rates r^i and predictor errors ε^i. First, the advantage function is penalized whenever |Δ̃_t| exceeds ω/2, enforcing the observed-fairness half of the sufficient conditions. Second, a Renyi divergence regularization term L^Renyi = Σ_i c_t^i · E[w(x_t,i)² | Z=i] is added to the policy loss, where w(x,i) are the IPW propensity weights; this incentivizes the policy to keep accepted and rejected feature distributions overlapping, tightening the generalization bound on predictor error and preventing the IPW variance from destabilizing learning. The predictor φ is trained in parallel with binary cross-entropy under self-normalized IPW on the accepted-data buffer. The combined objective J(θ_π) = L^PPO + β₂·L^Renyi, together with the advantage penalty weighted by β₁, ensures that the sufficient conditions of Theorem 3.6 (bounding both |Δ̃| and the imputation bias term) are satisfied using only observable quantities.

## Results

SELLF achieves the lowest true disparity across all three semisynthetic environments: 0.03 (lending, equality of opportunity), 0.05 (criminal recidivism, accuracy parity), and 0.12 (school admission, qualification parity), matching or beating the oracle-access POCAR baseline in every setting. In the lending environment, SELLF also obtains a higher cumulative reward (1246.24) than POCAR with oracle access (1156.82) while maintaining the same 0.05 disparity constraint. All baselines that do not account for selective labels (PPO, POCAR, FOCOPS, ELBERT) exhibit disparity above 0.30 in the lending environment, confirming that evaluating fairness only on the accepted population is a fundamentally flawed objective.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SELLF decisively outperforms all four baselines (PPO, POCAR, FOCOPS, ELBERT) on true disparity in every environment, with the gap being most dramatic in the lending setting where baselines achieve disparity of 0.30–0.41 versus SELLF's 0.03. This large gap is explained by Proposition 3.1: for equality of opportunity, the accepted-population disparity Δ^{A=1} is identically zero, so baselines that rely on it cannot detect or correct unfairness in the full population. Compared to POCAR with oracle access to true labels, SELLF matches or beats the oracle on disparity in all three environments (0.03 vs 0.05 in lending, 0.05 vs 0.08 in recidivism, 0.12 vs 0.12 in school admission) while also achieving higher reward in lending (1246 vs 1157) and school admission (1132 vs 1069). In the criminal recidivism environment, SELLF is the only method to satisfy the ω=0.05 disparity constraint, with POCAR (0.06) and PPO (0.09) falling just short. The semi-stochastic variant SELLF (Semi-sto.) confirms robustness under mild violations of the overlap assumption, still achieving disparity below 0.05 in lending and the lowest disparity (0.02) in recidivism, albeit at a small reward cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two previously separate literatures: long-term fairness in sequential decision-making (POCAR, ELBERT, FOCOPS, all of which assume fully observed labels) and selective-labels fairness (Kilbertus et al., Chang & Wiens, Creager et al., all of which operate in static or single-step settings). The key departure is combining both: the authors formalize an F-MDP where labels are only revealed upon acceptance and show that prior long-term fairness algorithms, which evaluate disparity on the accepted subset, are fundamentally broken (Prop. 3.1 shows Δ^{A=1} is identically zero for equality of opportunity). Methodologically, SELLF extends PPO's advantage regularization (from POCAR) with a novel Rényi-divergence regularization term and trains a label predictor via self-normalized IPW, drawing on domain-adaptation generalization bounds (Cortes et al., 2010) to make the fairness constraint enforceable from observable quantities alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap that is common in high-stakes deployment (lending, hiring, criminal justice) where outcome labels are only observed for accepted individuals, and provides the first theoretical framework guaranteeing true long-term fairness under this constraint. The clean decomposition (Theorem 3.2) and the actionable sufficient conditions (Theorem 3.6) give practitioners a principled recipe: constrain observed disparity and the predictor's confidence bound simultaneously. Limitations include the stationary-group-dynamics assumption, the overlap/positivity requirement, binary actions only, and evaluation restricted to semisynthetic environments with simple transition dynamics. Open questions flagged by the authors include adapting the framework to offline learning from historical data and extending to non-binary action spaces.
