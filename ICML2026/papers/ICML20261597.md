## Problem

A prevailing assumption in the model merging literature is that stronger fine-tuned experts yield better merged models, yet the impact of expert training duration on downstream merging performance has received little systematic attention. This paper challenges that assumption by showing that prolonged fine-tuning of experts—optimizing for individual task performance—actually degrades merging performance across vision and language modalities, multiple model scales, and both fully fine-tuned and LoRA-adapted settings.

## Input / Output

The merging pipeline takes a pre-trained foundation model (ViT-B-32, ViT-L-14, T5-Base/Large/3B, or BERT) and multiple fine-tuned expert checkpoints (one per task), computing task vectors as parameter differences from the pre-trained weights. The merged model retains the original architecture and size, producing class labels for 8 vision classification tasks or text outputs for 7 NLP tasks (QA, paraphrase identification, sentence completion, coreference resolution). The key manipulated variable is the number of fine-tuning steps per expert (2 to 2048), with all experts in a given merge trained for the same duration, and merging hyperparameters (scaling factors, pruning fractions) tuned per experiment on held-out validation data.

## Method

The paper systematically varies expert fine-tuning duration and evaluates six merging methods (Average, Task Arithmetic, TIES, DARE, Iso-C/Iso-CTS, TSV-M) across ViT and T5/BERT models in both FFT and LoRA settings, revealing that merging accuracy peaks well before full convergence. Using EL2N data difficulty scores and Feldman memorization scores, they trace the degradation to late-stage memorization of a small fraction of hard examples: memorization scores for hard examples more than double from step 256 to 2048, while easy examples remain essentially unmemorized. This memorization produces idiosyncratic parameter updates that increase parameter interference (quantified via sign conflict, parameter overlap, magnitude ratio, and per-parameter variance), causing hard examples to be disproportionately forgotten during merging. As a practical remedy, they propose task-dependent aggressive early stopping—adaptive LR decay with plateau detection for vision, and validation-accuracy plateau stopping for NLP—that automatically adapts training duration per task and recovers or exceeds the best fixed-duration merging accuracy. The findings extend to model MoErging, where overtrained LoRA experts also yield approximately 2% lower final multi-task accuracy even after continued multi-task training.

## Results

Across 8 vision tasks (ViT-B-32) and 7 NLP tasks (T5-Base), merging accuracy peaks at 256 training steps for most methods, then degrades by 2.4–4.6% when experts are trained to 2048 steps, even though expert accuracy improves by 1.6–3.7% over the same interval. For LoRA adapters, the degradation is more severe: TIES merging accuracy drops by 16% (ViT) and 9.9% (T5) from peak to 2048 steps. Task-dependent early stopping recovers or exceeds the best fixed-step merging accuracy (e.g., TIES on T5-Base: 76.9% with early stopping v0 vs. 75.7% best fixed step and 72.2% at 2048 steps) while using only ~485 steps on average instead of 2048.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new merging method but instead shows that the expert training protocol interacts critically with all existing merging methods. Task Arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2023) and TIES (Yadav et al., 2023) are the most affected by overtraining: on T5-Base, TIES drops from 75.7% (256 steps) to 72.2% (2048 steps), a 3.5% loss, while TA drops from 73.7% to 70.1%. The paper's early stopping strategy outperforms both the overtrained experts and the best single fixed step for TA and TIES by roughly 1–4% (e.g., TIES: 76.9% early stop vs. 75.7% best fixed step vs. 72.2% overtrained). DARE (Yu et al., 2024) follows the same degradation pattern, losing 4.6% on T5-Base from 256 to 2048 steps. The SVD-based methods Iso-C/Iso-CTS (Marczak et al., 2025) and TSV-M (Gargiulo et al., 2025) are more robust to overtraining (only 0.6–1.2% drop on ViT-B-32) but still peak earlier than 2048 steps, confirming that the phenomenon is method-agnostic. Notably, merging undertrained experts with simple TA outperforms merging overtrained experts with the more sophisticated TIES, demonstrating that training duration can outweigh merging method choice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of model merging (Task Arithmetic, TIES, DARE, SVD-based methods) and the data difficulty/memorization literature (EL2N scores, Feldman memorization scores). While prior merging work has focused on designing better combination rules under fixed fine-tuning protocols, and a few recent papers (Zhou et al., 2025; Pari et al., 2024) have hinted that undertrained experts can merge better, this paper is the first to systematically link expert training duration to merging performance and to provide a mechanistic explanation via hard-example memorization causing idiosyncratic parameter updates. It repurposes tools from the data-pruning and memorization literature (Feldman 2020 influence framework, EL2N difficulty bins) to diagnose why overtraining degrades merges, rather than proposing a new merging algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that stronger experts produce weaker merges challenges a core assumption in the pre-train → fine-tune → merge pipeline and has immediate practical implications: releasing intermediate checkpoints and using task-dependent early stopping are free interventions that can recover 3–5% merging accuracy. Open questions include whether the effect persists at the scale of 70B+ LLMs where fine-tuning is typically very short, whether the optimal stopping point can be predicted from dataset properties without trial-and-error, and whether the memorization-to-interference mechanism holds for non-linear or architecture-specific merging methods. The paper's scope is limited to models up to 3B parameters and standard classification/sequence tasks, so generalization to generative or multimodal merging remains untested.
