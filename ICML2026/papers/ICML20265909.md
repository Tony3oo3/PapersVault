## Problem

Empirical scaling laws for low-precision training propose competing functional forms—quantization as effective model-size reduction versus additive error—but no unified theoretical framework exists to determine which mechanism is physically correct or to account for specific quantization schemes. This paper initiates a rigorous theoretical study of how numerical precision interacts with model size and data size in the scaling of generalization risk.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265909/cropped_page7_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes M-dimensional sketched covariates (Sx) and scalar response labels (y), with stochastic quantization applied independently at seven pipeline stages (data, sketch matrix, features, labels, parameters, activations, and output gradients). The output is a population risk bound of the form R* + M_eff^{-(a-1)} + N_eff^{-(a-1)/a} + δ(ε), where M_eff and N_eff are explicit functions of the quantization error ε and the spectral exponent a, and δ(ε) is an additive error floor. The analysis is conducted under a power-law data spectrum (λ_i ≈ i^{-a}, a > 1) and a Gaussian sketch matrix, with the key distinction being whether quantization is multiplicative (FP-like, error scales with signal) or additive (INT-like, error is signal-independent).

## Method

The paper analyzes one-pass quantized SGD with iterate averaging in a high-dimensional sketched linear regression setting, decomposing population risk into irreducible, approximation, and excess components, then further splitting the excess into an algorithm-dependent variance term and an algorithm-independent additive error. The central technical contribution is identifying a critical dichotomy: multiplicative quantization preserves the full model capacity (M_eff = M) because signal-dependent error decays with the spectral tail, while additive quantization strictly contracts M_eff because a constant error floor flattens the tail spectrum and renders high-index dimensions uninformative. Both schemes reduce N_eff through two shared mechanisms—noise amplification (captured by compound coefficient ε_2) and spectral distortion (captured by ε_3)—but only additive quantization additionally degrades M_eff. The authors establish both upper and lower bounds (the first lower bounds for low-precision training), with the lower bounds confirming the additive error floor and effective data-size reduction as fundamental rather than artifacts of the analysis. Numerical experiments with polynomial spectral decay validate the predicted scaling exponents α = -(a-1) and β = -(a-1)/a with R² > 0.99.

## Results

The paper establishes that multiplicative (FP-like) quantization preserves the full model capacity (M_eff = M) while reducing effective data size and introducing an additive error, whereas additive (INT-like) quantization additionally contracts the effective model size (M_eff < M) due to spectral flattening in the tail subspace. Experimental validation on synthetic data with power-law spectra (a ∈ {1.5, 2.0}) confirms the predicted scaling exponents: for a=1.5, fitted α=−0.50 (theory: −1/2) and β=−0.34 (theory: −1/3); for a=2.0, fitted α=−1.01 (theory: −1) and β=−0.50 (theory: −1/2). All fits achieve R² > 0.99 across 20 seeds, with multiplicative quantization at ε=10⁻³ and additive quantization at ε=10⁻⁸.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without traditional method-vs-method baselines; instead, it provides the first unified theoretical framework that explains two competing empirical formulations from prior work. The additive quantization scaling law (Theorem 4.2) mechanistically explains the effective model shrinkage observed by Kumar et al. (2024) under integer quantization, where M_eff(M,P) ≈ M(1−e^{−P/γ}). The multiplicative quantization scaling law (Theorem 4.1) corroborates Sun et al. (2025)'s finding that floating-point quantization introduces an additive loss term without shrinking model capacity. The key theoretical contribution is identifying *why* these two behaviors differ: additive (INT-like) quantization injects a constant error floor that overwhelms the decaying spectral tail, rendering tail dimensions uninformative and reducing M_eff, while multiplicative (FP-like) quantization preserves the relative spectral structure so the error decays with the signal. The paper also establishes the first population risk lower bounds for low-precision training (Theorems 4.3–4.4), confirming that the additive error and effective data size reduction are fundamental rather than artifacts of the upper-bound analysis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the full-precision scaling law framework of Lin et al. (2024) for sketched linear regression with power-law spectra to the quantized training setting, and simultaneously extends the quantized SGD analysis of Zhang et al. (2025) from standard linear regression to the high-dimensional sketched regime. The key conceptual departure is the formal distinction between multiplicative (FP-like) and additive (INT-like) quantization, showing they have fundamentally different effects on effective model size while both reduce effective data size. The paper also provides the first population risk lower bounds for low-precision training, complementing the upper bounds in Zhang et al. (2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first unified theoretical explanation for why integer quantization empirically reduces effective model capacity (Kumar et al., 2024) while floating-point quantization does not (Sun et al., 2025), grounding two previously separate empirical scaling law formulations in a single mechanistic framework. The practical implication is that the choice between FP and INT training formats has qualitatively different consequences for how model size should be allocated under a fixed compute budget. Key limitations include the restriction to linear models, one-pass SGD, and a commutativity assumption between quantized and sketched covariances; the lower bound for additive quantization does not yet explicitly capture the M_eff reduction, and extending to non-linear models or other optimizers remains open.
