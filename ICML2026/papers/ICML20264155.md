## Problem

Existing histology-to-spatial gene expression models treat each gene as an independent scalar regression, ignoring gene-gene regulatory coordination and gene-spatial correlations that are critical for downstream pathway and domain analysis. Furthermore, the natural remedy of jointly diffusing node expressions and functional edges suffers a 'Gene Dimension Curse': as gene panel size grows, correlation estimates concentrate, the consistency manifold thins, and the required score-field curvature exceeds network capacity, causing training collapse.

## Input / Output

The model consumes 224×224 H&E patches centered on each spot, encoded by the frozen UNI pathology foundation model into 1024-d visual features, together with 2D spot coordinates. A fixed tissue graph is precomputed from Gaussian distance kernels (σ=224) and cosine visual similarity between spot features. The output is a G-dimensional log1p-normalized gene expression vector per spot (G=200 for the standard HMHVG benchmark panel), generated via a 100-step PF-ODE integration from a broad Gaussian prior.

## Method

FLAG factorizes the generation problem into two complementary components: a static Spatial Graph Encoder (6-layer Graph Transformer with Edge-Modulated Attention) that aggregates neighborhood context from the fixed topology into per-spot spatial embeddings, and a 12-layer gene-level DiT that denoises noisy expression vectors conditioned on those spatial embeddings. Rather than jointly diffusing edges (which triggers the Gene Dimension Curse), the graph topology is treated as an immutable conditioning prior, shifting the high-dimensional generative burden entirely to the gene space. A GFM alignment loss (cosine similarity between intermediate DiT hidden states and frozen Geneformer/scGPT/CellPLM embeddings) injects large-scale gene-gene regulatory structure without requiring the model to learn it from limited ST data. The total training objective is L_score + λ_align·L_align, combining standard VE-SDE denoising score matching with the representation alignment term.

## Results

On the Top-200 HMHVG panel across three HEST-1k cohorts, FLAG achieves the best Gene Structural Correlation (GSC) on all datasets (0.893 HER2ST, 0.871 KIDNEY, 0.878 PRAD) and the best Spatial Structural Correlation (SSC) on all datasets (0.639, 0.341, 0.751), substantially outperforming the next-best generative baselines. On pointwise accuracy, FLAG is highly competitive: it attains the best PCC on KIDNEY (0.392) and PRAD (0.585), and is within 0.02 PCC of the best on HER2ST (0.684 vs. STFlow's 0.706). FLAG also achieves the lowest MSE on KIDNEY (1.211) and PRAD (1.377), confirming that the structural gains do not come at the cost of per-gene numerical fidelity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the two generative baselines, FLAG clearly dominates on structural metrics: on HER2ST it improves GSC by +0.06 over Stem (0.893 vs. 0.832) and +0.10 over STFlow (0.893 vs. 0.789), and improves SSC by +0.26 over Stem (0.639 vs. 0.381) and +0.35 over STFlow (0.639 vs. 0.289). On PCC, STFlow edges out FLAG on HER2ST (0.706 vs. 0.684) and TRIPLEX is marginally ahead (0.691 vs. 0.684), but FLAG wins PCC on KIDNEY (0.392 vs. 0.344 Stem, 0.315 STFlow) and PRAD (0.585 vs. 0.403 Stem, 0.534 STFlow). Against the three discriminative baselines, FLAG outperforms all of them on GSC and SSC across every dataset; the gap is most dramatic on KIDNEY where HisToGene's GSC is negative (−0.026) and BLEEP's SSC is near zero (0.089), while FLAG reaches 0.871 and 0.341 respectively. On PRAD, TRIPLEX is the strongest discriminative competitor (PCC 0.527, SSC 0.634), yet FLAG still leads on all four metrics (PCC 0.585, MSE 1.377, GSC 0.878, SSC 0.751). Overall, FLAG trades a small PCC margin on HER2ST for large, consistent gains in gene-gene and gene-spatial structural fidelity, which the authors argue is the more biologically meaningful axis of evaluation.

## Relation to Prior Work

FLAG sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) diffusion-based generative models for spatial transcriptomics (Stem, STFlow), which treat gene expression as a conditional denoising problem but ignore explicit spot-spot spatial structure; (2) graph diffusion (Jo et al., 2022; DiGress), which jointly denoises node and edge states but, as this paper demonstrates, suffers a 'Gene Dimension Curse' that causes catastrophic collapse beyond ~100 genes; and (3) representation alignment for diffusion (REPA, SVG), which shows that anchoring intermediate features to frozen pretrained embeddings improves generative fidelity. FLAG's key architectural departure is repurposing the graph from a generative target (joint node-edge diffusion) to a fixed spatial encoder that conditions a gene-level DiT, while injecting gene-gene biological structure via alignment to Gene Foundation Model (Geneformer/scGPT/CellPLM) embeddings rather than learning it from limited ST data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Gene Dimension Curse provides a concrete theoretical explanation (Ω(G) optimization gap) for why joint node-edge diffusion fails at biologically relevant gene panel sizes, redirecting the field toward conditioning-based rather than joint-generation architectures for high-dimensional spatial omics. The proposed structural metrics (GSC, SSC) expose a blind spot in current evaluation: models can achieve competitive PCC while producing gene expression maps with incoherent regulatory networks or spatial autocorrelation patterns, undermining downstream pathway analysis and domain discovery. Open questions include whether the GFM alignment transfers to zero-shot cross-tissue settings, whether 3D volumetric extensions of the graph backbone are feasible, and whether the iterative diffusion inference cost can be reduced without sacrificing the structural fidelity gains that distinguish FLAG from deterministic regressors.
