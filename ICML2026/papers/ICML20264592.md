## Problem

Most speculative decoding deployments treat speculator training as a standalone offline distillation problem, creating three deployment frictions: high time-to-serve (weeks of offline training before a drafter is ready), delayed utility feedback (true end-to-end speedup cannot be predicted from acceptance rate alone due to system-level overheads), and domain-drift degradation (the drafter tracks an outdated verifier distribution as traffic shifts). The paper argues that speculative decoding must be reconsidered as a joint learning-and-serving problem rather than a train-then-serve pipeline.

## Input / Output

The system consumes a live stream of user prompts (no ground-truth responses needed) alongside a frozen target model (verifier) and a trainable draft model (speculator) that may start from random weights for day-0 deployment. During serving, each speculative step produces accepted prefixes and rejected branches along with target-model hidden states from three selected layers, which are streamed via GPU-to-GPU RPC into a bounded training buffer. The outputs are (i) decoded text tokens whose distribution is identical to the target model's (verification guarantees quality preservation) and (ii) periodically hot-swapped speculator weight snapshots pushed back to the inference server without service interruption.

## Method

Aurora reframes online speculator learning as an asynchronous RL problem: the draft model is the policy, the target model plus verifier is the environment, and accept/reject decisions provide structured reward signals. The architecture decouples an SGLang-based inference server (running EAGLE-3 speculative decoding) from a separate multi-GPU training server, connected by a GPU-aware RPC channel (TensorPipe backend) that transmits pre-computed hidden states, logits, and rejection trajectories without loading a duplicate target model on the training node. Training uses a combined objective: a forward-KL acceptance loss on verifier-approved tokens plus a novel Discard Sampling rejection loss (weighted KL on rejected branches with top-k filtering) that explicitly teaches the speculator to avoid its own mistakes. A custom Tree Attention mask processes the entire branching speculative tree (accepted and rejected paths) in a single batched forward/backward pass, making per-verification-step training computationally feasible. A lazy synchronization policy hot-swaps updated speculator weights at a configurable interval (e.g., every 80 requests), balancing adaptation speed against inference-path disruption from cache invalidation and latency jitter.

## Results

On frontier open-source models, Aurora (trained from scratch) achieves a 1.57× throughput speedup (211.8 vs 134.9 tokens/s at BS=1) on MiniMax M2.1 229B and a 1.51× speedup (265.7 vs 176.4 tokens/s at BS=1) on Qwen3-Coder-Next 80B, with acceptance lengths of 2.62 and 3.06 respectively. On Qwen3-8B under mixed traffic, an untrained speculator deployed on day-0 converges to an acceptance length of 3.08 and 302.3 tokens/s, surpassing both the static pretrained baseline (2.63 acc. len., 288.8 tokens/s) and a pretrained-then-finetuned baseline (2.99). Under abrupt domain shift, Aurora recovers to 2.46 acceptance length within ~10,000 requests (vs. 2.57 for the static baseline), demonstrating that online adaptation from scratch can match or exceed carefully pretrained speculators.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is a frozen (static) pretrained EAGLE-3 speculator deployed without any online updates. Under mixed traffic on Qwen3-8B, Aurora trained from scratch achieves an acceptance length of 3.08 and 302.3 tokens/s, outperforming the static baseline (2.63 acc. len., 288.8 tokens/s) by 17% in acceptance and 4.7% in throughput, and also beating a pretrained-then-finetuned variant (2.99 acc. len.). Under abrupt domain shift, the static baseline degrades and remains lowest, while all Aurora online variants recover to a higher plateau; Aurora (scratch) converges to 2.46 acceptance length within ~10,000 requests, nearly matching the static baseline's 2.57. On frontier models (MiniMax M2.1, Qwen3-Coder-Next), Aurora from scratch delivers 1.45×–1.57× throughput over the no-speculation baseline, with the largest gains at small batch sizes (BS=1) where per-step overhead dominates. Among online training variants (FKL, RKL, RKL+NTP, RKL+discard), the differences are marginal under domain shift, with simple token-level RKL capturing most of the attainable gain; discarded-token training provides a noticeable additional benefit only when lookahead is increased to 10 (Llama-3.1-8B coding setting).

## Relation to Prior Work

Aurora builds on EAGLE-3's hidden-state-based draft tree architecture as its speculative decoding mechanism and on AReaL's asynchronous actor-learner system design for decoupling inference from training. It departs from Online Speculative Decoding (OSD, Liu et al. 2023), which performs open-loop online distillation from the target model, by closing the loop: updated speculator weights are hot-swapped back into the serving stack and real-time serving signals (accept/reject outcomes) directly drive the training objective. The paper also distinguishes itself from general async RL systems (slime, miles) by optimizing for serving efficiency under SLO constraints rather than rollout throughput, and by treating domain shift as the primary failure mode rather than policy staleness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The day-0 deployment capability—where a randomly initialized speculator becomes competitive within thousands of live requests—removes a significant operational bottleneck for onboarding new frontier models to production serving stacks. The finding that online adaptation from scratch can match or exceed a carefully pretrained speculator (3.08 vs 2.63 acceptance length in mixed traffic) challenges the assumption that extensive offline distillation is a prerequisite for effective speculative decoding. Open questions include how the approach scales to truly production-scale multi-tenant traffic, how it interacts with other serving optimizations (disaggregated prefill/decode, KV-cache management), and whether the gains persist as target models are updated more frequently than the speculator can adapt. The main experiments on 8B models limit confidence in the day-0 claim for the largest deployments, though frontier-model results (229B, 80B) provide partial validation.
