## Problem

Automated LLM-based evaluation faces three persistent gaps: heterogeneous reliability across judge models, substantial latency and token costs from stronger evaluators, and the absence of principled strategies for allocating evaluation resources under explicit accuracy-latency-cost constraints. Additionally, large reasoning models (LRMs) that expose intermediate thinking traces have not been systematically benchmarked as judges, and the relationship between reasoning quality and verdict correctness remains uncharacterized, especially across difficulty regimes.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance consists of a question paired with two candidate responses (A, B) generated by distinct models (GPT-4o and Claude 3.5), with A/B order randomized to mitigate position bias. The judge outputs a three-way preference verdict (A, B, or Tie) with a 1024-token cap on the final verdict/rationale field; LRM judges additionally produce untruncated thinking traces collected separately. Routing decisions consume only low-cost pre-decision features (input length, domain cues, difficulty-proxy bucket, budget tags) and never use thinking traces or human gold labels as inputs, preserving a clean separation between routing signals and evaluation signals.

## Method

R²Eval is a three-module framework: a fixed benchmark layer (849 pairwise instances across six domains with Easy/Medium/Diff stratification and parallel Think/Non-Think variants), a unified LLM/LRM judge interface, and a routing layer applied post-benchmarking. The routing layer defines a utility U(m|x) = E[ACC_m] − λE[T_m] − μE[C_m] and instantiates it through three offline policies (ACC-only, ACC-Time, ACC-Time-Cost) and two online strategies: a contextual LLM-as-Router that scores judges within difficulty-banded subsets, and a trainable 7–8B router (Llama-3.1-8B or Qwen2.5-7B) fine-tuned with cross-entropy on utility-maximizing judge assignments. A key design choice is that LRM thinking traces serve purely as post-hoc diagnostic evidence (confusion matrices between reasoning quality and verdict correctness, error-type taxonomy) rather than as pre-routing inputs, enabling the paper to reveal systematic mismatches such as 'good thinking but wrong verdict' in subjective domains while keeping routing decisions grounded in cheap, available-before-invocation signals.

## Results

Across 19 judge models on the R²Eval benchmark (849 pairwise items, 6 domains), Gemini-2.5-Flash achieves the highest overall accuracy at 73.82%, leading reasoning domains at 74.90% (Math 75.63%, Logic 74.07%). Claude-3.5-Sonnet is the strongest non-reasoning judge at 72.83% (Writing 73.33%, Roleplay 71.33%). Among routing strategies, ACC-only achieves 75.4% average accuracy but at 18.7s latency and 3.0k tokens cost, while Router 3 (Qwen2.5-7B) achieves 73.2% average accuracy at 15.4s latency and 3.0k tokens, reducing selected-judge latency by 20–45% relative to ACC-only. Inter-annotator agreement (Fleiss' κ) ranges from 0.68 (Writing) to 0.81 (Math), establishing the human gold standard reliability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On reasoning tasks, LRMs clearly outperform LLMs: Gemini-2.5-Flash (74.90% avg) and Gemini-2.5-Pro (73.63%) surpass the best LLM, Qwen3-32B (72.18%), by 2.7 and 1.5 pp respectively. On non-reasoning tasks, the advantage reverses: Claude-3.5-Sonnet (72.83%) edges out the best LRM, Gemini-2.5-Flash (72.74%), suggesting explicit multi-step reasoning is less beneficial when human preference is driven by tone and stylistic alignment. Among routing strategies, ACC-only (75.4% avg accuracy) dominates on raw accuracy but incurs the highest latency (18.7s) and cost (3.0k tokens); Router 3 (Qwen backbone) trades ~2 pp accuracy for 15.4s latency and achieves the best Coding (74.6%) and Math (76.3%) among online routers, while Router 2 (Llama backbone) leads on Writing (77.2%). Both online routers reduce selected-judge latency by 20–45% relative to ACC-only, with bootstrap tests confirming Router 3 significantly exceeds ACC-Time in Coding and Math (p < 0.05). A fixed GPT-5 baseline (66.0% avg) is outperformed by all routing strategies by 7–10 pp, demonstrating that adaptive judge selection substantially outperforms any single fixed evaluator.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm established by MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena (Zheng et al., 2023) in two directions: it incorporates Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) that produce explicit thinking traces as judges, and it applies cost-aware routing—previously studied for generation tasks via FrugalGPT (Chen et al., 2023)—to the judge-allocation problem. The authors explicitly position judge routing as underexplored relative to generation routing, and the LRM-as-a-Judge benchmarking (with thinking-verdict diagnostics) is framed as absent from prior work. The difficulty-aware stratification and dual-generator response protocol build on conventions from JudgeBench and FairEval while adding thinking-augmented variants not present in those datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The R²Eval benchmark (849 items, 6 domains, 19 judges, thinking/non-thinking variants) provides a practical reference for evaluating both LLM and LRM judges under unified pairwise settings, filling a gap where no systematic LRM-as-a-Judge benchmark existed. The finding that thinking traces and verdict correctness are substantially misaligned in subjective domains (e.g., 30.6% flawed-thinking-correct-verdict in writing) challenges the implicit assumption that explicit reasoning improves judgment quality uniformly. Key limitations include the endogeneity of difficulty buckets (derived from a fixed baseline panel rather than external ground truth) and the controlled dual-generator protocol (GPT-4o + Claude 3.5) that may limit external validity. Open questions include whether thinking traces could serve as pre-routing signals, whether routing generalizes under broader generator distributions, and whether the modest routing gains (2–3 pp over best fixed judge) justify the added system complexity in production settings.
