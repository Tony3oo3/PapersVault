## Problem

Frontier reasoning models sometimes fail on solvable problems not from insufficient capability but from premature self-doubt about token budgets—a phenomenon the authors term context anxiety. No prior work had systematically detected, quantified, or shown this failure mode to be behaviorally mutable rather than a fixed capacity limit.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261809/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline consumes Tower of Hanoi problem specifications (2–12 disks with varied peg labels to prevent template memorization) and shortest-path grid instances (5×5 to 12×12 with obstacles), producing move-sequence solutions. For measurement, reasoning traces are fed to a panel of LLM judges that output a count of anxiety-related phrases (e.g., 'too long to write out'), and the same model is prompted post-hoc to estimate its own token usage, yielding a winsorized token ratio. The fine-tuning stage takes filtered reasoning traces (correct solutions with no detected anxiety) and produces adapted GPT-OSS-20B weights, with loss applied only to the REASONING segment while final-answer tokens are masked.

## Method

The paper introduces a three-part methodology: (1) an LLM-judge detection protocol that classifies reasoning traces for semantic equivalents of output-infeasibility claims, separating anxiety-driven failures from capability-driven failures; (2) a winsorized token ratio metric measuring how accurately models estimate their own token consumption, revealing that anxious models overestimate by ~24% while non-anxious models underestimate by ~19%; and (3) a selective supervised fine-tuning protocol that distills reasoning traces from frontier teacher models, retaining only anxiety-free correct solutions and training with loss restricted to reasoning tokens (final answers masked). A fixed-effects regression (model + disk FE) isolates the causal impact of anxiety, showing a 15.3% accuracy reduction and 54% token inflation on correct answers. Critically, an all-correct SFT control (no anxiety filtering) degrades performance, demonstrating that the behavioral filter—not mere exposure to successful traces—is what drives the improvement.

## Results

Context anxiety reduces task accuracy by 15.3% (p<0.01) and inflates token usage by 2,323 tokens (~54%) on correct solutions, after controlling for model and difficulty fixed effects. Models exhibiting context anxiety overestimate their token requirements by ~24%, while non-anxious models underestimate by ~19%. Lightweight supervised fine-tuning on anxiety-free reasoning traces (Reasoning SFT) reduces detected anxiety rates by over 50% (e.g., from 0.63 to 0.34 at 7 disks) and improves accuracy across held-out difficulties, with the largest gain being +26.7% absolute on 5×5 shortest-path grids.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three configurations of GPT-OSS-20B: the unmodified baseline, Reasoning SFT (fine-tuned on anxiety-filtered correct traces), and All-Correct SFT (fine-tuned on all correct traces without anxiety filtering). Reasoning SFT consistently matches or exceeds the baseline across all evaluated difficulties, with the most pronounced gains at intermediate complexity (5-disk Hanoi: 83.3% vs. 76.7%; 5×5 shortest path: 30.0% vs. 3.3%). Critically, the All-Correct SFT control severely degrades performance on 5-disk Hanoi (46.7%, a −30.0-point drop from baseline), demonstrating that the anxiety-based filtering—not merely fine-tuning on correct solutions—is what drives the improvement. On the cross-task transfer to shortest-path grids, Reasoning SFT achieves a +26.7-point absolute gain over baseline on 5×5 grids despite no shortest-path data in the fine-tuning mixture, while All-Correct SFT achieves a smaller +20.0-point gain. Detected anxiety rates drop substantially under Reasoning SFT (e.g., 0.28→0.12 at 5 disks; 0.63→0.34 at 7 disks), confirming the behavioral shift rather than memorization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formalizing 'context anxiety'—a term informally coined by Cognition to describe Claude 4.5's behavioral shifts near token limits—into a measurable, systematic failure mode with a mechanistic explanation (miscalibrated token estimation). It draws on the long-context degradation literature (Liu et al., 2024; Levy et al., 2024), LLM calibration and self-knowledge work (Kadavath et al., 2022; Kapoor et al., 2024), and overthinking/underthinking analyses (Sui et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025), but departs from all of these by isolating a specific behavioral mechanism: models that overestimate required tokens by ~24% prematurely abandon solvable tasks. The mitigation strategy repurposes standard SFT and knowledge distillation but introduces a novel filtering criterion (retaining only anxiety-free reasoning traces), positioning the contribution as a behavioral adaptation protocol rather than a capability expansion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central implication is that a meaningful fraction of reasoning-model failures on long-horizon tasks are not capability-limited but calibration-limited, opening a complementary improvement axis (accurate self-assessment) alongside scaling and stronger reasoning supervision. The finding that lightweight SFT on anxiety-free traces reduces context anxiety by over 50% and improves accuracy without new information is practically actionable. Key limitations temper the generality claim: the primary evaluation is a single symbolic task (Tower of Hanoi), detection relies on explicit self-reports in reasoning traces (potentially missing implicit disengagement), and the fine-tuning demonstration uses a 20B model. Open questions include whether the mechanism and mitigation transfer to open-ended, multi-turn, or tool-use settings where 'token budget' is less cleanly defined, and whether implicit anxiety (without explicit verbalization) follows the same miscalibration signature.
