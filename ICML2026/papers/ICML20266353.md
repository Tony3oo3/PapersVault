## Problem

The AI community's rapid convergence on generalist foundation models leaves a critical gap for assistive AI: the problem of converting broad priors into stable, reliable competence for a specific user and environment over long horizons. Current research and benchmarks treat deployment-time personalization as a secondary product concern rather than a primary scientific objective, despite the fact that in assistive settings correctness is local, feedback is sparse and implicit, the environment is non-stationary, and mistakes are costly because they affect a real person in a real context.

## Input / Output

The systems described consume streaming, on-policy interaction data generated after deployment—web browsing traces with user edits and accept/reject signals, continuous egocentric video and audio from wearables, and proprioceptive/visual streams from home robots—rather than static curated datasets. What distinguishes this input regime is that it is non-i.i.d., temporally correlated, shaped by the agent's own prior actions (creating feedback loops), non-stationary as preferences and environments drift, and privacy-sensitive enough to resist centralization. Outputs are not single predictions but continuously evolving policies: personalized web actions, in-context action predictions and just-in-time guidance from wearables, and adapted manipulation policies for home robots, all improving over time within a bounded user-environment context.

## Method

As a position paper, the core contribution is a reframing of 'specialist' as deployment-adaptive—systems whose behavior is meaningfully shaped by local history, constraints, and feedback—rather than narrow-task, along with a proposed layered architecture: a generalist foundation-model backbone paired with persistent user-editable memory, environment-specific latent state, bounded skill/policy modules, and safety monitors that gate updates and support rollback. The paper argues the central learning problem is not pretraining but the post-deployment loop: continual learning from temporally correlated streaming data under feedback loops, fast adaptation from sparse implicit signals (accept/reject, corrections, observational learning), and change-point detection to separate durable preferences from transient contexts. It emphasizes organic/observational data as the primary learning substrate over intentionally scaled domain-specific datasets, drawing on cognitive science analogies (statistical learning, inverse planning, social learning) to motivate how humans achieve similar situated specialization. The paper also proposes new evaluation paradigms—adaptation curves measuring improvement over bounded interactions, and personalization-with-privacy benchmarks—that shift the metric from static cross-user performance to longitudinal within-user improvement under safety and privacy constraints.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not present quantitative experimental results, benchmarks, or performance metrics. Instead, it argues conceptually that deployment-adaptive specialist models—systems that specialize to a particular user, environment, and interaction history after deployment—are a necessary complement to generalist foundation models for reliable assistive AI. The paper's contribution is a reframing of the research agenda around three case studies (web agents, wearable assistants, home robots) and two core research challenges (continual learning from streaming data, fast adaptation with limited data), rather than empirical validation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare its proposed approach against specific baselines or competing methods on shared benchmarks. The closest analog to a 'comparison' is the conceptual contrast between generalist foundation models (e.g., GPT-4, RT-2, OpenVLA, π0.5) and the proposed deployment-adaptive specialist paradigm. The author acknowledges that generalist models provide strong priors and may continue to improve rapidly, but argues that even strong zero-shot competence leaves unresolved the problem of safe, reliable adaptation to one specific user and environment over time. The paper positions its specialist thesis as complementary to, not a replacement for, generalist pretraining, and notes that concurrent work by Goldfeder et al. (2026) similarly questions AGI-style generality as the central objective. No head-to-head empirical comparisons are provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper deliberately reframes rather than introduces new subfields, drawing on continual learning (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017; Parisi et al., 2019), meta-learning and few-shot adaptation (Finn et al., 2017; Duan et al., 2017), preference and inverse reinforcement learning (Christiano et al., 2017; Ziebart et al., 2008), and embodied robot learning (Brohan et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2024). It carves new ground by unifying these threads under a single 'deployment-adaptive specialist' objective for assistive AI, arguing that the distinctive deployment regime—on-policy, privacy-sensitive, non-stationary, safety-critical streaming data from one user in one environment—remains underemphasized relative to the dominant generalist-pretraining agenda. The paper aligns with concurrent work by Goldfeder et al. (2026) questioning AGI-style generality, and with cognitive science on situated, embodied, and extended cognition (Suchman, 1987; Clark & Chalmers, 1998), but positions its contribution as a research-program reframing rather than a new algorithmic or empirical result.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central impact is agenda-setting: it argues that the field's evaluation and algorithmic focus on static, cross-user generalization systematically neglects the 'last mile' of longitudinal, local specialization that determines real-world usefulness of assistants. Key open questions it surfaces include how to prevent self-reinforcing error loops in closed-loop on-policy learning, how to achieve fast adaptation from sparse implicit feedback without catastrophic forgetting, and how to design evaluation (adaptation curves, personalization-with-privacy benchmarks) that measures safe improvement over time rather than one-shot accuracy. As a position paper with no experiments, its practical impact depends on whether the community adopts the proposed evaluation templates and research challenges; the specific algorithmic mechanisms for safe streaming specialization remain entirely open.
