## Problem

Score-based and flow-based generative policies offer superior expressivity for capturing complex, multi-modal action distributions in offline RL, but their iterative multi-step sampling creates a severe computational bottleneck that limits their use in online fine-tuning and high-frequency decision-making. Existing acceleration methods like consistency models reduce sampling to one step but rely on L2 loss that only matches first- and second-order moments, potentially causing mode collapse and distribution mismatch in high-dimensional action spaces.

## Input / Output

The method takes a continuous state vector s as conditioning input and produces a continuous action vector a sampled from a learned generative policy. Action sampling is performed inductively via a neural network f_θ that maps from a noisy state at time t to a denoised state at time s in a single forward pass, requiring as few as 1–2 sampling steps (vs. 50–1000 for standard diffusion/flow). The policy network is a 3-layer MLP (256 hidden dim) conditioned on the state, and the critic uses standard double Q-networks. Training operates on offline trajectory datasets from D4RL (Gym locomotion, Adroit manipulation, Kitchen) with batch size 256.

## Method

MoMa QL learns a one-step action sampler by minimizing the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) between marginal interpolant distributions at two intermediate timesteps, using a consistency-training-inspired self-consistency constraint: given s < r < t, the model is trained so that p_{s|t}^θ(x_s) ≈ p_{s|r}^{θ⁻}(x_s) under an RBF kernel. Unlike consistency models that use L2 loss (equivalent to MMD with a non-characteristic kernel matching only mean and variance), the RBF-kernel MMD matches all orders of moments via an infinite-dimensional feature map, providing distribution-level convergence guarantees. The framework is built on marginal-preserving stochastic interpolants (DDIM), which provably satisfy the marginal-preserving property needed for inductive convergence (Theorem A.7), and is embedded in a behavior-regularized actor-critic (BRAC) framework with double Q-learning. The empirical MMD loss is estimated via V-statistics over grouped particles sharing the same (s, r, t) tuple, with a weighting function w(s,t) = 1/(α_t² + σ_t²) to balance noise levels. Consistency models emerge as a special case when the kernel is replaced by the squared L2 distance, unifying the two approaches under a single moment-matching framework.

## Results

On D4RL Gym locomotion tasks, MoMa QL achieves an average normalized score of 95.5, surpassing the strongest baseline Diffusion-QL (87.9) by ~1.09×. On Adroit manipulation tasks, MoMa QL reaches 56.7 average, edging out ReBRAC (55.4) and IQL (53.4). On Kitchen tasks, MoMa QL scores 73.1, outperforming X-QL (72.9) and Diffusion-QL (69.0). In the offline-to-online setting, MoMa QL achieves 101.4 average, beating Diffusion-QL (94.2) and Consistency-AC (88.0), with the largest gains on medium-replay datasets (e.g., halfcheetah-mr: 63.3→80.9, +28%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoMa QL outperforms Diffusion-QL (Wang et al., 2023) on Gym average (95.5 vs. 87.9) and Kitchen (73.1 vs. 69.0), but trails slightly on Hopper (99.9 vs. 101.0) and loses on hopper-me (88.9 vs. 111.1); in offline-to-online, Diffusion-QL wins on halfcheetah-m (99.6 vs. 83.1) and walker2d-m (118.3 vs. 99.1) but MoMa QL dominates on medium-replay tasks (hopper-mr: 113.5 vs. 68.4, walker2d-mr: 117.9 vs. 95.7). Against Consistency-AC (Ding & Jin, 2024), MoMa QL wins decisively on Gym (95.5 vs. 85.1), Kitchen (73.1 vs. 45.3), and offline-to-online (101.4 vs. 88.0). On Adroit, MoMa QL (56.7) surpasses ReBRAC (55.4) and IQL (53.4) on average, though ReBRAC retains an edge on pen-expert (152 vs. 142). MoMa QL also beats X-QL (83.7 Gym, 72.9 Kitchen), CQL (77.6 Gym), IQL (77.0 Gym), ARQ (76.2 Gym), and IDQL-A (82.1 Gym) across all Gym tasks. In the BC setting, MoMa QL (89.8) outperforms Diffusion-BC (76.3), Consistency-BC (69.7), and all other baselines by a wide margin, with the largest gap on medium-replay datasets (e.g., walker2d-mr: 103.0 vs. 81.8 for TD3+BC). The method is also ~6× faster than Diffusion-BC and ~1.5× faster than Consistency-AC in training time per 1k steps.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoMa QL sits at the intersection of three research threads: stochastic interpolants (Albergo et al., 2025) providing the unified flow/diffusion framework, consistency training (Song et al., 2023a) inspiring the self-consistency loss structure, and the BRAC actor-critic paradigm (Fujimoto & Gu, 2021) supplying the offline RL backbone. The key conceptual departure is replacing the pointwise consistency loss (which the paper shows corresponds to a non-positive-definite kernel matching only first- and second-order moments) with an MMD-based divergence using an RBF kernel, thereby matching all moment orders. The marginal-preserving interpolant framework from Zhou et al. (2025) provides the theoretical scaffolding that guarantees existence and inductive convergence of the minimizer, and the paper proves consistency models are a strict special case of its formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in eliminating the iterative-sampling bottleneck of diffusion and flow-based policies: MoMa QL achieves ~6× speedup over Diffusion-BC and ~1.5× over Consistency-AC while maintaining or improving performance, making generative policies viable for online fine-tuning where inference latency is critical. The offline-to-online results (e.g., +28% on halfcheetah-mr) demonstrate that the accelerated sampling enables stable adaptation without catastrophic forgetting, a common failure mode in fine-tuning generative policies. Limitations include evaluation restricted to low-dimensional D4RL continuous control (no vision-based or high-dimensional tasks), a notable underperformance on hopper-me (88.9 vs. 111.1 for Diffusion-QL), and the MMD advantage over consistency loss being theoretically clean but empirically modest in magnitude. Open questions include whether the all-moment matching benefit becomes more pronounced in higher-dimensional action spaces and whether the V-statistic estimator scales efficiently with batch size in large-scale settings.
