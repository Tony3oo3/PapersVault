## Problem

The standard Bradley-Terry loss for reward modeling couples update magnitude to both prediction error and representation distance between chosen/rejected responses, causing fine-grained pairs with small representation distance to receive vanishingly weak updates even when misranked, while large-distance pairs dominate learning. This structural bias systematically under-trains the subtle distinctions (e.g., a single logical error in code) that reward models most need to capture.

## Input / Output

The reward model takes a prompt-response pair (x, y) and produces a scalar reward score via a linear head on the LLM backbone's final-layer representation. Training consumes triplets (x, y_w, y_l) of prompt, chosen, and rejected responses. NORMBT additionally computes the L2 distance between the last-token embeddings of y_w and y_l during the forward pass to derive a per-pair normalization weight, requiring no extra backward pass.

## Method

The paper first decomposes the BT gradient norm into a product of prediction error |σ(d)−1| and representation distance ‖h_w−h_l‖ (Eq. 7), showing that the latter inflates or suppresses updates independent of model accuracy. NORMBT introduces a per-pair weight w̃ = μ_t / (‖h_w−h_l‖ + ε) that rescales each pair's loss contribution inversely to its representation distance, so that update magnitude is driven primarily by prediction error. The L2 distance of last-token embeddings serves as a computationally free proxy for the full gradient norm (validated with r > 0.9 correlation). An exponential moving average (EMA, β=0.9) of batch-level mean distances stabilizes the normalization against representation scale drift during training. The final objective is a weighted BT loss that is a drop-in modification requiring no architectural changes and negligible overhead.

## Results

On RewardBench, NORMBT consistently outperforms the BT baseline across all four experimental settings (two backbones × two datasets). With gemma-2b-it trained on Unified-Feedback (80K), NORMBT achieves 73.57 average accuracy vs. 72.25 for BT, with the largest gain in the Reasoning category (80.71 vs. 75.41, +5.3%). With Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct on the same data, the average improves from 75.24 to 76.96, and Reasoning jumps from 71.70 to 76.93 (+5.2%). On Skywork-80K, gains are also consistent (e.g., Llama Reasoning: 67.05 → 74.60, +7.6%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

NORMBT outperforms the standard BT baseline on average across all settings, with the most pronounced gains in the Reasoning category (+5.3% for gemma-2b-it, +5.2% for Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct on Unified-Feedback). Against BT+Margin (Touvron et al., 2023), NORMBT wins on average (73.57 vs. 71.23) and especially on Reasoning (80.71 vs. 72.98), while BT+Margin actually degrades Reasoning performance relative to vanilla BT. BT+Margin (out) (Wang et al., 2025) is the closest competitor on average (72.53 vs. 73.57) and even edges out NORMBT on Reasoning for gemma-2b-it (78.09 vs. 80.71 is actually NORMBT winning), but NORMBT still leads on average and on Llama. BT+Label Smoothing (Liu et al., 2024a) underperforms vanilla BT on average (69.95 vs. 72.25), with the largest drop in Reasoning (72.28 vs. 75.41), confirming that uniformly dampening gradients hurts fine-grained pairs. In ablation comparisons (Table 2), NORMBT also beats BT+BSR (Hong et al., 2025) on Reasoning (80.71 vs. 74.40), BT+LayerNorm (Ba et al., 2016) on Reasoning (80.71 vs. 73.22), and BT+Gradient Clipping (Pascanu et al., 2013) on average (73.57 vs. 72.69), though gradient clipping is competitive on Reasoning (77.96 vs. 80.71 still favors NORMBT).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Bradley-Terry loss as the standard pairwise objective for reward modeling in RLHF, providing a gradient-norm decomposition that reveals a previously unexamined coupling between prediction error and representation distance. It departs from margin-based variants (Touvron et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025) and label smoothing (Liu et al., 2024a), which modify the prediction-error term, by instead normalizing the representation-distance component per pair. The closest prior work is BSR (Hong et al., 2025), which also identifies representation-distance effects but intervenes at the batch level via a reward-scale regularizer, whereas NORMBT reweights individual pairwise gradients. The method is positioned as orthogonal to data curation, architectural changes, and calibration approaches, targeting a structural property of the BT gradient itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the de facto standard loss in RLHF systematically under-weights fine-grained preference pairs (e.g., subtle reasoning errors) due to representation distance is a useful diagnostic for practitioners building reward models, and NORMBT offers a nearly zero-cost fix. However, the average improvements are modest (~1–2%), the largest gains are concentrated in the Reasoning category, and a slight trade-off appears for large-distance pairs (e.g., Safety). Open questions include whether the effect persists at larger model scales (only 2B–3B backbones tested), how NORMBT interacts with auxiliary regularization or generative reward modeling, and whether full end-to-end RLHF policy training (beyond Best-of-N) translates the benchmark gains into downstream quality.
