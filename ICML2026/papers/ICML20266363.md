## Problem

Neural surrogates for NP-hard combinatorial problems are routinely evaluated as drop-in replacements for classical solvers, yet the community lacks a systematic framework for accounting for the full costs (training, label generation, distribution shift) and the guarantees (feasibility, approximation ratio, robustness) that classical methods explicitly provide. The paper argues that without system-level requirements such as vector-indexed retrieval or end-to-end differentiability, neural methods rarely offer compelling advantages, and that empirical gains are often attributable to the combinatorial decoders rather than the neural component itself.

## Input / Output

The empirical experiments consume graphs as adjacency matrices for clique number estimation across six benchmarks (IMDB, RB, Brock, DSJC, MUTAG, Enzymes) and produce MSE against ground-truth clique numbers; the retrieval experiments take query-corpus graph pairs and produce ranked lists scored by MAP@100 with per-query latency. A distinctive aspect of the decoder ablation setup is that the neural output is replaced with simple structural features—uniform random scores, node degrees, or core numbers—while the original decoding procedure is kept unchanged, isolating the decoder's contribution. The retrieval ablation compares full-corpus oracle scoring against LSH-filtered oracle scoring and LSH-filtered neural scoring on 100K-corpus PTC datasets.

## Method

The paper's central contribution is a costs-and-guarantees audit framework comprising five cost dimensions (per-instance inference, training, data, approximation failure, distribution shift) and five guarantee dimensions (feasibility, approximation ratio, robustness, incremental improvement, distributional generalization), applied systematically to surveyed neural combinatorial optimization methods. The key empirical finding comes from decoder ablation experiments on DIFUSCO, EGN, and SCT: feeding non-neural structural features (random, degree, core number) into each method's own decoder often matches or exceeds the full neural pipeline's MSE, demonstrating that the decoder performs most of the optimization work. Distribution shift experiments on PTC-MM-m show EGN's MSE increasing over 10× under an out-of-distribution size split (0.284→3.260) while a random baseline degrades gracefully (0.745→1.359), and SCT exhibits non-monotonic behavior (MSE decreases under OOD), highlighting the opacity of neural generalization. The paper closes with a five-point diagnostic checklist identifying three legitimate use cases for neural surrogates: enabling end-to-end differentiability in larger learning pipelines, satisfying system-level constraints that require embedding-based ANN retrieval, and augmenting classical solvers with learned branching or warm-start policies while preserving solver-level guarantees.

## Results

The paper's central experimental finding is that combinatorial decoders paired with simple non-neural inputs (random scores, node degree, core number) often match or outperform the full neural pipeline on clique number estimation. On the SCT method, the random-input decoder achieves lower MSE than the neural method on all six datasets (e.g., 0.000 vs. 4.102 on IMDB, 14.705 vs. 50.230 on RB). For DIFUSCO, the degree-based decoder beats the neural output on five of six datasets (e.g., 0.680 vs. 1.560 on DSJC, 0.504 vs. 0.950 on Enzymes). In the graph retrieval experiment, replacing the oracle scorer with the IsoNet neural scorer reduces per-query time by ~6× but drops MAP@100 by 0.35–0.5 across all three PTC datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three neural clique-estimation methods (DIFUSCO, EGN, SCT) against their own decoders fed with non-neural inputs (random, degree, core number). SCT is the most dramatically outperformed: the random-input decoder beats the neural method on all six datasets, with MSE dropping from 4.102 to 0.000 on IMDB and from 92.675 to 2.590 on DSJC. DIFUSCO's degree-based decoder beats the neural output on five of six datasets (e.g., 6.245 vs. 10.590 on Brock, 0.504 vs. 0.950 on Enzymes), while core number wins on IMDB (0.102 vs. 1.361). EGN's neural output retains an edge on DSJC (0.030 vs. 0.035 random) and MUTAG (1.010 vs. 2.715 random), but random inputs beat it on IMDB, RB, Brock, and Enzymes. In the separate graph retrieval experiment, the IsoNet neural scorer (Roy et al., 2022) is compared against an oracle scorer on LSH-filtered candidates: the neural scorer is ~6× faster per query (0.149–0.174 s vs. 0.915–1.153 s) but sacrifices 0.35–0.5 MAP@100, making the oracle the better choice when sub-second latency suffices.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the dominant narrative in neural combinatorial optimization (Pointer Networks, Attention-based routing, POMO, DIFUSCO, EGN, etc.) by arguing that wholesale solver replacement is rarely justified. Rather than building on a specific prior method, it repurposes the evaluation criteria long standard in classical algorithmics—approximation ratios, feasibility certificates, distributional robustness, and incremental improvement guarantees—as a diagnostic lens for neural surrogates. It carves new ground by introducing a five-cost/five-guarantee framework (Table 1) and a practitioner checklist that has no direct precedent in the neural combinatorial optimization literature, while acknowledging the legitimate niches (end-to-end differentiability, system-level embedding constraints, solver augmentation) where neural methods do earn their keep.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's decoder ablation experiments—showing that uniform-random or simple structural scores (degree, core number) fed into the decoders of DIFUSCO, EGN, and SCT often match or beat the neural network's own outputs—directly challenge the assumption that the neural component is the source of solution quality. Combined with the 10× OOD degradation of EGN (0.284→3.260 MSE) versus graceful degradation of random baselines (0.745→1.359), this raises the open question of how much of the reported progress in neural combinatorial solvers is attributable to the decoder rather than the learned representation. The costs-and-guarantees framework and five-question checklist offer a practical tool for practitioners, but the empirical evidence is confined to clique number estimation on six datasets, leaving the decoder-dependence claim untested for TSP, VRP, subgraph isomorphism, and other problem families. A key limitation is that the position, while well-argued, does not fully engage with the amortized-optimization defense under genuinely stable industrial distributions, nor does it quantify the compute at which a neural surrogate's inference speed advantage would overcome its training and retraining overhead.
