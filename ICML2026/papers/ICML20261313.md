## Problem

Standard LLM-as-a-Judge protocols for evaluating mathematical proofs correlate poorly with expert human judgment, particularly at the upper-undergraduate to early graduate level where proofs demand constructive reasoning beyond template retrieval. Existing benchmarks face a fundamental trade-off: autoformalization frameworks (e.g., Lean) provide correctness guarantees but are prohibitively expensive to scale, while LLM judges are scalable yet exhibit systematic biases—grade inflation, sycophancy toward persuasive formatting—that mask logical failures. No prior benchmark systematically quantifies this alignment gap across multiple mathematical disciplines and multiple judge models simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 272 expert-curated proof problems across 10 upper-undergraduate/early-graduate disciplines, paired with LaTeX solutions generated by 5 frontier models and graded against two distinct rubrics: a Course-Specific Rubric (penalizing advanced machinery without derivation) and an Expert-Domain Rubric (permitting implicit steps trivial to domain specialists). Outputs include granular numerical scores on a 6-level scale [0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9, 1.0], per-domain bias deltas (Δ = Score_AI − Score_Human), binary pass/fail determinations at the ≥0.9 threshold, and decomposed error metrics (leniency rate as false positives, harshness rate as false negatives) computed across the full 7×5 evaluator-solver matrix. All automated evaluations run at temperature T=0.0 for deterministic reproducibility, and a two-step pipeline separates the raw JSON score from post-hoc rationalization to prevent parsing failures.

## Method

QEDBENCH introduces a dual-rubric alignment benchmark that decouples generation quality from evaluation reliability via a fully crossed 7×5 evaluator-solver factorial design (7 judge models × 5 solver models) grounded in 1,000+ hours of PhD-level expert annotation across 48 domain-matched evaluators. The core mechanism contrasts the Course-Specific Rubric against the Expert-Domain Rubric to isolate whether judge misalignment stems from rubric specificity or model-internal priors; an ablation on GPT-5.2 Pro reveals 'Rubric Insensitivity,' where prompt constraints fail to significantly alter alignment (Pearson r ≈ 0.69 under both rubrics, MAE ≈ 0.13–0.14), indicating internal representations dominate over context-window instructions. Anti-contamination is enforced through a three-stage pipeline: source diversity from graduate texts and qualifying exams, automated adversarial web-search auditing via o3-deep-research, and manual syntactic rewording of all problem statements. The design enables statistical isolation of judge bias from solver skill, revealing a bidirectional alignment gap—discrete-domain inflation (e.g., Llama 4 Maverick +0.36 in Combinatorics) and continuous-domain punitiveness (e.g., DeepSeek-V3 −0.14 in Complex Analysis).

## Results

On QEDBENCH (272 problems, 1,300+ proofs), Gemini 3.0 Pro achieves the best solver performance with an 86.4% pass rate and 0.91 average human-evaluated score, followed by GPT-5 Pro at 76.8% and 0.84. Among the seven LLM judges, GPT-5.2 Pro shows the closest alignment to the human baseline (74.1% pass rate vs. 67.7% human; 0.830 vs. 0.795 average score), while Llama 4 Maverick exhibits severe grade inflation (90.2% pass rate, 74.8% Leniency Rate) and DeepSeek-V3 is the strictest (72.2% pass rate, 12.3% Harshness Rate). The dual-rubric ablation on GPT-5.2 Pro reveals Rubric Insensitivity: switching from Expert to Course-Specific rubric changes Pearson correlation only from r=0.69 to r=0.67 (MAE 0.13→0.14), indicating internal priors override prompt constraints.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between seven LLM judges and a human expert ground truth (48 PhD-level evaluators, 1,000+ hours). GPT-5.2 Pro is the best-aligned judge (Δ=+0.035 average score, 38.0% leniency), while Llama 4 Maverick is the worst (Δ=+0.106, 74.8% leniency, 90.2% pass rate vs. 67.7% human). DeepSeek-V3 is the strictest automated judge (12.3% harshness) but still slightly lenient overall (72.2% vs. 67.7%). On the solver side, Gemini 3.0 Pro dominates (86.4% pass, 0.91 avg), outperforming GPT-5 Pro (76.8%, 0.84) by ~10 points in pass rate. A critical domain-specific finding is that Claude Sonnet 4.5 achieves 100% pass in ODEs but collapses to 27.3% in Combinatorics, while DeepSeek-Prover-V2 drops to 9.1% in Combinatorics, revealing a discrete-continuous reasoning divide that uniform benchmarks mask.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023) and fine-grained proof evaluation efforts (ProofBench, RefGrader) by shifting focus from competition-level or research-level proofs to the upper-undergraduate/early-graduate curriculum, and from fine-tuned evaluators to off-the-shelf frontier models. It departs from prior work by explicitly decoupling solver capability from judge reliability through a 7×5 cross-model matrix and a dual-rubric strategy (course-specific vs. expert), rather than treating evaluation quality as implicitly tied to model performance. The dual-rubric design builds on U-MATH's meta-evaluation subset (μ-MATH) but generalizes it into a systematic calibration tool, while the anti-contamination pipeline and tiered expert scoring protocol draw on practices from FrontierMath and IMProofBench.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding of Rubric Insensitivity—that explicit pedagogical constraints in the prompt barely shift a judge's alignment (r=0.69 vs. 0.67)—challenges the common assumption that better prompt engineering can fix LLM judge calibration, pointing instead toward process supervision or fine-tuning as necessary next steps. The bidirectional alignment gap (leniency in discrete domains, punitiveness in continuous ones) means that no single 'stricter' or 'nicer' calibration will work across all of mathematics, complicating deployment of automated judges in educational settings. Open questions include whether the findings generalize beyond English-language proofs, whether the 272-problem scale is sufficient for stable per-domain estimates, and whether adversarial training or process-level reward signals can close the alignment gap that prompt-level interventions cannot.
