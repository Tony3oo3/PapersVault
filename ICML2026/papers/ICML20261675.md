## Problem

Existing multi-objective RL methods either condition on a fixed preference weight or handle robustness by selecting a single adversarial weight once per episode, neither of which captures state-reactive preference shifts that occur after outcomes materialize. This paper formalizes time-consistent robust MORL where an adversary re-selects the scalarization weight after every transition (observing the realized next state and reward), paying a Bregman-divergence switching cost, and shows that the resulting objective is governed by a Bellman-Isaacs fixed point with an inner weight minimization inside each backup.

## Input / Output

The method operates on the augmented state (s, w) where s is the environment state and w is the current preference weight on the d-dimensional simplex; the vector reward r(s,a,s') is revealed after each transition and is observed by the adversary before it selects the next weight w'. The output is a robust value function V*(s,w) (or Q_θ(s,w,a) in the deep setting) together with a Bellman residual certificate ε(V̂) = ‖TV̂ − V̂‖∞ that converts approximation error into a uniform performance bound of 2ε/(1−γ). Unlike standard MORL where w is an exogenous conditioning input, here w is an endogenous adversarial control that must be re-optimized at every backup step using the current critic.

## Method

The core operator is (TV)(s,w) = max_a E_{s'}[min_{w'∈W}{⟨w', r(s,a,s')⟩ + λD_Φ(w'|w) + γV(s',w')}], where the inner minimization over the next weight w' is nested inside the Bellman backup, enforcing time consistency. In the tabular regime, the weight simplex is discretized to a finite grid W_G and value iteration is run on the augmented state space S×W_G, yielding an exact Bellman residual certificate. In the deep regime, a DQN-style Q_θ(s,w,a) is trained with on-the-fly inner minimization: at each backup target, J mirror/prox steps (exponentiated-gradient for KL, projected gradient for ℓ₂) are executed through the target critic Q_θ̄ to approximate the inner argmin, keeping the learning target aligned with the Bellman-Isaacs operator. The paper proves γ-contraction of T, existence of stationary saddle equilibria (finite grid via Shapley, continuous W via Berge + measurable selection), and a residual-to-policy-performance certificate. For evaluation, the BR-K protocol trains K independently initialized step-wise best-response adversaries (each a DQN over the weight grid) against a frozen policy and reports the worst-case WRR, avoiding the optimism of reusing the agent's own critic.

## Results

Under BR-K evaluation (K=3 independently trained best-response adversaries, 200k BR steps, λ=1.0, w₀=(0.5,0.5)), the proposed method achieves the highest WRR across all three main 2-objective MO-Gymnasium benchmarks (Timespeed, Timemove, DeepSeaTreasure) while keeping DRIFT small, indicating that the robustness gain does not come from exploiting large preference drift. The GAP metric (max−min of the discounted vector return) shows the same pattern: higher WRR is not accompanied by a wider objective gap. Additional stress tests in Appendix F.3 on 4-objective MO-Lunar-Lander and 6-objective Fruit-Tree confirm the method generalizes beyond two objectives, and a continuous TD3 best-response adversary on DeepSeaTreasure further validates the discrete-grid evaluation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four preference-conditioned MORL baselines: CN-DQN (Abels et al., 2019), envelope methods (Yang et al., 2019), PCN (Reymond et al., 2022), and a fixed-weight DQN. Under the BR-K protocol (which trains K=3 independent step-wise best-response adversaries and reports the worst-case WRR), the proposed method consistently outperforms all four baselines in WRR on Timespeed, Timemove, and DeepSeaTreasure while maintaining comparable or lower DRIFT. The key structural advantage is that baselines condition on a fixed or exogenous preference weight and cannot adapt to state-reactive adversarial reweighting, whereas the proposed Bellman-Isaacs solver explicitly optimizes against a step-wise opponent that selects w_{t+1} after observing s_{t+1} (and r_t). The paper does not include static max-min robust MORL methods (e.g., Park et al., 2024) as baselines because those optimize against a single fixed weight chosen once per episode, a fundamentally different threat model. The WRR–DRIFT tradeoff is smoothly controllable via the switching-cost parameter λ: larger λ reduces DRIFT at the cost of some WRR, while smaller λ allows sharper adversarial switches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the stochastic-game / Bellman-Isaacs framework (Shapley, 1953; Littman, 1994) to multi-objective reinforcement learning by treating the preference weight as a step-wise adversarial control rather than a fixed conditioning variable. It departs from prior robust MORL work (Park et al., 2024; Byeon et al., 2025), which places a single max-min over the weight outside the Bellman backup (episodic robustness), by nesting the weight minimization inside each backup to enforce time consistency. The Bregman switching cost draws on online-learning regret bounds (Cesa-Bianchi et al., 2013) and mirror-descent proximal operators (Beck & Teboulle, 2003), while the nested one-step operator is explicitly connected to time-consistent dynamic risk measures (Ruszczyński, 2010). Preference-conditioned MORL methods (Abels et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2019; Reymond et al., 2022) serve as baselines but treat w as exogenous, not adversarial.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical resolution to a practical concern: a single adversarial weight chosen at episode start is dynamically inconsistent when the most adverse preference direction shifts with the state. The Bellman-residual certificate (Theorem 3–4) gives practitioners an auditable way to bound approximation error in the robust value, and the BR-K multi-restart evaluation protocol addresses a real gap in how robustness is measured (single-run opponents can be weak). Key limitations include the strong-opponent assumption (observing realized reward before choosing the next weight), the per-step computational overhead of the inner minimization, and the absence of formal residual-to-performance guarantees under function approximation. Open questions include extending to partial observability, richer switching constraints beyond Bregman penalties, and scaling to high-dimensional objective vectors.
