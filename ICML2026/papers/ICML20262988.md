## Problem

AI flaw and incident reporting is fragmented across dozens of incompatible channels with inconsistent scopes, taxonomies, and information requirements, forcing good-faith reporters to duplicate effort across multiple forms while recipients lack standardized, triage-ready data. No existing system enables interoperable, one-stop dissemination of a single report to all relevant developers, coordinators, and incident registries.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262988/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes structured flaw reports through an eight-step conditional form that begins with three binary classification questions (incident occurred, malicious actor plausible, CSAM involved) and adapts subsequent fields accordingly, requiring only 6 mandatory inputs out of up to 30 total. Output is a machine-readable JSON-LD document conforming to schema.org and a custom FLARE vocabulary, designed for direct compatibility with CVE/CWE, AVID, CERT/CC, and CISA workflows, plus optional automatic routing via APIs and email to selected recipients.

## Method

FLARE-AI is a stateless, open-source reporting system whose core mechanism is early classification with conditional branching: three initial Yes/No questions determine which of four report types (Real-World Incident, Security Incident, Vulnerability, or Hazard) the user proceeds through, surfacing only contextually relevant fields and reducing cognitive load. The system pairs a minimal required core (6 fields) with an optional long path (up to 30 fields) to balance reporter burden against triage completeness, and reuses earlier selections to auto-surface relevant policy links and suggest reporting destinations. For dissemination, FLARE-AI generates standardized JSON-LD reports that a routing layer can automatically distribute to multiple developers, security coordinators, and incident databases from a single submission, with model-specific tagging triggering inclusion of relevant stakeholders. Taxonomy choices (OWASP Top 10 for flaws, AIAAIC for harms) were selected for broad recognition and extensibility, and the open-source codebase allows downstream adopters to swap in alternative taxonomies or add new coordination recipients without modifying core logic.

## Results

The survey of 12 existing AI reporting systems reveals substantial fragmentation: only 3 of 12 allow reporter anonymity, required fields range from 4 (OpenAI Bugcrowd) to 29 (OECD), and total fields span 7 to 53 (MITRE ATLAS). Only 2 of 12 systems (AIID and FLARE-AI) provide CSAM handling guidance, and no system except CERT and Google VRP collects public disclosure status. FLARE-AI achieves the broadest intake scope (all flaws, hazards, vulnerabilities, and incidents), covers 22 information categories (the most of any system), and produces machine-readable JSON-LD reports enabling automated multi-stakeholder dissemination from a single submission.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLARE-AI is compared against 12 existing reporting systems spanning AI developers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google), coordination bodies (CERT, CISA, MITRE ATLAS), incident databases (AIID, AVID, AIAAIC), and policy frameworks (OECD, Mozilla 0DIN). On scope, FLARE-AI uniquely accepts all four flaw types (hazards, vulnerabilities, incidents, and general safety issues) in a single form, whereas most baselines restrict to one or two categories (e.g., OpenAI Bugcrowd accepts only vulnerabilities; OpenAI Report Content only safety incidents). On information coverage, FLARE-AI captures 22 of 22 defined information categories, exceeding the next-best system (OECD at 21) and substantially outperforming developer-specific forms (OpenAI Report Content at 8, Mozilla 0DIN at 9). On reporter burden, FLARE-AI's conditional logic keeps the required core at 6 fields (comparable to OpenAI Bugcrowd's 4 and AVID's 6) while offering up to 30 optional fields, avoiding the 29 required fields of OECD or 18–20 of CISA. On interoperability, FLARE-AI is the only system producing machine-readable JSON-LD output with automatic multi-stakeholder dissemination, whereas no baseline offers this capability. On CSAM guidance, FLARE-AI matches AIID as one of only two systems providing specialized handling, a critical gap given the legal risks of submitting such evidence through standard channels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the mature infrastructure of software Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure (CERT, CVE/CWE, Vultron protocol) and bug bounty platforms (HackerOne, Bugcrowd) for the broader AI safety and security domain, where no unified reporting mechanism has existed. It draws on existing AI incident databases (AIID, AVID, AIAAIC), adversarial taxonomies (MITRE ATLAS, OWASP Top 10 for LLMs), and the OECD's incident reporting framework, but positions itself as the first system to bridge these fragmented channels into a single interoperable, machine-readable reporting pipeline with automatic multi-stakeholder dissemination. The novelty is architectural rather than algorithmic: conditional form logic, JSON-LD report generation, and a routing layer that connects reporters to developers, coordinators, and registries simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As regulatory mandates (EU AI Act Article 73, U.S. AI Action Plan) begin requiring incident reporting for high-risk AI systems, the absence of standardized, interoperable infrastructure creates a practical bottleneck for both compliance and safety. The paper's key open questions include whether voluntary adoption can overcome competitive incentives against cross-vendor sharing, whether the system's primarily North American/European stakeholder input generalizes globally, and how to extend reporting to AI supply chain vulnerabilities (fine-tuning, API dependencies, shared training data). Empirical evaluation of whether FLARE-AI actually increases reporting rates and triage speed compared to existing siloed channels remains future work.
