## Problem

Current model cards for open-weight foundation models emphasize performance characteristics while leaving critical safety information—model heritage, alignment provenance, and empirically observed behaviors—underdeveloped or absent. Acceptable use policies appear in only ~21% of top models and are often legally unenforceable because permissive open-source licenses (Apache 2.0, MIT) grant unconditional rights that override use-based restrictions. This creates a structural governance gap where no single artifact adequately addresses the informational, normative, and legal dimensions of downstream risk.

## Input / Output

The empirical analysis consumes model card text, Hugging Face repository metadata (download counts, vendor identity), license terms, and AUP documents from the 500 most-downloaded OWFMs, applying NIST AI-601 safety keyword taxonomies, sentence-embedding clustering, and regression analysis. The outputs are a structured safety card template with mandatory heritage, alignment provenance, and operational safety sections; a redline revision of Apache 2.0 that replaces software-centric terms (Source/Object/Work) with model-specific definitions (Model/Output) and incorporates an AUP as a binding exhibit; and a three-layered governance framework specifying how informational, normative, and legal artifacts should interlock.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-layered governance framework in which model cards serve as informational governance (disclosing heritage, alignment provenance, and observed safety behavior), AUPs serve as normative governance (defining prohibited uses), and model licenses serve as legal governance (making restrictions enforceable). The empirical component analyzes 500 Hugging Face model cards using NIST AI-601 keyword taxonomies, PCA on sentence embeddings for vendor-level clustering, and multiple regression specifications to demonstrate that documentation depth is decoupled from model popularity (R² < 0.01) and driven by vendor-specific practices rather than ecosystem norms. The proposed safety card template operationalizes three design principles—heritage over capability, provenance over benchmarks, and operational over declarative—requiring disclosure of upstream influence mechanisms (synthetic data, distillation, RLAIF) and concrete observed behaviors under adversarial conditions rather than high-level disclaimers. The licensing contribution revises Apache 2.0 by expanding the copyright grant to cover IP and other rights, clarifying output usage including distillation, incorporating an AUP as Exhibit A with termination rights for material breach, and adding a dedicated Data section addressing training data and prompt provenance.

## Results

Across the 500 most-downloaded OWFMs on Hugging Face (as of January 2026), 99.6% include a model card, but only 75.2% contain safety-specific fields and just 21.2% reference an AUP. Explicit licensing is present in 85.0% of models, with 61.4% using permissive OSLs (Apache 2.0: 223, MIT: 63, Nvidia-open: 10, CC: 8, BSD-3: 2, Falcon 2: 1). Model popularity shows no meaningful association with governance documentation depth (R² < 0.01 across multiple regression specifications), indicating that download volume provides no predictive signal for documentation completeness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not benchmark a proposed method against baselines; instead, it empirically characterizes governance gaps across the OWFM ecosystem. The key comparative finding is that permissive OSLs (Apache 2.0, MIT) are structurally incompatible with AUPs: 61.4% of models use permissive OSLs and overwhelmingly lack AUPs, while the 17.8% of models employing customized licenses (Llama community license, RAILs, DeepSeek agreement) are associated with AUP presence. At the vendor level, Meta and Google models exhibit systematically higher safety-keyword density than Qwen models, which concentrate in lower-frequency ranges, revealing that documentation practices are shaped by developer-specific conventions rather than model capability or adoption scale. The paper further identifies that Qwen's Usage Policy, though publicly available, is not referenced in model cards or licensing terms, creating a legal ambiguity absent in vendors like Meta (Llama AUP integrated into community license) or Google (Gemma Prohibited Use Policy referenced in model cards). These structural comparisons motivate the paper's three-layered framework (informational, normative, legal) as a remedy for the fragmentation observed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model card framework introduced by Mitchell et al. (2019) by proposing a safety-specific template that adds heritage disclosure, alignment provenance, and operational safety evaluation—dimensions largely absent from existing model card templates. It also directly revises the Apache 2.0 license (the most widely used OSL for OWFMs) to accommodate model-specific concepts (weights, outputs, distillation) and incorporate AUPs as a binding exhibit. The empirical analysis builds on prior documentation audits (Liang et al. 2024, Oreamuno et al. 2024, Jiang et al. 2023b) but narrows focus to the OWFM-specific governance triad of model cards, AUPs, and licenses, and on the legal incompatibility between permissive OSLs and use-based restrictions identified in the licensing literature (Duan et al. 2025, McDuff et al. 2024, Contractor et al. 2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides actionable, immediately deployable artifacts—a standardized safety card template and a redlined OWFM-tailored Apache 2.0—that could reduce the current fragmentation in open-weight model governance. Its empirical finding that model popularity shows essentially zero correlation with documentation depth (R² < 0.01) challenges the assumption that market adoption pressure will organically improve safety disclosures, strengthening the case for baseline requirements. Open questions remain around risk-adaptive tiering, incentive design for voluntary adoption, automated provenance tracking across redistribution chains, and empirical measurement of whether layered governance actually reduces downstream misuse.
