## Problem

Safety evaluation datasets for AI alignment are largely geographically homogeneous, and it remains unclear whether geo-cultural value differences in safety perception persist after controlling for standard demographics (age, gender, ethnicity). No prior work provides a unified, statistically rigorous methodology to jointly quantify the independent and interactive effects of cultural values and demographics on safety ratings, nor to identify which specific items are at risk of being misclassified as safe due to missing cultural perspectives.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes existing safety annotation datasets (text, text-to-image, text-to-text) containing per-rater demographic attributes and geographic proxies (country of birth, residence, longest residence, or nationality), along with item-level safety ratings. Cultural zone assignment follows a prioritization rule (CoLR/CoB preferred over CoR; CoB over CoN) to map each rater onto the Inglehart-Welzel cultural map. Outputs include: (1) multilevel model fixed-effect estimates and variance-reduction metrics for demographics and cultural zones, (2) per-item, per-quadrant Bayesian cultural sensitivity scores S_iq computed via Beta(1+k, 1+n−k) posteriors with a validity filter requiring ≥3 votes and no single demographic group dominating a quadrant, and (3) fine-tuned LLM classifiers that flag culturally sensitive items for prioritized human annotation.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-part methodological framework. First, it fits multilevel logistic/linear regressions with random effects for raters and items, then adds fixed effects for demographics (ethnicity, age, gender) and Inglehart-Welzel cultural zones separately and jointly, using likelihood ratio tests, ΔAIC, and pseudo-R² (proportion reduction in rater variance) to show that cultural zone membership significantly improves prediction beyond demographics in 6 of 8 datasets. Second, it introduces a Bayesian cultural sensitivity scoring algorithm: for each item and quadrant, it computes the posterior probability H_iq = P(θ_iq > 0.5) under a Beta(1,1) prior, then calculates the joint probability S_iq that exactly one quadrant rates the item unsafe while all other valid quadrants rate it safe; items with S_iq > 0.5 for exactly one quadrant are flagged as culturally sensitive (~10% of items). Third, it fine-tunes DeBERTa-Large and Gemma-3-4B on quadrant-level safety prediction (showing LLMs cannot reliably emulate diverse cultural judgments) and on a safe-vs-sensitive binary classification task (showing fine-tuned LMs can triage culturally sensitive items for human annotation with ~0.71–0.72 F1, significantly above baseline).

## Results

Using multilevel modeling across 8 safety datasets, cultural zone membership significantly improves prediction of safety ratings beyond standard demographics in 6 of 8 datasets (p < 0.05, ΔAIC ranging from −3.97 to −179.88, average 4.64% reduction in rater variance). The cultural sensitivity analysis reveals that roughly 10% of items (range: 3.0% for Severity to 13.9% for DIVE) would be misclassified as safe if a single cultural quadrant's perspective were excluded. For LLM triage, fine-tuned Gemma-3-4B achieves 0.72 F1 on identifying culturally sensitive items (p = 0.044 vs. always-safe baseline), while all four tested LLMs (DeBERTa-Large, Gemma-3-4B, Gemini-3 Flash, GPT-5 Nano) fail to reliably emulate quadrant-level safety judgments, performing at or below the 'always unsafe' baseline on Quadrants II and IV.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between nested multilevel models: a base model (rater + item random effects only), a demographics-only model, a cultural-zone-only model, and a combined D+CZ model. Demographics significantly improve fit over the base model in 6/8 datasets (ΔAIC from −6.85 to −45.11), while cultural zones significantly improve fit in 6/8 datasets (ΔAIC from −6.23 to −195.93). The key finding is that adding cultural zones to the demographics model (D+CZ vs. D) yields significant improvement in 6/8 datasets, confirming culture is not reducible to demographics. For the interaction test (D×CZ vs. D+CZ), only D3 shows a significant moderation effect (ΔAIC = −94.91, p < 0.001), likely because it is the only dataset with balanced demographics within each cultural zone. In the LLM experiments, fine-tuned DeBERTa-Large and Gemma-3-4B outperform the 'always unsafe' baseline on DICES-990 and CREHate but fail on D3 (all four quadrants), and prompted reasoning models (Gemini-3 Flash, GPT-5 Nano) show no consistent advantage over fine-tuned models, indicating current LLMs cannot reliably substitute for culturally diverse human raters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Inglehart-Welzel cultural map (Inglehart & Welzel, 2005) as its operationalization of geo-cultural values, a framework previously applied to LLM alignment benchmarks (e.g., WorldValuesBench, Community Alignment Dataset) but not yet to safety annotation analysis. It departs from prior safety evaluation work (DIVE, PRISM, DICES-990, D3) in two key ways: (1) prior studies analyzed demographic or geo-cultural effects separately or with fragile IRR-based methods, whereas this paper uses multilevel regression to jointly model both while controlling for rater and item random effects; and (2) prior LLM-as-a-judge work assumed models could simulate diverse human perspectives, an assumption this paper empirically tests and refutes for cultural quadrant emulation. The meta-analytic survey of 1,062 candidate records revealing only 8 datasets with both demographic and geo-cultural reporting is itself a new contribution that no prior work had systematically conducted.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that roughly 10% of items in existing safety datasets would be misclassified as safe without culturally pluralistic raters provides a concrete, actionable motivation for diversifying annotation pools beyond demographics. The demonstration that fine-tuned and prompted LLMs cannot reliably emulate cultural quadrant judgments challenges the growing reliance on LLM-as-a-judge for safety evaluation, while the positive result that fine-tuned LMs can triage culturally sensitive items offers a practical middle ground. Key limitations include the reductionist nature of country-level cultural proxies (the IW map captures only two axes of a much richer value space), the WVS not covering all countries, and the inability to fully eliminate demographic confounding within quadrants. Open questions include whether individual moral values (beyond cultural zone membership) drive disagreement, whether findings generalize to multimodal settings, and how to collect data that is simultaneously diverse in both cultural and demographic dimensions.
