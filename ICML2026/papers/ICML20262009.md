## Problem

Reinforcement learning for non-verifiable tasks (story writing, humor generation, counseling, design) lacks reliable reward signals because no objective ground truth exists and subjective quality assessments are inherently noisy. Existing approaches—scalar rubric scores from LLM judges and single pairwise comparisons—are poorly calibrated, coarse, or provide sparse feedback that destabilizes policy optimization. The paper asks whether a more robust ordinal reward can be constructed from subjective evaluations to stabilize RL in these domains.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (a story prompt, a pair of constraint words for a joke, a natural-language webpage description, or a mental-health counseling query) and produces a generated text response; for webpage design the output is HTML code rendered to an image for multimodal evaluation. A fixed set of k anchor responses per prompt, sourced from a stronger LLM (Gemini-2.5-Pro) or human writers, serves as the reference pool throughout training. A rubric-guided LLM judge (Kimi-K2) performs pairwise comparisons between each candidate and every anchor, with task-specific rubrics decomposing quality into interpretable criteria such as coherence, creativity, and constraint satisfaction.

## Method

TSRL replaces brittle scalar rewards with a tournament-style win-rate reward built from rubric-guided pairwise judgments against a fixed set of anchor responses. For each prompt, k diverse anchors are pre-generated and ranked via a round-robin tournament under task-specific rubrics, establishing a stable reference ladder that does not shift as the policy improves. During training, each candidate response sampled from the current policy is compared pairwise against all k anchors by the LLM judge, and the reward is the fraction of anchors beaten (win-rate), which is fed into a GRPO update where group-relative advantages drive the policy gradient. The key theoretical contribution proves that aggregating k independent pairwise comparisons reduces reward variance as 1/k and makes the probability of misordering two candidates decay exponentially in k, in contrast to single-pair rewards (O(1) variance) and scalar rubric scores (irreducible σ²_rub per evaluation). This stable reference frame plus variance reduction yields smoother training dynamics, sample efficiency, and graceful degradation under weak or partially corrupted judges.

## Results

TSRL achieves an average win-rate of 78.0% (LLaMA-3.1-8B) and 78.3% (Qwen-3-4B) across four non-verifiable tasks (story generation, humor, webpage design, mental health counseling), improving by +43.8 points over the base model and +22.8 points over the strongest scalar-reward baseline (Rubrics). On story generation alone, TSRL reaches 74.0% win-rate (LLaMA-3.1-8B) versus 47.5% for Rubrics and 40.5% for RLHF(RM). Under a weak judge (LLaMA-3.1-8B instead of Kimi-K2), TSRL retains ~94.6% of its performance while Rubrics drops by 11.2 points on average. Human preference studies confirm alignment: TSRL achieves 82.9% win-rate on jokes and 84.2% on stories against all baselines pooled.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TSRL outperforms all four baselines across every task and both backbones. Against Rubrics (Gunjal et al., 2025), the strongest scalar-reward baseline, TSRL gains +26.5 points on Stories, +32.0 on Humor, +15.6 on Web, and +16.3 on MentalH. (LLaMA-3.1-8B averages). Against RLHF(RM) (Ouyang et al., 2022), which trains a reward model on anchor pairs, TSRL leads by +33.5 on Stories, +36.0 on Humor, +22.8 on Web, and +22.5 on MentalH. The Intuitor baseline (Zhao et al., 2025), which uses intrinsic entropy signals as reward, performs near the base model (e.g., 14.2 vs. 13.5 on Stories), confirming that internal signals are insufficient for non-verifiable tasks. DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), applied offline to the same anchor preference pairs, reaches only 57.5 win-rate on Stories versus 74.0 for TSRL, showing that on-policy optimization against a fixed reference ladder is substantially stronger than offline preference optimization over the same comparisons.

## Relation to Prior Work

TSRL builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as its policy optimizer, but departs from the standard reward construction in RLHF and rubric-based RL by replacing scalar or single-pair rewards with an aggregated win-rate against a fixed anchor set. Conceptually, it repurposes ideas from statistical ranking (Bradley-Terry, Elo aggregation) not to fit a global ranking model, but to define a stable ordinal reward whose variance provably shrinks as 1/k with the number of anchors. This positions TSRL between the preference-based RL line (Christiano, Ouyang) and the rubric-scoring line (Gunjal, Liu, Jia), addressing the shared weakness that both use a moving or ill-calibrated reference frame, while also distinguishing itself from intrinsic-reward methods (Zhao, Zhang) that risk reinforcing confidently wrong behavior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper tackles a genuinely open problem—reliable reward design for RL when no ground truth exists—and provides both a clean theoretical guarantee (exponential decay of misordering probability, 1/k variance reduction) and consistent empirical gains across four diverse subjective tasks and two backbones. The self-bootstrapped variant (Sec 4.10), where anchors are re-sampled from the improving policy and outperform externally-sourced anchors, is a promising but preliminary step toward removing the dependency on a stronger external model. Key open questions remain: how TSRL behaves once the policy saturates the anchor quality range, principled anchor-refresh schedules, and whether the approach extends to settings where the judge itself is the same model being trained.
