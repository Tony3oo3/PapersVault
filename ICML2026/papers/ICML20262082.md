## Problem

Despite decades of psychophysical research on mental rotation, no mechanistic neural model has been proposed that both solves the Shepard-Metzler task and offers a plausible account of the cognitive processes underlying it. Existing deep learning approaches can classify rotated objects but fail to explain the linear reaction-time curves, the discrete action patterns, or the hybrid spatial-symbolic nature of human mental rotation. The paper also identifies a gap in experimental methodology: traditional 2D-image paradigms provide no window into the sequential decision-making process, motivating novel interactive VR experiments.

## Input / Output

The model receives two 128×128 RGB images of 10-cube Shepard-Metzler objects rendered at a fixed 25° elevation and varying azimuth (in-depth rotation), with angular disparities of 0°, 60°, 120°, or 180°. It outputs either a binary similarity judgment (match vs. mirror) or a discrete rotation action (±90° or 180°) applied to the 3D latent representation of the source view. The process is iterative: after each rotation action, the updated spatial representation is re-encoded symbolically and re-evaluated by the decision module, with a maximum of six actions before the trial is marked failed. Evaluation uses held-out objects (58 of 288) never seen during any module's training.

## Method

The architecture stacks three independently trained modules. Module I is an Equivariant Neural Renderer (EqNR) that learns an SO(3)-equivariant 3D latent space from 2D image pairs via a convolutional auto-encoder, enabling rotation of the latent by direct matrix application without 3D supervision. Module II is a Vision Symbolic Model (VSM) combining a ViT encoder (operating on the 3D latent via 3D patch embedding) with an autoregressive Transformer decoder that generates a 9-token symbolic sequence of directional transitions (U/D/B/F/L/R) describing the object from the nearest cube. Module III is a 3-layer MLP trained in a Siamese fashion on concatenated symbolic descriptions to predict one of five classes: SAME, MIRROR, or three relative quadrant dispositions. The key design principle is the Quadrant Hypothesis: the symbolic encoding is invariant within each 90° quadrant and transforms equivariantly under the cyclic group C₄ across quadrants, reducing the rotation task to discrete quadrant-switching actions. At inference, when Module III predicts a rotation action, it is applied to the 3D spatial representation (not the symbolic one), which is then reprocessed through Modules II and III in a recurrent agentic loop until a similarity decision is reached.

## Results

The full model achieves 96.13% overall accuracy on the mental rotation task (96.39% match, 95.87% mismatch), matching or slightly exceeding human performance (91.14% No-Action, 95.33% Action setup). The model takes on average 0.00 rotation actions at 0° disparity, 0.71 at 60°, and 1.03 at both 120° and 180°, closely mirroring the human action distribution (dominated by 0 actions at 0° and 1 action at other disparities). The model explains the linear reaction time trend from 0° to 120° but cannot account for the additional slowing from 120° to 180°, as both conditions require the same number of actions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is a classical Siamese architecture (ResNet or ViT encoders trained directly on the same/different classification task). These Siamese models achieve near-perfect accuracy on training objects but collapse to ~50% (chance level) on novel test objects, except when rotations are restricted to the image plane — a stark contrast to human performance which is robust to in-depth rotations. The full three-module model (EqNR + VSM + MLP) achieves 96.13% accuracy on held-out objects, outperforming the Siamese baseline by ~46 percentage points and matching or slightly exceeding human accuracy (91–95%). A second comparison is against a rotation-invariant MLP (Module III ablation without actions), which reaches 97.03% accuracy but takes zero actions, failing to replicate the human behavioral signature of sequential rotation actions. The authors note this raises the open question of why humans take actions when an invariant decoder suffices, and that the agentic approach provides a mechanistic account of the process rather than just the outcome.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper assembles three existing architectural components — the Equivariant Neural Renderer (Dupont et al., 2020) for SO(3)-equivariant 3D spatial encoding, a Vision Transformer + autoregressive Transformer for neuro-symbolic object description, and a small MLP as a decision agent — into what the authors frame as the first mechanistic model of human mental rotation. It departs from prior deep-learning models of spatial reasoning (Hamrick 2019; O'Connell et al. 2025; Bonnen et al. 2026) by introducing an agentic, recurrent action-taking loop guided by a novel Quadrant Hypothesis (discrete 90° symbolic encoding), and by grounding model design in new interactive VR experiments rather than purely 2D image stimuli. The neuro-symbolic bridge between spatial and symbolic representations is the key conceptual departure from purely spatial or purely feedforward approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, testable mechanistic account of mental rotation that reconciles seemingly conflicting findings in the cognitive literature (continuous vs. discrete rotation, spatial vs. symbolic representations) and is validated against both classic reaction-time data and new VR behavioral data. Key limitations include: the model only generalizes within the Shepard-Metzler object family, it cannot explain the 120°→180° reaction-time increase, it is deterministic and thus misses human trial-by-trial variability, and all components rely on backpropagation. The ablation showing that a rotation-invariant MLP trained directly on symbolic descriptions solves the task without any actions (97% accuracy) raises a fundamental open question about why humans engage in sequential mental rotation at all, and whether the spatial latent space is truly necessary. Broader implications for spatial reasoning in ML (where current architectures struggle) and for JEPA-style latent simulation are discussed but left as future work.
