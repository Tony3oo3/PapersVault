## Problem

EEG foundation models are evaluated under inconsistent downstream datasets, preprocessing pipelines, and fine-tuning protocols, making cross-architecture comparisons unreliable and masking true pre-training advances. Beyond end-task accuracy, the field lacks diagnostic analyses of internal mechanisms—gradient conflicts, representation evolution, and scaling behavior—that explain why larger models or more pre-training data do not always yield proportional downstream gains.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265195/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes released pre-trained EEG-FM checkpoints (BENDR, BIOT, LaBraM, EEGPT, CBraMod, CSBrain, REVE) plus two general time-series models (Mantis, Moment), applying model-specific adapters (dynamic-routing convolutions, channel remapping, positional-encoding loading) to handle heterogeneous input montages and normalization conventions. It produces a matrix of classification metrics (Balanced Accuracy, Weighted F1, AUROC, AUC-PR, Cohen's Kappa) across 14 datasets under 3 fine-tuning strategies × 2 task setups × 3 classifier heads, alongside diagnostic outputs: per-component gradient cosine similarity, rank-6 PCA subspace affinity, gradient conflict frequency, layer-wise CKA/RSA trajectories, and t-SNE/Integrated Gradients visualizations.

## Method

EEG-FM-Bench is a unified open-source evaluation suite with three modules: (1) a standardized data pipeline (band-pass 0.1–100 Hz, notch filtering, resampling to model-specific rates, fixed-length segmentation, subject-independent splitting) applied identically across all 14 datasets; (2) a configurable evaluation grid that decouples backbone from decoder, enabling controlled ablations over frozen-backbone / full-parameter / LoRA fine-tuning, single-task vs. multi-task training, and average-pooling / attention-pooling / large-MLP classifier heads; (3) a diagnostic analysis layer that projects parameter gradients into a 1024-dim CountSketch space, computes pairwise cosine similarity and rank-6 PCA subspace affinity across tasks and model components, and tracks representation evolution via CKA and RSA on fixed probe batches every 16 steps. The key analytical insight is coupling gradient-space geometry (revealing near-zero or negative alignment between masked-reconstruction and cross-entropy objectives) with representation-space similarity (showing pre-training stabilizes the Transformer backbone while shifting adaptation burden to temporal embedding and normalization layers), thereby identifying objective misalignment and adaptation incompatibility as bottlenecks beyond nominal model or data scale.

## Results

Under the default protocol (full-parameter, multi-task, average-pooling head), REVE achieves the strongest average balanced accuracy across 14 datasets, though it does not dominate every task (e.g., it trails EEGPT on Siena at 81.87 vs. 83.28 B-Acc, and CSBrain on TUSL at 79.85 vs. 80.56). Full-parameter fine-tuning consistently outperforms frozen-backbone and LoRA across all seven EEG-FMs, while frozen-backbone transfer exhibits a severe generalization gap (e.g., BENDR drops from 60.82 to 34.12 B-Acc on SEED). Multi-task fine-tuning generally improves robustness over single-task (e.g., LaBraM on SEED-V: 43.26 vs. 24.35 B-Acc) but induces negative transfer on motor imagery (PhysioMI, BCIC-2a) and visual target detection (Things-EEG-2). Gradient analyses reveal near-zero or negative cosine similarity between reconstruction and classification objectives, indicating that pre-training functions primarily as a 'time-series-aware initialization' rather than effective semantic knowledge transfer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

REVE (El Ouahidi et al., 2026) achieves the highest average B-Acc under the matched protocol, benefiting from the largest pre-training corpus (25,000 subjects) and 4D Fourier positional encoding, yet it is not uniformly best: EEGPT (Wang et al., 2024) surpasses it on Siena (83.28 vs. 81.87) and SEED-VII (27.78 vs. 27.62), while CSBrain (Zhou et al., 2026) leads on TUSL (80.56 vs. 79.85) and BCIC-2a (42.50 vs. 41.87). CBraMod (Wang et al., 2025a) excels on Workload (74.12) and TUSL (79.56) with its criss-cross attention, and LaBraM (Jiang et al., 2024b) leads on SEED-V (43.26) despite a smaller pre-training corpus. BENDR (Kostas et al., 2021) and BIOT (Yang et al., 2023), the earliest EEG-FMs, are consistently outperformed by newer models on most tasks but remain competitive on TUAB (81.72 and 80.85) and Mimul-11 (52.55). The two general time-series baselines, Mantis (Feofanov et al., 2025) and Moment (Goswami et al., 2024), achieve competitive performance on several tasks despite being trained on non-EEG data, suggesting that generic temporal modeling captures part of the transferable structure. Across all models, frozen-backbone transfer shows a severe gap (e.g., BENDR on SEED: 34.12 vs. 60.82 full-parameter), and LoRA adaptation partially closes this gap but remains model-dependent, performing well for CBraMod and REVE but struggling for weaker backbones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of EEG benchmarking work (MOABB, BEETL, AdaBrain-Bench, EEG Foundation Challenge) by unifying evaluation of seven SOTA EEG foundation models (BENDR, BIOT, LaBraM, EEGPT, CBraMod, CSBrain, REVE) under a single protocol with standardized preprocessing, multiple fine-tuning strategies, and classifier configurations. It departs from prior benchmarks that report only end-task accuracy by introducing diagnostic analyses—gradient cosine similarity, subspace affinity, CKA, and RSA—borrowed from representation-learning literature but applied here to probe optimization dynamics and transfer mechanisms in EEG-FMs. The multi-dimensional evaluation design (frozen/full/LoRA × single/multi-task × three decoder heads) is novel in scope relative to prior EEG-FM evaluations, which typically fix one adaptation strategy per model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This benchmark provides the first standardized, reproducible infrastructure for comparing EEG foundation models, addressing a reproducibility crisis where inconsistent protocols have made cross-architecture comparisons unreliable. The key open question it surfaces is whether current masked-reconstruction pre-training objectives can ever produce task-ready representations, given the demonstrated gradient conflict with classification losses; this motivates a shift toward discriminative or contrastive pre-training objectives. Limitations include reliance on released checkpoints (preventing controlled pre-training ablations), restriction to classification tasks, and the acknowledged data-scarce ceiling of publicly available EEG corpora that constrains further scaling. Future work on neuro-informed architectures, conflict-aware multi-task optimization, and better adaptation interfaces are directly motivated by these findings.
