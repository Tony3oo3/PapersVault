## Problem

PFN-style tabular foundation models (e.g., TabPFN) are pretrained under non-strategic meta-priors where feature distributions are fixed, yet in real-world deployment individuals strategically modify their features in response to the deployed classifier, inducing a post-deployment distribution shift. The paper proves that this creates a fundamental meta-prior mismatch that induces an irreducible structural prediction bias, and asks whether these models can be adapted to strategic settings without retraining.

## Input / Output

SPN takes a frozen pretrained PFN backbone, a labeled non-strategic context table, a known manipulation function b_f (e.g., Mahalanobis-cost or Euclidean-cost best-response), and strategic test queries as input. It produces class predictions on the strategically manipulated test distribution without any parameter updates. The key operational detail is that the context is augmented at inference time by pairing each original example (x_i, y_i) with its post-manipulation counterpart (b_f(x_i), y_i), so the model sees both the pre- and post-manipulation feature vectors simultaneously, and the number of such pairs (K) controls the ICL scale.

## Method

SPN is a two-stage, inference-time prior-alignment framework that requires no retraining or architectural modification. In the inner stage, it constructs a strategic tabular context by pairing each observed labeled point with its post-manipulation feature vector under the deployed rule f, encoding the local manipulation direction g_i = b_f(x_i) − x_i into the context. In the outer stage, the frozen PFN performs a standard forward pass conditioned on this adjusted context to predict on strategic queries. The theoretical justification rests on two results: (i) the attention-induced ICL update under the strategic context approximates a one-step preconditioned gradient-ascent strategic manipulation update (Proposition 5.2), and (ii) the induced inference-time task distribution has strictly smaller uncovered strategic mass than the original non-strategic prior (Proposition 5.3), guaranteeing bias reduction. A case study further shows that this ICL-based adaptation is substantially cheaper in both wall-clock time and data consumption than repeated fine-tuning under frequent manipulation.

## Results

On non-strategic benchmarks (Table 1), SPN achieves mean AUC-ROC within 0.2–0.3% of the strongest tabular foundation models (e.g., 91.65% on Bank Marketing vs. 91.85% for TabPFNv2.5; 94.80% on Phishing vs. 95.02%), confirming no meaningful performance sacrifice. Under strategic manipulation (Figures 5, 7), SPN consistently outperforms TabPFN, TabDPT, and classical tree ensembles (XGBoost, LightGBM) across all four manipulation regimes (Mahalanobis, Euclidean, Noisy, Heterogeneous), with the largest gains appearing at high manipulation proportions (0.8–1.0) where vanilla models show clear accuracy degradation and rising false-positive error. SPN also generalizes across mismatched manipulation regimes, maintaining robustness even when the test-time manipulation model differs from the one used to construct in-context examples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TabPFNv2.5 (the primary backbone), SPN trades a marginal 0.2–0.3% AUC-ROC on non-strategic benchmarks (e.g., 91.65 vs. 91.85 on Bank Marketing) for substantially improved robustness under strategic manipulation, where TabPFN's accuracy drops and false-positive error rises as manipulation proportion increases. Compared to other PFN variants—Chunked TabPFN, Drift-Resilient TabPFN, TabDPT, TabICL, and TabFlex—SPN remains within the same narrow performance band on non-strategic tasks (all within ~0.5% AUC) while being the only method explicitly designed to handle post-deployment distribution shift. Against classical tree ensembles (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost), SPN outperforms under strategic settings across all four manipulation regimes (Mahalanobis, Euclidean, Noisy, Heterogeneous), with the gap widening at higher manipulation proportions. SPN also generalizes across mismatched regimes: when in-context examples are constructed with one manipulation model (e.g., Mahalanobis) but tested under a different one (e.g., Heterogeneous or a mixed distribution), SPN still consistently beats TabPFN, indicating the strategic context provides transferable alignment rather than regime-specific fitting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed threads: (1) tabular foundation models based on Prior-data Fitted Networks (TabPFN, TabDPT, TabICL) that perform inference via in-context learning, and (2) the strategic classification literature (Hardt et al., 2016; Zrnic et al., 2021; Chen, 2023) that studies how deployed classifiers induce endogenous distribution shifts through agent manipulation. Prior strategic classification work typically requires task-specific retraining or iterative optimization to achieve robustness, while prior PFN work has been developed and evaluated exclusively in non-strategic, passive-observation settings. The paper's key departure is identifying a fundamental meta-prior mismatch (non-strategic pretraining vs. strategic deployment) that induces an irreducible prediction bias in PFNs, and then showing that the in-context learning mechanism itself can be repurposed at inference time to simulate strategic manipulation without any parameter updates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides both a theoretical characterization of why tabular foundation models fail under strategic manipulation (an irreducible bias floor proportional to the uncovered strategic mass) and a practical, training-free fix that is sample-efficient (saturating around 50 in-context examples). This is important for high-stakes tabular decision pipelines (credit scoring, healthcare triage, policy allocation) where deployed models face adaptive adversaries. Key limitations include the assumption that the manipulation function b_f is known or can be accurately estimated, and the idealized setting where the decision maker can construct strategic contexts before deployment. Open questions include robustness to misspecified manipulation models, multi-agent strategic interactions, and whether the approach extends to regression or multi-class settings beyond binary classification.
