## Problem

Theoretical manifold-fitting bounds (Genovese et al., Fefferman et al.) depend on geometric quantities such as curvature, reach, and intrinsic dimension, yet these are rarely observable in real data where the generating process is unknown and sampling is irregular. Existing benchmarks are polarized: analytic manifolds offer known geometry but limited representational richness, while real-world datasets obscure the geometry entirely. The paper introduces a controlled benchmarking framework that provides ground-truth geometric quantities at near-ground-truth accuracy, bridging this gap between theory and empirical validation.

## Input / Output

Input consists of dense, axis-aligned grids of 64×64 grayscale images (adapted dSprites with 4 transformation factors; extended COIL-20 with 3 factors) or analytic manifold parametrizations (spheres, tori, hyperboloids), sampled at 16 values per continuous dimension with optional buffer margins on non-cyclic axes to support finite-difference stencils. The regular grid spacing is essential—it is what enables second-order central-difference approximations of partial derivatives that would be unreliable on unstructured point clouds. Outputs include pointwise and global geometric measures (scalar curvature, reach, volume element), fitted manifolds from MMLS or β-VAE, directed Hausdorff distances against the ground-truth manifold, and per-layer geometry profiles tracking how volume, curvature, reach, and inter-class distances evolve through a β-VAE's encoder–latent–decoder pipeline.

## Method

The framework combines two components: (1) construction of low-dimensional image families (d = 1–4) by systematically applying controlled transformations (rotation, translation, scale) to base images and sampling all combinations on a dense regular grid, yielding manifolds homeomorphic to [0,1]^r × (S¹)^s; and (2) finite-difference estimation of geometric quantities on that grid. The Riemannian metric g_ij = ⟨∂_i u, ∂_j u⟩ is computed via central differences with O(h²) accuracy, from which Christoffel symbols, the Riemann tensor, Ricci tensor, and scalar curvature are derived, all inheriting the same O(h²) error under C⁵ smoothness; reach is estimated via the plug-in estimator of Aamari et al. (2019) with finite-difference tangent spaces, achieving O(n^{-2/(3d-1)}) convergence. Two application studies demonstrate the framework: log-log regression of empirical Hausdorff error against the theoretical scaling of Genovese et al. and Fefferman et al. bounds using MMLS (restricted to local d-plane fits) and β-VAE, and tracking how geometric measures evolve across β-VAE layers. The key design choice is that the grid structure—unavailable in real data—enables near-ground-truth geometric estimation, making the framework a calibration testbed for general-purpose estimators and a sandbox for probing theoretical assumptions at controlled intrinsic dimension.

## Results

The finite-difference scalar-curvature estimator achieves O(h²) = O(n^{-2/d}) pointwise error, substantially outperforming the Sritharan et al. (2021) estimator (rate O((log n/n)^{3/d})) across all tested manifolds (S², S³, S⁴, H₂², T²), even when the latter is given an oracle per-point radius. For manifold fitting, the Fefferman et al. (2018) linear-fit scaling (exponent 1/d) matches the empirical Hausdorff error curves of MMLS more closely than the dimension-only minimax rate of Genovese et al. (2012) (exponent 2/(2+d)), consistent with MMLS operating in a local PCA (linear) regime. The β-VAE on 4D dSprites exhibits a comparatively flat error curve with a decay exponent at or below 1/d, indicating it does not behave as a high-order local fitter on these controlled manifolds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Sritharan et al. (2021), the paper's grid-aware finite-difference estimator achieves substantially lower scalar-curvature RMSE on all five benchmark manifolds (S², S³, S⁴, H₂², T²), even though Sritharan's method is evaluated with an optimistic per-point oracle radius from a 50-value grid search; the gap widens at lower sample sizes where the general-purpose estimator's interpolation-based approach degrades. Against the Genovese et al. (2012) minimax bound, the empirical MMLS error curves on dSprites (d=1–4) and on S², T², COIL-20, and dSprites (Figure 5) follow a log-log decay closer to the Fefferman et al. (2018) 1/d exponent than to the 2/(2+d) exponent, confirming that linear local fitting (PCA) is the operative regime. Against the β-VAE (Higgins et al., 2017), MMLS shows a steeper, more theory-consistent decay on 4D dSprites (Figure 4), while the β-VAE's error curve is flatter, suggesting the β-VAE in this configuration does not exploit higher-order local geometry. The paper positions these comparisons not as a method-vs-method contest but as a calibration exercise: the controlled grid setting provides ground-truth geometric quantities that validate both the finite-difference estimators and the theoretical scaling predictions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of manifold-fitting theory (Genovese et al., 2012; Fefferman et al., 2018) and geometric estimation practice (Aamari et al., 2019; Sritharan et al., 2021), addressing a gap the authors identify: theoretical bounds depend on geometric quantities (reach, volume, curvature) that are unobservable on real data, while existing synthetic benchmarks lack the representational richness of image datasets. The framework repurposes dSprites and COIL-20 with dense axis-aligned transformation grids and pairs them with finite-difference estimators that exploit the known grid structure, departing from general-purpose point-cloud estimators that must infer derivatives from unstructured samples. For manifold fitting, it builds on MMLS (Sober & Levin, 2020) in a simplified linear regime and on the β-VAE (Higgins et al., 2017) as a learned alternative, using both as probes of theoretical scaling predictions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the first controlled testbed where geometric ground truth (curvature, reach, volume) is exactly known for image-based manifolds, enabling direct calibration of estimators and empirical validation of theoretical bounds that have previously been unverifiable. The observation that the Fefferman 1/d linear-fit scaling tracks empirical MMLS error more closely than the Genovese 2/(2+d) minimax rate, and that β-VAE layer-wise geometry shows systematic curvature growth and reach decay toward the latent space, offers concrete evidence for when and how existing bounds are informative. Key limitations include restriction to intrinsic dimensions 1–4, simple topology ([0,1]^r × (S¹)^s), and rasterization artifacts at fixed image resolution that complicate curvature estimation at high grid density. Open questions include whether the framework can be extended to higher dimensions, more complex topologies, and whether the observed β-VAE geometry evolution holds across architectures and training regimes.
