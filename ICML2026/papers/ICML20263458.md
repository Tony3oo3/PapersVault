## Problem

Neural scaling laws predict power-law improvements in model performance with scale, but the mechanisms driving them remain poorly understood because existing theoretical accounts rely on idealized random-feature surrogates or require explicit eNTK computations that are infeasible at the scales where scaling laws are actually observed. This paper addresses the measurement bottleneck by introducing a scalable diagnostic that tracks which eigenvalues of the empirical NTK drive loss reduction throughout training, without ever constructing the kernel matrix.

## Input / Output

The method consumes per-sample parameter gradients (obtained via standard backpropagation on the current mini-batch) and the loss gradient with respect to network outputs, requiring no explicit Jacobian or kernel formation. For multi-dimensional outputs such as language-model vocabularies (m > 10⁴), it estimates sample-wise gradient norms via Hutchinson's trace estimator with M=10 Rademacher projections, reducing per-step overhead to ~22% additional forward/backward passes. The output is three instantaneous scalars: χ_pos ∈ [0,1] (scale-free spectral position), χ_net = Tr(eNTK) (network sensitivity), and χ_loss = ‖∇_f L‖² (loss sensitivity), from which the linearized loss change δL = χ_loss · χ_net · χ_pos is recovered exactly.

## Method

The core contribution is the Loss-Network-Position (LNP) decomposition, which factors the first-order loss change into χ_loss (loss-function sensitivity), χ_net (network sensitivity, equal to the eNTK trace), and χ_pos (a scale-free weighted average of eNTK eigenvalues, weighted by where the loss gradient projects). The key computational insight is that χ_pos = δL / (χ_net · χ_loss), so all three quantities are obtained from gradient evaluations alone, bypassing the O(D²) eNTK construction entirely. Applied to Llama 2 scaling runs on SimpleStories and CIFAR-5M, the diagnostic reveals that χ_pos decreases by orders of magnitude throughout training and that larger models reach significantly lower values—a capacity effect termed spectral reach. Linear-probing experiments on pre-trained versus random backbones identify feature learning as the enabler: it causes χ_net to grow adaptively, buffering roughly one order of magnitude of the χ_pos decrease and sustaining learning progress into the spectral tail where frozen representations stall.

## Results

Spectral position χ_pos decreases by orders of magnitude throughout training for all Llama 2 model sizes (from ~10M to ~350M parameters) on both SimpleStories and CIFAR-5M, with larger models consistently reaching lower values. In linear probing experiments, pre-trained backbones achieve lower spectral position than random backbones (χ_pos(random)/χ_pos(pre-trained) > 1) across all model sizes and both datasets, confirming that learned representations inherently increase spectral reach. Feature-learning models show increasing χ_net throughout training that buffers roughly one order of magnitude of the χ_pos decrease, while frozen-representation models maintain constant χ_net. The LNP decomposition adds only ~22% computational overhead (less than 2× total compute) and ~1× model-size memory overhead, enabling measurement at transformer scales where explicit eNTK computation is infeasible.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a diagnostic/measurement paper rather than a method-comparison paper, so there are no external baseline methods to benchmark against. The key internal comparisons are: (1) Pre-trained vs. random backbones in linear probing — pre-trained backbones consistently achieve lower χ_pos (i.e., greater spectral reach) than random backbones at every model size on both SimpleStories and CIFAR-5M, with the gap widening for larger models. (2) Feature-learning (all layers trainable) vs. frozen representations (linear probe on random backbone) — feature-learning models show increasing χ_net that partially offsets the χ_pos decrease, while frozen models have constant χ_net and stall at higher spectral positions. (3) Standard vs. muP parameterization — the spectral-reach ordering across model sizes is preserved under muP, ruling out width-dependent feature-learning intensity as a confound and confirming the effect is capacity-driven. The validation on random feature models with power-law data spectra (β ∈ [1.0, 3.0]) confirms that empirical χ_pos precisely matches the theoretical prediction χ_pos ∝ Σk⁻²ᵝ / (Σk⁻ᵝ)², with χ_net and χ_loss remaining constant at 1 as predicted.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NTK-based theoretical framework for understanding scaling laws (Bordelon et al., 2020; Bahri et al., 2024) from idealized random-feature models to practical deep learning by introducing the LNP decomposition, which makes spectral position computable from per-sample gradients alone. It builds on per-sample gradient techniques from differential privacy (Opacus) and Hutchinson trace estimation to bypass the infeasible explicit eNTK computation, and connects to kernel-target alignment (Cristianini et al., 2001) while offering a different normalization that arises naturally from the loss factorization. The key departure from prior work is the empirical demonstration at transformer scale that spectral reach—the capacity to learn from progressively smaller eNTK eigenvalues—explains the size-performance relationship, and that feature learning enables this reach through adaptive χ_net growth, a mechanism absent from frozen-representation theories.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The LNP decomposition provides a practical, low-overhead (<2×) diagnostic for tracking spectral learning dynamics at scales where explicit kernel methods are infeasible, potentially becoming a standard tool for monitoring and interpreting large-scale training. The spectral reach framework offers a concrete mechanistic explanation for why larger models achieve lower loss, moving beyond phenomenological power-law fits. Open questions include whether spectral modes correspond to hierarchical semantic abstraction levels, how to use χ_pos as a principled signal for paradigm transitions (e.g., SFT → DPO), and what controlled interventions (clamping χ_pos, targeted spectral reshaping) can do to accelerate or redirect spectral descent. Limitations include validation only on Llama 2 and ViT architectures at moderate scale, first-order analysis only, and the absence of causal interventions to confirm the feature-learning mechanism.
