## Problem

The alignment community has extensively addressed unintended harms of alignment (e.g., representation gaps, pluralistic value conflicts) but has not systematically examined how alignment methods themselves can be intentionally weaponized for censorship and manipulation by malicious actors. While isolated instances of model censorship have been documented, no prior work frames these as a broader dual-use risk inherent to the alignment techniques themselves, mapping each method to its specific misuse vectors and the actors positioned to exploit them.

## Input / Output

The paper takes as its analytical subject the three-stage control stack used in modern frontier LLMs—pre-training data curation, post-training preference alignment (RLHF, Constitutional AI, Deliberative Alignment), and inference-time interventions (system prompts, safety classifiers)—and produces a structured dual-use risk assessment for each stage. The output is a characterization of each technique along five dimensions (access requirements, computational resources, technical expertise, ease of modification, depth of modification), paired with documented real-world cases of ongoing weaponization and a set of proposed mitigation strategies.

## Method

The core analytical contribution is a systematic mapping of each alignment technique to its dual-use potential, organized by the three control stages and assessed along five practical dimensions that determine who can exploit each method and how deeply. For each stage, the authors identify the specific levers a malicious actor can pull—curating preference datasets to favor one ideology, modifying constitutional guidelines, adjusting system prompts, or filtering training corpora—and assess which actors (state governments or foundation model providers) have the capacity and incentive to do so. This mapping is grounded in empirical evidence of ongoing weaponization, including China's CAC-mandated refusal datasets and keyword filtering, the realignment of Grok via system prompt changes, and the filtering of publicly available training data that indirectly shapes Western models. The analysis is then situated within a broader ecosystem assessment covering AI's rapid adoption as a primary information source, the oligopolistic LLM market structure that creates concentrated points of control, and global democratic backsliding, which together amplify both the likelihood and societal consequences of deliberate misuse.

## Results

This is a position paper without quantitative experiments or benchmark evaluations. Its central contribution is a qualitative mapping of three alignment stages (pre-training data filtering, post-training preference alignment, inference-time control) to their dual-use potential for censorship and manipulation, supported by documented real-world cases (e.g., China's CAC regulations, Grok's system-prompt realignment, DeepSeek's output filtering). The paper's key argument is that alignment methods are purpose-agnostic tools whose safety or oppressive character is determined entirely by who defines the target 'values,' and that the convergence of AI's growing role as an information source, LLM market oligopoly, and global democratic backsliding makes weaponization increasingly likely and consequential.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method or compare against empirical baselines. Instead, it contrasts three alignment stages along five qualitative axes (access, compute, expertise, ease, and depth of modification) to show that inference-time controls (system prompts, safety classifiers) offer the lowest barrier to malicious repurposing, while pre-training data curation produces the most fundamental and hardest-to-reverse changes. The paper also contrasts its systematic dual-use framing against prior work on AI risks, arguing that existing literature addresses general AI systems from a high-level or hypothetical angle, whereas this paper maps specific alignment techniques to documented instances of weaponization (China's CAC refusal datasets, Grok's prompt-based realignment, DeepSeek's real-time output filtering) and situates them within a broader political-economic analysis of authoritarianism and market concentration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from prior AI risk literature (Berryhill et al., 2024; Hendrycks et al., 2023; Saeri et al., 2024), which addresses AI systems at a high level, by systematically mapping specific alignment techniques—pre-training data filtering, RLHF/Constitutional AI, and inference-time controls—to their dual-use potential for censorship and manipulation. It also builds on isolated documentation of model censorship (Huang et al., 2025; Naseh et al., 2025; Qiu et al., 2026) and pluralistic alignment work (Sorensen et al., 2024; Santurkar et al., 2023), but reframes these as evidence of a broader structural risk inherent to alignment methodology itself rather than as idiosyncratic failures. The conceptual lineage draws on the dual-use technology framework from nuclear physics, transplanted to the alignment context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central argument—that the same technical levers (preference curation, guideline modification, system prompts, output classifiers) that make models 'safe' also make them controllable—forces the alignment community to confront that improving alignment simultaneously improves censorship tooling. Open questions include how to develop standardized, cross-cultural benchmarks for verifiable alignment that go beyond the right-left continuum, how to maintain competitive pluralism in an oligopolistic market, and how to ensure genuine (rather than perfunctory) researcher reflection on dual-use implications. A key limitation is that, as a position paper, it relies on cited evidence rather than original empirical analysis, and its 'misuse' criteria, while grounded in the UDHR, inevitably involve value judgments about where legitimate safety ends and overreach begins.
