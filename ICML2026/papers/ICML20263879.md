## Problem

Unadjusted Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and Langevin Monte Carlo avoid the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance step, eliminating the d^{1/4} dimensional scaling that plagues adjusted methods in high dimensions. However, they are rarely used in practice because selecting an appropriate integration step size to keep asymptotic bias below the Monte Carlo variance requires domain knowledge or manual tuning. This paper closes that gap by providing a principled, automated step-size selection scheme that makes unadjusted samplers fully black-box.

## Input / Output

The method requires only the gradient of the log-density ∇L(x) (the standard computational bottleneck) and a user-specified bias tolerance expressed as a target EEVPD value (e.g., 3×10⁻⁴ for 10% relative RMSE). The energy error needed for tuning is a free byproduct of the velocity Verlet integration step, since the potential energy L(x) is already evaluated during gradient computation. After a short burn-in tuning phase, the step size, trajectory length, and diagonal preconditioner are frozen and the sampler produces samples from the target distribution with guaranteed asymptotic bias below the specified tolerance.

## Method

The central insight is that the Energy Error Variance Per Dimension (EEVPD) — the variance of one-step Hamiltonian energy changes normalized by dimension — provides a tight, computable upper bound on asymptotic bias. For Gaussian targets, the paper proves (Theorem 4.2) that EEVPD upper-bounds both the covariance matrix bias b_cov² and the Wasserstein distance W₂, with the bound being sharp for isotropic Gaussians. The tuning algorithm adaptively varies the step size ε using a weighted running average of per-step energy errors, exploiting the ε⁶ scaling of energy error at small step sizes to converge to the target EEVPD in tens of steps. The approach is validated numerically on non-Gaussian benchmarks (Rosenbrock, Funnel, Brownian motion, etc.) where the Gaussian bound holds or is conservative, and demonstrated on lattice field theory problems exceeding one million dimensions where unadjusted MCLMC achieves 100× speedup over NUTS.

## Results

The primary metric is the number of gradient evaluations needed to achieve b_avg² < 0.01 (average second-moment error below 1%). On six Bayesian benchmarks (dimensions 36–2429), unadjusted MCLMC with EEVPD tuning (uMCLMC) achieves the lowest gradient count on 5 of 6 problems, e.g. 246 calls on Standard Gaussian vs. 2,391 for NUTS, and 10,688 on Rosenbrock vs. 27,070 for NUTS. On the φ⁴ lattice field theory in >10⁶ dimensions, unadjusted MCLMC requires only 1,344 gradient calls versus 133,266 for NUTS (a 100× improvement). On the U(1) gauge theory at 2 million dimensions, unadjusted MCLMC converges in 198 calls compared to 127,875 for NUTS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NUTS (Hoffman & Gelman, 2014), unadjusted MCLMC with EEVPD tuning wins on all six Bayesian benchmarks, with the largest margins on Standard Gaussian (246 vs. 2,391 calls, ~10×) and Rosenbrock (10,688 vs. 27,070, ~2.5×). Against Metropolis Adjusted Langevin Trajectories / aLMC (Riou-Durand & Vogrinc, 2023), unadjusted methods dominate: uMCLMC needs 3,960 calls on German Credit vs. 7,924 for aLMC, and 16,854 vs. 37,904 on Stochastic Volatility. Against adjusted MCLMC (Robnik, 2025), unadjusted MCLMC consistently wins, e.g. 1,628 vs. 2,876 on Brownian and 16,854 vs. 38,357 on Stochastic Volatility. The one exception is Item Response, where uLMC with grid-searched hyperparameters (930 calls) slightly outperforms uMCLMC (1,612), though both still beat all adjusted baselines. On the high-dimensional field theory problems, the advantage is most dramatic: unadjusted MCLMC's cost remains flat with dimension while NUTS and adjusted MCLMC scale as d^{1/4}, yielding 100× and 645× speedups at 10⁶ and 2×10⁶ dimensions respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the long tradition of unadjusted Hamiltonian and Langevin dynamics used in molecular dynamics and lattice QCD, where domain knowledge and trial runs have historically guided step-size selection. It also extends the theoretical framework of Durmus & Eberle (2023) and Bou-Rabee & Eberle (2023), who established asymptotic bias bounds for unadjusted HMC but assumed global knowledge of the target unavailable in practice. The key departure is the introduction of Energy Error Variance Per Dimension (EEVPD) as a practical, online-computable proxy for bias, combined with a rigorous Gaussian analysis (Theorem 4.2) that upper-bounds both covariance matrix bias and Wasserstein distance in terms of EEVPD. This transforms unadjusted samplers from domain-expert tools into black-box algorithms comparable to NUTS, while also building directly on the MCLMC sampler of Robnik et al. (2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: unadjusted samplers with EEVPD tuning achieve a 100× reduction in gradient calls over NUTS on 1M+ dimensional lattice field theory problems, and their cost remains flat with dimension while adjusted methods scale as d^(1/4). This makes high-dimensional Bayesian inference and statistical physics simulation dramatically more accessible. The main limitation is the Brownian motion benchmark where the bias bound is violated by a factor of ~1.5, and the paper acknowledges the bound's universal validity for arbitrary distributions remains unproven. Open questions include whether the tuning scheme extends to non-smooth or multimodal targets, and whether the bias-variance tradeoff prescription (bias² = RMSE²/5) is optimal beyond the Gaussian analysis.
