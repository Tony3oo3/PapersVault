## Problem

Existing video game benchmarks for VLMs rely on a fire-and-forget evaluation paradigm that measures only instantaneous performance, ignoring the human-like reflective loop of observing failures and consulting tutorials to improve. They also lack a systematic cognitive taxonomy, often depend on privileged API access to internal game states, and support only a single action modality, making it impossible to assess whether VLMs can genuinely internalize visual experience and refine policies over repeated attempts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265862/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives the current game screenshot plus the previous screenshot-action pair (zero-shot) or the last five pairs with a compressed history summary and retrieved long-term memories (memory agent). During the reflection phase, the VLM ingests the recorded failure trajectory video and a retrieved expert walkthrough video (sampled at 1 fps for image-only models like GPT-4o) to produce condensed textual reflections. Outputs are either GUI actions (pixel-coordinate clicks, key presses, drags following the UI-TARS action schema) or semantic actions (grid positions), scored on a 0–100 normalized scale via intrinsic game metrics or a milestone-completion ratio computed by Gemini-3-Pro matching discrete playthrough frames against a reference milestone JSON.

## Method

GameVerse introduces a reflect-and-retry paradigm in four steps: the agent attempts the task and its failure trajectory is recorded; an expert walkthrough is retrieved from top-ranked online videos; the VLM acts as a reflector, contrasting its failure video against the expert demo to generate condensed empirical reflections (e.g., 'I targeted the wrong support beam; the expert targeted the central pillar'); and these reflections are injected into the system prompt for a re-attempt. The benchmark organizes 15 games along three cognitive axes (image structure, temporal dynamics, causal linearity) into five categories with three difficulty tiers, and supports a dual action space (semantic and GUI) to separately probe high-level reasoning and low-level visual grounding. For the seven games lacking intrinsic scores, a scalable milestone scoring pipeline uses Gemini-3-Pro to extract structured milestone JSONs from expert videos offline and then match agent playthrough frames against them online, yielding a process-oriented score without internal APIs or manual annotation. The key empirical finding is that jointly leveraging failure trajectories (analogous to RL's negative constraint) and expert tutorials (analogous to SFT's positive imitation) produces the largest gains, outperforming either source alone and mirroring the complementary benefits of RL+SFT in foundation model post-training.

## Results

On GameVerse (15 games, GUI mode), Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves the best average rank (7.5 without VR, 9.2 with VR), followed by Qwen3-VL-32B (7.8/9.5), Seed-1.8 (8.4/9.3), and Gemini-2.5-Flash (8.1/9.3). In easy games like Tic-Tac-Toe, Gemini-2.5-Pro reaches 100 (matching Human Expert at 98.9), but in hard games (Scene Investigators, RDR2), all VLMs collapse to 0, well below Human Rookie (58.4–70.4). Video-based reflection yields an average improvement of 6.47% for Gemini-2.5-Pro but only 1.60% for GPT-4o-mini, and gains shrink from 4.4% in non-real-time to 1.7% in real-time games. Semantic action mode averages 50.5 versus 33.5 for GUI mode, exposing a persistent knowing-doing gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All seven VLMs consistently outperform the Random baseline across nearly all 15 games, confirming they engage in meaningful visual reasoning rather than stochastic action selection. Among proprietary models, Gemini-2.5-Pro (avg rank 7.5) and Seed-1.8 (8.4) lead, while GPT-4o (13.3) and GPT-4o-mini (14.1) trail significantly, particularly on non-real-time and complex games where they score 0. The open-source Qwen3-VL-32B (7.8) is competitive with top proprietary models, narrowing the gap to Gemini-2.5-Pro by less than one rank position. Against Human Expert (avg rank 1.6), all VLMs fall far short on hard games (Scene, RDR2, Genshin), where models score 0–18 versus 94–100 for experts; however, on easy Markov Grid games, Gemini-2.5-Pro and Seed-1.8 match or exceed expert scores. Video-based reflection improves all models, but the gains are highly non-uniform: Gemini-2.5-Pro benefits most (+6.47% average) while GPT-4o-mini gains only +1.60%, and the combined failure+tutorial reflection (Self) outperforms either source alone by 3.6–4.7%, validating the RL+SFT analogy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of VLM game-playing benchmarks (VideoGameBench, V-MAGE, Orak, LMGAME-Bench) by introducing a reflect-and-retry evaluation paradigm that departs from the universal fire-and-forget protocol of all prior work. It builds on the GUI action space design from UI-TARS and FlashAdventure, and draws conceptual inspiration from self-reflection literature (Reflexion, Voyager, GROOT), but uniquely replicates the human gameplay loop of diagnosing one's own failure video against an expert tutorial before retrying. The cognitive hierarchical taxonomy (image structure × temporal dynamics × causal linearity) replaces the coarse commercial-genre categorization used by V-MAGE, Orak, and LMGAME-Bench, and the VLM-based milestone scoring pipeline addresses the scalability limitation of manual annotation in prior long-horizon game evaluations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GameVerse provides the first scalable, vision-centric benchmark where VLM agents must learn from video-based reflection, filling a gap that all prior game benchmarks leave open. The milestone scoring pipeline (using Gemini-3-Pro to extract and match milestones from pixels) is practically important for evaluating closed-source commercial games without API access. Key open questions remain: whether active multi-turn reflection (iteratively querying video segments) can overcome the identified cognitive bottleneck, whether the knowing-doing gap between semantic planning and GUI execution can be closed by better grounding, and whether the Rich-Get-Richer effect in reflection will persist as models improve. The finding that a stronger model's reflections do not transfer to a weaker model suggests the bottleneck is internalization rather than insight extraction, pointing toward execution-level improvements as the next frontier.
