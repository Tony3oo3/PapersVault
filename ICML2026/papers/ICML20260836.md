## Problem

Kalai and Vempala (STOC 2024) showed that calibrated language models must hallucinate at roughly the rate of the missing mass, but left open two fundamental questions: what is the simplest property that makes hallucination unavoidable, and can a model avoid hallucinating by simply not being calibrated? This paper identifies a much simpler property—innovation, the probability a model assigns to statements outside its training data—as the key driver, showing it is an almost characterization of hallucination.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on a finite statement space Ω with a training corpus X (a multiset of observed statements), a meta-distribution D_world over document distributions, and a language model's predictive distribution g = A(X). The central output is a lower bound on the hallucination rate g(H) expressed in terms of the innovation rate g(U) (the mass g assigns to unobserved statements), with probability guarantees conditioned on the observed corpus. A key translation step converts innovation-rate bounds into missing-mass bounds via a coarsening lemma, yielding bounds that depend only on the document distribution and corpus rather than the model's internal structure.

## Method

The paper introduces innovation (g(U) > 0) and proves a two-way connection to hallucination: hallucination trivially implies innovation, and under K-Sparsity and Regular Facts, innovation implies hallucination with probability at least 1 − K/|U|. It then shows calibration plus any positive missing mass implies innovation, making the Kalai-Vempala existence result a special case. For quantitative bounds, a Markov-style argument yields g(H) ≥ g(U)(1 − K/(δ|U|)) with probability 1−δ, while a high-confidence bound via a scatter-or-spike case analysis gives g(H) ≥ g(U)/(K+1) with probability 1 − K/|U|, removing dependence on corpus size n without requiring the Regular Probabilities assumption. A coarsening lemma (p^Π(U) ≥ p(U)/(K+1)) then translates these into missing-mass lower bounds that remain non-vacuous in regimes where the original Kalai-Vempala bounds collapse.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical contribution is showing that innovation (a model assigning positive probability to unseen statements) almost characterizes hallucination: under K-Sparsity and Regular Facts, innovation implies hallucination with posterior probability at least 1 − K/|U| (Theorem 3.3). Two quantitative lower bounds are derived: a Markov-style bound giving Pr[g(H) ≥ g(U)(1 − K/(δ|U|)) | X] ≥ 1 − δ (Theorem 4.1), and a high-confidence bound giving Pr[g(H) ≥ g(U)/(K+1) | X] ≥ 1 − K/|U| (Theorem 4.2), both requiring only K-Sparsity and Regular Facts. Empirically, on a customer reviews dataset (2,350 reviews, 3,722-word vocabulary) with n-gram models (n = 2–7), innovation rate closely tracks hallucination rate across all five judges (1 human + 4 LLM), confirming the theoretical prediction even when framework assumptions are violated.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against the Kalai–Vempala (STOC 2024) hallucination lower bounds. Under K-Sparsity and Regular Facts alone, Kalai–Vempala's Corollary 2.8 gives g(H) ≥ p(U) − Mis(g,p) − K(n+1)/(δ|U|), which becomes vacuous once n ≥ |U|/K; their Corollary 2.9 removes the n-dependence but requires the additional Regular Probabilities assumption. This paper's Corollary 4.4 achieves a comparable guarantee (g(H) ≥ p(U)/(K+1) − 1/(δ|U|) − Mis(g,p)) without Regular Probabilities and without any dependence on corpus size n, at the cost of a 1/(K+1) factor on the missing mass term. Corollary 4.5 further extends coverage to the regime δ = K/|U|, where both Kalai–Vempala bounds are vacuous, by guaranteeing g(H) ≥ p(U)/(K+1)² − Mis(g,p)/(K+1) with probability 1 − K/|U|. Thus the paper recovers the qualitative conclusion of Kalai–Vempala under strictly weaker assumptions while providing nontrivial bounds in parameter regimes where the original results collapse.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Kalai-Vempala (STOC 2024) probabilistic framework for hallucination, which showed that calibrated language models must hallucinate at roughly the missing-mass rate. The key departure is replacing the sophisticated calibration condition with the much simpler property of 'innovation' (assigning probability to unseen statements), which the authors show almost characterizes hallucination in a two-way sense. The paper also extends the quantitative bounds by removing the dependence on corpus size n without requiring the additional Regular Probabilities assumption that Kalai and Vempala needed, and it connects to the broader language-generation-in-the-limit literature (Kleinberg & Mullainathan, Charikar & Pabbaraju) as a complementary statistical perspective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that innovation — a trivially measurable property of a model — almost characterizes hallucination provides a practical diagnostic: model trainers can estimate innovation rate without knowing the true document distribution, offering a tractable proxy for hallucination risk. The finding that increasing training data alone cannot eliminate hallucination (even without Regular Probabilities) closes a gap left by Kalai-Vempala and has implications for scaling strategies. Open questions include whether the innovation-hallucination relationship persists for modern transformer-based LLMs, how semantic feedback mechanisms (RAG, RLHF) interact with innovation, and whether the framework extends to prompted generation settings.
