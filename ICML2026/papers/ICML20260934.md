## Problem

Classical sample complexity bounds for causal structure learning (e.g., Gao et al., 2022) are minimax, characterizing worst-case difficulty without distinguishing easy from hard instances. Dense graphs with many v-structures provide more orienting information than sparse chains, yet no existing theory precisely quantifies this instance-specific variation. The paper asks: what single parameter governs how many samples a particular MEC actually requires to recover?

## Input / Output

The method takes a known DAG structure G and its population covariance matrix Σ (or, in the estimation setting, n i.i.d. samples from the corresponding linear Gaussian SEM) and produces a scalar Fisher dimension F([G]) = 1/ρ_min². Computation via Algorithm 1 iterates over edges, computing partial correlations with canonical conditioning sets S = Pa(i)\{j}, requiring O(|E|·d³) time. In the post-hoc estimation setting, the learned CPDAG and sample covariance matrix yield a consistent estimate F̂_n([G]) with O(n^{-1/2}) convergence, usable as a retrospective difficulty diagnostic rather than a sequential stopping rule.

## Method

The Fisher dimension is defined as the inverse squared minimum nonzero partial correlation over a sufficient test set that guarantees MEC recovery, giving it a natural information-geometric interpretation as the minimum curvature among MEC-distinguishing directions in covariance space. The upper bound n = O(F([G])·log d) is proven via Fisher's z-transform analysis of the PC algorithm's conditional independence tests with a union bound over O(d^{2+Δ}) tests; the lower bound n = Ω(F([G])) uses Le Cam's two-point method on MECs differing by a single partial correlation, with KL divergence O(ρ₀²) and Pinsker's inequality. A key structural finding is that under spectrally well-conditioned SEMs (bounded noise variances, bounded covariance eigenvalues, Θ(1) edge coefficients), F([G]) = O(1) for all graph structures, so instance-specific variation arises from parametric rather than topological features. The paper further proves a curvature characterization F([G]) ≍ λ_min(C_[G])^{-1} for chains under weak-edge scaling β = 1/√d, with explicit constants 4 ≤ F·λ_min ≤ 2π².

## Results

The Fisher dimension F([G]) = 1/ρ_min² provides matching sample complexity bounds: n = Ω(F([G])) (lower) and n = O(F([G])·log d) (upper), tight up to a log d factor. Under spectrally well-conditioned SEMs (bounded noise variances, bounded covariance eigenvalues, Θ(1) edge coefficients), F([G]) = O(1) uniformly across all graph structures. Empirically, F([G])·log d correlates strongly with observed sample complexity: Spearman ρ = 0.94 for chains, 0.91 for trees, and 0.66 for Erdős-Rényi graphs (d=10, 500 graphs each), with 85% of predictions falling within a factor of 2 of empirical n* across all cells.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the Fisher dimension against seven structural proxies (graph density, maximum in-degree, average Markov blanket size, number of v-structures, MEC size estimate, curvature-based proxy, and number of edges) as predictors of empirical sample complexity. On structured families, Fisher dimension is the best predictor: Spearman r = 0.88 for trees and r = 0.93 for chains, outperforming all structural proxies. On heterogeneous Erdős-Rényi graphs, however, average Markov blanket size (r = 0.58) outperforms Fisher dimension (r = 0.37), because structural variance within the class dominates over parametric variation. The paper also validates tightness: for trees and chains, 82–98% of instances have empirical n* within a factor of 2 of the theoretical prediction C·F([G])·log d, while Erdős-Rényi achieves 55–80% depending on the coefficient regime. Overall, Fisher dimension captures parametric difficulty that coarse structural statistics miss within fixed graph types, but structural proxies remain competitive when graph topology varies substantially within a family.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the minimax sample complexity framework for MEC recovery (Gao et al., 2022) to an instance-dependent setting by introducing the Fisher dimension, defined as the inverse squared minimum partial correlation required for MEC recovery. The upper bound is achieved through the PC algorithm's Fisher's z-transform analysis, building directly on the constraint-based methodology of Spirtes et al. (2001), while the lower bound uses Le Cam's two-point method on MECs differing by a single partial correlation. The key departure from prior work is the identification that, under spectrally well-conditioned SEMs, the Fisher dimension is uniformly bounded regardless of graph structure—shifting the source of instance-specific difficulty from structural features (density, v-structures) to parametric ones (edge coefficient magnitudes, noise variances, covariance eigenvalues).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Fisher dimension provides the first principled, algorithm-independent measure of instance-specific difficulty for causal discovery, with matching upper and lower bounds (up to a log d gap) that identify exactly which partial correlations govern sample complexity. The finding that graph structure is irrelevant under well-conditioning is a useful negative result: it redirects attention to parametric regimes (decaying edge strengths, growing eigenvalues) as the true source of difficulty. Open questions include closing the log d gap, extending the curvature characterization beyond chains, developing adaptive or sequential algorithms, and generalizing to non-Gaussian or latent-confounded settings. Limitations include the restriction to linear Gaussian SEMs and the faithfulness assumption.
