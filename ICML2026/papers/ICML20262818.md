## Problem

Classical stochastic programming assumes the underlying probability distribution is known, but in practice limited data makes this assumption fragile, leading to overly optimistic and unstable decisions. Existing remedies like distributionally robust optimization require manually tuning an ambiguity radius with no direct statistical interpretation, while Sample Average Approximation is biased and unstable in small-sample regimes. The paper asks whether one can integrate statistical inference directly into the optimization objective so that the uncertainty knob is a transparent confidence level that automatically tightens as data accumulates.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an i.i.d. sample of N observations from an unknown distribution, a decision space (e.g., a polyhedral feasible set for two-stage linear programs), cost/recourse parameters, and a nominal confidence level α ∈ (0,1]. It produces an optimal first-stage decision x*, the APUB value (a 100(1−α)% upper confidence bound on the expected cost at x*), and the optimal value of the APUB-M model. In the two-stage setting, the adapted L-shaped algorithm iteratively generates feasibility and optimality cuts, with the second-stage evaluation requiring M bootstrap resamples (M ≪ the combinatorial number of exact bootstrap scenarios) whose costs are sorted to exploit the CVaR structure.

## Method

The core contribution is the Average Percentile Upper Bound (APUB), defined as the integral of Efron's bootstrap percentile UCB over [0, α], which admits an equivalent CVaR_α representation of the bootstrap sample mean. This dual identity makes APUB simultaneously a statistically valid upper bound on the population mean (with first-order asymptotic correctness: coverage ≥ 1−α + O(N^{-1/2})) and a coherent risk measure (satisfying sub-additivity, convexity, monotonicity, etc.). For solution, the paper develops a bootstrap sampling approximation that replaces the intractable exact expectation over all multinomial multiplicities with M Monte Carlo resamples, then adapts the L-shaped (Benders) decomposition by exploiting the sorted-order-statistic structure of the CVaR term: at each iteration, the M bootstrap scenario costs are sorted, the (1−α)M-th value serves as a threshold, and the optimality cut coefficients are computed as a weighted combination of the dual multipliers above that threshold. This sorting-based evaluation makes the second-stage computation nearly independent of M, enabling efficient scaling to large bootstrap ensembles.

## Results

On the two-stage product-mix problem (|I|=20, |J|=8), APUB-M with N=120 and an appropriately chosen nominal level already outperforms SAA-M trained with N=240 in mean out-of-sample cost, demonstrating significant robustness gains under data scarcity. The adapted L-shaped method for APUB-M runs in 7.2±1.2 s with 9.2±1.4 iterations (N=120, M=5000, 1−α=0.9), only ~26% slower than the standard L-shaped method for SAA-M (5.7±1.0 s, 8.0±1.4 iterations). Empirical coverage probability tracks the nominal confidence level closely and exceeds it for large N, confirming asymptotic correctness. On the 10-product newsvendor problem, APUB-M yields a solution shift of only 3.36 (ℓ₂-norm, 1−α=0.95) from N=30 to N=120, versus 7.89 for SAA-M, indicating materially more stable prescriptions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares APUB-M against two baselines: SAA-M (classical Sample Average Approximation, equivalent to APUB-M with 1−α=0) and Wasserstein DRO (WassDRO). Against SAA-M, APUB-M wins decisively in small-sample regimes (N=120, 240): it achieves lower mean out-of-sample cost, tighter 10th–90th percentile bands, fewer extreme realizations, and coverage probability that tracks the nominal level, whereas SAA-M maintains very low coverage probability even as N grows. As N increases to 480 and 960, the performance gap narrows and both methods converge, consistent with the asymptotic consistency theorem. Against WassDRO, APUB-M offers superior statistical interpretability (a frequentist confidence level 1−α versus a geometric radius ε requiring heuristic calibration) and automatic data-driven convergence without manual N-dependent rescaling. In low-data settings (N=30), APUB-M avoids the sharp over-conservatism seen with poorly chosen ε in WassDRO, maintaining a more stable mean-cost profile. Computationally, the adapted L-shaped method for APUB-M is only moderately slower than the standard L-shaped method for SAA-M (e.g., 7.2 s vs. 5.7 s at N=120), with iteration counts remaining around 10 across all settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Efron's bootstrap percentile UCB by averaging over the tail to construct the Average Percentile Upper Bound (APUB), which inherits a CVaR-like representation from Rockafellar & Uryasev (2000) and is optimized via an adapted L-shaped (Benders) decomposition from Van Slyke & Wets (1969). Conceptually, it carves a distinct niche from distributionally robust optimization (Wasserstein DRO, moment-based DRO, φ-divergence DRO) by replacing geometric ambiguity sets with a frequentist confidence level as the uncertainty knob, and from UCB maximization in reinforcement learning/bandits by using UCB *minimization* as a robustness-seeking criterion in stochastic programming. The key departure is that APUB's conservatism is governed entirely by sample size and a fixed α, eliminating the manual radius tuning that plagues DRO.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a statistically transparent, automatically converging alternative to DRO for two-stage stochastic programs with random recourse—a setting where DRO often lacks tractable dual reformulations and becomes NP-hard. The adapted L-shaped method with sorting-based APUB evaluation keeps computational overhead modest (roughly 20–30% slower than standard L-shaped for SAA), making the approach practical. Open questions include: principled selection of the nominal level α (the paper defers to cross-validation), extension to multi-stage and non-convex settings, and whether the first-order asymptotic correctness can be upgraded to second-order (e.g., via BCa corrections). The framework also does not address systemic biases in historical data, a limitation the authors acknowledge.
