## Problem

Enforcing hard linear constraints on neural network predictions remains an open problem: existing approaches either rely on reinforcement learning (poor generalization, expensive training), diffusion models (no theoretical guarantees), or tailored layers like LinSATNet/GLinSAT that require gradient descent with carefully tuned step-size hyperparameters and suffer from convergence instability. A general, efficient, and theoretically grounded scheme for arbitrary linear constraints was lacking.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265337/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The layer takes an unconstrained network output y ∈ R^n (or matrix Y ∈ R^{n×m} for graph matching) together with a fixed, known constraint matrix W ∈ R^{m×n} and vector b ∈ R^m, and produces a constrained output x satisfying Wx = b exactly. For BLCLayer, W must be binary and x is restricted to non-negative values; for GLCLayer, W can be any real matrix with m < n and full row rank, and x can take arbitrary real values. The constraint parameters (W, b) are not learned — only the backbone network is trained, and gradients flow back through the layer via the chain rule.

## Method

The paper unifies classic activation layers (Softmax, Sigmoid, Tanh, Sinkhorn) under a single implicit-optimization lens: each is the solution to minimizing KL-divergence between the output and an exponential of the input under a specific constraint. Building on this, BLCLayer generalizes to binary positive linear constraints (W ∈ {0,1}^{m×n}, x ≥ 0) by solving min KL(x‖e^y) s.t. Wx = b via Bregman iterative projections, where each projection step reduces to a closed-form element-wise multiply-and-divide update (analogous to Sinkhorn row/column normalization). For fully general linear constraints, GLCLayer replaces KL-divergence with Euclidean distance, yielding the closed-form solution x = y − W^T(WW^T)^{−1}(Wy − b) in a single step with no iterations or hyperparameters. For linear programming (which adds x ≥ 0 and an objective c^T x), a lightweight ADMM post-processing step refines the GLCLayer output. The entire layer is differentiable and plug-in, requiring no gradient descent or step-size tuning, and the WW^T factorization is precomputed once.

## Results

On partial graph matching (Pascal VOC Keypoint), BLCNet-10 achieves F1 0.6012, outperforming LinSAT-5 (0.5858) and Sinkhorn-NGMv2 (0.5453) while training in 1h37m vs. LinSAT-5's 1h56m. On portfolio allocation (S&P 500), GLCNet attains a Sharpe ratio of 3.053 with 7.20s inference time, matching cvxpylayers (3.058) but running ~73× faster at inference than LinSAT-100 (524.39s). On synthetic linear programming (up to 2000 variables), GLCNet achieves 0.90–0.95 optimality ratio vs. Gurobi while delivering 5×–25× speedup on large-scale instances (e.g., 0.16s vs. 4.49s for the 2k-variable setting).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BLCNet outperforms BBGM (differentiable blackbox, F1 0.5516) and Sinkhorn-NGMv2 (F1 0.5453) by 4.6–5.6 F1 points on graph matching, while also training ~15% faster than LinSAT-5. Against LinSATNet, BLCNet-5 achieves higher F1 (0.5972 vs. 0.5858) with 28% less training time, and the gap widens at higher iteration counts where LinSAT-40 gains only 0.0045 F1 yet doubles overhead. On portfolio allocation, GLCNet (Sharpe 3.053) matches cvxpylayers (3.058) but runs 3.7× faster at inference (7.20s vs. 26.66s), and BLCNet-5 (2.773) surpasses LinSAT-10 (2.508) with 4.8× less training time. On linear programming, GLCNet consistently outperforms HardNet in objective quality across all scales (e.g., 0.964 vs. 0.922 Pct. on small-scale (500,400,0)) while training ~15× faster per epoch. GLCNet also beats the SL-ADMM variant in speed on large instances (0.16s vs. 0.164s) while maintaining competitive optimality ratios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies classic activation layers (Softmax, Sigmoid, Tanh, Sinkhorn) under a single KL-divergence implicit optimization framework, then extends this to general linear constraints. It departs from LinSATNet (generalized Sinkhorn for positive linear constraints) and GLinSAT (accelerated gradient descent for general linear constraints) by replacing iterative gradient-based solvers with either Bregman projections (BLCLayer, for binary coefficient matrices) or a closed-form Euclidean projection (GLCLayer, for arbitrary real coefficients). The conceptual lineage traces through Bregman Iterative Projections for entropic optimal transport (Benamou et al., 2015) and the Sinkhorn algorithm, while the LP-solving component builds on the differentiable-optimization-as-a-layer paradigm of OptNet (Amos & Kolter, 2017) and the hard-constraint enforcement of HardNet (Min & Azizan, 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The closed-form GLCLayer eliminates the hyperparameter sensitivity and convergence instability that plague gradient-descent-based constraint layers (e.g., GLinSAT's step-size tuning), making it a drop-in replacement for any architecture requiring linear equality constraints. The practical efficiency gains (20–30× faster than LinSATNet-100 on portfolio allocation; 5–20× faster than Gurobi on large LP instances) are significant for real-time decision-making in finance and combinatorial optimization. Key limitations remain: BLCLayer requires binary constraint coefficients and non-negative outputs, GLCLayer requires precomputing the WW^T factorization (limiting scalability to very large dense systems), and the neural LP solver still achieves only ~90% of Gurobi's objective quality. Open questions include extending the framework to inequality-only or mixed-integer constraints, handling non-linear objectives within the same layer, and scaling to industrial-scale problems with O(10⁴) variables.
