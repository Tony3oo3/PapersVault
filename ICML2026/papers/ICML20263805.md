## Problem

Prior work reported that programmatic policies generalize better than neural policies on OOD RL benchmarks, but did not explain why or control for confounding experimental factors. This paper re-evaluates three influential benchmarks (TORCS, KAREL, PARKING) and shows that much of the reported generalization gap stems from uncontrolled factors—such as reward shaping and observation richness—rather than from inherent representational advantages of programs over neural networks.

## Input / Output

Each domain has a distinct observation structure: TORCS provides 29 continuous sensor readings (speed, RPM, track position, etc.) and outputs continuous steering/acceleration actions; KAREL provides local grid-cell perceptions (front/left/right clear, markers present) augmented with the agent's last action, outputting discrete move/turn/pick/put actions; PARKING provides car position, heading angle, and distances to neighboring cars, outputting discrete driving actions. The key experimental manipulation is modifying the input (sparse observations in KAREL) or the intrinsic reward (cautious β=0.5 in TORCS) while keeping the evaluation metric (lap time, success rate on unseen instances) unchanged, so the problem itself is not altered—only how the agent learns to solve it.

## Method

The paper re-runs three programmatic-policy baselines (NDPS for TORCS, LEAPS for KAREL, PSM for PARKING) alongside carefully modified neural baselines (DDPG, PPO, DQN) to isolate representational effects from experimental confounds. In TORCS, replacing the original speed-emphasizing intrinsic reward with a cautious variant (β=0.5) allows DDPG policies to match NDPS on OOD tracks; in KAREL, restricting observations to local cells plus the last action and using a simple fully-connected PPO (instead of LSTM or ConvNet) enables generalization to 100×100 grids, matching LEAPS. The paper frames the analysis through two properties—expressivity (the policy space contains a generalizing solution) and discoverability (the search algorithm can find it)—and argues that the DSLs used in prior work induce policy spaces comparable to ReLU networks, meaning both representations satisfy expressivity and the observed gap was purely a discoverability artifact of the training pipeline.

## Results

On TORCS, DRL with a cautious reward (β=0.5) generalizes to OOD tracks at 76% (G-TRACK-2) and 69% (E-ROAD) from G-TRACK-1, and 100% to both ALPINE-2 and RUUDSKOGEN from AALBORG, matching NDPS's programmatic policies. On KAREL, PPO with the last action appended (a simple feedforward network) achieves a perfect 1.00 return on STAIRCLIMBER, MAZE, TOPOFF, and FOURCORNER at 100×100 grid size, outperforming LEAPS (0.21 on TOPOFF, 0.00 on HARVESTER) and both original neural baselines (PPO ConvNet and PPO LSTM, both near 0.00). On PARKING, results are mixed: PSM shows a smaller train–test gap (0.10 vs. 0.68 for DQN), but DQN achieves a higher absolute test success rate (0.18 vs. 0.16), and neither representation reliably generalizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Verma et al. (2018, NDPS on TORCS): the original DRL (β=1.0) crashes on all OOD tracks, but with a cautious intrinsic reward (β=0.5), neural policies match NDPS's generalization—achieving comparable lap times (e.g., 1:48 vs. 1:40 on G-TRACK-2) with 76–100% of seeds successfully generalizing, showing the gap was due to reward shaping, not representation. Against Trivedi et al. (2021, LEAPS on KAREL): the paper's PPO with last-action augmentation (a simple feedforward net) clearly outperforms LEAPS on OOD 100×100 grids, achieving 1.00 return on four of five tasks versus LEAPS's 0.21 (TOPOFF) and 0.00 (HARVESTER), and also surpasses both original neural baselines (PPO+ConvNet and PPO+LSTM, both near 0.00 at 100×100). Against Inala et al. (2020, PSM on PARKING): results are inconclusive—PSM shows a smaller train–test degradation (0.26→0.16 vs. DQN's 0.86→0.18), suggesting better relative generalization, but DQN achieves a higher absolute test success rate (0.18 vs. 0.16), and neither method reliably solves all 100 test states (PSM: 2/30 models; DQN: 0/15 models). Overall, the paper demonstrates that the previously reported programmatic advantage is largely attributable to uncontrolled experimental factors (reward design, observation sparsity) rather than inherent representational superiority.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not introduce a new method but re-evaluates the experimental claims of three influential programmatic RL papers (Verma et al. 2018/2019 on TORCS, Trivedi et al. 2021 on KAREL, Inala et al. 2020 on PARKING) that reported programmatic policies generalizing better than neural ones out of distribution. It departs from this line of work by introducing an expressivity/discoverability framework to disentangle whether observed generalization gaps stem from the representation's solution space or from the search algorithm's ability to find generalizing solutions within that space. The paper also connects to the broader OOD generalization literature on removing visual distractions (Bertoin et al., 2022; Grooten et al., 2024) and to theoretical work on the computational limits of recurrent networks (Weiss et al., 2018; Delétang et al., 2023) to argue for when programmatic representations may be genuinely necessary.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a widely-cited narrative in programmatic RL that programs inherently generalize better than neural networks, showing that much of the reported gap is attributable to uncontrolled experimental factors such as reward shaping, observation richness, and model complexity. The expressivity/discoverability distinction provides a useful conceptual lens for future comparisons, and the PARKING results—where neither representation reliably generalizes—suggest the problem is harder than previously acknowledged. Open questions include whether there exist domains where programmatic representations have a genuine expressivity advantage (e.g., problems requiring memory that grows with input size), and whether the specific modifications identified (cautious rewards, sparse observations, simpler architectures) transfer to more complex, real-world RL settings.
