## Problem

Tabular Language Models (TLMs) claim strong zero- and few-shot generalization over gradient-boosted trees and prior-fitted networks, yet their evaluations systematically omit majority-class baselines, do not stratify results by task type, and lack rigorous contamination analysis. This paper asks whether TLMs genuinely learn tabular reasoning or whether reported performance reflects evaluation artifacts such as class-imbalance exploitation, data leakage, and format familiarity rather than learned structure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261320/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The re-evaluation uses 165 datasets from the UniPredict benchmark (64 binary, 55 categorical, 46 quartile classification), where each input is a serialized table row accompanied by a task instruction and 4-shot examples in either Tabula-8B's special-token format or the Alpaca instruction template. The output is a discrete class label: a binary value, a categorical category, or a quartile bin (e.g., 'between 764.1 and 7697.9'). The primary reported metric is lift over the majority-class baseline rather than raw accuracy, supplemented by Cohen's Kappa to control for class imbalance.

## Method

The paper conducts a three-part diagnostic re-evaluation of Tabula-8B. First, it computes majority-class baselines and stratifies performance by task type, revealing that binary and categorical classification achieve near-zero median lift while aggregate gains are driven entirely by quartile classification. Second, it searches the T4 training corpus (~100B tokens) for evaluation examples from top-performing datasets, identifying three contamination mechanisms: complete train-test overlap (e.g., all 753 rows of us-womens-labor), direct label exposure in financial time series, and task-level leakage (e.g., date-to-day mappings memorized from 844 unrelated T4 tables). Third, it trains two control models from the same Base Llama-3-8B: Alpaca (instruction-tuned on 50K general examples with no tabular data) and Alpaca+Q (Alpaca plus 10K quartile-format examples from held-out datasets), isolating instruction-following, format familiarity, and tabular pretraining as separate performance sources. The key finding is that instruction-tuning alone recovers 92.2% of Tabula-8B's classification accuracy, format familiarity closes 71.3% of the quartile gap, and the residual advantage exists only on datasets with documented contamination.

## Results

On 165 UniPredict datasets, Tabula-8B achieves 63.5% mean accuracy on classification tasks but only +6.5pp lift over the majority-class baseline, with 39.4% of datasets showing zero or negative lift. Performance is heavily skewed by task type: quartile classification yields +32.6pp median lift, while binary (+2.5pp) and categorical (−0.3pp) classification barely exceed or fall below the majority-class baseline. Critically, Alpaca (Llama-3-8B fine-tuned on general instructions with no tabular exposure) recovers 92.2% of Tabula-8B's classification accuracy, and on non-stock quartile datasets Alpaca+Q (format familiarity only) matches or slightly exceeds Tabula-8B (6.7pp vs 6.5pp lift). Eight of the top-10 performing datasets exhibit documented contamination, including complete train-test overlap and direct label exposure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Base Llama-3-8B, Tabula-8B gains 15.9pp in mean classification accuracy, but 69% of this gain (11.0pp) is attributable to instruction-tuning alone (Alpaca), with tabular pretraining contributing only 4.9pp (31%). Against Alpaca (no tabular exposure), Tabula-8B's 4.9pp accuracy advantage on classification is driven primarily by contaminated datasets; excluding 5 documented contaminated datasets, the gap narrows to 4.1pp and instruction-tuning's share rises to 73%. On quartile classification, Tabula-8B's apparent 28.5pp advantage over Alpaca decomposes into format unfamiliarity (closed by Alpaca+Q, recovering 71.3% of the gap) and contamination (the residual 17.9pp gap exists only on stock/financial datasets with documented label exposure). On non-stock quartile datasets, Alpaca+Q actually slightly outperforms Tabula-8B (6.7pp vs 6.5pp lift), indicating no genuine tabular reasoning advantage once contamination and format effects are controlled.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not introduce a new method but applies standard evaluation diagnostics—majority-class baselines, Cohen's Kappa, task-type stratification, and contamination probing—to critically re-examine Tabula-8B (Gardner et al., 2024) as a representative Tabular Language Model. It positions itself within the growing skepticism about foundation model evaluation (Rogers & Luccioni, 2024; Joshi et al., 2026) and extends contamination-detection practices from NLP (Brown et al., 2020) and VLMs to the tabular domain, where permutation invariance and multi-source duplication make standard deduplication insufficient. The controlled experimental design—parallel fine-tunings of the same Llama-3-8B base into Tabula-8B (tabular) and Alpaca (general instruction)—repurposes the ablation logic common in NLP to isolate tabular pretraining from instruction-following capability, a decomposition absent from prior TLM evaluations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the central claim of the TLM literature—that large-scale tabular pretraining yields genuine zero/few-shot generalization—by showing that reported gains are largely attributable to evaluation artifacts (task-type composition, contamination) and general instruction-following rather than learned tabular reasoning. Its seven recommendations (baseline reporting, task stratification, contamination auditing, instruction-tuned controls) provide a concrete framework the community can adopt, and the finding that these gaps recur across five major TLM papers suggests the issue is systemic. Open questions include whether other TLMs trained on different corpora or objectives exhibit similar patterns, how to build robust contamination detection for tabular data at corpus scale, and whether any architecture can genuinely learn tabular invariances from serialized text. Limitations include focus on a single TLM, one benchmark subset (165 of 329 datasets), classification tasks only, and a non-exhaustive contamination search.
