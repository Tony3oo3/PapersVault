## Problem

Existing Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) methods evaluate training levels using indirect proxy signals—regret estimates, GAE-based value errors, or Monte Carlo return differences—that suffer from estimation bias, high variance, or substantial computational overhead. None of these signals directly and reliably reflect the realized learning progress of the current agent at its training stage, making it difficult to construct curricula that match the agent's evolving capability.

## Input / Output

PACE consumes a trajectory τ collected under the current policy π_θ_old on a candidate level l (256 steps on MiniGrid, 4096 transitions on Craftax) together with the current policy parameters θ_old. It produces a scalar level score S(l) = (1/α)‖θ_new − θ_old‖²₂ from a single provisional gradient update, which is then used to populate a staleness-aware level buffer Λ of size K. During training, levels are sampled from Λ via a rank-based prioritized distribution P(l_i) ∝ (1/rank(S(l_i)))^(1/β), and the final output is a PPO-trained policy evaluated zero-shot on held-out OOD levels.

## Method

PACE's core insight is a first-order theoretical result: under a local gradient-aligned update θ_new = θ_old + α∇_θ J(θ_old, l), the objective improvement ΔJ(l) is proportional to ‖θ_new − θ_old‖²₂, establishing parameter change as a principled proxy for environment value. In practice, PACE collects a trajectory on a newly generated level, performs a single provisional policy update to obtain θ_new, computes the squared L2 norm of the parameter difference as the level score, and discards θ_new without writing it back to the policy. Levels whose scores exceed the lowest in the buffer replace the weakest entry, and a staleness-aware mechanism from PLR periodically re-evaluates under-sampled levels to prevent catastrophic forgetting. This design requires no additional rollouts beyond the scoring trajectory, no value-function estimation, and no Monte Carlo return comparison, yielding a deterministic, low-variance scoring signal that is computationally O(P) per level.

## Results

On MiniGrid, PACE achieves an IQM of 0.964 and an Optimality Gap of 0.172 across 12 held-out levels, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline PLR (IQM 0.808, Optimality Gap 0.299). On the large-scale Craftax-1B benchmark (1B interactions), PACE attains an IQM of 0.750 and an Optimality Gap of 0.268, beating the best baseline DR (IQM 0.603, Optimality Gap 0.407). On the 51×51 PerfectMaze environment, PACE reaches a 72.3% solve rate, far exceeding all baselines. PACE also exhibits the lowest score-estimation variability (Mean CV 0.0010 on MiniGrid, 0.5412 on Craftax) among all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PACE outperforms all four baselines (DR, PLR, PLR⊥, ACCEL) on every reported metric across both MiniGrid and Craftax. On MiniGrid, PACE improves IQM by 15.6 percentage points over the strongest baseline PLR (0.964 vs. 0.808) and reduces the Optimality Gap by 12.7 points (0.172 vs. 0.299); on the challenging Labyrinth and Maze3 levels specifically, PACE maintains solve rates above 90% with markedly smaller standard deviations. On Craftax, PACE leads DR (the strongest baseline in this setting) by 14.7 IQM points (0.750 vs. 0.603) and 13.9 Optimality Gap points (0.268 vs. 0.407), with the gap widening as training progresses under non-stationary dynamics. A preliminary comparison with MBeDED (Appendix B.4) shows PACE achieving IQM in [0.8, 1.0] versus MBeDED's [0.6, 0.8] on MiniGrid, though a full controlled comparison is deferred to future work. PACE also demonstrates the lowest coefficient of variation in score estimation (0.0010 on MiniGrid, 0.5412 on Craftax), confirming its low-variance scoring signal relative to GAE-based (PLR) and MC-based (PLR⊥, ACCEL) alternatives.

## Relation to Prior Work

PACE builds directly on the replay-based UED framework established by PLR and Robust PLR, reusing their rank-based prioritization and staleness-aware buffer, but replaces the level scoring mechanism entirely. Where prior methods (PAIRED, PLR, ACCEL, MBeDED) evaluate levels through indirect proxies—regret, GAE-based prediction errors, Monte Carlo returns, or marginal benefit comparisons—PACE scores a level by the squared ℓ₂ norm of the policy parameter change induced by a single provisional update on that level. This shifts the evaluation criterion from theoretical learning potential or estimated return gaps to a direct, deterministic measure of realized optimization progress, grounded in a first-order Taylor expansion linking parameter change to objective improvement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PACE offers a practical, low-variance, and computationally cheap scoring signal that can be dropped into existing UED pipelines without additional rollouts or value-function estimation, addressing a persistent weakness in the field where evaluation metrics introduce bias, variance, or overhead. The strong results on both MiniGrid (IQM 0.964 vs. 0.808) and the much harder Craftax-1B benchmark (IQM 0.750 vs. 0.603) suggest the signal remains informative under long-horizon, non-stationary dynamics. Open questions include whether the first-order approximation holds for non-PPO algorithms or very large parameter spaces, whether the approach transfers to continuous control, and how it interacts with generative or hierarchical UED frameworks that the authors leave to future work.
