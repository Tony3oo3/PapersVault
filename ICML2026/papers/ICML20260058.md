## Problem

Existing LLM inference-efficiency methods (quantization, pruning, sparse attention) apply a uniform compute budget to every generated token, yet token difficulty varies widely across a sequence. This static allocation over-computes on locally predictable steps and under-computes on steps requiring long-range context or precise intermediate calculations. The paper asks whether a single frozen LLM can learn, from its own internal state, how much compute to spend per token to track a better quality-efficiency Pareto frontier.

## Input / Output

At each decode step the policy consumes the frozen LLM's final-layer hidden state from the previous token, the current input token embedding, and an 8-dimensional scalar vector encoding episode progress, an effective-step flag, the requested budget targets (κ, ρ, η), and running averages of realized budgets. It outputs a single discrete action from a combinatorial set (up to 1560 joint choices) that simultaneously sets the token-attention keep fraction, the structured MLP activation keep fraction, and the activation quantization bit-width for that step. The base LLM then executes one decode step under that compute regime and produces the next token; the policy's KV cache is reset at every episode boundary (default T=16 steps), keeping inference-time overhead constant and negligible.

## Method

SOL pairs a frozen LLM with a lightweight autoregressive transformer policy (8.56M parameters for an 8B base) that selects a discrete efficiency action at each decode step, jointly controlling token-level attention sparsity (Quest), structured MLP activation pruning (TEAL-style top-k channel retention), and activation quantization bit-width (ZeroQuant-style symmetric fake-quantization). Because per-token compression decisions pollute the KV cache and degrade future steps, the problem is cast as a sequential decision problem over episodes of T=16 steps with periodic dense KV-cache refresh to bound pollution compounding. Training uses group-relative policy optimization (GRPO) on teacher-forced counterfactual schedules: for each input, K schedules share the same token path but differ only in efficiency actions, and group-relative advantages are computed by mean-centering across schedules at each timestep. The reward combines discounted token log-likelihood with a squared-hinge penalty that encourages episode-average realized budgets to match user-specified targets (sampled per sequence during training), enabling a single policy to operate across a range of compute budgets at deployment. The policy is conditioned on the requested budget triple as part of its observation, so at inference time the user simply specifies a target efficiency regime and the policy adapts without retraining.

## Results

SOL consistently achieves lower perplexity than fixed (static) budget allocation across all model sizes and action-space granularities, with paired perplexity improvements of 0.13–0.38 points (e.g., SOL-J-2L-T16: 11.51 vs. 11.89 PPL; Llama-3.1-8B: 7.93 vs. 8.20 PPL), all statistically significant (p < 10⁻⁵). Win rates over the fixed baseline range from 58.7% (shortest horizon T=4) to 95.5% (Llama-3.2-3B), confirming that the learned per-token allocation tracks a better quality-efficiency Pareto frontier. On downstream tasks, SOL improves MMLU accuracy by up to 7.3% over uniform budget allocation and consistently outperforms the fixed baseline on ARC-Easy/PIQA/WinoGrande (Llama-3.2-3B) and GSM8K-CoT (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B) at matched net keep-rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is a fixed (static) compute schedule that applies a constant efficiency action across all decode steps, matched to the same average budget. SOL outperforms this fixed baseline on perplexity in every configuration tested (Table 1), with the largest gains (Δ ≈ 0.38 PPL, 95.3% win rate) for the coarsest 8-action joint space (SOL-J-2L-T16) and the 3B model. Against the individual static methods annotated in Figure 5—Quest token sparsity at fixed keep-rates (Quest@50%, Quest@30%), ZeroQuant-style activation quantization at fixed bit-widths (ZeroQuant-8Bit, ZeroQuant-5Bit), and TEAL-style activation pruning at fixed levels (TEAL@80%, TEAL@60%)—SOL's learned policy traces a strictly lower perplexity frontier at every matched net keep-rate across all three model sizes (1B, 3B, 8B), demonstrating that jointly adapting all three axes per token dominates applying any single method at a fixed setting. Against best-of-500 random schedule search (Appendix A, Fig. 9), SOL matches or exceeds the best random schedule in 8 of 12 tested operating points and remains within the top 5.6% of the random distribution in the remaining 4 cases (max regret 0.085 PPL). Hand-crafted heuristics (entropy-matched and drift-aware criteria) do not consistently beat the fixed baseline, whereas SOL does, confirming that the learned multi-axis joint allocation is non-trivial.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on three established LLM compression techniques—Quest-style token sparsity, TEAL-style activation pruning, and ZeroQuant-style activation quantization—but departs from their standard use as static, uniform-budget methods by wrapping them in a learned per-token controller. The training recipe draws on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) from the DeepSeekMath line of work, repurposing group-relative policy optimization for a sequential compute-allocation problem rather than mathematical reasoning. Conceptually, SOL is positioned as orthogonal to early-exit/layer-skipping methods (Schuster et al., 2022; Elbayad et al., 2019), which adapt depth, whereas SOL keeps depth fixed and instead modulates precision, sparsity, and pruning per step. The KV-pollution analysis provides a mechanistic justification for why the problem must be treated as sequential (with episodes and periodic cache refresh) rather than as independent per-step decisions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SOL opens a practically important axis of inference optimization: a single lightweight controller (≈8.5M params for an 8B model) can be conditioned on a requested budget at deployment time, enabling operators to trade quality against latency without retraining or re-quantizing the base model. Key limitations include the use of monotonic keep-rate proxies rather than hardware-specific latency, uniform (layer-agnostic) action broadcasting, and the need for periodic KV-cache refresh to bound pollution compounding. Open questions include whether per-layer or per-head allocation would further improve the frontier, whether the approach transfers to hardware-aware objectives (real latency, power), and how the policy behaves under truly autoregressive (non-teacher-forced) generation where token paths diverge across counterfactual schedules.
