## Problem

In AI conference peer review, a single scalar score forces multi-dimensional reviewer judgments onto an under-specified and non-comparable scale: similar rationales routinely yield scores differing by 2+ points, and this scale drift worsens as submission volumes grow. The paper argues that the rationale-to-score mapping is the specific failure mode to target, and that an LLM-driven calibration layer can make this misalignment measurable and actionable without replacing human scientific judgment.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes the full paper text (capped at 15,000 characters for the scoring prompt), the reviewer's free-form strengths and weaknesses fields, and the reviewer's reported 1-10 rating. It produces (i) itemized rationale statements with per-item weight contributions (pros in [0,5], cons in [-5,0]), (ii) a single anchor score obtained by summing item weights around a base of 5 and clipping to [1,10], (iii) the residual r = s^H - s^A as the primary diagnostic signal, and (iv) a binary post-check trigger when |r| exceeds a threshold tau. The anchor is explicitly not a quality label but a rubric-consistent reference for auditing score-rationale coherence.

## Method

The core mechanism is a two-stage LLM pipeline: first, an extraction step converts heterogeneous free-form pros/cons into atomic, self-contained rationale items; second, a rubric-grounded scoring step assigns each item a context-aware weight conditional on the paper content, then aggregates them as s^A = clip(5 + sum of pro weights + sum of con weights, 1, 10). The key design choice is that the LLM never introduces new critiques or makes accept/reject decisions-it only checks whether the numeric score is coherent with the written rationale under a fixed, pinned prompt and rubric, making the mapping reproducible (test-retest variance below 0.13 for 2023-2024). When the residual exceeds a threshold, a lightweight post-check asks the reviewer to either add paper-grounded justification or revise the score, keeping intervention sparse. The paper validates the approach on ICLR 2023-2025 OpenReview data with leakage-aware controls (older-model reruns, post-cutoff datasets, gpt-5.4-mini variance checks), showing that residual dispersion grows over time, extreme disagreements concentrate on evidence-strength and novelty disputes, and anchor-based borderline ranking correlates with higher downstream citations.

## Results

Across ICLR 2023–2025, the mean within-paper bias span (max minus min residual) grows from 3.007 to 3.211, and mean absolute residual (MAE) rises from 1.357 to 1.714, indicating worsening non-comparable scoring as submission volumes increase. In the offline post-check simulation on 200 high-residual cases, the mean absolute residual drops from 2.93 to 1.06 (−1.87 points, p < 10⁻⁷⁰), with 76.5% of cases resolved via score revision. In the borderline zone (scores 4–6), anchor-selected papers achieve 53–94% higher mean citations than reviewer-score-selected papers in ICLR 2023, and 19–77% higher in ICLR 2024, suggesting the rationale-grounded anchor better preserves quality signals near the decision boundary.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not benchmark against competing calibration methods; instead, its central comparison is between raw reviewer scores and the LLM-derived anchor scores. The anchor (GPT-4o-mini, temperature 0.1, base score 5) correlates with actual reviewer scores at r = 0.437–0.483, confirming partial alignment while leaving substantial residual variance that the paper argues is miscalibration rather than noise. For leakage robustness, rerunning the pipeline with gpt-3.5-turbo-1106 on ICLR 2023–2024 yields bias spans of 2.665 and 2.691 (vs. 3.007 and 3.064 with GPT-4o-mini), and post-cutoff datasets (ICLR 2026, ICML 2025, NeurIPS 2025) show bias spans of 3.601, 3.950, and 2.980, confirming the dispersion pattern is not a memorization artifact. In the post-check simulation, replacing the GPT-4-turbo proxy reviewer with Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct reduces the residual drop from 1.874 to 1.778 points (flip rate 76.5% → 65.0%), showing the corrective effect is not a same-model-family artifact. For borderline citation outcomes, the anchor regime outperforms the reviewer regime in every score band across both years (e.g., ICLR 2023 band 5–6: 44.0 vs. 22.7 mean citations, +94%), and this advantage persists under the older-model leakage control (gpt-3.5-turbo-1106: +91.2% in the same band).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of peer-review inconsistency research (Cortes & Lawrence, 2021; Beygelzimer et al., 2023; Goldberg et al., 2025) and the LLM-as-judge literature (Zheng et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023), but deliberately departs from both. Rather than using LLMs to predict decisions or generate reviews (as in PeerRead-style work or LLM-as-reviewer systems), it repurposes the LLM-as-judge paradigm as a coherence auditor: the model maps a reviewer's own written rationale to an anchor score under a fixed rubric, and the residual becomes a triage signal. The design principles draw on human-centered AI guidelines (Shneiderman, 2020; Amershi et al., 2019) and the commensuration-bias framing from Goldberg et al. (2025), but the specific formulation—residual-triggered post-checks as a lightweight, selective intervention rather than a global scoring replacement—is novel within the peer-review intervention literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete, growing pain point in AI conference operations: as submission volumes triple from ICLR 2023 to 2025, rationale-score misalignment worsens (bias span increases, extreme-bias ratio jumps from ~33% to ~45%), making scores an increasingly unreliable coordination signal near decision boundaries. The main open question is whether the residual-triggered post-check works with real human reviewers under real incentives—the current evidence is an offline LLM simulation, and the authors explicitly flag this as the critical next step. Broader implications include a governance template (prompt pinning, leakage controls, shadow-audit-first adoption) that could generalize to other LLM-auditing applications, but the citation-based borderline evidence remains correlational and the system's resistance to strategic gaming (vague rationales, author-side optimization) is only partially addressed.
