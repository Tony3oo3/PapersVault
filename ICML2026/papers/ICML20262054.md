## Problem

Existing fairness-aware PageRank methods either cannot guarantee that a target group-fairness level is actually achieved, or lack optimality guarantees for the resulting PR vector. No principled optimization framework exists for computing fair PR vectors that provably satisfy a target fairness requirement across multiple (>2) vertex subgroups, and prior work has focused almost exclusively on a single fairness notion (φ-fairness) without exploring alternatives.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262054/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a (un)directed connected graph, a teleportation probability γ ∈ (0,1), a teleportation vector v (default: uniform), and a fairness constraint set X ⊂ Δₙ defined by a partition of vertices into K groups with target parameters (φ_k for group mass, α_k for minimum per-vertex score). The output is a fair PageRank vector x* that is a valid probability distribution over all n vertices, guaranteed to lie in X. The framework is plug-and-play: swapping the FAIR-PROJECTION subroutine handles different fairness criteria without modifying the core iteration, and all projections run in O(n) time, preserving the O(m+n) per-iteration cost of standard PR.

## Method

The key insight is a variational reformulation of PageRank as the unique minimizer of a strongly convex quadratic objective involving the random walk Laplacian Π⁻¹L_rw and a teleportation proximity term, which is equivalent to label spreading on the normalized adjacency matrix. Fairness is enforced by adding a closed convex constraint set X to this objective, yielding a strongly convex constrained QP whose unique minimizer is the fair PR vector. The FairRARI algorithm solves this via fixed-point iterations: each step performs a standard PR update (y = (1−γ)Px + γv) followed by Euclidean projection onto X, and provably converges at geometric rate (1−γ)^t to the unique solution. Three fairness criteria are instantiated—φ-sum-fairness (group mass equals target), α-min-fairness (minimum score per vertex in a designated subset, motivated by Rawlsian justice), and their combination—each admitting a linear-time projection computed via independent bisection on per-group dual variables. The framework subsumes the φ-fairness of Tsioutsiouliklis et al. (2021) as a special case and extends naturally to K > 2 groups, while the post-processing baseline is shown to produce coarser, graph-agnostic modifications that can zero out large fractions of vertices.

## Results

FairRARI consistently achieves TV distance values very close to the post-processing lower bound across all tested datasets (Deezer, Github, DBLP-G, TwitchDE-2), while prior methods (FSPR, LFPR variants) incur substantially higher TV, typically around 0.5 when the target fairness level φ equals the original PR's natural fairness level φ_o (where FairRARI attains exactly zero TV). In terms of ranking preservation, FairRARI achieves the highest Kendall Tau correlation with the original PR ranking across most fairness levels and datasets. Computationally, FairRARI incurs at most a 10% increase in runtime over standard PR, whereas prior in-processing methods can exceed 5000% overhead. In the multi-group (K=4) setting on TwitchDE, FairRARI achieves lower TV than prior methods even on the easier 2-group TwitchDE-2 dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares FairRARI against FSPR and the LFPR family (LFPR_N, LFPR_U, LFPR_P, LFPR_O) from Tsioutsiouliklis et al. (2021), as well as a post-processing projection baseline used as a utility lower bound. FairRARI wins decisively on both TV distance and Kendall Tau across all datasets and fairness levels: when φ = φ_o, FairRARI achieves zero TV (the original PR already satisfies the constraint) while FSPR and LFPR methods still incur TV ≈ 0.5, indicating they unnecessarily perturb the PR vector. On the φ-sum + α-min fairness criterion, FairRARI achieves TV close to the post-processing bound, and on TwitchDE-2 it even outperforms prior methods enforcing the simpler φ-sum-only constraint. The post-processing baseline, while achieving the smallest possible TV (by construction), produces qualitatively coarser solutions—zeroing out 75% of vertices in one group on POLBOOKS—whereas FairRARI assigns positive scores to all vertices. FairWalk, CrossWalk, FairGD, and AdaptGD were excluded from direct comparison because they substantially violate the target fairness level and thus cannot be fairly evaluated on utility.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the original PageRank fixed-point iteration (Brin & Page, 1998) and the fairness-aware PageRank line initiated by Tsioutsiouliklis et al. (2021), who introduced φ-fairness for two groups via teleportation-vector and transition-matrix modifications (FSPR, LFPR). The key departure is a variational reformulation of PR as the minimizer of a strongly convex quadratic involving the random-walk Laplacian, which enables fairness constraints to be imposed as a convex set and solved via a simple PR-step-plus-projection fixed-point iteration. Unlike prior methods that heuristically modify graph structure or transition probabilities without optimality guarantees, FairRARI provides a unified in-processing framework with convergence and optimality guarantees, and extends the two-group φ-fairness to arbitrary K groups and two new criteria (α-min-fairness and their combination).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FairRARI is the first framework to guarantee a target group-fairness level for more than two vertex subgroups in PageRank while retaining the original algorithm's asymptotic complexity, filling a clear gap identified in the literature. The introduction of α-min-fairness addresses a practical pathology of pure sum-fairness (vertices receiving zero scores), and the combined criterion offers fine-grained control over score allocation. Limitations include the focus on group-fairness only (no individual or popularity fairness), the time-reversibility assumption needed for directed graphs (though empirically mild), and the restriction to the PR objective rather than other centrality measures. Open questions include extending the projection-based framework to other graph ranking algorithms and to fairness notions beyond group-level constraints.
