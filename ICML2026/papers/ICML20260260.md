## Problem

DPO-aligned models inherit the biases of their SFT reference model: even the analytically optimal DPO policy can favor a lower-reward completion over a higher-reward one when the SFT prior strongly disfavors the preferred output. Standard decoding (greedy or sampling) underutilizes the alignment signal embedded in the DPO-SFT probability gap, and existing inference-time alignment methods typically require auxiliary reward models, complex search, or carefully tuned hyperparameters. The paper asks whether the DPO model itself, paired with its SFT predecessor, can serve as a sufficient token-level reward function for improved decoding without any additional training.

## Input / Output

At inference, AAD consumes a text prompt x and two model checkpoints: the DPO-aligned model π_DPO and the SFT model π_SFT from which it was trained. At each decoding step it performs two forward passes (one per model) to compute per-token log-likelihood ratios, then selects the next token from a min-α filtered candidate set (α=0.1 by default). Beyond producing aligned completions, AAD can generate synthetic preference pairs (AAD-generated chosen samples vs. nucleus-sampled rejected samples) that feed back into iterative DPO training, enabling alignment improvements under data scarcity without additional labeled data.

## Method

AAD treats the log-likelihood ratio log(π_DPO(v|x∘y_{1:t}) / π_SFT(v|x∘y_{1:t})) as a token-level advantage function, grounded in the Q-function interpretation of DPO where this ratio approximates the KL-constrained MDP advantage r_H(s_t,a_t) + V*(s_{t+1}) − V*(s_t). To prevent degenerate outputs from unreliable ratios on low-probability tokens, AAD applies min-α filtering: only tokens whose π_DPO probability exceeds α times the maximum π_DPO probability at that step are eligible, and the argmax of the advantage is taken over this restricted set. This mechanism is analogous to contrastive decoding but uses the SFT model (rather than a smaller 'amateur' model) as the reference, which the paper shows is critical for alignment gains. No additional training, reward model, or search procedure is required; the method operates purely at inference with a single hyperparameter α that transfers across datasets and model scales without per-task tuning.

## Results

Across 6 preference datasets (Ultrafeedback, Argilla, OpenRLHF Mixture, HHRLHF, Skywork, Nectar) and 4 model scales (Llama 3B/8B, Qwen 0.6B/4B), AAD consistently achieves the highest oracle reward (R) over all baselines. For example, on Skywork with Llama 8B, AAD reaches R=19.27 versus 15.57 (EFT), 14.15 (Bo2), 13.64 (Greedy DPO), and 13.25 (Greedy SFT). On the external-evaluation datasets (Skywork, Nectar), AAD still leads: R=−0.01 vs. −1.88 (EFT) on Skywork/Qwen 0.6B, and R=3.70 vs. 3.30 (EFT) on Nectar/Llama 8B. AAD also wins 58.7–73.5% of pairwise human judgments (40 raters, 502 comparisons) and reaches an Elo of 1610.1, ranking first by 85.7 points over the next baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AAD is compared against four baselines: Greedy SFT, Greedy DPO, Bo2 (best-of-2 with a picker reward model), and EFT (Mitchell et al., 2024). AAD outperforms EFT on every dataset and model scale in Table 1; the margin is largest on Skywork (e.g., R=19.27 vs. 15.57 for Llama 8B) and smallest on Nectar (R=3.70 vs. 3.30), but is never negative. Against contrastive decoding (Li et al., 2023b), which subtracts logits from a weaker 'amateur' model, AAD achieves substantially higher rewards: on Argilla, Skywork, and Nectar with Llama-8B-DPO, AAD scores R=5.9, 19.27, and 3.7 respectively, versus 2.79/13.08/2.65 for CD-1B-Instruct and 2.46/10.88/2.51 for CD-3B-Instruct (Table 3), confirming that the SFT reference model is critical rather than any generic weaker model. AAD also beats Bo2 under data scarcity (Figure 4), maintaining a win rate of ~0.70–0.80 even when trained on only 10% of Skywork preference data, whereas Bo2 degrades more sharply. In the iterative-DPO setting, AAD-generated synthetic data nearly closes the gap with a model trained on 100% of the data using only 10% of the original preference pairs (Figure 6).

## Relation to Prior Work

AAD sits at the intersection of DPO's implicit reward interpretation (Rafailov et al., 2023) and the Q-function credit-assignment framework of Rafailov et al. (2024b), repurposing the DPO–SFT log-likelihood ratio as a token-level decoding score. Mechanically, it borrows the min-α filtering idea from contrastive decoding (Li et al., 2023b) but applies it to the DPO probability rather than an external 'amateur' model. Conceptually, it departs from emulated fine-tuning (Mitchell et al., 2024) and energy-based decoding by requiring only the two models already present in a standard DPO pipeline, eliminating the need for a third unaligned base model or a separate reward model at inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AAD offers a drop-in inference-time improvement that requires no retraining and only two forward passes per token, making it attractive for practitioners who have already invested in a DPO pipeline but cannot afford further training. The data-efficiency result—using AAD-generated synthetic completions in iterative DPO to nearly match full-data performance from just 10% of preference pairs—addresses a real bottleneck in alignment where labeled preferences are scarce. Key limitations include the requirement to retain the SFT checkpoint, the restriction to DPO-trained models (PPO combinations yield mixed results), and the 2× inference cost; open directions include adaptive token filtering, integration with search, and extension beyond text.
