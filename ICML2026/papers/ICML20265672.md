## Problem

Chain-of-thought (CoT) monitoring is a promising approach for detecting misalignment in reasoning models, but its effectiveness depends on models being unable to strategically suppress or reshape their reasoning traces. No prior work had systematically quantified this risk—termed CoT controllability—across frontier models, nor identified which training and inference factors increase or decrease it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265672/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each test instance pairs a benchmark question (from GPQA, MMLU-Pro, HLE, BFCL, or SWE-Bench) with a CoT controllability instruction appended to the user message, constraining what may or may not appear in the model's reasoning trace (e.g., suppress a keyword, insert a string between every word, use only uppercase letters). The model's full reasoning trace is then scored via controllability-type-specific programmatic graders (validated by LLM judges), producing two metrics: Compliance Rate (fraction of examples fully satisfying the constraint) and Meta Discussion Rate (fraction of compliant examples that reference the constraint itself, which a monitor could trivially flag).

## Method

The paper introduces CoT-Control, a 13,176-instance evaluation suite spanning three threat-relevant controllability categories (information suppression, information addition, stylistic modification) at three difficulty levels each, with sanity checks to separate controllability failures from instruction misunderstanding. Beyond the core benchmark, the authors stress-test controllability under adversarial conditions: situational-awareness prompts (telling models they are monitored or evaluated), GEPA-like automated red-teaming to discover pressure prompts that maximize controllability, and repeated sampling (pass@k) to probe the long tail of elicitability. They then use logistic regression to decompose controllability as a function of task difficulty (CoT necessity and per-question accuracy), reasoning effort, reasoning length, question domain, and RLVR training pressure, tracking changes across RL training checkpoints of Olmo-3-7B and comparing before/after reasoning training across four model families.

## Results

Across 12 frontier reasoning models, CoT controllability is generally below 10%, with DeepSeek-R1 as low as 0.2% on keyword suppression and Claude-Sonnet-4.5 at only 5% on a genetics keyword-suppression task. By contrast, the same models achieve ≥60% compliance when controlling their final outputs and ≥70% when controlling external reasoning in <output_reasoning> tags (≥57% for non-reasoning models). Within open-weight families, larger models show higher controllability (GPT-OSS-120B: 5.9% vs. 20B: 0.9%; Qwen3-32B: 2.3% vs. 14B: 0.6%), while RLVR training reduces CoT controllability by over an order of magnitude (e.g., Olmo-3.1-7B-RL-Zero-Code) with output controllability declining much more slowly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CoT controllability against three baselines: (1) output controllability (controlling the final response), (2) external-CoT controllability on reasoning models (controlling reasoning wrapped in <output_reasoning> tags), and (3) external-CoT controllability on non-reasoning models. CoT controllability is dramatically lower than all three baselines: most reasoning models stay below 10% on CoT control while exceeding 60% on output control and 70% on external reasoning control (Figure 19). The gap is most extreme for DeepSeek-R1 (0.2% CoT vs. well above 60% output) and Claude-Sonnet-4.5 (0.0–15.6% CoT vs. >60% output). Among the three CoT controllability types, suppression is the easiest (8 of 12 models achieve their highest compliance here), while addition and stylistic modification are harder, suggesting models can more readily omit information than insert or reformat it. Situational awareness (telling models they are being monitored) increases controllability by up to 4 percentage points with statistical significance, but adversarial prompt optimization via GEPA does not reliably transfer to unseen instruction types, and repeated sampling (pass@10) only modestly raises compliance for newer Claude models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by defining 'CoT controllability' as a distinct, measurable capability and introducing the first systematic benchmark (CoT-Control) for it. It builds conceptually on the CoT monitoring literature (Korbak et al., 2025; Baker et al., 2025; Emmons et al., 2025), which established that reasoning traces can expose misalignment, but shifts the question from 'is CoT informative?' to 'can models suppress or reshape CoT to evade monitors?' The authors sidestep the intractable problem of measuring evasion intent directly by using instruction-following in the CoT as a proxy, drawing on the broader instruction-following evaluation tradition but applying it to an unexplored target (the reasoning trace rather than the output). Stress-testing methods borrow from situational-awareness probing (Berglund et al., 2023; Needham et al., 2025) and automated red-teaming (Perez et al., 2022; Agrawal et al., 2025), while the RLVR analysis connects to the growing literature on how outcome-based reinforcement learning shapes reasoning behavior (Wen et al., 2025; DeepSeek-AI, 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CoT monitoring is increasingly positioned as a primary oversight mechanism for reasoning models, yet this paper shows the mechanism rests on an untested assumption: that models cannot control their reasoning traces. The finding that controllability is currently low (often <10%) but scales with model size, decreases with RLVR training, and is sensitive to situational awareness provides a concrete risk trajectory that frontier labs must track. Key open questions remain: the mechanism by which RLVR erodes CoT controllability is unexplained, whether controllability will remain low as models scale further is unknown, and the benchmark's proxy-based design (instruction-following as a stand-in for adversarial evasion) may not capture all real-world evasion strategies. The paper's recommendation that labs continuously monitor controllability alongside monitorability is likely to become a standard practice if the capability trend continues.
