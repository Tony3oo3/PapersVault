## Problem

Current LLMs are trained to elicit and execute fully formed user intents through clarification questions, but in open-ended creative tasks users often have not yet formed their intents and cannot articulate what they want. This paper formalizes 'intent discovery' as a distinct problem from intent elicitation—where users must observe and explore model-generated options to progressively concretize their preferences—and proposes a training framework to teach models to support this process.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262135/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes multi-turn conversation history and user messages as input, where the user simulator maintains a latent intent hierarchy (a tree of requirements from abstract to specific) and constrains the user to only articulate intents that have been discovered through prior assistant responses. The model produces assistant responses that either diverge (offering multiple options to probe undiscovered intents) or converge (refining a single artifact to satisfy discovered intents), culminating in a final complete artifact (story, article, or SVG drawing). Intent trees are automatically constructed from existing artifacts via LLM-based abstraction, yielding an average of ~27 intents per artifact across ~5.5 independent trees.

## Method

DISCOVERLLM introduces a user simulator that models cognitive state as a hierarchy of intents with three discovery states (undiscovered, emerging, discovered), where assistant responses trigger state transitions through direct engagement (explicitly probing or satisfying an intent) or tangential engagement (cumulative exposure to related alternatives exceeding a random threshold). A per-turn reward combines discovery progress (count of newly discovered intents) with a capped efficiency penalty on response length, enabling training via SFT on synthesized conversations, offline DPO on pairwise preferences, and online GRPO. Applied with LoRA to Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B, the framework teaches models to adaptively balance divergence and convergence—SFT alone overfits toward always-diverging behavior, while DPO following SFT encourages the balanced pattern of exploring when intents are vague and refining when they concretize, yielding ~10% higher discovery scores and up to 40% shorter conversations.

## Results

Across three open-ended tasks (creative writing, technical writing, SVG drawing) and two base models (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen3-8B), the best DISCOVERLLM variant (SFT+DPO or SFT+DPO+GRPO) achieves 10–14% higher Intent Discovery scores and 9–14% higher Intent Satisfaction scores over the best baseline, while reducing average conversation length by 11–40% (e.g., 45.2 vs. 39.0 Discovery and 2.05k vs. 3.01k tokens for Qwen on Creative Writing). Interactivity scores (ITR) improve by 27–227% over baselines. In a 75-participant user study, DISCOVERLLM achieved 84% high-satisfaction ratings (vs. 80% Base, 72% Prompted Base) with lower variance in task completion time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the unmodified base models (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen3-8B), DISCOVERLLM consistently wins on Intent Discovery (e.g., 51.6 vs. 45.6 for Llama on SVG; 45.2 vs. 35.2 for Qwen on Creative Writing) and Intent Satisfaction, while reducing token counts by 11–40%. Against Prompted Base, results are mixed: for Qwen, prompting already improves Discovery (35.2→39.0 on Creative Writing) but DISCOVERLLM still leads (45.2) with fewer tokens (2.05k vs. 3.01k); for Llama, prompting actually degrades performance (45.6→40.0 on SVG) because the prompted model asks clarifying questions the simulated user cannot answer, whereas DISCOVERLLM provides concrete options. Against CollabLLM (Wu et al., 2025), which is trained to ask clarifying questions for fully-formed but unexpressed intents, DISCOVERLLM achieves higher Discovery (e.g., 42.4 vs. 37.3 for Llama on Creative Writing; 48.2 vs. 45.8 on Technical Writing) and substantially higher ITR scores (e.g., 31.3 vs. 24.9), confirming that the intent-discovery setting requires a fundamentally different strategy than intent elicitation. On the independent CollabLLM evaluation benchmark, DISCOVERLLM also achieves the best BLEU (0.274, +14.6% over next-best) and ITR (58.0, +20.8%), and on AmbigNQ it matches or exceeds all baselines on both Ambig F1 and Answer Recall.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant multi-turn dialogue training paradigm (CollabLLM, PPP, and clarification-focused fine-tuning) that assumes users possess fully formed but unarticulated intents, which the model can surface through clarifying questions. Instead, it draws on cognitive process theories of design and writing (Flower & Hayes, 1981; Schön, 2017; Dorst & Cross, 2001) to formalize a fundamentally different problem—intent discovery—where the user's goals are not yet formed and must be concretized through the assistant's exploratory responses. The user simulator with a hierarchical intent structure and progressive discovery states is a novel operationalization; prior simulators (Kong et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025) model users with hidden but static goals. The training pipeline itself (SFT, DPO, GRPO with LoRA) is standard, but the reward signal derived from discovery progress in the simulator is the key methodological contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper shifts the conceptual framing of human-AI collaboration from 'execute and elicit' to 'explore and co-discover,' which is arguably more aligned with how people actually approach open-ended creative tasks. The 75-participant user study confirms that simulated gains transfer to real users, and the generalization to unseen domains (travel planning, web development, etc.) suggests the learned collaborative behavior is not task-specific. Key limitations include the monotonic refinement assumption (validated at 94.7% compliance but still a simplification), the single-dimension focus constraint, and reliance on LLM-based simulators that may not capture the full diversity of human intent formation. Open questions include whether the framework can handle genuine backtracking, how to improve diversity in the model's exploratory options (a noted weakness in user feedback), and whether the collaborative exploration disposition increases susceptibility to safety attacks under ambiguous adversarial prompts.
