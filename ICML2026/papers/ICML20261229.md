## Problem

Estimating heterogeneous treatment effects in industrial settings faces a fundamental asymmetry: randomized controlled trials provide unbiased causal signals but are expensive and budget-limited, while observational data are abundant yet suffer from policy-induced selection bias, near-deterministic targeting, and overlap violations. Existing active learning approaches study either budgeted sampling within RCTs or label acquisition within observational studies, but the practically critical regime where abundant biased observational logs coexist with a small, adaptively collected RCT has received little theoretical and methodological attention, particularly regarding how observational data should guide adaptive experiment design while retaining finite-sample validity.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two data sources: a large observational log with covariates, historical treatment assignments, and outcomes (potentially near-deterministic in propensity), and an unlabeled candidate pool of covariates drawn from the target population. The output is a CATE estimator identified exclusively by the adaptively collected RCT samples (via pseudo-outcome ridge regression or a neural CATE learner), along with the sequence of selected units and their randomized treatment assignments. A key structural constraint is that observational data never enters the identifying estimating equations—it only shapes the representation map and the acquisition policy—so the estimator remains robust to arbitrary confounding in the observational source. Randomization probabilities are covariate-dependent and clipped to [f_min, f_max] to enforce positivity.

## Method

The framework cleanly separates experiment design from causal identification: observational data informs where to randomize (via a shared MLP encoder, a domain classifier, and a propensity model), while the final CATE estimator is identified purely by randomized experiments. At each round, a rank-normalized multi-criteria acquisition score combines three signals—epistemic uncertainty (ensemble CATE disagreement or ridge leverage score), domain discrepancy (sigmoid output of a pool-vs-training-set classifier acting as a density-ratio estimator), and overlap deficit (distance of the estimated observational propensity from 0.5)—to select the top-m most informative units for RCT enrollment. The estimator regresses inverse-propensity-weighted pseudo-outcomes onto the learned representation via ridge regression, and the paper proves that randomization induces a martingale difference structure preserving unbiasedness despite adaptive, non-i.i.d. selection, yielding finite-sample self-normalized deviation bounds, asymptotic normality via a martingale CLT, and a minimax lower bound establishing that the √(d/B) rate is information-theoretically optimal up to logarithmic factors.

## Results

On the industrial ride-hailing dataset (468 features, OOT protocol over 44 days), DRCFR with 50k actively sampled RCT units achieves an AUUC of 0.6534, surpassing random sampling with 300k units (AUUC 0.6466), demonstrating roughly 6× sample efficiency. Across all three CATE backbones (DragonNet, DESCN, DRCFR) and RCT budgets from 10k to 500k, active sampling consistently yields higher mean AUUC than random sampling under the biased observational setting. On synthetic benchmarks (d ∈ {20, 50, 100, 150}), √PEHE decreases with budget and increases with dimension, matching the predicted O(√(d/B)) scaling; active sampling achieves lower √PEHE than random sampling in most configurations, with the largest gains at small budgets and high dimensions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between the proposed active sampling strategy and uniform random sampling under identical RCT budgets, evaluated across three CATE model backbones (DragonNet, DESCN, DRCFR). Active sampling wins on AUUC in most budget regimes for all three models, with the most striking result being DRCFR at 50k active samples (AUUC 0.6534) outperforming 300k random samples (AUUC 0.6466). On synthetic benchmarks, active sampling also achieves lower √PEHE than random sampling, particularly when the RCT budget is small relative to the covariate dimension. The paper does not compare against other active learning baselines (e.g., Causal-EPIG, RWAS) in the main experiments; the comparison is strictly active vs. random under the same budget. The gains are most pronounced under distribution shift (biased observational data), where the domain-discrepancy and overlap-deficit signals in the acquisition function provide the largest marginal benefit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the active sampling literature for treatment effect estimation (Zhang et al., 2025; Addanki et al., 2022) by introducing a fundamentally new data regime: abundant but biased observational logs that guide WHERE to randomize, while causal identification is carried exclusively by the adaptively collected RCT samples. Prior work on combining OBS and RCT data (Cheng & Cai, 2021; Hatt et al., 2022) relies on reweighting or doubly robust estimators that require overlap assumptions the paper explicitly avoids; prior active learning for observational studies (Wen et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025) assumes treatment is pre-assigned and the budget is for labeling, not for running experiments. The key conceptual departure is the clean separation of roles—OBS shapes the design policy and representation, but never enters the identifying estimating equations—making the framework robust to arbitrary confounding in the observational source.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, theoretically grounded recipe for industrial teams that have large biased logs but only a small RCT budget: use OBS to diagnose uncertainty, overlap deficits, and covariate shift, then direct limited randomization to the most informative regions. The minimax lower bound clarifies an important limitation—active sampling improves finite-sample constants through better design conditioning but cannot beat the √(d/B) rate—tempering expectations about what adaptivity can achieve. Open questions include extending the theory beyond the linear-head analysis to nonparametric and deep function classes, handling multi-source settings with shifting covariates and treatment support over time, and incorporating operational constraints into the acquisition function. The 6× sample-efficiency gain on DiDi ride-hailing data (50k active vs. 300k random) suggests practical relevance for cost-sensitive deployment.
