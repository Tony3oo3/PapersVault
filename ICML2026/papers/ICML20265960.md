## Problem

Existing fairness-utility trade-off research either proves qualitative impossibility at feasibility boundaries, maps Pareto frontiers as a black box, or attributes conflicts to data bias under strong causal-graph assumptions. What is missing is a quantitative, mechanistic framework that measures the degree of structural compatibility between any pair of fairness and utility metrics and explains which specific attribute interactions drive that compatibility, without requiring causal graphs or unbiased labels.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a trained binary classifier v(x), a sampled test set (500 instances for computational tractability), and a set of 9 metrics (SPD, EOD, PED, AOD, GIFVR, CFVR, Accuracy, Recall, FPR) expressed as continuous linear proxies over predicted probabilities. It produces, for each metric, an interaction vector in R^{2^{|N|}} whose components are per-coalition expected contributions, a pairwise compatibility score in [-1,1] (cosine similarity), and a per-coalition attribution table identifying which specific attribute subsets drive synergy or conflict. Non-linear metrics (precision, F1, MCC, ROC-AUC) are excluded from decomposition but can be used for post-hoc validation.

## Method

The core mechanism exploits the linearity of continuous metric proxies (linear expectations of v(x)) to push the Harsanyi interaction decomposition through the expectation operator, yielding a faithful additive decomposition of each metric into a vector of attribute-coalition contributions I_m(C). Compatibility between two metrics is then defined as the cosine similarity of their interaction vectors, which is bounded in [-1,1], scale-invariant, and decomposable into per-coalition product terms that enable fine-grained attribution. A key structural result (Proposition 3.2) shows that under global baseline masking, the CFVR vector is non-zero only for coalitions containing at least one sensitive attribute, isolating the causal-discrimination signal. The framework is validated across 6 datasets, 7 model architectures, and 6 debiasing methods (2,700+ configurations), revealing that fairness-utility metrics are predominantly orthogonal (median C ≈ 0) rather than diametrically opposed, with conflicts driven by sparse low-order coalitions and dataset-level imbalance (empty-set contributions).

## Results

Across 6 datasets, 7 models, and 10 seeds (420 configurations), fairness-utility metric pairs exhibit structural orthogonality with median compatibility C ∈ [−0.1, 0] rather than diametric opposition (C ≈ −1), while utility-utility pairs show near-perfect alignment (C > 0.9). Debiasing compresses the overall compatibility space: Fair-SMOTE reduces average compatibility by 44.2% (0.2518 → 0.1405) versus MAAT's gentler 11.87% reduction (0.2518 → 0.2219). Group-Individual fairness synergy drops sharply under aggressive debiasing (GF-IF: 0.41 → 0.1271 for Fair-SMOTE), and the empty coalition (dataset imbalance) dominates compatibility attribution in pairs like Accuracy-AOD (−476.66% contribution on Census Income).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five debiasing methods (Reweighing, Flipping, Blindness, Fair-SMOTE, MAAT) against Vanilla models using the proposed compatibility framework. MAAT best preserves the overall compatibility structure (ALL: 0.2219 vs. Vanilla 0.2518, only 11.87% reduction) while still improving group fairness (AOD: 0.3483 → 0.2686) with minimal accuracy loss (0.7692 → 0.7687), and even increases U-U synergy (0.7454 → 0.7823). Fair-SMOTE achieves the largest fairness gains (AOD: 0.3483 → 0.1956, CFVR: 0.2625 → 0.1400) but at the highest compatibility cost (44.2% overall reduction; U-U drops from 0.7454 to 0.5683) and the largest accuracy drop (0.7692 → 0.7207). Blindness eliminates CFVR entirely (0.2625 → 0.0000) but collapses IF-IF compatibility to 0.0000, indicating it severs the structural link between individual fairness notions. Reweighing and Flipping occupy intermediate positions, with Flipping showing the weakest IF-IF synergy (0.1692) among the gentler methods. The key finding is that no method eliminates fairness-utility conflict (GF-U becomes more negative for all methods); debiasing redistributes rather than removes structural tension.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends HIFI (Zhang et al., 2025), which applied Harsanyi interactions to decompose individual group fairness metrics, into a unified framework that measures pairwise compatibility across diverse fairness and utility metrics. It departs from impossibility theorems (Chouldechova, 2017; Kleinberg et al., 2017) that certify incompatibility only at boundary conditions, and from Pareto frontier approaches (Agarwal et al., 2018; Taufiq et al., 2024) that map trade-offs empirically without mechanistic attribution. The key conceptual departure is replacing causal-graph-based explanations (Kusner et al., 2017; Plecko & Bareinboim, 2025) with an oracle-free, interaction-vector-based measure of structural alignment that attributes conflicts to specific attribute coalitions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides practitioners with a quantitative, interpretable diagnostic for understanding why specific fairness and utility metrics conflict in their particular data-model setting, without requiring causal assumptions. The finding that debiasing compresses the entire compatibility space (reducing both synergies and conflicts) rather than selectively eliminating conflicts offers a mechanistic explanation for why aggressive methods like Fair-SMOTE sacrifice more alignment than gentle methods like MAAT. Key limitations include restriction to linear metrics (excluding precision, F1, MCC, ROC-AUC), tabular data only, and the demonstrated context-specificity (predictive models fail with negative R²), meaning practitioners cannot bypass per-application empirical evaluation. Open questions include extending to high-dimensional/unstructured data and formalizing the relationship between compatibility dynamics and Pareto frontier geometry.
