## Problem

The EU AI Act's research exemptions were drafted without accounting for the standard practice of publishing models and code alongside papers at major AI conferences (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR). The paper argues that the act of uploading research artifacts to public repositories like GitHub or Hugging Face can void the research exceptions and trigger full provider compliance obligations—up to €35M in fines—for researchers who lack the time, budget, and legal expertise to meet them.

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes the full text of Regulation 2024/1689 (including recitals), the Blue Guide on EU product harmonization rules, and documented AI research publication norms as input. The output is structured as a position paper containing a decision flowchart mapping the nested exception system across prohibited, high-risk, and GPAI categories, a research lifecycle diagram identifying the precise point where publication triggers obligations, and concrete legislative revision recommendations.

## Method

The authors deploy legal doctrinal analysis, systematically interpreting each research exception (scientific research under Art. 2(6), product-oriented R&D under Art. 2(8), open-source under Art. 2(12), and GPAI-specific provisions under Art. 3(63) and Rec. 97) against the actual mechanics of AI research publication. They identify a critical structural gap: the exceptions cover 'putting into service' for research purposes but not 'making available on the market,' which is what uploading to public repositories constitutes under Art. 3(10), and they use the Blue Guide to argue that even free-of-charge, non-commercial publication qualifies as market placement. They further demonstrate that the narrow 'sole purpose' requirement in Rec. 25 excludes any system that could be used for product-oriented development, and that the open-source exception does not extend to high-risk systems or GPAI models with systemic risks. The paper constructs a visual decision flowchart to help researchers navigate the layered exception system and proposes three interpretive strategies (publishing models rather than systems, purpose-limitation labeling, and license-based usage restrictions) alongside calls for authoritative Commission guidance or legislative revision.

## Results

This is a position paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative results. The paper's central finding is qualitative: the EU AI Act's research exceptions (Art. 2(6), 2(8), 2(12)) are misaligned with standard AI publication practices (uploading models to GitHub, Hugging Face, Colab), meaning that the act of publishing research artifacts at conferences like NeurIPS, ICML, or ICLR can void the research exemptions and trigger full provider compliance obligations. The authors estimate a >50% probability that the AI Act will materially affect research, drawing a parallel to the GDPR's gradual impact on biomedical and data-science research. Non-compliance carries fines up to €35,000,000 (Art. 99(3)) for prohibited systems and €15,000,000 (Art. 99(4)) for high-risk system violations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not benchmark against empirical baselines. Instead, it engages with four alternative legal interpretations and argues against each: (1) the freedom-of-science argument (Art. 13 CFREU) — the authors counter that the Act explicitly carves out research exceptions, so the absence of a publication-specific carve-out does not imply protection; (2) the non-commercial-activity argument (Art. 3(10)) — the authors argue for an expansive reading of 'commercial activity' that includes professional research, consistent with the Act's obligations extending to deployers and public bodies; (3) the product-safety-law framing — the authors note that AI systems 'may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment' (Art. 3(1)), making it impossible to draw a clean line between a 'finished product' and a research output; and (4) the argument that regulating AI research is desirable — the authors concede this for genuinely dangerous systems but argue the Act's compliance machinery (risk management, data governance, documentation) is tailored for product manufacturers, not for filtering research outputs. The paper's position is that none of these alternative readings adequately protects the standard practice of publishing code and models alongside conference papers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the growing body of legal scholarship analyzing the EU AI Act's research exceptions (Colonna 2023; Smuha et al. 2021; Kazim et al. 2023; Helberger et al. 2024), but departs from them by specifically examining how standard ML publication practices—uploading models and code to GitHub, Hugging Face, or Colab—interact with those exceptions. The authors note that even the most in-depth prior study (Colonna 2023) gives limited attention to publication-triggered compliance, positioning their contribution as filling that gap. Methodologically, the paper draws on doctrinal legal interpretation (Möllers 2020) and the Brussels effect framework (Bradford 2020) to argue for extraterritorial relevance beyond the EU.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a potentially significant regulatory gap: the act of publishing research artifacts, a core norm at venues like NeurIPS, ICML, and ICLR, may void the AI Act's research exemptions and trigger full provider compliance obligations (risk management, documentation, data governance) with fines up to €35 million. This matters because it could create a chilling effect on open publication, particularly for researchers at commercial companies whose legal departments will err on the side of caution. Open questions include whether the EU's 'Digital Omnibus' proposal will clarify the research exceptions, how the GPAI Code of Practice will be finalized, and whether the Brussels effect will extend these compliance concerns to non-EU researchers. The paper's practical contributions—a navigational flowchart and a high-level compliance roadmap—aim to reduce legal uncertainty in the interim.
