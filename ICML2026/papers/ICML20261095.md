## Problem

Reward models (RMs) used for RLHF alignment remain vulnerable to reward hacking from spurious biases, yet prior work has not systematically measured which biases persist in state-of-the-art RMs nor established which are amenable to lightweight post-hoc correction. This paper identifies both persistent (length, overconfidence, sycophancy) and novel (position, model-style sensitivity) biases across five contemporary RMs, and introduces an empirical taxonomy distinguishing low-complexity biases tractable to linear intervention from high-complexity biases that resist it.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a prompt-completion pair processed through the RM's base transformer, extracting the final-layer hidden state at the last non-padding token (prior to the reward head). Pre-computed orthonormal probe vectors—constructed via difference-of-means from small contrastive datasets (~500 examples per bias type)—are projected out of this hidden state before the reward head produces the scalar debiased reward. A tunable projection strength α (default 1.0) controls intervention magnitude, and multiple probes can be jointly nulled after Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization to address several biases simultaneously.

## Method

The core contribution is mechanistic reward shaping via linear probe null-space projection: for each target bias, a DiffMean probe direction is constructed from contrastive activations (e.g., verbose vs. concise correct answers for length bias), and the RM's hidden state is projected onto the orthogonal complement of this direction before reward computation. The key insight is an empirical complexity taxonomy—low-complexity biases (length, uncertainty, position) are well-approximated by single linear directions and are effectively mitigated by null-space projection, while high-complexity biases (sycophancy, model-style sensitivity) are entangled with quality signals in activation space and resist linear correction. The approach is model-internal (operating on RM representations without modifying the policy or retraining the RM), data-efficient (probes from ~500 contrastive examples generalize out-of-distribution to RewardBench2 and best-of-N selection), and extensible to new biases by simply constructing additional probes. The paper further validates that interventions preserve RewardBench2 ranking accuracy (non-inferiority at δ=5pp) while reducing targeted bias metrics, and demonstrates OOD transfer of length probes to prompt-conditioned best-of-N candidate selection on AlpacaEval and GSM8K.

## Results

Across five reward models (including the RewardBench2 leader Skywork-Llama-8B), mechanistic reward shaping via DiffMean probe null-space projection significantly reduces length bias (e.g., DeBERTa verbose-correct accuracy drops from 83.7% to 46.6%, closing the gap with concise-correct at 45.8%), uncertainty bias (average penalty drops from 22.6 pp to ~0.5 pp), and position bias (DeBERTa std. dev. across answer positions drops from 27.7% to 4.3% on GSM8K-MC). On RewardBench2, all interventions (length, position, uncertainty, combined) achieve statistical non-inferiority to baseline at a 5 pp margin (mean accuracy 69.3–70.1% vs. 70.1% baseline). In best-of-N selection, the length probe reduces mean absolute within-prompt reward-length correlation from 0.10 to 0.04 and improves GSM8K BoN accuracy from 62.1% to 62.8% across five RMs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary quantitative comparison is against the global length-calibration method of Huang et al. (2025, ICLR). In the best-of-N candidate-selection setting on held-out AlpacaEval and GSM8K prompts (64 candidates per prompt), the mechanistic probe-nulling approach outperforms Huang et al.'s global calibration on both diagnostics: it achieves a lower mean absolute within-prompt reward-length correlation (0.04 vs. higher for the global method) and a higher AlpacaEval length-controlled win rate on four of five RMs. The key advantage is that the probe is prompt-conditioned (operating in the RM's latent space per input), whereas Huang et al.'s method applies a global length penalty that does not transfer well to prompt-conditioned selection. On GSM8K BoN, the probe moves the within-prompt correlation from 0.076 to 0.007 and improves mean accuracy from 62.1% to 62.8%, whereas the global calibration cannot achieve comparable prompt-level correction. The paper also implicitly contrasts with prior length-bias corrections (Park et al. 2024b, Zhao et al. 2025) that model the length-reward relationship explicitly; the probe approach requires no assumption about the functional form of the bias and generalizes out-of-distribution (probes built on GSM8K transfer to RewardBench2 and AlpacaEval).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the linear representation hypothesis and the DiffMean probe construction (Marks & Tegmark, 2024) combined with null-space projection (Ravfogel et al., 2020), repurposing both from interpretability/steering into reward-model debiasing. It departs from prior post-hoc length-bias corrections (Huang et al., 2025; Park et al., 2024b) that model the length-reward relationship explicitly, instead removing length-correlated directions in the RM's latent space without assuming a functional form. The paper also extends the reward-hacking literature by introducing an empirical low/high-complexity taxonomy that distinguishes biases amenable to linear first-order correction from those that resist it, a framing absent from prior work that largely treats all reward hacking as linear spurious correlation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discovery that model-style reward sensitivity is present across all five tested RMs—including state-of-the-art models—has direct implications for RLHF pipeline design, RM-policy pairing decisions, and preference data curation, since dominant model families (Llama, Qwen, Gemma) pervade widely used open-source preference corpora. The practical, data-efficient mechanistic intervention for tractable biases (length, position, uncertainty) offers a deployable tool that works model-internally without modifying policy optimization, applicable to best-of-N, red-teaming, and iterative filtering. Key open questions include whether the taxonomy holds for non-verifiable open-ended tasks, how biases propagate from RMs into policies during RLHF, and whether more sophisticated (non-linear) interventions can address the high-complexity biases (sycophancy, model-style) that resist linear correction.
