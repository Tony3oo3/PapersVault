## Problem

Existing TEE-based weight obfuscation schemes for LLMs on untrusted devices face a fundamental security-effencyity trade-off: direction-preserving transformations (scaling, permutation) are vulnerable to statistical direction-recovery attacks, while direction-hiding schemes (GroupCover, ArrowCloak) require per-layer TEE interaction, incurring prohibitive TEE-GPU transfer overhead across the tens-to-hundreds of layers in modern LLMs. No prior scheme simultaneously provides robust defense against state-of-the-art statistical attacks and near-GPU-level inference speed.

## Input / Output

The obfuscated LLM consumes a sequence of text tokens and produces predicted token indices (for causal language models) or classification labels (for sequence classification heads). For causal LMs with shared embedding weights, the backbone output is recovered inside the TEE, combined with a random One-Time Pad, forwarded to the lm_head on the GPU, and the TEE strips the pad to return only predicted indices—ensuring the transformation matrix Π is never observable in the output. The entire inference pipeline requires exactly one TEE-GPU round-trip at the first decoder block; all subsequent layers propagate the orthogonal state without further TEE involvement.

## Method

SLIM exploits the iterative decoder-block structure of LLMs to let a single orthogonal transformation Π cascade through all layers via a propagation-style authorization: the TEE applies Π once at the first block, and because orthogonal matrices are norm-preserving and invertible, RMSNorm/LayerNorm and residual connections propagate the transformed state algebraically through every subsequent layer. The key mechanism is the T-Way Mixing Algorithm, which constructs Π as a product of block-diagonal Householder matrices (block size k=4) interleaved with random permutations, requiring only O(log_k d) rounds to guarantee every output vector is a linear combination of all original vectors—thoroughly scrambling directional information while keeping TEE-side computation at O(ld) via the Householder factorization XH = X − 2(Xv)vᵀ. For Post-RMSNorm architectures, a branch-and-merge design with an auxiliary random orthogonal matrix U handles the non-propagatable residual path, and the entire scheme is compatible with RoPE, MoE, MLA, and arbitrary activation functions.

## Results

Under the state-of-the-art statistical analysis attack, SLIM's recovered model accuracy drops to levels close to a randomly initialized model (e.g., 40.65% on MNLI vs. 81.27% for SOTER and 81.25% for ShadowNet), while all prior obfuscation schemes are restored to near No-Shield performance. In terms of inference efficiency, SLIM achieves up to a 13.80× TTFT speedup over ArrowCloak and up to 21.31× over GroupCover, with 222.88× and 203.03× lower TEE-GPU transfer overhead respectively. For inference fidelity, SLIM attains 100% Top-1 Token Accuracy in FP32 across all four models and maintains 95.2%–99.5% in FP16, whereas ArrowCloak and GroupCover degrade to 0% in FP16 on three of four models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SOTER, ShadowNet, TransLinkGuard, CoreGuard, and TSQP, SLIM provides substantially stronger defense: after the SOTA statistical analysis attack, these schemes are restored to within 1–3% of No-Shield accuracy (e.g., SOTER recovers to 81.27% on MNLI vs. 82.03% No-Shield), whereas SLIM's recovered accuracy drops to 40.65%, approaching the Random baseline (35.83%). Against the two strongest security baselines, ArrowCloak and GroupCover, SLIM matches or exceeds their security guarantees while dramatically outperforming them on efficiency: ArrowCloak incurs 4.49×–13.80× higher TTFT and GroupCover incurs 5.68×–21.31× higher TTFT relative to SLIM. On inference accuracy, SLIM achieves 100% Top-1 Token Accuracy in FP32 (matching or exceeding all baselines) and 95.2%–99.5% in FP16, while ArrowCloak collapses to 0% in FP16 on Gemma3, LLaMA3.2, and Qwen3, and GroupCover drops to 0% on the same three models. SLIM also supports a broader range of LLM architectures (QK-Norm, RoPE, SwiGLU, Gated Attention, MLA) compared to ArrowCloak and GroupCover, which lack support for several of these components.

## Relation to Prior Work

SLIM builds directly on CoreGuard's propagation-style authorization paradigm, where a single TEE authorization is propagated through all subsequent layers without repeated TEE-GPU round trips. However, it departs fundamentally from CoreGuard's permutation-based transformation by replacing it with a direction-hiding orthogonal mixing scheme (block-diagonal Householder matrices combined with random permutations), addressing the vulnerability to statistical direction-recovery attacks identified by GroupCover and ArrowCloak. The T-Way Mixing Algorithm is the key technical novelty: it achieves full inter-vector coverage in O(log_k d) rounds while keeping TEE-side computation at O(ld) per round via the Householder factorization, bridging the security-efficiency gap that per-layer schemes like ArrowCloak and GroupCover could not close.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SLIM resolves the central tension in TEE-based LLM protection—strong directional obfuscation versus minimal TEE interaction—by making orthogonal transformations propagation-compatible with RMSNorm/LayerNorm, reducing the entire inference to a single TEE authorization. The 13.80× TTFT speedup over ArrowCloak and the 'Random-level' defense against the state-of-the-art statistical attack make this practically deployable for edge and cloud LLM serving. Key open questions include: (1) compatibility with INT8/INT4 quantization, which the authors explicitly do not support; (2) whether the non-cryptographic security guarantees (based on underdetermined systems and empirical attack resistance) suffice against future, more sophisticated recovery attacks; and (3) whether the FP16 sensitivity (95–99.5% Top-1 accuracy vs. 100% in FP32) becomes problematic for production low-precision pipelines.
