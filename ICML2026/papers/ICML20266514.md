## Problem

Fairness findings in generative models are systematically non-cumulative: two papers evaluating the same model can reach opposite conclusions because evaluation choices (prompt families, decoding settings, refusal handling, scoring pipelines) are underspecified and unreported. Existing documentation artifacts (Model Cards, Datasheets, AI FactSheets) do not address the generative-specific evaluation surface, making fairness evidence non-comparable across papers, versions, and deployments and preventing the field from telling whether a claimed mitigation genuinely reduces harm or merely shifts it.

## Input / Output

The evaluation protocol consumes a fixed model checkpoint and a full factorial design of evaluation conditions—demographic slices (e.g., gender × religion), occupations, prompt families with paraphrases, decoding regimes, and random seeds—producing 3,200 generations in the main Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct study. Outputs are per-slice rates for multiple harm dimensions (stereotype keywords, refusal, deflection, title mention, demeaning language, identity salience) computed via a deterministic regex-based lexical rubric, aggregated into max-minus-min disparity tables and a structured Fairness Card document that discloses all evaluation degrees of freedom including prompt templates, seed policy, refusal handling rules, and scoring thresholds.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the Fairness Card, a minimal generative-specific reporting standard with eight mandatory fields (model/system identification, protected attributes and intersectional slices, evaluation protocol, metrics and decision rules, mitigations and trade-offs, governance plan) that treats the evaluation procedure itself as a first-class disclosure object alongside dataset and training documentation. Empirically, they demonstrate the evaluation problem through a controlled audit on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct where the model is held fixed and only the evaluation setting varies across a 4×4×4×5×2×5 grid, showing that the worst-slice stereotype-keyword rate ranges from 0.04 to 0.23 depending solely on which prompt family is selected, so a simple 5% flag rule produces opposite audit outcomes. A companion probe on Mistral's role-assignment task shows that decoding regime and name order alone can flip the verdict, with the model rationalising the same trait in opposite directions under different sampling draws. The scoring layer deliberately uses a deterministic lexical rubric rather than a model-based judge, keeping the scoring pipeline auditable and removing a second source of evaluator instability.

## Results

The controlled Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct audit (3,200 generations: 4 demographic slices × 4 prompt families × 5 paraphrases × 4 occupations × 2 decoding regimes × 5 seeds) shows that aggregated max-minus-min disparity across slices is at most 6.0 percentage points (title mention) and 5.1 points (stereotype keywords), suggesting near-parity. However, when resolved by prompt family, the worst-slice stereotype-keyword rate ranges from 0.04 (F4, HR memo) to 0.23 (F2, story continuation), a 5.75× swing that flips a simple 5% flag rule from 'no slice flagged' to 'all slices flagged.' Seed variation alone (prompts held fixed) moves the worst-slice rate over [0.094, 0.125], and the complementary Mistral role-assignment probe (300 generations) shows the audit verdict flips from a 24-point Elena lead under low-entropy decoding to a 2-point Philippe lead under high-entropy decoding for the same prompt and context.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare against competing fairness methods or baselines in the traditional sense; instead, it compares evaluation protocols applied to the same fixed model. The central empirical comparison is across four prompt families (F1–F4) on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct: under F2 (story continuation), the worst-slice stereotype-keyword rate reaches 0.230 (M×Muslim) and 0.210 (M×Christian), exceeding the 5% threshold on every slice; under F4 (HR memo), the same slices drop to 0.040 and 0.005, falling below the threshold on all slices. Decoding regime (t=0.2 vs. t=0.7) shifts stereotype rates by up to +2.3 percentage points and title-mention rates for two of four slices. In the Mistral probe, name order alone (whether the prompt reads 'Philippe and Elena' or 'Elena and Philippe') is sufficient to swap which name receives the manager role in several cells, and the model's rationalisation of the same hobby cue inverts depending on the sampling draw. These protocol-level comparisons demonstrate that no single evaluation setting yields a stable, model-intrinsic fairness verdict, motivating the paper's Fairness Card reporting standard.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the documentation-artifact lineage (Model Cards, Datasheets for Datasets, AI FactSheets, reproducibility checklists) by shifting the disclosure object from the model or dataset to the evaluation protocol itself—prompt families, decoding settings, refusal handling, and scoring pipelines. It builds on prior evidence of prompt sensitivity (Teo et al., 2024a; Zhong et al., 2025) and selective refusal bias (Khorramrouz and Levy, 2025) but reframes these as symptoms of a systemic evaluation problem rather than isolated model defects. The Fairness Card proposal departs from all prior frameworks by making the generative-specific evaluation surface (prompt-family dependence, seed policy, base-versus-served system gaps) a mandatory, structured disclosure rather than an optional qualitative discussion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If fairness evidence remains non-cumulative and protocol-dependent, the field cannot distinguish genuine mitigation progress from prompt/metric gaming, and regulators lack a comparable basis for deployment decisions. The paper's full-factorial audit (3,200 generations on a single model) provides concrete evidence that the problem is not hypothetical, but the proposal's impact depends on community and regulatory adoption. Key limitations acknowledged: Fairness Cards can be gamed via cherry-picked prompt suites, do not resolve normative disagreement about which slices or harms matter, and are incomplete under proprietary constraints. Open questions include whether a lightweight academic card versus a full commercial card is the right granularity, and whether the standard can be enforced without mandating specific metrics or fairness definitions.
