## Problem

Existing attention-based hallucination detection methods rely on coarse aggregate statistics (entropy, variance, attention mass) that discard fine-grained sequential variation in attention patterns. The paper identifies that hallucinated tokens exhibit rapid local oscillations in attention that are invisible to these scalar summaries, and asks whether explicitly isolating high-frequency attention components can improve detection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263703/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes attention weight vectors from every transformer layer and head of a frozen LLM during teacher-forced decoding, partitioned into context-directed attention (over N source tokens) and generated-token attention (over previously emitted tokens). These are treated as 1-D discrete signals indexed by token position, transformed via DFT, DWT, or Laplacian, and the resulting high-frequency ℓ2-norm scores are concatenated across all L×H heads and both attention types into a single feature vector. A single-layer logistic regression classifier then outputs a binary hallucination/non-hallucination label per token, or per span when features are averaged over a fixed sliding window (default size 8).

## Method

The core idea is to model each attention distribution as a discrete temporal signal and apply high-pass spectral operators—DFT with a frequency-domain mask, DWT detail coefficients, or the discrete Laplacian—to isolate rapid local variation, whose ℓ2 energy (justified by Parseval's theorem) quantifies attention instability. A simplified toy proof shows that increasing the number of latent semantic sources monotonically increases expected adjacent-difference energy in attention, linking semantic heterogeneity to high-frequency roughness. In practice, the high-frequency energy scores from all layers, heads, and both attention types are concatenated and fed to a lightweight logistic regression classifier trained on token-level hallucination labels. Fourier-based features achieve the strongest overall performance, followed by wavelets and the Laplacian, and the detection signal is highly head-sparse (top 100 heads recover ~95% of AUROC) and concentrated in middle layers associated with semantic composition.

## Results

On the RAGTruth and HalluRAG benchmarks, Fourier-high (the paper's DFT-based method) achieves the best overall AUROC across all three models: 0.8052 on LLaMA-7B, 0.8219 on LLaMA-13B, and 0.8532 on Mistral-7B, with corresponding average F1 scores of 0.6615, 0.6723, and 0.7266. The strongest attention-based baseline, Lookback-Lens, achieves 0.7871 / 0.8031 / 0.8478 AUROC on the same models, meaning Fourier-high improves AUROC by 1.8%, 1.9%, and 0.5% respectively. On the summarization task (the most challenging), Fourier-high improves AUROC by up to 6.6% over Lookback-Lens on LLaMA-13B (0.7362 vs. 0.6702). Wavelet-high and Laplacian variants also outperform all baselines, confirming that high-frequency attention energy is a robust hallucination signal regardless of the specific spectral operator.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's frequency-aware methods (Fourier-high, Wavelet-high, Laplacian) consistently outperform all five baselines across all three models and four datasets. Against the strongest attention-based baseline Lookback-Lens, Fourier-high improves overall AUROC by 1.8% (LLaMA-7B), 1.9% (LLaMA-13B), and 0.5% (Mistral-7B), with the largest gap on summarization (up to +6.6% AUROC on LLaMA-13B). Verification-based methods SelfCheckGPT and RefChecker are substantially outperformed, with Fourier-high exceeding SelfCheckGPT by 11.5–12.6% AUROC and RefChecker by 20.5–22.6% AUROC across models. Internal-representation baselines EigenScore and ReDeEP are the weakest, with Fourier-high outperforming them by 26–32% AUROC; notably, EigenScore and ReDeEP are unsupervised while the paper's method uses a supervised logistic regression classifier. On Mistral-7B, Wavelet-high actually edges out Fourier-high on overall AUROC (0.8595 vs. 0.8532) and F1 (0.7284 vs. 0.7266), suggesting the operator ranking is model-dependent, though Fourier-high remains the best on LLaMA-7B and LLaMA-13B.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the attention-based hallucination detection line initiated by Lookback-Lens (Chuang et al., 2024a) and ReDeEP (Sun et al., 2025), which characterize grounding through attention allocation or entropy. Rather than summarizing attention with coarse scalar statistics, the authors repurpose classical signal-processing operators (DFT, DWT, discrete Laplacian) to decompose attention weight vectors into frequency bands and isolate high-frequency components as an instability signal. A simplified single-layer toy proof in the appendix formally links semantic heterogeneity (more latent topic sources) to increased adjacent-difference energy in attention, providing a mechanistic motivation that prior attention-based methods lacked. The approach thus carves new ground by importing a frequency-domain perspective into a task previously addressed only through spatial-domain summaries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean ablation showing that high-pass components outperform both low-pass and full-spectrum features, while low-pass and full-spectrum are comparable, provides a principled explanation for why aggregate attention statistics (entropy, variance) underperform: they are dominated by low-frequency structure shared by grounded and hallucinated tokens. The head-sparsity finding (top 100 heads recover ~95% of AUROC) suggests a small set of heads acts as implicit hallucination sensors, which could inform efficient monitoring or intervention. Key limitations include the requirement for internal attention access (excluding API-only models), dependence on labeled hallucination annotations for the final classifier, and a theoretical analysis that assumes independent token embeddings and a single attention layer, leaving the autoregressive multi-layer setting unaddressed. Reducing supervision and extending to closed-source or streaming settings remain open.
