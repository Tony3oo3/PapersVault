## Problem

Top AI conferences maintain relatively stable acceptance rates (typically 20–30%) as an implicit prestige signal, making the total submission count a controllable denominator that shapes each paper's marginal acceptance probability. This paper identifies and formalizes a new systemic threat—Agentic Conference Denominator Gaming—in which fully automated scientific agents flood a venue with large volumes of superficially plausible but intentionally low-quality manuscripts, not to be accepted themselves, but to inflate the denominator and force the acceptance boundary lower, thereby increasing the publication odds of a small set of targeted legitimate papers.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes three inputs: the target conference's LaTeX template (parsed from .sty and .tex files to replicate exact formatting), commercial LLM API access for manuscript generation, and OpenReview API credentials for programmatic submission. The output is a batch of stylistically correct, topic-relevant but scientifically invalid PDFs, each registered under a distinct (potentially Sybil) account and submitted through OpenReview's structured JSON form interface. Crucially, the generated papers are designed to survive desk-rejection scrutiny on formatting and structure while failing on scientific substance, ensuring they occupy the rejection quota without consuming deep review effort.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a formalized threat model implemented as a two-agent multi-agent pipeline: a Research Agent that leverages LLMs to generate plausible manuscripts by first extracting and mimicking the target conference's LaTeX template structure, and a Submission Agent that automates account registration (exploiting educational email verification), OpenReview form completion via the openreview-py Python library, and optional rebuttal responses. A mathematical formalization shows that under a fixed target acceptance rate α, the effective human acceptance rate becomes α̂ = α(1 + N_a/N_h), rising proportionally with the ratio of synthetic to human submissions. The authors validate practical feasibility with a controlled end-to-end test (one paper generated, registered, and submitted on OpenReview, then promptly withdrawn) and systematically evaluate defense strategies—technical detection, submission fees, multi-stage review, bounded acceptance ranges, and reputation systems—arguing that no single technical fix suffices and that durable protection requires system-level policy reform breaking the link between submission volume and acceptance count.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that top AI conferences maintain remarkably stable acceptance rates despite exponential submission growth: NeurIPS submissions grew from ~6,700 (2019) to 30,000+ (2025) at ~29% CAGR, yet its acceptance rate std is only 0.53 (mean 25.53%), with similarly low std for ECCV (0.33), ACL (0.60), SIGIR (0.83), and WSDM (0.90). The mathematical formalization shows that inflating the denominator by N_a AI papers raises the effective human acceptance rate to α̂ = α(1 + N_a/N_h), e.g., tripling submissions from 4 to 12 at a 25% target rate forces acceptances from 1 to 3. A single end-to-end feasibility test confirmed that a multi-agent pipeline (research agent + submission agent) can successfully register on OpenReview and submit a generated manuscript, with the submission agent costing less than $1 and the paper generation costing ~$2.33 per paper via GPT-4o.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare methods against baselines in the standard empirical sense. Instead, it qualitatively evaluates a range of defense strategies along two axes: technical defenses (AI text detectors, submission-platform rate limiting, identity verification) versus policy/systemic defenses (submission fees, multi-stage review, bounded acceptance ranges, reputation systems). The paper argues that technical defenses are fundamentally insufficient due to high false-positive rates, adversarial evasion (e.g., Adversarial Paraphrasing reducing detection by >98%), and the perpetual cat-and-mouse dynamic with generation tools. The most structurally effective defense identified is decoupling acceptance count from submission volume via a pre-committed bounded range, which directly neutralizes the attacker's premise. The economic asymmetry is stark: the attack costs ~$2–3 per paper in LLM API fees plus <$1 in submission automation, while defense imposes thousands of hours of uncompensated expert labor on the community, and the synthetic corpus is reusable across venues at near-zero marginal cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes three existing research threads—automated scientific agents (AI Scientist, Agent Laboratory, SciAgents), the paper-mill literature on industrialized academic fraud, and game-theoretic models of academic publishing—into a novel threat formulation it calls Agentic Conference Denominator Gaming. It departs from prior work on AI-generated papers (which focuses on detection and content quality) by reframing the threat as structural mechanism manipulation: the attacker's goal is not acceptance of synthetic papers but inflation of the submission denominator under a stable acceptance-rate norm. The proof-of-concept pipeline repurposes existing OpenReview APIs and LLM-based research agents rather than introducing new technical components, positioning the contribution as a conceptual and policy-level intervention rather than a methodological advance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central argument—that the stable acceptance rate, long treated as a prestige signal, is actually an exploitable structural vulnerability—could prompt concrete policy shifts (bounded acceptance quotas, submission fees, multi-stage triage) before the threat materializes at scale. Key open questions include whether breaking the acceptance-rate norm is politically feasible without alienating the community, how to design reputation systems that don't entrench incumbents, and whether the economic asymmetry can be closed without excluding under-resourced researchers. The empirical evidence is preliminary (a single proof-of-concept submission and AI-detection scores on recent NeurIPS/ICLR abstracts), so the threat remains hypothetical but technically grounded.
