## Problem

Conventional Graph Contrastive Learning (GCL) for Gene Regulatory Networks relies on artificial perturbations (e.g., node dropping) that can disrupt biologically critical structure, while the emerging augmentation-free trend treats all structural changes as nuisances to avoid. This overlooks the opportunity to exploit real biological perturbations from gene knockdown experiments as supervisory signals, which have become readily accessible through high-throughput profiling platforms like LINCS.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263517/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes patient-specific directed GRNs (975 genes, with gene expression as node features and B-spline regression coefficients as edge features) from TCGA cancer cohorts, along with teacher GRNs derived from shRNA knockdown experiments in LINCS (MCF7, A549, HT29 cell lines, 96h treatment). Augmentation of gene i is modeled by zeroing its node feature and all incident edge features. The shared GNN produces 64-dimensional node-level gene embeddings and graph-level patient embeddings (via mean pooling), which are then fine-tuned for gene-level functional annotation (GO BP/CC, OncoKB cancer relevance) and patient-level prediction (Cox survival hazard, PAM50 subtype classification).

## Method

SupGCL formulates supervised graph augmentation within a unified KL-divergence probabilistic framework, introducing an augmentation-level reference distribution p_φ(b|a) computed via Frobenius inner product similarity between teacher GRN embeddings {Y^a}, alongside the standard node-level contrastive distribution. Theorem 4.1 proves the total loss decomposes into (i) the expected node-level GCL loss under the supervised augmentation distribution and (ii) an augmentation-level KL divergence that aligns artificial perturbations with biologically measured knockdown effects. Corollary 4.2 shows that as the augmentation temperature τ_a → ∞, SupGCL continuously reduces to conventional unsupervised node-level GCL (e.g., GRACE), making existing methods special cases. A single 5-layer Graph Transformer encoder processes both target and teacher GRNs, and training uses importance sampling over augmentation pairs with AdamW optimization, achieving the same asymptotic computational complexity as standard node-level GCL.

## Results

SupGCL achieves the best performance across all 13 downstream tasks spanning three cancer types (breast, lung, colorectal). The most notable gains appear on graph-level tasks: for colorectal hazard prediction (C-index), SupGCL reaches 0.698, surpassing the next-best GraphCL (0.657) by 4.1 points; for breast subtype classification (accuracy), SupGCL scores 0.847 vs. GRACE's 0.841. On node-level tasks, SupGCL leads on BP subset accuracy (e.g., 0.282 for lung vs. 0.259 for GRACE) and CC subset accuracy (e.g., 0.291 for breast vs. 0.264 for no-pretrain). In embedding space analysis, SupGCL also yields the highest NMI and ARI for disease-subtype clustering without any task-specific training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SupGCL consistently outperforms all four baselines across every task and cancer type. GraphCL is the weakest baseline overall, particularly on node-level tasks where it drops to 0.069–0.207 subset accuracy on BP/CC (vs. 0.243–0.291 for SupGCL), suggesting that graph-level positive-pair contrastive learning fails to capture gene-level functional structure. GRACE (node-level GCL) is the strongest conventional GCL baseline, reaching 0.841 on breast subtype and 0.642 on breast hazard, but SupGCL improves on both (0.847 and 0.650) and shows larger margins on node-level tasks (e.g., +2.3 points on lung BP, +5.5 points on breast CC). SGRL, the SOTA augmentation-free method, is competitive on Rel and Hazard (within ~1–2 points of SupGCL) but degrades sharply on BP/CC for colorectal cancer (0.133–0.146 vs. 0.262–0.279 for SupGCL), indicating that removing augmentation entirely sacrifices biological signal. GAE (reconstruction-based) is consistently mid-pack, rarely exceeding the no-pretrain baseline, confirming that simple graph reconstruction does not yield task-relevant representations for GRNs.

## Relation to Prior Work

SupGCL extends the I-Con probabilistic framework for contrastive learning (Alshammari et al., 2025) from sample-level categorical labels to graph-level augmentation supervision, and subsumes node-level GCL methods like GRACE (Zhu et al., 2020) as a special case via a temperature limit (Corollary 4.2). It deliberately departs from the prevailing augmentation-free trend (BGRL, SGRL, simGRACE) and augmentation-adjustment approaches (AD-GCL, AutoGCL), arguing that structural changes from real biological perturbations—rather than being nuisances to avoid or objectives to optimize—are valuable supervisory signals. The key conceptual move is replacing heuristic graph perturbations with experimentally measured knockdown responses from LINCS as teacher distributions, a form of supervision that neither prior GCL line of work exploits.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a principled counter-narrative to the augmentation-free GCL trend: if you have access to real perturbation data, discarding structural changes throws away information that directly encodes biological causality. The theoretical decomposition (Theorem 4.1) cleanly separates node-level contrastive alignment from augmentation-level distribution matching, giving practitioners a tunable knob (τ_a) to interpolate between unsupervised GCL and fully supervised learning. However, the empirical gains over strong baselines are modest (typically 1–3 points), the evaluation is limited to 975-gene networks across three cancer types, and the authors acknowledge that scaling to tens or hundreds of thousands of nodes remains untested. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to other perturbation modalities (CRISPR, drug treatment), whether pan-cancer pre-training can yield a biological foundation model, and how performance scales with graph size.
