## Problem

Sampling from posteriors in Bayesian inverse problems is expensive when the exact forward operator is costly to evaluate. Existing IMH samplers that exploit a cheaper approximate operator either use the approximate posterior directly (causing poor acceptance rates as operator errors are amplified by 1/σ²) or require transforming the forward operator into a square, invertible form (as in Latent-IMH), which introduces ill-conditioning and high-dimensional latent-space sampling. The paper addresses the gap of a global IMH proposal that corrects approximate posterior samples toward the exact posterior without squaring the operator or sampling in latent space.

## Input / Output

The method consumes noisy observations y ∈ ℝ^{d_y}, an exact input-output operator A = OF (linear or nonlinear), a cheaper approximate operator Ã = OF̃, a prior p(x), and a known noise distribution q(e). It produces independent posterior samples from π(x|y) via a two-step proposal: draw x̃ from the approximate posterior, then apply a proximal correction. Proposals are fully independent of the current chain state, enabling embarrassingly parallel generation. The method supports nonsquare and nonlinear F, and the per-sample cost is 2n_CG·C_f exact forward/adjoint solves (linear case) or one Gauss-Newton step (nonlinear case), where n_CG is the number of inner CG iterations.

## Method

Proximal-IMH constructs each proposal by first sampling x̃ from the approximate posterior π_a(x|y) ∝ q(y − Ãx̃)p(x̃), then correcting it via the proximal optimization min_x ‖Ax − Ãx̃‖² + β‖x − x̃‖². For linear A, this yields the closed-form map x = Kx̃ with K = (AᵀA + βI)⁻¹(AᵀÃ + βI), equivalently x = x̃ + A†(Ãx̃ − Ax̃) where A† = (AᵀA + βI)⁻¹Aᵀ is a regularized pseudoinverse; the correction is applied by solving a symmetric positive-definite linear system via CG/PCG. For nonlinear A, a single Gauss-Newton step on the proximal objective replaces the closed-form solve, and the Jacobian determinant ratio in the acceptance probability is shown to be negligible in practice. The key theoretical result is that the correction operator K filters the observable perturbation ΔA = Ã − A through the regularized pseudoinverse, yielding smaller expected KL divergence (Theorem 3.2) and faster mixing times (Theorem 3.3) than both Approx-IMH (which scales with ‖A‖²/σ⁴) and Latent-IMH (which is amplified by the conditioning of F̃⁻¹), with the regularization parameter β = Θ(σ²) balancing data fidelity against stability around the approximate sample.

## Results

Across all numerical experiments (bimodal, MNIST imaging, and nonlinear Helmholtz scattering), Proximal-IMH consistently achieves substantially higher acceptance rates and faster convergence than Approx-IMH and Latent-IMH, with accuracy comparable to NUTS. In the KL-divergence sensitivity study (Figure 1), the expected KL divergence D_p of the Proximal proposal is lower than both D_a and D_l by orders of magnitude across all tested noise levels, operator errors, observation ratios, and dimensions. In the MNIST test, Proximal-IMH's relative mean error is largely insensitive to the hyperparameter β (varying by less than 0.4%), and the method achieves lower error, faster convergence, and reduced trial-to-trial variability compared to the Approx posterior baseline. The theoretical mixing-time bound shows ‖I − K⁻¹‖ ≤ ‖I − F̃⁻¹F‖, confirming that Proximal-IMH is more robust to operator perturbations than Latent-IMH.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against **Approx-IMH** (using the approximate posterior directly as the IMH proposal), Proximal-IMH wins decisively on all metrics: higher acceptance rates, faster convergence, and unbiased mode capture in the bimodal test where Approx-IMH exhibits biased mode weights. The advantage grows with increasing operator error ‖A − Ã‖/‖A‖. Against **Latent-IMH** (Chen & Biros, 2026), Proximal-IMH wins on robustness: in Test II (additive low-rank perturbation), the conditioning degradation causes ‖I − F̃⁻¹F‖ to grow rapidly while ‖I − K⁻¹‖ remains stable, explaining Proximal-IMH's superior acceptance rates. Proximal-IMH also avoids the ill-conditioning from squaring F and works with nonsquare and nonlinear operators where Latent-IMH cannot be applied. Against **NUTS**, Proximal-IMH achieves comparable sampling accuracy (relative mean error and second-moment error) while retaining independent proposals that can be generated and evaluated in parallel, offering a practical wall-clock advantage for large-scale problems where exact forward solves dominate cost. MALA is also included as a local-kernel baseline in the bimodal convergence plots, where Proximal-IMH converges faster due to its global proposal structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the authors' own Latent-IMH framework (Chen & Biros, 2026) by replacing the latent-space proposal construction with a proximal optimization correction in parameter space. The key conceptual departure is that Proximal-IMH avoids squaring the forward operator F (which Latent-IMH requires for invertibility) and avoids sampling in a potentially high-dimensional latent variable, instead correcting approximate posterior samples via a regularized least-squares step. The method also generalizes beyond the linear, invertible setting of Latent-IMH to handle nonsquare and nonlinear forward operators via a single Gauss-Newton step, positioning it as a more broadly applicable IMH variant within the multifidelity sampling literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For practitioners solving Bayesian inverse problems where the exact forward operator is expensive but a cheaper surrogate is available, Proximal-IMH offers a drop-in replacement for Latent-IMH that avoids ill-conditioning and works with nonsquare/nonlinear operators, while retaining the parallelizable independent-proposal structure of IMH. The theoretical guarantees (KL divergence and mixing time bounds) are currently limited to linear forward models with log-concave priors, and the nonlinear extension is approximate (omitting the Jacobian determinant ratio), leaving open whether the induced bias is acceptable in high-dimensional or highly nonlinear settings. Extending the mixing-time analysis to the nonlinear case and developing adaptive strategies for the regularization parameter β and number of Gauss-Newton steps are natural next steps.
