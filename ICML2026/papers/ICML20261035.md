## Problem

Existing clinical score-learning methods (RiskSLIM, FasterRisk, Optimal Checklists) optimize over fixed, pre-constructed feature encodings and cannot jointly construct and select clinically meaningful derived rules (physiologic ratios, temporal trends, shallow logical compositions) from raw EHR variables. This leaves rule design as a manual prerequisite rather than part of the learning problem, while the resulting scores often violate the unit-weighted, unordered, arithmetic-free checklist structure required for reliable bedside deployment.

## Input / Output

The method consumes patient trajectories (p clinical variables over T timepoints from EHR datasets), a task description, and a rule budget m. Critically, the LLM agent never observes raw patient records; it interacts exclusively through a restricted tool interface exposing feature metadata and aggregate evaluation statistics. The output is a unit-weighted N-of-M checklist S ⊆ R with |S| ≤ m binary rules plus an integer decision threshold τ, where the score S(x̃) = Σ_{r_j∈S} r_j(x̃) ∈ {0,…,m} and the binary decision is ŷ = 1 iff S(x̃) ≥ τ, enabling manual bedside execution without calculators or software.

## Method

AgentScore decomposes the NP-hard checklist learning problem into three stages: (1) an LLM proposal agent generates candidate rules from a restricted clinical grammar (thresholds, ranges, ratios, temporal deltas, shallow AND/OR compositions) in small batches, interacting with data only via aggregate statistics; (2) a deterministic validation module retains rules only if they meet a minimum AUROC threshold τ_rule and pass a Jaccard-similarity redundancy check (J⁺ ≤ δ) against the current pool; (3) a clinical plausibility agent eliminates semantically incoherent rules, and a score construction agent iteratively assembles a compact checklist from the retained pool, receiving evaluation feedback after each revision. The key design principle is decoupling semantic proposal from statistical verification: the LLM expands the candidate rule space but never determines the final score, assigns risk, or validates clinical utility, ensuring every retained rule is both interpretable and empirically supported by held-out data.

## Results

Across eight clinical prediction tasks (MIMIC-IV and eICU), AgentScore achieves a mean AUROC of 0.71±0.08, significantly outperforming all score-learning baselines: +0.05 over RiskSLIM (0.66), +0.03 over FasterRisk (0.68), and +0.09 over Optimal Checklists (0.62), with paired Holm-Bonferroni-corrected p < 0.001 for all score-based comparisons. On two externally validated tasks, AgentScore achieves higher discrimination than established guideline scores (SOFA, SAPS-I on PhysioNet ICU 2012; CF lung-transplant eligibility guidelines and FEV₁<30% threshold on a Canadian CF cohort). Less-constrained interpretable models (Decision Tree 0.73, Logistic Regression 0.72, AutoScore 0.72) achieve marginally higher mean AUROC but are not checklist-deployable by design.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among score-learning baselines, AgentScore (mean AUROC 0.71) clearly wins: it outperforms RiskSLIM (0.66) by +0.05, FasterRisk (0.68) by +0.03, Optimal Checklists (0.62) by +0.09, and both Pooled PLR variants (0.65, 0.64) by ~0.06–0.07, with all differences statistically significant (Holm-corrected p < 0.001). Against AutoScore (0.72), AgentScore trails by 0.01 on mean AUROC, but AutoScore produces multi-bin, non-unit-weighted point systems that are not unit-weighted N-of-M checklists and thus not directly bedside-deployable in the same sense. On external validation, AgentScore achieves higher AUROC than SOFA and SAPS-I on the PhysioNet ICU 2012 mortality benchmark, and higher discrimination than both the CF lung-transplant eligibility guidelines and the simple FEV₁<30% threshold when trained on a UK CF cohort and tested on an independent Canadian cohort. The paper notes that less-constrained models (Decision Tree, Logistic Regression) achieve marginally higher mean AUROC (0.73, 0.72) but rely on continuous coefficients or post-hoc discretization that violate the unit-weighted, arithmetic-free checklist constraint central to AgentScore's design.

## Relation to Prior Work

AgentScore extends the unit-weighted N-of-M checklist learning framework of Optimal Checklists (Zhang et al., 2021) by replacing the fixed candidate feature matrix with an LLM-guided rule construction stage over a restricted clinical grammar (thresholds, ratios, temporal summaries, shallow Boolean compositions). It also departs from integer-weighted score learners (RiskSLIM, FasterRisk) by enforcing unit weights and unordered checklist structure as hard deployability constraints rather than soft regularization. The LLM is used in a narrow, verifiable role—proposing candidate rules within a predefined grammar—while all acceptance decisions are made by deterministic, data-grounded statistical screening (AUROC threshold, Jaccard redundancy) and a clinical plausibility filter, distinguishing it from prior LLM-as-decision-maker approaches in clinical settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a tightly constrained, unit-weighted 5–6 rule checklist can match the discrimination of flexible interpretable models (decision trees, logistic regression) and outperform 30+ year-old guideline scores (SOFA, SAPS-I) under external validation, supporting the argument that deployability need not come at a large predictive cost. Key open questions include: whether the LLM-bounded rule space misses clinically relevant but semantically unusual constructs, the absence of explicit fairness or subgroup-robustness constraints, and whether the approach generalizes beyond the eight tasks tested. The framework also raises governance questions about aggregate-statistic interfaces as a privacy mechanism, which the authors acknowledge but do not formally bound.
