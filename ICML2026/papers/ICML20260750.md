## Problem

Existing LLM input moderation approaches face a fundamental trade-off: guard models deliver strong detection but require expensive training, add a separate model to the pipeline, and are difficult to customize, while latent-based methods are lightweight and flexible but underperform on detection accuracy. No prior method simultaneously achieves guard-level safety assessment performance, negligible inference overhead, and easy adaptation to user-specific safety policies.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260750/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

MLPM consumes a text prompt and extracts last-token hidden representations from the feed-forward network outputs of each transformer layer in an off-the-shelf LLM. Because these representations are already computed during the standard prefill forward pass, the only additional computation is a lightweight GDA scoring step (less than 0.001% of prefill FLOPs). The output is a single harmfulness probability in [0,1] produced by a sigmoid over the weighted sum of per-layer GDA scores, with a shared covariance matrix across layers to keep memory to roughly 24 KB per prototype in half-precision.

## Method

MLPM applies a Mahalanobis-distance-based Gaussian Discriminant Analysis (GDA) classifier independently to the last-token hidden state at each of L intermediate FFN layers, yielding a per-layer probability that the prompt is harmful. These L probabilities are then combined via a learned sparse aggregation: weights w are optimized with an L1 penalty so that only a small subset of informative layers contributes to the final score, automatically adapting to architecture-specific safety-relevant layers (e.g., middle layers for Mistral, final layers for OLMo). Training requires only a single forward pass over a small labeled dataset (as few as 1,000 samples) to compute class-conditional prototypes (means and a shared inverse covariance via a Bayes ridge estimator) and to fit the aggregation weights—no gradient updates to the LLM are needed. At inference, only layers with non-zero weights are scored, keeping the overhead negligible while the multi-layer aggregation captures complementary safety signals that no single layer provides in isolation.

## Results

On the primary benchmark (average F1 across 8 harmful prompt datasets), MLPM achieves 90.18 on OLMo2-7B-Inst, 88.30 on Llama-8B-Inst, 87.55 on Mistral-7B-Inst, and 85.95 on Qwen3-8B-Inst. These results surpass the strongest guard model WildGuard (88.93) when applied to OLMo2, and outperform all other guard models (Aegis 78.82, LlamaGuard3 79.56, GraniteGuardian 85.62, ShieldGemma 73.10) across every backbone. MLPM also exceeds prior latent-based methods (Ayub & Majumdar, Abdelnabi et al.) by 3–6 F1 points on the same backbone models, while adding less than 0.001% inference overhead and requiring as few as 1,000 training samples for competitive performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the two latent-based baselines, MLPM wins on every backbone: it outperforms Ayub & Majumdar (2024) by 3.8–6.2 F1 points and Abdelnabi et al. (2025) by 2.4–3.8 F1 points on average. Among guard models, MLPM on OLMo2-7B (90.18) surpasses the strongest guard WildGuard (88.93) by 1.25 points, and beats GraniteGuardian (85.62), LlamaGuard3 (79.56), Aegis-Guard-D (78.82), and ShieldGemma (73.10) by 2–17 points. On Llama-8B, MLPM (88.30) trails WildGuard (88.93) by 0.63 points but still exceeds all other guards. In the end-to-end output-moderation experiments (Table 3), using MLPM as a conditioning signal for steering methods (Lee et al. 2025a, Turner et al. 2023, Zheng et al. 2024) reduces false refusal rates by 1–5 points compared to unconditional steering, while the simple-refusal variant of MLPM achieves the highest F1 (81.5–83.9) across all four models, outperforming the unconditional steering baselines by 6–56 F1 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

MLPM sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: prototype-based classification (Nearest Mean Classifier with Mahalanobis distance, as in FECAM/Goswami et al. 2023) and latent-space LLM safety assessment (Ayub & Majumdar 2024; Abdelnabi et al. 2025). The key departure is the multi-layer aggregation strategy: rather than relying on a single layer's representation, MLPM learns sparse weights over per-layer GDA classifiers, drawing on the observation that safety-relevant information is distributed non-uniformly across transformer depths. This positions MLPM as a lightweight alternative to full guard models (LlamaGuard, WildGuard, ShieldGemma) that avoids the high training cost and deployment overhead of a separate moderation LLM while closing the performance gap that previously favored guard models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: MLPM adds less than 0.001% overhead to prefill computation and ~24KB of memory per prototype, making it deployable alongside any existing LLM without architectural changes. Its data efficiency (competitive performance with ~1,000 training samples) and model-agnosticism (validated across Llama, Mistral, OLMo, Qwen3, VLMs, and hybrid SSM-Transformer architectures) make it a practical tool for organizations that need customizable safety policies without retraining a full guard model. Open questions include whether the method's reliance on instruction-tuned representations limits its applicability to base or rapidly evolving models, whether the sparse layer selection is stable under distribution shift, and how it performs against adaptive or multi-turn jailbreak strategies that may not be captured by last-token representations alone.
