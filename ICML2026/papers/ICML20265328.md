## Problem

Rare disease diagnosis is persistently hindered by cognitive anchoring, where clinicians prematurely settle on common diagnoses and insufficiently revise when contradictory or incomplete evidence emerges. Existing ML classifiers improve raw accuracy but do not address the cognitive constraints that shape how clinicians interpret predictions, and no prior system explicitly models the divergence between human and AI diagnostic reasoning to generate targeted counterfactuals that challenge anchored thinking.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular patient clinical features (symptoms, lab values, genetic markers) with naturally occurring missing values retained, augmented by a binary missingness indicator per entry. Beyond the standard dual prediction (AI diagnosis from the full latent code and a simulated clinician diagnosis from a sparsely masked latent code), the system outputs a learned sparse attention mask identifying which latent dimensions the clinician 'attends to,' and—when a cognitive gap is detected—counterfactual outputs comprising an alternative diagnosis and a reconstructed synthetic patient profile that specifies which missing or atypical findings would shift the prediction toward a rare condition.

## Method

The architecture centers on a Denoising Masked AutoEncoder encoding patient features into a 32-dimensional latent space Z, with two separately parameterized prediction heads: an AI predictor on the full Z (trained with focal loss weighted by inverse class frequency) and a human-simulation predictor on a masked version Z̃ = m ⊙ Z, where the sparse attention mask m is learned via a self-attention module over the input and regularized with an L1 penalty to mimic limited cognitive bandwidth. A contrastive loss pushes rare-disease embeddings away from common-disease clusters, and a cognitive gap loss penalizes the AI's gradient sensitivity in dimensions the human mask attends to, steering the AI toward features clinicians systematically overlook. For counterfactual generation, the system optimizes a bounded latent perturbation ΔZ* that maximizes the entropy of the human-simulation model, then decodes the perturbed latent code back to a synthetic patient profile and passes it through the AI predictor to yield an alternative diagnosis with quantified probability shift. Training follows a four-stage curriculum—DMAE reconstruction warm-up, AI predictor training, joint human-predictor and mask-network training, and full-network fine-tuning with all loss terms active—ensuring stable latent-space initialization before introducing the cognitive-gap objectives.

## Results

Across seven rare-disease datasets, the proposed DMAE-based counterfactual framework achieves a perfect Label Flip Rate of 1.00±0.00 on all cohorts, while simultaneously producing the smallest perturbation magnitudes (RMSE). For example, on Gitelman syndrome the RMSE is 1.93±0.76 versus 5.40±0.89 (REVISE) and 12.0±1.77 (CFVAE), and on Acromegaly it drops to 0.18±0.10 versus 13.96±14.4 for both baselines. In the robustness experiment under extreme class imbalance (ratios up to 94:500), the method's AUC and PRAUC remain stable or improve as imbalance increases, outperforming XGBoost, LightGBM, SVM, and logistic regression baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two counterfactual generation baselines: REVISE (Joshi et al., 2019), which performs gradient-based optimization in a generative model's latent space, and CFVAE (Nagesh et al., 2023), which jointly trains a VAE with a binary predictor. The proposed method dominates both baselines on both evaluation metrics across all six reported datasets. On Label Flip Rate, the method achieves a perfect 1.00 on every dataset, whereas REVISE ranges from 0.92–1.00 and CFVAE from 0.85–0.98. The margin on RMSE (perturbation size) is particularly large on the private clinical cohorts: on Gitelman syndrome the method's RMSE of 1.93 is roughly 2.8× smaller than REVISE (5.40) and 6.2× smaller than CFVAE (12.0), and on Acromegaly it is 0.18 versus 13.96 for both baselines—a 77× reduction. On the public datasets the gap is narrower but consistent (e.g., GPA RMSE 0.12 vs. 0.18 for REVISE and 0.29 for CFVAE). The ablation row confirms that the full multi-loss objective (including contrastive and cognitive-gap terms) is responsible for the RMSE advantage, as removing any single component roughly doubles the perturbation magnitude.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Denoising Masked AutoEncoder (Wu et al., 2023) as a latent-state generative backbone for rare disease diagnosis, departing from its original role in robust image classification. It extends the counterfactual explanation literature (Joshi et al., 2019; Nagesh et al., 2023) by shifting from post-hoc explanation or recourse generation to actively probing and correcting cognitive anchoring in clinicians, a direction motivated by cognitive science work on diagnostic bias (Croskerry, 2002; Buçinca et al., 2021). The key architectural departure is the dual-predictor design with a sparse self-attention mask that explicitly models the cognitive gap between AI and human diagnostic reasoning, combined with a cognitive gap loss that encourages the AI to attend to features clinicians systematically overlook.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a clinically significant and underexplored problem—cognitive anchoring in rare disease diagnosis—by coupling generative counterfactuals with an explicit model of clinician behavior, offering a more actionable decision-support paradigm than static predictions. However, the evidence base is constrained: several datasets are very small (36 patients for HCM, 10 positive IHPRF3 cases), the 'human simulation' relies on logistic regression as a proxy for clinician judgment on public benchmarks, and the clinical evaluation covers only 30 expert-reviewed cases. Open questions include whether the counterfactual suggestions actually shift clinician behavior in real workflows, how the framework generalizes to multi-class rare disease settings beyond binary classification, and whether the cognitive gap loss captures genuine anchoring dynamics or merely a statistical regularity in the training labels.
