## Problem

PINN training is frequently paralyzed by gradient pathology: the PDE residual gradient and boundary-condition gradient point in opposing directions, trapping optimization in suboptimal local minima. The paper identifies the root cause as the intrinsic non-uniqueness of the differential operator, which creates a connected manifold of zero-residual solutions (a loss valley) in parameter space, and shows that existing remedies—adaptive weighting, hard constraints—either fail to eliminate this ill-conditioning or are restricted to simple geometries.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a PDE operator N with source term f, composite boundary conditions (Dirichlet, Neumann, Robin) on domain Ω, and spatial collocation points. The network outputs u_θ(x), but the final solution is u_θ(x) + c, where the additive offset c is solved analytically (closed-form weighted least squares for linear zeroth-order terms) or via a few Newton iterations (nonlinear case) at every training step and is detached from backpropagation. This means the network is never directly constrained to the correct absolute value, only to the shape of the solution up to a constant.

## Method

CAML (Constraint-Aligned loss with Manifold Lifting) has two mechanisms. First, aligned constraints introduce a solvable additive constant c into every zeroth-order term of both the PDE residual and the boundary loss, transforming the well-posed problem into a controllable ill-posed one that enlarges the overlap between PDE-admissible and boundary-admissible solution sets, thereby reducing the distance the optimizer must traverse along the residual valley. Second, a delay factor λ(t) gates the residual loss to zero during early training, preventing the optimizer from prematurely falling into a high-curvature region of the valley far from the true solution. Theoretically, the paper proves the existence of a connected zero-residual manifold (Theorem 4.1) and shows via a normal-tangent decomposition that the boundary gradient generically has a normal component opposing the residual gradient (Lemma 4.2), providing the geometric justification for why aligned constraints mitigate the conflict.

## Results

On the MLP backbone, CAML reduces the number of iterations to reach the target relative L2 error by 4–15× compared to the classic PINN baseline (e.g., 1,577 steps vs. 10,127 on Heat; 1,004 vs. 15,375 on Helmholtz). CAML achieves the lowest final L2 error in nearly all 12 architecture–PDE combinations, with the best single result being 1.56e-4 on Helmholtz (MLP) versus 2.21e-2 for PINN. Several baselines (PINN, L∞-PINN, DB-PINN) fail to converge within the iteration budget on at least one PDE–architecture pair, while CAML succeeds in all but one (Poisson with PirateNets). The gradient cosine similarity (fraction of iterations with cos(φ)>0) increases from ~5% for standard PINN to ~39–55% for CAML across architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CAML outperforms all five baselines on both convergence speed (Stp) and final accuracy (L2) across the four PDE benchmarks and three architectures. Against the classic PINN (Raissi et al., 2019), CAML reduces iteration counts by 4–15× and lowers L2 error by 1–2 orders of magnitude (e.g., 1.16e-3 vs. 7.52e-3 on Heat). L∞-PINN (Wang et al., 2022a) achieves a higher gradient cosine similarity (up to 87%) by forcing alignment at critical points, but this is local and passive; it still fails on Heat (MLP) and Poisson (PirateNets), and its L2 errors remain 10–100× worse than CAML where it does converge. SA-PINN (McClenny & Braga-Neto, 2023) and BRDR (Chen et al., 2025) both use adaptive weighting but cannot match CAML's convergence speed (e.g., 8,958 vs. 1,577 steps on Heat) or final accuracy. DB-PINN (Zhou et al., 2025a) shows the weakest performance among the adaptive-weighting baselines, failing on Poisson (MLP and PirateNets) and achieving L2 errors 10–50× higher than CAML on the remaining benchmarks. The consistent advantage across MLP, PirateNets, and PINNsFormer backbones confirms that CAML's gains stem from the loss-geometry modification rather than architecture-specific effects.

## Relation to Prior Work

CAML departs from the dominant line of PINN training work that addresses gradient pathology through adaptive reweighting (SA-PINN, BRDR, DB-PINN) or optimizer modifications (DCGD, ConFIG), arguing that these only balance gradient magnitudes without fixing the underlying ill-conditioned geometry. It also contrasts with hard-constraint ansatz methods (Sun et al., Sukumar & Srivastava) that eliminate boundary loss entirely but require geometry-specific distance functions. The paper's core departure is a geometric reinterpretation: by proving the PDE residual loss admits a connected manifold of global minimizers (Theorem 4.1), it reframes gradient conflict as a structural consequence of operator non-uniqueness rather than a mere scale mismatch, and introduces an additive offset c to 'lift' the solution manifold, enlarging the feasible overlap between PDE and boundary constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical characterization of the PDE residual loss valley (infinite Hessian condition number in function space, ~10^22 in parameter space) provides a principled explanation for a long-observed but poorly understood training failure mode, and the aligned-constraint mechanism is general across Dirichlet/Neumann/Robin conditions and architectures. However, the method requires at least one non-trivial zeroth-order term (degenerating under pure Neumann BCs) and is currently limited to steady-state PDEs; extending to time-dependent problems with initial conditions introduces a three-way gradient interaction that the authors flag as open. The ablation revealing that the delay factor adds no benefit for simple boundary conditions but is critical for strongly nonlinear ones (Poisson) offers useful practical guidance on when each component is needed.
